 ABSTRACT
 Micro-scaled high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (micro-HTGRs) offer a
promising option for reliable power in remote or off-grid locations. While the
safety characteristics of modular HTGRs have been widely studied, a micro-
HTGR configuration alters several key thermal-fluid phenomena that govern
both normal operation and passive decay-heat removal. In many proposed
concepts, the reactor vessel is oriented horizontally and integrated into an ISO
shipping container to enhance transportability and modular deployment. This
report documents a Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) exercise
focused on the thermal hydraulic safety phenomena relevant to all micro-HTGRs.
The objective is to systematically identify, describe, and rank the importance,
uncertainty, and modeling complexity of the key phenomena that control core
and vessel temperatures during normal operation, pressurized conduction
cooldown (PCC), and depressurized conduction cooldown (with air ingress)
conditions.

 v

 vi

 SUMMARY
 The PIRT exercise for prismatic-block micro-HTGRs concentrates on core
heat-transfer pathways, helium coolant behavior, external natural convection and
radiation from the vessel to the ISO container environment, and the influence of
graphite material properties and irradiation-induced changes. Attention is also
given to phenomena that are affected by vessel orientation, such as buoyancy-
influenced laminarization of forced convection in the fuel channels, stratification
of helium and air in plena and the container, and azimuthal variations in external
Nusselt number around the horizontal vessel. The identified phenomena are
grouped into four categories: (1) thermal hydraulics; (2) materials & structures;
(3) installation, fabrication and QA; and (4) environmental/external factors.
Subcategories within thermal hydraulics include (1.1) heat transfer; (1.2) fluid
dynamics; (1.3) thermodynamics; and (1.4) startup/shutdown and transient
behavior. Subcategories within materials and & structures include (2.1) material
properties; (2.2) physiochemical changes; and (2.3) aging and degradation. Each
category is described in terms of its impact on reactor safety performance,
associated uncertainties, and modeling/experimental complexity. A preliminary
ranking is then proposed to guide subsequent experimental design, code
development, and validation efforts.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Micro-scaled high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (micro-HTGRs) are being developed to provide
clean, resilient energy in remote locations and distributed applications. Due to changes in geometric scale,
several safety related and normal operation phenomena in wide range of physics can be impacted. For
example, a prominent design feature of some concepts is a horizontally oriented reactor vessel housed
within a standard ISO shipping container to facilitate transport by truck, rail, and ship without
disassembly. While this configuration offers clear logistical advantages, it also modifies several
thermal-fluid phenomena that govern core cooling during both normal operation and off-normal
conditions.
 Under normal operation, heat is removed primarily by forced convection of helium through prismatic
fuel blocks, followed by heat transfer from the vessel to the container environment via radiation and
natural or forced convection. During a pressurized conduction cooldown (PCC) event, decay heat is
transported by a combination of conduction and radiation within the graphite core, limited natural
circulation in the helium, and external natural convection and radiation from the vessel to the surrounding
air in the container.
 Historically, most HTGR safety assessments have focused on vertically oriented cores, such as the
MHTGR-350, where decay heat removal is facilitated by relatively symmetric vertical buoyancy-driven
flows and well-characterized reactor cavity cooling systems (RCCS). In contrast, a horizontally oriented
micro-HTGR experiences:
• Reduced gravitational head for natural circulation in helium loops,
• Strong azimuthal variations in external natural convection around the horizontal vessel,
• Potential stratification of helium in the core and plena, and
• Container-scale air recirculation patterns that couple tightly to reactor cooling.
 To support design, scaling, and safety analysis of such systems, a structured Phenomena
Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) exercise has been initiated for micro-HTGRs. The PIRT is
performed in a semi-structured way. A preliminary list of key phenomena and phenomena categories
were first pre-identified from past micro-HTGR studies and initial scoping analyses. The items and sub-
phenomena within each category are then refined and elaborated by participants based on their expertise.
This approach promotes focus on high-value issues while still allowing sufficient detail for meaningful
ranking.

 3. ORGANIZATION OF THE EXERCISE
 The organization of the Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) exercise for the micro-
HTGRs is designed to align closely with the preliminary PIRT tables (Ref. 6) already prepared for
Normal Operation (NO), General Loss-of-Forced Cooling (GLOFC), Pressurized LOFC (PC), and
Depressurized LOFC (DC). The exercise proceeded in a series of stages beginning in August 2025 and
culminating in a final report in first quarter of 2026.
1. Preliminary phenomena identiﬁcation (August 30, 2025)
 The organizing team ﬁnalizes the list of candidate phenomena for the micro-HTGRs, grouped
 into four categories and mapped to scenario-speciﬁc entries (NO, GLOFC, PCC, DLOFC, and
 TR).
2. Deﬁnition of PIRT participants (September 30, 2025).
 Participants are selected from DOE-NE programs, national laboratories, universities, the U.S.
 Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and industry microreactor vendors. Roles are assigned to
 ensure coverage of thermal-hydraulics, materials, fuel performance, and risk-informed
 analysis.
3. Distribution of original PIRT draft (November 2025).
 The original PIRT draft—containing detailed descriptions of the four categories, the
 preliminary NO / GLOFC / PC / DC PIRT tables, and a draft category-level ranking—is sent to
 participants for review. Importance and SOK rankings from the preliminary micro-HTGR
 tables are clearly indicated to facilitate discussion.
4. Collection of written feedback (December 15, 2025).
 Participants submit comments on completeness, clarity, and rankings, including proposed
 changes to Importance and SOK values in the Normal Operation and LOFC PIRT tables (e.g.,
 core coolant bypass ﬂow, core ﬂow distribution, vessel emissivity, decay heat, air ingress,
 etc.).
5. Integration of feedback and edited PIRT draft (January 15, 2026).
 The organizer consolidates comments into an edited PIRT draft.
6. Hybrid PIRT full-day meeting (February 2026).
 A full-day hybrid (in-person and virtual) meeting is held to:
 - Finalize the set of phenomena in each category;
 - Reconcile Importance and SOK rankings for the scenario-specific tables;
 - Develop a consensus category-level ranking that synthesizes the scenario-specific results.
7. Draft PIRT write-up (February 2026).
 A draft write-up is issued, containing:
 - Finalized NO, GLOFC, PC, and DC PIRT tables;
 - A cross-scenario summary of phenomena that consistently rank high in Importance or low in
 SOK;

 3

 - A category-level ranking (Section 5) derived directly from the scenario-specific tables.
8. Final participant comments (March 15, 2026).
 Participants may submit minor corrections and clariﬁcations; major technical disagreements
 are documented as “minority viewpoints” in an appendix if necessary.
9. Final PIRT report (April 15, 2026).
 The ﬁnal report is issued as the reference PIRT for the micro-HTGR, and will be used to
 prioritize separate-eﬀects testing, integral experiments, and code-validation work for the
 new designs.

 4. PIRT PLAN
 PIRT development process has been standardized in the nuclear industry, the present work adopts
its procedure from previous works such as Ref 7 . The 9-step PIRT procedure is described in the
present section.
 4.1. Challenge
 The challenge stems from the unique thermal-fluid behavior introduced by the micro-HTGR reactor
vessels. The configuration of such vessels can alter natural circulation, external convection patterns, and
helium stratification, creating uncertainties in heat removal during normal operation and pressurized
conduction cooldown events. Furthermore, the orientation of micro-HTGR reactor vessels (vertical or
horizontal) can give rise to different system responses to such events. These differences necessitate a
systematic evaluation of key phenomena to ensure accurate safety analysis and design validation
regardless of operational state and vessel orientation.
 4.2. Objectives
 The goals of this PIRT are to:
1. Identify the key thermal-ﬂuid and materials phenomena that control temperatures and
 safety margins in a micro-HTGR during normal operation and loss of forced circulation
 scenarios;
2. Qualitatively assess each phenomenon’s impact on safety performance, uncertainty in
 current knowledge, and complexity of modeling and experimentation;
3. Prioritize phenomena for focused research, separate-eﬀects testing, and code validation;

 4.3. Hardware and Scenario
 The hardware for the PIRT is designated as a helium-cooled prismatic-block micro-HTGRs such as
those found in Ref. 8, intended for deployment in a standard ISO shipping-container. During loss of
forced helium circulation, decay heat is removed primarily by block-to-block conduction and radiation
through the core, followed by heat rejection from the vessel/container surface to ambient air. Different
scenarios for the PIRT are identified as
 Normal Operation (NO): Covers steady and slowly varying conditions from 20%–100% power,
including start-up, power maneuvering, normal shutdown, and transportation from one site to another.

 4

 General Loss-of-Forced Cooling (GLOFC): Represents a reduction or loss of active forced cooling
where heat removal shifts from forced convection to conduction and radiation within the core, along with
passive heat rejection from the vessel to surroundings.
 Pressurized LOFC (P-LOFC): A loss or significant reduction of forced cooling where system
remains pressurized and the coolant remains in the system. Scenario is characterized by buoyancy-driven
helium flow, plumes, stratification, and passive heat transfer to vessel and surroundings.
 Depressurized LOFC (D-LOFC): A loss or significant reduction of forced cooling where system
depressurizes, reducing helium convection effectiveness and increasing risk of air ingress, oxidation.
 Transportable Reactors (TR): Covers phenomena relevant to shutdown, transportation, storage, and
post-transport deployment of containerized micro-HTGRs. TR is not a power-operation or LOFC
transient scenario but is treated as a separate PIRT condition because transportability introduces distinct
conditions and external hazards that can affect vessel/container heat rejection, accessible surface
temperature, shielding, structural integrity, subcriticality, and post-transport readiness.
 4.4. Evaluation Criteria
 Peak fuel and vessel temperatures, flow distribution in core, and stability margins, have been
identified by the participants as the figures of merit (FOMs) for the different scenarios.
 Peak Fuel and Vessel Temperatures: These represent the highest temperatures in the fuel and
reactor vessel, which are critical for maintaining fuel integrity and structural safety under both normal and
off-normal conditions.
 Flow Distribution in Core: Refers to how helium coolant is distributed among the prismatic block
channels; uneven distribution can lead to localized hot spots.
 Stability Margins: Indicates the reactor’s ability to maintain stable thermal-hydraulic behavior
without instabilities or oscillations to maintain safety and predictability.
 4.5. Review of Current Knowledge Base
 The PIRT panel members are all subject matter experts with extensive experience in high-temperature
gas-cooled reactor technologies and have recently contributed to research on HTGR or micro-HTGR
systems. The panel includes specialists in modeling and simulation, experimental testing, reactor design,
and licensing to comprehensively assess the importance of thermal-fluid and materials phenomena in
horizontally oriented micro-HTGRs from multiple perspectives.

 4.8. Knowledge Level Ranking
 For this PIRT exercise, a knowledge level was assigned to each phenomenon to reflect the current
understanding and ability of characterization and/or modeling. This ranking considers both the adequacy
of existing models and the availability of experimental data, providing an overall measure of confidence
in predicting or validating the phenomenon and its effects on the safety parameters. High knowledge
indicates that the phenomenon is well understood and can be accurately represented with existing tools

 6. IDENTIFIED PHENOMENA WITH HIGH IMPACT
 The PIRT is performed in a semi-structured way: key phenomena categories are pre-identified and
fixed, while the detailed items within each category are discussed and elaborated by participants. For
micro-HTGRs, the phenomena below are considered central to core and vessel thermal behavior during
normal operation and PCC considering their effects on the FOMs.
 6.1. Thermal Hydraulics
6.1.1. Heat Transfer Mechanisms

 Heat transfer in a micro-HTGR involves coupled mechanisms within the core, vessel, and container:
• Conduction in graphite and structural materials (NO-18, PC-5, DC-1) – Axial and radial
 conduction through fuel compacts, graphite blocks, reflector regions, and support structures governs
 how decay heat migrates from the fuel to the vessel during PCC. Conduction is more significant in
 microreactors compared to full-scale reactors due to the smaller size and higher surface-area-to-
 volume ratio. Irradiation-induced changes in graphite thermal conductivity are particularly important
 (see NO-19). Consideration should be given to impurities' impact on graphite thermal properties. At
 normal operating temperatures below 800°C, graphite conduction properties are well-studied;
 however, uncertainty increases at higher temperatures or under novel material configurations.
• Radiation between blocks and to the vessel (NO-20, PC-2, GLOFC-5, GLOFC-6) – Under PCC,
 radiative exchange between prismatic blocks and between the core and vessel can dominate the heat
 transfer path, especially as graphite conductivity degrades and inter-block gaps form. This is
 dependent on graphite emissivity, which can change due to surface topology, oxidation, or the
 presence of impurities. The emissivity of the core barrel and vessel is well understood for standard
 conditions (GLOFC-5, GLOFC-6), but surface changes due to oxidation or graphite grade variations
 introduce uncertainty (NO-20, SOK = L).
 - Vertical Orientation: Radiative exchange between prismatic blocks is azimuthally uniform. The
 vessel is uniformly cooled by the external environment, so the radiation heat transfer path is
 symmetric around the vessel circumference. Core barrel and vessel emissivity are well-
 characterized and relatively easy to calculate (GLOFC-6, SOK = H).
 - Horizontal Orientation: Block-to-block conduction and radiation vary along the azimuthal
 direction — a variation that is typically absent in vertically oriented reactors. External natural
 convection is highest at the lowest elevation of the horizontal cylinder (see Figure 2), due to
 boundary layer separation of the naturally rising air plume. This means blocks at the top of the
 core are less effectively cooled externally, making radiation between top-side blocks and from
 those blocks to the vessel more critical and less predictable. Variation in graphite emissivity
 across different graphite grades and surface changes due to oxidation compound this uncertainty
 (NO-20, Importance = H, SOK = L).
• Convection in helium coolant channels (NO-1, NO-2, NO-9, NO-10) – During normal operation,
 forced convection in hexagonal fuel and reflector channels is the primary mode of heat removal from
 the core. In a horizontal orientation, the flow direction is no longer aligned to intentionally oppose
 buoyancy forces — unlike vertically oriented HTGRs where downward flow promotes turbulent
 mixing. This means that buoyancy can either aid or oppose forced flow locally, altering heat transfer
 coefficients and promoting laminarization, particularly at low flow rates and high heat fluxes. The
 smaller dimensions of the microreactor core mean a large fraction of flow may be in the developing
 regime, potentially transitioning to turbulence scenarios (NO-9). Core flow distribution is also
 affected by non-homogeneous bypass flows (NO-1), which must be distinguished between the active
 core region and the reflectors.

 18

• Natural convection and radiation from vessel to container air (TR-1, TR-2) – During both PCC
 and reduced-flow events, external natural convection and radiation from the reactor vessel to the ISO
 container air, and ultimately to the environment, govern long-term heat removal. DOT regulations
 require that the vessel surface temperature be kept below 85°C during transport. Container geometry,
 ventilation layout, and any external piping or shielding structures all influence the SOK for this
 phenomenon.
 - Vertical Orientation: External natural convection around a vertical cylinder is axially uniform —
 the Nusselt number does not vary azimuthally, so the vessel is uniformly cooled around its
 circumference. Heat removal boundary conditions are symmetric, well-characterized, and
 supported by established RCCS designs in reactor cavity cooling systems.
 - Horizontal Orientation: Azimuthal variations in Nusselt number around the horizontal cylinder
 lead to non-uniform cooling of the vessel and asymmetric core temperatures. The vessel bottom is
 cooled more effectively than the top, creating a persistent thermal gradient across the vessel
 cross-section. The reactor is housed within a standard ISO shipping container rather than an
 engineered reactor cavity, meaning the external heat removal boundary conditions are geometry-
 and ventilation-dependent and less well-characterized (TR-1, Importance = H, SOK = M; SOK
 becomes M if external structures such as piping are attached to the vessel).
Impact
 Heat-transfer mechanisms directly determine peak fuel and graphite temperatures, which are central
metrics for core integrity and fuel performance. Accurate representation of conduction/radiation in the
graphite, convection in helium, and external vessel cooling is essential for demonstrating adequate safety
margins.
Potential Consequences
• Underprediction of conduction or radiation can lead to non-conservative estimates of fuel temperature
 during PCC.
• Misrepresentation of azimuthal external cooling may underestimate hot regions at the top of the core.
• Misestimation of helium heat-transfer coefficients during normal operation may affect limits for
 power level and flow.
6.1.2. Fluid Dynamics

 The fluid dynamics of helium and container air are strongly influenced by the horizontal orientation
and compact layout:
• Buoyancy-influenced forced convection and laminarization (NO-10, PC-6) – In heated channels,
 gas flow undergoes temperature increase and density reduction in the flow direction. This is
 compensated by a velocity increase — keeping Reynolds number unchanged — but this flow
 acceleration can cause flow stabilization or laminarization, resulting in heat transfer degradation
 (Degraded Heat Transfer, DTHT). The onset of laminarization is characterized by the acceleration
 parameter Kv = (ν/V²)(dV/dx), with laminarization occurring when Kv approaches approximately
 3×10⁻⁶.
 - Vertical Orientation: Under normal operating conditions, the flow direction in vertically oriented
 HTGRs is downward to promote turbulent mixing by forcing flow against buoyant forces. This
 counter-buoyancy flow actively suppresses laminarization and maintains turbulent heat transfer
 coefficients. Buoyancy-influenced convection and laminarization in vertical channels are well-
 studied, with experiments at CCNY in the last decade improving the SOK (PC-6 Vt: SOK = H).

 19

 - Horizontal Orientation: The flow direction is not opposite to the buoyancy forces, so the
 turbulence-promoting advantage of the downward flow configuration is absent. This makes flow
 laminarization one of the most significant expected differences between vertical and horizontal
 orientations. Laminarization can lead to degraded turbulent heat transfer even at supercritical
 Reynolds numbers, resulting in higher-than-expected temperatures in the solid fuel-moderator
 matrix. The effect is more pronounced at low flow rates and high heat fluxes. This phenomenon
 has high importance and low SOK (NO-10, Importance = H, SOK = L).
• Coolant bypass flow (NO-1) – Bypass flows in prismatic HTGRs refer to portions of the helium
 coolant that do not follow the intended coolant channel pathways within the fuel blocks. Instead,
 helium flows through unintended leakage paths formed by gaps between adjacent prismatic graphite
 blocks, including fuel elements, reflector blocks, and structural interfaces. These gaps may arise from
 manufacturing tolerances, irradiation-induced dimensional changes, or thermal distortions. A
 distinction should be made between bypass in the active core region and in the reflectors.
 - Vertical Orientation: Bypass gap distributions are primarily driven by manufacturing tolerances
 and irradiation-induced dimensional changes. Gravity assists downward forced flow, and bypass
 leakage patterns are relatively predictable. SOK is Low with respect to geometrical distortions,
 otherwise Medium (NO-1 Vt: Importance = H, SOK = L).
 - Horizontal Orientation: Gravitational body forces act perpendicular to the primary flow axis,
 creating azimuthal asymmetry in gap distributions. Blocks at the top of the core may have
 different contact conditions from those at the bottom due to gravitational settling and gravity-
 induced contact forces. Density gradients in the radial direction can lead to stratification, adding
 further complexity to bypass flow prediction (NO-1 Hz: Importance = H, SOK = L).
• Jet mixing and stratification in plena (NO-8, PC-1) – Hot helium exiting the core enters outlet
 plena where jet interaction, buoyancy, and geometry determine mixing. The impact of hot spots on
 the vessel ultimately depends on the external environment and is design-specific.
 - Vertical Orientation: Buoyancy acts vertically and is aligned with the primary flow direction. Hot
 helium jets from coolant channels enter the outlet plenum and mix relatively symmetrically.
 Stratification behavior is manageable and relatively well-studied. CFD calculations may be useful
 for detailed analysis (NO-8 Vt: Importance = H, SOK = M).
 - Horizontal Orientation: Gravitational effects cause pronounced vertical temperature stratification
 in plena and ducts, where hotter helium rises to the top and cooler helium settles to the bottom of
 the plenum cross-section. The difference in buoyancy force direction affects the mixing and
 temperatures of surrounding materials in the outlet plenum. Since gravity plays an important role
 under non-energetic convection regimes, the mixing of jets in the plenum is influenced by the
 flow direction, creating asymmetric hot-spot locations on the vessel surface. A smaller plenum in
 the microreactor further reduces mixing and increases uncertainty. Under D-LOFC conditions,
 horizontally oriented reactors may experience more rapid stratified flow and air ingress as the
 break occurs at the level of the core (NO-8 Hz: Importance = H, SOK = L).
• Container-scale air circulation — Mobile micro-HTGRs (TR-2) – Within the ISO container, air is
 driven by natural convection from vessel heating and by any engineered vents or forced cooling. The
 container's geometry and ventilation layout determine flow paths and local heat-transfer coefficients
 on the vessel.
 - Vertical Orientation: Vertically oriented reactors are housed in a permanent reactor building with
 a dedicated Reactor Cavity Cooling System (RCCS). The cavity geometry and cooling system are
 specifically engineered and qualified, providing well-characterized and symmetric heat removal
 from the vessel.

 20

 - Horizontal Orientation: The reactor is housed within a standard ISO shipping container,
 replacing the engineered reactor cavity and RCCS with an uncharacterized geometry-dependent
 air circulation environment. As the air circulation is induced in the container from the container
 doors to different container roof exhausts, the varying internal geometries lead to variations in
 heat transfer rates across the vessel surface. This represents an entirely new boundary condition
 not present in vertical designs (TR-2, Importance = H, SOK = M).
• Coolant flow friction and viscosity effects (NO-9, NO-28) – Viscous flow effects, including mixed
 convection at smaller dimensions, determine core temperatures. The smaller size of the microreactor
 core can lead to a large fraction of the region being in the developing flow regime, resulting in
 transition-to-turbulence scenarios.
 - Vertical Orientation: Flow friction and viscosity effects are well-characterized for standard
 prismatic HTGR geometries. The developing flow region is a smaller fraction of the total flow
 length in full-scale reactors, and mixed convection behavior is relatively well-understood with
 buoyancy and forced flow aligned.
 - Horizontal Orientation: The compact layout of the microreactor means a larger fraction of the
 core is in the hydrodynamic developing regime. Mixed convection effects — where buoyancy
 acts perpendicular to the forced flow axis — are less well studied for horizontal configurations
 and can lead to azimuthally varying friction factors and heat transfer coefficients across different
 channel positions (NO-9, Importance = H, SOK = M).
• Core flow distribution (NO-2) – For a given bypass, the non-homogeneity of distribution of flow
 within the core affects local fuel and coolant temperatures.
 - Vertical Orientation: Flow distribution within the active core is governed by pressure drop
 balance across parallel channels. With downward forced flow opposing buoyancy, turbulent
 mixing is promoted and the flow distribution is relatively stable and symmetric across the core
 cross-section (NO-2 Vt: Importance = H, SOK = M).
 - Horizontal Orientation: The flow direction is no longer aligned to oppose buoyancy forces.
 Radial density gradients in the horizontal plane can drive secondary flows and promote
 stratification across the core cross-section. Channels at different azimuthal positions experience
 different buoyancy contributions, leading to non-homogeneous flow distribution. The added
 influence of density gradients in the radial direction leading to stratification is unique to
 horizontal orientation (NO-2 Hz: Importance = H, SOK = M).

 Uncertainties in fluid dynamics can lead to substantial uncertainty in local heat-transfer coefficients,
stratification patterns, and mixing times, which translate into uncertainties in core hot-spot predictions
and vessel temperatures. Incorrect assumptions about stratification or natural-circulation strength can
misrepresent the time evolution of PCC temperatures.
6.1.3. Thermodynamics

 Thermodynamic phenomena relevant to micro-HTGRs include:
• Decay-heat generation and temporal evolution (NO-21) – The magnitude and time dependence of
 decay heat dictate the long-term load on conduction, radiation, and natural convection paths during
 PCC. The ANS standard for decay heat is based on LWR data; the applicable HTGR-specific
 reference is Gulf-GA-A12499. This phenomenon has high importance and medium SOK (NO-21,
 Importance = H, SOK = M).

 21

• Core specific heat — heat capacity of graphite and structural materials (GLOFC-3, NO-12) –
 The large thermal inertia of graphite moderates temperature transients, giving a slow accident
 temperature response. However, this stored energy must eventually be removed through vessel and
 container heat-transfer paths. This is a function of core design and is well-understood (GLOFC-3,
 Importance = H, SOK = H).
• Equation of state and thermodynamic properties of helium – At core operating pressures and
 temperatures, helium properties influence density differences, sound speed, and compressibility,
 affecting both forced convection and natural circulation.
• Helium stratification during PCC (PC-1, DC-13) – Without forced flow in horizontally aligned
 reactor geometries, gravitational body forces can stratify the helium due to temperature gradients.
 This stratification unevenly distributes heat, resulting in different temperatures in the graphite matrix
 across the core cross-section.
 - Vertical Orientation: During PCC, natural circulation is driven by the full gravitational head of
 the fluid column, which is aligned with the flow axis. Stratification in the plenum is manageable
 and well-studied. The natural circulation loop provides meaningful residual heat removal.
 - Horizontal Orientation: The stratified helium lies between the heat source (decay heat in the fuel
 compacts) and the heat sink (vessel wall), actively impeding the primary heat removal path
 during PCC. Hotter helium rises to the top of the core cross-section, resulting in higher
 temperatures in the upper graphite matrix. Furthermore, the gravitational head available to drive
 natural circulation is substantially smaller compared to a vertical configuration, leading to slower
 natural circulation flow and diminished convective heat removal (PC-1, DC-13: Importance = H,
 SOK = M).

 Thermodynamic properties are generally well-known, but their accurate implementation is necessary
to capture time scales of temperature rise and cooldown. Non-conservative assumptions about decay-heat
level or graphite heat capacity can underestimate peak temperatures during long-term PCC transients.
6.1.4. Start-Up/Shutdown and Transient Behavior

 Start-up, load-following, and upset transients introduce time-dependent couplings between
neutronics, thermal-hydraulics, and structural behavior:
• Initial heat-up of core and vessel (NO-21) – Spatially varying power and flow distributions
 influence how quickly different regions of the core reach operating temperature.
• Pump coastdown and partial-loss-of-flow events (NO-27, PC-7) – Horizontal orientation and
 reduced natural-circulation head influence the time evolution of temperatures during pump trips and
 coastdown events. The lower gravitational head in a horizontal configuration leads to slower natural
 circulation flow, lessening the convective contribution to heat removal. Mixed convection effects are
 less well studied, and degradation of heat transfer under partial loss of flow is a concern (PC-7, SOK
 = M).
• Reactivity-temperature feedback coefficients (NO-16) – Temperature-dependent reactivity
 feedbacks (Doppler, moderator temperature, and axial/radial expansion) interact with thermal-fluid
 behavior to shape transient power and temperature histories. The stability of the moderator at high
 temperatures should be measured, and the influence of added feedback stemming from the reduced
 size of the microreactor should be analytically determined (NO-16, Importance = M, SOK = L).

 22

 Transient behavior influences the time scales over which safety systems must respond and may
change the ranking of phenomena (e.g., stratification vs. mixing) compared to steady PCC conditions.
Accurate modeling of these transients is critical for demonstrating acceptable peak temperatures and
margins in design-basis events.
 6.2. Materials and Structures
6.2.1. Material Properties

 Materials phenomena are dominated by graphite behavior and vessel/insulation materials:
• Irradiation-induced changes to graphite thermal conductivity (NO-19, DC-1) – Neutron
 irradiation leads to significant reductions in graphite conductivity, particularly in regions of high
 fluence. This degradation alters radial and axial conduction paths from the fuel to the vessel during
 PCC. For temperatures below 850°C, this phenomenon is well-studied (NO-19, SOK = H). Above
 this temperature or with novel materials, uncertainty increases. The impurity content of graphite also
 affects its thermal properties and should be considered.
• Graphite emissivity changes (NO-20) – Graphite emissivity can be strongly dependent upon the
 surface characteristics and any morphological changes due to oxidation under air or moisture ingress
 events, or under the presence of impurities. Variation of emissivity across different graphite grades
 and surface changes due to oxidation are not well-understood, making this a high importance, low
 SOK phenomenon (NO-20, Importance = H, SOK = L).
• Variation in mechanical properties and cracking (NO-22) – Thermal gradients and irradiation can
 induce cracking, further modifying conduction pathways and potentially creating preferential flow or
 hot-spot locations. Matrix material can endure high temperature gradients, but properties are not fully
 standardized (NO-22, Importance = M, SOK = H).
• Block and channel dimensional tolerances — dimensional changes and gap formation (NO-23) –
 Irradiation-induced shrinkage and swelling can open gaps between blocks and at block–vessel
 interfaces, modifying contact conduction and increasing reliance on radiation across gaps. The SOK
 for this phenomenon has significantly improved in recent years (NO-23, Importance = M, SOK = H).
• Vessel and support degradation (NO-29) – Steel vessel, insulation, and support materials have
 temperature-dependent properties and limits, influencing stress, creep, and long-term integrity during
 elevated-temperature PCC. The flux level on the surface of the steel vessel is high due to the smaller
 size of the microreactor and low internal shielding; creep is the main mechanism of concern (NO-29,
 Importance = H, SOK = H).
• Air ingress into helium system (DC-10, DC-11, DC-12) – In certain accident sequences (e.g., a leak
 in the pressure boundary), air can enter helium volumes, leading to changes in density, heat capacity,
 and potential oxidation of graphite and fuel matrix. Chimney effects under a double-break scenario
 can cause air transport to both inlet and outlet plena, though this is very low probability (DC-12).
 - Vertical Orientation: Air ingress following a pressure boundary leak proceeds through buoyancy-
 driven countercurrent flow in the outlet plenum duct. Temperature decays more rapidly in
 microreactors, partially mitigating oxidation concerns. The ingress rate is relatively well-studied
 for vertical geometries (DC-10 Vt: Importance = H, SOK = M).

 23

 - Horizontal Orientation: The pipe break occurs at the level of the core rather than at the top or
 bottom of the vessel. This geometry means air ingress can be more instantaneous and severe, as
 the density difference between incoming air and outgoing helium is exploited directly along the
 horizontal duct at core level. Stratified exchange flow in the horizontal outlet plenum creates
 faster and more severe air ingress pathways compared to vertical designs. This is flagged as very
 high significance for the horizontal case (DC-10 Hz: Importance = H, SOK = M; DC-11 Hz:
 break at core level, Importance = H, SOK = M).
• Impurities and trace gases – Small concentrations of impurities (H₂O, CO, CO₂, N₂, etc.) can alter
 heat-transfer properties and, more importantly, drive chemical reactions with hot graphite.

 These materials phenomena govern how well the core maintains its structural integrity and how
efficiently heat is conducted to the vessel. Overlooking conductivity degradation or gap formation may
result in underpredicted fuel temperatures and misjudged safety margins. Regarding air ingress and
impurities, increased oxygen partial pressure can accelerate graphite oxidation, reducing strength and
changing surface emissivity. Changes in gas properties can also alter natural circulation patterns and
stratification behavior, especially in low-flow PCC regimes. Moreover, the uncertainty in leak size,
ingress rate, and mixing make prediction of these effects non-trivial.
6.2.2. Physiochemical Changes

• Graphite corrosion (DC-7, DC-9) – Stemming from interactions between graphite/oxygen and
 carbon/steam. Fuel/graphite matrix performance with oxygen or moisture attack involves
 consideration for long-term air and moisture ingress involving core (fueled area) degradation.
 Days/weeks are needed for oxidation; moisture ingress is only a concern for sustained exposure at
 850°C and above. Graphite oxidation kinetics are affected by temperature, oxygen content of air, and
 irradiation of graphite. Above 850°C with water/moisture attack, reactions can result in CO
 generation (DC-9, Importance = M, SOK = H).

 Graphite corrosion resulting from graphite/oxygen interactions is exothermic and significantly
reduces the integrity of structural members constructed of graphite.
6.2.3. Aging and Degradation

• Graphite aging (NO-19, NO-22) – Accumulated irradiation and thermal cycling cause gradual
 changes in graphite thermal and mechanical properties. For temperatures below 850°C these changes
 are well-studied; above this range or for novel graphite grades, significant uncertainty remains.
• Vessel and support degradation (NO-29) – High-temperature exposure can lead to creep, oxidation,
 or embrittlement of metallic components. Due to the smaller size of the microreactor and reduced
 internal shielding, flux levels on the vessel surface are high, making creep the main concern.
• Container environment (TR-3) – Long-term operation may change insulation performance,
 coatings, and vent operability inside the ISO container. Container walls may heat up or cool down
 depending on weather conditions, and radiation shielding is necessary to reduce dose to the public
 during transport (TR-3, Importance = H, SOK = M).

 24

 Aging affects the long-term robustness of passive safety. Gradual degradation can shift the balance
among conduction, radiation, and convection, potentially affecting PCC peak temperatures late in life.
While degradation mechanisms are often understood qualitatively, there can be significant uncertainty in
the rate and spatial distribution of changes for micro-HTGR geometries.
 6.3. Installation, Fabrication Processes, and Quality Assurance

 Manufacturing variability affects geometric and material attributes of the core and container:
• Block and channel dimensional tolerances (NO-23) – Variations in block dimensions, coolant
 channel diameters, and alignment can change local flow distribution, pressure drop, and conduction
 paths. The SOK for this phenomenon has significantly improved in recent years (NO-23, SOK = H).
• Surface roughness and coating quality (NO-24, NO-25) – Surface roughness impacts radiative heat
 transfer and convective heat transfer coefficients in helium channels. Coating quality affects radiative
 heat transfer, convective heat transfer, and wall friction (NO-25, Importance = H, SOK = M).
 Coatings on graphite or vessel surfaces affect emissivity and oxidation resistance.
• Container and ventilation geometry (TR-2) – Variation in as-built container internal structures or
 vent locations modifies container airflow patterns and vessel cooling. Container-scale air circulation
 may also be affected by shielding design (TR-2, Importance = H, SOK = M).

 While the overall impact on global heat removal may be moderate, local effects can be significant,
leading to channel-to-channel power/flow imbalances, localized hot spots, or non-uniform vessel
temperatures. Uncertainty in as-built geometry complicates scaling between experiments and the
prototypical design.
 6.4. Environmental/External Factors

• Ambient temperature and weather conditions (NO-30, TR-3) – External air temperature and wind
 affect container heat rejection and vessel surface temperatures. Ambient conditions affect heat
 rejection from the container (NO-30, Importance = L, SOK = M).
• Installation configuration – Whether the container is buried, partially buried, or installed on the
 surface changes boundary conditions for external heat transfer.
• External hazards — accidents and flood (TR-4, TR-5) – Seismic events may alter core alignment,
 gaps, or container ventilation pathways, indirectly impacting thermal-fluid behavior. Under accident
 conditions (e.g., crash), reactor and core integrity must be maintained, and shipping containers must
 be crash-qualified (TR-4, Importance = H, SOK = M). Under flooding conditions during
 transportation, the core should remain subcritical (TR-5, Importance = H, SOK = M).
• Movement and potential reconfiguration of reactor prismatic blocks (TR-4) – The potential
 misalignment of prismatic blocks during transport may alter the core bypass flow distribution.

 Environmental factors introduce variability in boundary conditions. While the direct impact on short-
term safety margins may be modest (if design conservatism is applied), high uncertainty in site conditions
and installation details motivates including these factors in scenario-based sensitivity studies.

 7. FUTURE RESEARCH NEEDS
 The PIRT exercise identifies several high-importance phenomena with critically low state of
knowledge (SOK = L) that must be prioritized in future research. Foremost among these is coolant bypass
flow (NO-1), whereby helium diverts through unintended leakage paths formed by gaps between adjacent
prismatic graphite blocks. These gaps arise from manufacturing tolerances, irradiation-induced
dimensional changes, and thermal distortions. In the horizontal orientation, gravitational body forces
acting perpendicular to the primary flow axis introduce additional azimuthal asymmetry in gap
distributions, with blocks at the top and bottom of the core experiencing different contact conditions, and
radial density gradients further complicating bypass prediction beyond what is observed in vertical
designs. Flow laminarization and degraded heat transfer in horizontal channels (NO-10) represent an
equally urgent gap: without the turbulence-promoting counter-buoyancy flow of vertical designs, helium
is susceptible to laminarization even at supercritical Reynolds numbers, leading to higher-than-predicted
fuel temperatures. Graphite emissivity variation with oxidation and surface condition (NO-20) and helium
stratification with asymmetric jet mixing in horizontal outlet plena (NO-8 Hz) further compound heat
transfer uncertainty during PCC and air-ingress transients, both phenomena carrying high importance and
low SOK specifically in the horizontal configuration.
 At medium SOK, significant research attention is needed for external natural convection and ISO
container cooling (TR-1, TR-2), where the uncharacterized container geometry replaces the engineered
RCCS of modular HTGRs with geometry- and ventilation-dependent boundary conditions; decay-heat
characterization (NO-21), where LWR-based standards do not fully represent micro-HTGR fuel
compositions; natural circulation effectiveness and air-ingress behavior in horizontal configurations (PC-
1, DC-10/11 Hz); and core flow distribution, pump coastdown response, and long-term container aging
(NO-2 Hz, PC-7, TR-3–5). Collectively, these gaps call for a coordinated, multi-scale experimental and
computational effort developed in early engagement with the NRC and aligned with DOE-NE's ART-
GCR program, with uncertainty quantification embedded throughout to ensure research outputs directly
support development and deployment needs.

 Appendix A

 Normal Operation Summary and PIRT Context
 A-1. Scenario Definition
 The Normal Operation (NO) scenario covers steady and slowly varying conditions from 20% to
100% power, including start-up, power maneuvering within operational limits, normal shutdown
evolution, and transportation from one site to another. The primary safety-relevant performance metrics
are: (1) peak fuel and graphite temperatures, (2) vessel temperature distribution (including azimuthal non-
uniformity in a horizontal vessel), (3) helium coolant outlet temperature and pressure drop, and (4)
margin to thermal limits that protect fuel integrity and structural components.
 A-2. Key Physical Picture for a Horizontally Oriented Micro-HTGR
 Under normal operation, heat is generated in the fuel compacts and transported through the prismatic
graphite to helium coolant channels, then carried to the power conversion system. Horizontal orientation
introduces buoyancy-driven secondary flows that can modify channel turbulence, mixing, and heat
transfer—particularly at lower flow rates and elevated heat flux—potentially
promoting laminarization and reduced convective heat transfer in some regions. In addition, inlet and
outlet plena can develop stratification and imperfect mixing, producing non-uniform temperatures that
affect vessel hot-spot locations and downstream component inlet conditions.
 A-3. How the NO PIRT table Should be Interpreted
 The Normal Operation PIRT ranks phenomena using Importance (H/M/L) and State-of-Knowledge
(SOK: H/M/L) consistent with NUREG/CR-6944. High-importance items are those that directly affect
fuel temperature, vessel temperature distribution, flow distribution, and stability margins under expected
operating envelopes. Low or medium SOK indicates where additional experiments, higher-fidelity
modeling, or uncertainty quantification are needed to improve confidence in predictions.
 A-4. Themes Emerging from the NO Table
 Across the NO entries, the highest-priority issues concentrate in three coupled areas:
1. Core ﬂow distribution and bypass behavior (including channel-to-channel maldistribution
 and leakage paths), which determine local heat removal and peak fuel temperature
 sensitivity.
2. Heat transfer and thermal resistances across fuel, graphite, reﬂectors, core barrel, and
 vessel, including emissivity and thermal contact eﬀects that shape azimuthal and axial
 temperature ﬁelds.
3. Plenum mixing / stratiﬁcation and buoyancy-inﬂuenced convection, which can alter outlet
 temperature uniformity and local thermal loads on the vessel and internal structures.

 28

 These themes should guide Normal Operation validation planning: (i) channel-scale heat transfer and
friction under buoyancy influence, (ii) plenum-scale mixing/stratification experiments, and (iii) integrated
system-level confirmation of predicted temperature distributions in a horizontal configuration.

 Appendix B

 General LOFC Summary and PIRT Context
 B-1. Scenario Definition

The General Loss-of-Forced Cooling (GLOFC) scenario represents a global reduction or loss of
active forced cooling in which core heat removal transitions from forced convection to
predominantly conduction and radiation within the core and passive heat rejection from the
vessel to its surroundings. The GLOFC table is intended to highlight the dominant, system-level
heat removal pathways and the key material properties governing them.

 B-2. Key Physical Picture
 In GLOFC, the core’s thermal response is governed primarily by:
• Effective thermal conductivity of the core (fuel + graphite + contact resistances),
• Core heat capacity, which sets the transient time scales, and
• Radiation and convection heat transfer from vessel surfaces, strongly influenced by vessel
 emissivity and external boundary conditions.
 For a horizontally oriented vessel, external natural convection is inherently non-uniform around the
circumference, which can create preferential hot regions if boundary conditions (container airflow,
venting, insulation) are not symmetric.
 B-3. How the GLOFC PIRT Table Should be Interpreted
 The GLOFC PIRT is intentionally compact and focuses on the most fundamental parameters
controlling peak temperature during passive cooldown. High-importance items (often with medium SOK)
identify where uncertainty in graphite conductivity, emissivity, or reflector properties can propagate into
significant uncertainty in predicted peak fuel and vessel temperatures.
 B-4. Themes Emerging from the GLOFC Table
 The GLOFC table emphasizes that passive cooldown confidence depends most strongly on:
1. Core eﬀective conductivity and contact resistances (including irradiation and temperature
 dependence),
2. Vessel emissivity and radiative exchange, and
3. Reﬂector and structural material thermal properties that shape the net
 conduction/radiation network.
 These items are natural anchors for a validation strategy that couples separate-effects graphite
property testing with integral vessel heat rejection experiments in a representative container boundary
condition.

 Appendix C

 Pressurized LOFC Summary and PIRT Context
 C-1. Scenario Definition
 The Pressurized LOFC (P-LOFC) scenario addresses events where forced cooling is lost or
significantly reduced, but the primary boundary remains pressurized, and helium remains the dominant
coolant in the system. The P-LOFC PIRT table focuses on the coupled roles of buoyancy-influenced
helium flow, plumes and stratification, and passive heat transfer to the vessel and surroundings.
 C-2. Key Physical Phenomenon
 At pressure, helium density remains relatively high compared to depressurized conditions, so natural
convection and buoyancy-driven motion in the core and plena can still contribute to heat redistribution—
though in a horizontally oriented system the available gravitational head and characteristic circulation
paths differ substantially from vertical HTGRs. Key behaviors include:
• Hot helium plume formation and stratification in outlet regions,
• Mixing effectiveness in plena and ducts,
• Flow redistribution among channels as density differences evolve, and
• Coupling to vessel heat rejection (radiation + external natural convection).
 C-3. How the P-LOFC PIRT Table Should be Interpreted
 The P-LOFC table identifies phenomena that control peak temperatures during the early and
intermediate phases of cooldown when helium motion is still relevant. Importance captures sensitivity of
peak fuel/vessel temperature to these behaviors; SOK captures the maturity of available data and
modeling capabilities for stratification, plume behavior, and buoyancy effects in a horizontal
configuration.
 C-4. Themes Emerging from the P-LOFC Table
 The P-LOFC entries highlight three linked priorities:
1. Buoyancy-inﬂuenced heat transfer and laminarization potential in heated channels at
 reduced ﬂow,
2. Plenum-scale stratiﬁcation and plume dynamics, which set vessel hot-spot distributions,
3. Radiation/conduction pathways and vessel emissivity, which dominate as the transient
 progresses.
 The recommended validation path is therefore multi-scale: channel-scale heat transfer + friction under
buoyancy influence, plenum-scale stratification experiments, and integrated vessel heat rejection in
representative container conditions.

 Appendix D

 Depressurized LOFC Summary and PIRT Context
 D-1. Scenario Definition
 The Depressurized LOFC (D-LOFC) scenario addresses events where the system depressurizes and
helium density drops, reducing the effectiveness of helium convection while increasing the likelihood
that air ingress (and associated oxidation and mixed-gas heat transfer effects) becomes relevant. The D-
LOFC PIRT table emphasizes gas exchange, oxidation chemistry, and aerosol/dust effects in addition to
the conduction/radiation-dominated heat transfer pathways.
 D-2. Key Physical Picture
 After depressurization:
• Helium-driven convection becomes weak and the core transitions rapidly
 toward conduction/radiation dominated heat transfer.
• Air ingress can occur through breaks or leak paths, driven by density differences and diffusion,
 introducing oxygen and nitrogen into the system.
• Oxygen can drive graphite oxidation, changing surface emissivity, thermal conductivity (through
 damage), and structural integrity, while also producing CO/CO₂ and potentially mobilizing dust.
 D-3. D.3 How the D-LOFC PIRT Table Should be Interpreted
 The D-LOFC table ranks both thermal phenomena and chemical/transport phenomena. In many
designs, D-LOFC peak temperature margins may remain acceptable through conduction/radiation alone,
but uncertainty in air ingress pathways and oxidation kinetics can dominate long-term confidence in
integrity and post-event recoverability. Thus, several D-LOFC items may have High Importance paired
with Low/Medium SOK.
 D-4. D.4 Themes Emerging from the D-LOFC Table
 Key D-LOFC priorities include:
1. Air ingress transport mechanisms (exchange ﬂow, diﬀusion, stratiﬁcation),
2. Graphite oxidation kinetics and coupled heat/mass transfer,
3. Mixed-gas thermophysical properties and heat transfer in partially mixed helium–air
 regions,
4. Dust/aerosol mobilization and deposition, which can aﬀect ﬂow paths, heat transfer
 surfaces, and source term considerations.
 These themes point to an R&D roadmap combining: (i) scaled ingress experiments, (ii)
oxidation/kinetics testing under representative temperatures and gas compositions, and (iii) coupled
thermal-fluid-chemical modeling with uncertainty quantification.
