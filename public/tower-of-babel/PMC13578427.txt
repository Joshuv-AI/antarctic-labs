Dissecting culm strength in wheat: anatomical, biochemical, and genetic insights into lodging tolerance
Authors: Ronak Majid, Jajati Keshari Nayak, Sofora Jan, Sadaf Salam, Amir Nazir, Mohd Anwar Khan, Neeraj Pal, Raju Ratan Yadav, Santvana Tyagi, Pooja Joshi, Narendra Singh Dhaka, Vinay Kumar Singh, Parvaze Ahmad Sofi, Sundip Kumar, Manish K Vishwakarma, Pradeep Bhati, Uttam Kumar, Rajeev Kumar Varshney, Reyazul Rouf Mir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLodging is a major constraint in wheat production, often resulting in substantial yield losses. Resistance to lodging is influenced by various morphological, biochemical, and anatomical traits of the stem, and the relative contribution of different stem parts, particularly individual internodes, to lodging tolerance remains unclear. In this study, key culm-related morphological internode length (IL), internode weight (IW), stem diameter (SD), culm wall thickness (CWT), pith diameter (PD), the stem diameter to culm wall thickness ratio (SD/CWT) and the internode length to internode weight ratio (IL/IW), biochemical (cellulose), and anatomical traits were evaluated across the first three internodes in diverse wheat genotypes. The results revealed that the second internode played the most critical role in lodging tolerance. Lodging-resistant genotypes presented higher PD, CWT, IW, and cellulose content in the second internode, whereas susceptible genotypes presented increased IL and IL/IW ratios. Anatomical analyses revealed that resistant genotypes possessed more vascular bundles, thicker sclerenchyma, well-developed parenchyma, and enhanced lignin deposition in the second internode, collectively contributing to superior mechanical stability. Furthermore, single-marker analysis using SSR markers revealed 12 significant loci associated with 16 culm strength traits, with Gwm337 and Wmc273 explaining the greatest percentage of the phenotypic variance (24.40% and 23.01%, respectively). These findings underscore the pivotal role of the second internode in lodging tolerance and provide valuable molecular markers to support marker-assisted breeding for enhanced culm strength in wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57468-4.Keywords: Wheat, Lodging tolerance, Internode strength, Culm anatomy, SSR markers, Marker-assisted selectionSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is among the most important cereal crops and is essential for food security, economic stability and human nutrition. Its adaptability, high nutritional value, and global prevalence make it a key crop in many regions of the world. However, wheat productivity faces significant challenges from abiotic and biotic stresses, with lodging being a major concern. Global climate change has intensified these abiotic stresses, increasing the susceptibility of modern wheat cultivars to lodging1. Crop lodging, described as the irreversible bending or displacement of crop stems, negatively impacts grain quality, delays harvesting, increases drying costs, and reduces overall yields. Yield losses due to lodging range from 27% to 40% during the heading stage, up to 40% in the first ten days after heading2, and over 24% at the soft dough stage3. Lodging tolerance is a complex quantitative trait shaped by environmental factors and by a combination of the morphological, mechanical, anatomical, and biochemical characteristics of the stem4,5. Biochemical components such as cellulose, lignin, and pectin contribute to culm rigidity5. Furthermore, more positive correlations between lodging tolerance and several anatomical features, such as the number of vascular bundles per mm² and mechanical tissue width, have been established5. Together, these anatomical and biochemical traits play a critical role in determining the resistance of stems to lodging6–10.

Understanding the genetic basis of lodging tolerance is crucial for targeted crop improvement efforts11. Wheat resistance to lodging is closely linked to its genetic makeup, with specific genes and regulatory elements playing crucial roles. Key genes, such as Ces A (Cellulose Synthase) for cellulose synthesis and COMT (Caffeic Acid O-Methyltransferase) for lignin biosynthesis, significantly influence stem strength12. Moreover, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have revealed quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with culm-related traits, providing valuable insights into the chromosomal regions governing lodging tolerance13. Molecular markers, particularly simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers, are highly effective for tracking these QTL-associated genomic regions because of their codominant nature, abundant polymorphism, and widespread genomic distribution. SSR markers facilitate early and precise selection of lodging-tolerant genotypes and are widely used to validate GWAS-identified QTLs, confirming their reliability across populations and environments. Such validation strengthens marker-assisted selection (MAS) pipelines and accelerates the development of lodging-resistant wheat varieties14.

In light of the above background, the present investigation integrates anatomical, biochemical, morphological, and molecular approaches to comprehensively understand lodging tolerance in the first three internodes of wheat. Specifically, in this study, diverse wheat germplasms across different environments were evaluated to identify the variability in lodging-related traits, the biochemical components contributing to culm rigidity were analysed, structural features across the first three internodes were examined, and SSR markers associated with culm strength were validated.

Materials and methods

Plant material

A set of 202 wheat genotypes (Supplementary Table 1) was used in the current study. These genotypes were selected from an initial panel of 4,600 accessions obtained from the DBT wheat collection. Climatic factors, including mean temperature, rainfall, and major wind or storm events, were recorded throughout the season to characterize the environmental influences on stem lodging (Supplementary Table 2). Crop growth stages from tillering to maturity were carefully monitored, and natural lodging events were documented in relation to these stages. Following the assessment of lodging in the field, genotypes showing clear lodging or nonlodging responses were identified and selected for further detailed evaluation. While selecting the final panel, we included all height categories (i.e., tall, medium, and semidwarf).

Experimental design and setup

The experiment was conducted using an augmented block design (ABD) consisting of 22 blocks, each containing 9 genotypes alongside four reference checks (SKU-B 33, SKU-B 233, SKU-B 101, and SKU-B 2591). Trials were conducted at two distinct locations: the Borlaug Institute for South Asia (BISA), Ludhiana, Punjab, India and the Faculty of Agriculture (FoA), Wadura, SKUAST-K, Kashmir, India.

Evaluation of morphological traits

The morphological traits that were measured included plant height (PH), internode length (IL), internode weight (IW), culm or stem diameter (SD), culm wall thickness (CWT), pith diameter (PD) and the ratios of stem diameter to culm wall thickness (SD/CWT) and internode length to internode weight (IL/IW).

Cellulose content estimation

The cellulose content was estimated by following a previously described method15. Three internodes from each wheat stem were collected from both field locations (Wadura and Ludhiana). Samples (internodes) were dried at 80 °C and then cut into smaller pieces. Fifty milligrams of each sample was mixed with a solution of acetic acid, water, and nitric acid (8:2:1 v/v) in preweighed 2 ml screw-capped Eppendorf tubes. The mixture was vortexed and heated in a water bath at 95 °C for 4 h. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was subsequently washed in distilled water followed by 90% ethanol and then dried at 80 °C. Culm strength

Culm strength was evaluated for the first three internodes of 44 selected genotypes (22 resistant and 22 susceptible) (Supplementary Table 1) from both field locations (BISA, Ludhiana and FoA, Wadura). Culm strength was measured using a universal testing machine (model AMT-SC-01521).

Histochemical staining

Lignin was assessed using the Weisner reaction16. The fixed samples were dehydrated with ethanol and xylene and sectioned to a thickness of 20 μm. The sections were immersed in a phloroglucinol solution (prepared at a 2:1 ratio of 3% ethanol and HCl) for 2 min. For toluidine blue staining, the sections were immersed in 0.02% toluidine blue solution for 2 min and then rinsed with distilled water17. Subsequently, the sections were mounted with DPX and examined under a bright-field microscope, and digital images were captured using an Axiocam MRm camera.

Validation of genes/QTLs for culm strength and related traits

Molecular analysis was conducted on the lodging-resistant and lodging-susceptible genotypes on the basis of the biochemical findings (Supplementary Table 1). Total genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using the CTAB method18. Twenty-one SSR markers (Table 1) associated with culm strength and related traits were selected from the literature and validated on 40 genotypes (20 resistant and 20 susceptible) using single-marker analysis (SMA).

Statistical analysis

Two-factor ANOVA was performed using the ‘Agricolae’ package in R, with genotype and location as factors. Tukey’s honestly significant difference (post hoc analysis) was used to assess variation among means. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships among lodging-related traits. SMA/simple linear regression tests were conducted to identify marker‒trait associations, and markers showing significant associations were confirmed as markers associated with the respective traits.

Results

The lodging traits of a total of 202 genotypes were evaluated on the basis of their morphological, biochemical, anatomical, and mechanical traits under both field and controlled laboratory conditions. The genotypes were categorized into two different groups: lodging resistant and lodging susceptible (101 each).

Variations in Culm traits with internode position and environment

Two-factor ANOVA was performed for different morphobiochemical traits in both susceptible and resistant genotypes. The results (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Table 4) revealed significant variation for all the traits examined. Notably, the mean sum of squares for the internodes was significant for most culm traits except for culm wall thickness. When the effect of location on culm-related traits was analysed (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Table 4), a significant mean sum of squares was observed for most culm traits. Interestingly, the interaction between internode and location was not significant for any of the culm traits, indicating a consistent response regardless of geographic setting.

Exploring genotypic variation in culm traits

Descriptive statistics revealed significant variations in several key traits (Figs. 1, 2 and 3c1–g7 and Supplementary Fig S2–S3). Lodging-resistant genotypes presented greater mean stem diameters (3.0, 3.3, and 3.6 mm for the first, second, and third internodes, respectively) than susceptible genotypes did (2.6, 2.9, and 3.1 mm, respectively) (Fig. 1c) (Table 2). The coefficient of variation (CV) for stem diameter (SD) was lower for the resistant genotypes (13.4%, 14.39%, and 12.65%) than for the susceptible ones (15.8%, 16.1%, and 15.3%), indicating less variability within the resistant group. Similar trends were observed for the cellulose content (Fig. 1a), culm wall thickness (Fig. 2c), pith diameter (Fig. 1c) and internode weight (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the mean internode length, internode length-to-weight ratio and stem diameter to culm wall thickness ratio were greater in the susceptible genotypes than in the resistant genotypes (Fig. 2a, d and e). Notably, resistant genotypes displayed greater variability in certain traits, including internode weight, cellulose content, and internode length. The mean culm strength in the second internode was also greater in the resistant genotypes (265 psi) than in the susceptible genotypes (Fig. 1d).

Association of key internode traits with lodging tolerance

To investigate the intertrait relationships, correlation coefficients (r) were calculated (Fig. 4) (Supplementary Fig. S4). The cellulose content was significantly negatively correlated with culm wall thickness (CWT) in the second internode (r = −0.24**) and with the stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (r = −0.38**). Conversely, it was significantly positively correlated with stem diameter (SD; r = 0.18*) and pith diameter (PD; r = 0.31***) in the second internode (Fig. 4b). The internode weight of the second internode was negatively correlated with the internode length-to-internode weight ratio (r = −0.52***) but positively correlated with plant height (r = 0.33***), internode length (r = 0.46***), stem diameter (r = 0.30**), pith diameter (r = 0.28**), culm wall thickness (r = 0.67**), and the stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (r = 0.33***) (Fig. 4b). Pith diameter (PD) was significantly positively correlated with stem diameter (SD) in the second internode (r = 0.95**). Furthermore, PD was negatively correlated with plant height (r = −0.22**), the stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (r = −0.25***), internode length (r = −0.30***), and the internode length-to-internode weight ratio (r = −0.64***) in the second internode (Fig. 4b). Notably, stem diameter was significantly negatively correlated with plant height, internode length, and the internode length-to-internode weight ratio across all three internodes (Fig. 4). Additionally, it was positively correlated with culm wall thickness in the second internode (r = 0.67**). Culm wall thickness was negatively correlated with plant height (r = −0.25***), internode length (r = −0.28***), and the internode length-to-internode weight ratio (r = −0.69***). Furthermore, it was negatively correlated with the stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (r = −0.45***) across all three internodes (Fig. 4).

Comparison of structural and biochemical traits among successive internodes

For Tukey’s post hoc test, internodes were grouped as follows: BA (first-second), CA (first-third), and CB (second-third), corresponding to internode positions A, B, and C, respectively. In the BA comparison (difference between second and first internode mean), lodging-resistant genotypes showed greater differences in pith diameter (0.31 mm) than susceptible genotypes (0.22 mm). Resistant genotypes also exhibited greater differenecs in culm strength (130.6 psi vs. 128.32 psi), internode weight (0.073 mg vs. 0.052 mg) and stem diameter differences (0.32 mm vs. 0.22 mm). The resistant genotypes also presented greater culm strength (130.6 psi vs. 128.32 psi), higher internode weight (0.073 vs. 0.052) and greater differences in stem diameter (0.32 mm vs. 0.22 mm). Biochemical analysis indicated that the cellulose content was significantly greater in the BA-resistant group (3.301 mg) than in the BA-susceptible group (2.52 mg). In contrast, internode length was greater in susceptible genotypes (5.54 cm) than in resistant genotypes (3.72 cm) (Supplementary Table 6).

Key trait groups explain variation in lodging tolerance

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the traits associated with the second internode revealed three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) with eigenvalues greater than 1, explaining 73.30% and 74.09% of the total variability in the lodging-resistant and lodging-susceptible genotypes, respectively. Among the resistant genotypes, PC1 accounted for 35.49% of the total variation, followed by PC2 (22.18%) and PC3 (15.64%). In the susceptible genotypes, PC1 explained 41.64% of the variation, whereas PC2 and PC3 contributed 18.90% and 13.55%, respectively (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 7). In resistant genotypes, PC1 was associated mainly with the stem diameter of the second internode (SD2), culm wall thickness (CWT2), pith diameter (PD2), and internode weight (IW2), indicating that structural stem traits contributed substantially to variability among resistant genotypes. In contrast, PC2 was influenced primarily by internode length (IL2), internode weight (IW2), and plant height (H). For susceptible genotypes, PC1 strongly positively contributed to stem diameter (SD2), internode weight (IW2), pith diameter (PD2), and culm wall thickness (CWT2). Moreover, PC2 was strongly influenced by the stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (SD2/CWT2), internode length (IL2), plant height (H), and internode weight (IW2). Positive and negative PCA loadings indicated corresponding positive and negative associations, respectively, between the principal components and the studied traits.

Genetic variability and heritability of lodging-related traits

The extent of variability for 16 traits, assessed through the phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV), genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV), broad-sense heritability (h2) and genetic advance (GA), is summarized in Supplementary Tables 8 and Supplementary Figs. S1–S3. Heritability was predominantly high across all the traits, indicating strong genetic influence. The genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) values revealed substantial variation for most traits, with internode weight exhibiting the highest GCV in resistant (42.16%) than susceptible (26.45%) genotypes. High GCVs were also observed for the culm wall thickness (CWT), internode length-to-internode weight ratio (IL/IW), and stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (SD/CWT) in both genotype groups. Median GCV value were observed for stem diameter (13.82% in resistant plants; 15.54% in susceptible plants), pith diameter (14.57% in resistant plants; 15.58% in susceptible plants), internode length (19.02% in resistant plants; 16.85% in susceptible plants) and plant height (13.72% in resistant plants; 8.43% in susceptible plants). Genetic advance as a percentage of the mean (GAM) was highest for internode weight (84.91%) in the resistant genotypes and for the internode length-to-weight ratio (56.76%) in the susceptible genotypes, highlighting significant gains for these traits (Supplementary Table 8).

Anatomical differences between lodging-resistant and lodging-susceptible genotypes

Phenotypic variation in pith filling and culm anatomy further emphasized the substantial genetic diversity in lodging-related traits. Visual assessment revealed striking differences in the pith filling trait between the groups. Among the resistant genotypes, 31% exhibited complete pith filling, 27% showed partial filling, and 42% displayed hollow pith structures. In contrast, 97% of the susceptible genotypes presented hollow pith structures, with only 3% exhibiting partial filling, indicating a striking difference between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S6). A detailed investigation of culm anatomy in two contrasting genotypes, SKU-B IW24 (lodging resistant) and SKU-B IW259 (lodging susceptible), revealed significant differences in stem pith characteristics. Lodging-resistant genotypes with solid stems contained substantial amounts of pith material (Fig. 6a), whereas susceptible genotypes presented hollow stems (Fig. 6b). Microscopic examination of transverse stem sections highlighted distinct structural layers: epidermis, sclerenchyma tissues, parenchyma tissues, large vascular bundles, and smaller vascular bundles within the parenchyma matrix (Figs. 6 and 7). Lodging-resistant genotypes displayed higher cellulose and lignin fluorescence (Fig. 6: c1–c6, e1–e6), thicker epidermal cell walls (Fig. 6: c3–c4, e3–e4), more abundant vascular bundles (Fig. 6: c1, e1), more sclerenchyma (Fig. 6: c2, e2), and more parenchyma tissues (Fig. 6: c6, e6). Morphological variations in parenchymal cell shapes were evident, with resistant genotypes featuring round and oval-shaped cells, while susceptible genotypes exhibited shrunken cells (Fig. 6: c5, d5, e5, f5).

SSR-based association analysis of lodging-related traits

The validation of genes and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with culm strength, cellulose content, and related traits was performed using single-marker analysis (SMA). The analysis revealed that 12 of the 21 SSR markers evaluated—Xbarc45 (3 A), Xcfa2262 (3 A), Xgwm191 (3D), Xgwm311 (2D), Xgwm337 (1D), Xgwm480 (3 A), Xgwm501 (2B), Xwmc273 (7B), Xwmc475 (5 A), Xwmc657 (4B), Xwmc702 (7D), and Xwmc705 (5 A)—were significantly associated with traits such as culm wall thickness, pith diameter, stem diameter, internode weight, plant height, and cellulose content across the three internodes. Among these, three markers, Xbarc170 (4 A), Xgwm311 (2D), and Xwmc264 (3 A), were revalidated, confirming their reliability, while the remaining markers represented novel loci associated with lodging tolerance in wheat.

Among the associated markers, Wmc705 was most strongly associated with 10 traits, SD and PD across multiple internodes, culm wall thickness (CWT), internode weight, and cellulose content. Similarly, markers Wmc273 and Gwm337 were associated with 8 traits, including cellulose content, plant height, and various internode structural traits. Markers Xcfa2262 and Gwm480 were associated with 6 traits, whereas Gwm311 and Gwm501 were associated with 4 traits each. The marker Gwm191 was associated with 3 traits, namely, plant height, stem diameter of the second internode, and length of the third internode. Notably, the marker Gwm337 was consistently associated with the cellulose content in the third internode across both environments: BISA (Ludhiana, Punjab) and FoA (SKUAST-K, Wadura, Kashmir. Unlike markers with multiple associations, Wmc475 was linked to only one trait: culm wall thickness (CWT) at the third internode. Details of the validated markers with linked traits are presented in Supplementary Table 9.

Discussion

Stem lodging is a complex trait influenced by environmental factors such as wind, rain, topography and soil conditions as well as plant characteristics, including morphological traits, biochemical composition, anatomical structure and mechanical properties of the culm. Identifying the internodes that most strongly influence lodging resistance is therefore essential for improving plant architecture and breeding lodging-resistant cultivars. Previous studies have demonstrated the significant contribution of specific internodes to lodging susceptibility across different crops. For example, lodging predominantly affects the second internode in rice23, the first internode in maize24, and both the first and second internodes in wheat25. Similar trends have been reported in barley, where the contribution of lower internodes to lodging varies with environmental stress and genotype26. Moreover, factors such as internode length, wall thickness, and pith filling significantly influence lodging dynamics, making targeted genetic improvement a viable strategy8,27.

Morphological determinants of lodging resistance

Our findings demonstrated that the structural traits of the lodging-resistant genotypes included increased culm strength, increased stem diameter, increased cellulose content, increased culm strength, decreased internodes, and a stable stem diameter-to-culm wall thickness ratio (SD/CWT) in the second internode. These results suggest that the second internode acts as the key morphological determinant of lodging tolerance. These characteristics improve stem rigidity and reduce susceptibility to bending under mechanical forces such as grain weight, wind, and rainfall28–32. Thicker culm walls and larger stem diameters reduce stress concentration and the risk of structural failure33 while protecting vascular tissues involved in water and nutrient transport34,35.

In contrast, susceptible genotypes exhibit longer internodes and greater internode weight, which can increase the center of gravity and lodging risk36,37. Thinner culm walls and elongated internodes reduce mechanical strength and increase the vulnerability of stems to bending and breakage under external stress38,39. Resistant genotypes also presented a lower internode length-to-internode weight ratio, indicating greater biomass per unit length, which contributes to stronger stems.

Pith diameter has also emerged as an important trait associated with lodging resistance. Resistant genotypes frequently exhibit solid stems with greater pith filling, which enhances structural strength and dissipates mechanical stress that would otherwise cause lodging28,35. These combined traits highlight the genetic potential of resistant genotypes and provide a foundation for the breeding of lodging-resistant cultivars. Field observations further indicated that stem bending occurred more frequently at the first internode, whereas stem breakage was more common at the second internode. Biochemical determinants of culm strength

Cellulose plays a central role in reinforcing plant cell walls and maintaining culm integrity. As a major structural component of the cell wall, cellulose provides rigidity and resistance to bending, enabling plants to withstand external mechanical stress5,40–45. Increased cellulose deposition in lodging-resistant genotypes likely contributes to improved mechanical strength and improves vascular function, facilitating efficient water and nutrient transport, which indirectly supports structural stability34,35. These findings support previous reports that increased cellulose deposition strengthens culm tissues and enhances lodging resistance36,38.

Culm strength is another key factor contributing to lodging resistance because it allows stems to bend without fracturing under mechanical stress, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of wind and rain. This property enables plants to absorb and dissipate energy effectively, preserving structural integrity during key growth stages5,46. Low culm strength compromises this flexibility, increasing lodging susceptibility. Studies suggest that optimizing culm strength alongside cellulose deposition could significantly increase lodging resistance in cereal crops36,38.

Plant height also influences lodging susceptibility. While semidwarf varieties are generally less prone to lodging, they may exhibit reduced biomass accumulation because of limitations in leaf area density and carbon assimilation efficiency47–49. Conversely, taller varieties often produce greater amounts of biomass but may be more susceptible to lodging because of their higher center of gravity14,29,50. Therefore, breeding strategies that balance plant height with enhanced culm strength could yield cultivars that are both high yielding and lodging resistant. However, our findings indicate that taller genotypes with strong culms can exhibit high lodging resistance, suggesting that enhanced mechanical traits can compensate for height disadvantages32,51.

Anatomical reinforcement of the culm structure

Culm anatomy also plays important roles in determining stem strength and flexibility, which affects a plant’s ability to tolerate external stresses and remain upright. The lodging-resistant genotypes in this study presented greater numbers of vascular bundles, higher vascular bundle density (bundles per unit area), increased sclerenchyma tissue, and higher lignin content35. The greater degree of cellulose deposition observed in resistant genotypes further supports the contribution of cell wall components to culm mechanical stability, which is consistent with previous reports8,52.

Trait interactions and developmental trade-offs

Correlation analysis revealed complex relationships among the morphological and biochemical traits associated with lodging resistance. The cellulose content was negatively correlated with plant height, internode length, and the internode length-to-weight ratio but positively correlated with stem diameter across all three internodes. This pattern suggests that the plant allocates a greater percentage of its biomass to structural support (cell wall synthesis) rather than to rapid elongation53. Moreover, the increased cellulose enhances water transport within the stem, thereby contributing to a greater stem diameter54,55. These relationships suggest that the coordinated regulation of structural traits is involved in maintaining stem strength53–55. The positive correlation between pith diameter and cellulose content in the first and second internodes further supports the hypothesis that early-stage cellulose accumulation enhances pith expansion and contributes to stem integrity5,56. Overall, these findings underscore the complex relationship between biochemical and structural components in the culm, where cellulose and lignin function synergistically to balance strength, flexibility, and water transport, providing a robust framework for improved lodging tolerance.

During early developmental stages, rapid growth and elongation increase plant height, whereas structural reinforcement occurs later through radial thickening of the stem. The negative correlation between internode weight and the internode length-to-weight ratio in the second internode suggests a developmental trade-off between elongation and radial growth9. As plants allocate resources toward vertical growth, investment in cell wall thickening may decrease, potentially reducing lodging tolerance.

Conversely, positive correlations among pith diameter, stem diameter, and culm wall thickness in the second internode indicate that the plant allocates resources to reinforce structural components (stem diameter and culm wall thickness) during the elongation phase. Previous studies have similarly shown that cellulose and lignin collectively contribute to mechanical strength, with cellulose enhancing flexibility and lignin providing rigidity5,29.

Comparative internode analysis and structural fragility

Comparative analysis of internodes revealed that the second internode plays a central role in determining lodging resistance. Compared with other internodes, resistant genotypes presented greater culm wall thickness, higher cellulose content, and stronger culm strength in the second internode. In susceptible genotypes, however, the third internode presented relatively greater culm wall thickness. Differences in traits such as stem diameter, pith diameter, culm wall thickness, cellulose content, and culm strength were more pronounced between the first and second internodes than between the second and third internodes.

Tukey's HSD test confirmed that stem diameter (SD), pith diameter (PD), internode weight (IW), and cellulose content were significantly higher in the "BA" group (second minus first internode) of resistant genotypes, while as internode length (IL) was greater in "BA" group of susceptible genotypes. These trait values indicate a positive association with stem strength. These findings highlight the structural importance of the second internode and suggest that pronounced differences among internodes may increase their vulnerability to external mechanical stress57. Maintaining balanced culm structural traits is therefore essential for minimizing fragility and improving lodging resistance.

Trait selection and genetic variability

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed several culm traits as key contributors to lodging resistance. High PC1 values for traits such as stem diameter, internode weight, culm wall thickness, pith diameter, and cellulose content indicate their importance for selection in breeding programs aimed at improving lodging tolerance58–60. Among the nine principal components (PCs), only three (PC1–PC3) with eigenvalues greater than one were retained, explaining most of the observed phenotypic variation.

Compared with susceptible genotypes, resistant genotypes exhibited slightly lower cumulative variance, suggesting greater trait complexity and heterogeneity among resistant lines. Traits such as internode weight, culm wall thickness, and the SD/CWT ratio presented high genotypic coefficients of variation, indicating strong potential for improvement through selection. High heritability combined with high genetic advance (GA) is more likely to respond robustly to selection61. Traits exhibiting high heritability, such as IL, SD, PD, and IW, reflect the dominance of additive gene effects, suggesting that selection strategies such as mass or pure-line selection would be effective for improving lodging resistance61–64.

Marker–trait associations and breeding implications

Marker–trait association analysis revealed that 12 of the 21 SSR markers evaluated were significantly associated with culm structural traits across three internodes and two environments. These markers are linked to traits such as culm wall thickness, stem diameter, pith diameter, internode weight, plant height, and cellulose content. Several markers have been revalidated19–21, confirming their reliability, whereas others represent potentially novel loci associated with lodging resistance in wheat22.

Markers associated with multiple traits indicate pleiotropic genetic effects or tight linkages among loci controlling related culm traits, enabling the simultaneous improvement of correlated traits such as stem strength and cellulose content. Such associations support the concept of shared genetic regulation of structural traits influencing lodging resistance65. These findings align with those of previous studies emphasizing the role of genetic markers in improving structural integrity and lodging resistance in wheat8,26. The incorporation of validated markers into marker-assisted selection (MAS) programs is essential for enhancing culm properties and lodging tolerance, which are critical for yield stability in wheat and other cereal crops9,29.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated understanding of the morphological, biochemical, anatomical, and genetic factors underlying lodging resistance in wheat. These results highlight the pivotal role of the second internode as a critical structural determinant of lodging tolerance, as resistant genotypes presented greater culm wall thickness, greater stem diameter, greater cellulose content, and greater culm strength. These structural traits collectively increase stem rigidity and mechanical stability, thereby reducing lodging susceptibility under environmental stress.

Through single-marker analysis, the strong associations between several culm-related traits and SSR markers were further identified, with 12 markers showing significant associations across multiple internodes and environments. Among these, three markers—Xbarc170 (4 A), Xgwm311 (2D), and Xwmc264 (3 A)—were revalidated, confirming their reliability, while additional markers represent potential novel loci linked to culm structural traits.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that lodging resistance is governed by a coordinated interaction of morphological, structural, biochemical, and genetic factors. The identified traits and associated molecular markers provide valuable resources for wheat improvement and can facilitate the development of lodging-resistant cultivars through the use of genomics-assisted breeding strategies aimed at improving yield stability under diverse environmental conditions.
