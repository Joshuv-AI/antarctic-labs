From concept to measurement: operationalizing and normalizing Integrity Risk Indicators by SCImago (IRIS)
Authors: Rodrigo Sánchez-Jiménez, Vicente P. Guerrero-Bote, Gali Halevi, Félix De-Moya-Anegón, Daniel Gómez González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Integrity Risk Indicators by SCImago (IRIS) framework, developed by the SCImago Research Group, addresses growing concerns about trust in science by shifting research assessment from traditional output and citation-based measures toward the evaluation of institutional exposure to research integrity risks. Rather than focusing on individual misconduct, IRIS recognizes the central role of institutional incentive systems, governance structures, and operational cultures in shaping research integrity. IRIS provides a constructive, data-informed model for identifying structural vulnerabilities associated with practices such as authorship and affiliation patterns, dissemination and citation dynamics, publication venue credibility, and the sustainability and transparency of publication practices. The indicators are derived from bibliometric and publication data and are intended as signals of potential risk, not as diagnoses or judgments. Applied to Higher Education Institutions included in the SCImago Institutions Rankings, IRIS enables meaningful comparison through z-score normalization, placing heterogeneous indicators on a common, interpretable scale centered on the global mean. The use of standardized scores enhances sensitivity to the magnitude of deviations, allowing IRIS to better function as a risk detection system, rather than a competitive ranking, and to support institutions in strengthening responsible and trustworthy research environments.

Introduction

Concerns about research integrity have moved to the center of scientometrics research and academic governance. This development reflects broader transformations in the governance of science, where increasing attention has been directed toward the reliability, transparency, and societal accountability of research systems [38, 46, 54]. At the same time, the growing reliance on quantitative evaluation mechanisms has intensified concerns regarding the unintended consequences of performance-based assessment, including publication pressure, metric-driven behaviors, and the emergence of questionable research practices [5, 22, 59]. These developments have prompted calls for assessment frameworks that move beyond traditional measures of productivity and citation impact toward a broader consideration of research quality, integrity, and responsible institutional conduct. The rapid growth of global publication output, the expansion of evaluation systems based on bibliometric indicators, and the emergence of questionable research practices have intensified debates about how institutions should be assessed. Traditional performance frameworks have largely focused on scientific impact, productivity, and visibility, while comparatively less attention has been paid to systemic signals of integrity-related vulnerabilities. In recent years, however, a growing body of work has begun to explore how bibliometric data can be used to identify patterns associated with questionable authorship practices, citation manipulation, hyper-prolific publishing, and the use of low-credibility publication venues. These developments have motivated the creation of new tools aimed not at ranking excellence, but at identifying structural risks within research systems.

Existing initiatives such as integrity-oriented dashboards, transparency metrics, and composite indices provide important contributions to this emerging field. Frameworks like RI2 and the Rigor and Transparency Index, for example, focus on dimensions such as reporting standards, reproducibility practices, and editorial policies. While these approaches advance the measurement of research quality and transparency, they primarily operate at the level of journals, disciplines, or reporting practices. Fewer frameworks address integrity-related signals at the institutional level using large-scale bibliometric data. This creates a methodological gap between institutional evaluation systems, which still rely heavily on performance indicators, and emerging integrity-focused approaches that lack institutional granularity. Recent initiatives such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA), the Leiden Manifesto for Research Metrics, and the Hong Kong Principles have further emphasized the need to align research evaluation systems with responsible research practices and integrity-related values [14, 22, 38]. Collectively, these initiatives argue that assessment systems shape researcher behavior and institutional priorities, highlighting the importance of developing indicators that can identify structural vulnerabilities without reducing integrity to simplistic performance metrics.

The Integrity Risk Indicators by SCImago (IRIS) framework is designed to address this gap by introducing a structured, data-driven approach to identify potential integrity-related vulnerabilities at the level of research institutions. Rather than measuring scientific excellence through output volume or citation impact, the framework focuses on patterns that may indicate structural exposure to integrity risks. The underlying premise is that institutions are not merely passive aggregations of individual researchers. Their incentive structures, governance models, and publication strategies can influence behaviors that affect research integrity. A systematic indicator-based approach can therefore help reveal patterns that may remain hidden when only traditional performance metrics are considered.

IRIS is intended as a guiding analytical model rather than an evaluative ranking. The indicators are designed to function as signals that may warrant further qualitative assessment. They capture patterns associated with potential integrity vulnerabilities, including authorship and affiliation configurations, dissemination and citation dynamics, the credibility of publication venues, and the sustainability and transparency of publication practices. Each indicator is derived from measurable bibliometric data aggregated from institutional outputs. Importantly, these measures are not meant to diagnose misconduct or attribute responsibility. The same observable patterns may arise from legitimate disciplinary norms, national publishing ecosystems, or institutional missions. The framework therefore emphasizes interpretation in context, positioning the indicators as prompts for investigation rather than definitive judgments.

All IRIS indicators are constructed at the institutional level by aggregating publication-level data. To ensure consistency and comparability across indicators, a unified notation is adopted, and each indicator is defined through explicitly specified numerators and denominators. This standardized structure allows the indicators to be transparent, reproducible, and comparable across institutions. At the same time, the framework explicitly distinguishes between signals of potential integrity-related risk and indicators that may primarily reflect structural characteristics of research systems. As a general principle, the indicators should not be interpreted as evidence of misconduct, but rather as context-dependent signals that may require further qualitative assessment.

IRIS is calculated for higher education institutions included in the SCImago Institutions Rankings and presented in a format that supports meaningful comparison. Because the indicators rely on heterogeneous measurements with different units, scales, and distributions, the framework applies z-score normalization to place all indicators on a shared standardized scale. On this scale, zero corresponds to the global mean. Positive values indicate higher-than-average exposure to integrity-related risk signals, while negative values indicate lower-than-average exposure. This standardization allows institutions to interpret their results without needing to understand the original distribution of each variable and facilitates comparisons across disciplines, regions, and institutional profiles.

The choice of standardized scores rather than percentile-based approaches is central to the design of the framework. Percentiles are effective for describing relative positioning, but they tend to compress extreme values and reduce sensitivity to anomalous behavior.

A percentile-based system would treat mild deviations and severe irregularities within the same band as equivalent, thereby weakening the detection of meaningful risk signals. Z-scores, by contrast, preserve information about the magnitude of deviations from the global norm. In a risk-oriented framework, this sensitivity is desirable because it allows unusually strong signals to emerge clearly. Although extreme outliers may compress variation among institutions with typical patterns, this effect stabilizes the interpretation of the “normal” range and ensures that alerts are triggered primarily by statistically meaningful deviations rather than by routine fluctuations.

By situating integrity-related signals within an institutional bibliometric framework, IRIS aims to complement existing approaches that focus on transparency, reporting standards, or journal-level practices. The framework contributes to the emerging literature on research integrity assessment by providing a multi-dimensional, institution-centered model designed to identify structural vulnerabilities and support evidence-informed governance. In doing so, it shifts the focus of evaluation from measuring scientific success to understanding the conditions that support trustworthy and responsible research systems.

Literature review

Research integrity has moved from being a side issue focused mainly on compliance to becoming a central pillar of scientific credibility and institutional accountability. Over the past twenty years, scholars have tried to unpack not only what integrity means but how it can be meaningfully assessed, both empirically and qualitatively, across different research environments. As research output grows and academic systems become increasingly global, long-standing gaps in transparency, reproducibility, and ethical oversight have become more visible [38, 46]. This shift has encouraged the development of methodological approaches that bring together data-driven precision and qualitative understanding to build multi-dimensional models of research integrity.

Early frameworks viewed integrity mainly through the lens of rule compliance and institutional ethics reviews [19, 24]. Yet, as Güneş and Kaban [21] point out, following formal codes does not automatically translate into an ethical research culture, especially in fast-evolving areas such as AI research, where moral reflection often outpaces regulation. Ventura and Oliveira [57] similarly warn that treating integrity as synonymous with compliance oversimplifies the complex social and epistemic realities of scientific work. Newer perspectives, like those of Briskin and Gunsalus [11], frame integrity as a dynamic relationship between institutional norms, researcher accountability, and epistemic responsibility.

These conceptual shifts have pushed researchers to look more closely at the lived experience of integrity and especially how it is interpreted, negotiated, and practiced in everyday research settings [9, 29]. Qualitative methods such as interviews, ethnography, and narrative analysis have been key to revealing the tensions between productivity pressures, recognition systems, and researchers’ own moral identities [8, 42]. Institutional culture plays a critical role here. Studies show that when organizations promote transparency, mentorship, and open dialogue, instances of misconduct decline [27, 44]. However, Alpay [2] notes that many institutions still struggle to respond effectively to proven misconduct, suggesting that the problem often lies within the system itself rather than individual failings. This insight aligns with work by Roje et al. [46] and Azzari et al. [4], who argue that compliance programs must evolve toward participatory and educational models that balance accountability with cultural development.

Cross-national research further shows how sociocultural factors shape integrity. Luthfiyah et al. [32] and Fondze-Fombele [17] demonstrate that globalization and digitalization influence ethical standards differently across regions, especially in non-Western contexts. Using mixed methods of policy analysis, interviews, and document review show that integrity must be understood as culturally situated rather than universally defined. Their findings echo Izarova et al.’s [25] call to move “from quantity to quality” by recognizing locally meaningful indicators of scholarly contribution.

One of the main challenges for integrity research is turning ethical ideas into measurable constructs. Traditional indicators like retraction rates or misconduct cases capture symptoms but not causes [10, 60]. To address this, researchers have started building tools that quantify integrity while keeping its qualitative depth. Examples include the Rigor and Transparency Index [36], the RipetaScore [56], and the QUOCCA checklist [23]. These tools assess transparency, reproducibility, and reporting quality, blending quantitative scoring with interpretive judgment. Machado and Anguiano [33] take this further by introducing qualitative indicators that capture social relevance, inclusivity, and community engagement as measurable dimensions of integrity. Similarly, Abdulwahid and Abed [1] propose the Research Integrity Risk Index (RI2), a mixed-methods framework that evaluates institutional vulnerability using both data and expert evaluation. These models highlight the importance of combining numerical precision with qualitative sensitivity. Recently, Meho published the Research Integrity Risk Index (RI2). While the validation of the indicator relies on a qualitative assessment of a selected group of institutions, the indicator itself is purely quantitative in nature. To systematically identify and quantify integrity-related risks, the study introduces RI2 as a field-normalized composite metric that integrates publications in delisted journals, retractions, and self-citation shares, and classifies institutions across five integrity-risk tiers. Applied to the study cohort, RI2 clearly distinguishes high-risk from benchmark institutions, providing a transparent framework for detecting systemic vulnerabilities, strengthening accountability in global research evaluation, and shifting the focus from performance maximization to integrity-sensitive assessment and governance.

Qualitative methods are especially effective for understanding integrity as an evolving process rather than a fixed outcome. Studies like those by Khoshmaram et al. [28] and Seymour and Meehan [51] apply phenomenological and grounded theory approaches to explore how researchers perceive bias, misconduct, and responsibility. They show that ethical lapses often stem not from ignorance but from the pressures of balancing institutional demands with moral ideals. Batinović et al. [6] further illustrate this by integrating narrative-based assessment into a structured integrity evaluation tool for educational research (QUERIES-EDU), showing that qualitative reasoning enhances structured evaluation frameworks.

Case-based qualitative studies add another layer of understanding by examining how institutions react to misconduct [10, 12, 18]. These studies reveal that effective governance depends less on punishment and more on embedding values within education and evaluation processes. This finding has influenced reforms that integrate integrity training into researcher development programs [15, 53].

A recent shift in the field is the integration of qualitative interpretation with empirical validation. Li [30] uses data-recognition algorithms to identify abnormal publication trends as early warning systems but emphasizes that human interpretation remains essential for context. Similarly, Piran and Tran [41] argue that evaluation frameworks should explicitly link methodological rigor with ethical responsibility, turning integrity research into a truly hybrid discipline. This synthesis reflects a larger shift in the philosophy of science, acknowledging that data and meaning are inseparable. As Strech et al. [55] and Schöpfel et al. [50] argue, the future of integrity research depends on developing approaches that are both empirically sound and ethically reflexive.

The current literature emphasizes that assessing integrity requires both measurement and interpretation that captures the practices researchers perform and the cultural contexts that shape them [31, 34]. Tools like the RI2 Index [1, 35] have advanced the field by offering composite measures of institutional vulnerability, though critics caution that raw metrics must always be contextualized [26]. Quantitative systems such as the Rigor and Transparency Index [36] and the RipetaScore [56] show that empirical structure and qualitative insight can complement each other.

Across studies, scholars agree that the most meaningful assessments combine empirical data with contextual interpretation. Bössel et al. [8] link structural academic pressures with unethical behaviors, while Briskin and Gunsalus [11] and Azzari et al. [4] demonstrate how mentoring, training, and participatory governance can mitigate integrity risks. Khoshmaram et al. [28] and Bonn and Pinxten [9] add that researchers’ ethical choices are shaped by the interplay of personal values, institutional culture, and disciplinary norms.

Our research builds on this growing integration of quantitative and qualitative approaches. We acknowledge that qualitative insight is essential for a meaningful interpretation of data; consequently, we provide our data in a format that facilitates this synthesis, while incorporating contextual information to ensure the data remains grounded in the reality from which it emerges. We developed a multi-dimensional indicator framework to identify institutions that show signs of systemic integrity issues and problematic research patterns. The framework combines bibliometric measures with ethical and organizational analysis, responding to calls for holistic, context-aware evaluation [3, 31]. It includes nine complementary indicators, ranging from rates of multiple affiliations and retractions to self-citations, hyper-authorship, and publication in discontinued journals. Together, these indicators reveal institutional behaviors that may compromise research credibility. Rather than judging output quantity alone, this model aims to map the ethical texture of institutional research practices, bridging the gap between quantitative accountability and qualitative understanding.

Methods

Data sources and unit of analysis

The IRIS framework draws on large-scale bibliometric data derived from Scopus™ and processed through the SCImago Institutions Rankings (SIR) infrastructure. Scopus™ is widely used in scientometrics research due to its broad coverage of peer-reviewed literature across disciplines and its structured metadata, which enables the identification of institutional affiliations, citation patterns, and publication characteristics [37, 58].

The analysis focuses on Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) included in the SIR database. The unit of analysis is therefore the institution, rather than the individual researcher or publication. All indicators are constructed by aggregating publication-level data to the institutional level, following established practices in bibliometric evaluation [20].

A key feature of the IRIS framework is that the unit of analysis is the institution rather than the individual researcher or publication. Although the indicators are derived from bibliometric metadata, citation relationships, authorship information, and affiliation records associated with individual publications, these data are aggregated to the institutional level before analysis. Consequently, the framework is designed to identify structural patterns and potential institutional risk factors rather than evaluate individual researchers, research groups, or specific publications.

The dataset covers a five-year observation window (2020–2024). This time span was selected in order to balance recency and stability: it is sufficiently recent to reflect current research practices, while also long enough to smooth short-term fluctuations in publication and citation patterns. All indicators are computed over this five-year period as aggregated measures, rather than year-by-year values.

Publications are assigned to institutions based on the affiliation information provided by authors in the source database. For each institution (i), total output (Ni) is defined as the number of publications attributed to that institution within the observation period. Depending on the indicator, subsets of this output are used (for example, publications with multiple affiliations, retracted publications, or publications appearing in specific journal categories).

Different types of metadata are used to construct the indicators. Citation-based measures rely on citation counts associated with each publication, while authorship-related indicators are derived from author-level information such as co-authorship patterns, affiliation structures, and publication frequency. These variables are first identified at the publication level and subsequently aggregated to the institutional level.

By adopting this aggregation strategy, the IRIS framework aims to capture structural patterns of institutional research activity, rather than focusing on individual behavior. This is consistent with recent approaches in research evaluation that emphasize the role of institutional environments, incentive systems, and governance structures in shaping research practices [38, 46].

Indicator construction

All IRIS indicators are constructed at the institutional level by aggregating publication-level data. To ensure consistency and comparability, a unified notation is used and each indicator is defined through explicitly specified numerators and denominators. This standardized construction makes the indicators transparent and reproducible across institutions.

At the same time, these indicators should not be interpreted as evidence of misconduct. They function as context-dependent signals that may require further qualitative assessment. It is important to distinguish between indicators that capture potential integrity-related risks and those that primarily reflect structural characteristics of research systems. In several cases, the same observable pattern may arise from legitimate organizational, disciplinary, or national factors rather than from problematic practices. For this reason, all indicators in the IRIS framework should be interpreted as contextual signals derived from aggregated data, not as direct proof of misconduct.

Let (i) denote an institution, and (Ni) its total number of publications within the observation period. Each indicator is defined as a function of a subset of these publications or of associated metadata (e.g., citations, authorship information, or journal characteristics). Unless otherwise specified, indicators are expressed as proportions relative to (Ni), allowing comparisons across institutions of different sizes.

The nine indicators included in the IRIS framework are defined as follows:

Rate of Multiple Affiliations

Rate of Multiple Affiliations

Rate of Multiple Affiliations

The Rate of multiple affiliations reflects the proportion of output in which at least one author lists more than one institutional affiliation. Multiple affiliations often arise from legitimate collaboration, researcher mobility, and shared appointments, all of which can contribute positively to scholarly work. At the same time, disproportionately high rates may indicate strategic or inflated affiliation practices that can blur institutional accountability and distort how credit is attributed. Interpreting this metric requires careful attention to context, especially in mature scientific environments. In many European and North American settings, dual affiliations are common and often built into the structure of the research system, such as partnerships between universities and teaching hospitals or joint appointments with national research institutes. For this reason, a high rate of dual affiliations should not be assumed to indicate a problem. Instead, it should prompt a closer look to distinguish genuine, well-established collaboration from possible "affiliation shopping" or artificial credit allocation. An unusually high rate of multi-affiliation may therefore be interpreted as a potential signal of integrity-related risk. In some research systems, particularly those based on joint research units or shared institutional structures, multiple affiliations are a structural feature rather than a strategic choice. In such contexts, high values of this indicator may reflect organizational design rather than integrity-related concerns.

Rate of Retracted Output

Retractions offer a clear and objective signal that something in the research output required correction, whether due to issues such as plagiarism, fabrication, falsification, or simple and unintentional errors. It is important to acknowledge that a portion of retractions result from publisher mistakes or are initiated by authors themselves after identifying honest errors. In many cases, strong post-publication quality control can lead to a higher number of retractions, which reflects responsible oversight rather than problematic practices.

Given the limitations in automated metadata to distinguish the cause of retraction, elevated values in this indicator should be treated as a prompt for qualitative verification, requiring managers to analyze whether the pattern stems from systemic misconduct or a rigorous commitment to correcting the scientific record.

(3)Rate of Institutional Self-Citation

Rate of Institutional Self-Citation

The Rate of Institutional Self-Citation reflects the proportion of citations received by an institution’s output that come from other works authored within the same institution. A certain level of self-citation is natural and often appropriate, as it can indicate continuity in research programs or the development of long-term scholarly lines. At the same time, unusually high levels may unintentionally inflate an institution’s apparent impact or limit the objectivity of citation patterns.

It is also important to recognize that some institutions naturally receive fewer external citations because of their disciplinary focus or collaboration patterns. Highly specialized institutions, or those with limited international partnerships, may depend more on internal citation networks simply because fewer external researchers work in their specific fields. Even when not driven by intentional misconduct, this pattern may still reflect a degree of insularity that limits external validation. In addition, there is no positive correlation between institutional size and self-citation rates, which suggests that scale may not play a major role in creating these insular citation patterns. Conceptually, it offers insight into how much an institution’s influence is shaped by external recognition versus internal citation dynamics, helping to highlight areas where additional engagement or visibility might be beneficial.

Rate of Output in Discontinued Journals

The Rate of Output in Discontinued Journals represents the proportion of an institution’s research outputs that appear in journals removed from major bibliographic databases such as Scopus™ due to concerns related to ethics or quality. Higher rates can suggest potential vulnerabilities, such as limited due diligence or exposure to questionable or predatory publishing practices.

This proportional approach helps mitigate situations in which publishing in a discontinued journal may be acceptable. For example, if only a small share of an institution’s outputs falls into this category, it is more likely that those reflect isolated or context-specific decisions rather than a systemic pattern. Conversely, consistently high proportions can draw attention to areas where additional guidance or support may be needed to help researchers avoid low-quality or problematic venues.

(5)Rate of Hyper-Authored Output

(5)Rate of Hyper-Authored Output

Rate of Hyper-Authored Output

The Rate of Hyper-Authored Output reflects the proportion of an institution’s outputs that include exceptionally large numbers of authors. In many fields, especially high-energy physics and large clinical studies, extensive collaboration is both common and essential, and high author counts naturally arise from the scale and complexity of the work. At the same time, unusually large author lists may occasionally blur the clarity of individual contributions or signal practices such as honorary or guest authorship, which can unintentionally weaken transparency and accountability.

A paper is considered hyper-authored when it is identified as an outlier in terms of the number of coauthors relative to its subject category and publication year [47, 49]. By using this contextual approach, the indicator helps distinguish between legitimate large-scale collaborations and patterns that may benefit from closer, constructive review.

For research integrity purposes, noticing these patterns is important because clear authorship practices support accountability, credit allocation, and trust in the scholarly record. The indicator therefore serves as a guiding signal, helping institutions reflect on the accuracy and transparency of author contribution practices while recognizing that high author counts are often entirely appropriate within certain disciplines.

(6)Leadership Impact Gap

(6)Leadership Impact Gap

Leadership Impact Gap

The Gap in Normalized Impact compares an institution’s average Normalized Impact (NI) across its total output with the NI of output in which the institution holds a leading authorship position, typically as first or corresponding author. A substantial positive gap may suggest that the institution relies on external collaborators for high-impact results, which could reflect limited institutional research leadership. At the same time, such a gap is often common for growing institutions or those in regions with fewer resources, where participation in collaborative research with more established partners is a critical path for building research capacity and establishing scientific lines.

By taking this approach, the metric accounts for the proportional contribution of lead authorship to overall impact, while recognizing the context of collaboration.

From a research integrity perspective, this indicator is important because it helps institutions reflect on the balance between collaborative contributions and autonomous research leadership. It can highlight whether high-impact outputs are the result of genuine collaborative efforts, or whether patterns of strategic authorship and positioning might be inflating visibility without corresponding responsibility or intellectual contribution. However, particularly in emerging economies, this gap often reflects necessary capacity building rather than ethical failure. Consequently, high values can be interpreted as a "sustainability risk”, signaling a structural vulnerability where impact is contingent on external partnerships rather than endogenous capacity. This metric is not intended to penalize institutions, especially smaller universities or those in underrepresented regions, but to provide a constructive signal about leadership and accountability in research outputs.

Let (NIalli) denote the average Normalized Impact (NI) of all publications associated with institution (i), where NI is defined as the ratio between the citations received by a document and the average number of citations of documents of the same type, publication year, and subject category [45]. This type of normalization allows citation impact to be compared across disciplines and time periods, where citation practices differ substantially.

Let (NIleadi) denote the average NI of publications in which the institution acts as research guarantor, operationalized here as holding the corresponding author position [39]. (7)Rate of Hyper-Prolific Authors

Rate of Hyper-Prolific Authors

High productivity can indicate strong research capacity, effective collaboration, or leadership in large-scale projects. In many fields, especially experimental sciences or large team-based research, it is normal for senior researchers or team leaders to appear in many publications due to legitimate coordination and oversight roles. Abnormal behavior, however, may arise when exceptionally high output is accompanied by minimal individual contributions, or questionable authorship practices.

This quantitative approach isolates extreme values, providing a standardized metric to monitor potential imbalances between production volume and the capacity for meaningful intellectual contribution. Although this metric is not normalized by discipline, the aggregate institutional focus provides a necessary dilution effect: legitimate high-output patterns in specific fields (e.g., High Energy Physics) are typically absorbed within the broader publication volume of generalist universities. Consequently, it is appropriate to review elevated results specifically within the context of the institution's specialization, distinguishing between structural risks and the natural footprint of niche, hyper-collaborative research centers.

(8)Rate of Output in Institutional Journals

Rate of Output in Institutional Journals

The Rate of Output in Institutional Journals reflects the proportion of an institution’s research outputs published in journals that are owned by the same institution. Institutional journals can serve an important role in supporting early-career researchers, providing venues for internal dissemination, and fostering the development of new research communities. At the same time, excessive reliance on such journals may raise concerns about editorial independence and peer-review objectivity, or their ability to publish in external, independent journals. This might also raise potential conflicts of interest that can arise when the institution is both publisher and evaluator of research quality.

It is important to recognize that this indicator should be interpreted in context. Smaller or less-resourced universities may rely on institutional or low-cost journals because they cannot afford high article processing charges in external venues. Similarly, elite or specialized institutions may naturally publish in their own specialized journals because those journals are highly relevant and respected within their fields. In other words, high rates of output in institutional journals are not inherently problematic and may reflect legitimate strategic or practical considerations. Consequently, the risk of insularity can be substantially lower when the institutional journals are positioned in the upper tiers of impact or possess recognized editorial quality seals, as these markers provide objective evidence of external scrutiny and adherence to competitive scientific standards.

Conceptually, it provides insight into the balance between internal publishing and open, independent dissemination and validation. From a research integrity perspective, monitoring this rate is important because it can help institutions ensure that editorial practices remain transparent, peer review is robust, and publication strategies do not unintentionally compromise the credibility or independence of their research outputs.

For this reason, the indicator should not be interpreted as inherently negative, but rather as a contextual signal that requires careful consideration of the role and positioning of institutional journals within specific research systems.

Rate of Redundant Output

The rate of redundant output, often referred to as “Salami Slicing” reflects the proportion of an institution’s outputs that show unusually high levels of bibliographic overlap with other works by the same authors within a short period. In focused research areas, it is normal and often necessary for authors to cite the same theoretical foundations and earlier studies to build cumulative knowledge and maintain continuity in their scientific work. This bibliographic overlap is common in longitudinal studies published in multiple parts, systematic reviews in highly specialized fields, or replication studies designed to confirm earlier findings.

At the same time, excessive overlap in references across simultaneous contributions may indicate “salami slicing” or data fragmentation, which involves dividing a single coherent study into multiple minimal publishable units. Although this is not the same as duplicate publication, it occupies an ethical grey zone. It can distort scientific evidence by introducing bias into systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and it places additional pressure on the peer-review system by reducing the substantive value of each manuscript. This indicator captures the share of output flagged for unusually high levels of bibliographic coupling. Legitimate research continuity, disciplinary publication practices, and institutional characteristics may generate false-positive signals for certain indicators. The frequency and magnitude of such effects have not yet been systematically evaluated and remain an important topic for future empirical investigation. From a research integrity perspective, this metric helps institutions distinguish between genuine thematic specialization and practices that artificially inflate productivity, ensuring that output counts represent meaningful knowledge creation rather than fragmented results.

Standardization of indicators

Since the indicators included in the IRIS framework are expressed in different units, scales, and ranges, a normalization procedure is required to ensure comparability. Without normalization, indicators with larger numerical ranges could dominate the overall analysis regardless of their relative importance.

All indicators are standardized using z-score normalization, a widely used method in statistical analysis and data modeling [7, 16, 40]. For each indicator (j), the standardized value for institution (i) is computed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{ij}=\frac{{x}_{ij}-{\upmu}_{j}}{{\upsigma}_{j}}$$\end{document}zij=xij-μjσjwhere (xij) is the value of indicator (j) for institution (i), (µj) is the global mean of indicator (j), and (σj) is the corresponding standard deviation.

This transformation places all indicators on a common scale centered at zero, with unit variance. As a result, the standardized values reflect how far each institution deviates from the global average in terms of standard deviations, allowing direct comparison across indicators.

Z-score normalization preserves differences in magnitude and is sensitive to the distribution of each indicator, which is particularly relevant in the context of risk detection. By retaining information about the intensity of deviations, this approach facilitates the identification of institutions that exhibit unusually high or low values relative to the global reference.

All indicators are standardized independently prior to aggregation.

This approach is based on pairwise comparisons between indicators, in which their relative importance is assessed using a standardized scale ranging from 1 (equal importance) to 9 (extreme importance). These comparisons were used to capture the relative strength of each indicator as a signal of potential research integrity risk.

Thresholds and classification criteria

Several indicators in the IRIS framework rely on operational thresholds to identify patterns that may be indicative of potential research integrity risks. These thresholds are not intended to establish definitive boundaries between acceptable and unacceptable practices, but rather to highlight unusually high or atypical values that may warrant closer examination.

For the identification of hyper-prolific authors, a threshold of 25 publications per year is applied. This value is intended to capture extreme levels of productivity that are unlikely to be explained solely by standard research practices in most contexts and may reflect patterns such as honorary authorship or fragmented contributions, although alternative explanations may also exist. The threshold is deliberately set at a high level in order to identify clear outliers rather than moderate variations in publication activity. It is important to note that the threshold of 25 publications per year should be understood as a pragmatic heuristic rather than a definitive boundary separating normal and abnormal research behavior. The objective is not to classify individual researchers as compliant or non-compliant, but to identify unusually high levels of productivity that may warrant closer examination at the aggregate institutional level. Alternative thresholds (e.g., 20 or 30 publications per year) could also be justified depending on disciplinary context, publication cultures, and the specific objectives of the analysis. The selected value therefore represents an operational compromise intended to capture extreme cases while maintaining interpretability and consistency across institutions.

Given the context-dependent nature of several indicators, future versions of the IRIS platform will incorporate configurable threshold parameters for selected metrics. This functionality will allow users to explore how alternative specifications affect institutional profiles and will increase transparency regarding the assumptions embedded in the framework. Rather than treating threshold values as fixed or universally valid, this approach recognizes disciplinary diversity and supports more context-sensitive interpretations of integrity-related indicators.

For the detection of redundant output (often associated with “salami slicing”), publications are flagged when they meet two criteria: (i) they include at least 10 references, and (ii) more than 70% of these references overlap with those of another publication by the same authors within a short time window (5 years). These conditions are designed to identify unusually high levels of bibliographic similarity that go beyond normal thematic continuity in research.

In addition, thresholds are implicitly embedded in the identification of hyper-authored publications, which are defined as outliers relative to the distribution of author counts within each subject category and publication year. This contextual approach avoids applying fixed numerical cutoffs across disciplines with very different collaboration patterns.

More broadly, the use of thresholds in the IRIS framework should be understood as a pragmatic methodological choice. Given the absence of universally accepted criteria for identifying questionable research practices at scale, these thresholds function as heuristic tools that help detect potential anomalies in large datasets. While they inevitably involve a degree of arbitrariness, their purpose is not to define misconduct, but to provide consistent and interpretable signals across heterogeneous institutional contexts.

Although the choice of thresholds may influence the identification of specific cases, the IRIS framework is primarily designed to capture structural patterns at the aggregate institutional level. As a result, moderate variations in threshold values are unlikely to substantially alter the overall risk profiles, particularly for large and diversified institutions where results are driven by aggregate patterns rather than individual cases. Nevertheless, the sensitivity of results to threshold selection remains an important methodological consideration. As with any threshold-based indicator, the choice of parameter values may influence the identification of specific cases. Future work should therefore include systematic sensitivity analyses examining the robustness of institutional risk profiles under alternative threshold specifications. Such analyses would help determine whether variations in operational cut-offs materially affect comparative results or whether the framework remains stable across a reasonable range of parameter values.

In addition, future versions of the IRIS platform may incorporate configurable threshold parameters for selected indicators, allowing users to explore alternative specifications and assess the impact of threshold choices in different disciplinary and institutional contexts.

Finally, structural risk scores can be grouped into qualitative categories (e.g., very low, low, medium, and significant risk) based on their position within the global distribution. These categories are intended to support interpretation and communication and should not be interpreted as discrete or definitive classifications of institutional behavior.

Methodological considerations and comparability

The IRIS framework is designed to provide a comparative and interpretable view of institutional exposure to research integrity risks. However, several methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the indicators and the resulting composite measure.

First, although all indicators are standardized and expressed on a common scale, they capture different dimensions of research activity and are influenced by disciplinary practices. For example, patterns such as hyper-authorship, self-citation, or multiple affiliations may be more prevalent in certain fields due to established collaboration models. While aggregation at the institutional level tends to mitigate these effects, it does not eliminate them entirely.

Second, the indicators are constructed using aggregated institutional data, which means that they reflect structural patterns rather than individual behaviors. As a result, high values should not be interpreted as direct evidence of misconduct, but rather as signals of potential exposure to practices that may require further qualitative assessment.

Third, some indicators rely on proxy measures derived from bibliometric metadata. While these proxies enable large-scale analysis, they cannot fully capture the underlying causes of observed patterns. For instance, retractions may result from a range of factors, including honest error, editorial decisions, or misconduct, which cannot always be distinguished in automated data sources.

Finally, the framework is intended to support comparative analysis across institutions, but differences in size, specialization, and research profiles should be taken into account when interpreting results. The indicators are therefore best understood as part of a broader diagnostic approach, in which quantitative signals are complemented by contextual and qualitative evaluation.

Taken together, these considerations highlight that the IRIS framework should be used as a tool for exploration and reflection, rather than as a definitive assessment of institutional research integrity.

While aggregation at the institutional level helps mitigate field-specific effects, this approach may be less effective for highly specialized institutions, where disciplinary concentration can still influence indicator values.

A further limitation concerns the current stage of validation of the IRIS framework. The present study introduces a conceptual model, indicator design, and platform architecture rather than a fully validated predictive or classification system. Consequently, performance characteristics commonly used in diagnostic evaluation, such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and false-positive rates, cannot presently be estimated. This limitation reflects the absence of an established external gold standard for institutional research integrity against which indicators could be systematically calibrated and validated.

As a result, the indicators should be interpreted as signals of potential areas requiring further examination rather than direct measures of misconduct or integrity breaches. Future research should focus on empirical validation studies, calibration exercises, and sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness, discriminatory capacity, and contextual applicability of individual indicators across disciplinary, regional, and institutional settings.

IRIS platform description

The IRIS platform is an advanced benchmarking tool designed to help Higher Education Institutions assess and compare performance in research integrity. Built on the Integrity Risk Indicators by SCImago (IRIS) framework, the platform allows institutions to visualize risk indicators in both national and global contexts, supporting informed, constructive decision-making.

The global context provides two complementary levels of assessment. The first is a structural assessment, which aggregates the full set of indicators to produce an overall measure of institutional risk, referred to as IRIS Structural Risk Assessment. The second is an indicator-level assessment, which provides detailed insights into specific risk areas, helping institutions understand the components driving the overall structural risk and identify areas that may benefit from focused attention.

Consistent with its objective of promoting transparency and responsible research governance, the IRIS platform is designed as an open-access resource that allows institutions and stakeholders to explore integrity-related indicators and benchmarking results without financial barriers to access.

Structural risk is categorized as follows. “Very low structural risk” indicates that the aggregated indicators are well below the world average, representing the lowest risk quartile. “Low structural risk” applies when the structural risk is below the global average. “Medium structural risk” describes cases where risk is above the global average but not considered unusual. Finally, “Significant structural risk” identifies institutions with values that can be considered outliers, signaling areas where closer attention may be warranted.

Overall, the IRIS platform is designed as a constructive, non-judgmental tool to support transparency, self-reflection, and targeted improvements in research integrity practices. The scheme uses color to indicate the level of risk (Table 1).

How the IRIS platform supports different stakeholders

The IRIS platform is designed to serve a broad range of stakeholders by providing clear, data-driven insights into research integrity. It enables institutions to monitor, benchmark, and strengthen their integrity practices in a systematic and comparable way. What makes IRIS unique is its ability to transform complex integrity-related publication patterns into standardized risk indicators. These indicators are generated through a transparent and replicable methodology, allowing institutions around the world to evaluate themselves using the same criteria. As a result, IRIS offers a shared evidence base that supports both institutional decision-making and sector-wide learning, something that traditional audits or qualitative reviews cannot provide at scale.

University Leadership: including presidents, provosts, and deans, can use the Institutional Integrity Dashboard to track their institution’s integrity profile in relation to national and global peers. By presenting integrity risk in a structured and comparable format, the platform strengthens evidence-based governance and helps leadership ensure that research strategy is aligned with institutional values and long-term integrity objectives.

Research Integrity Officers and Compliance Units can use IRIS for Integrity Risk Profiling, identifying areas that may require targeted audits, training, or mentoring. The platform also supports the evaluation of integrity policies, allowing teams to assess the effectiveness of new procedures or training initiatives and enabling continuous institutional learning through quantitative feedback.

Research Offices and Vice Presidents for Research gain value from IRIS, which helps balance research impact with integrity considerations. Institutional Benchmarking and Reporting features allow research leaders to compare their performance with national and international peers. These insights support strategic planning, improve transparency, and strengthen reporting for internal governance, external evaluations, and funding applications.

Funding Agencies and Research Councils can embed integrity into their assessment processes through Integrity-Adjusted Funding Allocation, ensuring that ethical practices are recognized in resource distribution. Risk-based audit selection enables agencies to focus oversight where it is most needed and build a fair, data-driven compliance system.

National Research Integrity Bodies and Policy Analysts can use IRIS to map systemic vulnerabilities across fields or regions. The platform also supports understanding how national incentive structures influence researcher behavior, offering evidence to refine policies and reduce unwanted or distortive incentives.

Academic Publishers and Bibliometric Analysts can apply IRIS indicators to assess journal quality and editorial ethics. This supports stronger publication standards and enhances the reliability of bibliometric databases.

External stakeholders, including the media, students, and the public, can benefit from transparent integrity reporting, which demonstrates institutional accountability and reinforces trust in the research environment.

Internal Faculties and Department Heads can use IRIS for local integrity self-assessment. This allows the identification of trends within their own research output and reinforces a culture of shared responsibility, ensuring that ethical practice is maintained at every level of the institution.

Marketing, Communications, and Public Relations Units can also benefit from IRIS insights. They can highlight strong integrity practices in external communications or prepare accurate, responsible responses when questions arise about potential integrity concerns.

Discussion

The IRIS approach, methodology, and platform propose a structured and data-driven way of engaging with research integrity by translating publication patterns into interpretable indicators. In contrast to traditional approaches such as qualitative audits, narrative peer review, or post hoc misconduct investigations, IRIS seeks to operationalize integrity as a continuously measurable and comparable construct. This aligns with broader developments in research evaluation that emphasize metrics, benchmarking, and algorithmic governance, but also raises familiar tensions regarding reductionism and the risk of substituting complex normative judgments with proxy indicators (see e.g. debates around Responsible Research and Innovation and metric-based governance). These concerns have been widely discussed in the literature on research evaluation and responsible metrics. Scholars have argued that quantitative indicators can become performative, influencing behavior rather than merely describing it, and may generate unintended consequences when employed as governance tools [5, 13, 43]. The Leiden Manifesto and related responsible-metrics initiatives similarly caution against treating indicators as objective representations of quality detached from their social and institutional contexts [22, 59].

A central contribution of IRIS is its attempt to standardize heterogeneous signals of publication behavior into a shared framework that can be used across institutions and governance levels. In doing so, it reflects a growing policy trend toward scalable infrastructures for research assessment and integrity monitoring. However, this standardization also inevitably involves interpretive choices: what counts as “risk,” how patterns are weighted, and how indicators are contextualized are not neutral decisions. While the framework is presented as non-punitive and primarily diagnostic, the categorization of behaviors into risk profiles inevitably introduces an evaluative logic. This tension between descriptive analytics and normative interpretation is not fully resolved and warrants further conceptual clarification.

From a governance perspective, IRIS may support institutional leadership in identifying structural patterns in publication practices and aligning research strategies with internal integrity policies. Yet its value does not lie solely in benchmarking or external comparison. More critically, it should be seen as a tool that can potentially reshape internal accountability regimes, influencing how “good” research behavior is defined and operationalized within institutions. This raises questions about feedback effects: once integrated into decision-making, such systems may not merely observe behavior but also actively shape it, a phenomenon widely discussed in the literature on audit cultures and performativity in higher education. This phenomenon is consistent with broader analyses of audit cultures and metric governance in higher education, which demonstrate how evaluation systems can reshape organizational priorities, academic identities, and research practices through feedback effects and incentive structures [13, 43, 52]. From this perspective, integrity indicators should be understood not only as measurement tools but also as governance instruments capable of influencing the behaviors they seek to monitor.

For research integrity officers and compliance units, IRIS offers the possibility of moving from reactive investigation toward proactive monitoring. However, this shift also risks narrowing the understanding of integrity to what is measurable within the system. Many well-known limitations of quantitative integrity indicators such as susceptibility to gaming, disciplinary variation, and contextual ambiguity remain relevant here and should be explicitly acknowledged when interpreting outputs.

At the policy level, the framework’s potential use by funding agencies, publishers, and national bodies reflects a broader movement toward data-informed governance of science. While this may improve transparency and enable earlier detection of systemic anomalies, it also raises concerns about over-reliance on standardized indicators in environments that are inherently heterogeneous. The literature on research assessment has repeatedly cautioned that metric systems can produce unintended consequences when embedded in incentive structures without sufficient reflexivity. These concerns have informed recent efforts to develop more reflexive and context-sensitive approaches to research assessment, including DORA, the Leiden Manifesto, and the Hong Kong Principles, all of which emphasize that quantitative indicators should complement rather than replace expert judgment and qualitative evaluation [14, 22, 38]. The IRIS framework adopts a similar position by treating its indicators as signals for further inquiry rather than definitive assessments of institutional integrity.

The implications of IRIS extend beyond institutional management to the broader research ecosystem, including public communication and trust in science. While increased transparency may strengthen legitimacy, it also risks simplifying complex scholarly practices into publicly legible scores or categories that may be misinterpreted outside expert contexts. This dual effect enhancing transparency while potentially increasing interpretive distortion—should be explicitly considered in future development of the platform.

A more critical reading of IRIS therefore positions it not only as a technical infrastructure but also as part of an emerging epistemic regime in research governance. Its effectiveness will depend less on the sophistication of its indicators alone and more on the governance frameworks, interpretive safeguards, and disciplinary contextualization that accompany its use. Without such reflexive layers, there is a risk that the platform reproduces some of the well-documented limitations of metric-driven evaluation systems rather than overcoming them.

Therefore, IRIS represents a promising but also contestable step toward operationalizing research integrity at scale. Its value lies in its capacity to structure discussion, reveal patterns, and support institutional reflection. At the same time, its adoption should be accompanied by sustained critical engagement with the assumptions embedded in its categorization logic, as well as with the broader socio-technical implications of transforming integrity into a measurable object.

Conclusions

The IRIS platform represents a significant advancement in the way research integrity is measured and managed. By transforming complex and often opaque research behaviors into standardized, data-driven indicators, IRIS offers institutions an innovative tool for monitoring and improving their research practices. The platform’s broad applicability to a range of stakeholders; from university leadership to external policy makers, makes it a versatile tool for fostering a culture of ethical research and transparent governance.

While IRIS provides valuable insights into research integrity, it is important to recognize the limitations inherent in any automated or quantitative approach. The platform is designed to signal potential areas of risk, not to make definitive judgments. Therefore, it is essential for institutions to interpret IRIS data in context, considering disciplinary norms, institutional structures, and local research practices.

IRIS provides a unique opportunity for institutions to systematically address integrity challenges, benchmark their practices against global peers, and implement evidence-based improvements. As the platform continues to evolve, its capacity to support continuous institutional learning and sector-wide accountability will be critical in strengthening the trustworthiness of the global research enterprise.

Future development of the framework should prioritize empirical validation of the proposed indicators and assessment of their performance across diverse research environments. Establishing the robustness, interpretability, and practical utility of the indicators will be essential for ensuring responsible application of the framework in institutional research integrity assessment.
