Abstract
An electromotive force (emf ) can be generated along a temperature gradient between the
cold end and hot end of a thermoelectric material, termed the Seebeck effect. Based on
the Seebeck effect, metallic alloys have been extensively employed to detect temperatures
for centuries, named thermocouples. However, commercially available thermocouple alloys
suffer from limitations, such as oxidation, chemical degradation, and poor long-term stability
under high-temperature harsh environments.
 This DOE-funded project aimed to develop high-temperature, chemically tolerant ther-
mocouples suitable for operation in extreme environments relevant to semiconducting ther-
moelectric materials. The research focused on boride-based semiconducting thermoelectric
compounds as candidates for next-generation thermocouples with enhanced oxidation resis-
tance, chemical stability, and thermal robustness under conditions representative of charcoal-
fired electricity facilities.
 During the funded years, boride materials were synthesized using an arc-plasma technique
under ambient air and argon atmospheres, enabling scalable and cost-effective production
compared with conventional boride fabrication methods. The synthesized borides were pro-
cessed into nanostructured powders, followed by consolidation into dense bulk materials
using a spark plasma sintering (SPS) bottom-up approach. Comprehensive characterization
was performed, including microstructural analysis, electrical transport measurements, and
optical and thermal property evaluation. Both p-type and n-type boride electric legs were
fabricated and integrated into boride-based thermocouples. The thermal and irradiation
stabilities of the boride nanomaterials and bulk thermoelectric materials were systematically
evaluated to assess suitability for long-term operation in harsh environments.
 Additionally, 12 students were broadly hands-on trained spanning the full research work-
flow, including word processing and technical editing (e.g., LATEX for manuscript and poster
preparation), data collection and analysis (using Python and related libraries and hardware
interfaces), sample preparation (including arc-plasma synthesis and spark plasma sintering),
and advanced characterization techniques (such as X-ray diffraction, UV–vis spectroscopy,
electron microscopy, differential thermal analysis (DTA), and Seebeck coefficient measure-
ments, etc).
 Overall, this project demonstrated the feasibility of boride-based thermoelectric materi-
als as durable high-temperature thermocouples, providing a promising pathway toward ro-
bust temperature sensing technologies aligned with DOE energy infrastructure and extreme-
environment monitoring needs.

1 Project Objectives
Coal-based energy systems that provide approximately 50 % of the electricity in the United
States usually operate under extreme conditions with high temperature (750 − 1, 800 ◦ C)
and high pressure (1, 000 PSI and above) along with high corrosion (from oxygen and sul-
phur) and erosion rates. Accurately temperature monitoring and optimal controlling are
key roles to achieve high efficiency and optimal performances. The overall objective of the
proposed project is to develop a novel ceramic-based thermocouples working under such
extreme conditions. The ceramic thermocouples are economic as traditional metal-based
thermocouples while work under extreme conditions as acoustic and optical thermosensors.
Specially, this project is designed to a kind of novel thermocouples that are 1) cost-effective,
2) less maintenance, 3) oxidization resistive, and 4) sulfidization resistive under high pressure
/ high temperature.
 The National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) aims at innovation to explore so-
lutions for environmentally sustainable and economically beneficial energy, with a mission
focused on ensuring affordable, abundant, and reliable energy that strengthens the U. S. eco-
nomic growth and national security. The funded award has focused on one kind of thermal
sensors that could potentially be employed in coal-based power plants to improve energy
conversion efficiency.

2 Background
In 1821, Thomas Johann Seebeck discovered that two dissimilar metals generated an elec-
tromotive force (emf ) when a temperature difference was applied at their junction, which
arised from the temperature gradient between the metallic contacts. This phenomenon was
later termed the Seebeck effect. In 1826, Antoine César Becquerel deduced that, for certain
metal combinations, the generated current intensity was proportional to the temperature
gradient. The Seebeck effect was subsequently applied to temperature measurements. Over
time, various metal combinations were developed to form thermocouples and calibrated for
different temperature ranges. To date, numerous metal-based thermocouples have been de-
veloped and widely used in industry, science, and daily life, including platinum-palladium,
steel-nickel silver, copper-zinc, iridium-platinum, and Pt/rhodium (10 %)-platinum thermo-
couples.
 Currently, various commercially alloy-based thermocouples have been developed, includ-
ing nickel-alloy types (Type E, J, K, M, N, and T), noble-metal platinum/rhodium-alloy
types (Type B, R, and S), tungsten/rhenium-alloy types (Type C, D, and G), as well

as chromel-gold/iron-alloy thermocouples, Type P (Platinel II) thermocouples, platinum
/ molybdenum-alloy thermocouples, iridium/rhodium-alloy thermocouples, and pure noble-
metal thermocouples (e.g., Au-Pt and Pt-Pd). Among these commercial thermocouples,
some alloy types (such as Type K and Type N) are suitable for temperatures up to 1, 000 ◦ C,
noble-metal types (such as Type B) can operate up to approximately 1, 800 ◦ C, and certain
tungsten-based thermocouples (such as Type C) can function at temperatures exceeding
2, 000 ◦ C.
 Unfortunately, most alloy-based thermocouples can typically operate only in inert at-
mospheres or under vacuum conditions, particularly at high temperatures. To function in
oxidizing environments, these alloy thermocouples are usually inserted into gas-tight ceramic
protecting tubes made of various sheath materials, known as thermowells. The protecting
tubes or thermowells act as barriers between the process medium and the sensing element,
providing protection against corrosive environments, abrasive media, high pressure, and/or
flowing fluids. For example, Type K (NiCr-Ni) and Type N (Nicrosil-Nisil) thermocouples
can be designed for abrasive gas measurements up to 1, 600 ◦ C when protected by thermow-
ells against wear. Platinum-rhodium thermocouples of Types R, S, and B, when used with
ceramic, silicon carbide, tantalum, or titanium protective tubes, can measure temperatures
up to 1, 800 ◦ C in harsh environments. However, the protecting tubes or thermowells gen-
erally increase cost significantly, and slow the sensor response time. Furthermore, it has
been reported that sulfidation and oxidation can result in serious problems for nickel-based
superalloys at high temperatures (650 − 1, 000 ◦ C), leading to alloy embrittlement. In coal-
fired systems, high pressure, vibration, elevated temperature, and corrosive conditions may
cause failure of the protecting tubes or thermowells as well as the alloys. In other words,
these protective barriers have increased maintenance requirements and the overall cost of
individual thermocouples, while also degrading thermocouple performance.
 With the understanding of thermoelectrics, various semiconducting materials have been
identified as thermoelectric materials since the 1960s. Depending on their composition, semi-
conducting thermoelectric materials can exhibit high Seebeck coefficient at low temperatures,
room temperature (such as Bi2 Te3 and MgAgSb), medium temperatures (such as PbTe), or
high temperatures (such as Si1−x Gex ), and even at extremely high temperatures (such as
boride- and carbide-based thermoelectric materials). Among these reported semiconducting
thermoelectric materials, borides are particularly tolerant of high temperatures and are resis-
tant to erosion, remaining chemically and thermally stable at elevated temperatures because
of their strong chemical bonding. The melting points of these materials exceed 2, 000 ◦ C, and
some are classified as special-grade refractory materials. Borides are hard and resistant to
particle flow under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions too. For example, borides

exhibit resistance to SiO2 .
 Semiconducting borides possess high melting points, high hardness, high thermal con-
ductivity, and excellent thermal shock resistance, enabling them to operate in the harsh
environments of coal-fired power plants, including oxidizing and sulfidizing atmospheres,
high particle flow, high pressure, and high temperature. In this work, borides are selected
for super-high-temperature thermocouples because of their superior oxidation resistance.
Compared with current acoustic and optical laser thermosensors, which are bulky and ex-
pensive, the proposed boride-based thermocouples combine the advantages of traditional
alloy thermocouples (simple structure, low cost, ease of maintenance, and high sensitivity)
with those of acoustic and optical sensors (high-temperature resistance, corrosion resistance,
and erosion resistance).

3 Summary of Research Activities
The principal objective of this project is to develop innovative ceramic-based thermocouples
tailored for coal-based power plants. These thermocouples are designed to endure high tem-
peratures (ranging from 750 ◦ C to 1, 800 ◦ C) and high pressures (1, 000 PSI and above), while
exhibiting strong resistance to corrosion and erosion. The goal is to develop cost-effective
thermocouples capable of reliable operation in harsh environments, including temperatures
up to 2, 000 ◦ C. Furthermore, the project comprehensively evaluates the physical properties
and long-term stability of these thermocouples under a variety of harsh operating conditions.
The primary scientific and technical objectives of the proposed project are as follows:

3.1 Task-by-Task Progress
 • Synthesize and characterize thermoelectric borides with high melting points. These
 borides are p-type and n-type semiconductors with significant Seebeck coefficients.

 • Fabricate composite bulks from boride nanopowders. The fabrication process is sys-
 tematically investigated to understand the effects of compaction conditions, such as
 pressures and temperatures, on the Seebeck coefficients of the resulting composites.

 • Conduct thorough characterizations of the Seebeck coefficients of the boride compos-
 ites. In addition, optimizations of the experimental conditions for boride synthesis,
 composite fabrication, and thermocouple instrumentation are carried out to enhance
 overall performance.

 • Calibrate the boride-based thermocouples to enable precise electromotive force (emf )
 measurements at elevated temperatures and under harsh operating environments.

 The project initiated with the synthesis and characterization of boride powders. Precur-
sor materials were ground and mixed at room temperature, followed by high-temperature
reactions, yielding pure boride powders under various experimental conditions. The charac-
terization process evaluated their purity, chemical phases, crystallographic structures, and
thermal properties.
 The characterized p-type and n-type borides were then processed into bulk forms using
the spark-plasma-sintering (SPS) method. Subsequently, their microstructures and Seebeck
coefficients were examine to optimize the fabrication conditions.
 Advancing further, p-type and n-type boride bulks were integrated to fabricate thermo-
couples. The electromotive force (emf ) of the thermocouples was then measured at high
temperatures.
 Finally, the stability of the boride materials and thermocouples were assessed under harsh
environments, estimating their resistance to oxidization, sulfidation, and gas exposures at
high pressures and high temperatures.

 During the project, boride compounds were synthesized, milled into nanoscale powders,
and compacted into bulk forms. Their physical properties were characterized and their
stability was examined. The detailed results are discussed below.

4.1 Boride Synthesis
As proposed, the boride compounds were synthesized through an atmospheric carbothermic
reaction. One water-cooled carbothermal reactor was set-up during the project. Figure 1
shows the plasma reactor, consisting of an anode, a cathode, and a cooling system, which
is placed in a blast cabinet (equipped with sensors for monitoring pressure, temperature,
humidity, and dust, all controlled by a single-board computer) for environmental and healthy
safety. The graphite tubes (contained the precursor mixture) worked as anodes connected to
a DC power supply. A graphite cylindrical crucible was employed as the cathode, connected
to the cathode of the DC power supply and grounded. The crucible was positioned on an
electrical insulator. To maintain a cool environment in the reactor, the graphite crucible was
surrounded by a copper water tube which was piped to an industrial chiller, which maintained
a constant temperature of 5.0 ◦ C. The entire reaction system was economical and operated
in air, eliminating expensive vacuum components and complex auxiliary systems.
 For details, zircorim dioxide (ZrO2 ), boric acid (H3 BO3 ), and graphite powder (C) were
mixed and ground as precursors, and then loaded into the graphite tubes. A carbothermal
reaction occurred in air.

where ∆H is the enthalpy change during the chemical reaction.
 The whole synthesis procedures were then carried out in air at atmospheric pressure.
Under high DC currents, an arc was created between the cathode (graphite crucible) and
anode (graphite tube). Amount of electrons flowed from the cathode to the anode, creating
a localized high-temperature region near the tip of the anode, which heated the precursor
mixtures inside the anode. Zirconium dioxide (ZrO2 ) and boric acid (H3 BO3 ) were reduced
by graphite (C) and reacted to produce ZrB2 . Additionally, the graphite from the anode and
carbon powder in the mixtures were also heated, reacting with environmental oxygen in air
to create a localized carbon monoxide (CO) gas reduction environment. The CO gas would
help reduce the precursors while insulating against oxidation reactions from oxygen in air.

 Figure 2: Graphite tubes (left) before and (right) after carbothermal reactions.

 As the graphite anode was consumed and electrons collided, the produced materials fell
into the graphite crucible (acting as the cathode) due to gravity. Figure 2 shows the graphite
tubes before and after the reactions. The dropped materials were mechanically collectedd
for subsequent characterization.
 Additionally, a second kind of arc-plasma reactor was setup to synthesize boride com-
pounds under argon atmosphere, as shown in Figure 3. Argon gas was delivered through
a ceramic tungsten-inert-gas (TIG) nozzle to produce an arc between the tungsten elec-
trode and substrates. The precursor mixtures were located within the generated arcs. The
shielding argon gas protected the heated mixtures from potential oxygen and nitrogen con-
tamination.

reaction. The reaction mechanics is same as that of the first type of arc-plasma synthesis.

 The products were cleaned to remove any residual unreacted carbon and boron oxide.

 The final products were further characterized using transmission electron microscopy
(TEM). Figure 7 shows a typical TEM image of a product, with a crystalline size of several
microns. Its selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern indicated that the particle is
crystalline and corresponds to the hexagonal ZrB2 phase, consistent with the XRD results.
 Further X-ray energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis indicated that the final
products consisted of boron and zirconium, as shown in Figure 7. The spectrum confirmed
that the particle was composed of zirconium and boron. The Zr/B ratio was not calculated
because the intensity of the boron peak was reduced due to absorption by the Be window
in the EDS detector. Taking into account the TEM data from EDS and SAED, it can be
concluded that the products are the hexagonal Zr2 phase.
 The boride phase was further confirmed by Raman spectroscopy. Figure 8 presents a
typical Raman spectrum of the product, showing the presence of boron-metal bonds.
 A possible synthesis mechanism of the plasma carbothermal reaction was proposed based
on the experimental conditions. When the temperature is above 150 ◦ C, H3 BO3 decomposed
to B2 O3 and H2 O. Between 450 ◦ C and 500 ◦ C, both crystalline and amorphous B2 O3 melt.
When the temperature was over 1500 ◦ C, a carbothermal reaction takes place. Graphite
may react with B2 O3 to form B4 C, and with ZrO2 to produce ZrC. These two carbides could
be intermediate products during the synthesis process, subsequently reacting with ZrO2 and
B2 O3 , respectively, to finally yield ZrB2 . The reaction diagram is illustrated in Figure 9.
Residual graphite, ZrO2 , B2 O3 , ZrC and B4 C were detected in the final products from the
XRD patterns, supporting the proposed reaction mechanism. Therefore, more ZrB2 could

 Optical properties of the products were further examined. Figure 10 shows an UV-vis
absorption spectra of the product. The band gap of the sample was determined using Tauc-
Mott plots derived from the UV-vis absorption spectrum. The Tauc-Mott approximation
was based on the following equation to determine the band-gap Eg :

 α × hνphoton = [A(hνphoto − Eg )]n (2)

where h, α, ν, and A are the Planck constant, absorption coefficient, photon frequency,

DOE/NETL Final Scientific and Technical Report 18

and a constant, respectively. The value of n depended on the type of transition, where
n = 21 for direct allowed transitions and n = 2 for indirect allowed transition, respectively.
Here, n = 1/2 was employed due to the direct transition of ZrB2 . A Tauc-Mott plot was
constructed by plotting (α × h̄ω)1/n against hνphoton , with the linear section of the plot
extrapolated to intersect the hνphoton axis. From the Tauc-Mott plot, the bandgap of the
sample was approximately 5.5 eV, comparable to ultrawide bandgap semiconductors such
as B-N-C compounds. The wide band gap indicated the material’s potential applications in
high-irradiation environments such as outer space and near nucleation reactors. As discussed
later, ZrB2 was structurally stable under 120 keV high-energy electron irradiations, which
should come from the wide band-gap.

 Figure 12 - 15 show X-ray diffraction patterns of the milled powders under various milling
duration. The un-milling powder was single phase with grain sizes with microns. After
milling, the intensities of the diffraction peaks decreased with milling time. The right panels
show the zoomed strongest peak (< 101 >). The intensity significantly decreased, even to
the level of background noise after 16 h milling for 1g loading.
 Figure 16 shows the intensity of < 101 > peak (strongest peak) with milling time. The
decreasing diffraction peak intensities indicated that the milling degraded the crystallinity
of the powder and weakened their X-ray scattering ability. For the boride, about 3 h milling
could decrease the intensity down to 30 % of the original intensity even for 8 g loading. In
literature, a 1-2 h milling duration was usually employed to produce nanomaterials.
 The peak positions slightly shifted to higher anglers because of increasing lattice release.
 Additionally, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peaks increased
with milling duration, as shown in Figure 17, indicating the crystalline size of the powders
decreased. Under the four different boride loadings, X-ray diffraction peaks showed similar
behaviors with milling time. Additionally, 16-hour milling could amorphize two grams boride
while 4-8 hour milling could amorphize one gram boride, per run.

4.4 Seebeck Coefficient
The electrical conductivity of the boride disks was measured. In addition, a Seebeck coeffi-
cient measurement system was established during the project, as shown in Figure 29. The
system consisted of a furnace and multiple thermocouples for temperature measurements.
Voltage and temperature signals were acquired through analog-to-digital converters (ADC)
and controlled via digital-to-analog converters (DAC). All signals were collected and con-
trolled by a Raspberry Pi single-board computer. A graphical user interface was developed
using the Python programming language.

4.5 Stability
Nanoparticles under electron-irradiation
 The irradiation tolerance of the boride nanoparticles was characterized using transmission
electron microscopy.

 Figure 29: Customized Seebeck efficient system.

 Figure 30 shows CBED patterns of one typical ZrB2 crystalline particle under electron
irradiations. The diffraction patterns indicated that the crystallographic structure of the
compound was totally tolerant to high-energy (120 keV) electron irradiation up to 60 min
under 500 pA/cm2 . SAED patterns and TEM images confirmed the structure stability under
the irradiations.
 However, more detailed analyses showed that the CBED Kikuchi bands were slightly
blurred after irradiations. Additionally, a faint diffraction ring was naked-eye observed in
the SAED patterns of the irradiated ZrB2 crystals. The faint diffraction ring and blurred
Kikuchi bands indicated that some kinds of disorders were produced in the crystals under
the irradiations.
 In order to explore the original of the possible disorders, EDS spectra of the ZrB2 crystals
were in-situ recorded during the irradiations. Figure 31 shows elements of the crystal
under irradiation. Boron content continuously decreased with irradiation time. Because
of the EDS window absorption, it is hard to measure the accurate boron content of the
ZrB2 crystals. However, the decreasing boron content indicated that the boron atoms lost
during the electron irradiation. With the lost of boron atoms, the Zr content increased with
irradiation time. Most possibly, the boron atoms were kicked off the original lattice sites.
Therefore, some surface boron atoms left the crystal while some inside boron atoms occupied
disordered positions, resulting in faint or blurred diffraction rings / bands. At the same time,

Nanoparticles under high temperature in air
 The thermal stability of the p-type boride powders was also examined using Thermo-
gravimetric Analysis (TGA) and Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC). Figure 33 shows
the thermal properties of the boride particles. The boride particles were thermal stable up
to 800 ◦ C in argon.
 Additionally, to investigate the thermal stability of borides in air, a customized ther-
mogravimetric analysis (TGA) system was developed during the project. The TGA system
consisted of an electric furnace and a precision balance, from which temperature and mass
changes of the boride samples were collected through a single-board computer. A data
acquisition software was written, and a graphical user interface (GUI) was being developed.

 Additionally, the oxidation tolerance of the boride nanocomposites was evaluated in air.
The oxidation resistance of both p-type and n-type boride nanocomposites was investigated
in air using high-temperature ex-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD). The boride nanocomposite
disks were heated in air at a rate of 50 ◦ C/min from 500 ◦ C to 1,200 ◦ C in increments
of 50 ◦ C, followed by XRD measurements at each step. Figure 34 shows X-ray diffraction
patterns of the heated nanocomposites. The diffraction patterns indicated that the boride

tolerance.
 Based on the stability of the boride nanoparticles and nanocomposites, it is expected that
thermocouples fabricated from these nanocomposites could exhibit good oxidation tolerance.

4.6 Key Findings
This project demonstrated that boride compounds can be synthesized via a carbothermal
reduction method. The resulting boride nanomaterials exhibited strong tolerance to high-
energy electron irradiation in vacuum and high-temperature exposure in oxygen-containing
environments. These properties indicated that borides are promising thermoelectric materi-
als for high-temperature applications as thermal sensors. In addition, several measurement
systems were developed and instrumented through Python programming using single-board
computers.
