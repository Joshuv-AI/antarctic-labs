Mortality and morbidity in very preterm infants in Switzerland spanning 16 years (2006–2021)
Authors: Anaïs Mons, Otis Olela, Nicolas Silvestrini, Mark Adams, Riccardo E Pfister, Francisca Barcos Munoz, The Swiss Neonatal Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo report population-based gestational age (GA) stratified mortality and morbidity for very preterm infants spanning 16 years in Switzerland.DesignSwiss population-based study with prospectively collected data for infants born at less than 32 weeks GA, spanning three time periods: 2006–2011 (P1), 2012–2016 (P2), 2017–2021 (P3).SettingThe Swiss Neonatal Network, covering all Level III neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and affiliated paediatric hospitals.PopulationPreterm live-born infants (12 453) less than 32 weeks GA.Main outcome measuresTrends in GA-related mortality, survival without major complications and short-term morbidities among very preterm infants in Switzerland.ResultsOverall survival increased significantly from 85.4% in P1 to 89.7% in P3 (p<0.01), with greatest gains among infants born before 26 weeks GA. Delivery room and NICU mortality declined significantly, from 6.0% to 4.1% and from 8.6% to 6.2%, respectively.Survival without major complications—such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) and cystic periventricular leukomalacia (cPVL)—remained stable (around 72%). Major morbidities occurred in 17.3% of survivors.Among survivors, BPD and ROP significantly increased across periods while NEC increased only in infants born at 25 weeks GA. Conversely, cPVL declined over time.ConclusionsThe survival rate of very preterm infants in Switzerland increased from 2006 to 2021, especially in those born before 26 weeks GA. Survival without major comorbidities among infants born at 23–31 weeks remained unchanged. In survivors, BPD and ROP increased over time; NEC increased only in 25-week infants.Keywords: Neonatology, Epidemiology, Mortality

Introduction

Preterm births represent 6.3%–13.3% of live births in Europe (35 countries)1 and 7.8%–10.4% in North America (Canada and USA).2 3 The Swiss Federal Statistical Office reported 89 644 newborns in 2021, with 6.3% classified as preterm, including 0.8% born before 32 weeks of gestation, and 0.9% as very low birth weight. Prematurity remains the single most important contributor to infant mortality and morbidity in developed countries. Although births occurring before 32 weeks of gestation represent only a small proportion of all births (approximately 1.3% in Europe and 1.6% in the USA), they account for a disproportionate share of neonatal mortality, contributing to 30%–51% of neonatal deaths.1 3–5 Advances in patient management and the resulting decrease in the limit of viability by gestational age (GA) have led to changes in mortality and morbidity. The Swiss Neonatal Network reported 84% survival of extremely preterm infants during their first hospitalisation.6 This 2016 prospective observational study in Switzerland (2000–2012) reported improved survival rates and decreased grade 3–4 intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH). However, there was no improvement in cystic periventricular leukomalacia (cPVL), necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD; moderate and severe).

An up-to-date comparative analysis of major short-term morbidities is essential for assessing obstetrical and neonatal care quality, counselling families and planning patient management strategies.

The goal of the present work was to provide an essential national update for Switzerland. The study examined trends in GA-stratified overall mortality, as well as delivery room and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) mortality from 2006 to 2021. It also examined short-term morbidities and survival without major complications at discharge. The analysis focused on preterm infants below 32 weeks GA during this period.

Methods

We analysed prospectively collected, observational data from the Swiss Neonatal Network (SNN), covering 96% of the Swiss preterm population below 32 weeks GA, verified through the Swiss Birth Registry.7 Live-born preterm infants from 22+0/7 to 31+6/7 weeks GA between January 2006 and December 2021 were included.

The SNN includes nine level III and eleven level IIB neonatal centres, which systematically collected data on all live-born infants less than 32 weeks GA or with a birth weight less than 1501 g. Since 2006, data collection has been exclusively electronic with built-in algorithms monitoring data completeness, plausibility and consistency; centres are required to address discrepancies annually. Missing data accounted for only 4%, primarily from infants not delivered at participating centres. Given the minimal loss, listwise deletion was used without imputation. GA was determined by the last menstrual period of the mother and confirmed or adjusted via first-trimester ultrasound. Very preterm infants were defined as those born before 32 weeks GA, and extremely preterm as those born before 28 weeks GA. Major short-term morbidities included grade 3 or 4 IVH,8 cPVL,9 NEC (grades II/III),10 severe retinopathy of prematurity (ROP, grades 3–5)11 and moderate-to-severe BPD, diagnosed using oxygen and/or positive pressure support at 36 weeks GA with reduction testing where applicable.12

Mortality outcomes included overall mortality (any death after live birth), delivery room mortality and NICU mortality—stratified into neonatal (<28 days) and post-neonatal (≥28 days). Survival without major morbidities was defined as NICU survivors without IVH, cPVL, NEC, ROP or BPD. Composite outcomes (death or major morbidity) were assessed only among NICU-admitted infants.

Antenatal corticosteroids were considered complete if two doses were administered at least 24 hours apart, with the final dose administered more than 24 hours before delivery. Inborn status was defined by delivery within a participating centre. Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as a birth weight <10th percentile for the GA. Major congenital malformations included lethal anomalies or those requiring neonatal surgical intervention.

Primary outcomes evaluated GA-specific mortality trends across three periods: 2006–2011 (P1), 2012–2016 (P2) and 2017–2021 (P3). Secondary outcomes included overall survival, survival without major morbidity at discharge and short-term neonatal complications.

Statistical analysis

Rates were reported as percentages with 95% CIs for categorical variables, and medians with IQRs for continuous variables. Differences across three time periods were analysed using χ² tests for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis for continuous variables, with Holm-adjusted p values and Wilcoxon tests for pairwise comparisons. Two multiple logistic regression models were run on the occurrence of morbidities and overall mortality, and used for adjusted OR (aOR) with 95% CI. Delivery room and NICU deaths were excluded. The multiple logistic regression models met all requirements.

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (V.4.3.1; R Core Team, 2021).

Results

Over 16 years (2006–2021), Switzerland recorded 1 325 526 live births, of which 12 453 (0.9%) were preterm infants born before 32 weeks GA: 4456 in P1 (2006–2011), 4105 in P2 (2012–2016) and 3892 in P3 (2017–2021). Infants born at 22+0/7 to 22+6/7 weeks (n=232) were excluded due to 100% delivery room mortality across all periods (P1: 69; P2: 74; P3: 89).

Delivery room mortality decreased from P1 to P3 for infants born at 23 weeks (90.4%–62.5%, p<0.01), 24 weeks (33.8%–10.6%, p<0.01). A significant reduction was also observed between P1 and P2 for infants born at 23 and 24 weeks GA, and remained significant between P2 and P3 for infants born at 24 weeks GA. NICU mortality decreased between P1 and P3 overall (8.6%–6.2%, p<0.01), with the most pronounced decline at 25 weeks GA (33.6%–15.2%). Neonatal mortality decreased from 7.1% in P1 to 5.1% in P3 (p<0.01), while post-neonatal mortality (≥28 days) remained unchanged (see online supplemental table).

Survival

Overall survival improved from P1 to P3 (85.4%–89.7%, p<0.01), specifically in infants born at 23, 24, 25 and 29 weeks GA. Survival also significantly increased from P2 to P3 for infants at 24 and 25 weeks GA (table 2).

Survival without major complications remained stable across periods for infants between 23 and 31 weeks: 71.5% (95% CI 70.1% to 72.8%) in P1, 73.0% (95% CI 71.6% to 74.3%) in P2 and 72.9% (95% CI 71.4% to 74.3%) in P3 (figure 1B). Improvements were observed among the 24-week (15%–25.4%) and 29-week infants (80.7%–86.0%) from P1 to P3 (p<0.05), though these differences were not statistically significant between periods (table 2).

IVH incidence among survivors remained stable across all three periods. The composite outcome of IVH or death significantly declined from P1 (13.0%) to P3 (10.1%), particularly in infants born at 25 weeks (44.5%–22.6%, p<0.01) and 29 weeks (7.9%–3.5%, p<0.01) GA.

cPVL incidence among survivors decreased from 2.5% in P1 to 1.6% in P2 and 1.2% in P3. However, this reduction was statistically significant only between P1 and P3. The composite outcome of cPVL or death in the NICU significantly decreased from P1 (12.5%) to P3 (7.9%), with the most significant reduction between 24 and 27 weeks GA.

NEC incidence among survivors significantly increased from P1 to P3 among infants at 25 weeks only (1.7%–10.7%, p<0.01). However, no difference was found overall. The composite outcome of NEC or death in the NICU revealed a statistically significant decrease from P1 to P3 for infants at a GA of 25 weeks (36.9%–24.5%, p<0.01) and 29 weeks (6.1%–1.9%, p<0.01).

ROP incidence increased significantly from 2.0% in P1 to 3.4% in P3. However, the composite outcome of ROP or death at 25 weeks GA significantly decreased from P1 to P3 (40.4%–26.5%, p<0.01).

BPD incidence (23–31 weeks) increased from 10.2% (95% CI 9.2%–11.2%) in P1 to 11.9% (95% CI 10.9%–13%) in P2, and 12.9% (95% CI 11.8%–14%) (p<0.01) in P3. The composite outcome of BPD or death did not change between periods.

Regarding morbidities, SGA was significantly associated with higher risks of BPD and ROP. Antenatal corticosteroids were protective against severe IVH and cPVL, but associated with increased BPD risk. Female gender was protective for BPD. Congenital anomalies increased the risk of BPD and IVH, while sepsis was linked to BPD, IVH and NEC. Higher GA reduced the risk of all major morbidities.

Discussion

This observational, population-based study examined mortality trends, major short-term complications and survival without major complications in very low birth weight infants in Switzerland across three periods spanning 16 years. Its strengths include a large sample size, nationwide design, and an inclusion rate of 96% of all live-born infants. It provides up-to-date information for family counselling prior to and during hospitalisation of very preterm infants.

Survival of very preterm infants in Switzerland

Overall, survival increased significantly by 4.3% from 85.4% in 2006 to 89.7% in 2021, confirming previous trends in very preterm infants in Switzerland,6 mainly among the youngest infants (23–25 weeks GA). Our results are similar to the main European studies. The EPIPAGE-2 cohort reported substantial gains in survival and survival without major morbidities in infants born at 25–31 weeks between 1997 and 2011; however, no improvement was reported for infants born before 25 weeks.13 Similarly, the German Neonatal Network found a 1.6% increase in survival between 2011 and 2013 and between 2014 and 2016 for infants born at 22–28 weeks GA receiving active care, specifically from 53% to 64% for infants 22–24 weeks and 73% to 84% for those at 25–26 weeks.14 A Swedish cohort showed a significant increase in 1 year survival rates among live-born infants at 22–26 weeks, rising from 70% during the 2004–2007 period to 77% in 2014–2016.15 Comparable trends were reported in the USA, where survival at 22–28 weeks GA improved from 76% (2008–2012) to 78.3% (2013–2018).16

Survival at the lowest gestational ages

Perinatal care at very early gestational ages was guided by Swiss ethical recommendations from 2002,17 updated in 201118 and recommended palliative care between 22+0/7 and 22+6/7 weeks GA. Although not recommended at 23+0/7 to 23+6/7 weeks, the 2011 guideline specified that intensive care may be acceptable in individual cases.

Since 2012, survival rates have improved, particularly among infants less than 26 weeks GA, likely driven by reduced delivery room mortality. Survival-focused care before 24 weeks has gradually increased, challenging the 2011 guidelines.18 This trend likely reflects growing clinical experience with advances in obstetrical, perinatal and neonatal management, and international influence. Survivors at 23+0/7 to 23+6/7 weeks rose from 3.2% in P1 to 20.6% in P3, while delivery room mortality declined from 90% to 62.5% (p<0.01).

No infant born before 23 weeks GA survived during the study period, consistent with national recommendations. In contrast, a 2018–2020 Japanese survey reported survival rates of 63% at 22 weeks and 80% at 23 weeks among actively treated infants.19 These differences underscore the impact of ethical guidelines, based on outcomes of extremely preterm infants, though long-term follow-up beyond 3 years remains limited.20 21

Building on up-to-date and additional data such as ours, including modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors, as well as differentiating between palliative and survival-focused care, the new 2025 Swiss guidelines now advocate for individualised risk assessment from 23+0/7 weeks GA.22

Morbidities in very preterm infants

From 2006 to 2021, survival rates among extremely low GA newborns increased in Switzerland, with a non-significant rise in survival without major complications. Improved delivery room survival, without a corresponding increase in major short-term morbidities, is reassuring, although long-term outcomes warrant further evaluation. The observed decline in composite outcomes of death, IVH, cPVL, NEC and ROP at the lowest gestational age is likely attributable to the reduced mortality. These findings are consistent with data from Europe, North America and Japan, which show decreased mortality with limited improvement in neonatal morbidities, although not across all gestational ages.13 14 19 23

The combined outcome of IVH or death declined over time, while IVH among survivors remained unchanged. The cPVL incidence nearly halved. These trends are consistent with findings from France, Sweden, the USA and Canada,13 15 16 24 25 though Swiss rates remain comparatively lower. The EPIPAGE study13 showed a non-significant reduction in cPVL at 24–26 weeks, whereas the Swedish cohort15 reported a significant decrease at 22–26 weeks, reflecting broader improvements in perinatal care.

While NEC or death declined at 25 and 29 weeks GA, its incidence among survivors at 25 weeks GA significantly increased from P1 to P3. This contrasts with earlier French data13 but aligns with Dutch and Swedish reports linking rising NEC rates to more active treatment and improved survival.26 27 Consistently low NEC rates in Japan28 have been attributed to widespread breastfeeding, early minimal enteral feeding and probiotic use. Our findings may reflect improved survival of vulnerable infants born at or near 25 weeks GA, potentially including cases of spontaneous intestinal perforation that were misclassified, particularly if they did not receive surgical treatment.27

Similar to the international data,13 15 16 24 25 ROP incidence among survivors remained stable, except for a non-significant increasing trend among infants born at 23 and 25 weeks GA. This trend probably reflects improved survival, similar to what was observed with NEC.

Overall, no significant change in BPD or death was observed across periods. However, BPD among survivors increased significantly from 10.2% to 12.9% between P1 and P3 (p<0.01), likely due to improved survival at lower gestational ages. The EPIPAGE-2 study13 reported a 4% decrease in BPD between 1997 and 2011 among infants born at 27–31 weeks, but an overall incidence of 12.3%.29 Our incidence in this group remained stable at approximately 8.5%. In the USA, studies noted rising BPD rates in infants below 25 weeks,14 26 and Löfberg et al30 in Sweden linked greater use of respiratory support to a 10% increase in moderate BPD, but a 5% decline in severe BPD, with unchanged overall rates. As BPD was analysed as a combined outcome of moderate and severe BPD in our study, we were unable to assess potential changes in the distribution of BPD severity over time. Similarly, changes in respiratory management, such as surfactant use and less invasive ventilation, may have influenced our BPD outcomes differently across GA strata. Despite evolving practices and definitions, a recent systematic review found no significant change in overall BPD rates over three decades. However, similar to our findings, pooled data may obscure trends, particularly at low gestational ages, where published sample sizes are limited, warranting caution in interpretation.31

Influence of perinatal factors

As expected, our Swiss data confirmed strong associations between low birth weight, congenital anomalies, NEC and IVH with mortality. At the same time, protective factors included higher GA, antenatal corticosteroid exposure and being born during the most recent study period, which were consistent with existing literature.30

Despite unchanged antenatal corticosteroid use of 70% receiving a full course over the three periods, similar to international reports,24 29 32–34 steroids were significantly correlated to higher moderate and severe BPD rates, as published earlier in Switzerland.6 While corticosteroids are known to reduce mortality and offer neuroprotection, their association with higher BPD rates was unexpected. A similar observation was reported in an Australian review, which found an increased risk of BPD (OR 1.32; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.67) in infants born before 25 weeks GA.35

Increased rates of BPD have been documented in other cohorts, notably in Sweden,27 despite consistent antenatal corticosteroid administration. In that setting, moderate BPD incidence rose by 10%, whereas severe cases declined by 5%.27 As we have not separated moderate from severe BPD in our data, direct comparisons are limited. However, the overall increase may be solely attributable to a rise in moderate cases.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations, notably the exclusion of infants born at 22 weeks of gestation and stillbirths, which underestimate perinatal mortality. We acknowledge that for meaningful international comparisons, it is essential to specify appropriate denominators for mortality rates, which should include stillbirths and neonatal deaths from 22 weeks of gestation onward. Therefore, such comparisons require alignment in legislation and clinical practices across countries.36 Comprehensive perinatal decision-making at the earliest gestational ages may also require an obstetric perspective.

During the study period, no national Swiss recommendations mandated the implementation of specific practices such as Less Invasive Surfactant Administration or prophylactic postnatal corticosteroid therapy. Consequently, evidence-based interventions were introduced at different time points across neonatal units according to local protocols. Importantly, some strategies were not consistently applied throughout all epochs. In the absence of harmonised nationwide data on the timing and uptake of these interventions, their contribution to the observed temporal trends could not be formally assessed and should be considered when interpreting the results. Future studies should incorporate data on the adoption of these and other emerging therapeutic practices.

Finally, our study focused exclusively on short-term outcomes, while acknowledging that a long-term follow-up to adulthood is essential.37

Conclusion

In conclusion, our population-based data from 2006 to 2021 on very preterm infants in Switzerland report a high standard of neonatal care and provide essential information for medical and parental decision-making. While overall survival has increased, particularly for infants below 26 weeks GA, survival without major complications has remained stable. However, BPD, NEC and ROP rates increased in the most immature survivors and will impact future healthcare needs.
