Association of Sodium‐Glucose Cotransporter‐2 Inhibitors With Incident Gout Risk: A Population‐Based Comparative Cohort Study With Genetic Evidence From Mendelian Randomisation
Authors: Hong Sang Choi, Jaejun Heo, Sungho Won, Sang Heon Suh, Chang Seong Kim, Eun Hui Bae, Juhee Ahn, Soo Wan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimSodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are known to reduce serum urate levels, yet their specific impact on gout incidence in the Korean population remains underexplored. We investigated the association between SGLT2i use and incident gout using real‐world data and Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a population‐based cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database (2015–2020). New users of SGLT2i were compared to dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor (DPP4i) users. After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), 20 866 pairs were analysed using Cox proportional hazards models. The primary outcome was incident gout. Additionally, a two‐sample MR analysis utilised genetic variants in SLC5A2 as proxies for SGLT2 inhibition and gout summary statistics from the FinnGen consortium.ResultsIn the cohort study, SGLT2i use was associated with a significantly lower risk of gout compared to DPP4i (incidence rate: 2792.6 vs. 3346.7 per 100 000 person‐years). The hazard ratio in the PSM model was 0.80 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.76–0.84, p value < 0.001). This finding was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, including landmark and time‐varying exposure models. In the MR analysis, genetically proxied SGLT2 inhibition was associated with a reduced risk of gout (odds ratio 0.10 per 1‐standard deviation decrease in HbA1c; 95% CI: 0.017–0.463; p value < 0.001), with consistent results across robust MR methods.ConclusionSGLT2i therapy is associated with a lower risk of incident gout compared to DPP4i in patients with type 2 diabetes. While these findings suggest potential pleiotropic effects of SGLT2i beyond glucose control, they should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of observational study designs.

Introduction

Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) frequently exhibit hyperuricemia and are at elevated risk for gout, largely due to shared cardiometabolic risk factors such as obesity, insulin resistance, and chronic kidney disease [1]. Insulin resistance in T2DM can impair renal urate excretion, contributing to hyperuricemia and gout in this population [1]. These intersecting pathophysiological pathways underscore the need to explore whether contemporary T2DM therapies can effectively modulate gout risk.

Sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have emerged as a cornerstone therapy in T2DM, not only improving glycemic control but also conferring cardiovascular and renal benefits. Several post hoc analyses of early randomised controlled trials showed significantly reduced serum urate levels in the SGLT2i group compared to the placebo group [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], and attenuated gout incidence and associated outcomes such as gout flare or initiation of gout therapy [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In these studies, SGLT2i showed a meaningful urate reduction of 0.6–1.5 mg/dL, and are postulated to reduce gout risk by approximately 30%–50% [10]. In contrast, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP4i) are not known to possess urate‐lowering properties.

Emerging epidemiological evidence supports a gout‐protective effect of SGLT2i. A nationwide cohort study in Taiwan reported that T2DM patients on SGLT2i had a significantly lower incidence of gout than those on DPP4i [11]. Similarly, a Hong Kong study demonstrated a 51% relative risk reduction in new‐onset gout with SGLT2i versus DPP4i [12]. Consistent findings have been observed in other populations. A U.S. cohort study found a 36% lower gout rate in SGLT2i initiators compared to GLP‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1RA) users [13]. Nevertheless, no study to date has specifically examined this association in a Korean population, leaving an evidence gap given potential ethnic and healthcare system differences.

Against this caveat, we designed a real‐world cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) database to evaluate the association between SGLT2i use and incident gout risk, in comparison to DPP4i, among T2DM patients in Korea. Furthermore, we applied Mendelian randomisation (MR) to provide complementary genetic evidence on the association between genetically proxied SGLT2 inhibition and gout risk. By integrating large‐scale, real‐world epidemiological data with robust genetic evidence, this study aims to fill the existing knowledge gap and clarify the potential role of SGLT2i in the prevention of gout.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We employed two complementary study designs: a population‐based cohort study and a two‐sample MR (TSMR) analysis. The overall study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (IRB no. CNUH‐EXP‐2025‐066).

For the cohort study, we constructed a T2DM cohort using the KNHIS database (Research No. NHIS‐2025‐04‐1‐054). KNHIS is a mandatory, government‐induced universal health insurance system that covers nearly the entire Korean population and maintains comprehensive medical records on all reimbursed healthcare services. The dataset includes demographic characteristics, diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), prescription and procedure records, and mortality data. All personal records were fully anonymized in compliance with data protection regulations. This nationally representative dataset enabled a robust assessment of real‐world health outcomes in a large‐scale population [14].

For the MR analysis, we used publicly available summary‐level data. Genetic variants associated with SGLT2 inhibition were obtained from the epidemiological study by Xu et al. [15]. Six single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), previously validated as reliable instrumental variables (IVs), were selected. Outcome data were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (release R12) [16], which provides large‐scale, population‐based genetic association summary statistics for a wide range of diseases.

Population‐Based Cohort Study

Study Population

Individuals with T2DM who were prescribed either SGLT2i or DPP4i between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020, were identified. To minimise immortal time bias, we applied a 3‐month landmark analysis. The cohort entry date was defined as the date of the initial prescription of an SGLT2i or DPP4i, and the index date was set 3 months later. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) prior use of an SGLT2i or DPP4i before the cohort entry date; (ii) history of haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation before the cohort entry date; (iii) history of gout before the index date; (iv) use of a combination drug containing both SGLT2i and DPP4i; (v) switching to the alternative drug class after the cohort entry date (117 946 of 422 397 DPP4i initiators and 26 581 of 51 053 SGLT2i initiators); (vi) a proportion of days covered (PDC) < 70% during the first 3‐month landmark period; or (vii) missing key variables, including health examination data. Further details of the study are presented in (Figure 1A and Table S1).

SGLT2i Use and Outcome of Interest

SGLT2i users were defined as individuals prescribed with dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ipragliflozin, or ertugliflozin at cohort entry date. DPP4i users were selected as the active comparator to reduce confounding by indication, as these agents are typically prescribed at a similar stage of T2DM management [17]. To ensure the incident and regular drug use, a PDC ≥ 70% during the first 3 months following the initial prescription was required in both groups.

The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of gout, defined as a new diagnosis with ICD‐10 code M10 after the index date. Eligible individuals were followed from the index date until the first occurrence of gout, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2023), whichever occurred first. In the sensitivity analyses that included individuals who switched to the alternative drug class (e.g., from SGLT2i to DPP4i), treatment switching was treated as a censoring to account for changes in treatment during follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as means with standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Between‐group differences were compared using Student's t‐test for continuous variables and χ 2 test for categorical variables.

Propensity scores (PS) were estimated for each individual using multivariable logistic regression with baseline covariates, including age at prescription, sex, household income, residential area, body mass index, waist circumference, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), diabetes duration, comorbidities (hypertension and dyslipidemia), other antidiabetic medications use, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and prescription year. One‐to‐one PS matching (PSM) between SGLT2i and DPP4i users was performed using a greedy nearest‐neighbour method with a calliper width of 0.2, ensuring exact matching for sex and prescription year. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardised mean differences (ASMDs), with ASMD < 0.1 considered acceptable.

Cumulative incidence curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier (KM) method and compared with the log‐rank test. Additionally, a covariate‐adjusted survival curve was constructed using a Cox proportional hazards model to account for baseline risk differences. Incidence rates (IRs) per 100 000 person‐years with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated assuming a Poisson distribution. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, and the proportional hazards assumption was confirmed for all covariates using Schoenfeld residual‐based tests. Three models were evaluated: (i) unadjusted (crude); (ii) adjusted for all baseline covariates (Model 1); and (iii) PSM‐adjusted (Model 2).

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, individuals were analysed to their initially assigned treatment, including those who switched to the alternative drug class, without applying adherence restrictions. Second, a 1‐year lag analysis excluded individuals who developed gout within 1 year after the index date to account for diagnostic delay. Third, multiple landmark times (6, 9 and 12 months) and two PDC thresholds were explored. Fourth, Cox models with inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) were conducted to address informative censoring [18] due to treatment switching. Fifth, time‐dependent Cox models were used, in which drug exposure status was updated every 3 months and categorised as high or low based on a PDC threshold of 70% [19]. Sixth, both IPCW and time‐varying exposure were simultaneously applied. Seventh, analyses were restricted to patients who did not use other glucose‐lowering medications during follow‐up. Eighth, analyses additionally accounted for baseline outpatient visit frequency and hospitalisation count during the 12 months prior to landmark time. Lastly, a subdistribution hazard model was performed to account for all‐cause death as a competing risk.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the treatment effect varied by baseline clinical characteristics. Additional analyses assessed outcomes by individual SGLT2i sub‐classes.

All analyses were two‐sided with a significance threshold of p value < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Mendelian Randomisation Study

Study Outcome and Genetic Instruments for SGLT2i

The primary outcome of the MR analysis was the development of gout, defined as at least three occurrences of gout diagnoses or prescriptions for gout‐specific medications. The outcome dataset included 6926 gout cases among 520 210 individuals, and detailed definitions are provided in Table S1. Six SNPs in the SLC5A2 gene region associated with SGLT2 inhibition were selected based on their reported associations with SLC5A2 expression in GTEx and eQTLGen, as well as with haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels in the UK Biobank [20]. The exposure was defined as genetically proxied HbA1c reduction through SGLT2 inhibition. The overall process for instrument selection and outcome definition is illustrated in Figure 1B.

Statistical Analysis

To confirm the validity of the MR framework [21], we examined the three IV assumptions. Instrument strength was assessed using F‐statistics, with values greater than 10 considered sufficient. Heterogeneity across IVs was evaluated using Cochran's Q and Rücker's Q′ statistics. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed with the MR‐Egger intercept and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier test (MR‐PRESSO) global test. In addition, the I 2 statistic was calculated to evaluate potential violation of the NO Measurement Error (NOME) assumption.

Causal associations were derived using multiple MR methods: inverse‐variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, weighted mode, simple mode, and MR‐Egger regression [22]. To complement the primary analyses and assess robustness, we additionally applied MR‐Egger with simulation extrapolation (SIMEX), MR‐PRESSO, generalised summary‐data‐based MR (GSMR), and robust adjusted profile score (MR‐RAPS) [23, 24, 25, 26]. Results are presented as β coefficients scaled to a 1‐SD decrease in inverse rank‐normal transformed HbA1c (≈6.75 mmol/mol, 1.09% on the raw scale), along with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses included single‐SNP and leave‐one‐out (LOO) analyses to examine the effect of individual variants on the overall estimates. Funnel and scatter plots were generated to visually inspect potential heterogeneity.

All MR analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) with the TwoSampleMR, simex, MR‐PRESSO, GSMR, and MR‐RAPS packages.

Population‐Based Cohort Study

Demographics of the Study Population

Before PSM, all baseline characteristics except for alcohol consumption differed significantly between the two groups. Compared with DPP4i users, SGLT2i users were younger (56.0 vs. 62.8 years), more frequently males (55.2% vs. 54.0%), and had higher BMI (27.9 vs. 25.3 kg/m2) and higher eGFR (90.8 vs. 86.7 mL/min/1.73 m2), but a shorter duration of diabetes (5.5 vs. 6.9 years). The Effect of SGLT2i Group on Gout

During the follow‐up, 2730 gout events occurred in the SGLT2i group and 42 413 events in the DPP4i group. The IRs of gout were 2792.6 per 100 000 person‐years (95% CI: 2689.8–2899.4) in the SGLT2i group and 3346.7 per 100 000 person‐years (95% CI: 3315.0–3378.7) in the DPP4i group. Unadjusted KM curve demonstrated a lower cumulative incidence of gout in the SGLT2i group in the overall population (p value for log‐rank test < 0.001; Figure 2A). This pattern remained consistent in the covariate‐adjusted survival curve and PSM‐adjusted KM curve (Figure 2B,C). In Cox regression models, SGLT2i use was significantly associated with a reduced risk of gout compared with DPP4i use in crude model (HR 0.84, 95% CI: 0.81–0.87, p value < 0.001; Table 2) and fully adjusted model (HR 0.79, 95% CI: 0.76–0.83, p value < 0.001; Table 2). PSM‐adjusted model showed a similar result (HR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.76–0.84, p value < 0.001; Table 2).

Sensitivity and Additional Analyses for Cohort Study

The protective effect of SGLT2i use was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. First, the association persisted when individuals were analysed by their initial treatment assignment, regardless of adherence or switching (HR 0.93, 95% CI: 0.90–0.96, p value < 0.001; Table 3). Second, excluding individuals who developed gout within 1 year of the index date did not alter the findings (HR 0.79, 95% CI: 0.75–0.84, p value < 0.001; Table S2). Third, results were consistent across alternative landmark times and PDC thresholds (Table S3). Fourth, accounting for treatment switching with IPCW, time‐varying exposure, and their combined application all yielded similar results (Figure S1; Tables S4–S6). Lastly, restricting the analysis to patients without other glucose‐lowering medications during follow‐up, accounting for baseline healthcare utilisation, and performing competing risk analysis for all‐cause death also produced consistent results (Tables S7–S9).

Mendelian Randomisation Study

Selected Genetic Variants and Verification

Tables S12 and S13 summarise the six genetic instruments for HbA1c used in MR study and their summary of diagnostics. Their F‐statistics ranged from 13.20 to 46.61, all above the conventional threshold of 10. The three core IV assumptions were satisfied, supporting use of the IVW estimator (F‐statistic = 26.33, p value for Q > 0.05, p value for Q′ > 0.05, p value for MR‐Egger intercept > 0.05, p value for MR‐PRESSO global test > 0.05). However, the NOME assumption was violated since I 2 was less than 20%. Taken together, there was no evidence of weak instrument bias, heterogeneity, or horizontal pleiotropy; MR‐Egger results should be interpreted with caution given the NOME violation.

Sensitivity Analyses for MR Study

The scatter plot indicated consistent causal estimates (Figure S2A). Results were directionally consistent across multiple MR methods, except for MR‐Egger. Meanwhile, the funnel plot showed asymmetry, suggesting potential heterogeneity (Figure S2B). However, robust methods, including GSMR and MR‐RAPS, yielded similar inverse associations, which indicate that the overall findings were not materially affected.

Single‐SNP analyses showed concordant effect across all genetic variants (Figure S2C), and LOO analyses produced consistent overall estimates (Figure S2D). Since no outliers were detected by MR‐PRESSO, the results were not driven by any single variant.

Discussion

In this nationwide Korean cohort of T2DM, initiation of SGLT2i was associated with a lower incidence of gout than initiation of DPP4i. The reduced risk of gout showed a similar protective trend as in our primary analysis across multiple sensitivity analyses. However, the magnitude was attenuated under alternative definitions of treatment strategy, although the direction of the association remained consistent. In drug‐specific analyses, significant associations were observed for dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, but not consistently across all SGLT2i. Complementing the observational analysis, a TSMR using genetic proxies for SGLT2 inhibition yielded results consistent with a lower gout risk, providing supportive genetic evidence. In short, our data suggest that SGLT2 inhibitor use was associated with a lower risk of incident gout in Korean patients with T2DM, although the magnitude was attenuated under alternative definitions of treatment exposure. Regarding drug‐specific analyses, significant associations were observed for dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, but not consistently across all SGLT2i. However, these analyses were secondary and exploratory in nature, and the substantially smaller subgroup sizes for ipragliflozin and ertugliflozin compared with dapagliflozin and empagliflozin limited our statistical power. Thus, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of differential benefit across individual SGLT2i agents.

Several mechanisms have been known as the urate‐lowering and anti‐gout effects of SGLT2i. First, SGLT2 blockade induces a metabolic state similar to starvation or fasting [10]. By promoting glycosuria and mimicking a nutrient‐deprived state, SGLT2i shift cellular metabolism away from anabolic pathways [27, 28]. Specifically, they decrease flux through the pentose phosphate pathway which generates ribose‐5‐phosphate for purine synthesis, thereby reducing the endogenous production of purine and urate [10, 29]. Second, increased urinary glucose directly affects renal urate handling [30, 31]. Animal studies show that glycosuria raises luminal glucose concentrations in the proximal tubule, which in turn interacts with urate transporters, notably GLUT9 and URAT1, to block urate reabsorption [31]. Novikov et al. [31] demonstrated in SGLT2‐inhibited or knockout mice that fractional excretion of urate more than doubles, even without a direct drug effect on urate transporters. Third, indirect effects may contribute. Weight loss and improved metabolic profile on SGLT2i might further reduce urate production. Taken together, these mechanisms explain why large‐scale randomised cardiovascular and renal trials have consistently reported reduced urate and substantially fewer gout events among SGLT2i‐treated patients [10].

Our results align qualitatively with earlier epidemiologic studies showing SGLT2i confer a gout‐protective benefit, though the magnitude of risk reduction has varied. A Taiwanese nationwide cohort found that SGLT2i users had an ~11% lower adjusted risk of incident gout than DPP4i users (HR 0.89; 95% CI: 0.82–0.96) [11]. In contrast, Hong Kong cohort reported a much larger effect: SGLT2i use was associated with a 51% lower gout risk (HR 0.49; 95% CI: 0.42–0.58) [12]. A U.S. population study comparing SGLT2i to GLP1‐RA also showed a significant benefit—about 36% lower gout incidence risk (HR 0.64; 95% CI: 0.57–0.72) [13]. Our estimated risk reduction in Koreans falls between these figures, consistent in direction but differing in magnitude. Several factors may explain these differences. First, the choice of comparator drug differs, which affects the baseline risk of gout in the referent group [11, 13]. Second, population characteristics such as age, diet, genetic predisposition, prevalence of hyperuricemia and healthcare practices may vary by region. For example, the Hong Kong cohort had longer follow‐up and more rigorous control of confounders, which might amplify the observed effect. Third, methodological differences, for example, propensity‐score methods, outcome definitions, diagnostic criteria, could influence effect estimates. Notably, in the Taiwanese study gout diagnosis was based on ICD codes, and even accounting for medications still yielded a smaller effect than in Hong Kong, where detailed lab data and competing‐risk models were used [11, 12]. Nonetheless, all studies uniformly show SGLT2i users have fewer gout events than appropriate comparators, suggesting a protective effect across diverse settings.

To support our epidemiological evidence, we performed a TSMR analysis using genetic variants proxying SGLT2 inhibition. In our MR analysis using six independent SNPs as instruments for SGLT2 inhibition, genetically predicted SGLT2 blockade was associated with a lower risk of gout. The IVW estimate indicated an inverse association (OR 0.10, p value = 1.32 × 10−4), and complementary methods (weighted median, MR‐PRESSO, GSMR, MR‐RAPS) yielded directionally consistent inverse associations (all p value < 0.01). Sensitivity analyses (single‐SNP MR, leave‐one‐out) showed that no individual variant drove the result, arguing against horizontal pleiotropy and supporting robustness of our findings. However, given the small number of instruments and the use of HbA1c as a proxy, these findings should be interpreted with caution and do not constitute quantitative confirmation of the observational estimate. These findings are congruent with prior genetic evidence on urate transport. For example, common variants in the urate transporter gene SLC2A9 are a major determinant of serum urate and gout risk [32]. In meta‐analyses of gout case–control data, the minor allele of SLC2A9 (e.g., rs1014290) is associated with a 58% reduction in gout odds (OR 0.42), and analogous alleles in ABCG2 (e.g., rs2231137) similarly confer about half the risk (OR 0.43) [33]. In other words, genetic predictors of lower serum urate might translate into lower gout risk. To our knowledge, our study is the first MR analysis to evaluate genetically proxied SGLT2 inhibition in relation to gout risk. Prior MR work has focused on classical urate loci rather than SGLT2 per se. Taken together, the results are directionally consistent with epidemiologic findings and suggest the biological plausibility that long‐term SGLT2 blockade is associated with a lower risk of gout.

This study has several strengths. First, the magnitude of risk reduction from the prior international studies has varied across populations. By evaluating this association in a nationwide Korean cohort, our study provides population‐specific evidence from a different national setting and further supports the generalizability of prior findings. Second, the use of a nationally representative claims database enables systematic longitudinal follow‐up of health‐related information, which enhances the robustness of our findings. Third, we defined sustained treatment users based on PDC and evaluated it at multiple landmark time points, additionally incorporating time‐varying exposure and accounting for treatment deviation during follow‐up using IPCW. These approaches were introduced to address limitations commonly acknowledged in previous studies, including challenges in defining sustained treatment adherence, limited handling of treatment switching, and relatively short follow‐up duration. Accordingly, this framework enabled a more detailed assessment of whether the observed association remained consistent under more comprehensive representations of real‐world treatment trajectories. Fourth, we combined a drug‐target MR approach leveraging independent external genetic datasets. By incorporating this complementary framework with the cohort analysis, our study provides additional methodological support for the observed association. This integration enables interpretation of the findings within a biological context.

Nonetheless, our study has limitations. First, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Unmeasured factors such as diet, over‐the‐counter diuretic use, or physician prescribing bias could influence gout risk. Second, the magnitude of the association was attenuated when time‐varying exposure and treatment switching were considered. This indicates that the estimated effect depends on the definition of treatment exposure. The interpretation of effect size should be made with caution, particularly given differences in treatment persistence between groups. These differences may reflect patient characteristics and clinical decision‐making associated with non‐random treatment trajectories, such as drug intolerance or reimbursement‐related factors. This may lead to differences in underlying risk between groups. However, these factors may not be fully captured by measured variables. Accordingly, selection bias may still remain despite weighting. Third, serum urate levels were not available in the claims data, so we could not directly measure how much lowered urate or adjust for baseline hyperuricemia. Fourth, the possibility of outcome misclassification cannot be fully excluded. Since the definition of gout relied on recorded diagnostic codes, under‐capture of milder cases and variation in coding practices may have introduced uncertainty in outcome ascertainment. Such misclassification is likely to be largely non‐differential and would be expected to bias effect estimates toward the null. Furthermore, if SGLT2i users had systematically fewer healthcare contacts, gout may have been underdiagnosed in this group, potentially introducing differential ascertainment bias that could overestimate the protective association. To partially address this concern, we further adjusted for baseline healthcare utilisation, and the primary results remained consistent. Fifth, the cohort included only Korean adults with T2DM. Thus, generalizability to other ethnic groups or to patients without diabetes is uncertain. Interestingly, some reports suggest SGLT2i may lower urate even more in non‐diabetic individuals [5, 34]. Sixth, we focused on DPP4i as comparator; different results might emerge if compared to other agents. Lastly, the MR findings may not directly correspond to the pharmacologic inhibition achieved by SGLT2i and do not fully address residual confounding in the observational study. Genetically proxied SGLT2 inhibition reflects lifelong genetic variation influencing levels of HbA1c. In addition, differences in outcome definition, exposure scale, and study population may also have contributed to the larger effect estimate observed in the MR analysis. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as directly comparable to the observational effect size. Rather, the MR results provide complementary triangulation evidence supporting the direction and biological plausibility of the association.

Our findings may carry clinical relevance, though their implications should be framed cautiously. Beyond glycemic control, SGLT2i may confer an additional pleiotropic benefit by lowering the risk of gout in patients with T2DM. However, our observational design cannot establish gout risk reduction as a determinant of treatment selection. Rather, a lower gout risk is best regarded as one of several potential pleiotropic considerations that may be weighed alongside the established cardiorenal and metabolic benefits of SGLT2i, particularly in patients who also have hyperuricemia or gout. Dedicated prospective trials would be required before such a consideration could directly inform therapeutic choice. Further studies could investigate SGLT2i as a strategy for gout prevention or treatment, even in non‐diabetic patients with hyperuricemia, and whether the degree of glycemic control modifies the urate‐lowering effect. Additionally, mechanistic studies are needed to fully elucidate the specific renal and metabolic pathways involved.

In conclusion, our integrated epidemiologic and genetic analysis suggested that SGLT2i was associated with a lower risk of incident gout compared to DPP4i in patients with T2DM. These findings support the possibility that SGLT2i may have pleiotropic effects beyond glucose control.
