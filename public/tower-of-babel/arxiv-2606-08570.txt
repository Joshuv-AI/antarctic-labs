Abstract
. We construct Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties in partial flag varieties of arbitrary type using the cluster structure on a unipotent cell. When the governing cluster algebra is of infinite type, we prove that for any very ample homogeneous line bundle over a simply laced partial flag variety, the resulting family of Newton–Okounkov polytopes contains infinitely many pairwise nonequivalent polytopes up to integral affine transformation. As an application to symplectic geometry, we construct infinitely many distinct monotone Lagrangian tori in a broad class of simply laced partial flag varieties.



1.
Introduction

Newton–Okounkov bodies, introduced by Lazarsfeld–Mustaţă
[
LM09
]
and Kaveh–Khovanskii
[
KK12
]
, provide a powerful framework for studying linear system on projective varieties via convex geometry. These convex bodies associated to a polarized projective variety
(X,\mathcal{L})
effectively encode its geometric information. For a suitable choice of valuation, this body becomes a rational polytope
\Delta
, which in turn yields a flat degeneration of
X
to the toric variety associated with
\Delta
in
[
And13
]
. It has been useful to study the geometry of
X
via its toric degeneration in a wide range of contexts.

Many foundational examples of Newton–Okounkov bodies of homogeneous spaces were initially studied via combinatorial models such as triangulations, ladder diagrams, plabic graphs, tropicalizations, and wiring diagrams, see
[
GL96
,
GP00b
,
Cal02
,
AB04
,
KM05
,
SS04
,
Kav15
,
RW19
,
CMZ24
]
for instance. While these constructions are explicit and combinatorially accessible, they typically produce only finitely many Newton–Okounkov polytopes of a single variety.

The theory of Newton–Okounkov bodies has been further developed using the machinery of cluster algebras, see
[
GHKK18
,
RW19
,
BFMN20
,
FH21
,
EH22
,
BCMN24
,
FO25
]
for instance. Cluster algebras provide a systematic method for constructing families of Newton–Okounkov bodies and recurrence relations describing how they are related. In many important cases, it is of particular interest to understand how previously known constructions can be unified with those arising from cluster algebras.

More interestingly, when the governing cluster algebra is of infinite type, this method yields infinitely many Newton–Okounkov bodies together with their associated geometric objects. Consequently, it provides a systematic and controlled way for constructing infinitely many geometric objects with nice properties on a single projective variety.

In this vein, a natural question would be whether these objects are genuinely distinct, up to a meaningful notion of equivalence. To answer this question, the authors in
[
CKKP25
]
developed a practical criterion for detecting infinitely many pairwise nonequivalent Newton–Okounkov bodies arising from a cluster algebra, up to integral affine transformation. Using this criterion, they showed that there are infinitely many distinct Newton–Okounkov bodies, toric degenerations, and monotone Lagrangian tori for a broad class of complete flag varieties.

The main purpose of this paper is to construct and distinguish families of Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties in partial flag varieties governed by a cluster algebra. More specifically, we investigate whether the resulting family contains infinitely many pairwise distinct combinatorial and geometric objects for Schubert varieties when the underlying cluster algebra is of infinite type.

Let
G
be a simply connected semisimple algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
with the index set

\mathfrak{g}

for the set of minimal length representatives of the cosets in
W/W_{K}
. For each

be the associated Schubert variety in

Following
[

,
Wil13
,
GLS11
,
GY17
,
FO25
]

. Fix a reduced expression of

,

\underline{w}=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{m}}.

For each
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, set

k^{+}\coloneqq\min\bigl(\{m+1\}\cup\{k+1\leq j\leq m\mid i_{j}=i_{k}\}\bigr)

and define index sets

J\coloneqq\{1,2,\cdots,m\},\,J_{\mathrm{fz}}\coloneqq\{j\in J\mid j^{+}=m+1\},\mbox{ and }J_{\mathrm{uf}}\coloneqq J\setminus J_{\mathrm{fr}}.

We introduce a collection of algebraically independent variables
\{A_{j}\mid j\in J\}
. Using the Cartan matrix
A
, we define an extended exchange matrix
\varepsilon_{0}\coloneqq(\varepsilon_{r,s})_{r\in J_{\mathrm{uf}},\,s\in J}
by

(1.1)

\varepsilon_{r,s}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }r=s^{+},\\
1&\text{if }r^{+}=s,\\

a_{i_{s},i_{r}}&\text{if }r<s<r^{+}<s^{+},\\

For each seed
\mathsf{s}

is regular. To each seed
\mathsf{s}
of

arising from a dominance order and a Newton–Okounkov body
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
of
(X_{w}^{K},\mathcal{L}^{K}_{\lambda})
by following
[

,
FO25
]
. The resulting family of convex bodies reflects the underlying cluster structure. Accordingly, it has a tropicalized cluster structure, which we now recall.

For an unfrozen index
k\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}
and a pair
(\mathsf{s},\mathsf{s}^{\prime})
of seeds with
\mu_{k}(\mathsf{s})=\mathsf{s}^{\prime}
, the

\mu^{{T}}_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{J}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{J}

is defined to be the piecewise-linear transformation given by

(1.2)

\mathbf{u}\coloneqq(u_{j})\mapsto\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\coloneqq(u^{\prime}_{j}),\quad u_{j}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}-u_{j}&\mbox{ if $j=k$,}\\
u_{j}+\left[\varepsilon_{k,j}\right]_{+}u_{k}&\mbox{ if $j\neq k$ and $u_{k}\geq 0$,}\\
u_{j}+\left[-\varepsilon_{k,j}\right]_{+}u_{k}&\mbox{ if $j\neq k$ and $u_{k}\leq 0$}\end{cases}

where

\Delta_{\mathsf{s}^{\prime}}=\mu_{k}^{T}(\Delta_{\mathsf{s}})
whenever

is obtained from

In the case where
K=\emptyset

X_{w}\coloneqq X_{w}^{\emptyset}
in the complete flag variety

Proposition A

(Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
G
be a simply connected semisimple algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
with the index set
I
for simple roots of its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. For a subset
K\subseteq I
, choose
w\in W^{K}
and a dominant integral weight
\lambda
with respect to
K
. Consider the Schubert variety
X_{w}^{K}\subseteq G/P
together with the homogeneous line bundle
\mathcal{L}^{K}_{\lambda}
. Then there exists a family

\bigl\{\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}\mid\mbox{ $\mathsf{s}$ is a seed of the cluster algebra $\mathcal{A}(w)$}\bigr\}

of Newton–Okounkov bodies of
\bigl(X_{w}^{K},\mathcal{L}^{K}_{\lambda}\bigr)
indexed by the set of seeds such that

(1)

each underlying value semigroup is saturated and finitely generated,

(2)

each
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
is a rational polytope, and

(3)

the family
\{\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}\}
has a tropicalized cluster structure.

Combining this construction with
[

,

]
, we obtain families of toric degenerations and completely integrable systems on Schubert varieties.

Corollary B

(1)

If
\lambda
is a regular, then there exists a family of toric degenerations
\{\mathcal{X}^{\mathsf{s}}\}
of Schubert variety

indexed by the set of seeds of
\mathcal{A}(w)
such that the central fiber

is the normal toric variety associated with
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
.

(2)

If

\Phi_{\mathsf{s}}

.

Remark 1.1
.

In
[
BCMN24
]
, Bossinger et al. constructed a family of Newton–Okounkov bodies of
G/P
using the cluster algebra parametrizing the
\theta
-basis in
[
GHKK18
]
. In contrast, the construction of Proposition
A
relies on the cluster algebra parametrizing the dual canonical basis
[
Lus90
,
Lus91
,
Kas90
,
Kas91
,
Kas93
]
.

In our setting, we derive and exploit certain non-negativity properties of the dual canonical basis to distinguish Newton–Okounkov polytopes. For this purpose, it is essential to work with Newton–Okounkov bodies whose integral points correspond to elements of the dual canonical basis.

If a cluster algebra is of infinite type, then it admits infinitely many distinct seeds and hence gives rise to infinitely many associated Newton–Okounkov bodies, toric degenerations, and completely integrable systems of
(X^{K}_{w},\mathcal{L}^{K}_{\lambda})
. We consider the equivalence relation on the set of Newton–Okounkov polytopes given by integral affine transformations (a.k.a unimodular equivalences). Recall that if two polytope are related in this way, then the associated toric varieties are isomorphic. A natural question is whether these objects are pairwise distinct up to integral affine transformation.

In
[
CKKP25
]
, the authors introduced a criterion to distinguish a family of Newton–Okounkov polytopes equipped with a tropicalized cluster structure under the additional assumption that each polytope is
\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein. One advantage of this criterion is that it can be applied with minimal reliance on an explicit polyhedral description. Recall that extended
g
-vectors or valuations, provide points of these polytopes. Determining an explicit polyhedral description of Newton–Okounkov bodies has been an important and challenging problem, see
[
BZ01
,
RW19
]
for instance. In this paper, we refine this criterion so that it applies to full dimensional Newton–Okounkov polytopes having a tropicalized cluster structure, see Theorem
4.14
.

To apply the criterion, we assume that
G
is of simply laced type. Let
w_{K}\in W^{K}
be a (unique) minimal length representative of the left coset
w_{0}W_{K}
where
w_{0}
is the longest element of
W
. We then have
X^{K}_{w_{K}}=G/P
.

Theorem C

(Theorem
4.11
)
.

Assume that
G
is a simply connected semisimple algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
of simply laced type. For a subset
K\subseteq I
, let
w_{K}
be a minimal length representative of
w_{0}W_{K}
in
W
and
\lambda
a dominant integral weight with respect to
K
. Let
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
be the Newton–Okounkov polytope of
(G/P,\mathcal{L}^{K}_{\lambda})
associated to a seed
\mathsf{s}
of the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(w_{K})
in Proposition
A
. If
\mathcal{A}(w_{K})
is of infinite type, then the family

\bigl\{\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}\mid\mbox{ $\mathsf{s}$ is a seed of the cluster algebra $\mathcal{A}(w_{K})$}\bigr\}

contains infinitely many Newton–Okounkov polytopes, no two of which are related by any integral affine transformation.

.

We expect that the result analogous to Theorem

When the line bundle

Theorem D

(Theorem

)
.

Assume that a simply connected semisimple algebraic group

over

is simply laced type and

is the anticanonical line bundle on

\mathsf{s}

Remark 1.3
.

In the case where
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})/P
is a Grassmannian in
[
RW19
]
, plabic graphs provide combinatorial models for constructing Newton–Okounkov bodies and describing them via the mirror superpotentials. This framework can be used to produce monotone Lagrangian tori, see
[
NU14
,
NU20
,
Cas23
]
. However, for a fixed
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})/P
, there are only finitely many plabic graphs and hence this approach produces only finitely many such tori. In contrast, Proposition
A
produces monotone Lagrangian torus for every seed and therefore yields infinitely many tori when the associated cluster algebra is of infinite type.

In the setting of Proposition
A
, the valuations arise from
A
-cluster mutations, while the mirror superpotentials are related via
X
-cluster mutations. This contrasts with the situation in
[
RW19
]
where the valuations are given by
X
-cluster mutations and the mirror superpotentials transform via
A
-cluster mutations. We refer to
[
BCMN24
, Section 7]
for a discussion of the relationship between them.

To prove Theorems
C
and
D
, we apply the criterion established in Theorem
4.14
. Roughly speaking, this requires verifying two conditions: non-negativity and multiplicity.

The non-negativity condition asserts that there exists a frozen index
j\in J_{\mathrm{fz}}
such that every Newton–Okounkov polytope
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
is contained in the half space given by
u_{j}\geq 0
. Since the lattice points of each
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
parametrize elements of the dual canonical basis, this condition admits the following representation-theoretic interpretation
;
the
j
-th component of the extended
g
-vector of every dual canonical basis elements is non-negative.

To prove this statement, there are two inputs.
The cluster algebra structure on the unipotent group in
[
GLS11
,
KO21
]
and the monoidal categorification of the quantum cluster algebra structure of a unipotent quantum coordinate ring and its localizations in
[
KKKO18
,
KKOP21
,
KKOP23
]
. Indeed, the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})
in (
3.3
) is isomorphic to the coordinate ring of the unipotent cell. According to
[
GLS11
]
, the algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})
is obtained by localizing the coordinate ring of the unipotent group and the corresponding cluster structures are compatible via the inversion of frozen variables.

A subtle point arises from this localization process. Additional factors appear in the denominator, and one must ensure that these do not affect the relevant components of dual canonical basis elements. To address this, we pass it to a categorical framework. Using structural properties of simple modules and determinantial modules in Kashiwara–Nakashima
[
KN25
]
, we deduce the non-negativity of the extended
g
-vectors. Conceptually, this approach replaces intricate ring-theoretic computations with more tractable module-theoretic arguments.

In more concrete terms, the result can be stated at the level of Newton–Okounkov bodies, which may be of independent interest.

Proposition E

(Corollary
5.3
)
.

For
w\in W
, set

(1.3)

I(w)\coloneqq\{i\in{\rm supp}(w)\,|\,ws_{i}>w\}.

For each
k\in{\rm supp}(w)\setminus I(w)
, let
j_{k}\in J_{\mathrm{fz}}
be the index corresponding to the frozen variable corresponding to
k
. Then for each seed
\mathsf{s}
, the Newton–Okounkov polytope
\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}
in Theorem

satisfies

(1.4)

\Delta_{\mathsf{s}}\subseteq\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\mid u_{j_{k}}\geq 0\}.

Remark 1.4
.

The mutation rule in (
1.2
) is obtained by tropicalizing the
X
-cluster mutations. Under this transformation, frozen variables are affected indirectly through mutations at unfrozen variables, and hence their values may change. Nonetheless, Theorem
E
claims that the non-negativity is preserved under any iterated mutations. At present, the authors do not know how to prove Theorem
E
by purely combinatorial arguments.

Remark 1.5
.

For the longest element
w_{0}\in W
, (
1.4
) holds for every frozen index. In contrast, for a general element
w
, not every frozen component has the non-negativity property, see Remark
5.4
.

Finally, we address the multiplicity condition. It is worth mentioning that distinct seeds may still yield equivalent Newton–Okounkov polytopes, see Remark
4.13
. Thus, even when the governing cluster algebra is of infinite type, additional input is necessary to distinguish the constructed polytopes. The key input is the existence of a sequence of seeds whose exchange quivers have arrows with arbitrarily large multiplicity emanating from a frozen variable corresponding to an index not in (
1.3
).

Motivated by
[
GLS08
]
, we introduce extended exchange matrices equipped with makings reflecting (
1.3
) together with a partial order on them. We then analyze their relationship with the weak Bruhat order and compare exchange matrices across different Weyl groups. We reduce the number of cases that we examine. We use the classification of cluster algebras of finite mutation type in
[
FST12
]
to observe that
\mathcal{A}(w_{K})
is of infinite type if and only if it is of infinite mutation type. By identifying suitable subquivers of finite mutation type and determining the threshold at which the addition of an appropriate marked point yields infinite mutation type, we construct a sequence of seeds in which the multiplicities of arrows from the designated frozen variables become arbitrarily large.

2.
Partial flag varieties and Schubert varieties

In this section, we fix notation from Lie theory and review basic definitions and properties of partial flag varieties and their Schubert varieties.

Let
G
be a simply connected semisimple algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Denote by
\mathsf{A}=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
the Cartan matrix of
\mathfrak{g}
where
I=\{1,2,\cdots,n=\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{g})\}
is the index set of simple roots. Let
B
be a Borel subgroup of
G
,
H
a maximal torus in
B
,
B^{-}
the opposite Borel subgroup, and
U
(resp.
U^{-}
) the unipotent radical of
B
(resp.
B^{-}
).

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be the Lie algebra of
H
, and set
\mathfrak{h}^{*}\coloneqq\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathfrak{h},\mathbb{C})
for its dual. We denote by
\langle-,-\rangle
the natural pairing between
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, and often write
\lambda(x)\coloneqq\langle x,\lambda\rangle
for
x\in\mathfrak{h}
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. Let
\Phi=\Phi(G,H)
be the root system and let
\Phi^{+}=\Phi^{+}(B,H)
denote the set of positive roots. The Weyl group
W
of
\mathfrak{g}
is given by
N(H)/H
where
N(H)
is the normalizer of
H
in
G
. For each root
\alpha\in\Phi
, we denote by
U_{\alpha}
the corresponding root subgroup of
(G,H)
.

For each
i\in I
, let
\alpha_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
be the
i
-th simple root,
s_{i}\in W
the corresponding simple reflection, and
h_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}
the
i
-th simple coroot, defined by
\langle h_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle=a_{i,j}
for all
i,j\in I
. The
i
-th fundamental weight
\varpi_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is defined by
\langle h_{j},\varpi_{i}\rangle=\delta_{j,i}
for all
j\in I
. The
weight lattice

\mathsf{P}
is

\mathsf{P}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathbb{Z}\varpi_{i}

and the set of
dominant integral weights
is

\mathsf{P}^{+}\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathsf{P}\mid\lambda(h_{i})\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in I\}.

Every Weyl group element
w\in W
can be expressed as a product of simple reflections. The length function
\ell\colon W\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is defined by the number of simple reflections of a reduced expression of
w
. For a subset
K\subseteq I
, the subgroup

W_{K}\coloneqq\langle s_{k}\mid k\in K\rangle

is called a
parabolic subgroup
of
W
associated with
K
. Define

(2.1)

W^{K}\coloneqq\{w\in W\mid\ell(wv)\geq\ell(w)\mbox{ for all $v\in W_{K}$}\}\simeq W/W_{K},

so that
W^{K}
is the set of minimal length representatives of cosets in the factor group
W/W_{K}
. We also set

\Phi_{K}\coloneqq\Phi\cap\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{k}.

The
parabolic subgroup

P^{K}
associated with
K
is defined by

P^{K}\coloneqq\langle H,U_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\cup\Phi_{K}\rangle.

The quotient
G/P^{K}
is a smooth projective variety, called the
partial flag variety
. If
K=\emptyset
, then
P^{K}
is the Borel subgroup
B
and hence
G/P^{K}=G/B
is the complete flag variety. There is the canonical projection given by

(2.2)

\pi_{K}\colon G/B\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}G/P^{K},\quad gB\mapsto gP^{K}.

Now fix a subset
K\subseteq I
. For
w\in W^{K}
, we choose a representative of
w
in
N(H)
, and by abuse of notation, we denote this representative again by
w
when the choice does not affect the discussion. The
Schubert cell
is defined by

C^{K}_{w}\coloneqq B{w}P^{K}/P^{K}

and the
Schubert variety

X^{K}_{w}
is its Zariski closure in
G/P^{K}
, which is a subvariety of the partial flag variety
G/P^{K}
. The Schubert variety admits the following Schubert cell decomposition

(2.3)

X^{K}_{w}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W^{K};\,v\leq w\end{subarray}}BvP^{K}/P^{K}

where
v\leq w
denotes the (strong) Bruhat order. For an arbitrary
w\in W
, there exists a unique decomposition

w=w^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}\quad\mbox{with $w^{\prime}\in W^{K}$ and $w^{\prime\prime}\in W_{K}$}

and we set
X^{K}_{w}\coloneqq X^{K}_{w^{\prime}}
. In particular, if
K=\emptyset
, then
W^{K}=W
, and we simply write
X_{w}
instead of
X_{w}^{\emptyset}
.

3.
Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties of
G/B

The goal of this section is to recall the construction of Newton–Okounkov bodies and the cluster algebra structure on unipotent cells. We then review the construction of Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties of the complete flag variety
G/B
using this cluster structure and summarize their key properties in Fujita–Oya
[
FO25
]
.

3.1.
Newton–Okounkov bodies

Let
X
be an irreducible normal projective variety over
\mathbb{C}
of complex dimension
m
together with a globally generated line bundle
\mathcal{L}
over
X
. Fix a total order
\geq
on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
compatible with the addition. To construct a Newton–Okounkov body, we choose a valuation
v
and a reference section
h
of
\mathcal{L}
.

A
valuation

v

with one-dimensional leaves
is a function
v\colon\mathbb{C}(X)\backslash\{0\}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}^{m}
on the function field such that for all rational functions
f,g\in\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

(1)

v(fg)=v(f)+v(g)
,

(2)

v(f+g)\geq\min(v(f),v(g))
,

(3)

v(cf)=v(f)
, and

(4)

for all
f,g\in\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{0\}
with
v(f)=v(g)
, there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that

v(g-cf)>v(g)\mbox{ or }g-cf=0.

Consider the section ring of
X
given by

R=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}R_{k}\,\,\mbox{ where $R_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}$ and $R_{k}\coloneqq H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k})$}.

Choose a reference section
h\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L})
with
h\neq 0
and define the value semigroup

(3.1)

S(X,\mathcal{L},v,h)=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\bigl\{(k,v(f/h^{k}))\mid f\in R_{k}\backslash\{0\}\bigr\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The
Newton–Okounkov body
associated with the quadruple
(X,\mathcal{L},v,h)
is defined by

(3.2)

\Delta(X,\mathcal{L},v,h)\coloneqq\overline{\operatorname{conv}\left(\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\{x/k\mid(k,x)\in S(X,\mathcal{L},v,h)\}\right)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}.

We say that a convex body
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
Newton–Okounkov body of

(X,\mathcal{L})
if there exist a valuation
v
and a nonzero section
h\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L})
such that
\Delta=\Delta(X,\mathcal{L},v,h)
.

3.2.
Cluster algebra structures on unipotent cells

In this subsection, we recall the cluster algebra structures on unipotent cells, which will be used to construct Newton–Okounkov bodies of Schubert varieties in partial flag varieties. For
w\in W
, the
unipotent cell
is defined by

U_{w}^{-}=U^{-}\cap BwB.

It can be regarded as an open subset of
X_{w}
via an open embedding
U^{-}_{w}\hookrightarrow X_{w}
induced by the natural inclusion
U^{-}\hookrightarrow G/B
.

The cluster algebra structure on the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}_{w}]
was constructed in
[
BFZ05
,
Wil13
,
GLS11
,
GY17
,
FO25
]
. Fix a reduced expression of
w\in W

\underline{w}=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{m}}.

The
support
of
w
is defined as
\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\}\subseteq I
. This set is independent of the choice of reduced expression. By replacing
G
with the subgroup corresponding to
\operatorname{supp}(w)
if necessary, we may assume that
\operatorname{supp}(w)=I
.

For
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{+}
and
u,v\in W
, let
\Delta_{u\lambda,v\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}[G]
denote the
generalized minor
associated with
u,v
, and
\lambda
. Its restriction to
U_{w}^{-}
defines a regular function

D_{u\lambda,v\lambda}\coloneqq\Delta_{u\lambda,v\lambda}|_{U^{-}_{w}}

In type
A
, the functions
\Delta_{u\varpi_{j},v\varpi_{j}}(g)
coincide with the usual matrix minors.

For
k=1,\dots,m
, set

w_{\leq k}\coloneqq s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k}}\,\mbox{ and }\,k^{+}\coloneqq\min\bigl(\{m+1\}\cup\{k+1\leq j\leq m\mid i_{j}=i_{k}\}\bigr).

Define index sets

J\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,m\},\,J_{\mathrm{fz}}\coloneqq\{j\in J\mid j^{+}=m+1\},\mbox{ and }J_{\mathrm{uf}}\coloneqq J\setminus J_{\mathrm{fz}}.

The
unipotent minors
are defined by

D_{j}\coloneqq D_{w_{\leq j}\varpi_{i_{j}},\varpi_{i_{j}}}\qquad\text{ for $1\leq j\leq m$}.

Next, define the extended exchange matrix
\varepsilon_{t_{0}}\coloneqq(\varepsilon_{r,s})_{r\in J_{\mathrm{uf}},\,s\in J}
by

(3.3)

\varepsilon_{r,s}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }r=s^{+},\\
1&\text{if }r^{+}=s,\\
-a_{i_{s},i_{r}}&\text{if }s<r<s^{+}<r^{+},\\
a_{i_{s},i_{r}}&\text{if }r<s<r^{+}<s^{+},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in I}
is the Cartan matrix
A
of
G
. The principal submatrix
(\varepsilon_{r,s})_{r,s\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}}
is skew-symmetrizable, and it is skew-symmetric if and only if
A
is symmetric.

Consider the field of rational functions

(3.4)

\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{C}\left(A_{j,t_{0}}\mid j=1,\dots,m\right).

A
seed

\mathsf{s}=(\mathcal{A},\varepsilon)
consists of an algebraically independent set
\mathcal{A}
generating
\mathcal{F}
and an exchange matrix
\varepsilon
. We take the initial seed as

(3.5)

\mathsf{s}_{0}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{s}_{0}}\coloneqq\{A_{j,t_{0}}\mid j\in J\},\,\varepsilon_{t_{0}}=(\varepsilon_{r,s})_{r\in J_{\mathrm{uf}},\,s\in J}\right)

All other seeds are obtained from
\mathsf{s}_{0}
by iterated mutations. For
k\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}
, the mutation
\mathsf{s}^{\prime}=\mu_{k}(\mathsf{s})
is defined as follows. Set
[a]_{+}\coloneqq\max\{a,0\}
. The cluster variables mutate by the rule

(3.6)

\mu_{k}\colon\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{s}}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{s}^{\prime}},\quad\mu_{k}^{*}(A^{\prime}_{j})=\begin{cases}A_{k}^{-1}\left(\prod_{i\in J}A_{i}^{[\varepsilon_{k,i}]_{+}}+\prod_{i\in J}A_{i}^{[-\varepsilon_{k,i}]_{+}}\right)&\text{if }j=k,\\
A_{j}&\text{if }j\neq k,\end{cases}

and the exchange matrix mutates by the rule

(3.7)

\varepsilon^{\prime}=(\varepsilon_{r,s}^{\prime})_{r,s\in J},\qquad\varepsilon_{r,s}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}-\varepsilon_{r,s}&\text{if }r=k\text{ or }s=k,\\
\varepsilon_{r,s}+\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{k,s})[\varepsilon_{r,k}\varepsilon_{k,s}]_{+}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the exchange graph with initial vertex
t_{0}
corresponding to
\mathsf{s}_{0}
. For each
t\in\mathbb{T}
, let
(A_{j,t})_{j\in J}
denote the corresponding cluster variables defined by the rule (
3.6
). We define the cluster algebras associated with
\mathsf{s}_{0}
as follows
\colon

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be given as in (
3.4
).

•

The (
ordinary
)
cluster algebra
is the subalgebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})
of
\mathcal{F}
generated by

\left\{A_{j,t}\mid t\in\mathbb{T},j\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}\right\}\cup\left\{A_{j,t}^{\pm}\mid t\in\mathbb{T},j\in J_{\mathrm{fz}}\right\}.

•

The
upper cluster algebra
is the subalgebra of
\mathcal{F}
given by

\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{s}_{0})=\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{C}\bigl[A_{j,t}^{\pm}\mid j\in J\bigr].

Remark 3.2
.

Different choices of reduced expression of
w
yield isomorphic cluster algebras. Accordingly, we write
\mathcal{A}(w)
in place of
\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})
when we want to emphasize the dependence on
w
.

Theorem 3.3

(Appendix B in
[
FO25
]
)
.

The ordinary cluster algebra agrees with the upper cluster algebra, that is,

\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})=\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{s}_{0})

Moreover, there is a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra isomorphism

\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{s}_{0})=\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{s}_{0})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}[U_{w}^{-}],\quad A_{j,t_{0}}\mapsto D_{j}.

In this sense,
\mathbb{C}[U_{w}^{-}]
is endowed with the cluster algebra structure.

3.3.
Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties of
G/B

By utilizing the above cluster algebra structure, we associate to each seed
\mathsf{s}
a valuation
v_{\mathsf{s}}
on the function field
\mathbb{C}(U^{-}_{w})
, which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}(X_{w})
. Let
\mathsf{s}=\left((A_{j,t})_{j\in J},\varepsilon_{t}\right)
be the seed of
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}_{w}]
associated with a vertex
t\in\mathbb{T}
. For
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{J}
, we define

\mathbf{a}\preceq_{\varepsilon_{t}}\mathbf{b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}+\textbf{v}\varepsilon_{t}\text{ for some $\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{J_{\mathrm{uf}}}$.}

This order
\preceq_{\varepsilon_{t}}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{J}
is called the
dominance order
associated with
\varepsilon_{t}
in
[
Qin17
]
.

Consider the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}[A_{j,t}^{\pm 1}\mid j\in J]
. Let
<_{t}
be a total order refining the opposite dominance order
\preceq^{\mathrm{op}}_{\varepsilon_{t}}
associated with the exchange matrix
\varepsilon_{t}
corresponding to
t
. By identifying a Laurent monomial
\prod_{j\in J}A_{j,t}^{a_{j}}
with its exponent vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{Z}^{J}
, the order
<_{t}
induces an order on the set of Laurent monomials in
\mathcal{F}
. We denote by
v_{\mathsf{s}}
the lowest term valuation on
\mathcal{F}
corresponding to
<_{t}
. Namely, for
f/g\in\mathcal{F}
with
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[A_{j,t}\mid j\in J]
, we define

v_{\mathsf{s}}(f/g)\coloneqq(a_{j})_{j\in J}-(b_{j})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{Z}^{J}

where

f=\prod_{j=1}^{m}cA_{j,t}^{a_{j}}+(\mbox{terms of higher order}),\,\,g=\prod_{j=1}^{m}c^{\prime}A_{j,t}^{b_{j}}+(\mbox{terms of higher order})

for some
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
We write
v_{t}
for
v_{\mathsf{s}}
if no confusion arises.

For a certain class of elements in
\mathcal{F}
, the valuation can be calculated by the extended
g
-vector, which we now recall. Following
[
FZ07
,
FG09
]
, we set

X_{i,t}\coloneqq\prod_{j\in J}A_{j,t}^{\varepsilon_{i,j}}.

An element
f\in\mathcal{F}
is said to be
weakly pointed
at
(g_{j})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{Z}^{J}
if
f
can be expressed as

(3.8)

f=\left(\prod_{j\in J}A^{g_{j}}_{j,t}\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}^{J_{\mathrm{uf}}}_{\geq 0}}c_{\mathbf{a}}\prod_{j\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}}X_{j,t}^{a_{j}}\right)

for some coefficients
c_{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{C}
with
c_{\mathbf{0}}\neq 0
. In this case,

g_{\mathsf{s}}(f)\coloneqq(g_{j})_{j\in J}

is called the
extended g-vector
of
f
. We sometimes write
g_{t}
in place of
g_{\mathsf{s}}
. By
[
FO25
, Proposition 3.9]
, for every weakly pointed element
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

(3.9)

v_{\mathsf{s}}(f)=g_{\mathsf{s}}(f).

For a dominant integral weight
\lambda\in\mathsf{P}^{+}
, we define a line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\coloneqq(G\times\mathbb{C})/B
over the flag manifold
G/B
, where
B
acts on
G\times\mathbb{C}
from the right as
(g,c)\cdot b\coloneqq(gb,\chi_{\lambda}(b)c)
for
g\in G,c\in\mathbb{C}
, and
b\in B
. Restricting to
X_{w}
, we obtain the line bundle on
X_{w}
which is also denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
. If
\lambda
is regular in addition, that is,
\langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle>0
for every

, then the line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
is very ample. We fix a lowest weight vector
\tau_{\lambda}
in
H^{0}(G/B,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda})
and restrict it to
X_{w}
. Following Section
3.1
and using the valuation
v_{\mathsf{s}}
on the function field

, we produce the semigroup
S(X_{w},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},v_{\mathsf{s}},\tau_{\lambda})
defined in (
3.1
) and the Newton–Okounkov body
\Delta(X_{w},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},v_{\mathsf{s}},\tau_{\lambda})
defined by (

). In sum, we have the quadruple

(3.10)

(X_{w},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},v_{\mathsf{s}},\tau_{\lambda})

By following the procedure in Section
3

(3.11)

\left\{\left(S(X_{w},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},v_{\mathsf{s}},\tau_{\lambda}),\Delta(X_{w},\mathcal{L}_{\lambda},v_{\mathsf{s}},\tau_{\lambda})\right)\mid\mbox{$\mathsf{s}$ is a seed}\right\}

be the smallest closed convex cone containing

.

Consider the family

For an unfrozen index

(2)

We also define

4.
Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties in
G/P

The aim of this section is to construct Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties in partial flag varieties
G/P
using the cluster algebra structure on unipotent cells, and to investigate their applications. We also state the main result, the existence of infinitely many pairwise non-equivalent Newton–Okounkov bodies of
G/P
constructed from cluster algebras of infinite type.

4.1.
Construction of Newton–Okounkov polytopes of Schubert varieties in
G/P

We begin with a general setup. Let
X
and
Y
be irreducible, normal, projective varieties of dimension
m
over
\mathbb{C}
, and suppose that

\varphi\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Y

is a birational morphism. Assume that
X
is equipped with a valuation

v_{X}\colon\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{0\}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}^{m}

with one-dimensional leaves, with respect to a fixed total order on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Since the induced pullback on function fields
\varphi^{*}\colon\mathbb{C}(Y)\mathchoice{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}}^{\sim}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}}^{\sim}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}}^{\sim}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}}^{\sim}}}\mathbb{C}(X)
is an isomorphism, we obtain a valuation with one-dimensional leaves on
Y
by

(4.1)

v_{Y}\coloneqq v_{X}\circ\varphi^{*}\colon\mathbb{C}(Y)\setminus\{0\}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}(X)\setminus\{0\}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}^{m}.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{Y}
be a basepoint-free line bundle on
Y
and set

\mathcal{L}_{X}\coloneqq\varphi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{Y},

which is also basepoint-free. Fix a reference section
\tau_{Y}\in H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y})
, and define

\tau_{X}\coloneqq\varphi^{*}\tau_{Y}=\tau_{Y}\circ\varphi\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}_{X})

as the reference section for
\mathcal{L}_{X}
.

In this way, we obtain two quadruples

(X,\mathcal{L}_{X},v_{X},\tau_{X})\mbox{ and }(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y},v_{Y},\tau_{Y}),

which are input data for defining Newton–Okounkov bodies. The following proposition compares the Newton–Okounkov bodies associated with these two quadruples.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be irreducible, normal, projective varieties of dimension
m
over
\mathbb{C}
, and let
\varphi\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Y
be a birational morphism. Fix a total order on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and a valuation
v_{X}\colon\mathbb{C}(X)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with one-dimensional leaves. Let
\mathcal{L}_{Y}
be a basepoint-free line bundle on
Y
with a reference section
\tau_{Y}\in H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y})
and define

\mathcal{L}_{X}\coloneqq\varphi^{*}\mathcal{L}_{Y},\tau_{X}\coloneqq\varphi^{*}\tau_{Y},\mbox{ and }v_{Y}\coloneqq v_{X}\circ\varphi

Then the corresponding Newton–Okounkov bodies coincide, i.e.,

(4.2)

\Delta(X,\mathcal{L}_{X},v_{X},\tau_{X})=\Delta(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y},v_{Y},\tau_{Y}).

Proof.

We claim that if
\psi\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Y
is a projective birational morphism and
Y
is normal, then for any bundle
\mathcal{L}
of
Y
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have an isomorphism

\psi^{*}\colon H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}H^{0}(Y,\psi^{*}\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}),\quad f\mapsto f\circ\psi.

Recall that if
\psi:X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Y
is a projective birational morphism and
Y
is normal, one has that

\psi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y}

by Zariski’s main theorem
[
Hart77
, Corollary III.11.4]
. Hence, by the projection formula,

\psi_{*}\psi^{*}{\mathcal{L}}\cong{\mathcal{L}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{Y}}\psi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\cong{\mathcal{L}}.

Moreover, taking global sections of
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}
, we obtain
a desired isomorphism

H^{0}(X,\psi^{*}{\mathcal{L}}^{\otimes m})\cong H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{L}}^{\otimes m}).

Therefore, we have a graded ring isomorphism between section rings

R(Y,{\mathcal{L}})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{L}}^{\otimes m})\cong\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}H^{0}(X,\psi^{*}{\mathcal{L}}^{\otimes m})\eqqcolon R(X,\psi^{*}{\mathcal{L}}).

Now, we return to our situation. Since
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow Y
is a projective birational morphism and
Y
is normal, we have a graded ring isomorphism

\varphi^{*}\colon R(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y})\rightarrow R(X,\mathcal{L}_{X}),\quad\varphi^{*}(f):=f\circ\varphi.

For each
f\in H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y}^{\otimes k})
, by definition of
v_{Y}
, we have

(4.3)

v_{Y}\!\left(\frac{f}{\tau_{Y}^{k}}\right)=(v_{X}\circ\varphi^{*})\!\left(\frac{f}{\tau_{Y}^{k}}\right)=v_{X}\!\left(\frac{f}{\tau_{Y}^{k}}\circ\varphi\right)=v_{X}\!\left(\frac{\varphi^{*}f}{\tau_{X}^{k}}\right).

Hence, it yields that

S(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y},v_{Y},\tau_{Y})\subseteq S(X,\mathcal{L}_{X},v_{X},\tau_{X}).

Conversely, since
\varphi^{*}
is an isomorphism, each
g\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}_{X}^{\otimes k})
can be written as
g=\varphi^{*}f
for some
f\in H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}_{Y}^{\otimes k})
. Applying (
4.3
) yields the opposite inclusion, and hence the two semigroups coincide, and (
4.2
) follows.
∎

•

Let

For

For each

.

To establish non-negativity, we employ two key ingredients. The first is the cluster algebra structure on the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}(w)]
of the unipotent group
U^{-}(w)
. Since the frozen variables are not inverted, their corresponding components remain nonnegative, and any element coming from
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}(w)]
inherits this property. Each element in
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}\cap X_{w}]
lies in a localization
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}(w)]_{D_{w}}

a priori. We need to show that the frozen variables indexed by
{\rm supp}(w)\setminus I(w)
do not appear in the denominators of elements of the dual canonical basis.

Rather than performing ring-theoretic calculation, we pass the problem to the setting of the monoidal categorification. We realize the ring homomorphism
\mathbb{C}[U^{-}\cap X_{w}]\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}[U^{-}(w)]_{D_{w}}
via the localized functor between their categorifications (
5.12
) and (

). Moreover, using structural properties of the simple modules corresponding to dual canonical basis elements in this monoidal categorification (Proposition
5.7
), we show that the frozen variables indexed by
{\rm supp}(w)\setminus I(w)
do not appear in the denominator.

Remark 5.5
.

We expect that the statement remains valid in the symmetrizable case. The main obstacle to such a generalization is that the required monoidal categorification is available only in the symmetric case.

6.1.
Marked exchange matrices and partial order

We begin by recalling various notions of finiteness in the context of cluster algebras.

Definition 6.1
.

A cluster algebra is said to be of
finite type
if it has only finitely many distinct seeds under iterated mutations. Otherwise, it is said to be of
infinite type
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the field of rational functions over
\mathbb{C}
in
m

m\coloneqq|J|
). An initial seed
\mathsf{s}_{0}=(A_{\mathsf{s}_{0}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}_{0}})
consists of an

and an extended exchange matrix
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}_{0}}

[

]
, a cluster algebra is of finite type if and only if its exchange matrix is mutation equivalent to one whose Cartan counterpart is a finite-type Cartan matrix. Equivalently, a cluster algebra is of finite type if and only if it has finitely many distinct unfrozen cluster variables.

We next recall the notion of finite mutation type.

Definition 6.2
.

A cluster algebra is said to be of
finite mutation type
if it admits only finitely many distinct extended exchange matrices under iterated mutations. Otherwise, it is said to be of
infinite mutation type
.

If a cluster algebra is of infinite mutation type, then it is necessarily of infinite type. However, the converse does
not
hold in general, i.e., a cluster algebra may be of infinite type while still being of finite mutation type. This distinction plays a crucial role in verifying the multiplicity condition in Theorem
4.14

We now focus on the cluster algebras
\mathcal{A}_{P}
in Definition

be the index set of an extended exchange matrix

\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{r,s})_{(r,s)\in J_{\mathrm{uf}}\times J}

.

Let

be an extended exchange matrix of size

6.2.
Weak Bruhat orders and marked extended exchange matrices

To compare the cluster algebras
\mathcal{A}_{P}
associated with parabolic subgroups, we introduced a partial order on exchange matrices in Definition
6.3
. Since extended exchange matrices are determined by the Weyl group elements as in (
3.3
), the partial order is closely related to the weak Bruhat order on the Weyl group, which we now recall.

Let
W
be a Weyl group, let
I
be the index set for simple roots, and let
S=\{s_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be the set of corresponding simple reflections. For
w\in W
, denote its length by
\ell(w)
. A reduced expression
\underline{w}
then has the form

s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{m}},\,\mbox{where $m=\ell(w)$.}

Definition 6.9
.

Let
v,w\in W
. The
left weak Bruhat order
on
W
is defined by

v\leq_{L}w\quad\mbox{if $\ell(w)-\ell(v)=\ell(wv^{-1})$.}

Equivalently,
w
can be expressed as
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\nu}}v
with
\ell(w)=\ell(v)+\nu
.

Similarly, the
right weak Bruhat order
on
W
is defined by

v\leq_{R}w\quad\mbox{if $\ell(w)-\ell(v)=\ell(v^{-1}w)$.}

Equivalently,
w
can be expressed as
vs_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\nu}}
with
\ell(w)=\ell(v)+\nu
.

Both
\leq_{L}
and
\leq_{R}
define partial orders on
W
.

For a subset
K\subseteq I
, let

•

W_{K}
denote the parabolic subgroup generated by
\{s_{{k}}\mid k\in K\}
, and

•

\mathsf{D}_{K}
denote the Dynkin diagram associated with
W_{K}
.

We also denote by

•

w^{K}_{0}
the longest element of
W_{K}
, and

•

w_{K}
a (unique) minimal length representative of the left coset
w_{0}W_{K}
in
W
.

Recall that
w_{K}=w_{0}w^{K}_{0}
. Note that if
K=\emptyset
, then
w^{K}_{0}=\mathrm{id}
and
w_{K}=w_{0}
.

We define an involution on
\mathsf{D}_{K}
by

*^{K}\colon\mathsf{D}_{K}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathsf{D}_{K}\quad\mbox{ determined by $\alpha_{i^{*^{K}}}=-w_{0}^{K}\alpha_{i}$}

For
K\subseteq M\subseteq I
, set

K^{*^{M}}=\left\{i^{*^{M}}\mid i\in K\right\}.

When
M=I
, we simply write
K^{*}
instead of
K^{*^{M}}
.

Although
w_{0}
and
w_{0}^{K}
do
not
commute in general, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.10
.

For every subset
K\subseteq I
, we have

w_{0}^{K}w_{0}=w_{0}w_{0}^{K^{*}}

Proof.

It follows from the identity
w_{0}s_{i}w_{0}=s_{i^{*}}
.
∎

Note that

•

w_{0}^{K}w_{0}
is the minimal length
right
coset representative of
W_{K}w_{0}
in
W
, and

•

w_{0}w_{0}^{K}
is the minimal length
left
coset representative of
w_{0}W_{K}
in
W
.

The next proposition is an immediate consequence of the construction of marked extended exchange matrices in Definition
6.7
.

Proposition 6.11
.

If
w_{K}\leq_{L}w_{{K^{\prime}}}
in the
left
weak Bruhat order, then there exist reduced expressions
\underline{w}_{K}
and
\underline{w}_{{K^{\prime}}}
such that the associated marked extended exchange matrices
(\varepsilon,\mathsf{L})
and
(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathsf{L}^{\prime})
, constructed from
\underline{w}_{K}
and
\underline{w}_{{K^{\prime}}}
respectively, satisfy

(\varepsilon,\mathsf{L})\leq(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathsf{L}^{\prime})

in the sense of (
6.2
).

Using Proposition
6.11
, we compare the minimal length left coset representatives. We begin by comparing
{w}_{K_{1}}
with
{w}_{{K_{2}}}
within a fixed Weyl group
W
.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq I
. If
K_{1}\supseteq K_{2}
, then

(6.4)

w^{{K_{1}}}_{0}w_{0}\leq_{L}w^{{K_{2}}}_{0}w_{0}.

Moreover,

(6.5)

w_{K_{1}}\leq_{L}w_{K_{2}}.

Proof.

Since
K_{1}\supseteq K_{2}
, we have
w_{0}^{K_{2}}\leq_{L}w_{0}^{K_{1}}
. Observe that

w^{{K_{1}}}_{0}w_{0}=w^{{K_{1}}}_{0}w^{{K_{2}}}_{0}w^{{K_{2}}}_{0}w_{0}

and compute

\displaystyle\ell(w^{K_{2}}_{0}w_{0})-\ell(w^{K_{1}}_{0}w_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(\ell(w_{0})-\ell(w^{K_{2}}_{0})\right)-\left(\ell(w_{0})-\ell(w^{K_{1}}_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=\ell(w^{K_{1}}_{0})-\ell(w^{K_{2}}_{0})=\ell(w^{K_{1}}_{0}w^{K_{2}}_{0})=\ell(w^{{K_{1}}}_{0}w_{0})^{-1}(w^{{K_{2}}}_{0}w_{0})).

which establishes (
6.4
).

Since
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}
implies
K_{1}^{*}\subseteq K_{2}^{*}
, applying (
6.4
) to the pair
(K_{1}^{*},K_{2}^{*})
yields

w_{K_{1}}=w_{0}w_{0}^{K_{1}}=w_{0}^{K_{1}^{*}}w_{0}\leq_{L}w_{0}^{K_{2}^{*}}w_{0}=w_{0}w_{0}^{K_{2}}=w_{K_{2}}.

∎

Combining Propositions
6.11
and

, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.13
.

If

, then there exist reduced expressions
\underline{w}_{K_{1}}
and

and

and
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
. For

, let

.

Let
W_{i}
,

, and

be as above. Suppose that there exists an embedding of Dynkin diagrams

where

in

. If

.

Lemma 6.15

(Mackey decomposition)
.

For

X_{K}=\bigsqcup_{d\in X_{K}\cap X^{-1}_{J}}d\cdot X^{J}_{K^{d}\cap J},\,\,\mbox{where $K^{d}=\{d^{-1}s_{i}d\mid i\in K\}$}.

Proof.

See Lemma 2.1.9 in
[
GP00a
]
.
∎

Lemma 6.16
.

Let

. Then

w_{0}^{K\cap J}w_{0}^{J}\leq_{R}w_{0}^{K}w_{0}^{I}.

In particular, if
J\subseteq I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}
, then

w_{0}^{I_{1}\setminus J}w_{0}^{I_{1}}\leq_{R}w_{0}^{I_{2}\setminus J}w_{0}^{I_{2}}.

Proof.

For

w_{0}^{K\cap J}w_{0}^{J}\leq_{R}w_{0}^{K}w_{0}^{I}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
6.14
.

Without loss of generality, after reindexing via

where the second inequality

.
∎

7.
Detecting thresholds for appearance of infinite mutation type subquivers

The goal of this section is to complete the proof of Theorem
4.11
. We show that each case in Table
3
admits an arrow of arbitrarily large multiplicity. This is achieved by identifying an induced quiver of infinite mutation type that arises from a quiver of finite mutation type after adding a marked frozen vertex.

Since we work with complex simple algebraic groups of simply laced type, the exchange matrix of the initial seed
\mathsf{s}_{0}
is skew-symmetric, so every extended exchange matrix
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{s}}
can be represented by a quiver. As a quiver determines a cluster algebra, we say that the quiver is of finite, infinite, finite mutation, or infinite mutation type according to the type of its associated cluster algebra.

Let
\mathsf{Q}_{0}
be a quiver containing a subquiver
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
of
finite mutation type
. Let
\mathsf{V}_{0}
(resp.
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
) be the vertex set of
\mathsf{Q}_{0}
(resp.
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
). Suppose that there is a vertex
f\in\mathsf{V}_{0}\setminus\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
such that the induced subquiver
\mathsf{Q}\coloneqq\mathsf{Q}_{0}\big|_{\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\cup\{f\}}
is of
infinite mutation type
. Since
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
has a finite mutation class, the mutation class of
\mathsf{Q}
contains quivers in which the multiplicity of arrows from
f
to some unfrozen vertex becomes arbitrarily large.

Proposition 7.1

(Lemma 6.2 in
[
CKKP25
]
)

be a quiver with vertex set

Remark 7.4
.

We conclude with a remark on the role of marking. The classification into finite or infinite type does not depend on the presence of frozen vertices. In particular, removing all frozen vertices from an infinite-type quiver

still yields a quiver of infinite type.

To prove Proposition
7.2

7.1

for an explicit example.
