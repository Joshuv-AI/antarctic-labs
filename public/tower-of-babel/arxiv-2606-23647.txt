Genuine certification of incompatible quantum instruments through sequential communication tasks
Arindam Mitra, Satyaki Manna, Debashis Saha

Abstract
Quantum instruments constitute the general description of quantum dynamics, encompassing both quantum measurements and quantum channels as special cases. Consequently, the incompatibility of quantum instruments represents a fundamental manifestation of nonclassicality in quantum theory. Here, we establish the operational significance of this notion by demonstrating communication tasks with classical inputs and outputs that enable the semi-device-independent certification of incompatible quantum instruments. We introduce a class of three-party communication tasks involving a sender, a relayer, and a receiver, and derive the tight upper bound of the figure of merits of these tasks achievable by all compatible instruments implemented by the relayer and this bound coincides with the optimal performance attainable in a classical communication subject to the same dimensional constraints. Violation of this bound certifies the incompatibility of the pair of quantum instruments implemented by the relayer. This identifies certification of incompatible instruments as a manifestation of quantum advantage in communication. This certification protocol is genuine as it is able to certify the incompatibility of a pair of instruments where the measurements and channels induced by the instruments are pairwise compatible and, therefore it does not depend on the incompability of measurements and channels induced by the instruments. Finally, we identify the simplest instances of our communication scenario that enable the certification of incompatible quantum instruments.

II
Quantum measurements, channels and instruments and their compatibility

A quantum measurement
M
acting on
\mathcal{H}
is mathematically described by a set of positive semi-definite matrices i.e.,
M:=\{M(a)\geq 0,M\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\}^{n}_{a=1}
such that
\sum^{n}_{a=1}M(a)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of linear operators on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
is the identity operator on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
;
Masahito 2006
. Measurements are also known as POVMs. The set
\Omega_{M}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is known as the outcome set of
M
and each
M(a)
is known as the POVM elements of
M
. We denote the set of all measurements on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
and the set of all quantum states on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
. If the measurement
M
is implemented on a quantum system with an arbitrary quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, the probability of an arbitrary outcome
a\in\Omega_{M}
is given by
p(a|\rho,M)=\text{Tr}[\rho M(a)]
. Outcomes of an arbitrary measurement can be post processed. A measurement
M^{\prime}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
is said to a post processing of
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
if for all
b\in\Omega_{M^{\prime}}
,
M^{\prime}(b)=\sum_{a\in\Omega_{M}}\nu_{ba}M(a)
where
\nu_{ba}\geq 0\forall a,b
and
\sum_{b\in\Omega_{M^{\prime}}}\nu_{ba}=1
. We denote it as
M^{\prime}\preceq M
. A pair of measurements
(M_{1},M_{2})\subset\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be compatible or jointly implementable if there exists a joint measurement
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
with outcome set
\Omega_{M}=\Omega_{M_{1}}\times\Omega_{M_{2}}
such that for all
a\in\Omega_{M_{1}}
,
b\in\Omega_{M_{2}}
, the equations
M_{1}(a)=\sum_{q\in\Omega_{M_{2}}}M(a,q)
and
M_{2}(b)=\sum_{r\in\Omega_{M_{1}}}M(r,b)
hold
Heinosaari et al. 2016
;
Gühne et al. 2023
. Therefore, implementation of
M
on a quantum system is equivalent to the simultaneous implementation of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
on it. We denote it as
M_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}M_{2}
. It is known that if
M^{\prime}_{1}\preceq M_{1}
and
M^{\prime}_{2}\preceq M_{2}
then
M_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}M_{2}\Rightarrow M^{\prime}_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}M^{\prime}_{2}
.

A quantum channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is mathematically defined as a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) linear map
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
;
Masahito 2006
. We denote the set of all quantum channels with input Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and output Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
as
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. Acting on a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, it provides an output state
\Lambda(\rho)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})
. We denote the identity channel acting on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
as
\mathbbm{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
. The action of a CP linear map
\Phi
in Heisenberg picture, denoted as
\Phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is defined by the equation

\text{Tr}[\Lambda(X)Y]=\text{Tr}[X\Lambda^{\dagger}(Y)]\penalty\ \forall X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),Y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}).
The linear map
\Phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is CP as
\Phi
is CP. In Heisenberg picture, a quantum channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
acts on a measurement
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K})
as
\Lambda^{\dagger}[M]:=\{\Lambda^{\dagger}(M(a))\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
. A pair of quantum channels
\Lambda_{1}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
is said to be compatible if there exists a joint quantum channel
\Lambda:\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that for an arbitrary quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
\Lambda_{1}(\rho)=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\Lambda(\rho)
and
\Lambda_{2}(\rho)=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\Lambda(\rho)

Heinosaari et al. 2016
;
Heinosaari and Miyadera 2017
. In short-hand notation, we denote these as
\Lambda_{1}=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\circ\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{2}=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\circ\Lambda
. Such short-hand notation will be used throughout the paper. The implementation of
\Lambda
on a quantum system is equivalent to the simultaneous implementation of
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
on it. We denote it as
\Lambda_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\Lambda_{2}
.

A quantum instrument
\mathcal{I}
is defined as a set of CP trace-non increasing maps i.e.,
\mathcal{I}:=\{\Phi_{b}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\}^{n}_{b=1}
such that
\sum^{n}_{b=1}\Phi_{b}=\Phi
is a quantum channel
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
;
Masahito 2006
. The outcome set of the instrument
\mathcal{I}
is
\Omega_{\mathcal{I}}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Each
b
is known as the classical output of
\mathcal{I}
and given a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, it occurs with the probability
p(b|\mathcal{I},\rho)=\text{Tr}[\Phi_{b}(\rho)]
. The quantum output
\mathcal{I}
, given
b
is already obtained, is
\frac{\Phi_{b}(\rho)}{\text{Tr}[\Phi_{b}(\rho)]}
. Besides inducing the channel
\Phi
, instrument
\mathcal{I}
also induces a measurement
\hat{M}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\{\hat{M}_{\mathcal{I}}(b):=\Phi^{\dagger}_{b}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}})\}
. We denote the set of all quantum instruments with input Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and output Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
as
\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. It can be easily seen that quantum measurements and channels can be considered special cases of quantum instruments. A quantum instrument
\mathcal{I}=\{\Phi_{b}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
acts on a measurement
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K})
and provides the measurement
\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}[M]:=\{\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}[M](b,a):=\Phi^{\dagger}_{b}(M(a))\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
. Clearly,
\Omega_{\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}[M]}=\Omega_{\mathcal{I}}\times\Omega_{M}
. A pair of instruments
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{\Phi^{1}_{a}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{\Phi^{2}_{b}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
is said to be (parallel) compatible if there exists a joint instrument
\mathcal{I}=\{\Phi_{ab}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{2})
with outcome set
\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
such that the following holds:

\Phi^{1}_{a}=\sum_{b\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}}\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\circ\Phi_{ab}\penalty\ \forall a\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}},\penalty\ \Phi^{2}_{a}=\sum_{a\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}}\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\circ\Phi_{ab}\penalty\ \forall b\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Mitra and Farkas 2022
;
Mitra and Farkas 2023
;
Leppäjärvi and Sedlák 2024
;
Ghai and Mitra 2025
.
We denote it as
\mathcal{I}_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\mathcal{I}_{2}
. Clearly, the implementation of
\mathcal{I}
is equivalent to the simultaneous implementation of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
. If a pair of instruments is compatible, their induced measurements, are compatible and induced channels are compatible
Mitra and Farkas 2022
. In this work, we highlight that the converse is not true. It should be mentioned here that the notion of measurement-channel compatibility also exists in the literature. A measurement
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
and a channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
are said to be compatible if there exists a quantum instruments
\mathcal{I}=\{\Phi_{b}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
such that
M=\{\Phi^{\dagger}_{b}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}})\}_{b\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}}=\Omega_{M}}
and
\Lambda=\sum_{b}\Phi_{b}

Heinosaari et al. 2016
;
Mori 2020
;
Heinosaari et al. 2018
. We denote it as
M\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\Lambda
.

III
Sequential Communication Task

First, let us establish the notation
[K]
to represent the set
\{0,\cdots,K-1\}
for any positive integer
K
.
We define a sequential communication task involving three parties: Alice (the sender), Bob (the relayer), and Charlie (the receiver). In this task, Alice and Bob receive inputs
x\in[n_{A}]
and
y\in[n_{B}]
, respectively. Upon receiving the input
x
, Alice sends a
d_{A}
-dimensional classical or quantum system to Bob. After receiving both the input
y
and the message from Alice, Bob produces an output
b_{y}=f(x,y)\in[d_{y}]
and subsequently sends a
d_{B}
-dimensional classical or quantum system to Charlie. Charlie does not receive any input; based solely on the message received from Bob, Charlie produces an output
c\in[d_{c}]
. The performance of the communication task is characterized by the set of conditional probability distributions
\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}
. Any figure of merit can be written as the linear function of these probabilities, which means, the figure of merit

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\sum_{x,y,c}\alpha(x,y,b_{y},c)p(b_{y},c|x,y).

(1)

In classical communication, the classical messages transmitted by Alice and Bob are denoted by
m_{A}
and
m_{B}
respectively. Any typical probability in this case can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(b_{y},c|x,y)=\sum_{m_{A},m_{B}}p_{e}(m_{A}|x)p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|m_{A},y)p_{d}(c|m_{B}).

(2)

The individual encoding, transformation and decoding probability satisfies non-negativity and normalization conditions which reads as,

\sum_{m_{A}}p_{e}(m_{A}|x)=\sum_{b_{y},m_{B}}p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|m_{A},y)=\sum_{c}p_{d}(c|m_{B})=1
.
Similarly, the optimum classical figure of merit emerges as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{C}_{op}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{p_{e}(m_{A}|x)\},\\
\{p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|y,m_{A})\},\\
\{p_{d}(c|m_{B})\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{x,y,c,m_{A},m_{B}}\alpha(x,y,b_{y},c)

(3)

\displaystyle\times p_{e}(m_{A}|x)p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|y,m_{A})p_{d}(c|m_{B}).

In quantum communication, Alice encodes her input into a quantum state
\rho_{x}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=d_{A}
and sends it to Bob. Depending on the input
y
, Bob implements an instrument from the set
\{\mathcal{I}_{y}\}_{y}
on it. Bob produces a classical output
b_{y}
with probability
\text{Tr}[\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})]
and transmits quantum output
\frac{\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})}{\text{Tr}[\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})]}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
to Charlie where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d_{B}
. Having no input, Charlie performs a fixed measurement
N:=\{N(c)\}_{c=1}^{d_{c}}
on the state sent by Bob. The expression of the probabilities reduces to,

\displaystyle p(b_{y},c|x,y)=\text{Tr}\left(\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})N(c)\right).

(4)

Consequently, optimum figure of merit,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{Q}_{op}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\rho_{x}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\},\\
\{\mathcal{I}_{y}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})|\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{y}}=d_{y}\},\\
\{N\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H}_{B})|\Omega_{M}=d_{c}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{x,y,c}\alpha(x,y,b_{y},c)\text{Tr}\left(\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})N(c)\right).

(5)

We denote this sequential communication scenario by the set of natural numbers
(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})
. Furthermore, both
\mathcal{S}^{C}_{op}
and
\mathcal{S}^{Q}_{op}
depend on these parameters. However, for notational simplicity, we sometimes omit the explicit dependence of
\mathcal{S}^{C}_{op}
and
\mathcal{S}^{Q}_{op}
on these parameters when it is obvious from the context. For a given quantum strategy in a given task, whenever the figure of merit
\mathcal{S}^{Q}>\mathcal{S}^{C}_{op}
, we interpret this as a
quantum advantage
by that quantum strategy in that task.

Now, we define the set of all classical statistics which is generated according to Eq. (
2
) by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c}):=\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}.

(6)

Similarly, set of all probabilities generated by quantum communication (i.e., from Eq. (
4
)) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c}):=\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}.

(7)

We can define another type of statistics generated in quantum communication when the instruments
\{\mathcal{I}_{y}\}_{y}
is compatible i.e, when
\{\mathcal{I}_{y}\}_{y}
is compatible in Eq. (
4
). We call this

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{C}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c}):=\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}.

(8)

The optimum figure of merit in this case is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}}_{op}
. The merit looks like (
5
); the only difference is that the instruments are compatible. As compatible instruments form a subset of all quantum instruments, we can infer,

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c}).

(9)

Trivially, we know,

\mathcal{C}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c}).

(10)

Figure 1:
Fig. (a) depicts the scenario of instrument incompatibility certification that we explore in this work. The scenarios depicted Fig. (b) and Fig. (c) are the scenarios of measurement and channel incompatibility certification scenarios respectively and are the special cases of the scenario depicted in Fig. (a) which is unified.

Now we want to compare the set of classical statistics with the set of statistics coming from quantum communication with compatible instruments.

Proposition 1
.

For any sequential communication task denoted by a set of natural numbers
(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})
,

\mathcal{C}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})

\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{C}(n_{A},m_{A},n_{B},d_{y},m_{B},d_{c})
. Consequently, for any figure of merit
\mathcal{S}
, we have
\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{C}}_{op}\leq\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}}_{op}.

Proof.

We have to prove that any probability distribution
\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}
obtained from any classical strategy (given in Eq. (
2
)) can be obtained using quantum strategy where Bob uses compatible quantum instruments. For notational simplicity, we prove it for
n_{B}=2
. The generalization of the results for arbitrary
n_{B}
is straightforward.

Consider the orthonormal basis
\{\ket{m_{A}}\}
of the Hilbert space
d_{A}
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\{\ket{m_{B}}\}
of the Hilbert space
d_{B}
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Assume that Alice encodes her input
x
as
\rho_{x}=\sum_{m_{A}}p_{e}(m_{A}|x)\ket{m_{A}}\bra{m_{A}}
, Bob uses a pair of quantum instruments
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{y}=\{\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}\}
, where
\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho)=\sum_{m_{A},m_{B}}p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|m_{A},y)\text{Tr}[\rho\ket{m_{A}}\bra{m_{A}}]\ket{m_{B}}\bra{m_{B}}
for an arbitrary
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and Charlie uses a measurement
M=\{M(c)=\sum_{m_{B}}p_{d}(c|m_{B})\ket{m_{B}}\bra{m_{B}}\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
. Then

\displaystyle p(b_{y},c|x,y)=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Phi^{y}_{b_{y}}(\rho_{x})M(c)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{A},m_{B}}p_{e}(m_{A}|x)p_{t}(b_{y},m_{B}|m_{A},y)p_{d}(c|m_{B}).

(11)

Now, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
are compatible with the joint instrument
\mathcal{I}=\{\Phi_{b_{0}b_{1}}\}
where for an arbitrary
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})

\displaystyle\Phi_{b_{0}b_{1}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{A},m_{B},m^{\prime}_{B}}p(b_{0},m_{B}|m_{A},0)p(b_{1},m^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}|m_{A},1)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\rho\ket{m_{A}}\bra{m_{A}}]\ket{m_{B}}\bra{m_{B}}\otimes\ket{m^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}}\bra{m^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}},

(12)

where for all

and
b_{1}\in\{0,\ldots,d_{1}-1\}
,
\{\ket{m^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}}\}
is a basis of Hilbert space

where
\mathcal{H}_{B}=\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
and
\ket{m_{B}}=\ket{m_{B^{\prime}}}
. The direct implication of this result indicates that

in different contexts.

IV
Certification of incompatible instruments

A certification protocol is said to be genuine if it is able to certify a pair of incompatible instruments even when the measurements and channels induced by the instruments are pairwise compatible. In other words, it
should not depend
on the incompatibility of the measurements and channels induced by the instruments. Now, it is easy to see that if the figure of merit depends only on
\{p(b_{y}|x,y)\}
or
\{p(c|x,y)\}
, there is no advantage when the measurements and channels induced by the instruments are pairwise compatible. Therefore, the figure of merit of a genuine certification protocol must involve
\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}
non-trivially.

Before going into our main claims, it is useful to discuss the following Proposition.

Proposition 2
.

If a pair of instruments
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{\Phi^{1}_{x}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{\Phi^{2}_{y}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
is compatible (i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\mathcal{I}_{2}
), then for an arbitrary pair of measurements
M_{1}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
M_{2}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\dagger}[M_{1}]\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{\dagger}[M_{2}]
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{I}_{1}\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\mathcal{I}_{2}
, we have a quantum instrument
\mathcal{I}=\{\Phi_{xy}\}_{(x,y)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\times\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that
\Phi^{1}_{x}=\sum_{y}\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\circ\Phi_{xy}
and
\Phi^{2}_{y}=\sum_{x}\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\circ\Phi_{xy}
. Therefore, for all
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
, we have
(\Phi^{1}_{x})^{\dagger}(X)=\sum_{y}\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}(X\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}})
and for all
Y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, we have
(\Phi^{2}_{x})^{\dagger}(Y)=\sum_{x}\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\otimes Y)
. Consider a set of operators
M=\{M(x,y,s,t):=\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}(M_{1}(s)\otimes M_{2}(t))\}
where
x\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
,
y\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
,
s\in\Omega_{M_{1}}
and
t\in\Omega_{M_{2}}
. As
\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}
is CP for all
x,y
and
\Phi^{\dagger}:=\sum_{x,y}\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}
is CP unital, we have
M(x,y,s,t)\geq 0\penalty\ \forall x,y,s,t
and
\sum_{x,y,s,t}M(x,y,s,t)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Hence,
M
is a valid measurement. Now, for all
x\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
and
s\in\Omega_{M_{1}}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{y,t}M(x,y,s,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\Phi^{\dagger}_{xy}(M_{1}(s)\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Phi^{1}_{x})^{\dagger}(M_{1}(s)).

(13)

Similarly, for all
y\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
and
t\in\Omega_{M_{2}}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x,s}M(x,y,s,t)=

\displaystyle(\Phi^{2}_{x})^{\dagger}(M_{2}(t)).

(14)

Hence,
M
is the joint measurement of
\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{1}[M_{1}]
and
\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{2}[M_{2}]
. Hence, we have
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\dagger}[M_{1}]\hbox{\hskip 2.13394pt$\circ\hskip-2.84526pt\circ$\hskip 2.13394pt}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{\dagger}[M_{2}]
.
∎

The inference of this proposition is true for any number of compatible instruments. We omit that proof as this is a straight-forward generalization of Proposition
2
.

Now, consider a pair of qubit binary quantum instruments

Lemma 1
.

The pair
(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})
is incompatible.

Proof.

Consider two qubit projective measurements
\bar{M}_{0}=\{\ket{0}\bra{0},\ket{1}\bra{1}\}
and
\bar{M}_{1}=\{\ket{+}\bra{+},\ket{-}\bra{-}\}

\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}

\sigma_{z}

\{\ket{+}.\ket{-}\}

\sigma_{x}

\bar{M}_{0}\preceq\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}_{0}[\bar{M}_{0}]
,
\bar{M}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}_{1}[\bar{M}_{1}]
. Therefore, as the pair
(\bar{M}_{1},\bar{M}_{2})
is incompatible, the pair

2

(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})
is incompatible.

In this case, we call
(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})

. We describe the peculiarity of intrinsically incompatible instruments in Figure

.

Figure 2:
The instrument
\mathcal{J}_{0}
induces a channel
\Psi_{0}
and a measurement
M_{0}
. Similarly, the instrument
\mathcal{J}_{1}
induces channel
\Psi_{1}
and measurement
M_{1}

\mathcal{J}_{o}
and
\mathcal{J}_{1}

\mathcal{J}_{0}
and
\mathcal{J}_{1}

does not depend
on the incompatibility of induced measurements and channels and therefore, it is

Now, to certify a pair of incompatible instruments, we introduce a specific sequential communication task as demonstrated below.

IV.1
\boldsymbol{(m_{A},d)}
-sequential XOR task

Consider a sequential task which is characterized by natural numbers
(n_{A}=2^{d},m_{A},n_{B}=2,d_{y}=d,m_{B}=m_{A},d_{c}=d).
Alice is given a string of
2
dits, denoted by
x=x_{0}x_{1}
, chosen uniformly at random from the set of all

2^{d}
number of strings. Each component
x_{y}
belongs to the alphabet
[d]
for every
y\in[2]
. Alice sends
m_{A}
-dimensional message to Bob.
After receiving the message from Alice, Bob produces an output
b_{y}
given an input
y
which is chosen uniformly at random. Then Bob sends the message of dimension
m_{B}
to Charlie. After receiving the message from Bob Charlie produce
d
possible outcomes. Here, Charlie has no inputs. The task is successful if
b_{y}=x_{y}\ominus_{d}(c.y)
. Mathematically, the figure of merit of this task is given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(m_{A},d)=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{x,y,c}p(b_{y}=x_{y}\ominus_{d}(c.y),c|x,y)

(15)

where
\ominus_{d}
denotes subtraction modulo
d
. As the task depends on the parameters
m_{A}
and
d
(all other parameters are functions of
m_{A}
or
d
), we refer it as the

(m_{A},d)
-sequential XOR task.

Now, we show that this sequential communication task can certify the incompatibility even for an intrinsically incompatible pair of instruments
(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})
implemented by Bob. The certification of incompatibility in such an extreme case implies that this certification protocol
does not depend
on the incompatibility of measurements and channels induced by the given pair of instruments and therefore, it is
genuine
.

IV.4
Certification in minimal scenario

In the previous section, we have presented a specific sequential communication tasks that certifies the incompatibility of any pair of
d
-dimensional quantum instruments. The simplest case was the
(2,2)
-sequential XOR task. We now investigate whether the incompatibility of instruments can be certified in an even simpler scenario where the number of Alice’s inputs, i.e.,
n_{A}<4
and other parameters
m_{A}=n_{B}=d_{y}=m_{B}=d_{c}=2
as it is not possible to consider any of these parameters less than
2
for a non-trivial case. The choice
n_{A}=2
leads to a trivial scenario, since Alice can directly encode her input in the communicated system, allowing Bob to recover it perfectly and yielding classical success probability to be
1
and therefore, there is no quantum advantage. Therefore, the only non-trivial possibility is
n_{A}=3
.

Motivated by this observation, we introduce a sequential communication task with
(n_{A}=3,m_{A}=n_{B}=d_{y}=m_{B}=d_{c}=2)
, where the statistics can be written in the same manner
\{p(b_{y},c|x,y)\}
and the figure of merit is given by,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[p(0,0|0,0)+p(0,1|0,0)+p(0,0|0,1)+p(1,1|0,1)

(24)

\displaystyle+p(1,0|1,0)+p(1,1|1,0)+p(0,0|1,1)+p(1,1|1,1)

\displaystyle+p(0,1|2,1)+p(1,0|2,1)\Big].

Proposition 5
.

If Bob uses two compatible instruments in this task, the figure of merit
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}}_{op}=4
. Furthermore, the optimum figure of merit using the classical strategy
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{op}=\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}}_{op}
.

For the proof of this proposition, we refer the reader to Appendix
B.1
.

Theorem 3
.

The quantum advantage in this task implies that the pair of instruments implemented by Bob is incompatible.

Proof.

From the Proposition
5
, we know that
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{op}=\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{C}}}_{op}
. Therefore, whenever
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}}>\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{op}
, it follows that the instruments implemented by Bob must be incompatible.
∎

Now, in the following Proposition, we show that the incompatibility of the pair of instruments
(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})
can be certified through this task.

Proposition 6
.

In this task, the optimum quantum figure of merit
\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{op}=3+\sqrt{2}>4
and it is achieved by the intrinsically incompatible pair of instruments
(\mathcal{J}_{0},\mathcal{J}_{1})
implemented by Bob.

The proof of this proposition is postponed to Appendix
B.2
.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have demonstrated the certification of the incompatibility of quantum instruments using sequential communication tasks. The
importance
of this work lies in the following aspects. Firstly, before this work, the certification of (parallel) incompatibility of instruments has not been demonstrated to the best of our knowledge, Secondly, our certification protocol enables certification of instrument incompatibility, where the measurements and channels induced by the instruments are pairwise compatible (even in minimal scenario). In other words, it
does not depend
on the incompatibility of the measurements and channels induced by the instruments and therefore, it is
genuine
. Thirdly, our approach is
unified
; i.e., the scenarios of measurement incompatibility certification and the scenarios channel incompatibility certification become special cases of our certification scenario (see Fig.
1
). Lastly, this protocol can also be viewed as an example of sequential communication task where instrument incompatibility is necessary to get quantum advantage.

Our work opens up several research directions. It is interesting to explore self-testing of quantum incompatible instruments through our sequential communication scenario. Furthermore, while the proposed certification protocol is restricted to two instruments, it can be generalized to scenarios involving more than two instruments. In such cases, different layers of incompatibility structures may arise within a set of instruments. It would be interesting to certify these structures through the construction of suitable sequential communication tasks.
Additionally, the application of these kinds of certification protocols in cryptography and secure communication task is an interesting aspect to investigate.

Given an input
x
, let
\rho_{x}
be the state prepared by Alice,
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{\Phi^{0}_{x}\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{\Phi^{1}_{x}\}
be binary qubit instruments implemented by Bob, and
M=\{M(c)\}
be the dichotomic measurement implemented by Charlie. The quantum figure of merit corresponding to this strategy can be written from Eq. (
24
) as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{Q}}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big[\rho_{0}\Big((\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(1)+(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)

\displaystyle+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)\Big)+\rho_{1}\Big((\Phi^{0}_{1})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{0}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)

\displaystyle+(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)\Big)

\displaystyle+\rho_{2}\left((\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(1)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(0)\right)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle||(\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(1)+(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)||

\displaystyle+||(\Phi^{0}_{1})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{0}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)+(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)||

\displaystyle+||(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(1)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(0)||

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle||X_{0}+X_{1}||+||X_{1}+\mathbbm{1}-X_{0}||+||\mathbbm{1}-X_{1}||,

(31)

where
X_{0}=(\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{0}_{0})^{\dagger}M(1)
and
X_{1}=(\Phi^{1}_{0})^{\dagger}M(0)+(\Phi^{1}_{1})^{\dagger}M(1)

X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\mathbbm{1}+\vec{c}_{i}.\vec{\sigma}

\displaystyle 2+\sqrt{2}+\alpha_{1}+|\vec{c}_{1}|
