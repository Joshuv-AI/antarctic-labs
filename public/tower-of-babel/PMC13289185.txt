Equitable lipid optimisation through a data-driven, pharmacist-led secondary prevention pathway
Authors: Joshua Xu, Jasjit Syan, Hussein Alhakem, John Michael Foster, Grant McQueen, Sadia Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the UK, with raised cholesterol contributing to 25%–28% of ASCVD deaths. Despite well-established guidelines, cholesterol levels remain suboptimally managed and can vary based on ethnicity, sex and socioeconomic deprivation, potentially leading to disproportionate cardiovascular risk for those living at these intersections. Pharmacist-led lipid clinics may reduce variation through structured review and continuity of care. This study evaluates whether automating the detection and referral of high-risk patients to a pharmacist-led clinic is associated with improved attainment of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) among post-acute coronary syndrome patients in a West London borough, and whether outcomes vary by sex, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation.MethodsPatients with established ASCVD and residual elevated lipid levels were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision and Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine coding and referred to a pharmacist-led review. Sex, ethnicity, age and index of multiple deprivation (IMD) were automatically extracted. The review involved a structured consultation, treatment intensification where required, and follow-up at agreed intervals. The primary outcome was attainment of LDL-C ≤2.0 mmol/L in line with National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommendations assessed using a pre-post design.ResultsAmong 204 secondary prevention patients, 65% were from ethnically diverse backgrounds and 71% were male. Mean baseline LDL-C was 3.39 mmol/L and 13% were not actively receiving lipid-lowering therapy. Attendance by IMD quintile was 7.4% (most deprived), 34%, 33%, 13% and 12% (least deprived). Following the intervention, mean LDL-C reduced to 1.97 mmol/L with 70.6% achieving targets. Reductions were similar across sex, ethnicity and IMD quintile.ConclusionsA data-driven, pharmacist-led pathway was associated with significant LDL-C reduction. Among those who engaged, target attainment was similar across sex, ethnicity and IMD quintile, although low engagement from the most deprived quintile highlights that equitable reach has not yet been achieved. Further work is required to address upstream barriers and evaluate long-term sustainability.

What is already known on this topic

Pharmacist-led lipid services improve attainment of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) goals, but lipid management varies by socioeconomic status, sex and ethnicity, which may contribute to inequalities in cardiovascular outcomes.

What this study adds

This study provides real-world evidence that a data-driven, automated referral pathway can identify and enable the treatment of suboptimally managed secondary prevention patients.

Among patients who engaged with the clinic, LDL-C target attainment was similar across sex, ethnicity and index of multiple deprivation quintile.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

System-level interventions may reduce variation in care delivery, but further work is needed to address upstream barriers to engagement that limit equitable reach in the most deprived and under-represented communities.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the UK, accounting for a quarter of all deaths each year.1 Many of these deaths can be prevented through better management of risk factors such as dyslipidaemia, which contributes up to 28% of cardiovascular deaths in the UK.2 Multiple randomised controlled trials have shown that every 1 mmol/L reduction in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is associated with a 23% relative risk reduction in cardiovascular events.3 Lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), including statins, ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors, is effective and has reduced the incidence of coronary heart disease in multiple trials.4

Despite well-established evidence and treatment guidelines, cholesterol levels remain suboptimally managed in the UK, including among patients who have already experienced an acute coronary syndrome (ACS) event.5 Recent studies report that up to 80% of high and very high risk patients are not treated to guideline-recommended LDL-C levels.6 7 The EuroPath III project identified several contributors to this current gap, including underutilisation of LLT at discharge, inconsistent guidance to general practitioners, inadequate optimisation, gaps in knowledge and patient concerns.8

The British Heart Foundation’s Bridging Hearts Report highlights that cardiovascular outcomes are also strongly influenced by ethnicity, sex and socioeconomic deprivation.9 In the absence of consistent treatment pathways, treatment intensification and follow-up become clinician-dependent, enabling system pressures, health literacy and clinician bias to amplify inequity. Patients at these intersections are therefore potentially left at a disproportionately increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events. In this study, we define equity across two variables: access to the care pathway and the outcomes achieved following engagement.

Pharmacist-led lipid clinics have the potential to improve access to and adherence with LLT through structured review and patient education.10 A meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials involving 22 095 patients found that pharmacist-led interventions significantly reduced LDL-C levels compared with usual care.11 More recently, a study of post-ACS patients showed that a telehealth cardiology pharmacist clinic both improved adherence to secondary prevention medications and reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in the first 12 months.12 However, it remains unclear whether system-level initiatives such as automated identification and referrals can consistently improve treatment gaps at scale.

Building on a locally re-established lipid clinic to provide structured follow-up and treatment intensification, we set out to trial a data-driven pathway using electronic health records to automate identification and referral of high-risk patients. We hypothesised that this approach may reduce clinician-dependent variation and support a more consistent secondary prevention pathway. While pharmacist-led models and equity in lipid management have been studied independently, the integration of automated patient identification with a pharmacist-led pathway and its evaluation across demographic and socioeconomic subgroups have not previously been explored.

This study evaluates whether a data-driven pharmacist-led pathway is associated with improved LDL-C outcomes in a real-world secondary prevention population and whether these outcomes differ across sex, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation groups.

Methods

Data collection

Between April 2023 and April 2024, patients with established ASCVD who attended the hospital and had lipid panels measured were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) and Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED) codes.

Trust electronic patient records (EPR) systems generated monthly automated reports of patients who met these criteria and had residual elevated LDL-C, which was defined as LDL-C >2 mmol/L.13

Identified patients initially underwent notes review using hospital and primary care records to confirm eligibility against prespecified referral criteria, including statin intolerance or contraindication, LDL-C remaining above target despite maximal tolerated therapy, suspected familial hypercholesterolaemia or consideration for advanced therapy such as PCSK9 inhibitor or inclisiran. Eligible patients were then contacted by the pharmacist to review lipid-lowering therapies as part of a virtual pharmacist-led clinic. This was a single-centre, observational service evaluation using a pre-post intervention design.

The primary outcome was target attainment of LDL-C ≤2.0 mmol/L, consistent with national secondary guidance.13 This threshold was prespecified and used as the clinical treatment target within the pharmacist-led pathway. For patients with multiple follow-up lipid measurements, the most recent available LDL-C value within the follow-up period was used for analysis.

Clinic structure

One virtual clinic was conducted weekly and delivered by a trained clinical pharmacist (HA) who was able to prescribe the full range of National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) approved secondary prevention lipid-lowering therapies. Clinical management followed current national guidelines, with a treatment target of ≤2.0 mmol/L.13 All patient contact was in the form of telephone consultations initiated by the pharmacist.

The central data warehouse within the EPR collated information on patient sex, age, ethnicity, index of multiple deprivation (IMD) and treatment status. Ethnicity was derived from EPR and recorded in line with NHS data standards, reflecting patient-reported categories during routine clinical care. Demographic data and information relevant to confirm eligibility were recorded on a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

The review process included obtaining and reviewing up-to-date full lipid profiles, reviewing, assessing previous lipid-lowering therapies and adherence. Secondary prevention targets, the rationale for medications and potential harms were discussed. Lifestyle advice to support lipid management was provided universally. Using shared decision making to incorporate patient preferences, LLT was initiated or optimised unless contraindicated.

The specific approach to treatment escalation involved a stepwise protocol aligned with the NHS England Summary of National Guidance and Local Trust Guidelines, prioritising high-intensity statin optimisation, followed by the addition of ezetimibe and a PCSK9 inhibitor where indicated. Following the initial assessment, if desired, patients could undergo follow-up reviews at predefined intervals.

All follow-up consultations were carried out by telephone. They were conducted in line with routine clinical practice, typically within 8–10 weeks of treatment initiation or optimisation. The LDL-C value recorded at the first available follow-up appointment was used as the postintervention measure for primary analysis. During follow-up, based on updated lipid panels, if available, lipid-lowering therapies were reviewed and efficacy was evaluated against secondary prevention targets. Where targets were not met, further medication changes were introduced according to current guidelines and patient agreement. An overview of the pharmacist-led lipid optimisation pathway is shown in figure 1.

To support sustained lipid management following transfer to primary care, long-term follow-up plans were created with the patient. This process was coordinated with pharmacists within the Hounslow Primary Care Network, and an educational session was delivered to support lipid optimisation and referral of complex cases.

To enhance patient education and support medication counselling, a short educational video was developed for patients prescribed cardiac medications following a myocardial infarction. This video aimed to improve understanding of lipid management, the rationale behind treatment targets, streamline medication counselling and ensure consistent information delivery during hospital stays.

Conceptual framework

This service evaluation was structured around the Donabedian framework for healthcare quality, examining the Structure (automated patient identification through the EPR and pharmacist-led clinic pathway), Process (structured consultation, stepwise treatment escalation aligned with national guidelines and follow-up review) and Outcome (LDL-C target attainment, both overall and stratified by ethnicity, sex and IMD quintile).

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct or reporting of this service evaluation. Shared decision-making was used during consultations and educational materials were developed to further support patient understanding.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean and SD and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline and follow-up LDL-C values were compared using paired t-tests. LDL-C target attainment across sex, ethnicity and IMD quintiles was analysed using χ2 tests. Mean absolute LDL-C reduction was compared across subgroups using analysis of variance. Multivariable linear regression was used to identify predictors of LDL-C reduction, with age, sex, baseline LDL-C, ethnicity, IMD quintile as covariates. Multivariable logistic regression was similarly applied to identify predictors of LDL-C target attainment using the same covariates. The comparison of target attainment between statin monotherapy and combination therapy was assessed using χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout.

Analyses were conducted on the 204 patients who attended the clinic. Those without follow-up LDL-C measurements were excluded from the outcomes analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel for data handling and R (V.4.5.2) for statistical testing and figure generation.

Results

Demographics and LLT baseline

A total of 204 individuals were evaluated by the pharmacist in the lipid clinic. The mean age was 63±10 years. The proportion of men and women attending the clinic was 71% and 29%, respectively. Overall, 65% of attendees were from ethnically diverse backgrounds, while 35% were white British. Attendance by IMD quintile was 7.4% (most deprived), 34%, 33%, 13% and 12% (least deprived).

At the start of the intervention, 39% of participants had an LDL-C between 2.0 and 2.9 mmol/L, 32% had an LDL-C between 3.0 and 3.9 mmol/L and 27% had an LDL-C >4.0 mmol/L. The mean LDL-C of participants was 3.39 mmol/L (SD 1.01). Participant demographics and baseline lipid profiles are summarised in table 1.

Intervention stratified by backgrounds

LDL-C target was defined as ≤2.0 mmol/L in line with NICE secondary prevention guidelines. Following the intervention, 143 out of 204 participants (70.6%) achieved LDL-C targets (figure 2). Mean LDL-C decreased from 3.39 mmol/L to 1.97 mmol/L (41.8% reduction, p<0.001). Without a comparator group, the contribution of the intervention compared with regression to the mean or usual care cannot be determined.

Overall LDL-C target attainment (≤2.0 mmol/L) following the pharmacist-led intervention (n=204). LDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.

The proportion of males achieving LDL-C targets was 106 out of 145 (73.3%), compared with 37 out of 59 females (62.7%). There was no statistically significant difference between sexes.

Among white British participants, 49 out of 72 (68.1%) achieved LDL-C targets and for participants from Asian backgrounds, 51 out of 76 (67.1%) achieved targets. No statistically significant difference in LDL-C targets was observed across subgroups (all p>0.5); however, these analyses were likely underpowered and should be interpreted cautiously.

The proportion of participants achieving LDL-C targets by IMD quintile was 73.3% (most deprived), 68.6%, 72.1%, 74.1% and 64% (least deprived). No statistically significant difference in LDL-C target attainment was observed across IMD quintiles.

Type of LLT used at attainment

Among patients receiving high-intensity statin therapy alone, 66.3% (67/101) achieved LDL-C targets. Among those receiving the combination of high-intensity statin and ezetimibe specifically, 89.4% (59/66) achieved targets. Overall, patients on any form of combination therapy (statin plus ezetimibe, fibrate±statin, bempedoic acid±statin) were more likely to achieve LDL-C targets compared with those on statin monotherapy alone (82.6% vs 66.3%, χ2=6.32, p=0.012; online supplemental table S3). 17 patients receiving no LLT or non-statin therapy were excluded from this comparison.

Discussion

Key findings

In this study, we implemented a data-driven, pharmacist-led lipid clinic to identify and optimise LLT as per national guidelines. Through leveraging SNOMED and ICD-10 codes, high risk patients with residual elevated lipid levels were identified and reviewed by a clinical pharmacist.

Overall, the intervention was associated with meaningful LDL-C reduction, with a large proportion of patients achieving guideline recommended targets. Importantly, attainment of targets was similar across sex, ethnicity and IMD quintile, although this should be interpreted in the context of low attendance from the most deprived group. The extent to which this reflects equitable access to the pathway still requires further evaluation.

Equity

Socioeconomic deprivation

Socioeconomic deprivation is a well-established determinant of cardiovascular outcomes. Individuals living in the most deprived areas of England experience significantly higher rates of premature ASCVD compared with those from the least deprived areas.14 Prior studies have found that although prescribing rates of LLT are higher in socioeconomically deprived groups, cholesterol levels frequently remain elevated at follow-up, which highlights disparities in adherence, continuity of care and medication optimisation.15 Our results are consistent with prior evidence that found in a deprived population, structured, pharmacist-led pathways led to significant improvements in adherence and LDL-C attainment.10

However, a key finding of this study is the unequal distribution of engagement across socioeconomic groups. Only a small proportion of patients from the most deprived quintile attended the clinic, despite being at higher cardiovascular risk. This suggests that barriers to access persist, and that system-level interventions may not sufficiently address engagement factors. As such, while the pathway appears effective for those who engage, it does not yet achieve equitable reach for the entire local population.

Biological sex

It is well documented that there are sex-based differences in cardiovascular care; women are less likely to be treated to guideline targets following ASCVD events, resulting in higher mortality rates following a heart attack compared with men.16 17 National and international datasets have also found that women with established ASCVD are less likely to be prescribed LLT and when treated, are less likely to have cholesterol levels adequately treated.15 18 Disparities in medication prescription are multifactorial and are likely attributable to a combination of provider, patient and systemic biases.19 In this cohort, once patients entered the pharmacist-led pathway, there was similar LDL-C attainment irrespective of biological sex. While the study was not powered to demonstrate equivalence, these findings are encouraging and warrant further evaluation in larger cohorts. The higher proportion of male attendees likely reflects the higher burden of ASCVD among men.

Ethnicity

Ethnic disparities in cardiovascular risk and management in the UK remain. Individuals from South Asian populations have a higher prevalence of ASCVD and are less likely to receive consistent monitoring or achieve treatment targets in primary care.20 We found that a high proportion of attendees were from ethnically diverse backgrounds and LDL-C target attainment was similar across all subgroups. Although this study was not designed to assess cultural competence or patient engagement, the findings may reflect the structured and standardised nature of the pathway, but further work is needed to understand the factors that affect engagement across different communities. Although contemporary data show lower post-myocardial infarction mortality in South Asian groups, which may reflect increased awareness, they still remain at greater risk of developing ASCVD.

Further work is therefore required to explore extending such strategies to primary prevention. In addition, the intersection between ethnic background and socioeconomic deprivation likely compounds cardiovascular risk and warrants specific attention in future pathway design.

System level enablers

Importance of accurate coding and patient identification in the community

Accurate clinical coding and record keeping of demographic variables is essential for identifying patients that have suboptimal lipid management. Previous studies have shown that improved recording of this data enhances the identification of disadvantaged patients and may reduce the influence of clinician bias in treatment decisions.21 22

Automation in treatment pathways

Automation provides an opportunity to reduce clinician-dependent variation in care by ensuring timely identification and referral for patients who are not at guideline treatment targets. Prior studies that used interruptive electronic alerts to prompt the escalation of lipid therapy have shown limited success due to alert fatigue and increased clinician workload.23 Alternative models that embed automated referral pathways to other members of the multi-disciplinary team may be a more effective solution. Our findings are consistent with evidence from a randomised controlled trial in the USA that implemented a combined referral-based automation with a pharmacist-led intervention and found significant improvements in cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting that such approaches are a scalable solution to further reduce the gap in lipid management.24 Within the Donabedian framework, these system-level changes help to provide the infrastructure that facilitates the delivery of consistent, guideline-aligned care without over-reliance on individual clinician variation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Some patients were unable to be contacted by telephone and despite communication with general practitioners, follow-up lipid measurements were not always obtained. Follow-up duration was variable across patients and was not adjusted for in the primary analysis. The clinic was not configured within the electronic patient record system to capture data on patients who were eligible but did not attend, meaning the proportion of non-attenders by IMD quintile and ethnicity could not be reported. This is an important limitation of the equity analysis, as the conclusions are based on those who engaged with the pathway rather than the full eligible population. This study was also only at a single centre which may limit generalisability. We did not include a control group that was receiving usual care so we cannot directly compare outcomes with current care models, and the contribution of regression to the mean or temporal trends cannot be excluded. Given the sample size, subgroup analyses were likely underpowered. Finally, long-term adherence remains a challenge in secondary prevention, and selection bias could result in recruitment of a pre-existing motivated cohort of patients.

Future work

Future work should focus on addressing upstream barriers to access that currently limit the engagement with lipid pathways, particularly those from the most socioeconomically deprived communities. Extending the model into primary care and community-based settings may achieve greater reach among underrepresented individuals who are at the highest risk of ASCVD. In addition, it is important to assess this pathway in multiple centres and for a longer duration of time to determine the long-term sustainability and generalisability, and most importantly the impact on cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

By integrating automated high-risk patient identification with multidisciplinary pharmacist-led care, this study describes a pathway associated with improved lipid management in secondary prevention of ASCVD. Among those who attended the clinic, similar LDL-C target attainment was observed regardless of sex, ethnicity and socioeconomic background. In the context of the NIHR’s challenge to address cardiovascular health inequalities, this work provides an example of how system level interventions can reduce variation in care delivery. Further work is still required to address the upstream barriers to access and evaluate generalisability across other centres.
