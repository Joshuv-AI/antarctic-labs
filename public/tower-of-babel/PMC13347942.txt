Single-cell profiling of DNA methylation in autism spectrum disorder prefrontal cortex reveals distinct regulatory and aging signatures
Authors: Katherine W Eyring, Cuining Liu, Nasser Elhajjaoui, Kevin D Abuhanna, Yi Zhang, Zachary von Behren, Min Jen Tsai, Eleazar Eskin, Daniel H Geschwind, Chongyuan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition. Studies of postmortem ASD brain tissue have revealed convergent molecular changes across the cortex. Whether these features are reflected in cell-type-specific epigenetic signatures is unknown. Here, we present a single-cell analysis of DNA methylation (DNAm) coupled with transcriptomics in ASD. Using snmCT-seq, we profiled DNAm and transcript levels from over 60,000 nuclei derived from the prefrontal cortex of 49 donors. We identified over 30,000 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) in ASD that were enriched in promoters and cell-type-specific regulatory elements active across the lifespan. ASD-related methylation changes were uncorrelated with transcript levels and were small in magnitude compared with age-associated effects. Age-DMRs were concentrated in excitatory neurons and revealed distinct roles for CG and non-CG methylation. Age-varying methylation signatures of ASD identified neuron projection development as a key process perturbed in ASD, highlighting the heterogeneous impact of ASD across the lifespan.Keywords: neuroscience, neurodevelopment, autism, ASD, DNA methylation, single-cell genomics, multi-omics

Introduction

DNA methylation (DNAm), or the addition of a methyl group to the fifth carbon of cytosine (mC), is a ubiquitous epigenetic mark known to vary across tissues,1,2,3 age,4,5,6,7,8 and cell types.9,10,11 DNAm plays a critical role in cellular differentiation and the regulation of gene expression.12 Epigenetic regulation generally,13,14,15,16,17 and DNAm specifically, has also been implicated in the etiology of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Genetic variation in “writers” and “readers” of DNAm causes profound neurological syndromes. Variants in de novo methyltransferases (DNMTs), which establish and maintain DNAm,18,19,20,21 cause autosomal-dominant cerebellar ataxia, deafness, and narcolepsy (DNMT122) and Tatton-Brown-Rahman syndrome (TBRS; DNMT3A23,24), while Rett syndrome is caused by variation in methylcytosine binding protein 2 (MECP225). Autistic features are often observed in Rett syndrome and TBRS, and DNMT3A itself is a high-confidence ASD risk gene.17,26,27,28,29 Loss of DNMT3A or the introduction of patient mutations into mice impairs neuronal maturation and elicits behavioral phenotypes.30,31

Genomic context informs the function of mC. Most CpG (cytosine followed by guanine) sites in the adult human genome are methylated (>70%), while regulatory regions, like promoters and enhancers, are distinctly hypomethylated in a cell-type-specific fashion.9,11,32 In addition, methylation outside of CpG sites (hereafter referred to as mCH, where H = A, T, or C) accumulates early in life, specifically in neurons,1,7,33 implicating the developmental stage and cell types most relevant to NDDs.

A convergent transcriptomic34,35,36,37,38,39 and epigenomic profile40,41,42,43 is emerging from the study of postmortem bulk cortical tissue from autistic donors. Generally, these studies describe downregulation of synapse-related transcripts, upregulation of transcripts related to immune signaling, and loss of regional patterning. These observations have been refined by recent single-cell studies39,44 that identified compositional and cell-type-specific changes in the transcriptome that, together, contribute to what’s observed in bulk tissue. ASD molecular pathology has also been observed in regulatory microRNAs (miRNAs) and long-non-coding RNAs,37,43 genomic enhancers (implicated by H3K27Ac binding41), and CpG methylation detected at sites covered by the Infinium Human Methylation 450K BeadChip42,45,46 and in bulk whole-genome bisulfite sequencing.47 However, it is unknown whether previously described methylomic changes in bulk tissue reflect compositional changes,48 bona fide changes in DNAm in specific cell types, or a combination of both. In this study, we expand upon existing knowledge by profiling DNAm patterns in ASD with cell-type resolution. We interpret our results by integrating methylation and RNA data jointly generated from individual cells using the recently developed single-nucleus methylcytosine and transcriptome sequencing (snmCT-seq) method49 and in the context of published data on molecular phenotypes in autism. In addition, we leverage this unique dataset to define age- and cell-type-specific methylomic variation. To our knowledge, these data represent the first population-scale single-cell study to investigate ASD-associated changes in the DNA methylome.

Results

Single-cell multi-omic profiling of ASD and neurotypical prefrontal cortex

To investigate cell-type-specific methylation patterns in autism, we first isolated nuclei from the prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 9) of 26 idiopathic autistic donors and 23 non-autistic controls (Figure 1A, donor metadata in Table S1). Donors ranged in age, from 2 to 60 years, and age did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 1B, p = 0.65, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). The majority of our donors were under the age of 30, while 12 were over the age of 40. Due to the strong male bias in ASD prevalence, we profiled only male donors. Isolated nuclei were sorted, one per well, into 384-well plates based on the presence or absence of the neuronal marker NeuN (RBFOX3), maintaining an approximate 2:1 neuron-to-non-neuron ratio per donor. We then jointly assayed transcript levels and DNAm from individual cells using snmCT-seq49 for 236 plates.

We distinguished reads derived from genomic DNA and RNA based on the methylation levels in the non-CG (CH) context as previously described49 (RNA reads were engineered to have high methylation levels; see –“mCT processing and library preparation”). Data from each modality were preprocessed separately before integration (cell metadata in Table S2). Following methylome quality control (QC) checks (Figures S1A–S1E), we recovered data from 66,174 nuclei (median 1,347 per donor). Nuclei were sequenced to an average depth of 1.5 million reads, 83% of which derived from genomic DNA. We observed anticipated global methylation levels (Figures S1C–S1E) and recovered high-quality RNA libraries from 27,515 nuclei following QC filtering (Figures S1F–S1H; see “nuclei-level quality control”). The yield of nuclei per donor did not significantly differ between groups (Figure S1I, p = 0.82, Welch’s t test). We leveraged information from both modalities (genome-wide 100-kb bin methylation in CG and non-CG contexts and transcript levels) to define and annotate the clusters (Figures 1C and 1D). Donors were well represented in every cluster (Figure S2A), and clusters were readily annotated by projection into reference datasets (Figure S2B). Annotations were generally concordant across RNA and methylation modalities (Figures S2C and S2D), aligned with our NeuN staining and the known accumulation of mCH in neurons (Figures S2E and S2F), and reflected anticipated cortical cell types and canonical marker gene transcript levels and gene body hypomethylation (Figure S3).

We identified 28 clusters in our unsupervised integration of RNA and DNAm data that were annotated and aggregated into subclasses (hereafter referred to as cell types). Five clusters correspond to non-neuronal cell types: astrocytes (Astros), oligodendrocytes (Oligos), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), microglia (Micros), and one small cluster containing both endothelial (Endos) and vascular-associated cells/pericytes (or vascular leptomeningeal cells; VLMCs). We observed 10 inhibitory neuron clusters, including those originating from the medial and caudal ganglionic eminences in development (MGE and CGE, respectively) and included MGE-derived parvalbumin (PV)- and somatostatin (SST)-expressing cells and CGE-derived VIP (vasoactive intestinal peptide), SNCG, and LAMP5 subtypes. Twelve clusters corresponded to different subtypes of excitatory neurons. Two clusters were annotated as superficial-layer excitatory intra-telencephalic neurons (Exc L2/3 IT) and constituted the largest neuronal population (Figure 1E). Four clusters were aggregated into the L4/5 IT subclass, reflecting a gradient of laminar-specific transcript levels (Figure S3). A number of smaller, yet distinct, clusters were annotated as different deep-layer excitatory subtypes: L6 IT, L6 corticothalamic-projecting (CT), L6b, and L5/6 near-projecting (NP). To analyze methylomes under this unified annotation schema, including cells for which high-quality RNA data were not available, we trained a weighted nearest-neighbor classifier to assign cell-type annotations based on the methylome alone (–see “modality integration and label transfer”; hold-out accuracy ≥97.4%).

Transcriptomic signatures of ASD

We first assessed transcriptomic differences between our control and autistic donors. Between groups, donors did not differ in sample quality metrics (Figure S4) or cell-type abundance (Figure S5). We assessed cell-type abundance using two different approaches: one based on our subclass and cluster labels and the other using a label-free nearest-neighbor-based method.50 We did not observe any significant changes to subclass or cluster abundance after false discovery rate (FDR) correction nor any consistent changes across methods.

We next assessed differential gene expression (DGE) between neurotypical control (NT-CTL) and ASD samples in pseudobulk (generated by summing gene counts from the same cell type and donor) using DESeq2 (Figure S6; Table S3). As has been reported previously,39,44 we detected the largest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in excitatory L2/3 IT neurons (608 DEGs, padj < 0.05). Of note, two clusters (Figure S6A) comprised the L2/3 IT subclass and differed in RNA levels of genes known to distinguish L2 and L3 neurons, including PDGFD and LINC01500 (L2) and COL5A2 (L351; Figure S6B). When we performed DGE analysis at the cluster level, DEGs were concentrated specifically in the putative L2 cluster (Figures S6C and S6D). The increased number of DEGs in putative L2 neurons compared with L3 neurons was robust to downsampling analysis (Figure S6E). We observed a significant correlation between transcriptomic ASD effects in our data and those published in a previous single-cell study of transcriptional changes in ASD44 (Figures S6F and S6G), supporting the robustness of our data. DEGs were enriched in 129 functional pathways (gene set enrichment analysis, padj < 0.1; Table S4), 81% of which were observed in Exc L2/3 neurons. Many of the top pathways suggested that upregulated transcripts converged on the cellular response to stress, including the unfolded protein response pathway (Figure S6H), as has been observed previously.39

Strategies to identify ASD-associated DNAm signatures

Genetic variants such as single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the genome can have direct effects on the estimation of DNAm in whole-genome bisulfite data when they overlap with a cytosine (position 0) or the base downstream of a cytosine (position 1). For example, a C>T SNV may be interpreted as a complete loss of cytosine methylation. In addition, methylation strongly varies by +1 sequence context (Figures S1C–S1E). To control for high-impact donor genetic variation on the methylome profiling, we performed whole-genome sequencing (40.9× ± 10.3×; mean ± SD coverage) and genotyping to identify and mask high-impact variants in our methylome data. In this “genotype-masked” data, we then performed differential methylation analysis in pseudobulk data (aggregating by donor and cell type).

Human brain development is associated with DNAm dynamics at different scales, ranging from individual differentially methylated cytosines (base resolution) to megabase-scale methylation valleys.7,8,52 We investigated cell-type-specific differential methylation in ASD using two complementary methods, each tuned to detect different genomic features. First, we called de novo differentially methylated regions (DMRs) using DSS,53,54,55 a sensitive two-group DMR caller that exploits correlation between adjacent CG-context cytosines. In the second approach, we analyzed predefined features, including genes, promoters, and 500-bp bins tiling the genome. While the former provides single cytosine resolution, the latter offers higher coverage due to the region binning (Figures S7A and S7B), more flexible covariate adjustment structures, and testing of non-CG methylation levels. Henceforth, de novo regions identified by DSS will be referred to as DMRs, whereas predefined regions will be referred to as differentially methylated bins, promoters, or genes (DMBs, DMPs, or DMGs, respectively). We focused on the eight most abundant subclasses in our methylation analyses (Figure 1E), further aggregating some subclasses (for example, combining different MGE-derived interneurons) to increase our coverage (achieving up to 10× pseudobulk coverage in our most abundant cell types).

We began by characterizing the largest sources of overall methylome variance in our dataset (Figures 1F and 1G). As expected, cell type explained 80%–99% of the global variance across combined pseudobulk methylomes (Figures S7C and S7D). Within each cell type, age explained more variance than any other known donor feature (Figure 1G; Figure S7E), consistent with past reports.4 We then examined differential methylation by diagnosis and age, in addition to an exploratory analysis of age-dependent ASD effects.

ASD-associated DMRs overlap with cell-type-specific regulatory elements

The level of CG methylation at promoters and enhancers is a reliable indicator of regulatory activity; the depletion and enrichment of CG methylation are correlated with active and inactive states, respectively.32 Consistent with past reports,42,47 we did not find significant differences in global methylation levels between diagnostic groups (p > 0.15, beta regression, Wald test). We began by investigating predefined features (promoters and genes) whose methylation levels varied by diagnosis but identified few (244 genes or promoters total, 1–27 per cell type; Table S5). Testing in predefined regions may be unable to detect differential methylation that, for example, overlaps only one portion of a gene or a short region. We thus also identified pan-age de novo CG DMRs associated with ASD in a genome-wide and cell-type-informed fashion, using a rigorous computational strategy including permutation of the diagnosis label (see –“de novo DMR calling with DSS”; Figure 2A). We identified 31,165 DMRs that were more often hypomethylated in ASD than hypermethylated (Figure 2B; Table S6). DMRs ranged in size (median ±SD = 132 ± 185 bp, examples in Figures S8 and S9) and were very cell-type specific (<2% of DMRs overlap across cell types). The number of DMRs detected per cell type was not a function of the number of cells per cluster, which ranged from 2,548 to 13,887 (Figure 2B). Upon hierarchical clustering of ASD-DMRs, most DMRs cluster by cell type. In excitatory neurons, DMRs further segregate by age, consistent with methylation patterns associated with cell-type and age effects being much larger than those that vary with diagnosis (Figure S9).

We next assessed whether specific transcription factor (TF) binding motifs were overrepresented in our ASD-DMRs using HOMER56 (Table S7). TGA∗TCA motifs, characteristic of Fos/AP1-family TFs (which include Fos, ATF3, and Jun), were notably enriched in two ASD-DMR sets: those hypomethylated in MGE-derived interneurons and regions hypermethylated in Exc L2/3 IT neurons (Figure 2C). This enrichment is particularly interesting given that Fos-family TF binding is altered in ASD, based on TF footprinting analysis from bulk57 and single-cell data.39

We further validated this TF motif analysis by assessing ASD-DMRs that overlap with chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) peaks58,59 and observed concordant enrichment patterns (Figure S10A). For example, we found that hypo-ASD-DMRs in MGE interneurons significantly overlapped JUN peaks, suggesting that the DMRs overlap not only the known JUN motif sequence but also empirically identified JUN binding sites.

In addition, we used the SCENIC framework60 to identify TF-RNA networks (regulons) present in our paired transcriptomic data (Table S8). We tested for between-diagnosis differences in regulon levels, as measured by the transcript levels of the putative downstream gene targets of TF (see “SCENIC TF-RNA (regulon) analyses”; Figure S10B). Only one regulon was differentially associated with diagnosis following multiple testing correction; however, in a systematic comparison to a similar case-control regulon analysis,39 we observed concordant effect direction estimates in most regulons, including those for TFs implicated by HOMER motif analysis. For example, both studies show ASD-associated increases in JUND and JDP2 regulons in neurons and decreases in the TCF12 regulon in oligodendrocytes (Figures S10B and S10C). Beyond the TFs implicated by ASD-DMRs in motif analyses, we reproduced the directionality of previously reported ASD regulons (e.g., decreased CUX1 regulon levels in Exc L2/3 neurons; Figures S10D and S10E).

Consistent with the known distribution of genomic features, most DMRs fall into intergenic and intronic regions, though ASD-DMRs overlap promoters twice as often as expected by chance (5.3% promoters in the ASD-DMR set compared with 2.2% promoters in the genomic background—significant in all but two DMR sets [OPC_HYPER and MICRO_HYPO], Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.004). This promoter enrichment was evident relative to a background of all candidate DMRs and in a smaller background set matching the GC content of our ASD-DMRs (Figure 2D) and is supported by chromHMM genomic annotation analysis61,62 and a previous report47 (Figure S11A). To test whether the observed promoter enrichment of ASD-DMRs might reflect a bias inherent to DSS’s use of the correlation between nearby CG sites, we contrasted the genomic feature enrichment of ASD-DMRs with alternative sets of DMRs called between cell types or age groups using the same dataset and de novo DMR calling methodology. Promoter enrichment was broadly observed in age (but not cell type) DMR sets, but it was most pronounced in the ASD control comparison set (Figure S11B).

We next asked whether our ASD-DMRs overlapped known regulatory regions in the brain by assessing enrichment in five sets of previously published regions: H3K27Ac peaks called in prenatal brain,63 accessible chromatin in prenatal brain,64 H3K27Ac peaks and high-confidence enhancers in adult cortex,63 and a reference dataset of “trajectory DMRs” that are dynamic in their methylation levels over pre- and post-natal development65 (Figures 2E–2G). We observed significant enrichment of ASD-DMRs with H3K27Ac-bound regions in the prenatal brain (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.02, excluding OLIGO_HYPO; Figure 2E) and in fetal brain enhancers (Figure S11A). This enrichment was not specific to ASD-DMRs, however. DMRs that distinguish cell types were also strongly enriched in prenatal enhancers and open chromatin (Figure S11C), consistent with methylation patterns generally reflecting activity in the developing brain. Our cell-type DMRs significantly overlapped those previously identified65 (Figure S11D) using different donors and methods, reinforcing the validity of our data and approach. Hypermethylated ASD-DMRs in OPCs also overlapped adult brain enhancers (Figure 2E).

We observed significant, and largely cell-type-specific, overlap between neuronal ASD-DMRs (particularly those in excitatory neurons) with trajectory DMRs that are variable between the second and the third trimesters and in post-natal development (Figure S12A). The overlap of ASD-DMRs with fetal brain enhancers (Figure 2E) and regions dynamically methylated during prenatal development (Figure S12A) is consistent with recent reports of prenatal regulatory regions that are associated with ASD risk genes.4 In sum, ASD-DMRs implicate regulatory regions active during prenatal and post-natal development and exhibit significant cell-type specificity.

We then extended our analysis to cell-type-informed, candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) identified in an independent single-cell ATAC-seq (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing) analysis of the adult brain66 (Figure 2F). We found that ASD-DMRs significantly overlapped cCREs in their corresponding cell types (36% of ASD-DMRs; 11,271/31,165 were in cCREs). Hypermethylated ASD-DMRs in Exc L2/3 IT neurons significantly overlapped cCREs observed in Exc IT neurons, while DMRs identified in MGE- and CGE-derived interneurons and OPCs also significantly overlapped cell-type-aligned cCREs (Figure 2F). ASD-DMRs also significantly overlapped regions that are uniquely hypomethylated in their corresponding cell types,65 as may be expected from cell-type-specific regulatory regions (Figure 2G).

To develop our understanding of the functional implications of differential methylation in ASD, we next sought to connect our DMRs to genes (hereafter, mGenes). We annotated DMRs based on their overlap with genes and promoters as well as using cCRE-based annotations66 (Table S6). We evaluated when mGenes are expressed throughout the lifespan using bulk transcriptomic cortical data from BrainSpan.67 The union of mGenes across cell types showed indistinguishable transcript levels across pre- and post-natal periods (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.19). After stratifying mGenes to cell types, we found that, in every cell type, transcript levels of mGenes were significantly more likely to vary with age relative to non-mGenes in the BrainSpan background (Figure S12B). For the majority of cell types, mGenes exhibited the highest RNA levels in prenatal as opposed to post-natal tissue (Figure S12C). We then investigated common trajectories of mGene transcript levels (Figures S12C and S12D) across development periods, which included seven prenatal stages and eight post-natal stages.67 The three most common trajectories observed were marked by higher RNA levels during prenatal periods, but we did not observe a peak of mGene transcript levels in any particular stage of development (Figures S12E and S12F). Overall, we find that mGenes are highly and dynamically expressed in prenatal brain development; however, their expression is not limited to the prenatal period.

Next, we explored the relationships between our ASD-DMRs and published knowledge of the molecular pathology of ASD. First, we assessed whether ASD-DMRs overlap putative enhancers that are dysregulated in ASD41 and observed strong overlap between DMRs hypermethylated in microglia, which are associated with genes involved in morphological and immune signaling (Figure S13A), and overlapped downregulated regions identified in H3K27Ac profiling of ASD cortex (Figure S13B).

We then considered how the ASD methylome signature that we’ve identified relates to known transcriptomic changes in ASD. We quantified the overlap between DEGs in this dataset and those implicated by DMRs (mGenes) in the corresponding cell types using Fisher’s exact test. We did not observe any significant overlap of DEGs and mGenes. We repeated this analysis using methylomic data from this study and transcriptomic data from a larger study,39 but did not observe cell-type-aligned changes. In a complementary analysis, we correlated ASD effect sizes from analysis of RNA and DNA methylation levels in genes and promoters. We observed a small, but significant, negative correlation between ASD effect sizes in transcript levels and gene body mCA in Exc L2/3 IT neurons (Pearson’s correlation r = −0.08, p = 4.1E−7; Figure S13C). A similar trend was observed for gene body mCA in Exc L4/5 IT neurons (coefficient = −0.048, p = 5.7E−3).

Next, we assessed whether our cell-type-resolved ASD-DMRs overlapped differentially methylated sites and regions identified in previous (bulk) analysis from ASD cortical tissue.42,47 We found limited overlap between different bulk ASD methylome analyses (Figure S13D). While the absolute overlap between published ASD-DMRs and those identified in this study was small (<3% of previously reported ASD-DMRs were detected in our dataset), it was more than expected by chance (Figure S13E). For example, DMRs hypermethylated in bulk dup15q cortex42 overlapped hypermethylated ASD-DMRs identified in this study (Figure S13E) as well as cell-type DMRs (Figure S13F). When considering genes implicated by each set of ASD-DMRs, we similarly found limited overlap between studies (Figures S13D and S13H; cross-study Jaccard index 0.00056 to 0.058).

To visually display the limited correlation between DNAm and transcript levels, we depict gene body mCA, promoter mCG, and the transcript levels of four top DEGs (selected from the top 20 ASD DEGs, ranked by p value). We represent transcript levels measured in our study and those from a larger study39 with highly overlapping donors (Figure S14).

As an exploratory analysis to identify distal methylation-RNA associations, we used a semi-supervised network approach (i.e., donor-diagnosis-aware)68 to identify modules with features that both discriminated between ASD and CTL and were correlated across modalities (Table S9). We detected many modules containing high-confidence autism risk genes (SFARI syndromic and gene score >1) for future hypothesis generation. For example, we detected an L2/3 IT subnetwork with significant enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms for Wnt signaling and brain development that contained the SFARI risk gene CHD8 as well as FOXK2 and CDK5RAP1 as hub genes. These genes showed higher RNA levels in ASD cases and were, in turn, correlated with five ASD-DMRs that were not spatially adjacent to the genes. This suggests the potential for methylation and RNA to be associated in spatially distal and multivariate regulatory networks, despite limited correlation of conventional pairwise correlation analyses of genic and promoter methylation.

Age-associated remodeling of the DNA methylome—De novo DMR discovery

A particular strength of this dataset is the broad distribution of ages represented by our donors (2–60 years old), which allowed assessment of cell-type-resolved methylation signatures of aging across the lifespan. Age explained the largest proportion of methylome variation within almost all cell types, in genic and intergenic features, and in CpG- and CpA-cytosine contexts (Figure 1G; Figure S7E). Global methylation and its accumulation over age varied by cell type; mCG/CG significantly increased with age in excitatory neurons (beta regression likelihood ratio test: p < 5E−4 in L2/3 IT and L4/5 IT on age, p = 0.05–0.60 in other cell types; Figure 3A). There was no significant difference in either CpG or CpA global methylation levels nor the rate of global methylation accumulation in ASD cases after multiple test correction (diagnosis and age-by-diagnosis interaction term; lowest unadjusted p = 0.03 in Inh MGE; Figures S15A and S15B). Cell-type composition also varied with age (Figure S15C). The abundance of OPCs decreased, while the relative abundance of oligodendrocytes increased with age, as has been observed in mouse69 and human.70 The abundance of inhibitory neurons decreased with age, as has been reported previously,71 with a significant decrease in the abundance of CGE-derived neurons specifically in autistic donors relative to controls (Figure S15C; significant age ∗ diagnosis interaction term).

We went on to identify just over 1.4 million de novo CpG DMRs associated with age (age-DMRs; Table S10) in a series of pairwise comparisons between young donors (2–16 years), young adults (17–30 years), and adult donors (40–60 years; –see “de novo DMR calling with DSS”). Age-DMRs generally accumulated methylation with age and spanned 625,306 non-overlapping regions (10.3% of genome; Figure 3B). Age effects were most pronounced in variance-explained (Figure 1G; Figure S7E) and sheer number of DMRs (Figure 3B) in excitatory neurons, followed by non-neuronal cells and then inhibitory neurons (Figure 3B). The number of age-associated features detected per cell type was uncorrelated to cluster size (Spearman’s ρ ≤ 0.18), mirrored in number of age-DEGs in our paired transcriptome data, and was consistent with previous reports71 (Figure S16; Tables S11 and S12). In most cell types, an order of magnitude more age-DMRs were detected when comparing young with young adult donors than when comparing young adult with adult donors, suggesting significant remodeling before adulthood; in contrast, inhibitory neurons and microglia had more mCpG changes later in life (Figure 3C).

TF motif enrichment was abundant in age-DMRs (Figure 3D; Table S13) and was often shared among multiple subclasses. We noted a striking loss of methylation with age in AP-1 TF complex (FOS and JUN) motifs and an accumulation of methylation in motifs associated with TFs involved in neurodevelopment, including neurogenin/atonal TFs (e.g., NEUROD1) and the RFX TF family.72 Almost all cell types exhibited significant hypomethylation at CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) binding motifs with advancing age. We additionally reproduced cell-type-specific aging signatures, including age accumulation of methylation over EGR/EGF motifs in excitatory neurons71 and accumulation in Ets-family motifs (including PU.1 and ELK) in microglia.70 Binding for 16 of 18 TFs presented in Figure 3D (all but NF-E2 and the interferon-regulatory class of TFs) has been previously reported to be sensitive to methylation levels.73,74,75 Methylation of ZNF and BTB/POZ family motifs was positively associated with binding affinity, while other TF families were negatively associated.

We observed significant overlap of age-DMRs with external reference ChIP-seq datasets, including the broad pan-cell-type enrichment in CTCF motifs, oligodendrocyte-specific TFE3, and neuronal enrichments in FOSL and neurogenins (Figure S17A). The motif analysis was further supported by TF-RNA regulon networks observed within our paired transcriptomic data: we detected 920 regulon-by-cell-type combinations significantly changing with age. We detected age-varying regulons containing TFs implicated by motif enrichment in age-DMRs that exhibited the expected inverse association between RNA and motif methylation levels (e.g., MEF2C, RORB, NEUROD2, and EGR in excitatory neurons). We also noted significant changes in TFs previously associated with aging (Figure S17D), such as increased regulon levels of well-known aging genes NNT and FOXO3, which are associated with senescence and cell stress.76

Last, we assessed the overlap between our age-DMRs and the well-established “epigenetic clocks,” which use array-based measurements of cytosine methylation to predict chronological age. Although the clocks have shown high accuracy across multiple bulk tissues and cohorts, there has been limited assessment70 on brain cell types from single-nucleus data. We considered the canonical “Horvath” clock,6 trained on pan-tissue data, and a “cortical clock” trained specifically on the human prefrontal cortex.77 Clock performance was highly cell-type dependent, with high performance of Exc L2/3 in both clocks and poor performance of MGE-derived inhibitory neurons and microglia in both clocks (Figure 3E; Figure S18). Additional analyses (Figure S19) suggested that this differential performance by cell type may be attributable to true biological differences in epigenetic aging between cell types rather than technical factors like sequencing depth. There were large differences in the degree of overlap between clock probes and age-DMRs (range of 0%–35% of probes overlapped by cell type; Figure 3F) as well as between clock probes and cell-type DMRs (0.51%–49.4% of probes overlapped; Figure S19). For example, Inh MGE had among the lowest-percentage overlaps and remained one of the least age-predictive cell types even when subsampling all pseudobulk data to the same depth, whereas higher-performance cell types, such as Exc L2/3, approached the performance of a high-coverage “pseudotissue,” constructed by summing counts across all cell types for a given donor (Figure S19). Cell-type-specific performance was also not clearly attributable to differences in cell-type abundance and, by extension, pseudobulk coverage: MGE and microglia were outperformed by OPCs, a cell type with similar or lower cell abundances in our dataset. Last, we found no evidence of differences in predicted epigenetic age between ASD cases and neurotypical controls in any cell type (Figure S18), consistent with a previous bulk array study.42

Age-associated remodeling of DNA methylome—Predefined features

We extended our analysis of age-related changes to the DNA methylome by quantifying changes in predefined features (genes, promoters, and 500-bp chromosomal bins; available on GEO), which allowed us to model age as a continuous numerical variable and study cytosines at non-CpG dinucleotides. We focused on analysis of CpA-sequence contexts due to its relatively high prevalence and correlation with other non-CH motifs.

We found that the trajectory of global mCA accumulation did not vary by diagnosis (Figure S15B) but did vary significantly by cell type; mCA increased with age in excitatory neurons, astrocytes, and MGE-derived interneurons (Figure 4A). In excitatory neurons, specifically L4/5 neurons, mCA accumulation plateaued by 25 years of age (Figure 4A; Figure S16). This pattern was supported by analysis of age-associated differentially methylated 500-bp bins (DMBs; Figure 4B). We examined common methylation patterns over age in bins and found that ≥75% of CA-DMBs in Exc L2/3 neurons plateaued to mCA/CA >0.60 by age 25 (Figure S20). By contrast, inhibitory neurons derived from MGE or CGE only modestly gained mCA in the age range sampled in this study, although rapid gain of mCA has been reported earlier in development.65

The global accumulation of mCA with age was particularly dramatic in Exc L4/5 IT neurons, wherein 89% of bins tested (500-bp windows with between-donor inter-quartile range [IQR] >0.05) significantly gained methylation with age. In a complementary analysis, we included each pseudobulk’s global methylation fraction as a covariate in our regression models and reidentified chromosomal bins associated with age. We found that the majority of DMBs varied in tandem with the global mC level. In excitatory neurons only 5.5% (L2/3) and 0.4% (L4/5) of DMBs remained significantly associated with age after adjusting for global methylation. That is, most age-varying CA effects do not deviate from this genome-wide plateau pattern.

We then examined enrichments between genomic annotations and age-DMBs, first assessing transposable elements, which are theorized both to be a destabilizing element in aging78 and to be regulated by CH methylation.79 In excitatory neurons, mCA accumulated with age in transposable elements, including long terminal repeats (LTRs) and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs/Alu), while mCG accumulated in long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) with age (Figure S20). We then examined overlap of age-DMBs with chromatin states. In excitatory neurons, regions gaining mCA (and mCG) with age significantly overlapped regions of inactive chromatin, marked by polycomb repression and heterochromatin; whereas regions losing mCG with age were more likely to overlap active regions such as transcriptional start sites (Figure 4C). However, this finding should be contextualized by the relative paucity of mCA loss over age (Figures 4A and 4B).

Within a given cell type, CpA and CpG methylation were more correlated within genes and promoters (median Spearman’s ρ = 0.17, up to maximum of 0.57 between excitatory neurons; Figure 4D) than in chromosomal bins (ρ = 0.07, maximum 0.48). Age-associated changes in gene body mCG were often shared across cell types (Figure 4D). In multiple cell types, genes losing mCG and/or mCA with age were enriched for pathways (gene set enrichment analysis [GSEA], FDR <0.05; Table S14) related to the cellular response to stress (e.g., DNA damage response or cellular response to stress), oxidative phosphorylation, chromatin remodeling, and synapse organization.

To further interpret age-associated changes, we used a semi-supervised (i.e., donor-age-aware) sparse canonical correlation network approach68 to identify small networks whose features were correlated with age and across modalities (age-mC-RNA networks; Table S15). In the lead module in Exc L4/5 neurons (Figure 4E), we observed significant enrichment in genes related to glutamatergic synapses, dendritic spine development, and axonogenesis that decrease in transcript levels with age while accumulating methylation within the gene body (e.g., GRM3, MCTP1,and RELN). This module also includes TFs active in development (e.g., MEF2C, CELF2, and CUX2) that exhibit high inverse correlations between methylation and RNA over age (Figure 4F).

Age-dependent ASD effects on the methylome

We next leveraged the broad age range of donors in this dataset to identify diagnosis effects that vary with age, in contrast to pan-age ASD effects (examples in Figure 5A; Figures S21A and S21B). We first turned to modeling predefined features (promoters, genes, and 500-bp bins) to be able to formally model and test for age-by-diagnosis interaction effects (Table S16). In total, 1,644 genes were associated with age-diagnosis interactions. As an example, the promoter of STXBP1, a gene that has been confidently linked to ASD and Ohtahara syndrome,80,81 is hypermethylated specifically in Exc L2/3 neurons in young autistic donors (Figure 5A).

We detected similar numbers of age-dependent ASD effects in each cell type (Figure S21C), but the timing of effects varied between cell types: the largest mean differences observed in neuronal cell types and astrocytes were concentrated in young donors (Figure 5B; Figure S21D). Age-dependent ASD-DMBs were enriched in intergenic regions and quiescent or chromatin-repressed regions (Figures S21E and S21F). We considered whether age-dependent diagnosis effects were reflected at the transcriptional level but did not detect any significant age-dependent diagnosis effects in this dataset or in RNA data from a larger single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) study39 (Figure S22).

Given the extensive effects of age on the DNA methylome and the detection of age-by-diagnosis interactions in predefined features, we also repeated the de novo ASD-DMR discovery procedure (including rigorous permutation of case-control labels; Figure 2A) within three age-stratified subsamples of the cohort (Figure 1B; age strata: young 2–16, middle 17–30, and older 40–60 years of age) to control for age and potentially detect age-dependent diagnosis effects (Figures S23A and S23B; Table S17). We confirmed that age-stratified diagnosis DMRs are not more likely to be caused by genetic variation than all-donor DMRs (Figure S23C). In each age stratum, we again observed enrichment in promoters (Figure S11B) and enrichment of the AP-1/Fos TF family motifs in hypomethylated DMRs in MGE-derived interneurons (Figure 5C; Figure S23D). However, we also detected additional stratum-specific motifs, for example, ETS-like TF motifs that were specifically enriched in hypermethylated DMRs in microglia of older adult donors (Figure 5C; Table S18). GO enrichment analysis on promoters implicated by age-stratified diagnosis DMRs exhibited stronger enrichment than when called in all-donor DMRs (Table S19). As an example, promoter DMRs that were hypermethylated in ASD in Exc L4/5 IT neurons across all donors showed no significant GO enrichment, while those identified in young ASD donors were enriched in terms related to neuron projection development, dendrite morphogenesis, and cell differentiation (Figure 5D). Stronger GO enrichment in age-stratified diagnosis DMRs relative to those identified in unstratified comparisons suggests a heterogeneous methylation signature of ASD across age. This analysis highlights the potential of age-specific functional pathways impacted by ASD.

Discussion

Identifying molecular phenotypes in ASD is essential for understanding the underlying biology and developing therapeutics. Here, we provide a comprehensive analysis of cell-type-resolved DNAm in idiopathic ASD, profiling methylation and transcripts in cortical cells from 49 donors across the lifespan. We report moderate differences associated with diagnosis that do not explain differential transcription but do implicate altered methylation of gene regulatory sites, including fetal brain enhancers and cell-type-specific candidate cis-regulatory elements in the adult brain. We describe profound changes to the methylome associated with age and highlight age-dependent analysis of epigenetic changes as a promising avenue for future investigation in ASD research. The concordance between age-associated changes we report and those previously published supports the validity of our analytic framework.

The potential value of using a single-cell approach to identify DMRs in ASD is underscored by the cell-type specificity of DNAm10,11 and increased numbers of DMRs identified here compared with previous efforts using analysis of bulk DNA, which identified hundreds of DMRs in the prefrontal cortex of autistic donors.42,47 ASD-DMRs were generally hypomethylated, consistent with loss-of-function DNMT3A mutations observed in patients.30 We observed significant overlaps between ASD-DMRs and gene regulatory regions identified in a series of independent studies.63,65,66 ASD-DMRs, across cell types, overlapped promoters more than expected by chance, even after accounting for potential technical bias from CG content. This observation is also consistent with promoter enrichment observed in DMRs identified in a bulk, whole-genome bisulfite analysis of ASD cortical tissue.47 All DMR sets called in this study significantly overlapped fetal brain enhancers,63 reinforcing the idea that much of the DNAm landscape is established before 2 years of age.7

Both mCG and mCH can inhibit gene expression,33,82 though the correlation between DNAm levels of regulatory elements and gene expression varies significantly across cell types.7 Gene and promoter methylation levels were not correlated with DGE in ASD, and most genes accumulated methylation with age independent of transcript levels. Strong anti-correlation between gene body/promoter methylation levels and transcript levels is pronounced in cell-type markers but not observed in the majority of genes.49 Thus, the limited overlap between DMRs and DEGs in ASD, here, is not surprising.

We considered whether gene methylation levels might reflect historical gene expression by assessing RNA levels of genes implicated by de novo DMRs in prenatal and post-natal samples. Many ASD-DMR-associated genes were expressed at their highest levels prenatally, although we did not identify enrichment in any particular period of prenatal development. De novo ASD-DMRs overlapped candidate cis-regulatory regions, defined by accessible chromatin, in their corresponding adult cell types as well as fetal brain enhancers. Taken together, our results suggest that differential DNAm impacts regulatory sites that are active in both the fetal and the adult brain and that ASD may impact cell-type-specific gene regulatory programs.

Activity-dependent immediate-early genes, like Fos, have been implicated in ASD previously.39,57 This is further supported by differential methylation of Fos family motifs observed in ASD in this dataset. The majority of the TF families implicated by ASD-DMRs in our analysis (all but NF-E2) have been reported to be sensitive to CpG-methylation levels. In cell line collections like ENCODE, decreased TF binding was associated with increased methylation levels in SELEX assays75 and/or TF binding levels were better predicted from methylation levels than sequence motifs alone.73,74 The sensitivity of these TFs to methylation supports the functional relevance of ASD-DMRs. The causal directionality is unknown, however, and more data are needed on motif methylation sensitivity specifically in primary brain tissue and within cell types.

Overlap of ASD-DMRs with regions previously implicated in ASD was limited to hypermethylated DMRs in microglia. These DMRs functionally implicated pathways related to morphology and immune signaling and overlapped downregulated H3K27Ac sites in bulk ASD cortex.41 Differential methylation in microglia in ASD has been suggested previously40,47 and supports microglia being one of the most impacted cell types in ASD.39 Moreover, in our exploratory analysis of age-dependent diagnosis effects, age-dependent diagnosis effects were observed in microglia, which could be consistent with an age-dependent microglial ASD signature, which has been suggested previously.37

In addition, we leveraged multimodal single-cell data to perform differential abundance and transcriptional analyses in well-defined clusters. Previous work has reported an increased abundance of gray-matter astrocytes and reactive microglia in ASD samples.39,44 We hypothesize that we were unable to detect these changes due to the limited number of our non-neuronal cells in this dataset, within which we could not discern different subtypes (e.g., gray-matter- vs. white-matter-resident astrocytes or a subset of reactive microglia). While multiple studies have implicated superficial-layer excitatory neurons as a nexus of ASD transcriptional dysregulation,15,39,44 we extend this work by our data showing that L2 neurons specifically exhibit the greatest transcriptional changes.

We expand upon prior studies of DNAm changes throughout adulthood by profiling donors under the age of 2070,71 and considering non-CpG methylation. Excitatory neurons show that rapid methylation increases that plateau by ∼25. We report that Exc L2/3 and L4/5 neurons exhibit a rapid increase and then plateau before age 25 in methylation levels within both CpG- and CpA-chromosomal bins. This observation reinforces previous reports of the accumulation of mCH at this approximate age in bulk cortical tissue7 and shows that the phenomenon may be driven by DNAm specifically in excitatory neurons. Consistent with past reports,71 we find that excitatory neurons uniquely accumulate DNAm in repressed genomic regions. Age-associated increases in methylation may compensate for heterochromatin that is lost with age78 and protect against age-associated “epigenetic erosion.”83,84 Almost all cell types exhibited significant hypomethylation at CTCF binding motifs, which complements mounting evidence of changes to 3D chromatin conformation during neuronal maturation, aging, and Alzheimer’s disease.65,70,85,86 Last, we assessed the performance of epigenetic age clocks across different cell types. We found that clock performance varied widely by cell type, including poor performance of these clocks in inhibitory neurons, as reported by others.70 Our findings suggest that there are opportunities to develop cell-type-specific age clocks using single-nucleus data. Further, variable clock performance supports the highly cell-type-specific nature of methylome-age associations.

Limitations of the study

Our study has some important limitations. First, a substantial portion of nuclei did not pass transcriptomic QC. This dropout was particularly pronounced in glial cells, which have lower RNA content than neurons.44 While we are confident in the quality of our RNA data, which reflect expected cell type, age, and diagnosis transcriptional patterns, our dataset is limited in size, broadly limiting our power to detect changes and specifically limiting our ability to profile non-neuronal cell types and age-diagnosis interactions in older donors. Second, our stringent filtering criteria for selecting predefined features for differential methylation testing excluded many expressed genes from analysis. The focus on variable regions may bias our predefined region analysis toward regions of the methylome under less regulatory constraint (e.g., regions overlapping non-expressed genes or genome quiescent regions) and result in underestimates of methylome-transcriptome pairing, although de novo DMR discovery was not limited by these inclusion criteria. As with all studies of human postmortem tissue, we are able to capture only a snapshot of the molecular landscape, measured at time of death. Many factors, including medications, diet, and cause of death, may influence our measurements and be influenced by diagnosis—we have limited metadata to be able to resolve these confounders.

Our ASD-DMRs did not cover enough of the genome to be able to robustly partition heritability, leaving open the question of whether regions that impart genetic risk for ASD are differentially methylated. The wide age range of our donors is a particular strength of our dataset, though a larger sample size is needed for robust age-dependent diagnosis analysis. Last, we have studied DNAm using bisulfite conversion, which is unable to resolve differences between methylcytosine and hydroxymethylation cytosine. The latter is known to be highly dynamic with age87,88 and can positively correlate with transcript levels, complicating the functional interpretations of our DMRs.

Our data indicate that changes to the DNA methylome, within and beyond CG sites, are moderate in idiopathic ASD and independent of transcriptional changes, consistent with previous work in bulk tissue suggesting that DNAm profiles in ASD were distinct from those identified in other modalities.40 There is a large degree of heterogeneity in the causes, comorbidities, and severity of idiopathic ASD that may limit the ability to identify reproducible differentially expressed and methylated genes. A more dramatic methylomic signature may be detectable in individuals with syndromic forms of ASD (e.g., Rett syndrome) or rare variation in DNMT3A, as has been suggested in bulk analysis.42,47 Our results also provide insight into cell-type-specific changes in brain that accompany aging as well as potential interactions between aging and diagnosis, both of which are exciting areas of future investigation.

Nuclei isolation from human brain tissue

Small pieces (<50 mg) of tissue were ground in liquid nitrogen with pre-chilled mortar and pestle to generate a homogenous sample and stored at -80°C until use. On the first day of processing, ground tissue was resuspended in nuclei isolation buffer and dounced to break up tissue. Nuclei were then isolated using an iodixanol gradient, counted and stained (using a fluorescent NeuN antibody) prior to being sorted into individual wells of a 384-well plate for processing.

Method details

mCT processing and library preparation

All solutions were prepared in a laminar flow cabinet, and solution distribution and mixing were performed using Beckman Biomek i7 liquid handler.

sn-mCT-seq processing and library preparation were performed as previously described49. A detailed bench protocol is shared through protocol.io (https://www.protocols.io/view/snmcat-v2-x54v9jby1g3e/v2); the in vitro GpC methyltransferase treatment step in the snmCAT-seq protocol was omitted in this study. The following text refers to specific sections in the snmCAT protocol.

Nuclei were kept on ice until sorting (Step 10) into pre-cooled 384-well PCR plates (ThermoFisher 4483285) containing reverse transcription reaction reagents (1uL per well, prepared in Step 9). We prepared cDNA amplification mix as in Step 12, and ran 16 amplification cycles for non-neuronal nuclei and 14 cycles for neuronal nuclei.

We performed bisulfite conversion (Step 14) as follows: to each bottle of CT conversion reagent (Zymo D5003-1), we added 7.9 mL of M-solubilization buffer (Zymo D5021-7) and 3 mL of M-dilution buffer (Zymo D5002-2). This mixture was vigorously mixed for at least 15 minutes before the addition of 1.6 mL M-reaction buffer (Zymo D5021-8). 25 μL of this reaction mixture were added to each well of the 384-well plate. Plate were then vortexed, sealed and incubated for 98 °C - 8 min, 64 °C - 3.5 h and then held at 4 °C.

Reaction mixtures (30 μl) were transferred to filter plates (Agilent 201025-100) and mixed with 90 μL of M-Binding Buffer (Zymo D5006-3) before incubating for 5 minutes at room temperature. Plates were then sealed and centrifuged for 5 minutes. Flow-through was discarded, then samples were washed with 30 uL per well of M-Wash Buffer (Zymo D5040-4) and centrifuged again. Flow-through was again discarded. 30 uL of M-Desulphonation Buffer (Zymo D5040-5) was added to each well and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 15 min, then centrifuged. The plates were washed twice with 30 μL / well of M-Wash Buffer, then eluted into a clean 384-well plate with 7 μL EB Buffer (Qiagen 19086) containing 500 nM well-indexed random primers.

A detailed bench protocol for single-cell methylome library preparation is shared through protocol.io (https://www.protocols.io/view/snm3c-seq3-kqdg3x6ezg25/v1). We performed steps 13 - 22 of the snm3c protocol.

Libraries were sequenced using Illumina Novaseq 6000 instrument on S4 and XP flowcells and 100 bp paired-end configuration with a 2-5% PhiX spike-in.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Bioinformatic alignment and feature quantification

The workflow for going from raw fastq files to modality-specific, aligned reads is comprehensively outlined in the following repository https://github.com/chooliu/snmCTseq_Pipeline and are summarized below.

Deviations from analysis performed in the flagship paper49 include the implementation of paired-end alignment and quantification methods, which increased mapping rate and coverage, while ensuring that read 1 and read 2 features were not quantified twice when insert size is low. Additionally, we deduplicated RNA reads.

Samples submitted for sequencing contain pooled libraries from across 384-well plates. Plate identity is indicated by i5/i7 indices and plate-level demultiplexing is performed prior to the snmCTseq pipeline. We then used cell barcodes specific to each reaction well (of each 384-well plate) to demultiplex sequence reads into individual nuclei with custom scripts, then trimmed Phred score base calls, Illumina adapter sequences and components specific to snmCT-seq library (the well-barcode, adaptase tail) using fastp v0.23.294 (-f 17 -t 10 -F 15 -T 10 -l 30 --cut_right -q 20 -u 50 -y -Y 15 -x --adapter_fasta).

Reads were aligned to the GENCODE v40 reference genome (GRCh38.p13 genome and genic annotation files). Methylome reads for each nuclei are then mapped using Bismark v0.24.095 in a two-stage process in order to increase mapping efficiency for PBAT-based reactions and other single-cell methylome preparations133: (i) first, reads are mapped in paired-end mode, then (ii) reads unable to map in stage i and singleton reads are mapped in single-end mode. We define singletons as reads without its pair passing trimming quality control. The --maxins 2000 and --pbat (for paired-end and read 1) flags are used, with default settings otherwise. PCR and sequencer optical duplicates (pixel distance 2500) are then removed using Picard MarkDuplicates96. Reads with mapping score ≤ 10 were excluded (samtools view -h -q 10; samtools v1.16.197). A core innovation of the snmCT-seq bench workflow is its simultaneous profiling of RNA and DNA in one reaction well without requiring their physical separation; instead, we perform in silico separation of fully methylated RNA libraries introduced by methylated dNTPs (resulting in full methylation, including at CpH- contexts) from the low mCH/CH levels present in libraries originating from genomic DNA. Using the “XM:Z” alignment flag, we then therefore classified the Bismark alignments containing at least three CpH-context cytosines and mCH/CH ≤ 0.50 as DNA reads. Finally, the final resultant DNA reads were used to infer cytosine-level methylation states: for each cytosine c, we tabulated the number of alignments suggesting that the cytosine is methylated (mc; C>T bisulfite conversion) versus unmethylated (uc; no conversion) using allcools v1.0.898 (“.allc” file, github.com/lhqing/ALLCools).

In parallel, RNA-originating reads were mapped with STAR v2.7.10a99, also using the two-stage (paired-end, followed by single-end) mapping procedure for RNA quantification. Non-default mapping settings included --alignEndsType EndToEnd --sjdbOverhang 149 --outFilterType BySJout --outFilterMultimapNmax 20 --alignSJoverhangMin 8 --alignSJDBoverhangMin 1 –outFilterMismatchNmax 999 --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04 --alignIntronMin 20 --alignIntronMax 1000000 --alignMatesGapMax 1000000 as established previously. Alignments that were duplicates identified by Picard or had mapping score ≤ 10 were excluded. To retain RNA reads, each alignment with at least three CpH- context cytosines and mCH/CH ≥0.90 based on the “MD:Z” flag were retained. Finally, RNA counts of each gene were then quantified using featureCounts100.

We re-validated the performance of the in silico separation of RNA and DNA in this paired-end workflow by using three datasets generated with the related, DNA methylation-only assay (snmC-seq2)89. Using existing data of this DNA-only assay profiling similar nuclei (BA10 cortex from 25 year old male; Sequence Read Archive SRR6911760, SRR6911772, SRR6911776), we again found high in silico discrimination between RNA and DNA based on alignment mCH/CH. When the RNA pipeline (STAR - mCH/CH filtering) was applied to these DNA-only reads, the majority (98.9%) of these bisulfite-converted DNA-only reads failed to align to the genome, and only 0.09% of reads were ultimately incorrectly classified as RNA reads and 0.0089% were quantified as transcript counts using featureCounts. With the DNA pipeline (Bismark - mCH/CH filtering), 0.075% of reads were incorrectly classified as RNA reads and discarded.

Data clustering, integration and annotation

Methylome

We used the allcools pipeline (v1.0.8)98 to perform variable feature selection, dimension reduction and clustering based on CpG- and the CpH- methylation fractions in non-overlapping 100kb genomic bins (on autosomal chromosomes) that had less than 20% base overlap with the ENCODE unified exclusion list (encodeproject.org/files/ENCFF356LFX). We also excluded bins that were outliers in terms of coverage (<200, ≥2000). We then derived principal components (PCs) from variable bins (the top 25th percentile), which were used in UMAP embedding. Clusters were determined using an iterative approach in which leiden clustering was repeated 1,000 times with random seeds and an 80% subsample of the nuclei (ConsensusClustering; k neighbors = 20, resolution = 1.5), resulting in a tally of 62 leiden clusters across samples. Clusters were annotated on the basis of gene body (CpG and CpH) hypomethylation in canonical marker genes11,134.

Transcriptome

Variable feature selection, dimension reduction and clustering were performed using the default Seurat pipeline101. Counts were scaled and normalized using the LogNormalize method. We derived PCs from 3,000 variable genes and used 20 PCs to find neighbors and in UMAP embedding (leiden resolution = 0.8). We identified 17 clusters, using only RNA data, and annotated them on the basis of canonical marker gene transcript levels and predicted subclass labels based on data from134 embedded in Azimuth102.

Modality integration and label transfer

We integrated transcriptome and methylome data from individual nuclei using a weighted nearest-neighbor approach implemented using methylome and RNA PCs and default parameters in the FindMultiModalNeighbors function in Seurat102. Dual modality clusters were annotated as described above.

To annotate nuclei that passed only methylome QC using cell type labels derived from both data types, we transferred cluster labels constructed from the joint RNA-methylome WNN embeddings to the methylome-only nuclei. We predicted cluster labels (model outcome) from the 100kb-mCH and 100kb-mCG principal components (model predictors) using the weighted k-nearest neighbors algorithm115 (L1-distance, kernel = “optimal”, k = 35; implementation from the kknn v1.3.1 and tidymodels v1.1.1116 R packages). We mitigated training biases due to class imbalances by applying the Synthetic Minority Oversampling TEchnique algorithm135 prior to each iteration of model fitting (SMOTE implementation in themis R package117 ; 50% oversampling). A grid search across 50 iterations of 5-fold cross-validation was applied to training set to select the model hyperparameters based on mean accuracy, followed by ROC-AUC, PR-AUCIn the final hold-out test set, accuracy was 98.7% at the subclass annotation level. After applying the final model to methylome-only nuclei, we excluded nuclei that either had “Unannotated” labels or were members of methylation-only based leiden clusters with majority “Unannotated” labels, resulting in the final nuclei count of 65,023 single-nucleus methylomes.

Whole genome sequencing, genotyping and ancestry inference

Genomic DNA was extracted using Zymo’s Quick-DNA mini-prep plus kit (#4068) and libraries were prepared using Roche’s KAPA Hyper kit (#07962363001) and sequenced on a Novaseq6000 (in 2x100bp and 2x150bp configurations). Reads were processed using the GATK4 best practices workflow110 for short variant germline discovery (SNPs and indels; accessed in January 2023). The reads were aligned using bwamem111 v0.7.17.

The alignments were deduplicated (gatk MarkDuplicates) then quality-corrected on a per-sample, -run, and -lane read group basis using Base Score Quality Recalibration (GATK ApplyBQSR v4.1.0) with reference to four databases of known variants (Homo_sapiens_assembly38.dbsnp138.vcf, hg38_v0_1000G_phase3_v4_20130502.sites.hg38.vcf, hapmap_3.3.hg38.vcf, Mills_and_1000G_gold_standard.indels.hg38.vcf; via GATK Google Cloud bucket). SNPs and indels were called per-sample using GATK HaplotypeCaller, then hard-filtered using the best practices recommendations for SNPs (QD < 2, QUAL < 30, SOR > 3, FS > 60, MQ < 40, MQRankSum < -12.5, ReadPosRankSum < -8) and indels (QD < 2, QUAL < 30, FS > 200, ReadPosRankSum < -20, SOR > 10).

The final mean autosomal alignment depths were 40.9X ± 10.3 (across-sample mean and sd), with no significant mean difference in depth or other quality metrics by diagnosis or age in years (t-test p-value > 0.59).

WGS genotypes were joined to the 1000 Genomes reference panel (Byrska-Bishop90 variant-filtered and phased release; internationalgenome.org/data-portal/data-collection/30x-grch38), subset to unrelated individuals. Using plink2 v2.00a3.7112 and bcftools v1.1697, we removed variants that were lower frequency (MAF < 0.05) or overlapped previously reported regions of long-range LD (e.g., HLA region; regions extracted from Price, et al.136 and converted to GRCh38 coordinates using the liftOver R/Bioconductor package v1.18.0 with UCSC hg19ToHg38.over.chain.gz), followed by LD pruning (plink2 --indep-pairwise 1000 100 0.25). We ran principal component analysis on the resulting joined genotypes (flashPCA v2.0113) and retained the first two PCs for modeling. Continental ancestry was assigned to each donor using the weighted k-nearest neighbor algorithm115 calculated on the first ten PCs (kknn, tidymodels implementations; hyperparameters k = 18, distance kernel = 1, power = “biweight” selected to optimize multiclass-accuracy and PR-AUC via 10-fold cross-validation on the 1000 Genomes dataset, with balanced sampling of each superpopulation via SMOTE135). In parallel, the joined case-control and 1000 Genomes genotypes in ADMIXTURE v1.30.0114 with k = 5 clusters, for one cluster per 1000 Genomes superpopulation. We assigned each donor to the continental ancestry with the majority admixture fraction. Both PCA and ADMIXTURE continental ancestry predictions were also consistent with biobank records of donor Self-Identified Race/Ethnicity, where available.

Genotype masking of the methylome

Donor genetic deviation from the reference genome introduces noise into methylation quantification: for example, alignments over a loss-of-cytosine C>T mutation would incorrectly be interpreted as a bisulfite-converted demethylated cytosine, when it might more appropriately be quantified as “not applicable” for the given donor. Further, a change of dinucleotide context strongly changes the biophysical/enzymatic mechanisms of methylation deposition and loss, with CpG- methylation having higher methylation levels compared to CpH- methylation, where H is the IUPAC code for A, C, or T. We thus set the methylation counts and coverage of a given donor’s cytosines to zero if the cytosine overlapped a (1) loss-of-cytosine, a (2) change in the cytosine’s +1 context from CG>CH or CH>CG, or (3) an indel. These “genotype-masked” calls were subsequently used prior to any differential methylation testing by diagnosis, age, or cell-type.

Methylome percent variance explained

To assess the overall methylome variance explained by each known covariate, we extracted methylation corresponding to different features: genes, promoters, 100kb bins and 50kb bins. For the bins, we also considered a “-genes” version excluding bins with ≥0.20 fractional overlap with protein coding genes. For each feature set and for CpA-, CpG-, and CpH- methylation, we used the allcools pipeline described previously to calculate global methylation level level-normalized methylation fractions across features and extracted the top ten PCs. We then ran PERMANOVA (vegan v2.6-8130 with 10,000 permutations, Euclidean distance) on the top k PCs to extract the variance explained R2 and significance of the association (permutation F-test). The value of k was selected using findPC v1.0129, using the median cross-method ‘elbow’ suggested, with a minimum of k = 2. We note that we elected to normalize for pseudobulk-level global mC levels; this can result in non-zero percent variances of because global methylation levels vary with age, the normalized values may underestimate the proportion of variance explained by age in some cell types.

Differential methylation identification strategies

De novo DMR calling with DSS

We modeled the mc and uc counts at single CpG-context cytosines using the DSS v2.50.1 R package137, which applies a beta-binomial model, but tests for a significant mean difference between two categorical groups at individual cytosines, and applies a shrinkage adjustment that incorporates spatial correlation in methylation levels between adjacent CpG-context cytosines. This local smoothing adjustment may lend to DSS’s high sensitivity, with benchmarks by independent DMR methods developers finding DSS to have the highest power among de novo DMR callers at <10X genomic count levels138. After removing cytosines overlapping the ENCODE Unified Exclusion list or had zero coverage in most samples (zero in ≥75% of samples), we ran DSS with the default prior and a 500bp smoothing window. To err on being conservative with reported results, we further elected to report significant regions instead of single cytosines (de novo regions called “DMRs” throughout the text for short). We thus aggregate the single cytosine results into larger candidate regions by aggregating sites within ±250bp, then retaining regions with at ≥2 multiple testing adjusted significant cytosines (Storey q-value < 0.05) and at least one significant cytosine with methylation fraction (f) estimated effect size |Δ| > 0.10.

For the ASD-DMRs, which we expected to have smaller effect sizes, we additionally required a stringent criterion of low colocalization with permuted DMRs (POF < 0.20). We define the POF = Permutation Overlap Fraction := [# of overlaps with permuted DMRs] / [# permutations] for a given candidate region. The tabulation entailed a rigorous procedure in which the diagnosis labels corresponding to each donor were shuffled then the full DMR calling procedure was re-run (DSS, aggregation into candidate regions). We performed 500 permutations within each subclass, then tabulated how many times the observed candidate DMRs intersect with the permuted DMRs. Candidate ASD-DMRs with low POF represent regions that were unlikely to be observed under the permuted null distribution. We repeated this process including the stringent permutation for “all-donor” ASD-DMRs containing all participants, as well as age-stratified subsets of the data: “young” (2-16 years of age), “mid” (17-30), and “older” (40-60) samples within a given subclass as a method to control for age effects. The threshold delineating young and mid for age-stratified ASD-DMRs were selected to approximately maintain the same sample sizes in each group (n=19, n=20, n=13).

Using this DSS to DMR procedure, we additionally called age-DMRs within each cell type subclass by pairwise testing between the young, mid, and older age strata. Unless otherwise noted, we primarily report and analyze the union of the three pairwise tests within each subclass. To call cell-type DMRs, we repeated the DMR calling procedure between each pairwise comparison of the eight subclasses (e.g., Astro versus Exc L2/3, Astro versus Inh MGE, etc.). A cell-type DMR for subclass s is identified as hypo-methylated in cell-type s compared to ≥5 other subclasses.

DMRs were annotated to genes using the annotatePeaks.pl function in HOMER56 and the same genome annotation file used for alignment.

DMR calling with pre-defined features

We considered pre-defined features of genes (GENCODE v40 protein coding and non-coding), promoters (+2kb, -1kb from first exon TSS), and full-genome non-overlapping genomic bins tiling the genome (500bp). For each region r within a given pseudobulk (subclass s and donor i) we summed the values of methylated counts (mc) and unmethylated counts (uc) for each cytosine c within the region: i.e., mr = ∑cmc and ur = ∑cuc. The mr and ur values were calculated separately for cytosines c within two dinucleotide sequence contexts (CpA- and CpG- cytosines), with the focus on CpA-methylation as it is the most prevalent form of non-CpG methylation and is correlated with other forms of CpH-methylation. We did not consider strandedness in any analysis and counts for CG- sites were merged to the “+” strand during pseudobulking.

Regions exhibiting high between-donor variability (IQR > 0.05 across donors in methylation fraction fr, where fr := mr/(mr + ur)) and sufficient coverage (≥5 counts in at least 75% of donors for genes and promoters; ≥2 counts in 75% of donors for 500bp bins) were modeled as the outcome of a beta-binomial model (aod v1.3.2 R package implementation, logit-link) with diagnosis (indicator variable for case), age (coded as linear and log(age) terms, then scaled and centered), and an diagnosis-by-age interaction (ASD-indicator, times linear- and log-age), and population stratification (first two genotype PCs) as model covariates. Features that failed to converge after 25,000 iterations were excluded; these were frequently bins at the edge of the parameter space and often exhibited quasi-complete separation (i.e., strong age or diagnosis effects, but computationally unstable due to low between-donor variance from mean f close to 0 or 1).

We tested the significance of each regression coefficient with a Wald test, followed by Storey q-value < 0.05 multiple testing correction to define statistical significance. We defined two forms of diagnosis effect. First, pan-age ASD effects are defined by significant diagnosis coefficient, but no significant ASD-by-age interaction effects. In contrast, age-dependent ASD effects are defined by a significant interaction term (q-value < 0.05). Due to the correlative nature of these analyses, the significant interactions can also be conceptualized as a different pattern of aging observed between ASD cases and controls. We also defined age-effects as significant linear- or log-age effects, focusing on interpretation of age-bins without significant interaction terms.

Annotating DMRs to genes and motif enrichment analysis

All differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were annotated to genes using the annotatePeaks function in the HOMER suite56, using the same genome annotation file as during alignment. In addition, DMRs that overlapped candidate cis regulatory elements identified in66 were annotated with gene assignments made in66. If a DMR overlapped multiple cCREs, we prioritize annotations as in annotatePeaks. Both sets of annotations are provided in Table S6.

DMR motif enrichment analysis was performed using the findMotifsGenome function in HOMER56 and the fasta file corresponding to the same genome used for alignment. As the HOMER motif database includes redundant motifs, we used TFClass118 classifications to aggregate TF names into TF families (e.g., the highly similar NEUROD1 and NEUROD2 position weight matrices were summarized as “Neurogenin/Atonal”). We also excluded TFs that were non-mammalian or unexpressed in the brain by requiring TFs with at least a log2(RPKM) > 2 expression value in any sample of the BrainSpan Developmental Transcriptome Study RNA-sequencing profiles67.

SCENIC TF-RNA (regulon) analyses

We applied pySCENIC v0.12.192 to quantify levels of transcription factors and their putative downstream gene targets (candidate TF-RNA sets, “regulons”) within our data, based on reference data compiled by the SCENIC developers (pyscenic grn, pyscenic ctx; hg38_10kbp_up_10kbp_down_full_tx_v10_clust.genes_vs_motifs.rankings.feather, motifs-v10nr_clust-nr.hgnc-m0.001-o0.0.tbl), then quantified within each RNA-based pseudobulk (pyscenic aucell). AUCell quantifications within each pseudobulk were quantile-normalized, then regressed on diagnosis, age, and log(age) within each cell type. We tested for associations between ASD and regulon levels by testing for the significance of the diagnosis effect using a t-test and for age-regulon associations using a likelihood ratio test comparing this model to a diagnosis-only model. A multiple testing corrected Storey q-value < 0.05 was deemed significant.

Transcriptome

We performed gene set enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes using the fgseaMultilevel function of fgsea107 and the biological process (BP) and molecular function (MF) subsets of the C5 ontology gene sets available here: https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/collections.jsp (download July 2024). Methylome

We assessed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on DMRs annotated to transcriptional start sites using gprofiler2108. We observed an association between genic DMRs (in transcriptional start and termination sites, exons and introns) and gene length. To adjust for this, we performed GO analysis of genic DMRs using goseq109.

Enrichment analyses

Enrichment analyses were performed using regioneR / regioneReloaded119,120.

To assess overlap between DMRs and genomic features, we compared the number of observed overlaps to a distribution of values (# overlaps) across 1x10ˆ6 permutations, sampling from a GC-content and coverage-matched background.

To assess the enrichment of DMRs and DMBs with external annotation datasets, we used a permutation-based approach that compared the number of overlaps in the observed DMRs and DMBs with the external dataset with that of 500 - 1 million random background sets (a subset of which were length matched). Each background was respectively extracted from the resampleRegions and randomizeRegions functions of regioneR and regioneReloaded v1.4.0119,120 restricted to the candidate regions (i.e., at least two CG sites tested in DSS in ±250bp span, but without the Δ and significance criteria described above) or candidate bins (tested in model; fr > 0.05, ≥2 counts in 75% of donors). Empirical p-values were generated by comparing the observed overlap counts to the distribution of background overlaps: p = (1 + number of permutations where |x_i - mean_permuted| ≥ |x_obs - mean_permuted|) / (1 + N) (Granges v1.54.1121, findOverlaps). The permutation based p-values for each covariate-annotation set (where covariates were ASD-, Age-, or Interaction-) were corrected for multiple testing (Benjamini-Hochberg139 FDR < 0.05).

Modeling global methylation and cell composition with covariate adjustment

To test for differences in global CpG-methylation fraction with a given donor feature while correcting for covariates, we modeled mCG/CG minus mCCC/CCC fraction as the outcome of a beta regression model (betareg v3.2-0), where the mCpCC fraction is a proxy for bisulfite conversion rate49. This adjusted methylation fraction was modeled by age (coded as linear age and log-age terms), diagnosis, and a age-by-diagnosis interaction term and significance of each term was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test (lmtest v0.9-40) comparing a full beta regression model to the model omitting the term of interest, then correcting for multiple testing (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR). This modeling procedure was repeated for mCA/CA and mCT/CT (highly correlated to CpA- and yielded the same results).

We repeated this beta regression procedure testing for associations between age, diagnosis, and age-by-diagnosis interaction with cell composition. As our data was obtained from NeuN-based sorting, cell composition fractions are calculated for a given subclass as (# of donor’s nuclei in target subclass)/(total # of donor’s nuclei in NeuN+ or NeuN- classes), using NeuN+ for neuronal cell types and NeuN- for non-neuronal.

Cross-modality correlation analyses

The criteria outlined in DMR Calling with Pre-Defined Features precluded analysis of gene body mCA in many protein-coding genes. To be able to correlate gene body mC and gene expression (Figures S13 and S16), we also assessed differential promoter and gene body cytosine methylation following the steps outlined in Section 4.8 of the edgeR132 user guide : https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/devel/bioc/vignettes/edgeR/inst/doc/edgeRUsersGuide.pdf.

Due to limited overall pairwise correlations between methylation and RNA features, we additionally attempted to prioritize genic features of interest by generating age-mC-RNA networks with SmCCNet68, a semi-supervised (donor age-aware) network approach that maximizes across-modality canonical correlations with a L1-penalty on the gene weights to induce sparsity in the networks. The network adjacency matrix is constructed using the canonical weights; that is, each network module contains features correlated with age and/or across ‘omes. Note that genes selected do not necessarily have high rg. Each methylome gene fraction (CpA- and CpG- gene fractions; concatenated across sequence context and restricted to genes tested i.e., IQR fr > 0.05) and RNA (log2-normalized counts) was quantile-normalized across donors and residualized for diagnosis effect (mean ASD effect regressed out) prior to network construction. Only protein-coding genes were included. Enrichr125 was used to perform over-representation analysis on Gene Ontology91 Biological Process terms on genes present in each module (GO_Biological_Process_2025; Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.10).

We created (1) ASD-DMR-RNA networks by extracting each pseudobulk’s methylation fraction across the de novo regions identified in our DMR analysis (restricted to higher across-donor interquantile range in CpG-methylome fractions, i.e., IQR fr > 0.05) and protein-coding genes observed in the RNA transcriptome (log2-normalized counts) for the cell type. We quantile-normalized each feature before network generation and residualized out age and log-age from each feature. This was then repeated for (2) Age-DMR-RNA networks, using instead de novo age-DMRs and regressing out diagnosis as a covariate. For excitatory L2/3 and L4/5 neurons, we restricted DMRs to those IQR fr >0.15 for age-DMR-RNA correlations in excitatory neurons due to computational memory limitations. Lastly, (3) we considered Age-mCgene-RNA networks in which methylome fractions were quantified over all protein-coding gene bodies (CpA- and CpG- gene fractions; concatenated across sequence context. We used the DMR-based networks in order to better interpret the de novo regions, whereas the mCgene-based methylome features were used to investigate gene-based pairings. Age trajectory clustering

In BrainSpan data

To identify common patterns of either RNA levels or methylation levels changing with age (modeled as a continuous variable), we performed “trajectory clustering” for two analyses within the manuscript. First, to assess the prenatal versus postnatal expression of mGenes, we considered BrainSpan reference RNA-seq data67 subset to cortical regions. For each gene reported in BrainSpan, we modeled RPKM levels as a function of age in a linear mixed model (lme4, v1.1-34) correcting for a random intercept for brain region and for a random intercept for donor to correct for repeated measures. Age was coded with a linear period and log-period term, where age was coded as a linear period and log-period effect, based on the fifteen age binned “periods” defined by67. We identified genes as age-varying based on a significant likelihood ratio test of this model compared to a model with no age terms (Storey q < 0.05). We considered significantly age-varying genes with a >1 standard deviation average effect size difference between prenatal and postnatal periods as “prenatally biased.” We also used trajectory clustering modeling to identify similar patterns of RNA level by BrainSpan period using dtwclust v1.23-1128 with the dtw_basic distance and the Partitioning Around Medoids clustering algorithm with k = 15. RNA levels were quantile-normalized to focus on relative levels with period for the given gene (in contrast to group genes based on absolute RPKM levels). Each cluster thus contains genes with similar “trajectory” patterns. We then assessed whether mGenes were randomly distributed among the 15 trajectory clusters by applying Fisher’s exact tests for independence on 15x2 contingency tables (cluster by mGene status), corrected for multiple testing (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.05).

Among age-varying DMBs

Second, in our exploration of age-varying 500bp bins within our snmCT-seq single-nucleus data, we selected the top 2,500 age-DMBs of each subclass by likelihood ratio (full beta-binomial model compared to reduced model without age or interaction terms) to identify sets of bins exhibiting similar patterns with age. The model coefficients were used to predict methylation values (assuming NT-CTL samples, age 2 to 60) was performed repeating the dtwclust clustering procedure with k = 10, a larger k to err on identifying more unique patterns.

Epigenetic age clocks

The probe lists were extracted from the 353-probe and 111-probe Horvath age clocks6 and the cortex age clock77, then converted to their corresponding GRCh38 single-cytosine coordinates using sesameData v1.20.0131. We extracted a ±1bp region in each of the donor-by-subclass methylome pseudobulk to calculate the corresponding methylation fractions as a proxy for fp for probe p (often “β” in array literature) at each probe, with the ±1bp expansion due to the high cross-strand symmetry and correlation of CpG-methylation. The epigenetic age estimate was then calculated for each of the pseudobulks, per usual clock calculations ˆAge = g(∑pwpfp) where wp are the probe weights (LASSO coefficients) and where g(x) is the calibration function shared by both clocks to account for the faster epigenetic changes reported before adult maturation6,77: g(x) = (1+ τ)exp(x)-1 for x < 0 and (1 + τ)∗x + τ for x ≥ 0 for τ = 20.

We evaluated evidence of cell-type specific epigenetic aging differences between ASD and CTL by evaluating whether the residual epigenetic age (ˆAge - ˆAgeloess) differed by group with a t-test, where the loess mean trend (ˆAgeloess, span=1) between the true age and epigenetic age was used as a “calibration curve” correcting for prediction errors between the clock age and chronological age specific to our dataset. We also evaluate the performance of each clock in predicting the biobank ground truth donor age by tabulating the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Spearman correlation between the predicted and ground truth ages.

We performed three additional analyses to assess the robustness of our findings to coverage. First, we constructed a high coverage “pseudo-tissue” by calculating a given donor’s methylation fraction from all eight major subclasses together; as each clock is validated in bulk data, this composite thus represents a performance bound due to technical effects of transferring the array-based technology to our sequencing-based measurements. Second, we hypothesized that given the spatial correlation of CG-methylation—particularly with the high proportion of clock probes overlapping either a promoter or CpG-island (55% in cortex clock, 81% in Horvath-353)—that padding fractional methylation calculations to include sites within a ±w base window of each probe may reduce noise. We thus also evaluated the impact of padding the f calculation with all cytosines in a ±25bp, ±50bp, or ±100bp range. Third, we calculated the mean across-pseudobulk coverage at each probe site and repeated all epigenetic age prediction when each pseudobulk and pseudo-tissue were sampled with replacement to this mean coverage level. This coverage resampling was performed 100 times.
