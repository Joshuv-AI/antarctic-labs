Examining the association between spatial accessibility to emergency cardiac care centers and cardiovascular mortality in Georgia, United States
Authors: Nima Kianfar, X Angela Yao, Behzad Kiani, Abe Mollalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality in Georgia, U.S., accounting for over 21,000 deaths annually. Due to the acute nature of cardiac complications and patients' urgent need for immediate medical attention, optimal access to emergency healthcare is essential. Despite this urgency, spatial accessibility to emergency cardiac care (ECC) centers and its relationship with CVD mortality remains underexplored.MethodsWe implemented an enhanced two-step floating catchment area method to explore spatial disparities in access to ECC centers in Georgia. Moreover, we proposed two complementary equity metrics, Health Disparity Index (HDI) and Localized Health Outcome Index (LHOI), to quantify disparities. We further examined the potential association between spatial access to ECC centers and CVD mortality rates using Social Vulnerability Index-adjusted ordinary least squares regression models. ResultsECC centers were clustered primarily in the Atlanta Metropolitan area, while more remote and rural areas in the central and southernmost regions have the lowest access to emergency medical resources. The estimated HDI value of 0.61, along with high LHOI values observed in 64 counties, indicates substantial inequity in CVD mortality and highlights that many high-mortality counties lack adequate emergency cardiac resources. Poorer access to ECC centers was significantly associated with higher CVD mortality rates (P < 0.05). ConclusionsOur integrated framework reveals pronounced spatial disparities in access to ECC across Georgia and underscores the need for targeted, place-based interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06109-9.Keywords: Spatial Accessibility, Cardiovascular Disease, GIS, Enhanced Two-Step Floating Catchment Area, Health Disparity Index, Emergency Cardiac Care Centers

Background

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality in the U.S., accounting for almost 1 million deaths in 2021 [1], corresponding to one CVD-related death every 34 s [2]. Among U.S. states, Georgia ranks 14th in terms of the highest death rates from CVDs. As of 2022, the death rate from CVDs in Georgia reached 183.9 per 100,000 individuals, which is 12.31% higher than the national average [3], with the total number of fatalities exceeding 21,728, making up approximately one in three deaths statewide [4]. A significant portion of CVD-related deaths are premature and are potentially preventable through timely and effective interventions [5, 6]. Given the time-sensitive nature of cardiovascular emergencies, access to emergency cardiac care (ECC) plays a key role in reducing disparities in emergency healthcare delivery [7]. In response, several hospitals in Georgia have been designated as ECC centers to strengthen the chain of survival for individuals experiencing acute CVD events [8]. These facilities represent some of the most essential components of the healthcare system [9]. However, the extent to which they are accessible to the population in need has not been adequately examined.

To better understand disparities in access to care services, spatial accessibility methods have increasingly been used. Spatial accessibility to healthcare services is commonly defined by three primary characteristics: the availability of healthcare facilities, the presence of people in need to access healthcare centers, and the travel cost or distance between the population in need and healthcare facilities [10]. A variety of spatial accessibility approaches have been developed to investigate the availability of healthcare services and evaluate their access dynamics. These approaches are commonly categorized into regional availability measures [11], gravity-based models [12], and two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) methods. Regional availability approaches calculate the ratio of healthcare services to the population in demand but lack insight into spatial variations and interactions between supply and demand [13]. Gravity-based models are theoretically robust but require more complicated computations and can be challenging to interpret in public health settings [14]. Conversely, the 2SFCA approach provides a practical and interpretable framework for integrating healthcare supply, population demand, and travel-time thresholds, helping prioritize interventions to enhance healthcare access equity [15].

The 2SFCA approach was originally proposed by Radke and Mu [16] and later modified by Luo and Wang [17] in the geographic information system (GIS) environment. The method has been widely applied to examine spatial disparities in healthcare access across diverse contexts, including mental health facilities [18], mammography services for breast cancer diagnosis [19], and primary care services for rural populations [20]. Nevertheless, the traditional 2SFCA approach assumes equal access to services for all populations within a catchment area and no access for populations outside the catchment, which may not reflect real-world healthcare utilization patterns [21]. To address these limitations, Luo and Qi [22] proposed the enhanced 2SFCA (E2SFCA), in which a distance decay function is incorporated to assign different weights to healthcare facilities based upon their proximity to population districts. Unlike the original 2SFCA, the E2SFCA more accurately reflects spatial variations in healthcare utilization [21]. Subsequent extensions have incorporated road-network characteristics [23], multiple transportation modes [24], flow-based population movement [25], and empirical Gaussian decay functions [26] to further improve estimates of healthcare accessibility.

Despite the critical need for timely and equitable access to emergency medical resources, a limited number of studies delved into investigating spatial accessibility to emergency medical resources [27]. To our knowledge, no prior study has focused on the accessibility to ECC centers in any region in the U.S. To address this gap, we proposed two complementary measures, Health Disparity Index (HDI) and Localized Health Outcome Index (LHOI), to evaluate disparities in cardiovascular outcomes from both statewide and county-level perspectives. By introducing HDI and LHOI indices along with E2SFCA method, our framework aims to integrate both spatial access to ECC centers and CVD mortality, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of disparities. More specifically, we intend to examine the spatial distribution of ECC centers throughout Georgia, quantify county-level accessibility to these facilities, and assess the potential relationship between spatial accessibility to ECC centers and CVD death rates.

The findings of this study are expected to support public health authorities in making more informed decisions on resource allocation, thereby reducing disparities in access to emergency healthcare.

Materials and methods

Study area

Georgia is the 8th most populous state in the U.S [28]. and ranks 14th in CVD mortality [29]. Containing more than 57% of Georgia’s total population, Atlanta is the 8th most populous metropolitan area in the U.S [30]. Data collection and preparation

We used the Online Analytical Statistical Information System (OASIS) website (https://oasis.state.ga.us/) to obtain county-level CVD mortality rates in Georgia [31]. The outcome variable was the annual county-level age-adjusted cardiovascular mortality rate per 100,000 population. Cardiovascular mortality was defined using ICD-10 codes I00–I78, and age-adjusted rates were standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population. Mortality data from 2022 were used in the primary analysis, while county-level CVD mortality rates for 2023 and 2024 were used for temporal validation analyses. These data were used to examine the potential association between county-level spatial accessibility and CVD mortality rates. Given the potential association of social vulnerability with CVD outcomes and healthcare access, we incorporated the 2022 CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) data, obtained from: https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/svi-data-documentation-download.html. The SVI ranks communities using 16 social variables grouped into four themes: socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation [32, 33]. The overall SVI ranking variable, RPL_THEMES, represents the overall percentile ranking and was incorporated as an adjustment covariate in the regression models.

This study focused on the spatial aspect of accessibility to healthcare using E2SFCA methodology. For this purpose, three types of spatial datasets have been utilized. First, the GIS shapefile of Georgia counties, including the demographic census data in the attribute table was retrieved from American Community Survey (ACS) 2021 data from the U.S. Census Bureau [34]. Second, the road network, comprised of attributes such as name, type (primary, secondary), length, and speed limit was also retrieved from U.S. Census Bureau Data.gov [35]. Based on Georgia Speed Limit (https://www.speed-limits.com/), there are five different road types in Georgia, including rural freeways, urban freeways, divided roads, undivided roads, and residential roads. These road types were categorized into primary and secondary roads, and the permissible speed limit for each of these road types was evaluated as 65 miles per hour (~ 105 km per hour) and 50 miles per hour (~ 80 km/hour), respectively. Third, the ECC centers dataset includes information about name, state-designated service level (I, II, and III), location address, and number of active hospital beds which were obtained from Georgia Department of Public Health (DPH) [36] and supplemented using the interactive map (available at: GA Emergency Cardiac Care Centers - Google My Maps). The ECC center dataset was geocoded using ArcGIS software version 10.8.1. (ESRI, Redlands, CA). These facilities are categorized under Georgia’s state-designated ECC system as Level I, Level II, or Level III centers. Based on Georgia Code § 31-11-132, these levels represent different roles within the statewide emergency cardiac care network, including 24/7 cardiac catheterization and angioplasty/percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) capability for Level I and Level II centers, on-site cardiothoracic surgery capability for Level I centers, and ECC protocols and coordinated transfer pathways for Level III centers [37]. All three levels were included to estimate spatial accessibility to Georgia’s state-designated ECC network overall. Detailed descriptions of the ECC centers are provided in Table S1 of Additional file 1. All datasets were processed and integrated within the ArcGIS environment to perform network-based analyses.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation analysis using Global Moran’s I tool in ArcGIS was used to evaluate the spatial distribution pattern of accessibility to ECC centers. Moran’s I ranges between − 1 and + 1, and indicates whether ECC centers are clustered (positive spatial autocorrelation), dispersed (negative spatial autocorrelation), or random [38]. Moreover, a local spatial autocorrelation analysis, the optimized hotspot technique, was applied to determine the statistical significance of spatial clusters of high values, known as hotspots [39]. In the optimized hotspot approach, the Gi* spatial statistic is used to identify statistically significant clusters of high and low ECC accessibility based on z-scores [6]. A county with a statistically significant positive Gi* indicates the presence of a hotspot cluster of ECC accessibility, indicating this county is surrounded by neighboring counties with similarly high accessibility to ECC center. Conversely, a negative z-score depicts a cold spot, where counties with low ECC center presence are clustered together. Larger absolute z-scores reflect stronger clustering intensity [40].

Network analysis

We measured the spatial accessibility of each county’s centroid to ECC centers based on the road network data and its related attributes, including road types and speed limits. Network Analyst tools in ArcGIS environment were used to perform various essential approaches, such as identifying the closest healthcare centers based on travel distance or time, determining service areas within a specific travel threshold, and constructing the origin-destination (OD) cost matrix to estimate the travel time from each county to its nearest ECC center. A threshold of driving time was calculated and attributed to each road type as the travel time in the OD matrix. Consistent with prior studies and given the time-sensitive nature of emergency cardiovascular care, we applied a maximum travel-time threshold of 30 min to represent reasonable access to acute and emergency care services [41, 42].

E2SFCA implementation

The traditional 2SFCA method consists of two main steps. First, the model searches and identifies nearby counties around each ECC center to calculate provider-to-population ratios (PPRs) within the catchment area. Second, for each county’s centroid, it scans all the centers within the specified travel time limit and aggregates the PPRs to determine the spatial accessibility score. The 2SFCA approach offers several advantages over classic spatial accessibility methods, including simplicity and adaptability of data requirements [43], the ability to account for population mobility across the desired boundaries [44], the comprehensive coverage within a specified catchment area [45], and the ability to incorporate travel time thresholds to overcome distance-related challenges [46]. However, the method has well-recognized limitations. In particular, it presumes that all counties have equal access to healthcare within the specified catchment, and the population outside of the catchment have no access to healthcare providers at all [44].

To mitigate these drawbacks, we implemented the enhanced 2SFCA (E2SFCA) which incorporates spatial decay weights to differentiate time zones within the catchment [47]. Health outcome disparity and spatial equity analysis

In this study, we introduce two complementary equity measures, a global and a local index, to quantify disparities in CVD mortality across Georgia. Although traditional inequality measures such as the Gini coefficient [50] and Lorenz curve [51] are useful for summarizing overall inequality, they are less suited to identifying localized county-level deviations or interpreting mortality disparities alongside spatial accessibility patterns [52]. Therefore, HDI is proposed as a Gini-inspired global measure that evaluates inequities based on the overall distribution of mortality rate values across all counties [52]. The HDI coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, where values closer to 0 indicate greater equity in county-level CVD mortality and values closer to 1 indicate greater inequality. Following commonly used interpretive ranges, HDI values were classified as very high equity (0–0.2), relatively high equity (0.2–0.3), moderate equity (0.3–0.4), low equity (0.4–0.6), and high inequity (0.6–1.0) [53].

In the next step, the LHOI was applied. This index captures both a county’s relative position (deviation from the mean) and the overall variability in mortality rates, measured by the standard deviation. Compared with global inequality measures such as the Gini coefficient, which provide a single overall summary of inequality, the LHOI provides county-level information on localized mortality deviations. When interpreted alongside ECC accessibility scores, this measure helps assess whether counties with elevated mortality burden also experience limited access to ECC services [54]. The high-disparity threshold was used to distinguish counties with the greatest relative mortality burden. Incorporating these equity assessment measures alongside ECC accessibility scores enables a more comprehensive understanding of health disparities and helps identify counties for improvement in emergency healthcare resource allocation [55].

Statistical analysis and temporal validation

Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models were fitted to quantify the association between ECC accessibility and CVD mortality at the county level. The primary model used the 2022 CVD mortality rate as the dependent variable and ECC accessibility score as the main explanatory variable, adjusted for county-level SVI score. For temporal alignment, the primary model used 2022 CVD mortality, 2022 SVI score, and the state-designated ECC center network corresponding to the same study period. Temporal robustness was then assessed by fitting additional OLS models using 2023 and 2024 CVD mortality rates as dependent variables. Potential multicollinearity among variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) [56], while Global Moran’s I was calculated for the OLS residuals to evaluate spatial autocorrelation. Results

Spatial distribution pattern of ECC centers

Preliminary descriptive statistics demonstrated that the age-adjusted CVD mortality rates in Georgia counties ranged from 119 per 100,000 population in Columbia County to 414.7 per 100,000 population in Clinch County. The mean value of CVD death rates in Georgia was 201.49, with a standard deviation of 44.63. Figure 3a depicts the spatial distribution of age-adjusted CVD mortality rates. The obtained value of Moran’s I was 0.51, with a high level of significance (z-score: 10.07, p < 0.01), indicating that the distribution of ECC centers was spatially clustered. Optimized hotspot analysis further showed that the ECC centers were significantly clustered within the Atlanta Metropolitan area and surrounding regions at a 99% confidence level (Fig. 3b).

Geospatial distribution of (a) CVD mortality rates, (b) optimized hot spot analysis of ECC centers in Georgia

Spatial accessibility to ECC centers

Spatial accessibility scores to ECC centers in Georgia counties are depicted in Fig. 4. The counties closer to ECC centers usually benefit from higher accessibility rates, such as counties around Atlanta, northeast, northwest, west central, and coastal zones. These regions with higher rates of accessibility are generally placed in the 4th, 5th, and occasionally the 3rd quintiles. In contrast, counties located one or more other counties away from the nearest ECC center had the lowest accessibility scores due to increased travel distance (Fig. 4). These counties were mostly located in the southwest, southeast, central and north central regions of Georgia, and were classified within the 1st and 2nd quintiles. The five counties with the highest obtained accessibility scores to ECC centers were Fulton (99.83), Gwinnett (98.21), Cobb (97.90), Douglas (97.65), and Hall (96.85), all of which were located in the Atlanta Metropolitan area. Conversely, Seminole (8.34), Treutlen (9.27), Echols (9.43), Montgomery (10.48), and Wheeler (11.34) obtained the least accessibility scores to ECC centers (Fig. 4). Residents in these predominantly rural counties must typically travel through at least two neighboring counties to reach an ECC center, reflecting substantially longer travel distances and potential delays in accessing emergency cardiac care (Fig. 4).

Evaluation of health outcome disparity and spatial equity

The estimated HDI value of 0.61 indicates high inequity across Georgia. Moreover, as indicated in Figs. 5, 64 counties were classified as high LHOI counties, representing substantial divergence from the average mortality rates, signifying counties with the most pressing health outcome inequities. Clinch (2.58), Early (2.45), Emanuel (2.33), Wilkinson (2.17), and Jeff Davis (2.14) counties demonstrated the highest LHOI values, respectively. These counties were consistently within the 1st and 2nd quintiles of accessibility scores, suggesting an overlap between low ECC spatial access and elevated CVD mortality rates in these regions. In contrast, Columbia (-1.94), Schley (-1.90), McIntosh (-1.82), Gwinnett (-1.78), and DeKalb (-1.74) counties exhibited the lowest LHOI values. These counties fell within the 4th and 5th quintiles of spatial access scores, indicating an inverse pattern between LHOI and access scores (Additional file 2, Figure S1). This pattern was consistent across almost all counties in Georgia, suggesting that spatial accessibility may be related to county-level differences in CVD outcomes.

Spatial distribution of LHOI categories (low, moderate, high)

Just over half of counties in the low accessibility group experienced high LHOI values (50.94%). Counties in the moderate accessibility group were more evenly distributed across LHOI value ranges. However, a notable proportion of counties (41.51%) still fell within the high LHOI category, suggesting that moderate spatial access was not uniformly accompanied by lower health outcome disparities. In contrast, counties in the high accessibility group had the largest proportion in the low LHOI category (45.28%) (Additional file 2, Figure S2).

Counties classified in the high LHOI category had the lowest median access score (median score = 61.0), followed by counties in the moderate LHOI category (median score = 66.9), and the low LHOI category had the highest median score (median score = 76.1) (Additional file 2, Figure S3). These findings further support a strong inverse association between spatial accessibility and health disparities, indicating that higher access to ECC centers is associated with reduced inequities in CVD outcomes. Therefore, we further examined this relationship using SVI-adjusted OLS regression and temporal validation analyses.

Statistical association and temporal validation

OLS regression showed that higher ECC accessibility was significantly associated with lower CVD mortality rates in the SVI-adjusted model (β = -0.38, 95% CI: -0.65 to -0.12, p = 0.005; adjusted R² = 0.13). This inverse association remained consistent in the 2023 and 2024 temporal validation models. In 2023, higher ECC accessibility remained significantly associated with lower CVD mortality rates (β = -1.14, 95% CI: -1.75 to -0.53, p < 0.001; adjusted R² = 0.19), with a similar pattern observed in 2024 (β = -1.64, 95% CI: -2.36 to -0.91, p < 0.001; adjusted R² = 0.23). All VIF values were below 5, indicating no substantial multicollinearity among the variables. Moreover, residual spatial autocorrelation was not significant in any model, suggesting that the observed associations were unlikely to be driven by residual spatial dependence (Additional file 2, Figure S4).

Discussion

Although health authorities and policymakers have made significant strides in reducing fatalities from heart attacks and cardiac arrests by designating ECC centers throughout Georgia [8], opportunities for further improvement remain. Optimizing access to ECCs is critical to alleviating the burden of these diseases. Due to the limited research on disparities in access to ECC resources across the U.S., this study evaluated spatial inequities in ECC accessibility and related cardiovascular outcomes in Georgia. Our findings showed that ECC distribution follows a clustered pattern, particularly in the Atlanta Metropolitan area, while suburban and more remote areas experience markedly lower ECC availability. Moreover, the derived HDI and LHOI values at the state and county levels indicated that a considerable proportion of the population residing in counties with elevated CVD mortality rates also faces limited spatial access to ECC services. Consistent with this pattern, the SVI-adjusted regression analyses showed that higher ECC accessibility was associated with lower CVD mortality rates in the primary 2022 model and remained consistent in the 2023 and 2024 temporal validation models, supporting our hypothesis that limited access to ECC centers might be related to higher CVD mortality.

Consistent with our findings, several previous studies have shown that limited access to CVD-related healthcare services is associated with higher CVD complications and mortality. For instance, Jilani et al. [57] examined the association between social determinants of health and CVD, suggesting that populations facing greater social adversity, such as economic instability and limited access to healthcare facilities, experience a higher burden of CVD risk factors and outcomes, including stroke, coronary heart disease, heart failure and mortality. Similarly, Wenger et al. [58] indicated that the gap in life expectancy between urban and rural areas in the U.S. has widened over the years, partly due to poor access to healthcare, outpatient care, public health infrastructure and insurance coverage in rural regions. In line with our findings, Patterson et al. [59] identified substantial inequities in emergency medical services (EMS) access across rural U.S. counties, particularly near county borders where residents often rely on neighboring jurisdictions for emergency care.

Comparable spatial patterns in healthcare accessibility have been reported internationally. Studies from Canada [60], China [27], Romania [61], Australia [62], Japan [63], South Africa [64], and several regions within the U.S. [65, 66] consistently demonstrate that healthcare resources tend to be concentrated in urban cores, leaving peripheral and rural areas underserved. However, not all evidence points to spatial accessibility as the dominant driver of cardiovascular outcomes. Pathak et al. [67], for instance, found that unfavorable household and neighborhood resources may play a more influential role than spatial proximity to heart-related healthcare resources. Their findings suggested that many individuals who died from heart failure were not transported to ECCs, even though they had better spatial accessibility and lived slightly closer to hospitals than those who were transported. Together, these studies underscore that geographic accessibility, while critical, operates within a broader social and structural context.

Beyond the observed association between ECC accessibility and CVD mortality rates, this relationship is likely intertwined with a broader range of potential variables, including socioeconomic contexts (e.g., poverty [68], income discrepancies [69], employment dynamics [70]), demographic characteristics [71], and environmental exposures [72]. Incorporating county-level SVI into models helped account for broader social vulnerability, while still recognizing that the relationship between ECC accessibility and CVD mortality should be interpreted within a wider social, demographic, environmental, and healthcare-system context. In addition, long travel distances and extended travel times may further discourage individuals from seeking emergency care, even in acute situations [73, 74]. Future studies should incorporate these intricacies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of barriers affecting access to emergency care. Nevertheless, evaluating all potentially relevant determinants within a single study may not be feasible, given the multitude of factors involved and the challenges associated with credible data availability.

From a methodological standpoint, this study advances prior applications of the E2SFCA approach. Most previous studies (e.g [75] and [76]), applied the traditional Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve to assess spatial inequalities. Although these measures are useful for summarizing overall inequality across geographic units, they do not directly identify which specific counties contribute most to localized health outcome disparities [77]. In contrast, the proposed HDI and LHOI move beyond purely distributional measures by explicitly incorporating health outcomes. More specifically, HDI provides a statewide summary of mortality inequality, whereas LHOI identifies county-level deviations from the statewide mortality distribution. Therefore, the two measures should be interpreted as complementary rather than competing indicators. Because HDI and LHOI operate at different spatial scales, any differences between them should not be interpreted as contradictory findings. While HDI reflects the statewide magnitude of mortality inequality, LHOI identifies where this burden is most pronounced at the county level. These measures are therefore most directly useful for population-level public health planning and emergency care resource allocation rather than individual clinical risk prediction, although they may help clinicians and health systems identify communities where prevention, referral coordination, telecardiology, and emergency response planning may be most needed. By linking spatial access with disease burden, this approach offers a more policy-relevant assessment of emergency healthcare inequities and provides finer spatial resolution for identifying areas that may require targeted intervention.

The combined assessment of ECC accessibility and localized health outcome disparities can help identify counties where elevated CVD mortality overlaps with limited ECC access. While the designation of ECC centers at three different levels represents important progress in addressing the CVD mortality burden across the state, targeted expansion or upgrading of ECC capacity in rural and sparsely populated counties could help reduce persistent geographic disparities in access to ECCs. Enhancing existing centers by increasing specialist staffing, bed capacity, ambulance availability, and EMS transfer coordination may also improve system efficiency. Telemedicine, particularly telecardiology, represents another promising avenue for bridging the gap between rural patients and specialized care available in metropolitan areas. In parallel, strengthening community outreach and public education related to CVD prevention, symptom recognition, and emergency response could encourage earlier care-seeking behavior. Improved coordination between ECC centers and local healthcare providers may further support continuity of care for populations with limited healthcare access. Together, these strategies suggest that the proposed framework may support resource allocation, geographic equity monitoring, and targeted planning efforts aimed at reducing avoidable delays in emergency cardiovascular care.

There are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although county-level SVI was incorporated to account for social vulnerability, this measure does not fully capture all demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, cultural, environmental, insurance-related, rurality-related, or healthcare-system variables that may be related to CVD mortality and access to emergency care. In addition, due to the ecological nature of the study, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal relationships. Second, ECC centers were treated as the sole providers of emergency cardiac care, although other public and private healthcare facilities may also play important roles in providing emergency cardiovascular services. In addition, ECC centers vary in capability across Level I, Level II, and Level III designations. Although all state-designated ECC centers were included to estimate accessibility to Georgia’s ECC network overall, the analysis did not separately quantify access to specific advanced cardiac care capabilities, such as on-site cardiothoracic surgery or PCI-capable services. Therefore, accessibility estimates should be interpreted as geographic access to the designated ECC network rather than equivalent access to all levels of cardiac care. While we explored the accessibility of population in all counties to ECC centers throughout the state, an examination on a finer spatial resolution (e.g., sub-county level) may provide more accurate estimates. Edge effects may also compromise the credibility of the outputs, as individuals living at the borders may seek healthcare in neighboring states such as South Carolina, Florida, and Alabama. Similarly, residents near county boundaries may use ECC centers in nearby counties, which may not be fully captured by county centroid-based accessibility estimates. The observed relationship between poorer ECC access and higher CVD mortality may also exist in other regions that requires further external validation.

Future studies should apply this framework to other states or regions to evaluate the broader generalizability of the findings and incorporate temporal aspects such as seasonal or annual variations in ECC capacity, traffic conditions, EMS response times, and healthcare demand. Nonetheless, the findings of this research can help public health authorities and agencies by providing useful insights for access to emergency CVD care centers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated substantial spatial inequities in access to emergency cardiac care and cardiovascular mortality disparities across Georgia by integrating the E2SFCA framework with two equity measures, the HDI and LHOI. ECC centers were heavily concentrated within the Atlanta Metropolitan area, leaving many rural and less populated counties with limited access to time-sensitive cardiac services. Beyond conventional accessibility assessments, the proposed framework simultaneously quantified statewide mortality inequality and localized county-level disparities by linking spatial access to cardiovascular health outcomes. These findings suggest that current patterns of ECC resource distribution may contribute to persistent geographic disparities in cardiovascular outcomes across rural regions of Georgia. Targeted strategies, such as expanding ECC capacity, strengthening pre-hospital care, and leveraging telemedicine may help improve equity in emergency cardiac care across the state.
