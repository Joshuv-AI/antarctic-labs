Multiomics Insights Into the Role of Spondin‐1 in the Pathogenesis and Prognosis of Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Xianglin Long, Yeshen Zhang, Xinyue Gao, Hao Chen, Yunshu Li, Weiwu Xiong, Xiaoyan Wang, Zhihui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is characterized by progressive structural remodeling, yet molecular drivers remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify novel protein targets for AF using an integrative multiomics approach.MethodsWe performed a multilayered genetic screen combining Mendelian randomization and colocalization analyses of plasma protein (protein quantitative trait loci) and atrial expression quantitative trait loci with AF genome‐wide association studies. SPON1 (spondin‐1) was identified as a priority candidate and further characterized using single‐nucleus RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and experimental validation. Clinical relevance was evaluated in the UK Biobank cohort by assessing associations with incident AF, cardiac magnetic resonance phenotypes, and long‐term prognosis.ResultsThe genetic screen identified SPON1 as a potential target for AF, supported by colocalization evidence across the plasma proteome (posterior probability of hypothesis 4=0.87) and atrial transcriptome (posterior probability of hypothesis 4=0.86). Single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics revealed that SPON1 expression was specific to fibroblasts and enriched in extracellular matrix pathways. In the UK Biobank cohort (N=39 372), elevated plasma SPON1 levels were independently associated with an increased risk of incident AF (hazard ratio [HR], 1.17; P<0.001). Furthermore, high SPON1 levels were associated with increased left atrial volumes (P<0.05) and stratified the risk of heart failure and death (HR, 1.51; P<0.001) in patients with established AF. Experimental validation showed that recombinant SPON1 can induce a profibrotic phenotype in fibroblasts.ConclusionsIn conclusion, this integrative study identifies SPON1 as a candidate with potential causal and prognostic relevance for AF. These findings suggest that targeting this protein may offer a strategy for modifying the structural substrate of AF.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia worldwide, with an increasing global prevalence that represents a major public health challenge. 1 , 2 It is associated with markedly increased risks of ischemic stroke, heart failure, and cognitive decline, contributing substantially to cardiovascular morbidity and death. 3 , 4 Current therapeutic strategies, including anticoagulation and rhythm or rate control, primarily focus on symptom control and complication prevention rather than modifying the underlying disease processes that initiate and sustain AF. 5 Central to AF pathogenesis is adverse structural and electrical remodeling of the atria, particularly progressive atrial fibrosis. 6 , 7 Emerging evidence also highlights the contribution of epicardial adipose tissue to this process. Epicardial adipose tissue can act as a metabolically active organ, secreting paracrine factors that may infiltrate the atrial myocardium and promote fibrotic remodeling. 8 , 9 However, the development of targeted antifibrotic therapies has been limited by incomplete knowledge of the molecular determinants of this process. As a result, there is an unmet clinical need to identify novel molecular targets with established causal relevance to enable early risk stratification and mechanism‐based treatment of AF.

Large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have identified numerous genetic loci associated with AF susceptibility, advancing understanding of its genetic architecture. 10 , 11 , 12 However, their clinical translation remains limited. Most AF‐associated variants reside in noncoding regions, complicating the identification of causal genes and underlying biological mechanisms. 13 Mendelian randomization (MR) provides a robust framework for causal inference by leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables to reduce confounding and reverse causation. 14 , 15 Integration of MR with molecular quantitative trait loci for gene expression (eQTLs) and protein abundance (pQTLs) enables systematic prioritization of candidate causal genes and proteins. 16 Nonetheless, MR analyses may lack tissue specificity and be influenced by linkage disequilibrium. Therefore, a multiomics strategy incorporating single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics is required to resolve cell type–specific mechanisms within relevant cardiac tissue contexts.

In this study, we used an integrated, multilevel analytical framework to identify and characterize novel protein candidates with potential causal associations with AF. Through a systematic screen that integrated large‐scale plasma proteomics and cardiac transcriptomics with genetic evidence, we identified SPON1 (spondin‐1) as a high‐priority candidate. We subsequently used single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic data from human atrial tissue to delineate the cellular landscape of SPON1 expression and its distribution. The potential role of SPON1 in contributing to fibroblast activation was further explored through functional validation in cell culture and a murine model. Finally, we leveraged the UK Biobank prospective cohort to evaluate the associations between circulating SPON1 and incident AF, cardiac structural phenotypes, and long‐term prognosis. This integrative approach aimed to establish a comprehensive evidence chain from genetic causality to clinical relevance, thereby providing new insights into the structural substrate of AF.

Study Design and Overview

We implemented an integrative multiomics framework to identify and validate protein targets for AF. The study was structured into 3 distinct phases: (1) genetic discovery, which involved MR and colocalization analyses to screen potential targets using plasma proteome and heart transcriptome data; (2) mechanistic characterization, which used single‐cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and both in vivo and in vitro experiments to delineate cellular origins and investigate molecular pathways; and (3) clinical translation, which used the UK Biobank prospective cohort to validate the association of the prioritized target, SPON1, with AF incidence, cardiac remodeling phenotypes, and long‐term prognosis (Figure 1). MR Analysis

Data Sources and Instrument Selection

In the context of eQTL analyses, cis‐single nucleotide polymorphisms (cis‐SNPs) were characterized as genetic variants situated within ±1 Mb of the transcription start site. Similarly, for pQTL analyses, cis‐variants were delineated as SNPs positioned within ±1 Mb of the respective protein‐coding gene. The selection of instrumental variables necessitated that they achieve genome‐wide significance (P<5 × 10−8) and maintain independence, as indicated by a linkage disequilibrium r 2 value of <0.001, with a clumping window set at 10000 kb.

Causal Inference

For pQTLs with multiple instrumental variables, the inverse‐variance weighted method was used as the primary analytical approach. Sensitivity analyses incorporated MR‐Egger regression to detect potential horizontal pleiotropy, alongside the weighted median method. In the case of eQTLs, due to the limited number of independent cis‐instruments, we used the summary data–based Mendelian randomization (SMR) method. 21 Specifically, SMR represents an MR approach that performs a Wald test of the strongest cis‐eQTL for each gene to evaluate its causal effect on the trait. A nominal threshold of P_SMR <0.05 was adopted, and results were further refined through colocalization analysis.

Colocalization Analysis

To differentiate genuine causal targets from spurious associations influenced by linkage disequilibrium, we conducted colocalization analyses using 2 distinct approaches. First, alongside the SMR analysis for eQTLs, we applied the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test. HEIDI functions as a colocalization method to differentiate causal pleiotropy from linkage, with a P_HEIDI value >0.05 indicating linkage. Second, we conducted a Bayesian colocalization analysis using the coloc R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 22 This analysis assessed the colocalization probability between 2 sets of data: (1) pQTLs and AF GWAS, and (2) atrial eQTLs and AF GWAS. For each genomic locus, a window of ±500 kb surrounding the lead SNP was defined. The colocalization analysis calculates posterior probabilities for 5 distinct hypotheses: H0, indicating no association with either gene expression or the phenotype; H1, indicating an association solely with gene expression; H2, indicating an association exclusively with the phenotype; H3, indicating associations with both gene expression and the phenotype via independent SNPs; and H4, indicating associations with both gene expression and the phenotype via a shared causal SNP. The posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PP.H4) quantifies the likelihood that both traits share a common causal variant. A PP.H4 value >0.8 was interpreted as strong evidence supporting colocalization.

Single‐Nucleus RNA Sequencing Analysis

We conducted an analysis of a publicly accessible single‐nucleus RNA sequencing data set of human atrial cells, identified as GSE255612. 23 The raw gene expression matrix was processed using the Seurat R package. 24 Quality control measures were implemented to exclude low‐quality cells. 23 Data normalization was achieved through the SCTransform method, followed by principal component analysis and uniform manifold approximation and projection for dimensionality reduction. Major cell types were annotated using canonical markers. 23 To explore fibroblast heterogeneity, we isolated the fibroblast population and performed reclustering at a resolution of 0.2. Fibroblast subclusters were categorized into homeostatic and activated states based on marker expression profiles. 25 Visualization of the expression feature plot and density plot of marker genes was accomplished using the scCustomize and Nebulosa R packages. 26 Differentially expressed genes between homeostatic and activated fibroblasts were identified via the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, with criteria of adjusted P<0.01 and log2 fold change >0.25. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the upregulated differentially expressed genes and SPON1 coexpressed genes using the clusterProfiler R package. 27

Spatial Transcriptomics Analysis

High‐resolution spatial transcriptomics data (Visium) were sourced from the Human Heart Cell Atlas (https://www.heartcellatlas.org/). 28 The processed data set (in .h5ad format) was imported into the R statistical environment and subsequently converted into a Seurat object using the schard package (https://github.com/cellgeni/schard). Data preprocessing adhered to the standard Seurat workflow, encompassing normalization, identification of variable features, and scaling. To ascertain the spatial niche of SPON1, its expression intensity was visualized via the SpatialFeaturePlot function. Anatomic regions, such as the epicardium and areas of fibrosis, were delineated on the basis of preexisting expert annotations within the data set and visualized using the SpatialDimPlot function. 28

We validated our findings using independent data sets: Single‐cell validation was achieved by confirming the cellular specificity of SPON1 within an independent human atrial nonmyocyte single‐cell RNA sequencing data set (GSE224959) as well as the integrated Human Heart Cell Atlas data set. 28 , 29 , 30 Visualization was facilitated using the SCP package (available at https://github.com/zhanghao‐njmu/SCP).Protein validation involved the examination of immunohistochemistry images sourced from the Human Protein Atlas (accessible at https://www.proteinatlas.org/) to ascertain the localization of SPON1 protein in human heart tissue, using antibody HPA077546. 31 A phenome‐wide association study was conducted to evaluate the potential pleiotropic effects of SPON1 genetic instruments across a comprehensive array of clinical phenotypes, using the AstraZeneca Phenome‐Wide Association Study Portal (available at https://azphewas.com/). 32

UK Biobank Clinical Cohort Analysis

Study Population

We conducted an analysis of participants from the UK Biobank who had available SPON1 proteomic data (N=52 997). 18 , 33 Data acquisition and analysis for this study were carried out under UK Biobank Application No. 1198847. Quality control measures were implemented by excluding participants with missing or outlier protein values. To minimize population stratification bias, the analysis was confined to participants of White British ancestry (Data‐Field 21 000, code 1001). 34 Participants lacking baseline covariate data were also excluded. The final study cohort comprised 40 323 participants, as detailed in the inclusion and exclusion flowchart (Figure S1). This cohort was further stratified into 2 groups: (1) the incident AF prediction cohort, which included participants free of AF at baseline (N=39 372); and (2) the prognosis cohort, consisting of participants with prevalent AF at baseline (N=951).

Outcome Definitions

Incident AF was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code I48, as recorded in hospital inpatient records and death registry data. Secondary outcomes encompassed the onset of heart failure and all‐cause death. The follow‐up period was censored at the occurrence of the event, death, or the date of the most recent data update.

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance

A subset of participants (n=6928) who underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging was included in the study. 35 The left atrial volume index (LAVI), left ventricular mass index, left atrial ejection fraction, and left ventricular ejection fraction were obtained through automated image analysis pipelines.

Animal Model and Tissue Preparation

A cohort of ten 8‐week‐old C57BL/6J male mice was obtained from the Department of Laboratory Animal Sciences at Central South University. After a 1‐week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly allocated into 2 groups: a control group (n=5) and an AF model group (n=5). The AF model was induced via daily tail vein injections of a sterile solution comprising acetylcholine (66 μg/kg; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) and calcium chloride (CaCl2, 10 mg/kg; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) in a total volume of 0.1 mL for 28 consecutive days. The control group received daily injections of an equivalent volume of sterile saline. The arrhythmic phenotype was monitored throughout the study. At the end of the 4‐week intervention period, all mice were euthanized, and atrial tissues were promptly harvested. The tissues were either flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Fixed atrial tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned to a thickness of 5 μm, and subsequently mounted on glass slides. For immunofluorescence staining, the sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval using a citrate buffer at pH 6.0. The slides were then blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting SPON1 (1:100; Abcam) and α‐smooth muscle actin (1:500; Proteintech). After the primary antibody incubation, the sections were washed and incubated with the corresponding Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Servicebio) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using an LG‐FS80 digital slide scanning system (Servicebio).

In Vitro Fibroblast Stimulation Experiments

Cell Culture

The NIH‐3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line was used for mechanistic investigations conducted in vitro. The cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% calf serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

SPON1 Stimulation

NIH‐3T3 cells were seeded and cultured to 70% to 80% confluency. Cells were then serum‐starved in serum‐free DMEM for 12 hours to synchronize the cells. Subsequently, cells were treated with recombinant mouse SPON1 protein (, 100 ng/mL; R&D Systems) or vehicle control (the same buffer used to reconstitute/prepare recombinant SPON1) for 24 hours under serum‐free conditions. Each experiment was performed with 5 independent biological replicates.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA expression levels of Acta2, Col1a1, Fn1, and Postn were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with Gapdh as the internal reference. The primer sequences used were listed as follows:

Gapdh (forward 5′‐GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAT‐3′; reverse 5′‐GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG‐3′).

Acta2 (forward 5′‐CTTCCAGCCATCTTTCATTGG‐3′; reverse 5′‐ GTTCTGGAGGGGCAATGAT‐3′).

Col1a1 (forward 5′‐GGTGAGCCTGGTCAAACGG‐3′; reverse 5′‐ ACTGTGTCCTTTCACGCCTTT‐3′).

Fn1 (forward 5′‐TCACCTACGGAGAGACAGGA‐3′; reverse 5′‐ TGTTGTTGATGGTGGCTGTG‐3′).

Postn (forward 5′‐GTACAACAATCTGGGGCTTTTT‐3′; reverse 5′‐ AATCTGGTTCCCATGGATGA‐3′).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean± SD or median (interquartile range) and compared using Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the χ2 test. Plasma SPON1 levels were normalized using Z score transformation for all regression analyses. A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Survival Analysis and Risk Prediction

The association between plasma SPON1 levels and incident AF in the prediction cohort, as well as heart failure and all‐cause death in the prognosis cohort, was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 CIs were estimated for continuous SPON1 levels, representing the risk per 1‐SD increase, and for SPON1 quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group to assess potential dose–response effects.

To systematically evaluate the robustness and independent association of SPON1 with clinical outcomes, we constructed 3 hierarchical models based on a prespecified adjustment strategy. Model 1 was a minimally adjusted model that included only fundamental demographic factors such as age and sex. Model 2 further incorporated traditional clinical risk factors and comorbidities, including body mass index; smoking status; alcohol consumption; Townsend deprivation index; systolic blood pressure; and history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and heart failure. The rationale for this model was to determine whether the associations between SPON1 and the study outcomes were independent of established cardiovascular risk predictors. Finally, model 3 added a comprehensive set of laboratory biomarkers, including estimated glomerular filtration rate, C‐reactive protein, hemoglobin A1c, and lipid profiles. Of note, history of heart failure was excluded from the adjustment covariates when analyzing incident heart failure as the outcome. This stage of adjustment aimed to ensure that the observed effects of SPON1 were not secondary to systemic inflammation, renal impairment, or shared metabolic pathways. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to visualize survival probability across SPON1 quartiles, and the log‐rank test was applied to compare differences between groups.

Dose–Response and Imaging Association

To explore potential nonlinear relationships between SPON1 and outcomes, restricted cubic splines with 4 knots were fitted. For the mechanistic substudy, multivariable linear regression was used to assess the association between normalized SPON1 levels and cardiovascular magnetic resonance parameters (LAVI, left ventricular mass index, left atrial ejection fraction, left ventricular ejection fraction).

RESULTS

Genetic Causal Inference and Colocalization Analysis Identify SPON1 as a Candidate Causal Gene for AF

The proteome‐wide screening using cis‐pQTLs from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project and AF GWAS identified 91 proteins with potential causal associations with AF risk (P<0.05) (Table S3). Concurrently, the transcriptome‐wide analysis of human atrial appendage tissue (Genotype‐Tissue Expression version 10) identified 867 genes showing significant pleiotropic associations with AF that passed the HEIDI test (P_HEIDI >0.05) (Table S4). The intersection of these candidates revealed 7 genes supported by both proteomic and transcriptomic evidence: SPON1, CD40, IL6R, TNN, LRRC37A2, NAGA, and SIRPA. In pQTL MR analysis, genetically predicted higher plasma levels of SPON1 (odds ratio, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.04–1.19]; P=0.001) were associated with increased AF risk (Figure 2A; Figure S2), while CD40 and IL6R were associated with lower risk (Figure 2A; Figure S3, Tables S3 and S5). SMR analysis of atrial eQTLs confirmed that increased SPON1 expression in atrial tissue was associated with AF (Figure 2C).

Among the 7 candidates, SPON1 demonstrated the most consistent evidence of colocalization in both the plasma proteome (PP.H4=0.87, Figure 2B; Table S6) and the atrial transcriptome (PP.H4=0.86, Figure 2D; Table S6). CD40 also showed support for colocalization in the eQTL analysis (PP.H4 >0.8), while other candidates such as IL6R and TNN displayed lower posterior probabilities for a shared causal variant (Table S6). LocusCompare analysis demonstrated a linear correlation between the association strengths of plasma SPON1 pQTL and AF GWAS data (Figure 2E). Stacked regional association plots revealed aligned signal peaks for pQTL, eQTL, and AF GWAS data at the rs10832164 locus (Figure 2F). In the phenome‐wide association study study, genetically predicted SPON1 levels showed no significant associations with major noncardiac disease categories such as cancer, immune diseases, or metabolic disorders (Figure 2G; Table S7). Based on these MR and colocalization results, SPON1 was prioritized for further mechanistic and clinical characterization.

Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Maps SPON1 Expression to Activated Fibroblasts and Extracellular Matrix Pathways

Major cell types identified in the human atrial tissue included cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells (Figure 3A). Density plot revealed that SPON1 expression was highly concentrated within specific cell clusters (Figure 3B). Dot plot and violin plot showed that SPON1 expression was highly specific, being predominantly enriched in fibroblasts and adipocytes, with negligible expression in other cell types such as cardiomyocytes or immune cells (Figure 3C and 3D). Within the fibroblast population, SPON1 levels showed a positive correlation with COL1A1 (Pearson R=0.678, P<0.001; Figure 3E). Dual feature plots showed spatial colocalization of SPON1 and COL1A1 within the fibroblast clusters (Figure 3F), while feature plots displayed a gradient of SPON1 expression across the cellular landscape (Figure 3G). The top 50 genes coexpressed with SPON1 in fibroblasts were enriched in biological processes such as extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, collagen fibril organization, and cell substrate adhesion (Figure 3H). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and molecular function analyses showed enrichment in integrin binding, focal adhesion, and the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway (Figure 3H).

External Validation of SPON1 Specificity in Independent Human Cohorts

Consistent with our primary single‐cell analysis, density plot visualization in the Human Heart Cell Atlas data set revealed that SPON1 expression was concentrated within specific clusters (Figure 5A). Uniform manifold approximation and projection clustering and dot plot analysis of 704 256 cells confirmed enrichment in fibroblasts and adipocytes (Figure 5B and 5C). Analysis of representative tissue sections from the left atrium (Figure 5D through 5F) and right atrium (Figure 5G through 5I) revealed a distinct spatial expression pattern. SPON1 expression was nonuniformly distributed and was specifically localized to regions of subepicardial fat and fibrosis (Figure 5F and 5I). In the left atrium, SPON1 was spatially colocalized with both subepicardial and subendocardial fibrosis (Figure 5F), while in the right atrium, it showed enrichment in the subepicardial adipose layer (Figure 5I). Immunohistochemistry data from the Human Protein Atlas showed that SPON1 protein was localized to the interstitial space of heart muscle (Figure 5J). Furthermore, Human Protein Atlas single‐cell data ranked adipocytes, epicardial cells, and fibroblasts as the top‐expressing cell types (Figure 5K).

Circulating SPON1 Is Associated With Incident AF and Restrictive Cardiac Remodeling in the UK Biobank

In the prospective cohort of 39 372 participants free of AF at baseline (Table 1), we observed a robust association between circulating SPON1 levels and incident AF risk. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship, with participants in the highest quartile (quartile 4) of plasma SPON1 exhibiting the lowest AF‐free survival probability compared with those in the lowest quartile (quartile 1) (P<0.001; Figure 6A). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship (P nonlinear=0.003), with risk escalating more rapidly at higher protein concentrations (Figure 6B). In multivariable‐adjusted Cox regression models, each SD increase in plasma SPON1 levels was associated with a 17% increased risk of developing AF (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.12–1.22]; P<0.001; Table 2). Subgroup analyses confirmed the consistency of this association across major clinical strata.

To investigate the structural mechanism underlying the increased AF risk, we analyzed cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging data in a subcohort of 6928 participants free of AF at baseline. Multivariable linear regression revealed a distinct pattern of cardiac remodeling associated with elevated SPON1. Higher plasma SPON1 levels were significantly associated with an increased LAVI (β=0.280, P=0.005; Figure 6D). No significant associations were observed between SPON1 and systolic function parameters, including left atrial ejection fraction and left ventricular ejection fraction (Figure 6D).

Elevated SPON1 Is Associated With Heart Failure and Death in Patients With Established AF

In 931 participants with prevalent AF at baseline, baseline plasma SPON1 levels significantly stratified the risk of adverse outcomes over a median follow‐up of 14.4 years (Tables S8 and S9). Patients in the highest quartile of SPON1 exhibited lower heart failure‐free survival (P<0.0001; Figure 7A) and overall survival (P<0.0001; Figure 7B). Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a linear association between SPON1 levels and the risk of incident heart failure (P nonlinear=0.059) and death (P nonlinear=0.865; Figure 7C and 7D).

In fully adjusted Cox regression models, each SD increase in plasma SPON1 was associated with a 35% increased risk of incident heart failure (HR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.18–1.55]; P<0.001) and a 51% increased risk of all‐cause death (HR, 1.51 [95% CI, 1.36–1.68]; P<0.001; Table 2). Experimental Validation of SPON1 In Vivo and In Vitro

To provide direct experimental evidence for the biological function of SPON1, we performed validation studies in both murine models and cell cultures (Figure 8A and 8B). Immunofluorescence staining of atrial tissue from a murine AF model revealed that SPON1 protein was localized to the perivascular and interstitial spaces (Figure 8C). SPON1 showed spatial colocalization with the myofibroblast marker α‐smooth muscle actin (Figure 8C). In cell culture experiments, stimulation of fibroblasts with recombinant SPON1 resulted in a significant upregulation of markers associated with fibroblast activation, including Acta2 (P<0.001) and Postn (P<0.001; Figure 8D). Furthermore, the expression of major ECM components, including Col1a1 (P<0.01) and Fn1 (P<0.01), was markedly elevated following stimulation (Figure 8D). These findings observed in murine models and cell cultures were consistent with the fibroblast‐derived origin identified in the single‐cell analyses.

DISCUSSION

In this multiomics and clinical cohort study, we identify SPON1 as a potential target and prognostic biomarker for AF. By integrating genetic, molecular, and clinical analyses, we constructed a coherent evidence chain supporting a role for SPON1 in AF pathogenesis. Genetic prioritization using plasma proteomic and cardiac transcriptomic MR, along with colocalization analysis, identified SPON1 as a candidate causal factor. Subsequent single‐cell and spatial analyses elucidated its primary cellular sources and linked SPON1 expression to ECM remodeling pathways. These findings were further validated in a clinical context, where circulating SPON1 levels were associated with incident AF, adverse cardiac structural remodeling, and independently stratified the risk of heart failure and death among patients with established AF.

While SPON1 has been historically recognized for its roles in neuronal development and inflammatory regulation, its functional significance within the cardiovascular system remains a relatively new area of exploration. 36 Although recent large‐scale proteomic studies suggested a potential link between circulating SPON1 and cardiovascular disease, the specific cellular niche and biological contribution of this protein within the human atrium have not been previously characterized. 37 , 38 Our study fills this critical knowledge gap by providing a high‐resolution mapping of SPON1 within the cardiac microenvironment. By demonstrating its spatial enrichment in remodeling regions and identifying activated fibroblasts and adipocytes as its primary sources, we provide the necessary biological context to interpret the association between systemic SPON1 levels and AF risk.

Specifically, our single‐cell transcriptomic analyses suggested that the involvement of SPON1 in AF may be mediated through atrial remodeling. We observed that SPON1 is not only predominantly expressed by activated fibroblasts but is also highly coexpressed with established fibrosis markers, including COL1A1. Notably, the spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that SPON1 is enriched within the subepicardial adipose and fibrotic regions. The accumulation of epicardial adipose tissue and its infiltration into the atrial myocardium are recognized substrates for AF, potentially promoting conduction heterogeneity through structural separation of cardiomyocytes or local paracrine effects. 8 , 9 , 39 The dual expression pattern of SPON1 suggests that cardiac adipocytes, in addition to fibroblasts, may serve as a local cellular source of SPON1, thereby contributing to the ECM environment at the fibro–fatty interface. Meanwhile, our bioinformatic analysis identified activated fibroblasts characterized by the upregulation of contractile and matrix‐synthetic genes, within which SPON1 expression was enriched. This suggests that SPON1 may also participate in fibroblast phenotype transition. Our in vitro experiments showed that recombinant SPON1 induced the expression of ACTA2, Fn1, Col1a1, and Postn in fibroblasts. Enrichment of SPON1‐associated genes in ECM organization, integrin signaling, and focal adhesion pathways further supports a role in regulating cell–matrix interactions.

Consistent with these molecular data, elevated circulating SPON1 level was associated with increased LAVI in individuals free of baseline AF. This association aligns with the classical paradigm of atrial remodeling, where fibroblast activation and excessive matrix deposition lead to changes in atrial compliance and subsequent chamber dilation. 40 , 41 Such structural remodeling provides a potent substrate for AF initiation and maintenance by promoting conduction slowing and heterogeneity. 42 , 43 The observation that SPON1 levels are associated with a higher relative risk for all‐cause death than for incident heart failure is noteworthy. This divergence may suggest that SPON1 reflects aspects of disease severity. One potential explanation is that elevated SPON1 serves as a marker for a more advanced stage of atrial structural remodeling and fibrotic substrate. Severe atrial fibrosis is a well‐recognized risk factor for thromboembolic complications and stroke, which are leading causes of death in patients with AF. 44 Furthermore, considering its established roles in other biological pathways, circulating SPON1 might also integrate noncardiovascular risks or systemic vulnerabilities that influence overall survival in this population. Future studies with specific causes of death are needed to further elucidate the biological basis of this risk divergence.

To reinforce the causal inference of our study, we used MR to address the challenge of reverse causation. Because genetic variants are randomly assorted at conception and remain largely fixed throughout life, they precede the development of clinical AF. 45 Under the core assumptions of instrumental variable analysis, this temporal precedence minimizes the risk that the observed associations are simply the result of the disease state altering protein levels. Consequently, MR can proxy the design of a randomized controlled trial to some extent, offering stronger causal support than traditional observational studies by reducing susceptibility to confounding and reverse causality. 46

Our multiomics framework further corroborated the involvement of additional biological pathways in AF pathogenesis. The identification of CD40 as a high‐confidence causal target highlights the established role of inflammation, complementing the fibrotic mechanisms associated with SPON1. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of the AF substrate and suggest that effective disease modification may necessitate combination strategies addressing both inflammatory and structural remodeling.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although MR supports causal inference, the clinical associations observed in cohort analyses remain observational and may be affected by residual confounding. The genetic analyses were conducted predominantly in individuals of European ancestry, and extrapolation to other populations requires further study. Proteomic measurements were obtained at a single time point, limiting assessment of temporal changes in SPON1 during disease progression. In addition, while we provided initial experimental validation of SPON1 effects, the precise molecular signaling pathways require further elucidation in comprehensive in vivo models, such as genetic knockout or overexpression. Finally, selection bias may exist in cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging cohorts.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this integrative multiomics study identifies SPON1 as a suggestive candidate with potential causal associations and prognostic value for AF. Our findings implicate SPON1 in a pathway of adverse atrial remodeling driven by fibroblast activation and ECM deposition. Together, these results support SPON1 as a potential therapeutic target for future strategies aimed at modifying the structural substrate of AF. Future prospective studies are warranted to formally evaluate its predictive utility for clinical risk stratification.
