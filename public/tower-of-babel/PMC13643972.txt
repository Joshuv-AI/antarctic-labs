Differential effects of primary and latent syphilis co-infection on immune reconstitution and virological failure in HIV-positive adults on ART
Authors: Lingen Shi, Xiaoyan Liu, Tao Qiu, Qunyi Ding, Liping Chen, Yuheng Chen, Jing Lu, Haiyang Hu, Xiaoqin Xu, Danan Yang, Zhiwei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV and syphilis are sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) that share transmission routes and risks factors. Many countries face the dual challenge of HIV and syphilis epidemics. Co-infection can mutually accelerate disease progression and increase transmission risk, posing a major challenge to global public health.MethodsThis retrospective study was based on a cohort of people living with HIV (PLWH). All PLWH diagnosed between 2016 and 2023 were included. The Chi-square test was used to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between groups. Factors associated with syphilis co-infection were assessed using multivariate logistic regression, with adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (95%CI) estimated. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate the cumulative probability of immune reconstitution (IR) and virological failure (VF) in syphilis co-infection versus HIV mono-infected individuals. Multivariate Cox regression was used to evaluate adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) and 95% CI for factors associated with IR and VF. To reduce potential confounding, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline covariates between the syphilis co-infection and HIV mono-infection groups. All statistical analyses were performed by SPSS 23.0 and R 4.3.3.ResultsAmong 39,924 PLWH in Jiangsu Province (2016–2023), the prevalence of syphilis co-infection was 14.1% (5,645/39,924). Factors independently associated with higher odds of co-infection included age < 60 years, male sex, current HIV stage, single, Han ethnicity, history of STDs, and homosexual HIV transmission. In the IR analysis, 56.6% of PLWH achieved IR. After stratifying by syphilis stage, primary syphilis was associated with a higher probability of IR before matching (log rank P < 0.001), but this difference disappeared after PSM (log rank P = 0.99). Latent syphilis showed no significant association with IR. In the virological failure (VF) analysis, latent syphilis co-infection was associated with an increased risk of VF in Cox regression models both before (log rank P = 0.0011) and after PSM (log rank P = 0.0032). Primary syphilis had no significant effect on VF. Younger age at ART initiation, early HIV stage, higher baseline CD4+ T cell count, and college or higher education were protective against VF and/or promoted IR.ConclusionsHIV/syphilis co-infection prevalence was high among PLWH in Jiangsu. Latent syphilis co-infection independently increases the risk of VF. Younger age at ART initiation, current HIV stage, and higher education protected against VF and/or promoted IR; male, migrant, and homosexual HIV transmission were risk factors for poorer IR. Introduction

Both HIV and syphilis are sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) that share transmission and risk factors [1, 2]. Many countries currently face the dual challenges of HIV and syphilis epidemics. Globally, estimated new syphilis cases among adults aged 15–49 rose from 7.1 million in 2020 to 8.0 million in 2022 [3], while the number of people living with HIV (PLWH) increased from 32 million in 2010 to 40.4 million in 2023 [4]. The prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection has also increased in recent years, with reported rates varying by region: 2.99% in Asia (2000–2020) [5], 7.3% in Africa (2011–2022) [6], and 36.6% in Europe (2018–2020) [7]. In China, a similar trend exists: the incidence of syphilis increased from 26.86/100,000 in 2010 to 37.60/100,000 in 2023 [8], and the number of HIV infections grew from 780,000 in 2011 [9] to 1,230,000 in 2018 [10]. A meta-analysis further reported that a syphilis co-infection rate of 19.9% among Chinese PLWH between 2013 and 2022 [11].

Syphilis and HIV have a mutually reinforcing relationship. HIV infection affects syphilis acquisition, clinical presentation, diagnosis, progression, and treatment. By impairing immunity, HIV increases the risk of syphilis acquisition and may accelerate disease progression, blur stage distinctions, and worsen neurocognitive impairment [12]. PLWH also have a higher rate of syphilis treatment failure than HIV-negative individuals [13].

Conversely, syphilis infection facilitates HIV acquisition and transmission through inducing inflammation and epithelium disruption, which recruit immune cells susceptible to HIV [14, 15]. Among untreated PLWH, syphilis co-infection is associated with decreased CD4+ T cell count and elevated plasma HIV-1 RNA levels [16].

Despite this well-established bidirectional interaction, previous findings have been limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent definitions of serological failure, high rates of loss to follow-up, and unclear temporal sequence between syphilis and HIV infections. Moreover, the impact of syphilis co-infection on long-term immune reconstitution (IR) and virological response among PLWH receiving long-term antiretroviral therapy (ART) remains poorly understood. To address this gap, this study used large-scale, long-term surveillance data from Jiangsu Province, China, to describe the characteristics of HIV/syphilis co-infection and evaluate its effect on IR and virological failure (VF) in PLWH receiving ART.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective study, based on an HIV-related healthcare system, collected date from web-based Comprehensive Response Information Management System (CRISM), a real-time notifiable infectious disease reporting system in China. We screened 39,924 patients diagnosed with HIV/AIDS from 59 ART centers between 2016 and 2023. During the same period, 215,567 patients diagnosed with syphilis were also recorded by CRISM. Thus, we used patients diagnosed with HIV/AIDS as reference cases and matched syphilis cases to HIV/AIDS cases by identification card. This yielded 5,645 PLWH with syphilis co-infection. First, we analyzed risk factors for syphilis co-infection, then examined potential risk factors for IR and VF, focusing on the impact of syphilis co-infection (see Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study inclusion and exclusion criteria

For IR, the inclusion criteria were as follow: (1) ART-naive patients enrolled in the HIV surveillance system between 2016 and 2023, (2) complete data on age, sex, syphilis infection stage, ART initiation time, transmission route, specimen origin, history of STDs, marital status, and education level, (3) CD4 testing (baseline CD4+ T cell count < 500 cells/µL) pre- and post-diagnosis and HIV RNA, with continuous ART for ≥ 24 months. For VF, the inclusion criteria were as follow: (1) ART-naive patients enrolled in the HIV surveillance system between 2016 and 2023, (2) complete data on age, sex, syphilis infection stage, ART initiation time, transmission route, specimen origin, history of STDs, marital status, and education level, (3) ART duration > 24 weeks.

Definitions

Baseline CD4+ T cell count was defined as any measurement obtained within the 6 months prior to 7 days after ART initiation. For repeated measurements, the one closest ART initiation was selected. PLWH receive one free CD4+ T cell count and viral load (VL) test annually to assess treatment outcomes. Virological suppression was defined as sustained VL below the detection limit (50 copies/mL) after 24 weeks of continuous ART throughout the observation period. IR was defined as sustained complete viral suppression on ART and a CD4+ T cell count ≥ 500 cells/µL at any time during the observation period. VF was defined as either (1) VL > 200 copies/mL after 24 weeks of continuous ART, or (2) virological rebound (VL ≥ 200 copies/mL after prior complete suppression) at any time during the entire observation period.

Data collection and management procedures

Retrospective demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were retrieved from CRISM. Demographic variables included sex, age at diagnosis, registration status, marital status, education level, and ethnicity, recorded at the time of HIV or syphilis diagnosis. Clinical and laboratory variables included HIV transmission route, specimen origin, syphilis infection stage, WHO-defined HIV stage, and CD4+ T cell count.

Diagnosis of syphilis and HIV

Treponemal tests were performed using Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA) or enzyme-linked Immunosorbent assay (TP-ELISA). When TP antibodies were positive, non-treponema pallidum testing was performed using the toluidine red untreated serum test (TRUST) or rapid plasma regain (RPR). Diagnosis was made by trained clinicians based on sexual history, clinical manifestations, and laboratory results, according to established syphilis diagnostic criteria for syphilis [17]. All diagnosed syphilis cases were classified as primary, secondary, tertiary, or latent. Data for primary and secondary syphilis were categorized as “primary syphilis” in this study. Data for latent syphilis was categorized as “latent syphilis”. No tertiary syphilis cases were identified in this study.

HIV infection was identified via a two-step process. Blood samples were first screened for HIV antibodies using ELISA; positive specimens were then confirmed by Western Blot.

Individuals positive on both assays were classified as having HIV infection. Clinical staging of HIV infection was based on clinical manifestations and CD4⁺ T cell count at diagnosis.

Individuals were classified as HIV stage if they had no or only mild symptoms at diagnosis (e.g., unexplained weight loss > 10%, recurrent upper respiratory infections, or herpes zoster) or a CD4⁺ T cell count ≥ 200 cells/µL. Individuals were classified as AIDS stage if they had an AIDS-defining illness (e.g., persistent diarrhea, prolonged fever, or pulmonary tuberculosis) or a CD4⁺ T cell count < 200 cells/µL. For participants whose current clinical stage changed during follow-up, the most recent classification was used.

Statistical analysis

The Chi-square test was used to compare demographic differences between groups. Factors associated with syphilis co-infection among PLWH were assessed using multivariate logistic regression, with adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the cumulative probability of IR and VF following ART initiation, comparing patients with HIV/AIDS alone versus those with syphilis co-infection. Multivariate Cox regression was used to evaluate adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) with 95% CI for factors associated with IR and VF. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0.

To reduce potential confounding, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline covariates between the syphilis co-infection and HIV mono-infection groups. Propensity score-defined as the probability of HIV/syphilis co-infection conditional on baseline covariates-were estimated using a multivariate logistic regression model. We then performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, using a caliper of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score.

Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean difference (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 indicating acceptable balance. Variance ratios and propensity score distributions were also examined. Any covariate with residual imbalance (SMD ≥ 0.1) was additionally adjusted for in subsequent outcome models.

All matching procedures were implemented in R version 4.3.3. using the MatchIt package.

Ethical statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Chinese National Center for AIDS/STD Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, P.R. China (IRB Application no.:X170113431). All participants provided written informed consent. Before data analysis, we removed patient identifiers (name, ID number, phone number, and home address). All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant regulations and institutional guidelines.

Participant characteristics

Between 2016 and 2023, 39,924 PLWH were reported in Jiangsu Province, China, of whom 5,645 (14.1%) had syphilis co-infection. Of these, 85.9% (34,277) initiated ART, and 67.1% (22,985) of those continued ART for > 2 years. Baseline CD4+ T cell count was available for 85.6% (19,671) of PLWH, of these, 82.5% (16,225) had baseline CD4+ T cell count < 500 cells/μL. Among those with syphilis co-infection, 2,480 met the inclusion criteria for IR analysis, and 4,794 for VF analysis (Fig. 1).

Table 1 presented the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. The median age was 39.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 28.0-53.0). Most PLWH were male (87.1%), and 71.9% were registered residents of Jiangsu Province. Sexual contact was the reported route of HIV acquisition in 99.0% of participants. Over half (55.1%) were diagnosed at medical institution. Significant differences were observed between the syphilis co-infection and the HIV mono-infection groups across multiple factors, sociodemographic characteristics (age at diagnosis, gender, migrant status, current clinical stage, marital status, ethnicity, and education level), infectious features (HIV transmission route and specimen origin), baseline CD4+ T cell count, and history of STDs.

Factors associated with syphilis co-infection in PLWH

In multivariate logistic regression, several factors were significantly associated with increased risk of syphilis co-infection among PLWH. These included age < 60 years (vs. ≥60, < 30: aOR:1.407, 95% CI:1.238–1.599; 30–59: aOR:1.239, 95% CI:1.111–1.381); male sex (vs. female, aOR:1.948, 95% CI:1.682–2.256); current HIV stage (vs. AIDS stage, aOR:1.141, 95% CI:1.032–1.260); single status (vs. married, aOR:1.173, 95% CI:1.089–1.262); Han ethnicity (vs. other ethnicity, aOR:1.771, 95% CI:1.361–2.303); college or higher (vs. high school or lower, aOR:1.092, 95% CI:1.018–1.172), and history of STDs (vs. no such history, aOR:3.788, 95% CI:3.538–4.056). Additionally, participants who acquired HIV via homosexual contact were more likely to have syphilis co-infection than those via heterosexual contact (aOR:1.328, 95% CI:1.226–1.438) (Table 2).

Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that PLWH who initiated ART at age < 60 years (< 30: aHR:1.655, 95% CI:1.507–1.817; 30–59: aHR:1.479, 95% CI:1.363–1.605) had a significantly higher likelihood of achieving IR than those aged ≥ 60 years (Table 4). PLWH with current HIV stage was significantly more likely to achieved IR than those with AIDS stage (aHR:4.017, 95% CI:3.707–4.353). PLWH with college or higher education were significantly more likely to achieved IR than those with high school or lower (aHR:1.132, 95% CI:1.079–1.189). Compared with PLWH specimen origin medical institution, those specimen origin key population (aHR:1.274, 95% CI:1.168–1.390) or detention facility (aHR:1.290, 95% CI:1.184–1.406) were more likely to achieve IR. Compared with PLWH with baseline CD4+ T cell count < 100 cells/µL, those with higher count were more likely to achieved IR (100–199 cells/µL: aHR:1.763, 95% CI:1.581–1.965; 200–349 cells/µL: aHR:1.303, 95% CI:1.166–1.456; 350–499 cells/µL: aHR:2.566, 95% CI:2.291–2.875). In contrast, male sex (vs. female, aHR:0.860, 95% CI:0.800-0.926), migrant (vs. non-migrant, aHR:0.728, 95% CI:0.690–0.769), and homosexual transmission (vs. heterosexual, aHR:0.906, 95% CI:0.859–0.955) were associated with a lower likelihood of achieving IR (Table 4).

Effect of syphilis co-infection on Post-ART VF in PLWH

In total, 31,028 PLWH met the VF analysis criteria. Among them, 4,794 with syphilis co-infection and 26,234 HIV mono-infected individuals were included (Fig.1). Overall, 2.3% (721) experienced VF (Table 3).

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study of PLWH in Jiangsu Province, China, (2016-2023), Syphilis co-infection was prevalent among PLWH in Jiangsu from 2016 to 2023, with an overall prevalence of 14.1%.

Meanwhile, syphilis co-infection was significantly associated with IR and VF after ART. Our key findings include: (1) younger age at ART initiation, current HIV stage, and higher education were protective factors for IR and against VF; (2) male sex, migrant, and homosexual HIV transmission were risk factors for IR; (3) primary syphilis co-infection was associated with a higher probability of achieving IR before PSM, but this advantage disappeared after PSM, suggesting confounding by sociodemographic and clinical factors; (4) latent syphilis co-infection was associated with an increased risk of VF both before and after PSM, suggesting a potential adverse effect.

In this study, the overall prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection was 14.1%, substantially higher than rates reported in neighboring provinces (e.g., Shanghai and Zhejiang) [18, 19]. This finding indicates that PLWH constitute a key high-risk population and highlights the urgent need for integrated prevention strategies targeting this group. Although HIV screening in Jiangsu Province expanded considerably from 4.87 million in 2008 to 10.92 million in 2020, syphilis screening did not keep pace: the volume of syphilis tests in 2020 (4.89 million) was comparable only to that of HIV tests in 2008. The co-infection rate was higher among younger individuals. Several factors may explain this distribution among younger PLWH: high sexual activity, frequent condomless sex [20], declining age of sexual debut [21, 22], a disconnect between HIV-related knowledge and actual sexual behaviors [23], and risk compensation among some pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) users who mistakenly believed that PrEP also protects against other STDs [24]. Among men who have sex with men (MSM), high-risk behaviors (e.g., multiple irregular partners, condomless sex) and widespread asymptomatic syphilis transmission persist [25, 26]. Although HIV prevalence among MSM declined from 8.8% in 2013 to 4.5% in 2019, syphilis prevalence increased from 5.5% to 11.2% over the same period [27, 28]. Collectively, these observations underscore the urgent necessity for integrated HIV/STDs prevention strategies that simultaneously address gaps in screening coverage and high-risk sexual behaviors, especially among young PLWH.

Our findings indicate that latent syphilis co-infection facilitates VF, consistent with previous studies showing that co-infection with other STDs impairs viral suppression. Previous studies have suggested that HIV/Syphilis co-infection may negatively affected IR and ART effectiveness, possibly through TP and its lipoproteins inducing HIV-1 gene expression in monocytes via NF-KappaB activation. However, these mechanistic insights were largely derived from in vitro or observational data and require further validation.

Several studies have reported that syphilis co-infections may increase HIV VL and delay immune recovery.

Our crude analysis showed that primary syphilis co-infection was associated with better IR after ART; however, this association disappeared after PSM. After controlling for confounding via PSM, this association disappeared, indicating that the crude association was likely confounded rather than directly causal. This highlights the importance of rigorous adjustment-a point-less emphasized in some previous studies. Our results contrast with some smaller studies that found no significant impact of syphilis co-infection on ART outcomes, maybe explained by differences in sample size, follow-up duration, and adjustment for confounders [29].

Several mechanisms may explain our findings. First, primary syphilis is typically accompanied by a strong acute inflammatory response that may temporarily enhance immune surveillance. TP can stimulate immune activation in PLWH through pathogen-associated molecular patterns recognized by dendritic cells [30], induces interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion by macrophages [31], and promotes the production of tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-6 production by activeated T cells in early syphilis lesions [32].

Concurrently, HIV-1 infection modulates host innate immunity by altering Toll-like receptors expression, thereby regulating the killing of HIV infected T cells [33]. However, this effect is likely confounded by sociological and behavioral factors. In contrast, latent syphilis represents a chronic persistent infection that may induce sustained immune activation and inflammation, leading to increased HIV replication and impaired ART efficacy or adherence.

Second, the protective effects of younger age, HIV stage, and higher baseline CD4+ T cell count reflect better immune function and treatment adherence. Third, the persistent risk of VF after PSM suggests that latent syphilis may affect virological outcomes, although unmeasured factors (e.g., adherence and treatment related variables) cannot be excluded, and the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Our findings have important implications for strengthening HIV healthcare in regions with high syphilis burden. First, the high prevalence of syphilis co-infection and its impact on VF underscored the need for integrated sexual health services for PLWH. Second, expanded screening and timely treatment of syphilis-especially latent syphilis-should be integrated into HIV management to reduce VF. Third, PLWH with latent syphilis required frequent VL monitoring and adherence support. Fourth, the observed disparities of sociodemographic and behavioral factors can guide resource allocation, particularly for PLWH who are older, male, or have lower education.

This study has several strengths. It is based on a large, real-world cohort with long-term follow-up and comprehensive data collection, enabling robust adjustment for multiple confounders. The PSM model minimized selection bias and improved comparability between the syphilis co-infection and HIV mono-infection groups. We also distinguished primary from latent syphilis, improving nuanced insights.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, Sselection bias may pose a threaten to the representativeness of our: nearly 15% of PLWH had no recorded baseline CD4+ T cells count or did not initiate ART, underestimating the co-infection or IR rate. Second, the observational design cannot establish causality; residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., behavioral risks such as condomless sex and multiple partners, self-reported data on sexual orientation and condom use, ART adherence, and genetic factors) may persist, with self-reported variables particularly susceptible to reporting bias. Third, syphilis status was determined by routine clinical diagnosis and serology, which may misclassify infection or stage. Moreover, detailed information on syphilis, treatment history and longitudinal RPR titers was unavailable, limiting our ability to assess the effect of syphilis dynamics. Fourth, data on specific ART regimens and adherence were lacking, both of which could influence VF. Fifth, co-infection with HBV or HCV-which may further affect IR-was not considered in this analysis. Sixth, the sample size for some subgroup analyses (e.g., primary syphilis after PSM) was relatively small, reducing statistical power. Seventh, generalizability may be limited to the Jiangsu population, which had a high proportion of male and sexually active PLWH. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and long-term follow-up reveal a high prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection, underscoring the urgent need for integrated prevention efforts targeting both infections.

Conclusions

In this large cohort of PLWH in Jiangsu Province, latent syphilis co-infection was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of VF, whereas primary syphilis showed no sustained effect on IR after confounders adjustment. Younger age at ART initiation, current HIV stage, and higher education were protective factors for IR and against VF, while male sex, migrant status, and homosexual transmission were risk factors for poorer IR. Given the high prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection, these findings underscore the urgent need for integrated syphilis screening and management within HIV care to optimize long-term treatment outcomes in China
