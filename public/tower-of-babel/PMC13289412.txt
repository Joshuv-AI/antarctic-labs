Assessing mpox knowledge, attitude and willingness to accept the mpox vaccine among people living with HIV and men who have sex with men in selected health facilities in Rivers State, Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chizaram Onyeaghala, Vivian Ifeoma Ogbonna, Ifeyinwa Philippa Ugboma, Nelson Oruh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLimited data exist on the knowledge of and attitudes towards mpox and willingness to accept the repurposed mpox vaccine among vulnerable populations, such as people living with HIV (PLHIV) and men who have sex with men (MSM), in countries with overlapping HIV and mpox epidemics, like Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from 26 August 2024 to September 2024, involving 290 PLHIV and 14 MSM at two selected health facilities in Rivers State, Nigeria, using systematic and snowball sampling techniques, respectively. A self-administered web-based Google form was used to collect sociodemographic data, mpox knowledge, attitudes towards the disease and willingness to receive the mpox vaccine. The χ2 test was used to explore associations between sociodemographic factors and vaccine acceptance. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to identify determinants of the willingness to vaccinate.ResultsAlthough most participants (72.7%; n=221) were aware of the disease, 54.3% (n=165) had poor knowledge of mpox. While 60.5% (n=184) considered mpox a serious disease, 60.2% (n=182) were willing to receive the mpox vaccine, though concerns about side effects and mistrust in health systems were major barriers. Sex (χ2=16.19; p<0.001), level of education completed (χ2=37.63; p<0.001) and marital status (χ2=15.01; p<0.019) were associated with knowledge of mpox. Age and knowledge level were significantly associated with willingness to accept the vaccine. Those aged≤24 years (n=14; 100.0%) and those with good knowledge (n=58; 72.5%) of mpox were more willing to accept the vaccine than those with poor knowledge (χ2=7.272; p<0.027).ConclusionsThe study revealed limited knowledge of mpox, low perceived risk and poor vaccine acceptance, despite relatively high awareness in the study population. Targeted public health education and confidence-building strategies are valuable to boost vaccine uptake in these at-risk groups.Keywords: HIV, Disease Outbreaks, Vaccination

Introduction

Human mpox is an emerging zoonotic infection caused by the monkeypox virus (MPXV), a DNA virus in the genus Orthopoxvirus and family Poxviridae.1 2 Historically confined to parts of Central and West Africa, the disease became a global concern following the 2022 outbreak that predominantly affected non-endemic countries.3 There are essentially two clades of MPXV (clades I and II), subdivided into ‘a’ and ‘b’ variants. Clade II typically causes milder clinical disease than clade I, which is associated with a more severe clinical course and higher mortality.4 Mpox typically begins with systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, muscle pain and back pain, followed by skin eruptions and lymphadenopathy, and spontaneously recovers, although it can be severe in pregnant women, newborns, cases of chickenpox coinfection and people living with HIV (PLHIV).5,8

The 2022–2023 global mpox outbreak disproportionately involved men who have sex with men (MSM) and brought to the forefront the contribution of sexual routes of MPXV transmission.3 Studies from Nigeria have historically not involved the MSM population because of stigma, discrimination, criminalisation of same-sex relationships and limited access to healthcare.9 10 PLHIV are at increased risk for severe mpox and death due to their immunocompromised state and other social factors.11 Despite the high vulnerability of these at-risk groups, knowledge about mpox and its prevention remains limited.

Vaccination remains one of the most effective public health interventions to control the spread of mpox in the community.12,14 However, vaccine uptake is influenced by several factors, including individual knowledge, cultural beliefs, healthcare trust and stigma—factors that are particularly salient in Nigeria, where PLHIV and MSM often experience systemic healthcare discrimination.15 16 Because vaccine hesitancy (refusal or delay of vaccination despite available services) emerged as an increasingly important global health threat following the COVID-19 pandemic, it became imperative to explore willingness to receive vaccines to successfully design and implement targeted public health strategies to control the spread of the virus.17 18

As Rivers State reports new cases of mpox, there is a pressing need to evaluate awareness levels, attitudes towards prevention and the willingness to accept the repurposed mpox vaccine among these high-risk populations. Without addressing these knowledge gaps, public health responses may fall short in mitigating the mpox outbreak. This is particularly important, as Rivers State is one of the high-burden states for mpox and is prioritised for vaccine deployment.

This study aims to address this gap by evaluating knowledge, attitudes and the willingness to receive vaccines among PLHIV and MSM in selected health facilities in Rivers State, Nigeria. The findings of this study are expected to support policymakers, public health professionals and HIV programme managers in designing effective mpox vaccination strategies. By addressing knowledge and perception gaps, the study also seeks to enhance vaccine confidence, reduce transmission and ultimately protect those most at risk.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted at two health facilities in Rivers State, Nigeria: the University of Port Harcourt Teaching Hospital (UPTH) and the Initiative for Advancement of Humanity (IAH). Rivers State, located in southern Nigeria, is an oil-rich state that serves as a major centre of commerce and healthcare in the Niger Delta. The state features a diverse population, including urban and peri-urban communities, with a mix of ethnic and socioeconomic groups. The tertiary hospital chosen for this study, UPTH, is a leading healthcare institution in Rivers State, known for its comprehensive medical services, including specialised care for PLHIV. This hospital has more than 500 beds and offers tertiary, secondary and primary healthcare services, especially as many other facilities in the region are nearing collapse. The hospital maintains a strong HIV/AIDS treatment and care programme, providing antiretroviral therapy, counselling and other support services. It also serves as a referral centre for many infectious diseases, including emerging zoonoses such as mpox. The other facility, IAH, caters to a significant number of MSM, a high-risk group for mpox, making it an ideal site to study knowledge, attitudes and willingness to accept the mpox vaccine. The HIV clinic at the UPTH in Rivers State has approximately 5700 active clients, the largest HIV care and treatment clinic in Nigeria’s South-South geopolitical zone. The study area also benefits from support groups that work to increase awareness and access to healthcare among key populations. Rivers State’s public health infrastructure emphasises disease surveillance and vaccination campaigns, making it a relevant setting for examining vaccine acceptance.

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to survey participants at the two selected health facilities in Rivers State.

Study population

The study participants include PLHIV attending the HIV clinic at UPTH and MSM attending IAH in Rivers State, Nigeria. Those who were too ill to respond to the questions and those aged less than 18 years were excluded.

Sample size determination

We used Cochran’s formula (n=Z2pq/d2) for sample size calculation (where n=required sample size; Z=1.96 at a 95% CI; p=estimated proportion of the population with the attribute; q=1-p; e= 5% margin of error) for cross-sectional studies to estimate the sample size for the study.19 Since we found no existing data evaluating the knowledge, attitudes and willingness of PLHIV to accept the mpox vaccine in Nigeria, we used the proportion of mpox vaccination intention among high-risk populations from a previous study in Nigeria, which was 83%.20 With a 10% non-response rate, our minimum sample size was 241. We surveyed a total of 304 respondents.

Sampling technique

A systematic sampling method was used to recruit participants from the clinic. The HIV clinic operates 4 days a week (from Monday to Thursday) and attends to 200–250 clients per clinic day. On each clinic day, the first eligible participant was selected using simple random sampling by balloting. Subsequently, every fourth eligible client presenting to the clinic was recruited into the study. Recruitment was carried out consecutively on each clinic day in accordance with the study’s inclusion criteria until the required sample size was reached. Eligible clients who declined to participate or did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded to maintain the sampling interval. With regard to enrolling participants from the MSM population, a snowball sampling technique was used.

Data collection method

Data were collected using a questionnaire developed from a review of the relevant literature on mpox and mpox vaccines (see online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was developed using Google Forms. Google Forms is an electronic questionnaire software distributed to study participants. It was a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire included the following sections: (a) knowledge of the disease, (b) perception about mpox and (c) willingness to receive the mpox vaccine. All study data were de-identified and stored on password-protected electronic devices. The tool’s reliability and validity were affirmed through pretesting (Cronbach’s alpha=0.78) and expert review. In assessing knowledge, 7 items were used; the correct answers were scored 1, and the wrong answers were scored 0. Knowledge levels were recategorised as Poor (0–2), Fair (3–4) and Good (5–7).

Statistical analysis

The de-identified research data (Excel sheet) was transferred to SPSS V.27 for statistical analysis. Sociodemographic characteristics as well as participants’ responses to the questions were uniformly defined as categorised data and summarised using frequencies and proportions. A χ2 test was used to assess the relationship between the independent variables (sociodemographic information) and the outcome variable (participants’ knowledge of the mpox vaccine, attitude towards the mpox vaccine and willingness to accept the mpox vaccine). Variables with p<0.05 in the χ2 test were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify determinants of awareness and willingness to be vaccinated against mpox among participants. For those variables included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were reported, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Discussion

As far as we know, this is the first comprehensive study to examine knowledge levels and attitudes towards mpox, as well as the willingness to accept the mpox vaccine among PLWH and MSM in Nigeria. The existing literature shows a lack of studies assessing the willingness to receive mpox vaccination among PLHIV and MSM.21,31 Our study data reveal poor knowledge of mpox, limited perceived risk and low vaccine acceptance, despite relatively high awareness of the disease. These findings provide important insights into the behavioural and informational gaps that may influence vaccine uptake among these high-risk groups.

The finding that over half of respondents had poor knowledge of mpox, with only one-quarter demonstrating good understanding, is a significant public health concern that can hinder prevention efforts. Although 72.7% of participants were aware of mpox, this awareness did not lead to a fully accurate or comprehensive understanding of the disease. This knowledge gap is especially concerning since PLHIV and MSM regularly attend HIV treatment and care centres where they often receive counselling and educational sessions, which may have boosted their awareness of the disease. The disparity between awareness and deep understanding of mpox may have contributed to the spread of misinformation, delayed recognition of symptoms and poor health-seeking behaviour during outbreaks, as seen in previous studies about mpox in Nigeria and Cameroon.32 33

These findings align with the results of Al-Mustapha and colleagues, who found that while a large proportion of Nigerians had heard of mpox, only a fraction could correctly identify its transmission routes or prevention methods.34 The limited knowledge level may stem from insufficient public education campaigns and mpox being relatively unfamiliar compared with other common viral infections such as HIV or COVID-19.

Despite limited overall knowledge, 60% of the study participants perceived mpox as a serious disease, consistent with findings from previous studies indicating that perceived disease severity often coexists with poor factual understanding.35,37 This means that the perception of mpox’s severity among the study population might be influenced by other factors such as misinformation, cultural beliefs and historical mistrust in public health interventions. It could also imply that public health campaigns are effectively highlighting the disease’s potential severity. These low levels of risk perception may hinder vaccine uptake and the adoption of safe behaviours, especially in a population already vulnerable due to underlying immunosuppression or risky sexual networks.

Willingness to accept the mpox vaccine was modest at 60.2%, similar to a study among PLHIV in northern Nigeria, which reported willingness to receive the mpox vaccine in 64.4% of respondents.11 Key barriers included low-risk perception, concerns about vaccine safety and mistrust of authorities and pharmaceutical companies. Other reasons for vaccine hesitancy, reported by Iliyasu et al,22 differ from those in the index study and include anxiety about vaccine-antiretroviral drug interactions, perceived protection from antiretroviral treatment and unfamiliarity with the vaccine. Vaccine hesitancy due to low-risk perception, fear of serious adverse events and mistrust of vaccines has also been observed in Nigeria during the COVID-19 pandemic. This hesitancy is likely linked to broader structural mistrust in the healthcare system.38,40

MSM often face criminalisation, stigma and healthcare discrimination, which can discourage engagement with health services and vaccine access. These structural barriers should be addressed through policies that promote equity and inclusion. Community-based organisations working with key populations can act as trusted intermediaries to provide accurate public health information and encourage vaccine uptake.

Age, a key predictor of vaccine hesitancy, was significantly associated with willingness to accept the mpox vaccine in this study. Young people aged below 24 years, followed by seniors, were much more likely to be willing to receive the mpox vaccine. These results may imply that other factors outweigh age in shaping vaccine attitudes within the study population.41 42 Similarly, participants with good knowledge of mpox were much more likely to accept the vaccine. This association underscores the importance of health literacy and targeted messaging to younger, digitally connected populations who may be more receptive to risk communication. Previous studies support the idea that knowledge is a strong predictor of vaccine acceptance, as observed during the COVID-19 vaccination efforts in Africa.43 44

Additionally, educational level significantly influenced vaccine acceptance in this study. Although not statistically significant, 64.2% of individuals with a tertiary education were willing to receive the mpox vaccine, compared with 58.3% of those without formal education and 33.3% of those with informal education. This indicates that informational gaps may hinder vaccine uptake. This finding aligns with a recent Nigerian study in which higher educational attainment was associated with greater knowledge and increased vaccine acceptance.45 Education can improve understanding of disease mechanisms, build trust in the healthcare system and encourage participation in public health initiatives. Therefore, vaccine communication strategies should address educational disparities by providing clear, accessible information about mpox vaccines, especially in stigma-free local languages and simple formats.41

Demographic analysis further revealed that males, individuals with tertiary education and single participants were more likely to demonstrate good knowledge of mpox. This suggests a gender disparity in mpox knowledge in the study population, emphasising the need for targeted vaccine communication strategies incorporating gender-sensitive approaches and prioritising less-educated populations and those in marital relationships who may not perceive themselves as at risk, even when they belong to high-risk groups.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study possesses some strengths that underscore its relevance to the field of public health.

First, including the MSM population, a high-risk but often understudied group in Nigeria, could provide valuable data to guide public health efforts. Second, the timing of this research was particularly strategic, as it coincided with the preparatory phase of Nigeria’s first deployment of the mpox vaccine across seven high-burden states, targeting high-risk groups, including PLHIV. The findings from this study could offer crucial insights to support the deployment of subsequent phases of the mpox vaccine in Nigeria. Finally, combining systematic and snowball sampling methods allowed for accurate data collection from these vulnerable and frequently underrepresented populations.

The study, however, has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design used in our study provides a snapshot of data at a single point in time. While useful for describing prevalence and associations, it does not allow for the examination of changes, such as shifts in mpox knowledge over time or the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Selecting variables for multivariate modelling solely on univariate statistical significance may not fully account for potential confounders, as some variables may not show significant univariate associations yet still influence adjusted estimates. Second, although our sampling method employed a systematic random sampling approach, a design effect was not included in determining the sample size; this may introduce sampling bias, as the findings might not fully represent the broader population of PLHIV in Rivers State or Nigeria. Similarly, the use of snowball non-random sampling to enrol a small number of MSMs may not adequately reflect the wider community’s knowledge and attitudes, thus limiting generalisability. However, the findings can serve as a foundation for further studies in the MSM population. Finally, data collection relied on self-administered questionnaires, which could introduce response bias. Participants may provide socially desirable responses or inaccurately report their knowledge and attitudes towards mpox, potentially affecting the validity of our results.

Conclusions

The gap between disease awareness, actionable knowledge and willingness to vaccinate underscores the need for comprehensive public health education among PLWH and MSM. Integrating mpox education and vaccination services into existing HIV care programmes could also improve accessibility and build trust among patients. These findings have direct implications for Nigeria’s national mpox preparedness and response plan, which now needs to emphasise culturally sensitive education, leverage peer-led and trusted community networks and address structural mistrust. Future policies should aim to promote inclusive, stigma-free access to vaccines, especially for MSM, who often remain underrepresented in public health programmes.
