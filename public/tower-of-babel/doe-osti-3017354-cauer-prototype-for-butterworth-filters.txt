 Abstract
 I had a need to develop a real, physical, analog filter with real RLC components.
Having spent a career in theory, I had only ever designed filters for signal processing.
I needed only the a and b coefficients. But converting a and b coefficients into RLC
values was beyond me. Web searches returned nothing useful until I found Dr. Aaron
Danner’s Designing Passive Butterworth Filters (10 - Passive Filters), part of an ex-
cellent YouTube series which introduced me to the Cauer prototype for Butterworth
filters and Cauer topologies. Failing to find documentation on Cauer filter prototypes,
I asked Anthropic’s Claude AI for assistance. Claude returned a few errors which I
corrected. This is a comprehensive report on what I learned.

1 Introduction
Having spent my career on theory, I never had to design a real, physical, analog filter with
real RLC components. For the 25+ years I have used MATLAB, I used either, for example,
the butter() function or the filterDesigner app shown in Figure 1.
 With the arrival of digital engineering principles at LLNL, I asked MathWorks present
their System Composer using an analog sixth order lowpass Butterworth filter with a 6 Hz
cutoff as an example. Forty plus years ago as an electrical engineering undergraduate, I
learned analog circuitry was not within my academic strengths. I took the required two
semesters of analog circuits and then moved onto controls, signal processing and applied
mathematics.
 My request to MathWorks prompted me to ponder how to design a real, analog Butter-
worth filter. MATLAB’s filterDesigner is excellent at deriving the a and b coefficients
but does not return RLC component values. An internet search eventually led me to Dr.
Aaron Danner’s Designing Passive Butterworth Filters (10 - Passive Filters), part of an ex-
cellent YouTube series which introduced me to the Cauer prototype for Butterworth filters
and Cauer topologies.
 Other than the video series, I found no useful, practical comprehensive documentation
on Cauer Butterworth filter prototypes. I consulted Anthropic’s Claude AI. It returned a
few errors which I corrected. This report is the result of my investigations.

2 Introduction to Cauer Prototype
The Cauer prototype for a lowpass Butterworth filter refers to a ladder network realization
of the Butterworth transfer function using inductors and capacitors. This is one of the
standard prototype circuit topologies for implementing analog filters. I did not learn the
Cauer prototype as an undergraduate. I find it extraordinarily useful.
 For a lowpass Butterworth filter, the Cauer ladder structure prototype consists of:

 • Series inductors (L) in the signal path,

 • Shunt capacitors (C) to ground, and

 • Alternating L-C elements forming a ladder network.

 In a normalized Butterworth lowpass prototype with cutoff frequency ωc = 1 rad s−1 and
source/load impedances of 1 Ω, the element values follow the pattern:

 • For filters starting with a series fed inductor:

 – g1 , g3 , g5 , . . . are inductances,
 – g2 , g4 , g6 , . . . are capacitances.

 • For filters or starting with a shunt fed capacitor:

 – g1 , g3 , g5 , . . . are capacitances,
 – g2 , g4 , g6 , . . . are inductances,

5 Lowpass to Bandstop Transformation
The lowpass-to-bandstop transformation creates frequency traps centered at the frequency
f0 :

 1. Series inductors become parallel LC resonant circuits (in series path): L becomes
       L||C in the series path, creating infinite impedance at f0 .

 2. Shunt capacitors become series LC resonant circuits (to ground): C becomes
 L-C in series to ground, creating zero impedance at f0 .

 3. Each element becomes a trap that blocks or diverts signals at the bandstop frequency.

7 Lowpass Prototype to Highpass, Bandpass and Band-
 stop Filter Design Considerations, Parameters and Ob-
 servations
Table 3 lists the key design parameters for bandpass and bandstop filters.

 Table 3: Filter design parameters.

 Parameter Formula
 Lower Band Limit fL
 High Band Limit fH
 Center Frequency f0 = (fH − fL ) /2
 Bandwidth BW = fH − fL
 Quality Factor Q = f0 /BW
 Fractional Bandwidth FBW = BW/f0 = 1/Q

 Component considerations and observations:

 1. Highpass Filters:

 • Series inductors become series capacitors,
 • Series capacitors become series inductors.

 2. Bandpass Filters:

 • Each LC pair resonates at the center frequency ω0 = 2πf0 ,
 • The bandwidth is controlled by the impedance level of each resonator,
 • Narrower bandwidth requires higher Q components.

 3. Bandstop Filters:

 • Bandstop depth determined by Q of resonators,
 • Component losses limit achievable bandstop depth,
 • Narrow notches require tight component tolerances.

 4. Physical Implementation:

 • Real inductors have finite Q, limiting filter performance.
 • Parasitic elements affect high-frequency response.
 • Temperature stability is crucial for narrow-band designs.

 5. Insights:

 • Highpass: Simple component substitution with value inversion.

 • Bandpass: Each element becomes a resonator tuned to f0 .
 • Bandstop: Each element becomes a trap that blocks/diverts at f0 .
 • The Butterworth response shape is preserved in all transformations.
 • Component count doubles for bandpass and bandstop filters.

7.1 Complete Design Process
  1. Start with normalized lowpass prototype values, gk , of Eqn. (1).

  2. Choose filter type and specifications:

 • For highpass: Cutoff frequency fc .
 • For bandpass/bandstop: Center frequency f0 and bandwidth BW.

  3. Apply appropriate transformation formulas.

  4. Scale for actual impedance R and frequency.

  5. Verify component values and resonance conditions.

  6. Consider practical implementation constraints.

9 Conclusion
The Cauer prototype provides a systematic approach to implementing Butterworth filters in
various configurations. The ladder network topology is particularly advantageous because:

 1. It uses the minimum number of components

 2. It provides excellent stop band rejection

 3. The modular structure simplifies analysis

 4. Component sensitivities are well-understood

 5. It can be easily transformed to other filter types

 Understanding these transformations enables designers to create filters for any frequency
selectivity requirement while maintaining the desirable maximally-flat Butterworth response
characteristic.
