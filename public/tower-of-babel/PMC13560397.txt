Integration of GWAS and WGCNA reveals novel candidate genes for cottonseed oil content in Gossypium hirsutum L.
Authors: Beibei Lv, Zhuanxia Pan, Hangsong Gao, Xueyu Zhang, Liuliu Yang, Gang Wang, Gang Lan, Zhanglong Huangfu, Cairong Cao, Baoguo Hou, Zhi Xia, Meng Zhang, Xiuqin Qiao, Chaozhu Xing, Pengbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic improvement of cottonseed oil content represents a crucial strategy for enhancing the comprehensive utilization of cotton. Here, genome-wide association study (GWAS) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) were integrated to elucidate the genetic control underlying oil content. Phenotypic evaluation of 159 cotton accessions revealed extensive genetic variation, with kernel oil content ranging from 17.81% to 39.50%. Population structure analysis based on 20,213 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) classified the accessions into two major subpopulations. A total of 18 SNPs exhibited significant associations with oil content, two of which were stably detected across multiple environments using the FarmCPU model. Further haplotype analysis within linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks confirmed a favorable haplotype on chromosome A05 that was strongly correlated with elevated oil content. Integration of publicly available transcriptome data from 11 ovule developmental stages with WGCNA identified modules significantly linked to oil content. Of the 74 candidate genes within LD intervals, 17 were assigned to WGCNA modules. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses highlighted four putative candidate genes (GH_A05G1503, GH_A05G1506, GH_A05G1531, and GH_A10G2150) involved in oil biosynthesis. These findings deepen our understanding of the genetic mechanisms governing cottonseed oil biosynthesis and lay a foundation for breeding high-oil cotton varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09340-w.Keywords: Cotton, Oil content, GWAS, WGCNA, Candidate genes

Introduction

Upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is an important economic crop, serving not only as the primary source of natural fibers for the textile industry but also as a significant provider of edible oil and biodiesel feedstock [1, 2]. Cottonseed, a major product of cotton, typically contains 17–22% oil [3], while the oil content of dehulled kernels ranges from 20% to 45% [4]. These kernels are enriched in health-promoting unsaturated fatty acids, with linoleic acid comprising over 50% of the total fatty acid composition. The favorable carbon chain length of cottonseed oil also renders it an ideal feedstock for biodiesel synthesis [2]. Historically, cottonseed has been considered a byproduct of the cotton industry, and conventional cotton genetic improvement has predominantly focused on fiber quality, yield, and stress resistance traits. Despite its considerable value and potential for improvement, research on cottonseed traits, such as oil content, lags far behind that of other major oil crops [5, 6], leaving substantial room to enhance cottonseed oil content through advanced breeding technologies. In the context of industrial restructuring, this improvement has emerged as a key objective to meet the growing global demand for edible oil and biodiesel.

Cottonseed oil content is a typical quantitative trait controlled by multiple genes [7]. A comprehensive understanding of its complex genetic mechanism is the prerequisite for efficient transgenic breeding aimed at improving cottonseed oil traits. Currently, linkage mapping and genome-wide association study (GWAS) have been widely used to identify quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and candidate genes associated with agronomic traits in various crops [8–10]. Notably, these powerful techniques have also been successfully applied in cotton research, enabling the identification of QTLs associated with key traits, including oil content. Song et al. [11] utilized a BC1S1 population derived from a cross between ‘TM-1’ and ‘Hai7124’ to identify a major QTL for kernel oil percentage, designated qOP-D8-1, which explained 29.35% of the phenotypic variation. Zhu et al. [12] employed a chromosome segment substitution line (CSSL) population derived from ‘Emian22’ and ‘3–79’ to map a QTL, q-SOCA01-1. Within this interval, a candidate gene was identified that likely regulates lipid accumulation during the late stage of cottonseed development. Using an immortal F2 (IF₂) population, researchers mapped four QTLs associated with cottonseed oil content to chromosomes 18, 22, and linkage group 11 [13]. Meanwhile, a multi-environment GWAS identified 13 QTLs associated with cottonseed oil content, which were linked to 53 common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) distributed across 13 chromosomes [14]. By integrating linkage analysis of a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population with GWAS of a natural population, a major shared QTL for kernel oil content was pinpointed on chromosome 3, and four genes, LtnD, PGK, LPLAT1, and PAH2, were identified as hub genes participating in the interaction network highly correlated with oil content [15].

However, in specific genetic populations, limited recombination events and insufficient parental polymorphism generally restrict the power of conventional linkage mapping to detect additional QTLs [16]. Among the numerous QTLs reported for cottonseed oil content, only a small subset has been consistently identified across multiple environments or genetic backgrounds, and most are localized to large genetic intervals [17, 18]. These limitations pose significant challenges to the identification and dissection of genes controlling cottonseed oil content. In contrast, GWAS based on natural populations exhibits a higher frequency of recombination events, thereby improving the accuracy of phenotype-genotype associations [19]. Moreover, the availability of the upland cotton TM-1 reference genome and high-quality SNP assays has facilitated the investigation of the genetic basis of cottonseed oil content [18]. Although several key genes, such as GhWRI1 [20], GhKASI_A05 [21], GhPRXR1 [14], GhSAD-1, GhFAD2-1 [22], GhDGAT1 [23], and MYB44 [24], have been identified through GWAS, offering valuable genetic resources, GWAS focusing on cottonseed oil content remains limited compared with that targeting fiber and yield traits. Therefore, identifying additional key loci across diverse genetic backgrounds is essential to further elucidate the genetic basis of cottonseed oil biosynthesis.

In this study, the CottonSNP40K chip was used to genotype a natural population comprising 159 upland cotton accessions. The FarmCPU model was applied to perform a GWAS on kernel oil content across three environments and Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) values. Putative candidate genes were then prioritized by integrating stable GWAS signals with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, and functional annotation. This study enriches our understanding of the genetic basis underlying cottonseed oil content and identifies promising candidate genes for future improvement.

Materials and methods

Materials and field experiments

A total of 159 upland cotton accessions were collected for this study (Table S1). These materials were obtained from (1) the Institute of Cotton Research, Shanxi Agricultural University, (2) the Research Institute of Agricultural Sciences, Sixth Division of Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, and (3) the Institute of Cotton Research, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. They represent major cotton production regions in China, including the Yellow River Basin (64 accessions), Northwest Inland Region (82 accessions), and Northern Extra Early Maturation Cotton Region (1 accession), as well as historically introduced varieties and germplasm lines from abroad (12 accessions).

Field trials were conducted over two consecutive growing seasons in 2023 (23YC) and 2024 (24YC) in Yuncheng, Shanxi Province, China (35°19′N, 111°09′E), using row spacing of 40 cm and plant spacing of 25 cm. A parallel trial in 2023 (23XJ) was established in Wujiaqu, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China (44°16′N, 87°55′E), with row spacing of 76 cm and plant spacing of 10 cm. Across all environments, the accessions were planted under a randomized complete block design with two biological replicates per accession. Field management followed local agronomic recommendations.

Phenotypic investigation and analysis

Cottonseed kernels were used for oil content determination. For each accession and environment, 30 fully open cotton bolls were harvested from the central part of each plant. After drying under natural ventilation, seed cotton samples were ginned, and lint was removed following standard delinting procedures. The resulting seeds were then dried at 40 ℃ prior to manual dehulling. Subsequently, 100 healthy kernels were randomly selected and finely ground into powder. The Soxhlet extraction method was applied to determine the kernel oil content [25]. Briefly, approximately 1.50 g of kernel powder was placed into filter paper packets and extracted with petroleum ether for 8 h. After extraction, the residues were dried at 103 ℃ to a constant weight, and oil content was calculated on a dry matter basis. Each sample was subjected to three biological replicates.

The BLUP values for oil content across three environments were estimated using the ‘lme4’ package in R v4.4.2. Broad-sense heritability (H²) was estimated following Knapp et al. [26].

Descriptive statistical analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed in R. The ‘ggplot2’ package was used to generate frequency histograms with density curves, and the ‘GGally’ package was applied for correlation analysis and visualization.

Population structure and principal component analysis (PCA)

Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaf tissues using a modified CTAB method [27]. Genotyping of the 159 accessions was performed by Molbreeding Co., Ltd. (Shijiazhuang, China) with the CottonSNP40K chip [28]. The SNP dataset was filtered via bcftools v1.19 to retain loci with a call rate ≥ 0.85 and a minimum allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05, yielding 20,213 high-quality SNP markers for subsequent analysis.

Population structure was estimated via ADMIXTURE v1.3.0, with the number of hypothetical ancestral populations (K) ranging from 1 to 8. The optimal K value was determined based on the lowest cross-validation error, and the results were visualized with the R package ‘pophelper’. PCA was performed via PLINK v1.9 to further evaluate population structure, and results were plotted with the R package ‘ggplot2’. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay was assessed using PopLDdecay v3.43 [29].

GWAS and favorable haplotype identification

GWAS was conducted using the Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) method [30] implemented in GAPIT v3 in R [31]. The first three principal components were incorporated as fixed covariates to correct for population structure. Phenotypic data used for association analysis included both single-environment phenotypes and BLUP values. To identify SNPs associated with oil content, a suggestive significance threshold was set at p < 1/n (where n is the number of SNPs), corresponding to –log10(p) > 4.31 in this study [18]. Manhattan and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots were generated with the R package ‘CMplot’. In this study, significant loci that were consistently detected in at least two environments and BLUP were considered as stable major SNPs [17].

LD blocks surrounding stable SNPs were defined based on LD decay and visualized using LDBlockshow v1.41 [32]. Haplotype analysis was subsequently performed with the R package ‘geneHapR’ [33]. The mean phenotypic BLUP value was calculated for each haplotype, and a two-tailed t-test was performed to compare the two haplotypes and identify the favorable one.

Co-expression network construction and candidate gene mining

To identify gene modules associated with oil content, RNA-seq data of upland cotton TM-1 spanning 11 ovule developmental stages (-3, -1, 0, 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 35 days post-anthesis, DPA) were retrieved from the NCBI SRA database via Cottonomics (http://cotton.zju.edu.cn/index.htm) [34]. Raw reads were aligned and quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) using Salmon with the G. hirsutum TM-1 reference genome [35]. A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the R package ‘WGCNA’ [36]. Genes with TPM ≥ 1 in at least 20% of samples were retained, and after log2(TPM + 1) transformation, those with a median absolute deviation (MAD) above the 75th percentile were selected. The network was constructed with soft-power set to 12, minModuleSize to 30, and mergeCutHeight to 0.2. Arabidopsis thaliana orthologous IDs for cotton genes, curated in Cottonomics, were submitted to the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov) [37] for GO enrichment analysis across biological process, cellular component, and molecular function.

Genes within the LD decay distance of each lead SNP were retrieved from Cottonomics. Those retained in WGCNA were visualized using the R package ‘igraph’ based on topological overlap matrix (TOM) values > 0.1. Putative candidate genes were prioritized according to functional annotations related to lipid metabolism. In addition, protein-protein interaction networks were predicted via the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [38] with a high confidence threshold of 0.7.

Results

Phenotypic variation in kernel oil content

Phenotypic variation in kernel oil content was analyzed across three environments and BLUP values (Table 1). Extensive genetic variation in kernel oil content was observed within the association panel. Specifically, the 23XJ environment exhibited the highest mean oil content (30.32%) with the lowest coefficient of variation (CV, 9.00%), whereas the 24YC environment had the lowest mean oil content (25.12%) with the highest CV (11.98%). Frequency distribution histograms showed that kernel oil content followed a continuous and approximately normal distribution across all environments and BLUP values (Fig. 1). This pattern demonstrates that this trait possesses typical quantitative genetic properties suitable for GWAS. Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations among kernel oil content across different environments (r = 0.56–0.86, p < 0.001), reflecting the relatively stable performance of this trait under variable environmental conditions.

Population structure and PCA

An accurate understanding of population structure is critical for reliable mapping in GWAS populations [39]. The genetic structure of the association panel was inferred using ADMIXTURE, with the number of ancestral clusters (K) tested from 1 to 8. The cross-validation error curve reached its minimum at K = 3 (Fig. 2A), however, the values for K = 2 and K = 3 differed only marginally. Based on the ADMIXTURE results (Fig. 2B) and the principle of parsimony [40], the association population was divided into two subgroups. This finding was further validated by PCA, which confirmed the existence of two distinct subpopulations (Fig. 2C). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together explained 14.54% of the total genetic variation, with PC1 accounting for 11.37% and PC2 for 3.17%. Among the two subpopulations studied, Group1 comprised 86 accessions, predominantly originating from the Yellow River Basin (72.09%; Table S1), whereas Group2 consisted of 73 accessions, mainly from the Northwest Inland Region (86.30%). Furthermore, LD decay was estimated at approximately 235 kb using PopLDdecay, a moderate decay distance well suited for subsequent GWAS.

GWAS of kernel oil content

The FarmCPU model was employed to identify SNPs associated with kernel oil content because of its improved control of false positives while retaining high statistical power compared with General Linear Model (GLM) and Mixed Linear Model (MLM) [30]. A total of 18 significant SNPs were detected in at least one individual environment (Fig. 3A; Table 3). These loci were distributed on chromosomes A01, A04, A05, A08, A09, A10, A12, D01, D05, D10, D11 and D13. Among these, one SNP (A10:106581756) was detected in two environments and BLUP values, and another SNP (A05_14121119) was consistently detected across all three environments and BLUP values. Notably, the A10:106581756 locus exhibited the highest –log10(p) value. Collectively, these two stable SNPs are considered the major effect loci underlying cottonseed kernel oil content.

Identification of favorable haplotypes for kernel oil content

To identify favorable haplotypes associated with kernel oil content, haplotype analysis was performed on two stable SNP loci. A high level of LD between the associated SNPs was observed only on chromosome A05, where three distinct LD blocks were formed. Notably, the A05_14121119 locus resided within an LD block spanning the 14.12–14.19 Mb genomic interval (Fig. 4A). Four SNPs within this block defined two haplotypes in our association panel (Fig. 4B). An overwhelming majority of accessions carried Hap1 (TTTA), whereas only 17 accessions possessed Hap2 (CCCG), which exhibited significantly higher average oil content (Fig. 4C). Moreover, phenotypic differences among alleles at each of the two stable SNP loci were evaluated, confirming their association with kernel oil content (Fig. S1).

Co-expression network analysis reveals candidate genes associated with oil biosynthesis

After quality filtering, 11,853 genes were retained and subjected to WGCNA to explore co-expression modules potentially involved in oil accumulation. 14 co-expression modules were constructed based on similar expression patterns (Fig. 5A; Table S2). GO enrichment analysis was subsequently performed for each module to annotate biological functions. The results showed that the magenta module (267 genes) was significantly enriched in lipid storage, lipid transport, monolayer-surrounded lipid storage body and lipid binding (Fig. 5B; Table S3), suggesting its involvement in core processes of oil biosynthesis. The blue module (1,655 genes) was enriched in fatty acid binding (Fig. 5C; Table S3). In contrast, the turquoise module (4,525 genes) displayed no direct enrichment in lipid-related terms but was primarily associated with regulation of DNA-templated transcription and protein binding (Fig. 5D; Table S3), implying a potential upstream regulatory role.

Building on the co-expression network results, strong candidate genes were prioritized around the two stable SNP loci. Using the TM-1 reference genome, 74 genes were retrieved from the ± 235 kb flanking region. Of these, 17 were assigned to the previously identified co-expression modules. Specifically, five were in the turquoise module, four in the blue module, and two in the magenta module (Table S4). Functional annotation based on Arabidopsis orthologs highlighted three putative candidates, including a Calcium-dependent lipid-binding (CaLB domain) family protein (GH_A05G1503, magenta module), a GDSL-like Lipase/Acylhydrolase superfamily protein (GH_A05G1531, turquoise module), and an ATP citrate lyase (ACL) family protein (GH_A10G2150, turquoise module). Co-expression network analysis also revealed that GH_A05G1531 and GH_A10G2150 were grouped within the same connected component subnetwork, whereas GH_A05G1503 resided in a distinct connected component, consistent with their distinct module-specific functional roles (Fig. 5E). Notably, a gene (GH_A05G1506) encoding 3-ketoacyl-acyl carrier protein synthase I (KASI) was not captured in any modules, but protein-protein interaction analysis using STRING indicated that it directly interacts with multiple enzymes involved in fatty acid chain termination and elongation, such as acyl-ACP thioesterase and trans-2-enoyl-CoA reductase (Fig. 5F), suggesting its potential involvement in fatty acid chain elongation. Collectively, these four genes contribute to different aspects of the oil biosynthesis pathway.

Discussion

Accurate phenotypic characterization is crucial for applying GWAS to identify favorable alleles and candidate genes underlying target traits in crops. Cottonseed oil content is strongly influenced by the combined effects of genotype, environment, and genotype-by-environment interactions, which vary across different growing seasons, locations, and years. Considerable genetic variation in oil content has been documented both between and within cotton species [41–43]. In the present study, we evaluated kernel oil content in 159 upland cotton accessions and employed the FarmCPU model to perform a GWAS across multiple environments and BLUP values. Substantial variation was observed among the tested accessions, with CV ranging from 9.00% to 11.98%. Notably, the 23XJ environment exhibited the highest average oil content, likely attributable to abundant sunlight in Xinjiang during the cotton growing season, as prolonged photoperiods have been shown to favor oil accumulation [44]. Consistent with previous studies [14, 44], both genotype and environment exerted considerable influence on variation in oil content, with kernel oil content showing a high H2 of 80.38%. This high heritability indicates a predominant role of genotype in shaping phenotypic performance. Furthermore, population structure analysis based on 20,213 high-quality SNPs identified two subpopulations reflecting distinct domestication histories and ecological origins [45], with several accessions exhibiting admixture patterns. These admixture patterns probably result from extensive germplasm exchange and introgression from diverse sources during cotton breeding programs [46].

Further GWAS results revealed that kernel oil content was closely associated with 18 significant SNPs, including 11 in the At subgenome and 7 in the Dt subgenome. Two stable loci were identified via GWAS. By aligning the physical positions of these SNPs with previously reported QTL intervals, three were found to fall within QTLs from earlier studies [14, 17, 47], confirming the reliability and robustness of our findings. Among them, the A05_14121119 locus was consistently detected across all environments and might belong to a genomic hotspot region for oil content. The remaining unreported SNPs may provide novel insights into the molecular genetic mechanisms underlying oil content. In addition, accessions carrying CCCG haplotype identified in this study exhibited higher oil content, which can assist breeders in marker-assisted selection to quickly screen cotton accessions with high oil content in a breeding cycle [48]. This favorable haplotype exhibited the lowest frequency among the tested accessions, with the majority (70.59%) originating from the Northwest Inland Region, implying that upland cotton from this region may hold substantial potential for improving oil content.

Integrating GWAS with WGCNA provides an efficient platform to prioritize regulatory factors involved in the oil biosynthesis pathway [49]. Although the A05 locus co-localized with a known QTL interval [47], Dong et al. [47] further narrowed this region through GWAS, and the refined interval does not overlap with the LD decay range of our lead SNP. Consequently, the candidate genes identified in our study were not reported in that earlier work. Furthermore, the modules identified by WGCNA were tightly linked with crucial pathways of oil biosynthesis, including lipid storage, lipid transport, lipid binding and fatty acid binding. Building on these results and functional annotation, we proposed four putative candidate genes. GH_A05G1503 was annotated as a CaLB domain family protein. In Arabidopsis, the CaLB domain exhibits high binding specificity for the inositol phospholipid PI(3)P in vitro [50]. PI(3)P has been identified as a novel regulator of lipid droplets (LDs) initiation and may coordinate the biogenesis of LDs in mammalian cells [51]. LDs are central organelles for lipid storage and homeostasis, and their biogenesis in plants shares evolutionarily conserved mechanisms with other eukaryotes [52]. In addition, the Arabidopsis AtCLB protein, which contains a C2 domain implicated in lipid binding, binds specifically to ceramide [53]. Ceramide was found significantly accumulated in the oil fraction, accounting for 40% of the Arabidopsis seed amount [54]. Given its assignment to the lipid-enriched magenta module, it is plausible that GH_A05G1503 may participate in oil accumulation through binding PI(3)P or ceramide. GH_A05G1531 and GH_A10G2150 were assigned to the turquoise module. GH_A05G1531 was annotated as a GDSL-like Lipase/Acylhydrolase superfamily protein. Members of the GDSL lipase family, also known as Seed Fatty Acid Reducers (SFARs), hydrolyze diverse lipid substances to regulate kernel oil content [55–57]. Ectopic expression of the GDSL lipase gene Gh_D01G2016 in yeast resulted in an 18.1% decrease in oil content [16]. Similarly, constitutive overexpression of GDSL1 enhanced lipid catabolism while suppressing seed oil accumulation in Arabidopsis and Brassica napus [58]. These findings suggest that GH_A05G1531 may negatively regulate oil accumulation by promoting lipid hydrolysis during seed development. GH_A10G2150 was annotated as an ACL family protein. ACL is a key cytosolic enzyme that converts mitochondria-derived citrate into oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA. It plays essential roles in fatty acid biosynthesis and elongation [59, 60]. Reduction in ACL expression altered seed fatty acid accumulation in Arabidopsis [61]. In B. napus, ACL activity supplies approximately half of the acetyl-CoA required for fatty acid biosynthesis during seed development [62]. Heterologous overexpression of the rat ACL gene in tobacco plastids led to a 4-fold increase in total ACL activity, and a 16% increase in fatty acid content [60]. Thus, GH_A10G2150 likely promotes oil accumulation by supplying acetyl-CoA for de novo fatty acid biosynthesis. GH_A05G1506 was not captured in any co-expression module, likely due to its low expression in the developing ovule transcriptome. This gene was annotated as KASI, which catalyzes the elongation step in de novo fatty acid synthesis. Arabidopsis KASI deficiency significantly changes polar lipid composition and dramatically reduces fatty acid levels in seeds [63]. In tobacco, KASI also regulates fatty acid synthesis and plant growth and development. Overexpression of NtKASI-2 reduces fatty acid content in leaves [64]. In cotton, seed-specific overexpression of GhKASI_A05 resulted in increased seed size, weight, and protein content, as well as elevated C16:0 and C18:1 contents, but reduced cottonseed oil content [21]. Notably, protein–protein interaction analysis using STRING revealed that GH_A05G1506 directly interacts with acyl-ACP thioesterase and trans-2-enoyl-CoA reductase, providing independent evidence for its involvement in fatty acid metabolism despite its absence from the co-expression network. Collectively, these four previously unreported candidate genes may play important roles in cottonseed oil biosynthesis. Nevertheless, given the complexity of oil biosynthesis and its regulation by multiple metabolic pathways, further experimental validation is required to confirm their specific functions.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified two loci associated with cottonseed oil content on chromosomes A05 and A10. A favorable haplotype was found to significantly enhance oil content. Four putative candidate genes involved in lipid metabolism were prioritized as key regulators of oil accumulation. The present research provides new genomic resources to further understanding of the genetic basis of cottonseed oil content, and facilitate the development of high-value cotton cultivars.
