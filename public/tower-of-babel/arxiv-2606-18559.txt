Principal Component Analysis and Power Indices
Xavier Molinero, Enric Monsó, Daniel Samaniego

Abstract
Measuring the influence of a player in a simple game is a widely studied topic. Shapley-Shubik power index is perhaps the maximum exponent in terms of relevance. Furthermore, other power indexes have been proposed over time. In this paper, we propose yet another power index, defined in terms of winning coalitions. We show that this index coincides with eigenvalues obtained with the Principal Component Analysis method, a broadly used technique in data science to determine the influence of different features given a dataset. Furthermore, we provide a characterization of this proposed index in terms of four properties. Keywords: game theory; simple games; influence measures; power index; principal component analysis, singular value decomposition.

Notions on simple games

Let

be the set of

. Also denote

belongs to the coalition

and
s_{i}=0

i
does not belong to
S

belongs to the coalition
S
while

indicates that play
i
does not belong to
S
) . As it is customary in the literature
2^{N}=\{S\ |\ S\subseteq N\}
is the set of all coalitions. A
simple game
is a pair
\Gamma=(N,v)
where

\displaystyle v\colon 2^{N}

\displaystyle\to\{0,1\}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\mapsto v(S)

indicates whether a coalition wins the game (i.e.
v(S)=1
) or does not (i.e.
v(S)=0
), and satisfies the following properties: (en una enumeration millor?)

a.

v(\emptyset)=0,
(void coalition has to lose the game);

b.

v(N)=1,
(grand coalition wins the game for sure);

c.

\forall{}S\subseteq{}T\subseteq{}N
then
v(S)\leq v(T)
, the
monotonicity
property (incorporation of players do not diminish chances to win the game).

For a comprehensive introduction to simple games, we refer to
30

Given a simple game
\Gamma=(N,v)
, let
\mathcal{W}(\Gamma)
be the set of all winning coalitions. It is customary to define a game
\Gamma
by listing the coalitions in
\mathcal{W}
. For
i\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{W}_{i}(\Gamma)
be the set of all winning coalitions containing player

, that is,

\mathcal{W}_{i}(\Gamma)=\{S\in\mathcal{W}\ |\ i\in{}S\}
,
and

\mathcal{W}^{i}(\Gamma)
denotes the set of winning coalitions of size
i

\mathcal{W}^{i}(\Gamma)=\{S\in\mathcal{W}(\Gamma)\mid|S|=i\}
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{W}(\Gamma)=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{W}^{i}(\Gamma)
, and these sets are pairwise disjoint.
A winning coalition which strictly does not contain another winning coalition is called
minimal winning coalition
. Finally,
\mathcal{W}^{m}(\Gamma)=\{S\in\mathcal{W}\ |\ v(T)=0,\forall{}T\subsetneq S\}
denotes the set of all minimal winning coalitions.

If there is no ambiguity, we simply use the notation
\mathcal{X}
to refer to
\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)
, where

\mathcal{X}\in\{\mathcal{W},{\cal W}_{i},{\cal W}^{i},{\cal W}^{m}\}
, for
i\in[n]
.

To classify the players of the game according to its influence in the game,
15
introduced the following relation. Let
\tau_{ij}:N\rightarrow N
be the transposition of players
i,j\in N
. We say that
i
is
at least as desirable as

j
,
i\succsim j
, if and only if
\tau_{ij}(\mathcal{W}_{j})\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{i}
.
We say that both players are
equally desirable
when
i\succsim j
and
j\succsim i
and we denote it by
i\simeq j
.
In other words, replacing player
j
in any winning coalition by player
i
, maintains the coalition winning. The relation
\succsim
is a preorder.

A
null player

i
is a player whose decision does not affect the outcome of the game, formally
v(S\cup\{i\})=v(S)
for any coalition
S\subseteq N\backslash\{i\}
. A
dummy player

i
satisfies
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)=v(\{i\})
for any coalition
S\subseteq N\backslash\{i\}
. We say a player

is a

As usual, given two simple games
\Gamma_{1}=(N,v)
and
\Gamma_{2}=(N,w)
, we define the union and the intersection as
\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}=(N,v\cap w)
and
\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}=(N,v\cup w)
, respectively, where
\forall{}S\subseteq{}N

(v\cap w)(S)=\max(v(S),w(S))\ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ (v\cup w)(S)=\min(v(S),w(S))

Moreover, if
\pi
is a permutation of
N
, we define
\pi{}v(S)=v(\pi^{-1}(S))
.

Let
\Gamma=(N,v)
be a simple game. We define the minimum size of a winning coalition as
k(\Gamma)=\min\limits_{S\in\mathcal{W}}|S|
, and the number of winning coalitions of cardinality
k(\Gamma)
by
p(\Gamma)=|\{S\in\mathcal{W}\ |\ |S|=k(\Gamma)\}|

Definition 1.1

Given a simple game

1.2
Singular Value Decomposition and (supervised) Principal Component Analysis

In this section, we review principal component analysis (PCA), supervised PCA, and the role of singular value decomposition (SVD) in these methods.

It is a well known result in linear algebra
20
;
14
that every real matrix
\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{M}^{m\times n}
admits a factorization, so called singular value decomposition (SVD), such that

\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{U}\Sigma\mathsf{V}^{T}\,,

where

\mathsf{U}\in{\cal M}^{m\times m}
and
\mathsf{V}\in{\cal M}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal matrices, and

\Sigma\in{\cal M}^{m\times n}
has non-negative diagonal values and zero off-diagonal elements. These diagonal values
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}
are the singular values of
\mathsf{M}
.
Moreover,
the columns of
\mathsf{U}\in{\cal M}^{m\times m}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the columns of
\mathsf{V}\in{\cal M}^{n\times n}
give an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. See Figure
1
.

M\in{\cal M}^{m\times n}
=
\mathsf{U}\in{\cal M}^{m\times m}
\sigma_{1}
\ddots
\sigma_{n}
\Sigma\in{\cal M}^{m\times n}
\mathsf{V}^{T}\in{\cal M}^{n\times n}

Matrix representation of the SVD. W.l.o.g., we assume
m\geq n
.

Since the singular value decomposition uniquely determines the matrix
\Sigma
, but not the associated matrices
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{V}
(for more detail, see Theorem 2.6.3 in

\displaystyle\texttt{SVD}{}:\ \mathcal{M}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{q})

where
q=\min\{m,n\}
.

Note that, in the literature, eigenvalues are typically arranged in decreasing order for the purposes of component analysis
14
;
23
. However, in this work we preserve their original ordering, as is common in statistics. Given a data matrix, principal component analysis (PCA)
25
;
18
is a well-known dimension-reduction method that relies on its singular value decomposition (SVD) to assess the relevance of the components. This procedure can also reduce computational costs in data analysis by discarding non-essential information.

Furthermore, the PCA procedure can be extended to include the analysis of principal components with respect to a target, name it
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, (
1

2
) in the so called supervised PCA. Given a data set matrix
\mathsf{X}\in{\cal M}^{m\times n}
and a target vector

\mathsf{X}|y\in\mathcal{M}^{m\times(n+1)}

\displaystyle\tau:\ \mathcal{M}^{m\times(n+1)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}|y

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathsf{X}^{*}

where, we consider the columns of
\mathsf{X}
centred at
0
removing its column’s mean, as
x^{cent}_{i,j}=x_{i,j}-\overline{x}_{j}
, and

x^{*}_{i,j}=x^{cent}_{i,j}\frac{(x^{cent}_{i})^{T}\cdot y}{\sqrt{(x^{cent}_{i})^{T}\cdot x^{cent}_{i}}},\quad\forall i\in[m]\text{ and }j\in[n],\,

(1)

with

denoting the average of the elements of the column

. Also recall that, given two vectors

, we define the real number
u\cdot v=\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}v_{j}
. Note that
\texttt{SVD}\ \circ\tau
is a well defined mapping and, for simplicity, we set
\texttt{SVD}_{\tau}=\texttt{SVD}\ \circ\tau
whose domain is

The
SVD
power index

The next definition states the proposed power index that will be studied in the main results of the article.

Definition 2.1

SVD
power index. Let
\Gamma=(N,v)
be a simple game. The
SVD
power index for game
\Gamma
is defined, for each player
i\in N
, as

\texttt{SVD}_{i}=|\mathcal{W}_{i}|-\frac{|\mathcal{W}|}{2}.

Consequently the normalized
SVD
power index for game
\Gamma
and player
i
is the corresponding ratio

\overline{\texttt{SVD}}_{i}=\frac{\texttt{SVD}_{i}}{\sum_{j\in N}\texttt{SVD}_{j}}.

We shall denote
\texttt{SVD}_{i}(\Gamma)
and
\overline{\texttt{SVD}}_{i}(\Gamma)
by
\texttt{SVD}_{i}
and
\overline{\texttt{SVD}}_{i}
whenever ambiguity arises.

Given a simple game

Proof.

(a)

(Bicameral join postulate. Definition 2.4 in

Given two games
\Gamma_{1}=(N_{1},v_{1})
and
\Gamma_{2}=(N_{2},v_{2})

\phi

, its ratio of power is preserved when joining another disjoint game, that is,

\frac{\phi_{i}(\Gamma_{1})}{\phi_{j}(\Gamma_{1})}=\frac{\phi_{i}(\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2})}{\phi_{j}(\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2})}.

Proposition 2.10

The SVD power index satisfies the bicameral join postulate.

Proof.

Note
\phi_{i}(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2})=|\mathcal{W}_{i}(\Gamma_{1})|\cdot|W(\Gamma_{2})|-\frac{|\mathcal{W}(\Gamma_{1})|\cdot|W(\Gamma_{2})|}{2}
and
\phi_{j}(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2})=|\mathcal{W}_{j}(\Gamma_{1})|\cdot|W(\Gamma_{2})|-\frac{|\mathcal{W}(\Gamma_{1})|\cdot|W(\Gamma_{2})|}{2}
. The term
|W(\Gamma_{2})|
factors out and simplifies in the quotient, recovering the original ratio. Note that non-dumminess guaranties the denominator is non-zero. ∎
