Dataset for self-practice of the peg transfer: Tool and wrist motion, eye gaze, and video measurements for multimodal skill analysis
Authors: Jinwoo Oh, Shiyu Deng, Nathan Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes a multimodal dataset on self-directed practice of the peg transfer task in the Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery (FLS) curriculum. Nineteen university students self-practiced the FLS peg transfer task in multiple one-hour sessions consisting of alternating 10-minute practice blocks and 2-minute rest breaks. Data collection continued until participants met the proficiency criterion—ten non-consecutive trials completed within 48 s without dropping objects—or reached a maximum of six sessions. Across all participants, the dataset contains 85 sessions and 1971 trials.Each trial includes measurements from bilateral wrist inertial measurement units (IMUs), tool-handle IMUs, eye tracking, and trainer-box video that were concurrently recorded using a custom C# data logging software application. Trial-level annotations and performance metrics include task completion time, number of objects dropped, and experimenter comments. The dataset is publicly accessible from an institutional repository.Keywords: Hand-eye coordination, Minimally invasive surgery, Fine motor control, Performance assessment, Training, Learning curve, Human factors

Value of the Data

The dataset includes eye-tracking data, wrist and tool-handle inertial measurements, video recordings, and task completion times collected concurrently by the custom C# data logging software application for 1971 trials of self-directed practice of the peg transfer task, a standardized training task in the Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery (FLS) [3]. This dataset could contribute to research in the following ways:

•Skill acquisition in laparoscopic surgical skills: The dataset captures multimodal data across a large number of trials and repeated sessions, reflecting the full spectrum of skill progression for the FLS peg transfer task. Thus, the dataset affords a detailed investigation into learning curves, including within and between participant variability, changes in motion or performance metrics with practice, and the development of hand-eye coordination at different skill levels. The dataset also enables research to formulate and evaluate new behavioral metrics for assessing surgical skills. More broadly, it can advance understanding of how specific laparoscopic skills are acquired, thereby informing training and self-practice strategies for surgery trainees.

Background

Laparoscopic surgery has been widely adopted due to its clinical advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, and shorter hospital stay compared to open surgery [4]. However, these benefits come with increased technical demands on surgeons. The loss of three-dimensional visual depth cues and motion inversion caused by the fulcrum effect introduces non-intuitive motor transformations, making the acquisition of laparoscopic skills particularly challenging [4].

To address these challenges, the Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery (FLS) curriculum prescribes five standardized training tasks for assessing core psychomotor skills, including bimanual coordination, efficiency, and precision required for laparoscopic surgery [3,5]. Understanding how these skills are acquired through practice is important for improving both surgical training and our broader understanding of human motor learning. However, laparoscopic training and assessment still rely heavily on expert observation and (formative) feedback. This creates a need for intelligent tutoring or coaching systems that can help relieve the burden of continuous expert supervision, augment human observation with objective behavioral measurements such as gaze and motion [6,7], and provide immediate feedback and frequent performance assessment [8,9].

Developing such systems requires datasets that capture how surgical skills are acquired across repeated practice and multiple behavioral measurements, which remain limited in existing public datasets. Existing open datasets vary in task scope and measurement modality. JIGSAWS provides video and kinematic data concurrently recorded by a software application for robotic surgical tasks and has been widely used for gesture segmentation and skill assessment [10]. Ahmidi et al. further provided benchmark annotations for segmentation and recognition of robotic surgical gestures [11]. Choudhry et al. introduced a 7-DoF peg transfer dataset for surgical skill assessment based on instrument motion data [12]. In addition, the list-of-surgical-tool-datasets repository catalogs publicly accessible datasets for surgical computer vision tasks such as tool detection, segmentation, and workflow analysis [13]. However, these datasets show two major limitations. First, multimodal coverage is often incomplete, with motion, video, and gaze typically collected in isolation rather than in full synchronization with each other. Second, open datasets generally do not capture longitudinal skill acquisition across repeated training sessions. For example, the JIGSAWS [10] dataset includes multimodal measurements and performance metrics across multiple tasks from operators of varying expertise to provide benchmarks for gesture and skill analysis rather than a comprehensive longitudinal dataset to investigate individual learning or skill acquisition over time. Understanding individual differences in skill progression can inform personalized feedback and guidance [14]. Consequently, datasets that support both multimodal modeling and longitudinal analysis of surgical skill development would be an invaluable addition to the public domain.

This dataset provides multimodal measurements concurrently recorded by the custom C# data logging software application on participants repeatedly practicing the FLS peg transfer task. Unlike datasets based primarily on single-session demonstrations or cross-sectional comparisons of expertise, this dataset captures the longitudinal progression of novices with task video, eye-gaze recordings, wrist- and tool-handle IMU signals, and trial-level performance and proficiency metrics for each practice trial. By combining these complementary data streams across repeated training sessions, the dataset enables multimodal analysis of how gaze, motion, and performance co-evolve throughout skill acquisition. This dataset is unique in combining concurrently recorded gaze, motion, video, and performance measurements within a longitudinal self-practice framework.

Data acquisition software and computing environment

Data were acquired on a Windows 11 system using a custom C# data logging software application built on .NET 5.0. The application interfaced with the Tobii Pro SDK for C# for eye-tracking data, the Shimmer C# API for wrist-worn IMU data, and Adafruit sensor libraries for tool-handle IMU data.

Participant-level organization (Contents of each participant folder)

The repository contains participant folders labeled P01–P28. Non-sequential identifiers reflect exclusion of participants who withdrew prior to completing the protocol. Recordings of omitted participants were not included in the repository. Participants who attended the sessions but did not meet the proficiency criteria after six sessions were retained in the dataset.

Each participant folder (e.g., P01) contains up to six compressed archives (.zip), the number of which corresponds to the number of sessions completed. Each ZIP file corresponds to a single practice session and follows the naming format: PXX_FLS Task A_YY.zip, where PXX is the participant identifier, FLS Task A denotes the peg transfer task, and YY denotes the session number in chronological order (e.g., 01, 02, …). For each participant, session archives are included up to the session in which the proficiency criterion was met (a maximum of six sessions).

Session-level contents of each session/ZIP folder

Each ZIP archive contains all data files for every practice trial of the participant in that session (Fig. 2). The data files were named with following consistent naming convention for organization: [RecordingStartTime]_Trial[BlockNo]-[TrialNo].*, where RecordingStartTime is formatted as YYYYMMDDHHMMSS and corresponds to the system clock time at the beginning of recording. BlockNo refers to the practice block number within the session, and TrialNo indicates the sequential trial number within that block (refer to Study Protocol below for definition of a practice block and trial). The overall dataset folder and file structure are described in Fig. 2.

Example directory layout for a single session, illustrating trial-level file organization and naming conventions.

Trials with [BlockNo] = 0 or [TrialNo] = 0 correspond to baseline pupil diameter recordings, during which participants were instructed to fixate on a static visual target (a black cross centered on a white background) for around 30 s. These recordings are not part of the peg transfer task and may be excluded from task-performance analyses. However, they may be useful as baseline references in analyses involving pupil size, such as those examining changes in mental workload.

Five data files are associated with each trial: a video file, an eye-tracking file, a tool-handle IMU file, a wrist IMU file, and a log file. Some trials may be missing one or more data files due to recording failures. In other cases, a data file may be present but contain no valid entries. The *_log.txt (Comments) field captures the known reason for any recording failures.

The root-level README files summarize the dataset folder structure, file-naming conventions, modality-specific file formats, and basic parsing notes. They are intended to duplicate the essential repository-use information from this manuscript rather than introduce separate analytical procedures.

File-level contents of each trial

Detailed descriptions of each modality are provided below.

(1) Video (*.mp4)

Filename: [timestamp]_Trial[BlockNo]-[TrialNo].mp4

Sensor: Webcam (native resolution 1920 × 1080)

Format: MP4 video file

Sampling rate: Approximately 60 frames per second

Key variables: Video frames (1280 × 720 resolution)

Description: Each video file corresponds to a single trial and records the task workspace inside the trainer box at 1280 × 720 resolution. Audio is not recorded. The original trial videos were recorded in AVI format and later re-encoded to MP4 using HandBrake 1.9.2 to reduce file size. The final MP4 files use H.264 video encoding and preserve the original resolution. The frame-rate setting was “same as source,” and no intentional temporal subsampling or frame-rate reduction was applied during re-encoding. HandBrake was configured with the constant-quality option set to 16, so the encoded bitrate is not fixed and may vary across videos. The recording start time embedded in the filename provides a temporal reference for alignment with data of other modalities.

(2) Eye-tracking (*_eye.txt)

Filename: [timestamp]_Trial[BlockNo]-[TrialNo]_eye.txt

Sensor: Tobii Pro Nano eye tracker

Format: Comma-separated text file with a single header row, containing sequential eye-tracking measurements for the left and right eyes. Eye-tracking data were recorded at approximately 60 Hz. The files do not include absolute device timestamps; samples are indexed sequentially and aligned to video frames using proportional mapping.

Sampling rate: Approximately 60 Hz

Missingness: Invalid samples are indicated by Tobii validity flags and corresponding NaN values. Missing data may also appear as dropped rows or time gaps.

Key variables: Left and right gaze point validity flags, normalized gaze coordinates on the display (0–1), pupil-related variables, and gaze origin information in user coordinates.

Coordinate system: Gaze positions are reported in display-relative normalized coordinates, where 0 corresponds to the top or left edge of the display and 1 corresponds to the bottom or right edge. Because the task video filled the full display during data collection, gaze coordinates can be mapped to video-frame coordinates using linear scaling based on the display resolution.

(3) Tool-handle IMU (*_IMU.txt)

Filename: [timestamp]_Trial[BlockNo]-[TrialNo]_IMU.txt

Sensor: Adafruit LSM6DSOX (accelerometer and gyroscope) and LIS3MDL (magnetometer), interfaced via an Arduino Mega 2560 Rev3.

Format: Comma-separated text file with no header row. Each row corresponds to one received sample from one instrumented grasper. Files from P01–P07 begin each row with an instrument label, LH or RH, indicating the left-hand and right-hand graspers, respectively. Files from P08 onward begin each row with the corresponding video-frame index, followed by the LH or RH label. The remaining entries are key:value pairs for time, temperature, accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer fields, as shown in the example rows below.

Sampling rate: Approximately 30 Hz per hand

Key variables: Time (milliseconds since device start), temperature ( °C), tri-axial accelerometer measurements (Acc_X/Y/Z; m/s²), gyroscope measurements (Gyro_X/Y/Z; deg/s), and retained magnetometer log fields (Mag_X/Y/Z; see important note below).

Description: IMU sensors were mounted on the handle portion of each grasper to capture motion of both instruments. Data for both hands are stored within the same file, with each row explicitly labeled as LH or RH. Temperature is recorded as part of the sensor output.

Important note on magnetometer fields: During internal review, we identified a data-logging implementation issue in the tool-handle IMU magnetometer fields. Specifically, Mag_X, Mag_Y, and Mag_Z appear to contain duplicated values rather than independent tri-axial magnetometer measurements. These fields are retained in the dataset for transparency, but users should not interpret them as independent X/Y/Z magnetic-field axes. This issue does not affect the trial log, video, eye-tracking, wrist Shimmer data, or the tool-handle accelerometer and gyroscope fields.

(5) Trial log (*_log.txt)

Filename: [timestamp]_Trial[BlockNo]-[TrialNo]_log.txt

Format: Plain-text metadata file with key:value entries, with one key:value pair per line and no tabular header.

Key fields: ParticipantID, Task, SessionID, TrialID, Time elapsed (seconds), # dropped, and Comments. The # dropped field is based on direct observation by the researcher running the data collection session. Description: The Comments field includes experimenter-documented contextual notes, including task-apparatus issues, procedural events, participant or experimenter interactions with the apparatus, and cases in which the experimenter observed potential recording-quality concerns. Multimodal recording and time alignment

All data streams, including video, eye-tracking, tool-handle IMU, and wrist IMU, were concurrently recorded using the custom C# data logging software application. A single user interface was used to start and stop recording for all sensors simultaneously for every trial. File names include timestamps generated from the system clock of the computer running the application to provide a common temporal reference across data modalities. Device-specific timestamps were retained in the raw files, and cross-modal alignment was achieved using trial-start synchronization together with file-level system timestamps.

The custom C# data logging software application was updated during data collection, leading to participant-dependent file-format differences. Files from P01–P07 do not include explicit video-frame fields in the eye-tracking, tool-handle IMU, or wrist Shimmer data streams. Files from P08 onward include video-frame information in these data files, providing a direct frame-level reference to the trial video. Therefore, users should account for this file-format difference when aligning data samples across modalities.

No external hardware trigger or synchronization pulse was used. Therefore, the data streams of different modalities should be considered as concurrently recorded by the custom C# data logging software application rather than hardware-synchronized across sensors. For post hoc cross-modal alignment, video frames can be treated as the reference timeline. Eye-tracking samples, which do not include absolute device timestamps, can be mapped to video frames using proportional index mapping, whereas IMU samples can be aligned using the elapsed time derived from their recorded timestamps, where available.

Data completeness and empirical sampling rates

Data completeness was quantified using the final published file manifest. Baseline recordings with block number 0 or trial number 0 were excluded from this summary, leaving 1971 task trials across 19 participants. For each task trial, the expected files included one video file, one eye-tracking file, one tool-handle IMU file, one wrist IMU file, and one trial log file. The data file of a modality was considered complete for a given trial when the corresponding file was present in the published dataset and had non-zero file size. Trials were considered incomplete when at least one of the expected files was absent or empty.

Participant-level completeness was summarized by counting the number of sessions, task trials, no-drop trials, drop/failure trials, incomplete trials, and trials with experimenter-documented quality flags for each participant. No-drop and drop/failure trials were counted based on the “# dropped” field in the trial log file, as described above. Trials with quality flags were identified from experimenter comments containing recording- or device-quality related notes. At the participant level, 8 of the 19 participants had no trials with missing or empty modalities. Among the remaining 11 participants, 5 had missing or empty modalities in no >10% of their task trials, while 6 had missing or empty modalities in >10% of their task trials. Participant-level completeness and failure summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Across the 1971 task trials, non-empty files were available for 1968 video recordings, 1949 eye-tracking files, 1902 tool-handle IMU files, 1847 wrist IMU files, and 1965 trial log files. Empirical sampling-rate estimates were computed separately for each modality when sufficient timing information was available. For eye-tracking data, the sampling rate was estimated as the number of eye-tracking samples divided by the trial duration recorded in the trial log. For tool-handle IMU data, sampling rates were calculated separately for the left- and right-hand graspers using sequential `Time' values from rows labeled `LH' and `RH', respectively. For wrist IMU data, sampling rates were calculated separately for the left and right wrists using stable Shimmer device identifiers and sequential `System Timestamp' values. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Participants

Data were collected from non-medical university students recruited via flyers. Participants were compensated in accordance with an institutionally approved protocol (VT #23-265). Eligibility criteria included normal or corrected-to-normal vision. No age range restrictions were applied at recruitment. Handedness was self-reported, and no handedness-based exclusion was applied. All participants included in the dataset were right-handed by coincidence.

A total of 29 participants were recruited. Data from 19 participants are included in the dataset. Recordings of ten participants were not deposited in the public repository because they withdrew from the study before meeting the termination criteria. Participants who completed six sessions without achieving the predefined proficiency criterion were retained in the dataset. Compensation was provided for all sessions attended, consistent with the approved protocol.

Apparatus

Participants performed the peg transfer task using a trainer box placed on a height-adjustable desk. The participants were provided with two Maryland graspers, each with its handle instrumented with an IMU (with an average of 31 Hz sampling rate) (Fig. 3). A 19-inch monitor (1440 × 900 resolution, 60 Hz refresh rate) was mounted on a tripod and displayed a 1280 × 720 video stream that filled the display. The video stream was provided by a standard webcam inside the trainer box (with maximum resolution of 1920 × 1080, but recorded at 1280 × 720 resolution at 60 frames per second). In addition, a remote eye tracker was mounted on the monitor to collect eye-gaze data. Furthermore, participants wore a Shimmer motion tracker on each wrist.

IMU Instrumented on Maryland graspers.

Participants stood at the desk during practice. Desk height was adjusted to the participant's preference, while monitor height was adjusted to the participant's eye level. The average distance between the participant’s eyes and the monitor was approximately 60 cm. Data collection took place in a standard laboratory room under typical indoor lighting conditions (Fig. 4).

Data collection apparatus.

Study task and trial definition

Participants performed the FLS peg transfer task [3]. In this task, participants use two Maryland graspers to transfer six triangular objects between peg locations within the trainer box (Fig. 5). One trial consists of transferring all six objects from the left side to the right side and then returning them from the right to the left. For each transfer, the object is grasped with one instrument, passed to the other instrument, and then placed on the target peg.

Peg transfer task.

A trial began when the participant first grasped an object and ended when the final object was placed. Trial start and stop in the interface of the custom C# data logging software application were manually triggered by the experimenter based on direct observation of task execution. These manual triggers were used to initiate and terminate trial-specific recordings, such that each video, eye-tracking, IMU, and log file corresponds to a single observed trial. Thus, manual triggering may affect the exact behavioral onset/offset annotation, but it does not necessarily introduce additional relative timing offsets among modalities within the same recorded trial.

A trial was classified as a failure if any object was dropped. Failed trials were recorded and retained in the dataset. A drop did not terminate the trial, so the participant continued transferring the remaining objects until the task was completed. The researcher running the data collection session entered the trial-level data, including completion time, number of dropped objects, and notes on irregularities (e.g., device connectivity issues or equipment problems) that were recorded in log files.

Practice protocol and stopping criteria

Participants provided informed consent and received task instructions at the beginning of the first session. At the beginning of each session, a five-point eye-tracker calibration was performed. Sessions began only after successful calibration, and no mid-session recalibration was conducted. Participants then self-practiced the peg-transfer task following a structured protocol across sessions. Each session lasted approximately one hour and consisted of alternating cycles of 10 min of practice and 2 min of rest period. The number of practice blocks and rest breaks varied across sessions. Before each rest break, participants completed brief questionnaires, which are not included in the published dataset.

Participants did not receive any demonstrations beyond the initial task instructions. The experimenter’s role was limited to operating the custom C# data logging software application and manually triggering trial start/stop based on direct observation of task execution. The experimenter also entered the number of dropped items and any data quality concerns in the application. Upon request, participants could receive basic feedback such as whether an object had been dropped or the task completion time.

Data collection continued until participants met the proficiency criteria of completing the task within 48 s for 10 non-consecutive trials without dropping objects, or until a maximum of six sessions. The qualifying trials for proficiency could occur across multiple sessions. All data up to and including the session in which the proficiency criteria were achieved were included in the dataset. Data inclusion

For the 19 participants included in the public dataset, no trials or sessions were excluded prior to publication. All recorded trials for these participants were retained in the repository. This includes trials with dropped objects as well as trials with experimenter-documented notes, such as task-apparatus issues.

Ethics and privacy

The study protocol was approved by the Virginia Tech Institutional Review Board (VT #23-265). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant. Video recordings captured only the interior of the trainer box; therefore, they do not contain any personally identifiable information.

Limitations

This dataset includes data from a limited number of participants and does not represent the full range of surgical trainees or expert-level performers, because the participant pool consisted of non-medical university students. However, participants who completed six sessions without achieving proficiency were retained in the public dataset, preserving variability in early-stage learning trajectories.

Eye-tracking, tool-handle IMU, and wrist IMU data were recorded without correction for sensor drift or additional signal filtering after data collection at the time of data publication. In addition, calibration stability and IMU measurement consistency were not independently validated after data collection using a separate reference instrument or repeated calibration-check procedure. Eye-tracker calibration was performed at the beginning of each session, but mid-session calibration drift was not quantified. Similarly, the IMU streams should be interpreted as calibrated device outputs recorded by the custom C# data logging software application, not as measurements independently validated against an external kinematic reference. While validity flags and metadata are provided to support data filtering, signal quality may vary across trials due to calibration quality, transient device connectivity issues, or participant movement. Temperature channels from the tool-handle IMU were logged but were not part of the experimental design.
