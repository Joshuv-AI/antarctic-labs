Diffusion Operator Geometry of Feedforward Representations
Kanishka Reddy

Abstract
Neural networks transform data through learned representations whose geometry affects separation, contraction, and generalization. Recent work studies this geometry using discrete curvature on neighborhood graphs, suggesting Ricci-flow-like behavior across layers. We develop a smooth operator-theoretic alternative for feedforward representation snapshots. Each feature cloud induces a Gaussian-kernel diffusion Markov operator, and transport, spectral, label-boundary, and local-scale observables are derived from this single object via Bakry–Émery Γ \Gamma -calculus. In a balanced Gaussian class-conditional snapshot model with shared covariance, the population operator has closed-form class affinities, leakage, and coarse spectra, all controlled by pairwise regularized Mahalanobis separations c ε ( a , b ) c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)} . We also prove that the resulting operator observables vary smoothly under feature perturbations, while hard neighborhood-graph diagnostics can change discontinuously. Synthetic experiments validate the closed-form Gaussian bridge, while learned MNIST experiments show that the same operator observables track training, width, and perturbation stability. Together, these results give a stable operator-geometric framework for analyzing feedforward representation geometry.

Introduction

Neural networks reshape data through their learned representations. If

\Phi_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}
denotes the map from
input space to layer
\ell
, then the feature cloud

Z^{\ell}=\{z_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{n},\qquad z_{i}^{\ell}=\Phi_{\ell}(x_{i}),

evolves with both depth and training. These evolving snapshots often become
more separable, lower-dimensional, and aligned with label structure

[
3
,
9
,
2
,
34
,
44
,
7
]
. Understanding their
geometry is therefore a natural route toward understanding representation
learning.

A recent line of work studies this geometry by constructing neighborhood
graphs on feature clouds and measuring discrete geometric quantities such as
graph curvature, curvature gaps, and Ricci-flow-inspired evolution
coefficients
[
5
,
19
]
. This viewpoint is
appealing because it connects class separation with community formation and
local representation changes with geometric flows. However, hard graph
constructions introduce a basic instability. Small perturbations of the
feature cloud can rewire a
k
-nearest-neighbor graph, and the resulting
geometry can depend strongly on graph parameters such as neighborhood size,
radius, symmetrization, and curvature discretization

[
33
,
13
,
29
,
41
,
15
]
.

We take an operator-first view. Instead of first constructing a hard graph,
we associate each representation snapshot with a smooth diffusion Markov
operator, following the kernel-normalization viewpoint of diffusion maps
[
11
,
10
]
. For a bandwidth
\varepsilon>0
, define

W_{ij}^{\ell}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|z_{i}^{\ell}-z_{j}^{\ell}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right),\qquad P^{\ell}=(D^{\ell})^{-1}W^{\ell},\qquad L^{\ell}=\frac{P^{\ell}-I}{\varepsilon}.

The associated carré du champ operator is

\Gamma^{\ell}(f,h)(i)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{j}P_{ij}^{\ell}(f_{j}-f_{i})(h_{j}-h_{i}).

From this single operator we derive class-level transport, coarse spectra,
label-boundary energies, soft diffusion radii, and local metric surrogates.
Thus the primitive object is not a graph curvature score, but a Markov
operator from which several geometric observables are derived

[
4
,
22
,
23
,
21
]
.

The main theoretical object is a solvable Gaussian snapshot model. At a
fixed layer and training time, suppose

z\mid y=a\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma),\qquad a\in[K],

with balanced classes and shared covariance. We show that the coarse
operator geometry is controlled by

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\tfrac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}).

These separations determine normalized affinities, cross-class leakage, and
the spectrum of the induced coarse class-transition operator. In the
homogeneous case, the resulting formulas become closed-form and monotone.
Increasing regularized class separation decreases leakage and changes the
coarse spectral gap in an explicit way.

We also prove a stability result. For fixed
\varepsilon>0
and bounded
feature clouds, the map
Z\mapsto P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
is locally Lipschitz.
Consequently, the induced leakage, boundary energy, coarse transition, and
soft local-scale observables vary continuously under feature perturbations.
By contrast, hard
k
-nearest-neighbor adjacency-dependent quantities are
discontinuous at configurations where neighbor orderings change. This gives
a formal explanation for the stability advantage of the operator
construction
[
40
,
8
]
.

Contributions.

We make four contributions. First, we formalize feedforward representation
snapshots as diffusion Markov operators and derive transport, spectral,
label-boundary, and local-scale observables from this single object. Second,
under a balanced shared-covariance Gaussian snapshot model, we prove a
closed-form bridge showing that coarse operator geometry is governed by the
regularized Mahalanobis separations
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
, yielding
explicit formulas for affinities, leakage, and class spectra. Third, we
prove that the diffusion operator and its induced observables are locally
Lipschitz under feature perturbations, while hard
k
-nearest-neighbor
graph observables can be discontinuous. Finally, synthetic Gaussian experiments validate the closed-form bridge,
while learned ten-class MNIST experiments show that the operator
observables give stable, interpretable measurements across training, width,
and perturbations.

3
Operator representation snapshots

We associate each feedforward representation snapshot with a diffusion
operator and derive all observables from this single object.

Definition 1

(Operator representation snapshot)
.

Let
Z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a feature cloud from a fixed
layer and training time. For bandwidth
\varepsilon>0
, define

W_{ij}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right),\qquad D_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij},\qquad P_{ij}=\frac{W_{ij}}{D_{i}}.

We call
P=P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
the diffusion Markov operator of the
snapshot, with generator
L=(P-I)/\varepsilon

[
11
,
10
]
.

The corresponding carré du champ is the local quadratic form induced by

P

[
4
,
22
,
23
,
21
]
.

Definition 2

(Discrete carré du champ)
.

For
f,h:\{1,\dots,n\}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

\Gamma(f,h)(i)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}(f_{j}-f_{i})(h_{j}-h_{i}),\qquad\Gamma(f)=\Gamma(f,f).

With labels
y_{i}\in[K]
, let
C_{a}=\{i:y_{i}=a\}
and
n_{a}=|C_{a}|
.
The empirical coarse class transition matrix, cross-class leakage,
classwise label-boundary energy, and soft diffusion radius are

\widehat{P}_{ab}=\frac{1}{n_{a}}\sum_{i\in C_{a}}\sum_{j\in C_{b}}P_{ij},\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j:y_{j}\neq y_{i}}P_{ij},

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma(g_{a})(i)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon n}\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\big(g_{a}(j)-g_{a}(i)\big)^{2},\qquad g_{a}(i)=\mathbf{1}\{y_{i}=a\},

and

\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}.

For the one-hot label map
g(i)=e_{y_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, the total
label-boundary energy satisfies

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j:y_{j}\neq y_{i}}P_{ij}.

Thus label-boundary energy is the
\Gamma
-calculus counterpart of
cross-class diffusion mass.

For finite-sample class transport in the experiments, we remove diagonal
self-loop entries and renormalize rows before aggregating by class. This
does not change the population formulas, where individual point self-loops
vanish in the large-sample limit, but avoids a finite-sample artifact from

W_{ii}=1
. Local quantities remain defined directly from

P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
.

Transport, spectra, label-boundary energy, local
\Gamma
-geometry, and
soft local scale are therefore downstream summaries of the same Markov
operator. This differs from hard neighborhood graph diagnostics, which first
select a combinatorial adjacency pattern and then compute geometry on the
resulting graph.

4
A Gaussian population bridge

Before specializing to Gaussians, we separate the population operator
construction from the parametric model. Let
\nu_{a}
be the
class-conditional distribution of a representation snapshot given
y=a
,
and define

\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{a},\;X^{\prime}\sim\nu_{b}}\left[\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right)\right].

For balanced classes, row-normalizing these averaged affinities gives the
class-level kernel-overlap chain

T_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}=\frac{\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}\alpha_{ar}^{(\varepsilon)}}.

This is a distribution-free overlap closure for the population operator.
The exact row-normalized population transition is instead

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{a}}\left[\frac{r_{b}^{(\varepsilon)}(X)}{\sum_{c}r_{c}^{(\varepsilon)}(X)}\right],\qquad r_{b}^{(\varepsilon)}(x)=\int k_{\varepsilon}(x,x^{\prime})\,d\nu_{b}(x^{\prime}),

so the closure is most accurate when these classwise kernel-degree profiles
are homogeneous within each source class. A symmetric operator-native
separation is

\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=-\log\frac{\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{aa}^{(\varepsilon)}\alpha_{bb}^{(\varepsilon)}}}.

The Gaussian model below is one solvable case in which these affinities are
explicit functions of means and covariance.

Assumption 1

(Balanced Gaussian snapshot model)
.

At a fixed layer and training time,

z\mid y=a\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma)
for
a\in[K]
, with

\mathbb{P}(y=a)=1/K
and shared covariance
\Sigma\succeq 0
.

Define the regularized Mahalanobis separation

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}),\qquad c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,a)}=0.

The following theorem gives the closed-form bridge between class geometry
and the induced diffusion operator.

Theorem 1

(Gaussian operator bridge)
.

Let
X_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma)
and

X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma)
be independent samples from classes

a
and
b
, and let

k_{\varepsilon}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right).

Then

\alpha_{ab}:=\mathbb{E}[k_{\varepsilon}(X_{a},X_{b})]=\alpha_{0}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}},\qquad\alpha_{0}=\det\!\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}.

Consequently, the mean-field coarse class transition matrix is

\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{\alpha_{ab}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}\alpha_{ar}}=\frac{e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,r)}}}.

Thus the coarse class transition structure is determined entirely by the
pairwise separations
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
, and

\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
in this model.

Proof sketch.

Since
X_{a}-X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b},2\Sigma)
, the affinity is a
Gaussian quadratic integral. Evaluating it gives the determinant factor

\alpha_{0}
and the exponential term involving

(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}
. Row-normalizing the resulting affinities
gives the coarse transition matrix. The full derivation is given in
Appendix
A
.
∎

Thus the theorem is a closed-form evaluation of the general kernel
affinities
\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}
, not a claim that learned
class-conditional feature distributions are universally Gaussian. The operator therefore does not read Euclidean class-mean distances
directly. In this model, it reads class separation through the
covariance-regularized metric
(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}
, with

\varepsilon
setting the diffusion scale.

Let

w_{ab}=e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}},\qquad q_{a}=\sum_{r=1}^{K}w_{ar}.

Since
w_{ab}=w_{ba}
, the coarse chain is reversible with stationary
distribution
\bar{\pi}_{a}=q_{a}/\sum_{s}q_{s}
.

Proposition 1

(Population leakage)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the stationary cross-class
leakage of the coarse population chain is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\bar{\pi}_{a}\sum_{b\neq a}\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{\sum_{a\neq b}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}}{K+\sum_{a\neq b}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}}.

Proof.

The stationary mass crossing from class
a
to class
b
is

\bar{\pi}_{a}\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{q_{a}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}\frac{w_{ab}}{q_{a}}=\frac{w_{ab}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}.

Summing over
a\neq b
gives the numerator. Since
w_{aa}=1
,

\sum_{s}q_{s}=\sum_{a,b}w_{ab}=K+\sum_{a\neq b}w_{ab}
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Homogeneous separation)
.

If
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=c
for all
a\neq b
, then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\frac{(K-1)e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}},\qquad\lambda_{1}=1,\qquad\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{K}=\frac{1-e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}},

and hence

1-\lambda_{2}=\frac{Ke^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}=\frac{K}{e^{c}+K-1}.

Thus increasing regularized class separation decreases leakage and makes
the coarse chain more class-persistent, equivalently decreasing the coarse
mixing gap.

The corollary gives a snapshot-level interpretation of representation
trajectories. If effective separations
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
increase
along depth or training, the operator model predicts lower cross-class
leakage and stronger class persistence.

Proposition 2

(Coarse versus local geometry)
.

Under Assumption
1
, coarse affinities,

\bar{P}
, and stationary leakage depend on

\{\mu_{a},\Sigma\}_{a=1}^{K}
only through the pairwise scalars

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
. In contrast, local quantities such as

\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(x_{r},x_{s})(X_{a})]
and

\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}(X_{a})]
also depend on covariance directions
and on the orientations of the mean differences
\mu_{b}-\mu_{a}
.

Proof sketch.

The affinity calculation in Theorem
1
integrates
out all directional information except the quadratic form defining

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
. By contrast,
\Gamma(x_{r},x_{s})
and

\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}
involve second moments of the displacement
X^{\prime}-X

under the kernel-weighted transition distribution. These moments retain
directional information through
\Sigma
and
\mu_{b}-\mu_{a}
. See
Appendix
A.5
for explicit formulas.
∎

5
Stability of operator observables

We now show that the operator construction is stable under small feature
perturbations. For feature clouds
Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
and

\widetilde{Z}=(\widetilde{z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{z}_{n})
, define

\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\|z_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{i}\|.

For a matrix
A
, let

\|A\|_{\infty\to\infty}=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|A_{ij}|

denote the row-sum operator norm.

Theorem 2

(Stability of the diffusion operator)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
R>0
. On the set of feature clouds satisfying

\max_{i}\|z_{i}\|\leq R
, the map
Z\mapsto P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
is Lipschitz
with a constant independent of
n
. More precisely, for any

Z,\widetilde{Z}
satisfying

\max_{i}\|z_{i}\|\leq R,\qquad\max_{i}\|\widetilde{z}_{i}\|\leq R,

we have

\|P_{\varepsilon}(Z)-P_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq\frac{4R}{\varepsilon}\exp(R^{2}/\varepsilon)\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

In particular, the same bound controls the entrywise maximum norm.

Proof sketch.

On bounded feature clouds,

\big|\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}-\|\widetilde{z}_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{j}\|^{2}\big|\leq 8R\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Since
u\mapsto e^{-u/(4\varepsilon)}
has derivative bounded by

1/(4\varepsilon)
, the kernel entries satisfy

\|W(Z)-W(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

All points lie in the radius-
R
ball, so
W_{ij}\geq\beta=\exp(-R^{2}/\varepsilon)
and
D_{i}\geq n\beta
. Row-normalization is
therefore stable in row-sum norm, with the factor of
n
from summing row
perturbations canceled by the lower degree bound. The full proof is given
in Appendix
B
.
∎

The observables inherit this stability.

Corollary 2

(Stability of operator observables)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, the empirical
coarse class transition matrix
\widehat{P}
, raw cross-class leakage

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{leak}}
, label-boundary energies

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}
, total one-hot label-boundary energy

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}
, and squared soft diffusion radii

\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i)=\sum_{j}P_{ij}\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}

are Lipschitz functions of
Z
on bounded feature clouds, for fixed
labels.

Proof sketch.

The coarse transition matrix, raw leakage, and boundary energies are finite
linear combinations of rows of
P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
, with coefficients
determined by the labels. For the soft radius, both the weights
P_{ij}

and squared distances
\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}
vary Lipschitzly on bounded feature
clouds.
∎

The self-loop-removed class transport used in the empirical section obeys
the same conclusion on subsets where the off-diagonal row masses

\sum_{j\neq i}P_{ij}
are bounded away from zero. This excludes the
degenerate finite-sample regime in which a row is essentially all self-loop
mass.

This behavior contrasts with hard neighborhood graphs. Let
A_{k}(Z)
be
the adjacency matrix of a
k
-nearest-neighbor graph built from
Z
,
with any fixed tie-breaking rule.

Proposition 3

(Discontinuity of hard
k
-NN adjacency)
.

For
1\leq k<n-1
, the map
Z\mapsto A_{k}(Z)
is discontinuous at any
feature cloud where, for some point
z_{i}
, the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-st
nearest-neighbor distances are equal. Consequently, any graph observable
that depends nontrivially on this adjacency can change discontinuously
under arbitrarily small feature perturbations.

Proof.

At such a tie, arbitrarily small perturbations can reverse the order of the
two neighbors. Along one perturbation, one neighbor is included in the

k
-nearest-neighbor set of
i
; along another, the other is included.
Thus an adjacency entry changes by one under perturbations of arbitrarily
small norm, and any observable that distinguishes these adjacency patterns
inherits the discontinuity.
∎

Together, Theorem
2
and
Proposition
3
give a stability-discontinuity
dichotomy. Under the same feature perturbation, the diffusion operator
changes by
O(\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty})
, while hard
k
-NN adjacency
can change by
\Omega(1)
. This is the formal content of the stability
advantage tested empirically in Section
6
.

6
Empirical evaluation

We evaluate the operator framework along three axes. First, we test whether
the Gaussian bridge predicts empirical operator observables in the
controlled setting where its assumptions hold. Second, we measure how the
operator observables evolve across training and width in learned
feedforward representations. Third, we test whether operator observables
are more stable than hard-neighborhood graph observables under small
perturbations.

Unless otherwise stated, all learned-representation experiments use a
two-hidden-layer ReLU MLP trained on ten-class MNIST. We report the
mean pairwise separation
\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}
, cross-class leakage, the
coarse mixing gap
1-\lambda_{2}(\widehat{P}^{(K)})
, and the root-mean-square
soft diffusion radius. For the perturbation experiment we also report the
one-hot label-boundary energy
E[\Gamma(g,g)]
.

For empirical coarse-transition calculations, we remove point self-loops
from the diffusion operator and renormalize rows before aggregating by
class. This avoids a finite-sample artifact from the diagonal kernel
entries
W_{ii}=1
. The population formulas in
Section
4
describe the class-level large-sample
limit, where individual point self-loops vanish. Implementation details,
including bandwidth selection, subset sizes, and hard-graph baselines, are
given in Appendix
C
.

For consistency with Proposition
1
, the leakage
we report from the empirical class-transition matrix is the stationary
leakage of the coarse chain. The raw node-average leakage defined in
Section
3
is a related but distinct finite-sample
observable.

6.2
Training dynamics

We next track the operator observables over training. To isolate geometric
change from bandwidth drift, each hidden layer uses a fixed bandwidth chosen
from its initialization snapshot. This convention measures the learned
representation at a fixed diffusion scale. In later epochs, especially in
deeper layers, sharp changes in leakage or gap indicate that feature
separation has expanded beyond the initial kernel scale. We also inspect
the full pairwise separation matrix

\{c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\}
in the deepest layer.

Top:
across hidden layers, training increases mean separation

Bottom:
the full

The trends in Figure

show that the operator
observables track the emergence of class structure during training. As
regularized class separation increases, cross-class diffusion weakens and
the class-level chain becomes more persistent. The heatmaps show that this
evolution is not captured by a single global statistic. Different class
pairs separate at different rates, so the full matrix

\{c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\}
contains information lost by its mean alone.

6.3
Width dependence

To study model scale, we fix depth and vary hidden width. For this
experiment, each feature cloud is centered and divided by its RMS feature
norm before constructing the operator. All widths are then evaluated using
a common reference bandwidth
\varepsilon^{\star}
, chosen from a baseline
model after the same normalization. This isolates relative representation
geometry from global feature-scale changes.

Figure

regularized class separation
\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}

Cross-class leakage decreases modestly, while the coarse mixing gap
changes only weakly. Thus, increasing width primarily improves class-pair

Stability versus hard neighborhood graphs

Finally, we test the main methodological motivation for replacing hard

-NN graph observables, and measure variability across

7
Discussion

This paper develops an operator geometry for feedforward representation
snapshots. A feature cloud
Z
is represented by a diffusion Markov
operator
P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
, from which transport, spectra,
label-boundary energy, local scale, and
\Gamma
-geometry are all
derived. The Gaussian bridge gives one solvable instance of this
abstraction. Under a balanced shared-covariance Gaussian snapshot model,
the coarse operator geometry is controlled by

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}),

so the operator measures class separation at diffusion scale

\varepsilon
through a covariance-regularized metric. Coarse quantities
such as affinities, leakage, and class spectra reduce to the separation
matrix
\{c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\}
, while local quantities such as

\Gamma
-Gram matrices and soft radii retain directional covariance
information. The stability theorem adds a second structural point. At
fixed bandwidth, the Gaussian-kernel operator is a smooth function of

Z
and its observables inherit this stability, while hard neighborhood
graphs instead pass through a discontinuous adjacency-selection step.

The framework is built for feedforward snapshots, where a layer is
treated as a map from one feature cloud to the next. This viewpoint is
complementary to kernel-limit, neural tangent kernel, and
signal-propagation analyses

[
12
,
27
,
20
,
38
]
, since our object is
the diffusion operator induced by a finite learned feature cloud. Other
architectures induce different operator objects. Recurrent models suggest

conditional Markov kernels rather than Euclidean clouds

. A further direction within
feedforward snapshots is to study pointwise leakage scores and their

Appendix A
Proofs for the Gaussian operator bridge

This appendix gives the derivations behind
Theorem
1
,
Proposition
1
,
Corollary
1
, and
Proposition
2
. Throughout the appendix we consider a
fixed representation snapshot and suppress layer and training-time indices.

We assume the balanced shared-covariance Gaussian snapshot model

z\mid y=a\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma),\qquad a\in[K],\qquad\mathbb{P}(y=a)=\frac{1}{K},

and use the Gaussian kernel

k_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|u-v\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right).

For general class-conditionals
\nu_{a}
, define

\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{a},\;X^{\prime}\sim\nu_{b}}\left[k_{\varepsilon}(X,X^{\prime})\right].

The Gaussian calculation below is the special case in which these
population affinities can be written explicitly in terms of class means and
a shared covariance. In the balanced shared-covariance Gaussian model,

\alpha_{ab}^{(\varepsilon)}=\alpha_{0}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}
, so the
general kernel-overlap chain reduces to the closed-form coarse chain in
Theorem
1
.

For two classes
a,b
, write

\Delta^{(a,b)}:=\mu_{b}-\mu_{a}

and

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}).

We set
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,a)}=0
.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Let

X_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma),\qquad X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma)

be independent. Then

X_{a}-X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b},2\Sigma).

We need to compute

\alpha_{ab}=\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|X_{a}-X_{b}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right).

Diagonalize
\Sigma=U\Lambda U^{\top}
, where

\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
, and define

\widetilde{\Delta}^{(a,b)}=U^{\top}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}).

Since the kernel is rotation invariant, the expectation factorizes over
coordinates. If
G\sim\mathcal{N}(m,s^{2})
, then

\mathbb{E}[e^{-tG^{2}}]=(1+2ts^{2})^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{tm^{2}}{1+2ts^{2}}\right).

Applying this with

t=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon},\qquad s^{2}=2\lambda_{r},\qquad m=\widetilde{\Delta}^{(a,b)}_{r},

gives

\mathbb{E}\exp\!\left(-\frac{G^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right)=\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\widetilde{\Delta}^{(a,b)}_{r})^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+\lambda_{r})}\right).

Multiplying over coordinates yields

\alpha_{ab}=\det\!\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}(\varepsilon I+\Sigma)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})\right).

Thus

\alpha_{ab}=\alpha_{0}\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right),\qquad\alpha_{0}=\det\!\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}.

Since
\alpha_{0}
is common to every pair of classes, it cancels under
row-normalization. Therefore the population coarse class transition matrix
is

\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{\alpha_{ab}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}\alpha_{ar}}=\frac{\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right)}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,r)}\right)}.

This proves Theorem
1
.

Special cases.

If
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I
, then

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{\|\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}\|^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+\sigma^{2})}.

If
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I+\beta vv^{\top}
, with
\|v\|=1
, and

\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}=\Delta_{\parallel}v+\Delta_{\perp},\qquad\Delta_{\perp}\perp v,

then

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{\Delta_{\parallel}^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+\sigma^{2}+\beta)}+\frac{\|\Delta_{\perp}\|^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+\sigma^{2})}.

If
\Sigma
is invertible and
\varepsilon\to 0
, then

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\to\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}).

A.2
Reversibility of the coarse chain

For convenience, define

w_{ab}=\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right),\qquad q_{a}=\sum_{r=1}^{K}w_{ar}.

Then

\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{w_{ab}}{q_{a}}.

Since

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=c_{\varepsilon}^{(b,a)},

we have
w_{ab}=w_{ba}
. Hence the coarse chain is reversible with
stationary distribution

\bar{\pi}_{a}=\frac{q_{a}}{\sum_{s=1}^{K}q_{s}}.

Indeed,

\bar{\pi}_{a}\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{q_{a}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}\frac{w_{ab}}{q_{a}}=\frac{w_{ab}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}=\frac{w_{ba}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}=\bar{\pi}_{b}\bar{P}_{ba}.

A.3
Proof of Proposition
1

The stationary cross-class leakage of the coarse chain is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\bar{\pi}_{a}\sum_{b\neq a}\bar{P}_{ab}.

Using the reversibility notation above,

\bar{\pi}_{a}\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{w_{ab}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}.

Therefore

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\frac{\sum_{a\neq b}w_{ab}}{\sum_{s}q_{s}}.

Since
w_{aa}=1
,

\sum_{s}q_{s}=\sum_{a,b}w_{ab}=K+\sum_{a\neq b}w_{ab}.

Thus

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\frac{\sum_{a\neq b}\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right)}{K+\sum_{a\neq b}\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right)}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=1-\frac{K}{K+\sum_{a\neq b}\exp\!\left(-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\right)}.

This proves Proposition
1
.

A.4
Proof of Corollary
1

Assume

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=c\qquad\text{for all }a\neq b.

Then

w_{aa}=1,\qquad w_{ab}=e^{-c}\quad(a\neq b),

and

q_{a}=1+(K-1)e^{-c}

for every class
a
. Hence

\bar{P}_{aa}=\frac{1}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}},\qquad\bar{P}_{ab}=\frac{e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}\quad(a\neq b).

The leakage formula becomes

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{leak}}=\frac{K(K-1)e^{-c}}{K+K(K-1)e^{-c}}=\frac{(K-1)e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}.

The coarse transition matrix can be written as

\bar{P}=\frac{1-e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}I+\frac{e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

Therefore
\mathbf{1}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
1
, and every
vector orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue

\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{K}=\frac{1-e^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}=\frac{e^{c}-1}{e^{c}+K-1}.

Thus the coarse spectral gap is

1-\lambda_{2}=\frac{Ke^{-c}}{1+(K-1)e^{-c}}=\frac{K}{e^{c}+K-1}.

This proves Corollary
1
.

A.5
Local geometry and proof of Proposition
2

The preceding formulas show that coarse class affinities, the coarse
transition matrix, and stationary leakage depend on the Gaussian snapshot
parameters only through the scalars
c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
. Local
operator geometry retains more information.

To see this explicitly, consider the displacement

\delta=X_{b}-X_{a},\qquad X_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma),\qquad X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma).

Then

\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(\Delta^{(a,b)},2\Sigma),\qquad\Delta^{(a,b)}=\mu_{b}-\mu_{a}.

The diffusion transition reweights this displacement by the Gaussian kernel

\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\delta\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right).

The resulting tilted distribution is Gaussian with mean

m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}\Delta^{(a,b)}

and covariance

V_{\varepsilon}=2\Sigma\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}.

This follows by completing the square in the Gaussian density, or
equivalently by diagonalizing
\Sigma
and applying the one-dimensional
Gaussian tilting identity coordinatewise.

Now let
x_{r}(z)=z_{r}
denote the
r
-th coordinate function. At
mean-field level, for a point in class
a
, the local coordinate

\Gamma
-Gram matrix is

G_{a}=\left(\Gamma(x_{r},x_{s})\right)_{r,s=1}^{d}\approx\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\bar{P}_{ab}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{tilt},a,b}\left[\delta\delta^{\top}\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{tilt},a,b}
denotes expectation under the
kernel-tilted displacement law from class
a
to class
b
. Since

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{tilt},a,b}[\delta\delta^{\top}]=V_{\varepsilon}+m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)\top},

we obtain

G_{a}\approx\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\bar{P}_{ab}m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)\top}.

Equivalently,

G_{a}\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Sigma\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\bar{P}_{ab}m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)\top}.

Thus the local
\Gamma
-Gram depends not only on the scalar separations

c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
, but also on covariance directions and the
orientations of the mean differences
\Delta^{(a,b)}
.

The same phenomenon appears in the soft diffusion radius. For a point in
class
a
, define

\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\sum_{j}P_{ij}\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}.

At mean-field level,

\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mid y_{i}=a]\approx\sum_{b=1}^{K}\bar{P}_{ab}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{tilt},a,b}\left[\|\delta\|^{2}\right].

Using the tilted mean and covariance above,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{tilt},a,b}\left[\|\delta\|^{2}\right]=\mathrm{tr}(V_{\varepsilon})+\|m_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\|^{2}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mid y_{i}=a]\approx 2\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}\right)+\sum_{b=1}^{K}\bar{P}_{ab}\left\|\left(I+\frac{\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}\Delta^{(a,b)}\right\|^{2}.

These formulas prove the claim in
Proposition
2
. Coarse transport quantities collapse
to the scalar separation matrix

\{c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\}_{a,b}
, while local
\Gamma
-geometry and
soft local scale retain directional covariance and mean-difference
information.

A.6
Mean-field interpretation

The formulas above are population and mean-field statements. The affinity
identity in Theorem
1
is an exact population
expectation. The coarse chain is the class-level Markov chain obtained by
row-normalizing those population affinities. Finite empirical operators
fluctuate around this limit because they depend on the sampled feature
cloud, finite class counts, and the self-loop entries
W_{ii}=1
.

For large balanced samples, the contribution of a single self-loop to a row
sum is
O(1)
, while the total off-diagonal affinity mass is typically

O(n)
. Thus the block-constant population chain provides the natural
large-sample approximation to the empirical class transition matrix.

A.7
Beyond the shared-covariance Gaussian model

The operator definitions do not require Gaussianity. The Gaussian model is
used only to obtain closed-form formulas.

If classes have unequal covariances,

z\mid y=a\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma_{a}),

then kernel affinities remain closed form, since

X_{a}-X_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b},\Sigma_{a}+\Sigma_{b}).

In that case,

\mathbb{E}[k_{\varepsilon}(X_{a},X_{b})]=\det\!\left(I+\frac{\Sigma_{a}+\Sigma_{b}}{2\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})^{\top}\left(I+\frac{\Sigma_{a}+\Sigma_{b}}{2\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}(\mu_{a}-\mu_{b})\right).

Unlike the shared-covariance case, the determinant factor now depends on

(a,b)
, so the affinity cannot be summarized by a single shared
multiplicative constant times
e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}
.

For non-Gaussian or multimodal class-conditionals, the same operator
observables are still well-defined, but the reduction to means and
covariances need not hold. A useful population analogue of the Gaussian separation is the symmetric
operator-native quantity

\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}:=-\log\frac{\mathbb{E}[k_{\varepsilon}(z,z^{\prime})\mid y=a,\ y^{\prime}=b]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[k_{\varepsilon}(z,z^{\prime})\mid y=a,\ y^{\prime}=a]\,\mathbb{E}[k_{\varepsilon}(z,z^{\prime})\mid y=b,\ y^{\prime}=b]}}.

In the shared-covariance Gaussian model, the within-class affinities are
equal and the cross-class affinity is

\alpha_{ab}=\alpha_{0}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}
. Hence

\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=-\log\frac{\alpha_{0}e^{-c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}}}{\alpha_{0}}=c_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}.

More generally,
\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}
remains an
operator-native measure of class affinity, even when it no longer reduces
to a regularized Mahalanobis distance.

Appendix B
Proofs for stability and hard-neighborhood discontinuity

This appendix proves Theorem
2
,
Corollary
2
, and
Proposition
3
. Throughout, let

Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},\qquad\widetilde{Z}=(\widetilde{z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{z}_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},

and define

\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|z_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{i}\|.

For fixed
\varepsilon>0
, define

W_{ij}(Z)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right),\qquad D_{i}(Z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}(Z),\qquad P_{ij}(Z)=\frac{W_{ij}(Z)}{D_{i}(Z)}.

For matrices we use the row-sum operator norm

\|A\|_{\infty\to\infty}=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|A_{ij}|.

B.1
Distance and kernel perturbation bounds

We first record two elementary estimates.

Lemma 1

(Squared-distance perturbation)
.

Assume

\max_{i}\|z_{i}\|\leq R,\qquad\max_{i}\|\widetilde{z}_{i}\|\leq R.

Then for every
i,j
,

\left|\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}-\|\widetilde{z}_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{j}\|^{2}\right|\leq 8R\,\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Let

a=z_{i}-z_{j},\qquad b=\widetilde{z}_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{j}.

Then

\big|\|a\|^{2}-\|b\|^{2}\big|=|\langle a+b,a-b\rangle|\leq\|a+b\|\,\|a-b\|.

Since

\|a+b\|\leq\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|+\|\widetilde{z}_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{j}\|\leq 4R

and

\|a-b\|=\|(z_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{i})-(z_{j}-\widetilde{z}_{j})\|\leq 2\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty},

the result follows.
∎

Lemma 2

(Kernel perturbation)
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
1
,

|W_{ij}(Z)-W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}

for every
i,j
.

Proof.

The function
u\mapsto e^{-u/(4\varepsilon)}
has derivative

-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}e^{-u/(4\varepsilon)},

whose absolute value is bounded by
1/(4\varepsilon)
for
u\geq 0
.
Therefore

|W_{ij}(Z)-W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left|\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}-\|\widetilde{z}_{i}-\widetilde{z}_{j}\|^{2}\right|.

Applying Lemma
1
gives

|W_{ij}(Z)-W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
2

Let

\eta=\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty},\qquad L_{W}=\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}.

By Lemma
2
,

|W_{ij}(Z)-W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq L_{W}\eta

for every
i,j
.

Because all points lie in the radius-
R
ball, every pairwise distance is
at most
2R
. Hence

W_{ij}(Z)\geq\exp(-R^{2}/\varepsilon)=:\beta,\qquad W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})\geq\beta.

Consequently,

D_{i}(Z)\geq n\beta,\qquad D_{i}(\widetilde{Z})\geq n\beta.

Fix a row
i
, and write

w_{j}=W_{ij}(Z),\qquad\widetilde{w}_{j}=W_{ij}(\widetilde{Z}),\qquad D=\sum_{j}w_{j},\qquad\widetilde{D}=\sum_{j}\widetilde{w}_{j}.

Let

\Delta_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|w_{j}-\widetilde{w}_{j}|.

Then

\Delta_{i}\leq nL_{W}\eta,\qquad|D-\widetilde{D}|\leq\Delta_{i}.

Now

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left|\frac{w_{j}}{D}-\frac{\widetilde{w}_{j}}{\widetilde{D}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}\frac{|w_{j}-\widetilde{w}_{j}|}{D}+\sum_{j}\widetilde{w}_{j}\left|\frac{1}{D}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{D}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{i}}{D}+\widetilde{D}\frac{|D-\widetilde{D}|}{D\widetilde{D}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Delta_{i}}{D}.

Using
D\geq n\beta
, we get

\sum_{j}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq\frac{2nL_{W}\eta}{n\beta}=\frac{2L_{W}}{\beta}\eta.

Taking the maximum over rows yields

\|P_{\varepsilon}(Z)-P_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq\frac{2L_{W}}{\beta}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Substituting

L_{W}=\frac{2R}{\varepsilon},\qquad\beta=e^{-R^{2}/\varepsilon},

gives

\|P_{\varepsilon}(Z)-P_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq\frac{4R}{\varepsilon}\exp(R^{2}/\varepsilon)\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

This proves Theorem
2
. Since the entrywise
maximum norm is bounded by the row-sum norm, the same estimate also
controls individual entries.

B.3
Proof of Corollary
2

We now show that the observables used in the main text inherit the
Lipschitz stability of
P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
. Throughout this subsection,
labels are fixed. Let

C_{P}=\frac{4R}{\varepsilon}\exp(R^{2}/\varepsilon),

so that

\|P(Z)-P(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq C_{P}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Coarse class transition.

Recall

\widehat{P}_{ab}(Z)=\frac{1}{n_{a}}\sum_{i:y_{i}=a}\sum_{j:y_{j}=b}P_{ij}(Z).

Then

\displaystyle|\widehat{P}_{ab}(Z)-\widehat{P}_{ab}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{a}}\sum_{i:y_{i}=a}\sum_{j:y_{j}=b}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{a}}\sum_{i:y_{i}=a}\sum_{j}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\|P(Z)-P(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Thus each coarse transition entry is Lipschitz.

Raw cross-class leakage.

Recall

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{leak}}(Z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\sum_{j:y_{j}\neq y_{i}}P_{ij}(Z).

Then

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{leak}}(Z)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{leak}}(\widetilde{Z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\sum_{j:y_{j}\neq y_{i}}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\|P(Z)-P(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Label-boundary energy.

For the class indicator
g_{a}(i)=\mathbf{1}\{y_{i}=a\}
,

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(Z)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon n}\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(Z)(g_{a}(j)-g_{a}(i))^{2}.

Since

0\leq(g_{a}(j)-g_{a}(i))^{2}\leq 1,

we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(Z)-\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon n}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|P(Z)-P(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{P}}{2\varepsilon}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

The total one-hot label-boundary energy

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}
is a finite sum of the

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{a}
, and is therefore Lipschitz as well. Equivalently,
using

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}(Z)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\sum_{i}\sum_{j:y_{j}\neq y_{i}}P_{ij}(Z),

one obtains the sharper bound

\left|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}(Z)-\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{label}}(\widetilde{Z})\right|\leq\frac{C_{P}}{\varepsilon}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Soft diffusion radius.

The soft diffusion radius is

\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;Z)=\sum_{j}P_{ij}(Z)\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}.

Add and subtract

\sum_{j}P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;Z)-\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;\widetilde{Z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})\left|\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}-\|\widetilde{z}_{j}-\widetilde{z}_{i}\|^{2}\right|.

On the bounded set,
\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|\leq 2R
, so

\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}\leq 4R^{2}.

Therefore the first term is bounded by

4R^{2}\|P(Z)-P(\widetilde{Z})\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq 4R^{2}C_{P}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

For the second term, Lemma
1
gives

\left|\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2}-\|\widetilde{z}_{j}-\widetilde{z}_{i}\|^{2}\right|\leq 8R\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Since
\sum_{j}P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})=1
, the second term is at most

8R\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

Thus

\left|\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;Z)-\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;\widetilde{Z})\right|\leq\left(4R^{2}C_{P}+8R\right)\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\infty}.

This proves Lipschitz stability of the squared soft diffusion radius.

For the root soft radius

\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(i;Z)=\left(\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;Z)\right)^{1/2},

the square-root map is locally Lipschitz on any subset where

\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(i;Z)
is bounded away from zero. The paper
reports aggregate soft radii computed from the squared quantities, so the
squared-radius stability is the relevant structural statement.

Combining the preceding bounds proves
Corollary
2
.

B.4
Self-loop-removed finite-sample transport

The main theoretical operator
P_{\varepsilon}(Z)
includes self-loops
because
W_{ii}=1
. In finite empirical class-transport calculations, we
also use the self-loop-removed row normalization

Q_{ij}(Z)=\frac{P_{ij}(Z)\mathbf{1}\{i\neq j\}}{\sum_{m\neq i}P_{im}(Z)}

whenever the denominator is nonzero. This matches the convention used in
the empirical coarse-transition and leakage computations.

Let

s_{i}(Z)=\sum_{m\neq i}P_{im}(Z).

On any subset of feature clouds where

s_{i}(Z)\geq\gamma>0\qquad\text{for all }i,

the map
P\mapsto Q
is Lipschitz in row-sum norm. Indeed, for each row,
the same normalization argument used in the proof of
Theorem
2
gives

\sum_{j}|Q_{ij}(Z)-Q_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\sum_{j}|P_{ij}(Z)-P_{ij}(\widetilde{Z})|.

Therefore the self-loop-removed coarse transition and leakage observables
are also Lipschitz on such subsets. The condition
s_{i}(Z)\geq\gamma

excludes the degenerate finite-sample regime in which a row is essentially
all self-loop mass. In the population limit considered in
Section
4
, individual point self-loops vanish
relative to the total affinity mass.

B.5
Discontinuity of hard neighborhood graphs

We now prove Proposition
3
. Let

A_{k}(Z)
denote the adjacency matrix of a directed
k
-nearest-neighbor
graph, with an arbitrary but fixed deterministic tie-breaking rule. The
same argument applies to symmetrized
k
-nearest-neighbor graphs whenever
the changed directed edge affects the symmetrized adjacency.

Fix
1\leq k<n-1
. Suppose there exists a point
z_{i}
for which the

k
-th and
(k+1)
-st nearest-neighbor distances are equal. That is,
there are two distinct candidate neighbors
z_{p}
and
z_{q}
such that

\|z_{i}-z_{p}\|=\|z_{i}-z_{q}\|=r,

and exactly
k-1
other points are strictly closer to
z_{i}
.

Assume that the tie-breaking rule selects
p
rather than
q
, so that

A_{k}(Z)_{ip}=1,\qquad A_{k}(Z)_{iq}=0.

For any
\eta>0
, we can perturb
z_{p}
and
z_{q}
by less than

\eta
so that
z_{q}
becomes slightly closer to
z_{i}
than
z_{p}
,
while all other neighbor orderings remain unchanged. Denote the perturbed
cloud by
Z^{(\eta)}
. Then

\|Z^{(\eta)}-Z\|_{\infty}<\eta,

but

A_{k}(Z^{(\eta)})_{ip}=0,\qquad A_{k}(Z^{(\eta)})_{iq}=1.

Thus an adjacency entry changes by one under an arbitrarily small
perturbation. Hence
Z\mapsto A_{k}(Z)
is discontinuous at
Z
.

If a graph observable
M(A_{k}(Z))
takes different values on these two
adjacency patterns, then

M(A_{k}(Z^{(\eta)}))\not\to M(A_{k}(Z))

as
\eta\to 0
. Thus any adjacency-dependent observable that nontrivially
distinguishes the two graphs is also discontinuous at such a
neighbor-swap configuration.

Appendix C
Implementation Details

This appendix summarizes the computational choices used in the reported
experiments.

Reported runs.

The unified experiment script supports both reduced multiclass and larger
multiclass runs. By default, the script is configured for reduced
multiclass experiments, while the learned-representation figures reported
in the paper are generated within the ten-class MNIST setting.

These reported learned-representation figures include training dynamics,
pairwise separation heatmaps, width sweeps, and stability experiments. The
synthetic bridge validation remains a
K=4
shared-covariance Gaussian
mixture experiment.

Operator construction.

Given a feature snapshot

Z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

we form the Gaussian affinity matrix

W_{ij}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right),

the degree vector

D_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij},

and the row-stochastic diffusion operator

P_{ij}=\frac{W_{ij}}{D_{i}}.

When needed for spectral calculations, we also form the symmetric operator

H=D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}.

Unless otherwise stated, we use the raw-kernel construction, corresponding
to
\alpha=0
in the diffusion-maps normalization family.

The default bandwidth is the median heuristic

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{med}}=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{median}_{i<j}\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}.

When the snapshot contains more than
2000
points, this median is
estimated from a random subset of at most
2000
points. Depending on the
experiment, we either fix
\varepsilon
from an initialization snapshot or
use a single reference bandwidth across a sweep.

Empirical separation matrix.

All reported operator experiments are
K
-class experiments. Let

\hat{\mu}_{a}
denote the empirical mean of class
a
, and let

\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pool}}
denote the pooled within-class covariance.
We compute the empirical pairwise separation matrix

\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}=\frac{1}{4}(\hat{\mu}_{a}-\hat{\mu}_{b})^{\top}\big(\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pool}}+(\varepsilon+\tau)I\big)^{\dagger}(\hat{\mu}_{a}-\hat{\mu}_{b}),\qquad a\neq b,

where
\tau=10^{-8}
is a small numerical ridge and
†

is the numerically stable pseudoinverse used in the implementation.
We report the mean off-diagonal separation

\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{a\neq b}\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}.

Class-level transport observables.

To define class-level transport observables, we construct a coarse
transition matrix from
P
. For this aggregation only, we remove the
diagonal self-loop entries of
P
, renormalize each row, and then average
transition mass from class
a
to class
b
. This finite-sample
convention matches the population coarse-chain calculation, where the
single-sample self-loop contribution is ignored and same-class mass is
represented by transitions to other samples from the same class.

The resulting coarse matrix is denoted
\bar{P}^{(K)}
. Let
\pi
be its
stationary distribution. We report

\mathrm{leakage}=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\pi_{a}\bigl(1-\bar{P}^{(K)}_{aa}\bigr),

and

\mathrm{gap}_{\mathrm{coarse}}=1-\lambda_{2}\!\bigl(\bar{P}^{(K)}\bigr),

where
\lambda_{2}
is the second largest eigenvalue in real part.

Label-boundary energy.

For labels, the code uses the one-hot label map

g(i)=e_{y_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},

not the scalar digit value. It reports the total one-hot label-boundary
energy

E[\Gamma(g,g)]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Gamma(g_{a},g_{a})(i).

Using the definition of
\Gamma
, this becomes

E[\Gamma(g,g)]=\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j:\,y_{j}\neq y_{i}}P_{ij}.

This is the node-average one-hot label-boundary Dirichlet energy induced by
the diffusion operator.

Soft diffusion radius.

For local scale, we define

r(i)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}\|z_{j}-z_{i}\|^{2},

and report the root-mean-square soft diffusion radius

\bar{r}_{\mathrm{soft}}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r(i)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Synthetic bridge validation.

The synthetic bridge experiment uses
K=4
classes in ambient dimension

d=16
. Class means are placed in a simplex configuration, and samples
are drawn from a shared-covariance Gaussian model

\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I+\mathrm{spike}\cdot vv^{\top},

with
\sigma^{2}=1
and spike strength
0.5
. The separation parameter is
varied over

\{0.8,\,1.4,\,2.0,\,2.8,\,3.8\},

the bandwidth over

\{0.5,\,1,\,2,\,4\},

the number of samples per class is
250
, and the code runs three trials
per configuration. For each trial, we compare empirical

\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}
, leakage, and

\mathrm{gap}_{\mathrm{coarse}}
against the corresponding population
predictions.

Training-time dynamics and separation heatmaps.

For the reported learned-representation figures, we use a two-hidden-layer
ReLU MLP with hidden widths
(128,128)
, trained on ten-class MNIST for
15 epochs with Adam, learning rate
10^{-3}
, and batch size
256
.
Unless otherwise stated, we subsample
4000
training examples and use a
balanced evaluation subset of
600
examples for operator computations.

For the scalar training-dynamics figure, metrics are recorded at epoch
0

and after every training epoch for both hidden layers. Each hidden layer
uses a fixed bandwidth estimated from its initialization features,

\varepsilon_{\ell}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{med}}\bigl(\{z_{i}^{\ell}(0)\}\bigr),

so that the reported curves reflect representation change rather than
bandwidth drift. The recorded quantities are

\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}
, leakage,

\mathrm{gap}_{\mathrm{coarse}}
,
E[\Gamma(g,g)]
, and

\bar{r}_{\mathrm{soft}}
. The main training-dynamics panel reports

\bar{c}_{\varepsilon}
, leakage,
\mathrm{gap}_{\mathrm{coarse}}
, and

\bar{r}_{\mathrm{soft}}
.

For the heatmap figure, we use the deepest hidden layer only. The full
matrix

\{\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}^{(a,b)}\}_{a,b=0}^{9}

is recorded at epochs

\{0,1,5,15\}

using the initialization bandwidth for that layer.

Width sweep.

The width sweep fixes depth and varies only the hidden width. The reported
configuration uses widths

\{16,32,64,128,256,512,1024\},

15 training epochs, Adam with learning rate
10^{-3}
, and seeds

\{0,1,\dots,9\}.

For this experiment, each feature cloud is centered and divided by its RMS
feature norm before operator construction. A single reference bandwidth

\varepsilon^{\star}
is then computed from the normalized deepest hidden
layer of a baseline width-128 model after one epoch of training and used
for all widths. Reported curves show the mean across seeds together with
Student-
t

95\%
confidence intervals,

\mathrm{mean}\pm t_{0.975,\,9}\frac{\mathrm{std}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{seeds}}}},\qquad t_{0.975,\,9}\approx 2.262.

We also computed the soft diffusion radius for completeness; after scale
normalization it varies weakly with width and is omitted from the main
width figure to focus on class-separation and coarse-transport observables.

Stability experiment.

For the stability experiment, we train the same
(128,128)
MLP for
15 epochs and extract features from each hidden layer on the balanced
evaluation subset. For each layer, we fix the operator bandwidth using the
unperturbed snapshot and compute baseline operator and graph metrics.

The reported main-text figure uses additive Gaussian perturbations of the
feature cloud,

\widetilde{z}_{i}=z_{i}+\sigma\xi_{i},\qquad\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

with
\sigma=10^{-3}
. For each seed and each hidden layer, we generate
15 perturbations and recompute operator leakage,
E[\Gamma(g,g)]
, and

\bar{r}_{\mathrm{soft}}
as well as hard-graph leakage, graph-based label disagreement, and graph local
radius.

For the hard-graph baseline, hard-graph leakage is the average fraction of
mutual neighbors with a different label, and the hard-graph local radius is
the root mean squared edge length averaged over mutual neighbors. The
label-disagreement score serves as the hard-graph analogue of the operator
boundary energy, being a normalized graph quantity and isn’t scaled by

1/\varepsilon
, so the relative standard deviation is the
scale-comparable stability statistic. Nodes with zero mutual degree
contribute zero to all node-averaged hard-graph quantities.
