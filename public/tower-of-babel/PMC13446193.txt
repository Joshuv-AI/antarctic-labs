Reused skin grafts for treating hidradenitis suppurativa: A systematic review
Authors: Nouf Z. AlBattal, Anas A. Sayegh, Mohammed A. Althobaiti, Rawan Alanazi, Razan A. Alsuayri, Joud A. Alrashoud, Rnda F. Almahfouz, Hatun M. Alghuson, Alhassan H. Hobani, Abdelkhalig Hussein Elhilu, Ahmad Assiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disease in which wide excision remains the most reliable option for durable disease control in moderate to severe cases. Reconstruction after excision is challenging. Reused or recycled split-thickness skin grafts, harvested from excised HS tissue and reapplied after processing, have been proposed to reduce donor-site morbidity, but their clinical value remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the outcomes of reused skin grafts following surgical excision of HS, with emphasis on recurrence, graft take and procedure-related morbidity.MethodsA PRISMA-compliant systematic review was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science and Google Scholar from inception to the final search date. Studies reporting HS excision followed by reused or recycled skin grafting were included. Outcomes of interest were recurrence, graft survival, complications and follow-up duration. Given heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was performed. Risk of bias was assessed using design-appropriate tools.ResultsSixteen studies met inclusion criteria, including five systematic reviews, two retrospective cohorts and nine case reports or small case series. Primary reused-graft reports comprised approximately 41 patients with follow-up ranging from 6 to 113 months. Across these series, no surgical-site recurrences were reported (0/41), corresponding to an exact 95% confidence interval of 0–7.0%. Complications were generally minor. However, in the largest reused-graft series, which included 18 patients, 8 (44%) required supplemental conventional donor grafts. Broader pooled estimates from conventional graft literature reported recurrence rates ranging from 2% to 18%, reflecting substantial heterogeneityConclusionsReused skin grafting appears feasible and safe in selected HS patients, with encouraging short- to medium-term durability. Current evidence supports feasibility rather than definitive comparative effectiveness, underscoring the need for well-designed prospective studies.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), also termed acne inversa, is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis marked by recurrent deep nodules, abscesses, sinus tracts and fibrotic scarring. Lesions predominantly affect intertriginous, apocrine gland–bearing regions, including the axillae, groin, perianal and inframammary areas.1 HS arises from a multifactorial interplay of genetic, environmental and behavioral factors, with heritability estimated at 30–40% of cases.2,3 For the purpose of this review, a 'reused' or 'recycled' skin graft is defined as a split- or full-thickness skin graft harvested from the patient's own excised HS-affected tissue, which is then processed (e.g., de-fatted, meshed) and reapplied to cover the surgical defect created by the same excision, thereby eliminating a separate donor site.

Management aims to control active inflammation, reduce pain and drainage, limit recurrence and prevent disease progression. Treatment is severity-dependent and includes topical and systemic antibiotics, hormonal agents, immunomodulators and surgical interventions ranging from limited deroofing to wide excision.1 Non-surgical therapies rarely achieve durable remission; consequently, surgery remains a cornerstone for moderate to severe disease.4 The choice of surgical technique depends on disease extent, anatomic location and scarring pattern.5 Surgical options include local destructive modalities (cryosurgery, electrosurgery, photodynamic therapy), incision and drainage, deroofing with debridement of sinus tracts and wide excision with subsequent reconstruction or closure.6 Evidence favors wide excision for sustained disease control. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported lower recurrence following wide excision compared with limited procedures, particularly when defects were closed using flaps or grafts, with reported recurrence rates of 15% with primary closure, 8% with flaps and 6% with skin grafts.7 When primary closure is not feasible, reconstruction typically involves skin grafts or flaps. Split-thickness skin grafts (STSGs), commonly harvested from the thigh using a dermatome and meshed at ratios of 1:1.5 to 1:3, are frequently used for large defects. STSGs are attractive in HS because they lack hair follicles and sweat glands, structures implicated in disease pathogenesis. However, they carry notable limitations, including donor-site morbidity, graft contraction is problematic in axillary and groin regions delayed healing and immunologic or inflammatory complications.8-10

Despite advances, management of chronic and extensive HS remains challenging. While wide excision minimizes recurrence, morbidity associated with traditional grafting has driven the development of alternative reconstructive strategies. One such approach is the use of “reused” or “recycled” skin grafts, harvested from the patient’s excised HS tissue and reapplied after appropriate processing. This technique eliminates the need for a new donor site, reduces surgical invasiveness and may shorten hospitalization. Reused skin grafts have been proposed particularly for chronic gluteal HS with mild to moderate epidermal involvement and represent a tailored reconstructive option for this patient population.9,10 While preliminary reports on reused skin grafts are promising, a comprehensive synthesis of their outcomes, including recurrence rates, graft take and complications, is lacking. This systematic review aims to fill that gap by evaluating all available evidence on reused skin grafts in HS surgery, contextualizing its findings within the broader literature on surgical reconstruction.

Methodology

Study design and reporting framework

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The objective was to critically synthesize available evidence on the use of reused or recycled skin grafts following surgical excision of hidradenitis suppurativa, with primary focus on recurrence, graft take and procedure-related morbidity. The review protocol was developed a priori, with a formal registration in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42023475559)

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was undertaken across multiple electronic databases to maximize sensitivity. The primary search was conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science and Google Scholar from database inception to the final search date. PubMed was prioritized given its coverage of dermatologic and surgical literature. The initial search identified 11068 records. After removing 1966 duplicates, 9120 unique records remained for screening. A secondary targeted search was subsequently performed, yielding an additional 52 records from Google Scholar. Other databases contributed a negligible number of additional citations and were not pursued further.

Search terms were constructed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to the disease, intervention and surgical context. Core terms included “hidradenitis suppurativa,” “acne inversa,” “wide excision,” “skin graft,” “split-thickness skin graft,” “reused skin graft,” “recycled skin graft,” and “reconstruction.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to combine concepts and reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. No language restrictions were applied during the initial search. Non-English articles were included when sufficient outcome data could be extracted.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

After removal of 1948 duplicate records, 9120 unique articles underwent title and abstract screening. Screening was performed against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria aligned with the research question.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria:1.Included patients diagnosed with hidradenitis suppurativa.2.Reported surgical excision followed by reconstruction using reused or recycled skin grafts, defined as grafts harvested from the patient’s excised HS tissue and reapplied after processing.3.Reported at least one relevant clinical outcome, including recurrence, graft take, wound healing, complications or follow-up duration.4.Study designs included randomized or nonrandomized comparative studies, cohort studies, case series, case reports and systematic reviews addressing surgical reconstruction in HS.

Following title and abstract screening, 9069 records were excluded as irrelevant. Fifty-one full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 14 studies were excluded because they did not involve reused skin grafts and one study was excluded due to absence of relevant outcome reporting. Ultimately, 16 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis. Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using a standardized template developed for this review. Extracted variables included study design, year of publication, sample size, patient demographics, anatomical site of disease, Hurley stage where reported, surgical technique, graft characteristics, use of adjunctive therapies such as negative-pressure wound therapy, duration of follow-up, recurrence rates, complications and key functional or patient-reported outcomes. When outcomes were inconsistently reported, original study definitions were retained to avoid misclassification.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, patient populations, anatomical sites, follow-up durations and outcome definitions, quantitative meta-analysis of reused-graft outcomes was not performed. A structured narrative synthesis was undertaken instead. Where possible, raw recurrence proportions were summarized and contextualized using exact binomial confidence intervals to quantify uncertainty. Findings from reused-graft studies were interpreted alongside pooled estimates from broader systematic reviews of HS surgical reconstruction to provide comparative context.

Methodological heterogeneity, inconsistency in outcome reporting and small sample sizes were explicitly considered in the synthesis and conclusions were framed to reflect feasibility and signal strength rather than definitive comparative effectiveness.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using design-specific, validated tools where appropriate. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were appraised using AMSTAR 2, with attention to protocol registration, search strategy, duplicate screening, assessment of risk of bias in primary studies, handling of heterogeneity, publication bias and conflict of interest reporting. Formal risk-of-bias assessment was not applied to individual case reports, as these are inherently descriptive and hypothesis-generating and do not aim to establish causal inference. Case series were appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist to assess reporting quality, follow-up adequacy and outcome ascertainment. For studies undergoing appraisal, domain-level judgments were recorded and summarized into an overall assessment with justification. Given the descriptive nature of case reports and the inherent limitations of the assessment tools when applied to very small case series, the overall assessments of "Low" or "Moderate" risk of bias for these study types should be interpreted with caution. The evidence base for reused skin grafting in hidradenitis suppurativa is largely composed of small case series and descriptive case reports. While these studies consistently report clinically relevant outcomes such as graft take and recurrence, their design precludes causal inference and limits generalizability. For this reason, individual case reports were not subjected to formal risk-of-bias grading. Systematic reviews provide pooled estimates but predominantly synthesize heterogeneous observational data, resulting in reasonable internal consistency but limited certainty. Comparative cohort studies, particularly within-patient designs, offer stronger methodological signals but remain nonrandomized and subject to residual confounding. Overall, the current evidence supports feasibility and safety rather than definitive superiority, underscoring the need for prospective, multicenter comparative studies.

Results

Of the 16 reviewed studies, 9 were single/limited case reports or series17,19–25 5 were systematic reviews/meta-analyses7,11–13, 15 and 2 were retrospective cohorts16,18 . The median follow-up period is not consistently calculable, with reported means up to 61.3 months.18 Collectively, data demonstrate that skin graft reconstruction, particularly using recycled grafts from excised tissue, is associated with successful healing and low recurrence rates in reported cases. Across the body of evidence, the reused or recycled split-thickness skin graft (STSG) technique yields consistently favorable raw outcomes in small series but sits within a broader and heterogeneous literature that tempers confidence. Summing primary reused-graft reports gives approximately 41 patients (Kuo17 n = 6; Maeda18 n = 18; Yamada21 n = 3; Hamaguchi16 recycled-graft episodes n = 9; plus isolated case reports19-23), with follow-up intervals ranging from 6 months to 113 months. Maeda18 reports a mean follow-up of 61.3 months (range 17–113). Kuo17 reports follow-up 8–36 months. Across these reused-graft cases, observed surgical-site recurrence was 0/41. The exact 95 percent Clopper-Pearson confidence interval around zero events in 41 patients is 0 to 7.0 percent, which quantifies uncertainty and makes clear that a modest recurrence rate cannot be excluded by available case-series data.

Complications after reused grafting were generally minor and reported as isolated hematoma, small focal necrosis, transient discharge and one wound infection in small series. A key finding from the largest reused-graft series18 is that additional conventional donor grafts were required in 8 of 18 patients, meaning 44 percent of cases ultimately needed supplemental donor tissue despite the “reused” approach. Afsharfard25 ’s within-patient comparative cohort (n = 30) reports mean age 35.2 ± 9.3 years and disease duration 6.5 ± 2.1 years, with no recurrences at 1 year on either STSG or flap sides and restoration of range of motion in 80 percent at 1 month rising to 100 percent by 6 months.

When placed against pooled estimates from larger systematic reviews, the small reused-graft series present a signal rather than definitive proof. Mehdizadeh7 pooled 22 surgical studies and estimated recurrence after wide excision overall at 13.0 percent (95% CI 5.0–22.0). In the wide excision subgroup, graft closure pooled recurrence was 6 percent (95% CI 0–24). Ovadja11 pooled 79 studies and estimated recurrence for skin grafts at 2 percent (95% CI 0–5), with a statistically significant overall difference across reconstruction groups (p < 0.001). Riddle12 reported pooled recurrence for wide/radical excision at 8 percent (95% CI 2–16). A recent network meta-analysis of 121 papers (Cucu/Paradisi13) estimated skin-graft recurrence at 18 percent (95% CI 14–22) and found flaps to have lower network-estimated recurrence than grafts. The multicenter retrospective cohort (Hamaguchi16) reported overall recurrence 31.8 percent with distribution by closure type: primary closure 48 percent, secondary intention 16 percent, STSG 29 percent and fasciocutaneous flap 10 percent.

These divergent pooled estimates expose large between-study heterogeneity and methodological variability. Confidence intervals for closure-specific estimates are wide; for example, primary-closure CIs as broad as 0 to 72 percent in some syntheses, signaling unstable pooled estimates. Important effect modifiers are inconsistently recorded. Anatomical site influences recurrence; Ovadja11 found below-umbilicus disease associated with higher recurrence (p = 0.006). Hurley stage distributions vary across series. Follow-up durations differ widely, and longer follow-up increases the likelihood of detecting recurrence, which biases comparisons when follow-up is uneven. Many larger syntheses pool observational studies of varied quality and suffer from incomplete reporting, selective publication and nonstandardized outcome definitions such as “recurrence” versus “new lesions elsewhere.”

Reused STSG demonstrates promising short- and medium-term surgical-site durability in small series with low apparent complication severity. However, pooled meta-analytic estimates for graft closure vary from approximately 2 percent to 18 percent depending on methods and higher-quality comparative studies with standardized recurrence definitions and adequately long follow-up are required before the reused-graft technique can be recommended as equivalent or superior to conventional grafts or flaps. Discussion

Despite advances in medical and surgical management, hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) remains difficult to control because of its relapsing course and the limited durability of non-surgical therapies.5 Surgical excision is therefore central to management of moderate-to-severe disease, yet optimal reconstruction remains debated. This systematic review synthesizes available evidence on reused or recycled skin grafts following HS excision, with emphasis on recurrence, complications and comparative context within established reconstructive strategies.

A key challenge in this synthesis is the inclusion of both primary clinical studies and systematic reviews. The systematic reviews provide broad, pooled estimates of recurrence for various closure methods, which are essential for context. However, their inclusion alongside small primary studies of the reused graft technique does not create a well-defined hierarchy of evidence. The primary reused-graft data are best understood as hypothesis-generating signals, while the pooled estimates from reviews define the benchmark against which these signals should be compared. We have attempted to clarify this distinction throughout the narrative. The main limitation remains the very weak nature of the direct evidence on reused skin grafting, which is based mainly on small case series and case reports. Therefore, the findings from these direct reports should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than demonstrative of clinical effectiveness.

Across primary reports specifically addressing reused skin grafts, outcomes were consistently favorable. Summation of individual reused-graft series yields approximately 41 patients with reported follow-up ranging from 6 to 113 months17–23 . Notably, no surgical-site recurrences were reported in these series. However, the absence of recurrence must be interpreted cautiously. The exact 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence interval for zero events in 41 patients ranges from 0 to 7.0%, indicating that clinically meaningful recurrence cannot be excluded given the small sample size. These findings therefore represent a promising signal rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

Longer follow-up strengthens confidence in durability. Maeda et al. reported a mean follow-up of 61.3 months with no recurrence, suggesting sustained benefit in selected patients.18 Kuo and Ohara similarly reported no recurrence at 8–36 months in a small gluteal–perianal cohort.17 However, follow-up across studies was highly variable, ranging from 1 month to 8 years, limiting comparability and likely biasing pooled impressions toward favorable outcomes in short-term reports. Late recurrence may therefore be underrepresented.

Adjunctive therapies appear to modify outcomes. Case reports combining reused skin grafts with negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) demonstrated rapid epithelialization and complete graft take without recurrence.19,23 Similarly, fistulotomy and staged approaches were associated with fewer wound complications in isolated reports.20 While these findings suggest synergy between reused grafting and supportive surgical strategies, they remain hypothesis-generating due to the absence of controlled comparisons.

Complications following reused grafting were generally minor and infrequent, including small hematomas, focal necrosis, transient discharge and isolated infection.17,18 Importantly, Maeda et al. reported that 8 of 18 patients (44%) ultimately required supplemental conventional donor grafts, indicating that reused grafting alone may be insufficient for complete defect coverage in a substantial proportion of cases.18 This nuance is critical, as it tempers claims of reduced donor-site morbidity and suggests that reused grafts function best as part of a flexible reconstructive algorithm rather than a standalone solution.

When contextualized within the broader surgical literature, reused graft outcomes must be interpreted against heterogeneous pooled estimates. Large systematic reviews and meta-analyses report recurrence rates after wide excision with graft closure ranging from approximately 2% to 18%, depending on methodology and inclusion criteria.7,11–13 Mehdizadeh et al. estimated a pooled recurrence of 6% for graft closure after wide excision, albeit with wide confidence intervals and high heterogeneity.7 Ovadja et al. reported a lower pooled estimate of 2% for skin grafts, while a recent network meta-analysis estimated recurrence at 18% and favored flaps over grafts.11,13 These discrepancies reflect substantial methodological variability, inconsistent outcome definitions and uneven follow-up durations across studies. This non-uniformity, particularly regarding what constitutes a "recurrence," further limits comparability across studies and contributes to the wide range of reported estimates.

Comparative cohort data suggest that reconstruction type influences functional and recurrence outcomes but does not clearly establish graft superiority. In a large retrospective cohort, Hamaguchi et al. reported recurrence rates varying by closure method, with fasciocutaneous flaps demonstrating lower recurrence than STSG.16 Wormald et al. similarly found faster recovery and improved quality-of-life outcomes with flaps compared with STSG in axillary disease, though recurrence differences were not definitive.24 These findings underscore that anatomical site, disease severity and functional demands may be as influential as graft type itself.

Risk-of-bias assessment highlights significant limitations across the reused-graft evidence base. Most reused graft studies were case reports or small case series which are, by design, hypothesis-generating and prone to selection and reporting biases. They lacked control groups and standardized outcome definitions. Larger systematic reviews largely synthesize heterogeneous observational data of low certainty. Even comparative cohorts remain nonrandomized and vulnerable to confounding. Consequently, current evidence supports feasibility and short- to medium-term effectiveness rather than definitive comparative efficacy. The favorable outcomes reported, particularly the absence of recurrence in small case series, must therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Strengths of this review include comprehensive synthesis of a novel surgical concept, integration of reused graft data within the wider reconstructive literature and explicit quantification of uncertainty. Collectively, findings suggest that reused split-thickness skin grafts are a viable reconstructive option for selected HS patients, particularly in chronic gluteal or perianal disease, where donor-site preservation is advantageous.

However, reused grafting cannot yet be considered equivalent or superior to conventional grafts or flap reconstruction. Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter comparative studies with standardized definitions of recurrence, adequate follow-up durations and stratification by anatomical site and Hurley stage. Clarifying the role of adjunctive therapies such as NPWT and defining patient subsets most likely to benefit will be essential to refining clinical indications.

This assessment indicates that the evidence for reused grafts originates primarily from studies with designs prone to selection and reporting biases. Consequently, the favorable outcomes reported, particularly the absence of recurrence, should be interpreted as preliminary signals of feasibility rather than robust evidence of superior effectiveness.

Conclusion

Reused split-thickness skin grafting represents a pragmatic reconstructive option following wide excision of hidradenitis suppurativa in chronic gluteal and perianal disease where donor-site preservation is desirable. Available evidence suggests high graft take, low complication severity and an absence of reported surgical-site recurrence in small series with follow-up extending beyond five years in some cohorts. Confidence in these findings is constrained by small sample sizes, noncomparative designs, variable follow-up and inconsistent outcome definitions. The frequent need for supplemental conventional grafts further indicates that reused grafting is not universally sufficient as a standalone technique. When viewed alongside heterogeneous pooled recurrence estimates for conventional grafts and flaps, reused skin grafts should be considered complementary rather than superior. Robust prospective comparative studies with standardized definitions of recurrence and adequate long-term follow-up are required before firm recommendations can be made regarding their routine use.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that must be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. First, the included primary studies on reused grafts consisted solely of case series and reports; no randomized controlled trials or comparative studies were identified. While this reflects the novel and specialized nature of the technique, it limits the ability to draw rigorous comparative conclusions about its efficacy versus conventional reconstruction methods. Second, there was considerable heterogeneity and variability in follow-up duration across studies, ranging from 1 month to 8 years. This inconsistency complicates the assessment of long-term durability and recurrence; as shorter follow-up may underestimate true recurrence rates. Third, the definition of clinical success particularly "recurrence" was not standardized across studies. Some reported surgical-site recurrence specifically, while others may have reported disease recurrence more broadly. The lack of objective, consistent criteria for healing and recurrence complicates the synthesis and comparison of outcomes. Finally, the risk of bias in the available evidence is high, as most studies are small, single-center reports without control groups, which inherently affects the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn.
