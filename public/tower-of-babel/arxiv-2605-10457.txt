Geometrically Approximated Modeling for Emitter-Centric Ray-Triangle Filtering in Arbitrarily Dynamic LiDAR Simulation CCS: Computing methodologies Ray tracing CCS: Computing methodologies Rendering
Rabin Gajmer, Joonas Haapala, Zoltan Beck

Abstract
. Real-time Light Detection And Ranging (LiDAR) simulation must find, per emitted ray, the closest intersecting triangle even in dynamic scenes containing large numbers of moving and deformable objects. Dominant acceleration-structure approaches require rebuilding each frame for dynamic geometry—a cost that compounds directly with scene dynamics and cannot be amortized regardless of how little actually changed. This paper presents the Gajmer Ray-Casting Algorithm (GRCA), which inverts the question: instead of asking what does each ray hit? it asks which rays can each triangle possibly hit? GRCA geometrically models spinning LiDAR emitters as rotation-traced cones or planes and uses each triangle’s emitter-centric apparent area to cull, per triangle, which channels and the rays within those channels can possibly reach it—without any acceleration structure. GRCA is compute-based and vendor-agnostic by design, targeting highly dynamic, high-resolution simultaneous multi-sensor simulation. At its core, GRCA is a general-purpose ray-casting algorithm: the emitter-centric inversion applies to any setting where rays originate from a known position, not only LiDAR. Benchmarks evaluate 2–8 simultaneous 128 × 4096 128\times 4096 -ray LiDARs ( 360 ∘ 360^{\circ} / 180 ∘ 180^{\circ} ) over complex dynamic scenes—with just two sensors casting ≈ 1 {\approx}1 M rays per frame. With range culling inactive, GRCA reaches up to 7.97 × \times over hardware-accelerated OptiX (GPU) and 14.55 × \times over Embree (CPU). Two independent extensions further boost performance even in the most complex scene ( ∼ {\sim} 22M triangles, ∼ {\sim} 9M of which are dynamic, 8 LiDARs): range culling at realistic deployment ranges (10–100 m) reaches up to 7.02 × \times GPU and 9.33 × \times CPU; a hybrid pipeline—GRCA for dynamic geometry, OptiX/Embree for static—reaches up to 10.5 × \times GPU and 19.2 × \times CPU.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Problem Statement

A Light Detection And Ranging (LiDAR) device fires a large number of rays into a scene composed of triangles;
for each ray the simulation must find the closest triangle it intersects and
its distance. This must be done every frame at real-time rates.

The brute-force cost per frame, in terms of Ray-Triangle Intersection Checks (RTIC), is:

(1)

\text{Total RTIC}=\sum_{n=1}^{\Omega}\gamma_{n}\times\chi_{n}\times\tau

where
\Omega
is the ray origin count (independent LiDAR scan instances),

\gamma_{n}
the total vertical channels for origin
n
,
\chi_{n}
the horizontal rays per
channel for origin
n
, and
\tau
the triangle count in the scene.

For a representative scene of
\tau=10\,\text{M}
triangles with
\Omega=1
,
\gamma_{n}=128
,
\chi_{n}=4096
:

(2)

1\times 128\times 4096\times 10^{7}\approx 5.2\times 10^{12}\text{ tests per frame}

\gamma_{n}
,
\chi_{n}
, and
\tau
can all be cut dramatically by identifying which ray–triangle
pairs are actually worth testing; at brute-force scale, per-ray atomic contention
alone becomes a significant bottleneck.

1.2.
State of the Art in LiDAR Simulation

Ray-tracing-based solutions are the state-of-the-art approach for LiDAR simulation.
A Bounding Volume Hierarchy (BVH)
(
Wald 2007
;
Karras 2012
)
is a tree
of nested axis-aligned boxes that encloses the scene geometry; each ray
descends the tree, skipping entire subtrees whose bounding boxes it misses,
and tests only the triangles in the leaves it reaches.
OptiX
(
Parker et al. 2010
)
and Embree
(
Wald et al. 2014
)
are the leading
software libraries built on this structure. Modern GPUs are fitted with dedicated Ray Tracing (RT) cores
that exclusively handle BVH traversal and RTIC in hardware.
Isaac Sim
(
NVIDIA 2023
)
exposes RTX LiDAR similarly backed by RT cores;
AWSIM
(
TIER IV 2023
)
(RobotecGPULidar
(
Robotec.AI 2023
)
) also drives LiDAR ray casting through OptiX directly.
For LiDAR, BVH-based methods impose three structural shortcomings.

BVH Shortcoming 1 (S1): Dynamic scenes.

LiDAR simulations inherently involve fast-moving objects—robots, vehicles, and
human models that move, rotate, and deform every frame.
BVH-based methods require a full per-frame rebuild for such scenes; refitting
is only valid for small deformations
(
Wald et al. 2007
;
Kopta et al. 2012
)
, so a full
rebuild is the only correct option (more details in Section
4.1.2
)—at cost proportional
to total triangle count regardless of how little actually moved
(
Karras 2012
)
.

BVH Shortcoming 2 (S2): 360° LiDARs.

Efficient BVH traversal relies on ray coherence: adjacent rays taking similar paths down the hierarchy amortise node-test cost
(
Aila and Laine 2009
)
.
A 360° spinning LiDAR emits rays in every direction simultaneously, eliminating directional culling and by definition leading to incoherent traversal.
No BVH implementation or hardware acceleration can recover coherence that the sensor geometry does not provide.

BVH Shortcoming 3 (S3): No upfront triangle filtering.

In standard BVH APIs, all culling—including node pruning—is conditioned
on an active ray; no triangle is eliminated before traversal. LiDAR range and
orientation limit the hittable set, yet out-of-range or back-facing triangles
that share a bounding volume with contributing geometry are still tested per ray.
In dynamic scenes this is compounded by BVH quality degradation: as objects
move, bounding volumes grow loose and enclose increasing amounts of
non-contributing geometry, which is why refit-based strategies catastrophically
degrade under deformation (Section
4.1.2
).

1.3.
Related Work

Beyond OptiX
(

)

)

For fully dynamic scenes, refit strategies
(

Kopta et al. 2012
)

partially amortise rebuild cost but cannot avoid
O(\tau)

Rasterization-based approaches.

Denis et al.
(
Denis et al. 2023
)
back-project depth
images to generate LiDAR point clouds via GPU rasterization. This approach is impractical for the multi-origin spinning LiDAR setting:
(1) full 360°/180° coverage requires
\geq
8 depth cameras per origin, scaling linearly with ray origin count with no sharing;
(2) projection distortion near frame edges corrupts the back-projected point cloud;
(3) the depth-camera pipeline contends with the simulator’s rendering pipeline and onboard image sensors, and aggregate GPU dispatch cost is prohibitive at real-time rates.
GRCA uses GPU compute with no rasterization stage and scales to arbitrary
ray origin counts with no per-origin camera overhead.

Physics-engine-based LiDAR simulation.

Simulators such as CARLA
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2017
)
, LGSVL
(

)
,
AirSim
(

)
, and Gazebo
(

)
implement LiDAR
by issuing rays through general-purpose collision backends (ODE, Bullet, DART, PhysX).
These backends expose a single-ray query interface: ray grouping is left to the caller,
GPU parallelism is absent, and each query carries per-call overhead that compounds
linearly with ray count—making
{\sim}4
M rays per frame infeasible at real-time rates.
GRCA bypasses the physics engine entirely and operates directly on the mesh.

Offline LiDAR simulation.

A complementary body of work targets data generation for machine-learning training.
LiDARsim
(
Manivasagam et al. 2020
)
re-simulates scans from real-world

(

(

)
add a learned residual for physical realism.
Tóth et al.
(
Tóth et al. 2025

take a hybrid approach, combining neural

All assume a pre-built static scene representation: adapting them to arbitrary

pipeline’s core advantage and adding cost that dwarfs real-time ray casting.

Neural LiDAR simulation.

Neural approaches learn scene representations to synthesise novel LiDAR viewpoints.
UniSim
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
enables photorealistic closed-loop replay but is bound
to pre-captured reconstructions and cannot generalise to unseen dynamic configurations.
Wu et al.
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
extend neural simulation to dynamic scenes via
per-object fields, but training and inference are both offline; novel object
arrangements require retraining.
All maximise photorealism at the cost of scene flexibility; none targets real-time
throughput over arbitrary dynamic geometry.

Directional ray classification.

Arvo and Kirk
(
Arvo and Kirk 1987
)
partition 5D ray space into angular cells and
pre-index each primitive into the cells it could intersect, so a traced ray
tests only its cell’s primitives.
The structure is ray-centric, built once for a static scene, and requires

O(\tau\cdot\nu)
re-indexing whenever geometry changes (
\nu
= angular cell count)—at least as costly as a BVH
rebuild—making the inversion incidental rather than primary for dynamic
scenes.
GRCA maintains no index; each triangle drives its own work at
O(1)
per
frame regardless of scene dynamics.

Source-driven light transport and apparent-area-based systems.

Backward ray tracing
(
Arvo 1986
)
, photon mapping
(
Jensen 1996
)
,
radiosity
(
Goral et al. 1984
)
, and many-lights methods
(
Walter et al. 2005
;
Keller 1997
)

share an emitter-centric perspective but still fire individual rays and rely on scene-side
structures; none uses the ray origin’s angular layout to predict per-triangle ray reachability.
GRCA discretises the ray origin into structured channels, enabling that prediction without
firing any ray upfront.

Schütz et al. 2026
partition geometry by apparent screen coverage for GPU dispatch;
GRCA applies the same idea at the ray origin, using apparent area to drive culling.

1.4.
Gajmer Ray-Casting Algorithm’s (GRCA) Approach

GRCA addresses
S1
–
S3
by asking

which rays can each triangle possibly hit?
—no ray is fired first, no acceleration structure is maintained.

It is built on three observations about spinning LiDAR geometry:

Observation 1: the ideal solution tests only the channels and rays that intersect each triangle.

For a given triangle
T
, the minimum necessary work is to predict the small
subset of vertical channels that could possibly contain a ray hitting
T
, and
within those only the subset of rays whose directions pass through
T
.
These form a rectangular index span
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]\times[R_{\mathrm{from}},R_{\mathrm{to}}]
in the
\gamma_{n}\times\chi_{n}
ray grid, giving channel span
C_{\mathrm{span}}
and ray span
R_{\mathrm{span}}
.
Running intersection tests solely on those pairs eliminates all guaranteed
misses without checking them.

Figure 2.
Per-triangle ray filtering: only the span
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]\times[R_{\mathrm{from}},R_{\mathrm{to}}]
is tested.
Diagram showing per-triangle ray filtering where only a subset of channels and rays are tested.

Observation 2: each vertical channel traces a conic surface.

As the LiDAR rotates, all rays in a channel collectively form a cone surface
around the spin axis—or a horizontal plane at zero elevation. This is
distinct from cone tracing
(
Amanatides 1984
)
and beam
tracing
(
Heckbert and Hanrahan 1984
)
, which widen individual rays into volumetric
primitives—GRCA keeps each ray a directed half-line and instead tests the Geometrically
Approximated Cone or Plane (GACP) surface against the triangle. Where this surface intersects
T
, the
candidate rays trace conic sections across the triangle face
(Figure
2
, red dots). Each channel therefore reduces to
a single GACP-Triangle (GACP-T) surface test that predicts the intersecting subset when partial overlap exists.

Observation 3: the apparent-area law partitions the scene into two
processing classes with different costs and contributions.

The
origin-centric apparent area
measures a triangle’s footprint as seen from the ray origin:

(3)

A_{\mathrm{origin}}\propto A_{T}\cos\phi_{\mathrm{view}}/\delta^{2}

where
A_{T}
is the triangle area,
\phi_{\mathrm{view}}
the angle between the triangle normal and the line of sight, and
\delta
the sensor-to-triangle distance.
In the
emitter-centric
view, this maps to the span from Observation 1, with dimensions:

(4)

\gamma_{\mathrm{span}}=C_{\mathrm{to}}-C_{\mathrm{from}}+1

(5)

\chi_{\mathrm{span}}=R_{\mathrm{to}}-R_{\mathrm{from}}+1

(6)

A_{\mathrm{emitter}}\approx\underbrace{\gamma_{\mathrm{span}}}_{\text{height}}\;\times\;\underbrace{\chi_{\mathrm{span}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{widest}}}}_{\text{width}}

where
\chi_{\mathrm{span}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{widest}}}
is the maximum
\chi_{\mathrm{span}}
across all channels in the triangle’s span.
Triangles whose
A_{\mathrm{emitter}}
fits within classification thresholds
\gamma_{T}
(channels)
\times\,\chi_{T}
(rays) are classified as

Smaller-Appearing Triangles
(SAT); all others are
Bigger-Appearing Triangles
(BAT).
Four implications follow:

•

SAT triangles vastly outnumber BAT in practice.

Footprint falls as
1/\delta^{2}
, so triangles at twice the distance subtend
one quarter the apparent area; mixed-distance geometry produces far more
SAT than BAT.

•

BAT triangles dominate the scan output.

Relatively large or close-up surfaces are the closest hit for most rays and occlude
SAT geometry behind them; precise per-channel prediction is most critical
for BAT, while conservative approximate prediction for SAT affects only a small
fraction of output rays.

•

SAT triangles below the angular sample spacing are unlikely to produce hits.

When
A_{\mathrm{origin}}
falls below the angular spacing between
adjacent sample directions, no ray passes close enough to register a
hit, further supporting the conservative approximate prediction of SAT triangles.

•

SAT/BAT classification is per ray-origin.

In multi-origin configurations, the same triangle can be SAT for one LiDAR origin and BAT for another depending on viewing angle and distance.

Observation 1 defines the ideal per-triangle span to test; Observation 2 shows it reduces to a single GACP-T
surface test per channel; Observation 3 shows that the vast SAT majority can be handled with a conservative approximate span prediction
while the few BAT triangles dominate output and require precise treatment.
Together they naturally yield a two-pass design addressing
S1
–
S3
: an
early pass
processes all triangles with a
conservative approximate span prediction, completing SAT intersections inline;
BAT triangles are deferred to a
late pass
that applies the full precise GACP-T intersection per channel;
per-triangle geometry precomputed by the early pass is carried in the deferred list and reused by the late pass,
avoiding redundant calculation.

The contributions of this paper are:

•

Paradigm inversion
: We propose a novel ray-casting algorithm: GRCA inverts the conventional
question—instead of
what does each ray hit?
, it asks

which rays can each triangle possibly hit?
—yielding a
per-triangle process that scales with scene geometry, not ray count.

•

Emitter-centric model using GACP
(Sections
2
–
3
):
a closed-form GACP surface approximates all rays in a spinning LiDAR channel,
producing per-triangle integer index spans

[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]\!\times\![R_{\mathrm{from}},R_{\mathrm{to}}]

without scene partitioning.
Apparent-area equations (Eqs.
3
–
6
) then classify
every triangle into SAT or BAT, driving a two-pass pipeline with GPU-side

O(\tau)
rejection and no CPU round-trip.
No prior system groups rays into a
single geometric surface for per-triangle intersection prediction while keeping
each ray an individual directed half-line, nor applies emitter-centric
apparent area as a per-triangle culling primitive (Section
1.3
).

•

GRCA implementations and benchmarks

(Sections
A.3
–
5
):
GRCA
GPU
(CUDA) outperforms OptiX by up to
7.97\times
;
GRCA
CPU
(AVX2/SSE4.1, single-threaded) outperforms Embree by up to
14.55\times
;
evaluated over 216 unique configurations across six scenes and five hardware platforms,
with sensitivity analyses, deployment recommendations, and correctness validation.

•

Hybrid static/dynamic pipeline (demonstration)

(Section
6
):
directing GRCA to dynamic geometry while a static BVH handles static geometry
achieves up to
10.5\times
GPU and
19.2\times
CPU speedup on the most complex scene
(
{\sim}
22M triangles,
{\sim}
9M of which are dynamic, 8 LiDARs),
remaining faster than the best incremental BVH update mode for standalone OptiX and Embree.

2.
Emitter-Centric Geometric Modeling

This section formalises the geometric model underlying GRCA.
Throughout,
bold
denotes vectors,
\hat{\cdot}
denotes unit vectors, and
italics
denote scalars and other variables.

2.1.
Ray Direction Formula

The coordinate system follows the left-hand rule:
\hat{f}
(forward),
\hat{r}
(right), and
\hat{u}
(up). Rays are indexed
horizontally by
R_{i}
, increasing clockwise as seen from above, and channels
vertically by
C_{i}
, with
C_{i}=0
at the bottom (most negative elevation) increasing
upward.

Each ray is defined by horizontal angle
\theta
(azimuth) and vertical angle

\phi
(elevation). Given the orientation, the ray direction is:

(7)

\hat{d}=\cos\theta\cos\phi\,\hat{f}+\sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat{r}+\sin\phi\,\hat{u}

Let
\Delta\theta
,
\Delta\phi
be the angular step sizes and
\theta_{0}
,
\phi_{0}
the start offsets,
centred so that
\theta_{0}=-\lfloor\chi_{n}/2\rfloor\cdot\Delta\theta
and
\phi_{0}=-\lfloor\gamma_{n}/2\rfloor\cdot\Delta\phi
.
Angles are then:

(8)

\theta=\theta_{0}+R_{i}\cdot\Delta\theta,\qquad\phi=\phi_{0}+C_{i}\cdot\Delta\phi

For a
360^{\circ}
/
180^{\circ}

sensor this gives
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
\phi\in[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2})
;
in that case the middle-most ray in the middle-most channel points along
\hat{f}
.

2.2.
GACP Definition

As established in
Observation 2
, every ray direction in a channel with
elevation
\phi
satisfies

(9)

\hat{d}\cdot\hat{u}=\sin\phi

GACP surface and angular indexing (channel: vertical; ray: horizontal).
Diagram of the GACP cone surface with vertical channel and horizontal ray angular indexing.

This places every direction on a cone around
\hat{u}
. When the azimuth spans the full
[-\pi,\pi)
range, the union of all rays from this channel is the following set,
where
\mathbf{o}
is the ray origin and
\mathbf{p}
a world-space point:

(10)

\bigcup_{\theta\in[-\pi,\,\pi)}\bigl\{\,\mathbf{o}+\kappa\,\hat{d}(\theta,\phi):\kappa\geq 0\,\bigr\}\;=\;\bigl\{\,\mathbf{p}:(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}=|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o}|\sin\phi\,\bigr\}

When
\phi=0
the cone degenerates
to the horizontal plane through
\mathbf{o}
. This surface is the GACP
(Geometrically Approximated Cone or Plane), illustrated in Figure
3
.
The cone half-angle
\alpha=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-|\phi|
is the angle from axis
\hat{u}
to the cone surface; equivalently,
\cos\alpha=\sin|\phi|
.

2.3.
Angular Channel Indexing

The nearest channel index for
\mathbf{p}
is:

(11)

C_{i}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\arcsin\!\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o}}\cdot\hat{u}\bigr)-\phi_{0}}{\Delta\phi}\right),\;0,\;\gamma_{n}-1\right)

2.4.
Angular Ray Indexing

The horizontal projection of
\mathbf{p}
(removing the vertical component) is:

(12)

\mathbf{q}=(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o})-\bigl((\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}\bigr)\hat{u}

The azimuth and its ray index are:

(13)

\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\!\bigl(\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{r},\;\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{f}\bigr),\qquad R_{i}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_{0}}{\Delta\theta}\right),\;0,\;\chi_{n}-1\right)

\operatorname{atan2}
is scale-invariant so normalizing
\mathbf{q}
is
unnecessary.

2.5.
GACP-T Intersection

Both passes share the GACP-T intersection logic.
The full version, used by the late pass, computes world-space intersection points for exact span computation; it is described in the following three subsections.
The boolean predicate, used by the early pass, returns only a yes/no answer and is described at the end of this section.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}
be the triangle vertices and
(i_{0},i_{1})
an edge index pair.

2.5.1.
Plane case (
\phi=0
)

When the elevation is zero, the GACP degenerates to the horizontal plane
through
\mathbf{o}
. Each vertex has signed axial height

h_{k}=(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}
. An edge crosses the plane when

h_{i_{0}}\cdot h_{i_{1}}<0
, and the intersection point is:

(14)

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}+\frac{h_{i_{0}}}{h_{i_{0}}-h_{i_{1}}}(\mathbf{v}_{i_{1}}-\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}})

No quadratic is needed; at most two edges cross.

2.5.2.
Per-edge quadratic (
\phi\neq 0
)

For each edge from
\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}
to
\mathbf{v}_{i_{1}}

(
Eberly 2025
)
, define edge vector
\mathbf{e}
and its axial (
\hat{u}
) component
e_{A}
:

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{i_{1}}-\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}},\quad e_{A}=\mathbf{v}_{i_{1}}\cdot\hat{u}-\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}\cdot\hat{u}

Let
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)=(\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})+\lambda\mathbf{e}
, where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is the edge parameter.
Substituting into the cone equation
(\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{u})^{2}=\sin^{2}\!\phi\;(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{p})
gives:

(16)

c_{2}\lambda^{2}+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}=0

(17)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=e_{A}^{2}-\sin^{2}\!\phi\;(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{e})

(18)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=2\bigl(e_{A}\,((\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u})-\sin^{2}\!\phi\;((\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\mathbf{e})\bigr)

(19)

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=((\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u})^{2}-\sin^{2}\!\phi\;\|\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o}\|^{2}

(20)

\lambda=\frac{-c_{1}\pm\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}-4c_{2}c_{0}}}{2c_{2}}

A root is valid when
\lambda\in[0,1]
and the point lies on the forward half of the
cone:
(\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}+\lambda e_{A}\geq 0
for
\phi>0
, or
(\mathbf{v}_{i_{0}}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}+\lambda e_{A}\leq 0
for
\phi<0

(rejecting the mirror cone behind the apex).

2.5.3.
Vertex classification and early exit

Let
\sin\phi_{k}=\widehat{\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o}}\cdot\hat{u}
be the normalized elevation of vertex
k
.
The following cases are checked in order to skip the quadratic:

(1)

Wrong half-space:
(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}<0
for all
k
(when
\phi>0
),
or
(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{u}>0
for all
k
(when
\phi<0
) —
the triangle lies entirely in the opposite half-space; no intersection possible.

(2)

All inside:
\sin\phi_{k}\geq\sin\phi
for all
k
(when
\phi>0
),
or
-\sin\phi_{k}\geq\sin|\phi|
for all
k
(when
\phi<0
) —
the cone surface cannot cross the triangle. No crossing.

(3)

Mixed: at least one vertex inside, at least one outside —
the cone surface must cross at least one edge; proceed to the per-edge quadratic to compute intersection points.

(4)

All outside:
\sin\phi_{k}<\sin\phi
for all
k
(when
\phi>0
),
or
-\sin\phi_{k}<\sin|\phi|
for all
k
(when
\phi<0
) —
two sub-cases:

•

Apex-ray check (axial height check):
cast the apex ray (
\hat{u}
for
\phi>0
,
-\hat{u}
for
\phi<0
) against the triangle face.
If it hits and
\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}\leq D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}\sin|\phi|

(
D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}\sin|\phi|
is the cone’s maximum axial reach within range;
\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}
pre-computed in Step 1 of Section
3
),
the cone surface surrounds the triangle; proceed to the per-edge quadratic for exact intersection points.

•

Per-edge quadratic: the remaining case; run the
quadratic above on each edge.

2.5.4.
Boolean predicate

The early pass uses the boolean predicate: no intersection points are computed.
All-inside short-circuits to false; mixed and a passing apex-ray check short-circuit to true — skipping the quadratic entirely.
Only the all-outside fallthrough reaches the per-edge quadratic, returning true on the first valid root.

3.
The Two-Pass Pipeline

The pipeline splits into two passes:

(1)

Early pass
: applies fast origin-centric filters, predicts

C_{\mathrm{span}}
and
R_{\mathrm{span}}
using inexpensive approximate checks,
classifies each triangle as SAT or BAT, and runs inline RTIC for SAT triangles.
BAT triangles are written to a deferred list.

(2)

Late pass
: applies the full precise GACP-T intersection per channel
to obtain exact
R_{\mathrm{span}}
endpoints, resolves CW/CCW arc ambiguity
for seam-straddling triangles, and runs RTIC across the span.

3.1.
Early Pass

The following steps execute in sequence per triangle; most are rejected or resolved inline.

3.1.1.
Step 1 — Origin-centric filters

Before any span prediction, each triangle is checked against three inexpensive
filters that use only simple geometry.

(1)

Back-face rejection:
let
\mathbf{c}
be the triangle centroid,
\mathbf{o}
the ray origin, and

\hat{n}
the outward unit normal. The triangle faces the ray origin if:

(21)

(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{n}>0

Otherwise skip.

(2)

Apparent area:
substituting
\cos\phi_{\mathrm{view}}=(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{n}\;/\;\|\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o}\|
into Eq.
3
:

(22)

A_{\mathrm{origin}}=\frac{A_{T}\,(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{n}}{\|\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o}\|^{3}}

Both sides are positive by back-face rejection; squaring gives the square-root-free skip condition:

(23)

\bigl(A_{T}\cdot(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{n}\bigr)^{2}<\varepsilon_{A}^{2}\;\|\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o}\|^{6}

with
\varepsilon_{A}=10^{-6}
(
1\,\text{mm}^{2}
apparent area threshold). Triangles this small rarely intercept any ray; accuracy and scene-size robustness are quantified in Sections
4.2.4
and
5
. Skip if so.

(3)

Range:
let
\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}

(
Ericson 2004
)
be the closest-point distance from
\mathbf{o}
to the triangle surface,
D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}
the near-field blind-zone limit, and
D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}
the maximum detection range. Skip if:

(24)

\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}<D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}\quad\text{or}\quad\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}>D^{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}

\delta^{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}
is stored for reuse in later steps.

3.1.2.
Step 2 — Channel-span prediction

Figure 4.
C_{\mathrm{span}}
prediction: two binary searches bracket
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
in
O(\log\gamma_{n})
.
Diagram showing binary search bracketing of $C_{\mathrm{span}}$ for a triangle.

This step finds
[C_{\mathrm{from}},\;C_{\mathrm{to}}]
(Figure
4
) via two binary searches, each iteration calling the boolean predicate from Section
2.5.4
.
Each probe is
O(1)
, giving a full bracket in
O(\log\gamma_{n})
rather than
O(\gamma_{n})
.
If no channel intersects, the triangle is discarded entirely.

The midpoint channel is computed from the three vertex elevation indices (using the channel indexing
formula from Section
2.3
):

(25)

\underset{k\,\in\,\{0,1,2\}}{C_{i}^{(k)}}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\arcsin\!\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o}}\cdot\hat{u}\bigr)-\phi_{0}}{\Delta\phi}\right),\;0,\;\gamma_{n}-1\right)

(26)

C_{\text{mid}}=\left\lceil\frac{\max_{k}C_{i}^{(k)}+\min_{k}C_{i}^{(k)}}{2}\right\rceil

3.1.3.
Step 3 — Fast ray-span prediction

Figure 5.
R_{\mathrm{span}}
prediction: vertex projection gives conservative span; CW vs. wrap-around cases shown.
Diagram showing fast $R_{\mathrm{span}}$ prediction via vertex projection with clockwise and wrap-around cases.

Three vertex projections give a conservative
R_{\mathrm{span}}
without any GACP-T intersection.
The span applies uniformly across all channels, so the triangle must not straddle
the
180^{\circ}
wrap-around seam.
For FOV
\leq 180^{\circ}
this always holds; a per-origin flag gates the seam check entirely.
For wider FOV, let
\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}}
be the center ray of the sensor
(center channel
\lfloor\gamma_{n}/2\rfloor
, center ray
\lfloor\chi_{n}/2\rfloor
).
A right vector is built from
\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}}
via Gram–Schmidt:

(27)

\hat{u}_{\perp}=\operatorname{normalize}\!\bigl(\hat{w}-(\hat{w}\cdot\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}})\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}}\bigr),\qquad\hat{r}_{\perp}=\operatorname{normalize}\!\bigl(\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}}\times\hat{u}_{\perp}\bigr)

where
\hat{w}=(0,1,0)
if
|\hat{d}_{\text{ctr},y}|<0.9
, else
(1,0,0)
.
Three dot-product tests check whether the triangle lies entirely on one side
of the ray origin:

(28)

all-front

\displaystyle=\forall k:\;(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{d}_{\text{ctr}}>0

(29)

all-right

\displaystyle=\forall k:\;(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{r}_{\perp}>0

(30)

all-left

\displaystyle=\forall k:\;(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{r}_{\perp}<0

(31)

\text{all-CW}=\text{all-front}\;\vee\;\text{all-right}\;\vee\;\text{all-left}

If none hold, the triangle straddles the seam and is classified as BAT.
Otherwise, project each vertex to a ray index using the ray indexing formula
from Section
2.4
with

\theta_{k}=\operatorname{atan2}((\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{r},\;(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{f})

(Figure
5
):

(32)

R_{i}^{(k)}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\theta_{k}-\theta_{0}}{\Delta\theta}\right),\;0,\;\chi_{n}-1\right),\quad k\in\{0,1,2\}

(33)

R_{\mathrm{from}}=\min\!\bigl(R_{i}^{(0)},R_{i}^{(1)},R_{i}^{(2)}\bigr),\qquad R_{\mathrm{to}}=\max\!\bigl(R_{i}^{(0)},R_{i}^{(1)},R_{i}^{(2)}\bigr)

Since vertex projections discard elevation, this span is a conservative upper bound
for every channel in
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
.

3.1.4.
Step 4 — SAT/BAT classification

Classification is applied independently per (triangle, ray origin) pair.
For a given origin
n
, the triangle is
SAT
if:

(34)

\text{all-CW}\;\wedge\;\gamma_{\mathrm{span}}\leq\gamma_{T}\;\wedge\;\chi_{\mathrm{span}}\leq\chi_{T}

Otherwise it is
BAT
for that origin. Triangles that failed the
all-CW predicate always become BAT; the late pass resolves their CW/CCW arc
correctly per channel.
A triangle enters the deferred list only if at least one origin classifies it
as BAT; origins that classify it as SAT have their RTIC handled inline in Step 5.

Choosing
(\gamma_{T},\chi_{T})
:

\gamma_{T}
caps the predicted channel span and
\chi_{T}
the predicted ray span.
Larger values reduce BAT deferrals but increase RTIC work per triangle and
amplify span over-prediction, since the early-pass bounds are conservative
upper estimates.
Smaller values push more work to the late pass, which computes exact spans but
carries deferred-list overhead.
The optimal
(\gamma_{T},\chi_{T})
pair was determined by a joint grid search
described in Section
4.2.1
.

3.1.5.
Step 5 — Inline SAT RTIC

For each SAT triangle, Möller–Trumbore (MT)
(
Möller and Trumbore 1997
)
RTIC runs over every (channel, ray)
pair in the predicted spans from Steps 2 and 3. For channel index

j\in[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
and ray offset
i\in[0,\chi_{\mathrm{span}})
,
the global ray index is:

(35)

g(j,i)=j\cdot\chi_{n}+\bigl(R_{\mathrm{from}}+i\bigr)\bmod\chi_{n}+O_{n}

where
n\in[0,\Omega)
is the current ray origin index and

O_{n}
is the ray-index offset for origin
n
.

The closest hit is recorded via the sortable encoding (Section
A.3.2
):

(36)

D_{g(j,i)}\leftarrow\min\!\bigl(D_{g(j,i)},\;f_{\mathrm{sort}}(D)\bigr)

3.2.
Late Pass

The early pass records SAT hits and produces a
BAT deferred list
consumed by the late pass; the late pass records BAT hits.
The origin-active flag in each deferred-list entry marks which origins are BAT for that triangle.
SAT and BAT output entries therefore occupy disjoint index ranges; neither pass reads the other’s writes.
Each deferred-list entry stores exactly the data the late pass needs,
pre-computed once by the early pass:

(1)

Triangle index: uniquely identifies the triangle; no geometry is duplicated in the deferred list.

(2)

Per-origin
C_{\mathrm{span}}
:
the
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
channel range for each ray origin.

(3)

Per-origin closest vertex distance: pre-computed in Step 1 and forwarded
for the apex-ray check in the late-pass GACP-T (Section
2.5
).

(4)

Per-origin flags (one bit each):

•

Origin-active: set for origins that classify the triangle as BAT;
unset for origins that processed it as SAT inline.

•

Apex-ray hit (positive and negative arc): forwarded for the apex-ray check (Section
2.5
).

•

All-CW: predicate result from Step 3 (Section
3.1.3
),
forwarded so the late pass can skip arc disambiguation for origins
that passed all-CW but exceeded the span thresholds.

These fields allow the late pass to proceed without re-accessing scene geometry.

3.2.1.
Step 1 — Precise GACP-T check per channel

For each channel in
[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
, the full GACP-T check
(Section
2.5
) runs, collecting the world-space intersection points
for exact
R_{\mathrm{span}}
computation in Step 2.
The number of intersection points determines the outcome:

•

Zero crossings: skip this channel.

•

One crossing: tangent touch; both span endpoints map to the same ray.

•

Two crossings: the two points give the exact span endpoints.

•

Full
R_{\mathrm{span}}
fallback: when the apex-ray check or the

>
2-crossings degenerate case applies (Section
2.5
),

R_{\mathrm{span}}
is set to
\chi_{n}
for that channel.

3.2.2.
Step 2 — CW/CCW arc disambiguation

Figure 6.
CW vs CCW arc: mid-ray plane test selects the correct arc (bold).
Diagram showing CW versus CCW arc disambiguation via mid-ray plane test.

This step resolves the CW/CCW arc ambiguity for seam-straddling triangles
(all-CW false, Figure
6
) via an arc-midpoint plane test:

(1)

CW
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
: the ray index at the midpoint of the CW arc,
where
\chi_{\mathrm{span}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}=R_{\mathrm{to}}-R_{\mathrm{from}}+1

is the CW arc ray count:

R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}=\bigl(R_{\mathrm{from}}+\lfloor\chi_{\mathrm{span}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}/2\rfloor\bigr)\bmod\chi_{n}

(2)

CCW
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
by reflection:
\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}]
is obtained by reflecting

\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}]
across the horizontal plane spanned by

\hat{u}
:

\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}]=2\,(\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}]\cdot\hat{u})\,\hat{u}-\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}]

This works because
R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}
and
R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}
are azimuthally opposite at
the same elevation, so one is the up-plane mirror of the other.

(3)

Plane-intersection test: for each of
\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}]
and

\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}]
, let
\hat{d}_{m}
denote the candidate and compute:

\rho=\frac{\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o})}{\hat{n}\cdot\hat{d}_{m}}

Define
\operatorname{hit}(\hat{d}_{m})=(\hat{n}\cdot\hat{d}_{m}\geq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{face}})\;\wedge\;(\rho\geq 0)
where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{face}}=10^{-4}
: the ray
faces the triangle from the front and the plane is not behind the origin.

(4)

Decision:

\text{arc-CW}=\begin{cases}\text{true}&\text{if }\operatorname{hit}(\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}])\text{ and }\neg\operatorname{hit}(\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}])\\
\text{false}&\text{if }\operatorname{hit}(\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CCW}}}])\text{ and }\neg\operatorname{hit}(\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}])\\
\lx@intercol\text{full M\"{o}ller--Trumbore on }\hat{d}[R_{\mathrm{mid}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CW}}}]\text{ (tiebreaker)}\hfil\lx@intercol\end{cases}

3.2.3.
Step 3 — RTIC

The intersection formula is identical to Step 5 of the early pass,
applied over the exact
C_{\mathrm{span}}
and
R_{\mathrm{span}}
from Steps 1 and 2.
Let
j\in[C_{\mathrm{from}},C_{\mathrm{to}}]
be the channel index,
i\in[0,\chi_{\mathrm{span}})
the ray offset,

\mathbf{e}_{1}=\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{0}
,
\mathbf{e}_{2}=\mathbf{v}_{2}-\mathbf{v}_{0}
,
and
g(j,i)=j\cdot\chi_{n}+(R_{\mathrm{from}}+i)\bmod\chi_{n}+O_{n}
:

(37)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\operatorname{MT}\!\bigl(\hat{d}_{g(j,i)},\,\mathbf{o},\,\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\bigr)

(38)

\displaystyle D_{g(j,i)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\min\!\bigl(D_{g(j,i)},\;f_{\mathrm{sort}}(D)\bigr)

3.3.
Implementation Summary

The GPU backend runs the two-pass pipeline as four CUDA kernels per frame, using warp ballot to reduce atomic pressure on the BAT list and warp shuffle to compute Late Pass dispatch dimensions (Appendix
A.3
).
The CPU backend uses AVX2 8-wide and SSE4.1 4-wide SIMD for the early filter and GACP-T intersection, with runtime packet-size selection; wrapping sweeps fall back to scalar; both GRCA
CPU
and Embree run single-threaded for algorithmic ablation (Appendix
A.4
).
While noise simulation is supported, this paper does not evaluate using it; its implementation and limitations are discussed in Appendix
A.2
and Section
7
.

3.3.1.
Tunable Parameters

Three parameters govern GRCA’s behaviour; their defaults are used throughout this paper.

(\gamma_{T},\chi_{T}){=}(64,64)
: adjust for imbalanced SAT/BAT splits (more in §
4.2.1
).
BAT deferred-list capacity
8{\times}10^{5}
entries: increase if the list overflows for unusually large scenes (see Appendix
A.3
).

\varepsilon_{A}{=}10^{-6}
: decrease for sub-millimetre mesh density (more in §
3.1
).

Final Analysis

The following sections analyse all backends across key structural variables; all use 500 frames unless stated otherwise.

4.4.1.
Cost Contributors

Frame cost has five structurally distinct contributors:

•

Static scene.

BVH backends rebuild the full static geometry every frame; cost scales
with triangle count
\tau
and physical scene size.
GRCA’s early pass filters all triangles in one flat
O(\tau)
pass,
incurring the same cost regardless of how much moved, and only once
regardless of ray origin count.

•

Static scene bounding box.

BVH traversal cost grows with scene extent: rays to distant geometry
descend more tree levels.
For GRCA, a larger scene places more triangles within sensor range,
increasing the BAT list and late-pass workload.

•

Average triangle area.

BAT list size scales with per-triangle apparent solid angle
(
Observation 3
): large-triangle scenes
(Rungholt, Vokselia Spawn,
{\approx}0.50\,\text{m}^{2}
/tri) promote more
triangles to BAT, growing the late-pass workload.

•

Dynamic mesh count.

Each dynamic object forces a full BVH rebuild per frame.
GRCA re-filters the dynamic subset in the same early pass at no
additional structural cost.

•

Ray origin count.

Both architectures scale linearly with
L\times C\times R
;
GRCA’s per-ray cost is bounded by the BAT list, capped by the apparent-area filter.

Hit-distance correctness is measured against OptiX (no-deform) at

5.1.
Identified Causes

The following are the identified possible causes of below-threshold miss rates.

Cause 1: Apparent-area discard (
\varepsilon_{A}
).

Triangles whose apparent area falls below
\varepsilon_{A}
are discarded before any RTIC is attempted; OptiX tests all triangles unconditionally, so these appear as misses even though they subtend sub-sensor-resolution area.

Cause 2:
C_{\mathrm{span}}
binary search initialization.

For obliquely oriented triangles, vertex projections can underestimate
C_{\text{mid}}
, causing the binary search to run in a region with no GACP-T crossings and miss valid intersections.

Cause 3: GACP-T early-exit misclassification (both passes).

When all three vertex projections exceed the cone threshold (
\sin\phi_{k}\geq\sin\phi
), the predicate exits early—correct in exact arithmetic since the cone interior is convex—but near-grazing rounding can push a tangent triangle’s vertices just over threshold, producing a false negative in both the early-pass channel search and the late-pass crossing test—both can compound.

Cause 4: CW/CCW
R_{\mathrm{span}}
misprediction.

For BAT triangles spanning the scan seam, the late pass selects CW or CCW via a plane test on arc midpoints; the CCW midpoint is a reflection of the CW midpoint (not the actual buffer ray), which can be wrong for asymmetric scan patterns, and when ambiguous a Möller–Trumbore fallback on the CW midpoint decides—a near-tangent miss routes to the wrong arc.

)—which itself degrades to 301 ms under deformation while the hybrid holds at
{\approx}19

A fully GPU-driven merge pass for the GRCA
GPU
+OptiX hybrid would eliminate the CPU-side hit-merge cost, further reducing wall-clock frame time.

7.
Limitations

The
C\times R
grid does not directly support arbitrary scan patterns; non-spinning solid-state sensors (e.g. flash, MEMS, OPA) have bounded FOV, making the following paths practical.

Bitmap-based pattern generation.
Any scan pattern expressible as a 2D bitmap can be imposed by masking the
C\times R
output grid; the per-ray noise model can further randomise hit locations per active cell. Its limitation: hit-point candidates are drawn from the GACP cone geometry, so the noise model must compensate for the conic-section locus. Origin-offset and noise-based approaches to pattern diversity and gap filling are discussed in Section
9.2.1
.

GAP-only channels.
Each channel can be modelled as a
Geometrically Approximated Plane
(GAP, the
\phi{=}0
degenerate case of GACP), giving an exact hit-point locus with no conic-arc error. Sensors with FOV below 180° map each channel to a single GAP; full 360° coverage decomposes into two 180° passes.

Angular noise constraints.
Distance and azimuth noise are supported provided no perturbed ray crosses an adjacent ray boundary; per-channel elevation noise is supported provided no channel offset crosses an adjacent channel boundary (Appendix
A.2
). Per-ray vertical noise—distinct perturbations per ray within a channel—is not supported: GACP-T evaluates at a single nominal elevation per channel, so independent per-ray elevation offsets cannot be reflected in the filtering predicate.

Static-only scenes.
GRCA provides no advantage over BVH for purely static geometry, where BVH rebuild cost is absent; Section
4.2.3
characterises this case.

8.
Conclusion

BVH construction dominates frame time in dynamic LiDAR simulation and offers no mechanism for directional or apparent-area culling.
GRCA eliminates it entirely: each triangle predicts which rays can reach it, outperforming OptiX and Embree in dynamic practical scenes without a BVH.

9.2.
General Purpose Ray Caster

Any ray origin—point light, spot light, area light, or directional sensor—defines
a bounded ray set from a known position, exactly the structure GRCA exploits.

Beyond the per-ray noise model described in Appendix

) offers a simpler route

Point, Spot and Area Light Shadow Rays

Standard ray tracing casts shadow rays
from each shading point toward each light

(

)

(

9.2.3.
Other Sensors

At its core, GRCA is a general-purpose, emitter-centric ray casting algorithm:
given any ray origin whose rays span a bounded angular region, it
predicts triangle visibility without per-frame structure updates.
LiDAR simulation is the motivating application, but the same pipeline applies
wherever rays originate from a known position—time-of-flight and depth
cameras
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
, sonar and ultrasonic
sensors
(
Jansen and Steckel 2026
)
, radar cross-section evaluation, or any
real-time simulation that needs to cull irrelevant geometry before intersection
testing.
The key requirement is a bounded ray origin, not a specific sensor type.

A.2.
Ray Noise Simulation

Distance noise (range uncertainty) is a post-processing perturbation added after output conversion.
Horizontal angular noise is compatible provided no perturbed ray crosses an adjacent ray; the ray index lookup replaces the nominal index with a three-neighbour argmin over pre-stored perturbed angles
\theta_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}
:

(40)

i^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k\,\in\,\{i-1,\,i,\,i+1\}}\bigl|\theta-\theta_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}\bigr|

The search is
O(1)
and no valid hit is excluded.

Per-channel vertical noise—where each channel carries a distinct
pre-stored perturbed elevation
\phi_{j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}
—is also supported under the same
small-perturbation precondition as the horizontal case: no channel offset may
cross an adjacent channel boundary (
|\phi_{j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}-\phi_{j}|<\Delta\phi
).
Each channel stores its pre-perturbed elevation
\phi_{j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}
;
the channel assignment uses a three-neighbour argmin identical in form to
Equation
40
:

(41)

j^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k\,\in\,\{j-1,\,j,\,j+1\}}\bigl|\phi-\phi_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle*}}\bigr|

The GACP-T intersection is evaluated at the nominal channel elevation

\phi_{j}
(conservative: possible slight over-prediction, no missed hits);
correctness is guaranteed because the final RTIC uses the actual perturbed ray
direction.

A.3.
GPU Implementation

GRCA is implemented in CUDA and Vulkan/HLSL; both backends share the same algorithm and produce identical output. This section describes the CUDA reference; the mapping to Vulkan/HLSL is direct: thread groups
\to
warps, group-shared memory
\to
shared memory, wave intrinsics
\to
warp ballot and shuffle.

Targeting a wide range of GPU hardware, the implementation is designed to
stay within the Shader Model 5.0 / Vulkan compute minimum of
32 KB

shared memory and
16 384
registers per thread
group
(
Khronos Group 2022
)
: exceeding either spills to slower memory and
collapses occupancy
(
Guthmann 2023
)
.
The late pass is the harder case — a
32{\times}32
block assigns 32 BAT
triangles and 32 ray-partition rows simultaneously, so each warp lane must
hold GACP math, arc disambiguation, and RTIC state in sequence.
The early pass (
32{\times}16
, one triangle per thread) is more register-forgiving but still uses shared memory for ray-origin descriptors.

A.3.1.
Pipeline Overview

The full algorithm runs four GPU passes per frame on a single command queue.
The ray-setup pass computes all
\Omega\times\gamma_{n}\times\chi_{n}
ray
directions (Eq.
7
) and initializes each per-ray distance to

f_{\mathrm{sort}}(+\infty)
.
The output-conversion pass decodes each stored integer with
f_{\mathrm{sort}}^{-1}

and writes the output record
(\hat{d},\,D)
; rays with no intersection retain

D=+\infty
as a sentinel, matching the convention used by OptiX and Embree
miss shaders.

Ray-setup and output-conversion dispatch

Both bookend passes use the same grid layout:

\text{Grid}=\bigl(\Omega,\;\lceil\chi_{n}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}/1024\rceil\bigr),\quad\text{Block}=(32,\,32)

Each thread decodes its flat ray index into
(i_{h},i_{v})
, evaluates the spherical direction (Eq.
7
) with one sine/cosine per axis, and writes the direction and
f_{\mathrm{sort}}(+\infty)

A.3.2.
Per-Ray Distance Encoding

The bijection
f_{\mathrm{sort}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{U}_{32}
encodes
floats as unsigned 32-bit integers that compare in the same order,
enabling atomic minimum over unsigned integers to find the nearest hit:

(42)

f_{\mathrm{sort}}(x):\quad u\leftarrow\langle x\rangle_{\mathrm{u32}},\quad m\leftarrow-(u\gg 31)\;|\;2^{31},\quad\text{return}\;u\oplus m

(43)

f_{\mathrm{sort}}^{-1}(u):\quad m\leftarrow\bigl((u\gg 31)-1\bigr)\;|\;2^{31},\quad\langle u\oplus m\rangle_{\mathrm{f32}}

Several guards (

•

Skip quadratic root if

A.4.
Single-Threaded CPU Implementation

This section serves as an
algorithmic ablation
: single-threading both sides with equivalent SIMD width isolates GRCA’s emitter-centric geometric gain from GPU throughput, warp width, or multi-core bandwidth. GRCA
CPU
implements the same two-pass structure using x86 SIMD intrinsics (SSE4.1, AVX2, AVX2
\times
2).

Unlike the GPU implementation, GRCA
CPU
places no fixed capacity on the BAT deferred list; all deferred triangles are always processed. The remaining tunable parameters (
\gamma_{T}
,
\chi_{T}
, and
\varepsilon_{A}
) are algorithmic and carry over unchanged. Embree is restricted to a single thread for all internal work (BVH build, refit, and traversal) with SIMD at the same width as GRCA
CPU
. Any observed speedup therefore reflects the algorithmic difference—emitter-centric triangle filtering versus BVH traversal—not a difference in threading or SIMD exploitation.

A.4.1.

Ray directions are stored in SoA layout (

A.4.2.
Early-Pass Triangle Filter (Vectorized)

\tau

The scalar back-face and apparent-area conditions (Section
3.1

vectorize directly as
N

(\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{o})\cdot\hat{n}_{i}

their bitwise AND-NOT collapses to a per-triangle pass/fail bitmask, and only set bits proceed.

A.4.3.
GACP-T Intersection (Vectorized)

Where the GPU achieves parallelism across triangles and channels via warps, the CPU exploits it across edges within a triangle: all three edge quadratics (Section
2.5
) are packed into a single 4-wide SIMD register and solved with one square root (lane 3 padded).
The five precomputed scalars from Section
2.5.2
are laid out as 4-wide vectors (one lane per edge), and the quadratic coefficients
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
become
\mathbf{c}_{0,4},\mathbf{c}_{1,4},\mathbf{c}_{2,4}
solved in a single pass.
Root-validity and arc-disambiguation checks (Section
2.5
) reduce to per-lane integer bitmask tests;
intersection points are extracted via a single bit-scan on the validity mask, avoiding any scalar fallback for the common two-root case.

A.4.4.

Per-triangle statics are precomputed and broadcast once:

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\quad\mathbf{s}_{\times e_{1}}=\mathbf{s}\times\mathbf{e}_{1},\quad t_{\mathrm{num}}=\mathbf{e}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\times e_{1}}

\times
