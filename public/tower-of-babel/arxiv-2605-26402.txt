Abstract
. We study the fluctuations of the number of real roots of random polynomials with independent, nonzero-mean coefficients. Such non-centered ensembles arise naturally in signal-plus-noise models and in random perturbations of deterministic polynomials. While Ibragimov and Maslova (1971) established the leading asymptotics of the expected number of real roots for non-centered polynomials with i.i.d. coefficients, the corresponding variance asymptotics and central limit theorem have remained open for more than fifty years. This stands in sharp contrast to the centered case, where the fluctuation theory is now well understood across a wide range of ensembles. We resolve this gap by developing novel comparison principles that reduce the fluctuation theory of a non-centered ensemble to that of its centered counterpart. These principles yield sharp variance asymptotics and central limit theorems for broad classes of ensembles, including Kac and hyperbolic polynomials, their derivatives, and related extensions. In particular, for both Kac and hyperbolic polynomials, the leading variance constant equals exactly one-half of that in the centered case, reflecting asymmetric suppression of fluctuations across the two regions where roots concentrate. Our results provide the first comprehensive fluctuation theory for the number of real roots of non-centered random polynomials.

Theorem 1.1

(Non-centered Kac polynomials)
.

Let
P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}x^{j}
, where
(\xi_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
are real-valued independent random variables, with

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{j}]=\mu,\quad\Var[\xi_{j}]=1,\quad\mbox{and }\quad\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{j}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C_{0}

for all
j
and some constants
\mu\neq 0
,
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, and
C_{0}>0
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\Var[N_{n}]=\left[\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n.

Furthermore,
N_{n}
satisfies the CLT; that is, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{N_{n}-\mathbb{E}[N_{n}]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n}]}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The variance constant
\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)
is exactly one-half of that obtained by Maslova
[
54
]
for centered Kac polynomials. This reduction reflects a genuine asymptotic effect of the nonzero mean. In the centered case, real roots accumulate symmetrically near both
\pm 1
, and both regions contribute to the leading-order variance. In the non-centered setting, the deterministic component suppresses roots on one side, leaving a single dominant concentration region that governs the leading-order variance.

1.2.
Comparison principles

Our comparison method relies on decomposing the random polynomial
P_{n}
, defined in (
1.1
), into its deterministic and stochastic components. Specifically, we isolate the deterministic part

M_{n}(x):=\mathbb{E}[P_{n}(x)]

and define the centered fluctuation by

R_{n}(x):=P_{n}(x)-M_{n}(x).

Building on earlier work by the first author
[
15
]
, we develop novel comparison principles for random polynomials whose coefficients exhibit polynomial growth, commonly referred to as
generalized Kac polynomials

[
20
]
. A central object in our analysis is the normalized deterministic profile

m(x):=\frac{M_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}(x)]}},

which establishes the natural scale for comparing the relative influence of the deterministic and random components of
P_{n}
.

Before stating the comparison principles, we introduce some notation and conventions that will be used throughout.

Condition 1.4

(Polynomial growth)
.

We say that the coefficients
(\omega_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
of the polynomial
P_{n}
in (
1.1
) exhibit polynomial growth of order
\tau
if,

\omega_{j}=m_{j}+v_{j}\xi_{j},\quad j\geq 0,

where
(m_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
and
(v_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
are deterministic, and
(\xi_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
are independent real-valued random variables with zero mean and unit variance, such that the following conditions hold for some constants
\varepsilon_{0}>0
,
C_{0}>0
, and
N_{0}>0
:

(A1)

\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{j}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C_{0}
for all
0\leq j\leq n
;

(A2)

|m_{j}|,|v_{j}|\leq C_{0}(1+j)^{\tau}
for all
0\leq j\leq n
; and

(A3)

|v_{j}|\geq\frac{1}{C_{0}}(1+j)^{\tau}
for
N_{0}\leq j\leq n
.

These assumptions ensure that for
j
sufficiently large, the magnitude of
\omega_{j}
is, with high probability, comparable to
j^{\tau}
. Under this decomposition, we may write

M_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}m_{j}x^{j},\quad R_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}\xi_{j}x^{j},

yielding the representation

P_{n}(x)=M_{n}(x)+R_{n}(x).

This places
P_{n}
in a natural signal-plus-noise framework, which is central to our analysis. Such models arise, for example, when a deterministic polynomial is perturbed by measurement or sampling noise, as in polynomial regression
[
25
]
. In particular, if
M_{n}
is a true deterministic polynomial regression function and the errors are centered Gaussian, then the least-squares estimator
P_{n}
is a random polynomial satisfying
\mathbb{E}[P_{n}(x)]=M_{n}(x)
, and hence fits within the present framework.

Notation 1.5
.

Unless otherwise specified, all asymptotic statements are understood in the limit
n\to\infty
. For real quantities
X
and
Y
, we write
X=O(Y)
,
X\ll Y
, or equivalently
Y\gg X
, if there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
Y
, such that
|X|\leq CY
. When needed, subscripts (e.g.,

) indicate the dependence of

on specified parameters. If both

and

X\asymp Y
or
X=\Theta(Y)
. For real sequences
(\alpha_{n})
and
(\beta_{n})
, we write
\alpha_{n}\sim\beta_{n}
if
\alpha_{n}/\beta_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, and
\alpha_{n}=o(\beta_{n})
if
\alpha_{n}/\beta_{n}\to 0

For a nonempty set

, we adopt the notation

-I:=\{-x:x\in I\}\quad\text{and}\quad I^{-1}:=\{x^{-1}:x\in I\}.

For a nonempty set

and a function

the number of zeros of

Finally, let
P^{*}_{n}(x):=x^{n}P_{n}\left(1/x\right)
be the reciprocal polynomial associated with

M_{n}^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}[P_{n}^{*}(x)]

R_{n}^{*}(x)=P_{n}^{*}(x)-M_{n}^{*}(x)

m^{*}(x)=\frac{M_{n}^{*}(x)}{\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}^{*}(x)]}}.

Definition 1.6

(Enlargement of an interval)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval (open, closed, or half-open) with endpoints
a<b
. An interval
J\supset I
is called an
enlargement
of
I
if it is obtained by extending

beyond
a

\Theta(|1-|a||+n^{-1})
and beyond
b

\Theta(|1-|b||+n^{-1})
.

Remark 1.7
.

Since our results impose conditions on
J
and draw conclusions about

|1-|a||
is bounded below by a positive absolute constant. An analogous statement holds for the right endpoint

I\in\{(-\infty,0),(0,\infty)\}
, one may take

.

Definition 1.8

(Domination)
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer. We say that
M_{n}

dominates

R_{n}
on a set
J\subset\mathbb{R}
up to order
k
with factor function
\phi
if, for all
i=0,1,\dots,k
,

|M_{n}^{(i)}(x)|\geq\phi(1-|x|+1/n)\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}^{(i)}(x)]},\quad x\in J\cap[-1,1],

and

|{M_{n}^{*}}^{(i)}(1/x)|\geq\phi(1-|1/x|+1/n)\sqrt{\Var[{R_{n}^{*}}^{(i)}(1/x)]},\quad x\in J\backslash[-1,1].

If the above inequalities are reversed, we say that
M_{n}
is
dominated by

R_{n}
on
J
up to order
k
with factor function
\phi
.

For brevity, when
k=0
we omit the phrase “up to order
k
”. Roughly speaking, when
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
, the deterministic polynomial is large compared to the size of the random fluctuations, and the same comparison holds for their derivatives up to the prescribed order.

Remark 1.9
.

The domination conditions admit an equivalent formulation in terms of the normalized profile
m
. Indeed, under Assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
, Lemma
3.2
shows that for any integer

, there exist constants
C_{i}
and

such that, uniformly for
x\in[-1,1]
,

\Var[R_{n}^{(i)}(x)]\asymp\frac{C_{i}\Var[R_{n}(x)]}{(1-|x|+1/n)^{2i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d^{i}}{dx^{i}}\Var[R_{n}(x)]\asymp\frac{C_{i}^{\prime}\Var[R_{n}(x)]}{(1-|x|+1/n)^{i}}.

Consequently, the condition

|M_{n}^{(i)}(x)|\gg\phi(1-|x|+1/n)\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}^{(i)}(x)]},\quad x\in[-1,1],

is equivalent to

|m^{(i)}(x)|\gg\frac{\phi(1-|x|+1/n)}{(1-|x|+1/n)^{i}},\quad x\in[-1,1].

An analogous equivalence holds for
m^{*}
. Although the formulation in terms of

is often less convenient to verify in applications, it yields a more concise statement.

3.
Probability of multiple roots

In this section, we estimate the probability that
P_{n}
has many real roots in small neighborhoods of
\pm 1
. These estimates will be used in subsequent sections to reduce the proof to the Gaussian setting.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that the coefficients of
P_{n}
in (
1.1
) have polynomial growth of order
\tau>-1/2
and that
(\xi_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
are independent standard normal random variables. Fix constants
C,\theta,B>0
, and set
B_{n}=[1-B/n,1)
. Then there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
\beta
such that the following holds. For every integer
k\geq 2
, every constant
\delta\in(0,1/\beta)
, and every interval
I=[a,b)\subset B_{n}
satisfying

\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}=\delta,

if
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
on an enlargement
J\supset I
with factor function
Cx^{\theta}
, then

(3.1)

\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k)\leq C_{1}(\beta\delta)^{2k/3}.

Analogous results hold for
N_{P_{n}}(-I)
and
N_{P_{n}^{*}}(\pm I)
.

3.1.
Auxiliary results

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we first establish some preliminary results.

Lemma 3.2
.

If Condition
1.4
holds, then for
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and uniformly over
x\in[x_{0},1]
,

(3.2)

\Var[R_{n}(x)]\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{2\tau+1}}

and for
c=(2\tau+3)/x_{0}
and any integer
k\geq 1
,

(3.3)

\Var[R_{n}^{(k)}(x)]\leq\frac{(c^{k}k!)^{2}}{(1-x+1/n)^{2k}}\Var[R_{n}(x)].

Proof.

We first prove (
3.2
). By Assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
,

\Var[R_{n}(x)]=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{j}^{2}x^{2j}\asymp\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2j}.

Assume first that
1-1/n\leq x\leq 1
. Clearly,

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}\ll n^{2\tau+1}\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{2\tau+1}}.

To prove the lower bound, note that
x^{2j}\geq\left(1-1/n\right)^{2j}\gg 1
, so

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2j}\gg\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}\gg n^{2\tau+1}\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{2\tau+1}}.

Assume now that
x_{0}\leq x\leq 1-1/n
. By
[
19
]
*Lemma 3.1,

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2j}\sim\frac{\Gamma(2\tau+1)}{(1-x^{2})^{2\tau+1}}\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{2\tau+1}},

from which (
3.2
) follows.

Similarly, for each
k=1,2,\ldots,
it holds uniformly for
x\in[x_{0},1]
that

\Var[R_{n}^{(k)}(x)]\leq\frac{C_{0}}{x_{0}^{2k}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau+2k}x^{2j}\sim\frac{C_{0}}{x_{0}^{2k}}\frac{\Gamma(2\tau+1+2k)}{(1-x+1/n)^{2\tau+1+2k}}.

To prove (
3.3
), it suffices to show that

\Gamma(2\tau+1+2k)\leq(x_{0}c)^{2k}(k!)^{2}\Gamma(2\tau+1),

or equivalently,

\prod_{j=0}^{2k-1}(2\tau+1+j)\leq(x_{0}c)^{2k}(k!)^{2}.

With
x_{0}c=2\tau+3
, this follows easily by induction on
k
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all integer
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[a,b]}|M_{n}^{(k)}(u)|\leq\frac{c^{k}k!}{(1-b+1/n)^{k}}\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}(b)]}.

Proof.

We may assume
k\geq 1
, since the case
k=0
is trivial. We follow the approach of Borwein and Erdélyi
[
10
]
.

Let
I=[a,b)\subset B_{n}
be as in Theorem
3.1
and let
J\supset I
be an enlargement. Denote by
\ell
the length of
J
and by
w
its midpoint. Consider the conformal map
f:\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

f(z)=\frac{\ell}{4}\left(z+\frac{1}{z}\right)+w.

The map
f
sends the unit circle
\{|z|=1\}
onto the interval
J
. For
\eta>1
, let
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
denote the ellipse centered at
w
with semiaxes

\frac{\ell}{4}\left(\eta+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\ell}{4}\left(\eta-\frac{1}{\eta}\right).

Then
f
maps the circle
\{|z|=\eta\}
onto
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
.

Let
\eta_{1}=1+\epsilon
and
\eta_{2}=3/2
, where
\epsilon>0
is a sufficiently small constant. By Hadamard’s three-circle theorem (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
),

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{1}}}\log|M_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(\eta_{2}/\eta_{1})}{\log\eta_{2}}\sup_{u\in J}\log|M_{n}(u)|+\frac{\log\eta_{1}}{\log\eta_{2}}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{2}}}\log|M_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in J}\log|M_{n}(u)|+\frac{\epsilon}{\log(3/2)}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{2}}}\log|M_{n}(z)|.

Since
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
Cx^{\theta}
and

\Var[R_{n}(u)]\asymp\frac{1}{(1-u+1/n)^{2\tau+1}}\asymp n^{2\tau+1},\quad u\in J,

we have

\sup_{u\in J}\log|M_{n}(u)|\leq\left(\tau+1/2-\theta\right)\log n.

By the definition of an enlargement,

\ell=\Theta(1-a+n^{-1})+|I|+\Theta(1-b+n^{-1}).

In particular, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, independent of
I
and
\epsilon

c_{1}n^{-1}+|I|\leq\ell\leq c_{2}n^{-1}+|I|.

Consequently, there exist constants

By Condition

(independent of

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{2}}}\log|M_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\log\bigg(C_{0}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{\tau}(1+C_{2}/n)^{j}\bigg)\leq C_{\tau}\log n.

and hence

Finally, by Cauchy’s integral formula, for every

\displaystyle|M_{n}^{(k)}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k!}{2\pi}\bigg|\oint_{\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{1}}}\frac{M_{n}(z)}{(z-u)^{k+1}}dz\bigg|

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
P_{n}
has at least
k
real roots in an interval

, then

|P_{n}(b)|\leq\int_{x}^{y}\int_{x}^{y_{1}}\dots\int_{x}^{y_{k-1}}|P^{(k)}_{n}(y_{k})|dy_{k}\dots dy_{1}=:Q_{n,k}^{I}.

Since

has at least

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We prove the result for
N_{P_{n}}(I)
, as the remaining cases follow by analogous arguments.

For notational convenience, set
\epsilon:=(\delta\beta)^{2k/3}
and

V(b):=\Var[P_{n}(b)]=\Var[R_{n}(b)].

By the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(|P_{n}(b)|\leq\epsilon V(b)^{1/2})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(|P_{n}(b)|>\epsilon V(b)^{1/2},N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k).

Since
P_{n}(b)
is Gaussian with mean
M_{n}(b)
and variance
V(b)
, a standard anti-concentration bound yields

\mathbb{P}(|P_{n}(b)|\leq\epsilon V(b)^{1/2})\ll\epsilon.

Thus, to establish (
3.1
), it suffices to prove that

\mathbb{P}(|P_{n}(b)|>\epsilon V(b)^{1/2},N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k)\ll\epsilon.

By (
3.4
), on the event
\{N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k\}
we have
|P_{n}(b)|\leq Q_{k,n}^{I}
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|P_{n}(b)|>\epsilon V(b)^{1/2},N_{P_{n}}(I)\geq k)\leq\mathbb{P}(Q_{k,n}^{I}\geq\epsilon V(b)^{1/2}).

Using (
3.4
), Hölder’s inequality, and Fubini-Tonelli’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Q_{k,n}^{I}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(b-a)^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}\sup_{u\in[a,b]}\Big(|M^{(k)}_{n}(u)|^{2}+\mathbb{E}|R_{n}^{(k)}(u)|^{2}\Big).

Applying Lemma
3.3
, (
3.3
), and Lemma
3.2
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[a,b]}\Big(|M^{(k)}_{n}(u)|^{2}+\mathbb{E}|R_{n}^{(k)}(u)|^{2}\Big)\ll\frac{(c^{k}k!)^{2}}{(1-b+1/n)^{2k}}V(b).

Since

\frac{b-a}{1-b+1/n}\leq\frac{b-a}{1-b}=e^{\delta}-1\leq 2\delta,

by choosing
\beta
very large compared to

, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Q_{k,n}^{I}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(b-a)^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}\frac{(c^{k}k!)^{2}}{\left(1-b+1/n\right)^{2k}}V(b)

\displaystyle=\bigg(c\frac{b-a}{1-b+1/n}\bigg)^{2k}V(b)

\displaystyle\leq(\beta\delta)^{2k}V(b).

Using Markov’s inequality, we derive

\mathbb{P}(Q_{k,n}^{I}\geq\epsilon V(b)^{1/2})\ll\frac{(\beta\delta)^{2k}}{\epsilon^{2}}=\epsilon.

4.
Comparison principles: Reduction to the Gaussian case

In this section, we reduce the comparison principles in Theorems
1.10
and
1.12
to the Gaussian setting, where one can subsequently invoke the Kac–Rice formula together with the structural properties of Gaussian processes. The key input is a universality principle asserting that the asymptotic behavior of real roots is largely insensitive to the precise distribution of the coefficients.

We state the required universality results here and use them to reduce the problem to the Gaussian case, deferring their proofs to the next two sections. Since the statements are inherently local, we partition the real line into a core region
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, which captures the bulk of the real roots, and a complementary region whose contribution is negligible.

We now define
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. Let
0\leq b_{n}<a_{n}<1
be such that for any
A>0
,

\displaystyle a_{n}\ll_{A}\log^{-A}n.

A convenient choice is
a_{n}=\exp(-\log^{d/4}n)
for some fixed
d\in(0,1)
, though we retain flexibility for later applications. Set
I_{n}=[1-a_{n},1-b_{n})
and define

\displaystyle U_{n}=I_{n}\cup\left(-I_{n}\right),\quad U_{n}^{*}=I_{n}^{-1}\cup\left(-I_{n}^{-1}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{n}=U_{n}\cup U_{n}^{*}.

We assume that the coefficients of
P_{n}
exhibit polynomial growth of order
\tau>-1/2
(see Condition
1.4
), and define the Gaussian analogue

P_{n,G}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(m_{j}+v_{j}\widetilde{\xi}_{j})x^{j},

where
(\widetilde{\xi}_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables.

The reduction proceeds by comparing the statistics of the number of real roots of
P_{n}
and
P_{n,G}
on the core region
\mathcal{I}_{n}
via universality, and then showing that the contribution from
\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n}
is negligible.

4.1.
Main ingredients for the reduction

The first ingredient is a universality result that controls the contribution from the core region.

Theorem 4.1

(Universality on the core region)
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
J
be an enlargement of
I
. Assume that either
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, or that
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
on
J
up to order 2 with an arbitrary factor constant
C>0
. Then there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
c
such that, for any function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
whose derivatives up to order 3 are bounded by
1
, and for all sufficiently large
n
,

(4.1)

|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}))]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}))]|\leq C_{1}(a_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}).

The second result provides a moment bound for the number of real roots outside the core region
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Moment bound outside the core region)
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
J
be an enlargement of
I
. Assume that either
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, or that
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
C|x|^{\theta}
for arbitrary constants
C,\theta>0
. Assume that
a_{n}=\exp(-\log^{d/4}n)
for some fixed
d\in(0,1)
and
b_{n}=B/n
for some constant
B>0
. Then, for every integer
k\geq 2
, there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}^{k}\left(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)]\leq C_{k}|\log a_{n}|^{2k}.

The proofs of these results will be given later. For the reduction, we will in fact use the following corollary of Theorem
4.1
, whose derivation will also be provided.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, there exist constants
C_{1},c>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

(4.2)

|\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}^{k}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}^{k}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]|\leq C_{1}(a_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}).

Consequently, for

,

(4.3)

|\Var[N_{{P}_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]-\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]|\leq C_{1}(a_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}).

4.2.
Reduction to the Gaussian case

In this subsection, assuming that Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.3
hold, we will reduce the proof of Theorems
1.10
and
1.12
to the Gaussian setting.

Let
a_{n}=\exp(-\log^{d/4}n)
for fixed
d\in(0,1)
and let
b_{n}=B/n
for some sufficiently large constant
B
. Applying Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.3
with
k=2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]=\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I)]+O(\log^{d}n).

Combined with the universality result for
\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I)]
from
[
60
]
*Corollary 2.2, this reduces Theorem
1.10
to the Gaussian case.

For the reduction of Theorem
1.12
, assume that
\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I)]\geq\epsilon\log n
. Then

\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]\gg\log n.

This assumption, combined with estimate (
4.3
), implies

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]=\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})](1+o(1)).

Write

\displaystyle\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}}\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}.

Applying Theorem
4.2
, we deduce that

\Var[N_{P_{n}}\left(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)]=o(\log n)\quad\text{and}\quad\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]\gg\log n.

This leads to

\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}}\xrightarrow{d}0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I)]}}=1+o(1).

By Slutsky’s theorem,
N_{P_{n}}(I)
satisfies the CLT if and only if
N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})
does.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
x<0
, then

\mathbb{P}(N_{{P}_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq x)=0=\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq x).

Now, suppose that
x\geq 0
. Since
N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)
is always a non-negative integer, it follows that

\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)\leq x)=\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)\leq\lfloor x\rfloor)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}))],

where
\varphi
is any smooth function that takes values in
[0,1]
and satisfies
\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,\lfloor x\rfloor]}\leq\varphi\leq\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,\lfloor x\rfloor+1/2]}
. By applying (
4.1
), we can assert that

|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}))]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N_{P_{n,G}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right))]|\leq Ca_{n}^{c},

and so

|\mathbb{P}(N_{{P}_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq x)|\leq Ca_{n}^{c}.

Together with Corollary
4.3
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}(1+o(1))+o(1)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}\leq x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]}}\leq x\bigg)+o(1).

Therefore, by Slutsky’s theorem, if
N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})
satisfies the CLT, then so does
N_{{P}_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})
.

5.
Universality for real roots, revisit

In this section, we establish the universality results stated in Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.3
. Our approach relies on a replacement principle introduced by Tao and Vu
[
69
]
and further refined by Do, Nguyen, and Vu
[
20
]
and Nguyen and Vu
[
60
,
59
]
. This principle allows one to compare local statistics, such as root densities and correlation functions, of zeros of random functions whose logarithmic magnitudes are close in distribution and satisfy suitable anti-concentration bounds.

As the method is inherently local and most effective when the expected number of real roots is
O(1)
, an additional argument is required to lift local estimates to global conclusions. For simplicity of exposition, we restrict to the case
I\subset[0,1]
; the general case follows by a straightforward modification.

To implement the local comparison, we partition
I_{n}=[1-a_{n},1-b_{n})
into dyadic subintervals. Let
T\ll\log n
be the smallest positive integer such that

\frac{a_{n}}{2^{T}}\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{n},b_{n}\right\}.

Define

\displaystyle\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2^{-j}a_{n}&\text{if}\quad 0\leq j\leq T-1,\\
\max\left\{\frac{1}{n},b_{n}\right\}&\text{if}\quad j=T,\end{cases}

\displaystyle J_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}[1-\delta_{j-1},1-\delta_{j})&\text{if}\quad 1\leq j\leq T-1,\\
[1-\delta_{j-1},1-b_{n})&\text{if}\quad j=T.\end{cases}

Although
\delta_{0}>\dots>\delta_{T-1}
, it may happen that
\delta_{N}>\delta_{T-1}
(e.g., if
a_{n}<1/n
, then
T=1
and
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{n}>\delta_{0}=a_{n}
). Nevertheless, for all
1\leq j\leq T
,

\delta_{j}\gg\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{j}\geq\frac{\delta_{j-1}}{2}.

Thus,
\mathcal{P}:=\{J_{j}\}_{j=1}^{T}
forms a partition of
I_{n}
into intervals on which the expected number of real roots is uniformly bounded. For
j=1,...,T
, let

N_{j}=N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap J_{j}\right),

and let
G_{j}
denote the corresponding quantity for the Gaussian polynomial.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

It suffices to prove the following more general theorem.

Theorem 5.1

(General universality on the core region)
.

There exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
c
such that for every function
F:\mathbb{R}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
with all partial derivatives up to order
3
bounded by
1
,

|\mathbb{E}[F(N_{1},\dots,N_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[F(G_{1},\dots,G_{T})]|\leq C_{1}(a_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. Let
\left(\zeta_{\ell}\right)_{\ell=1}^{n}
be the (complex) roots of
P_{n}
. We will approximate
N_{j}
by

\varphi_{j}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\varphi_{j}\left(\zeta_{\ell}\right),

where, for each
1\leq j\leq T
,
\varphi_{j}
is a suitable bump function supported on a small neighborhood of
J_{j}
in the complex plane:

(5.1)

\varphi_{j}(x+iy):=\psi_{j}(x)\varphi\bigg(\frac{y}{\delta_{j}^{1+\varepsilon}}\bigg),

•

\varphi
is a smooth function supported on
[-1,1]
that equals
1
at
0
, and

•

\psi_{j}
is smooth and supported on the neighborhood
J_{j}+[-\delta_{j}^{1+\varepsilon},\delta_{j}^{1+\varepsilon}]
of
J_{j}
on the real line,
and equals to
1
on
J_{j}
.

By a standard construction, we can ensure that

\|\partial_{x}^{\ell}\partial_{y}^{k}\varphi_{j}(x+iy)\|_{\sup}=O\left(\delta_{j}^{-(k+\ell)(1+\varepsilon)}\right)

for any
k,\ell\geq 0
such that
k+\ell\leq 3
.

We will prove the following lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F\left(N_{1},\dots,N_{T}\right)]-\mathbb{E}[F\left(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{T}\right)]\ll a_{n}^{\varepsilon/4}.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}
be the Gaussian analogue of
\varphi_{j}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F\left(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{T}\right)]-\mathbb{E}[F\left(\widetilde{\varphi}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\varphi}_{T}\right)]\ll a_{n}^{\varepsilon}.

Theorem
5.1
follows directly from Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
via the triangle inequality.
∎

The proofs of Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
are obtained by adapting the arguments of Nguyen and Vu
[
60
]
to the non‑centered setting. For completeness, the details are deferred to Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
.

5.2.
Proof of Corollary
4.3

Fix
k\geq 1
and recall that
T\asymp\log n
. Let
\varphi
be a smooth function supported on the interval
[-\frac{1}{2},\lfloor\log^{4}n\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}]
, with
\varphi(x)=x^{k}
for all
x\in[0,\lfloor\log^{4}n\rfloor]
. This function
\varphi
can be constructed so that all of its derivatives up to order 3 are bounded by
A=O(k^{3}\log^{4k}n)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}:=\{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq\lfloor\log^{4}n\rfloor\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{G}:=\{N_{P_{n,G}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})\leq\lfloor\log^{4}n\rfloor\}
.
Since
N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)
is always an integer, we must have

N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}}=\varphi\left(N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)\right).

A similar conclusion holds for
N_{P_{n,G}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{G}}
. Therefore, by applying Theorem
4.1
to the rescaled function
\left(1/A\right)\varphi
, for some
c>0
it holds that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}]-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}]|

\displaystyle\ll\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{c}}]+\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{G}^{c}}]+a_{n}^{c/2}+n^{-c/2}.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{j}=\{N_{j}\leq\log^{2}(1/{\delta_{j}})\}
. Clearly,
\cap_{j=1}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{ij}\subset\mathcal{D}
. By Lemma
2.6
, for some
c>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}^{c}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}_{j}^{c}\right)\ll\sum_{j=1}^{T}\delta_{j}^{c}\ll a_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}.

Combining this with Theorem
2.5
and Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}\right)^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{c}}]

\displaystyle\ll T^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{k}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{c}}]

\displaystyle\ll T^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T}(\delta_{j}^{c}+|\log(\delta_{j})|^{C_{2}k}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}^{c}))

\displaystyle\ll a_{n}^{c/2}+n^{-c/2}.

Since the analogous estimate holds for
N_{P_{n,G}}
, (
4.2
) is verified.

The estimate (
4.3
) follows by the triangle inequality and the case
k=2
of (
4.2
).

6.
Moment bounds for the number of real roots

In this section, we will discuss the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

By the triangle inequality, we may assume without loss of generality that
I\subset[0,1]
. We then cover
I\backslash\mathcal{I}_{n}
by the intervals

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=[0,1-x_{0}],

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-x_{0},1-a_{n}),

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=[1-\frac{B}{n},1-\frac{a_{n}}{n}),

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=[1-\frac{a_{n}}{n},1],

where
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and
B>0
are constants. Thus, it suffices to prove the desired estimate for the real roots inside each
S_{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,4
.

For
S_{1}
, by Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}^{k}\left(S_{1}\right)]=O_{k}\left(1\right).

For
S_{2}=(1-x_{0},1-a_{n})
, we cover
S_{2}
using dyadic intervals

I_{j}=(1-x_{0}2^{1-j},1-x_{0}2^{-j}],\quad 1\leq j\leq 1+\log_{2}(x_{0}/a_{n}).

By the triangle inequality and Theorem
2.5
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}^{k}\left(S_{2}\right)])^{1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq 1+\log_{2}(x_{0}/a_{n})}[\mathbb{E}N_{P_{n}}^{k}\left(I_{j}\right)]^{1/k}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leq j\leq 1+\log_{2}(x_{0}/a_{n})}|\log(2^{j}/x_{0})|

\displaystyle\ll|\log a_{n}|^{2},

which implies

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}^{k}\left(S_{2}\right)]\ll_{k}|\log a_{n}|^{2k}.

For
S_{3}
and
S_{4}
, we first use Corollary
4.3
to reduce the estimates to the Gaussian setting. Applying Corollary
4.3
for the intervals
[1-\frac{B}{n},1-\frac{a_{n}}{n})
and
[1-\frac{a_{n}}{n},1]
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n}}(S_{3})]-\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n,G}}(S_{3})]|

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right)^{c}+n^{-c}\ll 1,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n}}(S_{4})]-\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n,G}}(S_{4})]|

\displaystyle\ll b_{n}^{c}+n^{-c}\ll 1.

Hence, it suffices to work in the Gaussian setting.

For
S_{4}
, the Kac-Rice formula (see Lemma
9.1
) yields

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(S_{4})]=O(a_{n}).

Combining this with Theorem
2.5
and applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}^{k}(S_{4})]

\displaystyle\leq(\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(S_{4})])^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}^{2k-1}(S_{4})])^{1/2}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}a_{n}(\log n)^{k-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}1.

We next consider
S_{3}
, distinguishing two cases.

If
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, then the Kac-Rice formula (see Lemma
9.1
) yields

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(S_{3})]=O(a_{n}).

Arguing as above, it follows that

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}^{k}(S_{3})]=O_{k}(1).

Assume instead that
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
on
J
with factor function
C|x|^{\theta}
for some constants
C,\theta>0
. We then apply Theorem
3.1
. To this end, cover
S_{3}
by intervals
I=[a,b)
satisfying

\log\left(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\right)=\delta,

where
\delta>0
is chosen sufficiently small so that Theorem
3.1
applies. By that theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n,G}}(I)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}j^{k}\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n,G}}(I)=j)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n,G}}(I)=1)+C_{1}\sum_{j\geq 2}j^{k}(\beta\delta)^{2j/3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[N_{P_{n,G}}(I)\right]+O_{k}(1).

Note that the number of covering intervals is at most
\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{B}{a_{n}}
, and since these intervals have finite overlap, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n,G}}(S_{3})]

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\log^{k-1}(1/a_{n})\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n,G}}(S_{3})]+O(\log^{k}(1/a_{n})).

Then, we use Lemma
9.1
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N^{k}_{P_{n,G}}(S_{3})]

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\log^{k}(1/a_{n}).

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

7.
Kac–Rice formulas for non-centered Gaussian processes

In this section, we derive the Kac–Rice formulas for the one-point and two-point correlation functions of real roots of non-centered Gaussian processes, which may be of independent interest.

Condition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{G(x)\}_{x\in I}
be a Gaussian process on an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, with mean

m(x):=\mathbb{E}[G(x)]

and covariance kernel

(7.1)

r(x,y):=\Cov[G(x),G(y)].

Assume that
\mathcal{G}
is normalized, namely
r(x,x)=\Var[G(x)]=1
for every
x\in I
. We further impose the following assumptions:

(G1)

Regularity:

\mathcal{G}
has
C^{1}
-paths, with
m\in C^{1}(I)
and
r\in C^{2}\left(I^{2}\right)
.

(G2)

Nondegeneracy
: For all distinct
x,y\in I
, the random vector

(G(x),G(y),G^{\prime}(x),G^{\prime}(y))

is nondegenerate.

(G3)

Gaussian structure
: For
x\in I
,
(G(x),G^{\prime}(x))
is a nondegenerate Gaussian vector.

Here and subsequently, for nonnegative integers
j
and
\ell
, and any function
f(x,y)
, we write
f_{j\ell}
instead of
\frac{\partial^{j+\ell}f}{\partial x^{j}\partial y^{\ell}}
whenever it is defined.

The key computation is summarized in the following result.

Theorem 7.2

(Two-point correlation function)
.

Let Condition
7.1
hold. Define

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle:=m^{\prime}(x)+\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(x)-\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(y),

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)-\frac{r_{10}^{2}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}},

(7.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle:=m^{\prime}(y)+\frac{r(x,y)r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(y)-\frac{r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(x),

(7.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{r_{11}(y,y)-\frac{r_{01}^{2}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}},

(7.6)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\bigg(r_{11}(x,y)+\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\bigg),

and let
\nu_{1}=\mu_{1}/\sigma_{1}
and
\nu_{2}=\mu_{2}/\sigma_{2}
. Then, we obtain the following formula for the two-point correlation function
\rho_{2}(x,y)
of the real roots of
G
when
x\neq y
:

(7.7)

\rho_{2}(x,y)=\frac{E(x,y)}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{5}\rho_{2,i}(x,y)\right),

where

\displaystyle E(x,y)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}(x)-2r(x,y)m(x)m(y)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}+\delta\arcsin\delta),

\displaystyle\rho_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\arcsin\delta,

\displaystyle\rho_{2,3}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)\nu_{j}^{2}}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}|\delta|\sum_{j=1}^{2}\nu_{j}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)|\nu_{j}t|}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/2)}\erf\bigg(\frac{|\delta\nu_{j}t|}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)dt,

\displaystyle\rho_{2,4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left(t-1\right)\nu_{j}}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/2)}\erf\bigg(\frac{\delta\nu_{j}t}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)dt,

and

\displaystyle\rho_{2,5}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}|}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(1-s)\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}-2\delta\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds.

The analogous formula for the one-point correlation function
\rho_{1}
is classical; see Leadbetter and Cryer
[
45
]
(see also
[
56
]
for the complex setting). It will also emerge as a byproduct of the proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Lemma 7.3

(One-point correlation function)
.

Let

(7.8)

\rho_{1}(x)=\rho_{1,1}(x)+\rho_{1,2}(x),

where

(7.9)

\rho_{1,1}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[m^{2}(x)+\frac{\left(m^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}}{r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg]\bigg),

and

(7.10)

\rho_{1,2}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}m^{\prime}(x)\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\bigg)\erf\bigg(\frac{m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}\bigg).

Then
\rho_{1}(x)
is the density of real roots of
G(x)
, so that

\mathbb{E}[N_{G}(I)]=\int_{I}\rho_{1}(x)dx.

The variance of the number of real roots of
G
can then be calculated via the following standard formula (see, e.g.,
[
37
]
).

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
be the one-point and two-point correlation functions for the real roots of
G
. Then

\Var[N_{G}(I)]=\iint_{I^{2}}[\rho_{2}(x,y)-\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)]dxdy+\int_{I}\rho_{1}(x)dx.

7.1.
One-point correlation: Proof of Lemma
7.3

First, it follows from hypothesis
(G1)
that
G(x)
has a quadratic mean derivative
G^{\prime}(x)
, which is continuous on
I
(see, for example,
[
5
]
*§1.4). Therefore, letting

\lambda(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[G(x)G(y)]=r(x,y)+m(x)m(y),

we see that
\lambda\in C^{2}\left(I^{2}\right)
and
\lambda(x,x)=1+m^{2}(x)
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G(x)G^{\prime}(x)]

\displaystyle=m(x)m^{\prime}(x),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G^{\prime}(x)G(y)]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{10}(x,y)=r_{10}(x,y)+m^{\prime}(x)m(y),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{11}(x,y)=r_{11}(x,y)+m^{\prime}(x)m^{\prime}(y).

From the above computation and hypothesis
(G3)
, we deduce that for each
x\in I
,
G(x)
and
G^{\prime}(x)
are independent.

Now, we recall the classical Kac–Rice formula
[
42
]
,

\mathbb{E}[N_{G}(I)]=\int_{I}\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)|\mid G(x)=0]p_{G(x)}\left(0\right)dx,

where
p_{G(x)}
is the density of
G(x)
. Since
G(x)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m(x),1\right)
, it follows that

p_{G(x)}\left(0\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\right).

Because
G^{\prime}(x)
and
G(x)
are independent and
G^{\prime}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m^{\prime}(x),r_{11}(x,x)\right)
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)|\mid G(x)=0]=\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)|]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi r_{11}(x,x)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|\exp\bigg(-\frac{\left(t-m^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}}{2r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t+\frac{m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}|e^{-t^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{\left(m^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}}{2r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg)+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}\erf\bigg(\frac{m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}\bigg)\bigg],

where
\erf
is the error function defined as

\erf(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}dt.

In particular, we recover the formula (
7.8
) for the one-point correlation stated in Lemma
7.3
.

7.2.
Two-point correlation: Proof of Theorem
7.2

Let
\mathcal{C}:=\{G(x)=0,G(y)=0\}
. Given that
\mathcal{G}
satisfies hypotheses
(G1)
and
(G2)
, the Rice formula
[
5
]
*Theorem 3.2 asserts that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{G}(I)(N_{G}(I)-1)]=\iint_{I^{2}}\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)|\big|\mathcal{C}]p_{(G(x),G(y))}(0,0)dxdy,

where
p_{(G(x),G(y))}
is the joint density of
(G(x),G(y))
. Since the diagonal set
\{(x,x):x\in I\}
has Lebesgue measure zero, in subsequent computations, we may safely assume that
(x,y)\in I^{2}
with
x\neq y
.

Thus, the two-point correlation function
\rho_{2}(x,y)
of real roots of
G(x)
is given by

\rho_{2}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)|\big|\mathcal{C}]p_{(G(x),G(y))}(0,0).

Recall that
r(x,y)
is defined by (
7.1
), so

\displaystyle p_{(G(x),G(y))}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}(x)-2r(x,y)m(x)m(y)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}\right).

For
x\neq y
, under hypothesis
(G2)
, it follows that
r(x,y)\neq 1
, ensuring the well-definedness of
p_{(G(x),G(y))}(0,0)
.

We will use regression to evaluate
\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)|\big|\mathcal{C}]
. To perform a regression of
G^{\prime}(x)
on
\mathcal{C}
, we must carefully select
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(x,y)
and
\eta_{1}=\eta_{1}(x,y)
such that

\mathcal{R}_{1}:=G^{\prime}(x)+\theta_{1}G(x)+\eta_{1}G(y)

becomes independent of
G(x)
and
G(y)
. This independence can be achieved by solving the following system of equations for
\left(\theta_{1},\eta_{1}\right)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(G^{\prime}(x)+\theta_{1}G(x)+\eta_{1}G(y))G(x)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[G^{\prime}(x)+\theta_{1}G(x)+\eta_{1}G(y)]\mathbb{E}[G(x)],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(G^{\prime}(x)+\theta_{1}G(x)+\eta_{1}G(y))G(y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[G^{\prime}(x)+\theta_{1}G(x)+\eta_{1}G(y)]\mathbb{E}[G(y)].

These equations are equivalent to

\displaystyle\theta_{1}+\eta_{1}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle r_{10}(x,y)+\theta_{1}r(x,y)+\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

which leads to

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{1}=-\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}.

Similarly, a regression of
G^{\prime}(y)
on
\mathcal{C}
is

\mathcal{R}_{2}=G^{\prime}(y)+\theta_{2}G(y)+\eta_{2}G(x),

where

\displaystyle\theta_{2}:=\frac{r(x,y)r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2}:=-\frac{r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}.

But then

\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)|\big|\mathcal{C}]=\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{2}|].

Given
\mu_{1}
,
\sigma_{1}
,
\mu_{2}
,
\sigma_{2}
from (
7.2
), (
7.3
), (
7.4
), (
7.5
), a computation shows that

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}_{1}],

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Var[\mathcal{R}_{1}]},

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}_{2}],

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Var[\mathcal{R}_{2}]}.

Indeed, using
\Cov[G^{\prime}(x),G(x)]=0
and
\Var[G(x)]=\Var[G(y)]=1
, we get

\displaystyle\Var[\mathcal{R}_{1}]

\displaystyle=\Var[G^{\prime}(x)]+\theta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}+2\theta_{1}\eta_{1}\Cov[G(x),G(y)]

\displaystyle\quad+2\eta_{1}\Cov[G^{\prime}(x),G(y)]

\displaystyle=r_{11}(x,x)-\frac{r_{10}^{2}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}.

The computation for
\sigma_{2}
is entirely similar, while the computations for
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are even simpler.

We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|G^{\prime}(x)G^{\prime}(y)|\big|\mathcal{C}]=\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{2}|]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|ts|p(t,s)dtds,

where
p(t,s)
is the joint density of the bi-variate Gaussian random variable
(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2})
. Note that
p(t,s)
depends on
x,y
but for brevity, we omit this dependence in the notation. Below, we will calculate the covariance of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

Recalling

\Cov[G^{\prime}(x),G(x)]=\Cov[G^{\prime}(y),G(y)]=0\text{ and }\Var[G(x)]=\Var[G(y)]=1,

we have

\displaystyle\Cov[\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\Cov[G^{\prime}(x),G^{\prime}(y)]+\theta_{2}\Cov[G^{\prime}(x),G(y)]

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{1}\Cov[G(x),G^{\prime}(y)]+\theta_{1}\theta_{2}\Cov[G(x),G(y)]

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{1}\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}\theta_{2}+\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\Cov[G(x),G(y)]

\displaystyle=r_{11}(x,y)+\theta_{2}r_{10}(x,y)+\theta_{1}r_{01}(x,y)+\theta_{1}\eta_{2}+\theta_{2}\eta_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\theta_{1}\theta_{2}+\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\right)r(x,y)

\displaystyle=r_{11}(x,y)+\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}.

From (
7.6
), we see that

\delta=\frac{\Cov[\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}]}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}.

Let

\mu:=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\Sigma:=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}&\delta\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\\
\delta\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence,

\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{1}\\
\mathcal{R}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\Sigma\right),

and the probability density function
p
is given by

\displaystyle p(t,s)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-\delta^{2})}[(\frac{t-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}})^{2}-2\delta(\frac{t-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}})(\frac{s-\mu_{2}}{\sigma_{2}})+(\frac{s-\mu_{2}}{\sigma_{2}})^{2}]\right)}{2\pi\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}.

The nondegeneracy hypothesis
(G2)
implies that the joint distribution of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
remains non-degenerate, thus ensuring the well-definedness of
p
for all distinct
x,y\in I
.

We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{2}|]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|ts|p(t,s)dtds=\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}I_{\delta}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right),

where
\nu_{j}:=\mu_{j}/\sigma_{j}
, and

I_{\delta}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right):=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\left(t+\nu_{1}\right)\left(s+\nu_{2}\right)|\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}-2\delta ts+s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds.

Write

\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}-2\delta ts+s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}+s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{\delta ts}{1-\delta^{2}}\right)^{j},

and hence

\displaystyle I_{\delta}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{j}}{j!(1-\delta^{2})^{j}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\left(t+\nu_{1}\right)\left(s+\nu_{2}\right)|\left(ts\right)^{j}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}+s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds.

For subsequent computations, we will utilize the following power series expansion:

\frac{\arcsin\delta}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(j!\right)^{2}}{\left(2j+1\right)!}\left(2\delta\right)^{2j+1}.

This expansion can be readily obtained by noting that the left-hand side is an odd function that satisfies the functional equation
f^{\prime}(\delta)=\frac{1+\delta f(\delta)}{1-\delta^{2}}
.
We will also employ the following series expansion for the error function:

\erf\left(s\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-t^{2}}dt=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-s^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{j!}{\left(2j\right)!}\left(2s\right)^{2j-1},

which follows since
\erf\left(s\right)e^{s^{2}}
is an odd function that satisfies the functional equation
f^{\prime}\left(s\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}+2sf\left(s\right)
.

Now, for each
j\geq 0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t+\nu|t^{j}\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\nu}^{\infty}(t+\nu)t^{j}\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dx-\int_{-\infty}^{-\nu}(t+\nu)t^{j}\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dt

\displaystyle=[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{\frac{j}{2}+1}\Gamma\Big(\frac{j}{2}+1\Big)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ even}}+\nu[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{\frac{j+1}{2}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{j+1}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ odd}}

\displaystyle\quad+2(-1)^{j}\nu^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(\nu t)^{j}\exp\left(\frac{-\nu^{2}t^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dt,

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function defined as

\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt.

Hence,

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\left(t+\nu_{1}\right)\left(s+\nu_{2}\right)|\left(ts\right)^{j}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}+s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds

\displaystyle=[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{j+2}\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{j}{2}+1\right)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ even}}+\nu_{1}\nu_{2}[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{j+1}\Gamma^{2}\Big(\frac{j+1}{2}\Big)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ odd}}

\displaystyle\quad+2\bigg([2(1-\delta^{2})]^{\frac{j}{2}+1}\Gamma\Big(\frac{j}{2}+1\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nu_{i}^{2}(1-t)(\nu_{i}t)^{j}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}dt\bigg)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ even}}

\displaystyle\quad+(-2)\bigg([2(1-\delta^{2})]^{\frac{j+1}{2}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{j+1}{2}\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nu_{i}^{2}\nu_{3-i}(1-t)\left(\nu_{i}t\right)^{j}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}dt\bigg)\boldsymbol{1}_{j\text{ odd}}

\displaystyle\quad+4(\nu_{1}\nu_{2})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(1-s)(\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts)^{j}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dsdt.

This gives

I_{\delta}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{5}V_{i},

where

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{2j}}{\left(2j\right)!(1-\delta^{2})^{2j}}[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{2j+2}\left(j!\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\Big(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}+\delta\arcsin\delta\Big),

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{2j+1}}{\left(2j+1\right)!(1-\delta^{2})^{2j+1}}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{2j+2}\left(j!\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\arcsin\delta,

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{2j}}{\left(2j\right)!(1-\delta^{2})^{2j}}2[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{j+1}j!\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nu_{i}^{2}(1-t)(\nu_{i}t)^{2j}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}dt

\displaystyle=4(1-\delta^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)\nu_{j}^{2}}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j!}{(2j)!}\bigg(\frac{2\delta\nu_{j}t}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)^{2j}dt

\displaystyle=4(1-\delta^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\nu_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\exp\left(\frac{-\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dt

\displaystyle\quad+2|\delta|\sqrt{2\pi(1-\delta^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\nu_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)|\nu_{i}t|}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/2)}\erf\bigg(\frac{|\delta\nu_{i}t|}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)dt,

\displaystyle V_{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{2j+1}}{\left(2j+1\right)!(1-\delta^{2})^{2j+1}}

\displaystyle\quad\times(-2)[2(1-\delta^{2})]^{j+1}j!\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nu_{3-i}\nu_{i}^{2}(1-t)(\nu_{i}t)^{2j+1}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-2)(1-t)\nu_{3-i}\nu_{i}^{2}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/(2-2\delta^{2}))}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j!}{(2j+1)!}\frac{2^{j+1}(\delta\nu_{i}t)^{2j+1}}{(1-\delta^{2})^{j}}dt

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2\pi(1-\delta^{2})}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left(t-1\right)\nu_{i}}{\exp(\nu_{i}^{2}t^{2}/2)}\erf\bigg(\frac{\delta\nu_{i}t}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)dt,

and

\displaystyle V_{5}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{j}}{j!(1-\delta^{2})^{j}}

\displaystyle\quad\times 4\nu_{1}^{2}\nu_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(1-s)(\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts)^{j}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dsdt

\displaystyle=4\nu_{1}^{2}\nu_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(1-s)\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}-2\delta\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds.

This completes the proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Let
\mu_{1}
,

8.1.
Estimates for the mean and variance functions

We begin with asymptotic estimates for

,

, and their derivatives.

Applying the same argument as in the proof of Lemma

, we find that for any constant

and

Thus, for each

, it holds that

(8.1)

8.2.
The pseudo-hyperbolic distance on the unit disk

We now review the pseudo-hyperbolic metric on the unit disk, which plays a central role in our analysis.

Let
\varrho
denote the pseudo-hyperbolic distance on
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
; that is,

\varrho(z,w)=\frac{|z-w|}{|1-\overline{w}z|},\quad(z,w)\in\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}.

For each
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
, let
\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}:\varrho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\}
. For
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
xy\neq 1
, we define

\alpha:=\alpha(x,y):=\frac{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}{(1-xy)^{2}}=1-\varrho^{2}(x,y).

We recall a property of the pseudo-hyperbolic distance
\varrho
that will be convenient later.

Lemma 8.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
be a fixed constant. Suppose that for
x,y\in\left(-1,1\right)
that have the same sign, we have
\varrho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
. Then for every
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}
between
x
and
y
it holds that

\frac{1}{1-z_{1}z_{2}}=\frac{1+O(\varrho(x,y))}{1-z_{3}z_{4}}

and the implicit constant may depend on
\varepsilon
. Consequently,

\varrho\left(z_{1},z_{2}\right)\leq\varrho(x,y)[1+O(\varrho(x,y))].

8.3.
Estimates via known results

The following lemma was shown in
[
19
]
*Section 3.2.

Lemma 8.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
b_{n}=1/\left(na_{n}\right)
. Suppose that
v_{j}=(1+j)^{\tau}
for all
N_{0}\leq j\leq n
. It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in S_{n}\times S_{n}
that

\displaystyle r(x,y)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\tau+1/2}(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle 1-r^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\alpha^{2\tau+1}\right)(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\sqrt{1-\frac{(2\tau+1)\left(1-\alpha\right)\alpha^{2\tau+1}}{1-\alpha^{2\tau+1}}}(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{r_{11}(y,y)}\sqrt{1-\frac{(2\tau+1)\left(1-\alpha\right)\alpha^{2\tau+1}}{1-\alpha^{2\tau+1}}}(1+o(1)),

and

r_{11}(x,x)=\frac{2\tau+1}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}(1+o(1)),\quad x\in U_{n}.

Moreover, if
(x,y)\in\left(-S_{n}\right)\times S_{n}
, then
r(x,y)=o(1)
, and hence

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(x,y)=\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}(1+o(1))\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{2}(x,y)=\sqrt{r_{11}(y,y)}(1+o(1)).

The proof of Lemma
8.2
relies on the mean value theorem and the following uniform estimates:

\frac{d^{i}}{dx^{i}}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{j}\bigg]=\frac{\Gamma\left(2\tau+i+1\right)}{(1-x)^{2\tau+i+1}}(1+o(1)),\quad x\in(1-a_{n},1-\frac{1}{na_{n}}]

for
i=0,\dots,4
(see
[
19
]
*Corollary 3.2).

We now extend the above asymptotics to the case
b_{n}=B/n
, where
B>0
is a sufficiently large constant. Using the asymptotic for generalized binomial coefficients (see, e.g.,
[
27
]
), we find that

\binom{j+1+2\tau}{j+1}=\frac{(1+j)^{2\tau}}{\Gamma(2\tau+1)}\left(1+O\left(1/j\right)\right).

It was shown in
[
20
]
*Lemma 10.4 that for each
i=0,1,2
, the bound

\frac{d^{i}}{dx^{i}}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{j+1+2\tau}{j+1}x^{j}-\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2\tau+1}}\bigg]=O\bigg(\frac{\left(1+[n(1-x)]^{2\tau+i}\right)x^{n+1}}{(1-x)^{2\tau+i+1}}\bigg)

holds uniformly over
x\in[x_{0},1)
, for some constant
x_{0}\in(0,1)
. The same proof extends to the cases
i=3
and
i=4
.

Consequently, for
i=0,\dots,4
and
x\in I_{n}=(x_{0},1-B/n]
, where
B>0
is a sufficiently large constant, we have

\left(1+[n(1-x)]^{2\tau+i}\right)x^{n+1}\ll e^{-B/2}.

Therefore, it holds uniformly over
x\in I_{n}
that

(8.2)

\frac{d^{i}}{dx^{i}}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{j}\bigg]=\frac{\Gamma\left(2\tau+i+1\right)}{(1-x)^{2\tau+i+1}}\big(1+O(e^{-B/2})\big).

Using these estimates, the mean value theorem, and the approximations

1-\alpha^{2\tau+1}\asymp 1-\alpha=\varrho^{2}(x,y)

and

\sqrt{1-\frac{(2\tau+1)\left(1-\alpha\right)\alpha^{2\tau+1}}{1-\alpha^{2\tau+1}}}\asymp\sqrt{1-\alpha}=\varrho(x,y),

we establish the following lemma.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
b_{n}=B/n
, where
B>0
is a sufficiently large constant. Assume
v_{j}=(1+j)^{\tau}
for
j\geq 0
. It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in S_{n}\times S_{n}
that

\displaystyle r(x,y)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\tau+1/2}(1+O(e^{-B/2})),

\displaystyle 1-r^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp\varrho^{2}(x,y),

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\varrho(x,y),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{r_{11}(y,y)}\varrho(x,y),

and

r_{11}(x,x)=\frac{2\tau+1}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}(1+O(e^{-B/2})),\quad x\in U_{n}.

Furthermore, if
(x,y)\in\left(-S_{n}\right)\times S_{n}
, then
r(x,y)=o(1)
.

8.4.
Estimates for the covariance kernel and its partial derivatives

In the following, we derive asymptotic estimates for
r(x,y)
and its partial derivatives in the absence of asymptotic structure for the deterministic coefficients
v_{j}
. From now on, we assume that
b_{n}=B/n
for a sufficiently large constant
B
.

Lemma 8.4
.

It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

(8.3)

1-r^{2}(x,y)\asymp\varrho^{2}(x,y).

Proof.

By elementary computation, for any
n\geq 0
and any sequences
\left(x_{j}\right)_{j=0}^{n}
and
\left(y_{j}\right)_{j=0}^{n}
, we have

(8.4)

\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n}y_{j}^{2}\bigg)-\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}\left(x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}\right)^{2}.

Let

(8.5)

A(x,y):=k(x^{2})k(y^{2})-k^{2}(xy).

By applying (
8.4
) with
x_{j}:=v_{j}x^{j}
and
y_{j}:=v_{j}y^{j}
, we find that

A(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})^{2}.

This gives

\displaystyle 1-r^{2}(x,y)=\frac{A(x,y)}{k(x^{2})k(y^{2})}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}x^{2i}y^{2j}}.

Since all summands are non-negative, it follows from assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
that

1-r^{2}(x,y)\asymp\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(i+1)^{2\tau}(1+j)^{2\tau}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(i+1)^{2\tau}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2i}y^{2j}},

which yields (
8.3
) when combined with Lemma
8.3
.

∎

Lemma 8.5
.

It holds uniformly that

(8.6)

r_{11}(x,x)\asymp\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{2}},\quad x\in U_{n},

and

(8.7)

r_{11}(x,x)\ll\frac{1}{(1-|x|+1/n)^{2}},\quad 1-\frac{B}{n}\leq|x|\leq 1.

Proof.

Write

(8.8)

a(x):=k(x^{2})[k^{\prime}(x^{2})+x^{2}k^{\prime\prime}(x^{2})]-(xk^{\prime}(x^{2}))^{2}.

Using (
8.4
) with
x_{j}:=v_{j}x^{j}
and
y_{j}:=jv_{j}x^{j-1}
, we obtain

a(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}x^{2(i+j-1)}(j-i)^{2}.

It follows from
(A2)
,
(A3)
, and Lemma
8.3
that

\displaystyle r_{11}(x,x)=\frac{a(x)}{k^{2}(x^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}x^{2(i+j-1)}(j-i)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}x^{2(i+j)}}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(1+i)^{2\tau}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2(i+j-1)}(j-i)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(1+i)^{2\tau}(1+j)^{2\tau}x^{2(i+j)}}.

Combining with Lemma
8.3
, we deduce (
8.6
). For
1-B/n\leq|x|\leq 1
, using
(j-i)^{2}\leq n^{2}
, we obtain

r_{11}(x,x)\ll n^{2}\ll\frac{1}{(1-|x|+1/n)^{2}},

proving the lemma.
∎

Lemma 8.6
.

It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

(8.9)

\sigma_{1}\asymp\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\varrho(x,y)\asymp\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|},

and

(8.10)

\sigma_{2}\asymp\sqrt{r_{11}(y,y)}\varrho(x,y)\asymp\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-r^{2}(x,y))r_{11}(x,x)-r_{10}^{2}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}=\frac{A(x,y)a(x)-B^{2}(x,y)}{(1-r^{2}(x,y))k^{3}(x^{2})k(y^{2})},

where
A(x,y)
and
a(x)
are defined using (
8.5
) and (
8.8
), and

B(x,y):=yk^{\prime}(xy)k(x^{2})-xk^{\prime}(x^{2})k(xy).

Let

a_{i,j}:=v_{i}v_{j}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})\quad\text{and}\quad b_{i,j}:=v_{i}v_{j}x^{i+j-1}(j-i).

We have shown that

A(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad a(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}b_{i,j}^{2}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle B(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}jx^{i+j-1}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}(j-i)x^{i+j-1}(x^{i}y^{j}-x^{j}y^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}b_{i,j}.

Therefore, using (
8.4
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle A(x,y)a(x)-B^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\bigg[\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}^{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}b_{i,j}^{2}-\bigg(\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}b_{i,j}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i,j,l,q=0}^{n}(a_{i,j}b_{l,q}-a_{l,q}b_{i,j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i,j,l,q=0}^{n}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}v_{l}^{2}v_{q}^{2}x^{2\left(i+j+l+q-1\right)}e_{i,j,l,q}^{2}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right),

where

e_{i,j,l,q}(t):=(t^{j}-t^{i})(q-l)-(t^{q}-t^{l})(j-i).

By employing assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
, and noting that each term in the final sum is non-negative, we can replace
v_{j}
with
(1+j)^{\tau}
, resulting in an expression comparable to
A(x,y)a(x)-B^{2}(x,y)
. A similar conclusion can be drawn for
(1-r^{2}(x,y))k^{3}(x^{2})k(y^{2})
. Thus, we can apply Lemma
8.3
to obtain (
8.9
).

In the same manner, we also have (
8.10
).
∎

Our next goal is to estimate
r_{10}
and
r_{01}
.

Lemma 8.7
.

It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

(8.11)

|r_{10}(x,y)|\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-x^{2}},

and

(8.12)

|r_{01}(x,y)|\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-y^{2}}.

Proof.

Using (
7.3
), (
8.3
), and (
8.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle r_{10}^{2}(x,y)\leq(1-r^{2}(x,y))r_{11}(x,x)

\displaystyle\asymp\varrho^{2}(x,y)\frac{1}{(1-x^{2})^{2}},

which implies (
8.11
).

Similar arguments apply to (
8.12
).
∎

Lemma 8.8
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
and let
\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}:\varrho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\}
. It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
that

(8.13)

1-(y-x)\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\ll\varrho(x,y)

and

(8.14)

1-(x-y)\frac{r_{01}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\ll\varrho(x,y).

Proof.

By (
8.3
), inequality (
8.13
) will be proved once we prove that

(8.15)

1-r^{2}(x,y)-(y-x)r_{10}(x,y)\ll\varrho^{3}(x,y),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}.

Fix
x\in U_{n}
and let
\varphi(y)=1-r^{2}(x,y)-(y-x)r_{10}(x,y)
, viewed as a function of
y\in U_{n}
. By the mean value theorem, there is some
z
between
x
and
y
,

\varphi(y)=\varphi(x)+(y-x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{(y-x)^{3}}{6}\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z).

Direct computation shows

\varphi(x)=0,\quad\varphi^{\prime}(x)=0,\quad\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=0,

and

\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)=-6r_{01}(x,z)r_{02}(x,z)-2r(x,z)r_{03}(x,z)-3r_{12}(x,z)-(z-x)r_{13}(x,z).

Note that if
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
, then
x
and
y
have the same sign. Thus, we can apply Lemma
8.1
to deduce that for each
0\leq i\leq 4
,

k^{(i)}(x^{2})\asymp k^{(i)}\left(xz\right)\asymp k^{(i)}(z^{2})\asymp k^{(i)}(y^{2})\asymp k^{(i)}(xy)

for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
and every
z
between
x
and
y
. But then

\displaystyle r_{01}(x,z)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{1-xy},

\displaystyle r_{02}(x,z)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(1-xy)^{2}},

\displaystyle r_{03}(x,z)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\left(1-xy\right)^{3}},

\displaystyle r_{12}(x,z)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\left(1-xy\right)^{3}},

and

r_{13}(x,z)\ll\frac{1}{\left(1-xy\right)^{4}}.

Therefore,

\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)\ll\frac{1}{\left(1-xy\right)^{3}}+\frac{|z-x|}{\left(1-xy\right)^{4}}\ll\frac{1}{\left(1-xy\right)^{3}}.

Summarizing, we have

\varphi(y)=\frac{(y-x)^{3}}{6}\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)\ll\frac{|y-x|^{3}}{\left(1-xy\right)^{3}}=\varrho^{3}(x,y),

proving (
8.15
).

The inequality (
8.14
) can be proved in much the same way.
∎

8.5.
Estimates for the mean functions

Recall that
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are defined in (
7.2
) and (
7.4
), respectively.

Lemma 8.9
.

It holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

(8.16)

\mu_{1}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg)

and

(8.17)

\mu_{2}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg).

Proof.

Assume first that
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
. Write

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=m^{\prime}(x)+\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(x)-\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(y)

\displaystyle=\left[1-\frac{(y-x)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\right]m^{\prime}(x)-\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1+r(x,y)}m(x)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}[m(y)-m(x)-(y-x)m^{\prime}(x)].

Using (
8.13
) and (
8.1
), we have

\bigg(1-\frac{(y-x)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\bigg)m^{\prime}(x)\ll\varrho(x,y)\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{3/2}}.

By (
8.11
) and (
8.1
),

\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1+r(x,y)}m(x)\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}.

By the mean value theorem, (
8.1
), and Lemma
8.1
,

\displaystyle m(y)-m(x)-(y-x)m^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2}m^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{5/2}}

\displaystyle\ll\varrho^{2}(x,y)\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg)

for some
z
between
x
and
y
. Combining (
8.3
) and (
8.11
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\left[m(y)-m(x)-(y-x)m^{\prime}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-x^{2}}}{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}\varrho^{2}(x,y)\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg).

Therefore, we obtain (
8.16
) for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
.

If
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}\backslash\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
, then
\varrho(x,y)\asymp 1
and hence

\frac{1}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\ll\frac{1}{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}\ll 1.

Combining with (
8.1
) and (
8.11
), we find that

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\ll|m^{\prime}(x)|+|\frac{r(x,y)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(x)|+|\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}m(y)|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg),

which gives (
8.16
).

Similar arguments apply to (
8.17
).
∎

The subsequent lemma demonstrates that refining the bounds for the normalized mean
m(x)
and its derivatives leads to enhanced estimates for
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.

Lemma 8.10
.

Fix constants
C,\theta>0
, and let
\phi(x)=Cx^{\theta}
for
x\in[0,1]
. Assume that uniformly for
x\in U_{n}
,

(8.18)

|m^{(i)}(x)|\ll\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{(1-|x|)^{i}},\quad i=0,1,2.

Then it holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

(8.19)

\mu_{1}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)]

and

(8.20)

\mu_{2}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)].

Proof.

Assume first that
(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
. By Lemma
8.8
and (
8.18
),

\displaystyle\bigg(1-\frac{(y-x)r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\bigg)m^{\prime}(x)\ll\varrho(x,y)\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}

and

\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1+r(x,y)}m(x)\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\phi(1-|x|).

Using the mean value theorem, (
8.18
), Lemma
8.1
, and the monotonicity of
\phi
, we obtain, for some
z
between
x
and
y
,

\displaystyle m(y)-m(x)-(y-x)m^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2}\frac{\phi(1-|z|)}{(1-|z|)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{(1-xy)^{2}}\left[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)\right]

\displaystyle=\varrho^{2}(x,y)\left[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)\right].

Together with (
8.11
) and (
8.3
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{r_{10}(x,y)}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}[m(y)-m(x)-(y-x)m^{\prime}(x)]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)].

Combining the above estimates yields

\mu_{1}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)],\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}.

We now consider
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}\backslash\mathbb{U}_{\varepsilon}
. Then

\varrho(x,y)\asymp 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\ll\frac{1}{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}\ll 1.

Together with Lemma
8.7
and (
8.18
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\ll|m^{\prime}(x)|+|r_{10}(x,y)||m(x)|+|r_{10}(x,y)||m(y)|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}+\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\phi(1-|x|)+\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\phi(1-|y|)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)],

and (
8.19
) is proved.

Similar arguments apply to (
8.20
).
∎

In this section, we prove the Gaussian case of Theorems

9.1.
Expected number of real roots

We first establish the Gaussian results for the expected number of real roots, which form the foundation for the proofs of Theorem
4.2
and Lemma
5.2
in Section
6
and Appendix
A
, respectively.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
I\subset[1-a_{n},1)
.

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, if
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
on
I
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
, then

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I)]=O(a_{n}).

(2)

Fix
C>0
. If
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
up to order 1 on
I
with a constant factor
C
, then the one-point correlation function
\rho_{1}
of the real roots of
P_{n}
satisfies

(9.1)

\rho_{1}(x)\ll\frac{1}{1-x+1/n}\quad\text{uniformly on }I.

In particular, for any constant
B>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left([1-a_{n}/n,1]\cap I\right)]=O(a_{n}),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left([1-B/n,1-a_{n}/n)\cap I\right)]=O(1).

Analogous bounds hold on
(-1,-1+a_{n}]
and for the reciprocal polynomial
P_{n}^{*}
.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
7.3
that

\rho_{1}(x)=\rho_{1,1}(x)+\rho_{1,2}(x),

where
\rho_{1,1}
and
\rho_{1,2}
are defined in (
7.9
) and (
7.10
), respectively.

Assume first that
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
on
I
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
. It follows from (
7.9
) and (
8.7
) that uniformly for
x\in I
,

\rho_{1,1}(x)\leq\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\right)\ll(1-x+1/n)^{-1+C^{2}/2}.

Next, using (
7.10
) and (
8.1
), we see that uniformly for
x\in I
,

\rho_{1,2}(x)\leq|m^{\prime}(x)|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\right)\ll(1-x+1/n)^{(-3+C^{2})/2}.

Therefore, for
C>\sqrt{3}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I)]=\int_{I}\rho_{1}(x)dx\ll\int_{1-a_{n}}^{1}(1-x+1/n)^{(-3+C^{2})/2}dx\ll a_{n}.

Assume now that
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
up to order 1 on
I
with a constant factor
C
, so that

|m(x)|\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad|m^{\prime}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{1-x+1/n},\quad x\in I.

Then, uniformly for
x\in I
,

\rho_{1}(x)=\rho_{1,1}(x)+\rho_{1,2}(x)\leq\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}+|m^{\prime}(x)|\ll\frac{1}{1-x+1/n},

which implies (
9.1
).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left([1-a_{n}/n,1]\cap I\right)]

\displaystyle\ll\int_{1-\frac{a_{n}}{n}}^{1}\frac{dx}{1-x+1/n}=O(a_{n}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}\left([1-B/n,1-a_{n}/n)\cap I\right)]

\displaystyle\ll\int_{1-\frac{B}{n}}^{1-\frac{a_{n}}{n}}\frac{dx}{1-x+1/n}=O(1),

completing the proof.
∎

By Theorem
4.2
, we only need to focus on the real roots of
P_{n}
inside
\mathcal{I}_{n}=U_{n}\cup U_{n}^{*}
, where
U_{n}=I_{n}\cup\left(-I_{n}\right)
and
U_{n}^{*}=I_{n}^{-1}\cup\left(-I_{n}^{-1}\right)
.

9.2.
Fluctuations of real roots in the core region, part (1)

We now discuss the first part of Theorem
1.10
. We show that when
M_{n}
dominates
R_{n}
, the polynomial
P_{n}
has few real roots.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval. There exists a constant
C>0
such that if

(9.2)

|m(x)|\geq C|\log(1-|x|)|^{1/2},\quad x\in I\cap U_{n},

then

(9.3)

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=o(1).

Proof.

By Lemma
7.4
, we have

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}[\rho_{2}(x,y)-\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)]dxdy+\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\rho_{1}(x)dx,

where
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are the one-point and two-point correlation functions defined in (
7.8
) and (
7.7
), respectively.

We first show that

\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\rho_{1}(x)dx=o(1),

and hence,

\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)dxdy=o(1).

Recall that
\rho_{1}(x)=\rho_{1,1}(x)+\rho_{1,2}(x)
, where
\rho_{1,1}
and
\rho_{1,2}
are given in (
7.9
) and (
7.10
), respectively. Together with (
8.1
), (
8.6
), and (
9.2
), we see that

\displaystyle|\rho_{1,1}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\Big)\ll\frac{1}{1-|x|}(1-|x|)^{C^{2}/2},

and

\displaystyle|\rho_{1,2}(x)|\ll|m^{\prime}(x)|\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\Big)\ll\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{3/2}}(1-|x|)^{C^{2}/2}.

Thus, for
C>1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\rho_{1}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{I\cap U_{n}}[\rho_{1,1}(x)+\rho_{1,2}(x)]dx\ll\int_{I\cap U_{n}}(1-|x|)^{\frac{C^{2}-3}{2}}dx=o(1).

The proof is completed by showing that

\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\rho_{2}(x,y)dxdy=o(1).

It follows from Theorem
7.2
that

\rho_{2}(x,y)=\frac{E(x,y)}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\rho_{2,i}(x,y).

To estimate
E(x,y)
, we utilize the assumption (
9.2
) and find that

\displaystyle E(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{|r(x,y)|(m(x)-m(y))^{2}}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}-\frac{m^{2}(x)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1+|r(x,y)|)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}(x)+m^{2}(y)}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-|x|)^{C^{2}/4}(1-|y|)^{C^{2}/4}.

We now estimate
\rho_{2,i}
, for
1\leq i\leq 5
. First, by Lemma
8.6
,

|\rho_{2,1}|=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}+\delta\arcsin\delta)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}.

According to Lemma
8.9
, we have

|\rho_{2,2}|=|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\arcsin\delta|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}|\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}\bigg).

For
j=1,2
, it holds that

0\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/2(1-\delta^{2}))}dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}dt=1,

and

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)|\nu_{j}t|}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/2)}\erf\bigg(\frac{|\delta\nu_{j}t|}{\sqrt{2(1-\delta^{2})}}\bigg)dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\nu_{j}|}{\exp(\nu_{j}^{2}t^{2}/2)}dt\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.

Moreover, it follows from (
8.9
) and (
8.16
) that

\nu_{1}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\ll\frac{\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\Big)^{2}}{\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)^{2}}}\ll\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}.

Similarly,

\nu_{2}^{2}\ll\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}.

Therefore, using the fact that
|\delta|\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|\rho_{2,3}|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}\left(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}|\delta|\left(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}[\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}\bigg).

Likewise,

\displaystyle|\rho_{2,4}|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}|\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}\bigg).

Note that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\nu_{1}\nu_{2}|}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(1-s)\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}-2\delta\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\nu_{1}\nu_{2}|}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{1}^{2}t^{2}-2\delta\nu_{1}\nu_{2}ts+\nu_{2}^{2}s^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right)dtds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{2}+\arcsin\delta\leq\pi.

Hence, by (
8.19
) and (
8.20
),

\displaystyle|\rho_{2,5}|\leq\pi|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}|\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}\bigg).

Combining these, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{E(x,y)}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{5}\rho_{2,i}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(1-|x|)^{C^{2}/4}(1-|y|)^{C^{2}/4}}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-|x|}+\frac{1}{1-|y|}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1-|x|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-2}(1-|y|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-1}+(1-|x|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-1}(1-|y|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-2}.

Thus, for
C\geq 2
, we have

\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\rho_{2}(x,y)dxdy\ll\int_{I\cap U_{n}}(1-|x|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-2}dx\int_{I\cap U_{n}}(1-|y|)^{\frac{C^{2}}{4}-1}dy=o(1),

and the theorem follows.
∎

9.3.
Fluctuations of real roots in the core region, part (2)

We now turn to the second part of Theorem
1.10
as well as the central limit theorem stated in Theorem
1.12
. We show that when
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
through an appropriate factor function
\phi
, the number of real roots of
P_{n}
exhibits the same asymptotic behavior as that of its centered counterpart
R_{n}
.

Theorem 9.3
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
J
be an enlargement of
I
. Fix constants
C,\theta>0
, and let
\phi(x)=Cx^{\theta}
for
x\in[0,1]
. Assume that uniformly for
x\in J
,

(9.4)

|m^{(i)}(x)|\ll\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{(1-|x|)^{i}},\quad i=0,1,2.

Then

(9.5)

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]+o(1).

Moreover, if
\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]\geq\epsilon\log n
for some constant
\epsilon>0
, then
N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})
satisfies the CLT; that is,

(9.6)

\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

To prove (
9.5
), we compare the one-point and two-point correlation functions of the real roots of
P_{n}
and
R_{n}
. For this purpose, we first recall the corresponding correlation functions for
R_{n}
, as established in
[
19
]
.

Observe that

\Cov[R_{n}(x),R_{n}(y)]=\mathbb{E}[R_{n}(x)R_{n}(y)]=\frac{k(xy)}{\sqrt{k(x^{2})k(y^{2})}}=r(x,y).

Therefore, by letting

(9.7)

\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}

and

(9.8)

\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)=\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\left(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}+\delta\arcsin\delta\right),

we have

\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)dx

and

\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}[\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(y)]dxdy+\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)dx.

Note that we can derive the formulas for
\widetilde{\rho}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\rho}_{2}
from Lemma
7.3
and Theorem
7.2
, respectively, by setting
m\equiv 0
.

To prove Theorem
9.3
, we begin with three auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 9.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9.3
, it holds uniformly for
x\in J
that

(9.9)

|\rho_{1}(x)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]+o(1).

Lemma 9.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9.3
, it holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in J\times J
that

(9.10)

|\rho_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}.

Consequently,

(9.11)

\mathbb{\iint}_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\rho_{2}(x,y)dxdy=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)dxdy+o(1).

Lemma 9.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9.3
, as
n\to\infty
, we have

(9.12)

\frac{N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})}{\sqrt{\log n}}\xrightarrow{d}0.

Granting these lemmas, we now proceed to the proof of Theorem
9.3
.

Proof of Theorem
9.3
.

We first prove (
9.5
). By Lemma
9.4
and the definition of
\phi
,

\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\rho_{1}(x)dx=\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)dx+o(1)

and

\displaystyle\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)dxdy=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(y)dxdy+o(1).

Combining with (
9.11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]

\displaystyle=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}[\rho_{2}(x,y)-\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)]dxdy+\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\rho_{1}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}[\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(y)]dxdy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{I\cap U_{n}}\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)dx+o(1)

\displaystyle=\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]+o(1),

proving the claim.

To establish (
9.6
), we recall that in
[
60
]
*§6, Nguyen and Vu proved that if
\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]\geq\epsilon\log n
for some
\epsilon>0
, then
N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})
satisfies the CLT; that is,

\frac{N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Assuming Lemmas
9.4
and
9.5
, we have

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]+o(1)\asymp\log n,

and

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]=\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]+o(1)\gg\log n.

Therefore, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]}}(1+o(1))+o(1)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Thus, (
9.6
) follows from Lemma
9.6
and Slutsky’s theorem.
∎

Our next task is to prove Lemmas
9.4
,
9.5
, and
9.6
.

Proof of Lemma
9.4
.

Note that

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\right)\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad|\erf\bigg(\frac{m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}\bigg)|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{|m^{\prime}(x)|}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}.

So, by (
8.6
) and (
9.4
),

\displaystyle|\rho_{1,2}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}|m^{\prime}(x)|\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(x)\bigg)|\erf\bigg(\frac{m^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{2r_{11}(x,x)}}\bigg)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}|m^{\prime}(x)|\frac{|m^{\prime}(x)|}{\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}.

Now, using
1-x\leq e^{-x}\leq 1
for
x\geq 0
, (
8.6
), and (
9.4
), we find that

\displaystyle|\rho_{1,1}(x)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg|1-\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[m^{2}(x)+\frac{\left(m^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}}{r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg]\bigg)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg(m^{2}(x)+\frac{\left(m^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}}{r_{11}(x,x)}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}.

Therefore,

|\rho_{1}(x)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)|\leq|\rho_{1,1}(x)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)|+|\rho_{1,2}(x)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)}{1-|x|},

which proves (
9.9
).

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]-\mathbb{E}[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I\cap U_{n}}|\rho_{1}(x)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(x)|dx=o(1).

proving the assertion.
∎

To prove Lemma
9.5
, we first need to estimate
E(x,y)
.

Lemma 9.7
.

We have

(9.13)

|E(x,y)-1|\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|),\quad(x,y)\in J\times J.

Proof.

We first assume that
\varrho(x,y)\leq 1/3
. Using
1\geq e^{-x}\geq 1-x
for
x\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle|E(x,y)-1|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}(x)-2r(x,y)m(x)m(y)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}\right)-1\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{2}(x)-2r(x,y)m(x)m(y)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(m(y)-m(x)\right)^{2}}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}+\frac{|m(x)m(y)|}{1+r(x,y)}.

By the mean value theorem, (
9.4
), Lemma
8.1
, and the monotonicity of
\phi
, we see that, for some
z
between
x
and
y
,

\displaystyle|m(y)-m(x)|

\displaystyle=|y-x||m^{\prime}(z)|

\displaystyle\ll|y-x|\frac{\phi(1-|z|)}{1-|z|}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{|y-x|}{|1-xy|}\left[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)\right]

\displaystyle=\varrho(x,y)\left[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)\right].

Combining with (
8.3
), we get

\frac{\left(m(y)-m(x)\right)^{2}}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}\ll\left[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)\right]^{2}\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|).

Since
1+r(x,y)\asymp 1
, it follows from (
9.4
) that

\frac{|m(x)m(y)|}{1+r(x,y)}\ll\phi(1-|x|)\phi(1-|y|)\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|),

and hence that

\displaystyle|E(x,y)-1|\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|).

Let us now consider
\varrho(x,y)>1/3
. Then

\varrho(x,y)\asymp 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{1-r^{2}(x,y)}\ll\frac{1}{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}\asymp 1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|E(x,y)-1|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{2}(x)-2r(x,y)m(x)m(y)+m^{2}(y)}{2(1-r^{2}(x,y))}

\displaystyle\ll m^{2}(x)+m^{2}(y)

\displaystyle\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|),

proving (
9.13
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
9.5
.

We first prove (
9.10
). For this purpose, we need to estimate
|\rho_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)|
, where

\rho_{2}(x,y)=\frac{E(x,y)}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\rho_{2,i}(x,y)

and

\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)=\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\left(\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}+\delta\arcsin\delta\right).

According to (
8.3
) and Lemma
8.6
, we have

\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{2\pi\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}.

Together with Lemma
9.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{E(x,y)}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\rho_{2,1}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)|E(x,y)-1|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}.

To establish (
9.10
), it remains to show that

(9.14)

\frac{|E(x,y)|}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\sum_{i=2}^{5}|\rho_{2,i}(x,y)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}.

It follows from the proof of Theorem
9.2
that

\sum_{i=2}^{5}|\rho_{2,i}|\ll|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}|+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\left(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}\right).

On account of Lemma
8.6
and Lemma
8.10
, we obtain

\nu_{j}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\ll\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|),\quad j=1,2,

and hence

|\mu_{1}\mu_{2}|+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\left(\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}\right)\ll\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\left[\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)\right].

Because
|E(x,y)|\leq 1
and
1-r^{2}(x,y)\asymp\varrho^{2}(x,y)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{|E(x,y)|}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-r^{2}(x,y)}}\sum_{i=2}^{5}|\rho_{2,i}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\frac{\varrho^{2}(x,y)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}\left[\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)\right]}{\varrho(x,y)}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)},

and (
9.14
) is verified.

It follows from (
9.10
) that

\displaystyle\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}|\rho_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)|dxdy

\displaystyle\ll\iint_{(I\cap U_{n})^{2}}\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|y|)}dxdy

\displaystyle=o(1),

which proves (
9.11
).
∎

We now turn to the proof of Lemma
9.6
.

Proof of Lemma
9.6
.

We first outline the proof. The main strategy is to compare the statistics of
N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})
with those of
N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})
, and then invoke Markov’s inequality. To this end, we partition
I\cap U_{n}
into
O(\log n)
subintervals and show that, on each subinterval, the number of real roots is well approximated by the number of sign changes of the polynomial. We further control the discrepancy between the sign changes of
P_{n}
and
R_{n}
in terms of the normalized mean
m
, which is small under the assumption that
M_{n}
is dominated by
R_{n}
.

Before proceeding, we introduce the necessary notation.

Let
\delta_{j}=a_{n}/2^{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,T
, where
T
is the smallest positive integer such that
a_{n}/2^{T}\leq B/n
. Then,
\delta_{j}\geq B/n
for
j=1,...,T
, and
T\ll\log n
.

For
j=1,...,T
, let
I_{1j}=[1-2\delta_{j},1-\delta_{j})
and
I_{2j}=-I_{1j}
. Let
N_{ij}
and
R_{ij}
represent the number of real roots of
P_{n}
and
R_{n}
, respectively, in the intersection
I\cap I_{ij}
, where
i=1,2
and
j=1,\dots,T
. Since the family
\{I_{ij}:i=1,2,j=1,...,T\}
is pairwise disjoint and covers
U_{n}
, we have

N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}N_{ij}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}R_{ij}.

Let
a_{ij}
and
b_{ij}
denote the endpoints of the interval
I\cap I_{ij}
whenever it is nonempty. If
P_{n}
has at most one root in
I\cap I_{ij}
and does not vanish at
a_{ij}
and
b_{ij}
, then
N_{ij}=1
provided that
P_{n}(a_{ij})
and
P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)
have different signs, and
N_{ij}=0
otherwise. Thus, on
I\cap I_{ij}
, it is reasonable to approximate
N_{ij}
by the number of sign changes
N_{ij}^{\dagger}
, defined as follows. Let

\displaystyle N_{ij}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sgn\left(P_{n}(a_{ij})P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)\right)&\text{if }I\cap I_{ij}\neq\emptyset,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sgn\left(R_{n}(a_{ij})R_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)\right)&\text{if }I\cap I_{ij}\neq\emptyset,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

N^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}N_{ij}^{\dagger},\quad\text{}\quad R^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}R_{ij}^{\dagger},

where

\sgn(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad x>0,\\
0&\text{if}\quad x=0,\\
-1&\text{if}\quad x<0.\end{cases}

We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1

(Approximating the number of real roots by the number of sign changes)
.

The main result in this step is the following lemma.

Lemma 9.8
.

If
|m^{\prime\prime}(x)|\ll(1-|x|)^{-2}
uniformly on
J
, then there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
c
such that

\mathbb{E}[|N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N^{\dagger}|]\leq C_{1}a_{n}^{c}.

Consequently, by letting
m\equiv 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}[|N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-R^{\dagger}|]\leq C_{1}a_{n}^{c}.

Proof.

We first show that there exists a positive constant
\varepsilon
such that for
i=1,2
and
j=1,\dots,T
,

(9.15)

\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}-N_{ij}^{\dagger}|]\ll\delta_{j}^{\varepsilon}.

If
I\cap I_{ij}=\emptyset
, then
N_{ij}=N_{ij}^{\dagger}=0
, hence (
9.15
) is trivial. Otherwise, since
N_{ij}^{\dagger}\in\{0,1/2,1\}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}-N_{ij}^{\dagger}|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}-N_{ij}^{\dagger}|\boldsymbol{1}_{E_{ij}^{c}}]+\mathbb{E}[N_{ij}\boldsymbol{1}_{E_{ij}}],

where
E_{ij}:=\{N_{ij}\geq 2\}
and
E_{ij}^{c}=\{N_{ij}\leq 1\}
. Since
P_{n}(x)
is a Gaussian random variable for any
x
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(P_{n}(a_{ij})P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)=0\right)=0.

If
P_{n}(a_{ij})P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)\neq 0
and
N_{ij}\leq 1
, then
N_{ij}=N_{ij}^{\dagger}
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}-N_{ij}^{\dagger}|\boldsymbol{1}_{E_{ij}^{c}}]=0.

Since
E_{ij}\subset\{N_{P_{n}}(J\cap I_{ij})\geq 2\}
, Lemma
2.4
yields the estimate

\mathbb{P}(E_{ij})\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{P_{n}}(J\cap I_{ij})\geq 2)\leq C_{1}^{\prime}\delta_{j}^{1.5\varepsilon}

for some constants
C_{1}^{\prime}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Combining this with Theorem
2.5
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}[N_{ij}\boldsymbol{1}_{E_{ij}}]\leq C_{1}\left(\delta_{j}^{M}+|\log\delta_{j}|^{C_{2}}\delta_{j}^{1.5\varepsilon}\right)

for any
M>0
and some
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. By choosing
M>\varepsilon
, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}[N_{ij}\boldsymbol{1}_{E_{ij}}]\ll\delta_{j}^{\varepsilon}.

Bringing these estimates together yields (
9.15
).

Finally, using the triangle inequality and (
9.15
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N^{\dagger}|]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}-N_{ij}^{\dagger}|]\ll Ta_{n}^{\varepsilon}\ll a_{n}^{c},

for some
c\in\left(0,\varepsilon\right)
. This completes the proof of Lemma
9.8
.
∎

Step 2

(Approximating the sign changes of
P_{n}
with those of
R_{n}
)
.

This step aims to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 9.9
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9.3
, there exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
c
such that

\mathbb{E}[|N^{\dagger}-R^{\dagger}|]\leq C_{1}a_{n}^{c}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
I\cap I_{ij}\neq\emptyset
for all
j=1,...,T
and
i=1,2
. We begin by proving that for
j=1,...,T
and
i=1,2
,

(9.16)

\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}^{\dagger}-R_{ij}^{\dagger}|]\ll|m(a_{ij})|+|m(b_{ij})|.

Let

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{n}(a_{ij})}{\sqrt{\Var[P_{n}(a_{ij})]}},

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)}{\sqrt{\Var[P_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)]}},

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{n}(a_{ij})}{\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}(a_{ij})]}},

\displaystyle Y_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)}{\sqrt{\Var[R_{n}\left(b_{ij}\right)]}}.

Since
|\sgn(x)|\leq 1
for all
x
and
\sgn(x)
is multiplicative, it follows that

\displaystyle|N_{ij}^{\dagger}-R_{ij}^{\dagger}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\sgn\left(X_{1}X_{2}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}|\sgn\left(X_{1}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}\right)|+\frac{1}{2}|\sgn\left(X_{2}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{2}\right)|.

Note that
X_{1}=Y_{1}+m(a_{ij})
and that
|\sgn\left(X_{1}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}\right)|\in\{0,1,2\}
. If
m(a_{ij})=0
, then
X_{1}=Y_{1}
. Hence, we may assume
m(a_{ij})\neq 0
. In this case,
|\sgn\left(X_{1}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}\right)|>0
only if
X_{1}Y_{1}\leq 0
, and thus this occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}Y_{1}\leq 0)
. Since
Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}Y_{1}\leq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}(-m(a_{ij})\leq Y_{1}\leq 0)+\mathbb{P}(0\leq Y_{1}\leq-m(a_{ij}))\ll|m(a_{ij})|.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[|\sgn\left(X_{1}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}\right)|]\ll|m(a_{ij})|.

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}\left[|\sgn\left(X_{2}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{2}\right)|\right]\ll|m(b_{ij})|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}^{\dagger}-R_{ij}^{\dagger}|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[|\sgn\left(X_{1}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{1}\right)|]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[|\sgn\left(X_{2}\right)-\sgn\left(Y_{2}\right)|]

\displaystyle\ll|m(a_{ij})|+|m(b_{ij})|,

proving (
9.16
).

Finally, by the triangle inequality together with (
9.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|N^{\dagger}-R^{\dagger}|]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|N_{ij}^{\dagger}-R_{ij}^{\dagger}|]\ll\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}(|m(a_{ij})|+|m(b_{ij})|).

It follows from the assumptions that

|m(a_{ij})|\leq\phi(1-|a_{ij}|),\quad\int_{U_{n}}\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}dx\ll a_{n}^{\theta},

and that
\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}
is monotone on
U_{n}
. Writing the sum as a Riemann sum with mesh sizes
\delta_{j}
and using monotonicity to compare with the corresponding integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}|m(a_{ij})|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{|\phi(1-|a_{ij}|)|}{1-|a_{ij}|}\delta_{j}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{U_{n}}\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{1-|x|}dx+O(a_{n})

\displaystyle\ll a_{n}^{\theta}+O(a_{n}).

Similarly,

\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}|m\left(b_{ij}\right)|\ll a_{n}^{\theta}+O(a_{n}),

and the lemma follows.
∎

Step 3

(Proving (
9.12
))
.

By the triangle inequality, Lemma
9.8
, and Lemma
9.9
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})|]

\displaystyle\ll\mathbb{E}[|N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N^{\dagger}|]+\mathbb{E}[|N^{\dagger}-R^{\dagger}|]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[|R^{\dagger}-N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})|]

\displaystyle\ll a_{n}^{c}.

Using Markov’s inequality, we see that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})-N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})|}{\sqrt{\log n}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\ll\frac{a_{n}^{c}}{\varepsilon\sqrt{\log n}}\to 0,

which implies (
9.12
).

This completes the proof of Lemma
9.6
.
∎

9.4.
Fluctuations of real roots via reciprocal polynomials

This subsection is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.10
and
1.12
for
I\backslash[-1,1]
. In particular, we study the real roots of
P_{n}
in the core regions outside the interval
[-1,1]
.

To study the real roots of
P_{n}
in
U_{n}^{*}
, we will work with the reciprocal polynomial

P_{n}^{*}(x):=x^{n}P_{n}(1/x),

which transforms roots of
P_{n}
in
\left(-\infty,-1\right)\cup\left(1,\infty\right)
into roots of
P_{n}^{*}
in
\left(-1,1\right)
.

We begin with asymptotic estimates for the mean and the variance of
P_{n}^{*}
. Note that

P_{n}^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(m_{n-j}+v_{n-j}\xi_{n-j}\right)x^{j}=M_{n}^{*}(x)+R_{n}^{*}(x).

Let
k^{*}(x)
denote the corresponding variance function of
R_{n}^{*}
,

k^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}v_{n-j}^{2}x^{j}=x^{n}k\left(1/x\right).

By conditions
(A2)
and
(A3)
,

\frac{v_{n-j}^{2}}{n^{2\tau}}\asymp 1+O(j/n),\quad j\geq 0.

Thus, it holds uniformly for
x\in U_{n}
,

\displaystyle k^{*}(x)=n^{2\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{v_{n-j}^{2}}{n^{2\tau}}x^{j}

\displaystyle\asymp n^{2\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\big(1+O(j/n)\big)x^{j}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{n^{2\tau}}{1-x}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{n(1-x)}\bigg)\bigg).

Since
n(1-x)\geq B
, it follows that

(9.17)

k^{*}(x)\asymp\frac{n^{2\tau}}{1-x},\quad x\in U_{n}.

Similarly, for
1\leq i\leq 4
, it holds that

(9.18)

{k^{*}}^{(i)}(x)\asymp\frac{n^{2\tau}}{(1-x)^{i+1}},\quad x\in U_{n}.

By
(A2)
, we also have

{M_{n}^{*}}^{(i)}(x)\ll\frac{n^{\tau}}{(1-x)^{i+1}},\quad x\in U_{n}.

Then

m^{*}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[P_{n}^{*}(x)]}{\sqrt{\Var[P_{n}^{*}(x)]}}=\frac{M_{n}^{*}(x)}{\sqrt{k^{*}(x^{2})}},

and hence, for
0\leq i\leq 2
,

{m^{*}}^{(i)}(x)\ll\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{i+\frac{1}{2}}},\quad x\in U_{n}.

We will denote by
r^{*}
,
\sigma_{1}^{*}
,
\sigma_{2}^{*}
,
\mu_{1}^{*}
,
\mu_{2}^{*}
, the analogous quantities. We have

r^{*}(x,y)=\frac{k^{*}(xy)}{\sqrt{k^{*}(x^{2})k^{*}(y^{2})}}\asymp\sqrt{\alpha},\quad(x,y)\in S_{n}\times S_{n}.

An argument similar to the previous treatment for
P_{n}
(see Section
8.4
), specialized to the case
\tau=0
, shows that for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
,

\displaystyle 1-\left(r^{*}(x,y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp\varrho^{2}(x,y),

\displaystyle r_{11}^{*}(x,x)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{(1-|x|)^{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}.

The same conclusion can be drawn for
\mu_{1}^{*}
and
\mu_{2}^{*}
. That is, it holds uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
that

\mu_{1}^{*}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg)

and

\mu_{2}^{*}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|x|}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|y|}}\bigg).

Assume, in addition, that uniformly for
x\in U_{n}
,

|{m^{*}}^{\left(j\right)}(x)|\ll\frac{\phi(1-|x|)}{(1-|x|)^{j}},\quad i=0,1,2.

Then, uniformly for
(x,y)\in U_{n}\times U_{n}
,

\mu_{1}^{*}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|x|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)],

and

\mu_{2}^{*}\ll\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{1-|y|}[\phi(1-|x|)+\phi(1-|y|)].

Observing that
N_{P_{n}^{*}}(U_{n})=N_{P_{n}}(U_{n}^{*})
and
N_{R_{n}^{*}}(U_{n})=N_{R_{n}}(U_{n}^{*})
, we obtain the following theorem. Its proof follows verbatim the arguments used in the proofs of Theorem
9.2
and Theorem
9.3
, and is therefore omitted.

Theorem 9.10
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
J
be an enlargement of
I
.

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that if

(9.19)

|m^{*}\left(1/y\right)|\geq C|\log(1-|1/y|)|^{1/2},\quad y\in I\cap U_{n}^{*},

then

(9.20)

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]=o(1).

(2)

Fix constants
C,\theta>0
, and let
\phi(x)=Cx^{\theta}
for
x\in[0,1]
. Assume that uniformly for
y\in J
,

(9.21)

{m^{*}}^{(i)}\left(1/y\right)\ll\frac{\phi(1-|1/y|)}{(1-|1/y|)^{i}},\quad i=0,1,2.

Then

(9.22)

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]=\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]+o(1).

Furthermore, if
\Var[N_{R_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]\geq\epsilon\log n
for some constant
\epsilon>0
, then
N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})
satisfies the CLT.

9.5.
Completion of the proofs of Theorems
1.10
and
1.12

We conclude the proofs of Theorems
1.10
and
1.12
by studying the contribution of real roots of
P_{n}
lying in the region
\mathcal{I}_{n}=U_{n}\cup U_{n}^{*}
, as formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 9.11
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
J
be an enlargement of
I
.

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, if conditions (
9.2
) and (
9.19
) hold, then

(9.23)

\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap\mathcal{I}_{n})]=o(1).

(2)

Fix constants

, and let

for

. If (

) holds uniformly for

) holds uniformly for

(9.24)

Combining Theorems
9.2
,
9.3
, and
9.10
, we see that to prove Theorem

, it suffices to estimate the correlations between real roots lying inside and outside

. More precisely, we have the following lemmas.

Lemma 9.12
.

If (

) holds, then

(9.25)

|\rho_{1}(y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(y)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|1/y|)}{1-|1/y|},\quad y\in I\cap U_{n}^{*},

where
\rho_{1}
and
\widetilde{\rho_{1}}
are given in (
7.8
) and (

), respectively.

Lemma 9.13
.

Assume that (

) and (

) hold. Then it holds uniformly for

that

(9.26)

|\rho_{2}(x,y)-\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)|\ll\frac{\phi^{2}(1-|x|)+\phi^{2}(1-|1/y|)}{(1-|x|)(1-|1/y|)},

where
\rho_{2}
and

are given in (

) and (

), respectively.
Consequently,

(9.27)

\int_{I\cap U_{n}}dx\int_{I\cap U_{n}^{*}}\rho_{2}(x,y)dy=\int_{I\cap U_{n}}dx\int_{I\cap U_{n}^{*}}\widetilde{\rho}_{2}(x,y)dy+o(1).

Assuming Lemmas
9.12
and
9.13
hold, we now proceed to prove Theorem
9.11
.

Proof of Theorem
9.11
.

Since

\Cov[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}),N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]\leq\sqrt{\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n})]\Var[N_{P_{n}}(I\cap U_{n}^{*})]},

the asymptotic estimate (

In this section, we employ Theorems

,

to establish Theorems

,
1.2
, and

.

To begin, we recall a result from
[
19
]
concerning the variance of the number of real roots of centered random polynomials whose coefficients have polynomial asymptotics.

Proposition 10.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
R_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}v_{j}x^{j}
, where
(\xi_{j})_{j=0}^{n}
satisfy
(A1)
and
|v_{j}|=C_{0}j^{\tau}\left(1+o_{j}\left(1\right)\right)
for some positive constant
C_{0}
. Let
\kappa_{\tau}
be defined by (
1.2
). Then, for
I=(0,\infty)
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Var[N_{R_{n}}(\pm I)]=\left[\kappa_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n,

and

\Var[N_{R_{n}}]=\left[2\kappa_{\tau}+\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n.

Since the Kac polynomials belong to the class of hyperbolic polynomials, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
follow directly from Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

It suffices to consider the case
\ell=0
, as the remaining cases are analogous.

Let
P_{n,L}(x)
be given by (
1.3
), and decompose

M_{n,L}(x):=\mathbb{E}[P_{n,L}(x)]\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n,L}(x):=P_{n,L}(x)-M_{n,L}(x).

For each
i=0,1,2
, we have

M_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)\sim\mu\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\frac{L\left(L+1\right)\cdots\left(L+j-1\right)}{j!}}j^{i}x^{j},

and

\Var[R_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)]\sim\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{L\left(L+1\right)\cdots\left(L+j-1\right)}{j!}j^{2i}x^{2j}.

Since

\frac{L\left(L+1\right)\cdots\left(L+j-1\right)}{j!}\asymp j^{L-1},

it follows that, for each
i=0,1,2
,

M_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{i+\left(L+1\right)/2}},\quad x\in[-1,1],

and

\Var[R_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)]\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x^{2}+1/n)^{L+2i}},\quad x\in[-1,1].

To treat the region
\mathbb{R}\backslash[-1,1]
, consider the normalized reciprocal polynomial

P_{n,L}^{*}(x):=\frac{x^{n}}{v_{n,L}}P_{n,L}\left(1/x\right),

where

v_{n,L}=\sqrt{\frac{L\left(L+1\right)\cdots\left(L+n-1\right)}{n!}}.

We also define

M_{n,L}^{*}(x):=\mathbb{E}[P_{n,L}^{*}(x)]\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n,L}^{*}(x):=P_{n,L}^{*}(x)-M_{n,L}^{*}(x).

Proceeding as above (noting that the reciprocal ensemble behaves like the case
L=1
), we obtain for
i=0,1,2
,

{M_{n,L}^{*}}^{(i)}(x)\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{i+1}},\quad x\in[-1,1],

and

\Var[{R_{n,L}^{*}}^{(i)}(x)]\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x^{2}+1/n)^{2i+1}},\quad x\in[-1,1].

Let
I=[0,\infty)
. Then for
i=0,1,2
,

M_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)\asymp\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{1/2}}\sqrt{\Var[R_{n,L}(x)]},\quad x\in I\cap[0,1],

and

{M_{n,L}^{*}}^{(i)}(1/y)\asymp\frac{1}{(1-1/y+1/n)^{1/2}}\sqrt{\Var[{R_{n,L}^{*}}(1/y)]},\quad y\in I\backslash[0,1].

Moreover,

\frac{1}{(1-x+1/n)^{1/2}}\geq C\sqrt{|\log(1-x+1/n)|},\quad x\in[0,1],

for any fixed constant
C>0
and all sufficiently large
n
. Hence,
M_{n,L}
dominates
R_{n,L}
on
I
with factor function
C|\log x|^{1/2}
. Applying Theorem
1.10
with
d=1/2
, we obtain

\Var[N_{n,0}(I)]=O(\sqrt{\log n})=o(\log n).

Next, for each
i=0,1,2
,

M_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)\ll(1-|x|+1/n)^{L/2}\sqrt{\Var[R_{n,L}^{(i)}(x)]},\quad x\in(-I)\cap[-1,0],

and

{M_{n,L}^{*}}^{(i)}(1/y)\ll(1-|1/y|+1/n)^{1/2}\sqrt{\Var[{R_{n,L}^{*}}^{(i)}(1/y)]},\quad y\in(-I)\backslash[-1,0].

Thus,
M_{n,L}
is dominated by
R_{n,L}
on
-I
up to order
2
with factor function
|x|^{\theta}
, where
\theta=\min\{L/2,1/2\}>0
and
C>0
is a constant depending on
L
. Therefore, Theorem
1.10
together with Proposition
10.1
yields

\Var[N_{n,0}(-I)]=\left[\kappa_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n,

where
\kappa_{\tau}
is defined in (
1.2
) and
\tau=(L-1)/2
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\Var[N_{n,0}]

\displaystyle=\Var[N_{n,0}(-I)]+\Var[N_{n,0}(I)]+2\Cov[N_{n,0}(-I),N_{n,0}(I)]

\displaystyle=\left[\kappa_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n+o(\log n)+o(\log n)

\displaystyle=\left[\kappa_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\right)+o(1)\right]\log n.

To prove the CLT for
N_{n,0}
, we write

\frac{N_{n,0}-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}=\frac{N_{n,0}(-I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(-I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}+\frac{N_{n,0}(I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}.

By Theorem
1.12
,
N_{n,0}(-I)
satisfies the CLT, and hence

\frac{N_{n,0}(-I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(-I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}=\frac{N_{n,0}(-I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(-I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}(-I)]}}(1+o(1))\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

On the other hand, by Markov’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{N_{n,0}(I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}\bigg|\geq\varepsilon\bigg)\leq\frac{\Var[N_{n,0}(I)]}{\varepsilon^{2}\Var[N_{n,0}]}=o(1),

which implies that

\frac{N_{n,0}(I)-\mathbb{E}[N_{n,0}(I)]}{\sqrt{\Var[N_{n,0}]}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

The conclusion follows from Slutsky’s theorem.
∎

T\ll\log n
