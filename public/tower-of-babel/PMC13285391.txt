Diagnostic and therapeutic impact of PCR in uveitis: real-world data from intraocular fluid analysis of 45 uveitis patients in a tertiary referral center in Turkey
Authors: Nilüfer Zorlutuna Kaymak, İpek Pekel, Serap Demir Tekol, Güzide Akçay, Murat Oklar, Burak Tanyıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic utility and real-world clinical impact of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of aqueous humor (AH) and vitreous samples in patients with uveitis including suspected infectious and mixed (infectious/non-infectious) etiologies. Although intraocular PCR is a well-established diagnostic technique, its contribution to diagnostic revision and clinical decision-making in routine practice remains incompletely characterized. This study addresses this gap by assessing the impact of PCR on diagnostic revision, clinical management and regional epidemiological patterns in a tertiary referral center in Turkey.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 45 eyes of 45 patients with uveitis. PCR testing was performed on AH (n = 24) and vitreous samples (n = 21) for Herpes simplex virus (HSV), Varicella zoster virus (VZV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Toxoplasma gondii, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis based on clinical suspicion. Pathogen-specific uniplex real-time PCR assays providing qualitative and quantitative results were used. PCR findings were evaluated in conjunction with clinical presentation to assess their contribution to diagnostic revision, diagnostic certainty, and clinical management.ResultsThe mean patient age was 47.4 ± 17.3 years, and 51.1% of the patients were female; 28.9% were immunosuppressed. Overall PCR positivity was 42.2% (19/45). PCR positivity was higher in immunosuppressed patients than in immunocompetent patients (62.0% vs. 34.4%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.094). PCR positivity was higher in AH samples than in vitreous samples (50.0% vs. 33.3%). PCR analysis led to diagnostic revision in 33.3% (15/45) of patients and influenced clinical management decisions in 80.0% of cases. Positive PCR results were significantly associated with diagnostic certainty (p = 0.006), whereas PCR-negative patients had a markedly higher likelihood of requiring diagnostic and therapeutic modification compared to PCR-positive patients (OR 21.0, 95% CI 2.4–182, p = 0.001).ConclusionPCR analysis of intraocular fluids provides meaningful diagnostic value in the evaluation of uveitis. Both positive and negative results inform clinical decision-making by guiding diagnostic revision and supporting appropriate management strategies. These findings provide region-specific epidemiological data and reinforce intraocular PCR as a key adjunct in complex uveitis cases where clinical features alone are insufficient.Keywords: Uveitis, Polymerase chain reaction, Aqueous humor, Vitreous, Infectious uveitis

Introduction

Uveitis comprises a heterogeneous group of intraocular inflammatory disorders with infectious and non-infectious etiologies and may also occur in association with systemic disease. Globally, uveitis remains a major cause of visual loss, accounting for up to 20.0% of cases of legal blindness [1]. Its prevalence and underlying causes vary according to demographic, geographic, environmental, genetic, and socioeconomic factors [2]. Timely and accurate diagnosis is essential, as delays in appropriate treatment may lead to irreversible structural damage and permanent visual impairment.

A definite diagnosis can be reached in approximately 70.0% of patients through detailed history, comprehensive ocular and systemic examinations, and targeted laboratory testing [3]. However, in the remaining cases, diagnostic challenges frequently arise due to the absence of clinically evident systemic disease, overlapping clinical manifestations among different uveitic entities, phenotypic variability within a single disease spectrum, prior antimicrobial or corticosteroid therapy, and the limited sensitivity of conventional diagnostic tests. Additional limitations may occur when dense vitreous opacities or haze obscure fundus visualization [3, 4].

In such scenarios, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of intraocular fluids including aqueous humor (AH) or vitreous samples can serve as a valuable adjunct by enabling pathogen-specific identification or the reasonable exclusion of infectious etiologies directly at the site of inflammation. This molecular approach supports more precise diagnostic classification and informed clinical decision-making, particularly in complex cases where treatment strategies depend on accurately distinguishing infectious from non-infectious causes.

Polymerase chain reaction-based techniques have been applied to intraocular fluids for several decades to aid in the etiological diagnosis of uveitis. Previous studies have demonstrated that the diagnostic yield of intraocular PCR varies widely depending on patient selection, clinical presentation, immune status, and indications for sampling [5–7]. These variations highlight that PCR utility should be interpreted in the context of clinical suspicion rather than as a standalone diagnostic test or a direct comparison between sampling sites.

The present study primarily aims to evaluate the impact of intraocular PCR analysis on diagnostic revision and real-world clinical management in patients with uveitis with suspected infectious or mixed etiologies. Secondary objectives include assessing diagnostic yield and describing sampling site distribution across clinical subgroups to provide region-specific epidemiological data from a tertiary referral center in Turkey.

Materials and methods

A retrospective review was conducted on 45 adult uveitis patients (≥ 18 years) who were followed up at the Uveitis Department of our clinic and underwent intraocular PCR analysis between March 2017 and April 2025. The study included adult patients presenting with active uveitis who required intraocular sampling due to a strong clinical suspicion of infectious uveitis, an indeterminate clinical presentation, or a mixed infectious/non-infectious differential diagnosis. Intraocular sampling was performed either to confirm suspected infectious etiologies (such as viral retinitis or CMV anterior uveitis) or to reasonably exclude infection in diagnostically challenging cases, including selected patients with idiopathic panuveitis or frosted branch angiitis. Patients younger than 18 years of age were excluded from the study.

Aqueous humor (n = 24) and vitreous (n = 21) samples obtained from 45 eyes with a preliminary diagnosis of suspected infectious or mixed-etiology uveitis were analyzed by PCR for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Toxoplasma gondii, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex when clinically indicated.

Demographic characteristics, immune status, anatomical localization and etiology of uveitis, laterality, sampling site, PCR results, and initial and final diagnosis were recorded. The final diagnosis was not based on PCR results alone. In cases where PCR results were negative but clinical suspicion of infection remained high, PCR negativity alone was not considered sufficient to exclude infection. Final diagnostic decisions were established through an integrated clinical assessment incorporating characteristic clinical findings, multimodal imaging (OCT, FA), serologic testing (e.g., TST/IGRA, VDRL/TPHA), systemic evaluation, and response to targeted therapy. PCR-negative results were interpreted in light of known sensitivity limitations, particularly for paucibacillary infections such as ocular tuberculosis.

PCR results were categorized as “confirmed,” or “changed” relative to the initial diagnosis. A ‘confirmed’ diagnosis was defined as PCR positivity that was concordant with the initial clinical suspicion. A ‘changed’ diagnosis was defined as a revision of the initial clinical diagnosis based on PCR results, including cases where a positive result identified a different pathogen or where a negative result (negative predictive value) led to the exclusion of agents, thereby guiding the clinician toward alternative non-infectious or indeterminate diagnoses.

All patients underwent a complete ophthalmologic examination at each visit, including measurement of the best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), assessment with Snellen charts, slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination before and after dilatation with mydriatics, dilated fundus examination and intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement with applanation tonometry and optical coherence tomography (OCT). Fluorescein angiography (FA), indocyanine green angiography (ICG) and ocular ultrasonography were performed as indicated. Based on the initial clinical diagnosis, laboratory and systemic investigation was carried out for infectious or noninfectious diagnosis. Best corrected visual acuity values were converted to logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR) for statistical analysis. Uveitis was classified according to the SUN Working Group criteria [8]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate the strength of the association between PCR results and diagnostic and therapeutic modification.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Health Science University, Kartal Dr. Lutfi Kırdar City Hospital, Istanbul, Turkey on 29.09.2025 with the approval number 2025/010.99/20/24. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to intraocular fluid sampling and related clinical procedures. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, no additional retrospective consent for study participation was required. Patient confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Intraocular fluid sampling

Intraocular fluid sampling was performed at operating room under sterile conditions. Aqueous humor sampling was performed via an anterior chamber paracentesis with a 30 G needle. At least 0.1 ml of aqueous was extracted for PCR analysis. Vitreous sampling was performed via vitreous tap using a 25 G needle or during pars plana vitrectomy.

Molecular analysis

DNA extraction was performed using the automated EZ1 Advanced XL instrument with the EZ1 Virus Mini Kit v2.0 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), which utilizes magnetic bead-based technology, operating with a standardized sample input volume of 200 µL and an elution volume of 60–120 µL, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Between March 2017 and March 2024, molecular detection of HSV, VZV, and CMV was carried out using Artus® PCR kits (Qiagen, Germany) on the Rotor-Gene Q real-time PCR system. In March 2024, the workflow was further optimized through the implementation of NeuMoDx® 288 molecular system (Qiagen, Germany), which integrates automated nucleic acid extraction and real-time PCR amplification within a single closed platform. Due to the long study period, different molecular platforms were utilized as part of routine laboratory evolution. To ensure consistency, all platforms employed pathogen-specific uniplex real-time PCR assays targeting the same genomic regions. Inter-platform variability was minimized by adhering to standardized Conformité Européenne–In Vitro Diagnostic (CE-IVD) validated protocols and validation through internal quality control procedures. While minor differences in analytical sensitivity may exist between systems, all assays were operated within established clinical diagnostic thresholds.

For Toxoplasma gondii, DNA extraction was performed using the Unio B24 automated extraction system (Unio Diagnostics, Turkey), and amplification was conducted with the Bosphore®Toxoplasma Detection Kit (Anatolia Geneworks, Turkey) based on real-time PCR methodology. For Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, nucleic acid amplification testing was performed using the Cepheid® GeneXpert® MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, USA), which enables simultaneous detection of M. tuberculosis DNA and rifampicin resistance-associated mutations in the rpoB gene region. Complementary analysis of ocular samples was defined according to the clinical suspicion and diagnostic indication. The PCR assays utilized in this study provided both qualitative and quantitative data; specifically, molecular testing for HSV, VZV, and CMV provided quantitative viral load measurements, whereas testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Toxoplasma gondii was performed qualitatively. To ensure diagnostic reliability and prevent cross-contamination, internal controls were included in every run, and all molecular procedures were performed in separate dedicated laboratory areas for reagent preparation, sample extraction, and amplification.

Results

Forty-five eyes of 45 patients were included in the study with a mean age of 47.4 ± 17.3 (21–88) years. Among them 51.1% (n = 23) were female and 48.9% (n = 22) were male. Disease was unilateral in 33 (73.3%) and bilateral in 12 (26.7%) of the patients. Regarding immune status, 32 (71.1%) patients were immunocompetent and 13 (28.9%) were immunosuppressed. Among the immunosuppressed patients, six (46.2%) were Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) positive. Anatomically, uveitis was classified as panuveitis in 25 patients (55.6%), anterior uveitis in 12 (26.7%), posterior uveitis in seven (15.6%), and intermediate uveitis in one patient (2.2%).

The median time from admission to PCR sampling was 5 days (range 1–370 days; interquartile range [IQR] 28 days). Sampling was performed from the AH in 24 (53.3%) patients and from the vitreous in 21 (46.7%) patients. The overall PCR positivity rate was 42.2% (n = 19/45), with a median viral load of 790.50 copies/mL (range 24–1.957.306 copies/mL; IQR 11.492 copies/mL). Among the 19 PCR-positive cases, CMV was the most frequently detected pathogen, accounting for 10 cases (5 in aqueous humor, 5 in vitreous), followed by HSV/VZV in 8 cases (7 in aqueous humor, 1 in vitreous), and Toxoplasma gondii in 1 case (in aqueous humor). No positive results were obtained for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

PCR positivity was observed in 34.4% (11/32) of immunocompetent patients and in 62.0% (8/13) of immunosuppressed patients, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.094). Median viral load was higher in immunosuppressed patients than in immunocompetent patients (790.50 vs. 620.50 copies/mL), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.360).

According to sampling site, PCR positivity was detected in 50.0% (12/24) of AH samples and in 33.3% (7/21) of vitreous samples, with no statistically significant difference observed between sampling sites (p = 0.259). Among the 12 PCR-positive AH samples, 75.0% (9/12) were obtained from immunocompetent patients and 25.0% (3/12) from immunosuppressed patients. Among PCR-positive vitreous samples, 28.6% (2/7) were from immunocompetent patients and 71.4% (5/7) from immunosuppressed patients. The initial diagnoses were as follows: acute retinal necrosis (ARN) (15, 33.3%), CMV anterior uveitis (8, 17.8%), CMV retinitis (6,13.3%), frosted branch angiitis (4, 8.9%), Toxoplasma uveitis (4, 8.9%), viral anterior uveitis including VZV, HSV (3, 6.7%), idiopathic panuveitis (3, 6.7%), Fuchs uveitic syndrome (1, 2.2%) and tuberculosis uveitis (1, 2.2%).

The final diagnoses following PCR analysis were: ARN (9, 20.0%), CMV anterior uveitis (8, 17.8%), CMV retinitis (5, 11.1%), frosted branch angiitis (4, 8.9%), Toxoplasma uveitis (3, 6.7%), syphilitic uveitis (3, 6.7%), idiopathic panuveitis (3, 6.7%), primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL) (2, 4.4%), Fuchs uveitic syndrome (1, 2.2%), tuberculosis uveitis (1, 2.2%), viral anterior uveitis (1, 2.2%), peripheral exudative hemorrhagic chorioretinitis (PEHC) (1, 2.2%), vasoproliferative tumor (VPT) (1, 2.2%), presumed ocular sarcoidosis (1, 2.2%), orbital apex syndrome (1, 2.2%) and subacute endophthalmitis (1, 2.2%).

PCR results changed the clinical diagnosis in 15 (33.3%) of the 45 patients while in 30 (66.7%) patients the initial diagnoses were confirmed. Overall, PCR results contributed to clinical management decisions in 80.0% of cases. Correspondingly, treatment modification was required in 15 cases (33.3%). When stratified by PCR result, diagnostic and therapeutic modification occurred significantly more frequently in PCR-negative patients than in PCR-positive patients (53.8% vs. 5.3%; χ² = 11.66, p = 0.001). PCR-negative patients had a 21-fold higher likelihood of requiring diagnostic and therapeutic adjustment than PCR-positive patients (odds ratio 21.0, 95% CI 2.4–182), underscoring the substantial clinical impact of negative PCR findings in refining management strategies. In three cases initially suspected as acute retinal necrosis due to limited fundus visualization, PCR testing did not confirm viral infection, and subsequent serological evaluation established the diagnosis of syphilitic uveitis.

Considering the 24 AH samples, PCR was positive in 12 (50.0%) and negative in 12 (50.0%) of them. Among PCR-positive AH samples, the diagnosis was confirmed in 11 (91.7%) samples and changed in one (8.3%) whereas in PCR-negative AH samples, the diagnosis was confirmed in six (50.0%) and changed in six (50.0%). For 21 vitreous samples, PCR was positive in seven (33.3%) and negative in 14 (66.7%) cases. Diagnostic consistency was observed in all PCR-positive vitreous samples (7/7, 100%). Among PCR-negative vitreous samples, the diagnosis was confirmed in six (42.9%) and changed in eight (57.1%) them. Discussion

The principal finding of our study is that intraocular PCR testing significantly influenced diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making in patients with uveitis. When stratified by PCR result, patients with negative PCR findings had a markedly higher likelihood of requiring diagnostic revision and treatment modification compared to those with positive results (odds ratio 21.0, 95% CI 2.4–182). This finding underscores that the clinical value of PCR extends beyond pathogen detection alone; negative results, in particular, play a critical role in refining differential diagnosis and guiding subsequent management strategies in diagnostically uncertain cases.

The overall PCR positivity rate in our study was 42.2%, aligning well with previously reported rates ranging from 26.2% to 59.0% in the literature [7, 9–11]. However, most prior studies evaluating the diagnostic utility of PCR results in uveitis have focused predominantly on suspected infectious etiologies [5, 9, 12, 13]. In contrast, our study included a heterogeneous cohort comprising both infectious and non-infectious causes of uveitis, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

Notably, PCR testing was also performed in patients in whom an infectious etiology was not initially suspected. In these cases, the primary objective was not pathogen identification, but rather the exclusion of infectious causes to support non-infectious diagnostic pathways. For example, PCR testing was used to rule out cytomegalovirus infection in patients with frosted branch angiitis and to exclude infectious etiologies in cases of idiopathic panuveitis. In such scenarios, negative PCR results contributed to reinforcing a non-infectious diagnosis and guiding appropriate management. This underscores the dual role of intraocular PCR as both a tool for pathogen detection and for supporting the exclusion of infection in complex uveitis cases.

PCR analysis of intraocular fluids, especially vitreous, is often performed in severe inflammatory conditions with imminent risk of visual loss, in cases undergoing diagnostic vitrectomy, or when intraocular malignancy is suspected [14, 15]. In our study, vitreous sampling during pars plana vitrectomy was undertaken in selected cases of panuveitis. In these patients, PCR analysis aided in narrowing the differential diagnosis.

In one such case, atypical B lymphocytes were identified in the vitreous sample, supporting a presumptive diagnosis of primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL). Although retinal biopsy could not be performed due to the absence of an accessible lesion, the presence of atypical lymphocytes in the vitreous, while not definitive, contributed to the diagnostic evaluation. Nevertheless, the exclusion of infectious etiologies by PCR contributed to the diagnostic evaluation and clinical decision-making process. Importantly, PCR negativity for infectious agents alone is insufficient to establish a diagnosis of malignancy. Definitive diagnosis of PVRL requires a multidisciplinary approach incorporating cytology, immunophenotyping, and molecular clonality studies. In another case of idiopathic panuveitis, negative vitreous PCR results were consistent with our expectation of a non-infectious etiology, further illustrating the adjunctive value of intraocular fluid analysis in selected clinical contexts.

Regarding the sampling site, previous studies have reported AH PCR positivity rates ranging from 19.0% to 78.5% and vitreous PCR positivity rates between 19.0% and 48.0% [5, 10, 16–18]. In our study, although PCR analysis was performed on AH samples in 24 patients and vitreous samples in 21 patients—indicating that nearly half of the intraocular samples were obtained from each site—AH sampling demonstrated a higher PCR positivity rate (50.0%) than vitreous sampling (33.3%). This finding is in line with previous studies suggesting that anterior chamber paracentesis is often sufficient for the diagnosis of both posterior uveitis and panuveitis [7, 15]. However, our findings should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of diagnostic yield between sampling sites. The choice of sampling site in our study was guided by clinical presentation, suspected etiology, and the need for concurrent diagnostic or therapeutic interventions rather than anatomical classification alone.

In posterior or panuveitis cases requiring pars plana vitrectomy, vitreous samples were obtained prior to surgery to maximize diagnostic yield without additional procedural risk. Conversely, in cases not requiring vitreoretinal surgery, aqueous sampling was preferred based on its diagnostic adequacy and lower invasiveness. Consequently, differences in PCR positivity rates are more likely to reflect patient selection, sampling indications, and the specific distribution of suspected pathogens across clinical subgroups (such as a higher concentration of CMV and herpetic anterior uveitis cases undergoing aqueous sampling rather than inherent superiority of one sampling site over another, consistent with previous reports emphasizing the context-dependent value of vitreous analysis [5, 14, 19].

Several factors are known to influence PCR positivity, including disease prevalence in the tested population, pathogen characteristics, sample type and volume, sampling and transport conditions, assay methodology, disease activity, immune status, and prior antimicrobial or corticosteroid therapy [7, 9, 20]. Given the heterogeneous nature of our cohort and the retrospective design, our primary focus was on the diagnostic contribution and clinical impact of PCR testing rather than absolute positivity rates. Importantly, negative PCR results should be interpreted with caution, as false-negative findings may occur due to low pathogen load, delayed sampling, or prior treatment.

In cases where pathogen-specific PCR assays are negative despite persistent clinical suspicion, broad-range molecular techniques such as pan-bacterial 16 S rRNA or pan-fungal 18 S/28S rRNA PCR may assist in identifying uncommon pathogens. Although these assays were not performed in our study, their diagnostic utility in challenging, culture-negative intraocular infections has been reported in the literature [21].

In our cohort, PCR findings confirmed or led to a revision of the initial diagnosis in a substantial proportion of patients and contributed meaningfully to clinical management decisions. While PCR positivity was strongly associated with diagnostic certainty, negative PCR results also played an important role by excluding infectious etiologies and prompting consideration of alternative diagnoses. Notably, in three patients initially managed as presumed acute retinal necrosis due to poor fundus visualization, PCR testing played an adjunctive role by excluding viral infection while awaiting serological confirmation of syphilis. These cases highlight the complementary rather than standalone diagnostic role of PCR in complex infectious uveitis presentations.

A similar principle applied to the case of tuberculosis-associated uveitis presenting with choroidal granulomas in a patient with underlying HIV infection. At the time of ocular sampling, systemic tuberculosis had not yet been microbiologically confirmed. Given the patient’s immunosuppressed status and the need for rapid diagnostic clarification, intraocular PCR analysis was performed as part of an individualized diagnostic approach. The objective was not solely to establish ocular tuberculosis but to support the systemic diagnostic workup. Systemic evaluation subsequently confirmed active pulmonary tuberculosis. Intraocular PCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative, which is consistent with existing evidence demonstrating that ocular TB is often paucibacillary and that PCR sensitivity may be limited due to low organism load and sequestration within ocular tissues [21]. In the literature, while the specificity of intraocular PCR for M. tuberculosis is generally high, its sensitivity varies considerably and remains relatively limited. Therefore, false-negative results are not uncommon [22]. Consequently, a negative intraocular PCR result should not be used as the sole criterion to exclude ocular tuberculosis when clinical suspicion remains high. Integration of PCR findings with systemic investigations and clinical presentation remains essential for accurate diagnosis.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, the use of different molecular platforms over the study period reflects real-world practice but may introduce variability in terms of analytical sensitivity. Although inter-platform variability was carefully minimized through the use of identical target genomic regions and standardized CE-IVD validated protocols, the potential for minor differences in detection performance remains a limitation of this long-term retrospective analysis. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and standardized molecular methodologies are warranted to further define the diagnostic performance of intraocular PCR testing in uveitis.
