A sharp product bound for non-trivial cross-intersecting families
Yang Huang

Abstract
Two families 𝒜 , ℬ ⊂ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k} are cross-intersecting if A ∩ B ≠ ∅ A\cap B\neq\emptyset for all A ∈ 𝒜 A\in\mathcal{A} and B ∈ ℬ B\in\mathcal{B} , and non-trivial if neither 𝒜 \mathcal{A} nor ℬ \mathcal{B} is a star. Pyber proved that any two cross-intersecting families 𝒜 , ℬ ⊂ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k} satisfy | 𝒜 | ​ | ℬ | ≤ ( n − 1 k − 1 ) 2 |\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2} , and the maximum is attained by two full stars. Frankl, as well as Frankl and Wang, conjectured that the sharp bound, when both families are required to be non-trivial, is h ​ ( n , k ) 2 h(n,k)^{2} , where h ⁡ ( n , k ) = ( n − 1 k − 1 ) − ( n − k − 1 k − 1 ) + 1 h(n,k)=\binom{n-1}{k-1}-\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}+1 , the size of the Hilton–Milner family. The cases k = 3 k=3 , and the range k ≥ 8 k\geq 8 and n ≥ 4 ​ k n\geq 4k , were established earlier by Frankl and by Frankl and Wang, respectively. In this paper, we prove their conjecture in the full range. We show that every non-trivial cross-intersecting pair 𝒜 , ℬ ⊂ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k} with n ≥ 2 ​ k n\geq 2k and k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 satisfies | 𝒜 | ​ | ℬ | ≤ h ​ ( n , k ) 2 |\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq h(n,k)^{2} . Moreover, we characterize all extremal pairs. Whereas the corresponding sum problem admits asymmetric and unbalanced extremizers, the product extremum forces a balanced, symmetric-or-dual structure: the two families are isomorphic when n > 2 ​ k n>2k and complement-dual when n = 2 ​ k n=2k . Independently and contemporaneously with the present work, Frankl and Wang obtained the same bound for k ≥ 8 k\geq 8 and n ≥ 2 ​ k + 1 n\geq 2k+1 by a different method. Our proof combines a diversity technique with several new properties of an extended shift operation. Moreover, we show that the problem behaves differently for different uniformities, exhibiting new extremal configurations. In particular, we disprove a related conjecture proposed by Frankl and Wang.

Introduction

A
k
-set is a
k
-element set.
We use the standard notation
[n]
for
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
{X\choose k}
for the collection of all
k
-sets of
X
.
Let
[a,b]:=\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
, with the convention that
[a,b]=\emptyset
if
a>b
.
A
family
is a collection of sets, and it is called
k
-
uniform
if every member is a
k
-set.
A
star
is a family in which all members contain a common (fixed) element, called its
{\it center}
, and
a
full star
is the family of all
k
-sets containing a fixed element.
A family is said to be
non-trivial
if it is not a star.
A family
\mathcal{F}
is
intersecting
if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
.
Let us recall two classical results in extremal combinatorics.

Theorem 1.1

(Erdős–Ko–Rado
[
2
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 4
, and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be an intersecting family. Then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}.

Moreover, when
n>2k
, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a full star.

Let us introduce the
Hilton–Milner family
, which is a non-trivial intersecting family.

\begin{matrix}\mathcal{HM}(n,k):=\Bigl\{G\in{[n]\choose k}:1\in G,G\cap[2,k+1]\neq\emptyset\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{[2,k+1]\Bigr\}.\end{matrix}

Denote

h(n,k):=|\mathcal{HM}(n,k)|={n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1.

Theorem 1.2

(Hilton–Milner
[
13
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be
an intersecting family.
If
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1.

When
n>2k
, equality holds if and only if either
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
, or
k=3
and
\mathcal{F}
is isomorphic to

\mathcal{T}(n):=\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{3}:|F\cap[3]|\geq 2\right\}.

These two theorems have inspired a vast body of work,
including numerous reproofs, refinements, and extensions.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, Hilton and Milner
[
13
]
were the first to extend the intersection problem to the cross‑intersection setting.
Two families
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are
cross-intersecting
if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
for all
A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}
. In addition, if neither
\mathcal{A}
nor
\mathcal{B}
is a star, then we say that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are
non-trivial cross-intersecting
.

Pyber
[
17
]
showed that the maximum of

|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
over cross-intersecting families

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}

is attained when both are full stars.

Theorem 1.3

(Pyber
[
17
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 4
and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
be cross-intersecting. Then

|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}.

For
n>2k
, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\left\{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}:i\in S\right\}
for some
i\in[n]
.

Frankl and Kupavskii
[
6
]
gave a short proof of Theorem
1.3
.
Pyber
[
17
]
also showed that

|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n-1}{a-1}\binom{n-1}{b-1}

for all cross-intersecting families

\mathcal{A}\subset\binom{[n]}{a}
,

\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{b}

with
n\geq 2a+b-2
and
a\geq b
.
Matsumoto and Tokushige
[
16
]
then proved that Pyber’s result holds for all
n\geq 2a,a\geq b
.

Frankl
[
3
]
proposed the non‑trivial cross‑intersection problem, and established the best possible bound
|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq{n\choose k}-2{n-k\choose k}+{n-2k\choose k}+2
for non-trivial cross-intersecting families
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
.
Determining the maximum of
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
for non-trivial cross-intersecting families seems to be a harder task, and it had remained open in the full range
n\geq 2k\geq 6
. As a first step in this direction, Frankl and Wang
[
9
]
proved that if
k\geq 8
,
n\geq 4k
, and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
are non-trivial cross-intersecting, then
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq h(n,k)^{2}
.
Furthermore, they remarked that ‘the most intriguing problem is whether this bound holds in the full range’. The same problem was independently raised by Frankl
[
3
]
. More recently, Frankl
[
4
]
confirmed this bound
in the case
k=3
.

In this paper, we completely solve this problem by determining the maximum of
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
for all
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and establishing all the extremal pairs. Let us state our main results.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
n\geq 2k
. If
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
are non-trivial cross-intersecting, then

|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq h(n,k)^{2}.

Theorem
1.4
gives the maximum value of the
product. We now describe all pairs attaining it. The
extremal pairs are built from the following three types.

Construction 1.5
.

We define the following non-trivial cross-intersecting
pairs
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
in
\binom{[n]}{k}
.

•

Cross-HM type.
Let
n>2k\geq 6
. For
x\in[n]
,

A_{0},B_{0}\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{k}
with

A_{0}\cap B_{0}\neq\emptyset
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x}(A_{0};B_{0}):=\{A\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in A,A\cap B_{0}\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{A_{0}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x}(B_{0};A_{0}):=\{B\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in B,B\cap A_{0}\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{B_{0}\}.

We say that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is of cross-HM type if
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=(\mathcal{H}_{x}(A_{0};B_{0}),\mathcal{H}_{x}(B_{0};A_{0}))
for some
x,A_{0},B_{0}
.

•

Cross-triangle type.
Let
n>2k
and
k=3
. For
x\in[n]
and

P,Q\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{x\}}{2}
with
P\cap Q\neq\emptyset
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{x}(P;Q):=\{A\in\tbinom{[n]}{3}:x\in A,\ A\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{A\in\tbinom{[n]}{3}:x\notin A,\ P\subset A\}.

We say that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is of cross-triangle type if

(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=(\mathcal{T}_{x}(P;Q),\mathcal{T}_{x}(Q;P))
for some
x,P,Q
.

•

Complement-dual type.
Let
n=2k
. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\binom{[2k]}{k}
be an arbitrary non-trivial family
with
|\mathcal{C}|=h(2k,k)=\tfrac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k}
, and let

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\binom{[2k]}{k}\setminus\{[2k]\setminus C:C\in\mathcal{C}\}.

Note that the fact
\mathcal{C}
is non-trivial forces
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
to be non-trivial as well.
We say that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is of complement-dual type if
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\perp})
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and let

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
be non-trivial
cross-intersecting families with
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|=h(n,k)^{2}
.
Then
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is isomorphic to one of the pairs in
Construction
1.5
.

Remark.

Each pair in Construction
1.5
attains

h(n,k)^{2}
, so these are exactly the extremal pairs.
Taking
A_{0}=B_{0}
in cross-HM type recovers

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
, and taking
P=Q
in cross-triangle type recovers
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{T}(n)
.

Theorem
1.4
provides a product extension of the classical Hilton–Milner theorem and provides a sharp nontrivial analogue of Pyber’s product theorem.
While the sum extremum is typically attained by
asymmetric

pairs, the product extremum forces a balanced,
symmetric-or-dual structure: the two families are isomorphic when

n>2k
and complement-dual when
n=2k
; the full list of extremal pairs is
given in Theorem
1.6
. This reflects a general
phenomenon that product objectives favor balanced configurations.

[

]
,
which proves the same bound
for
k\geq 8
and
n\geq 2k+1
by a different method. The two works were
developed independently.

Take a family

proved the following sharp bound for cross-intersecting families.

Theorem 2.5

(Hilton
[
11
]
)
.

Let

and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
be cross-intersecting. Then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n}{k}.

(1)

Frankl and Tokushige
[
8
]
generalized the Hilton–Milner theorem by giving the following result.

Theorem 2.6

(Frankl–Tokushige
[
8
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subset\binom{[n]}{a}
and
\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{b}
are non-empty cross-intersecting families with

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq\binom{n}{b}-\binom{n-a}{b}+1.

\mathcal{A}=\{A\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in\binom{[n]}{b}:B\cap A\neq\emptyset\}
, or for
a=b=2

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\{S\in\binom{[n]}{2}:x\in S\}
for some

.

Theorem 2.7

(Hilton
[
12
]
)
.

Let
n\geq a+b
, and
\mathcal{A}\subset{[n]\choose a},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose b}
be cross-intersecting. Then
\mathcal{L}([n],|\mathcal{A}|,a)

\mathcal{L}([n],|\mathcal{B}|,b)
are cross-intersecting.

.

Let

, and let
\mathcal{A}\subset\binom{[n]}{a}

(ii)

Proof of Theorem

We start with the following definition, which will be frequently used in this paper.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
be non-trivial cross-intersecting.
The pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is
lex-minimal
if for every pair of
cross-intersecting families
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
with

|\mathcal{C}|=|\mathcal{A}|
,
|\mathcal{D}|=|\mathcal{B}|
and
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\precneqq(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, at least
one of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
is a star.

We prove the product bound through the following three lemmas. The first lemma shows that, after choosing an extremal pair in a lex-minimal form, at least one of the two families has a large maximum degree.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be non-trivial cross-intersecting families such that
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
is maximum among all pairs of non-trivial cross-intersecting families.
Suppose further that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is lex-minimal.
If
|\mathcal{A}|\geq h(n,k)
, then

\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq{n-2\choose k-2}+1
.

The second lemma treats the case where both families have large maximum degree; in this regime, a sum-type estimate is strong enough to imply the desired product bound.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be non-trivial cross-intersecting families such that
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
is maximum among all pairs of non-trivial cross-intersecting families.
Suppose further that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is lex-minimal.
If
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq{n-2\choose k-2}+1
and
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\geq{n-2\choose k-2}+1
, then

|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq 2h(n,k)
.

The third lemma treats the remaining degree-asymmetric case.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 2k\geq 6
, and let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be non-trivial cross-intersecting families such that
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|
is maximum among all pairs of non-trivial cross-intersecting families.
If
|\mathcal{A}|\geq h(n,k)
,

\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq{n-2\choose k-2}+1
and
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\leq{n-2\choose k-2}
, then
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq h(n,k)^{2}
.

We now assemble Lemmas
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
into a proof of the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Denote

\Pi:=\max\{\,|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|:\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\tbinom{[n]}{k}\text{ are non-trivial cross-intersecting}\,\}.

The Hilton–Milner family
\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
is non-trivial and intersecting,
so
\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
and
\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
are non-trivial cross-intersecting families with product of sizes
h(n,k)^{2}
. The pair above gives

\Pi\geq h(n,k)^{2}.

(2)

It remains to prove
\Pi\leq h(n,k)^{2}
.
Recall the weight
w(\mathcal{F}):=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}i_{\mathrm{lex}}(S)
and the relation
\precneqq
on pairs. By definition,

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\precneqq(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
entails
w(\mathcal{C})\leq w(\mathcal{A})
,

w(\mathcal{D})\leq w(\mathcal{B})
with at least one strict inequality, and hence

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\precneqq(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\text{ implies }w(\mathcal{C})+w(\mathcal{D})<w(\mathcal{A})+w(\mathcal{B}).

(3)

Among all non-trivial cross-intersecting pairs
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with

|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|=\Pi
, fix one minimizing
w(\mathcal{A})+w(\mathcal{B})
.
We claim that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is lex-minimal. Indeed, let

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
be a cross-intersecting pair with

|\mathcal{C}|=|\mathcal{A}|
,
|\mathcal{D}|=|\mathcal{B}|
and

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\precneqq(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. If both
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}

were non-trivial, then
|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{D}|=\Pi
and, by (
3
),
the total weight would be smaller, contradicting the choice of

(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

By (
2
),
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|=\Pi\geq h(n,k)^{2}
, so

\max\{|\mathcal{A}|,|\mathcal{B}|\}\geq h(n,k)
.
By symmetry, we may assume
|\mathcal{A}|\geq h(n,k).

Lemma
3.2
now yields
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq\binom{n-2}{k-2}+1.

We split into two cases according to
\Delta(\mathcal{B})
.
If
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\geq\binom{n-2}{k-2}+1
, then
\Delta(\mathcal{A}),\Delta(\mathcal{B})\geq\binom{n-2}{k-2}+1
, so
Lemma
3.3
gives
|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|\leq 2h(n,k)
, and hence

\Pi=|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq\Big(\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|}{2}\Big)^{2}\leq h(n,k)^{2}.

If
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\leq\binom{n-2}{k-2}
, then
Lemma
3.4
gives directly
\Pi=|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|\leq h(n,k)^{2}.

In either case
\Pi\leq h(n,k)^{2}
. Together with (
2
), this gives

\Pi=h(n,k)^{2}
, and the value is attained by
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{HM}(n,k)
. This
completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

Proposition 5.4

Characterization of extremal families: Proof of Theorem

In this section, we characterize all extremal families that attain the

Theorem 7.1

(Frankl–Kupavskii

\mathcal{A}
and

maximum degree.

[

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{A_{0}\}\cup\left\{A\in\binom{[n]}{a}:1\in A,\ A\cap B_{0}\neq\emptyset\right\},

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{B_{0}\}\cup\left\{B\in\binom{[n]}{b}:1\in B,\ B\cap A_{0}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Frankl and Wang
[

]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 8.1

(Frankl–Wang
[
10
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2a\geq 2b\geq 4
. Suppose that

\mathcal{A}\subset\binom{[n]}{a}
,

\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{b}
are non-trivial cross-intersecting families. Then

We show that Conjecture
8.1
fails in general.
Let us introduce a counterexample.

Construction 8.2
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{a}=\left\{C\in{[n]\choose a}:\{1,2\}\subset C\right\}\cup\left\{[3,a+2]\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{b}=\left\{C\in{[n]\choose b}:\{i,j\}\subset C,i\in[2],j\in[3,a+2]\right\}.

Taking
a=b=k
in the above construction, we have
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{a}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{b}
(where
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
are defined in Construction
6.3
). By Proposition
6.4
, if

, then
|\mathcal{C}_{a}||\mathcal{C}_{b}|<h(n,a)h(n,b)=|\mathcal{A}_{0}||\mathcal{B}_{0}|
. However, when

, for example,
a=9,b=3,n=18

|\mathcal{C}_{a}||\mathcal{C}_{b}|>|\mathcal{A}_{0}||\mathcal{B}_{0}|

Appendix

In this section, we give the proof of Proposition
7.2
. We need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
n\geq 7
, and let

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{3}
be non-trivial
cross-intersecting families. If
|\mathcal{A}|=|\mathcal{B}|=3n-8
, then
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq n-1
or
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\geq n-1
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\leq n-2
and
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\leq n-2
.
We may w.l.o.g. assume
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\mathcal{B}(1)
. Then
|\mathcal{B}(1)|\leq n-2
, and hence,
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\geq(3n-8)-(n-2)=2n-6.

If
n=7
, then
|\mathcal{B}|=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{x\in[n]}d_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\leq\frac{1}{3}n\Delta(\mathcal{B})\leq\frac{35}{3}<3n-8
, a contradiction. Hence we may assume
n\geq 8
.

We show that
\Delta(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 2
. Indeed, if
\Delta(\mathcal{B}(1))\geq 4
, then there is
x\in[2,n]
and four different elements
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in[2,n]\setminus\{x\}
such that all
\{1,x,x_{1}\},\{1,x,x_{2}\},\{1,x,x_{3}\},\{1,x,x_{4}\}
belong to
\mathcal{B}
. This forces every set in
\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})
to contain
x
. This implies that
|\mathcal{A}(x)|\geq|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\geq 2n-6
, contradicting
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\leq n-2
. If
\Delta(\mathcal{B}(1))=3
, then w.l.o.g., we may assume
\{1,x,x_{1}\},\{1,x,x_{2}\},\{1,x,x_{3}\}
belong to
\mathcal{B}
, this implies that
\mathcal{A}(\bar{1}\bar{x})\subset\{\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\}
, and hence
|\mathcal{A}(x)|\geq|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|-1\geq 2n-7
, contradicting
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\leq n-2
.

Note that
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 3
.
If
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 2
, then using
\Delta(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 2
, we obtain
\Delta(\mathcal{B})\leq 4
, and hence
|\mathcal{B}|=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{\mathcal{B}}(i)\leq\frac{4n}{3}<3n-8
(by
n\geq 8
), a contradiction.
It remains to consider
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))=3
. Let
\nu(\mathcal{B}(1))
denote the matching
number of
\mathcal{B}(1)
. Then
\nu(\mathcal{B}(1))\in\{2,3\}.

If
\nu(\mathcal{B}(1))=3
, then
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 2^{3}=8
, and hence
|\mathcal{A}|\leq n-2+8<3n-8
, a contradiction.
If
\nu(\mathcal{B}(1))=2
, fix a maximum matching:
E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(1)
. Given a

3
-element vertex cover, choose one of its vertices from each of

E_{1},E_{2}
. There are at most four possible choices for this pair.
Since
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))=3
, the chosen pair is not a vertex cover. The third
vertex must therefore cover all sets missed by the pair, and there
are at most two possibilities for this element. Thus
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 8
, again
|\mathcal{A}|\leq n-2+8<3n-8
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
n\geq 7
,
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset{[n]\choose 3}
be non-trivial cross-intersecting families.
If
|\mathcal{B}(1)|\geq n-1
, then
|\mathcal{B}(1)|+|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 3n-8
, and equality holds only in one of the following three cases.

(i)

There exists
3
-set
S\subset[2,n]
such that

\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})=\{S\}
and

\mathcal{B}(1)=\left\{E\in\binom{[2,n]}{2}:E\cap S\neq\emptyset\right\}
.

(ii)

There exists
2
-set
P\subset[2,n]
such that
\mathcal{B}(1)=\left\{E\in\binom{[2,n]}{2}:E\cap P\neq\emptyset\right\}
and

\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})=\{E\in\binom{[2,n]}{3}:P\subset E\}
.

(iii)

There exist
x\in[2,n]
and
P\in\binom{[2,n]\setminus\{x\}}{2}
such that

\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})=\{E\in\binom{[2,n]}{3}:x\in E,P\cap E\neq\emptyset\}
and

\mathcal{B}(1)=\left\{E\in\binom{[2,n]}{2}:x\in E\right\}\cup\{P\}
.

Moreover,
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 2n-7
.

Proof.

Note that
every set of
\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})
is a
3
-element vertex cover of

\mathcal{B}(1)
. Thus,
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 3
.
Since
|\mathcal{B}(1)|>n-2
,
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\geq 2
. Together with
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\leq 3
, we have
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))\in\{2,3\}.

Case 1:
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))=2
.

Let
\{u,v\}
be a minimum vertex
cover. Put

a:=|\mathcal{B}[\{1,u\}\overline{\{v\}}]|,\quad b:=|\mathcal{B}[\{1,v\}\overline{\{u\}}]|,\quad\eta:=|\mathcal{A}(\overline{\{1,u,v\}})|,\quad\varepsilon:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }\{1,u,v\}\in\mathcal{B},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
|\mathcal{B}(1)|=a+b+\varepsilon,

a,b\geq 1
,
|\mathcal{A}(\{u,v\}\overline{\{1\}})|\leq n-3
,
a,b\leq n-3
,
and if
\eta=1
, then
\varepsilon=0
.

First, consider the case
a,b\geq 3
. If
\varepsilon=1
, then every set in
\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})
contains
\{u,v\}
, i.e.
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq{n-3}
, so
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|+|\mathcal{B}(1)|\leq{n-3}+a+b+1\leq 3(n-3)+1=3n-8
.
If
\varepsilon=0
, then either
\eta=1
, in which case
a=b=3
,
|\mathcal{B}(1)|=6
, and
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-2
, and hence
|\mathcal{B}(1)|+|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|<3n-8
; or
\eta=0
, in which case

|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-3
and
|\mathcal{B}(1)|\leq 2(n-3)
, and hence,

|\mathcal{B}(1)|+|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 3(n-3)<3n-8
. Thus, if
a,b\geq 3
, then
|\mathcal{B}(1)|+|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 3n-8
with equality only if
a=b=n-3
and
\varepsilon=1
, which is case (ii). Moreover,
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-2<2n-7
.

Next consider the case
a=2
or
b=2
. If, say,
b=2
, then
a\geq n-4
,
\eta=0
,
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-3+1=n-2
, and
|\mathcal{B}(1)|\leq 1+2+n-3=n

|\mathcal{B}(1)|+|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-2+n<3n-8
. In this case,
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq n-2<2n-7
.

Finally consider the case
a=1
or
b=1
. If, say,
b=1
, then
|\mathcal{B}(1)|\geq n-1
and
a\leq n-3
forces
a=n-3
and
\varepsilon=1
.
Consequently,
|\mathcal{B}(1)|=n-1
and
|\mathcal{A}(\bar{1})|\leq 2n-7
,
and case (iii) follows.

Case 2:
\tau(\mathcal{B}(1))=3
.

Let
S=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}

be a minimum vertex cover.
For each
i\in[3]
, let
a_{i}:=|\mathcal{B}(\{1,x_{i}\}\overline{S\setminus\{x_{i}\}})|

.

for every

Besides

. Let
