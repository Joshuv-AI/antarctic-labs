When are Two Subgroups Independent?
Alexa Gopaulsingh

Abstract




Abstract

In
[
6
]
, Rosenmann and Ventura asked "What is the right definition of dependence of subgroups for general groups?". Here we aim to answer this question and generalise the notion first used by Rosemann in
[
5
]
. We consider a definition of subgroup independence which is a special case of the category-theoretic one introduced in
[
1
]
. It is that: Two subgroups of a group are
independent
if and only if any two endomorphisms, one acting on each subgroup, can be extended to an endomorphism of the group generated by these subgroups. This definition helps to illuminate that the usual condition of almost disjointness of subgroups (two subgroups
A
and
B
are almost disjoint if and only if
A\cap B=\{e\}
, where
e
is the identity element) is not enough to force independence and here we find necessary and (different) sufficient conditions for subgroup independence. The aim of this note is to introduce this general notion of subgroup independence to the group theory community and to pose the open question of its characterisation. We present the partial results known up to this point. Moreover, we use the progress made so far to give a heuristic algorithm that decides subgroup independence for many cases.

1
Introduction

Independence notions have long been of interest in many fields. For example, they are important in physics in quantum field theory, see
[
7
]
. Here, many unequal notions of independence were introduced. For a review and comparison of these, see
[
8
]
. In
[
6
]
, Rosenmann and Ventura asked for a definition of independence which generalises the notion first used by Rosemann in
[
5
]
for free groups. In Section 3.2, we mention how the introduced definition extends that one. In
[
1
]
, a general approach and a category-theoretic definition of subalgebra independence of structures was introduced. It generalises and continues work done in
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
to give a categorical definition of independence as subobject morphism coexistence. For the category of Groups, this essentially reduces to: Two subgroups
A
and
B
of a group, are independent if and only if any endomorphisms,
\alpha
on
A
and
\beta
on
B
, can be extended to an endomorphism of the minimal group generated by
A
and
B
(their join). This extension, if it exists must be unique, see
[
1
]
. Here, it uses the fact that in the categories of Sets, Boolean Algebras, Vector Spaces and Abelian Groups, the co-product of two algebras in these categories are the join of these algebras. This results in the independence definitions evaluating to the expected notions in these categories: For the category of Sets, subset independence is disjointness; for Vector Spaces, subspace independence is linear independence; for Boolean Algebras, subalgebra independence is logical independence and for the category of Abelian groups, subgroup independence is almost disjointness. However, surprisingly, for the category of Groups the situation was found to be mysterious and not equivalent to the usual requirement of almost disjoint subgroups. In this category, the internal co-product of two subgroups is not necessarily isomorphic to their join. We found that it is possible for a pair of subgroups to satisfy the usual requirement of almost disjointness and yet still their endomorphisms affect each other. That is, there exists endomorphisms of almost disjoint subgroups of a group which do not have an extension in their join, see Example
2.1
. This category-theoretic definition helped to shed light on this situation and we see that we have to be more careful and require more than simply almost disjointness to determine if the endomorphisms of two subgroups influence each other. Informally, we found that part of the reason for this is that even if two subgroups
A
and
B
are such that
A\cap B=\{e\}
(i.e they are almost disjoint), it still may be that case that
A
contains a non-trivial conjugate of an element
B
(or a product of
B
’s conjugates). Homomorphism mappings on elements restrict what their conjugates can be mapped to and hence
A
can still affect
B
in this way. This prompts us to define a notion of subgroup separateness which states that not only is
A
almost disjoint from
B
but it must be almost disjoint from the minimal normal group containing
B
in their join. We found that requiring both
A
to be separate from
B
and for
B
to be separate from
A
, is indeed necessary for subgroup independence. However, we will show that even this is not sufficient. Additionally, we will see that if we were to take a stronger version of this separateness for
A
and
B
which states that their minimal normal subgroups in their join must be almost disjoint, then this is too strong to characterise subgroup independence. Though we have gotten closer, the exact sweet spot of this conjugate influence is yet to be found and we motivate it as an interesting open problem.

Moreover, this article contains the progress so far, for determining when two subgroups are independent. The necessary and (separately) sufficient conditions in the following sections can help us to decide independence or dependence of subgroups for many cases. We have used these results to condense a heuristic algorithm given in Section
5
which can be useful to scientists or anyone who may need to detect subgroup dependence.

2.2
Main Results about Subgroup Independence

Next, we develop the main results obtained so far about subgroup independence. We will see how the notion of subgroup separateness influences independence and we will obtain characterisations for subgroup separateness in this and the following sections.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups of a group. For any endomorphisms
\alpha
and
\beta
of
A
and
B
respectively, if their extension to an endomorphism exists in their join,
\gamma
say, then for any
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\in\langle A\cup B\rangle
, it is the case that
\gamma(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\beta(b_{i})
.

Proof.

Immediate from the notion of extension of mappings.
∎

Lemma 2.1
.

(id, triv) is compatible for
(A,B)\implies

A

is
B
-separated.

Proof.

We will use proof by the contrapositive. Assume that
A
is not
B
-separated. Then
\exists\ a\in A
such that
a
is not
B
- separated. This means that
a\not=e
and
a\in\langle Conj(B)\rangle
. Consider
(\alpha,\beta)=
(id, triv) for
(A,B)
. We observe that, all elements in
Conj(B)
must be mapped to
e
in a homomorphism extension of
\beta
since in any homomorphism, conjugate elements in the domain must be mapped to conjugate elements in the range and
e
is conjugate only to itself. It follows that every element of
\langle Conj(B)\rangle
must be mapped to
e
since these elements can be expressed as a product of elements in
Conj(B)
. Since
a\in\langle Conj(B)\rangle
, taking the triv homomorphism on
B
implies that in the extension homomorphism,
a
must be mapped to the identity in the join. However, by the id homomorphism,
a
should be mapped to itself in the join. This implies that
a=e
which is a contradiction. Hence, (id, triv) is not compatible for
(A,B)
.
∎

Next, we use this lemma to show that a stronger condition than almost disjointness is required for independence.

Theorem 2.1
.

A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B\implies

A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
and
B\ \cap\langle Conj(A)\rangle=\{e\}
.

Equivalently,
(A,B)
is an independent pair
\implies

A
is
B
-separated and
B
is
A
-separated.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
2.1
, we get that if
A
is not
B
-separated that (id, triv) is not compatible with
(A,B)\implies A\not\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
. Similarly, if
B
is not
A
-separated then (triv, id) is not compatible with
(A,B)
and so the pair is dependent.
∎

This gives us the (expected) corollary that almost disjointness is necessary for independence.

Corollary 2.1
.

A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B\implies A\cap B=\{e\}

Proof.

Immediate from the theorem.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

If there is a non-separated pair
(a,b)
in
A\times B\implies A\not\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B

Immediate from the theorem.

We need the following proposition which is known in group theory. It states that the elements commute between almost disjoint normal subgroups.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
A
and
B
are normal subgroups of a group such that
A\cap B=\{e\}
, then for any
a\in A,b\in B,ab=ba
.

Proof.

Let
a\in A
and
b\in B
. Consider
aba^{-1}b^{-1}
. This is in
A
since
aba^{-1}b^{-1}=a(ba^{-1}b^{-1})
and
A
is normal. Similarly,
aba^{-1}b^{-1}\in B
since
aba^{-1}b^{-1}=(aba^{-1})b^{-1}
and
B
is normal. However,
A\cap B=\{e\}.
This gives us that
aba^{-1}b^{-1}=e
and so
ab=ba
.
∎

If we consider
A
and
B
as subgroups in their join, then we get a type of converse to the above. This states that if the elements commute between
A
and
B
, then
A
and
B
are each normal in their join.

Proposition 2.4
.

If for any
a\in A
,
b\in B
, it is the case that
ab=ba
, then
A
and
B
are normal subgroups of their join.

Proof.

Let
a_{k}\in A
and
a_{1}b_{1}\dots a_{n}b_{n}\in\ \langle A\cup B\rangle
. Then,

(a_{1}b_{1}\dots a_{n}b_{n})a_{k}(a_{1}b_{1}\dots a_{n}b_{n})^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}\dots a_{n}b_{n}a_{k}b_{n}^{-1}a_{n}^{-1}\dots b_{1}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =a_{1}\dots a_{n}a_{k}b_{1}\dots b_{n}b_{n}^{-1}\dots b_{1}^{-1}a_{n}^{-1}\dots a_{1}^{-1}
since by

assumption, the

s and

s commute for

. After reducing, this becomes

which is in

.

Proof.

This follows by combining Proposition

and Proposition
2.4

The next result gives an important sufficient condition for independence.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
A
and
B
are subgroups which are normal in their join such that
A\cap B=\{e\}
, then
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
.

Proof.

We proceed using proof by contradiction. Suppose that
A
and
B
are normal subgroups in their join but
A\not\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
. Then by Proposition
2.2
, there exists homomorphisms
\alpha:A\rightarrow A
and
\beta:B\rightarrow B
such that for some
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}=e\in\ \angle A\cup B\rangle
and it is the case that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\beta(b_{i})\not=e
. By Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.2
, we know that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=e
and
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=e
. Now by Lemma

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\beta(b_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\beta(b_{i})
. Now, since
\alpha
and
\beta
are homomorphisms this implies that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\beta(b_{i})=\alpha(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i})\beta(\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i})=e
as
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=e
and
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=e
. This gives us that,
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(a_{i})\beta(b_{i})=e
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

If
A
and
B
are subgroups of an abelian group such that
A\cap B=\{e\}
, then
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
.

Proof.

If
A
and
B
are subgroups of an abelian group, then this implies that their join is abelian and so they are normal in the join and we can apply Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Note that the above corollary does not mean that any two abelian groups are independent, see Example
2.1
for two non-independent abelian groups (however, they are not subgroups of an abelian group). This result shows that for the category of Abelian Groups, almost disjointness is enough to force independence.

Commuting Pair check to Decide Independence:

The previous results give us the following very useful statement which can help us decide independence in some cases. It states that if
A
and
B
are almost disjoint subgroups whose elements commute between each other, then they are independent.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
A
and

are subgroups such that

and for any

and
b\in B,\ ab=ba\implies A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
.

Proof.

Combining Lemma
2.4
and Theorem
2.3
gives the result.
∎

This leads us to the next result, which shows us that not only is it the case that almost disjoint normal subgroups,
A
and
B
say, are independent, but that for any subgroups
A^{\prime}\leq A
and

will also be independent. Not only that, but they will be normal in their join regardless of whether the subgroups were chosen to be normal in
A
and

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
A
and

be subgroups such that

and
B^{\prime}\leq B

and

are normal in

and so are independent.

Proof.

Suppose that
A
and

be subgroups such that

and they are normal in
\langle A\cup B\rangle
. Let
A^{\prime}\leq A
and
B^{\prime}\leq B
. Then by Lemma
2.4
, we get that for any

and
b\in B
that

. This implies that for any
a\in A^{\prime}
and
b\in B^{\prime}
that
ab=ba
as
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
. Since
A\cap B=\{e\}
, we also get that
A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime}=\{e\}.
Then by Lemma
2.4
, it follows that
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are normal in
\langle A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}\rangle
and so by Theorem

, they are independent.

Theorem 2.5
.

If
A
and
B
are subgroups such that
A
is a normal in their join but

B
is not, then
A
and
B
are not independent.

Proof.

Assume to get a contradiction that in their join
A
is normal and

is not but that
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
. Since

is not normal in
\langle A\cup B\rangle
, then by Lemma
2.4
, there exists
a\in A,\ b\in B
such that
ab\not=ba\implies b^{-1}ab\not=a
. Since
A
is normal in
\langle A\cup B\rangle
, then
b^{-1}ab=a_{k}
for some

. Consider the morphisms, (id, triv) of the pair
(A,B)
. As

\gamma

such that one is normal in their join and the other is not, then this pair is never independent. This might prompt us to think that two subgroups which are both not normal in their join would also never be independent. However, interestingly, this is not the case as the following examples show:

is

Non-commuting Pair Check to Decide Dependence:

Now, we know by Theorem
2.4
and that if two subgroups

and

are such that for all

and
b\in B

, then
A
and
B
are independent subgroups. So, for a pair of subgroups to be dependent there must exist an

and

such that

. However, this is not sufficient for non-independence as Example

illustrates. There,
A=\{e,(12)\}
and
B=\{e,(13)(24)\}
were shown to be independent, however (12) and (13)(24) do not commute. Nonetheless, as the next result shows, in many cases, a quick of check pairs of non-commuting elements can help us detect non-independence.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups such that
\exists\ a\in A
and
b\in B
and
ab\not=ba
. If
|ab|
is finite and
|a|\not|\ |ab|
or
|b|\not|\ |ab|
, then
A\not\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
.

Proof.

Suppose, without loss of generality that
|a|\not|\ |ab|
.

Consider the homomorphism pair (id, triv) for
(A,B)
.

Now,
(ab)^{|ab|}=e
.

If the extension of (id, triv) exists for
(A,B)
, we get that,

(\text{id}(a)\text{triv}(b))^{|ab|}=\text{id}(e).

\implies(\text{id}(a))^{|ab|}=e
.

\implies a^{|ab|}=e
.

However, this contradicts that
|a|\not|\ |ab|
. Therefore, the extension of (id, triv) does not exist for
(A,B)
and
A
is not independent to
B
.
∎

We now show how this theorem can be used to quickly decide non-independence of subgroups.

Example 2.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups of
S_{3}
, the symmetric group on 3 elements, defined by
A=\{e,(12)\}
and
B=\{e,(123),(132)\}
. Consider
a=(12)
and
b=(123)
(notice that these two elements do not commute). Then

. Therefore, the order of
b
is 3 and the order of
ab
is 2 and so
|b|\not|\ |ab|
. Hence,
A
and
B
cannot be independent groups.

Remark 1:
Notice that for commuting elements
a\in A
,
b\in B
of finite orders, it is always the case that
|a|\ |\ |ab|
and
|b|\ |\ |ab|
. So, this check for non-independence is (as expected) to be performed on non-commuting elements. In the case of Example
2.2
, even though
A=\{e,(12)\}
and
B=\{e,(13)(24)\}
are independent with non-commuting elements
a=(12)\in A
and
b=(13)(24)\in B
, we observe that
|(12)|=|(13)(24)|=2
and
|(12)((13)(24))|=|(1324)|=4
. So in this case,
|a|\ |\ |ab|
and
|b|\ |\ |ab|
. However, it can be seen that this check would work to decide non-independence in "most" such cases with a non-commuting pair. This "most" is loose because we are comparing infinite sets. However, we may conjecture that if we were to linearly order the finite permutation groups by lexicographical ordering of their elements, then the number of counterexample groups (groups with non-commuting pairs which are nonetheless independent) in the first
n
groups would be low for all
n
. Even, if this conjecture is not true, this check would still work for many (infinite) cases, so this will be an important step in our heuristic algorithm in Section

.

Relationship between Commuting and Independence:

Let us say that the pair of subgroups
(A,B)

commutes
if
AB=BA
. Overall, the relationship between commuting and independence is as follows:

A\cap B=\{e\}
and
(A,B)
commutes
\implies(A,B)
is independent.

Remark 2
: The converse is "almost" but not actually true for the reasons just described. Another point is that, informally, a reason that the existence of a non-commuting pair does not automatically imply non-independence is that the groups may not be big enough for the homomorphisms on them to detect the "dependence" that non-commuting (non-free) pairs intuitively indicate. In this case, enlarging the groups can detect the independence. See, the enlargement of Example
2.2
to Example
4.1
(mentioned later as an important counterexample). The "dependence" of the non-commuting pair of elements
((12),(13)(24))
was not detected in the first example but in the enlarged second example, there are enough homomorphisms for this pair to be a problem (i.e. there exists a pair of endomorphisms
(\alpha,\beta)
which is not compatible with
(A,B)
).

A
is

-separated then

3
Further Connections following from Subgroup Separateness

3.1
Factoring the Join of Two Subgroups by One of the Subgroups

If
A
is independent to
B
we might expect that factoring their join by one group would give back the other (and not something smaller) as their join is the minimal group containing
A
and
B
. Since, we cannot technically factor by a non-normal subgroup, what we will find, using the second homomorphism theorem, is that if their join is factored by the minimal normal subgroup of one, then that quotient group will be isomorphic to the other. We will see that each subgroup being separated from each other is enough to get this result.

We need following known result in group theory, see
[
3
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
H
and
N
be subgroups of a group where
N
is normal in
G
. Then
\langle N\cup H\rangle=N\cdot H
and so
N\cdot H=H\cdot N
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups. Then,
\langle A\cup B\rangle\ =\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cdot B=B\cdot\langle Conj(A)\rangle\ =\ \langle Conj(B)\rangle\cdot A=A\cdot\langle Conj(B)\rangle
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups. Then,
\langle A\cup B\rangle\ =\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cdot B=B\cdot\langle Conj(A)\rangle\ =\ \langle Conj(B)\rangle\cdot A=A\cdot\langle Conj(B)\rangle
.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
3.1

∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups. Then,

A
is
B
-separated
\implies

\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong A

Proof.

Applying,
A
and
\langle Conj(B)\rangle
in the second homomorphism theorem with
\langle Conj(B)\rangle
being the normal subgroup, we get that,

\frac{A\cdot\langle Conj(B)\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong\frac{A}{A\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle}
.

Since
A
being
B
-separated means that
A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
and using that
\langle A\cup B\rangle=\langle Conj(B)\rangle\cdot A
from Corollary
3.2
, we get that,

\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong\frac{A}{\{e\}}\cong A
. Similarly, we can show that, if
B
is
A
-separated then,

\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(A)\rangle}\cong B.

∎

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be subgroups. Then,

A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B

\implies

\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong A

and
\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(A)\rangle}\cong B
.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
2.1
and Lemma
3.1
, we get the result.
∎

If
A
and
B
are finite subgroups if a group, then we have the following converse to Lemma
3.1

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are finite subgroups of a group. Then,

\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong A

\implies

A
is
B
-separated.

Proof.

We use proof by contrapositive. Suppose that
A
is not
B
-separated. Then,
A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle\not=\{e\}
and
|A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle|\rangle 1
and so
|\frac{A}{A\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle}|\langle|A|
. Therefore,
\frac{A}{A\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\not\cong A
. Since,
\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong\frac{A\cdot\langle Conj(B)\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\cong\frac{A}{A\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle}

\large\frac{\langle A\cup B\rangle}{\langle Conj(B)\rangle}\not\cong A
.

∎

Question
:
Is this result still true if we consider all groups (infinite ones as well)?

3.2
Element-Writing Independence

In
[
5
]
, Rosemann defined an element
g\in G
as dependent on a subgroup
H
of
G
if
g
can be written as a non-identity product of elements of
H
. Note that
A
being
B
-separated means that elements of
A
cannot be written as a non-trivial combination of not only elelments in
B
but elements in
\langle ConjB\rangle
as
A\ \cap\langle ConjB\rangle=\emptyset
. While
A
being
B
-separated and
B
being
A
-separated are necessary conditions for their independence by Theorem
2.1
, Example
4.1
given in the next section, shows that this is not sufficient to conclude that
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
. Hence,
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
is a generalisation of element-writing independence.

Following this direction,
independent sets
in a group will be defined as a set of elements such that no element can be written in terms of the other elements of the set:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a group and
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
. Then
G^{\prime}
is said to be an
independent set
of
G
, if there does not exist
g_{k}\in G^{\prime}
which is such that
g_{k}=\prod g^{\prime}_{i}
where
g^{\prime}_{i}\in G^{\prime}-\{g_{k}\}
for all
i
.

Next, we give a result which shows that if
A
and
B
are independent subgroups, then the union of two independent subsets of these, one belonging to
A
and the other to
B
, forms an independent set.

Proposition 3.2
.

A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B\implies

if
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
are independent sets then
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
is an independent set.

Proof.

By contradiction. Assume that
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
and there exists
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
which are independent sets but
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
is not an independent set.

Then without loss of generality, there exists
a_{k}\in A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
and
a_{k}\in A^{\prime}
is such that
a_{k}=\prod a_{i}b_{i}
where
a_{i}\in A^{\prime}-\{a_{k}\}
and
b_{i}\in B^{\prime}
for all
i
.

Consider the homomorphisms, (id, triv) on
(A,B)
. Since
A\mathrel{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr\raise-3.87495pt\hbox{$\smile$}\crcr}}}}B
, a homomorphism extension of this exists and hence,

\text{id}(a_{k})=\prod\text{id}(a_{i})\text{triv}(b_{i})
.

\implies\text{id}(a_{k})=\prod\text{id}(a_{i})
.

\implies a_{k}=\prod a_{i}
.

Since
a_{i}\in A^{\prime}-\{a_{k}\}
, this contradicts that
A^{\prime}
is an independent set.
∎

4
The Open Problem of Subgroup Independence

Despite the nice connections discovered from the notion of a subgroup being separated from another, it was somewhat surprising that it was found to not be strong enough to characterise subgroup independence. The following counterexample illustrates that while two groups being mutually separated from each other is necessary for them to be independent, it is not sufficient (this example is a modification of Example
2.2
):

Example 4.1
.

Let
A=\{e,(12)\}\cdot\{e,(56)\}=\{e,(12),(56),(12)(56)\}
and

B=\{e,(13)(24)\}
which are subgroups of the symmetric group
S_{6}
.

Then
\langle A\cup B\rangle=\{e,(12),(34),(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23),(1324),(1423)\}\cdot\{e,(56)\}

\langle Conj(A)\rangle=\{e,(12),(34),(12)(34)\}\cdot\{e,(56)\}

\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e,(13)(24),(12)(34),(14)(23)\}.

Notice, that
A
is
B
-separated and
B
is
A
-separated, that is;

A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
and

B\ \cap\langle Conj(A)\rangle=\{e\}.

However, consider the following homomorphisms,
\alpha:A\rightarrow A

\alpha(e)=e,\ \alpha((12))=(56),\ \alpha((56))=(12)
and
\alpha((12)(56))=(12)(56)
and
\beta:B\rightarrow B

\beta
is the identity mapping. If a join mapping exists,
\gamma

\gamma((56)((13)(24)))=\gamma(((13)(24))(56))

\alpha(56)\beta((13)(24))=\beta((13)(24))\alpha(56)

However,
\alpha(56)\beta((13)(24))=(12)(13)(24)=(1324)
whilst,
\beta((13)(24))\alpha(56)=(13)(24)(12)=(1423)
. Therefore,
\gamma
does not exist and these homomorphisms are incompatible. Hence, despite
A
being
B
-separated and
B
being
A
-separated, we observe that
A
and
B
are not independent.

Remark 3
: In the above example, we can informally see that while the elements
(12)
and
(56)
behave symmetrically from the point of view of
A
,
B
however, can detect their difference. That is,
B
"sees" that they are made from different numbers via the element
(13)(24)
. This element commutes with
(56)
but not with
(12)
. Hence, the automorphism of
A
,
\alpha
, used above which swaps these two elements, cannot coexist with the identity mapping of
B
in their join, as
B
does not "view" these elements as indistinguishable. It is for this type of reason that if we consider independence as a relation between groups, then this relation is not invariant upon factoring groups by isomorphism. That is, let
A
be isomorphic to
A^{\prime}
and
B
be isomorphic to
B^{\prime}
, then
(A,B)
being an independent pair does not imply that
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is an independent pair. This can be witnessed by the following counterexample: Let
A=\{e,(12)\},\ A^{\prime}=\{e,(13)\},\ B=\{e,(34)\},\text{and}\ B^{\prime}=\{e,(34)\}
. Then, even though clearly
A\cong A^{\prime}
,
B\cong B^{\prime}
and
(A,B)
is an independent pair (as the elements between them commute, see Theorem
2.4
), however
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is not an independent pair (as
|13|\not|\ |(13)(34)|=|(134)|
, see Proposition
2.6
). This is because isomorphisms lose information about the details of what particularly makes up groups and these details can influence independence. In other words, two groups can "locally" view themselves as the same when considered individually but a larger context (like in their join) can illuminate their differences and these can influence whether or not they affect each other in the wider context.

So we see that that for two subgroups,
A
and
B
that satisfying the separateness conditions that
A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
and
B\ \cap\langle Conj(A)\rangle=\{e\}
, is not enough to force independence. At this point, one might be tempted to conjecture that the stronger separateness condition
\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
gives the characterisation but this still misses the sweet spot as the following considerations show: Notice that for any
a\in A
and
b\in B
,
aba^{-1}b^{-1}\in\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle
since
aba^{-1}b^{-1}=a(ba^{-1}b^{-1})=(aba^{-1})b^{-1}
. So if
\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
, then
aba^{-1}b^{-1}=e\implies ab=ba\ \forall\ a\in A\ \text{and}\ b\in B
. By Theorem
2.3
this means that
A
and
B
are each normal in their join and therefore independent. However, it is not the case that all pairs of independent subgroups are normal in their join (see Example
2.2
, where
\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e,(12)(34)\}
), and so this condition, whilst sufficient to conclude independence, is therefore not necessary for it.

Overall, we are left with the strange situation with respect to characterising that a subgroup pair
(A,B)
, is independent:

Too loose
(Necessary but not sufficient):

A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}
and
B\ \cap\langle Conj(A)\rangle=\{e\}
.

Too tight
(Sufficient but not necessary):

\langle Conj(A)\rangle\cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle=\{e\}

This prompts the question:
Is there something elegant in between those that would work?

Hence, we pose the open problem to the community:

Open Problem:

What is a group-theoretic characterisation of subgroup independence?

5
What to Do if You Need to Determine if Two Subgroups Affect Each Other

Heuristic algorithm for Detecting if Two Subgroups are Independent:

If given two groups
A
and
B
, the following steps can be taken to test if they are independent:

1.
Check if there is an non- identity element in
A\cap B
. If yes, then
A
and
B
are not independent and you are done. If no, go to step 2.

2 (i).
Check if all pairs
a\in A
and
b\in B
are such that
ab=ba
. If yes, then
A
and
B
are independent and you are done. If no, then go to step 2 (ii).

(ii).
Check if any of the non-commuting pairs of
a\in A
and
b\in B
, found in part (i), is such that
|a|\not|\ |ab|
. If any such pair is found then, groups
A
and
B
are not independent and you are done.

3.
Calculate
\langle A\cup B\rangle
. Then, depending on the subgroups, do the easier of the following steps, moving on to the next easier step if a decision is not found:

(i).
Calculate
\langle Conj(A)\rangle
. Check if there is a non-identity element in
B\ \cap\langle Conj(A)\rangle
. If yes, then you are done and
A
and
B
are not independent.

(ii).
Calculate
\langle Conj(B)\rangle
. Check if there is a non-identity element in
A\ \cap\langle Conj(B)\rangle
. If yes, then you are done and
A
and
B
are not independent.

(iii).
Check, if
\exists\ a_{1},a_{2}\in A
such that
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are not conjugates in
A
but are conjugates in
\langle A\cup B\rangle
.

(iv).
Check if
\exists\ b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are not conjugates in
B
but are conjugates
\langle A\cup B\rangle
.

4.
If none of the above steps yield a decision, then you are left with checking that the remaining of the endomorphisms of
A
and of
B
can be extended to form an endomorphism of their join
\langle A\cup B\rangle
(if the above steps did not give a decision it means that (triv, id) and (id, triv) are compatible with your subgroups
(A,B)
. Hence, it is the remaining endomorphism pairs that need to be checked). This may have a high computational cost.

Proof.

Step 1 is supported by Corollary
2.1
. Step 2 is supported by Theorem
2.4
and Proposition
2.6
. Step 3 is supported by Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.6
.
∎

Steps 1 and 2 may be of relatively low computational cost to perform, depending on the groups. Steps 3 starts to become more costly, but again depending on the particular groups, there are many cases where this is still tractable. If steps 1-3 do not give a decision, then we are left to manually check the remaining endomorphisms pairs at step 4. It is yet to be solved a complete characterisation, which would lower the computational complexity of step 4. However, a great many cases (see Remark 1) can be decided in steps 1-3. so we hope that this algorithm can be nonetheless helpful.

Additionally, not only can the considerations in this paper can be used to determine if two groups are independent but it can be used to quickly design independent groups if so desired. As we have seen, simply requiring that
A\cap B=\{e\}
is not enough. However, if in addition you make sure that the elements
a\in A
and
b\in B
commute for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
, then that would be enough. Also, if you wanted to design a pair of non-obviously dependent groups (i.e. with
A\cap B=\{e\}
), then giving
A
and
B
a non-commuting pair of elements
a\in A
and
b\in B
such that
|a|\not|\ |ab|
would work.

Overall, we hope to motivate further research in the direction of the complete characterisation of subgroup independence.
Also, for the scientists who work with groups and subgroups, we give the practical heuristic algorithm to determine when two subgroups affect each other in many cases.
