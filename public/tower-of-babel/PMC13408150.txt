Annealing of skyrmion lattice in van der Waals magnet via field modulation
Authors: Jingwei Zhang, Yilian Xi, Hanqing Shi, Heping Li, Yaqi Li, Jiaqi Liu, Nvsen Ma, Yan Shi, Weichang Hao, Shilei Zhang, Ting Yu, Haifeng Feng, Yi Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In atomic solids, annealing transforms disordered structures into ordered crystals through thermal activation, whereas realizing a comparable process in quasiparticle lattices is highly desirable. Magnetic skyrmions are topological spin textures that can form ordered lattice and undergo equilibrium phase transitions, yet thermal activation is insufficient to overcome their kinetic constraints or provide tunable control. Here, we demonstrate field-driven annealing of skyrmions in two-dimensional van der Waals Fe3GaTe2 (FGaT) at room temperature. Oscillating magnetic fields drive the formation of polycrystalline skyrmion lattices, whereas bipolar current pulses generate anisotropic hexatic states through current-induced lattice distortion. Despite distinct external fields, both processes follow similar defect-mediated crystallization dynamics. The annealing is enabled by the interplay between DMI and MA, while the thermodynamics are primarily governed by dipolar interactions. Our findings demonstrate a pathway for annealing control of topological spin textures and establish FGaT as a platform for exploring skyrmion collective behaviour and reconfigurable spintronics.

Introduction

In crystalline solids, annealing is a thermally activated process that allows atoms to migrate and eliminate defects, and transforms a disordered structure into an ordered crystal1,2. This seemingly simple thermodynamic behavior, which reflects how complex systems overcome kinetic barriers to reach equilibrium, represents a broader paradigm extending far beyond metallurgy, underpinning structure formation in chemistry, materials science, and even computational optimization strategies1–3. In topological quasiparticle systems, it might, in principle, undergo analogous annealing processes; however, the interplay between thermal fluctuations and topological protection often leads to competing effects. The skyrmion lattice (SkL) can be viewed as a crystal of nanoscale spin vortices, each carrying a quantized topological charge that forbids smooth annihilation or continuous deformation4–7. Recent works have revealed that SkLs would undergo melting and recrystallization8,9. Although such equilibrium transitions have been observed, non-equilibrium dynamics, such as the annealing process, has remained experimentally elusive. Recent advances have demonstrated the controlled writing and spatial rearrangement of skyrmions using magnetic probes10,11, yet these approaches primarily focus on skyrmion nucleation and localized manipulations. Collective reorganization of a disordered skyrmion assembly, which means a true annealing process, has remained experimentally elusive. The key challenge lies in the kinetic constraints inherent to skyrmion assemblies. General thermal fluctuations are often insufficient to unlock these constraints, particularly in systems where skyrmions exhibit high stability, which suppresses their collective dynamics by imposing large energy barriers12–14.

To realize non-thermal annealing of SkLs, external fields have been proposed to drive collective reorganization without altering the topological identity of individual skyrmions. Skyrmions commonly originate from chiral Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) that competes with exchange energy to favor swirling spin textures4,5,15. While DMI-stabilized skyrmions can undergo equilibrium phase transitions8,9,14, they generally lack tunability and are sensitive to external perturbations9,16. In contrast, magnetocrystalline anisotropy (MA) can induce skyrmion-like domains that are intrinsically stable and tunable by external fields17–19. However, they lack topological protection and could thus be continuously deformed or annihilated in strong external fields19,20. Materials that combine DMI-induced topological robustness with MA-driven precise tunability are therefore highly desirable and remain challenging, as these two properties rely on distinct and often competing symmetry conditions of the crystal or interface4,5. Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) magnets are promising in this regard. They possess pronounced magnetic anisotropy, which stabilizes robust ferromagnetism at finite temperatures21–23, and soliton-like magnetic structures have been observed in several vdW magnets19,24,25, suggesting possible DMI. In particular, in the room-temperature vdW ferromagnet Fe3GaTe2 (FGaT), we have observed Néel-type skyrmions with tunable size and density26, and demonstrated the existence of DMI induced by Fe atomic vacancies. Moreover, its high Curie temperature and field resilience make it a promising system, in which field-driven annealing of SkLs may be achieved, yet to be explored.

Here, we demonstrate the annealing of skyrmions via external fields in 2D vdW FGaT thin flakes at room temperature. Starting from a disordered skyrmion assembly, an oscillating magnetic field drives the formation of polycrystalline SkLs with well-defined grain boundaries, whereas bipolar current pulses induce anisotropic hexatic states via current-induced lattice distortion. It was found that these field-specific processes share a common crystallization dynamics mediated by the defects and grains in SkLs. Monte Carlo simulation confirms that the annealing process of SkLs is enabled by the interplay of MA and DMI, while the external fields provide kinetic activation and anisotropic modulation. This work establishes field modulation and non-thermal annealing as a powerful tool for manipulating collective behaviors of 2D topological spin textures, and paves a way to develop reconfigurable, low-power, 2D-magnet-based spintronic applications.

Results and discussion

Room-temperature magnetic skyrmions are realized in a high-quality exfoliated FGaT flake (thickness ~300 nm; Supplementary Fig. 1) by field cooling under an out-of-plane field of 40 mT. The skyrmions remain stable under zero magnetic field (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Note 1), forming a robust phase separated from the stripe-domain state by a large MA-induced energy barrier. The as-prepared skyrmions exhibit a disordered liquid-like arrangement, where most skyrmions have five or seven nearest neighbors, as revealed by the corresponding Voronoi cells, as shown in lower panel in Fig. 1a. This configuration was held at temperatures up to 310 K, including a 24-h period at 310 K (Supplementary Fig. 2), which indicates the energy barrier is much larger than the thermal energy barriers at 310 K13,14. Such barriers guarantee the exceptional stability of the skyrmion assembly, while hindering its thermal-activated ordering13,14,27. To overcome these kinetic constraints and explore possible annealing pathways, we applied periodic modulations in the form of oscillating magnetic fields and bipolar current pulses. Within the field and current range where skyrmions remain stable, we qualitatively explored the effects of external field amplitude and waveform (Supplementary Note 2). We found that the ordering phenomenon occurs across a wide range of parameters, and the fundamental process remains robust. Tracking the positions of skyrmion and the evolution of the local order parameter |Ψ6| (Supplementary Fig. 3), we observed the emergence of two distinct ordered states after repeated cycling, as shown in Fig. 1b, c. Under magnetic-field cycles (H-cycles), skyrmions form locally hexagonal regions, with 5–7 defects concentrated along boundaries between domains (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 4). This distribution indicates the formation of multiple crystalline grains with distinct lattice orientations separated by defect-rich boundaries, characteristic of a polycrystalline lattice. In contrast, after bipolar current cycles (J-cycles), sixfold-coordinated regions also emerge, while 5–7 defects are distributed throughout the lattice and disrupt long-range translational order (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, unlike H-cycles, the lattice orientation is nearly uniform across the system and closely aligns with the current direction. This preserves long-range orientational correlation, resulting in a defect-rich hexatic phase with uniform orientation. These classifications are further supported by the correlation analysis presented below. Both ordered configurations persist stably at room temperature without external magnetic fields and remain robust against small magnetic perturbations.

The distinct ordered configurations and their defect distributions closely resemble the grain and defect microstructures observed in atomic and colloidal systems28,29. Similar particle-like behavior has also been reported in equilibrium phase transitions of DMI-dominated skyrmion systems, which provides an intuitive framework for understanding the collective behaviors8,30–32. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1d, the annihilation and motion of defects in SkLs serve as the key degrees of freedom governing the structural transitions in this scenario. These results demonstrate the tunability and stability of SkLs under a field-modulated annealing process in vdW magnets. It suggests a defect-mediated crystallization process, which is discussed in detail below.

To reveal how skyrmions crystallize and how defects evolve during the annealing process, we imaged intermediate states under successive driving cycles with representative parameters (Supplementary Movie 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Voronoi diagrams in Fig. 2 visualize non-sixfold cells (white) marking defects and hexagons colored by local bond orientations8,11. The initial disordered liquid-like state with numerous defects is shown in Fig. 2a. Details of the periodic perturbations and typical states are shown in Fig. 2b, c. In contrast, in J-cycles, a similar grain formation and coarsening process occurs, but with pronounced symmetry breaking. As shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6, grains oriented along the current direction progressively dominate, while misaligned domains gradually vanish. This anisotropy finally leads to a nearly single-orientation lattice. The FFT patterns evolve from isotropic rings (4–32 cycles) to anisotropic lobes (64 cycles) and finally hexagonal spots (128 cycles), evidencing strong orientational selection during the grain coarsening stage. Notably, the peak arrangement exhibits observable asymmetry, marked by the deformed hexagon in Fig. 2c, indicating uniaxial strain in the lattice. The peaks also show anisotropic broadening. In real space, this reflects a large number of dislocations whose Burgers vectors are aligned with the current direction (Supplementary Fig. 6). The agreement between the FFT anisotropy and Burgers vector orientation confirms that strain relaxation occurs primarily through dislocation glide33,34. The glide mode is energetically favorable compared with lattice distortion or climb32, allowing grains aligned with the current to grow at lower energy cost. Consequently, current-induced uniaxial strain biases the coarsening kinetics and results in an anisotropic lattice with abundant dislocations. Furthermore, the gradual development of FFT asymmetry suggests that lattice strain accumulates under repeated anisotropic driving, indicating that the ordering process can be continuously tuned by the external driving conditions. Despite the distinct external stimuli, the skyrmions follow a similar pathway of grain nucleation and competitive coarsening. Such similarity strongly underscores an intrinsic mechanism governing the thermodynamics1, which works for both magnetic fields and electric current pulses. On the other hand, the strain modulation explains the origin of anisotropy under J-cycles, which is analogous to stress-assisted annealing in atomic systems. These results indicate that both H-cycles and J-cycles follow a common defect-mediated annealing pathway, while the qualitative difference in the final ordered states originates from the symmetry of the external driving. Notably, at lower current densities (Supplementary Note 2(iii), Supplementary Fig. 13), grain coarsening can occur without evident orientational selection, supporting that the underlying annealing pathway is common while the strain modulation can be tuned. Together, these findings highlight the key role of defect dynamics and the tunability of skyrmion annealing processes in 2D vdW FGaT.

To obtain a quantitative understanding of the thermodynamic characteristics, we analyzed the evolution of correlation functions and defect densities. The radial distribution function g(r) reflects short-range translational order through the decay length of its oscillations35. Compared with the H-driven process, the growth of translational order under J-cycles is significantly suppressed (Fig. 3a, b). This suppression corresponds to the increasing dislocation density, which disrupts local positional order while releasing accumulated strain. The orientational correlation function g6(r) reveals the development of long-range orientation order35 (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7). Under magnetic-field driving, g6(r) decays exponentially throughout the process, and the correlations can be abruptly interrupted, reflecting orientation rotation by grain boundaries. In contrast, under current pulses, the decay evolves from exponential to power-law behavior, suggesting the emergence of quasi-long-range orientational order and the formation of a hexatic state35,36. This contrast shows that current-induced strain promotes orientational coherence over longer distances. The evolution of defect density, measured by the fraction of six-coordinated skyrmions (Fig. 3e), displays a sharp initial rise (shaded region) followed by a slow, heavy-tailed decay. Such behavior is well described by a Pareto cumulative distribution, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-1/{(1+t/{t}_{0})}^{\alpha}$$\end{document}1−1/(1+t/t0)α, which is characteristic of multi-barrier non-equilibrium relaxation37,38, consistent with that of an annealing process. Notably, the fittings of defect density for both H- and J-cycles resulted in nearly identical scaling exponents (α), and the curves collapse together after scaling the time with the fitted time t0. Such Pareto scaling indicates that defect annihilation follows an intrinsic barrier-dominated relaxation process, a hallmark of thermal-like annealing rather than deterministic field-driven rearrangement. Quantitative comparison under more driving conditions further confirms the Pareto scaling relationship (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 14a). This consistency supports that external fields modulate the kinetic rate without changing the fundamental energy landscape, similar to the picture of non-thermal activations. Moreover, by rescaling the timescale, we gain further insight into orientational modulation37. As shown in Fig. 3f, the orientation correlation length in J-cycles first matches that in H-cycles, then grows much faster during grain coarsening until it reaches the system size (~30 μm). This two-stage evolution confirms that strain-induced orientation selection starts only after well-defined grains have formed, and strongly supports the proposed mechanism where strain relaxation through dislocation glide biases grain growth34.

Our observations reveal that crystallization of SkLs driven by H- and J-cycles follows the annealing process and exhibits tunability through strain accumulation induced by current pulses. Understanding the mechanisms of field driving and modulation is crucial for uncovering collective physics and developing control strategies. Since magnetic skyrmions are spin textures, magnetic interactions determine their basic response to external fields5,39. In 2D vdW FGaT, MA, and dipolar interactions dominate skyrmion stability, while relatively weak DMI guarantees their topological structure26,40. Low domain-wall energy results in weak shape confinement, which allows skyrmions to deform under external fields, whereas strong MA provides stability but also restricts wall motion by increasing dynamic damping17,41–43. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, H-cycles induce a breathing mode, where Zeeman torque drives the skyrmion wall inward or outward. Bipolar J-cycles cause back-and-forth motion via alternating spin torques26. In both cases, skyrmion wall motion is activated. Notably, in MA-dominated skyrmions, both pinning and damping originate predominantly at the skyrmion wall44,45. Therefore, periodic wall motion allows skyrmions to overcome local pinning barriers and release kinetic constraints, which enhances skyrmion mobility and facilitates collective rearrangement into ordered lattices46,47. Both periodic stimuli thus provide kinetic acceleration in the same manner, effectively serving as a non-thermal driving force for annealing. This agrees well with the scaling relation observed in Fig. 3e, which supports the common intrinsic mechanism and differing kinetic speeds. Further analysis shows that long current pulses can induce noticeable elongation of skyrmions (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 8), arising from anisotropic forces acting on the deformable domain walls41. Despite the absence of visible deformation under individual short pulses, small distortions can accumulate over repeated cycles due to incomplete relaxation. In a dense-packed skyrmion assembly, these residual distortions can be further coupled by inter-skyrmion interactions and lead to a gradual increase in lattice spacing. This cumulative effect gives rise to lattice-scale strain, consistent with the observed evolution of lattice distortion and orientation anisotropy. The annealing process, thus, can be viewed as relaxation on a multi-valley energy landscape defined by the competition between MA and DMI. The anisotropy anchors the domain-wall structure and determines damping and pinning potentials, whereas DMI preserves the core topology and allows limited deformability. The periodic magnetic or current stimuli act as an external drive that pushes the system across metastable valleys. They reduce kinetic barriers for defect annihilation and reorienting the lattice. This perspective unifies our understanding of skyrmion annealing with a broader class of driven, defect-mediated phase transitions found in colloidal and atomic systems28,48,49.

Based on the above analysis, we constructed a simplified model for Monte Carlo simulation, as shown in Fig. 4b. The isotropic deformation of skyrmions has a negligible influence on the energy landscape (details in Supplementary Note 4) and is therefore considered only as kinetic acceleration. The anisotropic distortion is treated as a lattice strain gradually applied during the process. Accordingly, skyrmions are simplified as point particles with inter-particle interactions. In our FGaT system, although individual skyrmions are stabilized by MA and DMI, their long-range repulsion is dominated by dipolar repulsive potential40 (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Note 5). Moreover, since the periodic stimuli are applied with constant amplitude, the crystallizations resemble isothermal annealing under constant temperatures. Monte Carlo simulation of isothermal annealing is carried out in a large system with more than 100,000 particles50–52. Remarkably, the simulated isothermal annealing reproduces the same defect evolution kinetics (Fig. 4c) and grain coarsening behavior observed experimentally (Supplementary Fig. 9), revealing that the external stimuli serve as kinetic activations. Applying uniaxial strain does not alter defect evolution but strongly enhances orientation correlation length (Fig. 4d), reproducing current-driven effects. This strongly supports that the orientation selection under J-cycles originates from the uniaxial strain, which can be accumulated from the elongation effect of skyrmions under electric currents. Uniaxial strain breaks the orientation symmetry of the energy landscape and modifies the dynamics of dislocations. Notably, such anisotropy cannot arise from thermal annealing, which is inherently isotropic. This anisotropy, together with Pareto scaling and consistency of simulations, confirms that the external fields provide non-thermal kinetic activations rather than thermal heating or simple structural rearrangements. Overall, external stimuli drive non-thermal annealing, manipulate the kinetic rate through activation energy, and modulate lattice orientation through defect mediated strain modulation.

Furthermore, the simulations highlight the dominant role of the inter-skyrmion potential USS in determining the crystallization pathway. As a theoretical contrast to our system’s algebraically decaying dipolar potential, we also examine the short-range exponential USS typical of traditional DMI-dominated systems50 (Fig. 4e). Under simulated isothermal annealing, skyrmions governed by exponential interactions also crystallize via grain formation and coarsening. However, their defect-density evolution displays a much steeper distribution compared with dipolar skyrmions (Fig. 4f). This indicates that most defect-annihilation events occur at significantly lower energy barriers, resulting in faster ordering and larger grain size (Fig. 4f inset). These results suggest that enhancing DMI could potentially accelerate the annealing process and yield a more ordered SkL state. Nevertheless, strong DMI also suppresses skyrmion deformability and makes both their stability and USS more sensitive to external magnetic fields. Therefore, a precise and delicate balance between DMI and MA strengths is essential for achieving efficient, stable, and tunable SkL annealing manipulation. Moreover, it is the cooperation of MA and DMI that enables this process, ensuring both topological stability and tunable deformability under external fields. This cooperative interplay offers a versatile route for the field-mediated annealing manipulation of topological spin textures. We further note that the annealing processes via grain dynamics observed here differ substantially from the Kosterlitz–Thouless–Halperin–Nelson–Young (KTHNY) transitions reported in DMI-dominated skyrmion systems8,9. In the KTHNY scenario, defects remain as isolated dislocations or disclinations rather than forming grain boundaries, and crystallization proceeds through their gradual annihilation8,9,36. In contrast, our system evolves via collective grain dynamics, reflecting distinct defect energetics. The defect energy is highly sensitive to the inter-skyrmion potential51, and in DMI skyrmions, the sophisticated field dependence of the USS may result in distinct energetics and ordering pathways compared with dipolar-type skyrmions53. In addition, non-equilibrium effects of the annealing process hinder direct comparison with analytical 2D melting theory51, which assumes thermal equilibrium.

Our results highlight the tunability of the annealing process, and establish vdW FGaT as a feasible platform for systematic investigation of non-thermal collective annealing of skyrmions. The interplay between MA and DMI in FGaT governs skyrmion stability and deformability, providing both a wide parameter window of driving fields and a mechanism for SkL modulation. The layered atomic lattice structure allows low pinning and observation of the intrinsic global process. Therefore, the vdW FGaT provides a rare combination of high stability, tunability, and intrinsic nature at the same time, enabling the direct comparison of annealing processes under various driving conditions and clean extraction of the intrinsic thermodynamic scaling laws. While the exact value of MA and DMI is system-specific, the mechanism is expected to be universal to other skyrmion systems. Analogous to atomic or colloidal systems, modulation of the annealing process can be achieved by manipulating defect dynamics of SkLs or lattice symmetry28,48, for instance, through in-plane magnetic fields, strain fields, or pinning engineering54–56. Furthermore, the lattice order and orientation can be continuously tuned over a broad parameter space by controlling the annealing time and effective strain. The kinetics of skyrmions can be activated by driving their periodic motion or deformation, which effectively reduces the energy barriers at the skyrmion wall. Beyond the magnetic field and current used here, dynamic fields such as microwave excitations and surface acoustic waves would also provide similar driving forces57,58. Moreover, the possible existence of intrinsic spin-orbit torque in FGaT can further facilitate the overcoming of spin rearrangement barriers59,60, and enable more flexible pathways for manipulating SkLs, which deserves further investigations61. Taken together, this unique combination of broad tunability, zero-field non-volatility, and scalable large-area control demonstrates the great promise of non-thermal annealing for future reconfigurable spintronic applications.

In conclusion, we achieved and provided a fundamental understanding of non-thermal collective annealing of magnetic skyrmions, and demonstrated that vdW FGaT serves as an ideal platform for the field-modulated annealing investigations. Periodic external fields drive a global transition from a disordered skyrmion ensemble to ordered lattice states, including polycrystalline and anisotropic hexatic phases. This process is intrinsically collective and does not rely on local manipulation, distinguishing it from previously reported non-equilibrium approaches. These annealing processes follow a universal grain coarsening pathway, with current pulses introducing anisotropic strain to guide the orientational outcome. Quantitative analysis and Monte Carlo simulations further show that the interplay between MA and DMI ensures the stability and deformability of the SkLs, which enables dynamic driving and tunable collective behaviors. The inter-skyrmion interaction determines the intrinsic crystallization pathway and the defect energy barriers. Thus, these results provide a quantitative and system-level description of collective annealing kinetics. Overall, our study bridges non-equilibrium driving and thermodynamic-like ordering in skyrmion systems, and establishes a versatile platform for manipulating the grains, defects, and lattice structures of skyrmion quasi-particles, with implications for both fundamental studies and reconfigurable spintronic devices5,15,39,62.

Methods

Sample preparation

FGaT thin flakes were first mechanically exfoliated from the bulk crystal onto a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate using tapes in the glove box with an Ar atmosphere. Homogeneous flakes with well-defined rectangular shape were selected and then dry transferred onto SiO2/Si substrate with Au electrodes already prepared using a 2D materials transferring equipment. Then, liquid-metal electrodes fabricated using a home-built setup were used to connect the Au electrodes with the transferred FGaT flakes (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Au electrodes on the SiO2/Si substrate were fabricated by photolithography and thermal evaporation. A Ti adhesive layer with a thickness of about 3 nm is deposited before depositing the 30 nm Au layer. The thicknesses of the samples were estimated by their optical contrast before transferring, and determined by atomic force microscope (AFM) measurement (Quantum Scale, AFM-NanoTech 20). Results were collected on 300 nm-thick samples, unless otherwise noted. This thickness was chosen because the relatively large size of the exfoliated flakes compared to skyrmion size, which ensures a sufficient number of skyrmions to minimize finite-size effects and guarantee statistically reliable results26. To rule out the influence of sample boundaries, we take the central part of the sample as the region of interest (Supplementary Fig. 1). To ensure that the observed phenomena reflect the intrinsic physics of the skyrmion system, topography measurements are carried out by AFM, and areas with mesoscopic defects (e.g., bubbles and grain boundaries) are excluded from our data analysis.

Field modulations and MFM measurements

Skyrmions were generated by cooling from 390 to 300 K under an out-of-plane magnetic field of 40 mT24. Before each modulation process, the same field cooling protocols were carried out to obtain the initial state. MFM measurements were conducted in LiftMode in air at room temperature using a Bruker Icon system for conventional imaging and a Quantum Scale AFM-NanoTech 20 system with a customized sample stage for in-situ measurements. Each sample line was scanned using tapping mode to obtain topography data first, and then the topographic information was retraced with a height lift of 50 nm to collect the magnetic signal. MESP-RC-V2 magnetic probe coated with Co/Cr was used; the force constant and resonance frequency of this probe were 5 N/m and 150 kHz, respectively. The influence of the MFM tip on skyrmion configurations is negligible under our imaging conditions, as verified by repeated scans showing no measurable perturbation (Supplementary Note 6).

Periodical stimuli, including oscillating magnetic fields and dipolar electric current pulses, were provided in-situ on the MFM sample stage to keep the measured area consistent. The MFM probe was retracted during field or pulse application to prevent probe instability and possible electrical breakdown. In the main text, the oscillating magnetic field was an absolute-value sinusoidal field (Fig. 2b), the direction was out-of-plane, and the magnitude was HC, which was the critical value where skyrmions can still remain stable. For a sample with 300 nm thickness, HC = 120 mT26. The magnetic field was generated by a home-made electromagnet with an iron core, and the frequency is 2 Hz. Higher frequency would result in higher skyrmion mobility according to previous reports46, which would result in faster crystallization speed. The bipolar current pulse was applied along the ±x direction (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1), generated by the AFG31000 arbitrary function generator and monitored by an oscilloscope. The current density was JP = 6 × 109 A/m2, and the pulse width was 100 μs, which was also used for driving skyrmion motion24. Such a bipolar current pulse would drive skyrmions to move back and forth along the x direction. The interval between each pulse was 500 ms to avoid the heating effect, and the period was 1 s. Various field amplitudes, waveforms, and current densities explored in Supplementary Note 2 were also generated in the same way.

Analysis of lattice defects and long-range orders

The position of skyrmions, Voronoi diagrams, local order parameters, distribution functions, and correlation functions were extracted from the processed MFM images using a Python code and the Freud63 Python package. Both the original code and the Freud numerical statistics package were used. The MFM images were first flattened and equalized using WSXM64 to keep the same contrast. The FFT patterns were obtained afterward, also using WSXM. The identification of skyrmions and the extraction of the locations were carried out using the scikit-image package. Both the contour-finding algorithm and the Laplacian-of-Gaussian Blob detection algorithm were used. The results were manually checked, and the algorithms and parameters were tuned for the best performance. Noise can induce fake blobs with a size of only a few pixels, which were excluded by a size threshold. Manual corrections were carried out when necessary. The Voronoi diagram was generated using the scipy package based on the skyrmion positions. In a 2D hexagonal lattice, every regular site has six neighbors forming a regular hexagon, and sites with a different number of neighbors form defects. Sites with adjacent 5–7 pairs constitute dislocations. Isolated 5- or 7-fold sites are disclinations, and sites with more than three adjacent 5 or 7 are a chain of dislocations, which can form grain boundaries. Vacancies are missing lattice sites, which would manifest as a cluster of multiple under-coordinated sites (typically 5-coordinated sites). The coarsening process is primarily mediated by dislocations; disclinations are much less frequent, and no vacancy-like defects are identified (Supplementary Fig. 10). Defects are denoted in red and blue in Fig. 1, and in white in Fig. 2.
