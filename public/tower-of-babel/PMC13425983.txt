Neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein as biomarkers of peripheral nervous system involvement in multiple sclerosis: diagnostic and prognostic evaluation
Authors: Alina Flintere-Flinte, Vladimirs Krutovs, Elizabete Pantelejeva, Kaj Blennow, Henrik Zetterberg, Maksims Zolovs, Viktorija Kenina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis is a chronic demyelinating disease of the central nervous system caused by an immune-mediated inflammatory process. Its aetiology remains unclear, and numerous pathogenetic theories have been proposed. The diversity of clinical manifestations and the lack of reliable biomarkers make diagnosis challenging. Serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) have been investigated as potential biomarkers for disease progression and response to disease-modifying therapies; however, their specificity and sensitivity remain variable, particularly in the presence of other conditions associated with neuronal damage and neurodegeneration.MethodsThis retrospective study analysed serum NfL and GFAP levels in 149 patients with multiple sclerosis and 40 healthy controls. Additionally, the association between the two biomarkers was evaluated in patients with and without polyneuropathy to assess their reliability and prognostic value for disease severity and progression.ResultsSerum NfL was found to be a reliable marker of disease activity and progression. In contrast, GFAP demonstrated limited specificity and was not suitable as a single, independent biomarker. The presence of co-existing polyneuropathy influenced biomarker levels and complicated their interpretation.ConclusionSerum NfL shows promise as a reliable biomarker for monitoring disease activity and progression in multiple sclerosis. However, GFAP should not be used independently due to its limited specificity. Co-existing polyneuropathy may further reduce the reliability of these biomarkers, highlighting potential diagnostic challenges in clinical practice.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Glial fibrillary acidic protein, Neurofilament light chain, Biomarkers, Polyneuropathies

Introduction

Introduction (revised with reviewer 2 integration)

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS), caused by an immune-mediated inflammatory process [1]. The aetiology of MS remains unclear. Various theories suggest that certain genetic and environmental predisposition factors may contribute to the occurrence and progression of the disease [2, 3]. Although demyelination primarily occurs in the CNS, some reports indicate co-existing peripheral nervous system (PNS) damage in patients with MS [4]. Polyneuropathies (PNPs), when prominent, may complicate the clinical diagnosis of MS. Their occurrence in the later stages of the disease may impose an additional disability burden, leading to a heightened subjective perception of disease severity.

The absence of definitive aetiological factors, combined with complex clinical manifestations and limited diagnostic methods, underscores the growing need for reliable biomarkers to monitor disease-related processes. Serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an intermediate filament protein expressed predominantly by astrocytes in the CNS [5], reflects astrocyte activation and gliosis [6]. However, GFAP expression is not entirely restricted to the CNS; it is also found in non-myelinating Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and in enteric glial cells. Consequently, circulating GFAP levels may be influenced by peripheral or systemic processes, which may limit its specificity as a marker of purely astroglial CNS pathology.

GFAP was included as a comparator biomarker representing predominantly astroglial injury. In contrast, neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a structural protein expressed in axons of both the central and peripheral nervous systems and is released into the circulation following neuroaxonal damage [7]. Therefore, NfL may reflect cumulative injury from both CNS and PNS sources. This difference in biological origin allows exploratory assessment of whether concurrent PNS involvement differentially influences circulating axonal versus astroglial biomarkers.

Both markers have been widely studied, with higher serum concentrations reported in association with neuroaxonal damage and disease-related activity in MS [8–11]. However, previous studies have not specifically investigated whether these biomarkers can distinguish MS patients with concomitant PNS involvement from those without, or how peripheral nerve pathology may confound their interpretation.

This study aimed to examine cross-sectional associations between serum NfL and GFAP levels, MS disease course, and the presence of electrophysiologically confirmed large-fibre polyneuropathy. Specifically, we sought to explore whether concurrent peripheral nervous system involvement is associated with altered circulating biomarker levels in patients with MS, and to assess how age, sex, disease form, and treatment context may influence these associations. This study was not designed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy or prognostic prediction.

Materials and methods

This study incorporated data from investigations evaluating serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) and healthy controls. A total of 149 patients with MS and 40 healthy controls were included to compare biomarker levels and to establish reference values, thereby enabling differentiation between disease-related changes and physiological variability. Although the primary objective focused on comparing MS patients with and without polyneuropathy (PNP), the inclusion of a control group allowed contextualisation of biomarker concentrations.

Study population and selection criteria

Patients with MS were retrospectively identified from the registry of Pauls Stradins Clinical University Hospital between October 2022 and September 2023. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of MS according to the 2017 McDonald criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 70 years, (2) confirmed diagnosis of any MS subtype, (3) availability of serum NfL and GFAP measurements, and (4) Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score between 0.0 and 6.5. The EDSS is a validated clinical scale developed by John F. Kurtzke (1983) [12] and widely used to assess disability in MS.

Exclusion criteria included the presence of acute infection, recent trauma, or other neurological or systemic conditions known to significantly affect biomarker levels, where such information was available.

Healthy controls were recruited from individuals without neurological complaints or known systemic or inflammatory diseases. Medical history was reviewed to exclude comorbidities known to influence neuroaxonal biomarkers, including renal disease, diabetes mellitus, and other relevant systemic conditions. Controls were selected to represent a neurologically healthy population and were broadly comparable to the patient group in terms of age and sex distribution.

The study was approved by the Riga Stradins University Medical Ethics Committee (Institutional Review Board reference no: 2-PĒK-4/508/2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Clinical assessment and group classification

All patients underwent nerve conduction studies (NCSs) performed by a certified neurophysiology specialist following a standardized PNP examination protocol. Bilateral upper extremity NCSs included motor and sensory assessments of the ulnar and median nerves, while lower extremity studies assessed motor function of the peroneal and tibial nerves and sensory function of the sural nerve. Parameters evaluated included latency, amplitude, and conduction velocity.

Polyneuropathy was defined according to Latvian clinical practice standards as the presence of more than one abnormal parameter in at least two separate nerves. Patients meeting these criteria were classified as having large fibre PNP.

To reduce misclassification and account for common non-MS-related causes of PNP, clinical records were reviewed for relevant comorbidities and exposures, including diabetes mellitus, alcohol misuse, renal disease, thyroid disorders, prior chemotherapy or other neurotoxic treatments, and vitamin deficiencies when such data were available. Due to the registry-based nature of the study, a standardized laboratory screening panel (e.g., vitamin B12, folate, HbA1c, TSH) was not consistently available for all participants and therefore could not be applied as strict exclusion criteria.

Patients were further categorised according to MS clinical phenotype into relapsing-remitting (RRMS), secondary progressive (SPMS), and primary progressive (PPMS) forms.

Clinical and demographic variables collected included age, sex, disease duration, EDSS score, clinical phenotype, and MRI lesion count where available.

Blood sampling and biomarker analysis

Blood samples were collected by certified medical personnel in an outpatient setting and processed within one hour of collection. Samples were drawn into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes and centrifuged at 20 °C and 3500 rpm for 10 min. Serum was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Serum NfL and GFAP concentrations were measured using a single-molecule array assay (Simoa; Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed in a single batch by board-certified laboratory technicians blinded to clinical data. Intra-assay coefficients of variation were below 10%.

All patients were receiving disease-modifying therapy at the time of blood sampling. Consequently, measured biomarker levels reflect treated disease states rather than treatment-naïve conditions.

Statistical analyses were performed to investigate the relationships between NfL, GFAP, and relevant clinical parameters. The data distribution was assessed for normality using visual inspection of normal Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. These assessments revealed that serum NfL and GFAP concentrations, significantly deviated from a normal distribution. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to determine the strength and direction of the associations of NfL and GFAP with age, EDSS score, and lesion number. Rank-biserial correlation was conducted to assess the association between NfL and sex. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was performed to assess differences in NfL and GFAP levels across MS disease courses. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare NfL and GFAP levels between disease forms. Generalized Linear Model (GLM) analysis with Gamma distribution was performed to compare NfL and GFAP levels between distinct research groups, with age and sex included as covariates to control for their potential confounding effects. Because disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) can influence circulating NfL levels by reducing inflammatory activity, treatment status was considered a potential confounder. Medication categories were summarized descriptively, and exploratory comparisons of NfL across treatment groups were performed (Fig. 1). Given the limited sample size in several medication strata, treatment category was not included as a primary covariate in the main regression model; therefore, medication-related confounding cannot be excluded. Statistical data analysis was performed using Jamovi (v.2.5). Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

We examined NfL level differences across MS disease courses and forms as an additional finding. A statistically significant difference in NfL levels was observed across MS disease courses (χ2(2) = 6.33, p = 0.042). Specifically, NfL levels were significantly lower in patients with RRMS (median = 8.25, interquartile range [IQR]: 6.36–10.4) than in patients with SPMS (median = 13.7, IQR: 9.74–16.3). Furthermore, NfL levels also differed significantly between cerebral (median = 9.55, IQR: 7.89–16.0) and cerebrospinal (median = 8.16, IQR: 6.33–10.6) disease forms (Mann–Whitney U = 863, p = 0.043, r = 0.28). No statistically significant differences in GFAP levels were observed across MS disease courses or between disease forms (p > 0.05). These between-treatment differences should be interpreted cautiously, as treatment allocation is non-random and may reflect baseline disease activity and disability.

Finally, we compared NfL levels across different medication groups and observed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001). These findings are visually represented in Fig. 1.

Discussion

A biomarker is an objectively measurable indicator that reflects a biological state or condition and response to therapy [13]. Ideally, biomarkers should be safe, specific, sensitive, widely available, and cost-effective. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), with particular emphasis on assessing whether coexisting large-fibre polyneuropathy (PNP) influences circulating biomarker concentrations.

From a mechanistic perspective, neurofilament light chain is expressed in axons throughout both the central and peripheral nervous systems, and its release into the circulation is not specific to central nervous system (CNS) pathology. Therefore, in patients with MS and concomitant PNP, serum NfL levels may reflect cumulative neuroaxonal injury arising from both CNS demyelination and peripheral nerve damage. This complicates interpretation, particularly when attempting to attribute biomarker elevations solely to MS-related processes.

In our study, serum NfL levels were significantly higher in patients with MS compared to healthy controls, consistent with previous reports [14, 15]. However, differences between MS patients with and without PNP were attenuated after adjustment for age and sex, suggesting that these factors act as important confounders. Although NfL levels were numerically higher in the MS + PNP group, the lack of a robust independent effect limits definitive conclusions regarding the specific contribution of peripheral nerve damage. An additional consideration is the lack of detailed electrophysiological classification of polyneuropathy into axonal and demyelinating subtypes. Given that NfL primarily reflects axonal injury, differences in neuropathy subtype could influence biomarker levels. The absence of this distinction limits more precise interpretation of the contribution of peripheral nerve pathology to circulating NfL concentrations.

The interpretation of NfL is further complicated by its known elevation in a range of neurological conditions, including chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Alzheimer’s disease [16–18]. This reinforces the concept that serum NfL reflects general neuroaxonal injury rather than disease-specific pathology.

Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) represent an additional important confounder. Recent work emphasizes the importance of context-aware interpretation of NfL, including the use of age-adjusted Z-scores and consideration of treatment status [19]. In the present study, absolute biomarker values were analysed, with age included as a covariate in regression models to account for its confounding effect. Age-adjusted Z-scores were not utilised, as the study design was cross-sectional and not intended to establish clinical reference thresholds or individual-level risk stratification. Additionally, validated population-specific reference datasets required for Z-score calculation were not available for our cohort. Therefore, the analytical approach focused on relative group comparisons rather than standardised individual-level interpretation. In our cohort, differences in NfL levels across treatment groups likely reflect both therapeutic effects and confounding by indication, as patients with higher disease activity may be more likely to receive certain therapies. Furthermore, biomarker measurements were obtained while patients were already receiving treatment, which may have influenced circulating NfL levels. As such, the observed biomarker concentrations likely reflect treated disease activity rather than baseline, untreated neuroaxonal injury. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations observed under real-world conditions rather than treatment-independent effects.

GFAP, as a marker of astroglial activation, has been proposed as a complementary biomarker more closely linked to CNS pathology. In this study, GFAP levels were higher in MS patients compared to controls; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between MS subgroups. These findings are consistent with reports suggesting that GFAP may have limited sensitivity as a standalone biomarker, particularly in heterogeneous clinical populations [20, 21]. Furthermore, GFAP expression is not entirely restricted to the CNS, which may further reduce its specificity in serum-based analyses. This broader expression profile may contribute to variability in serum GFAP levels and further limits its specificity as a marker of purely CNS-derived astroglial pathology, particularly in patients with potential peripheral or systemic involvement.

Exploratory analyses revealed associations between NfL levels and MS disease course, with higher levels observed in progressive forms compared to relapsing-remitting disease. While these findings are consistent with previous studies [18], they should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study was not designed to assess prognostic performance or disease progression. Rather, these results provide supportive, cross-sectional observations that align with existing literature on neuroaxonal injury in MS.

Although our findings are broadly consistent with studies demonstrating elevated NfL levels in MS, direct conclusions regarding disease monitoring, progression, or treatment response cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional design. Instead, our results highlight the importance of considering comorbid conditions—such as polyneuropathy—when interpreting circulating biomarkers.

Taken together, this study underscores the complexity of biomarker interpretation in MS, particularly in the presence of concurrent peripheral nervous system involvement. Future studies incorporating longitudinal designs and additional comparator groups, including patients with isolated PNP, would help clarify the relative contributions of central and peripheral neuroaxonal injury.

Limitations

Unfortunately, during the statistical analysis, we found that our patient group did not exhibit a normal distribution, likely because of the small cohort size. This introduced certain limitations and may have affected the results. Some studies suggest that serum NfL levels may vary depending on whether neuronal loss—typically associated with disease exacerbation—is occurring at the time of sample collection or during a prior relapse episode [22]. In this study, blood samples were collected randomly, without accounting for the presence or absence of exacerbation.

Another limitation is the use of absolute NfL values rather than age-adjusted Z-scores, which have been proposed to improve interpretability across diverse populations. The lack of validated reference datasets for the study population and the cross-sectional design limited the feasibility of applying such standardisation. Future studies incorporating Z-score-based approaches may provide more refined assessment of biomarker variability.

As a retrospective study involving patients who had received disease-modifying therapy, another limitation is the lack of detailed data on the impact of medications on serum NfL and GFAP levels. A larger study cohort and analysis of potential interactions between biomarker levels and therapeutic agents are needed in future research. Additionally, the inclusion of a separate group of MS-free but PNP-positive patients would be a valuable component of a further investigation.

An important limitation is that the aetiology of polyneuropathy could not be fully adjudicated for all participants. Although we used standardized nerve conduction criteria to define large-fibre PNP and screened medical records for common alternative causes, this cross-sectional registry-based design did not allow uniform exclusion of all non-MS contributors (e.g., subclinical diabetes, vitamin deficiencies, alcohol-related neuropathy). Therefore, the observed biomarker differences in the MS + PNP subgroup may reflect contributions from MS-related neuroaxonal injury, peripheral neuropathy of mixed aetiology, or both.

Conclusion

Our study supports serum NfL as a biomarker associated with MS disease course and disability, with higher levels observed in MS compared with healthy controls and differences across MS subtypes. In contrast, serum GFAP did not significantly differ between groups in this cross-sectional cohort, which may limit its sensitivity as an independent marker under real-world conditions. Importantly, comorbid large-fibre polyneuropathy may contribute to elevated NfL and potentially complicate interpretation of biomarker signals attributed solely to CNS pathology. Future studies with longitudinal follow-up and systematic evaluation of polyneuropathy aetiologies are warranted to clarify how combined CNS and PNS injury influences circulating biomarkers.
