COVID-19 infection prior to the onset of type 1 diabetes does not impair beta-cell function: a two-year nationwide follow-up study
Authors: Morten Bjerregaard-Andersen, Pernille Emilie Hostrup, Kristoffer Jensen Kolnes, Maj Bangshaab, Alin Razvan Andries, Jessica Da Silva, Eugenia Carvalho, Troels Krarup Hansen, Peter Vestergaard, Flemming Pociot, Kurt Højlund, Claus Bogh Juhl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA possible association between infection with the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and type 1 diabetes (T1D) development has been reported. Whether SARS-CoV-2 infection affects beta-cell loss in T1D over time is unknown. We investigated this in adults with new-onset T1D.MethodsIndividuals with new T1D were included from six Danish hospitals between December 2020 and January 2023. Within one month after the diagnosis of T1D, we assessed medical history, previous self-reported COVID-19 infection, COVID-19 vaccination, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), and SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (Cov2IgG). A mixed-meal tolerance test (MMTT) was performed after overnight fast at baseline, and after 12 and 24 months to assess plasma glucose and C-peptide at 15–60 min intervals for up to 150 min. The main outcome was C-peptide area under curve (AUCC−peptide), as a measure of residual beta-cell function, in Cov2IgG negative and positive individuals.ResultsA total of 94 individuals with T1D were included (34% were female) and 90 had available MMTT results. The mean age was 30.9 (SD 10.2) years, the mean HbA1c was 108 ± 25 mmol/mol (12.1 ± 2.29%), at enrolment. 17% were Cov2IgG positive at enrolment, indicating preceding COVID-19. The baseline median AUCC−peptide was 652 (25–75%: 458–937) pmol/l for Cov2IgG negative vs. 703 (564–1099) pmol/L for Cov2IgG positive individuals (P = 0.29). After 12 months, (N = 81), the AUCC−peptide was 662 (394–892) vs. 594 (532–973) pmol/L in the two groups, respectively (P = 0.57). After 24 months, (N = 47), the AUCC−peptide was 452 (244–792) vs. 535 (380–882) pmol/L (P = 0.55). Seroconversion to CoV2IgG positivity did not affect AUCC−peptide at 12 months (P = 0.53). Cov2IgG status did not alter HbA1c at baseline or follow-up (P = 0.86).ConclusionsAmong persons with new-onset T1D, 17% were Cov2IgG positive during the pandemic, which was higher than in the Danish background population. Background

The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the virus responsible for the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, has caused considerable mortality and morbidity worldwide [1]. Several reports suggest that the long-term consequences of COVID-19 extend beyond the acute infection, including persistent neurological and cardiopulmonary symptoms, collectively referred to as “Long COVID” [2, 3].

In the context of endocrine health, individuals with type 1 diabetes are at greater risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes, particularly those with poor glycaemic control and older age groups [4, 5]. However, the potential role of SARS-CoV-2 in the development of type 1 diabetes remains debatable. It has been suggested that SARS-CoV-2 may contribute to type 1 diabetes development by directly attacking the insulin secreting beta-cells via binding to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE-2) receptor, highly expressed in pancreatic tissue [6, 7], or by accelerating the autoimmune destruction of the beta cells, which is a key pathogenic mechanism in type 1 diabetes progression [8].

Several large-scale investigations and meta-analyses have reported an increased risk of diabetes following COVID-19 infection [9–11]. For example, a systematic review including 42 studies found higher incidence of type 1 diabetes and diabetes ketoacidosis during the pandemic, calling for further investigations to elucidate direct and indirect effects of the virus [12]. However, other register-based studies from Denmark and Scotland report no association between PCR diagnosed SARS-CoV-2 infection and type 1 diabetes [13–15]. Additionally, data from the global SWEET register demonstrated an unchanged annual increase in new-onset type 1 diabetes among children during the COVID-19 pandemic, though the seasonal patterns of type 1 diabetes development appeared to be affected [16]. Furthermore, a nationwide German study providing detailed spatiotemporal associations between the incidence of COVID-19 and type 1 diabetes did not find any association [17], and similar conclusions were reported from Japan [18] and Italy [19]. In Finland, a rise in type 1 diabetes incidence among children and adolescents was observed in the first 18 months of the COVID-19 epidemic, compared to previous years, but less than 1% of the study population had confirmed COVID-19 preceding type 1 diabetes [20] and COVID-19 was therefore not considered a factor driving the rising type 1 diabetes incidence. In Portugal, our group found no significant increase in predominantly paediatric type 1 diabetes when comparing the years before and during the COVID-19 epidemic, nor were glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) or fasting C-peptide affected [21]. A study in individuals with dysglycaemia due to acute COVID-19 reported no deterioration in C-peptide secretion after one year, though glycaemic control worsened [22].

Importantly, no studies have longitudinally assessed loss of beta cell function in individuals with new type 1 diabetes after a preceding COVID-19 infection. Some individuals with new type 1 diabetes retain an insulin secretion during the early years after the diagnosis [23], with beta cell loss generally occurring more rapidly in those below 18 years [24]. In the United States, residual insulin secretion was present in almost one third of patients with type 1 diabetes three years after the diagnosis [25]. Yet, whether prior SARS-CoV-2 infection accelerates loss of beta cell function is not determined. If confirmed, such a finding could have implications for monitoring of glycaemic control and insulin requirement in individuals with new type 1 diabetes with previous COVID-19.

We now report two years of longitudinal data on the beta cell function, using mixed meal tolerance test (MMTT), among adults with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes, according to SARS-CoV-2 antibody status.

Methods

Recruitment

Participants in the cohort were enrolled at University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Esbjerg (SVS); Steno Diabetes Center Odense (SDCO); Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen (SDCC); Steno Diabetes Center Aarhus (SDCA), Steno Diabetes Center Northern Jutland (SDCN), and University Hospital Lillebaelt (SLB), from December 2020 to early January 2023. The young adult patients were identified as newly diagnosed with type 1 diabetes at either hospital wards or outpatient clinics and were invited to participate. We aimed for enrolment to take place within a month of diagnosis, although this was not always possible for practical reasons. The diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (stage 3) was established through an overall clinical evaluation, supported by diabetes-related autoantibodies.

Clinical questionnaire and measurements

After obtaining informed consent, the local study investigators filled out an electronic questionnaire regarding demographic and clinical characteristics, medical history, indicative symptoms of and exposure to COVID-19. During the study, it was also added to the questionnaire whether COVID-19 had previously actually been diagnosed, confirmed with either PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) or Quick-test, according to the patient. Information regarding COVID-19 vaccinations was added in in early 2021. Height, weight and blood pressure were measured using standard clinical devices.

Biochemical analyses

Fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c had been measured at the time of diagnosis. In most cases, Glutamic Acid Decarboxylase (GAD) or Islet Antigen 2 (IA2) were also measured, and C-peptide when relevant to determine the T1D diagnosis (not reported).

Mixed meal tolerance test

A mixed meal tolerance test (MMTT) was carried out in all participants on the day of enrolment. The MMTT is considered the “gold standard” in measuring endogenous insulin secretion in T1D [26]. The test was performed in the morning, after an overnight fast, consisting of a standardised liquid meal (Nutridrink®, Nutricia, Aarhus, Denmark) ingested over 10 min. The meal was weight adjusted (6 mL/kg), to a maximum of 360 mL and contained 150 kcal per 100 mL. Major macronutrients composition was fat 5.8 g, carbohydrates 18.4 g and protein 5.9 g, per 100 mL. Long-acting insulin was administered only the day before the visit, whereas short-acting insulin could be administered up to two hours prior to MMTT. After the test was concluded, the patient was offered food and encouraged to take the appropriate units of insulin.

During the test, blood samples for serum C-peptide and plasma glucose analyses were collected at 30-minutes intervals over the next 150 min (0, 30, 60, 90, 150), to calculate C-peptide area under curve (AUCC−peptide). Individuals included at SDCC also took part in a randomized clinical trial testing fenofibrate in type 1 diabetes (EudraCT number: 2019-004434-41) and according to the protocol the MMTT terminated at 120 min. At the SDCC, there was also an additional C-peptide measurement at 15 min. All calculated AUCC−peptide were divided by test length in minutes to adjust for variation in duration and reported as pmol/L.

The samples collected at the SDCC were analyzed locally, while serum C-peptide measurements from SDCO and SVS were carried out at Odense University Hospital (OUH), within a few days of sampling. From the SDCA and SDCN the serum samples were centrifuged immediately and stored frozen (-80 °C) and transported to the OUH for C-peptide analyses once a sufficient number of samples had been obtained, meaning within 6–9 months of sampling. At both the SDCC and OUH, a Cobas e411 immunoassay analyzer assay (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland, ) was used for C-peptide measurement. A previous Danish study reported a good assay coefficient of variation of 3.0% [27].

At 12 months and 24 months after baseline a follow-up was conducted with repeat MMTT. We also repeated CoV2igG antibodies measurements here, although by the end of the follow-up period, the CoV2igG assay unfortunately became unavailable.

SARS-CoV-2 antibodies

During MMTT, we also collected blood for measuring SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (CoV2IgG). These analyses were all carried out at OUH, using a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) Alinity I assay (Abbott®, Lake Forest, Illinois, USA). The antibody assay quantified IgG antibodies targeting the SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid protein, and it was therefore unaffected by COVID-19 vaccination, which is based on the Spike protein. Except for samples from SDCO and SVS, which were analyzed shortly after collection, antibody analyses at the remaining sites were conducted using frozen plasma.

C-peptide90 analysis

In children and adolescents, C-peptide90 has been found to correlate to overall AUCC−peptide and hence suggested as a simpler alternative to a full MMTT [28]. We therefore included this comparison.

Statistics

All data were stored in RedCap and analysed using STATA 15® (StataCorp, Texas, USA) and GraphPad Prism® (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, California, USA). We display demographic, clinical, questionnaire, vaccination status and biochemical data of the patients with new-onset type 1 diabetes in percentages for categorical variables. For continuous data, we report in means with standard deviation (SD). Average daily insulin dosage was expressed as medians with 25–75 percentiles.

From the collected stimulated C-peptides, we calculated the AUCC−peptide in STATA. At the SDCC, the AUCC−peptide had already been calculated in the FENO trial, using the trapezoid method. Due to non-normal distribution, AUCC−peptide results were reported as medians. As the study’s primary outcome, we compared median AUCC−peptides among participants that were CoV2igG negative vs. positive at baseline, using the Mann-Whitney non-parametric test. This was done both at baseline, 12 and 24 months. Similarly, we also analysed whether conversion to CoV2igG positivity at 12 months affected AUCC−peptide, compared to those that remained negative. Trends in overall AUCc−peptide at enrolment and 12- and 24-months follow-ups were examined with ANOVA.

In addition, we examined whether lower age, lower BMI or higher GAD antibody levels at type 1 diabetes diagnosis resulted in a more rapid loss of beta-cell function, measured by AUCC−peptide at 24 months, using linear regression. This way, we also examined the correlation between the AUCC−peptide and C-Peptide90.

Change in HbA1c was stratified by SARS-CoV-2 antibody status and compared at enrolment, 12 months and 24 months, using ANOVA. Finally, at baseline, we analysed data on previously COVID-19 (confirmed by Quick-test or PCR, but self-reported) with the obtained CoV2igG results, using Chi- square test. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Power calculation

Prior to the study, we did not have exact Danish type 1 diabetes incidence numbers. The enrolment was therefore pragmatic, aiming to have good representation from each participating site. Assuming a 30% reduction in AUCc-peptide caused by SARS-CoV-2 infection, i.e. a clinically relevant finding, this would require approximately 74 vs. 15 individuals in the CoV2igG negative vs. positive group respectively, which is close to the achieved sample size.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 97 newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes patients consented to participate. In one patient it was not possible to collect blood samples due to very fragile veins, and one patient was excluded due to failed procedures during MMTT. Finally, one patient was later classified as having type 2 diabetes and was excluded. We, therefore, present results from 94 patients with new-onset type 1 diabetes (1enrolled in December 2020, 51 in 2021, 40 in 2022, and 2 in January 2023). The median time between type 1 diabetes diagnosis and inclusion was 28 days (25–75%: 21–38).

The mean age at enrolment was 30.9 years (SD 10.2) and 34% were female (Table 1). The mean BMI was 23.2 kg/m2 (SD 3.4). The total median daily doses of long acting and short acting insulin were 10 (25–75%: 6–16) and 9 (6–16) units, respectively. Insulin pumps were not used at inclusion, as diabetes had only recently been diagnosed. One patient did not require insulin at baseline. Information on diabetes ketoacidosis was not available.

At baseline, we collected data on the participants’ exposure to COVID-19 (self-reported), previous symptoms indicative of COVID-19 (self-reported) and previous COVID-19 (diagnosed with PCR or Quick-test, but self-reported). Symptoms data were available for all (N = 94), and exposure data for the vast majority (N = 92). Unfortunately, previous self-reported COVID-19 was not part of the initial questionnaire but only added later. This was due to SARS-COV-2 antibody testing being done for all participants, and that PCR and Quick-test was less available to the public in the early epidemic (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for study flow).

Thus, at baseline, 63 patients had data on previous COVID-19 (PCR/Quick-test confirmed), with COVID-19 reported in 35% (22/63). Previous exposure to COVID-19 (i.e. contact with a COVID-19 infected patient) was reported in 34% (31/92). 17% of the study participants (16/94) reported previous symptoms indicative of COVID-19, with the most common symptoms being fatigue (81%, 13/16), fever (63%, 10/16), cough (56%, 9/16) and sore throat (50%, 8/16).

GAD antibodies were analysed in 77 patients, with 56 being GAD positive (73%). IA2 antibodies results were measured in 23 patients, with 17 (74%) being positive, adding up to 85% (66/78) being either GAD or IA2 positive.

SARS-CoV-2 status

At enrolment

A positive SARS-CoV-2 antibody (CoV2igG) response was found in 17 of 94 individuals. However, two patients reported having COVID-19 after being diagnosed with diabetes, with symptoms beginning only shortly before enrolment. One of these two participants was CoV2igG negative, while the other turned out to be CoV2igG positive but was re-classified as negative, since the infection was deemed to have occurred after type 1 diabetes developed. Thus, a total of 16 of the patients (17%, 16/94) had a positive antibody test at the time of enrolment (Tables 1 and 2). This prevalence increased with calendar year, though not significantly, e.g. 0% (0/1) in December 2020, 12% (6/51) in 2021, 23% (9/40) in 2022 and 50% (1/2) in January 2023 were Cov2igG positive (P for trend = 0.31).

During follow-up

Many participants were infected by SARS-CoV-2 within the first two years. Thus, at 12 and 24 months the percentage of participants with positive CoV2igG results was 24% (19/78) and 38% (9/24), respectively. The reason for fewer available results at 24 months was loss to follow-up, partly due to lack of time, movement or gradually less interest in COVID-19. Also, towards the end of the follow-up period, the SARS-CoV-2 assay became unavailable.

Mixed meal tolerance test results

Ninety type 1 diabetes patients had valid MMTT results available, from which the AUCC−peptides could be calculated at enrolment. Due to loss of follow-up, the number of MMTTs was 81 after 12 months and 47 after 24 months, respectively. The median time between enrolment and 12-month follow-up was 366 days (25–75%: 362–383), and between the last two MMTTs the median was 364 days (358–373).

AUCC−peptide according to SARS-CoV-2 antibody status

At inclusion, for those patients with valid MMTT, the median AUCC−peptide was 653 (474–994) pmol/L (N = 90). Here, stratified by CoV2IgG status, the median AUCC−peptide was 652 (25–75%: 458–937) pmol/L in CoV2IgG negative vs. 703 (564–1099) pmol/L in CoV2IgG positive T1D patients (P = 0.29). At 12 months follow-up, the AUCC−peptide was 662 (394–892) vs. 594 (532–973) pmol/L in the two groups, respectively (P = 0.57). After 24 months, the AUCC−peptide was 452 (244–792) vs. 535 (380–882) pmol/L (P = 0.55) (Fig. 2).

AUCC−peptide at (a) enrolment, (b) 12 months follow-up, (c) 24 months follow-up by SARS-CoV-2 status

Overall, the median AUCC−peptide declined over time, and at two years it was 468 (302–882) pmol/L (P for trend = 0.025) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Among the 65 type 1 diabetes patients, who were CoV2IgG negative at enrolment, and where 12 months follow-up data was also available, 13 had converted to CoV2IgG positivity during the first year of study, while the remaining 52 remained negative. AUCC−peptide at 12 months was similar in these two subgroups, i.e. 655 (405–864) and 758 (287–1066) pmol/L, respectively (P = 0.53). Unfortunately, there were too few patient data to repeat this analysis at 24 months.

Stratifying the baseline comparison of AUCC−peptide according to SARS-CoV-2 antibody status in groups of high BMI ( > = 23 kg/m2) and low BMI (< 23 kg/m2) did not affect the results (P = 0.39 and 0.90, respectively). The same was observed when stratifying by age older or younger than 28 years (P = 0.86 and P = 0.12, respectively).

Loss of insulin secretion according to age, BMI and GAD autoantibodies at T1D diagnosis

We observed lower insulin secretion at 24 months if type 1 diabetes was diagnosed at a younger age (P = 0.04) or with a lower BMI (P = 0.02), while GAD autoantibody titre at diagnosis did not affect the rate of decline (P = 0.42) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, mean age and BMI was fairly even across the various enrolment sites.

AUCC−peptide and C-peptide90

We examined the value of a single C-peptide by 90 min, compared to full MMTT. At both enrolment, and 12- and 24-months follow-up, we observed a strong correlation between AUCC-peptide and C-peptide90 (P < 0.001, correlation coefficient = 0.98–0.99).

AUCC-peptide according to enrolment site

As different MMTT lengths (120 vs. 150 min) were applied at the SDCC vs. the rest of enrolment sites, we investigated whether this affected the CoV2IgG negative vs. positive comparison in AUCC-peptide. However, no differences were observed in baseline AUCC-peptide between the two groups, both at the SDCC (P = 0.39) and at the other centres combined (P = 0.55).

COVID-19 vaccination

A total of 52 participants (55%, 52/94) had received a COVID-19 vaccine before enrolment, with the vast majority vaccinated with a mRNA type (Pfizer-BioNTech (N = 30) or Moderna (N = 19)). When the analysis was restricted to unvaccinated individuals with available MMTT data (N = 39), there was no significant differences in AUCC−peptides levels at enrolment between CoV2igG negative vs. positive individuals, i.e. 622 (504–851) vs. 641 (570–955) (P = 0.64).

Among the 16 individuals that were CoV2igG positive at enrolment, 11 (69%) had received a COVID-19 vaccine prior to type 1 diabetes diagnosis.

Trend in glycaemic regulation

At enrolment, the mean HbA1c was 12.1 ± 2.29% (108.4 ± 25.0 mmol/mol) in the total group (N = 92). Approximately 12 and 24 months after the diagnosis, HbA1c was 6.67 ± 0.92% (49.4 ± 10.1 mmol/mol) (N = 77), and 6.77 ± 1.19% (50.4 ± 13.1 mmol/mol) (N = 43), respectively, with no differences according to SARS-CoV-2 status (P for trend = 0.86) (Fig. 3).

Self-reporting of previous COVID-19 and SARS-CoV-2 status

At enrolment, among 41 individuals who self-reported no history of COVID-19, only five (12%) tested positive for CoV2igG. In 22 persons who reported previous COVID-19 (PCR/Quick-test confirmed, but self-reported), the CoV2igG test was positive in nine cases (41%) (P = 0.009).

Using self-reported COVID-19 as the exposure variable, instead of Cov2IgG, did not make a statistical difference for the AUCC−peptide comparisons at both enrolment (P = 0.70), 12 months (P = 0.93) and 24 months (P = 0.84).

Noteworthy, in individuals with new type 1 diabetes, having a previous PCR or Quick test confirming COVID-19 correlated strongly with symptoms indicative of COVID-19 at that time (P < 0.001).

Discussion

Main findings

Previous SARS-CoV-2 infection verified by CoV2IgG antibody positivity was common among new adult type 1 diabetes patients and increasing over time, i.e. 17% overall. No detrimental effect on beta cell function by SARS-CoV-2 infection was observed at diabetes diagnosis, nor did the virus affect beta cell function over the subsequent 24 months. HbA1c trends were also unaffected.

Consistency with previous findings

The prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in our study was comparatively high, compared to the Danish background population. Since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, several SARS-CoV-2 serosurveys have been carried out in various groups such as blood donors, children’s day care and preschool staff and in persons identified by random sampling. The results have found a gradually increasing CoV2iG prevalence, with estimates ranging from 7.6% to 9.1% in early 2021 [29, 30]. The burden was particularly high in certain risk groups, i.e. in social housing areas, where people tended to live closer and self-isolation was more difficult [31]. The fact that we found an overall burden of 17% could signify susceptibility towards COVID-19 exposure, possibly due to the predominantly young age-group, or alternatively a role of the virus in type 1 diabetes development.

While the precise pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes remains elusive, it is assumed that a complex relationship between genetic susceptibility, environment and the immune system is the key in autoimmune beta cell destruction [32]. As for environmental factors, certain viruses such as enterovirus (e.g. Coxsackie B and Rhinovirus) and respiratory viruses (e.g. Influenza and Parainfluenza) are suggested as type 1 diabetes triggers [33–35]. Recently, a trial administering Pleconaril and Ribavirin against enterovirus in new-onset type 1 diabetes demonstrated a significant beta cell preservation [36]. Noteworthy, in the Northern Hemisphere, the type 1 diabetes incidence is higher in the colder winter months where viral respiratory infections are more common [33]. This theory, where the disease is triggered by specific viruses [34], has been named the “viral hypothesis”. Arguably, the higher proportion of CoV2igG antibodies in persons with new-onset type 1 diabetes in our study supports this theory.

It is currently well accepted that the SARS-CoV-2 virus can infect cells via the ACE-2 receptor [37], which is found throughout the body, including in the pancreas where this may cause beta cell damage and dysfunction [7]. The role of SARS-CoV-2 virus in autoimmunity is much less understood. In Portugal, we observed an increase in GAD antibodies in young persons with type 1 diabetes during the pandemic [21]. A study in children at high risk of type 1 diabetes, identified by genetic testing and family history, the development of islet autoantibodies was more common after SARS-CoV-2 infection [8]. In Finland, children with new-onset type 1 diabetes tested more frequently positive for GAD antibodies during the pandemic [20]. Interestingly, an increase type 1 diabetes cases was also observed, possibly suggesting that lockdown measures such as social distancing may have reduced the immune systems’ exposure to a broad range of viral and bacterial infections, resulting in a higher type 1 diabetes incidence [20]. This is sometimes referred to as the “hygiene hypothesis”, i.e. exposure to some infections could be protective towards the disease [38].

In our study, many participants retained significant insulin secretion, with significant correlations shown for higher age and elevated BMI. GAD antibody titres, however, did not affect the course of beta cell loss [39]. Previous studies find that autoimmunity and beta-cell loss is more pronounced in children and adolescents, and distinct sub-types are now recognised for type 1 diabetes [40].

Overall, SARS-CoV-2 did not affect beta cell function at type 1 diabetes onset, nor the gradual loss over the subsequent 24 months. Likewise, we did not find an effect of conversion to SARS-CoV-2 positive antibodies on AUCC−peptide during the first 12 months. This aligns with register studies from e.g. Denmark and Scotland where no effect of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection on type 1 diabetes development was found [13–15]. Notably, our cohort was well regulated, with near normalisation of HbA1c after one year, likely resulting in less beta cell stress. It could be speculated that this would be extra beneficial to those infected by the SARS-CoV-2, which also affects the beta cells, causing less difference in AUCC−peptides at follow-up.

The current investigation was planned during the early pandemic, and some participants were therefore included before the rapid rollout of COVID-19 vaccines. Assuming SARS-CoV-2 infection being part of type 1 diabetes pathogenesis, the vaccines may ameliorate this and protect against beta-cell loss. Yet, we found no difference in AUCc−peptide between the two CoV2IgG groups, when examining only those who were COVID-19 unvaccinated prior to type 1 diabetes.

We collected data on self-reported previous COVID-19 infection. However, anticipated previous COVID-19 infection did not affect the AUCC−peptide results either. Notably, while there was a good correlation between the self-reported COVID-19 and biochemical CoV2igG results, most participants with self-reported previous COVID-19 were in fact CoV2igG negative, likely indicating some antibody waning [41].

Finally, our study confirmed that C-peptide at 90 min correlated strongly with overall AUCC−peptide, at both baseline and follow-up. As others note, a stimulated C-peptide90 can often be used, if full MMTT is not feasible.

Strengths and limitations

Our study is the first examining beta-cell function over time in new type 1 diabetes in relation to SARS-CoV-2 infection. It features a relatively large sample and a well-distributed enrolment across geographic regions in Denmark. Most importantly, it comprises two years of follow-up of beta-cell function in new type 1 diabetes, with or without preceding COVID-19. As it was initiated during the early epidemic in 2020, it was also possible to study the effect of temporal trends in SARS-CoV-2 status and COVID-19 vaccinations.

The study has, however, several limitations. First, lack of follow-up was present, especially at two years.

SARS-CoV-2 antibodies were not measured at diabetes diagnosis, but at baseline MMTT. Participants could potentially develop COVID-19 between these two points. Yet, on average enrolment took place within one month of type 1 diabetes diagnosis (median 28 days), a reasonably short time span. Also, only two participants reported COVID-19 indicative symptoms in the time between type 1 diabetes diagnosis and enrolment.

The MMTTs had different lengths at the SDCC (120 min), as the test was already in use here prior to our study, and the other sites where we chose to apply the standard 150 min test. Although we adjusted for this by dividing AUCC−peptide by test length, further sensitivity analyses would be desirable. Unfortunately, C-peptide measurements at 120 min were only performed at the SDCC, and we have therefore not done this. AUCc−peptide according to SARS-CoV-2 status was, however, not affected when stratifying by the SDCC and the other centres combined. Importantly, although no specific harmonisation between the sites was done, the same type of C-peptide assay was used at both SDCC and at OUH, and the lack of harmonisation was therefore likely only a smaller issue.

The SARS-CoV-2 virus is constantly mutating, with different strains emerging [42]. These virus variants may affect beta cell function differently, but we do not have data on this.

Though we recorded some clinical symptoms prior to the type 1 diabetes diagnosis, particularly those indicative of COVID-19, we have limited data on disease severity. Others report that in severe COVID-19, i.e. requiring hospital treatment, the resultant inflammatory state may increase beta cell-stress [43].

Also, we did not have data available regarding diet and physical activity, although these factors may influence the immune system and thereby the degree of autoimmune beta cell loss. Likewise, the daily amount of insulin was only available at enrolment, but unfortunately not at follow-up.

Finally, individuals enrolled at the SDCC participated in a trial investigating Fenofibrate for preservation of beta-cell function. As reported by the investigators, however, no effect was found on C-peptide responses [44].

Implications

When initiating the study in late 2020, we hypothesized that persons with new type 1 diabetes would have a more rapid loss of beta-cell function if previously infected with SARS-CoV-2, thereby requiring more intensive insulin therapy and monitoring. Based on the present results, this is not the case. However, we recognize that there could be other lasting effects of COVID-19 among diabetes patients, including physiological such as increased anxiety and depression, complicating the daily care [45, 46].

Conclusions

At the time of type 1 diabetes diagnosis and at follow-up after 12 and 24 months, there was no difference in the endogenous insulin production, measured by MMTT, according to SARS-CoV-2 antibody status. Our study does therefore not support the presence of a significant or sustained detrimental effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on the beta-cell function in individuals with new-onset type 1 diabetes.
