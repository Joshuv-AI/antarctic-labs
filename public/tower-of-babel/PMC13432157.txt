Una doble amenaza: toxoplasmosis del SNC y encefalitis por CMV en un receptor detrasplante cardíaco
Authors: Salwa Alhammadi, Tianyu Zhang, Aliya Szpindel, Márcio Luís Duarte, Leonardo Freitas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 73-year-old immunocompromised woman with a history of heart transplant presented with cognitive decline and left-sided neurological deficits. Imaging revealed atypical brain lesions. Initial biopsy was inconclusive. Despite extensive infectious workup, diagnosis remained unclear until a second brain biopsy confirmed central nervous system toxoplasmosis and cytomegalovirus (CMV) encephalitis. Treatment with antiparasitic and antiviral agents was initiated, but the patient’s condition deteriorated, leading to palliative care. This case highlights diagnostic challenges in immunocompromised patients, the importance of considering atypical presentations of CNS infections, and the potential necessity of repeat biopsies when initial evaluations are non-diagnostic.

CONCEPTOS CLAVE

What is known on the subject?

Central nervous system (CNS) infections such as toxoplasmosis and cytomegalovirus (CMV) encephalitis are common in immunocompromised patients, particularly transplant recipients. Diagnosis can be challenging due to atypical imaging findings and non-specific clinical presentations. Brain biopsy is often necessary when imaging and laboratory results are inconclusive.

What does this work contribute?

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of CNS infections in immunosuppressed patients and underscores the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion even with negative initial tests. It emphasizes the value of repeat brain biopsy in achieving diagnosis and provides insight into the rare co-occurrence of CNS toxoplasmosis and CMV encephalitis.

Divulgación

La toxoplasmosis del sistema nervioso central (SNC) y la encefalitis por citomegalovirus (CMV) son infecciones oportunistas graves que afectan a personas inmunocomprometidas, especialmente receptores de trasplantes y pacientes con inmunosupresión. Aunque la toxoplasmosis suele mostrar hallazgos radiológicos característicos, la encefalitis por CMV presenta manifestaciones más inespecíficas y puede pasar desapercibida. Sus síntomas superpuestos y presentaciones atípicas dificultan el diagnóstico. Este contenido ofrece claves para el reconocimiento, diferenciación y manejo de estas infecciones, subrayando la utilidad de estudios avanzados y, en ciertos casos, la biopsia cerebral.

Introduction

A 73-year-old Russian female with a history of heart transplant 13 years prior for ischemic cardiomyopathy, maintained on cyclosporine and mycophenolate, presented in April 2023 with a 6-month subacute cognitive and functional decline. Symptoms included confusional episodes, hypersomnolence, apraxia, worsening mobility, and unintentional weight loss. Neurological exam showed left hemineglect, left pronator drift, and hemiparesis. She was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of atypical CNS infections in transplant recipients and underscores the role of repeat brain biopsy when initial evaluation is inconclusive.

Initial CT imaging revealed right-sided subcortical and periventricular white matter hypodensity, gyriform cortical calcifications, and focal cortical swelling (Figure 1). MRI confirmed vasogenic edema, leptomeningeal and cortico-subcortical enhancement, and a small non-enhancing lesion in the right caudate head (Figure 2). Additional non-enhancing lesions were seen in the left temporal lobe. Differential diagnoses included an inflammatory or infectious process versus post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD), while primary CNS tumor was considered unlikely.

Steroids (dexamethasone) were initiated, and an urgent brain biopsy was performed. Systemic imaging (thoracic, abdominal-pelvic CT) and PET-CT were negative for malignancy but showed hypermetabolic activity in the right cerebral lesion. Brain biopsy revealed a lympho-histiocytic infiltrate suggestive of a reactive process, with no evidence of lymphoma or progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for cytomegalovirus (CMV) and spirochetes was negative; toxoplasma staining was equivocal.

Extensive infectious and oncologic evaluation, including serologic tests, CSF studies, systemic imaging, and PET-CT, was unrevealing. The initial biopsy was non-diagnostic. Despite corticosteroid therapy, follow-up MRI demonstrated lesion progression and new enhancing foci, accompanied by worsening neurological deficits. Given the radiological progression and persistent diagnostic uncertainty, a second brain biopsy was performed.

This biopsy confirmed CNS toxoplasmosis via IHC and showed positive CMV PCR (Figure 3). Serum CMV levels also spiked significantly. Toxoplasma PCR from brain tissue remained negative. She was treated with oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and IV ganciclovir. Despite therapy, her condition deteriorated—developing left hemiplegia, acute kidney injury, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Given the poor prognosis, the decision was made to transition to palliative care.

CNS toxoplasmosis and CMV encephalitis are common in immunocompromised individuals1. Toxoplasmosis typically presents with characteristic MRI findings such as ring-enhancing lesions and surrounding edema; however, atypical manifestations may include solitary mass-like lesions that can mimic CNS lymphoma, as well as calcifications—which are uncommon and usually seen only in congenital cases or as a sequela of prior treatment1. CMV imaging findings are non-specific but may also present with mass-like lesions2,3.

Differential diagnoses in this patient included post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, primary CNS lymphoma, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, and other opportunistic infections. The absence of classic ring-enhancing lesions and the presence of cortical calcifications further complicated the diagnostic interpretation. A limitation of this case is the negative toxoplasma PCR results in both CSF and brain tissue, underscoring the limited sensitivity of molecular testing in focal CNS infections. In addition, prior corticosteroid therapy may have influenced histopathological findings. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in transplant recipients with progressive neurological deficits. When initial biopsy and ancillary studies are inconclusive, repeat tissue sampling should be considered. Chabaane et al. reported that a second brain biopsy provided diagnostic clarification in more than 86% of initially nondiagnostic cases3.

In conclusion, CNS infections in transplant recipients may present with atypical imaging patterns that mimic neoplastic or inflammatory conditions. A nondiagnostic initial biopsy does not exclude infectious etiology, particularly in immunosuppressed patients with progressive neurological deficits. In such scenarios, repeat brain biopsy may be decisive. Although uncommon, the coexistence of toxoplasmosis and CMV should be considered in severely immunocompromised individuals.
