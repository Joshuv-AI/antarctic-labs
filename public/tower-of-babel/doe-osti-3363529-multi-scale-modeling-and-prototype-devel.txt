Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
 In this CRADA project, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Stanford University, SLAC
 National Laboratory, and TotalEnergies collaboratively executed a multidisciplinary
 investigation of electrochemical reduction of CO2 to produce sustainable fuels and
 chemicals. Overall, the project led to an increased understanding of the fundamental
 processes involved in CO2 electrolysis, from the atomistic scale to the full electrolyzer device
 scale, ultimately leading to design guidelines for CO2 electrolyzers that will help in their future
 commercialization. As the model systems, Ag- and Cu-based catalysts were investigated in
 various forms depending on the electrochemical platform that was utilized to study the
 activity, selectivity, and durability towards electrochemical CO2 reduction. By employing
 experimental, theoretical, and computational techniques, the project team experimentally
 validated multi-physics models, evaluated the experimental levers that lead to increased
 electrolyzer reaction selectivity and energy efficiency, and used computational optimization to
 design higher performance electrodes. The learnings of this project were extensively
 documented in publicly available peer-reviewed journal publications and conference
 presentations, which serve as a foundation for further work to build from.

Summary of Research Results:

Technical Objectives:
The project team set out to complete a wide range of tasks for this CRADA project. Broadly, the main
scientific goals are described in the following.

 1. Developing validated models of half and full cell CO2 electrolyzers. In this objective, various
 models of CO2 electrolyzers at the cell level were developed and validated against
 corresponding experiments. The focus here was on understanding some of the key levers that
 dictate electrolyzer performance, such as catalyst morphology, electrode properties
 (thickness, porosity, roughness), and catalyst-electrolyte interfaces. Toward that end, the team
 investigated the eaect of altering the microstructure of the catalyst to increase
 electrochemically active surface area (Ref. 5), the mechanical compression of the cell to
 reduce Ohmic losses and control electrode ﬂooding (Ref. 16), and the ability to model the drop
 oa in performance due to liquid ﬂooding in the cell (Ref. 7). Through this task, validated
 computational modeling frameworks have been successfully developed for both aqueous- and
 membrane-based electrolyte cells which have served as numerical optimization tools for
 individual components and full electrolyzers at the lab scale.

 2. Developing tools to accelerate and validate atomistic simulations of CO2 electrolysis.
 Being able to understand the CO2 reduction reaction requires both an atomistic view of the
 reaction microenvironment and a way to connect this information to larger scale simulations.
 However, these types of models are typically computationally prohibitive. As a result, in this

 objective, the goal was to develop tools to accelerate these types of studies. First, we used a
 machine learning technique for deriving eaective interatomic potentials to be able to simulate
 CO adsorption on a Cu surface. This represents a much faster method for simulating these
 types of complex microenvironments compared to traditional density functional theory
 simulations. Next, a framework was developed to couple together atomistic-based microkinetic
 models of the CO2 reduction reaction with larger-scale continuum simulation (Ref. 9). We
 demonstrate a code that uses a microkinetic model of Au that receives inputs from a
 continuum simulation (e.g. CO2 concentration, pH) and returns ﬂuxes (e.g. partial current
 densities) back to the continuum simulation. We use the tool to simulate a ﬂow reactor
 conﬁguration. The development of this computational tool, which connects detailed reaction
 kinetics with larger-scale phenomena, enables researchers to validate microkinetic models.

3. Understanding the microenvironment near the electrode surface for CO2->CO
 catalysts. The activity and selectivity of the CO2 reduction reaction is intimately related to
 the microenvironment near the catalyst. We ﬁrst start by investigating catalysts, such as Au
 and Ag, that primarily reduce CO2 to CO. First, we study the eaect of cation identity on CO2
 reduction activity. It is known that using Cesium (Cs), which is expensive, can allow for the
 highest reaction rates due to its smaller hydration radius allowing for a greater packing
 density in the electrochemical double layer. Using a combination of simulations and
 experiments, we show that using mixtures of Cs:X, where X is either Lithium (Li) or Sodium
 (Na), can achieve near full-Cs performance when the mole fraction of X is 0.7 and 0.5,
 respectively. Next, we conduct a pair of studies aimed at understanding the reaction
 environment within a single electrolyte-ﬁlled pore of a gas-diausion electrode. First, using
 representative Tafel expressions of the CO2 reduction reactions, three mass transport
 regimes are identiﬁed based on the ratio of reaction strengths of CO2 buaer reactions and
 CO2 reduction reaction rates. Using realistic parameters, we show that CO2
 concentrations are typically radially uniform in the pore, justifying popular homogenized
 electrolyzer models (Ref. 27). Next, we conduct a study of a pore, but now using a more
 accurate microkinetic model of the reaction on Au. We show how the pore size and aspect
 ratio shifts the dominant CO2 consumption reaction from being in the electrolyte to being
 on the electrode (Ref. 8). By studying relatively simple CO2 to CO conversion systems, this
 task has allowed for improved understanding of the microenvironmental eaects and for the
 development of modeling frameworks that can be used to study the conversion of CO2 to
 further reduced products.

4. Understanding the microenvironment near the electrode surface for CO2->C2
 catalysts. Catalysts that can further reduce CO2 to C2 products, such as ethylene, instead
 of just C1 products, such as CO, are typically more diaicult to understand, requiring more
 sophisticated experimental and modeling techniques. First, using experiments, we
 demonstrate the development of an electrochemical cell that permitted operando
 spectroscopy measurements of the near-electrode CO2 concentration and adsorbed *CO
 concentration during CO2 electrolysis on Cu (Ref. 24). Doing so allows the team to
 correlate the concentrations of the reactants and intermediates to ultimate reaction

 activity and selectivity. Next, we perform experiments where the temperature of the
 electrolyte and electrode are independently controlled, showing that the selectivity of the
 reaction products can be tuned based on the set temperatures (Ref. 18). Next, using
 atomistic-scale density functional theory simulations, we explore the eaect of diaerent
 electrolyte compositions and diaerent Cu facets (Ref. 29, 30). Finally, we demonstrate
 pathways to bridging the coupled microkinetic-continuum simulation developed
 previously, but now with a two-step process (CO2->CO, CO->C2) for Cu catalysts.

5. Optimizing CO2 electrolyzer components. CO2 electrolyzers are comprised of a large
 number of components, presenting several opportunities for optimization. First, we
 demonstrate how advanced manufacturing techniques could be used to rapidly iterate and
 optimize the housing of a vapor-fed reactor in a cost-eaective way based on experimental
 measurements (Ref. 4). Next, we conduct experiments to optimize the composition of
 porous catalyst layers, varying the ratio of copper, carbon, Naﬁon, polytetraﬂuoroethylene
 (PTFE) and Sustainion, based on ink formulations given by Design of Experiments. The
 optimized catalyst layer composition demonstrates increased selectivity towards ethylene
 and the suppression of hydrogen evolution, highlighting relatively higher loadings of carbon
 and the removal of Naﬁon (proton conductor) in the formulation. Increased carbon is found
 to improve the distribution of copper nanoparticles and the porosity necessary for CO2
 mass transport. Finally, we conduct computational optimization to study tandem electrode
 conﬁgurations. Speciﬁcally, we show that the pattern of electrodes comprised of discrete
 sections of Ag (responsible for reducing CO2 to CO) and discrete sections of Cu (primarily
 responsible for reducing CO to C2 products) could be optimized to maximize the selectivity
 of the desired product (Ref. 11). These studies have shown the potential for computational
 optimization to signiﬁcantly improve catalyst design and device engineering.

6. Electrode design: catalyst discovery. We perform a series of studies to improve CO2
 electrolyzer performance through novel electrode design. First, we show that high Ag mass
 activity for CO production can be achieved with a gas diausion electrode comprised of a Zn
 matrix with highly dispersed Ag sites (Ref. 15). Next, we show that a gas diausion electrode
 catalyst can generate a high ethylene to CO ratio by mixing together copper nano-cubes
 and nickel-coordinated nitrogen-doped carbon (NiNC) (Ref. 23). This tandem catalyst is
 eaective due to the higher CO concentration available for reaction due to the presence of
 NiNC. Finally, we show that the durability of the electrode could be improved by tuning the
 recipe of the electrode microporous layer. The relationship between the composition and
 thickness of the material and the propensity for the electrode to degrade -- either through
 ﬂooding or salt precipitation -- are explored. This objective highlights improved CO2
 reduction through catalyst discovery and design, and the integration of the catalysts into
 realistic electrolyzer systems to demonstrate improved selectivity, activity and durability.

 Actual Accomplishments:
The tasks listed in the Statement of Work (SOW) are summarized below.
 - Task 1. Organizational: Establish data management and benchmarking procedures
 - Task 2. Develop Forward Model for Catalyst Chemical Kinetics (<10 nm length scales)
 - Task 3. Develop Continuum Forward Model (transport model)
 - Task 4. Connecting Interface Chemical Kinetics with Continuum Models
 - Task 5. Reactors and Catalysts for 3D Electrocatalysis

All tasks of the project were successfully completed, with a caveat for task 4. The software was
developed to capture the two-step process of CO2 reduction on Cu, where the continuum
simulation was coupled to two separate microkinetic models. Despite the successful
development of this coupled modeling framework, the ultimate results did not compare favorably
with the corresponding experiments, and this is likely due to the atomistic microkinetic model not
accurately capturing realistic rates of the reaction. The code was documented and made
available, and future tweaking may be done to improve agreement with experiments.

The teams at LLNL, Stanford, and TE have worked together collaboratively over the years, building
strong relationships, sharing ideas, and engaging in productive discussions. The teams held
multiple regular meetings throughout the CRADA, including monthly All-Hands Meetings, to report
up-to-date results and discuss opportunities to collaborate, particularly bridging experimental and
computational eaorts. The CRADA has produced 11 collaborative publications, with several
additional manuscripts currently being ﬁnalized for publication. In addition, the teams organized
regular multi-day workshops to discuss project progress and plan future activities.
