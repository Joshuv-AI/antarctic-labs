Adoption and sustainability of a virtual interprofessional team for primary care in Ontario: a qualitative study
Authors: Lucas M Seuren, Judith Versloot, Shipra Taneja, Muhammad F Zeeshan, Walter P Wodchis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary care providers across Canada face challenges caring for patients with increasingly complex care needs. Team-based care has been identified as an approach to improve care for patients with complex needs. The Mississauga Ontario Health Team recently adopted SCOPE, a telephone-based support line to improve care coordination between primary care, hospital and community services. To understand how Mississauga Health SCOPE supports primary care providers and how it can be improved, this study addresses the following research question: why do primary care providers adopt or not adopt Mississauga Health SCOPE, and what motivates sustained use or abandonment?MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted over Zoom with three stakeholder groups: primary care providers (n = 20), general internal medicine physicians (n = 3), and members of the project team (n = 6). Interviews focused on the adoption and continued use of Mississauga Health SCOPE. Data were analyzed inductively, using Proctor et al.’s Adoption and Sustainability concepts.ResultsWithin the broad concepts of Adoption and Sustainability, we identified seven themes that shaped whether PCPs would use and continue to use MHSCOPE: Broken healthcare system, Awareness of Mississauga Health SCOPE, Service misalignment, Useful and usable, Trying it out, Breaking the habit, and Making PCPs feel heard.ConclusionsTelemedicine support services like Mississauga Health SCOPE can help primary care providers deliver appropriate and timely care to patient in the community. Succcesful implementation requires that clinicians feel supported in using the intervention, have a clear understanding of procedures and benefits, and are prepared to take time to learn to work with new tools to overcome hurdle of initial adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03411-5.Keywords: Primary Health Care, Telemedicine, Team-based care, Implementation Science, Qualitative Research

Introduction

With an ageing population in high-income countries, including Canada, patients more frequently present to their primary care provider (PCP) with multiple morbidities, chronic illnesses, and complex health care needs [1, 2], which providers are not always equipped to address [3]. Consequently, patients face poor health outcomes [4] and increased Emergency Department (ED) visits [5] and hospitalizations [6], and healthcare systems are coping with increasing costs [3, 7]. Team-based care, in which two or more health care professionals work together to deliver comprehensive primary care [8], has been identified as an approach to improve care for patients with complex needs [9]. It can reduce healthcare utilization [10], reduce (unnecessary) ED visits and hospitalizations [10, 11], and lower health system costs [12]. The Ontario government has made considerable financial investments in interdisciplinary Primary Care Teams to provide patients with timely, high-quality care [13]. While these policies have focused on establishing co-located teams, emerging evidence suggests that virtual teams, where primary care and hospital care coordinate services, are a promising model to care for patients with complex needs [14].

In 2012, Women’s College Hospital in Toronto established SCOPE (Seamless Care Optimizing the Patient Experience), a navigation hub to support PCPs working in small practices (3 < PCPs), by providing a “virtual team of health professionals that provide direct clinical advice and enhance care coordination” [15–18]. SCOPE allowed participating PCPs to contact a hospital-based nurse via email or telephone to facilitate expedited access to specialist care from a general internal medicine (GIM) physician, diagnostic testing, or community services. The goal of SCOPE was to lower the number of ED visits of patients from participating PCPs.

An evaluation conducted of SCOPE based on its first 18 months of operations found no significant change in ED visits, potentially due to small case numbers (29 participating PCPs) to provide adequate power and patients bypassing their PCP by going to ED directly [15]. However, a parallel qualitative study found that PCPs did feel better supported and that SCOPE Toronto was seen to contribute to effective shared management of patients [16]. SCOPE has since achieved considerable spread across Toronto, with nearly 3,500 registered PCPs and a dozen affiliated hospitals.1

Based on the enthusiasm among PCPs around SCOPE Toronto, the Mississauga OHT (Ontario Health Team) adopted SCOPE in 2023. Similar as the original Toronto program, Mississauga Health SCOPE (MHSCOPE) offers a telephone-based “Navigation Hub” that (i) can inform PCPs of specialist services they may not be aware of or that have shorter waiting lists (i.e. navigation support), (ii) expedite urgent diagnostic imaging, (iii) connect PCPs with an on-call GIM for clinical queries or urgent patient assessment through video appointments, (iv) arrange an urgent in-patient orthopedic consult for certain MSK conditions, and (v) a warm hand over to Ontario atHome program. MHSCOPE is operated out of Trillium Health Partners, a large urban hospital in Mississauga.

Although SCOPE Toronto, like similar telephone-based support programs across Canada [19–22], has been adopted widely by PCPs [17] with 60% of solo primary care providers having access to SCOPE, it is unclear why many PCPs with access to SCOPE have not adopted it, do not use it regularly, or have stopped using it. Research has recurrently demonstrated that successful implementation of healthcare interventions is challenging [23]. There are many known domains that affect whether interventions actually get used, such as readiness for change among targeted users [24] and expectations around workload and effectiveness of the intervention [25]. Previous research on remote support services has mainly focused on their effectiveness, and researchers have only engaged PCPs who use these services.

To support the successful spread of MHSCOPE, we conducted a qualitative study to understand what factors drove adoption of MHSCOPE, as well as what factors drove non-adoption or discontinuation of use. Non-adoption or service abandonment can be important informers of how a service can be adapted and improved [23]. While a program may serve the needs of its active user groups, to optimally serve the community and promote health equity it is vital to understand not only why some PCPs do use MHCOPE, but also why some potential users do not or not anymore. Our study therefore addressed the questions of why do primary care providers adopt or not adopt MHSCOPE, and what motivates sustained use or abandonment?

Methods

Study design and setting

We used an implementation science approach to understand PCP motivations for (non)adoption and (dis)continued use of Mississauga Health SCOPE (MHSCOPE). We conducted a qualitative descriptive study [26, 27] using semi-structured interviews. This design was found to be appropriate for an in-depth understanding of the views of participants on the implementation of the MHSCOPE program and their experiences with the service. We designed the interview guides using the implementation taxonomy by Proctor et al. [28].

Mississauga is a highly diverse city of 720,000 people, where 62% identifies as being part of a racialized population and 53% is born outside of Canada, primarily South Asia (41.0%) and China (11.8%) [29]. The municipality also has a high burden of chronic disease and multimorbidity [30]. The population is served by around 700 PCPs. Only a small subset provide care through comprehensive interprofessional team-based care.

The study team consisted of researchers with expertise in qualitative methods (L.S. & S.T.), quantitative methods (J.V.), project management (M.Z.) and was supervised by a researcher with expertise in implementation science (W.W.). Interviews were conducted by a team member with experience in telemedicine and virtual, qualitative interviews (L.S.).

Analytic framework

Our study was designed using the implementation science taxonomy developed by Proctor et al. [28]. This framework distinguishes between implementation outcomes, service outcomes, and client outcomes. We found this valuable as our interest was in the implementation outcomes only (adoption and sustainability), which we wanted to keep conceptually distinct from service and client outcomes (e.g., effectiveness, satisfaction). Following the framework, we defined adoption as registering for MHSCOPE and making an initial call to the navigator for assistance. We thus defined non-adoption as either not registering for MHSCOPE or registering for MHSCOPE but never making a call. We defined sustainability as either the continued use of MHSCOPE following the first call or the intention to continue using MHSCOPE and abandonment as intentionally discontinuing use of MHSCOPE.

Participants and recruitment

To get a diverse set of perspectives on the implementation of MHSCOPE, we recruited among PCPs, GIMs, and the MHSCOPE project team. Our sample aimed to include PCPs that were active users of MHSCOPE, past users and non-adopters. We included the MHSCOPE project team as they led the co-design process and recruitment of PCPs for MHSCOPE. We anticipated they could provide valuable insights into the implementation of the service that would be informative as to why PCPs might adopt or not adopt MHSCOPE. GIMs were recruited as they provide direct service to participating PCPs, and we anticipated that they could provide valuable perspectives as to why PCPs who used the service found or did not find it to have added value for their practice.

To recruit PCPs, we used convenience sampling. Given the workload in primary care and expected difficulty in recruiting participants, our target was 20 PCPs, making sure this sample included both MHSCOPE users and non-users, as well as diversity in terms of practice organization (e.g., fee-for-service, family health organization). We used a range of recruitment strategies. We initially sent an email to PCPs who had been invited to participate in the MHSCOPE pilot (N = 80). We only received six responses at this point. We subsequently had an invitation included in the newsletters of the Mississauga Ontario Health Team Primary Care Network, and a few months later we sent another email to all PCPs who had signed up to MHSCOPE. PCPs were offered a 50 CAD gift card for completing an interview. PCPs were provided a participant information sheet to review before being asked to commit to an interview.

GIMs were initially emailed directly. As none responded, we then contacted their clinical director, who directly contacted all GIMs who supported MHSCOPE. Members of the project team (the nurse-navigator, operations manager, medical director, project director, senior project manager at Trillium Health Partners and the executive director of Mississauga Health OHT) were emailed directly and all participated in an interview. No compensation was offered to GIMs or members of the Project Team.

Quality assurance and quality improvement activities, when used exclusively for assessment, management or improvement purposes, do not constitute research under the Canada Tri-Council Policy Statement and do not require full ethics review. We submitted the research protocol and associated documents to the Trillium Health Partners Research Ethics Board, and they deemed the project quality improvement exempt from formal review. All participants provided written informed consent for their interviews being audio-recorded and anonymized quotes being used in research publications.

Data collection and analysis

Interview guides were tailored to each of the three participant groups (see Supplementary Material 1). Using the taxonomy by Proctor et al. [28], we designed questions to probe their knowledge and experiences of MHSCOPE, their motivations for using or not using MHSCOPE, and perceived areas for improvement. Interviews were conducted via Zoom by the lead author. All interviews except one were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by an external transcription agency. Field notes were taken during the interviews to capture initial reflections and potential key findings for further exploration. Transcripts were uploaded to NVivo 12.6 Plus and reviewed for accuracy. Two transcripts were initially coded independently (L.S. & S.T.) using the taxonomy of Proctor et al. [28], using adoption and sustainability as sensitizing concepts. Following Coxe-Hyzak et al. [31], we considered on how adoption and sustainability were driven by other implementation and service outcomes (e.g. how does acceptability influence adoption and continued use). Coding results were compared and two additional codes were subsequently added: usefulness and usability. While Proctor et al. [28] include these under appropriateness, through our initial analysis we found that they are best understood as distinct concepts. Both team members subsequently each coded the rest of the interviews. Coding results were compared and disagreements were resolved through discussion. Final themes were developed iteratively from the coding results by L.S. by focusing on identified pattern and through discussion with the larger team. Trustworthiness of the analysis and findings was supported by data triangulation from participant groups, maintaining clear records of the coding process and analytic decisions and independent coding of the data.

Results

We interviewed twenty-nine participants between October 2023 and April 2024: 20 PCPs from a diversity of experience and practice backgrounds, 6 project team members and 3 GIMs (Table 1). PCPs included mostly physicians (90%), but also one nurse practitioner and one physician-assistant. All six non-users indicated interest in using MHSCOPE. Participant characteristics

(Non-)Adoption and sustainability of MHSCOPE

Most participants found MHSCOPE helpful in improving their ability to provide care to patients, using it routinely after initial adoption. However, not all PCPs for whom MHSCOPE was available had adopted the service. Among participants who had registered for MHSCOPE, four had subsequently not used it, and of those who had used it, two had only used it once. Based on reflections of non-users as well as MHSCOPE users and service staff, we identified seven key factors that shaped (non-)adoption of the service as well as whether PCPs would continue using MHSCOPE after their first experience.

Broken healthcare system

Participants reported that initial enthusiasm and adoption of MHSCOPE was primarily driven by frustration with barriers and inefficiencies in the healthcare system. PCPs felt they lacked the ability to provide patients the right care at the right time, describing the health care system as broken. They experienced increasing difficulties in finding specialist care for patients, with referrals frequently getting rejected due to growing waitlists, unclear scope of practice, or even for no stated reasons. Finding a specialist who would take a referral depended on luck and a good support network (e.g., a referrals coordinator on staff, a Facebook group to ask for advice). One PCP told of a case where they needed a general surgeon to remove a patient’s ingrown toenail, but all the specialists in their network refused the referral (e.g., outside scope of practice, waitlist too long). They had to rely on their informal network for a solution:

So, I ended up asking, we have a Facebook group of physicians in my area, and just [cross-talk] there and somebody said, “Oh, this person does it” and it’s this one doctor in Oakville that does it, so yes, that’s the only way I could find out. (PCP06 – One-time MSCOPE User)

Communication by specialists’ offices was also frequently found to be lacking. It would take weeks for specialists to respond even for urgent referrals. Not only did these delays cause considerable frustration and anxiety, they put a significant burden on PCPs: they would have to support patients while they were waiting for specialists, even if their conditions fell outside the PCP’s scope of practice. One PCP described the whole process as guesswork: “You just by guess that whichever day it is and whichever number is it, you send some people to somewhere” (PCP16). In the face of routine rejections, PCPs also had to deal with the administrative load of multiple referrals, taking time away from their ability to see other patients. The lack of access to specialist care led PCPs to often send patients to the Emergency Department (ED), even though they knew this was inappropriate. But ED was seen as the only available pathway, especially for patients who required urgent care, as this PCP explains:

So as a consequence, like people were having to go the ER to seek care. And sometimes we would have to recommend that they go to the ER, because the system was in a gridlock, essentially, and the only way to move someone through was by sending them to the ER. (PCP10 – Repeat MHSCOPE user)

Participants expected that MHSCOPE would help them overcome these difficulties: it was seen to match a critical need. They reported that the option of getting patients seen faster would allow them to deliver appropriate and timely care and perform their job as primary care physician.

Lack of awareness

One barrier to adoption of MHSCOPE was that PCPs were not necessarily aware of all the exact services that MHSCOPE offered, and even users commented that they expected many of their colleagues might not see or read the email. Some expected that many of their colleagues had never heard of MHSCOPE. One PCP who did use MHSCOPE said that they had talked to a colleague in Toronto who worked in an area where SCOPE had been available for many years, but that PCP was not familiar with the program. This lack of awareness was partially due to the workload: PCPs were inundated with emails and always pressed for time. As a result, even among MHSCOPE users, it was seen to take time to learn how to make optimal use of the program.

But I do think we’re still learning how to use SCOPE, so probably a lot more ways we can still kind of take advantage. Yes. (PCP05 – Repeat MHSCOPE user)

Although the project team used in-person visits as part of its implementation strategy, reaching over 700 practitioners across a large geographical area was not realistic. One team member also remarked that getting PCPs to reliably use the service required routine reminders:

Since [PCPs are] really busy, you can’t use one way of engaging, and you can’t just engage with them once a month, you need to remind that a service like SCOPE exists and that would be through multiple different, multiple mediums and multiple times. (PRO02)

The most effective strategy was thought to be word of mouth: PCPs who use MHSCOPE informing colleagues. This could happen through established practice communities such as Facebook or WhatsApp groups, but also continuing medical education events or other informal networks. However, this snowball method leads to a relatively slow roll-out: MHSCOPE needs adopters, who can tell others, who then adopt it and in turn can tell others. It would also limit spread to PCPs who are connected, whereas MHSCOPE was designed to support isolated practitioners.

Service misalignment

One concern of PCP participants was whether MHSCOPE met their needs. MHSCOPE was implemented in part in response to PCPs who already knew of SCOPE Toronto and who expressed a desire for the service, indicating that it would address needs. Pathways had also been co-designed with PCPs, further guaranteeing that the service would be useful. In spite of this, participants who did not use or stopped using MHSCOPE, either completely or some of its pathways, commented that they did not see the service as offering clear value. Many PCPs already had alternative ways of meeting those needs, like a professional network to find referral options or working relations with a diagnostic imaging clinic to take urgent referrals. One PCP explained why they had not yet used MHSCOPE:

Do I think it’s better than what I have and so far I haven’t seen evidence of it. Now, you know, some of that is on me. I haven’t tried it, but I also have the advantage to be connected. (PCP14 – Non-user)

Furthermore, there was a discrepancy between what MHSCOPE was designed for and how it was perceived. Because MHSCOPE could facilitate expedited GIM referrals, many PCPs expected that through MHSCOPE they could get patients seen faster by any specialist. They were disappointed when MHSCOPE did not offer referrals or consultations with difficult-to-access specialist services (e.g., gynecology, psychiatry), particularly for urgent referrals.

But I’m a bit disappointed to know that the, you know, general internists are available. I hear there’s a new ortho referral. Well, there is the rapid access clinic. But, like, to me it’s fairly limited still as to who we can, you know. (PCP20 – Repeat MHSCOPE user)

Moreover, the specialists suggested by MHSCOPE could still reject referrals and waitlists remained extensive. Consequently, PCPs often still sent patients to the ED. Participants reflected that while rapid access to an on-call internist and virtual internal medicine clinic was valuable, they needed a comprehensive list of on-call specialists and urgent referral options to prevent unnecessary referrals and ED visits.

Useful and usable

Even when PCPs felt that MHSCOPE met their needs, that did not guarantee adoption or continued use. Not only did MHSCOPE need to be useful, it needed to be useable, which meant that MHSCOPE needed to fit into the PCPs’ organizational routines, and the benefits of the service needed to outweigh the perceived effort of integrating this new way of working. Although MHSCOPE was co-designed with PCPs and continues to be overseen by a team that includes PCPs, some participants commented that the process did not seem easily workable within the constraints of primary care. In fact, MHSCOPE was seen as particularly difficult to use for solo practitioners, the target population who was supposed to benefit most.

Yes, so being in a solo practice, I find that it’s challenging, and also being fee-for-service it’s challenge, the model of care is challenging in that there’s – you’re very, very pressed for time. And any additional time that you spend that the patient needs is always done, but it always comes at a literal cost. (PCP07 – One-off MHSCOPE user)

The perceived effort of making a phone call during MHSCOPE hours (8am to 4pm) could be restrictive. Facing the immediate pressure of a full waiting room, calling MHSCOPE was considered to take time away from (billable) patient care. Some PCPs relied on alternative modalities to contact MHSCOPE such as sending a text message by cellphone, but this could lead to problems if a different navigator was on-call. Many PCPs preferred to have options that would be easier to integrate into their day, such as fax, email, or ideally their EMR software.

Two, is referral pathways where I can fax the information to somebody without having to talk to somebody would be really helpful, not that I don’t like talking, but it just streamlines the process. (PCP15 – Non-user)

Where PCPs needed non-urgent specialist advice, they would often rely on eConsults, a platform for submitting written questions to specialists across Ontario. While many specialists were not available on this platform and writing a question could still take up to 15 min, eConsults were considered convenient, as PCPs could write them whenever they had time, and they typically would still get an answer within a few days. eConsults were thus seen as a service that was more valuable, due to the wider range of specialties, and that was easier to fit into their clinical routines.

Trying it out

PCPs did not always have a clear idea of how MHSCOPE worked or what services it offered. While they were aware of how to contact MHSCOPE and that it facilitated things like navigation, it was not necessarily clear what this meant in practice. This meant that PCPs needed to use the service to see whether it could actually be of assistance. This lack of clarity could act as a barrier to adoption and created uncertainty, with PCPs hesitant to use a service if they did not know what the potential outcome would be. They felt more comfortable relying on tried and tested methods, irrespective of whether those were potentially less efficient or effective than MHSCOPE. For example, PCPs did not want to send patients to the ED unnecessarily, but they could still opt for this as they would then know that the patient would be seen.

The concern here is that, because it’s more of an urgent thing you want to have assurance that they’re going to be seen. I think that thing, when we send to ER, it’s because there isn’t a lot of wait time and you just don’t want it to get lost. (PCP06 – Repeat MHSCOPE user)

Several participants reflected on how at some point they just decided to try it out. This concept of trying it out also meant that the first experience with MHSCOPE was potentially important for continued use. Two participants reflected on how they had expected that through MHSCOPE they could have a patient seen urgently by a specialist. However, both were offered information for a specialist they had already contacted. While that was precisely the remit of MHSCOPE, the PCPs experienced it as a negative outcome, and both were hesitant to call MHSCOPE in the future.

I’ve used it once and it was for a gynae – an urgent –what I deemed semi-urgent gynae consult. It seemed promising at the time, but it wasn’t. And I ended up getting like kind of phone numbers that I already had access to, so it wasn’t overly helpful that way. (PCP11 – One-time MHSCOPE user)

Breaking the habit

One thread that runs through all themes is the notion of breaking the habit. Participants from all stakeholder groups highlighted that PCPs are used to certain ways of working: they all have their own established practices. These organizational routines may not necessarily be more efficient or effective than calling MHSCOPE, but PCPs know how they work, what they offer, and they do not have to integrate new practices into their workday.

You know what, it’s on my list of things to do and, but the problem is you get so into the pattern of your nurses, so then taking it from that end, that you forget, in the moment, that you actually have another resource to go through. (PCP04 – Non-user)

Even for those PCPs who had used MHSCOPE, the service was not necessarily front of mind, not part of their routine. Before use of MHSCOPE could become part of their routine, it took active work in remembering that the service was available at the time when it could be helpful. PCPs therefore need support tools to help remind them of MHSCOPE at the point where it could be useful, and at that point it should be straightforward to use.

They’re used to running their offices the way they ran their offices for the last bazillion years, that it’s sort of hard I find sometimes to, to break that habit, their usual habits. (PRO01)

The project team provided regular email as reminders, but as mentioned above, emails often went unread due to the high workload and quantity of emails being received by PCPs. The project team also distributed mousepads, magnets, pens, sticky notes, and posters. These provided visible reminders to PCPs that MHSCOPE was available, and while these were considered helpful, most PCPs did not have them, which especially during a busy workday made them more likely to forget that MHSCOPE could be an option.

Making PCPs feel heard

Participating PCPs reported that one reason they continued using MHSCOPE was that it finally made them feel heard: it was seen as one of the few recent developments that provided primary care with what it needed. MHSCOPE was seen as a service that aligned with their needs and how they worked, compared to what they saw as many government initiatives that were supposed to improve primary care.

This could be the reason I don’t retire. It just feels like, honestly, one of the things that makes us pull out our hair, when we watch so many health care dollars, so much finding going into XYZ, or these programs, that program, and you question, like, was there even a physician at the table who said that this actually will impact people and how we do our jobs. (PCP18 – Repeat MHSCOPE User)

MHSCOPE helped PCPs to provide patients appropriate, timely care, keeping them off waiting list and out of the ED. Not only did participants feel better supported by having MHSCOPE services available, participants also highlighted MHSCOPE’s PCP-centered approach. The navigator was prompt to respond and worked with them to get patients appropriate care quickly. The on-call GIM was also appreciated as they would call back quickly, offer useful advice, and sometimes offered to either take over patient care or urgently refer a patient to a specialist. This efficiency was felt to be in line with the high-pressure environment of primary care where PCPs have limited time and are confronted by a wide array of clinical complaints.

As soon as I call, the phone call is answered within minutes and you’re talking to someone live on the phone who is telling you, you know, “This is what I think we can do. We can get this service. We can get that service. If you can send this over to us, and that over to us”, and then I was able to communicate to the patient right then and there. (PCP10 – Repeat MHSCOPE user)

Even PCPs who were part of a team found the MHSCOPE service useful, even though MHSCOPE was designed to support solo practitioners and isolated PCPs. Although team-based PCPs had more staff support (e.g. referrals coordinator), without MHSCOPE, they still struggled to secure urgent diagnostic imaging, manage complex patients, or navigate referral processes. In fact, team-based PCPs seem to benefit more from MHSCOPE as they could delegate tasks, thereby limiting the imposition on their work routines.

Discussion

Our findings showcase that even when healthcare innovations are grassroots led, are co-designed with stakeholders, and have achieved spread in other healthcare systems, implementation in new healthcare systems is challenging. While primary care providers in Mississauga felt that having access to a virtual team through Mississauga Health SCOPE helped them coordinate and deliver comprehensive care, many did not make it part of their routine practice. Even if PCPs recognized the potential value of MHSCOPE, the adaptations required to their organizational routines, the high workload in primary care, and uncertainty about the process and outcomes led to PCPs defaulting to their established ways of working. Successful implementation and roll-out was contingent on simple challenges like reaching PCPs in a way that works for them (e.g., through informal networks, practice visits, or continuing medical education), explaining how MHSCOPE works, and providing a clear overview of its remit and benefits.

PCPs in Canada are under increasing strain. A 2023 survey among PCPs in Ontario found that 65% planned to reduce their hours or leave the profession entirely due to stress and burnout [32]. Wait times in Canada have increased consistently over the last 20 years [33], reaching a median of 74.5 days in Ontario in 2020 [34]. Support models in which PCPs can get specialist advice via telemedicine have been successfully implemented across Canada (e.g. RACE, Virtual Hallway, Specialist LINK), helping PCPs deliver better care and prevent specialist referrals and (inappropriate) ED visits [19–22]. By providing access to a virtual team, MHSCOPE not only provides similar pathways of care, but crucially, it makes PCPs feel supported [16]. For SCOPE Toronto, this felt support has led many PCPs to adopt the service, and many are high-frequency users [16, 17].

Our study builds on a growing body of work using implementation science and Proctor et al.’s framework specifically to investigate implementation outcomes of healthcare innovations in primary care [35]. Previous studies, however, have rarely used qualitative methods to explore what drives adoption, which is generally assessed quantitatively (i.e., how many people use a service, not why they use the service), and especially non-adoption and sustainability [23]. By investigating non-adoption and sustainability specifically, our study provides crucial evidence for policymakers in how to allocate resources to support successful program implementation.

Previous studies of non-adoption found that perceptions around usefulness and fit with existing ways of working were important drivers of adoption of health innovation among PCPs [25]. Where an innovation was not found to offer clear value over existing clinical practice, PCPs were unlikely to adopt it, especially if it required changes to their routines [36]. This is in line with our study, where PCPs were unlikely to use MHSCOPE if they expected it would add more work and where first experiences did not demonstrate value over existing processes. In contrast, where PCPs found that it did make their lives easier, they were quick to integrate it into routine practice.

While our study used adoption and sustainability from Proctor et al.’s implementation taxonomy as distinct concepts, our study shows how the domains within this taxonomy are interdependent. It is not merely the case that implementation success is crucial for intervention success (e.g., effectiveness, efficiency) in a linear pathway, the process also operates vice versa: where users experience improvements from the intervention, this can drive implementation. There can be value in analyzing concepts separately in small-scale studies like this, but we also require more refined models that integrate these domains in a way that allows policymakers to identify implementation strategies as well as support decision-making around what interventions are worth investing in [23].

Future research needs to consider how programs like SCOPE can be adapted to meet the needs of a changing healthcare system. SCOPE was designed in 2012 to unburden primary care and to facilitate coordination with specialist services. Since then, the primary care landscape has gotten considerably more challenging. Improved access to specialists in particular has become a critical need for PCPs. Moreover, while qualitative studies like ours indicate that PCPs find these programs valuable, no study has investigated what the impact is of these programs on metrics like physician workload (e.g., do complex patients require fewer PCP appointments), healthcare costs (e.g., are support lines cheaper than established pathways), and patient health outcomes (e.g., does telemedicine support increase risk of missed symptoms).

Implications for practice

Despite the perceived value of MHSCOPE, rolling out the intervention across PCPs proved difficult. In line with a previous study of SCOPE Toronto [17], building knowledge of the intervention was found to be a challenge for spread across PCPs. To support successful spread and scale-up, policymakers need to focus on reaching clinicians in ways that work for them. Mailing lists make it easy for policymakers to share news, but achieve limited results. Clinical networks are a vital resource where clinicians share new tools with colleagues. Working with clinical champions to leverage these informal networks (e.g., WhatsApp and Facebook groups) might facilitate initial spread. In addition, while MHSCOPE used dedicated continual medical education events, clinicians would still need to read their email, sign up, and attend these specific events. In other words, this approach still requires the clinician to do considerable work to become informed about the platform. Finding events that clinicians attend anyway, such as other CME events, can make it easier to reach out to a broader audience of clinicians directly.

In addition, our study supports prior research demonstrating that it takes time and effort for potential users to adopt a new practice and see its promised benefits [23]. While MHSCOPE is designed to be easy to use by providing a single point of contact with a nurse navigator who takes charge of care coordination, our study shows that the threshold remains high. PCPs already feel overburdened, leading them to be resistant to taking on an additional program that changes how they work and for which the benefits are unclear up front [37]. We recommend that MHSCOPE and other digital intervention platforms focus on expectation management [25]. Clinicians need to have a clear sense of how intervention work, the benefits they offer, and that any tool takes time to learn. Trialing interventions is vital in demonstrating value as well as identifying potential bottleneck to successful scaling. Success stories from participating clinicians, shared via appropriate platforms, can lend credibility to potential users that interventions are worth trying and sticking with, even if the first experience is not perfect.

Limitations

Our approach and mixed success with recruiting participant limits the transferability of the results. Only six participants had not used MHSCOPE. Email was likely not the most effective recruitment strategy for PCPs, as they are already coping with a high administrative load. Even using established PCP newsletters provided only a few responses. Moreover, most participants were part of a group practice with support staff, whereas MHSCOPE was geared to support isolated, solo practitioners. Recruiting isolated, solo practitioners would have required more dedicated recruitment methods. Finally, findings of our study are limited to adoption in the first few months after launch. Previous research on SCOPE in Toronto indicates that while there was limited engagement 19 months out [15, 16], SCOPE was adopted by over 1500 PCPs after 10 years [17].

Conclusions

Telemedicine support lines like Mississauga Health SCOPE can provide valuable support to PCPs in navigating patients to appropriate care pathways, avoiding ED visits, and lowering administrative burden. Having a community-driven program that is co-developed with and overseen by PCPs helps guarantee that it meets community needs and can convince PCPs of the value of the program, helping adoption and sustainability. Non-adoption remains a concern and an impediment to health equity. Future research should ascertain long-term adoption and determine how programs can be continuously adapted for a changing healthcare system.
