Lecanemab use for early Alzheimer disease in Canada
Authors: Sophie M. Weiss, Jennifer A. Watt, Vivian Ewa, Sharon E. Straus
Published: 2026

Lecanemab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds soluble brain amyloid-β protein, promoting its removal and slowing the underlying process of Alzheimer disease.1 This mechanism aligns with the amyloid hypothesis, a debated model of Alzheimer disease pathogenesis.1 Only patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease are eligible for lecanemab treatment. Although approved for use in Canada effective January 2026,2 Canada’s Drug Agency and Quebec’s Institut national d’excellence en santé et en services sociaux recommended against public reimbursement; coverage may differ across jurisdictions. 3,4 Patient eligibility depends on clinical, laboratory, and diagnostic imaging criteria.1,5

Initial clinical eligibility will likely be determined in primary care settings where cognitive changes are first uncovered.6 A specialist (i.e., cognitive neurologist, geriatric psychiatrist, or geriatrician) will determine eligibility with APOE genotyping and confirmation of amyloid positivity by lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or positron emission tomography (PET), and coordinate infusions and follow-up. Blood-based biomarkers are not currently approved for confirming eligibility because of their limited accuracy.7

What is the evidence in support of lecanemab’s benefit?

Lecanemab’s approval was primarily based on results from the phase 3 Clarity AD trial, which enrolled 1795 participants with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease and positive amyloid determined by CSF analysis or PET.1 Participants received intravenous lecanemab (10 mg/kg biweekly) or placebo for 18 months.1 The primary outcome was the score on the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), which measures cognitive and functional impairment.1 Lecanemab slowed worsening in the CDR-SB score by 0.45 points versus placebo, a 27% relative reduction.1 This did not reach the minimum clinically important difference for the CDR-SB of approximately 1 point, suggesting that the observed benefit was not clinically meaningful.8 Longer-term data from an open-label extension study have suggested that clinical benefits are maintained with continued treatment for up to 36 months; however, these findings were descriptive and uncontrolled.9 Participants who were APOE4 homozygotes (who are ineligible for lecanemab in clinical practice) were a minority (15.1% to 15.8% across treatment groups); the overall treatment effect was largely derived from the APOE hetero-zygotes and noncarriers.1,5 The trial population does not reflect the demographic diversity of patients with Alzheimer disease in Canada, given its predominance of White participants and underrepresentation of other groups, limiting generalizability.1 Thus, when discussing lecanemab with patients and caregivers, clinicians should explain that lecanemab does not cure Alzheimer disease; rather, Alzheimer disease may progress more slowly.1,10,11 Unlike cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil, which provide modest symptomatic benefit in Alzheimer disease, lecanemab targets the suspected underlying pathology.12 The interpretation of benefits from lecanemab may be influenced by the limited availability of therapies for and the personal and societal burdens of Alzheimer disease. Clinical benefits are modest at best, with uncertain impacts on quality of life, independence, and caregiver burden.

A 2026 Cochrane systematic review of 17 randomized controlled trials involving 20 342 participants evaluated antiamyloid monoclonal antibodies among people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease.13 Across antiamyloid therapies, the review concluded that treatment effects on cognitive and functional outcomes were not clinically meaningful on average, while adverse events were increased compared with control groups.13 These findings underscore uncertainty regarding the clinical importance of observed treatment effects. Since the review pooled multiple antiamyloid therapies with differing efficacy and safety profiles, its conclusions should be interpreted alongside trial-specific evidence for lecanemab. What is the evidence describing harms associated with lecanemab use?

The main safety concern with lecanemab is amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), adverse effects from antibody-mediated mobilization of amyloid from cerebral vessels.1,5 These abnormalities are detected on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and classified as edema or effusion (ARIA-E) or cerebral microhemorrhage (ARIA-H).1 In the Clarity AD trial, ARIA occurred in 21.5% of patients receiving lecanemab (ARIA-E 12.6%, ARIA-H 17.3%) compared with 9.5% of those receiving placebo (ARIA-E 1.7%, ARIA-H 9.0%).1 Of patients with ARIA receiving lecanemab, approximately 85% were asymptomatic and identified through MRI surveillance.1 Symptomatic ARIA occurred in about 3% of patients (ARIA-E 2.8%, ARIA-H 0.7%) and typically involved headache, confusion, visual disturbances, dizziness, or seizures.1 The number needed to harm was 36 for symptomatic ARIA-E and 200 for symptomatic ARIA-H. Serious ARIA events, including intra-cerebral hemorrhage, occurred in approximately 0.7% of patients receiving lecanemab.1 Hemorrhagic complications were more frequent in patients receiving concurrent anticoagulation.1 Lecanemab may complicate management of conditions requiring antithrombotic or thrombolytic therapies. Single antiplatelet therapy may be reasonable, whereas use of anticoagulation or thrombolytic agents increases concern for hemorrhagic complications. Clinicians must balance the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage against the risks of withholding therapies.1,5,14 The rate of ARIA-E varied substantially by APOE4 status, occurring in 5.4% of noncarriers, 10.9% of heterozygotes, and 32.6% of homozygotes, informing the ineligibility of homozygotes for lecanemab.1,5 As a susceptibility gene, APOE4 indicates Alzheimer disease risk but is not deterministic; therefore, genetic counselling is not required when genotyping is performed for treatment decision-making.15

Given that ARIA-E occurs more frequently early in treatment, surveillance MRIs before the 5th, 7th, and 14th doses are required.5 In contrast, ARIA-H may occur throughout treatment.1 Suspected ARIA must be confirmed by MRI; computed tomography may be used to exclude large acute intracerebral hemorrhages when MRI is not immediately available.5,14 Most cases of ARIA-E resolve spontaneously; corticosteroids may be considered in severe cases, with dosing, route, and duration guided by severity.5 Management of ARIA-H includes monitoring with serial MRIs, modifying bleeding risk factors, and discontinuing lecanemab when predefined radiographic thresholds are met.5,14 Experts recommend discontinuation of lecanemab with the development of 10 or more cerebral microhemorrhages or any superficial siderosis on MRI.5 Patients experiencing severe headache, confusion, focal neurologic deficits (e.g., weakness or aphasia), or seizures necessitate urgent specialist evaluation, whereas milder symptoms (e.g., mild headache, dizziness) may be managed with close follow-up.

Infusion-related reactions, defined as adverse events during or shortly after early infusions, were common in the Clarity AD trial (26.4% lecanemab, 7.4% placebo) and were typically mild to moderate, including fever, headache, nausea, rash, and transient blood pressure changes.1 The rate of adverse events requiring drug discontinuation was higher among patients taking lecanemab (6.9% lecanemab, 2.9% placebo).1 Mortality was similar (0.7% lecanemab, 0.8% placebo).1

What are the resource implications associated with lecanemab treatment?

Patient access to treatment with lecanemab comes with resource and system challenges.6 In Canada, the cost is expected to be $35 000 to $40 000 per year per patient; this excludes the costs related to diagnostic testing, infusion services, and serial MRIs.6 In Canada’s publicly funded health systems, such costs present a challenge for drug budgets.6 In February 2026, Canada’s Drug Agency recommended against public reimbursement, and public coverage remains uncertain as each province and territory decides whether to list lecanemab on formulary.3,6,14 In December 2025, Quebec was the first to decide against public coverage because of lecanemab’s uncertain benefits and safety concerns.4 Whether private insurers will cover use remains unclear; patients may face substantial out-of-pocket costs.6

Delivering lecanemab is resource intensive. Patients require biweekly infusions; optimal treatment duration is unknown but could be for multiple years.5,6 Health care systems will need adequate infusion clinic capacity, which in most hospitals and clinics are already at or near operational limits.6 In the future, home infusions or subcutaneous injections may be possible.1,5,6 Increased capacity will be needed to perform PET and lumbar punctures in clinics, and to run biomarker assays and APOE4 testing in laboratories.5,6 Genotyping for APOE is not currently publicly funded or routinely available across Canada.5 The ongoing requirement for MRI is also a concern, with a minimum of 4 in the first treatment year.5,6 These MRIs must occur at specific time intervals, necessitating dedicated slots.5,6 Additional downstream health care utilization may occur, as patients presenting with new neurologic symptoms while receiving lecanemab will require emergency department visits, neuroimaging, and specialist consultation.5 Access for patients in remote or under-resourced areas or with lower socioeconomic status poses serious equity concerns.1,5,6 With the potential for out-of-pocket costs, access may exacerbate socioeconomic disparities.

Regions and provinces are developing coordinated care models to support safe and equitable implementation of lecanemab treatment, reflecting recognition that delivering antiamyloid therapies requires substantial system-level coordination.16 By integrating services, such models aim to reduce delays and ensure eligible patients are assessed and monitored within appropriate infrastructure.16

What can be expected in the foreseeable future?

Lecanemab is the first disease-modifying therapy approved for use in MCI and mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease in Canada. 2 Lecanemab’s benefit for patients is, at best, incremental.1,10 Appropriate referral and treatment initiation depends on coordinated care pathways; availability of diagnostic, treatment, and monitoring infrastructure; and drug coverage.5,6 Primary care physicians will play a key role in identifying potentially eligible patients, setting expectations, and supporting shared decision-making. 5,6 Given the uncertain clinical benefit, risks of adverse events, and resource and financial implications, the decision to initiate lecanemab should be individualized. Some patients who place a high value on small potential delays in disease progression may choose to proceed and accept the risks, monitoring requirements, and out-of-pocket costs. In some cases, deferring treatment pending further evidence may be a reasonable approach. The recent Cochrane systematic review reinforces uncertainty regarding the clinical importance of antiamyloid therapies, given their small average treatment effects, increased rates of adverse events, and substantial health system implications.13 Future research will shape treatment of Alzheimer disease, including refining eligibility criteria and monitoring protocols, and advancing evidence on blood-based biomarkers and other disease-modifying agents. For instance, donanemab, another antiamyloid monoclonal antibody included in the Cochrane review, was approved by Health Canada in April 2026, expanding options for early Alzheimer disease treatment.17 Whether the modest slowing in disease progression observed with antiamyloid therapies translates into preserved patient independence, reduced caregiver burden, and delayed long-term care admission is unknown.
