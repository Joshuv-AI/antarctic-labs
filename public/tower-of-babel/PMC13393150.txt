The ProTrieve Sheath funnel captures embolized filter fragments during complex inferior vena cava filter removal
Authors: Abhishek Jairam, Tust Techasith, Alexander S Misono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRemoval of embedded or malpositioned inferior vena cava (IVC) filters can require advanced techniques. Historically, operators have feared IVC filter device fracture but have lacked reliable methods to manage embolization of liberated fragments. We report the case of a 70-year-old male who presented with abdominal pain and bilateral lower extremity edema and was found to have iliocaval thrombosis secondary to a malpositioned and embedded IVC filter. A novel embolic protection strategy using the ProTrieve Sheath (Stryker Peripheral Vascular) was instrumental in capturing a liberated fragment resulting from filter fragmentation during the transfemoral inversion retrieval technique. While transfemoral inversion is an established tactic for complex filter retrieval, fracture and fragment migration remain a major concern. The availability of novel embolic protection devices may improve procedural safety while preserving high technical success.Keywords: ProTrieve Sheath, Complex IVC filter removal, Filter fragment embolization, Filter inversion, Deep venous thrombosis, Venous thromboembolism

Placement of inferior vena cava (IVC) filters is indicated in patients with venous thromboembolism who cannot tolerate or who have failed anticoagulation. When a patient can resume anticoagulation, there is no added mortality benefit to keeping the filter.1

Furthermore, longstanding IVC filters are linked with complications, such as thrombosis, perforation, fracture, and/or tilting, all of which increase retrieval difficulty.2,3 Filter removal is especially critical in the context of filter-related thrombosis and venous outflow obstruction.4 Numerous retrieval techniques are published, including the use of traditional vascular snares, multiloop snares, the “hangman” technique, endobronchial forceps, and others.5 Transfemoral filter inversion is an emerging technique with a high technical success rate; however, filter fracture and embolization into the cardiopulmonary system are known risks.6 Filter struts can embolize to the atrioventricular myocardium, tricuspid valve, or pulmonary arteries, where life-threatening complications, including arrhythmias, hemopericardium, valvular regurgitation, and/or pulmonary arterial injury, can occur.7,8 Embolized fragment removal may require either an endovascular or an open surgical approach—if it is even possible—both of which can carry significant procedural risk, with open surgery associated with greater morbidity.7

A novel transjugular funnel sheath is an elegant solution to nearly eliminate the risk of filter fragment embolization to the cardiopulmonary circulation and may increase operator confidence during complex filter retrieval.

Case presentation

The patient consented to the publication of this article. The patient is a 70-year-old male who presented with abdominal pain and severe bilateral leg swelling. Pertinent medical history includes a Janus kinase-2 mutation and a history of high-risk pulmonary embolism, for which he underwent pulmonary artery large-bore aspiration thrombectomy and IVC filter placement with an Option Elite IVC filter (Argon Medical) 1 month prior to presentation. He was discharged on 5 mg apixaban twice daily. On examination, the patient had normal vital signs, was in no acute distress, and had noticeably swollen limbs, but without signs or symptoms of phlegmasia cerulea dolens. All laboratory test results were within the normal range.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography venogram of the abdomen/pelvis demonstrated complete IVC and bilateral iliofemoral thrombosis extending to and above a tilted IVC filter, with its hook apparently embedded in the right adrenal vein (Fig 1, A and B).

In addition, there is acute thrombus in the IVC with extension above the filter (dashed white arrow) as well as chronic right retroperitoneal hematoma (black arrow). The patient was started on a heparin drip and scheduled for IVC thrombectomy and subsequent IVC filter retrieval. The patient had been tolerating anticoagulation, so maintaining the filter provided no additional survival benefit and increased the risk for deep venous thrombosis. Furthermore, it was felt that the malpositioned filter was the primary driver for caval thrombosis, especially in the setting of a prothrombotic mutation, and so filter removal was the ultimate procedural goal.

The patient was brought to the angiography suite, and iliocaval thrombectomy was pursued. Because the thrombus extended above the indwelling IVC filter, a transjugular ProTrieve Sheath (Stryker Peripheral Vascular) was placed for central embolic protection. Diagnostic venogram demonstrated extensive IVC and iliofemoral thrombosis (Fig 2, A). Using the Flash 16 aspiration catheter (Penumbra, Inc), multiple rounds of thrombectomy were performed of the bilateral iliofemoral veins and the IVC with technically successful recanalization of the iliocaval veins (Fig 2, B). At the time of the initial procedure, IVC filter removal was attempted using both conventional and advanced techniques, including snares and endobronchial forceps. However, this was not immediately successful, and the procedure was aborted.

The patient returned 1 month later for elective filter removal. The right internal jugular vein and right common femoral vein were accessed via the Seldinger technique, and conventional vascular sheaths were initially placed. After establishing stable transjugular access into the IVC, a super stiff Amplatz wire was placed. After serial dilation, a 20-French ProTrieve Sheath was placed into the suprahepatic IVC, and then its funnel was deployed in order to provide embolic protection. Subsequently, via the right common femoral access, a 16-French Kodiak sheath (Argon Medical) was placed into the IVC. A 6.5-French × 90 cm TourGuide Sheath (Medtronic) was then used to steer Raptor endoluminal forceps (Steris) and grasp the IVC filter neck/leg junction. The TourGuide Sheath was advanced over the forceps to lock the forceps and create one stable construct. With back-tension on the TourGuide Sheath/Filter System, the filter was inverted and oversheathed into the 16-French Kodiak sheath (Fig 3, A and B). Postretrieval cavagram was performed, showing rapid antegrade flow without evidence of caval injury or thrombus (Fig 3, C). However, an IVC filter fragment is visible overlying the IVC, presumably partially adhered to the caval wall (Fig 3, D). Before any retrieval maneuvers could be performed, the fragment was noted to have quickly embolized up the IVC. Fortunately, the filter fragment was caught within the ProTrieve funnel. The ProTrieve funnel was collapsed to trap the fragment, and the sheath was removed. Upon inspection of the ProTrieve funnel, the single fractured filter leg and strut—along with several pieces of chronic venous thrombus—were clearly visible, whereas the remainder of the filter was mangled but intact (Fig 4, A and B). Final full-exposure radiographs confirmed no evidence for further retained filter fragments (Fig 4, C).

The patient was monitored for 4 h in the postanesthesia care unit. After confirming stability, pain control, and vascular hemostasis, the patient was subsequently discharged with instructions to continue Eliquis 5 mg twice daily. At 1-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of leg swelling and abdominal pain.

Discussion

The transfemoral IVC filter inversion technique is a published and effective technique for the removal of challenging and long-dwelling IVC filters, with high reported technical success.6,9 However, during inversion, deformation, and collapse of the filter may mobilize adherent thrombus, disrupt endothelialized components, and ultimately liberate and/or create fractured struts, heightening the risk of central embolization of both fractured filters and thrombus. The true prevalence of IVC filter fracture embolization is not well established, as most published data consist of case reports, device-specific fracture series, and advanced retrieval cohorts that report overall major complications rather than this specific event. Available literature suggests that major complication rates during complex filter retrievals, including embolization events, are generally less than 3%. In patients with intracardiac or pulmonary artery filter fragments, the risk of major complications, including life-threatening cardiac tamponade, has been reported at approximately 1%.10,11 Despite this relatively low incidence, embolization of filter fragments can result in serious complications, including pulmonary embolism, arrhythmias, valvular injury, and pulmonary artery vascular injury, which may be fatal.12,13 In addition, retained intracardiac foreign bodies may predispose patients to chronic complications, such as endocarditis, mycotic aneurysm formation, pulmonary abscess, or stroke.14 Therefore, avoiding embolization into the cardiopulmonary circulation is critical, and strategies to prevent fragment migration should be prioritized, with retrieval of cardiopulmonary foreign bodies considered on a case-by-case basis.

Retrieval of embolized filter fragments is technically demanding and carries substantial risk. Although no clear guidelines on managing this complication exist, typical treatment options include conservative management, endovascular retrieval, or open surgical extraction, with the latter two described most commonly in symptomatic cases.12 Both endovascular and open-surgical retrieval techniques have been published as technically feasible options for filter fragment removal in the pulmonary artery or right ventricle.12,15 However, such procedures may be feared by most operators and are associated with prolonged recovery times, hospital admissions, and serious complications, particularly in those patients who require open surgical removal.16,17

Prior to the availability of the ProTrieve Sheath, there were very limited options, if any, to provide meaningful embolic protection for transvenous intervention. The ProTrieve Sheath’s large-bore platform, coupled with its dedicated expandable nitinol mesh funnel, allowing caval apposition up to 33.5 mm, allows for stability, advanced thrombectomy, and embolic protection.18 While ProTrieve has been extensively described in the literature for capturing embolized deep vein thrombus before it can reach the pulmonary arteries, its intended on-label indication, this unique case report shows ProTrieve’s power to further facilitate the safe retrieval of intravascular devices and debris such as fractured IVC filters.19,20 To our knowledge, no other device features a self-expanding basket that can be both manually deployed and recaptured while fully occupying the IVC to provide complete protection. This case represents the first off-label description of the ProTrieve Sheath facilitating the safe removal of intravascular device debris. Incorporation of ProTrieve has enabled a more confident application of complex retrieval maneuvers and expanded the reach of our IVC filter retrieval practice. Further refinements, including reducing the sheath’s hydrophilicity to prevent unintended proximal migration and increasing its inner diameter and length to enable single-sheath retrieval, may further enhance the device’s utility and advance the field.

Conclusions

Novel devices and techniques in endovascular embolic protection can facilitate complex IVC filter removal. The ProTrieve Sheath is uniquely positioned to capture not only intravascular thrombus but also medical device fragments such as those liberated during complex IVC filter removal.
