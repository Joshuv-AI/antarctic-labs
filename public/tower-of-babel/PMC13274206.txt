Lagged and non-linear effects of Aedes surveillance indices on dengue incidence in Malaysia: evidence from sentinel-site data
Authors: Mohamad Afiq Amsyar Hamedin, Kamarul Imran Musa, Mohd Rahim Sulong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue remains a major public health challenge in tropical and subtropical regions, with Malaysia experiencing sustained endemic transmission despite long-standing vector control efforts. Routine Aedes entomological surveillance, particularly the ovitrap index (OI) and larval index (LI) is widely implemented to guide control activities, yet their mechanistic relationship with dengue transmission and their comparative value as early-warning signals remain insufficiently quantified.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis using weekly dengue case counts and entomological surveillance data from 11 Aedes sentinel districts in Peninsular Malaysia in 2024. Dengue incidence was modelled using generalised additive models with a quasi-Poisson distribution and population offsets. Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) were applied to characterise non-linear and lagged associations between dengue incidence and OI or LI over a 12-week lag window. Early-warning performance was evaluated using a rolling-origin cross-validation framework with a 4-week forecast horizon. Three models were compared: DLNM(OI), DLNM(LI), and DLNM(OI + LI). Predictive discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals estimated using the DeLong method. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using extended lag windows and temperature-adjusted models.ResultsExposure–lag–response analyses showed modest and lag-dependent associations between both entomological indices and dengue incidence, with attenuation at longer lags. The LI demonstrated slightly more pronounced short-term associations at lower exposure levels, whereas the OI showed weaker and less consistent patterns. In early-warning evaluation, overall AUCs were 0.63 (95% CI 0.55–0.71) for the OI-based model, 0.68 (95% CI 0.60–0.76) for the LI-based model, and 0.65 (95% CI 0.56–0.73) for the combined model, indicating modest predictive performance. Findings were broadly consistent across lag-window specifications and after temperature adjustment.ConclusionsRoutinely collected entomological surveillance indicators exhibit non-linear and delayed associations with dengue incidence and provide modest early-warning signals. The LI showed slightly higher predictive performance than the OI, although overall discrimination remained limited. These findings highlight the importance of context-specific use of entomological indicators and support their continued integration into operational dengue early-warning systems to inform timely vector control interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00974-y.Keywords: Dengue, Aedes, Disease surveillance, Early warning systems, Distributed lag non-linear models

Background

Dengue is the most widespread mosquito-borne viral disease affecting humans and remains a major public health challenge globally. Transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus, dengue is recognised by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the most important mosquito-borne viral disease due to its rapid geographic expansion, frequent epidemics, and substantial health and economic burden [1, 2]. Recent global estimates indicate hundreds of millions of infections annually, with severe dengue contributing to significant morbidity and mortality, particularly during large-scale outbreaks [3, 4]. Despite sustained control efforts, dengue incidence has continued to rise over the past two decades, underscoring the need for more effective surveillance and prevention strategies [2].

The burden of dengue is concentrated in tropical and subtropical regions where environmental and climatic conditions favour Aedes mosquito survival and reproduction. Southeast Asia represents one of the global epicentres of dengue transmission, accounting for a substantial proportion of the global disease burden [3, 5]. Malaysia is a long-established dengue-endemic country, experiencing recurrent seasonal transmission with periodic large outbreaks [6]. National epidemiological analyses have documented increasing dengue incidence over time, with marked spatial heterogeneity across districts and states, driven by rapid urbanisation, population density, and environmental suitability for vector breeding [7, 8]. Dengue remains a major contributor to outpatient visits, hospital admissions, and vector control expenditure in Malaysia, reinforcing its priority status within the national public health agenda.

Dengue transmission is shaped by complex interactions between viral, host, vector, and environmental factors. Climatic variables such as temperature, rainfall, and humidity influence mosquito development, survival, biting behaviour, and the extrinsic incubation period of the virus, thereby modulating transmission dynamics [9–11]. Consequently, climate-based dengue prediction models have been widely developed and evaluated, with several studies demonstrating associations between meteorological variables and short-term dengue risk [9, 12]. However, systematic evaluations highlight that the predictive performance of climate-driven models varies substantially across settings and time periods, and their operational utility for routine early warning remains inconsistent, particularly in highly urbanised environments where local ecological conditions dominate transmission patterns [9, 13].

Beyond climate, entomological surveillance remains a cornerstone of dengue prevention and control. WHO recommends routine monitoring of Aedes populations as part of integrated vector management, with commonly used indices including the house index (HI), container index (CI), Breteau index (BI), and ovitrap index (OI) [2, 14]. These indices are intended to estimate vector infestation levels, identify high-risk areas, and guide targeted vector control interventions. In Malaysia, entomological surveillance forms a central component of dengue control programmes, with larval surveys and ovitrap monitoring used operationally to support outbreak response and preventive activities [7, 15].

Despite their widespread adoption, the relationship between entomological indices and dengue incidence remains inconsistent across studies [16]. A systematic review reported mixed and often weak associations between traditional larval indices and dengue transmission, questioning their reliability as predictors of outbreak risk [17]. In contrast, trap-based surveillance methods, including ovitraps and adult mosquito traps, have been shown in some settings to better capture spatial and temporal variations in Aedes populations and to precede dengue case clusters [18]. Nevertheless, empirical findings vary by ecological context, surveillance intensity, and analytical approach, suggesting that entomological indices may not uniformly translate into increased human infection risk without appropriate consideration of biological delays and contextual factors.

Methodologically, prior studies have relied on cross-sectional analyses or simple correlations that do not adequately capture the non-linear and delayed pathways linking vector abundance to dengue cases [17–19]. Such approaches often overlook biologically plausible lags related to mosquito development cycles, virus incubation, and reporting delays. Theoretically, dengue incidence represents a downstream outcome influenced not only by vector density but also by human immunity, behavioural factors, reactive vector control, and surveillance practices [20]. These complexities introduce challenges in causal interpretation, as entomological indices may act both as indicators of transmission potential and as targets of intervention, leading to feedback mechanisms that confound observed associations.

Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) provide a flexible statistical framework to address these challenges by simultaneously modelling non-linear exposure–response relationships and delayed effects over multiple weeks [21]. DLNMs have been increasingly applied in dengue research, particularly to examine climate–dengue relationships and to explore lagged effects consistent with vector and viral biology [9, 22, 23]. However, relatively few applied studies in endemic settings have systematically compared multiple entomological indices within a unified DLNM framework, while also incorporating rigorous out-of-sample validation aligned with operational early-warning needs.

Accordingly, this study aimed to quantify mechanistic vector–disease relationships by estimating the non-linear and lagged associations between dengue incidence and two routinely collected entomological indicators, the OI and larval index (LI) across Malaysian sentinel districts. We further compared the short-term early-warning performance of models incorporating each index, individually and in combination, over a 4-week forecast horizon. By identifying which surveillance signal provides timelier and more reliable early-warning information, this study generates actionable evidence to inform dengue prevention strategies, optimise surveillance investments, and strengthen decision-making for vector control in endemic settings.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in selected sentinel districts within Peninsular Malaysia, which comprises 11 states and two federal territories. Peninsular Malaysia is densely populated and highly urbanised, with marked heterogeneity in population density, land use, and urban structure across districts [24]. Rather than encompassing all districts nationwide, the study focused on eleven sentinel districts selected under the national Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme coordinated by the Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia. The sentinel districts—Petaling, Johor Bahru, Seremban, Lembah Pantai, Seberang Perai Tengah, Kinta, Kota Setar, Kota Bharu, Melaka Tengah, Kuantan, and Kuala Terengganu—were selected by the Entomological Sector of the Disease Control Division, MOH Malaysia. Selection was based on historical dengue burden, entomological risk profiles, and their role as major urban or administrative centres within their respective states, thereby capturing diverse epidemiological, demographic, and environmental contexts across Peninsular Malaysia. However, these sentinel districts were not intended to constitute a statistically representative sample of all districts nationwide. Rather, they function as strategically selected surveillance sites within the national Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme, designed to provide continuous monitoring of vector dynamics in areas with sustained dengue transmission. Routine entomological surveillance and dengue case reporting within these sentinel districts were conducted in accordance with national surveillance protocols, ensuring standardised data collection and comparability across sites [25].

Dengue surveillance data

This study was a sentinel-district retrospective secondary data analysis using weekly dengue surveillance data from 2024. Dengue case data were obtained from the national dengue surveillance system (eDengue), maintained by the Ministry of Health Malaysia. Confirmed dengue cases recorded in the surveillance system with disease onset between January and December 2024 were included, corresponding to the period during which sentinel entomological surveillance was consistently implemented across the selected districts. Case confirmation was based on a positive non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen test and/or dengue immunoglobulin M (IgM) serology, in accordance with national surveillance guidelines [25]. All confirmed dengue cases occurring within the selected sentinel districts during the study period were aggregated into weekly counts for analysis.

Entomological indices and population data

Entomological surveillance data were obtained from the Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme coordinated by the Ministry of Health Malaysia. Two routinely collected entomological indicators were used in this study: OI and LI. The OI was defined as the percentage of ovitraps positive for the presence of adult Aedes relative to the total number of ovitraps deployed at each sentinel site during a given surveillance week [25, 26]. Standard ovitraps were used across all sentinel districts and consisted of a 300-ml black plastic container with an opening and base diameter of 6.8 cm and a height of 9.1 cm. An oviposition paddle made of hardboard (10 cm × 2.5 cm × 0.3 cm), with two different surface textures, was placed diagonally inside each ovitrap with the rough surface facing upwards. Each ovitrap was filled with tap water to a depth of 5.5 cm and deployed according to national surveillance protocols [25, 26]. Ovitrap deployment followed the operational protocol of the national Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme. Within each sentinel district, ovitrap monitoring was conducted in designated surveillance locations where a minimum of 60 ovitraps were deployed within an approximately 200-m radius surveillance zone. This deployment density was applied consistently across sentinel districts to ensure comparable surveillance coverage. The 200-m radius was selected in accordance with operational vector surveillance practices and reflects the typical flight range of Aedes mosquitoes, which generally disperse within a few hundred metres of their breeding sites [27]. Ovitraps were placed in selected premises within the surveillance zone to capture local oviposition activity and were retrieved and replaced at weekly intervals (7-day sampling cycle). Eggs collected on the oviposition paddles were examined to determine the presence of Aedes mosquitoes, and the OI was calculated as the percentage of positive ovitraps relative to the total number deployed during each surveillance week.

LI data were derived from larvae collected from ovitraps deployed within the sentinel surveillance areas as part of the national Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme. During routine weekly ovitrap retrieval, larvae present in the ovitrap water were collected using standard entomological tools, including dippers and pipettes, and transported to the laboratory for identification. Species identification was conducted using a compound microscope based on established morphological characteristics, and only Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus larvae were included. In this study, the LI represents the weekly count of Aedes larvae recovered from ovitrap collections within each sentinel district. Although ovitraps are artificial breeding devices, they are deployed using a standardised protocol across sentinel districts, ensuring consistent sampling of vector activity over time and space. As such, the LI should be interpreted as a surveillance-derived proxy of local Aedes activity rather than a direct measure of natural larval abundance [26, 28]. The geographic locations of Aedes sentinel districts are shown in Fig. S1.

Population data for each sentinel district were obtained from the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) and incorporated into the regression models as a logarithmic population offset to account for differences in underlying population size when modelling dengue incidence [29]. The weekly mean temperature data were obtained from the weather database under the Malaysian Meteorological Department [30].

Separate DLNMs were specified for OI and LI, using natural cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom for both exposure–response and lag–response dimensions, and a maximum lag window of 12 weeks selected based on biological and operational plausibility [9, 21, 32]. Modelled relationships were summarised using overall exposure–lag–response surfaces and lag- or exposure-specific relative risk curves estimated from the combined dataset, while accounting for district-level heterogeneity through random intercepts. For graphical interpretation of lag–response relationships, relative risks were calculated at the 25th and 75th percentiles of the exposure distribution and expressed relative to the median value. The 25th and 75th percentiles represent the interquartile range (IQR) of exposure values and allow comparison between typical lower and higher levels of vector activity within the observed data distribution while avoiding instability associated with extreme exposure values.

The primary model aimed to quantify associations between routinely collected entomological indicators and dengue incidence under an operational surveillance framework. Meteorological variables such as temperature were not included in the primary model but were incorporated in sensitivity analyses. Model comparison

Early-warning performance was evaluated for three candidate models: (i) DLNM(OI), (ii) DLNM(LI), and (iii) DLNM (OI + LI). All models were fitted as single-stage models using data from all sentinel districts, with district-level random intercepts included to account for baseline differences in dengue incidence. Predictive performance was therefore assessed at the overall level by aggregating out-of-sample predictions across all districts.

Predictive performance was evaluated using a rolling-origin cross-validation framework that preserved the temporal ordering of the data [33]. At each prediction time point t, models were trained using all available data up to week t-1 and used to predict the probability of a dengue alert at week t+4. This process was repeated sequentially across the study period to generate out-of-sample predictions for each sentinel district.

Since there is no universally accepted definition for dengue early-warning thresholds [34], the outcome was defined as a binary dengue alert, occurring when weekly dengue cases at week t + 4 exceeded a district-specific threshold defined as the 75th percentile of historical weekly case counts. This percentile-based threshold was used to identify weeks with elevated dengue activity relative to the typical baseline within each district while avoiding overly rare events that could lead to unstable performance estimates. The 4-week forecast horizon was selected to reflect the biologically plausible delay between vector abundance and observed dengue cases, which includes the mosquito development cycle, the extrinsic incubation period of dengue virus in mosquitoes, the intrinsic incubation period in humans, and delays in healthcare seeking and case reporting [10]. From an operational perspective, a 4-week lead time also provides a practical window for public health authorities to implement targeted vector control interventions.

Predictive discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals estimated using the DeLong method [35]. As a general guide, AUC values of 0.5 indicate no discrimination, 0.7–0.8 acceptable discrimination, 0.8–0.9 excellent discrimination, and > 0.9 outstanding discrimination [36]. To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted by extending the maximum lag window from 12 weeks to 18 and 24 weeks. Additional temperature-adjusted models were fitted by incorporating weekly mean temperature as a smooth covariate. Overall AUCs were compared across model specifications to evaluate the stability of predictive performance.

This comparison framework was used to determine whether Aedes indices improved early-warning performance beyond single-index models and whether combining indices provided additional predictive benefit, thereby identifying the most operationally relevant entomological surveillance indicator for dengue early warning. Additional implementation details are provided in Supplementary Material Text S2. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2) [37].

In the primary unadjusted 12-week lag models, the LI demonstrated the highest predictive discrimination, with an AUC of 0.675 (95% CI 0.601–0.748), followed by the combined OI + LI model (AUC = 0.667, 95% CI 0.592–0.741) and the OI model (AUC = 0.649, 95% CI 0.576–0.722). These findings indicate modest predictive performance across all models.

Sensitivity analyses using extended lag windows showed broadly consistent results. For the unadjusted models, AUC estimates remained relatively stable between the 12-week and 18-week lag specifications. Extending the lag window to 24 weeks resulted in slightly higher AUC values for the LI-based model (AUC = 0.725, 95% CI 0.653–0.796) and the combined OI + LI model (AUC = 0.728, 95% CI 0.656–0.800), although these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the reduced number of evaluable observations at longer lag periods.

After adjustment for weekly mean temperature, model performance remained largely unchanged. In the temperature-adjusted 12-week lag models, the LI-based model continued to demonstrate slightly higher discrimination (AUC = 0.672, 95% CI 0.598–0.746), compared with the OI-based model (AUC = 0.641, 95% CI 0.567–0.714) and the combined model (AUC = 0.633, 95% CI 0.557–0.709). Similar patterns were observed for the 18-week and 24-week lag specifications, with only minor differences in AUC estimates.

Overall, these findings indicate that the predictive performance of entomological indices was modest and remained stable across alternative lag-window assumptions and after adjustment for temperature. Discussion

In this study, we quantified the non-linear and lagged relationships between dengue incidence and two routinely collected entomological surveillance indicators, the OI and LI, across sentinel districts in Peninsular Malaysia, and evaluated their utility as short-term early-warning signals. Using a distributed lag non-linear modelling framework embedded within generalized additive models, we characterised exposure–lag relationships and assessed predictive performance using a rolling-origin validation approach. Overall, the models demonstrated modest predictive performance, with the LI-based model showing slightly higher discriminatory ability compared with OI-based models. Sensitivity analyses indicated that these findings were broadly robust to alternative lag-window specifications and to adjustment for temperature.

The distributions presented in Supplementary Figure S2 illustrate substantial variation in dengue incidence and entomological indices across sentinel districts. For example, Petaling district exhibited the highest dengue case counts despite comparatively lower OI and LI values. This apparent discrepancy highlights the complex and multifactorial nature of dengue transmission. Entomological indices capture vector activity at specific surveillance locations, whereas dengue case counts reflect human infections aggregated at the district level. In highly urbanised districts with dense populations and substantial human mobility, transmission intensity may therefore appear disproportionately high relative to measured vector indices. These observations reinforce the importance of modelling frameworks that account for baseline differences across districts; in the present study, this was addressed through the inclusion of district-level random intercepts [38].

The LI demonstrated modest but more consistent lagged associations with dengue incidence compared with the OI. As illustrated in Figs. 4 and 5, relative risks increased at short to intermediate lags (approximately 3–6 weeks), followed by attenuation at longer delays. The lag–response patterns at the 25th percentile of LI further showed a transient increase in relative risk peaking around 5–7 weeks before declining towards unity. These findings are broadly consistent with biologically plausible delays between vector development, viral incubation, and observed dengue cases [39]. However, the apparent elevation in relative risk at lower LI values should be interpreted cautiously. Relative risks were expressed relative to the median LI, and an LI value of zero reflects absence of detected larvae in ovitrap collections rather than absence of vector presence at the district level.

This pattern may therefore reflect the imperfect correspondence between local ovitrap-based larval sampling and district-level dengue incidence, together with delayed case occurrence, reactive vector control, and other unmeasured determinants of transmission [32, 40, 41]. As described in Methods, the LI in this study was derived from larvae collected from ovitraps as part of a standardised surveillance system. While this approach ensures comparability across districts, ovitraps represent artificial breeding environments and should therefore be interpreted as surveillance proxies rather than direct measures of natural larval abundance. Consequently, the observed associations may capture relative changes in vector activity under routine surveillance conditions rather than absolute ecological relationships. The attenuation of associations at longer lags may reflect the effects of reactive vector control interventions, density-dependent constraints on mosquito populations, or temporal mismatches between larval habitats and human exposure [16, 42]. In addition, wide confidence intervals observed across both exposure–response and lag–response estimates indicate substantial uncertainty, particularly at higher exposure levels.

In contrast to the LI, the OI demonstrated weaker and less consistent lagged associations with dengue incidence. As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, relative risks remained close to unity across most lag periods, with only modest and non-sustained deviations. The lag–response patterns at both lower and higher percentiles of OI similarly showed limited variation, with wide confidence intervals indicating uncertainty in the estimated relationships. These findings likely reflect the nature of OI as an indicator of oviposition activity rather than vector abundance. Ovitraps capture the presence of egg-laying behaviour in a controlled environment and are influenced by factors such as trap placement, density, and competition with natural breeding sites [43–45]. As with the LI, the OI should therefore be interpreted as a proxy of vector activity derived from a standardised surveillance system rather than a direct ecological measure. Overall, the weaker associations observed for OI are consistent with its slightly lower predictive performance in early-warning analysis.

Because the models were fitted as single-stage models with district-level random intercepts, the exposure–lag relationships were estimated at the overall level rather than separately for each district. Accordingly, the present analysis does not infer district-specific exposure–lag relationships. Instead, model performance was evaluated using overall predictive discrimination aggregated across all sentinel districts. This distinction is important to avoid overinterpretation of location-specific effects from a model designed to estimate overall associations.

Beyond characterising mechanistic relationships, a key contribution of this study was the evaluation of early-warning performance under realistic forecasting conditions. Overall AUC values indicated modest predictive discrimination across all models, suggesting that entomological indices provide useful but limited early-warning signals when used alone. Sensitivity analyses indicated that model performance was stable across lag windows of 12, 18, and 24 weeks. Similarly, adjustment for temperature did not materially alter AUC estimates, suggesting that the observed predictive signal from entomological indices was not solely explained by temperature variation. A previous study also reported a low percentage of variation in dengue cases actually explained by the temperature factor [46].

From a public health perspective, these findings have important implications for dengue surveillance and control. First, they challenge the notion of a one-size-fits-all entomological early-warning indicator. Instead, the results support a tailored approach in which surveillance indices are selected, calibrated, and interpreted in light of local epidemiological and ecological conditions. Second, the moderate discriminatory performance observed across models suggests that entomological indices alone are unlikely to provide definitive early warnings. Rather, they should be integrated with other data sources, such as climate variables, epidemiological trends, and operational intelligence, within a broader decision-support framework [19–21]. Third, the use of rolling-origin validation aligns model evaluation with real-world forecasting conditions, addressing a key limitation of many previous studies that assessed associations without evaluating out-of-sample predictive utility.

Compared with earlier work, this study advances the literature in several ways. Many dengue modelling studies have focused primarily on climate drivers or have assumed linear relationships and fixed lag structures [11, 12, 47]. Entomological indices have often been analysed descriptively or as secondary covariates, without explicit assessment of their predictive performance. Few studies have directly compared OI and LI within a unified modelling framework using routine surveillance data and operationally relevant validation strategies [19, 48–50]. By applying DLNM techniques and evaluating early-warning performance using rolling validation, this study addresses important methodological gaps and provides evidence more directly applicable to public health decision-making.

This study has several strengths. It used routinely collected national surveillance data, enhancing feasibility for implementation. The DLNM framework allowed biologically plausible modelling of delayed and non-linear effects, while the rolling-origin validation approach provided a realistic assessment of early-warning performance. Sentinel district-level analyses enabled exploration of spatial heterogeneity, an important consideration for decentralised vector control programmes. Another consideration relates to potential variability in dengue case detection within routine surveillance systems. The dengue case data used in this study were derived from the national surveillance system, which relies on clinical suspicion, laboratory confirmation, and healthcare reporting practices. Although dengue is a notifiable disease in Malaysia and surveillance coverage is nationwide, differences in healthcare-seeking behaviour, clinician awareness, diagnostic testing practices, and access to healthcare services may influence the probability of case detection across districts. Consequently, the reported case counts may reflect both underlying transmission intensity and variations in surveillance sensitivity. While Malaysia maintains a relatively robust dengue surveillance system, the possibility of under-notification or differential detection across locations should be considered when interpreting district-level comparisons [51].

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the analysis was based on data from 11 sentinel districts participating in the national Aedes Sentinel Surveillance Programme. These sites were selected for operational surveillance purposes and therefore may not be fully representative of all districts in Peninsular Malaysia. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting vector–disease dynamics in sentinel surveillance settings rather than providing nationally generalisable estimates. Besides, analyses were limited to a single calendar year, precluding assessment of interannual variability and longer-term trends. The number of sentinel districts was limited, and unmeasured confounders such as meteorological conditions, human mobility, and intensity of vector control interventions were not explicitly modelled. The definition of early-warning thresholds, although operationally motivated, may influence performance metrics and should be interpreted as one of several possible approaches. Another factor that may influence dengue transmission dynamics is the circulation of different dengue virus serotypes across time and locations. Changes in dominant serotypes can alter population immunity and may contribute to epidemic cycles or fluctuations in dengue incidence. In the present study, district-level data on circulating dengue virus serotypes were not available for integration into the analysis. Consequently, the observed temporal and spatial variability in dengue incidence may partly reflect underlying changes in viral circulation that were not explicitly modelled. The analysis was conducted using routine surveillance data from 2024 and was not restricted to a predefined epidemic period; therefore, the findings reflect vector–disease relationships under typical surveillance conditions.

Interpretation of dengue surveillance data should also consider broader epidemiological factors beyond entomological indicators. Reported dengue incidence may be influenced by variations in case detection within routine surveillance systems, including differences in healthcare-seeking behaviour, clinical suspicion, diagnostic testing practices, and reporting sensitivity across districts. In addition, dengue transmission dynamics are shaped by underlying viral circulation patterns, including shifts in dominant dengue virus serotypes and population immunity, which were not explicitly captured in the present analysis. These factors may contribute to spatial and temporal variability in reported cases independent of measured vector indices. Future work should therefore extend this framework to multi-year datasets to assess the stability of lag structures, seasonality influence and predictive performance across different transmission seasons. Integration of entomological indices with additional epidemiological information, including climate drivers, intervention activities, and virological surveillance data, may further enhance early-warning accuracy while preserving interpretability. Prospective implementation and evaluation within routine surveillance systems would help clarify the operational value of these models and inform optimisation of dengue prevention strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, routinely collected entomological surveillance indices exhibit non-linear and lagged associations with dengue incidence and provide modest early-warning signals at short forecasting horizons. The predictive performance of these indices was consistent across alternative lag-window specifications and after adjustment for temperature, supporting the robustness of the main findings. While entomological indicators alone are unlikely to provide highly accurate forecasts, they represent valuable complementary tools within integrated dengue surveillance systems. Distributed lag non-linear modelling offers a practical framework for quantifying these relationships and supporting data-driven vector control decision-making.
