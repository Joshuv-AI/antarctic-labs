Inequities in maternal healthcare access in the Marrakech-Safi region, Morocco: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hajar Ouahid, Ouassim Mansoury, Majda Sebbani, Mohamed Amine, Latifa Adarmouch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGender norms may shape maternal healthcare utilization, yet evidence from Morocco remains limited. This study examined the association between gender norms, measured using the Moroccan Arabic Gender Equitable Men scale (MA-GEM), and maternal healthcare utilization (antenatal care, facility-based delivery, and postnatal care) in the Marrakech-Safi region.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey among 632 adults aged ≥ 18 years using mixed online and in-person convenience recruitment. MA-GEM was administered to both women and men to assess general gender-norm attitudes and household power relations, while maternal healthcare outcomes were assessed separately using direct service-utilization questions. Analyses of maternal healthcare outcomes were restricted to pregnancy-exposed respondents (n = 207). Chi-square tests and multivariable logistic regression were used to examine associations between MA-GEM categories, sociodemographic factors, and maternal healthcare utilization. Sex-stratified models were conducted as sensitivity analyses.ResultsOur findings reveal that participants adhering to inequitable gender norms were significantly less likely to use antenatal care (AOR = 0.169, 95% CI [0.061–0.469], p = 0.001) and facility-based delivery (AOR = 0.034, 95% CI [0.007–0.169], p < 0.001). Rural residence (AOR = 0.071, 95% CI [0.014–0.367], p = 0.002) and lack of paid employment (AOR = 0.101, 95% CI [0.027–0.380], p = 0.001) were also associated with lower facility-based delivery. No statistically significant adjusted associations were observed for postnatal care.ConclusionMA-GEM–measured gender norms were associated with antenatal care utilization and facility-based delivery in Marrakech-Safi. Findings support integrated interventions addressing both sociocultural norms and structural barriers to improve maternal healthcare utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09413-6.

Introduction

Gender norms encompass the societal and cultural expectations that define appropriate roles and behaviors for men and women. Deeply embedded within social structures, they play a pivotal role in shaping individuals’ health experiences and influencing health-seeking behaviors, including access to healthcare services [1]. In the realm of maternal health, these norms can dictate women’s autonomy, decision-making power, and interactions with health systems, influencing who gets access to care, when care is sought, and how services are utilized [2].

Evidence suggests that gender norms often lead to prioritizing the health needs of men over women, affecting how resources are allocated within healthcare systems [3]. In some cultures, there is a pervasive belief that women should endure pregnancy and childbirth with minimal medical intervention, viewing these as natural, women-only processes that do not require unnecessary medical attention [4]. Such perceptions may reduce the perceived importance of antenatal and postnatal care, despite the critical role these services play in preventing complications and ensuring the health of both mother and child [5, 6].

Moreover, the choice of place for delivery is often influenced by gendered expectations about women’s roles and capabilities. In some communities, there is resistance to institutional deliveries, which are perceived as impersonal or unnecessary unless a medical emergency arises. Decisions regarding place of delivery may depend not only on service availability but also on family dynamics, particularly the influence of partners or senior household members, which can limit women’s ability to seek skilled birth attendance [7, 8].

Gender norms may further shape women’s interactions with healthcare providers and their perceived acceptability of seeking care, particularly during the postnatal period, which is often undervalued in comparison to pregnancy and childbirth. Social expectations emphasizing endurance, modesty, or obedience may discourage repeated health visits or open communication with providers, reducing opportunities for follow-up care and early detection of postpartum complications [9].

This manuscript explores the association between gender norms and access to maternal healthcare services, including pregnancy monitoring, choice of delivery location, and postnatal care. Gender norms were assessed using the Moroccan Arabic Gender-Equitable Men (MA-GEM) scale, which captures general attitudes toward gender norms and household power relations within the local sociocultural context [10]. Maternal healthcare access outcomes were measured separately through direct questions on service utilization (pregnancy monitoring, place of delivery, and postnatal care). While the scale was originally developed among men, the Moroccan adaptation and subsequent applications have included both women and men [11, 12]; therefore, MA-GEM was administered to all participants in this study. This scale has been applied in studies of sexual and reproductive health; its use in examining access to maternal health services remains limited. By applying a standardized measure of gender norms, this study seeks to explore their association with access to key maternal health services in the Marrakech–Safi region. It aims to identify and discuss these influences to inform the development of more gender-sensitive maternal health policies and service delivery approaches. This study was a component of a research project that examined the relationship between gender norms and access to sexual and reproductive health.

Method

Design

We conducted a cross-sectional study to explore the association between traditional gender norms and the access to maternal healthcare services.

Study setting

The Marrakech-Safi region in Morocco, with its population exceeding 4 million, stands as the third largest region in terms of population. It is divided into 215 communes, comprising 18 urban centers, including the historic and bustling city of Marrakech, alongside 197 predominantly agricultural rural areas. This division underscores the region’s diverse demographic and socio-economic composition, blending vibrant urban life with traditional rural settings [13].

Participants and sample size

From June 2023 to January 2024, a final sample of 632 individuals aged 18 and older was recruited. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach through both online and in-person channels. Online recruitment facilitated access to a wider geographic reach, while in-person recruitment was used to include individuals with limited internet access, lower digital literacy, or who may be illiterate or not using smartphones. This mixed-mode strategy aimed to improve inclusiveness. The participants completed an online questionnaire comprising five sections, including consent, sociodemographic information, the Moroccan Arabic version of the GEM scale (MA-GEM) [10], and inquiries regarding access to maternal healthcare services. This study is part of a broader research project examining gender norms and access to sexual and reproductive health services in the Marrakech-Safi region. While a related article using the same dataset has been published, it addresses a different research question and a distinct SRH component. The present manuscript provides an independent analysis focused specifically on maternal healthcare access, which was not examined in previous publications. The only shared element between the studies is the baseline sociodemographic characteristics, which are presented as supplementary material in the current manuscript (Table S1 supplementary material 1). Apart from this table, no data, analyses, or results overlap between the studies [14].

Variables and description of the instrument

The study collected sociodemographic characteristics including age, gender, place of residence, education level, marital status, and employment status. Attitudes toward gender norms were measured using the Moroccan Arabic Gender Equitable Men scale (MA-GEM), which showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.861). In this study, MA-GEM was administered to both female and male respondents, consistent with its use in mixed-sex samples. MA-GEM was used to measure general attitudes toward gender norms and household power relations; maternal healthcare access outcomes were measured separately through direct questions on utilization of ANC (pregnancy monitoring), place of delivery, and postnatal care. The MA-GEM scale assesses norms across domains including violence, sexual relationships, reproductive health, and household decision-making. The questionnaire also included items on pregnancy and childbirth experiences and perceived barriers to accessing antenatal and postnatal services.

MA-GEM total scores were computed by summing the 32 items (possible range: 32–96), with reverse scoring applied where appropriate so that higher scores indicated greater endorsement of gender-equitable norms. Because GEM-based measures do not have universally established cut-offs, we dichotomized the total score using the theoretical midpoint of the possible range (64; equivalent to an average item score of 2), classifying scores 32–63 as lower endorsement and 64–96 as higher endorsement of gender-equitable norms [10, 15].

Maternal healthcare outcomes were ascertained by respondent sex: women reported their own pregnancy, delivery, and postnatal care experiences, whereas men reported these outcomes for their partners. Analyses of maternal healthcare outcomes were restricted to respondents with relevant pregnancy exposure (women who had ever been pregnant and men who reported having a partner who had been pregnant). Among the final sample (n = 632), n = 207 respondents reported relevant pregnancy exposure and were included in the bivariate and multivariable analyses of maternal healthcare outcomes.

Primary outcomes

Pregnancy monitoring (antenatal care; ANC). Pregnancy monitoring was operationalized as any antenatal care use, coded as 1 = reported at least one ANC visit/contact during pregnancy and 0 = reported no ANC [16]. This indicator reflects ANC coverage (any use) rather than adequacy of the recommended number of visits.

Place of delivery (facility-based delivery). Place of delivery was operationalized as facility-based delivery, coded as 1 = delivery in a health facility (hospital/health center/clinic; public or private) and 0 = delivery outside a health facility (home or other) [17].

Postnatal care (PNC). Postnatal care was operationalized as any postnatal care use, coded as 1 = reported at least one postnatal check after delivery and 0 = reported no postnatal care [18]. This indicator reflects PNC use and does not capture the recommended timing or number of postnatal contacts.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Bivariate associations between sociodemographic characteristics, MA-GEM categories, and maternal healthcare outcomes (antenatal care/pregnancy monitoring, place of delivery, and postnatal care) were assessed using chi-square tests (p ≤ 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression models were then fitted to examine associations between gender norms and maternal healthcare utilization while adjusting for sociodemographic covariates. Bivariate and multivariable analyses of maternal healthcare outcomes were restricted to respondents with relevant pregnancy exposure (women ever pregnant; men reporting a partner pregnancy). Respondent sex was not included in the combined adjusted models because maternal outcomes were reported differently by respondent sex (women self-report vs men partner-report); this was addressed through sex-stratified sensitivity analyses. We assessed multicollinearity among independent variables using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics; no evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed (VIF ≤ 1.70; tolerance ≥ 0.59).

As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted sex-stratified logistic regression models, estimating associations separately among women (self-reports) and men (partner-reports) using the same covariate set. Stratified results are provided in Supplementary Materials 2 and 3 (Tables S2–S7). Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23 [19].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in strict compliance with ethical research standards, with emphasis on participant anonymity and informed consent. Confidentiality was strictly maintained and participants were free to join or withdraw from the study without any repercussions. The Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Marrakech reviewed the research, granting approval under number 88/2024, thus confirming compliance with all necessary ethical guidelines.

Results

Sociodemographic profile of the study population

In this study involving 632 participants, the majority (74.5%) were women, of whom 65.8% were from urban areas. Employment was relatively low, with only 39.4% holding income-generating jobs. Most participants (90.7%) had education. The group was mostly single (58.5%), though a significant portion was married (35.9%). About half (50.8%) reported having a partner, the majority of whom were educated (82.2%). The participants’ ages varied, with an average of 29.5 years and a standard deviation of 10.3, reflecting a broad age range (see Table S1 in Supplementary Material 1).

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted among pregnancy-exposed respondents (n = 207, subset of total N = 632); descriptive distributions of ANC, place of delivery, and PNC by sex are presented in Supplementary Material 4 (Table S8).

The results demonstrated a strong association between MA-GEM scores (gender-norm attitudes) and reported maternal healthcare utilization outcomes (Table 1). Participants with higher GEM scores were significantly more likely to access antenatal care (91.9% vs. 51.7%, p < 0.001), deliver in a healthcare facility (96.6% vs. 60.3%, p < 0.001), and receive postnatal care (70.9% vs. 51.7%, p = 0.008) compared with those with lower GEM scores.

Access to antenatal care was also significantly associated with several sociodemographic factors, including gender, place of residence, personal education level, and partner’s education level. Women, urban residents, and individuals with higher levels of education were more likely to report pregnancy monitoring. Antenatal care utilization was notably higher among urban residents compared with rural residents (91.5% vs. 62.8%, p < 0.001) and among schooled participants compared with unschooled participants (86.2% vs. 57.5%, p < 0.001). In addition, participants with educated partners demonstrated higher rates of antenatal care utilization (p = 0.001).

Regarding place of delivery, significant associations were observed with place of residence, employment status, and educational attainment of both participants and their partners. Institutional delivery was substantially more frequent among urban residents (97.7% vs. 67.9%, p < 0.001) and among participants with formal education (p = 0.002). Participants engaged in income-generating activities were also more likely to deliver in a healthcare facility than those without paid employment (92.8% vs. 76.8%, p = 0.001).

Access to postnatal care was similarly influenced by sociodemographic factors, including place of residence, personal education level, and partner’s education level. Urban residents were significantly more likely to seek postnatal care compared with rural residents (72.7% vs. 53.8%, p = 0.005). Educated participants reported higher utilization of postnatal services than those without formal education (71.1% vs. 42.5%, p = 0.001). Partner’s education level was also positively associated with postnatal care access (p = 0.044) (Table 1).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that adherence to inequitable gender norms was independently associated with reduced access to antenatal care services (Table 2). Participants with low MA-GEM scores were significantly less likely to access antenatal care compared with those with high MA-GEM scores (adjusted OR = 0.169; (95% CI [0.061–0.469]; p = 0.001).

In contrast, participants without formal education showed higher odds of accessing supervised delivery services (adjusted OR = 4.712; 95% CI [1.142–19.450]; p = 0.032), while age, and partner’s education level were not significantly associated after adjustment.

No statistically significant associations were identified between gender norms or sociodemographic factors and access to postnatal care in the multivariate analysis (Supplementary Material 5, Table S9; pregnancy-exposed analytic subsample N = 207, PNC: yes = 135, no = 72).

In sensitivity analyses stratified by respondent sex (women vs. men), estimates in the women subgroup (self-reported outcomes) were generally more precise, whereas estimates in the men subgroup were less precise, which may reflect proxy-reporting misclassification and smaller stratum sizes. Detailed stratified results are presented in Supplementary Materials 2 and 3 (Tables S2–S7).

Discussion

This study indicates that higher MA-GEM scores (reflecting more gender-equitable norms) were associated with greater utilization of antenatal care and facility-based childbirth in the Marrakech-Safi region. Specifically, MA-GEM scores remained independently associated with antenatal care utilization and facility-based childbirth after adjustment for sociodemographic factors, underscoring the central role of gender relations in shaping maternal health service utilization. In contrast, no significant association was observed between gender norms and postnatal care in multivariate analysis, suggesting that different mechanisms may govern access across the continuum of maternal care.

Our findings indicate that individuals adhering to inequitable gender norms were significantly less likely to access antenatal care services. This result aligns with prior evidence highlighting the importance of supportive male involvement and shared decision-making during pregnancy, particularly in low-resource settings [20]. Shibeshi et al. reported that restrictive gender perceptions can limit women’s autonomy in seeking antenatal care, especially in rural contexts where traditional norms remain deeply embedded [20].

These findings are further supported by studies emphasizing that gender dynamics within households influence women’s ability to seek timely and adequate pregnancy monitoring [3, 7]. Gui et al. also highlighted the role of peer support in online health communities, reinforcing the need for interventions that leverage both community support and active male participation [21]. Together, this body of evidence suggests that interventions aimed at transforming inequitable gender norms may substantially improve antenatal care uptake.

With regard to place of delivery, our analysis revealed that adherence to inequitable gender norms was strongly associated with lower odds of supervised childbirth. This finding is consistent with evidence from Nepal, where Paul et al. demonstrated that social and descriptive norms significantly influence institutional delivery [22]. However, our results also suggest that gender norms alone are insufficient to guarantee access to supervised delivery. Structural factors, including rural residence and lack of paid employment, remained significant barriers after adjustment. Similar patterns have been observed in Zimbabwe, where Webb et al. reported that despite favorable norms toward facility-based delivery, women continued to give birth at home due to compounded vulnerabilities such as distance, poverty, and limited transportation [23]. Social norms related to stillbirths in South Africa further underline the complex role of traditional beliefs and pressures in shaping maternal health behaviors and outcomes [24]. These findings highlight the interaction between normative and structural barriers and underscore the need for integrated interventions that address both.

An unexpected finding was the higher adjusted odds of supervised (facility-based) delivery among participants without formal education (AOR = 4.712). This contrasts with most evidence showing that education is generally associated with higher utilization of facility delivery [25, 26]. This estimate should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the wide confidence interval and the possibility of sparse-data or model instability effects when categories are imbalanced. In addition, education is closely correlated with other determinants of facility delivery (rural residence, employment, and partner’s education), and mutual adjustment may yield suppression or residual confounding. Context-specific mechanisms may also contribute; for example, structural barriers (distance, transport costs, and service availability/quality) can dominate delivery decisions even when individual characteristics differ, and some subgroups may rely more on public facilities or present to facilities when complications arise [8]. Overall, this result should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrants confirmation in larger, more representative samples.

Although bivariate analysis suggested that equitable gender norms were associated with improved access to postnatal care, this association did not persist in multivariate models. This may indicate that postnatal care utilization is shaped by factors beyond gender norms, including health-system constraints and postpartum sociocultural practices.

For example, availability and perceived quality of postnatal services, continuity of care and referral practices, and indirect access barriers (distance, transport, and out-of-pocket costs) may influence uptake [18, 27, 28]. In Morocco, postnatal consultation remains underutilized and is influenced by multiple sociodemographic and access-related factors, suggesting that structural determinants may outweigh normative influences in the postpartum period [29]. In addition, postpartum cultural practices and expectations (including postpartum seclusion, family-centered care, and traditional beliefs) may restrict mobility or reduce perceived need for facility-based follow-up [30].

Nevertheless, previous research emphasizes the importance of family and partner support in facilitating postnatal care uptake. Finlayson et al. highlighted that involvement of fathers, partners, and other family members plays a crucial role in encouraging postnatal care utilization [31]. Interventions that foster supportive family environments may therefore remain relevant, even if gender norms alone do not independently predict postnatal care access.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from a substantial sample size and the use of the rigorously adapted Moroccan Arabic GEM scale, which strengthens the validity of our findings on the influence of gender norms on access to maternal healthcare services. Additionally, the broad geographic coverage across the Marrakech-Safi region ensures that our results capture the diverse experiences of the community, providing valuable insights into how gender norms interact with access to maternal healthcare services.

Despite these strengths, the study is subject to limitations inherent in self-reported data, particularly the potential for social desirability bias, where participants may provide responses they perceive as socially acceptable. We sought to minimize this bias by collecting responses anonymously, thereby promoting more accurate and honest reporting.

Second, male respondents reported some maternal healthcare outcomes about their partners, which may introduce reporting bias due to limited knowledge of care-seeking, social desirability, or misclassification of service use. Such misclassification is likely to be non-differential, with respect to MA-GEM and could bias associations toward the null. We therefore interpret findings from the men subgroup cautiously and present sex-stratified analyses as sensitivity checks.

In addition, we used convenience sampling with mixed online and in-person recruitment. The in-person component was included specifically to reach participants with limited internet access, lower literacy, or without smartphones, which helped reduce digital-selection bias. However, as the sample was non-probabilistic, it may not fully represent the Marrakech-Safi population, and generalizability should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that gender norms, measured using MA-GEM, are associated with maternal healthcare utilization in the Marrakech-Safi region. Higher MA-GEM scores were associated with greater utilization of antenatal care and facility-based childbirth, highlighting the potential importance of gender-equitable norms for maternal healthcare use. Conversely, inequitable household decision-making dynamics and structural constraints, particularly rural residence and unemployment, were linked to reduced access to these services. These findings underscore the need for integrated interventions that address both sociocultural determinants (gender norms and household power relations) and structural barriers to access (rurality, transport, cost constraints, and socioeconomic disadvantage) simultaneously. Future research should prioritize inclusive community engagement and targeted implementation strategies to improve maternal health outcomes among vulnerable populations across the region.
