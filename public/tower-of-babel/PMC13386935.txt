Association of IBS-associated E. coli strains with epithelial inflammatory responses and tight junction dysregulation in Caco-2 cells
Authors: Zeinab Merzaei, Hossein Kazemian, Zahra Bamzadeh, Mansoureh Paknejadi, Ebrahim Kouhsari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIrritable bowel syndrome (IBS) presents with symptoms of pain, abdominal discomfort, and altered bowel habits. Changes in the intestinal microbiota, including an increase in Enterobacteriaceae like E. coli, have been associated with IBS. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of IBS-associated E. coli strains on epithelial inflammatory responses, tight junction-related gene expression, and Toll-like receptor regulation in Caco-2 cells.ResultsResults showed that IBS-associated E. coli strains exhibited significantly higher invasion activity and increased production of IL-8 and TNF-α compared to H-E. coli. Furthermore, IBS-associated E. coli strains upregulated TLR-4 and TLR-5 expression and reduced the expression of tight junction proteins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07924-1.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Irritable bowel syndrome, Cytokines, Cytoskeletal proteins, Caco-2 cells

Introduction

Alterations in the intestinal microbiota contribute to the pathophysiology of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [1]. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is characterized by variable symptoms of pain, abdominal discomfort, and changes in bowel movement patterns. 3 to 36% of IBS cases are caused by bacterial gastroenteritis and are classified as post-infectious IBS (PI-IBS) [2]. It has been shown that the change in intestinal microbiota and the increase of the Enterobacteriaceae family such as E. coli is attributed to the development of IBS [3]. E. coli pathotypes are responsible for gastrointestinal diseases in humans [4].

On the other hand, molecules produced by immune cells can affect intestinal function and disrupt intestinal barrier integrity through alterations in tight junctions (TJ) proteins such as claudin and occludin [5].

This study evaluated the effects of IBS-associated E. coli strains on inflammatory cytokines and tight-junction-related gene expression in Caco-2 cells.

Methods

Bacterial isolates

Fecal samples were collected from 40 IBS patients (Rome IV criteria) and 40 healthy controls (2020–2023). E. coli were identified by conventional biochemical tests and confirmed by 16 S rRNA gene PCR [6].

Phylotyping

Phylogenetic groups were determined by multiplex PCR targeting chuA, yjaA, and TSPE4.C2 as described by Clermont et al. [7]. using primers listed in Table 1. Group B2: positive for chuA and yjaA; group D: positive for chuA only; group B1: positive for TSPE4.C2 only; group A: negative for all three markers.

Adhesion assay

Caco-2 cells (ATCC HTB37A) were cultured in EMEM with 10% FBS. For adhesion, 10⁸ CFU/well bacteria were added for 60 min. After washing, adhered bacteria were Giemsa-stained and counted on 25 random cells [8]. HMLN-1 and JM109 served as controls [9]. For invasion, cells were infected with 10⁷ CFU/well for 2 h, then treated with gentamycin (150 µg/mL) for 1 h, lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100, and plated on TSA [10, 11].

Cytokine assay

Caco-2 cells were infected with each isolate (multiplicity of infection 10:1, 24 h). Supernatants were collected and TNF-α and IL-8 measured by Quantikine® ELISA kits [12].

Gene expression assay of bacterial genes and cell lines

RNA was extracted and qPCR performed using qPCRBIO SyGreen Mix on an ABI StepOne system with primers listed in Table 1. GAPDH was the internal control. Relative expression was calculated by 2−∆∆CT method.

Statistical analysis

Data are mean ± SD of three independent experiments. Two-way ANOVA and Student’s t-test were used (SPSS).

Results

Phylotyping

All of H-E. coli and IBS-E. coli isolates phylogenetically were grouped. Among H-E. coli isolates, 4 (40%), 3 (30%), 2 (20%), and 1 (10%) were determined as B2, D, A, and B1 groups respectively. Also, the B2 group was the most common (n = 24, 60%) group between IBS-E. coli isolates followed by A, D and B1 groups (n = 11, 27.5%, n = 4, 10% and n = 1, 2.5% respectively) (Fig. 1). Only B2 showed significant association with IBS‑E. coli (p = 0.035). Representative agarose gel images for chuA and yjaA are provided as Supplementary Figures.

Study population and diagnostic criteria

Patients were recruited from individuals referred to the gastroenterology clinic and diagnosed IBS by an experienced gastroenterologist based on standard clinical diagnostic criteria (Rome IV criteria). To exclude IBS and active intestinal inflammation, fecal calprotectin testing was performed for all enrolled patients prior to sample collection. Only patients with clinical findings consistent with IBS and without evidence of organic IBS were included. The available demographic characteristics including age, sex, and IBS subtype classification (IBS-D, IBS-C, and IBS-M where applicable) were recorded and are summarized in (Table 2).

Bacterial adhesion and invasion assay

After preparation of Caco-2 cell lines, 40 IBS-associated E. coli strains and 40 H-E. coli were selected for adhesion assay. The adherence of IBS-associated E. coli strains isolates to Caco-2 cells (Mean = 9.8 CFU/cell, SEM = ± 0.6) was significantly (P < 0.001) more than H-E. coli isolates (3.8 ± 0.1 CFU/cell) (Fig. 2A). Also, the invasion rate by IBS-associated E. coli strains was 142 ± 9 CFU/Well which is significantly higher than H-E. coli strains with 50 ± 8 CFU/Well (p value < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

Cytokine assay

Pro-inflammatory cytokine assay was performed on Caco-2 cell lines supernatant. While both of H-E. coli and IBS-associated E. coli strains isolates elevated cytokines production in comparison to baseline in the Caco-2 cell line, the amounts of increase in IL-8 and TNF-α cytokines were not significant after inoculation of H-E. coli. On the other hand, both IL-8 and TNF-α cytokines were elevated significantly after inoculation of IBS-associated E. coli strains in comparison to baseline and H-E. coli inoculated cells (Fig. 3).

Gene expression assay

The comparison between IBS-associated E. coli strains treated Caco-2 cell lines and H-E. coli treated cell lines showed that expression of claudin-2, TLR4 and TLR5 were significantly (p-value < 0.001) increased in Caco-2 cell lines exposed to IBS-associated E. coli strains. Also, the mean fold of meprin and cingulin was elevated non-significantly. On the other hand, occuldin and ZO-1 expression were significantly lower than untreated cell lines (Fig. 4). In addition to IL-8 and TNF-α, this expanded marker panel revealed that IBS-associated E. coli differentially regulate distinct tight-junction components (increased claudin-2; decreased occludin and ZO-1) and Toll-like receptors (increased TLR4 and TLR5).

Discussion

There are four major phylotypes of E. coli including A, B1, B2, and D. phylogroup B2 is the most commonly associated phylogroup with intestinal diseases. Within this group, certain strains of E. coli, such as Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), and Enterohemorrhagic E. coli, are well-known for their association with gastrointestinal infections [7]. Additionally, phylogroup D has also been implicated in some intestinal diseases but it is less common in comparison to phylogroup B2. The association of this phylogroup and several diseases has been determined in several studies [18, 19].

Therefore, it is not surprising that phylogroup B2 is the most common phylogroup among IBS individuals in our study (Fig. 1). We also acknowledge that the original Clermont triplex PCR method used in this study has been subsequently updated to improve discrimination of cryptic clades and phylogroups. Therefore, future studies using the revised phylogenetic classification scheme may provide more refined strain characterization. On the other hand, the prevalence of A, B1, and D phylogroups was not significantly different between IBS and healthy individuals. Then, it is interpretable that phylogroup B2 is probably associated with the incidence of IBS or the bowel of IBS individuals is more suitable for colonization of this phylogroup. Gene expression assay results indicated that TJ proteins such as ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin were expressed lower when the cell lines were treated with IBS-associated E. coli strains rather with H-E. coli strains. Several studies indicated a positive correlation between barrier dysfunction and IBS symptoms including abdominal pain and changes in the bowel epithelium [20–23]. It has been proved that some bacterial species such as Salmonella Typhimurium, Campylobacter jejuni, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and E. coli pathotypes can manipulate epithelium integrity and trigger inflammation [10, 24, 25]. In addition, more inflamed epithelium is more prone to pathogen bacteria invasion [26]. In IBS as an inflammatory syndrome, it has been shown that the expression of TJ proteins such as ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin is lower than the normal rates [20]. The decrease in these proteins’ expression is responsible for elevated permeability and accumulation of fluids in the lumen [20]. Downregulation of occludin and ZO-1 might also contribute to a barrier dysfunction, but this should be confirmed with Western blotting and microscopy, which is up to future investigations. Interestingly the reason behind this decrease is not completely understood. However, our study results highlight the role of microbiota in the alterations in the bowel epithelium integrity. It seems that the domination of certain strains of E. coli interrupts the bowel epithelium and probably is one of the reasons for barrier dysfunction in IBS patients.

The increased claudin-2 expression may contribute to barrier dysfunction, while reduced occludin and ZO-1 are consistent with TJ disruption reported in other inflammatory conditions.

Increased TLR4 and TLR5 expression is consistent with previous reports in IBS [27–29].

Limitations

Limitations include the use of Caco-2 cells and the limited cytokine panel; validation in more physiologically relevant models is needed.

Conclusion

IBS-associated E. coli strains show increased invasiveness, altered cytokine production, TJ dysregulation, and TLR modulation in Caco-2 cells compared to healthy controls. These differences suggest a role in IBS pathophysiology, but clinical significance requires further in vivo validation with larger samples.
