Abstract
. Warning: This article includes red-teaming experiments, which contain examples of compromised LLM responses that may be offensive or upsetting. Large Language Models (LLMs) have the potential to create harmful content, such as generating sophisticated phishing emails and assisting in writing code of harmful computer viruses. Thus, it is crucial to ensure their safe and responsible response generation. To reduce the risk of generating harmful or irresponsible content, researchers have developed techniques such as reinforcement learning with human feedback to align LLM’s outputs with human values and preferences. However, it is still undetermined whether such measures are sufficient to prevent LLMs from generating interesting responses. In this study, we propose Amnesia, a lightweight activation-space adversarial attack that manipulates internal transformer states to bypass existing safety mechanisms in open-weight LLMs. Through experimental analysis on state-of-the-art, open-weight LLMs, we demonstrate that our attack effectively circumvents existing safeguards, enabling the generation of harmful content without the need for any fine-tuning or additional training. Our experiments on benchmark datasets show that the proposed attack can induce various antisocial behaviors in LLMs. These findings highlight the urgent need for more robust security measures in open-weight LLMs and underscore the importance of continued research to prevent their potential misuse.



Large Language Models (LLMs) are capable of generating information across various applications. However, these capabilities can be exploited by malicious actors for adversarial purposes, such as producing antisocial or racist content, or creating computer viruses. This highlights the importance of ensuring that LLM responses are guided by safety and ethical considerations. To achieve this, safety mechanisms are integrated into LLMs, such as employing reinforcement learning with human feedback

In a broader context, the development of LLMs involves implementing protection measures across every layer of the development stack to ensure the generation of responsible and safe responses. These measures include guidelines for developers that emphasize the importance of ethical considerations during the creation and deployment of such models. The typical development process for LLMs to ensure safety, responsibility, and alignment with human preferences consists of several key steps:

•

Data Collection: The process begins with the collection of high-quality, robust data to enable strong performance across a variety of tasks.

•

Use of Synthetic Data: Synthetic data is employed in critical areas such as coding, reasoning, and long-context scenarios to enhance the model’s capabilities.

•

Fine-Tuning and Testing: The developed models undergo fine-tuning to ensure they generate safe and responsible responses. These models are then rigorously tested using both automated and manual evaluations on tasks that involve sensitive content, including weapons, cyberattacks, and child exploitation.

•

Red Teaming Exercises: External and internal domain experts conduct red teaming exercises to assess the model’s potential for misuse or harmful content generation.

•

Supervised Fine-Tuning: Models are further refined through supervised fine-tuning, where they are trained on generating safe and helpful content. Additionally, human feedback is incorporated to reinforce the model’s responses, ensuring they align with human preferences.

These mechanisms are designed to mitigate the risk of irresponsible and unsafe response generation in LLMs, making LLM-based agents more reliable by preventing them from responding to potentially harmful queries. However, adversarial attacks have demonstrated the ability to bypass these safeguards and manipulate the safe and responsible behavior of LLMs

Contributions

In this study, we propose a novel lightweight adversarial attack to jailbreak open-source LLMs, inspired by
(

)

Amnesia

•

focuses on a

•

extracts an attack vector

•

during inference on new prompts, subtracts a scaled copy
\alpha\mathbf{V}_{\text{i}}

earlier

Compared to prompt-based black-box attacks

training-free

2.
Literature Review and Background

Language models are one of the most useful and challenging topics in artificial intelligence (AI) dating back to early 1950s. Earlier example of language models include ngram models and LSTM-based language models. With the advent of transformers, LLMs have seen significant improvements in their efficiency and wide adoptions across various applications. LLMs are now one of the most important building blocks of general-purpose AI
(
19
)
. With the increasing landscape of LLMs, we find many open-weight and private LLMs commercially available with significant improvements, thus making their usage more prominent in different applications
(
23
)
. For example, OpenAI’s ChatGPT is one of the most widely known commercially available LLM, whereas Meta’s LLama is one of the most widely known families of open-weight LLMs.

2.1.
Working Principle of LLMs

In this section, we provide a high-level overview of the working mechanism of LLMs, which is crucial for understanding the proposed attack. Essentially, LLMs are trained to estimate the probability distribution over an entire vocabulary, where the vocabulary refers to the set of words in the training data. Formally, let
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n}
represent a sequence of tokens in a given sentence. The probability of this sequence of tokens occurring in a specific order is determined by the chain rule of probability given by Equation
1
:

(1)

\displaystyle p(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=p(y_{1})\cdot p(y_{2}\mid y_{1})\cdots p(y_{n}\mid y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p\bigl(y_{i}\mid y_{<i}\bigr)

LLMs estimate the probability of the next token given a sequence of preceding tokens. In practice, LLMs estimate a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary. Sampling techniques, such as top-k and temperature scaling, are then applied to efficiently sample tokens based on the desired level of diversity and coherence for a given application.

2.3.
Knowledge Representation in Deep Neural Networks

Studies have shown that in deep neural networks, lower level layers are responsible for recognizing low-level characteristic of the training data, while recognition of higher level concepts increases with the depth of the layers
(
3
)
. In case of LLM, this also holds true that lower level layers are responsible for lexical features and higher level layers are more associated with semantic features
(
16
)
. Furthermore, studies have also shown that for some tasks only a subset of layers is responsible for the final output, i.e., for some tasks the final output is comparable to the output of some intermediate layers
(
8
)
. This presents two key observations:

Observation

(1) Different layers in a deep neural networks are responsible for capturing different levels or types of information. However, this representation could be sparse.

Observation

(2) The outputs of intermediate layers can be decoded to yield interpretable or practically useful representations.

2.4.
Intermediate Activations of LLMs

Existing research has demonstrated that it is possible to decode the intermediate states of different modules of LLM into understandable and interpretable tokens. For instance, Belrose et al.
(
5
)
trained an affine probe for each block in a pretrained model, enabling the decoding of hidden states across the entire training vocabulary. Moreover, studies have shown that such intermediate decoding is feasible at various levels within the model, such as after the multilayer perceptron (MLP) modules, the attention mechanisms, and the residual stream
(
24
;
26
)
. The key insight behind intermediate activations is that the activations of individual modules, particularly in the higher layers, can often be decoded into meaningful interpretations (observation 2). These intermediate decodings provide valuable information about the specific layers and/or individual modules at which LLMs begin to recognize different concepts. This helps identify critical layers or modules, where techniques such as concept mixing could be most effective.

2.5.
Understanding Word Embedding and Context Learning

In computational processing of natural language, textual input is represented as numerical feature vectors, widely known as word embeddings, which encode semantic meaning of each word or n-gram
(
1
)
. There are many different approaches of implementing word embeddings
(
10
)
. One of the key features of word embeddings is that they represent the meaning of a word and some of them also encode the context in which a word appears. For example, the embeddings for a “black dog” are different from the embeddings of a “white dog”. Another surprising property of the embeddings is that many semantic and syntactic relationship among different word embeddings are linear in the embedding space. For example, the semantic difference between the embeddings of word “man” and “women” is similar to the difference between the embeddings of word “husband” and “wife”. Moreover, the syntactic difference of word embeddings of “king” and embeddings of “man” and addition of embedding of word “women” will be similar to “queen”
(
28
)
. Later in the development of the attack, we will also use similar linear operations on embeddings to steer the models activations in desired direction.

2.6.
Threat Landscape: Attacks Targeting LLMs

Adversarial attacks on machine learning have been an extensive topic in the last decade. Such adversarial attacks attempt to design strategies to induce errors and irresponsible response
(
6
;
21
)
. Numerous types of attacks have been developed to exploit LLMs. One of the most outstanding types of attacks among such attacks are jailbreak attacks, where an attacker carefully designs user prompts to bypass the safety measures of the LLMs to generate harmful or unethical responses. There has been prevailing work on jailbreak attacks. For example,
(
15
)
evaluated and assessed various methods for prompt based jailbreaking.
(
30
)
proposed a method in the white box setting with adversarial attack to create effective prompts for jailbreaking. Their approach includes finding a suffix which, when combined with a wide range of queries for LLMs, produces harmful and unethical content. In the following sections, we briefly review different types of attacks against LLMs.

2.6.1.
Black-box Prompt-based Jailbreaks

Early jailbreaks operate purely at the prompt level, treating the model as a black box and exploiting its instruction-following bias. In-the-wild attacks such as “Do-Anything-Now” (DAN) and related personas show that carefully crafted role-play or system prompts can consistently override safety features of commercial and open models
(
25
)
. The
WildJailbreak
dataset collects 390 such “forbidden questions” across 13 OpenAI policy scenarios (economic harm, fraud, health, hate speech, physical harm, etc.), providing a standardized benchmark of organically discovered jailbreak prompts
(
25
)
.

Beyond manual authored prompts,
black-box optimization
methods iteratively refine instructions using only input–output feedback.
7
introduce PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement), which maintains a population of candidate jailbreak prompts, queries the target model and a safety judge, and applies mutation-type edits guided by success scores. Within roughly a few dozen queries, PAIR reliably discovers high-success prompts against diverse chat models, without any gradient access.

2.6.2.
White-box Gradient-Based Prompt Attacks

When gradients are available, attacks can move from heuristic edits to explicit optimization in token space.

30

frame jailbreaks as learning a short
universal adversarial suffix
: starting from a random token sequence, they use a Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) procedure to maximize the log-likelihood of an affirmative or non-refusal continuation across many harmful prompts. Once optimized, this suffix is simply appended to new inputs and often transfers strongly to other models, including aligned black-box APIs.

12
refine GCG-style attacks by importing ideas from adversarial training in vision. They introduce Language-SGM (LSGM), which dampens gradients from later residual blocks to focus on robust directions, and Language-ILA (LILA), which aligns updates with a mid-layer adversarial direction. Combined, these modifications substantially raise attack success rates on benchmarks like AdvBench while improving transfer and computational efficiency relative to vanilla GCG.

A complementary line of work shows that
fine-tuning
itself can compromise alignment.
22
demonstrate that modest supervised finetuning on harmful data, or even benign-looking data with adversarially chosen labels, can erode safety guarantees and resurrect unsafe behaviors, highlighting that post-hoc training is not a simple, monotone-safe knob.

2.6.3.
Activation-space and Weight-space Interventions

Recent mechanistic studies move from prompts to the model’s internal representation space.
2
argue that refusals are largely mediated by a single direction in the residual stream: by contrasting residual activations for harmful prompts that are refused versus complied with, they estimate a “refusal” vector and show that projecting this direction out at inference time (or editing weights to be orthogonal to it) can strongly affect safety behavior. However, this approach involves computing activations across extensive collections of harmful and benign prompts for each layer, which introduces significant computational cost and depends on the availability of curated datasets, making it computationally expensive, dependent on curated datasets, and therefore impractical for attackers.

3.
Proposed Attack

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the proposed attack.

3.1.
Threat Model

To describe the proposed attack, we first define the threat model under which the adversary operates.
The attacker operates under the follwoing assumptions:

•

We assume a white-box setting, where the attacker has full access to the target model’s internal parameters.
The adversary has complete access to the model’s residual stream and can manipulate it, i.e., can query the model freely and observe its intermediate representations (e.g., hidden states or layer activations)

•

The attacker possesses sufficient computational resources to perform inference and activation analyses on the model.

3.1.1.
Attacker’s Objective

The primary goal of the attacker is to manipulate the model by exploiting its internal representations during inference time and to cause targeted misbehavior (e.g., producing incorrect or harmful outputs). Furthermore, attacker’s goal is also to interpret or reconstruct intermediate layer outputs to gain insight into the model’s internal reasoning process.

The attack does not modify the training process or retrain the model, it is purely inference-time manipulation.

Experiments And Results

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed attack. We first define the criteria used to measure attack success, followed by a description of the datasets employed in our analysis.

4.1.
Attack Success Rate

We measure
attack success
at the response level. Given a harmful prompt, we compare the baseline model and the model under proposed attack Amnesia. A trial is counted as
successful
if the attacked model produces at least one non-refusal pattern where the baseline refuses, as detected by a refusal-pattern heuristics (e.g., detecting phrases such as “I cannot help with that” or “I am not allowed to”)
(
30
)
. For aggregate plots and tables, the
Attack Success Rate (ASR)
is the fraction of prompts where Amnesia induces such a non-refusal, i.e., where the model complies with the (potentially unsafe) request under the evaluation settings.

5.
Comparison

This section presents a comparison of the proposed method to existing white-box attack approaches, as summarized in Table
12
. We benchmark prior work along several key dimensions, including the requirement for objective-based training (i.e., whether the attack necessitates any model training), the need for fine-tuning or weight steering (i.e., whether the method relies on weight adjustment or direct weight manipulation), reliance on prompt modification (i.e., whether the attack alters the user’s input prompt), and the scope of activation steering (i.e., whether activations must be computed across all the layers or only within a localized layer). Together, these criteria provide insight into the operational efficiency and practical deployability of each attack. To ensure a fair comparison, we focus on broad, high-level methodological characteristics rather than narrow or implementation-specific details. This abstraction enables a more general and meaningful assessment of the applicability and effectiveness of each attack strategy.

The comparative results in Table
12
highlight several important distinctions among existing attack methods and the proposed approach. Methods such as
2
and AutoDAN
(
14
)
both rely on objective-based training, indicating a higher setup cost for an adversary (
2
do not directly train on objective function, however they still calculate activations using two datasets and edit the weights based on the mean values. Therefore, we consider it as objective training), while
(
7
;
29
;
12
)
similarly require model-driven optimization during attack construction. In contrast, the proposed method eliminates the need for any training, substantially reducing computational overhead and simplifying the attack pipeline.

With respect to model modification,
2
’s approach additionally requires fine-tuning or weight steering, which introduces further engineering complexity and restricts practical deployment under constrained computation. AutoDAN and PAIR avoid weight updates but instead depend on prompt modification. The proposed method, by avoiding both weight manipulation and prompt alteration, offers a more streamlined and stealthy attack mechanism.

The scope of activation steering further differentiates these approaches.
2
’s attack requires global activation computation across full model layers, which is computationally demanding and less scalable (for example, for bigger models). In contrast, the proposed method employs local activation steering, restricting computation to targeted regions/layers and thereby improving efficiency while maintaining effectiveness.

Overall, the comparison demonstrates that the proposed method achieves a favorable balance of minimal prerequisites, no training, no weight adjustments, no prompt modification while also reducing computational burden through localized activation analysis. These characteristics collectively suggest that the proposed approach is more practical and broadly deployable than prior methods.

6.
Discussion and Limitations

In this work, we demonstrate that an attacker can successfully jailbreak a model using a simple yet highly effective method based solely on modifying activations at a single layer, without any weight updates, objective-function training, or changes that compromise overall model coherence. Notably, the attack does not require large-scale harmful or benign datasets; it operates only on a set of keywords and a simple adversarial query.

6.1.
Limitations

Although the study introduces a simple and effective attack technique, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while we evaluate our approach on a selection of state-of-the-art models, we cannot claim its generalizability to architectures that were not tested. Second, our method relies on empirically identifying the scaling factor
\alpha
and the steering vector
\mathbf{S_{b}}
.
Rigorous investigation on optimizing this process, e.g., optimizing
\alpha
with respect to the ASR, remains open for future work.
Ultimately, we view this study as a proof-of-concept demonstrating that model refusals can be altered through simple additive steering of activations at a single layer.
