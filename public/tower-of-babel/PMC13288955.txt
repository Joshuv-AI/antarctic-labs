Brain atrophy rates vary with age in relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis
Authors: Sezgi Kaçar, David R van Nederpelt, Julia R Jelgerhuis, Eline M E Coerver, Joep Killestein, Maria Pia Sormani, Olga Ciccarelli, Douglas L Arnold, Zoé L E van Kempen, B M J Uitdehaag, Frederik Barkhof, Marcus W Koch, Menno M Schoonheim, Arman Eshaghi, Eva M M Strijbis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain atrophy is increasingly used as an outcome measure in clinical trials in relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS), but little is known about how chronological age interacts with MS-specific effects. For instance, while annual brain atrophy rates typically increase with age in healthy individuals, MS patients tend to exhibit decreasing atrophy rates over time.MethodsWe investigated the relationship between age and brain volume in a large dataset of 4241 trial participants with RRMS. We used pooled individual-participant data from phase 3 clinical trials with 96 weeks follow-up, which included both active treatment and placebo/comparator arms. Participants were categorised into seven groups based on chronological age (18–24 years, 25–30 years, 31–35 years, 36–40 years, 41–45 years, 46–50 years, 51–56 years). We performed multilevel linear mixed-effects regression analyses to examine differences between age groups in normalised whole brain volume (NWBV), thalamus grey matter volume (NThGMV), grey matter volume (NGMV) and white matter volume at baseline and their changes over follow-up. We also studied how disease duration influenced these relationships using similar models.ResultsOlder participants showed significantly lower NWBV, NGMV and NThGMV at baseline than younger participants. Most importantly, older participants exhibited lower rates of atrophy during follow-up, particularly in the thalamus. This association was consistent across all disease duration subgroups.ConclusionsOlder participants had more severe atrophy when enrolled into trials, but slower (thalamic) atrophy rates, independent of disease duration over time. Together, these findings emphasise that age should be taken into account when designing clinical trials that use brain atrophy as an outcome measure.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In a pooled dataset of 4241 RRMS trial participants, older patients had lower baseline whole-brain, grey matter and thalamic volumes, while showing slower atrophy rates during follow-up, which were most prominent in thalamic grey matter. These findings were consistent across disease-duration subgroups, while no age-related differences were found in white matter or lesion volumes, highlighting the importance of disentangling age-related from MS-specific effects.

Introduction

Brain volume change is increasingly used as a primary outcome measure in phase 3 clinical trials that evaluate immunomodulatory and neuroprotective compounds in multiple sclerosis (MS).13 In healthy individuals, brain atrophy accelerates from approximately 0.2% per year starting around the age of 35 years to about 0.5% by age 60 years.4 While earlier studies suggested a steady, linear increase in annual brain volume loss, more recent large-scale analyses reveal age-dependent, region-specific, non-linear trajectories that vary across the lifespan, alongside substantial interindividual variability.5 6

In contrast, people with MS experience substantially higher rates of whole-brain volume loss—typically ranging between −0.7% and −1.0% per year, largely driven by grey matter (GM) atrophy, although white matter (WM) atrophy can also be substantial in some individuals.7 8 Among these neurodegenerative changes, GM atrophy is particularly relevant due to its strong and consistent correlation with clinical disability.9 10

Several studies have shown that atrophy may not be a linear process in MS. For instance, GM atrophy begins early in the disease course, progresses rapidly in the initial stages and slows later on.11 12 This trajectory is, for example, evident in the thalamus, where early volume loss predicts conversion from CIS to relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) and is associated with cognitive decline over time.1214

Because both MS and normal ageing contribute to brain atrophy, and distinguishing between these processes is important—especially as patients age.5 7 Normal ageing leads to progressive brain volume loss, which plays a larger role in older individuals with MS.5 7 While MS-related neurodegeneration persists across the lifespan, age-related changes may overshadow MS-specific processes in older individuals, complicating the interpretation of MRI findings.7 In addition, there is some correlation between age and disease duration, which, in combination with the natural ageing process, makes it difficult to estimate the natural course of atrophy over the lifespan of MS patients and complicates the comparison of studies with heterogeneous patient populations.7 15 Understanding age-related differences is especially important when rate estimates serve as a basis for sample size calculations. Generalising over the full age range could result in inaccurate power calculations and increased risk of type II errors.16 17

Here, we investigated differences in brain volume measures and atrophy rate estimates in people with RRMS in different age categories, using patient-level clinical trial data.

Materials and methods

Dataset and study participants

We used aggregated data from five completed multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 clinical trials in participants with RRMS, including the BRAVO,18 CONFIRM,19 DEFINE,20 OPERA 1 and 2 studies,21 all of which have been described in detail previously and are summarised in online supplemental information S1. Data from all treatment arms were pooled, as the primary aim of this study was not to evaluate treatment effects, but to investigate overall patterns of brain atrophy across age groups regardless of treatment.

Eligibility criteria were in harmony for all the studies; participants should be diagnosed with RRMS according to the 2005/2010 revised McDonald criteria,22 23 be aged between 18–56 years and have an entry Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score between 0.0 and 5.5. Key exclusion criteria were the presence of a progressive disease course or previous use of certain MS-related treatment (the specific definitions differed between trials). The complete inclusion and exclusion criteria concerning the completed five phase 3 clinical trials are described in detail in the original publications.1821

Standard protocol approvals, registration and patient consent

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and each study protocol underwent ethical review.1821 Approval for this study was granted by the institutional review board at the Montreal Neurological Institute, Quebec, Canada (reference number: IRB00010120). Data from the BRAVO, CONFIRM, DEFINE, OPERA I and II studies were gathered under the auspices of the International Progressive MS Alliance.

Study design and outcome measures

Throughout the initial phase 3 clinical trials, participants underwent clinical visits every 3 months to assess disability progression, accompanied by annual MRI scans. Detailed information on clinical and radiological outcome measurements is contained in the original trial publications.1821 In this study, we used clinical and MRI data at baseline and 96-week follow-up. All volumetric MRI measures were derived from pre-processed outputs of the original clinical trial imaging pipeline.24

MRI protocol and brain image processing

T1-weighted (T1-w) and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were acquired as part of the original clinical trials. For each participant, baseline and follow-up scans were acquired using identical MRI protocols, with full acquisition details described in the respective publications.1821 For the present study, we used the preprocessed outputs provided from these trials that were published previously.24 As previously reported, 2D T1-w scans were used to segment grey and WM compartments and T2-FLAIR and T1-w scans for lesion segmentation.24 An identical cross-sectional pipeline, treating each visit independently, was implemented for all visits of patients with available T1-w and FLAIR MRI scans. Hyperintense lesions were automatically segmented on FLAIR images using a consensus approach of the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox25 and a deep convolutional neural network approach in DeepMedic.26 FLAIR and T1-w images were first bias-field-corrected and then registered to the corresponding T1-w images using Advanced Normalization Tools, after which lesion filling was performed with NiftySeg. Tissue segmentation and atlas-based parcellation were performed using the standardised cross-sectional MRI-processing workflow previously applied to these clinical trial datasets—based on the Geodesic Information Flows framework27—on the lesion-filled T1-w images.24 Brain regions were defined according to the Neuromorphometrics atlas (www.neuromorphometrics.com). For this study, we used normalised whole brain volume (NWBV), GM volume (NGMV), thalamic GMV (NThGMV; GM only, calculated as the mean of left and right ThGMVs), WM volume (NWMV) and T2 lesion volume (T2LV). Volumes were normalised by intracranial volume (ICV) in the statistical models.

Statistical analysis

We divided the trial participants into consecutive age groups (18–24 years, 25–30 years, 31–35 years, 36–40 years, 41–45 years, 46–50 years and 51–56 years) and disease duration groups (0–4 years, 5–9 years and ≥10 years), which were defined a priori. The age group 25–30 years served as the reference category. Only participants with data available at both baseline and at the 96-week follow-up were included in the analysis.

We used multilevel linear mixed models, in which the MRI outcome measure was the dependent variable, the patient’s age was the independent variable, and the study and site ID were included as random factors. All analyses were corrected for sex, medication arm (subdivided into three groups: placebo, first-line and second-line medication) and ICV, with the latter used to statistically normalise MRI volumetric measures for head size.

First, we investigated the relationship between age and brain volume measurements (T2LV, NWBV, NGMV, NThGMV, NWMV) at trial baseline using the complete dataset (analysis I). T2LV was log-transformed prior to statistical analysis. We then calculated the absolute differences in these brain volumes across different age groups. Next, we examined the association between age and changes in brain volumes over the follow-up period (analysis II). For each brain compartment, we calculated the percentage volume change relative to the individual’s own baseline volume. This atrophy rate was then annualised using the follow-up time in years, as described in formula 1.

We repeated these analyses when the dataset was divided into three separate datasets based on disease duration (0–4 years, 5–9 years and ≥10 years) to explore if potential age differences were similar over different disease durations. Furthermore, to assess potential sex differences, we conducted an analysis of baseline NWBV and its changes over time, stratified by sex. To evaluate the influence of acute inflammatory activity—which is generally more pronounced in younger patients with a higher risk of inflammatory disease activity—on brain volume changes, we performed a sensitivity analysis including only participants that did not develop new lesions during the trial. To further account for broader aspects of inflammatory disease activity, we repeated the longitudinal models with additional adjustment for baseline T2 lesion volume, Gadolinium (Gd)+ status and prior-year relapse rate. Additionally, we included disease duration and EDSS as covariates in the longitudinal models to assess whether these factors influenced the observed age-related patterns. As a third addition, we repeated the analysis for age differences and atrophy for the period between week 48 and week 96 to account for the effects of pseudo atrophy. We also repeated the primary models within the placebo-only and interferon-only treatment arms to assess whether treatment exposure influenced the observed age-related patterns. Moreover, we repeated all analyses with age as a continuous factor. In a further step, age and disease duration were modelled as continuous variables using restricted cubic splines, alongside an age-by-disease duration interaction term, to assess potential non-linear or interacting effects.

We conducted all analyses using RStudio V.4.3.2 for data analysis. Normality of data was assessed by visually inspecting histograms. To account for multiple comparisons—including sex, treatment and brain size—a Bonferroni correction was applied, setting the statistical significance threshold at p value<0.0167.28 Throughout the manuscript, ‘older participants’ refers to all age groups above the reference group (25–30 years).

As expected, older participants had a longer disease duration than younger participants, while younger participants had more contrast-enhancing lesions at baseline and higher annualised relapse rates (ARR) in the year prior to inclusion (table 2).

Older participants had lower brain volumes at trial baseline

NWBV and NGMV at baseline were significantly lower in all older age groups compared with the reference group, a pattern that reflects a linear decline with increasing age (p<0.0001) (table 3 and onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2, figure 1A,B). For instance, the NGM volume was 7 mL (1.7%, 95% CI 5.6 to 9.3) lower in the 25–30 years (reference) group compared with the 18–24 years group and 24 mL (5.8%, 95% CI 22.1 to 26.0) lower in the 51–56 years group compared with the reference group (p<0.0001) (onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2). Thalamic volume at baseline was also significantly lower in older participants (online supplemental table S1, figure 1C). For instance, thalamic volume was 0.16 mL (4.10%, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.21) lower in the 51–56 years age group than the reference group (p<0.0001) (onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in NWMV and T2LV across age groups, except for the ‘51 to 56 years’ age group, which showed a significantly lower NWMV compared with the reference group (p<0.05) (onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2, figure 1D,E).

Lower brain volumes at trial baseline in older patients independent of disease duration

The findings were consistent across all disease duration subgroups (figure 2). For example, among participants with the shortest disease duration (0–4 years), NGM volume was lower in older age groups compared with the reference group (25–30 years vs 18–24 years (reference): −5.82 mL (−1.40%); 51–56 years vs 25–30 years: −22.87 mL (−5.48%) (p<0.0001) (figure 2B)). Similar trends were observed in participants with a medium disease duration of 5–9 years (25–30 years vs 18–24 years: −4.20 mL (−1.02%); 51–56 years vs 25–30 years: −22.17 mL (−5.39%) (p<0.0001)) and in those with the longest disease duration of ≥10 years (25–30 years vs 18–24 years: −5.22 mL (−1.34%); 51–56 years vs 25–30 years: −16.53 mL (−4.24%) (p<0.0001) (figure 2B)). Thalamic volume also decreased with age in participants with a disease duration of 0–4 years (51–56 years vs 25–30 years: −0.14 mL (−3.49%) (p<0.001) (figure 2C)). In the group, 5–9 years, this pattern was less pronounced (51–56 years vs 25–30 years: −0.13 mL (−3.18%) (p<0.05)), which ultimately disappeared in the group, ≥10 years (figure 2C). Lastly, consistent with the entire cohort, no significant difference in baseline NWMV or T2LV was observed between younger and older age groups across the different disease duration subgroups (data not shown).

Older participants show less brain volume loss over trial follow-up

Over a median follow-up of 1.79 years (range 0.5–2.0), younger age groups demonstrated a greater percentage decline in brain volume compared with older participants (table 3, figure 3A). This pattern was consistent across both the 1-year and 2-year intervals, whereas older participants exhibited less loss. For instance, the age group 51–56 years showed 0.10% less brain volume loss than the reference group (95% CI –0.06 to –0.14, p<0.0001) (table 3). Participants older than 35 years also showed significantly slower GM atrophy rates over 2 years, with at least 0.18% less volume loss compared with the reference group (p<0.05) (online supplemental table S1, figure 3B). A similar pattern was observed for thalamic atrophy rates, with participants older than 35 years exhibiting at least 0.26% less volume loss than younger participants (p<0.05) (online supplemental table S1, figure 3C). Consistent with baseline observations, no significant differences in NWMV or T2LV change were found between younger and older MS participants across the entire cohort (onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2, figure 3D,E).

Older participants show less brain volume loss over time, independent of disease duration

The differences in atrophy rates persisted across all disease duration subgroups, with older participants consistently exhibiting lower whole-brain atrophy rates (figure 4). Again, a similar pattern was found for GM atrophy rates. For example, among participants with a disease duration of 0–4 years, the GM atrophy rate was significantly lower in older age groups compared with the younger reference group (51–56 years vs 25–30 years: +0.31%, 95% CI 0.19% to 0.44%, p<0.05), and this pattern persisted in participants with a disease duration of 5–9 years and ≥10 years (p<0.05) (figure 4B). The effect was less pronounced for thalamic atrophy (figure 4C). Lastly, consistent with findings in the total group, no significant changes in NWMV or T2LV were observed during follow-up in any of the disease duration subgroups (data not shown).

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate whether the findings were influenced by inflammatory activity, we performed a sensitivity analysis including only participants who did not develop new lesions during the trial; the observed patterns remained consistent. To further account for broader aspects of inflammatory disease activity, we re-ran the longitudinal models with additional adjustment for baseline T2 lesion volume, Gd+ status and prior-year relapse rate, which yielded results consistent with the primary analyses. Including disease duration and EDSS as covariates in the longitudinal models also did not alter the magnitude or direction of the age-related differences in atrophy measures. In addition, a separate sensitivity analysis investigating sex differences showed that males had lower NWBV at baseline compared with females (online supplemental figure S1). Over time, however, females showed a slightly steeper decline in WBV change, resulting in smaller sex differences by the end of the trial. Furthermore, to explore the potential influence of pseudo-atrophy, we also examined age-related atrophy between year 1 and year 2, which showed patterns consistent with the main findings. Restricting the analyses to the placebo-only and interferon-only treatment arms also yielded age-related patterns consistent with the primary analyses, although CIs were wider due to reduced sample sizes. Lastly, we found similar results when repeating the analyses with age as a continuous variable; similarly, the spline-based analyses using continuous age and duration confirmed our primary findings, showing that the age effect on atrophy remained unchanged and that no meaningful age-by-disease duration interaction was observed.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of age on multiple brain volume measures and atrophy rates in a pooled dataset of RRMS trial participants which enabled us to examine patterns of brain volume change in a diverse, well-characterised population under standardised conditions, something not typically possible in observational cohorts. At baseline, NWBV, global GM and thalamic volume were lower in older than in younger people, but older participants had significantly lower rates of WBV loss, compared with younger participants. In contrast, there were no significant associations between age and WM or T2-lesion volume at baseline, nor with changes in these measures over the follow-up period. These findings remained consistent across all disease duration subgroups as well as in participants with no inflammatory disease activity during the trial. Our findings also align with previous MRI studies showing that age significantly influences brain volume trajectories in MS. For example, earlier research has reported that older participants show lower baseline GM and thalamic volumes, alongside slower subsequent atrophy rates—a pattern that we also observed in our pooled dataset.29 Our study further extends these observations by demonstrating that this age-related reduction in atrophy persists independent of disease duration.

There is growing evidence that ageing fundamentally alters the biological course of MS.30 As people with MS age, inflammatory disease activity tends to decline—evidenced by fewer relapses, contrast-enhancing and new T2 lesions—31 32while the risk of slowly progressive disease course increases.33 Brain volume loss plays a key role in this process and is associated with greater physical and cognitive disability and may even precede clinical progression.3436 In patients with pronounced atrophy, this may already signal irreversible neurodegenerative processes—highlighting the need to closely monitor them and consider earlier inclusion in progression trials, before transition to a progressive disease stage. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of an early focus on preventing or slowing atrophy in younger patients, rather than relying solely on clinical progression, which typically manifests later.

The varying brain atrophy rates in our study have also important implications for clinical trial design in MS, where brain volume loss is commonly used as a ‘proxy’ for neurodegeneration.2 37 While brain volume measurements are commonly used as outcome measures in MS trials, such trials are often powered based on a single expected average atrophy rate for the general MS population. Since brain atrophy progresses at different rates across age groups (also seen in the ORATORIO and INFORMS trials),38 39 power calculations should instead be adjusted according to the estimated average age of the trials target population. This may significantly impact sample size calculations. For instance, based on our study estimates, a sample size of 237 participants per arm would be required for a population aged between 30 years and 40 years, given a yearly atrophy rate of 0.49% and a SD of 0.66. Conversely, in an older cohort (eg, average age ~55 years) where the atrophy rate is lower, at 0.44% per year, the same effect size would require 374 participants per arm. This underscores the importance of tailoring sample size calculations to the demographic characteristics of the target population and highlights that treatment effects observed in specific age groups may not be directly generalisable to other age groups.

To account for effects on outcomes other than age and disease duration alone, we also explored sex differences and inflammatory disease activity. We found that men had lower baseline NWBV than females. While both sexes showed brain atrophy over time, the rate of atrophy was slightly higher in females—a pattern consistent with previous research on sex-specific brain volume differences in MS.40 Notably, by the end of the trials, NWBV values in both groups appeared to move closer together, indicating that despite initial structural differences, the gap in brain volume between sexes narrowed over time. These findings support the notion that female brains may be more resistant to age-related atrophy, as suggested by earlier studies. Future research should therefore investigate whether stratification based on sex could serve to improve clinical trial outcomes.

To further examine whether inflammatory activity influenced these patterns, we analysed data from participants who did not develop new lesions during the trial. In this subgroup, atrophy trajectories remained consistent, suggesting that the observed differences are not solely attributable to ongoing inflammatory activity or lesion accumulation, but may instead reflect MS-specific processes—such as inflammation-related tissue loss—that evolve over time and vary with age. Additional longitudinal models, which included baseline T2 lesion volume, Gd+ status and prior-year relapse rate as adjustment factors, showed similar findings. While inflammation is traditionally considered the hallmark of early MS, our results suggest that neurodegenerative processes may contribute more to early-stage tissue loss than typically recognised.

There are some limitations. First, we pooled data from multiple clinical trials to increase sample size and enable stratified analyses, since data from older participants are generally limited. Pooling provided the advantage of greater heterogeneity, with atrophy rates in our sample (–0.397% to –0.617% per year) aligning with observational (real-world) MS cohorts (approximately –0.31% to –1.35% per year). This likely reflects that pooling captured broader variability in patient characteristics, disease stage and treatment exposure than a single trial would, which may improve generalisability. However, pooling may also have introduced variability related to differences in protocols, scanner hardware or other technical factors—sources of noise that could not be fully controlled despite harmonisation of imaging outputs. A second limitation is that atrophy estimates were based on normalised brain volume values derived from two discrete time points, which is often the case in pooled trial datasets. While this approach is not equivalent to a dedicated longitudinal pipeline (eg, SIENA) and may introduce greater measurement variability, NWBV-based metrics remain widely used and offer a practical, scalable solution for assessing brain atrophy in large datasets. Third, the uneven distribution of participants in the disease duration groups 5–9 years and ≥10 years reduced statistical power and increased the risk of type II errors, making it harder to draw definitive conclusions.41 42 The restricted age range of our cohort (18–56 years) also limits the generalisability of our findings to older individuals, who may show different patterns of disease activity and progression. Notably, within the longest disease-duration strata in our dataset, age-related differences appeared smaller, which likely reflects the limited age variation and reduced statistical power within these subgroups rather than a true absence of age effects. Future research should extend analyses to older MS patients to provide a more comprehensive understanding of disease progression in real-world settings. Another consideration is that older RRMS patients eligible for phase 3 trials may represent a lower-inflammation subgroup with prior effective therapy, which could partly explain slower atrophy. Nevertheless, the consistency across disease duration strata and in participants without new inflammatory activity suggests that our findings mainly reflect age-related changes. Finally, the absence of a healthy control group limited our ability to separate age-related changes from disease-specific neurodegeneration.

In summary, this study shows that MRI-derived outcomes in RRMS vary across age groups, with older participants exhibiting lower atrophy rates regardless of disease duration. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for age when analysing brain volume and designing clinical trials. In particular, lower atrophy rates in older individuals may require larger cohorts to detect small but clinically meaningful effects in some regions; also, results on treatment effects in younger individuals may not easily be generalisable to older patients. Future research should also investigate whether these effects similarly apply to progressive MS and critically assess the validity of MRI-derived outcome measures across different age groups. A deeper understanding of these interactions will be necessary for refining therapeutic decisions, optimising trial design and identifying subgroups most likely to benefit from disease-modifying therapies.
