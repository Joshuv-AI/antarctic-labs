Integrated drip-and-ship improves stroke transfer efficiency and outcomes: a comparison with traditional drip-and-ship and mothership models
Authors: Chun-Min Wang, Che-Wei Lin, Yu-Ming Chang, Ray-Chang Tzeng, Ming-Hsiu Wu, Si-Chon Vong, Tsang-Shan Chen, Shang-Te Wu, Yu-Tai Tsai, Yi-Ting Fang, Chuang-Chou Yang, Yu-Hsiang Su, Meng-Hua Huang, Mu-Han Wu, Feng-Yuan Chu, Yen-Chu Huang, Kuan-Hung Lin, Che-Chao Chang, Sheng‐Hsiang Lin, Pi-Shan Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) has revolutionized the treatment of acute large vessel occlusions, the appropriate patient transfer paradigm remains controversial. This study compares outcomes of three transfer models in a stroke network: mothership (MS), traditional drip-and-ship (DS), and an integrated DS model using a novel transfer system (TS).MethodsWe implemented a novel TS to streamline communication and coordination between primary and comprehensive stroke centers. We analyzed 1063 patients with suspected large vessel occlusion across three groups: MS (n=814), conventional DS without TS (DS TS (−), n=185), and DS with TS (DS TS (+), n=64). Primary outcomes included treatment time metrics, EVT rates, and functional outcomes.ResultsDS TS (+) showed improved time metrics, with onset-to-CT angiography (CTA) times comparable to MS (232 vs 255.5 min) and significantly faster than DS TS (−) (305 min). It also achieved the highest rates of both intravenous thrombolysis (51.56%) and EVT (48.44%). Among EVT patients, the DS TS (+) group had the shortest door-to-puncture time (98.0 min vs MS 132.0 min and DS TS (−) 127.0 min, P<0.001) and a shorter onset-to-puncture time compared with the DS TS (−) group. DS TS (+) also showed a promising trend towards superior functional outcomes at 3 months (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2: 54.84% vs MS 39.10% vs DS TS (−) 36.36%).ConclusionThis study shows that an integrated DS model using a structured TS can achieve outcomes comparable to the MS model. Enhancing transfer efficiency through innovative solutions tailored to the regional infrastructure may serve as a viable alternative alongside the MS model.

Introduction

Timely access to endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is critical for patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion (LVO). Delays in treatment are strongly associated with worse outcomes,1 making efficient patient triage and transfer strategies a cornerstone of modern stroke care. However, stroke systems of care vary widely across regions, influenced by differences in geography, hospital distribution, and resource availability. Streamlining workflows and ensuring timely patient access to EVT requires coordinated system-wide efforts at regional, city, and national levels.

With regard to patient transfer strategies, aside from a few regions with sufficient resources capable of implementing mobile stroke units2 or direct-to-angiography suite models,3 most areas use the mothership (MS) model, in which patients are transported directly to a comprehensive stroke center (CSC), or the drip-and-ship (DS) model, where patients are initially treated with intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) at a primary stroke center (PSC) before transfer.4 Several systematic reviews have reported that the MS model is associated with higher rates of functional independence after EVT compared with the DS model.5 6 However, recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including RACECAT7 and TRIAGE-STROKE,8 have shown comparable results between MS and well-coordinated DS models. These findings suggest that workflow design and communication protocols may be as important as the model itself and may even contribute to improving patient outcomes.

Our previous research showed that an integrated transfer system—implemented as a modified DS model within a regional stroke network in Taiwan—effectively reduced inappropriate transfers, shortened interhospital transfer times, and improved access to EVT, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes.9 Building on this experience and newly accumulated data, the present study aims to compare workflow efficiency and clinical outcomes among three transfer models: the traditional DS model, the integrated DS model, and the MS model. Specifically, we seek to determine whether an integrated DS model can achieve outcomes comparable to the MS model in real-world settings and to identify key predictors of functional outcomes among these patients undergoing EVT.

Methods

Regional stroke network setting

Tainan City, a special municipality in southern Taiwan, has a population of approximately 1.88 million distributed across 2200 km² and is served by the Tainan Stroke Network (TSN). The TSN consists of three CSCs that provide 24-hour IVT and EVT, as well as 10 PSCs offering 24-hour IVT capability (see online supplemental table S1 and S2). Among the CSCs, National Cheng Kung University Hospital (NCKUH) plays a pivotal role, managing over 50% of all EVT transfers within the network.

The TSN operates under a hybrid model that integrates both MS and DS strategies. In the DS model, patients with acute ischemic stroke are initially transported to the nearest available hospital—typically a PSC—for prompt IVT. If LVO is suspected, patients are subsequently transferred to a CSC for EVT. However, most PSCs within the TSN do not have round-the-clock access to CT angiography (CTA) or CT perfusion (CTP). Consequently, pre-transfer vascular imaging is not routinely performed.

To overcome inefficiencies in traditional transfer protocols—which often relied on multiple telephone calls and unsecured messaging applications—we developed the Endovascular Thrombectomy Transfer System (EVTTS), a secure web-based platform designed to streamline the interhospital referral process for EVT candidates. EVTTS provides PSC physicians with a structured questionnaire interface that captures key clinical data, including patient demographics, stroke severity (eg, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score), time metrics (eg, last known well time, IVT initiation), and imaging results when available. The system can be accessed via computer or smartphone and is designed for rapid completion within 2–3 min. Online supplemental table S3 lists the detailed items to be completed in the EVTTS interface. Online supplemental figures S1A and B show the screen capture of the EVTTS interface.

Once the form is submitted, the platform automatically displays available CSCs within the network and provides the contact information of the on-duty physician. This allows direct communication between the referring physician and the on-duty EVT specialist or neurologists, bypassing institutional switchboards and minimizing communication delays. If the first CSC is unavailable, the referring physician can seamlessly redirect the transfer request to another eligible CSC through the same interface. On referral approval by the CSC, the on-duty physicians at the PSCs initiate the transfer protocol. Concurrently, the on-duty neurologist or EVT specialist at the CSC instructs the CSC team to prepare for the potential arrival of EVT candidates and initiate EVT activation. Online supplemental figures S2 and S3 show a schematic diagram and workflow of the regional stroke network using the EVTTS.

EVTTS was launched on October 1, 2021 within the TSN. Its implementation has resulted in measurable improvements in stroke care coordination, including significantly enhanced interhospital transfer efficiency, better clinical outcomes among EVT recipients, and overall improvements in the quality of regional stroke care delivery compared with the traditional transfer model, as detailed in our 2024 publication.9

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a combined retrospective and prospective study from January 2019 to June 2024, enrolling acute stroke patients who presented to our emergency department (ED) with suspected LVO (typically NIHSS score ≥6 or the presence of cortical signs) and underwent CTA or CTP (n=1673). All the patients underwent CTA in the receiving CSC. We excluded patients with intracerebral hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage, as well as those who underwent CTA for cerebral aneurysm or tumor evaluation (n=395). We also excluded patients with in-hospital stroke (new stroke onset during ED stay for other non-stroke etiologies, n=53) and those with symptom progression from an atypical initial presentation (eg, dizziness, altered consciousness, seizure; n=162), as these cases do not trigger code stroke activation on arrival in the ED and therefore differ from code stroke patients. Their prolonged ED processing times are often attributable to preceding non-stroke diagnoses or atypical symptom presentations, which delay stroke recognition.

Patients were categorized into three groups based on their arrival method: the MS group included direct arrivals by private vehicle or ambulance, while transferred patients were assigned to one of two DS groups based on whether their transfer used the EVTTS platform: the DS TS (−) and DS TS (+) groups. Although EVTTS implementation began on October 1, 2021, some post-implementation transfers continued to use traditional methods. Data collection and outcome measures

We collected data on patient demographics (age, sex, premorbid modified Rankin Score (mRS)), stroke severity by NIHSS score, reception of reperfusion therapy (IVT and EVT), time metrics (onset-to-CSC door, CSC door-to-CTA, onset-to-CTA, onset-to-needle for IVT, CSC door-to-groin puncture, CSC door-to-reperfusion, onset-to-groin puncture, and onset-to-reperfusion times for EVT), angiographic outcomes by the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) score,10 and functional outcomes by 90-day post-discharge mRS score. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) was defined using the SITS-MOST criteria.11

Patient and public involvement

While patients were not directly involved in the study design or dissemination, they participated in the stroke transfer process through the TSN and their clinical data were analyzed with appropriate ethical approval.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. For the overall comparison among the three patient groups—MS, DS TS (–), and DS TS (+)—the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Significant overall results (P<0.05) triggered post-hoc testing with Bonferroni correction, where P<0.017 (0.05/3) was considered significant. Mann–Whitney U tests were applied for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables in post-hoc analyses.

Subgroup analyses were performed to address potential time-related confounders introduced by the EVTTS. Specifically, we compared outcomes between the MS and DS TS (–) groups in the pre-implementation period, and between the MS and DS TS (+) groups in the post-implementation period. These subgroup comparisons followed the same statistical procedures as the main group comparisons.

We conducted binary logistic regression analyses to identify independent predictors of favorable functional outcome at 3 months, defined as mRS score of 0–2 among patients who underwent EVT, regardless of their mode of arrival. Variables entered into the multivariable model included transfer group, age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, IVT administration, sICH, and angiographic reperfusion status. A second model was constructed by replacing the transfer group with the log-transformed onset-to-puncture time. Due to the small sample size and low event rates for some variables (eg, sICH), Firth’s bias-reduced logistic regression was used. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Results

This study included 1063 patients (614 men and 449 women) of median age 71.0 years (IQR 63.0–81.0), categorized into three groups: MS (n=814), DS TS (−) (n=185), and DS TS (+) (n=64) (see table 1). The median age across groups was similar (MS 72.0 years, DS TS (−) 69.0 years, DS TS (+) 68.5 years; P=0.121). Male predominance was observed in all groups, with no significant difference (MS 56.9%, DS TS (−) 60.5%, DS TS (+) 60.9%; P=0.574). The NIHSS scores differed significantly between the groups (P<0.001), with the MS group having a lower median score (MS 14.0 points, DS TS (−) 19.0 points, DS TS (+) 19.0 points).

The onset-to-CSC door time (O-to-CSC D) differed significantly among the three groups (P<0.001), with post-hoc analysis showing a significantly longer O-to-CSC D time in the DS TS (−) group (267.0 min) compared with the MS group (197.0 min). However, the O-to-CSC D time in the DS TS (+) group was only 16.5 min longer than the MS group (213.5 vs 197.0 min).

The CSC door-to-CTA time (CSC D-to-CTA) also differed significantly between the groups (P<0.001), with shorter times observed in the DS TS (−) (26.5 min) and DS TS (+) (22.0 min) groups than in the MS group (45.0 min). With regard to onset time, the onset-to-CTA time (O-to-CTA) was significantly longer in the DS TS (−) group (305.0 min) than in the MS (255.5 min) and DS TS (+) (232.0 min) groups (P<0.001).

IVT rates differed significantly among groups (P<0.001), with the DS TS (+) group showing the highest rate (51.56%), followed by the DS TS (−) group (30.81%) and the MS group (26.78%). EVT rates also varied significantly (P<0.001), with the DS TS (+) group having the highest rate (48.44%), followed by the MS group (19.29%) and the DS TS (−) group (17.84%).

Among patients receiving EVT (n=221), there were no significant differences in age, sex, or NIHSS scores (table 2). The CSC door-to-groin puncture time (CSC D-to-P) was significantly shorter in the DS TS (+) group (98.0 min) compared with both the MS group (132.0 min) and the DS TS (−) group (127.0 min) (P<0.001). Similarly, the CSC door-to-reperfusion time (CSC D-to-R) was shorter in the DS TS (+) group (142.5 min) than in the MS group (172.0 min) and the DS TS (−) group (172.0 min) (P=0.010). When considering onset time, the onset-to-groin puncture (O-to-P) and onset-to-reperfusion (O-to-R) times were still shortest in the MS group (255 and 310 min, respectively), followed by the DS TS (+) group (299 and 363.5 min), and longest in the DS TS (−) group (397 and 430 min) (P=0.003 and P=0.002, respectively). Post-hoc analysis further showed that the DS TS (−) group had a significantly longer median O-to-P time compared with the other two groups. Specifically, the median O-to-P time in the DS TS (−) group was 142 min longer than in the MS group and 98 min longer compared with the DS TS (+) group. Although the O-to-P time was shortest in the MS group, the difference between the MS group and the DS TS (+) group was only 44 min and did not reach statistical significance. This finding highlights that, while the MS model remains the most time-efficient, the DS TS (+) can significantly narrow the delay gap compared with the DS TS (−) model. The reperfusion rates, defined by mTICI 2b/3 scores, were high across all EVT patient groups, with no significant differences observed (MS 92.36%, DS TS (−) 93.94%, DS TS (+) 87.10%; P=0.504). This finding suggests consistent procedural success regardless of the arrival model. Similarly, there were no significant differences in the rates of sICH. The proportion of patients achieving favorable functional outcomes (mRS 0–2) at 3 months was numerically highest in the DS TS (+) group, but the difference was not statistically significant (DS TS (+) 54.84%, MS 39.10%, DS TS (−) 36.36%; P=0.225; see figure 3 and table 2), possibly due to the low case numbers. Mortality rates were also lowest in the DS TS (+) group, but the difference was not statistically significant (DS TS (+) 6.45%, MS 16.03%, DS TS (−) 18.18%; P=0.364).

The subgroup analysis comparing the MS group with the DS TS (−) group before EVTTS implementation showed that the MS group had a significantly lower NIHSS score, shorter O-to-CSC D time, and longer CSC D-to-CTA time than the DS TS (−) group and similar rates of IVT and EVT reception (see online supplemental table S4). In patients receiving EVT, the MS group had significantly shorter O-to-P times and O-to-R times. The MS group had lower rates of sICH and higher rates of favorable 3-month functional outcomes compared with the DS TS (−) group (see online supplemental table S5).

The subgroup analysis comparing the MS group with the DS TS (+) group after EVTTS implementation showed that the MS group had a significantly lower NIHSS score and longer CSC D-to-CTA time than the DS TS (+) group. However, the O-to-CSC D time was comparable (MS 206 min vs DS TS (+) 213.5 min), and the rates of IVT and EVT were significantly higher in the DS TS (+) group (see online supplemental table S6). In patients receiving EVT, the DS TS (+) group experienced a non-significant delay of 44 and 39.5 min in O-to-P and O-to-R times, respectively, compared with the MS group. However, the DS TS (+) group had numerically lower, although non-significant, rates of sICH and mortality, while achieving significantly higher rates of favorable functional outcomes compared with the MS group (54.84% vs 33.33%; P=0.033) (see online supplemental table S7).

In the binary logistic regression for favorable functional outcomes (mRS 0–2) at 3 months (table 3), after adjusting for confounding factors, significant predictors of good outcomes included younger age (adjusted OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.96, P<0.001), lower NIHSS scores (adjusted OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.99, P=0.021), administration of tissue plasminogen activator (adjusted OR 3.17, 95% CI 1.62 to 6.20, P<0.001), and absence of sICH (adjusted OR 0.03, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.50, P=0.015). Model 2 used the O-to-P times as factors instead of the method of arrival. The logarithm of O-to-P time was a significant predictor (adjusted OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.97, P=0.040), indicating that shorter times from onset to EVT treatment were associated with better outcomes. However, the method of arrival was not a significant independent predictor of outcomes.

Discussion

This study compared the MS model, an integrated DS model using a structured transfer system (EVTTS), with a traditional DS model without integration in a real-world stroke network. The integrated DS model improved transfer efficiency compared with the traditional DS model. The higher rate of favorable post-EVT outcomes at 3 months in the integrated DS model compared with the traditional DS model highlights the need for efficient use of transfer resources within a regional stroke network, as these efforts can ultimately help improve patient outcomes. In contrast, an uncoordinated DS model may significantly delay time metrics for EVT patients within the stroke network, potentially leading to worse functional outcomes than those who arrived at CSCs directly, as shown in previous meta-analyses.5 6

Most PSCs in our region lack round-the-clock CTA capability. Without a pre-transfer CTA to confirm LVO, a higher rate of futile transfers is inevitable. However, we found that nearly 50% of the patients in the DS TS (+) group ultimately received EVT compared with less than 20% in the DS TS (−) group. This improvement in the DS TS (+) group is likely attributable to enhanced communication between PSC and CSC via EVTTS, facilitating more selective and effective transfers. Additionally, longer interhospital delays in the DS TS (−) group may have contributed to infarct core progression, reducing EVT eligibility on CSC arrival. These findings suggest that an integrated DS model may reduce futile transfers and improve the allocation of EVT resources within a regional stroke network.

Both DS groups had higher NIHSS scores than the MS group, likely due to easier recognition and referral of severe cases. Under-referral may occur depending on PSC capabilities. MS cases were more heterogeneous, including patients with stroke mimics and lower NIHSS scores, potentially increasing the CSC burden. Each model carries inherent trade-offs in delivering stroke care across a regional network.

Notably, CSC D-to-CTA times were shorter for both DS groups than for the MS group. This may reflect earlier team awareness through EVTTS and the fact that transferred patients often arrive with stabilized vital signs, intravenous access, and preliminary assessments completed at the PSCs, allowing for expedited CTA at the CSC. In contrast, MS patients typically require full in-hospital triage and evaluation, leading to longer imaging times. Furthermore, CSC D-to-P times were also longer in the MS group. Without pre-notification, imaging and interventional team activation often occur sequentially rather than in parallel. Therefore, implementing early activation protocols—such as EMS-based pre-notification, analogous to the EVTTS in DS models—may facilitate the timely mobilization of imaging and interventional teams. However, careful consideration is still needed to balance the benefits of early activation in the MS model with the frequency of false alarms and the potential burden they place on CSC resources.

Although not statistically significant—possibly due to the limited sample size—outcome trends favored the DS TS (+) group compared with both the MS and DS TS (−) groups. Most retrospective and prospective studies have reported more favorable outcomes with the MS model, often with differences in O-to-P times exceeding 100 min between the two arrival modes. Our findings align with recent RCTs showing comparable outcomes between the MS and DS models when transfer systems are efficient. As shown in the RACECAT trial, similar outcomes occurred between the MS and DS models with a 56 min difference in O-to-P times.7 Similarly, the TRIAGE-STROKE trial reported a 35 min difference.8 In our study the median difference in O-to-P times between the MS and DS TS (+) groups was only 44 min. Additionally, the DS TS (+) group had the highest rate of IVT administration among all groups. As shown in a recent meta-analysis, patients who receive IVT at PSCs before transfer to CSCs for EVT are more likely to achieve excellent functional outcomes.12 This may partially explain the improved outcomes observed in the integrated DS group. In contrast, the DS TS (−) group had a substantial delay of 142 min in O-to-P time compared with the MS group and had the lowest proportion of favorable functional outcomes and the highest rates of sICH among the three models.

Our binary logistic regression analysis showed that the key predictors of favorable outcomes remained consistent with the established literature, including younger age, lower stroke severity, receipt of IVT, absence of sICH, and shorter onset to EVT time. Additionally, we observed a trend towards increased odds of good functional outcomes in the DS TS (+) group and reduced odds in the DS TS (−) group compared with the MS group. A systematic review has shown that various workflow interventions in EVT can lead to significant time reductions and improved outcomes.13 Given that the DS model will likely remain a component of many real-world stroke systems, healthcare systems should prioritize accelerating referral processes and enhancing communication within regional stroke networks. Our study may potentially serve as a practical reference for other regions facing similar challenges.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in bridging the gap between prior observational literature and RCTs such as RACECAT and TRIAGE-STROKE. This study has several limitations. First, the 5-year study period may have introduced temporal biases in patient selection due to evolving clinical guidelines with time. However, subgroup analysis of post-implementation data showed consistent trends, mitigating this concern. Second, although transfer patients came from all PSCs in the TSN, the CSC was limited to NCKUH. This limited generalizability but ensured consistent workflow assessment and reduced interhospital variability. Third, incomplete data from some PSCs, particularly door-in-door-out times in DS TS (−), may limit detailed time metric analysis. However, accurate onset times still enabled comparisons across models. Lastly, although the sample size was relatively modest, the study provides valuable real-world evidence derived from a regional stroke network, reflecting practical challenges and outcomes that may be highly relevant to similar healthcare settings worldwide.

Conclusion

The observed improvements in transfer efficiency in the integrated DS model, particularly through an efficient transfer system, can yield outcomes comparable to the MS model. Our real-world data further reinforce this concept, consistent with findings from the RACECAT and TRIAGE-STROKE trials. Rather than centering the discussion solely on whether the MS or DS model is superior, regional healthcare systems should also focus on optimizing and integrating stroke referral pathways based on available resources and infrastructure. Enhancing the DS model remains a viable and pragmatic alternative alongside the MS model for many regions.
