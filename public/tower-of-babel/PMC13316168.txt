Orbital Fat Involvement by Metastatic Multiple Myeloma Mimicking Involutional Lower Eyelid Fat Herniation: A Case Report
Authors: Bercin Tarlan, Onur Konuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report an unusual presentation of orbital extramedullary plasmacytoma in a patient with multiple myeloma, clinically mimicking involutional lower eyelid fat herniation.ObservationsA 57-year-old man with a history of multiple myeloma in remission presented with a three-month history of progressive fullness of the right lower eyelid. Clinical examination suggested minimal right-sided hyperglobus and asymmetric lower eyelid fat herniation without signs of inflammation. Orbital computerized tomography revealed a hyperdense anterior inferior orbital mass distinct from surrounding fat. Excisional biopsy demonstrated a plasmacytoma confirmed by immunohistochemistry showing CD138 and kappa light chain positivity. The lesion represented metastatic involvement of multiple myeloma.Conclusions and importanceOrbital metastases of multiple myeloma are rare. This case highlights anterior orbital involvement presenting as apparent involutional lower eyelid fat herniation, a potential diagnostic pitfall. Awareness of this presentation is important when evaluating new eyelid or orbital findings in patients with a history of systemic malignancy.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma is a malignant plasma cell neoplasm accounting for approximately 10% of all hematologic malignancies and represents the second most common hematologic cancer.1 Secondary extramedullary plasmacytomas may occur as manifestations of systemic multiple myeloma and tend to behave aggressively with metastatic potential.2

Orbital and ocular adnexal involvement by plasma cell tumors has been reported, including infiltration of the orbit, conjunctiva, uveal tract, lacrimal gland, and lacrimal sac.2,3 However, anterior orbital fat involvement presenting clinically as involutional lower eyelid fat herniation has rarely been emphasized as a diagnostic presentation. Early recognition of metastatic disease is clinically important, as detection of new extramedullary involvement may alter staging and systemic management.4

We report a case of metastatic plasmacytoma involving the anterior inferior orbital fat in a patient with multiple myeloma, clinically mimicking involutional lower eyelid fat herniation.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male presented with a sense of fullness of the right lower eyelid over a three-month period. He had a history of multiple myeloma diagnosed six years previously, complicated by renal insufficiency, and had been in remission for one year following high-dose chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplantation.

Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/25 in both eyes. External examination revealed minimal right-sided hyperglobus with bilateral lower eyelid fat herniation, more pronounced on the right side. There were no signs of infection such as redness or tenderness. The lesion showed no significant color difference compared with typical lower eyelid fat herniation. Careful palpation of the lower eyelids revealed that the lesion did not exhibit features typical of orbital fat, and the asymmetry could not be definitively attributed to fat herniation (Fig. 1). Considering the patient's history of multiple myeloma and the presence of a new symptom, surgical exploration and excisional biopsy were planned. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia with sedation. After fashioning a subciliary incision 4 mm inferior to the eyelash line, dissection was carried out between orbicularis oculi and the orbital septum. No spontaneous fat herniation was observed. The orbital septum was opened, revealing a solid lesion adherent to both the medial and lateral inferior orbital fat. Although it appeared as two components, the lesion represented a single contiguous mass and was carefully dissected and completely excised.

Histopathologic evaluation revealed a dense infiltrate of atypical plasma cells. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated moderate to strong perinuclear and cytoplasmic positivity for CD138. The tumor cells showed diffuse strong cytoplasmic positivity for kappa light chains, while lambda light chain and pancytokeratin staining were negative. These findings were consistent with plasmacytoma representing metastatic involvement by multiple myeloma.

The patient was referred back to his medical oncologist. Unfortunately, he died one month postoperatively due to complications of renal failure.

Discussion

Orbital metastases represent a small but clinically significant proportion of orbital tumors. Metastatic lesions account for approximately 1–13% of all orbital masses, and 2–5% of patients with systemic cancer develop orbital metastases.4, 5, 6 Breast, lung, and prostate carcinomas are the most common primary tumors metastasizing to the orbit, while hematologic malignancies such as multiple myeloma account for a smaller subset.6,7

Orbital metastatic lesions commonly present with proptosis, diplopia, pain, or a palpable mass.8 In contrast, our patient presented with a subtle sense of lower eyelid fullness initially suggestive of age-related fat herniation. Imaging demonstrated a discrete anterior inferior orbital mass distinct from normal orbital fat, prompting surgical exploration.

Plasma cell tumors may involve the orbit as solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma or as secondary manifestations of systemic multiple myeloma. Reported sites of ocular adnexal involvement include the lacrimal gland, conjunctiva, uveal tract, and intraconal orbital space. Orbital involvement occurs more frequently than intraocular involvement. In a case series reported by Chin et al., ocular involvement of multiple myeloma was described in three patients: one presenting with orbital involvement, one with iridociliary infiltration, and one with a retrobulbar orbital mass.3 Additionally, Wang et al. reported a series of orbital extramedullary plasmacytomas including one case arising from the lacrimal gland and four cases with retrobulbar localization.9

Unlike typical orbital plasmacytomas that present with proptosis or palpable firm masses, our case was uniquely localized to the pre-septal/anterior orbital fat planes, perfectly mimicking the soft, compressible appearance of involutional fat herniation. To our knowledge, anterior orbital fat involvement presenting primarily as involutional lower eyelid fat herniation has not been previously reported.

Early recognition of orbital metastatic disease is clinically relevant, as detection of extramedullary involvement may alter staging and systemic management. Orbital involvement may also serve as the initial manifestation or a sign of recurrence of systemic disease.

This case illustrates that new-onset eyelid changes in patients with a history of multiple myeloma should prompt consideration of orbital metastasis in the differential diagnosis. Surgical biopsy remains essential for definitive diagnosis when imaging findings are inconclusive.

Age-related lower eyelid fat herniation is frequently encountered in elderly patients and is typically symmetric, although mild to moderate asymmetry may occur. In younger individuals, asymmetric lower eyelid herniation has been attributed to orbital fat hyperplasia.10 In the present case, the presence of minimal associated hyperglobus, rapid symptom progression, and underlying systemic malignancy led to further radiologic evaluation and subsequent surgical intervention.

Despite advances in systemic therapy, orbital involvement in multiple myeloma is associated with poor prognosis, as reflected in our patient's rapid systemic deterioration.11,12

Conclusion

Anterior orbital fat involvement by metastatic multiple myeloma may clinically mimic involutional lower eyelid fat herniation. Awareness of this diagnostic pitfall is important in patients with systemic malignancy presenting with new eyelid contour changes. Prompt imaging and biopsy enable definitive diagnosis and guide systemic management.
