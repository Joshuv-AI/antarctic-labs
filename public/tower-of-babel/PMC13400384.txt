Does caregiver HRQOL and burden change over 2 years after heart transplantation or durable mechanical circulatory support implantation in older patients? Findings from SUSTAIN-IT
Authors: Tingqing Wu, Michael Petty, Mary Amanda Dew, James K Kirklin, Eileen Hsich, Maryl Johnson, Scott Silvestry, Andrew Kao, Brent Lampert, Salpy V Pamboukian, Margaret Murray, Melana Yusefpolskaya, Jerian Dixon-Evans, Kathleen L Grady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaregiver health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and burden are essential considerations for caregivers of older patients with heart failure (HF) undergoing advanced surgical therapies. We aimed to evaluate change in HRQOL and burden from baseline to 2 years following surgery and identify factors associated with HRQOL in caregivers of patients who underwent heart transplantation (HT, with or without pre-transplant mechanical circulatory support [MCS] HT MCS or HT Non-MCS, respectively) or long-term MCS.MethodsCaregivers, recruited from 13 U.S. sites, completed measures of HRQOL (EQ-5D-3L), burden (Oberst Caregiving Burden Scale), and other measures at baseline, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months after patients’ surgery. Analyses included multivariable linear mixed effects models.ResultsThe cohort of 229 caregiver-patient dyads included caregivers for patients who underwent long-term MCS = 99; HT MCS = 55; or HT Non-MCS = 75. Caregivers were median age = 64[59, 68] years, 87% female, 85% White, 98% spouses, and 86% with co-morbidities. Baseline-24 months post-surgery, caregiver EQ-5D-3L visual analog scale average score ranges were high and stable: long-term MCS = 81-83, HT MCS = 84-88, and HT Non-MCS = 84-87, with no significant change within groups nor cross-sectional differences between groups at all time periods. Burden was low-moderate. Caregiver HRQOL was positively associated with caregiver working and caregiver perception that their own health would remain the same/improve over time and negatively associated with >3 caregiver comorbidities, caregiver gastrointestinal disease, caregiver diabetes, and patient obesity.ConclusionsCaregivers had high/stable HRQOL and low-moderate perceptions of burden through the 2-year follow-up, providing important information when preparing caregivers of patients who will undergo advanced surgical therapies.Keywords: Caregiver, Heart failure, Heart transplantation, Mechanical circulatory support, Health-related quality of life (HRQOL)

Background

Heart failure (HF) in the U.S. has increased from earlier estimates of 6.7 million to 7.7 million adults, and the prevalence is high in older adults.1, 2, 3 Advanced surgical procedures (i.e., heart transplantation [HT] and mechanical circulatory support [MCS]) increase survival and improve health-related quality of life (HRQOL) for those with refractory Stage D HF, including older adults.4, 5, 6

Following discharge after advanced surgical procedures, patients have substantial self-care responsibilities (e.g., symptom monitoring, equipment management, medications, clinic visits, and incorporating lifestyle changes). Caregiver participation in patient self-care after HT or MCS is recommended in national guidelines.7, 8 This role is often assumed by the patient’s spouse9, 10 for whom such commitments can be burdensome and may impact their own HRQOL. Research regarding caregivers of HF, HT, and MCS patients reflects concerns related to lack of preparedness for caregiving, feeling overwhelmed and overlooked by the healthcare team, and lacking time for their own self-care, which can impact their perception of burden and HRQOL.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Most research articles examined caregiver HRQOL and burden while providing care for adults of all ages, often with small sample sizes at single centers and using short-term longitudinal or cross-sectional designs.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 A paucity of data exists regarding the impact of these advanced surgical procedures on caregiver HRQOL and burden later, after surgery, while caring for older patients. With anticipated growth in the number of older HF patients who undergo advanced surgical procedures,17, 18, 19 it is important to understand the impact of these procedures on caregiver HRQOL and burden over time to develop strategies to support caregivers, who are frequently older spouses.

We previously described caregiver HRQOL and burden before and early (6 months) after patients (aged 60-80 years) underwent HT or long-term MCS.10, 20, 21 In this report from the Sustaining Quality of Life of the Aged: Heart Transplant or Mechanical Support (SUSTAIN-IT) study, we explored caregiver HRQOL and burden through 24 months following HT or durable long-term (i.e., destination therapy) MCS implantation in these older patients for whom they provided care. The theoretical framework guiding this research, developed by Spilker and Revicki, focused on the influence of disease and its treatment on patient HRQOL, which we modified to include caregiver HRQOL.22 Study aims were to (1) describe change in caregiver overall HRQOL from before through 24 months after patients underwent HT with or without pre-transplant MCS (HT MCS and HT Non-MCS, respectively) or long-term MCS, if ineligible for HT; (2) describe change in caregiver burden for all 3 caregiver groups over time; and (3) identify factors associated with change in caregiver overall HRQOL across time. We also examined cross-sectional differences in HRQOL (overall and by dimension) and burden among the 3 caregiver groups at all time periods.

Methods

Study design and sample

This study used a prospective, longitudinal, multi-site, observational design. Our SUSTAIN-IT cohort included patients and caregivers recruited from 13 U.S. sites from October 1, 2015 to December 31, 2018 and followed for 2 years after surgical intervention (HT with or without pre-transplant MCS or long-term MCS).

We defined caregivers as unpaid family members, spouses, partners, or friends who the patient identified as the primary individual to assist with self-care after surgery. Caregiver inclusion criteria were >21 years of age and able to speak, read, and write in English. Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously reported.23 Caregiver-patient dyads were excluded if patients or caregivers withdrew from the study or died before HT or long-term MCS implantation or if enrolled caregivers failed to complete baseline and at least 1 post-operative HRQOL and burden measure. In the event of patient crossover (i.e., patients implanted with long-term MCS becoming HT candidates or vice versa), caregiver data were censored at that time. Patients in the HT Non-MCS group who required MCS after study enrollment remained in the HT Non-MCS group. Institutional Review Board approval was received from all sites, and written informed consent was provided by all patients and caregivers. Our study is compliant with the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation Ethics Statement.

Questionnaires and data collection

Caregiver HRQOL was measured using the generic EuroQoL-5 Dimension-3L (EQ-5D-3L)24 which has 5 dimensions (usual activities, mobility, self-care, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) with a 3-level response scale (none, moderate, or extreme problems) and a visual analog scale (VAS), wherein 0 = worst to 100 = best imaginable health state. This measure was selected as it has been used by other researchers for assessing the HRQOL of caregivers of patients who undergo advanced surgical therapies, which allows for comparison with our findings, and includes important dimensions that may be impacted by caregiving of older patients for caregivers who are often older as well. Burden was measured using the Oberst Caregiving Burden Scale (OCBS), 25, 26, 27 a 15-item measure of perceived time on task (range: 1 = none to 5 = a great amount) and task difficulty (range: 1 = not difficult to 5 = extremely difficult). Additionally, we measured depressive symptoms using the 8-item Personal Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8),28, 29 (range = 0-24, with a higher score indicating more depressive symptoms and a score >10 indicative of moderate depressive symptoms) and anxiety using the 20-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State (STAI-State),30 (range = 20-80, with a higher score indicating more anxiety).

Caregiver characteristics included demographics (e.g., age, sex, race, education, marital status, and working status), medical/surgical history, relationship to patient, perception that their health would be/was affected by caregiving (range = 1-10; higher score indicates health has remained the same or improved), surgical history, and comorbidities (e.g., arthritis, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia). All caregiver data were collected via self-report using paper and pencil questionnaires.

Patient demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, race, education, marital status, working status, insurance type, and body mass index [BMI]) and clinical data (e.g., etiology of HF, medical/surgical history, comorbidities, Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support [Intermacs] profile, New York Heart Association class, left ventricular ejection fraction, and type of MCS, including second- or third-generation FDA-approved or investigational durable continuous flow left ventricular assist devices [LVADs]) were also collected. Demographic characteristics and clinical data were abstracted by research coordinators or downloaded securely from The Society of Thoracic Surgeons Intermacs (STS Intermacs) database.

Caregivers independently completed paper and pencil questionnaires either in the clinic, hospital, or per mail at home. Caregiver baseline questionnaires were administered as follows: HT MCS: while patients were on MCS, after listing with the United Network for Organ Sharing; HT Non-MCS: while patients were on medical therapy, after listing with United Network for Organ Sharing; and long-term MCS: after patients were considered and/or scheduled for long-term MCS. Data from HT candidates and their caregivers were collected every 6 months post-enrollment until HT. For this report, we used baseline data that was closest to surgery. Post-HT and long-term MCS follow-up data were collected at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months.

Statistical analyses

Demographics, clinical characteristics, and questionnaire responses were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD), medians and quartiles (Q1 and Q3), or counts and percentages, as appropriate. Single imputation using the surgical cohort means (for continuous variables) or mode (for categorical variables) was used if less than 15% of item-level data were missing.

To model the primary outcome of caregiver EQ-5D-3L VAS trajectories from pre-HT or long-term MCS through 24 months post-surgery, patient and caregiver demographics (e.g., age, sex, education, and working status), clinical characteristics (e.g., medical/surgical history and comorbidities), caregiver-specific factors including marital status, relationship to the patient, and perception that their health would be impacted by caregiving, and patient-specific factors including surgical strategy and insurance coverage were screened univariably. Variables significant at the 0.2 level were used to construct the covariate pool for the multivariable linear mixed effects models. Multi-collinearity was assessed. Statistical significance was established at a 2-sided 5% level. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R v. 3.6.1 R Foundation, 2020).

Results

Among 305 patients who underwent HT or long-term MCS implantation, 233 caregivers were eligible and enrolled in SUSTAIN-IT. Of these, 229 caregiver-patient dyads were included in this report (long-term MCS = 99, HT MCS = 55, and HT Non-MCS = 75) (Figure 1). The most frequent reasons for caregivers not completing the study were patient death and caregiver/patient withdrawal.

Flow diagram for Caregiver-patient Dyad selection.

Baseline caregiver total cohort characteristics were median age = 64 [59, 68] years, majority female (87%), White (85%), with education greater than high school (75%), unemployed (54%), and married or domestic partnered (89%), which did not differ by group, except for age (Table 1). Additionally, 98% of caregivers were spouses of patients. The median baseline score for the perception of how their health would be affected by caregiving was 9 [8, 10] for all 3 groups, indicating that caregivers believed their health would remain the same or improve while caregiving. Caregivers in the total cohort had a median of 2 [1, 3] comorbidities and 2 [1, 4] previous surgeries, which did not differ by group.

Caregiver Demographics and Clinical Characteristics at Baseline

Baseline patient total cohort characteristics included a median age = 66 [64, 70] years, with the long-term MCS group being significantly older than both HT groups (Supplemental Table 1). The majority were male (81%), White (87%), married/partnered (85%), and 74% had greater than a high school education, which did not differ by group. See Supplemental Table for clinical characteristics.

Caregiver HRQOL overall and by dimensions

From baseline through 24 months post-surgery, group average VAS scores were high and stable (range: long-term MCS = 81-83, HT MCS = 84-88, and HT Non-MCS = 84-87) (Supplemental Table 2). There were no significant changes within groups over time nor cross-sectional differences between groups at all time periods (Figure 2, Supplemental Table 2).

Caregiver EQ-5D-3L visual analog scale.

Regarding EQ-5D-3L dimension scores, caregivers reported few problems with self-care, mobility, and usual activities, which were similar among the 3 groups at all time points (total cohort range = 1%-4%, 14%-24%, and 10%-21%, respectively) (Figure 3, Supplemental Table 2). Caregivers reported more problems with anxiety/depression and pain/discomfort (total cohort range = 24%-29% and 38%-47%, respectively), which were similar among groups at all but 1 time point.

Caregiver EQ-5D-3L dimensions.

Caregiver burden

Time on task was moderate for all groups over time. There were no significant within-group differences in burden (time and difficulty) from baseline to 24 months for all 3 groups (Figure 4). Regarding cross-sectional comparisons, long-term MCS caregivers reported significantly more time spent on tasks at most time periods than both HT groups (Figure 4, Supplemental Table 2). Task difficulty was low for all 3 caregiver groups, yet long-term MCS caregivers reported significantly more task difficulty than the HT groups at multiple time points (Figure 5, Supplemental Table 2). There were no cross-sectional differences in time on task or task difficulty between the 2 HT groups.

Oberst caregiving burden scale, difficulty.

Caregiver depressive symptoms and anxiety

Depressive symptoms, using the PHQ-8 questionnaire, and anxiety, using the STAI-State questionnaire, were examined separately. Caregiver depressive symptoms were low; no differences in scores were detected within groups across time or between groups at discreet time points (Supplemental Figure 1, Supplemental Table 3). The frequency of PHQ-8 ≥10 (indicating moderate depressive symptoms and a need for further mental health assessment) was low (<14% for all groups at all time periods) and did not differ between groups at any time period (Supplemental Table 3).

Caregiver anxiety decreased significantly in the long-term MCS group from baseline to 6 postoperative months and remained stable thereafter; no within group change was detected for the 2 HT groups over time. The average caregiver STAI-State score in the long-term MCS group was significantly higher (i.e., more anxiety) than the HT Non-MCS group at baseline and later post-surgery (Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Table 3). The HT Non-MCS group had a stable average STAI-State score that was significantly lower than the HT MCS and long-term MCS groups later after surgery.

Factors associated with caregiver HRQOL

Multicollinearity among independent variables was minimal at baseline. Using a multivariable linear mixed effects model, we examined patient- and caregiver-specific factors to identify associations with caregiver overall HRQOL, using the EQ-5D-3L VAS over time. Variables associated with improvement in caregiver HRQOL included caregiver working while caring for the patient, 3.1 points (CI, 0.2-6.0, p = 0.035), and the caregivers’ perception that their own health would remain the same or improve, 1.8 points (CI 0.9-2.7, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Variables associated with a decrement in caregiver HRQOL included caregivers having 3 or more comorbidities, −7.5 points, (CI, −12.5 to −2.5, p = 0.003); caregiver gastrointestinal disease, −5.7 points (CI, −9.6 to −1.9, p = 0.004); and caregiver diabetes, −4.9 points, (CI, −9.1 to −0.8, p = 0.020). Only 1 patient factor, obesity, was significantly associated with a decrease in caregiver HRQOL, −4.1 points, (CI, −7.0 to −1.2, p = 0.006). Patient surgical strategy was not associated with change in caregiver HRQOL over time.

Discussion

Our findings extend what is known about HRQOL and the burden of caregivers of older patients with long-term MCS and HT later after surgery. First, caregiver overall HRQOL remained stable through 2 years following HT or long-term MCS, with no significant changes within groups or differences between groups. Furthermore, factors associated with caregiver overall HRQOL over time were primarily caregiver demographic and clinical factors, not patient factors. These findings are consistent with those of other investigators wherein thoracic transplant caregivers (65.7%>age 50 years)31 and MCS caregivers (mean age = 54.7 years)32 experienced relatively high and stable HRQOL over the first year post-transplantation or device implantation.

EQ-5D-3L dimension scores revealed the presence of anxiety/depression and pain/discomfort as most prominent in all 3 groups across time. However, mood-specific questionnaires revealed depressive symptoms were low and stable in all groups across time; anxiety was moderate over time. Of note, long-term MCS caregiver anxiety was high at baseline but decreased by 6 months post-operatively and remained stable thereafter, similar to other authors who identified sustained emotional strain for the first 3 months following LVAD implantation.33

Caregiver burden was low to moderate in all groups across time, suggesting that caregivers successfully adapted to their roles. Notably, caregiver burden was greater in the long-term MCS group than the HT groups. Differences in burden by group may be related to the increased number and complexity of tasks undertaken by MCS caregivers. These findings are similar to those of other researchers who reported low caregiving-related burden for HT caregivers31, 32 and MCS caregivers.33

Multivariable analyses provide valuable insights into factors influencing caregiver HRQOL. Notably, obesity was the only patient-specific factor associated with caregiver overall HRQOL; other positive and negative associations were specific to caregiver factors.

Caregivers working and having a perception that their own health would not be affected by caregiving were positively associated with HRQOL. Since caregiver employment often provides insurance, a source of family income, and a respite from caregiving when a network of support is available, we speculate that such “advantages” might explain its positive impact on enhanced caregiver HRQOL, also reported in other studies.16

Caregiver comorbidities were negatively associated with caregiver HRQOL, which emphasizes the need for caregivers to care for themselves as well as provide support to patients. For example, diabetes self-care includes lifestyle changes, monitoring lab work, taking daily medications, and monitoring for potential complications. Our findings are consistent with those of other investigators describing caregivers’ needs related to adapting to changes in lifestyle, challenges in coping with their own emotions, economic hardship, and need to prioritize self.34, 35, 36, 37 Failing to recognize and develop strategies to address these needs may place caregivers and the patients they support at risk for negative outcomes.

Finally, there was a strong trend that being a male caregiver was positively associated with caregiver HRQOL. Morgan et al38 found that men approached their caregiver role as managerial, including an increased reliance on formal care services to support caregiving. In contrast, they reported that women viewed their role as nurturing while expecting to assume greater involvement in caregiving. Sex-specific differences in caregiving suggest variation in types of support needed.

Important clinical implications can be derived from our study. We recommend sharing with patients and caregivers the likelihood of high caregiver HRQOL and low to moderate burden following surgical interventions for advanced HF. Additionally, it is important to discuss the negative impact of caregiver comorbidities and the positive effect of working on caregiver HRQOL. Such transparency offers patient-caregiver dyads the opportunity to plan ahead for life after surgery. Potential interventions that may enhance caregiver outcomes include addressing concerns related to preparation for the caregiver role beyond clinical/technical education, providing ongoing caregiver support in the outpatient setting, integrating caregivers in care planning, and discussing the anticipated trajectory of the patient’s health over time.12, 13, 15, 16, 22, 23

This study has limitations. Our cohort represents only patients with HF who undergo advanced surgical therapies. Furthermore, we enrolled a demographically homogeneous group of patients and caregivers, although the racial distribution of patients in our study is consistent with national data for LVAD and HT recipients.6, 11 Thus, while caregivers were geographically representative across the U.S., our findings may not be generalizable due to lack of a more diverse cohort who may experience HRQOL and burden differently. We also recognize that U.S. guidelines for care of HT and MCS patients, which recommend availability of caregivers, may differ from guidelines outside the U.S., which may influence caregiver outcomes in other countries. Furthermore, sociocultural differences in caregivers of patients with advanced HF in other societies may reduce the generalizability of our findings. We recommend future research of caregiver outcomes and support include a more diverse population of patients and caregivers both within the U.S. and across the globe.

Finally, we did not collect more granular information on center-specific support programs (e.g., home health care, support groups, remote support strategies, etc.) which might have influenced caregiver experiences. Patients unwilling to identify a caregiver to enroll in our study and unmeasured confounders specific to our multivariable analyses also may have introduced bias.

Conclusions

These findings provide a foundation for educating and counseling caregivers of patients with HF considering advanced cardiac surgical therapies. Future caregivers may benefit from learning that caregivers in our study had relatively high and stable HRQOL from before through the first 2 years following surgery. Furthermore, sharing findings of low to moderate perceptions of caregiver burden from our study with future caregivers may alleviate concerns about the impact of caregiving on their lives, including roles, responsibilities, relationships, and emotions. Our findings point to the need for caregivers to care for their own health as well as provide support for others.
