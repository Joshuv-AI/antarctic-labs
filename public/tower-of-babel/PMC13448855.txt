One Week Exposure to a Somatostatin Receptor 2 Antagonist (SSTR2a) Enhances Glucagon Counterregulation to Insulin‐Induced Hypoglycaemia and Does Not Worsen Glycemia in a Male Rat Model of Insulin‐Requiring Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Ninoschka C D'Souza, Emily G Hoffman, Sara C Atherley, Sabrina Champsi, Nadia Aleali, Richard T Liggins, Owen Chan, Michael C Riddell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAdministration of a somatostatin receptor 2 antagonist (SSTR2a) increases glucagon responsiveness to hypoglycaemia in insulin‐treated type 2 diabetes (T2D), but the durability of this effect and the impact of repeated SSTR2a dosing on overall glycaemia in T2D are unclear. This study evaluated the effects of daily SSTR2a administration on glucagon counterregulation and overall glycaemia in a male rat model of T2D.MethodsT2D was induced in male Sprague–Dawley rats using high‐fat feeding and streptozotocin (35 mg/kg) (day 0). After a 7‐day insulin maintenance period, T2D and control rats were administered a single dose of SSTR2a (ZT‐01, 3 mg/kg) or vehicle (n = 7–8 per group) prior to a hypoglycaemic challenge with an insulin overdose (day 8). After a one‐week washout period, T2D and control rats were administered drug or vehicle for 8 days, with a second hypoglycaemic challenge performed on the last day.ResultsGlucagon counterregulation to both hypoglycaemic exposures increased with SSTR2a in T2D and controls (all p < 0.05). Dosed without hypoglycaemia, SSTR2a induced a transient rise in glucagon, c‐peptide, and glucose levels in both healthy and T2D rats, but food intake and body weight were unaffected. T2D rats on SSTR2a (days 8–22) had reduced daily insulin needs without significantly affecting blood glucose levels.ConclusionIn this male rat model of T2D, repeat dosing of SSTR2a increases glucagon counterregulation without worsening glycemia.Keywords: counterregulation, glucagon, hypoglycaemia, somatostatin, SSTR2a, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Widespread insulin resistance and a progressive decline in pancreatic β‐cell function are hallmarks of type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1]. However, other hormonal defects occur with disease progression, such as impaired glucagon‐like peptide 1 (GLP‐1) secretion at mealtime, elevations in baseline glucagon and somatostatin levels, and a failure in glucagon to drop after meal ingestion [2, 3]. While these endocrine changes aggravate hyperglycaemia in T2D, hypoglycaemia can still develop once sulfonylureas or insulin therapy is initiated [4]. Insulin‐ or secretagogue‐induced severe hypoglycaemia (SH; a failure to self‐treat low blood glucose level) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in people with diabetes [5]. The risk for severe hypoglycaemia in T2D is generally less than in T1D [4, 6], though up to one‐third of patients with T2D on insulin may have at least one SH event each year [7, 8]. In adults with T2D having at least one SH episode in the past 6 months, the median rate of hypoglycaemia (i.e., a glucose < 70 mg/dL for ≥ 15 min), detected by continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is 2.1 (interquartile range 1.0–4.3) events/week with level 2 hypoglycaemia (glucose < 54 mg/dL) occurring a median of 0.3 (0.0–0.6) times per week [9].

Several mechanisms likely contribute to the development of recurrent hypoglycaemia and SH in T2D which are similar to those described for individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D) [2, 10]. Recurrent episodes of hypoglycaemia are associated with impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia [11] and a failure in glucose‐sensing neurons in the hypothalamus and other brain regions to effectively coordinate a counterregulatory hormone response (increases in glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol and growth hormone) [12]. The failure of insulin secretion to halt when blood glucose levels fall below euglycaemia (i.e., < 70 mg/dL) likely contributes to a blunted glucagon counterregulation [13, 14], but other mechanisms for glucagon dysregulation likely exist. More specifically, impairments in the glucagon response to hypoglycaemia in T2D appear to be attributed to elevated somatostatin secretion from pancreatic δ‐cells, which may limit glucagon secretion from neighbouring α‐cells [15]. We have recently demonstrated, using rodent models of T2D, that the glucagon counterregulatory response to hypoglycaemia can be increased with administration of a somatostatin receptor antagonist (SSTR2a) in early [16] or late disease conditions [17]. Thus, studies of SSTR2a in T2D rats have evaluated a single SSTR2a dose and shown some efficacy on glucagon response, whereas repeated or prolonged exposure in the absence of antecedent hypoglycaemia has not been evaluated. Repeated treatment with a SSTR2a in T2D might stimulate the already elevated baseline and mealtime glucagon levels, which could exacerbate post‐prandial hyperglycaemia. On the other hand, enhanced endogenous glucagon secretions via SSTR2a treatment might promote insulin secretion, reduce food intake and increase energy expenditures, all of which could impact overall glycaemia in T2D [18]. This study explored, in a rat model of T2D, the effects of SSTR2a administration, after a first exposure or 7 daily treatments, on glucagon and overall glycaemic control, and on hypoglycaemia exposure caused by insulin administration.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The study protocol was approved by the York University Animal Care Committee, following Canadian Council for Animal Care guidelines. Male Sprague Dawley rats were received from Envigo RMS (Indianapolis, IN, USA) at 8 weeks of age, weighing ~200–250 g. Healthy control rats were fed normal chow (28% protein, 13% fat and 58% carbohydrate, 4.17 kcal/g; Purina Lab diet 5012, St. Louis, MO, United States) while T2D rats received a high‐fat diet (60% fat, 20% carbohydrates, 20% protein, 5.24 kcal/g; Research Diets Inc., New Brunswick, NJ; Cat#: D12492). After 3 weeks, a T2D phenotype was induced in the high‐fat‐fed rats using streptozotocin (35 mg/kg, Sigma‐Aldrich, Oakville, Canada), as previously described [17] (Figure 1). Twenty‐four hours after dosing streptozotocin (day 0), T2D rats began daily basal/bolus insulin treatments (insulin glargine; Sanofi‐Aventis, Bridgewater, New Jersey, USA; and insulin lispro, Eli Lily, Toronto, Canada), with doses titrated to achieve a fed morning glucose target between 15 and 30 mmol/L, simulating moderate to poorly controlled diabetes. The basal/bolus insulin dosages were titrated based on evening blood glucose levels as follows: rats with values > 25.0 mmol/L received 4 U of insulin glargine; animals with blood glucose between 24.0 and 20.0 mmol/L received 3 U glargine; rats between 15.0 and 19.9 mmol/L received 2 U glargine and rats with < 15.0 mmol/L blood glucose received no insulin. No nightly insulin was administered in the evening following a hypoglycaemic challenge. Glucose, food intake and daily body weight measurements were recorded daily.

Schematic of study timeline. Animals were received on day (D)‐21 and placed on a high‐fat diet (T2D) or continued on a regular chow diet. After 3 weeks of high‐fat feeding, animals were injected with streptozotocin (STZ) on day 0 with insulin administration beginning on D1. The first insulin‐induced hypoglycemic challenge (Hypo 1) and exposure to ZT‐01 occurred on D8 followed by a 1‐week washout period. A second exposure to SSTR2a (ZT‐01, 3 mg/kg) or vehicle alone was administered on D15 testing pharmacodynamics (PD), followed by daily administration of ZT‐01 or vehicle until D22. On D22, animals received a second hypoglycemic challenge (Hypo 2) and were terminated after the challenge, ending the study.

Experimental Procedures and Endpoint Measurements

Whole blood glucose concentration was measured from a tail vein bleed, using a ContourNext glucose meter (Ascensia Diabetes Care, Mississauga, Canada). Plasma samples were isolated via centrifugation from a saphenous vein bleed for hormone measurements using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay kits for c‐peptide (Crystal Chem Cat# 90055, RRID:AB_2893130) and glucagon (Mercodia Cat# 10–1271‐01, RRID:AB_2737304) levels. Hormone measurements below the assay limit of quantitation were imputed at half the value of the limit for analysis.

This study was divided into three parts (Figure 1) as follows:

Effect of a single SSTR2a treatment on hormone responses during a hypoglycaemic challenge (day 8).

Eight days following diabetes induction, T2D and healthy rats underwent their first drug or vehicle treatment and hypoglycaemic challenge, as previously described [17]. In brief, 60 min after first receiving either vehicle (2.1% glycerol in 10 mM acetate buffer) or SSTR2a (ZT‐01, 3 mg/kg, Zucara Therapeutics, Toronto, Canada), a bolus administration of insulin aspart (4 U/kg; at t = 0 min) was administered subcutaneously to induce hypoglycaemia (blood glucose < 3.9 mmol/L) [17]. Blood glucose concentration was measured before SSTR2a (or vehicle) administration and every 15 min thereafter for up to 3 h. Glucagon and c‐peptide were measured at baseline (−60 min) and then at 0‐, 30‐, 60‐, 90‐ and 120‐min after bolus insulin administration. After a one‐week washout, a pharmacodynamic (PD) study was conducted (day 15), followed by daily drug dosing (days 15–22) and a second hypoglycaemic challenge (day 22).

PD Testing, Daily Drug Dosing and 2nd Hypoglycaemic Challenge (Hypo 2)

In the same groups of rats tested above, daily (administered at ~9 AM) subcutaneous injection of SSTR2a (ZT‐01, 3 mg/kg), or vehicle, was initiated on day 15 and continued until day 22 (Figure 1). For PD testing, food was restricted overnight (~15 g) and baseline and post dose measurements were made, including blood glucose, glucagon and c‐peptide for 4 h. Throughout the subsequent 7‐day drug dosing period, blood glucose, body weight, food intake and insulin needs (T2D rats) were monitored. On day 22, a second hypoglycaemic challenge was conducted in the same manner as described above but with a lower insulin dose in healthy animals to accommodate differences in baseline glycaemia (4 U/kg for T2D rats and 2 U/kg for healthy rats). All rats were killed at the end of the second hypoglycaemic challenge (t = 120 min post insulin bolus).

Data Analysis

During the first hypoglycaemic challenge, low blood sample volumes obtained from control rats limited the measurement of glucagon levels to between t = −60 to 60 min. Missing values including those due to earlier rescue from a hypoglycaemic challenge were not imputed. Area under the curve (AUC) analysis for all experiments was calculated for each animal and summarised by group average and standard deviation. Statistical analysis was completed using Prism software version 10 (Graphpad Software) using an unpaired t‐test with Welch's correction or ANOVA where applicable. Hypoglycaemic challenge parameters were analysed by 3‐way ANOVA tests, examining the main effects of time (hypo challenge 1 on day 8 versus hypo challenge 2 on day 22), diabetes status (T2D vs. healthy) and drug treatment (SSTR2a vs. vehicle). Two‐ and three‐way interactions of the main effects were evaluated where necessary with significant p values reported. Any post hoc comparisons were made using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test.

Results

Body Weights, Food Intake Glycaemia, Exogenous Insulin Requirements

All health parameters were evaluated for the entire 22‐day period, including prior to SSTR2a exposure, acute dosing and daily SSTR2a dosing. Animals were in good health in terms of daily clinical observations throughout the 22‐day study. Body weight of all rats increased over the 22 days of the study; however, SSTR2a‐treated animals tended to gain less weight than the vehicle‐treated rats (Δ~2%, p < 0.01 for main effect of treatment) (Figure 2A). Rats with T2D demonstrated mild hyperphagia at baseline relative to healthy rats, but there was no significant effect of SSTR2a treatment on food intake in either group (Figure 2B). Healthy rats remained euglycemic throughout the study, and their morning glycaemia was not affected by SSTR2a administration (Figure 2C). On the other hand, glycaemia declined in the T2D rats from a mean of 23.4 and 21.1 mmol/L on day 8 to 18.6 and 11.8 mmol/L on day 22, in the vehicle and SSTR2a‐treated rats, respectively (Figure 2C), with 50% of SSTR2a‐treated rats being euglycemic by day 22, compared to 29% in the vehicle‐treated group (p = 0.06). As a result of lowering overall glycaemia from day 8–22, daily maintenance insulin requirements in the SSTR2a‐treated T2D rats also declined over the entire treatment period of the study. Overall maintenance insulin requirements were 24% lower in SSTR2a‐treated rats compared to vehicle‐treated T2D rats (2.5 ± 0.9 vs. 1.9 ± 0.7 U/day, p = 0.049; Figure 2D), with a majority of SSTR2a‐treated rats achieving euglycaemia by the morning of day 22 (Figure 2E). For both T2D and healthy rats, blood glucose, body weight, food intake and insulin maintenance requirements (T2D rats only) were observed in the baseline period (days 1–8). Although T2D and healthy rats showed differences in all parameters, for both healthy and T2D groups, no difference between rats allocated to SSTR2a or vehicle treatment was observed (Figure S1).

PD Study

On the first day of daily drug treatment (day 15), SSTR2a administration resulted in no significant increases in blood glucose levels over 240 min in healthy rats but a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the T2D rats (Figure 3A,B). In T2D rats, SSTR2a‐treated animals had higher baseline corrected glucose AUCs than vehicle‐treated rats (1962 ± 642 vs. 780 ± 300, p = 0.018), while no drug treatment effect was observed in the healthy rats. Increased glucagon levels were observed with SSTR2a over vehicle in the 240‐min post‐dose period in both groups (both p < 0.001, Figure 3C,D). Peak glucagon levels were similar between groups with SSTR2a (T2D: 233 ± 115 vs. healthy: 316 ± 173 pg/mL, NS). Prior to dosing SSTR2a, baseline c‐peptide levels were lower in T2D than in healthy rats (1.6 ± 1.0 vs. 2.71 ± 0.9; p = 0.005). C‐peptide levels increased from baseline (t = 0) to 30 min post‐SSTR2a dosing in both T2D (p = 0.003) and healthy (p < 0.001) rats. With SSTR2a treatment in the T2D rats, the AUC of c‐peptide was similar to that of the AUC for the vehicle‐treated healthy rats. On the day following PD testing (day 16), body weight, food intake and fed glucose concentration (T2D group only) all declined transiently for both drug and vehicle‐treated rats (Figure 2A–D), with the T2D rats requiring less maintenance insulin.

Hypoglycaemic Challenge 1

In response to the first hypoglycaemic challenge, blood glucose levels fell similarly in the vehicle‐ and SSTR2a‐treated groups in the healthy rats (Figure 4A). There was no drug effect in healthy rats on glucose nadir (1.6 ± 0.2 vs. 1.6 ± 0.3 mmol/L) nor on final glucose concentrations (1.7 ± 0.2 vs. 1.8 ± 0.3 mmol/L), despite increases in glucagon levels with SSTR2a relative to vehicle dosing (76 ± 26 vs. 16 ± 17 pg/mL at t = 0 min, p = 0.0002), with glucagon values tending to be higher overall with SSTR2a (Figure 4B). In the T2D rats, SSTR2a treatment increased glycaemia transiently (Figure 4A), delaying the onset of hypoglycaemia by ~7 min but resulting in similar hypoglycaemic nadirs (vehicle vs. SSTR2A: 3.0 ± 1.2 vs. 2.3 ± 0.5 mmol/L) and terminal (3.1 ± 1.4 vs. 2.4 ± 0.5 mmol/L) blood glucose levels despite higher glucagon levels following SSTR2a compared to vehicle both prior to hypoglycaemia (105.7 ± 37.3 vs. 8.5 ± 11.1 pg/mL at t = 0, p < 0.001) and during hypoglycaemia at t = 30 (18.1 ± 27.9 vs. 133.5 ± 87.7, p = 0.007) and at t = 60 min (26.4 ± 25.8 vs. 171.6 ± 119.2, p = 0.01) (Figure 4B). Plasma c‐peptide levels decreased in all groups following insulin administration, with a tendency for c‐peptide to be slightly elevated with SSTR2a relative to vehicle in T2D rats (Figure 4C). No drug by group interaction effect was observed, but analysis of c‐peptide levels in rats with T2D separately revealed a significant effect of time (p < 0.001) and drug treatment (p = 0.04) on c‐peptide levels.

Of note, all healthy rats had to be rescued within 60 min during the first hypoglycaemic challenge before the end of the testing period to prevent life‐threatening seizures. None of the T2D rats required rescue.

Hypoglycaemic Challenge 2

A second hypoglycaemic challenge was conducted after 7 days of daily SSTR2a administration, where the induction of hypoglycaemia was carried out using 2 U/kg insulin in healthy animals and 4 U/kg insulin in T2D animals. Less insulin was used in the healthy rats, so the duration of hypoglycaemia could be more comparable between the healthy and T2D rats. Healthy rats exhibited a similar decline in blood glucose following the insulin bolus; SSTR2a treatment resulted in a ~5‐min delay in hypoglycaemia onset (Figure 4D), that was accompanied by smaller increases in glucagon levels relative to the first hypoglycaemic and drug exposure (Figure 4E). In T2D rats, SSTR2a treatment was associated with a similar onset of hypoglycaemia as vehicle treatment (75 ± 36 vs. 63 ± 29 min in vehicle vs. SSTR2a). SSTR2a‐ and vehicle‐treated T2D rats had similar terminal glucose values (both ~2.2 mmol/L) and nadir values of 2.1 ± 1.0 and 2.6 ± 1.8 mmol/L, respectively. Seven days of SSTR2a treatment did not affect baseline (pre‐challenge) glucagon level (Figure 4E) but peak glucagon levels during the hypoglycaemic challenge were higher in SSTR2a‐treated animals in both the healthy (433.4 ± 151 vs. 362.9 ± 111.8 pg/mL) and T2D (336.5 ± 200 vs. 155 ± 116.2 pg/mL) groups compared to vehicle controls. Glucagon values were lower than the first hypoglycaemic challenge in the healthy group (470 ± 245.2 vs. 754.7 ± 353.6 pg/mL) but higher in the T2D group (66.1 ± 48.8 vs. 229 ± 99.8 pg/mL) for vehicle and SSTR2a‐treated animals respectively. C‐peptide levels declined similarly in all groups after the insulin bolus, with no differences between T2D and healthy rats, nor between SSTR2a and vehicle treatments (Figure 4F).

Discussion

Poorly controlled T2D stems from reduced insulin secretion relative to insulin needs [19], elevated glucagon levels post meals [20, 21] and possibly elevated somatostatin secretion during both hypo‐ and hyperglycaemia [22, 23, 24, 25], albeit the latter is difficult to confirm in vivo because of technical challenges in measuring pancreatic‐derived somatostatin [24]. Administration of the SSTR2a ZT‐01 has shown promise as a therapeutic strategy to improve the glucagon response to hypoglycaemia in rat models of type 1 diabetes [26] and in T2D [17], but some SSTR2a have been shown to elevate glucagon levels immediately after administration, even when baseline glucose levels are elevated [24]. In a rat model of advanced T2D, ZT‐01 increased the blunted glucagon response to hypoglycaemia and delayed hypoglycaemia onset, but a transient rise in blood glucose was also observed after dosing [17, 27]. The increases in glucagon and glycaemia with ZT‐01 were transient and did not appear to result in long‐term increases in daily insulin requirements once the drug was given repeatedly. Similarly, in this study T2D rats treated with daily SSTR2a had lower evening mean blood glucose levels resulting in a significantly lower maintenance insulin requirement, potentially indicating a higher insulin responsiveness.

The main objective in this study was to investigate the effects of daily ZT‐01 dosing on glucagon counterregulation and overall glucose metabolism in a rodent model of insulin‐treated T2D. The results of this study demonstrate that daily treatment with 3 mg/kg ZT‐01 is modestly effective in restoring glucagon counterregulation to insulin‐induced hypoglycaemia without deteriorating overall glycaemia, although it did not significantly delay hypoglycaemia onset in this model of T2D, potentially due to the apparent insulin‐sensitising and/or insulin secretory effect(s) of sustained drug exposure.

The apparent increase in insulin sensitivity with repeat ZT‐01 dosing might be explained by the drug's ability to elevate hepatic portal vein glucagon levels which could have effects on hepatic insulin sensitivity [28]. Indeed, glucagon can have both positive and negative effects on glucose metabolism in T2D [29]. For example, elevation of pancreatic α‐cell glucagon secretion clearly enhances first‐phase insulin secretion at mealtime when glucose levels are elevated [30, 31, 32, 33, 34] while glucagon counterregulation stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, particularly during hypoglycaemia [35]. In human clinical trials, glucagon receptor agonism appears to help induce weight loss in obese subjects, perhaps by promoting lipolysis in adipose tissue and liver and/or by reducing food intake [36, 37]. Interestingly, while there was no difference in body weight gain in the T2D rats treated with SSTR2a versus vehicle, a small attenuation was observed with SSTR2a treatment in healthy control rats that was apparent even after the first dose of treatment. However, no obvious differences in food intake were observed with SSTR2a treatment in either group and T2D rats remained relatively hyperphagic even with daily drug dosing.

In several mouse studies assessing glucagon receptor (GCGR) blockage or deletion, animals were protected from diabetes development, even with significant pancreatic β‐cell destruction [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. These studies have a common thread of mild hyperglucagonemia, normoglycaemia and reductions in body weight. However, Damond et al. [43] also demonstrate that normoglycaemia was only observed if functional β‐cell mass was maintained, without which mice still developed diabetes. Interestingly, T2D still occurred in dual knockout GCGR−/− and GLP‐1−/− mice [44], suggesting that glucagon and/or incretin action on the GLP‐1 receptor is likely protective against diabetes development. Whether ongoing glucagon agonism or antagonism could improve overall metabolism in T2D is debatable [29], but the use of a SSTR2a that modulates endogenous glucagon secretion may have utility since it could potentially improve insulin secretion at mealtime [17] via the GLP‐1 receptor [45]. However, the possible mechanism(s) for improvements in glucose metabolism in the T2D rats, and the apparent attenuation in body weight gain in the healthy rats requires further investigation.

There were no observed negative effects of daily administration of SSTR2a on the health or glycaemic status of T2D rats. In fact, SSTR2a‐treated T2D rats required 24% lower total daily insulin for glycaemic control and at the end of the study a greater proportion of SSTR2a‐treated rats were euglycemic than in the vehicle group. This was not related to body weight or food intake, as these parameters were similar between the treatment groups. Reduced dependence on exogenous insulin with SSTR2a treatment could indicate impacts on glucose or lipid metabolism and warrant further investigation. These results build on our recent findings that a lower HbA1c was observed with repeat SSTR2a dosing (ZT‐01 0.3 mg/kg/day, 11 days) and intermittent hypoglycaemia exposure in male T2D rats [27].

One hypothesis that might explain the lower insulin maintenance dose required for SSTR2a‐treated T2D rats is that insulin responsiveness to rising blood glucose increases with treatment, as observed in the pharmacodynamic measurements on Day 8 (Figure 3), where mean c‐peptide levels rose to 2.5 ± 1.4 ng/mL 1 h after SSTR2a administration compared to 1.3 ± 1.0 at t = 60 following vehicle treatment. SSTR2a can increase c‐peptide levels following oral glucose ingestion in this rat model of T2D [17]. This observation fits with the idea that β‐cell insulin secretion is augmented by local glucagon‐stimulation [45].

Although baseline glucagon levels did not vary with SSTR2a or vehicle administration from day 8–22 (Table 1), the effect of SSTR2a on increasing glucagon levels in hypoglycaemia was observed in both hypoglycaemic challenges and following administration in euglycaemia or hyperglycaemia (in healthy or T2D rats, respectively, on Day 8). Daily dosing stimulating glucagon secretion may affect blood glucose levels over time, as pancreatic glucagon is insulinotropic in nature [45, 46, 47, 48], and potentially acts on the ß‐cell GLP‐1 receptor [45].

There are several strengths and limitations of this study that must be acknowledged. A major strength is the study design, allowing for the assessment of both single and daily dosing of SSTR2a with insulin‐induced hypoglycaemic conditions as well as under non‐stimulated conditions. Additionally, this study is differentiated from earlier studies of SSTR2a in diabetic rats, evaluating both a single dose and repeat daily dosing, but avoiding effects of antecedent hypoglycaemia (multiple challenges within a one‐week interval), using a much lower insulin dose to induce hypoglycaemia without severe hyperinsulinaemia, and by using healthy controls fed with a normal chow diet instead of a high‐fat diet to avoid inducing the early stages of insulin resistance which normally occurs with high‐fat feeding. However, a limitation of this study is the use of a different supplier for rats than that used in previous studies and we believe that the sub‐strain used here is more insulin‐sensitive than those used in previous publications [17, 27]. This difference in animal sub‐strain and insulin sensitivity from animals used previously could have been the cause of lower baseline glucose levels. Additionally, although hypoglycaemia onset was expectantly faster in nondiabetic animals, it is unclear why this was also observed in SSTR2a‐treated T2D rats during the second hypoglycaemic challenge, despite an increase in glucagon levels. However, this may be related to the apparent insulin‐sensitising effect of daily SSTR2a treatment in the week prior to the second challenge. It should also be emphasized that we did not measure other key counterregulatory hormones, including catecholamines, growth hormone and corticosterone, which are deficient during hypoglycaemia in diabetes [49] and could be impacted by SSTR2a. Finally, we were unable to perform incretin analysis to further investigate if incretin effects are impacted by ZT‐01 administration. Future studies should include additional analysis such as glucose and insulin tolerance tests at baseline and after repeated SSTR2a exposure to explore potential impacts on glucose or insulin tolerance. These data, paired with measurements of plasma amino acid levels, would provide valuable insight on glucagon‐mediated control of amino acid metabolism. This is important as these levels can be elevated in T2D, but decrease with glucagon infusions [50, 51]. This has further implications on α‐cell proliferation, gluconeogenesis and insulin secretion [52, 53, 54].

In summary, daily SSTR2a maintains glucagon counterregulation to hypoglycaemia and does not cause baseline hyperglucagonaemia nor a worsening of overall glycaemia in male rats with T2D, suggesting it may have utility as a therapy to minimize hypoglycaemia exposure in people with T2D.
