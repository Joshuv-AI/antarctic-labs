PanCanBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models in Pancreatic Oncology
Yimin Zhao, Sheela R. Damle, Simone E. Dekker, Scott Geng, Karly Williams Silva, Jesse J Hubbard, Manuel F Fernandez, Fatima Zelada-Arenas, Alejandra Alvarez, Brianne Flores, Alexis Rodriguez, Stephen Salerno, Carrie Wright, Zihao Wang, Pang Wei Koh ∗, Jeffrey T. Leek ∗

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have achieved expert-level performance on standardized examinations, yet multiple-choice accuracy is a poor proxy for real-world clinical utility and safety. As patients and clinicians increasingly leverage LLMs for guidance on complex conditions like pancreatic cancer, evaluation must move beyond general medical facts. While recent frameworks such as HealthBench have introduced expert-designed evaluation rubrics, they are limited by a lack of disease-specific depth and a reliance on simulated patient queries which may not reflect real clinical complexity. Moreover, strong performance on rubric-based measures does not guarantee factual correctness, necessitating assessments of hallucination. We developed a human-in-the-loop pipeline to design expert rubrics for de-identified patient questions from the Pancreatic Cancer Action Network (PanCAN). The resulting benchmark, PanCanBench, comprises 3,130 question-specific rubric criteria across 282 authentic patient questions. We evaluated 22 proprietary and open-source LLMs under an LLM-as-a-judge framework, assessing clinical completeness, factual accuracy, and web-search integration. To address the scalability challenges of benchmarking, we then investigated how AI-generated rubrics compared to human expert-generated rubrics. Models showed substantial variation in rubric-based completeness, with scores ranging from 46.5% to 82.3%. Factual errors were common, with hallucination rates (the percentages of responses containing at least one factual error) ranging from 6.0% for Gemini-2.5 Pro and GPT-4o to 53.8% for Llama-3.1-8B. Importantly, newer reasoning-optimized models did not consistently improve factuality: although o3 achieved the highest rubric score, it produced inaccuracies more frequently than other GPT-family models. Web-search integration did not inherently guarantee better responses. The average score changed from 66.8% to 63.9% for Gemini-2.5 Pro and from 73.8% to 72.8% for GPT-5 when web search was enabled. Synthetic AI-generated rubrics inflated absolute scores by 17.9 points on average while generally maintaining similar relative ranking. PanCanBench enables the automated evaluation of LLM responses to complex inquiries with high agreement with oncology experts, serving as a scalable proxy for rigorous clinical evaluation.

Some efforts have attempted to address this gap. For example, HealthBench
16
evaluates LLM responses using conversation-specific rubrics designed by healthcare professionals. HealthBench relies on synthetic questions generated by GPT and Claude and focuses on broad healthcare topics, leaving gaps in assessing model performance on real patient questions and in sensitive and specialized areas, such as pancreatic cancer. Moreover, high rubric scores do not necessarily guarantee factual correctness or appropriate source grounding, motivating complementary evaluations of hallucination and attribution. However, existing healthcare benchmarks, including HealthBench, primarily emphasize rubric-based measures, with limited explicit factuality assessments. Although hallucination rates are sometimes reported in public model documentation, such as the system card for GPT-5

To bridge this gap, we introduce PanCanBench, a benchmark comprising de-identified real patient questions collected from the Pancreatic Cancer Action Network (PanCAN) Patient Services Help Line. PanCAN is a leading nonprofit organization dedicated to patient support, research funding, and advocacy in pancreatic cancer. We paired these questions with evaluation rubrics designed by oncology fellows to answer two main questions: (1) are AI models safe

2
Methods

We designed an evaluation framework encompassing question collection, expert-curated rubric development, factual error detection, web-search-augmented evaluation, and comparisons with synthetic, AI-generated rubrics. Below, we describe the dataset construction, rubric design and validation pipeline, scoring procedures, factual error detection, web search evaluation metrics, and model configurations.

2.1
Question collection and overview

We sourced questions from the PanCAN Patient Services Help Line. To ensure patient and caregiver privacy, all questions were first de-identified by PanCAN staff. Questions were grouped by AI into six broad categories: Treatment & Clinical Trials, Diagnosis & Screening, Supportive & Palliative Care, Genetics & Risk, Side Effects & Symptom Management.

2.2
Rubric-based evaluation of model responses

Building on recent frameworks that utilize rubrics for LLM evaluation
16
;
20
;
21
, we designed specific evaluation guides to enable consistent scoring of both human- and AI-generated responses. Tailored to specific questions, these rubrics consist of self-contained criteria written as complete sentences, with point values weighted by their relative importance. To maximize objectivity, each criteria is given either full or zero points, with no partial credit
21
. Additionally, the rubrics incorporate negative criteria to penalize misleading or undesirable content, see Figure 1. Full details are provided in the Appendix: Rubrics Design Guidelines in the Appendix Section-6.

2.3
Rubric collection pipeline and validation

We developed a human-in-the-loop pipeline to collect rubrics in a valid, consistent, and efficient manner (Figure 2). It comprised four main phases: (1) independent rubric design by human experts, (2) AI-assisted polishing and validation, (3) AI-based semantic merging, and (4) final human review.

First, we divided all questions into five groups and assigned each group to two oncology fellows, who independently designed rubrics for their assigned questions to reduce subjective bias and ensure broader evaluation perspectives. To assist with rubric design, we gave fellows representative responses from four AI models (Grok-4
14
, GPT-4.1
22
, Llama-3.1-70B
23
, Olmo2.5-13B
24
) (Figure 2, Phase 1).

Second, for each fellow’s rubric, we employed two AI graders (GPT-4.1
22
and Claude-Sonnet-4
25
) to score the same set of responses. The key idea is that if a rubric is clear and self-contained, the scores from both AI graders should be consistent. For questions where scores differed by more than 20 points (on a 0–100 scale), we asked a third AI model (GPT-4.1
22
) to refine and polish the rubric based on the question, the corresponding responses, and the AI judgments (Figure 2, Phase 2). After polishing, we performed a ‘sanity check’ to verify the validity of the rubrics. Specifically, we used the polished rubrics from Phase 2 to grade responses generated by two pairs of large vs. small models (Llama-3.1-8B
23
vs. Llama-3.1-70B
23
and Qwen3-8B
26
vs. Qwen3-32B
26
). Here, large and small refer to the number of model parameters. Under a reasonable and well-defined evaluation metric, models in the same family with more parameters are expected to achieve higher or comparable scores, as greater model capacity enables more effective knowledge representation and reasoning.

Third, we used AI to merge semantically equivalent rubric criterias across the two fellows’ while retaining unique criteria specific to each fellow (Figure 2, Phase 3). This approach streamlined rubric consolidation while preserving fellow-specific criteria, enabling scalable integration of independent fellow inputs. Finally, a third oncology fellow reviewed the merged rubrics to determine which criteria to retain and finalize the point allocations (Figure 2, Phase 4).

2.4
LLM as a judge to grade responses using rubrics

We used an LLM to grade model responses using human-curated rubrics, with GPT-5 serving as the grader. Validation was performed on a held-out set of 40 questions with 424 rubric criteria. Two oncology fellows independently graded this subset. We computed Cohen’s
\kappa
to assess inter-rater reliability between the two human graders (human–human agreement). We similarly computed Cohen’s
\kappa
between the LLM judge and each human grader and reported the mean as LLM-human agreement, enabling direct comparison of grading consistency. Using each human evaluation as the reference standard, we calculated the LLM judge’s F1 score and reported the average across the two human references. We also computed the F1 score between the two human graders to contextualize LLM performance relative to human-level agreement.

2.5
Calculation of final scores

To evaluate model performance, we utilized a rubric-based scoring system designed to capture the nuance of clinical responses. The final score for each response is calculated as a percentage of the total possible points, accounting for both the inclusion of essential information and the penalization of incorrect or contradictory statements:

S_{final}=\frac{S_{rubric}}{S_{total\_possible}}\times 100

(1)

where
S_{rubric}
is the sum of points awarded for reward criteria minus points deducted for penalty criteria, and
S_{total\_possible}
is the maximum achievable score for that question.

2.6
Using LLMs to check factual errors in model responses

2.6.1
Factual error detection pipeline

While existing rubrics address broad categories of undesirable content, they often fail to capture the exhaustive range of potential factual inaccuracies. For example, in a treatment related question, Llama-3.1-8B incorrectly described Abraxane, a chemotherapy agent, as a targeted therapy. This limitation necessitated a specialized procedure for detecting hallucinations in AI-generated responses. Inspired by prior research
27
;
28
;
29
, we incorporated factual error detection into our benchmark. The methodology decomposes long-form responses into atomic factual statements, defined as discrete assertions, and leverages LLMs to evaluate the accuracy of each claim (see Appendix Section-8.1). To improve the robustness of LLM-based judging, we employed both GPT-5
11
and Gemini-2.5 Pro
30
as independent judge models. Only claims classified as incorrect by both models were labeled as factual errors. Furthermore, we enabled web-search capabilities for both models to ground the verification process in external data. To quantify model reliability, we report the percentage of responses containing at least one factual error as our primary hallucination metric.

2.6.2
Validation

We evaluated whether the LLM could accurately detect factual errors while maintaining low false positive and false negative rates. We constructed a validation set by randomly selecting 50 responses from GPT-5 and 50 responses from Llama-3.1-8B. Each response was manually reviewed to determine the presence of factual errors.

2.7
Evaluation of citation-grounded responses

2.7.1
Citation-Required Evaluation Subset

To evaluate models’ ability to provide verifiable supporting evidence, we constructed a citation-required evaluation set by selecting questions whose rubrics explicitly requested references. This subset comprised 40 questions and was used to quantify retrieval-and-citation performance for models with browsing capabilities.

2.7.2
Web Search Performance Metrics

To characterize web search performance, we established three metrics: the web search triggering rate, the success rate for rubric criteria requiring direct references, and the supportive link percentage. First, we calculated the web search triggering rate. Under our application programming interface (API) configuration, the model determined whether to issue a search based on the prompt. This metric therefore reflected whether the model correctly recognized when external retrieval was necessary to support its response. Second, we evaluated whether the model retrieved the appropriate type of resource specified in the rubric for criteria requiring references. For example, if a rubric criterion specifies that “the response should not reference specific diets unless it provides PMIDs to studies examining the effect of diet on pancreatic cancer”, a model providing a link to a general health blog would fail this criterion. Finally, we quantified the supportive link percentage, using an LLM-as-a-judge
31
to systematically screen every link provided by the models and measure how many of the provided URLs actually contained information that directly supported the model’s claims.

Generating rubrics with AI

We conducted a controlled experiment to compare rubrics generated by AI versus oncology fellows. For a fair comparison, we provided GPT-5, the AI model used to generate rubrics, with the same information given to oncology fellows, including the Rubric Design Guidelines (Appendix) and model responses from Grok-4, GPT-4.1, Llama-3.1-8B, and Olmo2-13B (Figure 2b). We then compared the scores of responses using the AI-generated rubrics (from GPT-5) with those obtained using human-curated rubrics.

3.2
Validation of LLM-as-a-Judge for grading and detecting factual errors

The average Cohen’s
\kappa
between the LLM-as-a-judge and two oncology fellows was 0.528, while the inter-rater reliability between the two fellows was 0.518. This suggests that the LLM’s agreement with human raters was comparable to the level of agreement observed between experts. The LLM achieved an F1 score of 0.838, compared with 0.855 for human graders—results that align with benchmarks reported in prior work
20
. These findings support the use of GPT-5 as a scalable and reasonably reliable proxy for expert grader in subsequent analyses. The false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR) on the validation set were 5.8% and 3.2%, respectively, indicating the robustness of our pipeline in detecting factual errors.

3.3
PanCanBench: Benchmarking AI models on real patient questions for pancreatic cancer

3.3.1
Average score by model

Figure 4a presents the average score for each model. The o3 model achieved the highest average score (82.3%), followed closely by Grok-4 (80.4%) and GPT-5 (78.4%), which exhibited comparable performance. Among the open-source models, Olmo3-32B-think performed best, with an average score of 66.3%.

3.3.2
Factual error analysis

Figure 4b shows the proportion of responses containing factual errors across models. Overall, open-source models demonstrated substantially higher error rates than proprietary models. For example, Llama-3.1-8B produced factual errors in 53.8% of responses. In contrast, GPT-4o (6.0%), Gemini-2.5 Pro (6.0%), and Gemini-2.5 Flash (7.5%) exhibited the lowest error rates. We also identified the specific question categories where each model is most prone to failure, flagging any category with an error rate exceeding 10%. We report the total number of detected errors by model and the percentage of wrong claims by model in Supplementary Figure 3 and 4 and examples of factual errors in Supplementary Table S6.

3.3.3
Whether web search helps AI generate better responses

We found that the integration of web search capabilities does not guarantee superior model performance. As illustrated in the average score comparisons in Figure 4c, neither Gemini-2.5 Pro (paired t-test,
p=0.2544
) nor GPT-5 (paired t-test,
p=0.6494
) demonstrated statistically significant improvements when web search was enabled. To understand why, we decomposed our analysis into two primary aspects: (1) web search ability characterized by 3 metrics as introduced in the Methods Section, and (2) the ability to integrate external information while preserving existing knowledge (i.e., effective integration should introduce new, relevant information without omitting or degrading previously correct content).

Regarding the first aspect, all three models demonstrated strong intent recognition, with web search triggering rates consistently exceeding 80.0%. For rubric criteria that explicitly required references, GPT-5 achieved the highest resource appropriateness rate at 83.0% as opposed to 56.0% for Gemini-2.5 Pro and 52.5% for Claude-Sonnet-4.5. We summarized the reasons why models failed to receive credit on rubric criteria requiring references, even after using web search (Supplementary Table S4). When assessing the direct relevance of provided citations via an LLM-as-a-judge, Claude-Sonnect-4.5 achieved the highest precision, with 95.8% of its links verified as supportive of the prompt (Figure 4d).

The second aspect of our analysis reveals that the lack of aggregate improvement is largely due to failures in knowledge integration. We observed that models occasionally omitted critical information when web search was enabled, even when that same information was correctly included without web search. For example, in a clinical trial query, a model accurately described the different trial phases when search was disabled but failed to mention them entirely when search was enabled. This suggests that the process of incorporating external data can “crowd out” a model’s internal knowledge or distract it from comprehensive rubric fulfillment, leading to a net performance loss.

4
Discussion

Our study introduces PanCanBench, a clinically grounded benchmark using real-world patient inquiries (and human-derived rubrics) to evaluate LLM utility and safety in pancreatic cancer. By moving beyond synthetic queries, PanCanBench captures the disease-specific depth required for high-stakes oncology.

A primary finding is that rubric-based completeness alone is insufficient to characterize models’ performance. While reasoning models like o3 achieved the highest rubric scores (82.3%), they exhibited significantly higher factual error rates than other GPT-family models (Figure 4b). This mirrors the GPT-5 System Card: on HealthBench Hard, o3 outperformed GPT-5 on rubric score (31.6% vs 25.5%) but had a higher hallucination rate (12.9% vs 3.6%). Together, these results motivate joint evaluation frameworks that consider both rubric-based quality and factual reliability.

When these two dimensions were considered simultaneously, GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5 Flash emerged as the most reliable performers, both ranking in the top four across both metrics. In contrast, Claude models exhibited the lowest performance among proprietary systems. This was primarily due to response brevity, which failed to address the breadth of information required by expert rubrics (Supplementary Figure 5).

High hallucination rates were particularly pronounced among open-source models, some exceeding 50.0%. Errors at this magnitude carry serious consequences. For example, when queried about disease progression (Q127), Llama-3.1-8B incorrectly defined Stage III pancreatic cancer as disease that has spread to distant organs. This is a clinically significant error: Stage III is characterized by local vascular involvement where curative-intent treatments may still be viable, whereas spread to distant organs (Stage IV) is generally considered incurable
37
. Such hallucinations risk inflicting profound psychological trauma and could lead patients to prematurely abandon curative treatment options based on a false terminal prognosis. Given that proprietary models may be behind paywalls or restricted by data sharing policies, there is an urgent need for the development of more robust, medically-aligned open-source models to ensure equitable access to safe AI-driven information
38
. Overall, the domains of Diagnosis & Screening and Treatment & Clinical Trials constituted the primary sources of model failure.

Furthermore, our analysis indicated that the integration of web search capabilities
18
did not guarantee a superior clinical response. While search functions improved citation accuracy for some models, we found that search-enabled models sometimes omitted critical clinical details that were correctly provided in the baseline state. Furthermore, while models successfully triggered searches for the majority of complex queries, many of the retrieved materials lacked clinical rigor
39
. Specifically, Gemini-2.5 Pro and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 frequently rely on generic web links rather than the high-authority, peer-reviewed sources required by rubrics criteria. This instability in retrieval orchestration suggests that current systems are not yet optimized for synthesizing the complex, evidence-based data necessary for oncology.

Finally, our experiments showed that synthetic, AI-generated rubrics cannot replace human-curated benchmarks. Evaluation with AI rubrics resulted in an average score inflation of 17.9 points, with top-tier models approaching near-saturation. However, AI-generated rubrics have the potential to serve as a scalable proxy for determining relative model rankings.

The primary strength of PanCanBench lies in its foundation of real-world patient inquiries and rubrics developed by oncology fellows, which ensures clinical relevance compared to synthetic datasets. However, this study is limited by its small sample size and its focus on a single, high-stakes disease. Additionally, there is potential for biases inherent in any LLM-based judging framework. Future work should expand this human-in-the-loop methodology to other specialized medical fields to further validate AI safety across diverse patient populations.

5
Conclusion

In conclusion, while LLMs show promise in supporting patients, significant gaps remain in factual reliability and effective information integration. PanCanBench demonstrates that human expertise remains indispensable for creating rigorous evaluation standards. Ensuring that AI serves as a safe and helpful resource for cancer patients requires continued advances in factual grounding and the development of more reliable open-source alternatives.

Supplementary Appendix

Appendix A
Rubric Design Guidelines

A.1
Context

We designed rubrics to be used by large language models (LLMs) to evaluate responses generated by either LLMs or human authors. The goal is to ensure that the rubrics can be applied consistently and accurately, regardless of the response source. The following principles guided rubric development:

•

The LLM is assumed to be capable of following instructions, applying scoring rules consistently, and interpreting responses based on logical structure and language. However, the response being evaluated may contain outdated or incorrect factual information, particularly for rapidly evolving medical topics. Therefore, the rubric must be designed to detect and penalize factual inaccuracies.

•

Each rubric is question-specific. Generic rubrics are avoided; instead, each rubric explicitly defines the information that must be included or avoided for an ideal response to that particular question.

•

Each rubric is written such that even a pre-med undergraduate student could apply it accurately without requiring independent expert judgment or interpretation of ambiguous medical details.

•

Overall, the rubrics are designed to be straightforward to apply, clinically accurate, and aligned with current clinical guidelines, enabling both LLMs and non-expert human graders to score responses consistently and objectively.

A.2
Rubric Design Rules

Assigning Points to Each Rubric Item

Each rubric item is assigned a point value reflecting its importance. The total score for a response is calculated as the sum of points earned across all rubric items. Scoring is performed automatically by the AI model using the provided rubric; therefore, the primary responsibility of the rubric designer is to define the items clearly and assign appropriate weights.

•

Each rubric item may be assigned up to 10 points.

•

Points should be allocated based on the clinical and informational importance of the item.

•

Negative points may be assigned for incorrect, misleading, or undesirable content, with a maximum deduction of 10 points per item.

Binary Scoring Structure

Each rubric item is designed to be scored using a binary structure (full credit or no credit) whenever possible. Vague or partial credit is avoided. To aid rubric development and validation, multiple AI-generated responses are provided for each question to test rubric clarity.

Complete-Sentence Rubric Items

Each rubric item is written as a complete sentence to ensure that the scoring requirement is self-contained and unambiguous. For example, instead of writing "Discuss with oncologist," the rubric states: "The response should recommend that the user discuss this case with an oncologist."

Variable Number of Rubric Items

The number of rubric items varies by question. Some questions require detailed clinical evaluation and therefore include more rubric items, while others require fewer.

Appendix F

For each comparison, the AI judge was presented with two responses and returned one of three outcomes: A is better, B is better, or tie. The percentage agreement between the AI judge’s direct decisions and the rubric-based evaluations is summarized in
S3
