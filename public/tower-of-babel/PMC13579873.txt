Production and partial characterization of fructose-rich exopolysaccharide from a newly isolated halophilic strain Vreelandella sp. DT-Z4
Authors: Fengqian Yang, Fangyan Wang, Chengyang Wang, Longzhan Gan, Chunbo Dong, Zhongshun Xu, Xiao Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exopolysaccharide (EPS) synthesis of halophilic microorganisms is often reported as an adaptation to high-salt environments, and the unique structural and functional characteristics of these EPSs make them valuable biomaterials with promising application potential. A moderately halophilic strain, Vreelandella sp. DT-Z4, was isolated from Qinghai saline soil, and its EPS yield reached 3.09 g/L after fermentation optimization. The polysaccharide fraction (designated EPS-Z4) was partially purified using ethanol precipitation, deproteinization, and dialysis processes, and subsequently characterized for monosaccharide composition, spectroscopic features, and morphological properties. Analytical results revealed that EPS-Z4 was a fructose-rich polysaccharide composed of 97.6% fructose, 1.4% glucose, and 0.5% uronic acid. Functional group analysis confirmed the presence of absorption peaks characteristic of polysaccharides. The polysaccharide showed a degradation temperature (Td = 275.9 °C), water solubility (85.8%), and oil-holding capacity (321%). Morphological observations revealed a dense, layered network structure. Based on these physicochemical properties, the fructose-rich polysaccharide fraction EPS-Z4 may merit further investigation as a stabilizer in high-temperature food processing or as a biological agent for the remediation of saline-alkali soils.

Introduction

Microbial exopolysaccharides (EPSs) are high-molecular-weight polymers secreted by microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi. Owing to their distinctive rheological properties, biocompatibility, and functional versatility, EPSs have demonstrated significant potential in the food industry, biomedicine, and environmental remediation [1–4]. In recent years, the exploration of microbial resources from extreme environments has significantly advanced research on EPSs derived from halophilic bacteria. These microorganisms produce polysaccharides rich in sulfate groups and uronic acids, resulting in EPSs with high thermal stability, salt tolerance, and exceptional emulsification capabilities, thereby conferring biological activity and functionality [5]. For instance, their emulsifying properties, metal chelation ability, and antioxidant activities make these EPSs potential alternatives to traditional industrial polysaccharides.

Although studies have successfully isolated EPS-producing strains such as Kocuria rosea ZJUQH [5] and Halorubrum sp. TBZ112 [6] from extreme environments such as saline lakes and saline–alkali soils, the EPS biosynthetic mechanisms under high-salt stress conditions remain largely unexplored in halophilic strains. For instance, while Vreelandella sulfidaeris has been shown to produce EPS under saline conditions and its EPS secretion is associated with salt stress alleviation [7], the detailed structural features and composition of this EPS remain to be characterized. Furthermore, traditional lactic acid bacteria such as Leuconostoc mesenteroides BD170 can produce up to 32 g/L of EPS under low-salt conditions [8]; however, their reliance on sucrose substrates and limited salt tolerance (1% NaCl) make them challenging to adapt to high-salt industrial fermentation environments. Thus, developing new halophilic bacteria with higher EPS yields and greater environmental adaptability may help address current limitations. Exploring new EPS resources from such specially adapted strains is therefore of significant theoretical and practical value.

The genus Vreelandella is a newly established group of Gram-negative bacteria within the family Halomonadaceae. It was reclassified from the genus Halomonas by de la Haba et al. in 2023 through genomic analysis and currently comprises 38 valid species [9]. This genus has attracted attention due to its moderately halophilic characteristics (optimal growth salinity of 0–15% NaCl), broad alkaline tolerance (pH range of 7.0–10.0), and adaptability to diverse ecological environments, ranging from saline lakes to Arctic waters. Although Vreelandella exhibits considerable potential for environmental remediation, such as the degradation of polyethylene and organic pollutants [10], as well as biosynthesis applications, including polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) production [11] and D-amino acid utilization, the EPS associated with this genus remains largely unexplored [12]. By contrast, EPS production has been better documented in the closely related genus Halomonas. For example, Halomonas stenophila HK30 produces a sulfated heteropolysaccharide (haloglycan) at 3.89 g/L, composed of glucose, glucuronic acid, mannose, fucose, galactose and rhamnose [13]; Halomonas smyrnensis AAD6T produces levan (β-(2,6)-linked fructan) with yields up to 8.84 g/L under optimized conditions [14]; and Halomonas saliphila LCB169T produces a mannose/glucose-rich EPS with high oil-holding capacity [15]. However, none of these previously reported EPSs contain fructose as the predominant monosaccharide (> 97%). As a component of microbial adaptation mechanisms to extreme environments, the EPS of Vreelandella may possess distinctive structural properties and functions, including high salt–alkali tolerance, unique rheological characteristics, and enhanced biofilm formation capabilities. These attributes could not only provide valuable resources for developing new biomaterials, such as pharmaceutical dressings or food additives, but also facilitate innovative industrial applications such as microbial oil recovery and wastewater treatment. Furthermore, chemical characterization of EPSs can yield novel molecular markers that enhance our understanding of the taxonomy within this genus while elucidating the complex phylogenetic relationships among members of the family Halomonadaceae. Consequently, a systematic study on the isolation and characterization of EPS from Vreelandella will not only address existing gaps in functional research on this genus but also contribute to the understanding of extremophilic resources for industrial applications.

Although accurate structural resolution usually requires deep purification to obtain homogeneous polysaccharides, the economic feasibility of such an approach remains challenging for large-scale industrial applications. Separation and purification are the major cost drivers in bioprocessing: in waste-based production of high-value chemicals, downstream processes can account for 50–70% of the total cost [16]. In a typical biorefining system, this cost can account for up to 50% of the total capital and operating costs [17]. In particular, for polysaccharide purification, chromatographic techniques such as ion-exchange chromatography are not only technically complex but also costly due to the requirement for expensive consumables and stringent operating conditions [18, 19]. Therefore, from an application-oriented and cost-control perspective, evaluating the application potential of crude polysaccharides with well-defined major components and focused functionalities obtained through low-cost preliminary purification (such as ethanol precipitation and deproteinization) is a practical strategy offering greater industrialization prospects during the early stages of research and development. This facilitates rapid screening and validation of their commercial value before investing in costly deep purification.

The aim of this study was to isolate an EPS-producing strain from an extreme saline–alkali soil and to comprehensively characterize its structure and function. The moderately halophilic Vreelandella sp. strain DT-Z4 was successfully obtained, and its exopolysaccharide EPS-Z4 was characterized, revealing the suggested features of fructosyl EPS in halotolerant microorganisms, enriching the resource pool of extremophiles, and providing new insights into microbial adaptation mechanisms in high-salinity environments. The EPS exhibits a degradation temperature (Td = 275.9 °C), suggesting that it could be preliminarily evaluated as a high-temperature-resistant food additive. Its water solubility (85.8%) and biocompatibility might be considered for further exploration in drug carrier applications. Therefore, the isolation and characterization of EPS-Z4 from a newly identified halophilic strain may contribute to the exploration of extremophilic EPS resources and provide a basis for further investigation of its potential applications.

Materials and methods

Isolation and selection of EPS-producing halophilic bacteria

A gradient dilution coating method was employed to isolate strains from saline–alkali soil samples collected in Qinghai Province, China. A 1 g soil suspension was diluted in 9 mL of sterile saline containing 5% NaCl, followed by gradient dilutions ranging from 10−1 to 10−8. Subsequently, 100 µL of each diluted solution was evenly spread on solid medium (g/L) containing 0.5 K2HPO4, 0.15 MgSO4·7H2O, 1.5 peptone, 2.5 yeast extract, 20 sucrose, 10 agar, and 80 NaCl at pH 8.0 ± 0.2. After incubation, colonies with mucoid, slimy, or highly viscous morphology were identified as potential EPS producers and selected for further evaluation.

The target strains were inoculated in the above liquid fermentation medium without agar, and the EPS content was quantified after 48 h of incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The strains were preserved in a glycerol suspension at − 80 °C for future analysis. For EPS quantification, the absorbance value was determined at an optical density of 490 nm using the modified phenol–sulfuric acid method [20]; quantification was achieved through comparison with a glucose standard curve. Each experimental group included three biological replicates to ensure statistical validity.

Morphological and molecular identification of strain DT-Z4

Samples of strain DT-Z4 in the logarithmic growth phase were washed with isotonic PBS buffer (pH 7.4), fixed in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for 12 h, and then dehydrated in 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% gradient ethanol (10 min per stage). After critical point drying, the cells were mounted on a copper mesh and coated with gold film. Imaging was performed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apreo 2 C, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with a T2 secondary electron detector operating at 10 kV, and representative images were captured at 20,000× and 100,000× magnification to examine surface structure.

Gram staining was performed following standard protocols [21], while the morphological characteristics of the cells were systematically characterized according to Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology (9th edition, 2004). The DNA template of strain DT-Z4 was prepared using the Chelex-100 boiling method [22]. The universal primers 27 F and 1492R were used to amplify the 16S rDNA gene [23]. The purified products were cloned into the pUCm-T vector and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing. The nearly complete sequences obtained were compared against multiple databases via BLAST on the EzBioCloud server (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/) [24] to identify highly homologous sequences. A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on default parameters in the MEGA version 7.0 software [25]; specifically, the neighbor-joining method was applied to calculate genetic distances, while bootstrap analysis was conducted to assess topological reliability.

Optimization of culture conditions for EPS production

Based on the basic fermentation medium described above (pH 8.0, temperature of 30 °C, rotation speed of 200 rpm, and inoculum volume of 5% as the initial baseline condition), a single-factor optimization strategy was employed to systematically investigate medium composition and fermentation parameters for EPS production by the strain. Following activation in LB medium for 24 h, the strains were inoculated into the fermentation medium as a seed solution.

The following variables were examined sequentially: first, carbon sources (sucrose, fructose, lactose, maltose, mannitol, and sorbitol at a concentration of 4% (w/v)); second, nitrogen sources (yeast extract, peptone, tryptone, and beef extract at a concentration of 5% (w/v)). After identifying the optimal carbon and nitrogen sources, their concentration gradients were established (carbon source: 1–10% (w/v); nitrogen source: 0–20% (g/L) [26].

Building upon this optimized medium composition, we evaluated the effects of various fermentation parameters on EPS synthesis, including fermentation time (24–120 h with intervals of 24 h), inoculum size (1–13% with increments of 2%), agitation speed (50–250 rpm with increments of 50 rpm), pH levels (pH 5.0–10.0 with increments of pH 1.0), and temperature settings (ranging from 20 °C to 40 °C with increments of 5 °C). Throughout each parameter test, the other variables were maintained at their baseline values.

Crude extraction and purification of EPS

Based on Kim’s methodological framework and the characteristics of halophilic bacteria, the fermentation broth of the strain DT-Z4 was centrifuged at 5200 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was then mixed with absolute ethanol (1:3 v/v) and thoroughly stirred before being allowed to stand at 4 °C for 12 h to facilitate polysaccharide precipitation [5]. The resultant precipitate was collected via centrifugation at 5200 ×g for 20 min and subsequently redissolved in ultrapure water to yield a crude EPS solution.

Deproteinization was performed using a modified Sevage method: the crude extract was combined with chloroform-n-butanol (4:1, v/v) at a 2:1 volume ratio, vortexed for 20 min, and centrifuged at 5200 ×g for 15 min to transfer denatured proteins from the aqueous phase to the organic phase. This procedure was repeated until no white flocculent material remained at the interface. The deproteinized solution was transferred into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cut-off of 8–14 kDa and dialyzed against ultrapure water for 72 h at 4 °C [27]. Finally, vacuum freeze-drying for 48 h resulted in an off-white powder designated EPS-Z4, which was used for further characterization.

Ultraviolet–visible and fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The purity of EPS-Z4 was assessed using UV–visible spectrophotometry. Specifically, a 10 mg of EPS-Z4 sample was dissolved in 10 mL of ultrapure water. The resulting aqueous solution was analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (U-3900 H, Hitachi, Japan). Scanning was conducted across wavelengths ranging from 200 to 700 nm at intervals of 0.5 nm. Peaks corresponding to protein and nucleic acid absorption were identified at 260 nm and 280 nm, respectively [28].

To evaluate the functional groups and glycosidic bonds present within EPS-Z4, the freeze-dried powder was mixed with potassium bromide and compressed into pellets using a hydraulic press. A Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrophotometer (Nicolet iS10, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to record spectra over the range of 4000 to 500 cm− 1 at a resolution of 4 cm− 1 [28]. Data acquisition and processing were performed using OMNIC spectral software.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide composition of EPS-Z4 was determined using high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC) with some modifications [29]. Five milligrams of partially purified EPS-Z4 were hydrolyzed with 2 mL of 1.5 M trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) for 2 h at 80 °C in a sealed glass ampoule. The acid hydrolysate was aspirated into a tube, dried under a stream of nitrogen (N2), and the residue was redissolved in ultrapure water. The released monomers were detected using an ion chromatography system (ICS5000, Thermo Fisher) equipped with a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD). Standard monosaccharides (fucose, galactosamine hydrochloride, rhamnose, arabinose, glucosamine hydrochloride, galactose, glucose, xylose, mannose, fructose, ribose, galacturonic acid, guluronic acid, glucuronic acid, and mannuronic acid) were used as references for peak identification and quantification. Quantification was based on the chromatographic peak area and calculated using the standard calibration curve of each monosaccharide.

Zeta potential, particle size, and microstructure examination

The EPS-Z4 aqueous solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and subsequently diluted with ultrapure water to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. The particle size distribution and zeta potential of EPS-Z4 were assessed using a Zetasizer Nano ZSP (Malvern, UK) at 25 °C [30].

The morphology of EPS-Z4 was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Apreo 2 C, Thermo Scientific USA). Dried samples were affixed to copper stubs and coated with a conductive gold layer (~ 10 nm thick) for SEM characterization. Observations were conducted at magnifications of 1000× and 10,000× under an accelerating voltage of 7 kV [31].

Determination of thermal stability

To investigate the thermal properties of the obtained EPS-Z4, a 3 mg sample of EPS-Z4 was subjected to thermal stability testing in an Al2O3 crucible. The experiments were conducted using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG209F1, Netzsch, Germany) [32] and a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC214, Shanghai). The samples underwent TGA analysis with nitrogen as the carrier gas and were heated from 35 °C to 800 °C at a linear heating rate of 10 °C/min. Concurrently, DSC measurements were performed over a temperature range of 20 °C to 400 °C to ensure consistency with other parameters, according to DTG guidelines [33].

Water solubility index (WSI)

The water solubility index (WSI) of the samples was determined using a modified method. Briefly, 50 mg of EPS-Z4 was dissolved in 0.5 mL of distilled water and vigorously agitated at room temperature for 2 h using a vortex mixer to obtain a uniform suspension. The precipitate was collected using centrifugation at 6800 ×g for 20 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was decanted, freeze-dried, and the weight of the tube was recorded [34]. Statistical analysis

Data processing, analysis, and visualization were performed using Excel 2021, IBM SPSS Statistics 26, and Origin 24 software. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA to test the significance of the overall differences among multiple groups, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test to clarify the specific differences between groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening and identification of EPS-producing strain

The strain DT-Z4 was isolated from saline soil collected in Qinghai Province. The EPS production of the strain DT-Z4 was determined to be 1.66 ± 0.14 g/L under fermentation conditions of 48 h, pH 8.0, and 8% NaCl, indicating that it is a typical moderate halophile. Colonies of strain DT-Z4, incubated on Luria-Bertani medium at 30 °C for 48 h, measured 1.5–3.0 mm in diameter and were round with smooth, intact edges. They exhibited a mucoid, raised, opaque, ivory-white coloration (Fig. 1a). SEM and Gram staining revealed that the strain DT-Z4 is a Gram-negative short bacillus, with a length ranging from 1.5 to 1.8 μm and a diameter between 0.4 and 0.7 μm (Fig. 1b). The colonies appeared thick, milky-white, and round or oval-shaped with slightly convex edges.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the complete sequencing of 16S rDNA (1545 bp; GenBank accession number OR690787) demonstrated that the strain DT-Z4 shared a high sequence similarity of 99.58% with V. sulfidaeris ATCC BAA-803T (Fig. 2), surpassing the bacterial species threshold of 98.7% [36]. The phylogenetic tree constructed using the neighbor-joining method (MEGA 7.0, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates) indicated that the strain DT-Z4 and V. sulfidaeris ATCC BAA-803T formed an independent clade with high bootstrap support (99%), suggesting that strain DT-Z4 is closely affiliated with the genus Vreelandella. It should be noted that taxonomic assignment based solely on 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity is not definitive; whole-genome sequencing and calculation of ANI (Average Nucleotide Identity) and dDDH (digital DNA-DNA hybridization) would be required for precise species identification.

Optimization of fermentation conditions

Through systematic optimization of the types and concentrations of carbon and nitrogen sources, as well as fermentation parameters, the optimal culture conditions for EPS synthesis by strain DT-Z4 were identified (Fig. 3). The carbon-source screening experiment demonstrated that EPS production reached 1.83 g/L when sucrose was used as a substrate, significantly surpassing the levels obtained with other carbon sources such as fructose (0.40 g/L; p < 0.001), lactose (0.51 g/L; p < 0.001), mannitol (1.08 g/L; p < 0.001), and sorbitol (0.73 g/L; p < 0.001). No EPS production was detected in the experimental group using maltose as a carbon source (Fig. 3a). One‑way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of carbon source type on EPS production (p < 0.001). Post‑hoc comparisons (Duncan’s multiple range test) are denoted by different lowercase letters in Fig. 3. The sucrose gradient experiment showed that EPS production followed a bimodal curve with increasing concentration, reaching a maximum of 2.91 g/L at 9% (w/v) (Fig. 3b), followed by a decrease to 2.82 g/L at 10% (p < 0.001).

In nitrogen-source optimization, organic nitrogen sources significantly enhanced EPS synthesis. The highest yield was observed in the peptone-treated group (2.29 g/L), which was significantly higher than those in the other groups (yeast extract: 1.70 g/L; p < 0.001, tryptone: 0.74 g/L; p < 0.001, and beef extract: 0.63 g/L; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3c). When the peptone concentration was increased to 12 g/L, EPS production increased to 2.45 g/L (Fig. 3d); however, further increases to 16–20 g/L resulted in decreased EPS production (p < 0.001).

The analysis of fermentation parameters revealed that EPS production reached 2.76 g/L at an agitation speed of 200 rpm (Fig. 4a), which was higher than the yields observed at lower speeds (e.g., 1.97 g/L at 50 rpm; p < 0.001). The yield reached a maximum of 2.59 g/L at 96 h (Fig. 4b) but subsequently decreased to 2.53 g/L at 120 h (p < 0.05). Under optimal culture conditions, with an inoculum size of 7%, the highest yield reached 3.09 g/L. However, an excessively high inoculum level (13%) resulted in a reduced yield, yielding 1.13 g/L (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4c). Temperature optimization experiments indicated an optimal temperature at 25 °C, yielding approximately 1.76 g/L of EPS (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4d). Although the initial fermentation conditions were set at 30 °C (as described in Sect. 2.3), optimization revealed that 25 °C was the optimal temperature for EPS production by strain DT-Z4. Additionally, a neutral to weakly alkaline environment (pH range: 8.0 − 9.0) favored EPS synthesis, achieving a maximum production of 1.99 g/L at pH 9.0; conversely, an acidic culture environment (pH 5.0) yielded only 0.44 g/L of EPS (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4e). On this basis, the optimal culture conditions for EPS-Z4 biosynthesis were determined as follows: sucrose at 9% (w/v) as the carbon source, peptone at 12 g/L as the nitrogen source, initial pH at 9.0, cultivation temperature of 25 °C, agitation speed of 200 rpm, inoculum size of 7%, and a fermentation time of 96 h. Under these optimized conditions, the final EPS production reached 3.09 g/L, representing an 86.1% increase compared to the initial yield of 1.66 g/L.

UV–vis and FTIR analyses

The crude EPS was partially purified using ethanol gradient precipitation, Sevage deproteinization, dialysis (8–14 kDa cut-off), and freeze-drying processes to yield the final product, designated EPS-Z4. UV–visible spectral analysis (200–400 nm) showed that EPS-Z4 exhibited no significant absorption peaks at 260–280 nm (Fig. 5a). The A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ ratio was below 0.5. The FTIR analysis of EPS-Z4 revealed characteristic absorption peaks (Fig. 5c) at 3370.9 cm−1, 1645.9 cm−1, 1409.2 cm−1, 1241.1 cm−1, 1080 cm−1, 935.6 cm−1, and 850 cm−1.

Monosaccharide composition of EPS

By comparing HPAEC retention times (Fig. 5b), the composition of EPS-Z4 was characterized. The primary components identified were fructose (97.6%) and glucose (1.4%). Additionally, trace amounts of glucuronic acid (0.5%), mannose (0.1%), xylose (0.1%), galactose (0.1%), and arabinose (0.1%) were present. This compositional profile indicates that EPS-Z4 is a fructose-rich polysaccharide. Notably, the sensitivity of the pulsed amperometric detector (PAD) was lower for fructose than for glucose under the same experimental conditions. Thus, although the peak area of fructose is similar to that of glucose in Fig. 5b, the actual content of fructose is absolutely dominant (97.6%), which is consistent with the calculated molar ratios based on individual calibration curves. Minor monosaccharides (e.g., glucose, uronic acid) may originate from trace co-purified contaminants; further chromatographic purification would be required to confirm whether they are integral components of the polysaccharide.

Particle size and zeta potential examination

The colloidal properties of aqueous EPS-Z4 solution were characterized. The hydrodynamic diameter of the fructose-rich polysaccharide particles ranged from 68.1 to 342 nm, with a mean Z-average particle size of 172.9 nm (Fig. 6a). The zeta potential of the EPS-Z4 solution was measured to be − 5.36 mV (Fig. 6b).

Thermal stability of EPS-Z4

The thermal properties of EPS-Z4 were analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Fig. 6c and d). The TGA curve showed that EPS-Z4 underwent three distinct stages of weight loss during heating from 35 °C to 800 °C: the first stage (35 to 72.3 °C) involved a weight loss of 6.2%; the second stage (72.3 to 210.4 °C) showed no significant weight loss; and the third stage (210.4 to 461.2 °C) revealed a substantial weight loss of 56.3%. A further weight loss of 11.6% was observed between 461.2 and 800 °C. The derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curve indicated a degradation temperature (Td) of 275.9 °C. DSC analysis revealed a glass transition temperature (Tg) of 72.2 °C and a melting temperature (Tm) of 213.4 °C for EPS-Z4.

Scanning electron microscopy

The microscopic morphology of partially purified EPS-Z4 was examined using SEM (Fig. 7). At a magnification of 1000×, EPS-Z4 exhibited a complex, porous network structure composed of densely arranged irregular lamellae and interwoven dendrites. At a higher magnification of 10,000×, the surface morphology appeared as smooth, dense sheets.

Functional properties of EPS-Z4

The solubility and interface-related functional properties of EPS were quantified using the following three key indicators: WSI, WHC, and OHC. The WSI and WHC of EPS-Z4 were 85.8 ± 0.11% and 14.06 ± 4.62%, respectively. EPS-Z4 showed excellent oil holding performance, reaching a value of up to 321 ± 9.90%.

Discussion

The observed preference of the strain DT-Z4 for sucrose over the other tested carbon sources is consistent with the reported metabolic characteristics of many halophilic bacteria, which preferentially utilize disaccharide substrates [37]. The complete absence of EPS production with maltose as the carbon source indicates that the strain may lack the specific α-1,4 glycosidase activity required to hydrolyze this sugar into metabolizable monomers. Furthermore, the biphasic response of EPS production to increasing sucrose concentration, in which yield peaked at 9% before declining, could be explained by the potential inhibition of sugar transporter activity under hypertonic stress induced by very high sugar concentrations, a phenomenon that is consistent with previous studies on osmoregulation [38].

Regarding nitrogen metabolism, the superior performance of organic nitrogen sources, particularly peptone, in promoting EPS synthesis suggests that complex nutrients contribute to EPS production. This effect may be related to the vitamins and cofactors present in organic nitrogen, which can support microbial growth and EPS production pathways [39]. The decline in EPS yield at peptone concentrations exceeding 12 g/L may reflect a metabolic shift, where an imbalanced carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio redirects carbon flux away from EPS synthesis towards central metabolic pathways such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle.

The evaluation of fermentation parameters provides further insight into the fermentation process. The optimal EPS yield at 200 rpm suggests that a moderate level of dissolved oxygen is favorable for EPS production, possibly by supporting the activity of oxygen-dependent enzymes such as glycosyltransferases involved in EPS assembly. The temporal pattern of production, which peaked at 96 h before declining, is consistent with the growth-coupled kinetics of many bacteria, indicating that EPS synthesis is primarily active during the late exponential and stationary phases and may subsequently be limited by substrate depletion or by-product accumulation. Moreover, the reduced EPS yield observed at an excessively high inoculum size (13%) underscores the impact of nutrient availability and increased competition within the culture. Finally, the optimal growth and EPS production at 25 °C and within a pH range of 8.0–9.0 are consistent with the ecological niche of strain DT-Z4, reinforcing its identity as a moderately halophilic bacterium adapted to moderate temperatures and alkaline environments. The EPS yield of strain DT-Z4 under the optimized conditions reached 3.09 g/L. Compared with other halophilic EPS-producing strains, this yield is substantially higher than those of Halomonas ventosae and Halomonas anticariensis (0.28–0.50 g/L) [40], but lower than the yields of Halobacillus sp. EG1HP4QL (5.9 g/L) [41] and Chromohalobacter salexigens 3EQS1 (15.1 g/L) [42]. Thus, strain DT-Z4 shows promising productivity, especially when compared to closely related Halomonas species, and further optimization (e.g., medium composition, fed-batch fermentation) may enhance its EPS yield. Meanwhile, the one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach used in this study is suitable for initial screening of key variables and approximate optimal ranges, but it does not capture potential interactions between factors. For more precise optimization and understanding of factor interactions, a statistical design such as Response Surface Methodology could be employed in future studies.

Although the standardized purification procedure used in this study effectively separated major impurities and yielded samples suitable for physicochemical and functional characterization, it may be insufficient for resolving trace components with similar charge or molecular weight. Notably, the molecular weight distribution and polydispersity of EPS-Z4 were not determined in this study, and the partial purification does not guarantee absolute homogeneity. Future studies employing size-exclusion chromatography (SEC/GPC) are required to assess these parameters. Therefore, future studies should combine chromatography with other fractionation methods to enable more detailed structural characterization.

Based on the FTIR spectral interpretation, we inferred key structural features of EPS-Z4. The broad absorption peak at 3370.9 cm−1 could be attributed to the O-H stretching vibrations of the fructose-rich polysaccharide [43]. The strong peak at 1645.9 cm−1 might be due to the H-O-H bending vibration of bound water or the C = O stretching of the amide I band [44], which would be consistent with trace amounts of bound water or protein residues. An absorption peak was observed at 1241 cm−1, which might be assigned to the C-O-C and C-O stretching vibrations of the sugar ring [15]. Further complementary analyses, such as elemental analysis and specific chemical detection, are needed to fully elucidate the structural features of EPS-Z4. The absorption peak at 1080 cm−1 is characteristic of C-O-C stretching vibrations [45]. However, given the exceptionally high fructose content (97.6%) of EPS-Z4, it should be noted that fructose residues in polysaccharides predominantly adopt the furanose form. Therefore, the presence of pyranose rings cannot be conclusively determined from the current FTIR data alone, and further NMR analysis is required to confirm the ring conformation of the monosaccharide units.

The strong absorption peak at 1645.9 cm−1 is commonly observed in the FTIR analysis of polysaccharides. Although this region may also include contributions from the C = O stretching vibrations of carboxylate or amide groups [46], this contribution is likely minimal, given that the monosaccharide composition analysis revealed a low uronic acid content of 0.5%. Direct evidence for the presence of trace amounts of uronic acids, such as glucuronic acid, was obtained from monosaccharide composition analysis (HPAEC) results. Additionally, the weak peak observed at 1409.2 cm−1 could be attributed to the symmetric stretching vibration of COO− [47], consistent with the detection of uronic acid.

EPS-Z4 exhibited a lower C-O-H bending vibration peak intensity at 1035.5 cm−1 compared with that of V. elongata EPS [48], which may reflect differences in the distribution or connection mode of sugar ring substituents. A similar phenomenon has also been reported in V. sulfidaeris [7], which may be related to the substrate specificity of glycosyltransferases in the biosynthetic pathway. It should be noted that, based solely on the current monosaccharide composition analysis and FTIR data, the polymer cannot yet be classified as a fructan without definitive confirmation of its glycosidic linkage patterns and anomeric configurations. Future studies employing 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopy, along with methylation analysis (GC-MS), will be essential to fully resolve the structure of EPS-Z4 and to establish robust structure-function relationships.

Comparative monosaccharide composition analysis revealed distinctive structural features of EPS-Z4. EPS-Z4 exhibited a monosaccharide composition pattern distinct from those of several typical bacterial EPSs from the same family or closely related genera. For example, EPS-S6 produced by H. elongata S6 is a heteropolysaccharide dominated by glucosamine (33.5%), mannose (30.6%), rhamnose (23%), and glucose (12%) [48]; Halorubrum sp. CRSS can synthesize either mannose or fructo-glucan containing small amounts of fructose and glucose in different culture media [49]; and H. stenophila HK30 can produce haloglycan containing glucose, glucuronic acid, mannose, and no fructose [13]. Notably, within the same family, Halomonas smyrnensis AAD6T has been reported to produce high levels of levan, a β-(2→6)-linked fructan [50], demonstrating that fructose-rich EPS biosynthesis exists among halophiles in this taxonomic group. In contrast, EPS-Z4 contained over 97% fructose, which is rare among known EPSs from halophilic or halotolerant bacteria in the same family or genus, indicating that strain DT-Z4 may produce a fructose-rich polymer through a currently unknown biosynthetic mechanism. This predominant fructose composition may be directly related to its efficient utilization of sucrose and its distinctive physicochemical properties during fermentation.

Compared to homopolysaccharides, EPS-Z4 is characterized as a fructose-rich polysaccharide with minor amounts of other sugars detected in the purified fraction. Homopolysaccharides are macromolecules (molecular weight about 105–106 Da) formed by repeated polymerization of a single type of monosaccharide [51]. The presence of these minor sugars, whether as integral components or trace co-purified materials, suggests potential charge heterogeneity that may influence the rheological properties and functional activities of EPS-Z4. Moreover, the high fructose content (> 97%) observed in this fructose-rich polysaccharide suggests that it may possess a unique glycosidic linkage or synthetic pathway compared to conventional polysaccharides.

The measured zeta potential of − 5.36 mV for EPS-Z4 indicates a negatively charged polysaccharide surface, a critical parameter governing colloidal stability as it modulates particle aggregation behavior through a balance between electrostatic double-layer repulsion and van der Waals forces [52].

To evaluate the practical stability of the EPS-Z4 solution, the measured zeta potential value can be assessed within established stability frameworks. Colloidal systems with an absolute zeta potential exceeding 30 mV exhibit high stability, while those with |ζ| values between 10 and 30 mV indicate moderate stability. Thus, a value of − 5.36 mV (|ζ| = 5.36 mV) places the EPS-Z4 solution system in the medium–low stability category. Notably, this stability level can be influenced by additional factors, including steric effects from the polysaccharide’s molecular conformation and environmental physicochemical parameters such as pH and ionic strength [53, 54].

The thermal stability of a polysaccharide is a key factor determining its processing and application scope. The TGA profile of EPS-Z4 reveals a degradation pattern that provides critical insights into its thermal behavior. The initial weight loss of 6.2% below 72.3 °C is characteristic of the evaporation of adsorbed and bound water. This behavior is consistent with observations for other microbial polysaccharides, such as the glucan from Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides YF32, which exhibited a similar 6.4% weight loss in this phase [55]. The subsequent plateau region (72.3 to 210.4 °C), where no significant weight loss occurred, demonstrates the excellent structural integrity of EPS-Z4 within this temperature range, suggesting that its molecular chains remain stable under moderate heat [56]. This stability indicates that application temperatures should be maintained below approximately 210 °C to avoid degradation. The major weight loss (56.3%) between 210.4 °C and 461.2 °C is attributed to depolymerization of the polysaccharide backbone and cleavage of glycosidic bonds, followed by degradation of the resulting fragments into volatile products, which is typical for carbohydrate polymers. The final, slower weight loss up to 800 °C likely corresponds to the oxidative decomposition of residual carbonaceous material [57].

The degradation temperature (275.9 °C) is above the temperatures commonly used in food processing (typically ≤ 121 °C), indicating that EPS-Z4 could be considered for applications requiring thermal stability [29]. In contrast, Yang et al. reported a high Td of 307.62 °C for YF32 glucan [55], while Guo et al. did not provide a specific Td value for the exopolysaccharide ETW1 [57]. However, they noted that its antioxidant activity remained well-preserved at elevated temperatures. These results indicate that although EPS-Z4 exhibits relatively lower thermal stability compared to other reported similar polysaccharides, it still meets industrial requirements adequately.

The DSC results provide complementary information on the material’s phase behavior. The observed glass transition temperature (Tg) of 72.2 °C confirms its amorphous solid-state nature. The melting temperature (Tm) of 213.4 °C correlates well with the onset of major decomposition in TGA and is higher than those of common commercial thickeners such as xanthan gum (153.4 °C) and locust bean gum (109.11 °C) [58]. This property suggests that EPS-Z4 may be suitable for applications requiring thermal stability.

SEM analysis revealed the morphology of EPS-Z4. At lower magnification, a complex network of irregular lamellae and interwoven dendrites formed a hierarchical porous structure. This architectural feature bore a notable resemblance to the multi-branched flat sheet structure reported for mhEPS produced by the moderately halophilic Gracilibacillus sp. SCU50 [29]. The similarity in morphology between these two polysaccharides from halophiles may suggest convergent structural adaptations to saline environments, potentially influencing functional properties such as water interaction or rheology. However, upon closer examination at higher magnification, a key differentiating feature emerged: the surface of EPS-Z4 appeared as smooth, dense sheets. This stands in stark contrast to the porous and sparkling surface features described for the exopolysaccharide from Leuconostoc citreum NM105 [55]. This marked microscale difference in surface topography likely stems from variations in molecular chain arrangement, degree of polymerization, and intermolecular interactions specific to each producing strain and its biosynthetic machinery.

EPS-Z4 demonstrated remarkable water solubility, achieving a WSI of 85.8 ± 0.11%. This high solubility indicates good hydrophilicity, which correlates with the structural properties of the polysaccharide. According to similar studies on microbial EPS, high solubility is beneficial for its applications, such as food stabilization [55]. The WHC of EPS-Z4 was only 14.06 ± 4.62%, lower than that of Leuconostoc lactis KC117496 (117 ± 7.5%) [59]. Given the high water solubility of EPS‑Z4 (WSI 85.8%), the low measured WHC likely reflects that most of the polymer dissolved into the supernatant during centrifugation, leaving only a small amount of insoluble or aggregated material in the pellet. Under such circumstances, the measured WHC is better interpreted as a reflection of the assay’s applicability to highly water‑soluble samples rather than an inherently low water‑holding capacity of the polysaccharide itself. This observation is consistent with a previous report on another high‑WSI exopolysaccharide: the EPS from H. saliphila LCB169T (WSI 98.0%) also exhibited a relatively low WHC value (19.3%) under similar centrifugation‑based conditions [15]. Additionally, other high‑WSI halophilic EPS have been characterized for their functional properties, though direct WHC comparisons are not always available [48]. Thus, the low WHC value of EPS‑Z4 should be understood in the context of its high solubility and the methodological characteristics of the conventional WHC assay. Furthermore, EPSs exhibiting low WHC can enhance brittleness, minimize breakage, and improve the processability of extruded products. The OHC of EPS-Z4 was higher than that of most reported microbial EPSs [60], which may be related to the molecular conformation and surface characteristics of EPS‑Z4 and its interaction with lipid hydrocarbon chains. The high OHC indicates that EPS-Z4 exhibits affinity for and adsorption ability in oil systems, raising the possibility of its use in food systems that require oil retention, flavor release, or texture adjustment, such as serving as an auxiliary component for fat replacement or improving the stability and taste of oily foods [15, 61]. However, further studies are needed to explore its oil-binding behavior in real food matrices and its effect on product quality.

Compared with levan, EPS‑Z4 shows unique application potential in specific industrial scenarios. In terms of thermal stability, the degradation temperature of microbial levan reported in the literature is usually lower than 230 °C [62], while the degradation temperature of EPS‑Z4 is as high as 275.9 °C, which is more suitable for high-temperature baking, ultra-high temperature (UHT) sterilization, and hot extrusion. Meanwhile, the oil-holding capacity of EPS‑Z4 (321%) was significantly higher than that of most reported EPSs [48], making it more advantageous in oil replacement and flavor retention. In addition, EPS‑Z4 possesses a weak anionic character owing to its low uronic acid content (zeta potential − 5.36 mV), whereas conventional levan is a neutral polysaccharide. This property may facilitate binding to cationic active ingredients, thereby expanding its applications in functional foods or as a biological carrier [63]. It should be noted that the exact glycosidic linkage pattern of EPS‑Z4 still needs to be confirmed by NMR and methylation analysis.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully isolated and characterized a fructose-rich exopolysaccharide (EPS-Z4) produced by Vreelandella sp. DT-Z4. Through systematic optimization of the fermentation process, the EPS-Z4 yield reached 3.09 g/L under optimal conditions: 9% (w/v) sucrose, 12 g/L peptone, pH 9.0, 25 °C, 200 rpm, 7% inoculum, and a 96 h cultivation period, representing an 86.1% increase over the initial yield. Monosaccharide composition analysis indicated that EPS-Z4 is a fructose-rich polysaccharide composed of 97.6% fructose, with minor amounts of glucose and uronic acid. Preliminary FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of characteristic polysaccharide absorption bands. SEM observations indicated that EPS-Z4 exhibited a compact lamellar network structure with a smooth surface morphology. Functional characterization demonstrated that EPS-Z4 exhibited excellent water solubility, OHC, and high thermal stability. Its moderate colloidal stability further underscores its potential for various industrial applications. These functional properties suggest that EPS-Z4 may warrant further investigation as a potential candidate for food and industrial applications, for example as a high-temperature stabilizer or bioemulsifier.
