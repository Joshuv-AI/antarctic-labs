Commercial trends in universal adhesive evolution: updated formulations do not consistently improve dentin bond strength after aging compared with predecessors and a multistep reference
Authors: Sarah Abdel-Gawad, Stephane Le-Goff, Elisabeth Dursun, Maria-Antonietta D’Agostino, Valentine Orgueil, Charline Bacon, Ludovic Moreau, Laetitia Lavoix, Romain Ceinos, Jean-Pierre Attal, Philippe François
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate whether recent commercial evolutions of universal adhesives improve dentin bond strength after aging and interfacial characteristics compared with predecessor formulations and a multistep reference.MethodsThree updated universal adhesives (AFB-00, Scotchbond Universal Plus, and Clearfil Universal Quick 2) and their respective predecessors (G-Premio Bond, Scotchbond Universal, and Clearfil Universal Quick) were evaluated. OptiBond FL served as a multistep etch-and-rinse reference. One hundred eighty human molars were prepared to expose mid-coronal dentin surfaces and assigned to 15 groups (n = 12 per group). Adhesives were applied in self-etch (SE1) or etch-and-rinse (ER2) modes according to the applicable manufacturers’ instructions. The macroshear bond strength (SBS) was measured after 6 months of storage in water. Failure modes were recorded, and representative samples were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to assess the interfacial morphology and adhesive layer thickness. The data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsAfter aging, SBS differed among protocols. AFB-00 and Clearfil Universal Quick 2 performed comparably to their predecessors, whereas Scotchbond Universal Plus showed a significantly lower SBS than Scotchbond Universal. Most universal adhesive protocols showed SBS values comparable or superior to the multistep reference, except Clearfil Universal Quick and Clearfil Universal Quick 2 in self-etch mode. SEM revealed strategy-dependent interfacial patterns and marked differences in adhesive film thickness, particularly for AFB-00 without air drying.ConclusionsRecent commercial evolutions of universal adhesives did not systematically improve dentin bond strength after aging. The observed modifications primarily affected the interfacial morphology and adhesive thickness rather than demonstrating consistently higher SBS values after aging. For clinicians, these findings suggest that commercial evolutions of universal adhesives may reflect distinct formulation strategies rather than incremental improvements, and that the performance of updated materials cannot be assumed from that of their predecessors.Keywords: Adhesion, Dental materials, Bond strength, Dental adhesive, Scanning electron microscopy

Background

Current trends in the development of dental adhesives and related restorative dental materials focus on developing products that are more adaptable, less operator dependent, and easier to handle [1–6]. Universal adhesives emerged in approximately 2011 and represented a major evolution in adhesive dentistry [1]. These materials, which typically contain functional monomers, most commonly 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP), as the main functional component of the organic matrix combined with other functional monomers and methacrylate-based resins rapidly displayed favorable characteristics and clinical versatility [1, 7]. This combination largely explains why universal adhesives have gained widespread use in daily dental practice [4, 8].

By definition, universal adhesives combine three main characteristics [1, 9]. Their most widely recognized feature is their versatility, as they can be applied to both enamel and dentin using either an etch-and-rinse or a self-etch strategy, depending on the clinical situation or practitioner preference [1, 9]. The second characteristic is their ability to act as prosthetic primers on a wide range of restorative materials, largely because of the frequent incorporation of 10-MDP and silane in their formulation [1, 9]. Finally, many universal adhesives are designed to be compatible with both self- and dual-cure resin materials, although this latter aspect remains comparatively underinvestigated in the current literature [1, 9]. However, despite their clinical convenience, the dentin bonding effectiveness and durability of universal adhesives remain material- and protocol-dependent [10, 11]. Differences in pH, solvent composition [12], hydrophilicity [13], and application steps [14] may affect polymerization and interfacial permeability, thereby influencing long-term stability [15]. In addition, published data remain heterogeneous across commercially available universal adhesives and etching strategies [10, 15]. Consequently, when manufacturers introduce updated versions of established universal adhesives, independent comparative evidence is still needed to determine whether such modifications translate to meaningful improvements in dentin adhesion [4], particularly when these modifications are evaluated after an extended aging period. In this context, the emergence of successive commercial evolutions of universal adhesives raises the question of whether such modifications effectively translate into improved dentin adhesion when evaluated after aging.

More recently, a new generation of updated universal adhesive formulations has been introduced, aiming to further optimize clinical handling, chemical composition, or long-term interfacial stability [16, 17]. While these updated materials still comply with the original definition of universal adhesives, the nature of the modifications introduced varies across brands, with some developments primarily focused on clinical use and handling characteristics, as illustrated by AFB-00 (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and Clearfil Universal Quick 2 (Kuraray, Tokyo, Japan), whereas others mainly involve changes in chemical composition, as exemplified by Scotchbond Universal Plus (Solventum, St Paul, MN, USA) [16, 17]. These developments are primarily intended to further improve the longevity of the adhesive interface, as well as specific clinical or application-related characteristics [16, 17]. Accordingly, in the present study, three of the most extensively investigated universal adhesives in the international literature were selected, along with their respective recent evolutions, to evaluate whether these main modifications or some of them affected dentin adhesion, as assessed by shear bond strength (SBS) after six months of aging, and the resulting adhesive interface characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Because independent direct comparisons between updated universal adhesives and their predecessor formulations remain limited, particularly after aging, and because current evidence is still scarce for AFB-00, Scotchbond Universal Plus, and Clearfil Universal Quick 2, the present study aimed to evaluate whether these recent commercial evolutions translate into improved aged dentin bonding. The working hypothesis was that recent commercial evolutions of the tested universal adhesives would be associated with improved SBS values compared with their respective predecessor formulations after aging and would achieve performance comparable to that of the established three-step etch-and-rinse gold standard OptiBond FL (Kerr, Orange, CA, USA).

Methods

Materials used and experimental procedures

Three recently or soon-to-be commercialized versions of previously popular one-bottle universal adhesives, namely, AFB-00, Scotchbond Universal Plus, and Clearfil Universal Quick 2, were selected. These materials were chosen because they allow direct comparisons with their respective predecessor formulations, namely, G-Premio Bond (GC Corporation), Scotchbond Universal (Solventum), and Clearfil Universal Quick (Kuraray), respectively. In addition, they present specific chemical and/or clinical features that differentiate them from the earlier versions. OptiBond FL (Kerr, Orange, CA, USA) was also included in the experimental design as a gold-standard reference to better contextualize the performance and characteristics of the different universal adhesives.

These materials were evaluated for their dentin macroshear bond strength in self-etch or etch-and-rinse mode (except for OptiBond FL) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For AFB-00, both manufacturer-recommended application procedures were tested. All the materials were subsequently subjected to scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for interfacial analysis.

Sample preparation

One hundred eighty freshly extracted human permanent molars were collected from adults after extraction, cleaned of soft tissues, stored at 4 °C in a 1% chloramine solution, and used within 3 months. The teeth were obtained from the dental departments of AP-HP, France. All the experiments were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All teeth were collected after obtaining oral informed consent from the patients according to ethical guidelines established by French law and with the dedicated authorization of our dental school (n°DC-2009-927, Cellule Bioéthique DGRI/A5, Paris, France). Teeth were selected only if they were free of carious lesions, cracks, or other visible structural defects and were prepared according to a protocol consistent with that used in previous studies [4, 9, 18].

Most of the roots were removed to approximately three millimeters below the cementoenamel junction using 80-grit sandpaper on a polishing machine, and a visual inspection was performed to ensure perpendicularity between the root section surface and the long axis of the crown. The occlusal surface of each crown was then abraded with 800-grit sandpaper via water cooling to expose a flat dentin surface (> 7 mm2). The residual crowns were embedded in self-curing acrylic resin within plastic cylinders, with the flat dentin surface exposed. At the end of sample preparation, the dentin surface was oriented parallel to the root section to ensure a standardized and reproducible bonding surface. After grinding, the dentin surfaces were thoroughly rinsed with water and gently air dried. The surfaces were inspected under a microscope at 40× magnification to confirm complete enamel removal and to exclude samples with visible defects (e.g., residual debris or surface irregularities). The samples were randomly assigned to fifteen groups (n = 12 per group), each corresponding to a specific adhesive protocol tested (Table 2). The sample size was not determined through an a priori statistical power calculation. Instead, it was established based on previously published studies using comparable experimental protocols and outcome measures [18, 19], which consistently demonstrated the ability to detect significant differences between groups with similar sample sizes, as well as considering the storage medium selected for sample preservation [18, 20].

After the specific adhesive protocol was performed (Table 2), a cylindrical Teflon mold was used to construct a 3-mm-high cylinder of light-cured resin composite in two increments (Filtek Z 100 Restorative, Solventum) (diameter = 3 mm). Each increment was light-cured for 20 s. Light curing steps were performed with a polywave LED lamp (VALO Grand, Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) operated in standard mode (theoretical irradiance: 970 mW/cm²). The output of the curing unit was verified using a radiometer (Radiometer X, SDI, Bayswater, Australia) before and after the experimental procedures to ensure consistent irradiance.

After demolding, any excess material was gently removed with a scalpel, and the samples were inspected under a microscope at ×40 magnification to exclude major fabrication and interfacial defects, including visible cracks, partial debonding, bubbles, or marginal defects, and to ensure that the standardized bonding area was not exceeded. Specimens presenting any of these defects were discarded and replaced.

All samples were then stored in distilled water at 37 °C in an incubator for 6 months. The distilled water was not replaced during the storage period. Specimens belonging to the same experimental group were stored together in plastic containers sealed with paraffin to minimize evaporation and contamination.

For each group, 10 teeth were allocated to SBS testing, and two additional samples were prepared for SEM interface analyses.

Dentin shear bond strength tests and failure mode determination

The SBS was determined with a universal testing machine (LRX, Lloyd Instruments, Fareham, UK). Shear force was applied at the resin composite/dentin interface, with a chisel-shaped blade parallel to the dentin surface. A cross-head speed of 0.5 mm/min was chosen. No pre-test failures occurred.

The debonded samples were observed under a binocular microscope (BZH10 Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) at 40× magnification, and the failure modes were classified into the following four types:

Scanning electron microscopy

For the SEM examination, after the re-inclusion of the bonded samples in self-curing acrylic resin after storage in water, the resin was sectioned perpendicular to the bonded interface using a low-speed diamond saw (Isomet, Buehler, Coventry, UK) with water cooling, as close as possible to the center of the cylinder. The obtained sections were polished with abrasive disks of decreasing grit size (400, 800, 1,200, 2,400, and 4,000 SiC) followed by 3 and 1 μm diamond particles, and finally etched with orthophosphoric acid for 10 s (K-Etchant syringe, Kuraray) and then rinsed. The samples were cleaned by ultrasonication after each step. Finally, the sections were dehydrated in ethanol and metallized for the microscopic examination (JSM-6400, Jeol Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). SEM observations were primarily intended to provide a qualitative assessment of the dentin-adhesive interface, supplemented by descriptive measurements of adhesive layer thickness. For each SEM specimen, five measurements of adhesive layer thickness were performed perpendicular to the dentin–adhesive interface at locations where the adhesive layer was continuous and clearly identifiable, avoiding resin tags, defects, and irregular areas. Measurements were performed at the magnification used for the corresponding SEM images (×2,000 in most cases). When the adhesive layer exceeded the measurable field at ×2,000, additional measurements were performed at ×1,000 magnification. The magnifications used are indicated in the figure legends. The five measurements obtained for each specimen showed consistent values, and the reported thicknesses therefore reflect representative estimates of the adhesive layer. These measurements were considered descriptive only and were not subjected to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Normality and homoscedasticity assumptions were met, as indicated by the nonsignificant results of the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests (p > 0.05). The SBS data are presented as the mean values and standard deviations. Because the experimental design comprised 15 distinct adhesive protocols and was not fully factorial, with OptiBond FL evaluated only in ER3 mode and AFB-00 including an additional application condition (with or without air blowing), SBS was analyzed using a one-way ANOVA with adhesive protocol as a fixed factor. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. The chosen significance level was p < 0.05. Failure mode data were descriptive only because of the low frequency of non-adhesive failures. Statistical calculations were performed with R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

SBS values

After six months of water storage, the dentin SBS values differed among the tested protocols and ranged from 11.40 ± 1.85 MPa for CUQ in SE1 mode to 27.95 ± 2.79 MPa for SBU in ER2 mode. SBU used in ER2 mode yielded the highest mean SBS and was not significantly different from that of AFB‑00 applied in ER2 mode with air blowing (24.91 ± 2.95 MPa). For GPB, AFB‑00 (both manufacturer‑recommended procedures), SBU, and CUQ 2, the etch‑and‑rinse strategy (ER2) resulted in significantly higher SBS values than the corresponding self‑etch strategy (SE1) did (p < 0.05), whereas no significant effect of the etching strategy was observed for SBU Plus or CUQ (p > 0.05). The two manufacturer‑recommended AFB‑00 application procedures (with vs. without immediate air blowing) produced comparable SBS values in both the SE1 and ER2 modes (p > 0.05). Compared with the predecessor formulations, AFB‑00 and CUQ 2 had SBS values comparable to those of GPB and CUQ, respectively (p > 0.05), whereas SBU Plus had significantly lower SBS values than SBU in both the SE1 and ER2 modes (p < 0.05). The three‑step etch‑and‑rinse reference OptiBond FL (ER3) showed intermediate SBS values (17.81 ± 1.82 MPa).

Failure mode determination after dentin SBS tests

Adhesive failure predominated in all experimental groups, with only four non-adhesive failures recorded overall: two mixed failures for AFB-00 in ER2 mode with air blowing and two cohesive dentin failures for SBU in ER2 mode.

SEM interface analysis

SEM observations performed at ×2,000 magnification (Fig. 1) revealed continuous interfacial morphologies consistent with the bonding strategies applied and with the corresponding SBS results. For all the adhesive systems tested in etch-and-rinse mode (ER2/ER3), a more pronounced micromechanical interaction with dentin was observed that was characterized by longer and more numerous resin tags extending into the dentinal tubules. In contrast, specimens bonded using the self-etch strategy (SE1) generally exhibited shorter and less prominent resin tags, with a more superficial interaction pattern and partial preservation of smear plugs. These morphological differences were consistently observed across the various universal adhesives investigated.

Variations in adhesive film thickness were observed among the different materials and application protocols. GPB exhibited an adhesive layer of approximately 7–8 μm in SE1 mode and 6–7 μm in ER2 mode. AFB-00 applied with air blowing resulted in a markedly thinner and more well-defined adhesive layer, measuring approximately 1–2 μm in both SE1 and ER2 modes. In contrast, when air blowing was omitted, the adhesive layer appeared substantially thicker and exceeded the measurable range at ×2,000 magnification. SBU showed an adhesive thickness of approximately 9 μm in SE1 mode and 5 μm in ER2 mode, whereas SBU Plus presented values of approximately 2 μm in SE1 mode and 5 μm in ER2 mode. For CUQ, adhesive thickness was approximately 6 μm in SE1 mode and 5 μm in ER2 mode, whereas the values for CUQ 2 were approximately 3 μm in SE1 mode and 7 μm in ER2 mode. The three-step etch-and-rinse reference adhesive (OptiBond FL, ER3) exhibited the greatest measurable adhesive thickness of approximately 16 μm at ×2,000 magnification. Because the adhesive layer of AFB-00 applied without air blowing exceeded the measurable range at ×2,000 magnification, additional images were obtained at ×1,000 magnification (Fig. 2). At this lower magnification, representative sections revealed adhesive layers in the range of approximately 40–45 μm for both the SE1 and ER2 modes. Discussion

The working hypothesis was not confirmed, as recent commercial evolutions of the tested universal adhesives did not result in a consistent increase in dentin SBS after 6 months of water aging.

In addition to the comparison of successive commercial formulations, the present results also provide a framework to critically reflect on the directions currently employed in the development of universal adhesives and on the challenges that future evolutions will need to address to achieve more consistent and durable adhesion to dentin [1, 21]. The relevance of laboratory bond strength testing for predicting the clinical performance of dental adhesives remains debated, as does the question of which test configuration is the most clinically informative [22, 23]. Microtensile testing is often viewed as advantageous because it promotes a more favorable stress distribution [24, 25], whereas macroshear testing remains easier to implement and is therefore commonly considered more reproducible, even under controlled laboratory conditions [26, 27]. Regardless of the testing method, incorporating an aging protocol is widely regarded as essential. The first goal is to limit biases related to differences in setting kinetics and to the presence of different solvents in adhesive formulations [28], and the second goal is to improve the clinical relevance of in vitro bond strength outcomes [29]. However, even with aging, the correlation between laboratory results and clinical performance generally remains limited to, at best, moderate [29].

Studies that incorporate artificial aging, an interface analysis and a direct comparison of successive commercial formulations remain clinically and scientifically relevant, particularly because universal adhesives evolve through incremental changes that may affect solvent dynamics, polymer network formation, and ultimately hydrolytic stability; thus, the net effect on dentin bond durability cannot be inferred from product positioning alone [1, 30].

Importantly, the three evolutions examined reflect distinct development rationales. AFB-00 primarily aims to reduce the sensitivity of the technique by offering an application option that minimizes the dependence on immediate air drying, a step that is known to be critical for limiting the amount of residual solvent and water entrapment in simplified adhesive systems [12]. It also incorporates hydrophilic amide‑based monomers, a family to which acrylamides belong, which has been proposed as a promising strategy for increasing the long‑term stability of adhesive systems [31, 32]. It also adopts a low-solvent, water-free formulation strategy in which the interaction with dentin relies on the residual moisture within the substrate, an approach that had previously been proposed with one-step self-etch adhesives but was not widely adopted in routine clinical practice [33, 34]. Notably, for a given application mode (one-step self-etch or two-step etch-and-rinse), no significant differences were observed between G-Premio Bond and its newer version, AFB-00, whether the latter was applied with or without an air-drying step after 6 months of water storage. Higher SBS values were obtained when AFB‑00 was applied in the etch-and-rinse mode with air drying (24.91 MPa; Fig. 3; Table 3). However, these values remained statistically comparable to those of its predecessor GPB in the etch-and-rinse mode (22.41 MPa) and to those of the alternative AFB‑00 protocol performed without air drying (22.04 MPa). For both GPB and AFB‑00, compared with self-etch, etch-and-rinse resulted in significantly higher SBS, despite the marked difference in acidity between these formulations, as GPB can be classified as an intermediately strong universal adhesive (pH ≈ 1.5), whereas AFB‑00 is water-free and does not present a measurable pH (Table 1). Interestingly, this persistent advantage of phosphoric acid pre-etching on bonding to aged dentin is not uniformly reported for universal adhesives, as several reviews and meta-analyses have described comparable outcomes between bonding strategies and, for certain materials, a tendency toward better long-term stability when self-etch protocols are used [10, 35]. The present findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. In the present study, dentin surfaces were standardized by wet polishing with 800-grit silicon carbide paper. This preparation method was selected to obtain flat and reproducible bonding surfaces across all experimental groups, but it inevitably produced a smear layer whose characteristics may differ from those generated clinically by rotary instruments. The thickness, density, permeability, and composition of the smear layer are known to depend on the surface preparation method and abrasive grit, and these parameters may influence the interaction of self-etch adhesives with dentin [36–38]. This point is particularly relevant for universal adhesives applied in self-etch mode, as these systems do not remove the smear layer through a separate rinsing step but instead partially dissolve, modify, and incorporate it into the adhesive interface. Consequently, the standardized smear layer produced by 800-grit SiC polishing may have limited or modulated acidic monomer penetration into the underlying dentin, particularly for mild or ultra-mild universal adhesives, and may have partially contributed to the lower SBS values observed for some self-etch protocols compared with their corresponding etch-and-rinse protocols. However, because all groups were prepared using the same dentin surface standardization procedure, this factor is unlikely to invalidate the direct comparisons among materials and adhesive strategies within the present experimental design. Rather, it should be considered when extrapolating the present findings to clinical situations, where dentin is commonly prepared with burs and may present smear layers with different thickness, compactness, and permeability [36–38].

Graphical summary and comparison of shear bond strength across the various adhesive protocols tested

This consideration may also apply to the other universal adhesives discussed below. The absence of a significant difference between the two manufacturer-recommended AFB‑00 procedures further suggests that the updated formulation may reduce the sensitivity of the technique associated with solvent evaporation, which is known to influence polymerization and interfacial permeability in simplified adhesive systems [12, 39]. This simplified strategy, however, warrants further investigation. Previous experimental studies on water-free adhesive formulations have shown that eliminating water from the adhesive may shift the critical technique-sensitive step from solvent evaporation toward moisture control at the substrate level, particularly when such systems are used in etch-and-rinse mode [33, 34, 40]. In the present study, moisture-related factors were not specifically manipulated/quantified. Moreover, the use of extracted human teeth makes substrate hydration difficult to standardize, potentially contributing to variability [41]. Such a shift remains relevant to further investigation, given that the advent of universal adhesives has largely reduced the importance of strict wet-bonding conditions compared with earlier generations of adhesive systems [13, 42]. However, omitting the air-blowing step may have potential clinical implications that the present study was not designed to evaluate. Interface analyses clearly showed that the adhesive layer was substantially thicker when air drying was not performed, whereas air application resulted in an ultrathin adhesive film measuring approximately 1–2 μm in both SE1 and ER2 modes. Notably, this air-thinned film was even thinner than that observed for its predecessor GPB (approximately 7–8 μm in SE1 mode and 6–7 μm in ER2 mode) and thinner than that of CUQ 2 (approximately 3 μm in SE1 mode and 7 μm in ER2 mode), despite the latter emphasizing reduced film thickness as an improvement of the formulation. Although these thickness measurements were obtained from representative SEM images and were not subjected to statistical analysis, particularly as they were performed under laboratory rather than clinical conditions, they remain informative in the context of AFB-00. Nevertheless, the thicker adhesive film observed when air blowing was omitted raises questions regarding the clinical implications of this application protocol, and the absolute thickness values should be interpreted cautiously because they may depend on the amount of adhesive applied by the operator. Moreover, film thickness could potentially be modulated by variations in air-blow conditions, such as air pressure or duration, which were not investigated in the present study. Consequently, the measured thickness itself has limited standalone significance. Since no statistically significant SBS differences were observed between the protocols performed with or without air blowing, the clinical relevance of this thicker film remains uncertain and warrants further investigation [43, 44].

Clearfil Universal Bond Quick and its updated version, Clearfil Universal Bond Quick 2, align with the ongoing trend toward simplified universal adhesive systems designed to reduce the clinical application time and technique sensitivity. These goals are achieved not only through the implementation of so-called “no-waiting” application strategies [45–47] but also through the incorporation of hydrophilic amide-based monomers, similar to AFB-00. In addition, the manufacturer emphasizes chemical compatibility with the “touch-cure” mechanism [48] of the associated universal resin luting cement [5, 18]. However, the extent and clinical relevance of this interaction remain unclear in light of the limited and still emerging experimental evidence [18]. At the material level, compared with Clearfil Universal Bond Quick, Clearfil Universal Bond Quick 2 introduces urethane tetramethacrylate monomers and adds diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (TPO) as a cophotoinitiator in combination with camphorquinone [16]. The updated formulation can be regarded as a refinement of the original system, incorporating a camphorquinone/TPO photoinitiator combination that is expected to increase the light-curing efficiency [39], particularly when it is used with polywave LED units [49]. In parallel, a deliberate reduction in adhesive film thickness has been presented as a clinically oriented optimization objective. In the present 6-month aging dataset, Clearfil Universal Quick applied in self-etch mode yielded the lowest SBS among all the tested methods, with a mean value of 11.4 MPa (Fig. 3; Table 3). Compared with its predecessor, Clearfil Universal Quick 2 consistently exhibited higher mean SBS values in both bonding strategies, with mean values of 13.2 MPa in self-etch mode and 17.6 MPa in etch-and-rinse mode. However, for a given application mode, these predecessor–successor differences did not reach statistical significance. This observation is consistent with the overall trend observed across all the universal adhesives evaluated in this study, with higher bond strength values obtained using the etch-and-rinse protocol compared with the self-etch approach. Similar observations have recently been reported in a practice-based study for CUQ 2 [9]. SEM analysis did not clearly confirm a systematic reduction in adhesive film thickness between CUQ and CUQ 2: although CUQ 2 produced a thinner adhesive layer in SE1 mode, it generated a thicker layer in ER2 mode, preventing a consistent interpretation across bonding strategies. Nevertheless, despite the marketing emphasis placed on a reduced film thickness as an improvement in CUQ 2, other updated formulations evaluated in the present study, such as AFB-00 and SBU Plus, exhibited thinner adhesive layers under certain application protocols, although this feature was not specifically highlighted among their new clinical claims. More broadly, compared with the multistep reference OptiBond FL, which exhibited the greatest measurable adhesive thickness in the present dataset when observed at ×2,000 magnification (Fig. 1) (approximately 16 μm), these observations suggest that the trend toward thinner adhesive films may not be unique to a single system but rather reflects a broader evolution in contemporary universal adhesives. A thinner and more homogeneous film may be clinically relevant because it can improve the aesthetic integration of the adhesive joint between the resin composite and the tooth structure, particularly in anterior regions, while also helping to reduce pooling and preserve space for luting cements, thereby facilitating complete seating of restorations in indirect workflows when adhesive procedures are combined with appropriate air thinning and polymerization [43]. Moreover, the literature suggests that a thinner adhesive layer is not a trivial parameter, as it can negatively influence interfacial integrity and bond strength outcomes [44, 50–52]. Both an insufficient and excessive thickness may be detrimental depending on the material and application conditions [50–52]. With respect to universal adhesives, very thin films have been discussed as a potential limitation for long-term dentin bonding, which has motivated strategies aimed at improving the quality and sealing ability of the adhesive layer rather than assuming that “thinner is always better” [15]. Future studies should therefore generate controlled adhesive-layer thicknesses by standardizing adhesive volume and air-blowing parameters, and then correlate film thickness with degree of conversion, nanoleakage or interfacial permeability, and water sorption/solubility after aging. A complementary approach would be to compare the physicomechanical properties of isolated cured adhesive films with their aged bond strength on dentin, in order to determine whether an optimal thickness range exists for each formulation. Whether this progressive reduction in the adhesive layer thickness represents a true advance in interfacial optimization or primarily a handling-oriented adjustment remains to be clarified and warrants further dedicated investigations under clinically relevant conditions.

Scotchbond Universal Plus can be regarded as a chemistry-driven update of the original Scotchbond Universal adhesive rather than a purely protocol-driven simplification. The reformulated adhesive has been described as involving the replacement of Bis-GMA-containing cross-linking resin with a bisphenol-A-free derivative cross-linking resin [3], together with the incorporation of a built-in dual-cure activator and modifications to the silane component [30]. These changes in the formulation have also been presented as aiming to improve compatibility with “touch-cure” activation when it is used in combination with the associated universal resin luting cement [30]. However, as previously reported for other universal adhesive systems, including Clearfil Universal Quick, experimental evidence supporting a consistent benefit of this strategy remains limited [18, 53]. In contrast to a previous study [30], and in line with a large-scale practice-based study evaluating immediate SBS outcomes [4], Scotchbond Universal displayed significantly higher SBS than Scotchbond Universal Plus after six months of water storage, irrespective of the bonding strategy. This finding indicates that the chemical changes implemented in the formulation of Scotchbond Universal Plus did not translate into improved bonding to aged dentin under the present experimental conditions. Given the comparable acidity of the two materials (pH ≈ 2.7), differences in etching efficiency are unlikely to account for the observed discrepancies [10, 35]. A more cautious interpretation is that the lower SBS values observed for Scotchbond Universal Plus may be related to the combined effect of several formulation changes introduced in this material, including the replacement of the Bis-GMA-containing cross-linking resin, modifications to the silane component, and the incorporation of a built-in dual-cure activator [30]. Among these changes, the replacement of Bis-GMA may be relevant because Bis-GMA has traditionally been associated with the formation of relatively rigid and mechanically reinforced polymer networks capable of contributing to the mechanical integrity of the adhesive interface [54, 55]. Consequently, changes in the composition of the cross-linking resin may influence the mechanical behavior of the bonded interface and its resistance to stresses generated during aging. However, this interpretation should be regarded as only one plausible hypothesis among several, as other formulation modifications introduced in Scotchbond Universal Plus may also have contributed to the observed differences.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted under in vitro conditions using a macroshear bond strength test, which is known to induce a nonuniform stress distribution at the adhesive interface [24, 25], and the use of flat coronal dentin surfaces standardized by 800-grit silicon carbide polishing may have generated a relatively thick smear layer [36–38], which potentially influenced the interaction of self-etch adhesives with dentin. Moreover, aging was limited to six months of water storage and did not include thermomechanical cycling, enzymatic challenges, or pH fluctuations, which are known contributors to long-term interfacial degradation [56]. Interfacial parameters such as degree of conversion, water sorption, solubility, and permeability were not assessed. Adhesive layer thickness was estimated only descriptively on representative SEM sections and was therefore not statistically analyzed or correlated with the bond strength outcomes. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as indicators of short- to medium-term bonding behavior rather than as direct predictors of long-term clinical performance.

Overall, and within the limitations of laboratory studies, the present findings suggest that the commercial evolutions evaluated here mainly affected handling-related characteristics, formulation features, and interfacial morphology rather than producing a uniform improvement in aged dentin SBS [1, 21]. This distinction is clinically relevant because long-term dentin adhesion remains limited by incomplete resin infiltration, residual water/solvent, hydrolysis of hydrophilic polymer domains, and collagen degradation mediated by endogenous proteases [1, 21, 56]. However, except for Clearfil Universal Quick and Clearfil Universal Quick 2, which were applied in self-etch mode, the bond strength values of the aged dentin obtained for the tested universal adhesives were comparable or statistically superior to those measured for the multistep reference OptiBond FL (17.81 ± 1.82 MPa). These interesting findings should therefore be interpreted as indicating the substantial progress achieved by contemporary universal adhesives under short- to medium-term aging conditions rather than as evidence of superior or equivalent long-term performance compared with well-established multistep gold-standard systems [57].

Among the strategies that have effectively reached routine clinical formulations, 10‑MDP-based systems capable of forming stable MDP-Ca salts and interfacial nanolayering represent a key step toward more durable chemical coupling [58]. In contrast, approaches that most directly target the demineralized collagen matrix for protease inhibition, collagen cross-linking, a solvent-substitution/dry-bonding protocol, and biomimetic remineralization using ion-releasing particles or analogs remain largely research driven and have yet to be incorporated into simplified one-bottle systems without increasing complexity or relying on unproven long-term efficacy [1, 21].
