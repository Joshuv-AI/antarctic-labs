A Gaussian Comparison Theorem for Training Dynamics in Machine Learning
Ashkan Panahi

Abstract
We study training algorithms with data following a Gaussian mixture model. For a specific family of such algorithms, we present a non-asymptotic result, connecting the evolution of the model to a surrogate dynamical system, which can be easier to analyze. The proof of our result is based on the celebrated Gordon comparison theorem. Using our theorem, we rigorously prove the validity of the dynamic mean-field (DMF) expressions in the asymptotic scenarios. Moreover, we suggest an iterative refinement scheme to obtain more accurate expressions in non-asymptotic scenarios. We specialize our theory to the analysis of training a perceptron model with a generic first-order (full-batch) algorithm and demonstrate that fluctuation parameters in a non-asymptotic domain emerge in addition to the DMF kernels.



1
Introduction

A central problem in the theory of machine learning (ML) is to characterize the dynamics of training, i.e. to understand how the statistical properties of models evolve as training progresses. Solving this problem is proven to be a key to understanding the immense power of generalization in modern ML, such as in large language models and vision transformers
[
36
,
27
,
34
]
. However, the nonlinear structure of these models makes the dynamics complex and difficult to investigate, limiting the existing studies to significantly simpler setups. A successful remedy, in the recent years, has been to focus on asymptotically large models and dataset, where training dynamics often exhibit remarkably regular behavior despite the complexity of the training landscape
[
22
]
. Relying on the measure concentration phenomena, various high-dimensional theories predict a limit behavior, merely depending on the deterministic evolution of a small number of ”order parameters”, averaging the contribution of individual model parameters
[
5
,
14
]
. However, these theories are often specific to infinitely large setups and cannot be generalized to finite scenarios, where intricate fluctuations in the training dynamics may occur, due to the dependencies between the model parameters and the data. In many cases, convergence to the limit behavior is also mathematically unproven.

In this paper, we respond to the above shortcomings by presenting a novel analysis of a wide family of training algorithms over datasets with a Gaussian mixture distribution (see Section
2
). This approach has strong implications in finite dimensions and also leads to mathematically provable characterization of the limit behavior. Our method is linked to Gordon’s comparison theorem
[
15
]
, underlying the celebrated convex Gaussian min-max theorem (CGMT) used in the analysis of convex ML models
[
33
,
32
]
. Unlike CGMT, we employ this result to study the dynamics of training, addressing even nonconvex optimization problems. Specifically, we offer the following contributions:

1.

We present a non-asymptotic theorem (Theorem
1
) establishing a correspondence between two groups of stochastic dynamical systems, where corresponding instances have identical probability distributions. One of these systems, called the original dynamics, is a perturbation of the desired training procedure, while the other one, called the alternative dynamics is simpler to analyze, especially in the asymptotic scenarios, and can be used as a surrogate to investigate the dynamics of training.

2.

We prove our comparison theorem by framing the underlying stochastic dynamical systems as zeros of abstract Gaussian processes. Then, we prove an extension of Gordon’s comparison theorem for analyzing such zeros, leading to the desired result. In Appendix
D
, we present a more general version of our result for the analysis of the zeros of generic Gaussian processes, which do not necessarily correspond to a dynamical system.

3.

We specialize our comparison theorem to an asymptotic scenario, where the model size
n
and the number of samples
m
grow to infinity, and the perturbation terms in the original dynamics vanish. In this scenario, we calculate the limit of the alternative process, which recovers the classical asymptotic expressions using the dynamic cavity method and dynamic mean field (DMF) theory. In this way, we rigorously prove the validity of DMF expressions in the scenarios of interest (Theorem
2
).

4.

We further discuss how the perturbations in the original dynamics may be eliminated even in finite dimensions. We formulate our discussion as a claim (Claim
1
), the proof of which is differed to a future study. Under this claim, we present an iterative process, in the form of a fixed-point iteration, refining the asymptotic DMF expressions in the finite-dimensional cases (Algorithm
1
).

5.

We showcase our analysis in a scenario, involving training a perceptron model with a generic activation function. While previous studies typically consider a regression setup with a (perturbed) linear teacher model, we consider a classification scenario aligned with the mixture model of the data. Moreover, we consider a generic linear, first order (full-batch) optimization algorithm, encompassing various familiar methods such as momentum and acceleration, beyond the standard gradient descent algorithm. We calculate the asymptotic DMF dynamics, which depends on a single-dimensional
characteristic dynamics
, and a refinement, illustrating the emergence of fluctuation factors in finite dimensions leading to correction terms in the dynamics.

Problem formulation

2.1
Data Model

Consider a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
, consisting of statistically independent pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
of samples
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and corresponding class labels
y_{i}
belonging to a finite set
\mathcal{Y}
with cardinality
d
. We assume that these samples follow a
Gaussian mixture model
where the samples
x_{i}
are Gaussian when conditioned on a latent variable
\zeta_{i}
belonging to a finite set
\mathcal{Z}
with cardinality
d_{z}
, and there is a deterministic map
\zeta_{i}\to y_{i}=y(\zeta_{i})
. This model allows multiple Gaussian components to be associated with a class
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Accordingly, we define

\displaystyle\hat{x}(\zeta):=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\mid\zeta_{i}=\zeta\right],\quad R(\zeta):=\text{Cov}\left[x_{i}\mid\zeta_{i}=\zeta\right].

(1)

2.2
Training Algorithms

We consider a generic ML problem of fitting to the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, a model represented by a matrix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times J}
with
J
sub-models
\theta_{j}
for
j\in[J]
as columns. For this task, we focus on a family of sequential algorithms that calculate, at each step
l\in[L]
, a
query pair

(\theta(l),\omega(l))
, consisting of a model estimate
\theta(l)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times J}
with
J
sub-models
\theta(j,l)
for
j\in[J]
as columns, and a corresponding dual matrix
\omega(l)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times J}
. Within this family of algorithms, the query pair is utilized to calculate a
response pair

\left(q(l),p(l)\right)
with columns
q(j,l),p(j,l)
for
j\in[J]
, respectively of the following form:

\displaystyle q(l)=\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{X}\omega(l),\quad p(l)=\mathbf{X}^{T}\theta(l),

(2)

where
\mathbf{X}
is a matrix with
\{x_{i}\}
as its columns. We denote by
\hat{\mathbf{X}},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
matrices with
\hat{x}(\zeta_{i}),x_{i}-\hat{x}(\zeta_{i})
as their columns, respectively.
In return, each query pair is calculated by the previously observed response pairs using the following differentiable maps for
l\in[L]
:

\displaystyle\theta(l)=\theta_{l}\left(q^{l-1},p^{l-1};\mathbf{y}\right),\quad\omega(l)=\omega_{l}\left(p^{l},q^{l-1};\mathbf{y}\right),

(3)

where
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{i})
denotes the vector of all labels and
q^{l}:=\left\{q(l^{\prime})\right\}_{l^{\prime}=0}^{l},\ p^{l}:=\left\{p(l^{\prime})\right\}_{l^{\prime}=0}^{l-1}
are the collection of response pairs observed up to (and including) time
l
. Note that the initialization
\theta_{0}
may only depend on
\mathbf{y}
and the relations in (
3
) and (
2
) lead to a sequential procedure with the order
\ldots\to\theta(l)\to p(l)\to\omega(l)\to q(l)\to\theta(l+1)\to\ldots
. For simplicity, we introduce
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}
as row block matrices with
q(l),p(l)
in their
l^{\text{th}}
block, respectively.
Similarly, we denote by
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}),\bm{\Omega}=\bm{\Omega}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})
row block matrices with
\theta(l),\omega(l)
in their
l^{\text{th}}
block, respectively. Then, (
2
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{Q}\\
\mathbf{P}\end{array}\right]}_{\xi}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{X}\bm{\Omega}\\
\mathbf{X}^{T}\bm{\Theta}\end{array}\right]\to\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{m}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Omega}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{\Theta}\end{array}\right]}_{\phi(\xi)}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{m}\hat{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Omega}-\mathbf{Q}\\
\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\bm{\Theta}-\mathbf{P}\end{array}\right]}_{\rho_{0}(\xi)}=0,

which frames the dynamics as a zero of a vector (block matrix)-valued Gaussian process on the space of block matrices
\xi
. This interpretation is a key to our main result. We denote the space of the block matrices
\xi
by
\mathcal{B}
. Hence, we have
\phi,\rho_{0}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
. Our goal is to characterize the statistical properties of the solution of (
3
) and (
2
), or equivalently the zero point of
\phi(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)
in (
2.2
), which we denote by
\xi_{\phi}
.

3
Main Result

We show that the distribution of
\xi_{\phi}
can be characterized by analyzing the zero point of an alternative process
\psi(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)
, given bellow.

3.1
Alternative Process

Our alternative process

\psi:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
is defined based on additional constants
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
and the definition of the following
J\times J
overlap matrices:

\displaystyle V_{\theta}(l,l^{\prime};\zeta):=\theta^{T}(l)R(\zeta)\theta(l^{\prime})+\sigma^{2}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\mathbf{I},\quad V_{\omega}(l,l^{\prime};\zeta):=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i\mid\zeta_{i}=\zeta}\omega_{i}(l)\omega^{T}_{i}(l^{\prime})+\sigma^{2}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\mathbf{I},

(13)

where throughout the paper,

\mathbf{I}
denotes an identity matrix of a suitable size and

\omega_{i}(l)
for
i\in[m]
is the
i^{\text{th}}
column of
\omega^{T}(l)
. Note that our definitions generally depend on the point
\xi
and
\sigma,z
, but for simplicity, this dependency is neglected in the notation. We denote by
V_{\theta}(y),V_{\omega}(y)
the block matrices with
V_{\theta}(l,l^{\prime};y),V_{\omega}(l,l^{\prime};y)
as their blocks, respectively. These matrices can also be written as

\displaystyle V_{\theta}(\zeta)=\bm{\Theta}^{T}R(y)\bm{\Theta}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I},\quad V_{\omega}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{m}\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}^{T}\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I},

(14)

where throughout the paper we use the notation
\mathbf{E}_{\zeta}
for any matrix
\mathbf{E}
to denote its sub-matrix corresponding to the the rows
i
with
\zeta_{i}=\zeta
(in a predefined order). Next, we define the upper triangular block matrices
A_{\theta}(\zeta)=\left(a_{\theta}(l^{\prime},l;\zeta)\right)
and
A_{\omega}=\left(a_{\omega}(l^{\prime},l;\zeta)\right)
, where
a_{\theta}(l^{\prime},l;\zeta),a_{\omega}(l^{\prime},l;\zeta)
are
J\times J
blocks of
A_{\theta}(\zeta)
and
A_{\omega}(\zeta)
, respectively. These matrices correspond to a Cholesky-type decomposition of
V_{\theta}(\zeta),V_{\omega}(\zeta)
, respectively, which means that
a_{\theta}(\mu,l;\zeta)=a_{\omega}(\mu,l;\zeta)=0
for
\mu>l
and we have

\displaystyle V_{\theta}(l_{1},l_{2};\zeta)=\sum\limits_{\mu}a^{T}_{\theta}(\mu,l_{1};\zeta)a_{\theta}(\mu,l_{2};\zeta),\quad V_{\omega}(l_{1},l_{2};\zeta)=\sum\limits_{\mu}a^{T}_{\omega}(\mu,l_{1};\zeta)a_{\omega}(\mu,l_{2};\zeta).

(15)

Since for
\sigma>0
the matrices
V_{\theta}(\zeta),V_{\omega}(\zeta)
are positive-definite,
A_{\omega}(\zeta),A_{\theta}(\zeta)
are invertible and can be selected to be differentiable with respect to
\bm{\Theta},\bm{\Omega}
. However, they are not unique and our results are invariant to their choice. For example, one might focus on a choice with symmetric positive definite diagonal blocks, providing a unique definition.
According to these definitions, we introduce the alternative process as follows:

\displaystyle\psi(\xi):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}(\zeta)\left(\frac{\mathbf{G}_{\zeta}}{\sqrt{m}}\right)A_{\omega}(\zeta)+\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}R(\zeta)\bm{\Theta}A^{-1}_{\theta}(\zeta)\left(\frac{\mathbf{H}_{\zeta}^{T}\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}}{m}+\frac{\sigma\mathbf{W}_{\zeta}+z\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}A_{\omega}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{m}}\right)_{\text{u}}\\
\left[\mathbf{H}_{\zeta}A_{\theta}(\zeta)+\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}A^{-1}_{\omega}(\zeta)\left(\frac{\mathbf{G}_{\zeta}^{T}R^{\frac{1}{2}}(\zeta)\bm{\Theta}+\sigma\mathbf{W}_{\zeta}^{T}+z\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}^{T}A_{\theta}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{m}}\right)_{\text{U}}\right]_{\zeta_{i}=\zeta}\end{array}\right],

where
[\mathbf{E}_{\zeta}]_{\zeta_{i}=\zeta}
for any collection of matrices
\{\mathbf{E}_{\zeta}\}_{\zeta}
, denotes a matrix with the sub-matrix
\mathbf{E}_{\zeta}
at the position of the rows
i
with
\zeta_{i}=\zeta
(with the same order as in
\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}
). For example, we see that
\bm{\Omega}=[\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}]_{\zeta_{i}=\zeta}
. Moreover, for each
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
, the matrices
\mathbf{G}_{\zeta},\mathbf{H}_{\zeta},\mathbf{W}_{\zeta}
and
\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}
are random with independent, standard Gaussian entries, where
\mathbf{G}_{\zeta},\mathbf{H}_{\zeta}
have the same dimensions as
\bm{\Theta},\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}
, respectively and
\mathbf{W}_{\zeta},\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}
are
LJ\times LJ
. These matrices can be treated as block matrices agreeing with the block structure of
A_{\theta}(y),A_{\omega}(y)
. Finally,
(\ldotp)_{\text{u}}
and
(\ldotp)_{\text{U}}
denote the block
upper-triangular (including the diagonal blocks) and strictly
block upper triangular
(excluding the diagonal blocks)
parts, respectively.

3.2
Main Theorem

Our main theorem does not directly address
\phi
, but a perturbation of it, given by

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(\xi):=\phi(\xi)+\sigma\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}(\zeta)\mathbf{U}_{\zeta}\\
\mathbf{V}\end{array}\right]+\sqrt{\frac{1+z^{2}}{m}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}R(\zeta)\bm{\Theta}A_{\theta}^{-1}(\zeta)\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}A_{\omega}(\zeta)\right)_{\mathrm{u}}\\
\left[\bm{\Omega}_{\zeta}A_{\omega}^{-1}(\zeta)\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{\zeta}^{T}A_{\theta}(\zeta)\right)_{\text{U}}\right]_{\zeta_{i}=\zeta}\end{array}\right],

where
\mathbf{U}_{\zeta}
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathbf{V}
consist of independent, standard Gaussian entries. Despite the complex structure of
\psi
in (
3.1
) and the additional terms in (
3.2
), it is seen that the equations
\psi(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)=0
and
\phi^{\prime}(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)=0
both correspond to sequential procedures with the same order
\ldots\to\theta(l)\to p(l)\to\omega(l)\to q(l)\to\theta(l+1)\to\ldots
as the original problem
\phi(\xi)+\rho_{0}(\xi)=0
. Hence, these equations have unique solutions that we denoted by
\xi_{\psi}
and
\xi^{\prime}_{\phi}
, respectively. Then, we have the following result:

Theorem 1
.

For every
\sigma>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the solutions
\xi_{\psi}
and
\xi^{\prime}_{\phi}
have identical distributions. In other words, for any measurable function
h:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\xi_{\psi})]=\mathbb{E}[h(\xi^{\prime}_{\phi})].

(24)

When
m,n\to\infty
, the terms related to
z
vanish and we may also let
\sigma\to 0
to achieve
\phi^{\prime}\to\phi
. This approach leads to a rigorous proof of the dynamic mean field (DMF) approximation as we discuss in section
4.1
. However, we observe that the terms related to
\sigma,z
may also be eliminated in finite dimensions, which we discuss next.

3.3
Claim: Elimination of
\sigma,z

In finite dimensions, applying Theorem
1
to the analysis of the original dynamics (with
\phi
and not
\phi^{\prime}
) entails eliminating the additional terms in
\phi^{\prime}
. We note that this situation occurs when
\sigma=0
and
z^{2}=-1
. Although, this case is not well-defined within our framework, we claim that the result can be extended to this case in the following way:

Claim 1
.

For a test function
h:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, denote by
H(\sigma,z)
the value of
\mathbb{E}[h(\xi_{\psi})]
for different values of
\sigma>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
H(\sigma,z)
can be analytically extended to a region of complex plain for
z
, including
z=\sqrt{-1}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\xi_{\phi})]=\lim\limits_{\sigma\to 0}H\left(\sigma,z=\sqrt{-1}\right),

(25)

provided that the limit exists.

Note that
H(\sigma,z)
is an even function of
z
on the real line and therefore lacks odd terms in its Taylor expansion at
z=0
. As a result, it remains real-valued when extended to
z^{2}=-1
. We do not prove Claim
1
, but use this approach for obtaining expressions in our case study, i.e. obtain expressions for arbitrary
z,\sigma
and let
\sigma\to 0
and
z^{2}=-1
.

Literature review

Asymptotic Regimes

The theoretical understanding of training dynamics has emerged as a major direction in modern machine learning research. Despite limited studies in finite dimensions and through the lens of dynamical systems

[
29
,
35
,
24
,
31
]
, a major breakthrough came only from the study of large models in asymptotic limits
[
1
]
. Most notably, the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory analyzes networks in learning scales, where parameters remain close to initialization

[
17
,
4
,
19
,
10
]

, allowing a simple description of the training dynamics by data kernels. As such,
NTK primarily models a “lazy training” regime with limited feature evolution
[
2
,
16
,
20
]
.
A natural extension of NTK is in
the mean-field regime, where training can be described as the evolution of a probability measure over parameters

[
22
,
21
,
11
,
28
]
, better capturing feature learning and nonlinear parameter movement. A great advantage of this regime is that it admits statistical physics approaches. Relying on earlier parallels between artificial NNs and physics, particularly spin glass theory

[
3
,
18
,
26
]
, dynamical extensions, such as state evolution and dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) have gained a lot of interest and
provide recursive equations governed by a few order parameters for macroscopic observables during learning

[
14
,
23
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
13
]

. Although these expressions often match the experimental results, many of them remain mathematically unproven and cannot be generalized to finite regimes.

Gaussian Comparison

A more recent rigorous direction leverages Gaussian comparison principles, such as Slepian’s lemma
[
30
]
, Gordon’s min–max theorem
[
15
]
, and related Gaussian process inequalities
[
33
]
. These tools allow complex random optimization problems to be compared with simpler Gaussian surrogates, especially combined with high-dimensional probability results, such as universality
[
32
,
25
,
9
]
. However, Gaussian comparison is limited to the global solutions of convex optimization problems. Our paper generalizes this methodology to the analysis of dynamical systems.

Concluding remarks

In Theorem
1
, we establish a deep connection between the dynamics of training and an alternative process based on time kernels
A_{\omega},A_{\theta},B_{\omega},B_{\theta}
, depending on the solution overlaps in
V_{\omega},V_{\theta}
, as well as the bias terms
e,\beta
. This relation particularly proves the validity of the DMF limit, but more generally, shows that a similar relation holds in finite dimensions, but with random kernels. Studying finite dimensions requires eliminating the perturbation in
\phi^{\prime}
to arrive at
\phi
, which we
conjecture in Claim
1
.
This is the basis of our approximation scheme in Algorithm

1
. In this scheme, we expect after
r
iterations, the accuracy of the approximation to be improved with
O(1/m^{r})
.
The refined analysis in Section
6.2
agrees with this intuition as the correction terms are centered and of
O(1/\sqrt{m})
, which have only an effect of
O(1/m)
in the statistics. In the example studied here, the correction may have a negligible effect, but when the size of kernels
A_{\ldotp}
grow (e.g by considering
J=O(m)
or SGD scenarios where
L=O(m)
), then higher order terms become dominant and DMFT may fail. This phenomenon is an interesting direction of future study. Finally, we note that
our theorem may exhibit a universal behavior beyond Gaussian mixtures.

\mathcal{H}
of bounded
(0,2)-
tensors on
V

such that
D^{2}f\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\psi)g\circ\gamma_{1}^{-1}(\phi,\psi)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(\phi)g(\phi,\psi)\right]+\frac{c}{2}.

(88)

Proof.

See section

.
∎

Our goal is to replace
g
with the indicator function of a suitable set, from which
\phi,\psi
have little probability to escape. In this way, we achieve approximate relations between
\mathbb{E}f(\psi)
and
\mathbb{E}f(\phi)
. The next results describes a suitable example, but to express it, we need few more definitions. First, we make the following definition for any linear subspace
W
of the continuous dual space
V^{*}
of
V
:

\displaystyle\lambda(W)=\max\left\{\sup\limits_{u\in W\mid\|u\|_{*}\leq 1}\left\|\mathbb{E}[\braket{u,\phi}\phi]\right\|,\sup\limits_{u\in W\mid\|u\|_{*}\leq 1}\left\|\mathbb{E}[\braket{u,\psi}\psi]\right\|\right\},

(89)

where
\|\ldotp\|_{*}
denotes the dual norm of
\|\ldotp\|
. We also define

\displaystyle\tilde{T}:=\bigcap\limits_{t\in[0\ 1]}T_{t}

(90)

Then, we introduce the following notation:

Definition 5
.

For any subset
K
of a normed space and any
\epsilon>0
, we denote by
K_{\epsilon}
the covering of
K
by open
\epsilon-
balls. Further, we denote by
K_{-\epsilon}
the union of all sets
K^{\prime}
satisfying
K^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\subseteq K
. We take
K_{0}:=K
.

Accordingly, we introduce the following definition:

Definition 6
.

We say that a Borel set
T\subseteq V\times V
is
expanded
if there exists a family
\{g_{n}:V\times V\to[0\ 1]\}_{n}
of continuously differentiable functions with bounded derivatives, supported on
T
and converging pointwise to the characteristic function
\chi_{T}
of
T
.

Then, we have the following:

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that the conditions of Theorem
5
hold true. Moreover,
Df
belongs to a subspace
W
of continuous dual vectors and
\|D\log f\|_{*}<\beta
for a constant
\beta
. Take an expanded measurable set
T\subseteq\tilde{K}_{-\lambda(W)\beta}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\psi)\chi_{T}(\phi,\psi)]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(\phi)\chi_{T_{\lambda(W)\beta}}(\phi,\psi)]+\frac{c}{2}

(91)

Proof.

First, we note that the components of
v(\phi,\psi,t)
are bounded by
\frac{\beta\lambda}{\sqrt{t}},\frac{\beta\lambda}{\sqrt{1-t}}
, respectively. This shows that
\gamma^{-1}_{t}(T)\subseteq T_{\lambda\beta}\subseteq\tilde{K}
and hence
\gamma^{-1}_{1}(T)\subseteq S
. This observation allows us to apply Theorem
5
to
f,g_{n}\circ\gamma_{1}
and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\psi)g_{n}(\phi,\psi)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(\phi)g_{n}\circ\gamma_{1}(\phi,\psi)\right]+\frac{c}{2}

(92)

Letting
n\to\infty
and using dominated convergence theorem leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\psi)\chi_{T}(\phi,\psi)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(\phi)\chi_{\gamma^{-1}_{1}(T)}(\phi,\psi)\right]+\frac{c}{2}

(93)

We conclude the result by noting that
\gamma^{-1}_{1}(T)\subseteq T_{\lambda\beta}
and
\chi_{\gamma_{1}^{-1}(T)}\leq\chi_{T_{\lambda\beta}}
.
∎

D.1
Proof of Theorem
5

We consider the same interpolation
\rho_{t}:=\sqrt{t}\phi+\sqrt{1-t}\psi
as in Section
A
.
We define
g_{t}=g\circ\gamma_{t}^{-1}
on
S_{t}
and
g_{t}=0
, otherwise. Note that
g_{t}
is differentiable everywhere and is supported on
S_{t}
. We note that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(g_{t}\circ\gamma_{t})=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(g)=0
. Hence,

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial t}(\phi,\psi)+Dg_{t}(\phi,\psi)[v(\phi,\psi)]=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(\phi,\psi)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}D_{\phi}g(\phi,\psi)[\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\prime}}[\phi^{\prime}D\log f(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime}]]]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}D_{\psi}g(\phi,\psi)[\mathbb{E}_{\psi^{\prime}}[\psi^{\prime}D\log f(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime}]]]=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(\phi,\psi)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\prime}}[D_{\phi}g(\phi,\psi)[\phi^{\prime}]D\log f(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime}]]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\mathbb{E}_{\psi^{\prime}}[D_{\psi}g(\phi,\psi)[\psi^{\prime}]D\log f(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime}]]

(94)

Multiplying by
f(\rho_{t})
, we may write this relation as

\displaystyle 0=f(\rho_{t})\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial t}(\phi,\psi)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\prime}}[D_{\phi}g(\phi,\psi)[\phi^{\prime}]Df(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime}]]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\mathbb{E}_{\psi^{\prime}}[D_{\psi}g(\phi,\psi)[\psi^{\prime}]Df(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime}]]

(95)

Now, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\psi)g_{1}(\phi,\psi)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\phi)g_{0}(\phi,\psi)\right]=\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[f(\rho_{t})g_{t}(\phi,\psi)\right]{\text{d}}t=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left[\mathbb{E}f(\rho_{t})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{t}(\phi,\psi)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{E}g_{t}(\phi,\psi)Df(\rho_{t})[\phi]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\mathbb{E}g_{t}(\phi,\psi)Df(\rho_{t})[\psi]\right]{\text{d}}t

(96)

Invoking Stein’s lemma for
v(\phi)=g_{t}(\phi,\psi)Df(\sqrt{t}\phi+\sqrt{1-t}\psi)
(fixing
t
and
\psi
) and
v(\psi)=g_{t}(\phi,\psi)Df(\sqrt{t}\phi+\sqrt{1-t}\psi)
(fixing
t
and
\phi
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\psi)g_{1}(\phi,\psi)]-\mathbb{E}[f(\phi)g_{0}(\phi,\psi)]=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f(\rho_{t})\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial t}(\phi,\psi)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\prime}}[D_{\phi}g(\phi,\psi)[\phi^{\prime}]Df(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime}]]-\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\mathbb{E}_{\psi^{\prime}}[D_{\psi}g(\phi,\psi)[\psi^{\prime}]Df(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime}]]\end{array}\right]{\text{d}}t+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}(\phi,\psi)D^{2}f(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}]-g_{t}(\phi,\psi)D^{2}f(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}]\right]{\text{d}}t

(100)

According to (

), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\psi)g_{1}(\phi,\psi)]-\mathbb{E}[f(\phi)g_{0}(\phi,\psi)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t}(\phi,\psi)D^{2}f(\rho_{t})[\psi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}]-g_{t}(\phi,\psi)D^{2}f(\rho_{t})[\phi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}]\right]{\text{d}}t

D.2
Application to Analysis of Zero Points

Our next result shows how Corollary
1
can be used for the analysis of zero points of Gaussian processes. We follow the discussion in section
5.2
by selecting
T
to correspond to a suitable subset of functions on
K_{U}
, for which a unique zero point
\hat{\xi}(\rho)
of
\rho+\rho_{0}
exists and we take
f(\rho)=h\circ\hat{\xi}(\rho+\rho_{0})
as a test function
h
applied to this zero point. For this reason, we introduce few definitions:

Definition 7
.

For any open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
K_{U,\beta}
the set of all functions
\rho
on
V
with a unique zero point
\xi
on
U
, such that
D^{2}\rho(\xi)
is bounded by
\beta
in operator norm, and
D\rho(\xi)
is bijective and its inverse is bounded by
\beta
in operator norm.

Definition 8
.

We denote by
T_{U,\epsilon,\beta}
the set of all pairs of functions
(\phi,\psi)\in V\times V
such that for all
t\in[0\ 1]
and
\rho\in V
with
\|\rho\|<\epsilon
, it holds that
\sqrt{t}\phi+\sqrt{1-t}\psi+\rho+\rho_{0}\in K_{U,\beta}
. In other words, for all
t\in[0\ 1]
it holds that
\sqrt{t}\phi+\sqrt{1-t}\psi+\rho_{0}\in(K_{U,\beta})_{-\epsilon}
.

Now, we consider two centered Gaussian processes
\phi,\psi
on
U
, valued in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we define the following.

Definition 9
.

For every point
\xi\in U
, we define the covariance kernel functions
\Phi_{\xi},\Psi_{\xi}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as follows

\displaystyle\Phi_{\xi}(\xi^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}[\phi(\xi^{\prime})\phi^{T}(\xi)],\quad\Psi_{\xi}(\xi^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\xi^{\prime})\psi^{T}(\xi)].

(102)

Finally, we define the following:

Definition 10
.

We say that
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-1\ 1]
is a regular test function if
Dh,D^{2}h
are bounded by 1 in operator norm and there exists a point
\xi\in U
, where
h(\xi)=0
.

Now, we may restate Corollary
1
for zeros of functions:

Corollary 2
.

Assume the following:

1.

There exists a constant
\lambda
such that
\Phi_{\xi},\Psi_{\xi}
and their two first derivatives are bounded by
\lambda
for every
\xi\in U
.

2.

There exist a subspace of operators
\mathcal{J}
and constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that for any point
\xi\in U
and any
t\in[0,1]
, the inverese of

the processes satisfy:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}[\phi(\xi)\phi^{T}(\xi)]-\mathbb{E}[\psi(\xi)\psi^{T}(\xi)]\right\|_{*}<c_{1},

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{M\mid\|M\|\leq 1}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi}(\xi)[M\psi(\xi)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\xi}(\xi)[M\psi(\xi)]\right]\right\|<c_{2},

(103)

where
\|\ldotp\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm, i.e. the dual of the matrix operator norm. Take an expanded set
T\subseteq T_{U,\sqrt{2}\lambda\beta,\beta}
. Then, there exists a pair of random vectors
\left(\xi_{\phi},\xi_{\psi}\right)
which are the zero points of
\phi+\rho_{0},\psi+\rho_{0}
, respectively, with a probability higher than
\Pr\left[(\phi,\psi)\in T\right]
and the following holds true for every regular test function
h
:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\phi}\right)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\psi}\right)}\right]\right|\leq{c_{1}(\beta^{3}+\beta^{2})+c_{2}\beta^{2}}+\Pr\left[(\phi,\psi)\notin T\right]

(104)

Proof.

We define a pair
\left(\xi_{\phi},\xi_{\psi}\right)
of random vectors as follows. For
(\phi,\psi)\in T
, define
\xi_{\phi},\xi_{\psi}
as the unique zero points of
\phi+\rho_{0},\psi+\rho_{0}
, respectively. Otherwise, take
\xi_{\phi}=\xi_{\psi}=\xi_{0}
for a constant point
\xi_{0}\in U^{*}
, satisfying
h(\xi_{0})=0
. Further, we define the function
\hat{\xi}(\rho)
for
\rho\in K_{U}
as the unique zero point of
\rho
on
U
and define
f(\rho)=e^{h(\hat{\xi}(\rho+\rho_{0}))}
on
K_{U,\beta}
.
Now, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\phi}\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\phi}\right)}\chi_{T}(\phi,\psi)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\phi}\right)}\chi_{T^{c}}(\phi,\psi)\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h(\hat{\xi}(\phi+\rho_{0}))}\chi_{T_{1}}(\phi,\psi)\right]+\Pr\left(T_{1}^{c}\right)

(105)

Next, note that
Df(\xi)[\psi]
is always in the form of
a^{T}\psi(\xi)
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and some evaluation point
\xi
, which allows us to take
W
as the set of all such operators (of
\psi
). Then, it is straightforward to see that
\lambda(W)\leq\lambda
. On the other hand, we have
D\log f(\rho)
is bounded by
\beta
.
Moreover, according to proposition
1
on
K_{U,\beta}
, the constant
c
in corollary
1
is bounded by
c_{1}(\beta^{2}+\beta^{3})+c_{2}\beta^{2}
. Finally, we note that
T_{\lambda\beta}\subseteq T_{U,0,\beta}=\tilde{K}_{U,\beta}
.
These observations allow us to invoke corollary
1
to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\phi}\right)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h(\hat{\xi}(\psi))}\chi_{T}(\phi,\psi)\right]+{c_{1}(\beta^{3}+\beta^{2})+c_{2}\beta^{2}}+\Pr\left(T^{c}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{h\left(\xi_{\psi}\right)}\right]+{c_{1}(\beta^{3}+\beta^{2})+c_{2}\beta^{2}}+\Pr\left(T^{c}\right)

(106)

Switching
\phi,\psi
and repeating the argument gives the result.
∎
