Trabeculotomy outcomes in paediatric patients with steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma: a case series and literature review
Authors: Mathilde Mussmann von Arenstorff, Line Kessel, Marie Louise Roed Rasmussen, Per Riise, Daniella Bach-Holm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study is to assess the 24-month outcomes of trabeculotomy in paediatric patients with steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma who did not achieve adequate intraocular pressure (IOP) control under maximal medical therapy.MethodsA retrospective case series was conducted at Copenhagen University Hospital-Rigshospitalet between February 2018 and January 2022. 13 eyes of eight consecutive paediatric patients (aged 7–17 years) undergoing trabeculotomy for steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma were included. IOP, the number of pressure-lowering medications (PLMs) used, steroid exposure, visual acuity and postoperative complications were documented over a 24-month follow-up. Complete success was defined as achieving IOP of ≤21 mm Hg without the need for PLMs.ResultsMean preoperative IOP was 29.7 mm Hg despite intensive use of PLMs. Postoperatively, mean IOP decreased significantly, remaining below 16 mm Hg at all postoperative follow-ups with a mean IOP of 15.1 mm Hg at 24 months. All eyes were off PLMs postoperatively, and complete success was achieved in all eyes through 24 months with no postoperative complications. Visual acuity showed slight improvement, though this was not statistically significant. Despite continued corticosteroid use for underlying conditions, no IOP elevation recurred.ConclusionTrabeculotomy is a safe and effective surgical option for paediatric steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma, ensuring long-term IOP control without the need for further medication or surgical intervention. This procedure should be considered early in paediatric steroid responders when medical therapy fails to manage IOP adequately, minimising the risk of steroid-induced glaucoma.Keywords: Child health (paediatrics), Glaucoma, Inflammation, Ocular Hypertension, Ophthalmologic Surgical Procedures

Introduction

Corticosteroid therapy is widely used to treat ocular and systemic inflammatory disorders due to its potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties.1 However, steroids, whether administered topically, intravitreally, periocularly or systemically, can cause significant ocular side effects, including cataract formation and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP).2,5 The latter, also known as steroid-induced ocular hypertension or steroid response, can progress to glaucoma if untreated or undertreated, potentially leading to optic nerve damage, visual field loss and irreversible blindness. Although definitions have evolved, steroid-induced ocular hypertension is generally defined as an IOP increase of more than 10 mm Hg from baseline.6

The pathogenesis of steroid-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma is complex, involving morphological and biochemical changes in the microstructure of the trabecular meshwork (TM). In vitro studies of human TM tissue have shown that these changes include extracellular matrix deposition,7 protease inhibition8 and reduced endothelial phagocytosis9 within the TM. Together, these alterations increase resistance to aqueous outflow and elevate IOP.6 Paediatric patients are more likely to have steroid-induced ocular hypertension compared with adults.10 The cause is unknown, but it may be due to a stronger TM remodelling response to the medicine. Kwok et al studied topical corticosteroid use in children under the age of 10 and discovered that 56% were ‘high responders’ to topical dexamethasone with a peak IOP observed 8 days after starting treatment.11

Initial management of steroid-induced ocular hypertension typically begins with IOP-lowering medications. However, when pharmacological therapy is insufficient, surgical intervention becomes necessary. Among the IOP-lowering surgical techniques available, trabeculotomy is an option. Trabeculotomy, a procedure traditionally used to treat primary congenital glaucoma, creates an opening in the TM to enhance aqueous outflow, thereby reducing IOP. While trabeculotomy is well established in congenital glaucoma, its application in paediatric steroid responders is relatively novel, with limited literature documenting its outcomes.

At our institution, trabeculotomy has been adopted to manage paediatric steroid-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma. This study presents a case series of eight paediatric patients who underwent trabeculotomy for this condition, supplemented by a review of recent literature. Our findings demonstrate that trabeculotomy provides sufficient and long-term IOP control, highlighting its role as an effective surgical intervention for paediatric steroid responders.

Methods

Study participants

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records of paediatric patients who underwent trabeculotomy for steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma at the Department of Ophthalmology, Copenhagen University Hospital-Rigshospitalet between 1 February 2018 and 31 January 2022.

Inclusion criteria required paediatric patients (under 18 years of age) diagnosed with steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma who required surgical intervention due to inadequate IOP control despite maximal medical therapy. Only patients who were actively receiving corticosteroid therapy at the time of surgery were included, regardless of the route of administration (topical, supratarsal injection, subtenon injection or systemic). Patients with a history of previous glaucoma surgery or other forms of glaucoma unrelated to steroid use were excluded.

Data collected from patient records included demographics, clinical diagnosis, preoperative and postoperative IOP, use of pressure-lowering medications (PLMs), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), surgical details and complications. Surgical success was defined as either complete success (IOP ≤21 mm Hg without medication) or qualified success (IOP ≤21 mm Hg with medication). Surgical failure was defined as the need for additional glaucoma surgery or a postoperative IOP reduction of less than 20% from baseline.

Preoperative baseline data were taken from the last clinical examination before surgery. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, and the decision to perform trabeculotomy was made by the treating ophthalmologist in collaboration with one of the surgeons.

Surgical technique

The ab externo microcatheter-assisted trabeculotomy was performed under general anaesthesia by two experienced glaucoma surgeons (DB-H and PR). A peritomy spanning three clock hours was created to expose the sclera. Subsequently, a limbus-based scleral flap measuring 3×4 mm, with a thickness of 2/3 of the scleral thickness, was made. A side-incision was made to administer carbachol, and Schlemm’s canal was deroofed.

For patients operated before 2020, U-shaped probes were inserted into the opened canal, first temporally then nasally, and rotated into the anterior chamber to achieve a 120° opening of the TM. For patients operated after 2020, an illuminated microcatheter (iTrack) was passed through Schlemm’s canal in a 360° fashion. Both ends of the microcatheter were pulled to incise the TM and create an opening into the anterior chamber. If a complete 360° passage was not possible, a 120° opening was achieved using U-shaped probes, as described earlier. The scleral flap was sutured using 10–0 Ethilon to prevent outflow, and the conjunctiva was closed watertight with 10–0 Ethilon. All trabeculotomies were performed as standalone surgeries, not combined with cataract surgery. One eye received a supratarsal triamcinolone injection during surgery to address active inflammation. Glaucoma medications were discontinued immediately after surgery.

Postoperatively, all patients were prescribed topical pilocarpine 0.125%, to be administered four times a day for 1 week. They were given a combination of topical antibiotics and steroids (tobramycin/dexamethasone), with tapering at the surgeon’s discretion. All patients continued corticosteroid therapy to manage the underlying disease. One patient (eyes 11 and 12) discontinued corticosteroid 1 month before the 24-month follow-up.

Outcomes and time point selection

The primary outcome was IOP over time. IOP was measured using Goldmann applanation tonometry when feasible and iCare IC100 otherwise (iCare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland). BCVA was recorded in Snellen and converted to Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution(LogMAR) for analysis. No imputation was performed; analyses used all available observations.

Follow-up times varied. For analysis and graphical presentation, we selected for each operated eye the visit closest to the nominal postoperative time points: 1 day, 1 week, 3 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 18 months and 24 months. The time axis was therefore categorical and represents nominal targets rather than exact calendar times.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (V.4.3.1). Because patients contributed one or two eyes, we accounted for within-patient correlation using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for patient and for eyes nested within patient.

To assess change in IOP over time, we fit a mixed-effects model with time as a categorical fixed effect and random intercepts as above. Estimated marginal means and contrasts versus baseline were obtained with Kenward–Roger df; Dunnett adjustment was used for multiple comparisons across time points.

To compare 360° microcatheter-assisted trabeculotomy with 120° probe trabeculotomy, we performed two complementary analyses:

A repeated-measures mixed-effects model including time, technique (360° vs 120°) and their interaction, from which we compared estimated marginal means by technique at each time point.

A repeated-measures mixed-effects model including time, technique (360° vs 120°) and their interaction, from which we compared estimated marginal means by technique at each time point.

A baseline-adjusted endpoint model of change from baseline to 24 months per eye, which provides the between-technique difference in absolute IOP reduction while adjusting for baseline IOP.

A baseline-adjusted endpoint model of change from baseline to 24 months per eye, which provides the between-technique difference in absolute IOP reduction while adjusting for baseline IOP.

Two-sided α=0.05 was applied throughout.

Literature review

We conducted a literature review using the PubMed database to identify relevant articles on trabeculotomy and steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma. The following keywords were used: ‘trabeculotomy’ and ‘steroid-induced ocular hypertension’, ‘steroid-induced glaucoma’, ‘steroid responder’ or ‘steroid response’. Articles published in non-English languages or where full text could not be retrieved were excluded from the review.

Ethics approval

The study was evaluated by the Scientific Ethics Committee for the Capital Region of Denmark and deemed not to require ethics approval (Journal number: F-23043874).

Results

13 eyes from eight consecutive paediatric patients undergoing trabeculotomy for steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma were included in this study. Among these eight participants, 3 (37.5%) were female and 5 (62.5%) were male, with a mean age of 9.8 years (range: 7–17 years) at the time of surgery.

All patients had a history of steroid use and, per the inclusion criteria, were actively using steroids at the time of surgery. Steroid administration methods included topical dexamethasone eye drops (54%), supratarsal triamcinolone injections (23%), subtenon triamcinolone injections (8%) and systemic prednisolone (15%). Underlying conditions requiring steroid use included vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) (69.2%), uveitis (15.4%) and lupus nephritis (15.4%). Baseline IOP was higher in patients undergoing 120° trabeculotomy by 7.1 mm Hg (p=0.02). Postoperatively, IOP was similar between the two techniques at all visits. At 24 months, mean IOP did not differ (difference ≈ 0.8 mm Hg, p=0.80). The baseline-adjusted change from baseline to 24 months also did not differ (difference 1.5 mm Hg favouring 360° trabeculotomy, p=0.61). Overall, there was no evidence of a clinically meaningful or statistically significant difference between 360° and 120° trabeculotomy.

Before surgery, patients required an average of four PLMs (range: 2–6), with some also using systemic acetazolamide in addition to the regular glaucoma medication. Postoperatively, all 13 eyes were entirely off PLMs at all follow-up visits (figure 1) (p<0.0001).

BCVA showed a slight improvement, with mean preoperative LogMAR values of 0.12 (range: −0.10 to 1.05) improving to 0.10 (range: −0.08 to 1.10) at 24 months. However, this change was not statistically significant (p=0.34).

All 13 surgeries were free of complications, and complete success (IOP ≤21 mm Hg without medication) was achieved in all eyes throughout the 24-month follow-up period.

Although most patients remained on corticosteroids after surgery to manage their underlying conditions, no recurrence of IOP elevation occurred. Only one patient (eyes 11 and 12) discontinued corticosteroid 1 month before the 24-month follow-up. During the postoperative follow-up period, five eyes in three patients received supratarsal steroid injections without any subsequent increases in IOP. Additionally, one patient (eye 7) later underwent cataract surgery due to prior vitrectomy and ocular inflammation; the procedure was completed without complications or rise in IOP.

Discussion

Effective management of steroid-induced ocular hypertension and secondary glaucoma in paediatric patients is particularly challenging due to the complexities of underlying conditions and the frequent need for long-term corticosteroid therapy. The potential danger of irreversible blindness underscores the importance of effective IOP control in this vulnerable population.12 Findings from this case series demonstrate that trabeculotomy is an effective procedure for achieving sustained IOP control in these patients, with all treated eyes achieving complete success over 24 months of follow-up. Notably, no postoperative complications were observed, and patients were able to discontinue PLMs.

Trabeculotomy in adult steroid responders

Traditionally used for primary congenital glaucoma, trabeculotomy is increasingly used for secondary glaucoma, particularly steroid-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma. Iwao et al demonstrated in a retrospective cohort study involving 121 eyes of 121 adults with steroid-induced glaucoma and a follow-up period of 38.4±28.7 months that a 120° ab externo trabeculotomy effectively controlled IOP.13 Similarly, Honjo et al (follow-up: 15–120 months) and van Rijn et al (follow-up: 2.63–47.9 months) reported in retrospective cohort studies that ab externo trabeculotomy provides reliable, long-term IOP control in patients with steroid-induced glaucoma. Honjo et al’s study included 14 eyes from 7 adults, while van Rijn et al’s study examined 46 eyes from 35 adults.14 15 Ab interno trabeculotomy, including Trabectome surgery16 17 and gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (GATT)18,20 has also demonstrated success in managing IOP in adult steroid-responders, offering effective surgical alternatives. Our findings align with these results and suggest that paediatric steroid-responders may achieve comparable outcomes. However, evidence on surgical strategies for paediatric steroid-induced ocular hypertension remains limited.

Surgical strategies for paediatric steroid-responders

Khan et al conducted a study comparing the effectiveness of trabeculectomy with mitomycin-C (MMC) to medical treatment in managing steroid-induced glaucoma in 60 eyes from patients aged 12–25 years. All participants discontinued steroid use 2 weeks before treatment. The study appears to be randomised, as the sample was reportedly allocated using a ‘lottery method’. Results showed that 23 of 30 eyes (77%) treated with Timolol achieved successful IOP control, while all 30 eyes (100%) undergoing trabeculectomy with MMC achieved successful IOP control.21 Notably, the article does not indicate whether patients in the surgical group also received medical therapy, suggesting exclusive surgical treatment. These findings underscore the effectiveness of surgery, particularly for moderate-stage steroid-induced glaucoma, where medical therapy may be insufficient.

Ang et al analysed outcomes in patients with severe VKC requiring trabeculectomy with MMC for steroid-induced glaucoma. Their study demonstrated significant improvements in disease severity, IOP control and reduced corticosteroid use postoperatively.22 The observed improvement in disease severity supports the hypothesis that antiglaucoma medications exacerbate conjunctival inflammation, and discontinuing PLMs could alleviate inflammation in patients with VKC.23 Similarly, Balekudaru et al compared trabeculectomy with and without phacoemulsification (phaco-trabeculectomy) in children with uveitic and non-uveitic steroid-induced glaucoma. Both techniques resulted in comparable IOP control and visual outcomes, although younger age was identified as a significant risk factor for surgical failure.24 Senthil et al further emphasised that while trabeculectomy with MMC ensures durable IOP control, chronic steroid use and advanced disease stages increase the risk of surgical failure.25

Yalcinsoy et al assessed IOP elevation following posterior subtenon triamcinolone acetonide injections in paediatric uveitis. While most cases were managed medically, 21% required trabeculectomy with MMC to achieve IOP control.26 These findings, consistent with those of Ang et al and Balekudaru et al, highlight the necessity of surgical intervention when medical management fails in resistant cases.

Choy et al presented a case involving the ExPRESS mini-shunt in a paediatric patient with medically uncontrolled steroid-induced glaucoma. The device, combined with MMC, successfully maintained IOP without medication in one eye at 1-year follow-up; however, postoperative bleb failure occurred in the fellow eye.27 This case underscores the potential of the ExPRESS shunt in select paediatric cases and highlights the importance of vigilant postoperative care to manage complications.

Only one case report exists on trabeculotomy for a paediatric steroid-induced glaucoma. Hopen et al described the success of GATT in a child with steroid-induced glaucoma secondary to VKC. The procedure achieved effective IOP control without medication over a 2-year follow-up.28 GATT offers a minimally invasive alternative to trabeculectomy, mitigating risks associated with conjunctival-based surgeries, particularly in younger patients. Surgical considerations

Steroid-induced ocular hypertension and glaucoma result from biochemical and morphological changes within the TM, which decrease aqueous humour outflow, elevate IOP and ultimately threaten the optical nerve.6 Trabeculotomy addresses this root pathophysiology by incising the TM to directly relieve the aqueous humour outflow obstruction, restoring physiological drainage without creating an alternative outflow route.

While trabeculotomy has shown promise, comparing it with other surgical options is essential. Trabeculectomy and glaucoma drainage devices (GDD) are alternative surgical interventions for paediatric glaucoma, including steroid-induced cases. However, these procedures are associated with higher complication rates, such as infection, hypotony, bleb instability, device-related issues and tube exposure over time. Additionally, using anti-fibrotic agents like MMC during trabeculectomy and GDD implantation increases the risk of hypotony and long-term bleb failure.29 30 These complications are particularly problematic in paediatric patients, who exhibit robust healing responses and require long-term management.31

Trabeculotomy enhances the eye’s natural outflow system, avoiding complications linked to trabeculectomy and GDDs. It provides more reliable long-term IOP control, minimises the need for additional surgical interventions and reduces dependence on PLMs.13 32 33 Notably, the success of trabeculotomy in patients who continued corticosteroid therapy postoperatively suggests that the procedure helps maintain stable IOP even when steroid use cannot be discontinued. This is a critical advantage in managing paediatric patients with ongoing inflammatory conditions.14,1619 20 28 Our findings align with these reports, as our patients achieved sustained IOP control with reduced dependence on PLMs. All of our patients continued corticosteroid use postoperatively to manage their underlying conditions, yet none experienced a recurrence of elevated IOP. These results suggest that trabeculotomy offers a protective effect against steroid-induced IOP elevation, which is particularly valuable in paediatric patients requiring long-term steroid therapy. Furthermore, the absence of surgical complications and the need for additional interventions in our series support its role as a safe and durable option for paediatric steroid responders.

Although preliminary evidence suggests that a 360° trabeculotomy may improve aqueous outflow and stabilise IOP more effectively than a 120° trabeculotomy,34 our data did not show a difference from 120° trabeculotomy in IOP lowering through 24 months. Thus, either approach appears reasonable, and selection may be guided by surgeon preference, ocular anatomy and long-term surgical planning.

When comparing ab externo and ab interno trabeculotomy approaches in steroid responders, no significant differences have been observed.13,20 However, Kinoshita-Nakano et al suggested that ab externo trabeculotomy provides better IOP reduction and lowers the dependence on PLMs compared with Trabectome surgery for open-angle glaucoma.35 This finding indicates a potential trade-off between surgical technique and preserving the conjunctiva for future interventions, such as trabeculectomy or tube shunt placement. Given these considerations, the choice between ab externo and ab interno approaches should be tailored to the patient’s long-term management needs.

Limitations and future directions

While our results are promising, this study has some limitations. Its retrospective design, small sample size and lack of a control group constrain the generalisability of the findings. Additionally, although the follow-up period extended to 24 months, it may not adequately capture long-term outcomes or potential late-onset complications.

Larger, prospective, randomised studies comparing trabeculotomy with other surgical options for steroid-induced glaucoma in paediatric populations are essential to better define its role in clinical practice. Further studies are warranted to optimise surgical outcomes and expand the indications for trabeculotomy in paediatric steroid responders.

Conclusion

In summary, trabeculotomy has proven to be a safe and effective surgical option for managing steroid-induced ocular hypertension or glaucoma in paediatric patients. In this case series, the procedure achieved high success rates in long-term IOP control, significantly reducing the reliance on pharmacological therapy, and was not associated with any complications. Given the challenges in managing steroid-induced hypertension and glaucoma in children, particularly those requiring ongoing corticosteroid therapy, trabeculotomy should be considered as an early intervention when medical management fails to achieve adequate IOP control.
