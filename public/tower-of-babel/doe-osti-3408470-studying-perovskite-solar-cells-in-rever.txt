SECTION 3: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
When a solar cell in a panel is shaded, it can be placed into reverse bias to current match
the illuminated cells with which it is connected in series. A broad collection of perovskite
solar cells with strategically chosen perovskite compositions, contact layers and
electrodes were studied in reverse bias for this project. Several different types of reverse
bias current-voltage curves were observed. In every case degradation was observed, but
the nature of the degradation varied. Sometimes shunt formation led to electrode melting
and perovskite vaporization. In other cases, iodide was oxidized to form iodine that
escaped from the cells. The primary factors that seem to influence the type of charge
transport and the mode of degradation are whether the indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode
penetrates through the contact layer to touch the perovskite, the presence of pinholes in
the perovskite, the effectiveness of barrier layers that keep iodine from reacting with the
electrode and whether the electrode is made of metal or a transparent conducting oxide.
The composition of the perovskite is relatively unimportant.

The barrier properties of a tin oxide (SnOx) layer can be improved considerably by
implementing a surface ozone treatment step to the middle of the atomic layer deposition
process. The ozone reacts with the underlying exposed fullerenes and improves their
ability to nucleate the growth of SnOx. A high-quality barrier layers increases the
breakdown voltage of perovskite solar cells.

A major finding in this project is that the formation of shunts in reverse bias for most
perovskite solar cells occurs in perovskite pinholes. Avalanche breakdown can occur and
lead to device failure when the two electrodes are only separated by very thin electron
and hole contact layers.

Page 2 of 47

International interest in reverse bias degradation has picked up considerably over the last
three years as the importance of the partial shading problem has become appreciated.
While there has been progress in increasing the breakdown voltage and reducing the rate
of degradation at a fixed average current density, there are no demonstrations of
perovskite solar cells that can pass the short circuit current density for even an hour
without substantial degradation. It is therefore recommended that panel-level engineering
--- rather than just cell-level architectural engineering --- be used to ensure that perovskite
solar cells are not placed into reverse bias.

One strategy, employed by First Solar for their CdTe panels, is to make panels with long
vertically oriented solar cells and to deploy them in large fields where shading is less likely
to occur. When snow settles to the bottom of these panels, it covers each cell by a similar
amount. It will be important to field test full size perovskite panels to see if this strategy
can be effective.

Another strategy for protecting cells is to use bypass diodes. This strategy is used in
most silicon panels and should be highly effective for monolithic perovskite-silicon
tandems as well. The breakdown voltage of these tandems is the sum of the breakdown
voltage of the two subcells. Silicon cells typically have a reverse breakdown voltage of
15 V. Consequently, more than 10 cells can be protected by one bypass diode. It will
probably not be possible, however, to use bypass diodes in panels with long cells formed
by several laser scribes. The voltage drop due to series resistance will be too large if the
current must pass the length of a panel to reach a bypass diode.

SECTION 5: BACKGROUND
Metal halide perovskites are leading competitors for future thin-film solar cells, either as
single-junction devices [DOI: 10.1126/science.aat8235] or in tandem with well-
established Si solar cell technologies [DOI: 10.1126/science.adh3849]. Solar cells made
of these materials are, however, subject to a number of modes of degradation that include
light-induced phase separation [DOI: 10.1021/acsenergylett.8b00764], reactions with
metal electrodes and migration of metals into the device stacks [DOI:
10.1021/acsnano.6b02613], mechanical weakness [DOI: 10.1016/j.eml.2016.06.006],
and reverse-bias breakdown with permanent device failure at modest voltages (between
–0.5 V and -2 V in many cases) [DOI: 10.1002/aenm.201702365].

In this project, we focus on the reverse-bias instabilities in perovskite solar cells. The
issue of reverse-bias, at the time of this project’s start, had been studied by very few
research groups [DOIs: 10.1002/aenm.201702365, 10.1039/C9TA12032G]. Since then,
this research topic has garnered attention as one of the more pertinent obstacles to the
commercialization of perovskite solar cells. Several notable research reports have
recently been published on this topic [DOIs: 10.1021/acsaem.1c03206, 10.1038/s41560-
024-01579-7, 10.1016/j.joule.2022.06.014, 10.1016/j.chphma.2021.10.001], including
work from our group [DOI: 10.1038/s41560-024-01600-z].

Recent publications most relevant to the current work describe specific electrochemical
degradation pathways when perovskite solar cells experience reverse bias. The most
popular hypothesis for the common metalized p-i-n architecture is that metal electrode is
partially oxidized by iodine cations near the electron-transport layer (ETL) [DOI:
10.1038/s41560-024-01579-7]. The metal cations can then migrate into and across the
perovskite solar cell, eventually leading to permanent metallic shunts in the device. This
can be dramatically inhibited by using a dense oxide ETL, e.g., ALD-grown SnOx.
Additionally, a transparent conductive oxide, e.g., ITO or IZO --- instead of metal --- can
be used as the top contact.

This hypothesis, while seemingly true, also seems to be incomplete based on a few
observations: 1) even with a thick and dense SnOx layer between the metal and the
perovskite, rapid and spontaneous shunting at relatively low voltages is still frequently
observed; 2) the reverse-bias behavior can be dramatically influenced by changing the
hole-transport layer (HTL), an effect which is not directly described by the previous metal
migration hypothesis. Our work sets out to consolidate these issues.

SECTION 6: PROJECT OBJECTIVES
This project aimed to understand and improve reverse-bias resilience of perovskite solar
cells, thereby eliminating one major obstacle to their commercialization, enabling
substantially more efficient and cheaper solar cells compared to today’s current
technologies. In the first two years, we planned to characterize the mechanisms for
reverse bias breakdown, assessing the dynamics of the reversable and irreversible

Page 5 of 47

degradation as a function of time and applied reverse bias voltage for various
architectures. Once we had a sufficient understanding of the behavior, accelerated aging
tests would be developed to correlate reverse bias stability to long-term operational
testing at maximum power point. Reverse-bias testing and accelerated aging would be
assessed using a variety of high-performing perovskite solar cell architectures with initial
power conversion efficiency (PCE) > 18% that have been shown to have promising
stability, including n-i-p and p-i-n devices.

We planned to develop strategies to improve the reverse bias resiliency of perovskite
solar cells devices. We anticipated two approaches will be effective at improving reverse-
bias stability: 1) suppressing ion migration in the perovskite absorber, and 2) fabricating
exceptionally impermeable and inert contacts or electrodes. These strategies evolved as
our mechanistic understanding improves through continued reverse-bias testing. We had
a particular interest in improving the ETL to prevent p-type doping, which has been shown
to be partially responsible for performance loss [10.1021/acsenergylett.1c00227]. Within
three years, we planned to have developed and characterized a highly impermeable oxide
barrier with no measurable halide egress under reverse-bias stressing and to have
established reverse bias stressing. The data collected in this project can support
predictive qualities of reverse bias stressing as an accelerated testing strategy for
perovskite solar cells and elucidate inherent strengths and weaknesses with regard to
stability for n-i-p vs. p-i-n architectures as well as for specific active layers and contacts
commonly used in state-of-the-art perovskite photovoltaics.

Specifically, the proposed tasks of this project were to: 1) Characterize mechanisms for
breakdown and electrochemical processes in reverse bias; 2) Prevent the electron
transport layer (ETL) from being p-type doped in reverse bias; 3) Make exceptionally
impermeable contacts or electrodes; 4) Aging cells in reverse bias to rapidly predict
longer-term stability at forward bias under 1-sun illumination; 5) Developing a reverse-
bias resistant device stack; 6) Reverse bias acceleration factors and establish testing
protocol. Below we describe results from research which was directed toward each of
these tasks.

The most significant and finalized findings have been summarized in several peer-
reviewed articles which are now publicly available. Those works including all technical
findings, descriptions and figures can be found in the Products section of this report.

SECTION 7: PROJECT RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We have discovered that spontaneous, abrupt, and “premature” degradation of perovskite
solar cells under modest reverse-bias voltages (approximately –0.5 V to –2.0 V) is largely
caused by microscopic device imperfections like pinholes in the perovskite film itself, and
likely also in the hole-transport layers (HTLs) and electron-transport layers (ETLs). (These
discoveries have been summarized in a manuscript that is currently in the peer-review
stage with Joule; the submitted version of this manuscript is copied in the “Products”

Page 6 of 47

section below.) We developed a method for assessing the coverage of HTLs and ETLs
using simple current-voltage (JV) electrical measurements. This method involves
fabricating “perovskite-free solar cells” --- really ETL/HTL diodes --- and testing them in
the dark. The resulting JV curves reveal important features related to metal migration and
poor HTL coverage. We also found that such pinholes in various layers lead to
quantifiable power conversion efficiency losses. Nonetheless, while they do not seem to
cause rapid device failure under typical forward-bias operating conditions --- and can thus
be difficult to identify using forward-bias measurements --- they can lead to major
instabilities and rapid device failure under reverse bias. Reverse-bias measurements can
thus also be used as a way to assess fabrication quality of several different layers in the
solar cell.

We studied an even wider variety of perovskite solar cell architectures than we promised.
We have investigated Ag, Au, Cu, ITO and carbon electrodes. We have looked at 5 or 6
perovskites in cells made at the University of Colorado, NREL and Northwestern. We
have studied cells in the p-i-n and n-i-p architecture. This factor has a strong influence on
reverse bias behavior, along with electrode choice. In light of lessons learned during this
project, it is likely that the important difference is really in the contact choices and
particular ordering in n-i-p vs. p-i-n. To first order, all of the perovskite compositions seem
to behave similarly.

SECTION 8: SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS
Task 1 research has greatly improved the understanding of what happens in perovskite
solar cells when they are operated in reverse bias. Cells that have the p-i-n architecture
and are capped with metal frequently and rapidly shunt under moderate reverse bias (less
than |-3| V), even in visually good-quality cells. While recent work from other groups []
suggests that the top metal contact migrates across the organic and perovskite layers,
causing abrupt shunts and device failure, we regularly observed a distinct failure
mechanism at microscopic pinholes in the perovskite layer which are unavoidable when
using solution-processed deposition methods. Careful material and structural analyses at
these defects indicate that perovskite pinholes lead to regions where the contact layers
are in direct contact. Electrical measurements and modeling reveal that these small
regions are susceptible to passing very large currents at small reverse biases, down to |-
1| to |-2| V. These defects evidently experience very large power dissipation and heating,
thus causing rapid device failure and even explosive effects at the 10’s of micrometers
scale. This type of shunting can thus be largely prevented by using a dense SnOx ETL,
which improves the electrical reliability and thermomechanical durability of these
perovskite pinhole defects. This work was summarized in a manuscript which is currently
in the peer review stage at Joule; the submitted version of that manuscript is also copied
below

Still, when shunting does not occur, mobile ions pile up at the perovskite-contact interface,
creating sharp band bending that allows holes to tunnel into the perovskite. In many cases
with optimized contacts, we have seen this type of “smooth” reverse current behavior with
minimal device degradation, at least over short timescales on the order of minutes.

Page 7 of 47

However, there is no reason to believe that this inhibits iodide oxidation. Neutral iodine
is smaller than iodide and can become an interstitial, creating a halide vacancy as well.
The neutral iodine can escape the perovskite layer, which damages the perovskite and
potentially the electrode as well. At this time, we think this process is unavoidable, and
is likely accelerated if the cell must pass the short-circuit current density in reverse bias.
We thus strongly recommend the use of bypass diodes in modules, in which case the
current does not need to pass through the perovskite. Instead, the solar cell should be
able to withstand moderate reverse-bias voltages without passing current and without
significant additional electrochemical device degradation mechanisms allowed. This work
will be ongoing.

Task 2 was aimed at replacing the fullerene ETL because, at the time of proposal writing,
we thought that iodine might be p-type doping the fullerenes. If that were true, it would
create undesirable band bending at the electron extracting contact. We have not seen
evidence that iodine dopes fullerenes in an appreciable way. We therefore
deemphasized the development of non-fullerene acceptors. It is worth noting that other
research teams are trying to replace fullerenes since they are expensive and
mechanically fragile.

Task 3 was aimed at improving the ALD process to a create a highly impermeable
electron transport layer that could prevent halogen species from leaving the perovskite
layer. This barrier dramatically helps reverse-bias resilience, likely by eliminating the
possibility of metal migration into the device. We have been highly successful in making
denser SnOx films on C60 through ozone-treating C60 during the SnOx deposition. This
work has been summarized in a manuscript which is now published in Joule
[10.1016/j.joule.2023.10.009]. This work naturally led to a study of how perovskite film
defects influence reverse-bias stability. We found that when we put solvent on top of the
SnOx, the perovskite was mostly protected, only dissolving in spots where dust particles
introduced pinholes into the SnOx. This observation led us to a detailed study of what
causes pinholes in the first place. That work was described above in the summary of Task
1 research.

The goal of task 4 was to correlate stability in reverse bias with stability under one sun at
the maximum power point (MPP). Our original hypothesis was that iodide oxidation is the
most important degradation mechanism in cells that are made following all of the best
design rules for stability. While this may be true, our research is indicating that poor
coverage at the HTL is an even larger issue that has not yet been completely addressed.
When HTL coverage is poor, perovskite and ITO are in direct contact, and an undesirable
electrochemical reaction of the indium in the ITO is enabled at that interface. This new
issue will need to be addressed before investigating the detrimental effects of iodide
oxidation. We are seeing strong preliminary evidence that eliminating this effect through
HTL engineering leads to both markedly improved reverse-bias resilience and better long-
term stability at MPP conditions.

Page 8 of 47

Task 5 research to develop a reverse-bias resilient device architecture has largely been
accomplished, although perhaps not in the way we originally conceived. In the case where
micrometer-scale imperfections in the solution-processed perovskite (e.g., pinholes or
voids) exist --- which may be inevitable when deployed at large-area commercial scale --
- the device can exploit a proper choice of contact layers that don’t degrade quickly under
reverse bias conditions. For example, we have found that a perovskite-free
ITO/PTAA/C60/SnOx/Ag layer stack can be quite resilient to reverse bias conditions if the
PTAA layer exceeds about 20-30 nm. The SnOx appears to reduce the possibility of rapid
metal migration across the sub-100 nm stack, which can evidently happen even without
the perovskite materials there. However, PTAA of this thickness is not a great choice for
overall device performance since it typically introduces electrical resistances that
decrease power conversion efficiencies. There are, however, other robust HTL materials,
like nickel oxide (NiOx), that work similarly well, and may work especially well when
combined with other surface modifiers like “self-assembling monolayers”. We are
currently exploring how to best use NiOx for this purpose, and this will likely be
summarized in a manuscript for future peer review and publication.

Task 6 research to develop reverse-bias acceleration factors to predict long-term forward-
bias stability was not completed. The project went in unexpected directions. We ended
up spending a substantial amount of time figuring out how shunting occurs. This process
is not expected to correlate with degradation in forward bias. We also experienced
substantial setbacks due to the pandemic, medical problems and a one-year delay in
receiving lifetime testing equipment. Preliminary data suggests that our hypothesis is
correct and we are continuing our research to quantify the correlation between
electrochemical degradation that occurs in reverse bias with that which occurs in forward
bias.

SECTION 9: PATH FORWARD
The observation that pinholes in perovskite and transport layers are very common origins
of device failure leads us to make several recommendations for future research: 1)
Fabricators, at both lab and commercial scales, should emphasize the development of
deposition methods that provide the most uniform coverage possible in the cleanest
environments. Vapor-phase deposition is likely the most promising candidate, although
liquid-phase coating may be suitable with highly optimized processes. 2) If perovskite
pinholes cannot be completely avoided, transport layer device architectures that can
reliably pass only small currents at moderate voltages should be used. The use of dense
oxide layers on each transport-layer side seems like a highly promising approach for this
purpose. Avoiding perovskite pinholes is also important for lab-scale fundamental
research since they can also lead to power conversion efficiency losses which may be
easily misidentified as, e.g., poor interfacial electronic transport properties or
recombination, and can mislead research efforts. Continued efforts in our lab will be

Page 9 of 47

directed toward understanding the impact of pinholes in the HTL and ETL, especially in
regard to long-term device stability. Preliminary results suggest that several
electrochemical reaction pathways can be shut down if certain layers are rigorously kept
from contacting each other.

SUBSECTION 10-1 – Improving the Barrier Properties of Tin Oxide in Metal Halide Perovskite
Solar Cells using Ozone to Enhance Nucleation
Source: Joule, Volume 7, Issue 12, 2023, Pages 2873-2893)
Introduction
 Conformal barriers serve important functions for the stability, yield, and efficiency
of thin-film electronic devices.1–3 This is especially true for metal halide perovskite (MHP)
photovoltaic technologies where organic or small molecule selective contacts are often
used and the perovskite absorber itself is considered a “soft” material that is sensitive to
thermal degradation, ingress of water and oxygen, interdiffusion or egress of halogens or
organic cations.4 Integration of metals, transparent conducting oxides or other materials
with MHP semiconductors is a critical challenge for their use in devices where poor
chemical compatibility or ill-suited processing approaches can induce defects, generating
undesirable interfaces and undercutting device performance. To address these
challenges, internal and external barriers can be applied to photovoltaic devices.5 The
issues of water and oxygen ingress are of particular concern for MHP based photovoltaics
(PV) where multidecadal outdoor reliability is targeted. While impact of extrinsic stressors
can be addressed by external barriers, such as encapsulation in combination with
packaging, internal barriers may reduce the requirements of such packages or further
enhance reliability. 6,7 Several recent reports have highlighted the efficacy of robust
internal barriers towards the operational stability of MHP solar cells.8–11
 Organic or small molecule selective contact layers are commonly used directly on
top of MHP semiconductors. These organics are soft materials themselves and are not
effective at preventing the migration of halides, water and oxygen, especially considering
that the carrier transport layers are typically held below 50-nm-thickness to minimize
series resistance loss. For comparison, common organic encapsulants typically have
thicknesses on the order of 100 microns or greater and all have water vapor transmissions
rates (WVTR) on the order of 1 g/m2day.7 In contrast, ultrathin oxide layers can have
exceptionally high barrier qualities with films only several nanometers thick (e.g., Al2O3
thin films on polymers with a WVTR on the order of 10-5 g/m2day).12 C60/ALD SnOx
electron transport layers (ETLs) are frequently used in p-i-n MHP solar cells to enable
sputtering of transparent conducting oxides. We first demonstrated this approach in
201613,14 which has since gained traction in the MHP tandem community15, offering the
opportunity to create a multifunctional layer that serves as both robust internal barrier and
facilitates the device operation.

 There are two primary challenges for growing high-quality ALD oxide barriers on
top of MHP semiconductors: (i) potentially unfavorable interactions between the
perovskite surface and ALD precursor, necessitating the use of an interlayer between the
MHP absorber and tin oxide film16–18 and (ii) the lack of organic or small molecule selective
contact materials with reactive functional groups (e.g. -O-, -OH) suitable for initiating ALD
surface nucleation.

 Nucleation of ALD on unreactive, low-energy surfaces often leads to nucleation
delay, where no growth or non-conformal growth occurs in the first several growth cycles.

Page 11 of 47

In the case of nucleation delay on soft materials, this can enable to sub-surface diffusion
of ALD reactants. In the case of unreactive, yet more permeable materials, such as
polymers, organometallic precursors have been shown to diffuse tens to hundreds of
nanometers sub-surface and affect bulk mechanical properties.19 Exothermic subsurface
reactions can lead to expansion and contraction of and ALD oxide film throughout the
half-cycles of the ALD process producing a cracked and diffuse organic/oxide interphase.
This phenomena is illustrated by Parsons et al. for the growth of growth of Al2O3 via
trimethylaluminum/ water on low-energy polymeric fibers.20

 One strategy to suppress ALD subsurface growth on fullerene selective contacts
and improve film barrier properties is to add an ultrathin nucleation layer on top of the
fullerene selective contact. We previously demonstrated this concept by incorporating an
ultrathin hydroxylated polymer (polyethyleneimine ethoxylated, or PEIE) on top of the
fullerene as a nucleation layer in perovskite solar cells.21,22 This approach significantly
improved the barrier properties of ALD-grown aluminum-doped zinc oxide, tin oxide, and
vanadium oxide, but required smooth perovskite films for the best barriers. Nanoparticle-
based interlayers, such as aluminum-doped zinc oxide, have also been reported as ALD
growth surfaces on top of fullerene-based ETLs for improved MHP device stability23.

 An alternative strategy is chemical modification of the growth surface. Reduction
of nucleation delay for ALD-growth has been reported using oxidative ozone treatments
to enhance the reactivity of carbon-based substrates, such as graphite and
graphene.24,25. Ozone is one of the few gaseous oxidizers capable of opening and
functionalizing fullerene’s sp2 carbon cage and was employed as one of the first methods
to chemically modify fullerenes.26 Ozone is known to rapidly oxidize perovskite materials,
excess ozone will chemically interact driving the metals and halides (e.g. Pb2+ -> Pb4+
and I1- -> I5+) which is electronically unfavorable. 27,28 Films of bare MHP exposed to
ozone, or even hot oxygen, will bleach to a yellow, oxidized state. As such, ozone
processing and ozonolysis reactions directly on top of an MHP absorber are not yet known
to be device-compatible fabrication methods. However, successful in-situ
functionalization of fullerene requires a strong oxidant, such as ozone, to insert bonds
into fullerene’s carbon sp2 cage structure29.

 In this study, we demonstrate that ozone treatments can be used to initiate ALD
growth on fullerene films in p-i-n perovskite solar cells. We show that interfacial ozone
damage can be mitigated in devices through the deposition of an ultrathin (~5 nm), non-
conformal ALD-grown oxide layer prior to ozone exposure. Using this approach, ozone is
able to functionalize exposed fullerene within voids of the ultrathin oxide layer for
subsequent ALD oxide growth, as shown in Figure 1a, without loss of device
performance. This vapor phase nucleation process for growth on fullerene materials is
then demonstrated to improve the barrier properties of ALD tin oxide thin films and is
shown to be compatible with a broad range of perovskite compositions and band gaps.

Results and Discussion
Limited Diffusion of Ozone Through Capping Layer Enables Devices

Page 12 of 47

 We first investigate necessary capping layer thicknesses to mitigate device
degradation under high ozone exposure conditions. 1.73 eV band gap p-i-n devices with
the MHP composition DMA0.1FA0.6Cs0.3Pb(I2.4Br0.6)3, reported previously,15 were
fabricated with a fullerene electron transport layer (ETL) contact and varying thicknesses
of an ALD SnOx capping layer prior to ozone exposure. SnOx capping layers were
deposited at 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 cycles and exposed to a 60 s in-situ dose of
ozone at a concentration of 175 grams per normal cubic meter (g/Nm3). After ozone
treatment, the ALD process was continued for a cumulative deposition (capping layer and
post-capping layer growth) of 125 cycles, or approximately 15 nm (1.2 Å/cyc). Devices
were completed with an evaporated silver electrode. The power conversion efficiency of
the solar cells is plotted versus capping layer thickness (cyc) prior to the ozone exposure
in Figure 1b. Current-voltage curves for the 0, 20, 40 cycle capping layer thicknesses
and control cells are shown in Figure S1; device performance statistics can be found in

 The results show that ozone exposure to an unprotected perovskite/C60 surface is
detrimental to the performance of the solar cell. A 50% reduction in efficiency is observed
when the bare fullerene surface is ozone-treated relative to the ozone-free control. The
loss is primarily attributed to a reduction in fill factor and evident of the formation of a
transport barrier. This effect is consistent with interfacial degradation at the
perovskite/ETL interface resulting in inhibited electron extraction.30 A similar efficiency
drop was observed in the absence of a tin oxide capping layer with the a single short 150
ms ozone dose. Current-voltage for a short ozone exposure with no capping layer is
shown in Figure S2. Depositing 20 cycles of SnOx reduces the performance loss to an
approximate 25%. Increasing the capping layer deposition to 40 cycles SnOx (5 nm) and
thicker prior to ozone treatment results in no significant change in initial device power-
conversion efficiency. This finding suggests that a 5 nm SnOx capping layer can protect
underlying perovskite from strong oxidizers, such as ozone. Subsequent analysis
discussed in the next section shows that the C60/SnOx interface is quite diffuse at this
growth stage and is more appropriately referred to as an interphase. For the remainder
of these experiments, 40 ALD SnOx cycles is used as the minimum capping layer
thickness sufficient for enabling surface ozone treatment of PSCs.

In-situ Fullerene Oxidation
 X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is used to study the effect of ozone exposure
to fullerene thin films. Ozone is known to react with fullerene as addition of ozone directly
to the [6,6] double-bond junction to form the C60O3 [6,6]-closed ozonide; ozonide has a
short half-life at the elevated temperatures and is expected to liberate dioxygen gas to
form a 6,6-arene oxide on the cage surface.31–34 We target the protonation of the 6,6-
arene oxide with water to create a hydroxylated fullerene nucleation site, favorable for
subsequent ALD surface growth. For this reason, we investigate ALD nucleation by in-
situ functionalization through combined ozone and water pulses in the ALD reactor where
one ozone treatment cycle consists of a 3 s ozone dose immediately followed by a 150
ms H2O dose with no purge delay.

Page 13 of 47

 Surface oxidation of three structures are investigated: C60, C60 treated in-situ with
ozone and water, and C60/40 cycle ALD SnOx treated in-situ with ozone and water. XPS
survey scans performed on in-situ ozone and water treated C60 and C60/SnOx films
confirms oxygen uptake of the ozone-treated C60 layers (Figure S3). Analysis of the
accompanying high-resolution C 1s spectra (Figure 2a-c) reveals an increase in the
relative intensity contribution of oxidized carbon, specifically, O – C60 (sp2) species, with
ozone-treatment. A primary feature located at 285.0 eV is identified as the principal
aromatic peak, corresponding to the C60 (sp2 - cage) of the fullerene structure.35 This
signal is coupled to a higher binding energy feature at 290.0 eV, identified as the π - π*
shake-up satellite characteristic of aromatic carbon.36 Additional components of the C 1s
spectra are identified in Supporting Table 3, including two oxidized fullerene O – C60
(sp2) species at 287.0 eV and 288.9 eV with accompanying satellite shake-up features at
292.9 eV and 294.8 eV, respectively. For bare fullerene films (those without a SnOx
capping layer), ozone-treatment and subsequent analysis of the O – C60 (sp2) signatures
demonstrates a relatively uniform penetration of ozone-treatment into the XPS detection
depth (~ 5 nm; 2.5 times the 2 nm mean free path of a C 1s photoelectron with 1200 eV
kinetic energy).37 The relative ease of penetration into the bare C60 film highlights the
necessity of the SnOx capping layer.
 XPS analysis of the C 1s region for partial (40 cycles, Figure 2c) and full coverage
(125 cycles, Figure S2c) SnOx ALD layers reveals two additional chemical states of
carbon. These spectral features are attributed to C (sp3) and C ≃ O (sp3), located at
285.5 eV and 289.5 eV respectively. We have denoted these features as ‘ALD carbon’,
as their dominant signal contribution is unique to the ALD-processed films in these
experiments. These signal contributions are attributed to the presence residual precursor
ligand in the ALD film, as well as surface carbon acquired from the reactor itself. By
directly associating these spectral features with the presence of the ALD film, C 1s
contributions from the C60 and SnOx ALD films can be differentiated in C60/SnOx
bilayers. Fitted envelopes of the oxygen-free bare C60 (Fig. 2a) and full-coverage (125
cycles) SnOx ALD films (Figure S2c) are used to evaluate the C 1s XPS data to
understand the device relevant films with SnOx capping layers.
 Angle-resolved XPS (ARXPS) analysis of C60/40 cycle ALD SnOx stack treated in-
situ with ozone and water (Figure 2d) shows the relative carbon fraction of the primary
features in the C 1s spectra at increasing surface sensitivity from 0° (normal take-off
angle) to 80° (glancing take-off angle), as illustrated in Figure 2e. The fraction of ALD
carbon increases towards the surface of the stack, however, at 80°, a significant fraction
of fullerene C 1s contributions are maintained, where intensity attributed to sp2 and O –
C60 (sp2) peaks account for 14 % and 17 %, respectively. While the C 1s peak fraction
does not represent a quantitative surface coverage, a combined 31 % intensity at glancing
angles from these fullerene signatures suggest incomplete coverage of the ALD SnOx
capping layer. This is explained by poor nucleation on un-modified fullerene and cracking
of the SnOx film due to sub-surface growth. Surface fullerene after SnOx capping layer
deposition would be directly susceptible to oxidation via diffusion from the 5 cycles of [3
s O3 + 150 ms H2O] applied. These diffusion processes do not require line-of-site and
oxidation could occur both on directly exposed fullerene or through cracks in the tin oxide
layer. ARXPS of the ozone-treated capping layer shows a gradual increase in the ratio of
O – C60 (sp2) to C60 (sp2) towards the surface. This suggests an oxidation gradient within

the top 5 nm of the fullerene film with a higher degree of oxidation towards the surface,
pointing to the efficacy of the tin oxide capping layer at regulating penetration of ozone
towards the perovskite surface.
 The secondary electron cutoff was measured by XPS to calculate the effect of
ozone treatment on the Fermi-level of the tin oxide capping layer before and nucleation
treatment. Results are reported in Figure S4. The as-deposited 40 cyc SnOx capping
layer on C60 shows an electrically heterogeneous surface with two distinct Fermi-levels at
approximately 4.2 eV and 4.4 eV. The surface heterogeneity is at a length scale smaller
than the probe diameter of 100 mm, although some variability in the higher work function
surface material was also observed from spot to spot across the substrate. After 5 cycles
of [3 s O3 + 150 ms H2O] in-situ nucleation treatment, the workfunction of the capping
layer surface increased to 4.46 eV and became homogeneous to the resolution of the
instrument both within the 100 mm measurement probe and from spot to spot across the
sample, indicating the films become laterally more uniform in electronic properties. In
addition to the fullerene functionalization of C60 (sp2) to O – C60 (sp2), as previously
discussed, O 1s and Sn 3d 5/2 XPS spectra on these films (Figure S5) show an increase
in surface hydroxyl content upon in-situ ozone and water. A hydroxyl-induced chemical
shift of Sn 3d5/2 peak is expected to be small and not definitively resolvable with XPS
and therefore we are not able to determine whether the additional surface hydroxyls
reside on exposed fullerene or tin oxide, however, we would expect that both surfaces
exhibit an increase in surface hydroxyl content upon the nucleation treatment.38
 It is found that combined ozone and water modification on the capping layer has a
noticeable effect on the morphology of subsequent ALD SnOx growth. Cross-sectional
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images compare Si/C60 substrates
coated with 125 cycle of control SnOx (Figure 3a) and 125 cycle of ozone-treated SnOx
(Figure 3b). These figures illustrate morphological differences between the two growth
modes where distinct domains in the control tin oxide sample are observed, consistent
with sub-surface growth. In contrast, the ozone-treated tin oxide layer appears conformal
with no detectable domains or cracks within the oxide layer. High resolution transmission
electron microscopy (HRTEM), reported in Figure S6, provides further evidence for
suppressed sub-surface growth upon ozone treatment of the SnOx capping layer. Here,
the gap between the silicon substrate and tin oxide layer is measured across several
points on the sample and found to be 24.7 nm on the control SnOx sample and 27.4 nm
on the ozone-treated SnOx sample. The C60 films on these samples were loaded side-by-
side on a rotating evaporator stage at approximately the same radius from center of
rotation to ensure nearly identical initial C60 film thickness. The difference in spacing
between the silicon surface and the tin oxide layer of approximately 2.7 nm between the
two HRTEM samples is attributed to inhibited subsurface growth with ozone nucleation
applied to the 40 cycle tin oxide capping layer.
 The measured thickness of the tin oxide layers, as determined by HRTEM, are
very comparable at approximately 14.7 nm on the control sample and 13.6 nm for the
ozone-treated sample. These values are slightly lower than the nominal 15 nm thickness,
as determined by spectroscopic ellipsometry on silicon. This can be explained by a
nucleation delay on the fullerene growth surface. The ~ 1 nm thickness variation between
the two samples is attributed to the higher precursor mass uptake of the control sample
due to sub-surface growth.

 The STEM and HRTEM observations are further supported by x-ray reflectivity
(XRR). XRR modeling of samples prepared identically to the STEM and HRTEM study is
summarized in Figure S7. Several observations are made in this analysis. First, a thinner
C60 layer and thicker C60/SnOx interphase is modeled in the control sample (22 nm and 6
nm, respectively) compared to the ozone-treated sample (28 nm and 1 nm, respectively).
The thinner fullerene layer and thicker interphase in the control sample are consistent
with subsurface diffusion. These results also suggest that the control sample C60/SnOx
interphase may penetrate the fullerene layer further than can be resolved HRTEM.
Second, it is observed that the control sample interphase has a lower electron density
(0.61 e-/A3) than the ozone-treated interphase region (1.37 e-/A3). The higher electron
density of the ozone-treated interphase region is explained by reduced intermixing with
the fullerene layer and decreased oxygen vacancies as a result of ozone exposure. This
is consistent with an increase of surface hydroxyl upon ozone treatment of the capping
layer observed in Figure S5b and a reduction in oxygen vacancies associated with an
increase in tin oxide work function observed in Figure S4. The cross-sectional electron
density profile is overlaid on HRTEM images in Figure S7d,e to illustrate the differences
in the interphase between the control and ozone-treated samples. Third, the electron
density in the top SnOx layer of the control sample (1.19 e-/A3) and ozone-treated sample
(1.18 e-/A3) are nearly identical. The similar electron densities observed in the top tin
oxide films suggest that the electronic properties of these materials grown after nucleation
are substantially similar. This is expected as these layers are grown by the same ALD
chemistry at the same reaction temperature. XPS, STEM, HRTEM, and XRR results
support the hypothesis that ozone treatment can be applied to functionalize fullerene
materials and inhibit sub-surface ALD SnOx growth.

Enhanced Barrier Properties and Environmental Stability

 Solvent penetration testing is directly relevant to all-perovskite tandem processing.
Without a conformally deposited solvent barrier, solution processing one perovskite layer
on top of another with non-orthogonal solvent systems will result in dissolution of the
underlying perovskite at pinholes and defects in the barrier layer. Previous monolithic all-
perovskite tandem reports have relied on thick recombination layers to provide solvent
barriers over small area, but improving the ALD layer barrier properties can enable thinner
recombination layers with reduced lateral transport (reduced shunting), reduced optical
losses, faster deposition times, and improved mechanical properties.39,40
Glass/DMA0.1FA0.6Cs0.3Pb(I2.4Br0.6)3/fullerene/SnOx test structures were prepared with
increasing nucleation cycles of ozone and water applied after a 40 cycle SnOx capping
layer deposition. DMF penetration was tested by fully covering the test stack with
dimethylformamide for 10s. Image analysis was used to characterize pinhole formation
and capture the perovskite film retention after the DMF procedure; results are
summarized in Figure 3c. In this experiment, the untreated control SnOx depositions
show complete dissolution of the perovskite film (< 3.0% remaining perovskite film) while
increasing ozone and water dosage lead to improved film retention, culminating in >99.5%

Page 16 of 47

perovskite film retention for five or more cycles of ozone and water, or 15 s of cumulative
in-situ ozone dose.

 Microscopy was performed on the pinholes that appeared on ozone-treated
samples after DMF solvent testing. These images revealed that particulates resided in
the center of most pinholes for ozone-treated barrier films (Figure S8) We found that
these particles have a size on the order of 40 to 200 um. Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX)
analysis the particles suggests that they are primarily composed of glass shards,
fragments of device material (e.g. C60) and fibers from lab tissues, ultrasonic substrate
cleaning, handling substrate edges with metal tweezers, and spin coating can generate
these particles. Theoretically, an ALD barrier layer should conformally coat and protect
around such particulates; however, we speculate that because these particles are several
orders of magnitude thicker than our device layers, they may mechanically dislodge
during the DMF solvent test. Complete removal of particulates during processing is
beyond our laboratory capabilities and the scope of this project; however, an additional
barrier test was designed to test the efficacy of these barriers through ambient annealing
where mechanical forces would not be present. ALD tin oxide layers were deposited on
glass/perovskite/fullerene substrates with and without the ozone nucleation treatment.
These samples were aged in a 150°C oven fed with ambient air. In this way, the ingress
of only gaseous oxygen/water or egress of halogen/cation through contactless visual and
optical analysis is tested on the perovskite films and the mechanical disruption of
microscopic dust particles on the surface is minimized. After 1000 hours of aging under
these conditions, the ozone-treated ALD film outperforms the control ALD process with
nearly no visual evidence of oxidation and bleaching of the perovskite (Figure 3d, e).
These results suggest particulate accumulation on device surfaces may limit barrier
performance in scenarios where those particulates can become dislodged and particle
controls should be considered in a manufacturing setting.

 Internal halide barriers are important to reduce effusion from perovskite devices,
minimize halide corrosion of contact electrodes, and prevent X-site intermixing between
monolithic all-perovskite tandem subcells. Qualitative halide permeability of SnOx barriers
is tested by a solution-based halide exchange monitored by absorption spectroscopy.
Test structures of: Glass/FA0.8Cs0.2PbI3/fullerene with bathocuproine (BCP), 125 cyc
control ALD SnOx, and 125 cyc ozone-treated ALD SnOx (40 cycle ALD SnOx capping
layer, 5 cycle ozone nucleation, 85 cycle ALD SnOx barrier deposition) films were
submerged in solutions of formamidinium bromide (10 mg/mL in isopropanol) at 50 °C for
24 hrs. Absorptance was monitored via in-situ UV-Vis (Figure S9 a-c). ALD-free films
showed a rapid absorptance onset shift from 790 nm (1.55 eV) to 600 nm (2.10 eV),
indicating a nearly complete conversion to a pure bromine perovskite within 6 hours.
Control SnOx barrier samples showed a significantly slower, yet noticeable, absorptance
shift over the 24 hr test. In contrast, the ozone-treated SnOx barrier samples showed no
detectable onset or absorption shift after being submerged in the bromide ion solution for
24 hours, indicating the ozone-treated ALD barrier film suppresses halide migration
across the ETL to a greater degree than control ALD SnOx.

 The barrier quality of the SnOx is further investigated by measuring the water-vapor
transmission rates (WVTR). We thermally evaporated 30-nm-thick fullerene onto 15 mil x
100-mm polyethylene terephthalate and deposit 125 cycles of ALD SnOx with a control
process and with ozone treatment. The WVTR is measured using a MOTOC AquaTrans
2 instrument, opting for an automated liquid-phase moisture detection method over
traditional calcium tests.41 WVTR data shows an order of magnitude decrease from 1.34
g/(m2-day) for the control tin oxide ALD to 0.13 g/(m2-day) for the ozone-assisted tin oxide
ALD, with the PET wafer being approximately 1 g/(m2-day) by itself. While the order of
magnitude decrease in WVTR is an improvement, these values would not be considered
a high-quality water vapor barrier. We believe the WVTR metrics may be limited due to
the flexing of the PET as a result of the packaging procedure - a spacer block is heated
to bond a poly-isobutylene edge seal and cooling results in a slight vacuum and curvature
in the PET, causing tensile stresses in the SnOx barrier.

 We sought alternative methods to initiate ozone nucleation for researchers without
access to an in-situ ozone source on an ALD system. One common tool in device
fabrication laboratories is a UV-Ozone (UVO) cleaner, capable of generating ozone in the
presence of atmospheric moisture. To test UVO for ex-situ ozone nucleation treatment,
samples were removed from the ALD chamber after capping layer formation and then
treated with the UVO for 60 s. Samples were reloaded in the ALD system to complete the
15 nm SnOx barrier. Our experiments show that the ozone and ambient moisture within
UVO tool adds oxygen functionalization comparable to in-situ ozone treatment via XPS,
seen in Figure S2b. Optical pinhole analysis of DMF penetration testing on barriers
formed with ex-situ UVO nucleation treatments is shown in Figure S10. It is found that a
60 s ex-situ UV-ozone treatment provides equivalent nucleation and barrier behavior to
the 15 s in-situ treatment with 99.3% film retention. This ex-situ approach is a viable
alternative processing method for ozone nucleation.

 While performing these experiments, it was observed that exposure of thin film
devices to ambient air is also capable of functionalizing the fullerene surface. It was found
that samples left in ambient lab air prior to ALD SnOx deposition had improved DMF
barrier behavior relative to control samples handled under inert without any ozone
nucleation treatment. We postulate that atmospheric moisture and background ozone
sources could be responsible for addition of oxygen and C-O bonds to the fullerene
surface, see XPS data in Figure S2a. Similar observations are reported by Tiwari et al.
with fullerene aerosols, which are comparable fullerene thin films.42 Nucleation treatment
by 60 min of air exposure is barrier benchmarked against in-situ ozone nucleation via a
DMF penetration test in Figure S10. While the solvent durability of the ambient-exposed
ALD film is markedly improved over air-free controls, the comparison to ozone-treated
SnOx films shows that the air treatment does not reach the surface saturation levels
achievable with deliberate ozone treatment. Rather, air exposure for 60 minutes is
equivalent to approximately 25% of a saturation dose (4-5 s vs 15 s O3). We note that
researchers loading devices into their ALD reactors under ambient conditions may be
unintentionally modifying the fullerene layer during this exposure. This effect, along with
variability in the local atmospheric ozone content, and potentially humidity, might well be
the source of mixed reports of the post-processing durability of ALD SnOx on fullerene.

While this method does modestly improve ALD seeding and resulting barrier properties,
utilizing ambient exposure to improve nucleation may result in the adsorption of
atmospheric moisture with long-term stability implications.43,44

Device Integration

 To show the universality of ozone treatment, we demonstrate this process on four
different perovskite semiconductors, including FA0.6Cs0.3DMA0.10Pb(I0.8Br0.2)3 (wide band
gap – 1.73 eV), FA0.7MA0.15Cs0.05Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3 (intermediate band gap perovskite –
1.58eV), and FA0.76Cs0.25Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 (low band gap – 1.25 eV) in p-i-n single junction
devices and Cs0.3FA0.7PbI2.1Br0.9/FA0.6MA0.4Pb0.4Sn0.6I3 (1.75 eV/1.25 eV) in all-
perovskite tandem solar cells from recent reports.13,45–47 Ozone nucleation treatment on
the 1.58 eV and 1.73 eV compositions were performed in-situ while the ozone nucleation
treatment for the 1.25 eV and tandem devices were performed ex-situ in a laboratory UV-
ozone cleaner. ALD SnOx deposition temperatures on the 1.25 eV low band gap devices
were decreased from 90 °C to 80 °C due to thermal sensitivity of the device stack. Figure
4 shows the device power conversion efficiency (PCE) distributions as median and
quartiles for control ALD SnOx (no ozone treatment) and ALD SnOx with an ozone
nucleation treatment applied to a 40 cycle ALD SnOx capping layer. A full reporting of
device figure of merit statics is shown in Table S4.

 All devices utilizing the in-situ ozone nucleation treatments show a measurable
improvement in the median power conversion efficiency. Of the primary device figures of
merit in single-junction devices, we see that this improvement is mainly driven through a
stabilization of the fill-factor (FF), and partially through increases in open circuit voltage
(Voc), but the general narrowing of the performance spread goes beyond any single
performance metric.

 No significant efficiency improvement was observed with ozone nucleation
treatments in the widegap compositions, however, importantly, no interfacial damage was
detected. Figure S11 shows that the ozone-treated barrier layer does not induce
hysteresis or SPO instabilities in the intermediate-gap FAMACs devices. Furthermore,
the nearly identical champion performances suggest that the transport properties of the
fullerene are not negatively affected by limited surface oxidation as a result of the reported
ozone nucleation process. On these FAMACs devices, ozone nucleation did not impact
the median Fill Factor of 83 %, but narrowed the Q1/Q3 spread from 2.9 % FF to 0.7 %
FF. This FF spread improvement may be attributed to the increased homogeneity of the
ozone-treated ALD SnOx contact. Ozone nucleation treatment on low-gap tin lead
materials resulted in nearly a doubling of the device efficiency. This is significant on the
low-gap materials as Sn-Pb perovskite compositions are known to be highly sensitive to
tin oxidation and demonstrates the efficacy of the capping layer at protecting the
underlying perovskite interface. We attribute the relatively low initial PCE on control
devices to un-optimized tin oxide at low (80 °C) deposition temperatures. At these
temperatures, the ozone dose may further react with the tin oxide capping layer to form
a more stoichiometric contact near the fullerene interphase. A similar result is observed
in the all-perovskite tandem devices, however, a significant factor in the tandem device

Page 19 of 47

yields was the reduced solvent penetration when integrating the two subcells. Here, we
believe the solvent barrier properties enabled the high-yield integration of subcells with
figure of merit improvements on the short circuit current density and open circuit voltage.
Through the incorporation of in-situ ozone treatment in the recombination junction of an
all-perovskite tandem solar cell, we report an improved median device performance from
22.3 % to 23.7 % with a champion efficiency of 24.2 % PCE.

Conclusions
 We demonstrated that the deposition of an ultra-thin oxide layer can enable the
use of strong oxidizers on top of perovskite thin films and that ozone can be introduced
in-situ or ex-situ to promote nucleation on soft materials, specifically fullerene, to improve
the electronic uniformity, density, and barrier properties of ALD-grown oxide layers. These
barriers reduce transmission of water vapor, halides, and solvent. The universality of this
process is shown across four perovskite device stacks: 1.73 eV
FA0.6Cs0.3DMA0.10Pb(I0.8Br0.2)3, 1.25 eV FA0.76Cs0.25Pb0.5Sn0.5I3, and 1.58 eV
FA0.7MA0.15Cs0.05Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3 single-junction p-i-n architectures as well as 1.75 eV
FA0.7Cs0.3Pb(I0.7Br0.3)3 / 1.25 eV FAMASn0.6Pb0.4I)3 monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar
cells. Devices showed no performance loss, with an overall improvement in fill factor and
tighter performance distributions. These results provide a scalable, gas phase, in-situ
process for the formation of robust internal barriers in perovskite devices.

 An important feature of this vapor-phase treatment is that it does not require line-
of-sight and is effective on highly textured perovskite films. Furthermore, the ozone
treatment can be performed in-situ relatively quickly (~ 15 seconds) and could be scaled
using multi-zone spatial ALD systems for high-throughput manufacturing.48 Only a single
ozone treatment zone would be needed for a continuous deposition process and could
be integrated into production spatial ALD tools, enabling ultrathin, conformal, ALD SnOx
internal barriers for perovskite devices at commercial scale.49

SUBSECTION 10-2 – How Non-Ohmic Contact-Layer Diodes in Perovskite Pinholes Affect
Efficiency and Reverse Bias Stability of Solar Cells

Introduction
 Metal-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as a promising technology for an
efficient, yet inexpensive solution to harvesting solar energy. Significant progress has been made
in improving their power conversion efficiencies (PCE’s), bringing them on par with silicon solar
cells (> 26% PCE).1 Efficient cells can be made using inexpensive rapid, low-temperature solution
processing with bandgaps ideally suited for tandem cells with silicon.2–7 Significant progress has
also been made toward making the cells sufficiently stable under light and heat for commercially
relevant operational lifetimes. However, a potential result of using these inexpensive solution-
based processing methods is the formation of pinole defects within the thin-film layers. Pinholes
are easy to form in the liquid phase and pose a challenge to achieving reliable and stable solution-
processed PSCs.8–15 One type of defect, relevant to this work, is where the perovskite absorber
layer is either thin or completely missing. This results in direct contact between the P- and N-type
transport layers.

Although significant progress has been made towards commercialization, a crucial and often
overlooked aspect of PSCs is their instability under reverse bias. In general, a series-connected
solar cell will experience reverse bias during partial shading events to pass the current of the
illuminated sub-cells. Forcing a solar cell into reverse bias may result in thermal damage due to
localized current flow, i.e. “hot-spots”, as observed in other thin film solar technologies 16–18. To
address this issue, manufacturers incorporate bypass diodes at the end of a substring of series-
connected cells so that the current can flow around the shaded cell, bypassing the string17.
Unfortunately, the reverse bias potential needed to cause localized current flow in PSCs is often
lower than the open-circuit voltage (VOC) of a single solar cell.19 Integrating bypass diodes into a
thin-film module to protect such a system would require one diode per cell, which may be cost
prohibitive.20

PSCs generally display two types of electrical responses when stressed under reverse bias. From
here on, we will refer to any process that allows current flow in reverse bias as "breakdown,"
without implying that it results from any single mechanism or causes damage to the cell. For the
first type of breakdown, the current begins to gradually increase once the reverse voltage surpasses
approximately –4 V.21 Thermal imaging of this type of breakdown shows that the cells heat almost
uniformly across the active area, indicating that current flows through the active region with only
minor variations – likely due to small lateral ohmic voltage drops across the transparent conductive
oxide (TCO) electrode In contrast, the second type of breakdown involves large, sudden current
surges, marked by a distinct discontinuity at reverse voltages as low as |-0.5| V (often before
reaching |-2| V). Thermal imaging reveals small, localized spots where the temperature rapidly
increases, i.e., forms hot-spots. The formation of hot-spots during breakdown often results in the
Page 29 of 47

solar cell being permanently shunted and no longer capable of functioning as a diode. Although
hot-spots have been documented by several research groups, a general consensus on this hot-spot
mechanism has not been reached within the community. So far, this behavior has largely been
suggested to be caused by redox reactions which form metallic shunts that can occur anywhere in
the solar cell, even in a perfect initial cell.22

In this manuscript, we investigated the hot-spotting behavior observed to occur at low reverse bias
potentials (<|-2| V) that is related to pre-existing pinholes or other imperfections within the
perovskite absorber layer. We use microscopy, electroluminescence imaging, and thermal imaging
to provide evidence that these pinhole-type defects result in the direct contact of P- and N-type
contact materials to form a secondary, unintended diode within the perovskite solar cell’s circuit,
a model which has been described previously by Kaienburg et. al.27 To verify and better understand
the non-Ohmic behavior of these types of defects, we fabricated perovskite-free transport-layer
diodes and electrically characterized them under forward and reverse bias conditions similar to a
PSC. We then used these results to develop a circuit model to describe their potential impacts
within an “ideal” PSC. For most architectures, we find that the parasitic effect on performance
under forward bias is relatively small; they only reduce the cell’s maximum output power by
~0.03% PCE at coverages less than 1% of the total active area. Their non-Ohmic nature makes
them difficult to detect in forward bias current-voltage (JV) measurements because they do not
pass current near the zero-voltage point of the solar cell’s JV curve, as an Ohmic shunt would.
This observation undermines a common assumption that surface recombination dominates voltage
losses in PSCs. Remarkably, although they may degrade the forward-bias performance of the solar
cell in a relatively mundane way, in reverse bias they can cause rapid, explosive, and irreversible
breakdown, even under modest reverse bias (< |-1.5 V|). We found that these abrupt breakdowns
at pinhole sites create self-propagating and low-resistance Ohmic shunts via Joule heating to form
hot-spots. An atomic-layer-deposited (ALD) SnOx/fullerene electron transport layer (ETL) in
conjunction with TCO electrodes helps mitigates this shunt propagation and hot-spotting due to its
high melting point and sheet resistance compared to metals.

Results
An Analysis of Processing Defects in The Perovskite Absorber Layer
 Pinholes are easily formed by any particle or process that breaks the wet or partially dried
film’s surface tension during solution processing. This void formation is strongly promoted by
tensile stress that arises as the film undergoes rapid volume reduction during the drying process
23,24
 . During the annealing process, residual trapped solvents heat and rapidly vaporize. The
pressure of expansion forces the formation of voids as the solvent gases escape. In solution
processing, void formation is the mechanism by which both stresses can be relieved.25 We used
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and laser-scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) to survey

the surfaces of both bare perovskite films and complete devices to ascertain the size, shape, density,
and source of any visible defects. Analysis of these surfaces revealed numerous unique solution-
processing pinhole-type defects that can be initiated by foreign particles, substrate imperfections,
or other random processes. From this analysis of the surface, we identified the origins of at least 5
process-related defects: 1) bubbles that are either native to the solution or trapped by
craters/debossed defects on the underlying substrate (Figure 1A); 2) substrate defects such as
indium tin oxide (ITO) “mountains” and/or particles trapped in the hole transport layer (HTL)
layer (Figure 1B,C); 3) foreign particles landing on the surface of a wet film, causing the film to
retract around it during the drying process (Figure 1 D); 4) solution fling-off that splashed back
onto the substrate, either wet (Figure 1E), or dry (SI Figure 1) and 5) solvent craters, related to
solvent being released from its bound state during annealing, creating very small crater-like tunnels
on the surface (Figure 1F) which were most easily imaged and profiled with LSCM. While the
solution chemistry, processing method, and random nature of nucleation influences size, shape,
and density of film aberrations, we found that defects associated with the solution processing of
perovskites typically range in size between 2 μm and 200 μm in diameter – depending on the
source of the defect. Defects smaller than 1 um would not be easily detectable with the techniques
used in the current work.

Modelling the Impact of Defects on the Performance of PSCs in Forward Bias
 One of the significant consequences of non-Ohmic defects in solar cells is their effect on
extracting typical performance metrics such as PCE, VOC, fill factor (FF), and short-circuit current
(JSC) from JV scans. Traditionally, these parameters are calculated using a single-diode equivalent
circuit model. However, when non-Ohmic defects are present, the single-diode model yields RSh
and RS values that fail to account for potentially large current losses through the defect at higher
forward voltages. To address this problem, we employed a double-diode equivalent circuit to more
accurately account for these electrically active pinholes embedded within PSCs (Figure 4A). The
contributions from the PIN solar cell and the TLD represent diodes connected in parallel with their
own respective RS and RSh.

We constructed a semi-empirical model by using our experimental dark forward-bias JV curves
taken from TLDs and creating an ideal solar cell (shown in Figure 4B). This semi-empirical model
was motivated by Kaienburg et. al, who also investigated the impacts of directly contacted
transport layers in PSCs.27 In our semi-empirical model, we modeled an ‘ideal’ solar cell, since we
cannot guarantee the absence of TLDs or other defects within our own cells, to serve as a reference.
We adjusted the dark saturation current (J0) and ideality factor (n) to set the ideal solar cell’s VOC
to approximately +1.2 V. The JSC of the ideal cell was set to 20 mA/cm2, which is roughly
equivalent to that of our traditional wide bandgap composition (~ 1.67 eV). The remaining model
parameters and the Shockley equation used to simulate the ideal solar cell are listed in SI Fig. 13).

We fit our experimental TLD data to extract the circuit parameters (J0,TLD, nTLD, RS,TLD, and
RSh,TLD) via Shockley’s equation for current through a single diode. We found excellent agreement
between the fitted and experimental data. Both the fitted model and the experimental data for each
TLD architecture (SAMs, PTAA, and pTPD with either BCP or SnOx) are plotted together with
fit residuals in SI Figure 14.

(ATOT) within the total area on the cell (0.12 cm2).

attribute this retention in PCE to the lower leakage current observed in Figure 4B. ALD SnOx is
a more effective barrier against leakage current compared to the thinner, thermally evaporated
BCP layer. Therefore, to prevent the losses in PCE caused by these TLD pinholes, we either
recommend using materials with a larger turn-on voltage (Me-4PACz) or more robust buffer layers
between the C60 fullerene and electrode.

To summarize the primary effect of these defects under forward bias: they can reduce the device’s
VOC and FF, thereby reducing the device’s PCE even if the PSC functional regions are optimized.
Without acknowledging the existence of such defects, non-ideal VOC values may be misassigned
to bulk or interfacial recombination between the perovskite and transport layers. Therefore, we
proposed an alternative equivalent-circuit model including a secondary diode to properly account
for such defects. This model demonstrates that even single pinhole 50 um radius defects can have
detrimental effects on the device’s PCE under forward bias. These forward-bias effects can be
alleviated by proper choice of the transport layers.

Identifying Hot-Spot Formation and Sparkling Behavior in Reverse Bias Near
Pre-Existing Defects

 We now proceed to connect the reverse-bias instabilities of the TLDs to reverse-bias
instabilities of the PSCs at large. We do this through sequential electroluminescence (EL) imaging,
visual imaging, and thermal imaging before, during, and after reverse biasing numerous PSCs.
Before reverse biasing the solar cells, we confirmed the presence of any pre-existing electroactive
defects via EL imaging. This technique reveals areas of the device with diminished emission that
can be identified as pinhole defects (Fig. 5A, red circles). This device and numerous others were
also subjected to JV testing under 1-Sun illumination prior to reverse biasing to record their PCEs.
The device architecture for these PIN perovskite solar cells was ITO/HTL/Perovskite (1.67eV

pTPD. The device shown in Figure 5A had a 20.8% PCE before reverse bias, highlighting that
cells can exhibit respectable PCEs even with such defects. We then performed reverse-bias testing
on the device shown in Figure 5A and 16 other devices, starting at +1.3 V, moving toward negative
voltages, and stopping the scans once the cell passed a reverse current density of ~20 mA/cm2
(Figure 5B). This current density was chosen because it matches the maximum-power-point
current (JMPP) of the solar cell under 1-Sun illumination and is the average current density that a
shaded cell must pass.

We found that over 90% of the cells began passing current abruptly at reverse biases smaller (in
magnitude) than -4 V, and many other devices passed current rapidly before -1.5 V was reached
(Figure 5B). These abrupt increases in the current densities are commonly associated with the
formation of low-resistance shunts within the solar cell. EL imaging after reverse-bias testing
revealed burn marks and material degradation at the location of pinholes that were already
identified (Figure 5C, same device as shown in Figure 5A).

Shunt Propagation via the Melting of Metal Electrodes
 When devices with metallic top contacts break down via the defect-mediated hot-spotting
mechanism, they typically lose all photovoltaic capability and form low-resistance metallic shunts.
Burn marks that are much larger than their precipitating defect are often visible to the naked eye
across the substrate, and it was even possible to observe the propagation and expansion of an initial
hot-spot site into a large defect during reverse biasing in detail using LSCM (Figure 5D), it was
also common to see handling defects such as scratches creating the same types of defects (Figure
6A).

Using both SEM and LSCM, we were able to inspect these hot-spot burn marks and observe
physical features that suggest a fast, violent, and extremely hot process occurs within these hot-
spots (Figure 6B,C). We find evidence of metal melting and large (> 5 μm) beads of the top
contact metals present within the burn marks. This beading effect is a characteristic property of
the metals typically used in PSC fabrication (Ag, Au, Cu, Al), which tend to agglomerate via
capillary mechanisms when molten.

We reason that this rapid and extreme heating is due to the massive difference between the PSC
active area and the electroactive defect area; the current density carried during these breakdown
events will be extremely high. Simple calculations reveal that such TLD regions may pass three
orders-of-magnitude larger currents than is assumed from external measurements which sample
the entire cell (SI Figure 17). For example, using typical measured reverse-bias current densities
of 1 mA/cm² for the entire cell indicate that localized current densities can exceed 140 A/cm² for
a 6-μm diameter defect, and over 2800 A/cm² when the average current density for the whole
Page 40 of 47

device is 20 mA/cm², assuming the defect carries the entirety of the supplied current. For reference,
this is comparable to current densities required for arc welding of steel.

With this extremely high current density the metal likely requires only milliseconds to achieve its
maximum temperature. We observe the after-effects of materials vaporizing and melting; molten
metal and burned debris sprays outwards from these violent hot-spots and lands on the surrounding
area (Figure 6B-D), 0.5-5 μm molten metal beads (Figure 6D) are thrown out and land on the
surrounding device, appearing to pierce through the entire stack and creating a new hot-spot site
where the next defect can propagate. A cross sectional and top-down view of the mechanism can
be seen in SI Figures 18 and 19.

LSCM images and surface profiles of this defect propagation mechanism are shown in SI Figures
20 & 21 where the ITO is seen exposed in multiple craters across the surface marking where each
hot spot passed current. Previously, molten metal beads can also be seen scattered across the
surface of these burn marks across multiple device architectures and metals. SEM of defects after
reverse biasing reveals similar morphology, with beads of silver metal present at the edges of the
defect where Joule heating would be the highest (Figure 6E).

  In the center of these crater-like defects we find smooth pits extending several hundred nanometers

  temperatures exceeding 1000 oC, above the melting point of bulk silver.28 Although we cannot
  rule out chemical etching, damaged and melted ITO suggests that the very core of these defects
  reaches in excess of 1800oC, above the melting/softening point of ITO.29 This evidence, coupled
  with the fracturing and spatter patterns surrounding these burns is consistent with a rapid, explosive
  event akin to an arc flash (Figure 6B – D), which are known for generating explosive expansion
  and reaching incredibly high temperatures.30,31

In stark contrast to devices with metal contacts, we observed a profound lack of this hot-spot
formation and melting when we utilize the same HTL materials, but with a C60/SnOx/ITO top
contact. Instead, we observed uniform heating of the active area under both forward and reverse
bias. On only a few occasions when non-uniform heating associated with defects was observed,
these points were found to be only several degrees Celsius above the surrounding area, the cells
never shunted, and we did not see evidence of localized burning anywhere on the cell. [SI Figure
24]

We attribute the protective effect of this TCO to its relatively low conductivity and high melting
point when compared to the commonly used metal top contacts.29 The higher defect breakdown
voltage of SnOx-containing devices combined with the lower conductivity of ITO prevents the
solar cell from sending disproportionately large currents through the defect. In addition, ITO has
a melting point approximately 700oC higher than that of silver, copper, or gold.29 This means that
if localized heating and hot spots did briefly form, then the perovskite, fullerene and transport
layers would vaporize and become inactive before reaching the melting point of the electrodes.
This interrupts the observed metal melt propagation mechanism described in the previous section
and would lead to the formation of “open” shunts.

It is tempting to think that these defects can be removed or “burned out”, but this process is
destructive.32 At minimum, it creates thermally damaged “dead” areas in the cell. In cells with
metal contacts, the burn typically propagates until the solar cell is fully shunted, lowering the
resistance enough for the melting to stop.

Conclusions
 Using a combination of visual and thermal imaging techniques on PSCs before, during and
after reverse bias, we showed that irreversible breakdown in PSCs with metal contacts can
originate from pre-existing pinhole defects in the perovskite layer of fabricated devices. These
pinholes act as electrical shunts with diode-like, rather than Ohmic, electrical characteristics due
to the direct contact between the electron and hole transport layers. We demonstrated that pinholes
formed with Me-4PACz HTLs can be difficult to detect in dark JV scans, as they only slightly
degrade the cell's forward operation. However, these same microscopic defects can cause rapid,

Page 43 of 47

irreversible breakdown under even modest reverse bias. The diode-like behavior of these defects
also has significant implications for conventional forward-bias characterization.

To avoid hot-spotting at pinholes and increase reverse bias breakdown potential, researchers may
ALD tin oxide transport layers in conjunction with TCO electrodes which provide a more
thermomechanically robust extraction layer and limits current through defects due to its higher
resistance. This strategy to increase reverse bias breakdown voltage may be pushed to sufficiently
large values to enabling the use of bypass diodes in PSC modules, though it does not remove
existing defects within the device. While our work does not preclude reverse breakdown
mechanisms based on electrochemical formation of filaments, halide migration, or local PbI2
defects, it does demonstrate a much simpler and likely common breakdown mechanism that
absolutely must be avoided to push PSC technology toward commercialization. This motivates
cleaner and more uniform deposition techniques as the community attempts to develop cells with
commercially relevant stabilities under real-world conditions.
