Quantitative proteomic analysis was performed to evaluate the protein expression levels in the ovaries of goats with different litter sizes
Authors: Rui Li, Wenhao Zhang, Yifan Ren, Junmin He, Jingyi Mao, Xue Li, Guoping Zhang, Kailun Ma, Ming Wang, Fumei Nie, Kechuan Tian, Xixia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe litter size of goats is a key factor affecting their reproductive performance. The ovary plays a central role in the reproductive process of goats. However, the relationship between the dynamic changes in the ovarian proteome and the prolific traits of goats remains unclear. The Jining Grey Goat, an excellent dual-purpose meat and skin breed in China, is famous for its distinctive coat color characteristics and high reproductive performance.MethodsIt is an ideal animal model for studying goat reproductive performance. We selected 8 Jining Grey Goats and divided them into a single-birth group (n = 4) and a multiple-birth group (n = 4) to collect ovarian tissue samples. The iTRAQ technology, bioinformatics analysis, and Western blot were employed to investigate protein expression differences in ovarian tissues of goats with varying litter sizes.ResultsA total of 468 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified, of which 255 were up-regulated and 213 were down-regulated. GO enrichment analysis showed that these DEPs were mainly involved in biological processes such as cellular processes and metabolic processes, as well as molecular functions such as actin filament binding and calcium ion binding. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the focal adhesion, Oxytocin Signaling Pathway, and PPAR Signaling Pathway were significantly enriched. Protein interaction network analysis identified CAV1, FLNA, CNN1, TAGLN, and MYH11 as key candidate proteins, and Western blot verification results were consistent with the iTRAQ trends.ConclusionThis study revealed the molecular regulatory network of prolific traits in Jining Grey Goats from a proteomics perspective, providing a new insight into analyzing the genetic basis of goat fertility and serving as a reference for studying reproductive traits in other economically significant animals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13089-9.Keywords: Jining grey goat, Litter size, Differentially expressed proteins, iTRAQ

Background

As an essential economic animal species, goats provide high-quality protein and fur products for human beings. Goats play a vital socio-economic role in both low-income and high-income areas [1]. As the world’s largest producer of goat meat, China has consistently ranked among the top three globally in terms of goat meat imports over the past decade, with a substantial gap between supply and demand [2]. Domestic local goat breeds typically produce single births, and improving litter size is crucial for breeding efficiency [3]. Although key genes such as FecB and GDF9 have been identified in the study of sheep reproductive mechanisms [4–7], the molecular regulatory network of goat litter size has not been clarified; especially the association between the dynamic changes of ovarian proteome and multiple birth traits has not been systematically analyzed [8–11].

The ovary is the core organ of goat reproduction, and the regulation of ovarian gene and protein expression plays a decisive role in litter size. Miao et al. [12] compared the ovarian proteome of FecB ++ genotype Small Tail Han sheep, FecBB genotype Small Tail Han sheep, and Dorset sheep, and found that there were hundreds of differences among the groups, indicating that the regulation of ovarian protein expression plays a vital role in litter size. The ovary is not only the central place for follicular development, but also maintains reproductive function by regulating hormone secretion (such as gonadotropin, progesterone, etc.) [8–14]. Although studies have revealed the effects of some ovarian-related genes (such as GDF9 and BMP15) on litter size [15], the association between the dynamic changes of the proteome and multiple birth traits is still lacking systematic analysis. iTRAQ (Isobaric Tags for Relative and Absolute Quantitation) quantitative proteomics technology has been widely used in medicine [16], animal science [17], and other fields due to its high throughput, high sensitivity, and accurate quantitative advantages. In studies of goat litter size, ovarian proteomics offers a more precise enrichment of core pathways and key proteins directly linked to reproductive traits, such as steroid hormone biosynthesis, compared to transcriptomics alone. This approach thus overcomes the limitation of transcript-protein expression decoupling and establishes a more direct link between molecular mechanisms and phenotypic outcomes in litter size. Wang et al. [18] identified key proteins (such as CAV1) related to oocyte maturation in the ovary by iTRAQ technology. Qin et al. [19] identified differentially abundant proteins related to puberty in the ovary using iTRAQ technology, providing a new direction for studying the molecular mechanisms of multiple birth traits.

The Jining Grey Goat, mainly distributed in Jining, Heze and other cities in the southwestern part of Shandong Province, China, is a well-known indigenous goat breed with excellent phenotypic traits. Characterized by prominent features including high fecundity and early sexual maturity, it serves as an optimal animal model for investigating candidate genes associated with litter size in goats [20]. Building on the pivotal role of the ovary in reproductive regulation and the evolutionarily conserved biological functions of ovarian proteins in follicular development, steroid hormone synthesis, and cell proliferation across mammalian species, we propose a well-defined and testable hypothesis: distinct systematic differences exist in the ovarian proteomic profiles of Jining Grey Goats with high litter size (HL) versus low litter size (LL). Specifically, we hypothesize that proteins implicated in follicular development, steroid hormone biosynthesis, cell adhesion/proliferation, and calcium signaling—biological processes central to ovarian physiological function—will display significant differential expression between HL and LL goats, and these differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) will be enriched in key signaling pathways that modulate ovulation efficiency and follicular maturation.

To empirically test this hypothesis, we systematically compared the ovarian protein expression profiles of HL and LL Jining Grey goats using isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) technology, coupled with bioinformatic analyses (Gene Ontology [GO], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG], and protein-protein interaction [PPI] network analyses) and Western blot validation. The primary objectives of this study were to: (1) identify DEPs and significantly enriched signaling pathways associated with litter size in goats; (2) screen core candidate proteins and construct their molecular regulatory networks; and (3) elucidate the ovarian proteomic mechanisms underlying divergent fecundity phenotypes in Jining Grey Goats. Collectively, these findings will provide a novel molecular framework for understanding reproductive traits in goats and offer critical theoretical insights to inform the selective breeding of high-fecundity indigenous goat breeds.

Protein quantification and SDS-PAGE analysis

Total protein from the HL_Os and LL_Os groups of Jining Grey Goats was quantified via Bradford assay with three technical replicates per sample; the mean value was used for concentration calculation, and a standard curve with a correlation coefficient (R²) of > 0.99 was established, ensuring reliability for subsequent analyses.

Utilizing the previously generated standard curve, we determined the exact concentration of each individual protein sample and subsequently calculated the optimal loading volume for SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the resulting electrophoretogram exhibited well-resolved, sharp protein bands without any observable degradation. Collectively, these data confirmed that the isolated total protein met the stringent quality criteria necessary for downstream experimental procedures.

Screening of differential proteins

Based on iTRAQ labeling and LC-MS/MS analysis, proteomic profiles were generated from ovarian tissue samples of eight Jining Grey Goats. After filtering with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01, a total of 51,358 peptides were identified (Fig. 2A, B), corresponding to 7,606 expressed proteins. Using a threshold of fold change > 1.2 and P < 0.05, 468 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were screened. Compared with the LL_Os group, the HL_Os group showed 255 up-regulated and 213 down-regulated proteins (Fig. 2C). The repeatability, distribution, and fold change of these DEPs were further illustrated by volcano plot and coefficient of variation (CV) analysis (Fig. 2D). Overall, these results confirm that the data quality meets the requirements for iTRAQ-based proteomic analysis and support their use in subsequent investigations.

GO enrichment function analysis

To further analyze the functions of the 468 DEPs, GO annotation was performed using the Omicshare online platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/Home/Soft/gogsea), categorizing them into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Enrichment analysis (Fig. 4) was conducted via Fisher’s exact test (one-tailed) with BH FDR correction (corrected P < 0.05) for multiple comparisons across all GO terms, revealing that 413 DEPs were annotated in the GO database. These enriched DEPs were primarily associated with biological processes such as cellular processes and metabolic processes, as well as molecular functions such as actin filament binding and calcium ion binding.

The most significant GO terms among the top 25 are presented in Fig. 5. In the biological process category, these terms were primarily enriched in cell adhesion, cell migration, and positive regulation of cell-matrix adhesion. For cellular components, the main enrichments included the Z-disc and cytoplasm. Regarding molecular function, significant enrichment was observed in actin filament binding, calcium ion binding, and structural constituents of muscle.

KEGG pathway enrich analysis

To further investigate the signaling pathways involved in regulating the reproductive performance of Jining Grey Goats, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEPs was conducted using the Omicshare online platform. A total of 193 DEPs were mapped to 287 KEGG pathways. Using P < 0.05 as the significance threshold, 24 pathways were significantly enriched. Z-score-based bubble charts were generated to display the top 20 enriched KEGG pathways in the HL_Os and LL_Os groups (Fig. 7A, B). Enriched pathways included focal adhesion, the Oxytocin Signaling Pathway, and the PPAR Signaling Pathway, suggesting their potential involvement in reproductive regulation in this breed. As shown in Fig. 7B, among the top 15 KEGG pathways enriched in ovarian tissue, several were closely associated with reproductive traits, such as focal adhesion, the PPAR Signaling Pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction.

Discussion

Isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantification (iTRAQ), an emerging proteomic technique developed by an American biotechnology company, exhibits robust performance, simple operation, high quantification accuracy, and excellent reproducibility. It has been widely applied in plant and animal science, as well as in fundamental molecular biology research [21]. In this study, iTRAQ-based quantitative proteomics was employed to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) associated with litter size in the ovarian tissue of Jining Grey Goats. Key candidate proteins, including caveolin-1 (CAV1), filamin A (FLNA), calponin-1 (CNN1), transgelin (TAGLN), and myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11), were screened. The functional annotations and enriched pathways of these proteins provide important clues for deciphering the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying prolificacy traits in goats. By integrating with recent literature reports, our findings further substantiate both the conserved and species-specific aspects of ovarian function regulation, offering new directions for subsequent research.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that multiple differentially expressed proteins were enriched in biological processes such as cellular and metabolic processes, as well as molecular functions including actin filament binding and calcium ion binding. Regarding biological processes, the enrichment of functions related to cell adhesion and migration aligns closely with existing literature. During goat reproduction, the adhesion and migration of ovarian cells may play critical roles in follicular development, ovulation, and the formation and maintenance of the corpus luteum [22]. At the molecular function level, the enrichment of calcium ion binding functions is particularly significant. As an important intracellular second messenger, calcium ions regulate diverse cellular physiological activities [23]. Disruption of calcium signaling pathways in ovarian tissue may lead to impaired oocyte maturation, fertilization failure, and abnormal early embryonic development. The various calcium-binding proteins identified in this study provide new targets for further investigation into the specific mechanisms of calcium signaling in goat reproduction.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment of the focal adhesion and calcium signaling pathways in association with prolificacy traits. The focal adhesion pathway governs follicular cell proliferation and apoptosis through integrin-mediated interactions between the extracellular matrix (ECM) and cells. For instance, in bovine ovaries, the ECM-receptor interaction pathway modulates follicular development and ovulation efficiency by regulating the adhesive properties of the follicular extracellular matrix [24]. The focal adhesion pathway serves as a central regulatory mechanism for corpus luteum formation in goat ovaries post-ovulation. By mediating integrin-dependent anchoring to the extracellular matrix, this pathway facilitates the establishment of tight adhesive junctions among mural granulosa cells, thereby enhancing the overall mechanical rigidity of the cellular layer [25]. In this study, the differential expression of key proteins such as integrin α1 (ITGA1) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase regulatory subunit 2 (PIK3R2) further substantiates the pivotal role of extracellular matrix-mediated mechanical signaling in follicular selection. Moreover, the differential expression of calcium-binding proteins, including S100 calcium-binding protein A10 (S100A10) and myosin light chain 9 (MYL9), aligns with the previously reported mechanisms by which calcium signaling regulates oocyte maturation in ovine ovulation studies [26]. Recent studies have demonstrated that calcium oscillations play a crucial role in the activation of mammalian oocytes [27], while abnormal expression of calcium-binding proteins may impair fertilization rates [28]. Furthermore, the enrichment of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway is closely associated with energy metabolism and follicular development. In sheep, activation of PPARγ promotes lipid metabolism in granulosa cells, thereby providing energetic support for follicular development [29]. This mechanism is likely conserved and applicable to Jining Grey Goats as well. In bovine reproduction studies [30], alterations in the expression of key proteins within the focal adhesion pathway have been closely linked to follicular development and embryo implantation efficiency. The enrichment of this pathway in the ovarian tissue of Jining Grey Goats in the present study suggests its important role in regulating ovarian cell-extracellular matrix interactions and influencing follicular development and ovulation. The Oxytocin Signaling Pathway not only functions in parturition and lactation but is also involved in the regulation of ovarian function [31]. Sheep studies have revealed that changes in the expression of oxytocin and its receptor are associated with follicular development and ovulation rate [32]. Research by Salsabeel et al. [33] revealed that downstream genes of the Oxytocin Signaling Pathway regulate prostaglandin synthesis and cell proliferation through miRNA-mRNA interactions, thereby influencing lambing rate and embryo implantation in sheep. The enrichment of this pathway in the current study further corroborates its importance in the regulation of prolificacy traits in goats. The PPAR Signaling Pathway plays a significant role in lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation [34]. Porcine ovarian studies [35] have indicated that activation of the PPAR Signaling Pathway improves follicular development and oocyte quality. Its enrichment in Jining Grey Goats may be closely related to the regulation of energy metabolism and cellular functions during follicular development.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis identified CAV1, FLNA, CNN1, TAGLN, and MYH11 as key candidate proteins. In this study, the differential expression of these five proteins between high-litter size ovarian tissues (HL_Os) and low-litter size ovarian tissues (LL_Os) was confirmed by Western blot (WB). As a major component of membrane lipid rafts, CAV1 influences follicular development by regulating cholesterol metabolism and signal transduction. Our results showed that CAV1 was significantly upregulated in the HL_Os group, which aligns with findings by Bergeron Annick et al. [36] in porcine ovaries and Diouf Mame Nahé et al. [37] in bovine ovaries. The former study demonstrated that CAV1 enhances ovulation rate by promoting granulosa cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis; the latter revealed that CAV1 facilitates follicular development and ovulation through modulation of the epidermal growth factor receptor/mitogen-activated protein kinase (EGFR/MAPK) signaling pathway in granulosa cells. Moreover, in sheep ovaries, CAV1 expression level was positively correlated with oocyte maturation rate [38], suggesting its potential role in maintaining follicular microenvironment integrity via regulation of cell membrane stability. In mouse ovarian granulosa cells, deficiency of CAV1 leads to aberrant cell proliferation and apoptosis, thereby impairing follicular development [39]. These conserved patterns across species indicate that CAV1 may serve as an important regulator of mammalian fertility, with its functional role being highly conserved among different species. As a key component of the cytoskeleton, FLNA is involved in cell migration, adhesion, and signal transduction. Studies on human ovarian cancer cell lines have shown that FLNA expression levels are associated with cellular invasive and migratory capacities [40]. The expression difference of FLNA observed in the present study aligns with findings reported by Kulus Jakub et al. [41] in porcine granulosa cells, which demonstrated that FLNA mediates extracellular matrix remodeling through the integrin signaling pathway, thereby influencing the rate of follicular atresia. In murine studies [42], FLNA was found to be enriched in the cortical region and around chromosomes within oocytes. Knockdown of FLNA resulted in impaired spindle migration, abnormal polar body extrusion, and reduced rates of oocyte maturation and successful fertilization, ultimately compromising reproductive efficiency. Collectively, these cross-species findings suggest that both CAV1 and FLNA may play conserved functional roles in the regulation of mammalian reproduction.

As a key regulatory protein involved in smooth muscle contraction, the upregulation of CNN1 may influence follicular blood supply by modulating ovarian vascular tension. In the present study, the significantly elevated expression of CNN1 in the HL_Os group aligns with findings reported by Abdelnaby Elshymaa A. et al. [43] in dairy cattle. In studies on sheep ovaries [44], the expression level of CNN1 exhibits significant spatiotemporal variation across different developmental stages: it remains minimal during the embryonic period, peaks at the antral follicle formation stage, underscoring its critical role in follicular development. Additionally, murine studies have demonstrated that CNN1 deficiency disrupts the structure of the peritoneum and vasculature, compromising the homeostasis of the local ovarian microenvironment and consequently impairing follicular development and ovulation [45]. Further investigation revealed that CNN1 regulates the contractile - relaxation balance of vascular smooth muscle by suppressing myosin ATPase activity, thereby optimizing oxygen and nutrient supply within the follicular microenvironment. This mechanism may be directly associated with the formation of prolificacy traits in Jining Grey Goats. TAGLN, an actin-binding protein, participates in cytoskeletal rearrangement and cell migration. In ovarian tissue, elevated expression of TAGLN may enhance the migratory capacity of granulosa cells, facilitating follicular maturation. Studies have shown that TAGLN regulates cytoskeletal remodeling through activation of the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway [46], a process crucial for follicular rupture and ovulation. Moreover, silencing of TAGLN significantly suppresses cell invasion in human ovarian cancer cells [47], underscoring its importance in the dynamic regulation of ovarian cells. MYH11, as a core component of smooth muscle myosin, may influence luteal function through its role in mechanical regulation. The differential expression of MYH11 observed in goats with varying litter sizes in this study aligns with findings reported by Bingru Zhao et al. in sheep research [44]. Their study demonstrated that MYH11 expression was significantly higher in the ovaries of multiparous ewes compared to uniparous ewes, and its expression pattern closely corresponded with antral follicle formation and ovulation efficiency. This suggests that MYH11 may regulate the contractile tension of follicular wall smooth muscle, improve nutrient supply to follicles, and ultimately influence litter size in sheep. The MYH11 protein is distributed across multiple tissues in mice, including the cardiovascular and digestive systems, yet it shows notably high expression in ovarian tissue [48]. Moreover, MYH11 promotes spermatogenesis and participates in sperm acrosome formation through coordinated interactions with actin, subsequently involving vesicle transport and gene transcription in reproductive regulation [49, 50]. These findings offer a novel biomechanical perspective for understanding the prolificacy traits of Jining Grey Goats.

Conclusion

Based on isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantification (iTRAQ)-based quantitative proteomics, this study systematically analyzed the differential protein expression profiles in ovarian tissues between the LL_OS and HL_OS groups of Jining Grey Goats. A total of 468 DEPs were identified, among which CAV1, FLNA, CNN1, TAGLN, and MYH11 were screened as key candidate proteins. We propose that these DEPs can directly or indirectly regulate the litter size of goats. In addition, these proteins are mainly involved in biological processes closely associated with reproductive regulation, such as cell adhesion, calcium ion binding, focal adhesion, and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway. Nevertheless, further investigations are required in the future to elucidate the specific molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying the litter size of goats.

Methods and materials

Experimental animals and sample collection

This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No. IASVM-2022-003). Experimental animals were adult doe Jining Grey Goats provided by the National Jining Grey Goat Conservation Farm in Jiaxiang County, Jining City. Eight healthy adult does with comparable ages and body weights were selected and reared under a unified standardized feeding and management protocol. The does were housed in a standardized goat barn with relatively constant indoor temperature and humidity. The barn was cleaned daily and disinfected regularly; the experimental does were fed a uniform diet of hay combined with complete compound feed twice daily, with ad libitum access to water. The experimental animals were randomly divided into two groups: a high-fertility group (HL group, with ≥ 3 kids per litter for three consecutive births, n = 4) and a low-fertility group (LL group, with exactly 1 kid per litter for three consecutive births, n = 4). All does were treated with an intravaginal progesterone-releasing implant (CIDR). The CIDR was removed on day 14 after insertion, and pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) was administered intramuscularly. Estrus was detected by teaser buck exposure combined with vaginal examination. Within 48 h after confirmed estrus, the goats were anesthetized by intramuscular injection of ketamine (10 mg/kg) and xylazine (0.2 mg/kg), followed by euthanasia via intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg). Ovarian tissue samples were collected from the low-fertility group (LL group, single-kid, M) and high-fertility group (HL group, multi-kid, P), labeled as LL_OS and HL_OS, respectively. The collected tissue samples were rinsed thoroughly with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), minced, placed into 2 mL cryovials with corresponding labels, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then transferred to a -80 °C ultra-low-temperature freezer for long-term storage, pending subsequent total protein extraction and analysis.

Protein extraction

The left ovarian tissue sample from each goat and two sterile steel balls were placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. Sodium dodecyl sulfate lysis buffer L3 (SDS L3, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were added to the tube, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min. Subsequently, a 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) solution was supplemented, and the mixture was homogenized using a tissue grinder (Beijing Grinder Instrument, Beijing, China). The resulting homogenate was subjected to high-speed centrifugation at low temperature, and the supernatant was carefully collected. Additional DTT was added to the collected supernatant, which was then incubated in a water bath at 56 °C for 1 h. Next, iodoacetamide (IAM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added to a final concentration of 55 mM, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 45 min. Pre-chilled acetone was added to the protein solution at a volume ratio of 5:1 (acetone: protein solution), and the mixture was placed in a refrigerator at -20 °C for 30 min. After that, the mixture was centrifuged at 25,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting protein pellet was air-dried. An appropriate volume of SDS-free L3d lysis buffer was added to the dried pellet, and the mixture was homogenized again using a grinder (frequency: 60 Hz, duration: 2 min) to facilitate complete protein dissolution. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged once more, and the supernatant was collected as the purified protein solution.

Protein detection

The extracted protein solution was subjected to quantitative analysis using the Bradford assay. The protein concentration of each sample was determined by comparing the absorbance value at 595 nm (OD₅₉₅) of the sample with that of the standard curve. Based on the standard curve, the loading concentration and loading volume of each protein sample were calculated for subsequent sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

An aliquot of 10 µg of the quantified protein sample was mixed with 4× loading buffer at a volume ratio of 3:1 (sample: loading buffer). The mixture was heated in a water bath at 95 °C for 5 min to denature the proteins, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Vertical electrophoresis was performed using a 12% separating gel. In the initial phase, electrophoresis was conducted at a constant voltage of 80 V for 30 min to compress the samples into a single, compact band. Subsequently, the voltage was switched to 120 V (constant voltage), and electrophoresis was continued for 2 h. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained and destained for 10 min using a rapid staining-destaining kit (Beijing Kangrun Chengye Biological Technology, Beijing, China). Finally, the electrophoresis profile was acquired using a gel imaging system.

Proteolysis and iTRAQ labeling

The 100 µg protein solution was added to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and the enzyme solution was added according to Trypsin enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (µg): substrate protein (µg) = 1:20, vortexed, and centrifuged at low speed for 1 min. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, and the digested peptide solution was removed for desalination. The peptide solution obtained after desalination was freeze-dried.

The iTRAQ labeling reagent was prepared and allowed to return to room temperature. Then, 50 µL of isopropanol was added to each tube. After vortex oscillation, the tube was centrifuged at low speed for 1 min. The peptide sample was dissolved in 0.5 M Triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer, supplemented with the corresponding label reagent, and incubated at room temperature for 2 h.

LC-MS / MS mass spectrometric analysis

The lyophilized peptide samples were redissolved and injected with mobile phase A (5% acetonitrile, ACN; pH 9.8) as the loading buffer, and elution was performed at a flow rate of 4 µL/min. The end of the nanoliter liquid phase separation was directly connected to the mass spectrometer. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a Q-Exactive HF X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA).

iTRAQ data quantification

Through the combination of IQuant software (https://www.guosen.com.cn/gs/iquant/index.html) and the Mascot Percolator algorithm, 1% FDR screening was first performed at the spectra and peptide levels to ensure the significance of the identification results (PSM-level FDR ≤ 0.01). Then, according to the “parsimony principle”, the identified peptides were assembled into a set of reliable proteins. To control the false positive rate of protein identification, the Picked protein FDR strategy was employed, and 1% FDR screening was repeated at the protein level (Protein-level FDR ≤ 0.01).

Bioinformatics analysis

All the identified proteins were screened under the condition of |Fold Change| > 1.2 and P < 0.05. Omicshare online software (https://www.omicshare.com/) was used to screen for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway analysis. For this step, a threshold of P < 0.05 was set to define significantly different pathways for subsequent analysis. Finally, Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org/download.html) was used to visualize DEPs, construct a protein-protein interaction network, and perform Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis.

PPI constructs a complex regulatory network via intermolecular interactions and is extensively involved in key biological processes, including cell signal transduction, gene expression regulation, and cell cycle progression. The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) acts as a core platform for protein interaction research, enabling the prediction of protein interactions for proteins with unknown functions. As an open-source visualization tool, Cytoscape is capable of analyzing the network topology of genomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data, as well as generating graphical representations of molecular interactions. Based on the STRING database and Cytoscape platform, the interaction network of DEPs was thoroughly analyzed. Firstly, the STRING database was utilized to assess the correlation strength between proteins, and interaction data were retrieved in TSV format. Subsequently, Cytoscape was employed to construct the network map, with the CytoHubba plugin used to calculate node centrality indices. Finally, the key interaction modules and hub proteins governing kidding performance were identified.

Western blot

The samples were collected from the same ovaries used for iTRAQ-LC-MS/MS analysis, and the protein concentration was determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After adding the protein loading buffer, the mixture was boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 20 µg of total protein was subjected to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the resolved protein bands were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane.

The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk powder at room temperature for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-CNN1 antibody (1:3000, Cat. No. A3734, ABclonal), rabbit anti-CAV1 antibody (1:8000, Cat. No. A19006, ABclonal), rabbit anti-TAGLN antibody (1:800, Cat. No. A25499, ABclonal), rabbit anti-FLNA antibody (1:5000, Cat. No. A3738, ABclonal), and rabbit anti-MYH11 antibody (1:800, Cat. No. A22339, ABclonal). The membrane was washed three times with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween (polysorbate) (TBST) for 10 min each time. The primary antibodies used in this study are all rabbit-derived polyclonal antibodies against conserved epitopes of the target proteins, and the goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody has no cross-reactivity with goat-derived ovarian tissue proteins, thus being selected for specific detection of the primary antibody-antigen complex. Subsequently, the secondary antibody, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG), which specifically recognizes the Fc fragment of rabbit-derived primary antibodies, was added at a dilution ratio of 1:5000 (Cat. No. A11008, ABclonal), and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three additional washes with TBST. Finally, the membrane was imaged using a Luminescent Image Analyzer (ImageQuant LAS 500, GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden), and the band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54r, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The relative expression level of the target protein was calculated as the ratio of its band intensity to that of β-tubulin.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student’s t-test was employed to compare the relative expression levels of ovarian proteins between the single-lamb group and multiple-lamb group of goats. IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for data analysis. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
