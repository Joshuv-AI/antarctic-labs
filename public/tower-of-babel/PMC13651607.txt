Differentiation of Cotton Fabrics Using Stable Isotope
Ratio Mass Spectrometry

Jason D. Eckmann, Douglas J. Beussman

Abstract

White cotton fibers are of special
importance in forensics due
to bleached cotton’s ubiquity in the clothing industry. While
these fibers are often difficult to distinguish using conventional
chemical techniques, stable isotope ratio mass spectrometry has proven
a useful tool for their analysis. Our goal was to investigate the
potential for applying this technique to the differentiation of t-shirts
and other cotton products. Analysis was performed using an elemental
analyzer, a high-temperature conversion/elemental analyzer, and a
continuous flow isotope ratio mass spectrometer. Cotton samples of
various manufacturing origins were analyzed to investigate the homogeneity
and differentiability of the samples. Additionally, fibers of samples
originating from the same source were studied. Differences between
sewing thread and cloth in clothing samples were also analyzed. Differentiability
was determined using ANOVA tests, and visualization was achieved using
three-dimensional trivariate plot analysis. Each individual cotton
sample was found to be largely homogeneous, but samples from different
sources were distinguishable. Sewing thread and cloth fibers were
also found to be differentiable. Based on this pilot study, stable
isotope ratio mass spectrometry shows promise as a tool for the analysis
of cotton fibers, especially when multiple isotopes are used to discriminate
between samples.

Introduction

White cotton fibers are
of special importance to forensic analysis
due to bleached cotton’s ubiquity in the clothing industry
and potential use in criminal activity. Therefore, it is important
to analyze these materials for any differentiating characteristics
that could be used to connect a suspect with a fiber found at a crime
scene. In the past, various methods have been used to identify and
analyze cotton fibers. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy can discriminate
cotton from other fabrics with similar appearances.
,
 Further
IR and Raman spectroscopy analysis can differentiate fibers with different
dyes and contaminants. However, these
techniques were unable to distinguish between different white or bleached
cotton fibers. Therefore, without the differentiating chemical and
visible characteristics (such as color, texture, etc.) that allow
for the differentiation of dyed cotton fibers,
,
 different
methods need to be used to distinguish between white bleached cotton
samples. Oxygen stable isotope analysis has been shown to have potential
for the analysis of money. Research suggests
that using stable isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) to analyze
the ratios of stable isotopes in bleached cotton fibers, it is possible
to distinguish between samples based on their geographic origins. Recently, sources of stable isotope fractionation
in fibers including environmental conditions, biological source, and
industrial processing, were reviewed. Stable
isotope analysis has also shown promise for the ability to differentiate
unprocessed cotton originating in different geographic locations.

IRMS can differentiate between these cotton
samples because the
percent abundances of given elements (i.e., the ratios of heavy to
light stable isotopes) differ under various conditions. Variations in stable isotope abundances of light
elements are caused by processes that favor one isotope over another,
a process called fractionation. Carbon isotope variations in plants,
such as cotton, reflect the isotope composition of the reactant (CO2) in the ambient atmosphere around the plant, the diffusional
isotope fractionation of CO2 into the plant, and a large
biochemical/enzymatic isotope fractionation. The oxygen and hydrogen
isotope composition of plant tissues typically have strong geographic
correlations, reflecting largely geographic patterns of stable isotope
ratios of source water to the plant (typically in the form of precipitation
in which the isotope composition is affected by fractionation associated
with progressive condensation of water from the atmosphere). The plant
tissue stable isotope composition is further modified by processes
such as soil water evaporation, transpiration and the biochemical
processes associated with biosynthesis, all of which respond to local
environmental conditions. Furthermore, stable isotope ratios can change
with time, due to shifting water supplies and other environmental
factors. Fractionation may also occur as a result of manufacturing
and processing, leading to even more variation of stable isotopic
signatures.
,

Many other types of substances
have also been shown to have differing
ratios of stable isotopes based on geographic location, ranging from
olive oil and wine to human hair and illicit drugs.
−

 More specifically, cotton has been shown to have
differing stable isotope ratios based on growth stage and region of
the plant, as described above.
−

 Processed cotton has also been
analyzed and shown to have different stable isotopic signatures based
on geographic origin.

In this pilot
study, we extended this research on processed cotton
to investigate the stable isotope ratios of carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen
in commercial items that had been previously worn or used such as
cotton t-shirts, towels, and gauze. We analyzed eight cotton samples
of various known and unknown origins. We also examined three undershirts
of the same manufacturing origin, as well as the differences between
cotton fiber and sewing/stitching thread from the same shirt to test
for homogeneity of the shirts. With the small sample size of this
study, the results should be viewed as a proof-of-concept of the analytical
method.

Experimental Section

Materials

Three shirts were obtained
for analysis. Shirt A, a men’s Dockers undershirt. Shirt B
was a men’s Stafford undershirt. and Shirt C, a boy’s
Hanes undershirt. Two cotton gauze pads, Gauze A and Gauze B, were
obtained from Dukal Corp. and Dynarex Corp., respectively. Cloth A,
a sample bolt of Bleached Mercerized Cotton Twill manufactured by
TestFabrics, Inc., was also obtained for analysis. Two different towels
of unknown origins were also used as further sources of manufactured
cotton.

Preparation of Samples

Cotton samples
were equilibrated under the same atmospheric conditions for 72 h to
allow for equal deuterium exchange. Samples were then weighed into
tin and silver capsules for 13C and 2H/18O analysis, respectively, and introduced into an autosampler,
which was sealed and purged with helium to avoid atmospheric contamination.
For ease of use, roughly 0.200 mg (approximately 1 mm × 1 mm)
of each sample was cut and placed into each capsule. To demonstrate
that smaller forensic samples can be used, samples of roughly 0.200
mg, 0.100 mg, 0.050 mg, and 0.020 mg were cut from the same sample
and analyzed. No statistical differences in stable isotope ratio results
were observed in the 0.200 to 0.050 mg samples, with some differences
observed in the 0.020 mg samples. Thus, at least for our samples,
the smallest sample that should be used was determined to be 0.050
mg (approximately 1 mm of a single thread).

The results of the
trials are expressed as δ-values, which are numerical representations
of the ratio of the heavier isotope to the lighter isotope of a given
element in comparison with international stable isotope standards:
VPDB (Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite) for 13C analysis and VSMOW
(Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water) for 2H and 18O analysis. These values are derived from , where R
sample is the ratio of heavier to lighter isotopes in the sample, and R
standard is the same measured ratio of the corresponding
reference material.

13C Stable Isotope Analysis

Samples (∼0.200
mg) were weighed out and placed into tin capsules
for analysis of their carbon stable isotope ratios. The samples were
analyzed using a Costech ECS 4010 elemental analyzer (EA) in combination
with a ThermoFisher Scientific Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass
spectrometer. The EA contained a combustion tube (1020 °C) that
was filled with quartz wool, chromium oxide catalyst, and silvered
cobaltous/cobaltic oxide, as well as a reduction tube (650 °C)
filled with quartz wool and copper wire. To remove water from the
postcombustion gases, a water trap filled with magnesium perchlorate
was used. Two reference materials, sucrose (IAEA-CH-6, δ13C = −10.45 ± 0.03‰; NIST, Gaithersburg,
MD) and asparagine (δ13C = −24.45 ± 0.16
‰; in house standard), were used for two-point normalization
of data within each sample sequence run.

2H and 18O Stable Isotope
Analysis

Samples (∼0.200 mg) were weighed into silver
capsules for 2H and 18O analysis. A Thermo/Finnigan
high temperature conversion elemental analyzer (TC/EA) coupled to
a ThermoFisher Scientific Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer
was used to analyze the samples. The ceramic reactor tube in the TC/EA
was held at 1450 °C, and was packed with a graphite tube, glassy
carbon tube with glassy carbon granulate, and silver and quartz wool.
The temperature of the GC column was set to 90 °C with a He flow
rate of 95 mL/min. Two keratin standards, kudu horn (KHS, δ2H = −35.3 ± 1.1 ‰, δ18O = 21.21 ± 0.17 ‰; USGS, Reston, VA) and caribou hoof
(CBS, δ2H = −157.0 ± 0.9 ‰, δ18O = 2.39 ± 0.13 ‰; USGS, Reston, VA), as well
as coumarin (δ2H = 82.3 ± 1.2 ‰; Indiana
University, Bloomington, IN) were used for normalization of the 2H data within each sample sequence run, with benzoic acid
602 (BA602, δ18O = 71.28 ± 0.36 ‰; IAEA,
Vienna, Austria) replacing coumarin for normalization of the 18O data. The kudu horn standards were used as check standards
to verify instrument performance with the other standards used for
two-point normalization. It should be noted that the presented δ2H values do not represent true nonexchangable hydrogen values
and thus the reported values should not be compared to other results
not part of this study. As all hydrogen measurements were collected
under the same conditions and at the same time, a comparison of the
results with the study presented here is still valid for determining
differentiability between samples.

Experiments

Eight Cotton Types

Eight different
cotton samples were analyzed. Four fibers from each sample were taken
from different regions of the source to check for homogeneity and
to ensure the data was representative of the whole sample. A total
of three batches were run over the course of 3 days, for a total of
12 data points from each cotton sample.

T-Shirts
of Identical Origins

Three
shirts from the same batch and packaging were analyzed for homogeneity.
Two different regions of each shirt were tested, with three fibers
from each region being analyzed for a total of six samples per shirt.

Sewing/Stitching Thread Versus Cloth

Three samples of sewing thread from three different regions of stitching
(9 total), as well as three samples of cloth each from a different
region of cloth (3 total), were taken from shirts A, B, and C, and
analyzed for isotopic signatures.

T-Shirt
Homogeneity

Three samples
were taken from four different regions of shirt (front and back panels,
left and right sleeves) for a total of 12 samples per shirt. This
process was repeated for shirts A, B, and C to test for homogeneity
of different regions of the shirts.

Each of the procedures outlined
above for the four experiments (2.5.1–2.5.4) were repeated
twice; once for 13C isotopic analysis and a second time
for 2H and 18O stable isotope analysis.

Data Compilation and Analysis

Data
was collected across multiple days to ensure reproducibility of results.
Raw data was obtained from the proprietary software (Isodat 3.0) and
exported to Microsoft Excel. These values were then normalized using
the standards for each stable isotope, and averaged to obtain a representative
δ-value. After determination of averages and standard deviations,
ANOVA tests (at the 95% confidence level) were performed to determine
p-values demonstrating the differentiability of each sample. Data
was compiled into a three-dimensional trivariate plot in Wolfram Mathematica
for visualization. Averages were treated as points with the standard
deviations for each of the three isotopes extended in the x, y, and z directions
and covered in a surface, creating an ellipsoid around the central
average.

Results and Discussion

Eight Cotton Types

δ13C values range
from −27.2 to −25.0, while δ2H and
δ18O values varied more, ranging from
−64.7 to −12.0 and 21.9 to 33.8, respectively (). P-values show that all eight types of cotton are statistically different
in at least one of their δ13C, δ2H, or δ18O values (). In many cases, δ2H values
seem to be the least useful due to their large standard deviation,
but are still suitable for differentiation in the majority of cases.

A three-dimensional scatter plot
shows the clear differentiability
of the data (). The space filled by each of the eight different types of cotton
is isolated from all sides, demonstrating the unique isotopic signature
of each sample. Furthermore, rotation of the plot in three dimensions
reveals the isotopic differences between each type of cotton even
more clearly. Such clear differentiability between each of the samples
tested is of significant forensic importance, as a fiber with a given
isotopic signature may be correlated to a source with a significant
level of certainty in each of our samples. Further analysis of a larger
cohort of sources would demonstrate if this finding is generally applicable
to all cotton shirts and other products.

T-Shirts
of Identical Origins

When
three t-shirts from the same package were analyzed, the δ13C, δ2H, and δ18O values
for all three shirts were statistically indistinguishable (). Standard deviations
were also extremely low, indicating the homogeneity within shirts
of identical origins. Presumably, shirts that are packaged together
were manufactured by the same company in the same location at the
same time, with cotton from the same source, leading to the observed
similarities. From a forensic standpoint, these results could allow
for the testing of any shirt that comes from the same suspected batch
as the crime scene sample. This ability could prove to be a useful
tool in the event a suspect has disposed of the original shirt in
an effort to conceal evidence, since other shirts from the same packaging
could equally demonstrate a potential link between suspect and crime.

Sewing Thread Versus Cloth Fiber

Sewing thread and
cloth fibers are differentiable for all three shirts
tested (). This
presumably arises from the use of different cotton sources for cotton
thread and cotton cloth. Such distinct variation between the two parts
of the shirt illustrates the importance of testing both sewing thread
and fiber when carrying out forensic analysis. Careful analysis of
both types of fiber would help to avoid a false-negative correlation
originating from a δ-value from the wrong component of the shirt.

A three-dimensional
scatter plot illustrates the differentiability
of sewing thread and the cloth fibers from each shirt (). It also reveals that the
fibers are grouped tightly in very different regions of the plot,
with the sewing thread in the bottom left at low δ2H and δ18O values, and the cloth fibers grouped
in the upper right at higher δ2H and δ18O values. This is not surprising as sewing thread is often
made from synthetic material or a cotton-synthetic blend due to increased
strength of synthetic materials.

T-Shirt Homogeneity

All three shirts
show almost complete homogeneity in all three stable isotopes. This
demonstrates the homogeneity of the cotton material used in the shirt
manufacturing process, which is important for the matching of a fiber
found at a crime scene to a shirt in the possession of a suspect.
However, one shirt exhibited isotopic differences between the back
panel and the two sleeves (). This discrepancy between different parts of the
shirt could arise from different bolts of cloth being used for each
part of the shirt, with the various parts later stitched together.
This implies that in an IRMS forensic investigation, fiber samples
from each separate part of the shirt (front panel, back panel, sleeves,
pocket, etc.) should be analyzed and the stable isotope ratios of
each part compared to the evidence in question.

Conclusions

In this initial study, we investigated the potential for using
stable isotope ratio mass spectrometry for forensic analysis of cotton
fibers originating from t-shirts and other common cotton products.
We found δ13C, δ2H, and δ18O values to all be useful in this analysis. We demonstrated
the differentiability of eight cotton samples based solely on isotopic
signatures. We also showed the homogeneity of sources from identical
origins, and determined that sewing thread and cloth from the same
shirt have distinct isotopic signatures as might different cloth sections
of the shirt. These last findings demand caution for forensic investigations,
as different fibers from a shirt could lead to an erroneous negative
correlation between suspect and crime. With the relatively small number
of samples included in this preliminary study, care must be taken
to with respect to the ability to extrapolate results to all fabric
samples. Future studies should incorporate larger numbers of fabrics
in order to increase the statistical power of the presented method.
Dyes and other cloth treatments should also be investigated to determine
their effects on stable isotope ratios. Finally, cotton-polyester
and other cotton-cloth blends should be studied to establish the effects
of other fibers on the stable isotope ratios and differentiability
of samples.
