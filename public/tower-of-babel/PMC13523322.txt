Background

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounts for over 80% of all thyroid cancer cases and is the primary driver of the sharp rise in thyroid cancer incidence [1]. Despite its typically favorable prognosis, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 90%, up to 30% of patients may develop persistent or recurrent disease [2, 3]. These recurrent lesions tend to be more aggressive and may be linked to increased long-term mortality, particularly in high-risk patients [4]. Reoperation remains the mainstay of treatment or persistent or recurrent PTC. However, reoperation outcomes are highly variable. Biochemical remission rates following reoperation range from 26.8% to 63.5%, while re-recurrence rates reach 16% to 27% [5–7]. Given this marked heterogeneity, appropriate risk stratification tools are urgently needed to optimize clinical decision-making and resource allocation.

While the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guideline offers a well-established framework for recurrence risk stratification after initial surgery, there are no standardized criteria for assessing the risk of re-recurrence following reoperation [8]. This gap has led to postoperative adjuvant therapies often being empirical and controversial, including radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment [9]. Current studies on reoperation outcomes are largely retrospective and single-center, relying on conventional statistical methods to identify risk factors [10, 11]. Although several studies have reported associations between reoperation outcomes and individual factors such as age, tumor size, and reoperative lymph node metastasis (LNM), no clinically applicable predictive model has yet been established [12].

The complexity and multistage nature of these influencing factors highlight the need for more advanced analytical approaches. Machine learning (ML) algorithms offer a promising solution by modeling high-dimensional, nonlinear interactions among variables to deliver more accurate and personalized risk stratification [13]. Notably, the ATA dynamic risk stratification system has emerged as an effective tool for predicting long-term outcomes after reoperation, with excellent response status being strongly associated with favorable prognosis [14].

Therefore, we aimed to develop and validate an interpretable ML model to predict incomplete response after reoperation in recurrent or persistent PTC, as defined by the latest ATA guidelines [15]. This model is intended to facilitate early risk stratification and support individualized decisions regarding postoperative treatment and surveillance.

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective study conducted using data from patients who underwent reoperation for recurrent or persistent PTC in Wuhan Union Hospital (WHUH) between January 1, 2012, and January 31, 2024 (Fig. 1a). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) total thyroidectomy performed; (2) pathologically confirmed recurrent or persistent PTC. The exclusion criteria included: 1) age < 18 years;2) missing clinical data; (3) receipt of targeted therapy; (4) distant metastasis; (5) coexisting with other malignancies. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology ([2020]0340-01). The entire study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the study.

Data collection and preprocessing

Data were extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records (EMR) during hospitalization. Variables included: (1) basic information; (2) initial treatment details; (3) preoperative assessment before reoperation; (4) reoperation outcomes (Fig. 1b). During preprocessing, outliers and missing values were addressed. Key metrics related to surgical quality were included, such as the number of lymph nodes harvested (LNY), lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the ratio of positive lymph nodes to total lymph nodes, and the number of positive lymph nodes (PLN). Based on the preprocessing, inflammation-related factors were derived, including platelet-to- lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), eosinophil-to- lymphocyte ratio (ELR), basophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (BLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as SII = platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count. The dataset was then randomly split into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio.

Feature selection

Potential predictors were initially identified through univariate logistic regression analysis (Fig. 1c). To address multicollinearity, the LASSO method was applied for feature refinement. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was then employed to enhance model stability and performance. This process included 10 rounds of 10-fold cross-validation to identify the most relevant predictors for each machine learning model. The selection process for each model is shown in Fig. S1. The relative importance of the top 10 variables in each model is presented in Fig. S2.

Assessment of study outcomes

1) Response to Therapy Classification: Categorized according to the 2015 ATA guidelines as excellent response (ER), indeterminate response (IDR), biochemical incomplete response (BIR), and structural incomplete response (SIR) [8]. ER is defined as negative imaging and suppressed thyroglobulin (Tg) < 0.2 ng/mL (or stimulated Tg < 1 ng/mL), while the other three categories are classified as Non-ER status.

2) Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS): Monitored during follow-up after reoperation. Recurrence or persistent disease was defined as locoregional lesions identified by fine-needle aspiration, reoperation, or distant metastasis confirmed by radiography.

Model development and performance comparison

Twelve machine learning models were used to predict reoperation outcomes in PTC: logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), naive bayes (NB), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), stochastic gradient boosting (SGBT), neural network (NNET), elastic net (ENET), gaussian process (GP), and random forest (RF) (Fig. 1d). Hyperparameters for each model were optimized using 10-fold cross-validation with a grid search on the optimal feature subset, utilizing the default settings of the ‘caret’ package.

Model performance was evaluated using common metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1 score. Additionally, calibration curve analysis was conducted to assess the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) measured the clinical utility by calculating net benefits at varying threshold probabilities. Re-recurrence risk stratification, based on the best-performing ML model, was tested using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test.

Model explanation and web calculator

To enhance model interpretability, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which ranks the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction ability. Both global (feature-level) and local (patient-level) explanations were provided. The final model was integrated into a Shiny-based web calculator to allow clinicians to input relevant patient data and receive individualized predictions regarding reoperation outcomes.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.0 software. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) due to the non-normal distribution. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify independent risk factors for the entire cohort. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 670 patients with recurrent or persistent PTC from WHUH were included in this study. These patients were followed for a median of 53.0 months (IQR, 27–83 months), with 2 cancer-related deaths occurred during follow-up. According to their response to therapy classification in the last follow-up after reoperation, patients were categorized into ER (n = 259) and Non-ER (n = 411) groups. Among the 411 Non-ER patients, 198 (48.18%) were evaluated as IDR, 115 (27.98%) were BIR, and 98 (23.84%) developed SIR.

The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are shown in Table 1. The ER group and the Non-ER group exhibited significant differences in numerous clinicopathological features throughout the different stages of diagnosis and treatment. We found that Non-ER patients presented with larger total tumor sizes (2.4 vs. 1.7 cm, P < 0.01), more lymph node metastases (7 vs. 4, P < 0.01), and received more postoperative RAI treatments (P < 0.01) compared to ER patients in the primary surgery. Additionally, during the preoperative assessment, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR: 2.07 vs. 1.87), the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII: 435.6 vs. 395.6), neutrophil count (NE: 3.08 vs. 2.95), and total cholesterol (TC: 4.53 vs. 4.40 mmol/L) in the Non-ER group were significantly higher than those in the ER group, while the Tg level before reoperation in the Non-ER group was significantly higher than that in the ER group (3.86 vs. 1.30 ng/mL). For all variables, the data distributions were comparable between the training set and the validation set, with p-values all > 0.01.

Independent risk factors

We explored the independent risk factors for patients exhibiting incomplete response after reoperation based on the entire cohort. Using univariate logistic regression analysis, 30 potential risk factors associated with Non-ER were identified (P < 0.1). After multivariate logistic regression analysis, 14 factors independently associated with the risk of Non-ER in reoperation were finally identified, namely, age, body mass index (BMI), total tumor size, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), primary positive lymph node (PLN), primary lymph node ratio (LNR), intraglandular dissemination, initial surgery performed at a tertiary hospital, primary RAI, Tg level before reoperation, SII, site of suspicious LNM, reoperative LNR, and recurrent lesion size (Table S1).

To further investigate the relationships between Non-ER and the continuous variables in the independent risk factors, we plotted the dose–response curves of BMI, total tumor size, primary PLN, primary LNR, Tg level before reoperation, reoperative LNR, and recurrent lesion size (Fig. 2a). Meanwhile, Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) method was used to explore possible nonlinear associations and identify key knots. Primary PLN and reoperative LNR showed nonlinear dose-response relationships with Non-ER (both P-nonlinearity < 0.05), whereas other variables exhibited linear associations (P-overall < 0.05; P-nonlinearity > 0.05). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed using the median values of continuous variables as cutoffs, and the resulting odds ratios were visualized in forest plots (Fig. 2b). Correlation analysis showed no significant multicollinearity among the included variables (Fig. 2c).

Model development and performance comparison

Using the optimal predictor subsets selected by RFE, we developed 12 ML models (LR, DT, SVM, KNN, NB, XGB, LightGBM, SGBT, NNET, ENET, GP, RF) using 10 × 10-fold cross-validation.

Key performance metrics were compared across training and validation sets using radar plots (Fig. 3). All models achieved excellent performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.763 to 0.923 and accuracy between 0.695 and 0.832. Most models exhibited superior sensitivity (> 0.75), while NB and NNET demonstrated higher specificity (> 0.80). Calibration curves and DCA further confirmed the robustness and clinical utility of the models.

Model explanation

Given the importance of model interpretability in clinical applications, we utilized the SHAP method to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction. Both global (feature-level) and local (patient-level) explanations were provided.

As shown in the SHAP summary bar plot (Fig. 6a), the average absolute SHAP value was used to rank features by their overall contribution to the model. The top five predictors were Tg level, number of primary positive PLN, reoperative LNR, total tumor size, and recurrent lesion size. In the SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 6b), the direction and magnitude of each feature’s impact on the model’s output across all samples were visualized. Higher Tg level before reoperation, greater numbers of primary PLN, higher reoperative LNR, and larger tumor and recurrent lesion sizes were associated with an increased risk of Non-ER. For patient-level interpretation, the SHAP waterfall plot (Fig. 6c) illustrated how specific features influenced the prediction for patient #10 as an example. In this patient, a Tg level before reoperation of 16.2 contributed a notable + 0.137 to the predicted risk, while a reoperative LNR of 0.03 contributed − 0.081, indicating a negative effect on the prediction. By summing the SHAP values, the waterfall plot visually demonstrates the process by which the model’s base value 𝐸[𝑓(𝑥)] is adjusted to generate the final prediction 𝑓(𝑥) for a specific patient. These visualizations enhance the model’s clinical interpretability, supporting its use in identifying high-risk patients based on individualized risk profiles.

Validation of risk groups using RFS

To further assess the clinical utility of the final model, patients were evenly stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups based on predicted probabilities from the RF model. RFS was then compared across subgroups using follow-up data on re-recurrence and time-to-event. Cox proportional hazards regression in both training and validation cohorts confirmed that model-defined risk stratification was significantly associated with RFS (P < 0.0001; Fig. 7). These findings suggest that this ML model not only predicts ER status but also effectively distinguishes long-term re-recurrence risk after reoperation.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this study, we developed and validated twelve machine learning models using clinical and laboratory data to predict reoperation outcomes and identify risk factors for patients with recurrent PTC. To our knowledge, this is the first interpretable ML framework specifically designed for risk stratification in this population. Twelve algorithms were systematically compared to ensure optimal model selection.

In our reoperation cohort, the ER rate was 38.66%, and the re-recurrence rate was 14.63%, consistent with previous reports [16, 17]. Remarkably, this cohort represents the largest reported sample of Chinese patients undergoing reoperation for recurrent PTC to date. These results highlight the substantial variability in post-reoperative outcomes. However, the lack of a proper risk stratification system makes managing these diverse patients challenging. On one hand, without proper risk stratification, low-risk patients may be subjected to prolonged follow-up and unnecessary treatments, such as excessive TSH suppression, leading to avoidable healthcare costs and potential side effects [18]. Compared to the established benefits of initial RAI therapy, the contribution of repeated empirical RAI treatments administered before or after reoperation to long-term patient outcomes remains unclear [19–21]. On the other hand, some high-risk patients remain at significant risk even after reoperation, eventually developing distant metastases or undergoing multiple repeated surgeries, which places a heavy psychological and physical burden on them [22, 23].

To address these challenges, we used routinely collected EMR data and interpretable ML algorithms to develop a clinically applicable model for predicting reoperation outcomes in recurrent PTC. Among the twelve ML models developed, the RF algorithm demonstrated the highest predictive AUC, with strong performance in both discrimination and calibration. The RF algorithm enhances prediction accuracy and robustness by integrating multiple decision trees through an ensemble voting mechanism and capturing nonlinear feature interactions [24]. These characteristics make RF particularly applicable to clinical prediction modeling in thyroid cancer [25, 26]. In our study, the RF algorithm incorporating 13 features was selected as the final prediction model. These features are readily obtainable during hospitalization and reflect key aspects of the entire treatment course for patients with recurrent PTC.

We identified key predictors by applying multivariate logistic regression to determine independent risk factors, and refined feature selection using LASSO regularization and RFE for each ML algorithm. Notably, features related to both tumor burden (e.g., Tg level before reoperation, total tumor size, recurrent lesion size) and surgical complexity (e.g., primary PLN, reoperative LNR) consistently appeared across both regression-based and ML-based selection processes. This finding aligns with current guidelines, which include suspicious lesion size as a criterion for reoperation [27]. It also emphasizes the critical role of the initial surgical approach in patients who undergo reoperation, as adequate primary resection may avoid short-term reinterventions [28]. Supporting this, Lei et al. reported that harvesting fewer than 25 lymph nodes during initial lateral neck dissection independently predicted recurrence or persistence [29].

In addition to tumor-related features, several metabolic and inflammatory factors were retained in the final model, suggesting their potential relevance in recurrent PTC. High BMI and TC levels appeared across multiple ML models, implicating obesity as a possible risk factor. Obesity has been associated with increased cancer risk, including thyroid cancer, through mechanisms involving insulin resistance, altered thyroid hormone levels, and estrogen-related pathways [30, 31]. However, conflicting evidence also suggests a potential protective effect of obesity against LNM [32], indicating a controversy that warrants further investigation.

Inflammatory markers such as HT and SII were also identified as important features. The association between HT and PTC has been widely reported, with thyroglobulin antibody and thyroid peroxidase antibodies possibly conferring protection against metastasis [33]. Meanwhile, SII—often evaluated alongside NLR and PLR—has been shown to reflect tumor microenvironment and prognostic status [34]. Elevated SII may indicate increased tumor burden and systemic inflammation, which could partly explain its predictive value in our model [35]. This is supported by studies of Cao et al. and Hou et al., who demonstrated that elevated PLR, NLR, and MLR correlate with high-risk PTC features and distant metastasis [36, 37]. Collectively, these findings highlight the relevance of both systemic metabolic and immune-inflammatory states in influencing reoperation outcomes.

The final RF algorithm demonstrated the strongest predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.865 in the validation set, alongside favorable calibration and clinical utility, as shown by calibration curves and DCA curves. To facilitate clinical adoption, we addressed the common limitation of ML models—their ‘black box’ nature—by employing heatmaps and SHAP visualizations to elucidate the RF model’s decision-making process [38]. The SHAP beeswarm plot showed that most features influencing Non-ER aligned with odds ratios from multivariable logistic regression, supporting their clinical relevance. Furthermore, SHAP waterfall plots provided case-level insights by illustrating how individual features collectively influenced the model’s predictions—offering a level of interpretability beyond traditional statistical methods.

To evaluate the final model’s clinical utility, we conducted RFS analysis. Patients identified as high-risk by the RF-based model exhibited significantly shorter RFS. As all predictors are readily available after reoperation, the model enables early risk stratification. The web-based calculator aims to aid clinical decision-making by identifying high-risk patients early, guiding RAI treatment, and optimizing TSH suppression and targeted therapies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the cohort was relatively large and included long-term follow-up, the retrospective design inherently carries a risk of selection bias, including variation in disease severity and socioeconomic status. Second, the absence of external validation using independent cohorts limits the generalizability of our results. Third, most ML models demonstrated suboptimal specificity, which may be due to the lack of molecular biomarkers (e.g., BRAF and TERT mutations) that reflect the biological behavior of PTC [39, 40]. Despite these limitations, the final model achieved strong predictive accuracy and holds promise for clinical application. Future studies should emphasize multicenter collaboration and incorporate key molecular indicators to enhance model robustness and external applicability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed an interpretable ML model using multiphase clinical and laboratory data to predict reoperation outcomes in patients with recurrent or persistent PTC. The final RF model demonstrated strong discriminative ability and clinical applicability. Future prospective, multicentral, randomized controlled studies are warranted to evaluate whether risk stratification guided by this model can improve clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.
