A CD index guided ensemble framework for screening potentially disruptive patent candidates in artificial intelligence
Authors: Hong Li, Jie Wu, Xiaodong Xie, Yanliang Liu, Tingting Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisruptive technologies can reconfigure innovation trajectories and create new market opportunities, yet their early detection remains difficult because disruptive impact is uncertain and often becomes visible only years after invention. Building on the CD disruptiveness measure derived from citation-network dynamics, this study develops an interpretable machine-learning framework to screen and prioritize potentially disruptive patent candidates ex ante. Using IncoPat patent records in the artificial intelligence domain, we construct a multidimensional indicator system spanning technological, market, and legal signals. To ensure conceptual consistency with the CD framework, the main disruption label is defined as patents falling within the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution among patents with at least one backward citation. This definition allows disruptiveness to be assessed relative to identifiable prior art and reduces reliance on extreme CD values that may be sensitive to incomplete reference information. We train models on a time-split learning set covering patents filed from 2007 to 2021 and evaluate their ability to predict this stricter disruption label using early observable patent metadata. Among benchmarked classifiers, AdaBoost provides the most competitive screening performance after feature selection, reducing the feature space from 17 to 12 indicators. Under the main top-5% specification, the model achieves an accuracy of 0.914, a precision of 0.271, a recall of 0.431, and an F1 score of 0.333, indicating modest but nontrivial early-screening ability. Feature importance analysis highlights the predictive relevance of citation- and disclosure-related signals, including family citation activity, backward citations, and document length. The framework is best interpreted as a scalable candidate-generation tool for monitoring and expert review within the patented segment of AI innovation, rather than as a definitive classifier of realized disruptive impact.Keywords: Disruptive patents, CD index, Interpretable machine learning, Ensemble learning, Technology forecastingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Disruptive technological change is central to long run productivity growth and competitive advantage, yet it remains notoriously difficult to anticipate. When a technological discontinuity reorders performance trajectories, it can render established capabilities less valuable and reshape the basis of competition1,2. This unpredictability is especially consequential in fast moving domains such as artificial intelligence, where governments and firms must allocate scarce R and D attention under severe uncertainty. A practical implication is that decision makers need early and defensible signals of which inventions are likely to open new trajectories rather than merely extending existing ones3.

Patent data offer a scalable lens for this problem because they provide standardized, time stamped, legally grounded records of inventive activity and knowledge lineage. A long tradition in economics and innovation studies treats patents and their citation relations as informative proxies for technological impact and value creation4,5. Empirically, citation based signals correlate with economic outcomes such as market valuation, which motivates the widespread use of patent indicators for technology intelligence and strategic planning6. However, value and disruptiveness are not the same construct. Many highly cited inventions consolidate an established trajectory, while only a subset redirect subsequent innovation away from prior art.

Recent work has therefore shifted from raw impact counts toward network based measures that explicitly capture whether an invention destabilizes prior knowledge. A prominent approach is the CD index proposed by Funk and Owen Smith, which uses the structure of forward citations to assess whether subsequent inventions cite a focal patent while bypassing its predecessors, consistent with a mechanism of path creation and displacement7. Related bibliometric research links such disruption patterns to broader innovation dynamics, including differences in the disruptive roles of small versus large teams8 and long run trends suggesting that science and technology may be becoming less disruptive over time9. At the same time, disruption indicators are actively debated and refined, with studies examining convergent validity and proposing variants that address sensitivity to citation windows and related design choices10. Collectively, this literature provides an increasingly principled measurement foundation for identifying disruptive outcomes once sufficient forward citation history has accrued.

Yet the core managerial and policy challenge is ex ante prediction. The CD family of measures is inherently retrospective because it depends on future citations that emerge slowly and unevenly. This creates a timing mismatch: by the time disruptiveness can be measured with confidence, the most critical allocation decisions and strategic commitments may already be locked in. Prior patent forecasting research has developed tools to anticipate technological change using citation dynamics and other patent indicators11,12, and subsequent work has increasingly adopted supervised learning methods to classify or forecast emerging and high potential technologies from large patent corpora13. In parallel, surveys of patent analytics document rapid methodological progress in machine learning and deep learning pipelines for patent data, while also highlighting recurring concerns about label construction, feature validity, and out of sample generalization14. These advances make it feasible to pursue a predictive approach to disruptiveness, but they do not remove the central identification difficulty: a predictive model must rely on information observable near the time of publication, while its target outcome is defined by longer run citation network behavior.

This study addresses that gap by developing an operational framework for screening highly disruptive patent candidates using patent-level indicators that are observable before long citation histories have accumulated. To ensure conceptual consistency with the citation-network logic of technological disruption, the main prediction task is defined as identifying patents that fall within the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution among patents with at least one backward citation. This definition focuses on patents with explicit prior-art reference points, thereby allowing disruptiveness to be evaluated relative to identifiable predecessor technologies. It also provides a more conservative and interpretable target for ex ante prediction than extreme-value definitions of disruption. We then build a multidimensional indicator system based on the view that potential disruptiveness is jointly shaped by technological knowledge positioning, market-facing deployment signals, and legal scope characteristics. Consistent with the metadata available in patent records, the feature set covers technological indicators such as backward citations, non-patent literature references, family citation activity, IPC breadth, document length, and inventor team size; market-oriented indicators such as simple and extended family size, transfer, licensing, pledging, litigation, and applicant counts; and legal indicators that capture the breadth and layering of patent protection through first-claim length and claim counts, including independent and dependent claims.

To map these observable indicators to the disruptive label, we adopt an ensemble learning approach based on adaptive boosting. Boosting methods are theoretically grounded and practically effective because they combine many weak learners into a strong classifier by iteratively emphasizing previously misclassified observations15. In our empirical setting, AdaBoost is benchmarked against several widely used classifiers, including random forests16, to select a model that balances predictive performance and robustness. The framework is instantiated on a large scale patent sample in artificial intelligence retrieved from the IncoPat database and split along time to reflect real world forecasting: a learning set from 2007 to 2021 and an identification set from 2022 to 2024. The trained model is then used to score recent patents and to surface a set of high potential candidates for closer expert review, while feature importance rankings provide an interpretable audit trail of which indicators drive model decisions in this context.

This study makes three contributions. First, it connects disruption measurement and predictive analytics by reformulating the retrospective CD index as a supervised early-screening task, with the main focus placed on top-5% highly disruptive candidates among patents with backward citations. Second, it evaluates whether early observable patent indicators across technological, market, and legal dimensions contain useful signals for predicting this stricter disruption label. Third, it offers a time-split evaluation design and an application workflow for artificial intelligence patents that approximates real technology-intelligence settings, where only historical data are available at the time of prediction. The framework is intended to generate candidates for monitoring and expert review, not to make definitive claims about realized disruptive impact. The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the conceptual foundations of disruptive technological change and disruption measurement. Section 3 describes the indicator construction, label definition, and modeling strategy. Section 4 reports empirical results and identification outcomes. Section 5 concludes with implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Related work

Patent based measures of disruptive innovation

The concept of disruptive innovation emphasizes technological trajectories that reshape markets and competitive logics rather than simply extending existing performance frontiers. Christensen and Raynor argue that disruptive technologies open up new demand spaces and reconfigure value networks, thereby changing the basis of competition rather than merely improving incumbent offerings17. In this sense, disruptiveness is not synonymous with technical radicalness or novelty alone. It reflects the extent to which an innovation realigns search directions, organizational capabilities, and the structure of subsequent technological evolution.

Because major technological shifts are difficult to observe directly at the product or market level, patent data have become a central empirical lens for studying technological change and its economic implications. Griliches summarizes how patents codify knowledge claims, record inventor and assignee attributes, and embed citation links that can be used as proxies for knowledge flows and recombination patterns18. Harhoff et al., show that citations, family size, and opposition events correlate with patent value, which supports the use of patents as systematic indicators for technology management and policy19,20. Building on these insights, Grimaldi et al., develop portfolio level frameworks that integrate technological, legal, and market dimensions to support strategic planning based on patent information21.

Within this tradition, network based disruption metrics operationalize disruptiveness as a relational property of how later inventions cite a focal patent and its prior art. Funk and Owen Smith propose a dynamic network measure of technological change that captures how focal patents redirect subsequent search away from predecessor technologies and toward new recombination spaces7. Wu et al., show that small teams are more likely to produce disruptive work than large teams when disruption is measured using network based indicators, which highlights the structural nature of disruptive innovation8. More recently, Park et al., document that the average disruptiveness of papers and patents has declined in many fields over recent decades, raising questions about the changing balance between consolidating and disruptive research9.

On top of these empirical applications, a growing methodological literature scrutinizes the properties of disruption metrics. Bornmann et al., compare multiple disruption variants and peer assessments and conclude that disruption indicators can be sensitive to parameter choices and field specific citation practices10. Bittmann et al., analyze statistical matching procedures for comparing milestone and regular papers under disruption based metrics, illustrating how design decisions affect empirical results22. Leibel and Bornmann overview the empirical findings on disruption indices and emphasize open questions regarding robustness, interpretation, and appropriate use23.

Later studies focus more explicitly on specification uncertainty and the relation between disruption and other indicators. Leibel shows that small changes in implementation choices can lead to substantially different disruption scores, underscoring the need for systematic sensitivity analysis24. Using a large scale dataset, Guerrero-Bote and de Moya-Anegón compare the original disruption indicator DI and the DI5 variant with normalized impact, technological impact, excellence, journal quartiles, and collaboration patterns, and they find that documents with higher conventional bibliometric value do not receive higher disruption scores; reviews and short surveys, non-excellent papers, works without international collaboration, and papers published outside Q1 journals tend to display higher disruption values, and the correlations between disruption and impact indicators are small and often negative25. These findings highlight tensions between disruption and standard impact metrics and reinforce the view that disruption indicators capture a dimension of scientific contribution that is only weakly aligned with traditional measures of impact.

Research on knowledge combinations and novelty complements the disruption literature. Youn et al., demonstrate that invention is fundamentally a combinatorial process and that the structure of recombination shapes technological evolution26. Uzzi et al., show that atypical combinations of prior knowledge are associated with high scientific impact, which provides a micro level mechanism for disruptive contributions27. Wang et al., document systematic bias against novelty in scientific evaluation, which implies that unconventional and potentially disruptive work may be under recognized if evaluation relies on narrow indicators28. These insights strengthen the case for quantitative tools that can surface potentially disruptive inventions at an early stage.

In emerging and complex technological domains, patent based approaches have also been used to identify promising technologies and breakthroughs. Song et al., propose an outlier patent based framework for identifying promising technologies and demonstrate its effectiveness in retrospective and prospective analyses29. Jiang et al., construct multidimensional indicators to identify emerging technologies from a knowledge flow perspective and show that this approach captures the evolution of technological domains more accurately than single citation based measures30. Zhou et al., develop a deep learning framework for early identification of emerging technologies in the CNC machine tool field, illustrating how large scale patent data and machine learning can detect structural shifts in technological trajectories31,32.

Against this background, this study takes the disruption index as a theoretically grounded measure of disruptive potential that reflects how a patent restructures citation relationships between later inventions and prior art7. Importantly, disruptiveness as measured by the disruption index is conceptually distinct from popularity or visibility. Whereas citation counts capture the intensity of subsequent use, the disruption index reflects shifts in citation structure that signal changes in technological search trajectories. At the same time, it treats the disruption index as one component in a broader prediction framework that links disruption based labels to multidimensional patent indicators available at the individual patent level. This design is consistent with the view that disruption metrics are informative but need to be combined with richer patent analytics when they are used for early identification and technology foresight23. In this study, technology trajectories are explicitly conceptualized as citation-network lineages, where a trajectory represents a path of technological development shaped by patterns of knowledge inheritance and displacement observed through patent backward and forward citations. Under this view, trajectories are bounded within a predefined technological domain and unfold over time as citation relationships accumulate and evolve.

Machine learning prediction of high value and disruptive patents

Parallel to the development of disruption metrics, extensive research examines how observable patent attributes relate to ex post value measures and how these relationships can be modeled using machine learning. Harhoff et al., show that forward citations, family size, and opposition are associated with patent value, providing early evidence that patent level indicators can approximate underlying economic worth19. Grimaldi et al., extend this line of work by proposing a patent portfolio value analysis framework that integrates technological, legal, and market dimensions and demonstrates how such indicators can guide strategic technology planning21. Hsieh further illustrates how patent value assessment can inform commercialization strategy, highlighting the managerial relevance of indicator based valuation33.

Subsequent studies investigate portfolio level characteristics. Appio et al., analyze how patent portfolio diversity relates to firm profitability and find that both specialization and diversification can be beneficial depending on the competitive context34. Youn et al., emphasize that invention is a combinatorial process and show that the diversity and structure of recombined knowledge elements shape inventive outcomes26. These findings underline that patent value is multidimensional and that indicator systems need to capture technological quality, appropriability, and commercialization potential.

With increasing data availability, supervised learning methods have become central tools for patent value prediction35. Hu et al., construct a multidimensional indicator portfolio covering legal, technological, competitive, and scientific dimensions and use machine learning to identify potential high value patents in the integrated circuits domain36. Their results show that carefully designed indicator portfolios outperform models based on single citation measures. Wang et al., use configuration analysis to identify paths that lead to high value patents and demonstrate that different combinations of indicators can support high value outcomes37. Wu et al., propose a hybrid GAN BO XGBoost model for identifying high quality patents and report improvements in predictive performance compared with conventional classifiers38.

Other studies develop fusion schemes and ensemble models39. Deng and Zhang introduce a hybrid multi feature modeling approach that combines multiple feature sets to recognize high value patents, emphasizing the benefits of integrating complementary indicators40. He et al., apply a fusion of machine learning models to predict patent value in biomedical textiles and show that combining models improves accuracy and robustness41. Yao et al., employ Random Forest and a metaheuristic optimization algorithm to identify high value patents and illustrate how feature selection and hyperparameter optimization can further enhance performance42.

Machine learning has also been applied to predict patent lifetime and renewal behavior. Park et al., use robust ensemble algorithms to forecast patent life and demonstrate that survival related outcomes can be predicted from early patent indicators with useful accuracy for portfolio management43. Choi and Jeong develop a predictive model of patent lifetime and business potential based on machine learning and show that lifetime can serve as a proxy for commercialization prospects44. These contributions indicate that early available patent attributes contain rich information about long term outcomes.

Related work addresses portfolio structure and technological context. Li et al., analyze how patent portfolio structure affects patent value in global competition and find that the arrangement of technological fields within a portfolio is an important determinant of value45. Chang et al., propose a heterogeneous network message passing method for patent technology characterization and demonstrate that network based representations can capture nuanced technological relationships46,47. Hernández García et al., model patent licensing opportunities using a real option framework, showing how uncertainty and managerial flexibility influence the value of patent based projects48. Thoma and Torrisi further explore patents as real options and conduct sensitivity analysis under technological and market uncertainty49.

At a more aggregate level, patent based methods have been used to identify technology opportunities and emerging technologies. Feng et al., combine morphology analysis with USIT for technology opportunity discovery and show how structured creativity methods can be linked to patent analysis50. In later work, Feng et al., identify promising technologies in electric vehicles using patent information and machine learning, illustrating that indicator based models can support opportunity recognition in sustainability related domains51. Gao et al., integrate BERTopic with competition intensity indicators to analyze industrial robot patents and propose a framework for technology opportunity identification based on patent text and competitive dynamics52. Aristodemou and Tietze review intellectual property analytics and document how artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning methods are increasingly used to analyze IP data, including patents, for decision support in technology and innovation management53.

Despite these advances, most machine learning models in patent analytics focus on generic value, licensing, or lifetime outcomes rather than explicitly targeting disruptive potential. Target variables are typically defined in terms of citation based impact, value classes, licensing events, or renewal behavior, and only rarely distinguish disruptive from consolidating contributions36. Moreover, indicator selection in many studies is driven primarily by data availability instead of a conceptual framework of disruptiveness, and feature importance analysis is not always used to refine the indicator set40. These limitations motivate a focused framework that links a disruption based outcome variable to a carefully constructed multidimensional indicator system and employs an interpretable machine learning model to provide both predictive capability and insight into the drivers of disruptive potential.

Research gaps

Synthesizing the above literature and considering the design of this study, three research gaps can be identified.

First, most existing research treats disruption metrics as ex post analytical tools rather than as prediction labels. Studies using disruption indices focus on documenting long term trends in disruptiveness, comparing disruption across fields, and evaluating the properties of different metric variants7,8. Journal level analyses and methodological work refine disruption measurements and examine their relationships with other indicators, while also emphasizing specification uncertainty and sensitivity to parameter choices23,24,54. However, there is limited work that directly uses disruption based labels for supervised learning at the patent level using early available indicators. Empirical evidence on whether a patent’s disruptive potential, as captured by a structural citation metric, can be anticipated from its technological, legal, and market attributes at or shortly after grant remains scarce. This study addresses this gap by defining highly disruptive candidates as patents in the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution among patents with backward citations, and then using this label as the target in a supervised machine-learning setting.

Second, although many studies have developed multidimensional indicator systems for patent value prediction, these systems are rarely tailored to the specific characteristics of disruptive patents. Existing indicator portfolios typically emphasize citation intensity, family size, and technological breadth, and pay comparatively less systematic attention to legal and market attributes that are particularly relevant for disruptive innovations, such as litigation exposure, commercialization prospects, or strategic portfolio roles21,36,48. In addition, indicator selection is often driven by data availability rather than by a conceptual framework of disruptiveness, and feature importance analysis is not consistently employed to identify core indicators40. In contrast, this study constructs an indicator system that reflects the multidimensional nature of disruptive patents at the individual patent level, spanning technological, market, and legal dimensions, and uses an AdaBoost model both to predict disruption labels and to derive feature importance scores. The model output is then used to reduce an initial set of seventeen indicators to a core set of twelve, which balances predictive performance with interpretability and operational simplicity.

Third, despite substantial interest in artificial intelligence as a strategic technology domain, relatively few studies have developed domain specific frameworks for identifying potentially disruptive AI patents using structured indicators and machine learning. Existing work on emerging technologies and technology opportunities often focuses on sectors such as electric vehicles, machine tools, and industrial robots, with AI patents treated as part of broader samples or analyzed primarily through topic models and text mining29,32,51,52. Patents represent only one channel of technological change in AI, where innovation also occurs through publications, software, standards, and proprietary models. Nevertheless, AI patents provide a structured and scalable record of codified knowledge. Accordingly, this study adopts a patent-based lens to examine disruptiveness within patented AI technological trajectories. In this context, disruptiveness is defined at the intra-domain level, capturing how a focal AI patent reconfigures citation relationships within the established AI technological domain. Broader forms of disruption involving cross-domain recombination or the emergence of entirely new technological fields are conceptually related but lie beyond the scope of the present analysis. Moreover, disruptive patent studies tend to rely on generic citation network structures or qualitative assessments and do not systematically incorporate the institutional and market contexts of AI technologies, where platform effects, data access, and regulation can influence patenting behavior and the interpretation of indicator signals. By constructing training and identification datasets from an AI focused patent corpus, aligning indicator design with the characteristics of AI patents, and applying the trained model to identify potentially disruptive AI patents in recent years, this study provides a domain specific and operational framework for early identification that can support AI related technology strategy and policy.

Against this background, the present study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it integrates a theoretically grounded disruption metric based on citation structure with a multidimensional patent indicator system that spans technological, market, and legal dimensions at the individual patent level7,21. Second, it employs an interpretable machine learning model, AdaBoost, to learn the mapping from indicators to disruption labels and to distill the most informative features, thereby offering both predictive capability and insights into the drivers of disruptive potential36,40,43. Third, it demonstrates the framework in the artificial intelligence domain using temporally separated training and identification datasets, which mimics realistic ex ante screening conditions and illustrates how structured patent indicators can be used to identify potentially disruptive AI patents in a large scale national innovation context30,51.

Methodology

This study collects and preprocesses patent data from a comprehensive patent database and develops an indicator system aligned with the early observable characteristics of highly disruptive patent candidates. The main modeling task is to learn the relationship between patent-level indicators and a stricter CD-based disruption label, defined as patents falling within the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution among patents with at least one backward citation. This definition ensures that each evaluated patent has an explicit prior-art reference point and allows disruptiveness to be assessed relative to identifiable predecessor technologies. Machine-learning methods are then used to screen patents that are more likely to belong to this highly disruptive candidate group. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the study establishes a systematic research workflow for early-stage screening of potentially disruptive technologies, covering data preparation, indicator construction, model training, candidate identification, and analytical evaluation.

Research framework.

The selection of robust indicators is pivotal for the effective identification and assessment of disruptive technologies. In developing the proposed indicator framework, we synthesized the intrinsic characteristics of disruptive innovation with the applicability of established patent metrics, prioritizing indicators characterized by data accessibility and quantifiability. Prior work has shown that patent-derived metrics such as citations, family size, and claims capture distinct facets of innovation output and are correlated with both technological impact and market relevance55. Technical dimension indicators

The technical dimension encompasses six pivotal indicators: backward citation count, non-patent reference count, family citation count, IPC classification count, page count, and inventor count. The backward citation count delineates the technological scope of the target patent and the extent of its reliance on prior art; generally, a higher volume of backward citations suggests that the target patent leans heavily on existing technologies, implying a relatively lower degree of incremental novelty. The non-patent reference (NPR) count functions as a proxy for the patent’s “science linkage,” highlighting the scientific foundation and academic underpinnings of the technical solution. An elevated count in this metric indicates a robust translation of scientific knowledge into technical applications, which facilitates the realization of disruptive innovation. The family citation count is utilized to assess the interconnectedness between different technological trajectories, where an excessively high frequency may signal the presence of significant technical barriers within the relevant domain. Furthermore, the IPC classification count reflects the breadth of application and technological diversity; a greater number of categories suggests stronger cross-disciplinary potential and an enhanced capacity for technological disruption. The page count serves as a measure of complexity and the granularity of technical disclosure in the specification, with higher counts typically correlating with more complex underlying technologies and comprehensive descriptions of technical details. Finally, the inventor count reflects the scale of the research team and the degree of technological integration, effectively quantifying the intensity of human resource investment dedicated to the inventive process.

Market dimension indicators

The market dimension comprises seven indicators designed to capture commercial viability and strategic layout: simple family size, extended family size, litigation frequency, transfer count, licensing count, pledge count, and applicant count. The simple family size serves as a proxy for the patent’s strategic layout and market acceptance, where a larger family size typically signifies broader protection coverage and higher market recognition. Similarly, the extended family size reflects the breadth of market distribution; a higher count suggests robust capabilities in technological iteration and adaptive market adjustment. Litigation frequency indicates the level of market attention and competitive value, as frequent disputes often imply significant commercial stakes. Regarding economic metrics, transfer and licensing counts reflect market demand and monetization potential. Higher frequencies in these areas demonstrate strong economic value, extensive technology diffusion, and verified market application. Pledge counts further corroborate the patent’s financial and technical valuation. Lastly, the applicant count captures the intensity of R&D resource investment, the extent of collaborative efforts, and strategic operational trends.

Legal dimension indicators

The legal dimension focuses on the stability and scope of patent rights, encompassing four key metrics: first claim word count, total claim count, independent claim count, and dependent claim count. The word count of the first claim is utilized to gauge the breadth of the technical protection scope, while also implicitly reflecting the novelty of the technical solution. The total claim count represents the aggregate scope of protection and the number of technical solutions covered, serving as an indicator of patent right stability. Specifically, the independent claim count delineates the coverage of core technical solutions and their branches, highlighting the technology’s practicality and core protected content. Complementing this, the dependent claim count refers to claims that further refine independent claims. A higher number of dependent claims indicates a multi-layered legal protection structure, which enhances the patent’s stability and competitive strength by providing derivative legal value.

The systematic selection and construction of these characteristic indicators are designed to establish a multi-dimensional, comprehensive evaluation framework for the identification and assessment of disruptive technologies. By conducting a holistic analysis of these metrics, this framework aims to pinpoint technologies possessing latent disruptive potential, thereby providing a rigorous scientific foundation for technological innovation and strategic planning. Ultimately, these insights offer empirical guidance to assist enterprises and research institutions in devising tailored technological innovation and market strategies.

Measurement of technological disruption

To quantify the disruptive potential of patents, this study employs the CD (Consolidating and Destabilizing) index framework proposed by Funk and Owen-Smith7. In applying the CD index, this study defines the main disruption label among patents with at least one backward citation. This restriction is essential because disruption is measured relative to prior work. A patent without backward citations lacks an explicit prior-art reference point, making it difficult to determine whether later inventions are bypassing identifiable predecessor technologies. This percentile-based definition focuses on patents with comparatively strong disruptive citation patterns while avoiding reliance on extreme CD values that may be sensitive to incomplete reference information.

Identification of disruptive patents

Disruptive technologies, characterized by their breakthrough innovations and capacity to restructure existing market landscapes, exert a profound influence on socio-economic development. This section elucidates the methodology for effectively identifying such technologies utilizing machine learning algorithms. To address the retrospective lag of citation-based measures, this study establishes a mapping relationship between early-stage patent indicators and a later-observed top-5% CD-based disruption label. In the main framework, the target variable identifies patents that have backward citations and fall within the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution. The 17 multidimensional patent indicators serve as input features. By training on historical records with matured citation information, the model learns whether early technological, market, and legal signals contain predictive information about later highly disruptive citation structures. Machine learning model training and feature selection

In the technological landscape, the volume of patent data is vast and characterized by a multiplicity of descriptive metrics. Leveraging the inherent advantages of machine learning in processing high-dimensional datasets, this study employs machine learning algorithms to construct a predictive model mapping patent indicators to the CD-based disruptive label, thereby identifying potentially disruptive technologies. To enhance identification precision, the dataset is temporally partitioned into two subsets: a training set and a prediction set. The training set is dedicated to learning the underlying correlations between patent indicators and the CD index. The training protocol proceeds as follows: First, based on the established indicator framework, relevant indicators are extracted from the training set, and the CD index is computed for each patent. Second, uninitialized machine learning models are applied to fit the association between technical indicators and the CD index. Subsequently, feature importance scores derived from the models are utilized to rank variables, facilitating the exclusion of indicators that make negligible contributions to predictive performance. Finally, the refined subset of indicators is re-entered into the model for retraining. This rigorous process aims to construct a robust model capable of effectively identifying disruptive patents. During systematic training, grid search algorithms are employed for the hyperparameter optimization of the classifiers. Upon completion of the training phase, the model is finalized and serialized for subsequent evaluation and application.

Machine learning model prediction

By synthesizing the patterns encoded during the training regime, the prediction phase is initiated by extracting a comprehensive set of indicators spanning the technical, market, and legal dimensions from the target dataset. These indicators are then used as input features for the fully trained classifier. Rather than estimating a continuous CD score, the model outputs the predicted probability that a patent belongs to the disruptive class. For the final classification, patents whose predicted probability exceeds the selected operating threshold τ are flagged as high-potential disruptive candidates. This criterion is theoretically grounded in the premise that such patents are significantly more likely to be cited independently in future technological trajectories, rather than being co-cited with their predecessors—a hallmark of disruptive discontinuity.

Empirical evaluation

Research background: the field of artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a dominant paradigm of transformative innovation, exerting a profound influence on societal development and the restructuring of global competitive landscapes. The strategic importance of AI is underscored by global intergovernmental assessments, which identify it as a core driver of digital and green transitions across industrial sectors (OECD56,. Patent documents provide a structured and standardized record of codified technological activity, but they do not exhaust the channels through which AI innovation is disclosed. Given the rapid evolution and extensive socioeconomic integration of AI, the identification of potentially disruptive patents within this domain assumes paramount significance for governments and enterprises tasked with formulating strategic R&D roadmaps. The systematic identification and analysis of such patents not only provide prospective guidance for navigating future technological trajectories but also empower stakeholders to secure a first-mover advantage in intense international competition, thereby optimizing resource allocation and catalyzing industrial upgrading alongside economic structural transformation.

Data acquisition and dataset partitioning

The IncoPat database was selected as the primary data repository for this empirical study due to its comprehensive global coverage and high-quality standardized patent records. Drawing upon the “Classification System for Key Digital Technologies” and the inherent technical architecture of AI, we categorized the AI domain into two dimensions: General Fundamental Technologies and Key Application Technologies. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the artificial intelligence landscape, a rigorous retrieval strategy was executed using a specific search query syntax targeting the Title and Abstract (TIAB) fields: TIAB = (Natural Language Processing OR Machine Learning OR Computer Vision OR Augmented Reality OR Target Tracking OR Speech Processing OR Sentiment Analysis OR Scene Understanding OR Biometric Recognition). This methodology ensures the portability of the proposed framework. This procedure yielded a total of 26,935 relevant patent records. To capture temporal dynamics and ensure the currency of the analysis, the dataset was chronologically partitioned into two distinct subsets: a training set and a prediction set. The training set comprises 20,278 patents filed between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2021, serving as the foundational corpus for model calibration. Conversely, the prediction set encompasses 6,657 patents filed from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2024, which are utilized for the prospective identification of disruptive potential. This temporal segmentation strategy facilitates the elucidation of evolutionary trajectories within AI technology, thereby providing robust empirical support for future technology forecasting and strategic planning.

We further examined backward-citation coverage because the interpretation of the CD index depends on the presence of prior-art references. In the training set, 194 patents had no backward citations, accounting for 0.96% of the 20,278 training patents. Under the main top-5% CD label, 962 patents were labelled as highly disruptive candidates, and none of these positive cases lacked backward citations. Under the supplementary CD = 1 specification, 4,961 patents were labelled as positive cases, corresponding to 24.46% of the training set. Among these CD = 1 positive cases, 123 patents had no backward citations, accounting for 2.48% of the CD = 1 positive class. Although this share is small, 123 of the 194 patents without backward citations were labelled as CD = 1, indicating that the CD = 1 rule is relatively sensitive to patents lacking explicit prior-art references. This evidence supports our decision to use the top-5% CD label among patents with backward citations as the main specification and to treat the CD = 1 results as supplementary.

Feature selection for disruptive AI technologies

Machine learning model selection

To construct a robust mapping relationship between patent indicators and the CD index labels, this study employs the AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) algorithm. This model was prioritized due to its demonstrated computational efficiency in processing large-scale datasets, superior generalization capabilities, and exceptional predictive performance. The implementation was executed using the Scikit-learn library within the Python programming environment. Specifically, the AdaBoost ensemble was configured with Decision Tree classifiers serving as base estimators. The training protocol incorporated a 5-fold cross-validation strategy, using accuracy together with class-sensitive metrics as the primary evaluation criteria under class imbalance. Within the training subset, we further hold out a validation split to select an operating threshold for converting predicted probabilities into class labels (we choose by maximizing the F1 score on the validation set). Cost-sensitive learning is implemented via inverse-frequency class weights , where Nc denotes the number of samples in class c. Furthermore, to comprehensively assess the robustness and efficacy of AdaBoost in the specific task of identifying disruptive technologies, a comparative analysis was designed wherein the model was benchmarked against four other established machine learning algorithms: Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Neural Networks (NN), and Decision Trees (DT).

Model training and indicator feature selection

Pursuant to the framework outlined in Sect. 3.1, patent indicators were extracted from the training dataset, which comprises 20,278 samples, each characterized by a 17-dimensional feature vector. Under the main specification, the labelled dataset contains 962 highly disruptive candidates defined by the top-5% CD rule among patents with backward citations, corresponding to 4.74% of the 20,278 training patents. Therefore, model evaluation emphasizes precision, recall, and F1 score in addition to accuracy. Given the significant heterogeneity in the numerical scales of these indicators, feeding raw data directly into the model could deleteriously impact learning convergence and efficacy. To mitigate this, we apply a log(1 + x) transformation to all non-negative count-based indicators, i.e., , which compresses the dynamic range while preserving zeros. Concurrently, the CD index was computed for each sample to quantify its latent disruptive potential. This preprocessing regimen—integrating logarithmic scaling with CD index calculation—was designed to optimize the training trajectory and enhance the model’s accuracy in disruptive technology identification.

In the context of machine learning architecture, an excessive dimensionality of input indicators invariably escalates model complexity, potentially reducing learning efficiency and impeding robust generalization. High-dimensional feature spaces not only impose substantial computational overhead but may also introduce noise that obscures critical patterns. The effective elimination of low-quality, redundant, or negligible features is therefore a prerequisite for enabling the model to focus on the indicators most strongly associated with the identification target. Consequently, leveraging the intrinsic capabilities of the AdaBoost algorithm, a feature importance analysis was conducted to rank the predictive power of each indicator. Although the model does not provide strong definitive classification performance, it retains modest but nontrivial screening ability. From a practical standpoint, this trade-off is appropriate for ex ante screening and technology-foresight applications, where the objective is to generate a manageable candidate pool for expert review and continued monitoring rather than to make final judgments about realized disruptive impact. Accordingly, the model should be interpreted as an early-screening and prioritization tool rather than as a definitive classifier of patent disruptiveness.

Recent empirical work suggests that disruption indices often require extended time horizons, typically on the order of a decade, to stabilize57. Consequently, the inclusion of recent patents in the training set could potentially introduce labeling noise due to immature citation dynamics. To address this concern and verify that our model’s performance is not driven by transient artifacts or immediate recognition in recent data, we conducted a robustness check using a mature cohort. Specifically, we restricted the dataset to patents filed on or before December 31, 2013, ensuring a citation accumulation window of over ten years for all samples.

We re-evaluated the proposed cost-sensitive AdaBoost model on this mature subset using 5-fold stratified cross-validation. The results, summarized in Table 5, demonstrate that the model maintains, and even slightly improves, its predictive capability under this conservative setup. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.918, a precision of 0.286, a recall of 0.456, and an F1 score of 0.352, with low standard deviations across folds. These findings indicate that the structural signatures of disruption identified by our framework are persistent and become more distinct as citation networks mature. The superior performance on the mature cohort further suggests that the results reported in the main analysis (Table 4) are robust and represent conservative estimates constrained by the latent noise in newer patent records.

Conclusion

Focusing on artificial intelligence patents, this study constructed a patent-based indicator framework spanning technological, market, and legal dimensions and evaluated machine-learning models for ex ante screening of highly disruptive candidates. The main analysis defined highly disruptive candidates as patents in the top 5% of the empirical CD distribution among patents with backward citations. Under this stricter label, the AdaBoost model achieved an F1 score of 0.333, indicating that early patent metadata provide useful but limited screening power. The framework should therefore be used to generate candidate pools for monitoring and expert review, rather than to make definitive classifications of patent disruptiveness.

The supplementary CD = 1 specification produced stronger apparent predictive performance, but this result should be interpreted with caution. In the training set, only 2.48% of CD = 1 positive cases lacked backward citations, indicating that the CD = 1 positives were not numerically dominated by such patents. However, 63.40% of patents without backward citations were labelled as CD = 1, showing that the CD = 1 rule is sensitive to the absence of explicit prior-art references. This finding supports the decision to treat CD = 1 results as supplementary.

The main methodological contribution lies in the refinement of the indicator system through AdaBoost-based feature importance analysis. By reducing the original 17 indicators to 12 pivotal variables, the study lowered dimensional complexity while slightly improving predictive performance. This result suggests that a more parsimonious patent-based indicator system can still retain substantial explanatory power for early-stage screening of potentially disruptive AI patents.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, although the top-5% CD label reduces the interpretive problem associated with patents without backward citations, it remains dependent on a single cumulative citation snapshot. CD values may change as additional citations accumulate. Second, the model relies on structured patent metadata and does not incorporate semantic information from titles, abstracts, claims, or full specifications. Third, the F1 score under the strict top-5% label remains modest, indicating that highly disruptive patents are difficult to predict precisely from early metadata alone. Therefore, the model is best suited for candidate generation rather than automated decision-making. Fourth, the analysis is limited to patented AI innovation and does not capture disruptive activity disclosed through scientific papers, open-source software, technical standards, or proprietary models. Fifth, the study treats AI as an aggregate domain and does not model heterogeneity across AI subfields.
