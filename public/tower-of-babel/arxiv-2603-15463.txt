The Compilability Thresholds of 2-CNF to OBDD
Alexis de Colnet, Alfons Laarman, Joon Hyung Lee

Abstract
We prove the existence of two thresholds regarding the compilability of random 2-CNF formulas to OBDDs. The formulas are drawn from ℱ 2 ​ ( n , δ ​ n ) \mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n) , the uniform distribution over all 2-CNFs with δ ​ n \delta n clauses and n n variables, with δ ≥ 0 \delta\geq 0 a constant. We show that, with high probability, the random 2-CNF admits OBDDs of size polynomial in n n if 0 ≤ δ < 1 / 2 0\leq\delta<1/2 or if δ > 1 \delta>1 . On the other hand, for 1 / 2 < δ < 1 1/2<\delta<1 , with high probability, the random 2 2 -CNF admits only OBDDs of size exponential in n n . It is no coincidence that the two “compilability thresholds” are δ = 1 / 2 \delta=1/2 and δ = 1 \delta=1 . Both are known thresholds for other CNF properties, namely, δ = 1 \delta=1 is the satisfiability threshold for 2-CNF while δ = 1 / 2 \delta=1/2 is the treewidth threshold, i.e., the point where the treewidth of the primal graph jumps from constant to linear in n n with high probability.

1
Introduction

BDDs (Binary Decision Diagrams) are a very well-known model for representing Boolean functions. This model is quite natural and has various properties that make it attractive and therefore has been considerably studied for decades. Here we focus on
ordered
BDDs, or OBDDs, which form an important fragment of BDDs thanks to many desirable properties for practice (canonicity, fast
apply
, etc.). Because of their practical appeal, research on the limits of OBDD developed fast, centered around the following question: which functions admit a small OBDD representation? We will talk of
OBDD-size
, i.e., the size of a minimal-size OBDD for the function. It was soon realized that many function families, including some very natural ones, require exponential OBDD-size in the number
n
of variables. It was also quickly understood that, as
n
increases, almost all Boolean functions have super-polynomial OBDD-size. In practice, the function to represent as an OBDD may be given as Boolean formula, so understanding the OBDD-size of small Boolean formulas, and more specifically, CNF and DNF, is relevant. Here, negative results are also expected, and indeed it has been shown that many small CNF formulas have exponential OBDD-size; this was even proved for 2-CNFs and monotone 2-CNFs. So, what is the state of our knowledge about the OBDD-size of CNF today? We understand pretty well what makes a monotone CNF hard to translate or, to reuse the
knowledge compilation
terminology, to
compile
into OBDD. The answers are found in the parameters of its underlying graphs (primal graph, incidence graph, hypergraph, etc.). For general CNFs, we do not have such results to help us characterize those that have a large OBDD-size. We know that large OBDD-size implies that certain parameters are big, for instance, the
pathwidth
of the CNF primal graph
[
12
]
, and we can infer large OBDD-size from properties of the function represented by the CNF (
k
-mixity
for instance
[
22
, Chapter 6]
), but those are not easily checkable. In this paper, our aim is to better understand what makes a CNF formula hard to compile into OBDD using
random CNF formula
models.

Contributions.
We focus on random 2-CNFs drawn from
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,m)

. Our 2-CNFs are

Known Threshold Phenomena for CNF and Graphs

We review concepts and threshold phenomena related to (random) CNF formulas and (random) graphs. A formula

An assignment

is denoted by

. We denote by

the set of assignments to

that satisfy

.

{\mathsf{SAT}}

). For

3
Compilability Thresholds

The property of a 2-CNF we are interested in is its OBDD-size, that is, the size of the smallest OBDD that computes it.

OBDD
.

Binary decision diagrams
(BDD) are well-known decision diagram representations of Boolean functions. A BDD is a directed acyclic graph with a single source and one or two sinks labeled
0
and
1
. Each internal node is labeled with a Boolean variable and has exactly two outgoing edges called
0
-edge
and
1
-edge
. The set of variables labeling nodes of a BDD
B
is denoted
var(B)
. A BDD is
ordered
(OBDD) when, on every source-to-sink path, each variable appears only once and always in the same order. This total order is called the
variable order
of the OBDD. In a BDD
B
, every complete assignment
\alpha
to the variables corresponds to a single path: we start from the source and follow the
\alpha(x)
-edge for every node labeled with a variable
x
that is reached; the label of the sink reached is the value
B(\alpha)
computed by
B
on
\alpha
. Note that
B(\alpha)
is also defined for
\alpha
an assignment to any superset
X
of
var(B)
. Let
f
be a Boolean function over
X
. Then we say
B
computes
f
when
B(\alpha)=f(\alpha)
for every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{X}
.

Definition 6

(OBDD-size)
.

The size of an OBDD is its number of nodes, including sinks.
\text{OBDD-size}(f)
is the minimal size of an OBDD computing the Boolean function
f
.

The OBDD-size of a Boolean formula
F
, written
\text{OBDD-size}(F)
, is the OBDD-size of the Boolean function over
var(F)
that maps to
1
an assignment to
var(F)
if and only if that assignment is in
sat(F)
. Contrary to satisfiability or primal treewidth, OBDD-size is not monotone in the sense that, for a CNF
F
and a subformula
F^{\prime}
of
F
, we can have
\text{OBDD-size}(F)\leq\text{OBDD-size}(F^{\prime})
or
\text{OBDD-size}(F)\geq\text{OBDD-size}(F^{\prime})
.

As a warm-up, we consider

A monotone CNF formula is a CNF formula where every literal is positive. We denote by

In the rest of the paper, we dive into the proof of Theorem
1
(II). Before that, we shall try to provide some intuition behind the proof. We start from an arbitrary minimal-size OBDD

, so we have a bipartition of

G_{F}
such that there is a large matching between the two parts, i.e., many edges of
G_{F}

to
x_{\pi(k+1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)}
without sharing a vertex (Section

corresponds to at least one clause of

of

that has many solutions. We can show that if exponentially many solutions to
M(F)
have an extension that satisfies

To give a simple example, suppose

•

•

Then comes the issue of showing this nice property of

4
Random 2-CNF with Clause Replacement

4.1
The Distribution
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)

We are going to prove Theorem
1
(II) through another
2
-CNF distribution. Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
is the distribution for the random CNF formula
F=C_{1}\land\dots\land C_{m}
where each clause
C_{i}
is drawn uniformly at random and independently from the set of all
4\binom{n}{2}
clauses of length
2
over
X_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
. For
k\in[m]
let
F^{\leqslant k}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}C_{i}
and
F^{>k}=\bigwedge_{i=k+1}^{m}C_{i}
.

F=F^{\leqslant k}\land F^{>k}

A neat property of
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
is that
F^{\leqslant k}\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,k)
and
F^{>k}\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m-k)
. Even nicer,
F^{\leqslant k}
is stochastically independent of
F^{>k}
. The main drawback of
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
, on the other hand, is that the same clause may be drawn more than once. We call a CNF formula
F

simple
when no two of its clauses are identical. It is not unlikely that
F
has duplicate clauses, but for
m=\delta n
with
\delta>0
fixed, the probability that
F
is simple does not vanish to
0
as
n
increases. Let
N=4\binom{n}{2}
. This is the good old “birthday problem”: there are
N
days in a year (
N
possible clauses), and we draw
m
people and thus
m
birthdays (
m
clauses) at random, what is the probability that two people have the same birthday (that two clauses are identical)?
Here, since
m=o(N)
as
n\to\infty
, the probability that
F
is simple (no two people share a birthday) converges to a positive constant, and in particular remains bounded away from
0
. We can reuse the analysis of
[
14
, Section 1.3]
:

\displaystyle\Pr\limits_{F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)}[F\text{ is simple}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\binom{N}{m}m!}{N^{m}}\geq(1-o(1))\frac{N^{m}}{m!}\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}}{2N}-\frac{m^{3}}{6N^{2}}\right)\frac{m!}{N^{m}}

(1)

\displaystyle\geq(1-o(1))\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}}{2N}-\frac{m^{3}}{6N^{2}}\right)\geq(1-o(1))e^{-\delta^{2}-\delta}.

Another crucial feature of
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
is that a random formula
F
drawn from
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
, when conditioned on being simple, behaves as if it were drawn from
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,m)
. Formally, let
\mathscr{P}
be a formula property (a set of formulas), then

\Pr\limits_{F\sim\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,m)}[F\in\mathscr{P}]=\Pr\limits_{F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)}[F\in\mathscr{P}\mid F\text{ is simple}]

(2)

The combination of (
1
) and (
2
) in a Bayes rule shows that, if the event
F\in\mathscr{P}
is asymptotically negligible when
F
is drawn from
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,m)
, then the same event is asymptotically negligible when
F
is drawn from
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,m)
(provided
m=\delta n
and
\delta>0
).

Lemma 10
.

Let
\delta>0
. If
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\sim\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)\end{subarray}}[F\in\mathscr{P}]=0
then
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\sim\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n)\end{subarray}}[F\in\mathscr{P}]=0
.

Thus, to prove Theorem
1
(II), it is enough to show the correctness of the following lemma.

Lemma 11
.

Let
1/2<\delta<1
be constant and
F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
. There exists
v>0
such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr\left[\text{OBDD-size}(F)\leq 2^{|var(F)|^{v}}\right]=0.

Proof of Theorem
1
(II).

Combining Lemmas
10
and
11
with
\mathscr{P}
the class of CNF formulas such that
F\in\mathscr{P}
if and only if
\text{OBDD-size}(F)\leq 2^{|var(F)|^{v}}
, we get
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr_{F\sim\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n)}[F\in\mathscr{P}]=0
,
i.e.,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr_{F\sim\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n)}[\text{OBDD-size}(F)\leq 2^{|var(F)|^{v}}]=0
.
∎

Our objective now is to prove Lemma
11
.

4.2
High Treewidth in
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)

Corollary
5
tells us that a random CNF
F
almost always has high treewidth when drawn from
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n)
with
\delta>1/2
, but is it also true when
F
is drawn from
\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
? This better be the case, because high treewidth is an essential component in the proof of Lemma
11
. To prove that high treewidth indeed occurs almost always in
F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
, we show that
F
is unlikely to contain more than
\sqrt{n}
duplicate clauses. This will mean that the primal graph of
F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
is essentially a graph
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\delta n)
from which we remove at most
\sqrt{n}
. But then, since
G
has treewidth
\Omega(n)
with high probability (Theorem
4
), we will have that the graph of
F
has treewidth at least
\Omega(n)-\sqrt{n}=\Omega(n)
with high probability.

So let us show that we do not have too many duplicate clauses in
F=C_{1}\land\cdots\land C_{m}\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
. For
k\in[m]
fixed, the probability that the clause
C_{k}
is identical to a clause in
C_{1},\dots,C_{k-1},C_{k+1},\dots,C_{m}
is
1-\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{m-1}
, with
N=4\binom{n}{2}=4n^{2}-4n
, so the expected number of non-unique clauses (the number of people with non-unique birthday) is

m\left(1-\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{m-1}\right)=\delta n\left(1-\left(\frac{2n^{2}-2n-1}{2n^{2}-2n}\right)^{\delta n-1}\right)\leq\delta n\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)^{\delta n}\right).

(3)

Using the Markov bound, we then show that having more than
\sqrt{n}
non-unique clauses is getting very unlikely as
n
increases.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\delta>0
and
F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
. Then

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr[F\text{ has at least }\sqrt{n}\text{ non-unique clauses}]=0.

We can now prove that, when
\delta>1/2
, the treewidth of

is in
\Omega(n)
with high probability using the argument explained above.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\delta>1/2
and
F\sim\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)
. There is a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Pr[tw(G_{F})\geq\gamma n]=1.

From Large Treewidth to Large Matchings

We now invoke a concept closely related to treewidth:

. We recall that a matching in a graph

of its edges such that no two edges of

share an endpoint. Let

be the set of endpoints in

For

two disjoint subsets of

and

is a matching of

whose edges all have one endpoint in

and the other in

. We denote by

and

in

has high treewidth, then for every balanced bipartition

of

to

.

5.1

\mathcal{H}_{2}(n,\delta n)

We follow the definitions from
[
21
]
. A

. Removing an edge

Definition 14

(Maximum Matching Width)
.

Let

•

\displaystyle mmw(G,T):=\max_{e\in E_{b}}mm_{G}(V_{e},\bar{V}_{e})

•

\displaystyle mmw(G):=\min_{T}mmw(G,T)

One last comment about the proof. A careful reader could object that, in terms of graph parameters, Theorem

requires the treewidth to be large and the degree to be small, while we never discuss the degree for Theorem

, which may seem odd. And indeed, the fact that the maximum degree of
G_{F}
is generally small (less than

, but, in a sense, it is already baked into Theorem

8
Discussion and Future Work

Several natural variations of Theorem
1
need to be investigated. First, we can modify the
initial language
; for instance, instead of sparse
2
-CNF one may consider
k
-CNF for
k>2
, or even
k
-XORSAT or
k
-NAESAT formulas, sparse or dense. Second, the
target language
can also be altered. One direction is to constrain it more, for instance, by saying that the variable order of the OBDD is fixed in advance. In that case, we conjecture that the OBDD-size is large even below the treewidth threshold, as there is no way to exploit the structure of the primal graph through the variable order. In the opposite direction, there are many compilation languages that generalize and are more
succinct
than OBDD
[
9
]
, which could be considered as alternative target languages. We plan to show that Theorem
1
also holds for compilation to
structured DNNF
circuits, and we have, in a sense, paved the way for the proof by insisting on working with treewidth rather than pathwidth. We also believe that replacing sparse
2
-CNF with sparse
3
-CNF is feasible. Currently, the exact location of the satisfiability threshold for random
3
-CNF is unknown. In addition, to our knowledge, the satisfiability threshold is not the same as the treewidth threshold (which we are confident exists), but the result can perhaps be stated and proved using placeholder symbols instead of the actual values.

We also want to research alternative proofs of Theorem
1
. There could be a clever proof relying on the fact that, as
n
increases, almost all 2-CNF formulas represent
unate functions
, i.e., functions that are monotone modulo consistent literal renaming
[
3
]
. Note that this is
not
the same as saying that almost all 2-CNF are monotone modulo consistent literal renaming. Nevertheless, knowing Theorem
9
for monotone CNFs, there is some hope for a proof of Theorem
1
that would try to reduce the CNF to one that is monotone. But this requires showing that almost all 2-CNF formulas from
\mathcal{F}_{2}(n,\delta n)
,
for any fixed
\delta
, represent unate functions as
n
increases. This does not derive directly from
[
3
]
, but sounds plausible.

Lastly, as a follow-up to
[
16
]
, empirical evaluations would be insightful. Setting up these experiments is not exactly easy since finding the variable order that gives the smallest OBDD is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
7
]
, but that would allow us to see whether phase transitions are actually visible for reasonable
n
at the thresholds given in this paper.
