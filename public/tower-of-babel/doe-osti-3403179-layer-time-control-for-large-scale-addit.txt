 Abstract

 Large-scale additive manufacturing (AM) based on polymer extrusion has gained increasing attention
 across a wide range of industries due to its numerous advantages, including high production rates, the
 capability to process a diverse array of engineering thermoplastics, and the ability to fabricate large and
 geometrically complex structures that would be difficult or impossible to achieve using traditional
 manufacturing methods. Despite these advantages, the broader adoption of large-scale AM is still limited
 by the challenge of determining optimal process parameters, which are critical for achieving high-quality
 parts with consistent mechanical performance. The quality and performance of printed components are
 highly sensitive to key process parameters such as layer deposition time, extrusion temperature, extrusion
 rate, and printing speed. These parameters must be carefully calibrated based on the specific material
 properties, part geometry, and environmental conditions to ensure proper interlayer bonding, dimensional
 accuracy, and structural integrity.

 In this research, we present a comprehensive framework to optimize layer deposition time, one of the most
 critical parameters influencing thermal behavior and deformation in polymer extrusion-based large-scale
 AM. The framework integrates high-fidelity numerical modeling, iteration-based optimization, and
 machine learning-based surrogate modeling to provide accurate, rapid, and efficient process guidance.
 Thermal control plays a central role in determining the success of polymer extrusion-based AM, as
 temperature directly affects interlayer bonding strength, residual stress development, and the overall
 structural integrity of the printed parts. Layer deposition time, which is defined as the interval between
 successive layer depositions, directly governs the thermal history of the deposited material. If the layer
 time is too long, previously deposited layers may be overcooled, leading to poor bonding, delamination,
 or interlayer defects. Conversely, if the layer time is too short, the underlying layers may remain
 overheated and insufficiently solidified, resulting in structural collapse or dimensional distortion during
 subsequent deposition. Proper control of layer time is therefore essential to achieving defect-free parts
 with high mechanical strength.

 The objectives of this study are threefold. First, we aim to accurately predict the temperature history during
 the printing process using high-fidelity finite element simulation. Second, we seek to develop a layer time
 optimization framework by integrating a physics-based reduced-order heat transfer model with an
 iteration-based optimization algorithm, allowing rapid layer time control during printing. Third, we aim
 to enable rapid temperature prediction and adaptive layer-time optimization using a data-driven model
 trained on numerical simulation results. This approach facilitates efficient real-time process control and
 reduces reliance on time-consuming high-cost experimentation. By combining physics-based simulations
 with machine learning models, the proposed framework enhances process reliability, minimizes energy
 and material consumption, and promotes the industrial adoption of large-scale additive manufacturing
 across a broad range of applications, including tooling, automotive, aerospace, and construction industries.

This work is a continuation of research previously reported in ORNL/SPR-2022/2669 (2022). The project
was paused following the closure of the original industry partner, and resumed in 2024 with a new industry
partner, Haddy Inc. Accordingly, portions of this report contain material from the earlier publication.

 1. Introduction

Large scale additive manufacturing (AM) machines, such as LSAM® and BAAM®, have been designed
for large part production with a high deposition rate through layer by layer. Bonding strength of printed
parts is largely determined by layer temperature. Defects, such as warping, cracking and deformation,
often occur in the process due to an improper layer temperature before the next layer deposition. Typically,
the proper print surface temperature is achieved by controlling the length of time it takes to print a layer.
A new layer cannot be printed on either too hot or too cold of a surface of the previously deposited layer.
If the surface is too hot, the thermoplastic material in the lower layer is not stiff enough and it results in
risk of deformation. If the surface temperature is too low, the bonding between the surface and the new
layer cannot be guaranteed. Current practice on layer time control heavily relies on the experience of
skilled labor and uses fixed layer time throughout the entire layers, which is incapable of adapting to
dynamic changes of layer temperature. There is a lack of systematic guidance on layer time control based
on predictive thermal models and quantitative online monitoring for production efficiency and printing
quality, which becomes a major hurdle in making large scale AM process an economically viable solution
for any industry.

To address this problem, we propose a novel optimization framework by combining simulation techniques
using high-performance computing (HPC) and real-time feedback using thermal images. Current operation
three-dimensional (3D) printing involves depositing each layer at its maximum speed and pausing the
extruder for a certain time at the end of the layer. The layer time is determined based on an operator’s
intuitions and experiences. The objective of this project is to develop a framework of deposition time
control for large scale AM by combining an AM simulation technique, machine learning algorithms and
control methods. We will focus on layer time optimization using a temperature history profile predicted
from simulation and real-time thermal images. Because simulation-driven optimization often involves a
large number of resources for industrial application, we propose a machine learning approach based on
the pair data between the toolpath designs and the optimal layer times. To meet our objective, primarily
three frameworks are developed from this work: (a) Integrated framework running on HPC, (b) real-time
feedback and (c) AI framework (see Figure 1). The details of each framework in provided below in
Sections 2, 3 and 4.

 2. Integrated framework for layer time optimization

2.1 Layer time optimization in large-scale additive manufacturing using high-fidelity physics-
 based simulations

 Additive manufacturing (AM) technologies have significant advantages over conventional manufacturing
 processes by offering design freedom and eliminating the requirement for a mold or die. AM processes
 incrementally deposit materials and build a product layer by layer. To develop a stable structure, each new
 layer should strongly bond to the previously deposited layer. At or near room temperature, AM processes
 such as vat photopolymerization, material jetting, binder jetting, and direct ink writing are some of these
 processes that rely on chemical bonding or crosslinking of molecules to join layers [1]. In other AM
 processes such as directed energy deposition, powder bed fusion, and thermoplastic material extrusion,
 heat is used to melt and fuse new layers of material to the previous layer [1]. In these approaches, the top
 layer cools and shrinks against the constraint of the cooler rigid layers below, causing considerable stress
 within the printed substance.

 Figure 2.1: LSAM®, a large-scale additive manufacturing system and 3D printing of shuttle in
 process at Local Motors, Knoxville (image adapted from www.thermwood.com).

 Figure 2.2: Failures in large-scale 3D printing. (a) Debonding between layers due to overcooling and
 (b) Material collapse due to very high temperature of layers.

 Large-scale additive manufacturing approach is a material extrusion process that has been demonstrated
 to produce structures several meters in size at rates between 5 and 50 kg/hr, using a combination of
 extrusion and robotic deposition technologies [2]. The LSAM® system by Thermwood Corporation is an
 example of a large-scale 3D printer that uses thermoplastic pellet feedstock, as shown in Figure 2.1. One
 of the major challenges in large-scale thermoplastic material extrusion printing is predicting the cooling

                                                 8|Page

 behavior during printing. Due to a large amount of extruded substance with an arbitrary shape and size, it
 is difficult to estimate an optimal layer time. A long layer time leads to an over-cooled surface on which
 a new layer is deposited, which can yield weak bonding or debonding between layers, cracking, or
 warping. An example of debonding and warpage is shown in Figure 2.2.2 (a). Conversely, a short layer
 time leads to overheating of the surface due to insufficient cooling, which can cause structural instability
 and may lead to wall collapse during printing, as shown in Figure 2.2 (b). Therefore, an optimal layer time
 should be determined that is directly related to the surface temperature of the previously deposited layer
 to prevent the defects and reduce failure costs of the AM process.

 This study focused on designing a layer time optimization process in large-scale AM process based on the
 results of finite element analysis (FEA) and an optimization algorithm. Finding an optimal layer time
 through experiments requires multiple printing trials and post-processing of data for multiple combinations
 of design/printing variables. Therefore, we have performed AM process simulations to predict the
 temperature profile and surface temperature for various cases with different layer times. The simulations
 have been verified via experimental tests. A method for layer time optimization was designed based on
 combining a cooling function and a minimization function of temperature errors (difference between the
 target temperature and the layer temperature) and printing time.

2.1.1 Materials and methods

 The details provided here are taken from our previously published work [3]. Preliminary experiments were
 conducted to investigate the relationship between the layer temperature and the interlayer bonding
 property. Single bead wall structures were printed using the LSAM®, using fiber reinforced polycarbonate
 (CF/PC) with four different layer times. A low layer temperature was observed corresponding to long
 layer time as the previously deposited substance had sufficient time to cool down. The corresponding layer
 temperatures were 89 °C, 104 °C, 120 °C, and 140 °C. Tensile test specimens were extracted from the
 printed structures in the z-direction following ASTM D638. The experimental results on these specimens
 are summarized as shown in Figure 2.3. The results of these tests are consistent with findings from
 previous research considering the relationship between the strength of interlayer bonding properties and
 the layer temperature [4]. The z-direction tensile strength degrades by over 50% when the layer
 temperature drops from 140 °C to 89 °C, indicating a substantial association between layer temperature
 and z-direction tensile strength.

 The glass temperature of CF/PC is 145 °C and the z-direction ultimate strength of the LSAM® structures
 becomes higher as the layer temperature gets closer to the glass transition temperature. However, when
 the layer temperature exceeds the glass transition temperature, the influence of the temperature is not
 effective to the z-direction strength [5]. Therefore, the glass temperature can be a target layer temperature
 in the optimization process. If the layer temperature of the substrate is 20 °C above the glass transition
 temperature, the material collapse will occur due to a lack of stiffness. Thus, the upper limit for the layer
 temperature is set as 165 °C. The lower limit for the layer temperature is set as 125 °C as the interlayer
 bonding and tensile strength were observed to deteriorate rapidly for lower layer temperatures.

2.1.3 Temperature profile via simulation

 FEA simulations were carried out to predict the temperature profiles and layer temperatures of hexagon
 structures with different layer times. In the analysis, heat transfer occurs by thermal conduction,
 convection, and radiation. The material (CF/PC) used in experiments has an anisotropic thermal property
 due to the reinforced carbon fiber. The addition of carbon fiber may be expected to increase the thermal
 conductivity of the composite in the direction of the fiber [6]. Therefore, determining where the fiber
 orientation is crucial to predict the temperature profile and layer temperature. Since the reinforced fiber in
 the extruded substance is aligned with the tool path direction, the deposited substance has a principal
 direction along with the tool path direction and z-direction is chosen as the stacking direction. Material
 property values for the analysis are summarized in Table 2.3.

 For the analysis, progressive material activation was used to simulate the additive manufacturing process.
 The Tool paths used in the LSAM® were converted as simulation code with a developed tool path

converter. The converter changed G-code into an event series containing information on time, deposition
position, and extrusion status (on-off). To implement the layer-by-layer deposition, the elements were
generated vertically with the stacking direction, and the height of a mesh was made in the portion of the
height of a layer. When the tool path passed through the element, the element was activated, and the
boundary conditions continuously changed over the simulation. The process of progressive material
activation of the AM process is shown in Figure 2.6.

Time-dependent temperature profiles were obtained from the AM simulation. The analysis lasted 5,515 s,
8,748 s, and 11,652 s for three different layer time cases, respectively, and the results were recorded in
five consistent intervals. The temperature graphs (shown in Figure 2.7) show the temperature profile from
the base to the stacked layer over each time, which is produced to a height of 60 mm during each time
interval. 30 °C was set as the base temperature and ambient temperature boundary condition.

Comparisons of temperature profiles in simulations and experiments were conducted for the hexagon
prints, with results shown in Figure 2.7. In the figure, the dotted lines show the experimental results, and
the solid lines show the simulation results. In each case, as printing progresses, the temperature of the
previously deposited layers cools down to the base and ambient temperature. The more previous layers
approached the final temperature in the later printing interval. In the case of a long layer time, there is a
big temperature difference between the previous layer and the newly deposited layer. Therefore, the
temperature is more sharply decreased in the longer layer time case (Case 3) than the temperature in the
other cases. In all cases, the simulation results showed good agreement with the experiments. This proves
that the results of the conducted simulations are reliable in predicting the layer temperature for this use
case.

2.1.5   Results

 Optimized layer times for each layer were calculated using the optimization algorithm based on the layer
 temperature predicted from the FEA simulation, with results shown in Figure 2.8. The layers under the
 tenth layer were affected by the base temperature and showed a different tendency from the other part.
 These bottom layers were excluded so that the optimization process only considers the cooling behavior
 changes due to the layer times. Since the cooling pattern of the entire structure changed with different
 layer times, distinct optimal layer times were found for each case. However, the results of the optimal
 layer time to meet the target temperature show a tendency to get close to one value. Therefore, an overall
 optimal layer time for the hexagon geometry with a single bead wall could be found based on the
 optimization result’s trend. The optimal layer time decreases as the layer time increases, and the trend is
 clearly shown in Figure 2.9. With the correlation of the tested layer time and calculated optimal layer time,

the overall optimal layer time was determined as a cross-section point, which was 130 seconds per layer.

FEA simulation was conducted to predict the layer temperature for the 130 seconds layer time print, and

simulation result, the additional optimization process was conducted to find a better optimal layer time.

was 129 seconds for a layer, which was very close to the previously founded optimal layer time. This

and-error process, a very accurate optimal layer time can be found.

2.1.6    Summary

 In summary, we have shown the ability to determine the optimized layer time with finite element analysis
 (FEA) simulations and optimization algorithm. In this work, only a single bead wall and simple geometry
 were used to verify the optimized layer time determined. The simulation results were found to be reliable
 to predict the temperature profile and layer temperature for the LSAM® process and can be used to find
 the optimized layer time. The optimization algorithm can determine the optimized layer time for a given

 with various geometries; hence, the temperature-changing curves between different positions on an entire
 layer generated from the FEA simulation are analyzed. The variances of temperatures among all positions
 at each timestamp are extracted from the corresponding geometry simulation data. As shown in Figure
 2.16(a), the x-axis is recognized as the local layer time instead of the global layer time, and the y-axis is
 the temperature variance, which keeps increasing over time. Because the local layer time is applied here,
 the temperature cools as the x-axis timestamps go up. Therefore, the variance values are lower when the
 temperature is high and higher when the temperature is low. The hexagon geometry has all wide corners
 120◦; the star geometry comes with 36◦ sharp corners and 108◦ wide corners. According to the positions
 on the entire layer, the temperature variance for one type of corner hexagon shape is slightly lower than
 that in two types of a corner star shape in the first 10 s printing. After that, the temperature variance in the
 Hexagon geometry grows faster, and it is around 0.75, which is higher than a 0.5 variance in the star
 geometry after the 200 s. In order to explore the effect of corners, variances of the temperature from the
 whole structure and wall positions are compared under both hexagon shape and star shape. The wall
 positions are determined by excluding the corner positions were defined as a normalized distance of four
 times the printing bead width from the edges. According to Figure 2.17(a) and (b), the temperature
 variance of a wall position, denoted by the blue line, starts to drop gradually after a 5 s printing, and it is
 near zero after the first 25 s, which is trivial. However, the temperature variance of the whole structure,
 represented as a red line, rises all the time, and it increases more significantly in the hexagon shape, which
 means that corners positively influence the variance of layer temperature change. Because sharp corners
 in star geometry cool like a wall over time, as shown in Figure 2.15, the differences in temperature cooling
 rates between corner and non-corner positions will be more evident as corner degrees widen. In addition,
 denote 𝑇𝑚𝑎𝑥 and 𝑇𝑚𝑖𝑛 as the highest and lowest layer temperature at a time, respectively. Based on the
 FEA simulation data, Figure 2.16(b) shows that the temperature differences between 𝑇𝑚𝑎𝑥 and 𝑇𝑚𝑖𝑛
 under the hexagon shape case and star shape case match with the change of temperature variances. The
 smallest temperature difference is around 1.5 °C at the initial deposition time, and it increases to a stable

                                                  20 | P a g e

 value of 6 °C for the hexagon geometry and a stable value of 5 °C for the star geometry.

 Therefore, to generate the final prediction data as accurately as the FEA simulation data, more than the
 1D heat transfer model is needed, and it is reasonable to construct more sample data by considering the
 properties of corners. Here, a hybrid prediction model is proposed. We consider the corner property as
 variances based on the cooling data from an initial simulation, and the normal distribution is applied at
 each timestamp to collect more temperature samples. In the hybrid prediction model, the predicted
 temperature obtained from the 1D heat transfer model is set as a mean value, and variance extracted from
 the FEA simulation is added by timestamps as the variance value. Then, normalized random samples are
 able to be generated at each timestamp, so we have multiple temperature cooling curves as variate positions
 on an entire printing layer. For further details refer to our previous published work [10].

2.2.5   Summary

 We propose an optimization framework to determine an optimal layer deposition time for the large-scale
 additive manufacturing process implemented for two geometries including hexagon, and star shape. With
 the determined optimal layer deposition times, successive layers are deposited on the surface, which has
 the proper layer temperature. In the optimization framework, a reduced physics-based activation model (the
 1D heat transfer model) is used to predict the temperature history, substituting the time-consuming finite
 element analysis (FEA) and experiments. Temperature variances are extracted from a high- fidelity FEA
 model to consider the geometric characters. The variances are used to generate geometry-considered
 temperature data with the reduced model for different layer deposition times. An optimal control model is
 practiced suggesting a new layer deposition time, which is expected to have a proper surface temperature.
 The optimization process starts with a given initial layer deposition time and iterates until the predicted
 surface temperature converges to the target temperature. When the predicted surface temperature is the
 same as the target temperature, the optimization process stops and provides an optimal layer deposition
 time. The optimization process uses the reduced model for the iteration; thus, this process saves time for
 running complex simulations and the expensive costs of experiment.

 Adaptive layer time control is conducted with the FEA simulation model and the layer time optimization

3.2 Online layer time control farmwork

 In the previous experiments, we applied offline layer-time control using the developed layer-time
 optimization framework. However, during printing, environmental conditions such as the bed temperature
 and convection coefficient can change, which affects the accuracy of temperature regulation. To achieve
 more precise control, online layer-time control was developed using a 1D reduced-order heat transfer
 model.

 Figure 3.9 illustrates the flow of the online layer-time optimization framework. In Step 1, the deposition of
 a new layer begins. In Step 2, the surface temperature is measured for a few seconds, and the convection
 coefficient is calibrated based on the measured data. Step 3 involves predicting the temperature distribution
 using the updated convection coefficient. In Step 4, the optimal layer time is determined from the predicted
 temperature response. Finally, in Step 5, the next layer is deposited using the updated layer time. This
 iterative process enables adaptive control of the deposition conditions, ensuring that the surface temperature
 remains close to the desired target throughout the printing process.

Figure 3.9: Flow of the online layer-time optimization framework. Step 1: Start deposition of a layer; Step
  2: Measure the surface temperature for a few seconds and calibrate the convection coefficient; Step 3:
Predict the temperature field using the updated convection coefficient; Step 4: Determine the optimal layer
 time; and Step 5: Deposit the next layer using the updated layer time.

 Figure 3.10 shows the temperature histories of the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd layers during printing, comparing
 measured temperatures with predictions from the 1D reduced-order heat transfer model. The black dotted
 line represents the measured temperature, the blue line shows the temperature predicted by the 1D model,
 and the red line shows the temperature history after parameter calibration. A real-time layer-time
 optimization framework was developed based on this 1D model. In the framework, both the print bed
 temperature and convection coefficient are considered. For the first layer, the bed temperature and
 convection coefficient are calibrated using the measured temperatures from the initial few seconds. For
 subsequent layers, only the convection coefficient is calibrated using the initial temperature measurements
 of each layer. By using parameter calibration, the temperature can be predicted more accurately, enabling
 a more precise determination of the optimal layer time. This approach enables accurate prediction and
 control of the deposition surface temperature throughout the printing process.

3.3 Summary

 In summary, an adaptive layer time control framework was developed to address temperature mismatches
 that occur in the first few layers when using a fixed optimized layer time, primarily due to the lower print
 bed temperature. The proposed adaptive control system dynamically adjusts the layer time for each layer
 and improves the accuracy of temperature prediction through real-time parameter calibration. Using this
 framework, the temperature deviation was significantly reduced in the single-bead hexagonal wall
 structure, resulting in decreased residual deformation. Furthermore, the adaptive layer time control was
 successfully applied to different materials and geometries, showing good agreement between the measured
 deposition surface temperature and the target temperature. For real-time implementation, an online layer
 time control system was developed, in which the print bed temperature and convection coefficient were
 calibrated to enhance prediction accuracy during the printing process. This demonstrates the promising
 capability of the online control system in achieving accurate temperature prediction and determining the
 optimal layer time for consistent and high-quality large-scale polymer extrusion-based additive
 manufacturing.

 4. AI surrogate framework

 The details provided in this section are taken from our previously published work [12]. Physics-based
 models, including the high-fidelity and 1D heat transfer models discussed earlier, have been successfully
 demonstrated for simple geometries such as walls, hexagons, and P-shaped structures. However, extending
 these models to more complex geometries present several challenges. High-fidelity models are
 computationally expensive and require significant time and resources [13], while 1D models, which neglect
 geometric effects, are difficult to generalize for complex shapes. Moreover, because most physics-based
 models rely on specific assumptions and boundary conditions, their accuracy and generalizability can be
 limited when applied to scenarios involving environmental uncertainties. On the other hand, data driven
 approaches excel in capturing complex, non-linear processes with enhanced efficiency, suitable for
 complex geometries. Moreover, they can incorporate surface layer temperature monitoring [14] of the

                                                35 | P a g e

 whole geometry, which is essential for optimizing layer time control. When combined with in-situ sensors,
 data-driven approach can provide real-time estimates, even considering dynamic irregularities in the
 printing process. This project develops a data-driven approach, combining deep learning with in-situ
 infrared (IR) data to predict the temperature profiles of the layers yet to be printed. Specifically, we take
 3D thermal videos as input data and propose a combination of deep convolutional neural network (CNN)
 [15-17] and stacked Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [18] architecture that utilizes both temporal and
 spatial information from thermal frames [19]. This approach aims to develop a more generalized thermal
 prediction model, suited to the complex structures in large-scale AM.

4.1 Proposed architecture

 The overall framework integrates two main components: (a) a deep CNN-based convolutional autoencoder
 that captures spatial features from IR thermal images, and (b) a stacked LSTM encoder–decoder model that
 learns temporal dependencies among successive frames in the latent feature space. The combination of
 these two networks enables the model to efficiently capture both spatial and temporal dependencies in
 sequential thermal images while keeping computational cost low enough for real-time inference.

 The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 4.1. In this approach, a sequence of thermal images {X₁, X₂,
 …, XT } with a resolution 256 × 320 is provided as input, and a sequence of future images of length K {X̂T+1,
 X̂T+2, …, X̂T+K } is predicted as the final output. Each pixel value in the thermal frame corresponds to a
 temperature value, thus allowing full-field thermal forecasting across the printed region.

 To begin with, the sequence of thermal images captured from the IR camera {X₁, X₂, …, XT} is fed to the
 CNN encoder. The encoder is composed of five convolutional and pooling layers that progressively extract
 hierarchical features. Each convolution layer employs the ReLU activation function to introduce
 nonlinearity into the network. The output of the fifth convolutional layer is fed to a fully connected dense
 layer. The CNN encoder transforms each thermal image Xt of size (256 × 320) into a lower-dimensional
 latent feature vector Zt (size = 4096) that captures the key spatial temperature characteristics.

 The resulting sequence of encoded features {Z₁, Z₂, …, ZT} is then passed to a stacked LSTM encode-
 decoder model, which learns the temporal relationships between successive frames. The LSTM model is
 structured as a stacked configuration with three encoding layers, mirrored by t/hree decoding layers for
 symmetry and completeness. The three stacked LSTM layers contain 4096, 2048, and 1024 nodes,
 respectively. This architecture enables the generation of future prediction sequences of arbitrary length.
 LSTM network takes a specified number of lookback feature maps {Z₁, Z₂, …, ZT} and predicts the latent
 representations of future feature maps {ẐT+1, ẐT+2, …, ẐT+K}. These predicted latent vectors are subsequently
 decoded through the CNN decoder to reconstruct the corresponding future frames {X̂T+1, X̂T+2, …, X̂T+K}.
 This enables the model to forecast upcoming thermal distributions in the large-scale additive manufacturing
 process.

4.2 Demonstration of proposed approach

 The performance of the proposed 3D video-level temperature profile prediction model is evaluated on two
 sequential datasets recorded during the LFAM process. Additionally, results of the proposed model are
 compared with those from stacked LSTM and ConvLSTM models.

4.2.1 Data collection

 The large-scale additive manufacturing machine utilizes 30% glass fiber reinforced PETG as its material,
 constructing products by melting and extruding out the material layer by layer along a guided printing path.
 The newly printed hot and soft layer should cool down sufficiently to become stiff enough to support the
 upcoming layer. Additionally, a compression wheel applies pressure to each freshly deposited bead of
 material, effectively fusing it to the layer below by collapsing any air pockets. This ensures a strong,
 seamless bond between layers, resulting in a high-quality, virtually void-free printed structure. During the
 manufacturing process, the print head (the extruder) moves at a constant speed, and the layer deposition
 time represents the duration required to complete the printing of all geometries in the current layer before
 beginning the printing of the new layer.

 Two geometries are printed: C-shaped table and tree-totem geometries. The final dimensions of the C-
 shaped table are a depth of 30 inches, a width of 14.5 inches, and a height of 25 inches. The tree totem has
 a final diameter of 13 inches and a height of 76 inches. Figure 4.2 shows the full geometries printed as the
 C-shaped table and the tree totem. Two thermal video datasets are collected by a fixed-angle IR camera

                                                37 | P a g e

 during the printing of C-shaped tables and tree-totem geometries, named “Table” and “Tree totem”,
 respectively. Both thermal videos include either part of or the entire printed object, as well as the
 background printing bed and the moving extruder. Each frame in the thermal video has a resolution of 256
 × 320. The frames collected from each thermal video have a frequency of 1 frame every 2 s. The C-shaped
 table takes approximately 200 frames (400 s) per printing layer, while the tree totem typically has a layer
 time of around 115 frames (230 s). The thermal frames of the “Tree totem” dataset contain four identical
 single bead objects, printed at the same layer level, one after another. A single tree-totem features curved,
 sharp, and wide corners. Each frame of the “Table” dataset also includes multiple printed objects, with only
 one exhibiting a complete shape. Specifically, the C-shaped table has a double bead structure, which
 presents a more complex cooling dynamic compared to the single bead geometry. Furthermore, the corner
 shape of the C-shaped table changes over time, indicating that this geometry is not homogeneous.

4.3 Summary

 In summary, a data-driven hybrid CNN-LSTM model was developed to predict the spatiotemporal
 evolution of temperature fields for large-scale AM using infrared thermal imaging. The proposed model
 integrates an offline deep CNN auto-encoder, designed to capture essential spatial features within thermal
 images, with a real-time stacked LSTM encoder-decoder to bridge the CNN auto-encoder, incorporating
 temporal information for arbitrary-length future thermal images. The framework was validated using IR
 thermal images recorded during large-scale AM of C-shaped table and tree-totem geometries. The hybrid
 CNN-LSTM model achieved high prediction accuracy while reducing training time by more than an order
 of magnitude compared to conventional ConvLSTM models. The architecture supports online, real-time
 prediction and forms a foundation for future closed-loop thermal monitoring and layer-time control in large-
 scale polymer extrusion based additive manufacturing.

 5. Conclusions

 This study presented a comprehensive multi-level framework for layer time optimization in large-scale
 additive manufacturing, combining high-fidelity simulations, reduced-order thermal modeling,
 optimization algorithms, and data-driven control approaches. Through detailed finite element (FEA)
 simulations, the research demonstrated the ability to accurately predict temperature profiles and layer
 surface temperatures in the LSAM® process, providing a reliable foundation for determining optimized
 layer deposition times. The proposed optimization algorithm successfully identified the optimal layer times
 for simple geometries, such as single-bead walls, and can be extended as a guideline for LSAM® users to
 minimize failed prints caused by suboptimal parameter settings.

 Building on this foundation, a reduced one-dimensional physics-based model was integrated within the
 optimization framework to efficiently predict temperature histories and iteratively adjust layer deposition

                                                 42 | P a g e

times. This model captures the geometric influence of different shapes, such as hexagonal and star
geometries, while significantly reducing computational costs compared to high-fidelity FEA or
experimental approaches. By iteratively converging on a target surface temperature, the optimization
process determines an optimal deposition time that balances cooling and reheating cycles, ensuring proper
interlayer bonding and minimizing thermal-induced defects.

To further enhance process adaptability, an adaptive layer time control system was developed to
dynamically compensate for temperature mismatches in the initial layers, primarily influenced by lower
print bed temperatures. The adaptive system calibrates parameters such as bed temperature and convection
coefficients in real-time, achieving accurate temperature predictions and consistent layer deposition across
materials and geometries. This adaptive control reduced temperature deviations and residual deformations,
demonstrating its potential for real-time implementation in extrusion-based AM.

Finally, a data-driven hybrid CNN–LSTM model was proposed to predict the spatiotemporal evolution of
thermal fields during printing. By integrating spatial encoding through a deep CNN auto-encoder and
temporal sequence modeling via a stacked LSTM, the hybrid framework achieved high prediction accuracy
with significantly reduced training time. Validated using infrared thermal imaging of complex geometries
such as C-shaped and tree-totem structures, the model enables online, real-time prediction of temperature
distributions, paving the way for future closed-loop thermal monitoring and intelligent layer time control.
Overall, this integrated framework from FEA-based optimization to adaptive and data-driven control
establishes a scalable pathway for intelligent process management in large-scale additive manufacturing.
The findings underscore that optimized and adaptively controlled layer times are critical not only for
maintaining structural and thermal consistency but also for enhancing print quality, minimizing defects,
and improving production efficiency. The methodologies developed in this work can be extended to more
complex geometries involving multi-bead structures and curved paths, ultimately advancing the industrial
applicability of large-scale polymer extrusion-based additive manufacturing in tooling, aerospace,
automotive, and construction sectors.
