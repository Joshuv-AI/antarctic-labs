Preclinical Models of Rare Corneal and Ocular Surface Diseases: a Comprehensive Narrative Review
Authors: Erica A. L. Lecchi, Ava Dashti, Laura Frutos-Rincón, Silvia Palombella, Matteo Pederzolli, Juana Gallar, Neil Lagali, Giulio Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rare eye diseases of the cornea and ocular surface (REDs) remain a clinical challenge owing to their low prevalence, heterogeneous presentation, and limited therapeutic options. As REDs mechanisms are often complex, preclinical models are essential to advance mechanistic understanding and support the development of targeted treatments. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the experimental platforms currently available to study REDs, including in vitro models such as primary and engineered cell systems, coculture approaches, and emerging 3D organoid technologies. In addition, we summarize in vivo strategies ranging from surgically induced models to genetic and transgenic systems that reproduce the relevant ocular phenotypes. By comparing the strengths, limitations, and translational value of these complementary approaches, this review offers an integrated perspective on how preclinical modeling can be optimized to investigate REDs pathophysiology. Furthermore, we highlight how emerging tools—such as organ-on-chip platforms and artificial intelligence-assisted analytics—may enhance model fidelity and accelerate the identification of effective therapeutic strategies.

Key Summary Points

Rare eye diseases (REDs) are difficult to study as they affect only small population, and large clinical trials are challenging. Therefore, the development of in vitro systems (e.g., 2D cultures, organoids, bioprinted niches) and in vivo models (e.g., genetically modified mice, chemically or surgically induced disease) are essential not only to study the pathology itself but also to provide a translational pipeline from target discovery to preclinical testing.Each single RED model can be induced in different ways, ranging from physical debridement and chemical burns to genetic mutations or via stereotaxic nerve lesions or the application of topical capsaicin, enabling the mimicking of specific mechanisms and the testing of targeted interventions.A given pathological feature can be reproduced using different experimental approaches, allowing the exploration of the relative contribution of different etiological causes and supporting translational relevance, as human patients with the same clinical diagnosis often harbor heterogeneous underlying causes.REDs often share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms, such as epithelial stem cell dysfunction, chronic inflammation, tissue fibrosis, neovascularization, neurodegeneration, or impaired wound healing. Therefore, a single preclinical model can be used to investigate multiple diseases, facilitating cross-disease therapeutic drugs.

Introduction

Rare eye diseases of the cornea and ocular surface (REDs) pose significant and multifaceted clinical challenges owing to their low prevalence, heterogeneous clinical presentation, and often progressive, vision-threatening nature. These conditions comprise a diverse group of pathologies that affect the structural and functional integrity of the cornea and ocular surface. REDs can result in severe visual impairment and substantially diminish quality of life [1]. Moreover, conducting clinical studies is hampered by the scarcity and geographic dispersion of patients.

For these reasons, preclinical models of human diseases play an essential role in advancing our understanding of disease etiology, pathology, and progression and are essential for guiding the development of effective therapies. These include not only in vivo (mostly rodent) but also advanced in vitro systems, including traditional two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures and innovative three-dimensional (3D) models, which offer a controlled environment to investigate cellular responses, toxicity, and mechanism of action before proceeding to in vivo tests or clinical trials [2]. Collectively, a multifaceted approach employing both in vitro and in vivo models can enhance the reliability and translational potential of preclinical research advancing personalized and precision medicine strategies for REDs [3].

Method for Literature Research

This narrative review is based on a nonsystematic search conducted primarily on PubMed, including articles published between 2000 and 2026 and limited to English-language publications. This timeframe was selected to capture the most relevant and contemporary developments in preclinical models of ocular surfaces diseases. Earlier studies were also included when considered essential for understanding the pathophysiology of specific conditions. Additional studies were identified through reference lists and ResearchGate to include emerging or less indexed data. The depth of analysis across diseases reflects the heterogeneity in the available literature, with several conditions being more extensively characterized than others.

Limbal Stem Cell Deficiency (LSCD)

LSCD is characterized by a partial or complete loss of limbal stem cells or their function, resulting in an inability to properly heal or regenerate the corneal epithelium and maintain the limbal barrier, eventually leading to epithelial breakdown, persistent epithelial defects, and conjunctivalization of the corneal surface [4, 5]. Secondary complications such as corneal neovascularization, stromal scarring, and chronic inflammation often follow. Collectively, these changes cause a loss of corneal clarity, progressive visual impairment, chronic ocular pain and photophobia and may also result in the failure of corneal transplantation (keratoplasty) if the underlying stem cell deficiency is not addressed beforehand [6].

The etiology can be congenital when it is caused by mutations (for example in the PAX6 or TP63 gene) or acquired if it is associated with other conditions such as OCP, GVHD, infectious keratitis, chemical injuries, and contact lens-related hypoxia.

In addition to epithelial cell depletion, inflammatory processes play a key role in the progression of LSCD. Increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration contribute to disruption of the limbal niche and impair regenerative capacity. However, current in vitro and in vivo models only partially reproduce this complex microenvironment, particularly lacking the full integration of immune, vascular and neural components [6].

In Vitro Models of LSCD

In vitro models of LSCD comprise the classical coculture of limbal stem cells (LSCs) isolated from small limbal biopsies (1–2 mm2) with irradiated murine 3T3 fibroblasts as a feeder layer providing trophic support, as well as human fibroblast–based feeder systems for xeno-free applications; LiPSC-derived limbal epithelial cells, which offer an ethical and human-relevant platform for toxicity testing [7]; biological and synthetic carriers—such as amniotic membrane, fibrin, collagen scaffolds, and silicone hydrogel contact lenses—to enhance adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [8]; and three-dimensional niche models, including 3D bioprinting, which more reliably replicate the LSC microenvironment required for maintenance and function, enabling advanced disease modeling [9]. In vitro models of LSCD

Use of Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (LiPSCs)

An innovative approach is the use of limbal epithelial cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (LiPSCs). A study demonstrated that LiPSCs can serve as a cellular alternative for in vitro ocular toxicity testing, replicating the characteristics of normal corneal epithelial cells and showing appropriate responses to toxicity assays. This approach offers a more ethical and relevant method compared with traditional animal models [7].

Cultivation on Collagen Scaffolds

Another study developed modified collagen scaffolds to enhance the adhesion and proliferation of limbal epithelial stem cells. The results showed that cells cultured on these modified scaffolds exhibited significantly improved proliferation and the ability to differentiate into mature epithelial cells. The use of modified collagen scaffolds represents an effective strategy for the in vitro cultivation of LSCs, with potential applications in corneal regeneration and cell therapy [8].

3D Bioprinting the Limbal Niche

Recently, Kauppila et al. proposed an innovative 3D bioprinting model to create a compartmentalized limbal niche with distinct subpopulations of human pluripotent stem cell-derived limbal stem cells. This model demonstrated the ability to support the growth and differentiation of LSCs in a three-dimensional context, allowing for more realistic studies of cellular interactions and corneal pathology. The 3D bioprinting approach offers new opportunities for disease modeling and cellular therapy research, enhancing the understanding of pathological mechanisms and therapeutic responses [9].

Mouse

Among the various models used to study LSCD, mice, and particularly the C57BL/6 and BALB/c strains, are the most commonly employed owing to the availability of numerous transgenic and knockout lines. LSCD can be induced in mice through multiple approaches. Physical methods include epithelial debridement, with or without removal of limbus [11]. Another physical method, the AlgerBrush, provides uniform epithelial injury with minimal inflammation, though it may not fully replicate severe damage [11]. Chemical injury is also widely used, including sodium hydroxide (NaOH), which effectively removes the epithelium but can trigger unpredictable inflammation and neovascularization, or n-heptanol, which selectively depletes epithelial cells while sparing the stroma, particularly when combined with other surgical techniques [12]. Less common approaches include topical administration of benzalkonium chloride, which induces LSCD in mice, though its long-term effects remain unclear. Additionally, congenital models, such as the Pax6+/− mouse, are used to study inherited LSCD, while experimental methods such as forced eyelid opening after birth are under investigation [12]. Using multiple strains allows researchers to investigate strain-specific responses in LSCD pathogenesis and therapeutic outcomes [13].

Rabbit

LSCD is typically induced using the physical and chemical methods described for mice, allowing controlled and reproducible injury. Rabbits provide a reliable platform for studying LSCD pathophysiology, corneal wound healing, and testing regenerative or surgical therapies [12–15].

Rats

The Sprague Dawley strain is the most commonly used rat model. LSCD can be induced using the same physical and chemical methods as in mice [16]. Nude rats (F344/Njcl-rnu/rnu), which lack a functional thymus and have impaired cell-mediated immunity, are particularly valuable for transplantation studies, as they allow engraftment of human cells or tissues without immune rejection, providing a unique platform to evaluate the integration and function of limbal stem cell therapies [12, 14, 17].

Goat

Guanzhong dairy goats are a large-animal model with ocular anatomy similar to humans. LSCD can be induced via physical/surgical methods or chemical injury such as alkali burns. These models allow assessment of surgical techniques, limbal stem cell transplantation, and long-term tissue regeneration, bridging the gap between small-animal studies and clinical translation. While logistically challenging and costly, goat models are ideal for preclinical testing of therapies requiring human-scale eyes [18, 19].

Frog

Although not commonly used in LSCD research, Xenopus laevis has been considered a valuable alternative model. LSCD in Xenopus is typically induced using physical methods or psoralen administration combined with UVA light to selectively deplete limbal stem cells. This model allows detailed investigation of corneal epithelial regeneration, stem cell dynamics, and tissue plasticity owing to the species’ high regenerative capacity. Although the anatomy and size of the frog eye differ significantly from mammals, Xenopus provides unique insights into developmental processes and fundamental mechanisms of limbal stem cell maintenance and repair, which can inform translational approaches in mammalian models. Its use is particularly advantageous when studying basic stem cell biology, signalling pathways, and regeneration, even though it is less suitable for surgical or translational intervention studies compared with rabbit or goat models [20].

Conclusions on LSCD Models

For studying inflammatory responses and neovascularization following chemical injury, the mouse (C57BL/6 or BALB/c) and rabbit models are most suitable owing to the robust inflammatory phenotype induced by sodium hydroxide. When investigating immune rejection mechanisms and xenotransplantation, the nude rat (F344/Njcl-rnu/rnu) represents the optimal choice, as its impaired cell-mediated immunity permits engraftment of human cells without rejection. For inherited or congenital LSCD and related genetic pathways, the Pax6+/− transgenic mouse provides an invaluable tool. If the research focuses on surgical techniques, limbal stem cell transplantation, or long-term tissue regeneration in a human-scale eye, the Guanzhong dairy goat serves as the ideal preclinical model bridging small-animal studies and clinical translation. For fundamental investigations into stem cell biology, regenerative dynamics, and developmental signaling pathways, the frog (Xenopus laevis) offers unique advantages owing to its high regenerative capacity. Finally, for in vitro mechanistic studies, ocular toxicity testing, and disease modeling without the use of animals, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived limbal epithelial cells (LiPSCs) cultured on modified collagen scaffolds or within 3D-bioprinted limbal niches represent the most ethical, human-relevant, and technologically advanced alternatives.

Aniridia Associated Keratopathy (AAK)

Congenital aniridia is a developmental eye disorder resulting from disrupted normal eye formation showing to haploinsufficiency of the PAX6 protein, typically caused by heterozygous mutations in the PAX6 gene [21]. Aniridia comprises multiple ocular abnormalities, including AAK, a corneal condition in which a gradual and progressive loss of corneal transparency occurs, and is characterized by inflammation, neovascularization, and LSCD. Symptoms of AAK include breakdown of the corneal surface and transformation to a conjunctival phenotype, with epithelial thinning or loss, inflammation with immune cell infiltration, vascularization and chronic progressive opacification. The progression of AAK is also influenced by chronic low-grade inflammation and altered immune responses associated with PAX6 deficiency. Inflammatory mediators may contribute to epithelial instability and corneal degeneration. Nonetheless, existing models incompletely capture these immune-related mechanisms, limiting their ability to fully reflect disease progression in humans [21].

Primary Cells and Aniridia-Associated Keratopathy (AAK)

Primary cells are essential for studying aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK), a condition resulting in impaired cell migration and differentiation. Models include direct culture of patient-derived LESCs and transfection of healthy donor-derived LESCs with PAX6-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA). In both cases, cells display reduced proliferation and migration, altered morphology, and impaired colony-forming ability [22]. At the molecular level, the cells exhibit decreased expression of stem cell markers (p63α, ABCG2, K15) and increased expression of corneal differentiation markers (K3, K12). Importantly, siRNA-treated donor LESCs closely mimic the phenotype and molecular profile of patient-derived cells, providing a reliable platform for studying AAK pathogenesis and testing therapeutic strategies [22–25].

Immortalized Cell Lines and AAK Modeling

CRISPR–Cas9 technology has been used to create immortalized LESC models with PAX6 mutations, showing the potential to rescue the AAK phenotype. A notable example is the development of telomerase-immortalized limbal epithelial stem cells, where CRISPR–Cas9 was used to introduce a p.E109X mutation in the PAX6 gene associated with AAK. The edited clones (PAX6+/− T-LSCs) were validated and cultured in serum-free medium. Rescue experiments using recombinant PAX6 protein demonstrated restoration of normal phenotypes and its own efficient gene regulation improving impaired cell functions. This model accurately replicates the AAK phenotype in vitro and highlights the critical role of balanced PAX6 levels [22, 23].

3D Cellular Models (Organoids)

While 2D models fail to replicate the tissue complexity observed in vivo, organoids provide a more physiologically relevant system for studying ocular diseases, including AAK. Although current ocular organoid models still require further refinement to fully mimic in vivo conditions, Koc et al. recently developed corneal organoids derived from hiPSCs to model AAK [26–28]. These organoids recapitulate several of the key pathological features of aniridia, such as altered corneal epithelial organization and impaired differentiation. However, they only partially reproduce the human phenotype, meaning that while they represent a valuable platform for mechanistic studies and therapeutic testing, their translational relevance remains limited compared with patient-derived tissues [29].

Mouse

Mouse models are widely used to study congenital aniridia and AAK owing to the high conservation between human PAX6 and mouse Pax6 genes, which allows faithful replication of many features of the human disease. The heterozygous Pax6+/− “small eye” (Sey) mouse is the most commonly employed model. These animals show progressive corneal opacification, iris hypoplasia, shallow anterior chamber, and corneal vascular changes, making it ideal for studying disease progression and age-dependent phenotypes. On the 129Sey/SvImJ background, these mice exhibit mild features at birth that worsen with aging, closely mirroring human pathology [21, 30]. Importantly, recent work has shown that a mild central corneal epithelial wound markedly accelerates AAK progression in Sey mice, providing a robust and time-efficient model for preclinical studies [31].

A broad range of Pax6 mutant alleles have been developed to model different aspects of aniridia. Large deletion alleles, such as Pax6Sey-H and Pax6Sey-Dey, result in severe ocular malformations affecting Pax6 and neighboring genes, while point mutations, frameshifts, and premature termination alleles (e.g., Pax6Sey, Pax6Sey-Neu, Pax6ADD4802) generate hypomorphic or null phenotypes. Conditional alleles, such as Pax6flox or Pax6LacZ, allow spatial or temporal control of gene disruption, while specialized variants (Pax64Neu, Pax67Neu, Pax6Coop) provide further flexibility for studying tissue-specific effects [20].

Other models have been developed to examine specific disease features. The Aey80 mouse exhibits lymphatic and partial blood vessel invasion in the cornea, age-dependent increases in intraocular pressure, and progressive corneal thickening, modeling both vascular changes and glaucoma risk [32] Fey mice, which can present structural anomalies such as keratolenticular adhesions, have been used in experimental gene-editing studies. CRISPR–Cas9 delivered via lipid nanoparticles into the corneal stroma has shown potential for in vivo Pax6 correction, though technical challenges remain, including precise delivery in small eyes and low endogenous Pax6 expression in stromal and endothelial cells [33]. Potential pharmacologic therapies have also been tested in Sey mice [34, 35].

Zebrafish Model

The Sunrise (sri) mutant is the most widely explored zebrafish model with a pax6b mutation and exhibits aniridia-like phenotypes, for example, abnormal lens and corneal structure, thick cornea, iris hypoplasia, retinal malformations, shallower anterior chamber, and a smaller eye [36].

African Clawed Frog (Xenopus)

In Xenopus tropicalis, targeted mutations in Pax6 have been generated using transcription activator-like effector nucleases. Embryos with null mutations exhibit severe eye abnormalities, including malformed retinas and absence of lens formation. These phenotypes underscore the critical role of Pax6 in ocular development [37].

Rat

Rat small-eye mutations (rSey and rSey2) cause a similar phenotype. Pax6+/− small eye mice rSey is a new autosomal dominant mutation in rats and spontaneously arose in a colony of Sprague Dawley (SD) rats. The genetics and phenotype of the mutant rat closely resembled the Sey mutation in mice as the rat Pax-6 amino acid sequence is identical to the mouse gene product [38].

Conclusions on AAK Models

For in vitro studies of PAX6 haploinsufficiency, primary LESCs and siRNA-treated healthy donor LESCs reliably recapitulate the impaired proliferation, migration, and altered molecular signature (reduced p63α, ABCG2, K15; increased K3, K12) characteristic of AAK. For genetic manipulation and rescue experiments, CRISPR–Cas9-edited immortalized cell lines (PAX6+/− T-LSCs) with recombinant PAX6 protein restoration offer a powerful platform. For studies requiring three-dimensional tissue architecture, hiPSC-derived corneal organoids provide physiological relevance, though they only partially reproduce the human phenotype. Regarding in vivo models, the heterozygous Pax6+/− “small eye” (Sey) mouse on the 129Sey/SvImJ background represents the gold standard, faithfully replicating progressive corneal opacification and iris hypoplasia, with wounding accelerating disease progression for preclinical testing. Additional Pax6 mutant alleles (Aey80, Fey) and conditional models allow study of vascular changes, glaucoma risk, and gene-editing strategies. For nonmammalian alternatives, the zebrafish Sunrise (sri) mutant (pax6b mutation) and Xenopus tropicalis with targeted pax6 mutations model aniridia-like ocular defects, while the rat small-eye mutant (rSey, rSey2) closely mirrors the mouse phenotype. Thus, the Sey mouse remains the most comprehensive model, complemented by in vitro, organoid, zebrafish, Xenopus, and rat models depending on the specific research question.

Ectrodactyly-Ectodermal Dysplasia-Clefting (EEC) Syndrome

EEC syndrome is caused by heterozygous missense point mutations in the DNA-binding domain of TP63 gene, encoding for the p63 protein, a critical transcription factor involved in embryogenesis and stem cell differentiation in stratified epithelia. Most EEC syndrome cases are sporadic, arising from de novo mutations during early embryonic development, while familial cases follow autosomal dominant inheritance patterns [39, 40].

Patients with EEC syndrome typically exhibit split hand/foot malformations, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, and significant ocular surface disorders. Ocular symptoms include recurrent blepharitis, conjunctivitis, superficial corneal microlesions, spontaneous corneal ulcerations, and corneal perforation. These complications frequently progress to severe corneal clouding, resulting in significant visual impairment or blindness [40] [39]. Approximately 61% of patients with EEC also develop LSCD, with severity and prevalence increasing with age. Although EEC syndrome is primarily driven by genetic alterations affecting epithelial development, inflammatory pathways may contribute to disease severity and ocular surface deterioration. The contribution of immune mechanisms remains insufficiently explored in current models, which are mainly focused on genetic and developmental aspects [39].

In Vitro Models of EEC Syndrome

iPSC-Derived Corneal Epithelial Models (TP63-Mutant)

In vitro models have been established using patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from fibroblasts harbouring TP63 mutations. These EEC-iPSCs effectively replicated the impaired epithelial differentiation observed in epidermal and corneal tissues of affected individuals. While these models offer patient-specific insights and clinical relevance, variability among patient-derived cells and limited complexity compared with in vivo conditions pose significant drawbacks [41]

Genetically Engineered Corneal Epithelial Cell Lines (TP63 Mutations)

One study transfected human cells with hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged wild-type p63 and Myc-tagged R304W-mutant p63 to create an in vitro EEC-like model, allowing functional assays of the mutant’s effect. In this controlled system, the mutant p63 markedly interfered with normal p63 activity. Such engineered cell lines (including noncorneal lines used as reporters) have been used to test therapeutic interventions: e.g., allele-specific siRNA was designed to selectively silence the mutant TP63 transcript, which restored wild-type p63 function in the model [42].

In Vivo Model of EEC Syndrome

Mouse

Mouse models carrying heterozygous TP63 mutations have been developed to study the ocular manifestations of EEC and related syndromes. The Trp63R279HN/+ and Trp63R279H/+ knock-in mice harbor a point mutation in the DNA-binding domain corresponding to the human p.Arg279His mutation [40]. Other models, such as the Trp63L514F/+ knock-in mice, carry a mutation in the SAM domain associated with Ankyloblepharon-ectodermal defects-cleft lip/palate (AEC) syndrome. Although the clinical presentation differs from EEC—patients with AEC typically show fused eyelids at birth—these mice develop ocular surface abnormalities including mild LSCD, corneal neovascularization, and conjunctivalization, demonstrating that p63 dysfunction in different domains can similarly impair corneal epithelial integrity [44].

Conclusions on EEC Syndrome Models

In summary, for EEC syndrome, iPSC-derived corneal epithelial models offer patient-specific insights into impaired differentiation, while engineered cell lines (transfected with wild-type and R304W-mutant p63) enable therapeutic screening, including allele-specific siRNA to restore wild-type function. Neurotrophic Keratopathy (NK)

Neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) is a degenerative corneal condition resulting from corneal nerve damage, which leads to visual impairment from nonhealing corneal epithelial defects, stromal melting or ulceration, and neovascularization.

In vitro models of NK primarily consist of neuronal cell cultures used to study axonal growth, neuroprotection, and nerve regeneration. In Vivo Models of NK

Experimentally Induced NK

On the basis of the anatomic distribution of corneal sensory innervation, most experimental approaches to induce NK involve damage to the trigeminal nerve. This damage can be induced using different methods in various species. In the past, rabbits were the most common animal model for NK; however, recent studies are performed in mice [45–48].

Stereotaxic Thermic, Electric, or Chemical Disruption

Stereotaxic thermocoagulation involves the vertical insertion of a coagulating electrode through a hole in the skull to reach the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve, which is then coagulated by applying high temperatures (approximately 60 °C) for several seconds [49]. Stereotaxic electrocautery consists of inserting a monopolar electrode through a cranial opening to reach the ophthalmomaxillary nerve and applying an electrical power of 10 W for 60 s to achieve its ablation [50]; stereotaxic electrolysis, first described in mice, entails the destruction of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal ganglion by applying a 2 mA current for a few seconds at three different sites along the nerve [47]. Stereotaxic ethanol injection achieves corneal denervation by injecting 2 µL of ethanol into the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve at three different locations using a microsyringe [46]. Finally, surgical axotomy, a procedure widely used in mice, involves surgical transection or clamping of the ciliary nerves of the trigeminal nerve with sharp forceps between the sclera and the ciliary ganglion through the lateral fornices [45, 46, 48]. Corneal denervation can also be induced by topical exposure of the cornea to capsaicin (300 μM) for 30 min using a hydraulic column in anesthetized mice [51]. In addition to these experimental approaches for inducing NK, in vivo models originally developed to study other corneal diseases, such as corneal neovascularization, can also be used to investigate NK: for instance, alkali burn and HSV-1 infection models are commonly employed because of the corneal damage they induce, which includes sensory nerve impairment.

Transgenic Mouse Models

TRPV1-DTR Mice

Transient receptor potential channel vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), expressed in polymodal nociceptors, is crucial for heat sensing and chemical responses, and its ablation seems to produce NK in mice. After the intraperitoneal injection of diphtheria toxin to selectively eliminate TRPV1+ neurons in TRPV1-DTR mice, these animals present different clinical manifestations compatible with corneal NK, such as reduced corneal sensation and nerve density, reduced tear secretion and epithelial and neurotrophic defects, among others [52]. Taking all of these into account, TRPV1-DTR mice seem to be an excellent model for NK pathogenesis research.

Mice Expressing a Dominant-Negative ErbB4 Receptor in Nonmyelinating Schwann Cells

Some preliminary data from a transgenic line of mice expressing a dominant-negative ErbB4 receptor in non-myelinating Schwann cells suggest that these mice could represent a novel murine model of NK. The loss of ErbB4 signalling impairs corneal innervation and sensitivity, leading to a decrease in neuropeptide expression, as well as an increase in corneal inflammation and apoptosis [53].

Conclusions on NK Models

Experimentally induced models in mice (stereotaxic techniques, surgical axotomy, topical capsaicin) enable the study of acute denervation-induced loss of corneal sensation and epithelial breakdown, while alkali burn and HSV-1 infection models allow study of NK secondary to corneal damage with concurrent inflammation. Among transgenic models, the TRPV1-DTR mouse (after diphtheria toxin) is excellent for studying NK pathogenesis, including reduced corneal sensation, nerve density, tear secretion, and neurotrophic defects. The ErbB4 dominant-negative mouse model enables to study impaired innervation, neuropeptide downregulation, inflammation, and apoptosis. Thus, experimental models address acute nerve injury, while TRPV1-DTR mice providing a genetic platform for comprehensive NK pathogenesis.

Corneal Neovascularization (CN)

Under physiological conditions, the cornea is completely devoid of blood vessels [54], as a multitude of factors prevent new vessel ingrowth (angiogenesis) into the cornea, including pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), thrombospondins, soluble VEGF receptor-1 (sFlt-1), angiostatin, and endostatin, which together maintain corneal avascularity and transparency [55]. However, conditions such as inflammation, infection, or hypoxia can disrupt these homeostatic mechanisms, leading to CN and, in turn, to corneal opacity [56]. Angiogenesis is a multifactorial process which involves various cell types, such as vascular endothelial cells, pericytes and fibroblasts, among others, and pro-angiogenic factors [57].

Among pro-angiogenic factors, VEGF is probably the most extensively studied.

Current models are effective in studying angiogenesis, but they often fail to fully capture the complexity of immune regulation involved in vascular growth and regression.

In Vitro Models of CN

In vitro assays for studying angiogenesis mainly consist of evaluating the effects of pro- or anti-angiogenic stimuli on endothelial cells and assessing specific stages of the angiogenic process in a controlled environment (Table 8). One commonly used approach is the wound healing (scratch) assay, in which a scratch is manually created in a confluent monolayer of vascular endothelial cells (usually human umbilical vein-derived) using a sterile needle or pipette tip. Cells at the edges of the wound migrate into the cell-free area, and their movement can be recorded in real time at regular intervals for quantitative analysis [58, 59]. Although this assay is not specific for corneal neovascularization per se, it has been widely applied in CN research when performed on vascular endothelial cells and in the presence of cornea-derived factors or angiogenic stimuli relevant to the corneal microenvironment, allowing the assessment of endothelial cell migratory responses associated with neovascular processes. Another frequently employed technique is the Matrigel assay, which utilizes a gelatinous protein mixture extracted from Engelbreth–Holm–Swarm mouse sarcoma, rich in laminin (60%), collagen IV (30%), and growth factors (8%), that mimics the basement membrane environment [60]. In CN studies, this assay is used to evaluate endothelial cell organization and network formation in response to angiogenic cues. The spheroid sprouting assay is a three-dimensional model in which endothelial cells, alone or in combination with other vessel wall-associated cell types, form spheroids embedded within an extracellular matrix such as collagen or fibrin [61]. In Vivo Models of CN

Experimentally Induced Ocular Angiogenesis

For many years, experimentally induced ocular angiogenesis has served as a standardized model for evaluating angiogenesis in vivo.

Corneal Pocket Assay

The corneal micropocket angiogenesis assay is a quantitative, reproducible, flexible assessment of angiogenesis in vivo [57]. In this assay, a slow-release pellet containing pro-angiogenic factors is implanted into the cornea and the neoangiogenesis develops over several days, depending on the type and dose of growth factor administered. Neovascularization is typically induced by either basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). CN is usually assessed 5–7 days after pellet implantation. Initially, this model was used to evaluate nonspecific angiogenic stimuli in rabbit corneas [3] and has been further refined over time. The corneal micropocket assay has also been performed in mice [55] and rats [57] and is also effective in inducing corneal lymphangiogenesis [62]

Alkali Burn Model

Alkali burn is one of the most widely used injury models to induce CN. In this model, a NaOH-soaked paper with a specific concentration of NaOH (0.1–2 N) is applied to the cornea for a defined time (10–30 s), to induce the development of CN [63].

Suture Models

Suture models are created by placing one or more fine sutures (typically 10-0 or 11-0 nylon) in a radial pattern in the corneal stroma, close to the limbus area [16]. This model is described in mice [64], rats [65] and rabbits [66].

Removal of corneal and limbal epithelium

Extensive removal of the corneal and/or limbal epithelium is a commonly used experimental approach to induce LSCD; however, this model can also lead to the development of CN as a secondary consequence of epithelial barrier loss and stromal inflammation.

This method can be modulated by controlling location (central versus peripheral) and depth of debridement. Based on these criteria, epithelial debridement can be classified as superficial or deep and either partial or total for both the limbal and corneal epithelium [67, 68]. Epithelium removal can be performed using a sterile disposable scalpel, an ophthalmic spatula followed by surgical excision or a corneal rust ring remover. Epithelial debridement has been performed in mice [68], rabbits [69], and rats [69].

Corneal Infection Models

Corneal infections caused by various pathogens can lead to inflammation, which subsequently triggers the formation of new lymphatic and blood vessels. Therefore, another approach to produce CN is based on induction of infectious keratitis, typically by Pseudomonas aeruginosa for bacterial keratitis [56], herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) for viral keratitis, and Candida albicans for fungal keratitis.

Transgenic Mouse Models

Transgenic mouse models are genetically engineered animals that spontaneously develop CN and have been extensively used to study the mechanisms underlying corneal angiogenesis. Among these models, Destrin−/− (Corn1) mice, which carry a homozygous mutation in the destrin gene encoding actin-depolymerizing factor (ADF), develop spontaneous corneal hemangiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis [70]. The extent of CN in these mice is influenced by genetic background, with C57BL/6 mice showing reduced neovascularization compared with the A.BY mouse strain. LRIG1−/− mice, lacking the cell surface protein leucine-rich repeats and immunoglobulin-like domains 1 (LRIG1), develop spontaneous stromal neovascularization owing to the disinhibition of the STAT3 anti-inflammatory pathway, making them a model for inflammatory angiogenesis [71]. Similarly, inducible knockout mice for VEGF receptors 1 and 2 (VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2) exhibit spontaneous CN. JAM-A−/− mice, deficient in junctional adhesion molecule A (JAM-A), also develop CN and corneal opacification through upregulation of the VEGF-A/VEGFR-2 pathway, providing a valuable model for studying corneal angiogenesis. Conclusions on CN Models

In summary, in vitro CN models assess specific angiogenic stages: the scratch assay evaluates endothelial cell migration; the Matrigel assay assesses network formation; and the spheroid sprouting assay investigates capillary-like sprouting in response to pro-angiogenic factors (VEGF, FGF-2, PDGF) or corneal-derived signals. In vivo, the corneal micropocket assay (rabbits, mice, rats) enables quantitative assessment of angiogenesis/lymphangiogenesis induced by bFGF or VEGF pellets. The alkali burn model (NaOH) and suture model (nylon sutures near limbus) induce CN via chemical and mechanical injury, respectively. Epithelial debridement causes CN secondary to barrier loss and inflammation. Infection models (Pseudomonas, HSV-1, Candida) induce CN secondary to infectious keratitis. Among transgenic models, Destrin−/− mice spontaneously develop hemangiogenesis/lymphangiogenesis, and LRIG1−/− mice allow the study of inflammatory angiogenesis via STAT3 disinhibition. Moreover, JAM-A−/− mice demonstrated VEGF-A/VEGFR-2 upregulation, while FOXC1−/− and FOXC2−/− mice are a valuable alternative to study developmental vascular growth. Stromal neovascularization can be investigated with Pax6+/−models, whereas KLEIP−/− mice model explores progressive CN dystrophy with high penetrance, being particularly suitable for studying endothelial dynamics.

Ocular Graft-versus-host Disease (oGVHD)

GVHD is a major complication of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), occurring in 40–60% of human recipients. It is characterized by a multiorgan inflammatory response caused by donor immune cells attacking recipient tissues. Ocular GVHD (oGVHD) is reported in up to 90% of cases, presenting as conjunctivitis, dry eye disease (DED), and corneal ulcers, which may progress to perforation [73, 74].

oGVHD is typically classified as acute or chronic. Acute oGVHD occurs in up to 20% of patients but may be underdiagnosed owing to limited early ophthalmological assessments. It presents in patients as chemosis and pseudomembranous conjunctivitis. Chronic oGVHD, affecting 40–90% of patients, typically arises 5–24 months post Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (aHSCT) and progresses rapidly as an inflammatory condition [75]. It often leads to a DED-like phenotype with lacrimal gland impairment, blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction, and damage to the conjunctival and corneal epithelium, causing dryness, burning, redness, and visual disturbances. The pathogenesis of oGVHD is predominantly immune-mediated, involving donor-derived T-cell activation, cytokine release (e.g., IL-1, TNF-α, IFN-γ), and chronic inflammation leading to epithelial damage and fibrosis. Although available models reproduce several aspects of this immune dysregulation, they do not fully mimic the complexity of human systemic and ocular immune interactions [76].

In Vivo Models of oGvHD

Mice

Murine models have proven indispensable for investigating oGVHD, as they reproduce several aspects of the human disease and allow the study of underlying mechanisms in vivo. One of the most widely used systems relies on BALB/c mice preconditioned with myeloablative total body irradiation and transplanted with C57BL/6N-derived bone marrow cells, with or without additional donor splenic cells (BM + T group) [77]. Disease onset is monitored by serial light microscopy imaging to evaluate corneal transparency together with eyelid inflammation (blepharitis) scoring [73].

Beyond this model, several other combinations have been characterized in detail and together provide a broader picture of oGVHD. The B10.D2 to BALB/c system, for instance, has been extensively employed to mimic chronic oGVHD. After conditioning with irradiation and transplantation of bone marrow and splenocytes, these mice develop features strongly reminiscent of the clinical phenotype, including reduced tear secretion, goblet cell loss, conjunctival fibrosis, corneal epithelial injury, alterations of corneal innervation, meibomian gland atrophy, eyelid fibrosis, and lacrimal gland inflammation with marked immune cell infiltration [74]. Another relevant model is based on the 129S2/SvPasCrl to C57BL/6 combination, which avoids irradiation and instead uses busulfan for myeloablation. This approach leads to an early onset disease characterized by corneal epithelial injury, lacrimal gland inflammation, and blepharitis, accompanied by both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Importantly, corneal lymphangiogenesis correlates with fluorescein staining and systemic GVHD severity, and the phenotype is exacerbated under adverse environmental conditions such as low humidity, underlining its translational relevance [74].

The C57BL/6 to C3H.SW system provides yet another perspective, as ocular manifestations typically appear 3–4 weeks after transplantation and progress to keratitis by week six. These animals display goblet cell loss, lacrimal gland fibrosis, diminished corneal nerve density and sensation, and meibomian gland dysfunction, with infiltrates composed largely of macrophages and donor-derived T cells. Finally, the C57BL/6 to BALB.b model initially manifests with moderate acute GVHD but progresses to chronic disease over 6–12 months, affecting the eye alongside the skin and liver. In this setting, naïve CD4+ T cells have been identified as the main disease drivers, contributing to both systemic and ocular pathology [74].

Conclusions on oGvHD Models

In summary, murine oGVHD models allow study specific disease features through different donor-recipient combinations. The BALB/c to C57BL/6N system (with irradiation) enables the monitoring of corneal transparency and blepharitis. The B10.D2 to BALB/c system models chronic oGVHD, studying tear reduction, goblet cell loss, conjunctival fibrosis, corneal injury, innervation defects, meibomian atrophy, eyelid fibrosis, and lacrimal gland inflammation. The 129S2 to C57BL/6 system (busulfan, no irradiation) studies early onset disease with CD4+/CD8+ T cell infiltration, corneal lymphangiogenesis, and low-humidity-exacerbated phenotype. The C57BL/6 to C3H.SW system investigates delayed keratitis (weeks 3–6) with goblet cell loss, lacrimal fibrosis, reduced nerve density/sensation, meibomian dysfunction, and macrophage/T cell infiltrates. The C57BL/6 to BALB.b system examines progression from acute to chronic GVHD (6–12 months) with naïve CD4+ T cells as disease drivers.

Ocular Cicatricial Pemphigoid (OCP)

OCP is a sight threatening autoimmune disease characterized by a progressive cicatrization of the conjunctiva and development of keratopathy. Current experimental models provide insight into specific immunopathological mechanisms but remain limited in reproducing the full spectrum of immune-driven tissue remodeling observed in patients [78].

In vitro model of OCP

Primary Human Conjunctival Cell Cultures

Established from conjunctival biopsies (either from patients with OCP or normal donors) these cultures include epithelial cells and stromal fibroblasts, allowing the study of integrin expression, cytokine responses, and effects of autoantibodies against basement membrane proteins [79].

Human Organ Culture Model

A human organotypic culture model, first described by Chan et al., employs normal bulbar conjunctiva maintained in organ culture, preserving the full tissue architecture, including the stratified epithelium, basement membrane zone, and underlying stroma. When exposed to sera from patients with OCP or to anti-β4 integrin monoclonal antibodies, the model exhibits separation of the basement membrane zone, effectively recapitulating the hallmark subepithelial blistering observed in OCP. This system allows the investigation of the mechanisms by which autoantibodies disrupt epithelial–stromal adhesion and contribute to conjunctival scarring. Moreover, the model provides a platform for testing potential therapeutic interventions in a human-relevant context while avoiding the limitations of animal models [78].

In Vivo Models of OCP

Rabbit Model

Injection of the monoclonal antibody MAb63 into the basement membrane of the conjunctival epithelium induces conjunctival inflammation, neutrophil infiltration, and IgG deposition along the basement membrane, closely resembling the acute phase of OCP. This model provides valuable insight into the immune-mediated mechanisms underlying the disease. However, as OCP is an autoimmune disorder with currently unclear and multifactorial pathogenesis, faithfully reproducing the human condition in animal models remains challenging [80]. Conclusion on OCP Models

In summary, in vitro models of OCP focus mainly on mechanisms of conjunctival epithelial detachment. Primary human conjunctival cell cultures enable the study of integrin expression, cytokine responses, and autoantibody effects against basement membrane proteins. The human organotypic culture model preserves full tissue architecture and, when exposed to sera from patients with OCP or anti-β4 integrin antibodies, it recapitulates subepithelial blistering, allowing investigation of autoantibody-mediated disruption of epithelial–stromal adhesion and testing of therapeutic interventions while avoiding animal limitations. As for in vivo models, the rabbit model involving injection of monoclonal antibody MAb63 into the conjunctival basement membrane induces acute-phase features including conjunctival inflammation, neutrophil infiltration, and IgG deposition along the basement membrane, providing insight into immune-mediated mechanisms.

Emerging Technologies and Future Directions

Emerging technologies are rapidly reshaping the landscape of preclinical modeling for rare ocular surface diseases, offering new opportunities to overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional in vitro and in vivo systems. Among these, organ-on-a-chip and microphysiological systems are particularly promising, as they enable the recapitulation of key aspects of corneal physiology, including tissue–tissue interfaces, fluid dynamics, and mechanical stress under controlled and reproducible conditions. In parallel, advances in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived corneal models are facilitating the development of patient-specific platforms that capture disease heterogeneity and allow personalized mechanistic studies. Complementing these experimental approaches, artificial intelligence- and machine learning–based in silico models are increasingly being used to integrate multiomics, imaging, and experimental datasets, supporting predictive modeling of disease progression and therapeutic response. Looking forward, the convergence of these technologies within hybrid experimental–computational frameworks is expected to significantly improve translational relevance, reduce dependence on animal models, and accelerate therapeutic discovery for rare and currently intractable ocular diseases [81].

Conclusions

This review provides a comprehensive and up-to-date comparative analysis of preclinical models for rare corneal and ocular surface diseases, critically outlining their respective strengths and limitations across both in vitro and in vivo platforms. By systematically integrating and contrasting current experimental approaches, it offers a practical framework to guide model selection on the basis of specific research questions, while also highlighting key methodological constraints and existing translational gaps that continue to limit clinical applicability. Beyond established methodologies, emerging technologies such as organ-on-a-chip systems and artificial intelligence-driven in silico modeling represent a significant paradigm shift in the field [81]. These approaches enable the development of more physiologically relevant, dynamic, and patient-specific disease models, with improved capacity to capture complex pathophysiological mechanisms that are poorly recapitulated by conventional systems. Importantly, the convergence of bioengineered platforms with data-driven computational frameworks is expected to enhance predictive validity, support reduction and refinement of animal use, and facilitate a more efficient and mechanistically informed drug discovery pipeline. Collectively, these advances are poised to bridge critical translational gaps and accelerate the transition from experimental modeling to clinically meaningful interventions in rare ocular diseases.
