Distributed optimal control problems governed by poroelasticity equations
Arbaz Khan, Jeonghun J. Lee, Harpal Singh

Abstract
. In this paper, we propose and analyze a novel two-field symmetric formulation with solid displacement and fluid pressure as main unknowns for the Biot’s consolidation model in poroelasticity. Firstly, we prove the well-posedness of the new formulation and then show the existence and uniqueness of optimal control where the fluid sources in the model act as a control variable. We prove a priori error estimates for the fully discrete scheme with backward Euler time discretization and a variational approximation of the control variable. A numerical example is presented to validate the performance of the proposed novel scheme.

1.
Introduction

Mathematical models that describe how fluids move and how materials deform within porous structures are essential in many fields, such as geophysics, engineering, medicine and biological applications, such as in the development of the robust mechanical modelling of tissues and cells
[
17
]
and the research on the poroelastic component of the postseismic process
[
18
]
. These models help us understand fluid flow in natural and artificial materials like cartilage, bones, tissue scaffolds, and even blood flow through organs like the brain, liver, and eyes. Because of their wide range of applications, flow problems in porous media have become a major topic of study in mathematics. Many researchers have focused on understanding the stability of these models, analyzing how sensitive they are to changes, and developing reliable numerical methods to simulate them.

A key model in this area is Biot’s consolidation model
[
2
]
. This model describes the interaction between interstitial fluid flowing through deformable porous media and has many applications, such as groundwater
[
12
]
, biological tissues
[
23
]
, carbon sequestration
[
25
]
, and materials science
[
26
]
. It plays a central role in simulating organ behaviour in medical applications and predicting how porous rocks respond in geophysical studies. In recent years, finite element methods have been widely developed and studied as powerful tools for solving Biot’s model accurately and efficiently.

1.1.
Biot’s consolidation model

In this paper, we focus on quasi static consolidation problems, as discussed in
[
14
]
. We consider situations where the consolidation process occurs slowly enough that acceleration effects can be neglected. Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz polygonal/polyhedral boundary
\partial\Omega
. We assume that there are two independent partitions of boundary
\partial\Omega
:

\displaystyle\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{p}\cup\Gamma_{f}=\Gamma_{d}\cup\Gamma_{t},\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\Gamma_{p}\cap\Gamma_{f}=\Gamma_{d}\cap\Gamma_{t}=\emptyset,

with positive
(n-1)
-dimensional surface measures
|\Gamma_{p}|,|\Gamma_{d}|,|\Gamma_{t}|>0
. The Biot’s consolidation model consists of an equilibrium equation, prescribing the conservation of the momentum, and of a continuity equation, stating the conservation of the mass. Biot’s consolidation model is described as follows: find the displacement of the porous medium
\boldsymbol{u}(t):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the fluid pressure
p(t):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(1a)

\displaystyle-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u}))+\alpha\nabla p

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}

\displaystyle\text{in}\ (0,T]\times\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle s_{0}\dot{p}+\alpha\text{div}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}})-\text{div}(\kappa\nabla p)

\displaystyle=-g

\displaystyle\text{in}\ (0,T]\times\Omega,

where
\mathcal{C}
is the elastic stiffness tensor,
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right)^{T}\right)
is the strain tensor,
\alpha>0
is the Biot–Willis coefficient (usually close to 1),
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(t):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the body force acting on the solid structure,
s_{0}\geq 0
is the constrained specific storage coefficient, the dot over the variables represents time derivative,
\kappa
is the permeability tensor which is a
d\times d
symmetric positive definite matrix with constant entries, and
g(t):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the source or sink term representing fluid gain or loss. For isotropic elastic porous media,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})=2\mu\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{I}
,
where
\boldsymbol{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the identity matrix,
\lambda
and
\mu
are Lamé constants related to the Young’s elasticity modulus
E
and the Poisson’s ratio
0<\nu<1/2
as

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\nu E}{(1-2\nu)(1+\nu)},\qquad\qquad\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}.

The boundary conditions are given by

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma_{p},

\displaystyle-\kappa\nabla p(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma_{f},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(t)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma_{d},

\displaystyle\ \ (\mathcal{C}\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})-\alpha p\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on}\ \Gamma_{t},

for all
t\in(0,T]
, where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward unit normal vector field on
\partial\Omega
. The boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{p}
and
\Gamma_{f}
are the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in the Darcy flow problems and those on
\Gamma_{d}
and
\Gamma_{t}
are also Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in linear elasticity. In the rest of this paper we assume that all boundary conditions are homogeneous for simplicity of presentation.

The poroelastic locking typically occurs when
s_{0}\geq 0
is very close to
0
, which corresponds to the case that the fluid and the elastic medium are (almost) incompressible
[
20
]
.
In the literature, several numerical approaches with different formulations have been proposed to overcome poroelasticity locking. For instance, the Galerkin least-squares method based on a four-field formulation (with displacement, stress, fluid flux, and pressure as unknowns) was studied in
[
13
]
. The mixed and discontinuous Galerkin (dG) finite element methods that successfully avoid pressure oscillations were developed in
[
19
]
. A dG method for the two-field formulation was presented in
[
8
]
, while a locking-free non-symmetric interior penalty dG method was introduced in
[
21
]
. Moreover, non-conforming and stabilized finite element methods have been explored to address locking issues in
[
14
,
15
]
. More recently, hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods have been applied to the Biot’s consolidation model
[
9
]
. An embedded-hybridizable DG method was developed for the total pressure formulation in
[
7
]
, a locking-free three-field virtual element method (VEM) in
[
24
]
, a non-conforming locking-free VEM was proposed in
[
16
]
,
a non-conforming hybrid high-order method on general meshes in
[
4
]
, and a non-symmetric approach with a quasi-optimal and robust discretization in
[
11
]
.

Optimal control problems arise naturally in many biological and biomechanical applications, where controlling physical processes is essential for understanding and influencing systems. One prominent example is the optimization of flow pressure and the analysis of how key biological parameters influence and can be controlled within the lamina cribrosa–a porous connective tissue located at the base of the optic nerve head in the eye. This tissue is commonly modelled using poroelasticity, and variations in pressure and material parameters are widely believed to play a significant role in the onset and progression of ocular neurodegenerative disorders, most notably glaucoma. Despite the relevance of such models, the literature on optimal control problems governed by poroelastic systems remains rather limited. In this direction, Bociu et al.
[
3
]
studied a linear–quadratic elliptic–parabolic optimal control problem describing fluid flow in deformable porous media, under the assumption of a vanishing specific storage coefficient
s_{0}=0
. They established the existence and uniqueness of optimal controls and derived the associated first-order necessary optimality conditions.

1.2.
Primary contributions

To the best of our knowledge, the numerical approximation of optimal control problems governed by these models has not yet been investigated in the existing literature, and the present work constitutes a first step in this direction. Our main goal is to control the system in such a way that the displacement of the solid structure and the fluid pressure remain close to some desired target values. We treat the fluid sources in the model as a control variable. Next, we highlight the main contributions of this work:

•

Novel symmetric formulation:
We propose a novel two-field symmetric formulation with displacement and pore pressure as unknowns to deal with Biot’s consolidation model.

•

Existence and uniqueness of optimal control:
We establish the well-posedness of the new symmetric formulation and then prove the existence and uniqueness of an optimal control (see Theorem
2.3
) and derive the first order necessary and sufficient optimality conditions..

•

A priori error estimates for a fully discrete scheme:
We prove the a priori error estimates for a fully discrete scheme using the backward Euler time discretization. In particular, the estimates for displacement and pressure variables are derived in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
norm and in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
norm for the control variable which is approximated using a
variational discretization
approach proposed by Hinze in
[
10
]
, where no explicit discretization of the control variable is used and the discrete control is achieved by projecting the discrete adjoint state on the admissible control set.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section
2

3
, we introduce the finite element approximation and a fully discrete formulation for the optimality system. Section
4

L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

2.2.
A priori estimates

First we consider the following reformulation of variational equations by taking the time derivative of (
1a
):

(4a)

\displaystyle-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\epsilon(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}))+\alpha\nabla\dot{p}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{f}}

\displaystyle\text{in}\ (0,T]\times\Omega,

(4b)

\displaystyle s_{0}\dot{p}+\alpha\text{div}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}})-\text{div}(\kappa\nabla p)

\displaystyle=-g

\displaystyle\text{in}\ (0,T]\times\Omega

where
\boldsymbol{f}
is the time derivative of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
.
Compared to the original system (
1
) which has an algebraic equation (
1a
), this new formulation is a system of differential equations. In contrast to the standard theory of evolutionary partial differential equations which needs initial data of
\boldsymbol{u}
and
p
. However, the time derivative involved part is symmetric but not positive definite, so we cannot apply a standard well-posedness theory of PDEs to this system. In the discussions below we show existence and uniqueness of weak solutions of (
4
).

Remark 2.1
.

We remark that a solution of (
4
) becomes a solution of (
1
) if initial data
(\boldsymbol{u}(0),p(0))
satisfies (
1a
) at
t=0
, so the solution also solves the original system.

The bilinear forms
a_{\boldsymbol{u}}:\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

,
a_{p}:Q\times Q\to\mathbb{R}
are defined as

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}\epsilon(\boldsymbol{v}):\epsilon(\boldsymbol{w})\,dx=2\mu\int_{\Omega}\epsilon(\boldsymbol{v}):\epsilon(\boldsymbol{w})\,dx+\lambda\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{v})\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{w})\,dx,

\displaystyle b(q,\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\alpha q\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx,\qquad a_{p}(q,r):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla q\cdot\nabla r\,dx.

We set

(5)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}:=(a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}))^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\|q\|_{a_{p}}:=(a_{p}(q,q))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It is known that there exists
C>0
such that

(6)

\displaystyle\inf_{0\not=q\in L^{2}(\Omega)}\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}}\frac{(q,\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{v}))_{\Omega}}{{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}}\geq C.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\subset\boldsymbol{V}
,
Q_{n}\subset Q
are finite dimensional subspaces such that they satisfy the Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi (LBB) condition

(7)

\displaystyle\inf_{0\not=q\in Q_{n}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{n}}\frac{(q,\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{v}))_{\Omega}}{{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{H_{x}^{1}}\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}}\geq C>0

with
C
independent of
n
.
It implies that there exists a linear map

such that

and
\|R_{n}q\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
hold for all

with
C>0
. By a standard argument in the theory of saddle point problems, one can show from (
7

\delta_{0},C_{0}>0
satisfying

(8)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}+\delta_{0}R_{n}q)+b(q,\boldsymbol{v}+\delta_{0}R_{n}q)-b(q,\boldsymbol{v})+(s_{0}q,q)_{\Omega}\geq C_{0}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}^{2}+\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2})

for any

We consider the following variational equations derived from (

(9a)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t),\boldsymbol{v})+b(\dot{p}(t),\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega},

(9b)

\displaystyle b(q,\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t))-(s_{0}\dot{p}(t),q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}(p(t),q)

for all

For later use, we assume the following elliptic regularity property for the solutions of the differentiated poroelastic system. There exists
0<s\leq 1
such that, whenever the data are sufficiently regular, the solution satisfies

(10)

\|p\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1+s}(\Omega))}\\
\leq C_{\rm reg}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in C^{0}([0,T];\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\times Q_{n})\cap C^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\times Q_{n})
satisfy (

\displaystyle\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;\boldsymbol{V})}+\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|p\|_{C^{0}(0,t;Q)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|p(0)\|_{Q}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}),

(12)

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{C^{0}(0,t;\boldsymbol{V})}+\|{p}\|_{C^{0}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}+\|p(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\sqrt{t}(\|p(0)\|_{Q}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}).

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}=\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\delta_{0}R_{n}\dot{p}
and

in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left[a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(s),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)+\delta_{0}R_{n}\dot{p}(s))+\delta_{0}b(\dot{p}(s),R_{n}\dot{p}(s))+(s_{0}\dot{p}(s),\dot{p}(s))_{\Omega}\right]\,ds+\frac{1}{2}a_{p}(p(t),p(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}a_{p}(p(0),p(0))+\int_{0}^{t}(\boldsymbol{f}(s),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)+\delta_{0}R_{n}\dot{p}(s))_{\Omega}\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}(g(s),\dot{p}(s))_{\Omega}\,ds.

By (

\displaystyle C_{0}(\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;\boldsymbol{V})}^{2}+\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\|p(t)\|_{Q}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}}\phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}}\phi(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}}\phi(t)\,dt,

for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
and a compactly supported smooth function
\phi(t)
on
(0,T)
. Similarly, we can show that the weak derivative of
r_{0}
is
r_{1}
.

By integration in time,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right)+b\left(\dot{p}_{n}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right)+b\left(q(s),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(s)\right)

\displaystyle-\left(s_{0}\dot{p}_{n}(s),q(s)\right)_{\Omega}-a_{p}\left(p_{n}(s),q(s)\right))\,ds=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{n}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(g_{n}(s),q(s)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,ds

holds for all
\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right)\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left([0,T);\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\times Q_{n}\right)
.
For simplicity we define
B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right),\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{q}\right)\right)
for
\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
q,\tilde{q}\in Q
by

\displaystyle B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right),(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{q})\right)=a_{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}})+b({q,\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}})+b\left(\tilde{q},\boldsymbol{v}\right)-\left(s_{0}q,\tilde{q}\right)_{\Omega}.

The previous formula is

(16)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\left(B_{0}\left(\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(s),\dot{p}_{n}(s)\right),\left(\boldsymbol{v}(s),q(s)\right)\right)-a_{p}\left(p_{n}(s),q(s)\right)\right)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{n}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(g_{n}(s),q(s)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,ds.

Since
\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{w}_{1}
in
L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}
,
\dot{p}_{n}\rightharpoonup r_{1}
in
L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)
,
p_{n}\rightharpoonup r_{0}
in
L_{t}^{2}Q
,
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{f}
in
L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
,
g_{n}\to g
in
L_{t}^{2}Q^{*}
,

(17)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\left(B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}(s),r_{1}(s)\right),\left(\boldsymbol{v}(s),q(s)\right)\right)-a_{p}\left(r_{0}(s),q(s)\right)\right)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(g(s),q(s)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,ds.

Since
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{V}_{n}
and
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}Q_{n}
are dense in
\boldsymbol{V}
and
Q
, respectively, (
17
) holds for all
\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right)\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T);\boldsymbol{V}\times Q)
where
C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T);\boldsymbol{V}\times Q)
is defined as in (
14
).

It remains to verify the initial conditions. Since

\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(t)=\boldsymbol{u}_{n,0}+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(s)\,ds,\qquad p_{n}(t)=p_{n,0}+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{p}_{n}(s)\,ds,

passing to the limit in
C^{0}([0,T];\boldsymbol{V}^{*})
and

C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, respectively, and using

\boldsymbol{u}_{n,0}\to\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
in
\boldsymbol{V}
and

p_{n,0}\to p_{0}
in
Q
, we obtain

\boldsymbol{w}_{0}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and
r_{0}(0)=p_{0}
.
∎

2.4.
Existence of optimal control

Our main goal is to choose a distributed control
m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
in such a way that the corresponding solid displacement
\boldsymbol{u}
and fluid pressure
p
are the best possible approximations to the desired/target values (dictated by the applications), denoted by
\boldsymbol{u}_{C}
and
p_{C}
, respectively.

Definition 2.2
.

For given
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
p_{0}\in Q
, we define that

\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)\in H^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{V})\times(H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;Q))\times L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
is admissible for initial data
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p_{0})
if
\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)+b\left(\dot{p}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)_{\Omega}\,dt,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(b\left(q(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\right)-\left(s_{0}\dot{p}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}-a_{p}\left(p(t),q(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(m(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}\,dt,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\text{, }p(0)=p_{0}.

Let
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}
and
\alpha_{p,C}
be non-negative numbers such that
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}+\alpha_{p,C}>0
, and the constant
\gamma>0
is a control cost parameter. For given target velocity and pressure fields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{C}\in L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)),\quad p_{C}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

and an admissible pair
\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
, we define the objective functional
J\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
as

(18)

\displaystyle J\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)=\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}\left(x,t\right)\hskip-0.56905pt-\hskip-0.56905pt\boldsymbol{u}_{C}\left(x,t\right)|^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{p,C}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|p(x,t)-p_{C}(x,t)|^{2}\,dx\,dt+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|m(x,t)|^{2}\,dx\,dt,

where
\mathcal{M}_{ad}
is the closed and convex admissible set of control variable defined as in (
3
). The term
\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|m(x,t)|^{2}\,dx\,dt
is introduced to bound the control function and to prove the existence of an optimal control.

Definition 2.3
.

\bar{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
is called an optimal control if the admissible triple
\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{p},\bar{m}\right)
for given initial data
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle J\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)\geq J\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{p},\bar{m}\right)

for all
\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
admissible for given initial data
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p_{0})
and for
m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any given initial data
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p_{0})
there exists a unique optimal control
\bar{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
.

Proof.

Since
J\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)\geq 0
for any admissible
\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
with
m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
, there exists a sequence
\{m_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
m_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
such that the sequence of admissible triples
\{\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}J\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)=\inf_{\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)\text{: admissible, }m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}}J\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)=:J_{\text{inf}}\geq 0.

Since
\{J\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)\}
is bounded,
\{m_{n}\}
is a bounded sequence in
L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)
because
\|m_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}J\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)
. By the energy estimate,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}}+\|p_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\dot{p}_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p_{n}\|_{L_{t}^{2}Q}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}+\|p_{0}\|_{Q}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|m\|_{L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

therefore
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\}
,
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}\}
are bounded in
L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}
,
\{p_{n}\}
,
\{\dot{p}_{n}\}
are bounded in
L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\{p_{n}\}
is bounded in
L_{t}^{2}Q
. By Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there are weak limits
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}
,
r_{0}
,
r_{1}
,
\tilde{r}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V},\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\in L_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{V},\quad p_{n}\rightharpoonup r_{0}\in L_{t}^{2}Q,\quad\dot{p}_{n}\rightharpoonup r_{1}\in L_{t}^{2}L^{2}(\Omega).

Since
\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)
is admissible,

(19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)+b\left(\dot{p}_{n}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)_{\Omega}\,dt,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(b\left(q(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}(t)\right)-\left(s_{0}\dot{p}_{n}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}-a_{p}\left(p_{n}(t),q(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(m_{n}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}\,dt

for all
\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right)\in C^{\infty}\left([0,T];\boldsymbol{V}\times Q\right)
such that
\boldsymbol{v}(T)=0
,
q(T)=0
. If we take
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}(t),r_{1}(t)\right),\left(\boldsymbol{v}(t),q(t)\right)\right)-a_{p}\left(\tilde{r}_{0}(t),q(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(\bar{m}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,dt.

By the integration by parts in time, (
19
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(-B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(t),p_{n}(t)\right),\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t),\dot{q}(t)\right)\right)-a_{p}\left(p_{n}(t),q(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\ \ +a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}(0)\right)+b\left(p_{0},\boldsymbol{v}(0)\right)+b\left(q(0),\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right)-\left(s_{0}p_{0},q(0)\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(m_{n}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,dt,

where we use
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
p_{n}(0)=p_{0}
. If
n\to\infty
, the above converges to

(20)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(-B_{0}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}(t),r_{0}(t)\right),\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t),\dot{q}(t)\right)\right)-a_{p}\left(\tilde{r}_{0}(t),q(t)\right)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\ \ +a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}(0)\right)+b\left(p_{0},\boldsymbol{v}(0)\right)+b\left(q(0),\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right)-\left(s_{0}p_{0},q(0)\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t)\right)_{\Omega}+\left(\bar{m}(t),q(t)\right)_{\Omega}\right)\,dt.

Since
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{0},r_{0},\bar{m}\right)
satisfies (
20
),
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{0},r_{0},\bar{m}\right)
is admissible. Moreover, the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(0,T;X)\hookrightarrow C^{0}([0,T];X)
means that the evaluation operator at
t=T
is bounded from
H^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{V})\times H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
to
\boldsymbol{V}\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, so
J({\boldsymbol{w}}_{0},r_{0},\bar{m})
and
J({\boldsymbol{u}}_{n},p_{n},\bar{m}_{n})
are well-defined. In particular,
J
is weakly lower semi-continuous, so

\displaystyle J\left({\boldsymbol{w}}_{0},r_{0},\bar{m}\right)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf J\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},p_{n},m_{n}\right)=J_{\inf}.

For uniqueness, suppose that there are two solutions
\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{m}_{0}\right)
and
\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\bar{p}_{1},\bar{m}_{1}\right)
such that

J\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{m}_{0}\right)=J\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\bar{p}_{1},\bar{m}_{1}\right)=J_{\inf}
.

\mathcal{M}_{ad}
is convex, so
\bar{m}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{m}_{0}+\bar{m}_{1}\right)\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
with the admissible triple
(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{p},\bar{m})
. Since the map
m\mapsto\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
from
\mathcal{M}_{ad}
to the set of admissible triple
\left(\boldsymbol{u},p,m\right)
is affine, and
J
is strictly convex, so

\displaystyle J\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{p},\bar{m}\right)<J\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\bar{p}_{0},\bar{m}_{0}\right).

This contradicts the assumption that
\bar{m}_{0}
is a solution of the optimal control problem, so a solution of the optimal control problem is unique.
∎

2.5.
Necessary optimality conditions

To derive the first order necessary optimality conditions, we use the adjoint operator of control-to-state operator
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{V}\times Q
be the control-to-state operator that maps
m
to
\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{u},p)
, where
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
is the weak solution to the continuous problem with the initial condition, boundary and interior sources all set to zero except the control
m
.

Since
L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{V}\times Q)
is continuously embedded in
\boldsymbol{Y}:=L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{Y}
is still linear and continuous. We can write the optimal control problem equivalently as to find
\underset{m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}}{\min}J_{r}(m),

where the reduced cost functional
J_{r}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle J_{r}(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\|m\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(m)\left(x,t\right)+\boldsymbol{u}_{0m}-\boldsymbol{u}_{C}\left(x,t\right)|^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha_{p,C}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{S}_{p}(m)\left(x,t\right)+p_{0m}-p_{C}(x,t)|^{2}\,dx\,dt,

where
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{p}
just denote the two components obtained from the control-to-state operator
\mathcal{S}
corresponding to control
m
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0m},p_{0m})
denote a weak solution to the state system with the control
m=0
and the initial condition, boundary and interior sources are set as desired.
Since
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{Y}
is linear,
the cost functional
J_{r}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Frechét differentiable.

The corresponding variational equations for the adjoint problem are given by

\displaystyle-a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{v})-b(\dot{r},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{C},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega},

\displaystyle-b(q,\dot{\boldsymbol{w}})+(s_{0}\dot{r},q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}(r,q)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{p,C}(p-p_{C},q)_{\Omega},

for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
q\in Q
. Here, we use the notations
\boldsymbol{w}
and
r
to represent the corresponding displacement and pressure variables, respectively. The space-time integration by parts to derive the adjoint equation leads to the following terminal conditions
{\boldsymbol{w}}(\cdot,T)=\boldsymbol{0},{r}(\cdot,T)=0
.

Lemma 2.4
.

\bar{m}
is the optimal control solution to the OCP if and only if

(22)

\displaystyle\left(\gamma\bar{m}+r,m-\bar{m}\right)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\geq 0\qquad\forall m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}.

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of
[
3
, Theorem 3.7]
.
∎

Using the projection
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{ad}}:L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}_{ad}}
(see
[
6
, Theorem 3.3]
) defined as:

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{ad}}(r)(t,x):=\max\{m_{a},\min\{r(t,x),m_{b}\}\},\quad\text{for a.e.}\ (t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega,

the continuous
variational inequality
(
22
) can be expressed as:

(24)

\displaystyle\bar{m}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{ad}}\left(-\frac{1}{\gamma}r(\bar{m})\right).

Remark 2.2
.

Since
r\in L_{t}^{\infty}H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L_{t}^{2}H^{1}(\Omega)
, the projection (
24
) is well-defined for almost every
t\in(0,T)
.

3.
Discrete formulation

In this section we discuss discretizations of the optimal control problem with finite elements.

3.1.
Spatial and space-time Discretizations

To construct the finite element approximation, we consider a family of shape-regular partition
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
of
\overline{\Omega}
into triangles or rectangles
K
with diameter
h_{K}
.

Consider a pair of finite elements
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{V}\times Q
which satisfies the inf-sup condition: there exists
C>0
independent of
h
such that

(25)

\displaystyle\inf_{0\not=q\in Q_{h}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}}\frac{(q,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq C.

It is known that the MINI-element
[
5
, Sections 8.6 and 8.7]
and
the generalized Taylor-Hood-
\mathbb{P}_{l}
finite elements satisfy (
25
).

For time discretization let
\Delta t
represent the time step size, where
T=N\Delta t
and
N
is the positive integer. We introduce the notation
t_{k}=k\Delta t
and
I_{k}:=(t_{k},t_{k+1}]
for
k=0,\cdots,N-1
. For a continuous function
g
defined on
[0,T]
, let
g^{k}=g(t_{k})
. For a given sequence
\{g^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, the derivative is then approximated as follows:

\partial_{\tau}g^{k+1}:=\frac{g^{k+1}-g^{k}}{\Delta t}.

Now, we set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{hk}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{V}_{h}):\boldsymbol{v}|_{I_{k}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(I_{k};\boldsymbol{V}_{h})\ \text{for}\ k=0,\cdots,N-1\},

\displaystyle Q_{hk}

\displaystyle:=\{q\in L^{2}(0,T;Q_{h}):q|_{I_{k}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(I_{k};Q_{h})\ \text{for}\ k=0,\cdots,N-1\},

which means that
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{hk}
and
q\in Q_{hk}
are piecewise constant polynomials with respect to time.

3.2.
Finite element approximation

In the dG(0) method, on each interval
I_{k}=(t_{k},t_{k+1}]
, the discrete solution is sought as a constant in time, i.e.,
(U,P)|_{I_{k}}\equiv(U^{k+1},P^{k+1})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
. The scheme is obtained by integrating the equations over
I_{k}
and testing against constant-in-time test functions, so that

\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{I_{k}}\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{u}\,dt\approx\frac{U^{k+1}-U^{k}}{\Delta t}=:\partial_{\tau}U^{k+1},

and analogously for
P
. Throughout this section the notation

\left(g,v\right)_{I_{k}\times\Omega}:=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}g\,v\,dx\,dt=\Delta t\int_{\Omega}\bar{g}^{k+1}v\,dx,\qquad\bar{g}^{k+1}:=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g\,dt,

is used consistently for any source function
g
.

Discretization of forward equation
The numerical solution at the
(k+1)-
th time step is defined inductively as follows: For
0\leq k\leq N-1
find
(U^{k+1},P^{k+1})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

(26a)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(\partial_{\tau}U^{k+1},\boldsymbol{v}\right)+b\left(\partial_{\tau}P^{k+1},\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left({\boldsymbol{f}},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{I_{k}\times\Omega},

(26b)

\displaystyle b\left(q,\partial_{\tau}U^{k+1}\right)-\left(s_{0}\partial_{\tau}P^{k+1},q\right)_{\Omega}-a_{p}\left(P^{k+1},q\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(m,q\right)_{I_{k}\times\Omega},

for all
\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right)\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The system (
26
) has a unique solution.

Proof.

To establish the well-posedness of (
26
), we show that
(U^{k+1},P^{k+1})
is uniquely determined by the linear system (
26
) when
U^{k},P^{k},{\boldsymbol{f}}
and
m
are given. Equivalently, it is enough to show that
U^{k+1}=0
,
P^{k+1}=0
when
U^{k},P^{k},\boldsymbol{f},m
are zero. If we assume that
U^{k},P^{k},\boldsymbol{f},m
are zero, (
26
) can be written as:

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(U^{k+1},\boldsymbol{v}\right)+b\left(P^{k+1},\boldsymbol{v}\right)=0,

\displaystyle b\left(q,U^{k+1}\right)-\left(s_{0}P^{k+1},q\right)_{\Omega}-\Delta t\left({\kappa}\nabla P^{k+1},\nabla q\right)_{\Omega}=0,

for all
\left(\boldsymbol{v},q\right)\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
. Choosing
\boldsymbol{v}=U^{k+1}
and
q=-P^{k+1}
and adding both equations, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq a_{\boldsymbol{u}}\left(U^{k+1},U^{k+1}\right)+\left(s_{0}P^{k+1},P^{k+1}\right)_{\Omega}+\Delta ta_{p}\left(P^{k+1},P^{k+1}\right)=0.

This implies that
U^{k+1}=0=P^{k+1}
.
∎

Discrete optimal control problem
Consider the discrete optimal control problem: Find
m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
such that

(28)

\displaystyle\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}}J_{h}\left(U,P,m\right):=\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|U^{k+1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{C}\|_{L^{2}(I_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

Theorem 3.3

(Discrete first order optimality system). A control

,

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{u}},{p},{m})
be the solution of the optimal control problem (
18
). Suppose that
(U({m}),P({m}))\in\boldsymbol{V}_{hk}\times Q_{hk}
is a solution of (
26
) for the continuous optimal control
{m}
with numerical initial data
(U^{0},P^{0})
satisfying (
33
) for some
0<s\leq 1
. Assume also that

\boldsymbol{u}\in H^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)),\quad p\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega)).

Then,

(37)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}(m)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{k}-P^{k}(m)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}-U(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq h^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\quad+C(h^{s}+\Delta t)\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\|p\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}\right).

Proof.

We start our error analysis to split the error
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}-U^{k}({m}),{p}^{k}-P^{k}({m}))
into two parts as follows:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{j}-U^{j}({m})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{u}}^{j}-\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{j}+\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{j}-U^{j}({m})=:e_{I,j}^{\boldsymbol{u}}+\theta^{j}_{\boldsymbol{u}};

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526ptj=0,1,\cdots,N,

\displaystyle{p}^{j}-P^{j}(m)

\displaystyle={p}^{j}-\Pi_{h}^{p}{p}^{j}+\Pi_{h}^{p}{p}^{j}-P^{j}({m})=:e_{I,j}^{p}+\theta^{j}_{p};

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526ptj=0,1,\cdots,N.

Regarding

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{I_{k}\times\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{k}}\left[a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(s),\boldsymbol{v})+b(\dot{{p}}(s),\boldsymbol{v})\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=a_{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})+b({p}^{k+1}-{p}^{k},\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle({m},q)_{I_{k}\times\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{k}}\left[b(q,\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}})-(s_{0}\dot{{p}}(s),q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}({p}(s),q)\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=b(q,{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})-(s_{0}({p}^{k+1}-{p}^{k}),q)_{\Omega}-\int_{I_{k}}a_{p}(p,q)\,ds

for
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
,
q\in Q_{h}
, and the elliptic projections (
34
), (
35
), we obtain the following error equations:
for all
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
,

(38)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\partial_{\tau}\theta_{p}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-b(\eta_{p}^{k},\boldsymbol{v}),

(39)

\displaystyle b(q,\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})-(s_{0}\partial_{\tau}\theta_{p}^{k},q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}(\theta_{p}^{k},q)

\displaystyle=-b(q,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})+(s_{0}\eta_{p}^{k},q)_{\Omega}+D^{k}(q).

where

\displaystyle\eta_{p}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\tau}p^{k}-\partial_{\tau}\Pi_{h}^{p}p^{k}=(I-\Pi_{h}^{p})\partial_{\tau}p^{k},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-\partial_{\tau}\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{u}^{k}=(I-\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}})\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{u}^{k},

\displaystyle D^{k}(q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}a_{p}(p(s)-p^{k},q)\,ds.

We now derive a stability estimate directly from
(
38
)–(
39
). Introduce

F^{k}(\boldsymbol{v}):=-b(\eta_{p}^{k},\boldsymbol{v}),\qquad G^{k}(q):=-b(q,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})+(s_{0}\eta_{p}^{k},q)_{\Omega}+D^{k}(q).

Let
A_{h}:\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\to\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}
and

B_{h}:Q_{h}\to\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}
be defined by

\langle A_{h}\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:=a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}),\qquad\langle B_{h}r,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:=b(r,\boldsymbol{v}).

From (
38
), after summing in time, we obtain

A_{h}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}+B_{h}\theta_{p}^{k}=A_{h}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}+B_{h}\theta_{p}^{0}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{k}F^{i}=:H^{k}.

From this we obtain

\|H^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}\leq C\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|F^{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}\right),

and therefore,

(40)

\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|H^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|H^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}\right).

By the inf-sup condition (
25
),

(41)

\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|H^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}+\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right).

We next derive an energy estimate. Taking
q=\theta_{p}^{k}
in
(
39
), using

B_{h}\theta_{p}^{k}=H^{k}-A_{h}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}
, and arguing as in the
standard energy method, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k},\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})+(s_{0}\partial_{\tau}\theta_{p}^{k},\theta_{p}^{k})_{\Omega}+a_{p}(\theta_{p}^{k},\theta_{p}^{k})=\langle H^{k},\partial_{\tau}\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\rangle-G^{k}(\theta_{p}^{k}).

Multiplying by
\Delta t
and summing from
k=1
to
m\leq N
gives

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\frac{s_{0}}{2}\|\theta_{p}^{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{a_{p}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\frac{s_{0}}{2}\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle H^{k},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}-\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k-1}\rangle+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}|G^{k}(\theta_{p}^{k})|.

The first term on the right-hand side is handled by summation by parts:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle H^{k},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}-\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle H^{m},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\rangle-\langle H^{1},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\langle H^{k+1}-H^{k},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\rangle.

Since

H^{k+1}-H^{k}=\Delta tF^{k+1}
, Young’s inequality yields, for any

\varepsilon>0
,

(43)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle H^{k},\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}-\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|H^{m}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+C\|H^{1}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+C\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\Delta t\sum_{k=2}^{m}\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}.

We estimate
G^{k}(\theta_{p}^{k})
in the
a_{p}
-dual norm. Define

\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}:=\sup_{q\in Q_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|G^{k}(q)|}{\|q\|_{a_{p}}}.

Then

|G^{k}(\theta_{p}^{k})|\leq\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{a_{p}}.

Therefore, by Young’s inequality,

(44)

\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}|G^{k}(\theta_{p}^{k})|\leq\varepsilon\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{a_{p}}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}.

Combining (
42
),
(
43
), and (
44
), choosing

\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, and using (
40
),
we get

\displaystyle\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+s_{0}\|\theta_{p}^{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{a_{p}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+C(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}).

By the discrete Gronwall inequality,

(45)

\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{a_{p}}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+C\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}\right).

Using (
41
),
(
40
), and (

), we also obtain

(46)

\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+C\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}\right).

Consequently,

(47)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+C\left(\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}.

It remains to estimate
\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}
,
\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
F^{k}
, and
G^{k}
. By (
33
), the interpolation approximation (
36
), Poincare inequality, and the triangle inequality,

(48)

\displaystyle\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\|\theta_{p}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}).

By the continuity of
b
and Korn’s inequality,

\|F^{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{\prime}}\leq C\|\eta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Moreover, from the definition of
G^{k}
and Poincare’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|G^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+Cs_{0}\|\eta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|D^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}.

We now estimate the three residuals. Since

\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{u}^{k}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)\,ds,

the approximation property of the elliptic projection gives

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\eta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|(I-\Pi_{h}^{p})\partial_{\tau}p^{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2(1+s)}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}^{2}.

For the diffusion time residual, we use the

-dual norm. From the definition of

\displaystyle\|D^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\|p(s)-p^{k}\|_{a_{p}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\int_{s}^{t_{k}}\|\dot{p}(r)\|_{a_{p}}\,dr\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t^{1/2}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(t_{k-1},t_{k};H^{1}(\Omega))}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|D^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t^{2}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Combining the above bounds with (

(49)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{2}+\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+{s}}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\quad+C\Big[h^{s}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+h^{1+s}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\Delta t\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\Big].

To complete the proof, we estimate the difference of continuous and discrete solutions. Since

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}(m)\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{u}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\leq Ch^{s}\|u^{k}\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}},

the

part in (

\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|p^{k}-P^{k}(m)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
part in (

), (

\displaystyle\|p^{k}-P^{k}(m)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|p^{k}-\Pi_{h}^{p}p^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{s}\|p^{k}\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|\theta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

For the space-time

errors in (

Then

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-U(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}-\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}.

Here we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta t\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))},

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}-\Pi_{h}^{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{1+s}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}.

Since
\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{a_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\sim\|\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, using (

),

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-U(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq Ch^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+{s}}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle+C\Big[\Delta t\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+h^{1+s}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+h^{s}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+h^{1+s}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\Delta t\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\Big].

Similarly,

\displaystyle\|p-P(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|p-p_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+\|p_{\tau}-\Pi_{h}^{p}p_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\theta_{p}\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}.

The first two terms satisfy

\|p-p_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\Delta t\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))},

and

\|p_{\tau}-\Pi_{h}^{p}p_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Ch^{1+s}\|p\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}.

Using (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|p-P(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Ch^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+{s}}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle+C\Big[\Delta t\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+h^{1+s}\|p\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+h^{s}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+h^{1+s}\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\Delta t\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\Big].

Combining the two estimates gives

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-U(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P(m)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{s}(\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}+\|p(0)\|_{H^{1+{s}}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle+C\Big[\Delta t\left(\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\right)

\displaystyle+h^{s}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+h^{s}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\|p\|_{\ell_{\tau}^{2}(H^{1+s}(\Omega))}+\|\dot{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega))}\right)\Big].

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(adjoint error for a fixed control)
.

Let
m\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
be fixed. Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
be the
corresponding continuous state and let
(\boldsymbol{w},r)
be the
corresponding continuous adjoint. Let
(U(m),P(m))
be the fully
discrete state generated by the same control
m
, and let

(W(m),R(m))
be the corresponding fully discrete adjoint.

Assume that

r\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega)),\quad\boldsymbol{w}\in H^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)),

for some
0<s\leq 1
. Then

(50)

\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\leq C\left(h^{s}+\Delta t+\|U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|P(m)-p\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

Consequently, if the fixed-control state error satisfies

\|U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|P(m)-p\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t),

then

\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

Proof.

We introduce an auxiliary fully discrete adjoint

(\widetilde{W},\widetilde{R})
driven by the exact continuous state data. More precisely, the right-hand sides in the discrete adjoint equations are obtained from
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{C})
and
\alpha_{p,C}(p-p_{C})
, instead of

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}(U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}_{C})
,
\alpha_{p,C}(P(m)-p_{C})
.
Then

R(m)-r=\bigl(R(m)-\widetilde{R}\bigr)+\bigl(\widetilde{R}-r\bigr).

We first estimate
R(m)-\widetilde{R}
. Subtracting the two discrete
adjoint systems gives a discrete adjoint problem with zero terminal data
and right-hand sides

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}(U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}),\qquad\alpha_{p,C}(P(m)-p).

By the stability estimate for the fully discrete adjoint problem, which is
the backward-in-time analogue of the state stability estimate, we obtain a system

(51)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\partial_{\tau}\theta_{W}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\partial_{\tau}\theta_{R}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}(U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{I_{k}\times\Omega},

(52)

\displaystyle b(q,\partial_{\tau}\theta_{W}^{k})-(s_{0}\partial_{\tau}\theta_{R}^{k},q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}(\theta_{R}^{k},q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\alpha_{p,C}(P(m)-p),q\right)_{I_{k}\times\Omega}

where

\displaystyle\theta_{W}^{k}:=W(m)^{k}-\widetilde{W}^{k},\quad\theta_{R}^{k}:=R(m)^{k}-\widetilde{R}^{k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

After reversing the time indices, this system has the same algebraic structure as the forward perturbation system. Therefore, the stability
argument used in Lemma
4.1
, particularly the procedures from (
38
), (
39
) to (
45
), yields

\|R(m)-\widetilde{R}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\|U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|P(m)-p\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right)

because
R(m)-\widetilde{R}
is piecewise constant in time.

It remains to estimate
\widetilde{R}-r
. Since

(\widetilde{W},\widetilde{R})
is the fully discrete approximation of
the continuous adjoint problem with the exact state data in the source
terms, the dG(0) error estimate as in Lemma
4.1
gives

\|\widetilde{R}-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t),

where the constant depends on the stated regularity of

(\boldsymbol{w},r)
and on the target data, but is independent of

h
and
\Delta t
.

Combining the two estimates and using the triangle inequality yields

(53)

\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\leq C\left(h^{s}+\Delta t+\|U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|P(m)-p\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\right).

The final estimate follows from the fixed-control state error estimate.
∎

4.2.
Estimates for variational discretization of control
m

This subsection is devoted to deriving error estimates for the control
m
within the fully discrete
variational discretization
setting. Before proving the primary a priori bound, we first introduce a series of preliminary results that lay the foundation for the analysis.

Lemma 4.3
.

Define the reduced discrete functional

\displaystyle j_{h}(m):=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}}{2}\|U(m)-\boldsymbol{u}_{C}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{p,C}}{2}\|P(m)-p_{C}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|m\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

For
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
the following estimate holds:

(54)

\displaystyle\left(j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{1})-j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{2}),m_{1}-m_{2}\right)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\geq\gamma\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
S_{h}:\mathcal{M}_{ad}\to\boldsymbol{V}_{hk}\times Q_{hk}
be the discrete control-to-state map defined by

S_{h}(m)=(U(m),P(m))\in\boldsymbol{V}_{hk}\times Q_{hk}

where
(U(m),P(m))
is the solution of (
26
). Since the fully discrete state equations are linear with respect to the control variable, the map
S_{h}
is affine. Let
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
be arbitrary and set

\delta m:=m_{1}-m_{2},\qquad\delta U:=U(m_{1})-U(m_{2}),\qquad\delta P:=P(m_{1})-P(m_{2}).

Since the discrete control-to-state map
S_{h}
is affine, its derivative is
linear and independent of the control. Hence the second variation of

j_{h}
in the direction
\delta m
is given by

\displaystyle\bigl(j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{1})-j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{2}),\delta m\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}\|\delta U\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\alpha_{p,C}\|\delta P\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\gamma\|\delta m\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Since
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{u},C}\geq 0
,
\alpha_{p,C}\geq 0
, and

\gamma>0
, we obtain

\bigl(j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{1})-j_{h}^{\prime}(m_{2}),m_{1}-m_{2}\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\geq\gamma\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,m)
and
(U,P,M)
be the admissible solutions of the continuous and fully discrete optimal control problems. Suppose that
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
and the continuous adjoint solution satisfy

(55)

\displaystyle p,r\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Omega)),\qquad\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\in H^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)),

and the initial data assumption (
33
) holds.
Then, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t+h^{s})

with
C>0
depending on the solution regularities in (
55
).

Proof.

Let
m
and
M
denote the continuous and discrete optimal controls,
respectively. We use the convention that the adjoint pressure variable
already contains the tracking weights, so that

j^{\prime}(m)=\gamma m+r,\qquad j_{h}^{\prime}(M)=\gamma M+R(M).

Here
R(z)
denotes the discrete adjoint pressure corresponding to the discrete state generated by
z\in\mathcal{M}_{ad}
.

By the strong monotonicity of the reduced discrete gradient, we have

\gamma\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\bigl(j_{h}^{\prime}(m)-j_{h}^{\prime}(M),m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}.

Using the expressions for the continuous and discrete reduced gradients,
we write

\displaystyle\bigl(j_{h}^{\prime}(m)-j_{h}^{\prime}(M),m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\gamma m+R(m),m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}

\displaystyle+\bigl(\gamma M+R(M),M-m\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}.

The discrete variational inequality gives
\bigl(\gamma M+R(M),M-m\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\leq 0
. Moreover, the continuous variational inequality with the admissible test control
M
gives

\bigl(\gamma m+r,m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\leq 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\gamma m+R(m),m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\gamma m+r,m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}+\bigl(R(m)-r,m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(R(m)-r,m-M\bigr)_{(0,T)\times\Omega}.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\gamma\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

If
m\neq M
, dividing by

\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
yields

\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

The same estimate is trivial when
m=M
. Finally, using the discrete
adjoint error estimate,
\|R(m)-r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t)
,
we obtain

\|m-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\bar{m})
be the continuous optimal solution and
let
(U,P,M)
be the fully discrete optimal solution. Under the
regularity assumptions of Lemma
4.1
,
Lemma
4.2
, and
Theorem
4.4
, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of

h
and
\Delta t
, such that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{k}-P^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}-U\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

In particular, if
s=1
, then

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{k}-P^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}-U\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h+\Delta t).

Proof.

Let
(U(\bar{m}),P(\bar{m}))
denote the fully discrete state generated by
the continuous optimal control
\bar{m}
. We decompose the total state
errors as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}(\bar{m})\bigr)+\bigl(U^{k}(\bar{m})-U^{k}\bigr),

\displaystyle p^{k}-P^{k}

\displaystyle=\bigl(p^{k}-P^{k}(\bar{m})\bigr)+\bigl(P^{k}(\bar{m})-P^{k}\bigr).

The first terms are fixed-control discretization errors. By
Lemma
4.1
,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}(\bar{m})\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{k}-P^{k}(\bar{m})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}-U(\bar{m})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P(\bar{m})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

It remains to estimate the perturbation caused by replacing
\bar{m}
by
the discrete optimal control
M
. Set

\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}:=U^{k}(\bar{m})-U^{k}(M),\qquad\zeta_{p}^{k}:=P^{k}(\bar{m})-P^{k}(M).

Subtracting the two fully discrete state equations corresponding to

\bar{m}
and
M
, we obtain, for all

(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}
,

(56)

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{p}^{k},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=0,

(57)

\displaystyle b(q,\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k})-(s_{0}\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{p}^{k},q)_{\Omega}-a_{p}(\zeta_{p}^{k},q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\bar{m}-M,q)_{I_{k}\times\Omega}.

The initial data are the same for both discrete states, hence

\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}=0
,
\zeta_{p}^{0}=0
.
For the right-hand side
G_{m}^{k}(q):=\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\bar{m}-M,q)_{I_{k}\times\Omega}
,
we have

\|G_{m}^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}\leq C\Delta t^{-1/2}\|\bar{m}-M\|_{L^{2}(I_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Consequently,

\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|G_{m}^{k}\|_{a_{p},*}^{2}\leq C\|\bar{m}-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Applying the same stability argument as in the proof of
Lemma
4.1
, with zero consistency error and
with right-hand side
\bar{m}-M
, gives

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\zeta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+\|\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\zeta_{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{m}-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

By Theorem
4.4
,
\|\bar{m}-M\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\zeta_{p}^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)+\|\zeta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\zeta_{p}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

Combining the fixed-control discretization estimate and the control
perturbation estimate by the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k}-U^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{k}-P^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{u}-U\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|p-P\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C(h^{s}+\Delta t).

For
s=1
, this gives the asserted
O(h+\Delta t)
estimate.
∎

6.
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a novel two-field symmetric formulation for optimal control problems governed by Biot’s model. The proposed formulation is well posed, and the existence and uniqueness of the optimal control are established. For the fully discrete setting, employing a backward Euler scheme for time discretization, we derived a priori error estimates that guarantee optimal convergence. In particular, the estimates for displacement and pressure variables are derived in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
,
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
norms and the control variable error with
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
is approximated using a
variational discretization
approach.
