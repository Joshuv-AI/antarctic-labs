A new, self-contained proof of Shahgholian’s theorem using the thickness function
M. Barkatou

Abstract
This note presents a new, self-contained proof of Shahgholian’s geometric theorem on quadrature surfaces using the thickness function and level set methods. By relying on a radial parametrisation and fundamental maximum principles, the proof avoids the technical complexity of the moving plane method. It provides a more conceptual view, revealing that the overdetermined condition forces all level sets to be parallel to the convex hull of the support of the measure.



Keywords:
Quadrature surfaces; Thickness function; Level set methods; Maximum principle.

1
Introduction and Shahgholian’s theorem

In his foundational 1994 paper
[
1
]
, Henrik Shahgholian proved the following geometric result for quadrature surfaces:

Theorem 1.1

(Shahgholian, 1994)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain and let
\mu
be a positive measure compactly supported in
\Omega
. If
\partial\Omega
is a quadrature surface for
\mu
, i.e. there exists a solution
u
of the overdetermined problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mu&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=-1&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1)

then for every
x\in\partial\Omega
the inward normal ray to
\partial\Omega
at
x
meets the convex hull of
\text{supp}\mu
.

The theorem states that
\Omega
satisfies the
C
-GNP property with
C=\text{conv}(\text{supp}\mu)
. The original proof used the moving plane method and sophisticated comparison arguments. In this document, we show how the thickness function and level set analysis yield a more elegant and fully self-contained geometric proof.

2
The thickness function and its properties

Let
C=\text{conv}(\text{supp}\mu)
. Since
\mu
is supported in
C
and
C
is convex, we have
C\subset\Omega
. For each
c\in\partial C
denote by
\nu(c)
the outer unit normal to
\partial C
at
c
. When
\partial C
is not smooth, we work with the Clarke normal cone; the set of points where the normal is not unique has zero
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, hence all differential identities hold almost everywhere and the final geometric conclusion extends by continuity.

We first establish the fundamental properties of the level sets of
u
outside
C
.

Proposition 2.1

(Strict radial decrease)
.

For every
c\in\partial C
, the function
r\mapsto u(c+r\nu(c))
is strictly decreasing on
[0,d(c)]
, where

d(c)=\sup\{r>0:c+r\nu(c)\in\Omega\}.

(2)

Proof.

Consider the ray starting at
c
in the direction
\nu(c)
. Since
u
is strictly positive in
\Omega
, vanishes on
\partial\Omega
, and satisfies
\Delta u=0
in
\Omega\setminus C
, the Hopf maximum principle implies that its directional derivative along the outward normal is strictly negative. Specifically, defining
\phi_{c}(r)=u(c+r\nu(c))
, we have
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(r)=\nabla u(c+r\nu(c))\cdot\nu(c)<0
. Thus,
u
strictly decreases along these normal rays.
∎

Proposition 2.2

(Thickness function)
.

For
t\in[0,\max u]
, define the level set
\Omega^{t}=\{u>t\}
and its outer boundary
\Gamma^{t}=\partial\Omega^{t}
. Then
\Gamma^{t}
admits a radial parametrisation

\Gamma^{t}=\{c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c):c\in\partial C\},

(3)

with
d_{t}(c)=\sup\{r>0:u(c+r\nu(c))>t\}
. The map
t\mapsto d_{t}(c)
is strictly decreasing and
d_{0}(c)=d(c)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.1
, the function
\phi_{c}(r)=u(c+r\nu(c))
is strictly decreasing from
u(c)
to
0
. Therefore, for any
t\in[0,u(c))
, there exists a unique
r=d_{t}(c)
such that
\phi_{c}(r)=t
. The strict monotonicity of
t\mapsto d_{t}(c)
follows directly from the strict monotonicity of
\phi_{c}
. The equality
d_{0}(c)=d(c)
holds by definition of the free boundary
\partial\Omega
.
∎

Proposition 2.3

(Differential relation)
.

For almost every
c\in\partial C
and every
t\in(0,\max u)
,

\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}(c)=-\frac{1}{|\nabla u(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))|}.

(4)

Proof.

By definition of the thickness function, we have the identity
u(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))=t
. Differentiating both sides with respect to
t
yields:

\nabla u(c+d_{t}(c)\nu(c))\cdot\nu(c)\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}(c)=1.

Since
u
strictly decreases along the outward normal rays to
C
, the gradient
\nabla u
is collinear with the normal vector
\nu(c)
and points in the opposite direction (inward). Therefore,
\nabla u\cdot\nu(c)=-|\nabla u|
. Substituting this into the differentiated equation gives the result.
∎

3
Radial parametrisation of
\Omega

Proposition 3.1
.

Every point
x\in\Omega\setminus C
can be uniquely written as
x=c+r\nu(c)
with
c\in\partial C
and
0<r<d(c)
.

Proof.

For
x\in\Omega\setminus C
, consider the half-line starting from
x
in the direction opposite to the inward normal of
C
. Because
C
is convex and contained in
\Omega
, this half-line meets
\partial C
at a unique point
c
. Then
x=c+r\nu(c)
with
r=\|x-c\|>0
. By definition of
d(c)
, we have
r<d(c)
. Uniqueness follows from strict convexity of
C
and the fact that rays from distinct points of
\partial C
cannot intersect inside
\Omega\setminus C
.
∎

4
The key rigidity lemma

The following rigidity result is the heart of the proof. It is a direct consequence of the overdetermined boundary condition and the harmonicity of
u
in
\Omega\setminus C
.

Lemma 4.1

(Rigidity)
.

For every
c\in\partial C
and every
r\in[0,d(c)]
, we have
|\nabla u(c+r\nu(c))|=1
. Consequently,
|\nabla u|=1
on every level set
\Gamma^{t}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two parts: first we prove
|\nabla u|\leq 1
in
\Omega\setminus C
, then we prove
|\nabla u|\geq 1
.

Step 1: Upper bound
|\nabla u|\leq 1
.

Set
w=|\nabla u|^{2}
. A direct computation gives

\Delta w=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(u_{ij})^{2}+2\nabla u\cdot\nabla(\Delta u).

(5)

In
\Omega\setminus C
,
\Delta u=0
, hence
\Delta w=2\|D^{2}u\|^{2}\geq 0
. Thus
w
is subharmonic in
\Omega\setminus C
. On
\partial\Omega
we have
|\nabla u|=1
by the overdetermined condition, so
w=1
on
\partial\Omega
. The maximum principle for subharmonic functions yields

w(x)\leq\max_{\partial\Omega}w=1\qquad\forall x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus C,

i.e.
|\nabla u(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus C
.

Step 2: Lower bound
|\nabla u|\geq 1
.

Because of the radial structure (Proposition
3.1
), for each
c\in\partial C
the ray
r\mapsto c+r\nu(c)
is orthogonal to the level sets of
u
(the gradient is collinear with
\nu(c)
). Hence
|\nabla u|=|\partial u/\partial r|
where
\partial/\partial r
denotes the directional derivative along
\nu(c)
.

Consider the function
\phi_{c}(r)=u(c+r\nu(c))
. It satisfies
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(r)=\nabla u(c+r\nu(c))\cdot\nu(c)<0
.
Since
u
is harmonic in
\Omega\setminus C
, its directional derivative
v(x)=\nabla u(x)\cdot\nu(c)
(where
\nu(c)
is a constant vector along the ray) is also a harmonic function (as
\Delta v=\nabla(\Delta u)\cdot\nu(c)=0
).
By the minimum principle for harmonic functions, the minimum of
v(x)
on the segment
\{c+r\nu(c):r\in[0,d(c)]\}
must be bounded below by its minimum on the boundary of the domain
\Omega\setminus C
.
On the outer boundary
r=d(c)
we have
x\in\partial\Omega
and the overdetermined condition gives
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(d(c))=-1
.
On the inner boundary
r=0
we have
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
. Therefore
\phi_{c}^{\prime}(r)\geq-1
for all
r\in[0,d(c)]
.
Consequently,
|\nabla u(c+r\nu(c))|=-\phi_{c}^{\prime}(r)\geq 1
.

Step 3: Conclusion.

Combining Step 1 and Step 2 we get
|\nabla u|=1
almost everywhere in
\Omega\setminus C
. By continuity of
|\nabla u|
(elliptic regularity gives
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus C)
), the equality extends to all points in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus C
. Since each level set
\Gamma^{t}
(for
t>0
) is contained in
\Omega\setminus C
, we also have
|\nabla u|=1
on
\Gamma^{t}
; for
t=0
it is exactly the boundary condition.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For every
c\in\partial C
and every
t\in[0,\max u]
,

\frac{\partial d_{t}}{\partial t}(c)=-1.

(6)

Proof.

Combine Proposition
2.3
with Lemma
4.1
.
∎

5
Explicit form of the thickness function

Integrating the relation
\partial d_{t}/\partial t=-1
yields a simple linear expression.

Theorem 5.1
.

For all
c\in\partial C
and
t\in[0,\max u]
,

d_{t}(c)=d(c)-t.

(7)

In particular, all level sets
\Gamma^{t}
are parallel surfaces to
\partial C
.

Proof.

From Corollary
4.2
,
\partial d_{t}/\partial t=-1
for every
t
. Integrating from
0
to
t
gives
d_{t}(c)-d_{0}(c)=\int_{0}^{t}(-1)\,ds=-t
, and
d_{0}(c)=d(c)
by definition.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The linear relation implies that
u
varies linearly along each ray:

u(c+r\nu(c))=d(c)-r\qquad\text{for }0\leq r\leq d(c).

(8)

Indeed, setting
r=d_{t}(c)
gives
t=d(c)-d_{t}(c)
, hence
u(c+r\nu(c))=d(c)-r
.

6
Proof of Shahgholian’s theorem

We now assemble the pieces to prove the main theorem.

Theorem 6.1

(Shahgholian’s theorem revisited)
.

Let
\Omega
be a solution of the overdetermined problem with
\partial u/\partial\nu=-1
on
\partial\Omega
. Then
\Omega
satisfies the
C
-GNP property with
C=\text{conv}(\text{supp}\mu)
. More precisely:

1.

\Omega
admits the radial parametrisation

\Omega\setminus C=\{c+r\nu(c):c\in\partial C,\;0<r<d(c)\}.

2.

For every
x\in\partial\Omega
, the inward normal ray to
\partial\Omega
at
x
meets
C
.

3.

\partial\Omega\setminus C
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Step 1 (Radial parametrisation).

For each
c\in\partial C
, define
d(c)=\sup\{r>0:c+r\nu(c)\in\Omega\}
. By Proposition
2.1
, the function
r\mapsto u(c+r\nu(c))
is strictly decreasing, so there is a unique
r=d(c)
where it vanishes. Proposition
3.1
then gives the parametrisation.

Step 2 (Normal rays meet
C
).

Let
x\in\partial\Omega
. By the parametrisation,
x=c+d(c)\nu(c)
for a unique
c\in\partial C
. The inward normal ray to
\partial\Omega
at
x
is precisely
\{c+r\nu(c):r<d(c)\}
, which obviously meets
C
at
c
.

Step 3 (Lipschitz regularity).

From Theorem
5.1
,
d_{t}(c)=d(c)-t
. To show that the boundary is Lipschitz, we consider the distance function to the closed convex set
C
. It is a classical geometric fact that the distance function
f(x)=\text{dist}(x,C)
is globally
1
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since
\partial\Omega
is a
C^{2}
hypersurface, the restriction of
f
to
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz continuous. By the radial parametrisation, for any point
x=c+d(c)\nu(c)\in\partial\Omega
, the distance to
C
along the normal is precisely
f(x)=d(c)
. Thus, the thickness function
d(c)
is Lipschitz continuous on
\partial C
. The map
\Phi:\partial C\to\partial\Omega
given by
\Phi(c)=c+d(c)\nu(c)
is therefore bilipschitz, meaning
\partial\Omega\setminus C=\Phi(\partial C)
is a Lipschitz boundary.

Step 4 (
C
-GNP conditions).

(P1)
C\subset\Omega
holds by construction. (P2)
\partial\Omega\setminus C
is Lipschitz (Step 3). (P3) For every
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus C
where the normal exists, the inward normal ray meets
C
(Step 2). By continuity this holds for all
x
. (P4) For each
c\in\partial C
, the ray
\Delta_{c}
intersects
\Omega
in the open interval
(0,d(c))
because
u
is strictly decreasing along it. Thus
\Omega
satisfies the
C
-GNP property with
C
.
∎

7
Comparison with the original proof

The original proof by Shahgholian
[
1
]
used the moving plane method and comparison principles. The present self-contained approach based on the thickness function offers several advantages:

•

It is more geometric and conceptual.

•

It avoids the technical complexity of the moving plane method.

•

It naturally connects to isoperimetric inequalities and shape optimisation.

•

It generalises to other operators and problems.

8
Extensions and generalisations

•

Other elliptic operators:
For operators such as the
p
-Laplacian, an analogous thickness function can be defined.

•

Biharmonic problems:
The level sets can admit thickness functions satisfying coupled differential relations (see, e.g., Barkatou
[
2
]
for a general framework).

•

Parabolic problems:
For the heat equation, time-dependent thickness functions describe the evolution of level sets.

•

Free boundary problems:
The thickness function provides a natural parametrisation of the free boundary.

9
Conclusion

We have presented a new, autonomous proof of Shahgholian’s theorem using the thickness function and level set methods. The proof reveals the underlying geometric structure: the overdetermined condition forces all level sets to be parallel to
\partial C
, with the relation
d_{t}(c)=d(c)-t
. This approach is more conceptual, avoids the moving plane method, and generalises naturally without relying on external regularity theories.

Appendix A
Proof of the rigidity lemma (detailed)

We provide a concise but complete summary of Lemma
4.1
following the strategy outlined in Section
4
. The key points are:

1.

The function
w=|\nabla u|^{2}
is subharmonic in
\Omega\setminus C
because
\Delta w=2\|D^{2}u\|^{2}\geq 0
.

2.

On
\partial\Omega
, the overdetermined condition gives
w=1
. By the maximum principle,
w\leq 1
in
\Omega\setminus C
.

3.

To obtain the reverse inequality, use the radial parametrisation: for each
c\in\partial C
set
\phi(r)=u(c+r\nu(c))
. Then
\phi^{\prime}(r)=\nabla u\cdot\nu(c)
is the directional derivative along the normal.

4.

Since
u
is harmonic in
\Omega\setminus C
, the directional derivative
v=\nabla u\cdot\nu(c)
(where
\nu(c)
is constant) is also a harmonic function (
\Delta v=0
). By the harmonic minimum principle,
v
attains its minimum on the boundary of
\Omega\setminus C
.

5.

The boundary values are
\phi^{\prime}(d(c))=-1
and
\phi^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
. Therefore
\phi^{\prime}(r)\geq-1
for all
r
, which implies
|\nabla u|\geq 1
.

6.

Combining both inequalities yields
|\nabla u|=1
almost everywhere; continuity extends equality to the whole
\overline{\Omega}\setminus C
.
