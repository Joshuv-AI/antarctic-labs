Zeta-regularization and natural boundaries: Sums and products of integers and primes
P L Krapivsky, J M Luck

Abstract
Euler regularized the divergent product of all natural numbers and found beautiful formulas for regularized sums of integer powers of natural numbers. These derivations essentially relied on what is now called the zeta-regularization technique, although analytical continuation had not yet been invented. This classic method is however not applicable to the product of all primes, as the prime zeta function has a natural boundary along the imaginary axis. Muñoz García and Pérez-Marco overcame this obstacle and evaluated the product of all primes to 4 ​ π 2 4\pi^{2} by finding an appropriately regularized value of the derivative of the prime zeta function at the origin, lying on the natural boundary. We extend their approach in two novel directions. First, we show how to make sense of the sum of all primes. This regularization requires going a finite distance beyond the natural boundary. Second, we determine the regularized products of integers and primes in the nine imaginary quadratic fields where integers have a unique factorization into primes, and establish a general power-law relationship between products of integers and primes. Two well-known examples are Gauss and Eisenstein integers. The interest in this approach goes beyond number theory. In a variety of physical situations, the zeta-regularization technique is indeed not applicable because the relevant zeta function has a natural boundary.

2
Products of natural integers and primes

2.1
Product of natural integers

The product of all natural integers,

P=\prod_{n\geq 1}n,

(2.1)

can be regularized by relating it to the Riemann zeta function

\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-s}=\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}.

(2.2)

We have indeed formally

P=\exp\sum_{n\geq 1}\log n=\exp\left(-\zeta^{\prime}(0)\right).

(2.3)

This expression is the gist of the zeta-regularization,
consisting in regularizing divergent products such as (
2.1
)
by means of analytic continuation.
The series and product entering (
2.2
) converge for
\mathop{\rm Re}(s)>1

and can be analytically continued to a meromorphic function in the whole complex
s
-plane,
with a simple pole with unit residue at
s=1
.
In particular,
\zeta(s)
is analytic in a neighborhood of
s=0
, with

\zeta(0)=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\zeta^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi.

(2.4)

Inserting the second of these expressions into (
2.3
),
we recover Euler’s expression (
1.1
).

2.2
Product of natural primes

In the same vein, the product of all natural primes,

{\mathit{\Pi}}=\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}p,

(2.5)

is formally given by

{\mathit{\Pi}}=\exp\left(-{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(0)\right),

(2.6)

where
{\mathcal{P}}(s)
is the prime zeta function (see e.g.
[
24
,
38
,
14
,
20
]
):

{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\sum_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}p^{-s}.

(2.7)

As recalled above, at variance with (
2.3
),
the usage of (
2.6
) is compromised by the fact that
{\mathcal{P}}(s)

has a natural boundary along the imaginary axis
and is therefore not analytic in a neighborhood of
s=0
.
This obstacle has been circumvented by Muñoz García and Pérez-Marco
[
22
,
23
]
.
Their heuristic derivation
[
22
]
starts with the Artin-Hasse identity for the exponential
function,

\rme^{x}=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-x^{n})^{-\mu(n)/n},

(2.8)

where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius function:

\mu(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(-1)^{k}&\hbox{if $n$ is the product of $k$ distinct primes},\\
0&\hbox{else}.\end{array}\right.

(2.9)

Combining (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\exp({\mathcal{P}}(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}\exp(p^{-s})

(2.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-p^{-ns})^{-\mu(n)/n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n\geq 1}\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}(1-p^{-ns})^{-\mu(n)/n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n\geq 1}\zeta(ns)^{\mu(n)/n}\,.

Taking logarithms of both sides yields

{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(ns).

(2.11)

This expression was first established
[
24
,
38
,
14
,
20
]

by applying the Möbius inversion formula to the identity

\log\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{{\mathcal{P}}(ns)}{n},

(2.12)

that can be derived from the Euler product formula (
2.2
).
The expression (
2.11
) shows that the prime zeta function has a natural boundary along the
imaginary axis.
The zeros of the Riemann zeta function at
s_{n}=1/2+{\rm i}t_{n}
along the critical line
indeed generate logarithmic branch cuts accumulating along the whole imaginary axis.
The function
{\mathcal{P}}(s)
also has an infinite sequence of logarithmic branch cuts along the real axis
accumulating at the origin (see figure
1
).
These singularities are located at
s=1/k
whenever
\mu(k)\neq 0
, i.e.,
k
is squarefree.
More specifically, there are upward singularities
at
s=1/k
whenever
\mu(k)=1
, i.e.,
k=1
, 6, 10, 14, 15…
and downward singularities
at
s=1/k
whenever
\mu(k)=-1
, i.e.,
k=2
, 3, 5, 7, 11…

Figure 1:
Plot of the real part of the prime zeta function for real
s
,
illustrating its infinite sequence of singularities accumulating at the origin (see text).

The expression (
2.11
) can be used to evaluate regularized values of
{\mathcal{P}}
and its
derivatives at
s=0
.
In this approach, sums involving the Möbius function are consistently evaluated by means
of the zeta-regularization scheme, using

M(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)\,n^{-s}=\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\,.

(2.13)

In the simplest case of
{\mathcal{P}}(0)
, (
2.11
) yields

{\mathcal{P}}(0)=\log\zeta(0)\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n},

(2.14)

where the sum over
n
evaluates to
M(1)=1/\zeta(1)=0
, so that

{\mathcal{P}}(0)=0.

(2.15)

The value of
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(0)
is of special interest as it enters (
2.6
).
We have

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(ns)}{\zeta(ns)},

(2.16)

and in particular

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)}\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n).

(2.17)

The sum evaluates to
M(0)=1/\zeta(0)
.
We thus obtain, using (
2.4
),

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)^{2}}=-2\log(2\pi),

(2.18)

and so, using (
2.6
), we arrive at (
1.2
), the central result of
[
22
,
23
]
.

The same approach applies to higher-order derivatives as well.
In the case of
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}(0)
,
taking the derivative of (
2.16
) and specializing to
s=0
, we arrive at

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)}-\left[\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)}\right]^{2}\right)\sum_{n\geq 1}n\mu(n).

(2.19)

The sum evaluates to
M(-1)=1/\zeta(-1)=-12
.
Using the expression

\zeta^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+\gamma_{1}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}-\frac{[\log(2\pi)]^{2}}{2},

(2.20)

following from the reflection formula (
3.13
),
we obtain

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}(0)=12\gamma^{2}+24\gamma_{1}-\pi^{2}.

(2.21)

The constants

\gamma=\gamma_{0}=0.577\,215\,664\dots,\qquad\gamma_{1}=-0.072\,815\,845\dots

(2.22)

are the Euler and Stieltjes constants appearing in the Laurent series expansion of the Riemann zeta
function

\zeta(1+s)=\frac{1}{s}+\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\,\gamma_{n}s^{n}.

(2.23)

A similar computation yields
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=\infty
.
This quantity is indeed proportional to
M(2)=1/\zeta(-2)
, and
\zeta(-2)=0
.

Although evaluating the regularized product
{\mathit{\Pi}}
of all primes proved to be a challenging problem
resolved only recently,
some infinite products involving primes are easy to regularize.
For instance, the Euler product formula (
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}\big(1-p^{2k}\big)=\frac{1}{\zeta(-2k)}=\infty,

\displaystyle\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}\big(1-p^{2k-1}\big)=\frac{1}{\zeta(1-2k)}=-\frac{2k}{B_{2k}}

(2.24)

for any integer
k\geq 1
(see (
3.2
)).
Specializing these formulas to
k=1
we obtain

\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}\big(1-p^{2}\big)=\infty,\quad\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}(1-p\big)=-12.

(2.25)

This yields formally

\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}(1+p)=\infty,

(2.26)

and suggests that

\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}(p+x)

(2.27)

has no natural regularized expression.
Such an expression would be a prime analogue of the Lerch formula
[
39
,
37
]
(see (
4.10
))

\prod_{n\geq 0}(n+x)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(x)},

(2.28)

which is itself a renormalized avatar of the Weierstrass product formula

\frac{1}{\Gamma(x)}=x\,\rme^{\gamma x}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1+x/n)\rme^{-x/n}.

(2.29)

Finally, we mention that the product

\prod_{n\geq 1}n^{n}=\exp(-\zeta^{\prime}(-1))=1.179\,889\,917\dots

(2.30)

admits a prime analogue,

\prod_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}p^{p}=\exp(-{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(-1)),

(2.31)

where

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(-1)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(-n)}{\zeta(-n)}.

(2.32)

The regularized values of this quantity
could be determined along the lines of section
3
.

3
Sums of natural integers and primes

Why add prime numbers? Prime numbers are made to be multiplied, not added.

Lev Landau

Regularized sums of powers of natural numbers are more popular than regularized products.
The well-known examples like (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) are special cases of the
general Euler’s formula
[
31
]

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{m}=\zeta(-m),

(3.1)

expressing the regularized sums of powers of natural numbers in terms of the Bernoulli numbers,
namely

\zeta(-2k)=0,\quad\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k}\qquad(k\geq 1).

(3.2)

In view of the above, it is quite natural to consider the zeta-regularized sums of powers of
primes

{\mathcal{P}}(-m)=\sum_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}p^{m},

(3.3)

for which the formula (
2.11
) yields

{\mathcal{P}}(-m)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(-mn).

(3.4)

Investigating the above series is a far more formidable task
than evaluating the regularized product of all primes.
We recall that the prime zeta function has a natural boundary along the imaginary axis.
The regularized product of all primes involves
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(0)
,
where the origin sits on the natural boundary,
whereas the regularized sums of powers of primes involve the values of
{\mathcal{P}}(s)
at
s=-m
,
at a finite distance beyond the natural boundary.

The famous quote by the physicist Lev Landau, reproduced above,
was made in connection to the Goldbach conjecture
asserting that every even natural number is the sum of two prime numbers.
Regularizing divergent sums is admittedly a different story.
Finding an application, either in Physics or elsewhere, of
{\mathcal{P}}(-1)
, or of another
{\mathcal{P}}(-m)
, would be amusing.
One should keep in mind that more than two hundred years separate
the determination of
\zeta(-3)
by Euler (see (
1.5
))
and the measurement of the Casimir effect, involving
\zeta(-3)
.

We now explain how to make sense of the formula (
3.4
) in the first case of interest,
namely the sum of all primes,

{\mathcal{P}}(-1)=\sum_{p\;{\mathop{\rm prime}}}p.

(3.5)

Setting
m=1
in (
3.4
),
and taking the real parts of the logarithms,
as we expect the regularized value of
{\mathcal{P}}(-1)
to be real,
we obtain

{\mathcal{P}}(-1)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|\zeta(-n)\right|}.

(3.6)

The Riemann zeta function vanishes at even negative integers (see (
3.2
)),
so that we rather consider

{\mathcal{P}}(-1+\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|\zeta(-n+n\varepsilon)\right|}.

(3.7)

To linear order in
\varepsilon
, we get

{\mathcal{P}}(-1+\varepsilon)\approx\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|\zeta(-n)\right|}+\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|n\varepsilon\zeta^{\prime}(-n)\right|},

(3.8)

and have therefore to deal separately with odd and even values of
n
.
We introduce the Dirichlet series

M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}(s)=\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\mu(n)\,n^{-s},\quad M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(s)=\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\mu(n)\,n^{-s},

(3.9)

which can be related to
M(s)
introduced in (
2.13
) as follows.
First, we have the sum rule
M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}(s)+M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(s)=M(s)=1/\zeta(s)
.
Second, the definition (
2.9
) of the Möbius function implies

\mu(2n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\mu(n)&\hbox{($n$ odd)},\\
0&\hbox{($n$ even)},\end{array}\right.

(3.10)

so that
M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(s)=-M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}(s)/2^{s}
.
We thus obtain

M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}(s)=\frac{2^{s}}{(2^{s}-1)\zeta(s)},\quad M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(s)=-\frac{1}{(2^{s}-1)\zeta(s)}.

(3.11)

The terms proportional to
\log{\left|\varepsilon\right|}
in (
3.8
) sum up to
M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(1)=0
obtained by
specializing
M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(s)
to
s=1
and recalling that
\zeta(s)
has a pole to
s=1
.
Therefore (
3.8
) has a well-defined limit

{\mathcal{P}}(-1)=\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|\zeta(-n)\right|}+\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log{\left|n\zeta^{\prime}(-n)\right|}.

(3.12)

The reflection formula for the Riemann zeta function

\zeta(1-s)=2(2\pi)^{-s}\cos\frac{\pi s}{2}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)

(3.13)

yields

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\left|\zeta(-n)\right|}=\displaystyle{\displaystyle n!\,\zeta(n+1)\over\displaystyle\pi(2\pi)^{n}}&\hbox{($n$ odd)},\\
{\left|n\zeta^{\prime}(-n)\right|}=\displaystyle{\displaystyle n\,n!\,\zeta(n+1)\over\displaystyle 2(2\pi)^{n}}&\hbox{($n$ even)}.\end{array}

(3.14)

Both expressions share the common leading asymptotic behavior
(n/(2\pi\rme))^{n}
.
The corresponding regularized sum reads

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)(\log n-\log(2\pi)-1)=2,

(3.15)

where we have used

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)=\frac{1}{\zeta(0)}=-2,\quad\sum_{n\geq 1}\mu(n)\log n=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)^{2}}=-2\log(2\pi).

(3.16)

At this stage, we are left with

{\mathcal{P}}(-1)=2+{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathop{\rm odd}}+{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathop{\rm even}},

(3.17)

with

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathop{\rm odd}}=\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\frac{n!(\rme/n)^{n}\zeta(n+1)}{\pi}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathop{\rm even}}=\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\frac{n\,n!(\rme/n)^{n}\zeta(n+1)}{2}\,.

(3.18)

The above sums are still divergent.
The asymptotic behaviors of the arguments of the logarithms are indeed respectively

\sqrt{2n/\pi}
and
\sqrt{\pi n^{3}/2}
.
The corresponding regularized sums read

\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\,\frac{\log(2n/\pi)}{2}=-1,\quad\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\,\frac{\log(\pi n^{3}/2)}{2}=\frac{3}{2},

(3.19)

where we have used

\displaystyle\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}(1)=0,\quad\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm odd}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\,\log n=-M_{\mathop{\rm odd}}^{\prime}(1)=-2,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=M_{\mathop{\rm even}}(1)=0,\quad\sum_{n\;{\mathop{\rm even}}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\,\log n=-M_{\mathop{\rm even}}^{\prime}(1)=1.

Putting everything together, we arrive at our final expression,

(3.21)

announced in (

).
The series is convergent and sums to
0.425\,292\,456\dots
, and so

{\mathcal{P}}(-1)=2.925\,292\,456\dots

(3.22)

Regularized values of

for higher integers

could be evaluated along the lines of the above derivation.

Gauss integers form the ring
