Effects of Baduanjin combined with cognitive training in older adults with mild cognitive impairment of mixed etiologies: a study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Li-bing Liang, Kun-peng Li, Shan Wang, Qun-song Shen, Xue-min Li, Cai-qin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) represents an intermediary stage between normative age-related cognitive decline and dementia, with prevalence rates varying significantly across populations. Studies indicate that approximately 15%–20% of adults aged 65 and above are affected by MCI, with risk increasing by about 17% for every five-year increment in age [1]. In China, the prevalence of MCI is 12.2% among individuals aged 55 and older [2], rising to 20.8% in those aged 65 and above [3]. Annually, about 10%–15% of MCI patients progress to dementia, a rate substantially higher than that observed in cognitively healthy older adults [4]. Currently, pharmacological agents have demonstrated limited efficacy in improving cognitive function in MCI patients, whereas non-pharmacological interventions can mitigate or avoid medication-related adverse effects [5, 6]. Consequently, exploring effective non-pharmacological approaches to delay or reverse cognitive decline in MCI has become a research direction of paramount importance in geriatrics and neuroscience.

Cognitive Training (CT), supported by Level I evidence as a recommended intervention, not only systematically enhances overall cognitive performance in patients with MCI but also produces measurable improvements in core cognitive domains such as attention, working memory, and non-verbal learning [7]. Exercise interventions, on the other hand, exert a bidirectional regulatory effect on cognitive and motor function by modulating cerebral hemodynamics, promoting cerebral circulation, and enhancing neuroplasticity [8, 9]. Among these, aerobic exercise has demonstrated particularly significant cognitive benefits for individuals with MCI [10, 11]. However, older adults often experience declines in motor function and balance, which elevate the risk of falls associated with conventional aerobic activities such as brisk walking or jogging, thereby limiting their applicability in the elderly MCI population [12]. In contrast, the traditional Chinese mind-body exercise Baduanjin (BE), characterized by moderate intensity, ease of learning, and integrated somatic-mental regulation, has emerged as a promising intervention for cognitive rehabilitation in MCI [13–15]. A meta-analysis incorporating 14 randomized controlled trials indicated that BE significantly improves global cognitive function in MCI patients (MoCA score: SMD = 2.56, 95% CI: 2.17–2.96, P < 0.001) and yields positive effects on memory, executive function, and frailty status [16].

As a moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, BE enhances cognitive function through multiple mechanisms. First, it modulates cerebral hemodynamics, promotes cerebral blood circulation, and thereby supplies neurons with sufficient oxygen and nutrients [16]. Second, it regulates the neuroendocrine system, reduces stress hormone levels, and promotes the secretion of neurotrophic factors [17]. Together, these mechanisms optimize the physiological environment required for normal brain function, reflecting its role in “Strengthening the Foundation”. Furthermore, BE emphasizes the coordination of movement, breath, and conscious attention—characterized as “harmonizing form, breath, and mind” [18]. This practice activates cognitive-related brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and parietal lobe, enhances cognitive reserve [19], and improves neuroplasticity and psychological adaptability [20], which constitutes the essence of “Nourishing the Origin”. Although BE exerts positive effects on MCI, how to effectively integrate it with other non-pharmacological interventions to amplify therapeutic outcomes remains an area warranting further investigation.

In light of this, the 2021 Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Mild Cognitive Impairment identifies multimodal intervention as a core strategy for optimizing MCI management [21]. Within this framework, combined physical exercise and CT has become a focus of research. Notably, sequential combined intervention—where exercise and cognitive training are administered in distinct phases—demonstrates unique clinical value, as it may enhance adherence and safety among older patients while potentially maximizing comprehensive benefits within the same intervention period [22]. A meta-analysis further indicates that for individuals with MCI, sequential combined intervention yields greater cognitive gains compared to dual-task or integrated interactive approaches [23].

CT can be personalized according to the individual patient’s condition, with training difficulty progressively graded. Through intensive, quantifiable, and adaptive repetitive practice targeting specific cognitive deficits such as working memory, processing speed, and executive function [24], CT achieves “targeted symptom management”. However, clinical evidence regarding the sequential integration of BE and CT in older adults with MCI remains incomplete. In particular, the interactive mechanisms through which these interventions jointly influence activation levels and functional connectivity across distinct brain regions are not yet clearly understood.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a robust investigative tool for examining the aforementioned mechanisms. This technique is characterized by high spatiotemporal resolution, compatibility with movement, and suitability for resting-state assessments, enabling non-invasive, dynamic monitoring of cortical oxygenated hemoglobin concentration [25, 26]. It has been demonstrated to serve as a sensitive indicator of alterations in cognitive states [27, 28]. Existing fNIRS studies reveal that targeted cognitive training enhances neural efficiency in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) during cognitive task performance [29]. Regular physical activity, meanwhile, optimizes PFC activation patterns in older adults with MCI, improving both gait stability and cognitive function, although postural control remains inferior to that of healthy older individuals [30]. It is noteworthy that dynamic postural control relies on the coordinated engagement of an extensive cortical network, including the PFC, premotor cortex (PMC), and supplementary motor area (SMA) [31]. Relevant research further indicates that during dual motor-cognitive tasks, stroke patients exhibit enhanced activation in the right PFC, and functional connectivity between the PFC and PMC/SMA approaches patterns observed in healthy populations [32].

In summary, while existing research has predominantly focused on the effects of BE and CT on the activation and connectivity of the PFC and motor regions during interactive dual-task paradigms, there remains a systematic gap in understanding how sequential BE combined with CT modulates functional connectivity within the PFC–PMC–SMA network and its underlying synergistic mechanisms.

To address this, the present study proposes a three-arm randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effects of sequential BE combined with CT on cognitive and balance function in older adults with MCI. Using fNIRS, we will quantitatively assess pre- and post-intervention changes in regional activation and functional connectivity patterns within the PFC, SMA, and PMC during resting state.

The study aims to investigate the hypothesis that BE may establish a favorable neurophysiological foundation by enhancing local cortical oxygenation and strengthening network-level synergy, thereby optimizing the neural environment for subsequent CT. Furthermore, we will examine how CT, building upon this optimized substrate, enhances the efficiency of specific neural pathways. From a neurophysiological perspective, this work seeks to provide empirical evidence for the synergistic mechanism wherein BE “Strengthening the Root and Cultivating the Origin” and CT “Targeted Symptomatic Treatment”.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a 12-week, three-arm randomized controlled trial conducted in nursing homes and rehabilitation hospitals. It aims to systematically compare the effects of sequential BE combined with CT against those of single-modality interventions. Utilizing fNIRS, the research further investigates, from a neurofunctional and environmental optimization perspective, how BE creates a facilitative neural condition for subsequent CT. Notably, a 12-week intervention period was chosen to ensure a sufficient training dose to elicit measurable cognitive improvements. This duration aligns with the guideline recommending 3 months of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise for older adults with MCI [33] and with the requirement of a cumulative cognitive training time of ≥ 20 h [34]. It has also been successfully adopted in recent combined physical–cognitive intervention trials for MCI [35, 36]. A 4-week follow-up was designed to provide a preliminary assessment of short-term maintenance effects after the cessation of structured training, while balancing the need to minimize participant attrition—a known challenge in longitudinal studies involving frail older populations. The protocol was designed and reported in accordance with the SPIRIT 2025 statement.

Inclusion criteria

Participants must meet all of the following criteria: (1) Age ≥ 60 years; (2)Diagnosis of MCI according to the Petersen criteria [37] and the diagnostic guidelines for dementia and cognitive impairment in China (2018) [38], specifically meeting the following criteria: (1) Subjective cognitive decline reported by the participant or an informant, or objectively verified by clinician assessment. (2) A score of 18–25 on the Beijing version of the MoCA, indicating objective cognitive impairment.3) Possible mild impairment in instrumental activities of daily living, while basic activities of daily living remain independent (muscle strength ≥ grade 3; able to walk safely for at least 10 m without assistance)0.4) Does not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia. Additionally, patients will be classified as having amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) or non‑amnestic/multi‑domain amnestic MCI based on baseline performance on the Auditory Verbal Learning Test – Huashan version (AVLT‑H). A prespecified subgroup analysis stratified by MCI subtype will be performed to explore whether treatment effects differ between amnestic and non‑amnestic profiles. (3) Clear consciousness, stable vital signs, and medically stable condition. (4) Provides informed consent and voluntarily agrees to participate.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals will be excluded if they meet any of the following conditions: (1)Diagnosis of acute brain injury, Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia, or other major central nervous system disorders.;༈2༉Documented history or confirmation by a physician/family member of long-term use of cognition-enhancing medications prior to the onset of symptoms.༛༈3༉Severe visual, auditory, or speech impairments that would compromise neuropsychological assessment༛༈4༉Presence of unstable or severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, renal, or hematologic diseases, or malignancy with a poor prognosis༛༈5༉A regular habit of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical exercise within the past six months (≥ 30 min per day, ≥ 3 days per week)༛༈6༉Concurrent participation in other clinical trials involving cognitive interventions that may interfere with the outcome evaluation of this study༛༈7༉Illiteracy.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the primary hypothesis that the sequential combined intervention (BE + CT) would yield superior global cognitive function compared with either BE or CT alone. The calculation targeted the group × time interaction effect in a 3 (group) × 3 (time point) repeated-measures design. Based on an effect size (η² = 0.044, f = 0.2145) observed in a similar combined physical–cognitive training trial in MCI [36], a more conservative effect size of f = 0.15 was adopted, taking into account the three-group active-comparator design in which all arms, including the two control groups, were expected to improve. With this effect size, the expected between-group difference in post-intervention MoCA score was approximately 1.2 points (assuming SD = 4.0, d ≈ 0.30), which is detectable and conservative relative to the minimal detectable change (MDC₉₀ = 2.6 points) [39] and the minimal clinically important difference (MCID = 3–4 points) [40] for the MoCA in MCI populations.

Using G*Power 3.1 with the following parameters: f = 0.15, α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power (1 − β) = 0.80, number of groups = 3, number of measurements = 3, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5, and nonsphericity correction ε = 1, the estimated total sample size was 150 participants (50 per group). Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final recruitment target was set at 186 participants (62 per group).

Recruitment

Trained research personnel, in collaboration with social workers at the participating nursing homes, will conduct preliminary screenings of potential subjects according to the aforementioned criteria. For individuals without a documented prior diagnosis of MCI, eligibility will be confirmed using a standardized assessment with the Beijing version of the MoCA. All eligible candidates will then participate in a structured informed consent process. During this session, a researcher will provide a comprehensive overview of the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality protocols, and the participant’s right to withdraw voluntarily at any time without penalty.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization will be implemented at the level of the research personnel. A randomized block design will be adopted, using the time of enrollment as the blocking factor. Consecutive groups of six patients, aligned by enrollment time, will constitute a block, with a block length of six. Within each block, randomization will be performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). All parameters and details related to the randomization procedure will be concealed via opaque, sealed envelopes, which will be securely maintained by dedicated randomization personnel not involved in participant enrollment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis. Group assignments will remain undisclosed throughout the trial. In accordance with the study protocol, each of the three groups will receive its respective intervention. The double-blind principle will be strictly upheld during data collection and analysis. Unblinding will occur only after database lock but prior to formal data analysis, so as to preserve objectivity.

Intervention design

All three groups—the sequential baduanjin plus cognitive training group, the baduanjin exercise group, and the cognitive training group—will receive standardized health education on MCI. The educational content will encompass the definition of MCI, its epidemiology, etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnostic criteria, risk factors, therapeutic approaches, and preventive strategies. This education will be delivered through monthly in-person lectures supplemented by printed manuals, with each session lasting 20 to 30 min.

Baduanjin exercise group

In addition to the standardized health education, each training session will be conducted under the direct, on-site supervision of researchers experienced in BE. The training protocol will strictly adhere to the “Health Qigong: Baduanjin” standards issued by the General Administration of Sport of China in 2003 [41]. The sequence comprises ten movements: the preparatory posture, followed by the eight canonical forms—1) “Holding the Hands High with Palms Up to Regulate the Internal Organs,” 2) “Drawing the Bow to Shoot the Eagle,” 3) “Raising One Hand to Regulate the Spleen and Stomach,” 4) “Looking Backwards to Prevent Sickness and Strain,” 5) “Swinging the Head and Lowering the Body to Relieve Stress,” 6) “Moving the Hands Down the Back and Legs, and Touching the Feet to Strengthen the Kidneys,” 7) “Thrusting the Fists and Making the Eyes Glare to Enhance Strength,” and 8) “Raising and Lowering the Heels to Cure Diseases”—and concluding with the closing posture.

The training program was conducted three times per week for 12 weeks. Each session consisted of 30 min of BE—including a 5-minute warm-up, 20 min of BE practice, and a 5-minute cool-down—immediately followed by 30 min of non-cognitive activities designed to avoid any task-specific cognitive training effects. Specifically, the non-cognitive component comprised 15 min of guided body relaxation (e.g., progressive muscle relaxation, focusing solely on bodily sensations without requiring memory or decision-making) and 15 min of quiet reading of neutral health education materials (content unrelated to cognitive training, with no question-and-answer component). This design served as a control for social contact and time commitment without inducing cognitive gains. Exercise intensity will be monitored to maintain a moderate level, corresponding to a score of 12–14 on the Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale. Intensity will be carefully controlled to avoid exceeding an RPE score of 15 (“hard” or “very tired”) to prevent exercise-related injuries [42].

Cognitive training group

Building upon the foundational health education, CT sessions will be conducted via face-to-face, centralized instruction at fixed times and locations. All sessions will be overseen on-site by qualified research personnel to ensure proper guidance through the training procedures.

The core modules of the cognitive training program (detailed in Table 1) were established in strict accordance with standardized protocols recommended in the Summary of Best Evidence for the Application of Cognitive Training in Patients with Mild Cognitive Impairment [43] and the 2022 Chinese Guidelines for Cognitive Training [34]. This content framework is specifically designed to target the characteristic cognitive deficits of the MCI population, ensuring that the intervention is both evidence-based and population-specific in its efficacy to improve core cognitive domains such as memory, executive function, and attention.

Content of Cognitive Training Modules

Structure and dosage of implementation

To optimize the training protocol and enhance tolerance and adherence among elderly MCI participants, the organizational structure of the training was adapted from the validated modular framework applied by Li Jingrui et al. [44] in their 2025 study on BE combined with CT for community-dwelling older adults with cognitive frailty. Specifically, the guideline-based standardized training content was consolidated into discrete modular units. Each training session comprised two specific cognitive modules of 15 min each (total 30 min), followed by the same 30 min of non-cognitive activities as in the BE group—15 min of relaxation and 15 min of neutral reading. This ensured that this group did not gain a cognitive advantage from additional training time.

The selection of training modules and difficulty levels follows a progressive principle from simple to complex, i.e., from lower to higher difficulty tiers. Participants must achieve a success rate exceeding 80% for a given module at a specific difficulty level before advancing to the next level or module.

Sequential baduanjin plus cognitive training group

This arm of the study aims to evaluate the synergistic effects of a sequential BE and CT program on older adults with MCI. To ensure valid between-group comparisons, it is essential to control for key variables. Therefore, the cognitive training component in this arm is identical in content, dosage, and delivery to that administered in the CT-only group. The sole distinction lies in the execution context: here, CT is conducted sequentially immediately following the BE session. The overall sequence and rest intervals for the combined intervention were adapted from the integrated framework designed and validated by Li Jingrui et al. [44] for older adults with cognitive frailty, which underwent expert review to ensure safety and feasibility. Specifically, following the foundational health education, participants in this group receive the combined intervention sequentially: each session begins with 30 min of BE, followed by a 5-minute rest period, and concludes with 30 min of CT. This combined session is conducted three times per week over a total duration of 12 weeks.

Outcomes measures

Primary cognitive outcome: montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) total score

Global cognitive function will be assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Developed by Nasreddine et al., the MoCA is a well-validated screening instrument comprising 30 points and evaluating eight cognitive domains: attention and concentration, executive functions, memory, language, visuospatial abilities, abstraction, calculation, and orientation. Compared to the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the MoCA demonstrates superior sensitivity in detecting MCI, particularly in identifying early executive dysfunction and subtle memory deficits [45]. The study will employ the validated Chinese Beijing version of the MoCA [46]. For the MoCA, the minimal detectable change at the MDC₉₀ is reported to be 2.6 points, and a change of 3–4 points has been established as the MCID in intervention studies for dementia and MCI [39, 40]. These thresholds will serve as benchmarks for interpreting the clinical meaningfulness of between-group differences, rather than relying on statistical significance alone.

Primary neurophysiological outcome: resting-state Oxy-Hb changes in the PFC–PMC–SMA network

Changes in oxygenated hemoglobin (oxy-Hb) concentration will serve as the primary endpoint in NIRS measurements. Oxy-Hb is recognized as the most sensitive indicator of localized cerebral blood flow fluctuations in fNIRS, reflecting underlying neurovascular coupling and neural activity [47].

Secondary outcome: memory

Memory function will be assessed using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test–Huashan version (AVLT-H), developed by Guo Qihao et al. in 2001 [48]. This standardized instrument evaluates episodic memory and has established community-based normative data [49]. During the assessment, participants complete three consecutive learning-recall trials; the total number of words correctly recalled across these trials constitutes the immediate recall score. After a 5-minute delay, a free recall trial is administered to obtain the short-delay recall score. Following an additional 20-minute delay, a second free recall trial yields the long-delay recall score. Finally, a recognition test is conducted to evaluate the participant’s ability to distinguish previously learned words from novel distractors. Lower scores across these indices reflect poorer memory performance.

Secondary outcome: language function

Language function will be assessed using the Animal Fluency Test (AFT). During this test, participants are asked to verbally generate as many animal names as possible within a one-minute time limit. This task primarily evaluates semantic memory and verbal fluency [50]. The primary outcome measure is the total number of correct, non-repeated animal names produced. Higher scores indicate better language and semantic retrieval performance, while lower scores reflect greater impairment.

Secondary outcomes: attention and processing speed

Attention and processing speed will be assessed using the Trail Making Test Part A (TMT-A). As a component of the Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery, TMT-A is a widely utilized neuropsychological test that primarily assesses processing speed and visual attention [51]. The task requires participants to sequentially connect numbered circles in ascending order as quickly as possible. The time taken to complete the task serves as the primary outcome measure, with shorter completion times indicating better attentional efficiency and processing speed.

Secondary outcomes: balance function

Balance function will be evaluated using the Berg Balance Scale (BBS). Developed by Berg et al. in 1989, the BBS is a performance-based measure designed to assess static and dynamic balance abilities, as well as functional coordination [52]. The scale demonstrates excellent internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.96 for the total score and item-level coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 0.99 [53] .

Secondary outcomes: activities of daily living

Functional status in daily living will be assessed using the Activity of Daily Living (ADL) scale, originally developed by Lawton and Brody in 1969 [54]. The scale evaluates two primary domains: basic activities of daily living (e.g., feeding, dressing, grooming) and instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., managing finances, using transportation, handling medications). The total score is interpreted such that a higher score indicates greater impairment in daily functioning.

Secondary physiological outcomes: resting heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV)

Resting‑state HRV will be measured using a standardized 5‑minute short‑term recording, acquired simultaneously during the first 5 min of the resting‑state fNIRS scan. After at least 10 min of supine rest in a quiet environment, continuous interbeat interval data will be acquired with a validated portable heart rate monitor. Time‑domain (e.g., SDNN, RMSSD) and frequency‑domain (e.g., high‑frequency [HF] power, low‑frequency [LF] power, LF/HF ratio) indices will be derived using dedicated software.

Exploratory neurophysiological outcomes: resting-state functional connectivity (FC) indices

To generate hypotheses regarding network‑level synergistic mechanisms within the PFC–PMC–SMA network, a seed‑based correlation analysis of the preprocessed oxy‑Hb time series will be performed as an exploratory analysis.

fNIRS data acquisition and processing

Strictly following the International 10‑20 system, the same trained assessor located the PFC, SMA, and PMC for each participant using a ruler and anatomical landmarks. At every measurement session, the coordinates of each optode and detector were recorded with a 3D digitizer and mapped to standard brain space. These coordinates were used as references for precise repositioning at subsequent visits, ensuring that cross‑session positioning error was kept to a minimum. Before each recording, signal quality screening was performed; channels with excessive hair or poor contact were excluded to ensure that the optical signal attenuation rate for all retained channels fell below a preset threshold. Raw light intensity signals were first converted to optical density and then to changes in oxy-Hb concentration using the modified Beer–Lambert law [55]. A band-pass filter of 0.01–0.1 Hz was applied to remove physiological noise and baseline drift [56–58].

All fNIRS measurements were scheduled between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. to avoid circadian influences on cortical excitability. The measurement room was maintained at a constant temperature (24 ± 1 °C), with low ambient noise and constant, soft lighting. After arrival, participants sat quietly in the room for at least 15 min to acclimatise, allowing haemodynamic fluctuations induced by the environmental transition to subside before recording. Participants were instructed to abstain from vigorous exercise, alcohol, and caffeine‑containing beverages for 24 h prior to measurement. During the resting‑state scan, participants remained seated comfortably, eyes open and fixating on a black cross, and followed standardised instructions to stay awake, relaxed, and to avoid active thinking.

Data collection and analysis

In this study, statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with the significance level set at α = 0.05; a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the event of participant dropout during the trial, an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was employed to ensure the reliability and validity of the study findings.

Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were summarized using frequency (percentage). Baseline comparisons were conducted via one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis H test, or chi-square test as appropriate. The primary analysis utilized a 3 (group) × 3 (time point) repeated measures ANOVA to examine the main effects of group and time, as well as their interaction, for each outcome measure. In cases of significant interaction, post hoc simple effect analyses were performed: within-group comparisons across time points were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, whereas between-group comparisons were adjusted for baseline values. Prespecified subgroup analyses will be performed using stratified repeated‑measures ANOVA, with MCI subtype (amnestic vs. non‑amnestic/multi‑domain amnestic) as the stratifying variable, to examine intervention effects separately. This will explore whether treatment effects vary by baseline cognitive impairment profile and represent a key exploratory analysis for precision intervention in this study. To assess clinical significance, between‑group differences in post‑intervention MoCA scores will be interpreted against the MDC₉₀ (2.6 points) and MCID (3 points), with a focus on whether the 95% confidence intervals of the differences exceed these thresholds.

fNIRS data analysis: (1) Regional brain activation: A general linear model incorporating a hemodynamic response function was applied. Paired t-tests, corrected for false discovery rate (FDR), were used to analyze changes in activation within each region of interest during the resting state. (2) Functional connectivity: Based on preprocessed oxy-Hb concentration time series, seed-based correlation analysis was conducted to compute functional connectivity strength between brain regions/channels, with Pearson correlation coefficients ≥ 0.45 considered indicative of relevant connectivity. Functional connectivity matrices were constructed and compared across groups.

Additionally, an ANOVA will be used to compare changes in resting heart rate and HRV indices across the three groups, thereby verifying the cardiovascular health benefits of BE training. Where the data permit, exploratory analyses will examine whether cardiovascular improvements (e.g., HRV increases) in the BE and combined groups correlate with improvements in fNIRS neurophysiological markers (e.g., PFC oxygenation).

Sensitivity analysis for the “teaching-to-the-test” effect

To evaluate the potential influence of task‑specific training effects on the primary outcome, a prespecified sensitivity analysis will be conducted. Two neuropsychologists blinded to the study design will independently classify each MoCA item as either “directly trained” (i.e., sharing similar cognitive operations with the cognitive training modules) or “untrained” (e.g., orientation items). Total scores for these two item subsets will be calculated. A significant and clinically meaningful advantage for the combined intervention group on the “untrained” subset would provide evidence that the cognitive benefits reflect genuine domain‑general improvement rather than task‑specific strategy learning.

Handling of missing data

Primary efficacy analyses will follow the intention‑to‑treat (ITT) principle, including all randomised participants who initiated the assigned intervention. For the primary outcome (MoCA total score), missing data will be handled using a mixed‑model for repeated measures (MMRM) under the missing‑at‑random assumption, with group, time, and the group‑by‑time interaction as fixed effects and participants as random intercepts. This approach uses all available data to yield unbiased estimates without explicit imputation. The proportion of missing data at each time point will be reported, baseline characteristics of completers and dropouts will be compared, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted: (1) multiple imputation using chained equations (50 imputed datasets), incorporating baseline demographics and prior cognitive scores, and (2) a complete‑case (per‑protocol) analysis. A similar strategy will be applied to secondary outcomes.

Discussion

This study employs a 12-week, three-arm RCT design, systematically comparing the effects of BE, computerized CT, and their sequential combined intervention on cognitive and balance function in older adults with MCI. Concurrently, fNIRS is utilized to investigate the underlying synergistic mechanisms by monitoring pre- and post-intervention changes in oxyhemoglobin concentration and functional connectivity within the prefrontal cortex, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area during the resting state.

In contrast to previous studies that have predominantly focused on single-modality interventions [7, 16], the present investigation emphasizes the effects of a sequential combined intervention. It is anticipated that the sequential combination group will demonstrate superior improvements in overall cognitive function, specific cognitive domains, and balance ability compared to either the BE-only or CT-only groups. If confirmed, these findings would align with current expert consensus advocating multimodal interventions [21] and provide direct behavioral evidence supporting the sequential intervention rationale—whereby physical exercise optimizes the neurophysiological substrate, and cognitive training reinforces specific functional pathways.

At the theoretical level, the findings of this study can be interpreted within the framework of “guided neuroplasticity” [59]. This framework posits that physical exercise creates a favorable foundational environment for neuroplasticity through mechanisms such as enhanced cerebral blood flow and increased release of neurotrophic factors [60]. CT, in turn, can more effectively guide the functional remodeling and structural optimization of neural circuits on this optimized basis [61, 62]. BE, as a moderate-intensity practice integrating “postural adjustment, breath regulation, and mental modulation,” may exert more comprehensive effects. It may not only improve oxygenation levels and functional connectivity within the prefrontal–motor cortical network by modulating neurovascular coupling efficiency [63] but also enhance cognitive reserve and psychological adaptability. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypothesis: In a sequential combined intervention, BE first serves to “consolidate the fundamental state and nurture intrinsic capacity”—optimizing the neurovascular environment and plasticity foundation of the PFC–PMC–SMA network. Subsequently, CT acts to “precisely address the symptomatic manifestations,” delivering targeted reinforcement to specific cognitive pathways such as working memory and executive control.

The significance of this study is threefold. Theoretically, by validating the sequential synergistic model of “foundational optimization–targeted enhancement,” it provides a neurophysiological explanation for cognitive rehabilitation from an integrated Chinese–Western medicine perspective, enriching the application of guided neuroplasticity theory in complex intervention research. Practically, the findings offer evidence-based support for clinical and community healthcare professionals in developing personalized, integrated rehabilitation and care programs for individuals with MCI. The standardized intervention modules, clearly defined operational procedures, and adherence management strategies developed in this study—such as structured health education, WeChat-based reminders, social worker monitoring, and video feedback—possess the potential to be translated into clinical practice guidelines. In terms of interdisciplinary collaboration, this study integrates methodologies and concepts from traditional Chinese rehabilitation, neuroscience, nursing, and rehabilitation medicine, providing a practical exemplar for constructing a multidisciplinary integrated intervention model aimed at addressing age-related cognitive decline.

It should be noted that the execution of this study encountered several challenges, chief among them being the maintenance of good adherence and movement accuracy among elderly participants over the 12-week intervention period. To address this, a multi-tiered support protocol was designed, incorporating scheduled reminders, procedural monitoring, timely feedback, and necessary retraining sessions. Furthermore, as the participants were predominantly recruited from residential care facilities within a specific region, their relatively homogeneous sociodemographic profile may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader community populations with diverse cultural or socioeconomic backgrounds. Third, and most importantly, the inclusion strategy for MCI subtypes represents an inherent limitation of this trial. The central hypothesis posits that Baduanjin optimizes the neurovascular environment to broadly facilitate cognitive training gains across MCI populations. Accordingly, participants with pure amnestic MCI are not excluded, the subtype most strongly associated with prodromal Alzheimer’s disease. However, this flexibility comes at the cost of introducing significant etiological heterogeneity. The progressive neurodegeneration underlying amnestic MCI may fundamentally limit responsiveness to non-pharmacological interventions. Consequently, a high proportion of such participants in the final sample could dilute the overall treatment effect, potentially masking the benefits that might be observed in a more responsive, non‑amnestic population. Although a subgroup analysis stratified by MCI subtype to explore differential effects had been prespecified, the study was not powered for confirmatory between‑subgroup comparisons. All findings from these exploratory analyses must therefore be interpreted with extreme caution and regarded solely as hypothesis‑generating for future trials. Future studies should consider enriching for non‑amnestic MCI to provide a purer test of the hypothesized synergistic mechanism.

The MoCA is a screening tool with limited sensitivity to subtle domain-specific changes; this was partly mitigated by secondary cognitive tests and interpretation against MDC₉₀ and MCID thresholds. A high proportion of amnestic MCI, which carries greater Alzheimer’s disease risk, may dilute treatment effects, and the 4-week follow-up precluded assessment of long-term durability. Moreover, several cognitive training tasks structurally resembled MoCA subtasks, potentially introducing a “teaching-to-the-test” effect; thus, post-intervention gains may partly reflect task-specific strategies rather than domain-general cognitive improvement. To address this, a prespecified sensitivity analysis comparing the effects on trained versus untrained MoCA items will be performed. Future studies should validate long-term efficacy and real-world feasibility, and employ training modules structurally independent of the outcome measure.
