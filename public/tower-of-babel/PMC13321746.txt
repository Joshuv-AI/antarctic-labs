Evaluating the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and nursing staff satisfaction: a comparison of specialized and general wards
Authors: Mohsen Kamali, Amirmohammad Atashinsadaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe leadership style of head nurses is an important factor that can influence nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses and overall performance in hospital wards. This study aimed to examine the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses in specialized and general hospital wards.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among nurses and head nurses working in six teaching hospitals affiliated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Using a census sampling method, 1,354 nurses from 71 hospital wards (31 specialized wards and 40 general wards) participated in the study. Data were also gathered using a three-part questionnaire that included a demographic form, the Fiedler and Chemers Leadership Style Inventory, and a Supervisor Satisfaction scale. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26, with correlation, t‑tests, regression, and ANOVA at the ward level aggregation.ResultsThe mean (SD) satisfaction with head nurses score during this study was 68.94 (6.26) in specialized wards and 66.11 (5.14) in general wards. Nurses in both ward types reported relatively high satisfaction with their head nurses. Also, integrated leadership (a balanced combination of task‑ and relationship‑oriented styles) in specialized wards and relationship-oriented leadership in general wards had the highest frequency. In contrast, task-oriented leadership had the lowest frequency in both wards. In the next step, the findings also indicate that both relationship-oriented and integrated leadership styles resulted in higher levels of nurse satisfaction than the task-oriented style (P < 0.05). Finally, leadership styles explained 26% of the variance in nurse satisfaction with head nurses in specialized wards, and 16% in general wards.ConclusionNursing staff satisfaction with head nurses was higher under integrated leadership in specialized wards and relationship-oriented leadership in general wards. Using relationship-oriented and integrated leadership styles rather than relying solely on task-oriented style may be a potential strategy for supporting nurses’ satisfaction in both specialized and general hospital wards.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04905-4.Keywords: Head nurses, Leadership, Nurses, Job satisfaction

Introduction

Management and leadership are always recognized as important factors in promoting organizational goals in all social areas [1, 2]. Although these two concepts are related, they are conceptually different. Based on this, management especially focuses on planning, organizing, controlling, and administering resources, while leadership usually emphasizes guiding, motivating, and influencing employees to reach organizational goals. In this regard, leadership plays a more important role than management in workplace dynamics [3]. According to the complex nature of medical and nursing care, alongside high workload in these conditions, and the importance of accurate decision-making, effective leadership is particularly vital in the healthcare system [4]. In nursing care settings, this matter is more influential because they are always on the front lines of giving care, and the quality of their performance affects treatment outcomes in these systems [5]. In this situation, head nurses, as the closest management level to nurses, play an essential role in creating a suitable work environment for nurses to provide effective nursing care [6].

Head nurses usually use different leadership styles to guide staff and manage the work environment [7, 8]. However, one of the most applicable measures of leadership styles originated in studies at the University of Michigan, which classifies leadership behaviors into two dimensions: task-oriented and relationship-oriented [9]. Some later studies introduced a combination of these two styles as an “Integrated leadership style” and argued that many leadership styles can be explained by these three basic leadership styles [10, 11]. Head nurses’ leadership styles can have important outcomes for employees and the work environment, including satisfaction [8, 12, 13], creativity [14, 15], burnout [16, 17], intention to leave or migrate [18, 19], stress [20, 21], organizational commitment [22, 23], quality of patient care [24, 25], and motivation [26, 27].

In this context, one of the most important outcomes is nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses. Satisfaction with the immediate supervisor, as an important dimension of work attitude in nurses, plays a crucial role in their work experience and is related to factors such as motivation, organizational commitment, and job performance [28, 29]. Despite this importance, nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses has received less specific attention and study compared to overall job satisfaction. Along with the characteristics of leaders and employees, organizational factors can also affect how leadership styles work and the level of nurse satisfaction with head nurses. In this regard, differences in work environments are important contextual factors. Specialized (intensive care) and general wards differ in terms of the nature of care, the severity of patients’ conditions, the complexity of clinical decisions, and the need for team coordination. Based on this, it seems that these differences can create various conditions for practicing leadership manners and affect how nurses perceive head nurses’ performance and their overall level of satisfaction [30, 31].

From a theoretical viewpoint, the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with them in specialized and general wards is explained by three main theories. At first, based on Fiedler’s contingency theory, the usefulness of a leadership style depends on environmental conditions, and no single style is always effective in all situations [32, 33]. Based on this, it is expected that the fit between a leadership style and environmental conditions differs across clinical wards, and that this difference can affect nurses’ satisfaction. On the other hand, the Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) theory states that the quality of the relationship between leaders and employees plays a strong role in work attitudes, including satisfaction [34, 35]. Therefore, styles that strengthen interaction, trust, and support between head nurses and nurses are likely to be associated with higher levels of satisfaction. Also, the skills-based leadership theory emphasizes that leadership styles can be developed and are shaped by experience and work conditions; thus, individual characteristics and environmental contexts are expected to affect how head nurses use leadership styles, and consequently, affect nurses’ satisfaction with them [36, 37]. Although leadership styles have been widely studied in nursing research, two considerable gaps remain unclear. Most previous studies have assessed nurses’ overall job satisfaction, with less attention to their satisfaction with their head nurses. Also, the contextual role of ward type has not been specifically considered. Based on this, specialized and general wards have distinct clinical and managerial demands that may influence head nurses’ leadership styles. Thus, the present study aims to investigate the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with them in specialized and general wards, while also considering the role of the ward context in this relationship.

Materials and methods

Design and setting(s)

This is a cross‑sectional, descriptive‑correlational study that examined the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and the nursing staff satisfaction with head nurses working in teaching hospitals affiliated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences, namely Ayatollah Mousavi, Valiasr, and Shahid Beheshti in Zanjan, Emdadi and Al‑Ghadir in Abhar, and finally Bu‑Ali Sina in Khorramdarreh during September 2024 to March 2025.

In this study, all teaching hospitals affiliated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences (ZUMS) were selected. Based on this, the included hospitals represented the full set of eligible affiliated teaching hospitals, rather than a selected subset. Also, organizational characteristics such as hospital size or patient volume were not included as control variables in the analyses. In addition, a comparative approach was used to compare nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses between specialized and general wards.

Participants and sampling

Because hospital wards differ in their work environments (workload, shift patterns) and because some wards exist only in specific hospitals, a census sampling approach was adopted to include all eligible nurses and head nurses, aiming to reduce selection bias and enhance generalizability. Next, the statistical power for the ward-level (n = 71) analysis was also assessed. Accordingly, for a multiple linear regression model with two predictors, assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80, the required sample size is 68 (G*Power 3.1). Therefore, the available sample of 71 wards provides sufficient statistical power for the ward-level analyses.

Based on this, 1,548 eligible nurses and head nurses were initially selected. Then, hospital wards were classified into 31 specialized and 40 general wards based on national hospital accreditation standards and routine administrative classifications used in Iranian public hospitals supervised by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education. Participation was limited to nurses and head nurses with at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing and a minimum of six months of experience in their ward. Nursing assistants and staff without a bachelor’s degree were excluded. Participants were assigned to specialized or general wards based on their current workplace at the time of data collection.

Tools/instruments

To achieve the objectives of this study, data were collected using three validated instruments. First, a used for nurses and head nurses to collect basic information about the demographic questionnaire was participants, including age, gender, marital status, education level, ward type, shift type, work experience, hours. The content job title, employment type, mean monthly working hours, and mean monthly overtime validity of this questionnaire was confirmed using the comments of 10 faculty members of the university. where the study was conducted.

The second was the Fiedler and Chemers Leadership Style Inventory (LSI), a standardized tool thator integrated leadership styles. The Persian version, identifies relationship-oriented, task-orientedcomprises 18 items, rated on an 8-point Likert scale. In this study, participants in each ward completed the ward's LSI, and the ward's leadership style was determined by combining the head nurse's score with the average nurse score. Based on this, we used a composite scoring approach that combines the head nurse's self-assessment with the average perception of nurses in the same ward. This method was chosen for two self-assessments of leadership style carries the risk of bias. On principal reasons. Relying exclusively on the other hand, relying on the views of the nurses under leadership may be influenced by their relationships these issues, the and may ignore important dimensions and strategies of their leadership. Considering researchers of this study, after consulting with faculty members with research experience, concluded that combining the views of nurses and head nurses could be a more accurate, balanced, and precise measure. Also, combining in each department with their head nurse's score reduces random error and nurses' scores increases reliability. Therefore, the leadership style score for each department was calculated as follows: (the head nurse's LSI score + the nurses' mean LSI score) divided by 2.

The decision to use this composite scoring method was supported by consultation with experts in leadership research and nursing management. Also, we extracted the cut off points to classify leadership styles in this study (scores ≥ 64 = relationship oriented; ≤57 = task oriented; 58–63 = integrated) based on the original LSI guideline and with the help of other validation studies [33]. These cut-off values are based on the distribution of LPC scores that have been usually used in studies conducted between 1980 and 2022 (including research in healthcare environments). The suitability of these cut-off values for the present study was also reviewed and approved by several faculty members who are familiar with leadership research in Iranian clinical settings [38, 39]. Also, this study has previously been validated in the Iranian population. In a study by Nekooee Moghadam et al. [40], the instrument’s reliability was assessed and demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90). In the pilot phase of the present study, the LSI’s internal consistency was satisfactory (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90). In the final sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 (calculated and supplied).

The third instrument used in this study was the “Satisfaction with Head Nurse Questionnaire.” This researcher-made tool was designed to measure nurses’ satisfaction with the performance of their head nurses. The instrument focused on dimensions related to communication, supervisor support, fairness in assigning tasks and shifts, professional competence, and staff empowerment, and evaluated the nurse-head nurse relationship. Due to the lack of a specific, standardized tool to measure nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses, items related to interaction with the head nurse and supervisory/leadership dimensions were systematically extracted from 12 valid international instruments in the field of job satisfaction and organizational behavior (including JSS, Herzberg, Spector, Minnesota, and Brayfield & Rothe) to serve as the basis for developing this tool. During the item generation phase, an integrated (deductive-inductive) approach was used. In the deductive phase, an initial five-dimensional conceptual framework was developed based on the literature review and the aforementioned instruments. In the inductive phase, the items were modified to fit the study’s clinical context. In this step, overlapping items were recognized, duplicate items were removed, and some items were merged. For this purpose, an item-mapping matrix was used to assess conceptual similarity systematically.

Ultimately, 34 initial items were drafted. After several rounds of review for clarity, redundancy, and relevance to the clinical environment, the items were reduced to 24, then to a final version of 20. Before conducting the main study, the instrument was pilot-tested on 30 nurses, and minor modifications were made. The instrument was scored on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Based on this, the total score ranged from 20 to 100. Also, because the tool is researcher-made and lacks standard cutoff points, classification of scores was based on psychometric principles and data interpretability. The actual score range was considered to be 80 (20–100), and, for conceptual interpretation, the theoretical scale mean (60) was used as a directional reference. Then, to create interpretable levels, the score range was classified into three levels of low, moderate, and high satisfaction using an equal-interval approach. Based on this, scores of 20–38 indicated low satisfaction, 39–57 indicated moderate satisfaction, and 58–100 indicated high satisfaction. The content validity of the instrument was evaluated with the participation of 20 faculty members from Zanjan University of Medical Sciences, and their feedback mainly involved increasing clarity and improving cultural adaptation (Supplementary File 1). The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was 0.98, and the Content Validity Index (CVI) values for relevance, clarity, and simplicity were 0.89, 0.93, and 0.90, respectively. Construct validity was assessed using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA); the KMO value was 0.824, and Bartlett’s test was significant (P < 0.05). The results of the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation showed that the instrument has a five-factor structure, including communication, supervisor support, fairness in assigning tasks and shifts, professional competence, and empowerment, which altogether explained 68% of the total variance. The instrument’s reliability was also satisfactory; Cronbach’s alpha was 0.82, test-retest reliability was 0.792, and Cohen’s kappa was 0.817, indicating adequate stability. The complete item-level factor loading matrix is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Data collection methods

Because the study used a census approach, data collection took nearly six months. Researchers visited each ward during all three shifts: morning, evening, and night, to get more participants. After applying the inclusion criteria, 86 nurses were excluded for lacking enough work experience. Also, 48 participants were removed due to extended leave, 39 opted out, and 21 returned incomplete questionnaires. As a result, of the 1,548 selected nurses and head nurses, 1,354 completed the survey (response rate: 87.5%). Data collection took place in person. At this stage, the researchers first explained the study objectives to the participants and, after obtaining informed consent, distributed the questionnaires across all work shifts to maximize participation.

In this study, head nurses completed the demographic questionnaire and the Leadership Style Inventory, whereas nursing staff completed the demographic questionnaire, the Leadership Style Inventory, and the Supervisor Satisfaction Questionnaire. Based on this, leadership style was analyzed at the ward level. In such a way that the Leadership Style Inventory score for each participant (head nurses and nurses) was computed according to the standard scoring guidelines of this questionnaire. In the next step, the leadership style of each ward was calculated using the leadership style score obtained from the questionnaire completed by the ward head nurse, along with the mean leadership style score obtained by the nurses working in the same ward. In addition, each nurse completed the satisfaction questionnaire only regarding the head nurse of his/her current ward at the time of data collection. Therefore, nurses’ satisfaction scores reflected their evaluations of their current head nurse, not of head nurses from other wards.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Initially, descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were used to summarize the data. Normality of the variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and parametric tests were applied based on its results [41]. Inferential analyses included Pearson correlation, independent t-tests, and one-way ANOVA. In this study, the ward was considered the unit of analysis; therefore, leadership style scores and nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses scores were aggregated at the ward level before performing the one-way ANOVA. In the next step, multiple linear regression models were performed for specialized and general wards to examine the predictive role of head nurses’ leadership styles in explaining ward-level mean satisfaction with head nurses. In the regression models, leadership style was considered as a categorical variable with three categories (task-oriented, relationship-oriented, and integrated). For this purpose, dummy coding was used, and based on this the Relationship-oriented leadership style was used as the reference category [42]. Two dummy variables, including task-oriented and integrated leadership styles, were entered into the model. The dependent variable was the mean nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses score calculated for each ward. Also, before performing the regression analyses, the model assumptions were analyzed. Thus, data normality was assessed using Quantile–Quantile plots [43] and the Shapiro–Wilk test [41]. Homoscedasticity was also evaluated by visual inspection of residual scatterplots [44]. Moreover, multicollinearity in this study was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all VIF values were below the acceptable threshold, indicating no serious multicollinearity (All VIF values were below 2) [45]. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05, and results were presented in tables.

On the other hand, because nurses were nested within hospital wards, the ward was considered the main unit of analysis in this study. This approach was selected because the head nurse’s leadership style was regarded as a ward-level characteristic, and nurses in the same ward evaluated the same head nurse. Before aggregating individual responses to the ward level, aggregation indices were assessed. First, the ICC (1) index was evaluated to assess the relationship between satisfaction with head nurses and the type of ward in which they work. The results showed that this index was 0.14, indicating that 14% of the variance in nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses was related to ward membership.

In the next step, the ICC (2) index was used to examine the reliability of ward-level indices, yielding an ICC (2) of 0.79, indicating acceptable reliability of the average scores at the ward level. Finally, another index, rwg, was measured to assess the level of agreement among individuals within a group (ward). This index in our study sample was 0.73, which indicates acceptable agreement within the ward. Therefore, individual nurses’ satisfaction scores were aggregated by calculating the mean score for each ward. Leadership style scores were also aggregated at the ward level and then classified into task-oriented, relationship-oriented, or integrated leadership style based on predefined cut-off points. This aggregation was used to align the unit of analysis with the study aim and to reduce the risk of violating the independence assumption due to clustering of nurses within wards. Multilevel modeling was not applied because the main objective was to examine the association between ward-level leadership style and ward-level mean satisfaction, rather than to model both individual- and ward-level predictors simultaneously.

Results

Demographic characteristics of head nurses

Of the eligible population, 1,354 participants completed the questionnaires of this study (response rate: 87.5%). Initially, our findings on the demographic characteristics of participants evaluated at the ward level indicated that most head nurses in specialized wards were women, and the majority were married. Their mean (SD) age was 45.45 (5.23) years, and their mean (SD) work experience was 22.14 (4.25) years. A bachelor’s degree was the highest level of education for the majority (about 83%). All were employed permanently, and none were working under a contractual arrangement. More than 80% had fixed shifts, with the majority working morning shifts. Additionally, they worked a mean (SD) of 156.96 (12.98) hours per month (including both regular hours and overtime), with a mean (SD) monthly overtime of 21.82 (10.55) hours. Also, head nurses in general wards shared a profile similar to that of specialized wards: most were female (85%) and married (95%). Their mean (SD) ages and work experience were 42.50 (4.86) and 21.65 (5.06), respectively. Bachelor’s degrees were also most common (75.92%), with all in permanent employment. 80% worked fixed shifts. Mean (SD) total work time and overtime were 160.77 (15.21) hours and 22.09 (8.34) hours per month, respectively. In the next step, to assess differences in demographic variables between the two general and specialized wards, an independent t-test was used for continuous quantitative variables and a chi-square test for categorical variables. The results of these statistical tests showed that there was no statistically significant difference between the demographic variables of the head nurses participating in this study (P > 0.05). Demographic characteristics of nurses

In specialized wards, nurse characteristics were analyzed at the ward level. The ward-level mean (SD) age of nurses was 31.49 (SD = 4.52) years, with a mean work experience of 10.05 (SD = 5.20) years. The mean proportion of female nurses per ward was 83.55 (SD = 6.54) percent, and the proportion of married nurses was 70.23 (SD = 5.91) percent, as the majority per ward. On average, the proportion of nurses holding a bachelor’s degree was 86.91 (SD = 5.18) percent, 59.67 (SD = 3.11) percent had permanent employment status, and 83.12 (SD = 7.29) percent had rotating work shifts as the majority per ward. The mean (SD) monthly work time was 183.44 (16.91) hours, including 40.38 (10.72) hours of overtime.

In general wards, the results of nurse characteristics at the ward level showed that the ward-level mean (SD) age of nurses was 28.89 (SD = 5.24) years, with an average work experience of 8.22 (SD = 3.72) years. The mean proportion of female nurses per ward was 79.34 (SD = 5.40) percent, and the proportion of married nurses was 79.05 (SD = 6.37) percent, as the majority per ward. Also, the proportion of nurses holding a bachelor’s degree in general wards was 93.77 (SD = 6.39) percent, and 52.73 (SD = 3.64) percent had permanent employment status, and 85.34 (SD = 5.10) percent had rotating work shifts as the majority per ward.

Moreover, the mean (SD) monthly work time in these wards was 188.13 (SD = 20.27) hours, which included 36.16 (SD = 8.22) hours of overtime. As with head nurses, to compare the demographic characteristics between the two types of wards, an independent-samples t-test was used. This test was applied to both continuous variables (e.g., mean age at the ward level) and the mean proportions of categorical variables (e.g., the mean percentage of female nurses per ward). The results of these statistical tests also revealed no statistically significant difference in the demographic variables of the nurses participating in this study (P > 0.05). Satisfaction with head nurses

The evaluation of nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses at the ward level showed that the mean satisfaction score (SD) in specialized wards was 68.94 ± 6.26, while in general wards it was 66.11 ± 5.14. Based on this, it can be said that nurses reported moderate to high levels of satisfaction with head nurses in both ward types. In this regard, based on the differences in mean satisfaction with head nurses scores between specialized and general wards, the statistical analyses indicated that this difference is statistically significant (P = 0.04). This means that nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses in specialized wards is significantly higher than in general wards. Further evaluation of the different dimensions of satisfaction with head nurses showed that, in the supportiveness dimension, nurses in specialized wards had significantly higher satisfaction with head nurses than those in general wards (P = 0.04).

In contrast, in the communication dimension, statistical analyses showed that the mean satisfaction with head nurses score for nurses in general wards was higher than in specialized wards, and this difference was statistically significant (P = 0.03). On this basis, it can be said that nurses working in specialized wards were more satisfied with their head nurses in the supportiveness dimension than those in general wards. In contrast, nurses in general wards reported greater satisfaction with their head nurses in the communication dimension than those in specialized wards. A comparative evaluation of other dimensions of nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses in general and specialized wards showed that the differences in mean scores across these dimensions were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Relationships between nurses’ demographics and their satisfaction with head nurses

At this stage, to evaluate the relationship between ward-level aggregated demographic characteristics and mean nurse satisfaction with head nurses, Pearson correlation analysis was employed (specialized wards n = 31; general wards n = 40). The Pearson correlation analysis identified significant positive associations between certain ward-level demographic characteristics and overall satisfaction with head nurses in both specialized and general wards. Based on this, we found a statistically significant positive correlation between nurses’ permanent employment status and their satisfaction with head nurses scores in specialized (r = 0.35, p = 0.01) and general (r = 0.27, p = 0.02) wards. In addition, our results showed a significant positive correlation between the mean age of nurses and satisfaction with head nurses in both specialized (r = 0.37, p = 0.01) and general (r = 0.33, p = 0.01) wards. Also, a significant positive correlation was observed between the mean work experience and satisfaction with head nurses in both specialized (r = 0.29, p = 0.02) and general (r = 0.31, p = 0.01) wards. Apart from these, no significant correlations were found for the other demographic variables (Table 4).

Leadership styles

The results of this step revealed that in specialized wards, the integrated leadership style was the most common among head nurses. In contrast, the relationship-oriented leadership style was most common among head nurses in general wards. Meanwhile, in both specialized and general wards, the task-oriented leadership style had the lowest frequency. Subsequently, a comparative evaluation of the distribution of leadership styles between specialized and general wards using the Chi-square test showed that the distribution of head nurses’ leadership styles differed significantly between the two wards (P = 0.03). As shown in Table 5, the “Integrated” style was most prevalent in specialized wards (45.2%), whereas the “Relationship-oriented” style was most prevalent in general wards (52.5%).

Relationships between head nurses’ demographics and leadership styles

The analysis of the relationship between descriptive variables and leadership style scores showed that the differences in leadership style mean scores across age and work experience of head nurses were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Based on this, the results of the Pearson correlation analysis indicated that, in specialized wards, there was a negative correlation between the age (r = -0.26, p = 0.03) and work experience (r = -0.31, p = 0.02) of head nurses and their task-oriented leadership style mean score. Also, only in general wards was there a positive correlation between the age (r = 0.29, p = 0.02) and work experience (r = 0.33, p = 0.01) of head nurses and their integrated leadership styles. No significant correlations were observed for other demographic variables.

Relationship between head nurses’ leadership style and satisfaction of nurses with head nurses

In line with the primary objective of the present study, after determining leadership styles across different wards and assessing nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses levels, the differences in mean nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses scores across leadership styles in specialized and general wards were examined using one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The results of this statistical analysis revealed that there is a significant difference in satisfaction with head nurses scores of nurses based on the leadership styles of head nurses in both specialized wards (F(2, 28) = 5.12, p = 0.01) and general wards (F(2, 37) = 3.54, p = 0.03). The difference between the relationship-oriented and integrated styles was not statistically significant (P = 0.08). This result shows that both relationship-oriented and integrated leadership styles yielded higher levels of nurse satisfaction with head nurses compared to the task-oriented style. In general wards, satisfaction with head nurses was highest with relationship-oriented styles, followed by integrated styles, and lowest with task-oriented styles. Similarly, in these wards, a statistically significant difference was observed only between the relationship-oriented and task-oriented leadership styles, as well as between the integrated and task-oriented styles (P < 0.05). The difference between the relationship-oriented and integrated styles was not statistically significant (P = 0.09). Multiple linear regression

In the final step of statistical analysis in this study, a multiple linear regression was conducted to examine whether leadership style predicts nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses at the ward level. For this purpose, the leadership style was first entered as a categorical variable using dummy coding, with relationship‑oriented leadership as the reference category. The results showed that the regression model for specialized wards was statistically significant (F (2, 28) = 5.12, p = 0.01). This means that the leadership style explained a moderate proportion of the variance in ward‑level nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses (R² = 0.26, adjusted R² = 0.21). Also, our results revealed that compared with the relationship‑oriented leadership style, the task‑oriented leadership style was associated with significantly lower nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses (B = − 13.11, SE = 4.24, t = − 3.09, 95% CI [− 21.79, − 4.43], p = 0.004). In contrast, integrated leadership style was associated with non-significantly higher satisfaction with head nurses scores (B = 5.15, SE = 3.73, t = 1.38, 95% CI [-2.49, 12.79], p = 0.18).

The regression model for general wards was also statistically significant (F (2, 37) = 3.54, p = 0.03). Leadership style explained a modest proportion of the variance in nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses (R² = 0.16, adjusted R² = 0.11). Compared with the relationship-oriented leadership style, the task-oriented leadership style was negatively associated with nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses scores (B = − 15.06, SE = 5.28, t = − 2.85, 95% CI [− 25.76, − 4.36], p = 0.007). However, integrated leadership style was not significantly associated with nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses (B = − 4.24, SE = 5.09, t = − 0.83, 95% CI [− 14.55, 6.07], p = 0.40). Discussion

The findings of this study revealed that nurses’ satisfaction with relationship-oriented leadership style in general wards and with integrated leadership styles in specialized wards was higher than with task-oriented style. This finding aligns with evidence from some comparable previous studies. In this regard, a systematic review by Cummings et al. [46] analyzed 129 primary studies and found that relationship-oriented leadership styles are associated with positive work outcomes, including nurse satisfaction. In contrast, task-oriented or control-based styles show weaker, or even negative, associations with these outcomes. This result shows that, in general wards, nurses perceive leadership styles based on interaction, support, and participation as more desirable. Given the higher sensitivity and importance of nursing care, a combination of relationship-oriented and task-oriented (integrated) styles in specialized wards is preferred over purely task-oriented styles.

Furthermore, during another review study, Hult et al. [30] found that relationship-oriented leadership styles have a positive correlation with satisfaction of nursing staff and progress in their work environments. This review also showed that leadership effectiveness in nursing practice often results from combining relationship-oriented and task-oriented behaviors. Based on this, it can be said that the key strength of the present study compared to these previous studies is its focus on hospital wards (General and specialized) and on the relationship between leadership styles and satisfaction with head nurses in particular. In previous comparable studies, researchers have examined the relationship between head nurses’ leadership style and total job satisfaction of nursing staff in all hospital wards (without distinguishing between general and specialized wards). (e.g., Chang and Wang [47], Khajehpour et al. [48] and Ofei et al. [49]). However, in this study, we were able to analyze this relationship separately across specialized and general hospital wards. In conclusion, these results indicate that the relationship between leadership style and satisfaction with head nurses is not uniform and can vary across work environments and the nature of care in different hospital wards. In addition, most previous studies in this field have focused on the relationship between leadership styles and overall job satisfaction. However, job satisfaction is a multidimensional construct (comprising pay, work environment, colleagues, etc.) and does not accurately reflect specific satisfaction with the head nurse. Therefore, this study focused specifically on nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurse, allowing for a more precise analysis of the perceived relationship with leadership styles.

Despite differences in leadership styles based on the hospital wards, nurses’ satisfaction levels were relatively high in both specialized and general wards. We can explain this result using Fiedler’s Contingency Theory. Based on this theory, no single leadership style is better for all conditions and professions. In other words, this theory states that leadership effectiveness depends on “the match between the leader’s style and the situational control” [32, 33]. Accordingly, the high complexity of care, rapid changes in patient conditions, and the need for constant coordination in general wards may increase the need to use both task- and relationship-oriented behaviors simultaneously (an integrated style). Conversely, the relative stability and routine nature of care in general wards make relationship-oriented leadership styles more prominent. Our results showed that as head nurses became older and gained more work experience, they were less likely to use a task‑oriented leadership style. Instead, they showed a greater tendency to use integrated styles. This finding can be explained using skills‑based leadership theory. This theory states that leadership skills can change over time [36, 37]. In other words, as leaders gain experience, they become better at managing tasks, communicating with others, and understanding how work processes operate. As a result, leaders may gradually move toward more flexible leadership styles. In a similar study, Herman et al. [50] found that age and managerial experience are related to leadership behaviors and that some behavioral aspects become stronger over time. However, research in this area is still limited and scattered. There is, therefore, a clear gap in the literature regarding relationship‑oriented and task‑oriented leadership styles. The findings of the present study can help provide a basis for future research in this field.

On the other hand, statistical analyses showed that, in both specialized and general wards, nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses was significantly associated with age and work experience. This finding is consistent with previous studies in this field by Mamić et al. [51], Ghalah et al. [52], and Medeni et al. [53], and can be explained using the Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) theory [34, 35]. Based on this, as age and work experience increase, the frequency of interactions and shared experiences between nurses and head nurses grows. This result can be largely explained by the components of LMX theory, such as trust, perceived support, and mutual respect. It can help improve the quality of the relationship between head nurses and nurses and increase nurses’ satisfaction. In addition, it seems that more experienced nurses often have more realistic expectations of their job and work environment, and this factor could be another reason for their greater satisfaction.

The results of this study also showed that in both specialized and general sectors, permanent nurses reported more satisfaction with their head nurses. This result is somewhat consistent with the study of Panchal et al. [54]. They found that job satisfaction was higher among permanent nursing staff compared to temporary staff. However, there are other studies that have reported results that are contradictory to the results of our study. For example, Kolokythas et al. [55] found that job satisfaction was higher among nurses with temporary employment than among those with permanent employment. In this regard, it can be said that this discrepancy may be due to differences in employment status across countries. For example, in some countries, temporary employment is more attractive because it provides more flexibility in shift schedules and location of employment. In contrast, in some countries or locations, permanent employment can lead to better job security, stability, and benefits. In addition, differences in the dimensions of satisfaction measured (satisfaction with the head nurse in this study versus overall job satisfaction in other studies) may also account for these contradictory findings. Moreover, in this study, due to a relatively large sample size (1,354 nurses and 71 head nurses), the analyses of demographic characteristics (age and experience) and employment status had sufficient statistical power, thereby strengthening the generalizability of the findings.

Strengths

One of the main strengths of the present study is its large sample size, which included almost the entire population of nurses working in hospitals affiliated with Zanjan University of Medical Sciences. This sampling method increased the statistical power of the analyses. Also, this approach contributed to the stability of the results and improved the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. In the present study, data collection was conducted in person, face-to-face, within clinical environments. This method significantly reduced non-response error and strengthened data quality and internal validity. From a methodological perspective, the present study provides a distinct analytical advantage by using ward-level comparative analysis between specialized and general wards.

This approach allowed examination of the relationship between head nurses’ leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses across heterogeneous clinical settings with different working conditions, while avoiding analysis at an overall organizational level. As a result, the findings demonstrate greater analytical precision and richer interpretability than those from studies relying on macro-level analyses. In addition, nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses was assessed using a researcher-developed measurement tool, adapted to the cultural context and based on internationally validated questionnaires. Aligning this instrument with the organizational and cultural characteristics of Iran’s healthcare system meaningfully enhanced the construct validity and contextual credibility of the findings.

Limitations

The present study has several important limitations that should be considered. At first, its cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences between head nurses’ leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses’ leadership, and only associations observed at a single point in time can be examined. Although this study attempted to collect data from multiple sources, the possibility of common-method bias cannot be ruled out. In this study, the independent variable (head nurses’ leadership style) was measured from two sources: nurses’ perspectives and head nurses’ self-assessments. In contrast, the dependent variable (nurse satisfaction with head nurses) was measured solely from nurses’ reports. However, part of the data, including nurses’ evaluation of leadership style and satisfaction with head nurses, was provided by a single group of respondents (nurses). Therefore, since this part of the data was collected from a common source and over a period of time, it may have been affected by situational factors, transient affective states, and individual perceptions of participants at the time of completing the questionnaire, and this could have partially affected the estimation of relationships between variables. Also, using a researcher-made questionnaire to measure nurses’ satisfaction with their head nurses can limit the generalizability of the results. Although this tool was developed and evaluated according to scientific and psychometric principles, including content validity, construct validity, and reliability, its features have been investigated in only one organizational setting (Zanjan University of Medical Sciences). Therefore, its performance may differ across hospitals or healthcare systems and needs to be investigated and validated in future studies in different settings. Another limitation of the present study is that it was conducted within a single university healthcare system in Iran. Although the study included different hospital wards within this system, the organizational structure, managerial practices, cultural context, and healthcare policies may differ from those of other healthcare systems in Iran and other countries. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as their generalizability to other institutional, regional, or international healthcare settings may be limited.

Moreover, although ward-level analysis was used to examine context-based differences, the lack of consideration of key organizational factors and resources represents another limitation. These include workload intensity, nurse-to-patient ratios, staffing adequacy, access to ward resources and equipment, levels of support from management and head nurses, opportunities for education and professional development, clarity of hospital procedures and policies, reward and incentive systems, work–life balance, and the clinical complexity of patients. Previous studies have shown that these factors play a decisive role in nurses’ overall job satisfaction, particularly their satisfaction with their managers, and can substantially moderate the effect of leadership styles on employee satisfaction. Therefore, the lack of control for these variables may have influenced the observed relationships in the present study to some extent. Another important limitation of this study is the hierarchical structure of the data, which means that nurses were located within hospital wards. Ideally, potential clustering effects at the ward level should have been assessed using multilevel modeling; however, the relatively small number of participants per ward did not allow for robust multilevel analysis. Therefore, conventional statistical methods were used, which may not fully capture possible ward-level influences on the study outcomes.

Considering the limitations of the present study and the existing gaps in nursing leadership research, future studies are recommended to use longitudinal and intervention designs to identify better causal relationships between leadership styles and nurses’ satisfaction with head nurses. Researchers should also apply multilevel and mixed-methods approaches to understand differences between special and general wards better and to explore nurses’ perceptions in depth. Attention to organizational factors such as workload, staffing levels, resources, managerial support, training opportunities, and work–life balance is necessary, as these factors may influence the relationship between leadership styles and satisfaction with head nurses. In addition, future studies should examine the effects of leadership styles on quality of care, hospital performance, and patient satisfaction. Finally, conducting studies in different organizational and cultural settings can improve the generalizability of findings.

Conclusions

Head nurses play a key role in nursing staff satisfaction, given their close, continuous contact with nurses. Based on this, their leadership style is closely associated with nurses’ satisfaction, work performance, and the achievement of organizational goals. The results of the present study show that nurses in both specialized and general wards reported higher satisfaction under integrated and relationship-oriented leadership styles than under a purely task-oriented style. In other words, a task-oriented style, which emphasizes severe control and task completion, was associated with lower satisfaction in both wards. This study suggests that leadership styles that emphasize empathy, open communication, and collaboration are more aligned with nurses’ conditions.

Therefore, hospital administrators should avoid heavy dependence on task‑focused leadership styles. Instead, they should encourage head nurses to use leadership approaches that are supportive, relationship‑based, and flexible. In this regard, practical measures such as leadership training programs, mentoring systems, and regular performance feedback by hospital administrators can help head nurses strengthen leadership skills. In addition to leadership style, the study also identified other important causes of dissatisfaction, including limited managerial support and an unequal distribution of workloads and shifts. These problems need specific attention and appropriate action from hospital managers. Based on this, to improve nurse satisfaction, head nurses should keep fair work schedules, provide consistent support, and act as advocates for nurses’ needs. Therefore, it can be concluded that a fair and supportive leadership style can increase trust and a sense of belonging among staff. It can also strengthen organizational commitment. As a result, it helps keep staff in the organization and supports high‑quality patient care.
