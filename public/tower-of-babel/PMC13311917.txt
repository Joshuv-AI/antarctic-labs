Student-led pathology elective expands early exposure and strengthens specialty interest at an osteopathic medical school
Authors: Nicholas Barron, Meredith K Herman, Matthew Leung, Paul Kowalski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFew medical schools, particularly those without dedicated pathology departments, offer preclinical pathology electives. To address this gap, faculty at Michigan State University College of Osteopathic Medicine and the Michigan State University College of Human Medicine-College of Osteopathic Medicine Pathology Interest Group launched a student-faculty elective in 2025. The elective comprised eight sessions: seven microscope sign-outs and one grossing workshop. Each began with one hour of independent slide preview, followed by a 2-h sign-out led by a practicing pathologist and delivered in person and via Zoom. Across approximately 30 contact hours, students engaged in microscopic and macroscopic pathology. Twenty-three learners attended at least one session. Anonymous pre- and post-class surveys assessed prior exposure, self-rated confidence, specialty interest, and perceptions of curricular adequacy. Thirteen medical students completed the precourse survey, and fourteen completed the postcourse survey; eleven responses were paired. Baseline experience was limited: 54% had never grossed a specimen or attended a sign-out, and no learner reported high confidence in histology, gross pathology, or laboratory report interpretation. Among paired respondents, mean confidence increased by +1.1 to +1.3 points on a five-point scale, with 82% improving across domains. Post-survey data showed 93% reported the elective reshaped their perception of pathology, 93% planned a pathology rotation, and all respondents recommended the course. In the absence of a pathology department, this student-led, faculty-mentored elective provided rare early exposure. Beyond measurable confidence gains, the program offers a scalable model for institutions seeking to strengthen pathology exposure and the specialty pipeline.Keywords: Career exploration, Experiential learning, Medical education, Osteopathic medical school, Pathology elective

Introduction

Pathology exposure is limited for students in Doctor of Osteopathic (DO) medical schools due to structural disadvantages inherent in their clinical training environments, which contrast with traditional allopathic medical education settings.1 These limitations are often amplified by the distributed, community-based clinical training model common across many programs accredited by the Commission on Osteopathic College Accreditation (COCA), the accrediting body for colleges of osteopathic medicine in the United States. This model can reduce consistent access to academic medical centers, in-house pathology departments, formal pathology rotations, and longitudinal mentorship opportunities.1, 2, 3 This limitation is particularly pronounced because clinical training for DO students frequently occurs in community-based medical centers (CMCs), rather than Academic Medical Centers (AMCs).1,3 This reliance on community sites for clinical rotations places DO students at a potential disadvantage regarding pathology exposure because many osteopathic schools do not operate their own affiliated hospitals, leading students to rotate through numerous community settings.1,3 The resulting variability in hospital size, faculty availability, and departmental receptiveness makes standardized pathology education difficult to achieve.1 Furthermore, a lack of dedicated pathology academic departments and faculty in these settings correlates directly with limited DO pathology exposure.1,3 Compared with allopathic peers, DO students have been found to be significantly less likely to receive formal pathology exposure through formative methods like hands-on microscope use, autopsy demonstrations, gross pathology specimen demonstrations, and case-based presentations.1,3 In addition, factors associated with a greater likelihood of DO students choosing pathology include having clinical or research opportunities in pathology, autopsy observation or participation, and involvement in a pathology interest group, while limited understanding of a pathologist's day-to-day work and fewer curricular touchpoints were identified as modifiable barriers.4 National Resident Matching Program Main Residency Match data from 2021 to 2025 show that pathology accounted for approximately 0.9%–1.3% of PGY-1 matches among U.S. Doctor of Osteopathic seniors (55–104 matches annually) and 1.1%–1.4% among U.S. MD seniors (198–263 matches annually), reflecting a slight overall increase across the period, with MD proportions remaining relatively steady and DO proportions showing more year-to-year variability.5 Recent educational interventions using structured, experiential pathology workshops have demonstrated significant gains in learners' understanding of pathology and the practicing pathologist's role, reinforcing the value of early, structured exposure that supports informed specialty exploration rather than presuming increased career interest.6 Collectively, these findings suggest that increasing early experiential exposure and strengthening mentorship access may be key levers for improving recruitment of osteopathic trainees into pathology.

The Michigan State University College of Osteopathic Medicine (MSUCOM) provides a relevant example of this structural constraint. The Michigan State University College of Osteopathic Medicine, like its allopathic counterpart at MSU, lacks an affiliated university tertiary medical center, meaning clinical education is predominantly community-based.2 MSUCOM is affiliated with 29 hospitals in its Statewide Campus System (SCS), but not all routinely provide pathology electives for rotating DO students.2 Furthermore, MSUCOM does not have a dedicated pathology department, and the few existing pathology faculty members are generally employed through the MSU Department of Physiology.2 Despite these structural limitations, prior work surveying MSU medical students suggests that DO learners at MSU demonstrate meaningful awareness of and interest in pathology, underscoring an engaged learner population and a strong foundation for targeted exposure efforts.2 Building on these findings, the current elective was designed to translate interest and awareness into sustained experiential exposure through structured sign-out sessions, mentorship, and skills-based learning within the constraints of a community-distributed training model.

To address the documented gap in early pathology exposure and the lack of dedicated institutional resources, innovative and scalable approaches are essential. At MSUCOM, pathology education is supported through alternative institutional structures, including cocurricular programming and student-led initiatives. A pathology faculty member provides integrated pathology lectures within preclerkship courses and serves as a faculty advisor and mentor to the MSU College of Human Medicine–College of Osteopathic Medicine (CHM-COM) Pathology Interest Group.

The MSU CHM-COM Pathology Interest Group is a student-led organization that brings together learners across both medical schools to supplement the formal curriculum with accessible pathology-focused programming. Through faculty mentorship, peer leadership, and cross-college collaboration, the group develops opportunities for early exposure to pathology practice and career exploration, including panels, workshops, and sign-out style learning experiences. This infrastructure created a natural platform for piloting a longitudinal elective: by using its established student community, consistent communication channels, and committed faculty mentorship, the group designed and implemented a structured pathology elective intended to provide early clinical exposure and experiential learning despite institutional and accreditation-related constraints. In 2025, this platform supported a four-month, student-led faculty-sponsored pathology elective for first- and second-year medical students at MSUCOM and was accessible across MSUCOM's distributed campus model. A total of twenty-three different students attended at least one session and program evaluation used pre- and post-course surveys to characterize elective structure and summarize early outcomes related to pathology exposure, confidence, and specialty exploration.

Materials and methods

The elective consisted of eight instructional sessions: seven microscope-based sign-out sessions and one hands-on grossing workshop, totaling approximately 30 contact hours. The elective was designed to be accessible to students across MSUCOM's distributed campus model. Faculty-led instructional sessions were conducted synchronously in person and via Zoom, allowing remote participation. First and second-year medical students at MSUCOM were eligible to participate, including dual-degree DO/PhD trainees, representing approximately 300 students per class year (approximately 600 eligible learners). Twenty-three learners attended at least one session, including a small number of non-MSUCOM participants: two MSU undergraduate students, one MSUCHM medical student, and one nonmedical MSU graduate student.

Each instructional session followed a standardized format. Prior to each session, students were provided with representative histologic slides and allotted one hour of independent review to encourage active preparation. This was followed by a 2-h faculty-led mock sign-out session focused on guided case discussion and diagnostic reasoning. Early sessions emphasized foundational pathology concepts, including cellular adaptations, inflammation, hemodynamic disorders, and neoplasia. Subsequent sessions addressed organ-based and subspecialty topics, including gastrointestinal, gynecologic, dermatopathologic, and hematopathologic disease. Sample cases integrated clinical history, gross findings, histologic features, and ancillary studies where applicable. The grossing workshop provided supervised exposure to macroscopic specimen handling, including specimen orientation, margin assessment, section submission, and dictation principles. All instruction was delivered by board-certified anatomic and clinical pathology faculty using case-based discussion, guided questioning, and interactive sign-out methodology.

To evaluate educational impact, participants were invited to complete electronically administered pre- and post-course surveys. Surveys captured learner demographics, prior pathology exposure, perceived understanding of pathology concepts, self-rated diagnostic confidence, interest in pathology as a career, and perceptions of pathology education within the institutional curriculum. Quantitative items utilized Likert-type scales, and open-ended questions elicited qualitative feedback regarding course strengths, limitations, and suggested improvements.

Survey participation was voluntary, and completion did not affect elective participation or standing. Learners could optionally provide identifying information (name and email address) solely to enable pairing of pre- and post-course responses. The precourse survey (Supplemental Material 1), distributed prior to the first instructional session, assessed learner status and baseline exposure to pathology, including prior shadowing experiences and hands-on exposure to microscopy, gross specimen examination, laboratory techniques (e.g. staining and slide preparation), and pathology sign-out. Baseline confidence in histologic and gross interpretation, understanding of pathology reports, and initial interest in pathology as a career were also assessed. Additional items captured motivations for enrollment and learner expectations for the elective.

The postcourse survey (Supplemental Material 1), administered following the final session, reassessed confidence in core skills, including microscopy, histologic interpretation, gross specimen recognition, and pathology report comprehension, to permit evaluation of change over time. Additional items assessed perceived understanding of the pathologist's diagnostic workflow, the elective's influence on career interest, and the effectiveness of key instructional components, including presession slide review, faculty-led sign-out, grossing exposure, session length, and remote participation quality. The Michigan State University College of Osteopathic Medicine-specific items evaluated perceptions of the adequacy of pathology exposure within the broader curriculum and interest in further pathology learning opportunities.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and survey responses. Pre- and post-course comparisons of confidence and career interest measures were performed to assess changes over the duration of the elective. For quantitative analyses, responses from nonmedical students were excluded, and dual-degree DO/PhD respondents were categorized as medical students. Qualitative responses were reviewed using thematic analysis to identify recurrent themes related to educational value, instructional format, and opportunities for curricular refinement.

Results

The elective was offered to approximately 600 eligible first- and second-year MSUCOM medical students across the distributed-campus model. Preclass surveys were completed by 15 learners; after excluding undergraduate respondents, 13 medical students were included in quantitative analyses (Table 1; Supplemental Material 1). Postclass surveys were completed by 16 learners; after excluding undergraduate respondents, 14 medical students were included. Eleven medical students completed both surveys and comprised the paired analytic sample used for pre–post comparisons (Table 2; Fig. 1). Baseline exposure and experience varied, but experiential contact with diagnostic pathology was limited for many participants (Table 1; Supplemental Material 1). Although most learners reported at least some prior exposure to microscope slide analysis and prior experience using a microscope, fewer reported prior hands-on gross specimen examination or prior sign-out exposure. Prior exposure to clinical and research laboratory settings, experience performing laboratory techniques, and familiarity with common pathology laboratory methods ranged from low to moderate. Prior pathology-focused courses or electives were reported by a minority. Before the elective, learners reported variable interest in pathology and likelihood of considering pathology as a career choice, and MSUCOM respondents reported a range of satisfaction with pathology exposure in the MSUCOM curriculum to date (Supplemental Material 1). Learners most commonly reported using coursepack notes, Pathoma, histology laboratory notes, and First Aid to study pathology, and most used third-party resources at least occasionally (Supplemental Material 1).

Among paired respondents, confidence increased across all three competency domains assessed (Fig. 1; Table 2). Statistically significant pre–post improvements were observed for interpreting basic histology, recognizing gross pathology findings, and understanding laboratory reports and pathology test results.

Postclass survey responses further supported educational value and changes in learner perceptions and intentions (Table 3; Supplemental Material 1). All respondents agreed or strongly agreed that pathology plays a critical role in patient care. Learners reported improved postcourse confidence using a microscope, identifying key histologic features, recognizing gross pathology findings, and understanding laboratory reports. The elective was rated as very to extremely effective in helping learners understand how a pathologist thinks and works, and both microscope slide review and mock gross specimen analysis were commonly rated as very to extremely effective instructional components. Faculty-led sessions were consistently rated as very to extremely engaging; most learners reported that slide preview time was sufficient and that the duration of sign-out sessions was appropriate (Supplemental Material 1).

Course evaluation items indicated strong overall acceptability (Supplemental Material 1). Most respondents reported that the elective met or exceeded expectations and would recommend it to other students, and most learners felt the group size was “just right.” Reported preferred learning formats commonly included microscope-based sign-out and the grossing workshop.

Open-ended responses were reviewed thematically. Precourse narratives most commonly described seeking early experiential exposure to diagnostic reasoning, mentorship, and skill-building, with concerns primarily related to scheduling and logistics. Postcourse free-text feedback commonly requested additional sessions, including more grossing and/or autopsy exposure, additional hands-on opportunities, and topic expansion (e.g. hematopathology/oncology, cardiovascular pathology). Respondents who participated remotely generally noted that sessions were feasible to follow, although several commented that in-person participation felt more immersive. MSUCOM-only free-text suggestions emphasized increased access to pathology-related experiences and mentorship, as well as greater integration of pathology within the MSUCOM curriculum.

Discussion

Herein, we discuss a novel initiative to address preclinical pathology exposure by way of a student-led, pathologist-facilitated elective. Although conducted in a small cohort, this approach showed meaningful educational gains in a setting where pathology exposure is variable. These findings are particularly salient for osteopathic medical education, where clinical training is often distributed across community-based sites that lack robust pathology infrastructure and on-site sign-out experiences. Cross-sectional analyses highlight wide variability in faculty availability and curricular exposure, with some schools lacking pathologists entirely or offering minimal clinical pathology integration.1 In such environments, innovative, low-barrier strategies, such as student-led electives, structured hybrid models, and targeted experiential sessions, are not optional but essential for providing authentic exposure to pathology, bridging the gap between foundational content and clinical practice, and ensuring learners appreciate the specialty's role in patient care.2

A core strength of this elective was its emphasis on experiential learning, which participants consistently rated as highly engaging and effective. Live sign-out sessions and microscope review were identified as the most valuable learning formats, reinforcing the importance of direct faculty interaction and structured mentorship. These results align with broader evidence that early, authentic experiences are critical for building awareness, diagnostic reasoning, and informed specialty exploration.3,7 Importantly, these benefits extend beyond students considering pathology as a career, as improved confidence in interpreting reports and recognizing pathologic patterns can enhance clinician–pathologist collaboration and clinical decision-making across disciplines.

Career-interest outcomes, while reflecting a self-selected population, suggest that high-quality exposure can help students make informed decisions. Historically, pathology applicants across medical schools have remained at approximately 1%, likely attributed to limited early exposure opportunities. Although osteopathic schools may face distinct structural barriers to pathology exposure, the persistently small proportion of both MD and DO graduates entering pathology suggests that pipeline development remains an important priority across undergraduate medical education. Postelective, most learners remained highly interested in pathology, while some recalibrated their interest after gaining realistic insights into daily workflow and practice scope. This outcome is desirable as structured exposure should guide informed choice, not merely reinforce initial enthusiasm.3,7 The elective's hybrid design underscores the potential for scalable, innovative approaches in resource-limited settings. Remote participation was feasible and generally effective, though learners emphasized that in-person, hands-on sessions remain superior for microscope skill development. This suggests that hybrid models can expand access and inclusivity, but must be thoughtfully structured to preserve high-yield experiential learning.8, 9, 10, 11

The student-led structure further demonstrates how institutions can maximize impact with limited faculty resources, especially when a pathology interest group is established with support from the university and medical school, along with a shared community of interested-driven students. By using student leadership, programs can reduce logistical barriers, maintain continuity across academic years, and create a peer-driven pipeline for mentorship and recruitment. Structured curricula, asynchronous content, and platforms such as PathElective offer additional strategies to extend pathology education in schools without large departmental infrastructure.1,3,4,8,11,12 Beyond institution-specific programming, DO trainees can also access virtual communities and learning ecosystems that broaden exposure independent of local departmental resources, including virtual pathology student interest group (PathSIG) webinars and events, as well as broader engagement through social media communities such as #PathTwitter, which can facilitate mentorship connections and awareness of opportunities.3,4,8, 9, 10, 11 Collectively, these complementary pathways support recruitment of osteopathic trainees into pathology by expanding access to mentors, educational content, and authentic perspectives on the specialty.3,7 Providing early, structured, and authentic exposure to pathology through hands-on microscopy, grossing, and sign-out experiences can help students gain a deeper understanding of the specialty and its role in patient care. Such experiences empower learners to make informed decisions about their career paths, regardless of the resources available at their home institutions.13,14 Limitations include the single-institution design, small sample size, self-reported outcomes, and a subset of paired respondents (N = 11). Subgroup analyses by learner type and baseline pathology interest were considered but not performed because the sample sizes were too small for meaningful comparison. Objective assessments of skills and long-term follow-up were not included, and the reliance on a single engaged faculty pathologist may limit reproducibility.

Future directions should focus on strengthening scalability and rigor, including pre/post objective assessments, longitudinal follow-up, additional grossing and autopsy sessions, structured microscope-skills teaching, and integration into systems-based curricula. Investment in digital pathology and hybrid learning infrastructure can mitigate resource constraints while preserving essential hands-on experiences.1,8, 9, 10, 11 Structured pathology electives provide medical students with early, authentic exposure to the specialty, including hands-on microscopy, gross specimen review, and sign-out experiences. These opportunities enhance understanding of pathology's central role in patient care, strengthen diagnostic reasoning, and allow learners to evaluate the field based on direct experience. By offering such immersive experiences, medical schools can empower students to make informed decisions about pathology as a potential career path, regardless of the institutional resources available at their home campus. Early and meaningful exposure is particularly valuable in osteopathic programs, where clinical training is often distributed across community sites with variable access to pathology faculty and departmental infrastructure.
