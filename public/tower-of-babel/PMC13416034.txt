Constraining near-term projections of the South Asian high and Afro-Asian summer monsoon rainfall
Authors: Dapeng Zhang, Yanyan Huang, Botao Zhou, Qiaohong Sun, Huijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable projections of Afro-Asian summer monsoon precipitation (AfroASMP) are critical for sustaining regional development and managing climate risks for billions of people. The complex historical variability of AfroASMP highlights the central role of dynamical circulations, alongside the favorable thermodynamic conditions associated with the centurial global warming. Here, we identify that up to 90% of the variance in AfroASMP over past six decades is dynamically dominated by the South Asian High (SAH), an extensive upper-tropospheric mode spanning West Africa to the western Pacific. Using a multi-model weighting scheme, the constrained near-term SAH projects a larger amplitude than raw simulations across three emission scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5). SAH-based constraints indicate a reduction in AfroASMP of 10–50 mm, with the largest decrease in abnormally wet conditions over Africa (62%) under SSP5-8.5, and increases in dry conditions over Africa and North China (>10%). The framework offers physically grounded evidence for climate adaptation and policy decisions.

Introduction

Since the Industrial Revolution, rapid environmental and socio-economic transformations have intensified global challenges related to water scarcity, ecological degradation, and social inequality—issues particularly acute across the Afro-Asian monsoon region. This region receives nearly 80% of its annual rainfall during the summer monsoon, which underpins agriculture, water security, and the livelihoods of billions1. Reliable projections of Afro-Asian summer monsoon precipitation (AfroASMP) are therefore critical for sustaining regional development and managing future climate risks.

Extensive efforts have been made to project monsoon precipitation, including direct simulations from CMIP5 and CMIP6 models2. However, such projections remain highly uncertain due to differences in socioeconomic pathways, model structures, and stochastic internal variability3–5. Observational constraint techniques have been developed to reduce this uncertainty by leveraging stable historical relationships between observed variables and projected targets6–9. Factors such as sea surface temperatures in the cold tongue region10,11, interhemispheric thermal contrast6,12, and global mean temperature changes13 have been used to constrain long-term monsoon projections. Yet, these factors explain less than 40% of observed decadal rainfall variability in most regions (Supplementary Fig. S1) and are less effective for near-term projections, where internal variability dominates14–16. This underscores the need to identify internal variability modes that exert strong and dynamically robust control over monsoon rainfall.

Circulation-based constraints have proven effective in related contexts17–21. For example, Wang et al.20 demonstrated that sea-level pressure (SLP) can serve as a reliable predictor of Tibetan Plateau rainfall, exploiting stable relationships between SLP anomalies and precipitation responses in historical simulations. He et al.21 used the relationship between low-level circulation and monsoon in past period to predict future changes of South Asian rainfall. However, SLP and 850hPa winds reflects lower-tropospheric circulation patterns, which may not always capture the large-scale thermodynamic and dynamical drivers of monsoon rainfall. In contrast, upper-tropospheric circulation signals often provide more spatially coherent and dynamically consistent indicators of climatic variability, given their strong links to diabatic heating, planetary wave patterns, and basin-wide convection22–25. Among these, the South Asian High (SAH) stands out as the dominant upper-tropospheric mode in boreal summer, extending from West Africa to the western Pacific. Its variability exerts broad control over vertical motion, moisture transport, and rainfall across the entire Afro-Asian monsoon domain, and has been shown to explain a greater fraction of decadal rainfall changes than previously used constraint factors26–35. These physical characteristics make the SAH a promising basis for constraining near-term AfroASMP projections.

Here, we first quantify the physical influence of the SAH on AfroASMP using historical observations and multi-model simulations. We then apply a performance-based weighting scheme to refine near-term SAH projections and translate these into constrained rainfall estimates for each monsoon subregion, based on their established physical relationships. This approach explicitly incorporates the dominant mode of internal variability into near-term projections—an element often overlooked in previous constraints. The resulting projections diverge sharply from the uniformly wetter future suggested by raw CMIP6 outputs, instead revealing substantial regional contrasts, including heightened drying risks over North China, India, and North Africa. By integrating physically grounded variability modes into climate projections, this work provides more actionable information for water resource management and adaptation planning under a changing climate.

Results

A robust historical link between the South Asian High and AfroASMP

As shown in Fig. 1, SAH is a large-scale anticyclone in the upper troposphere covering the whole monsoon region. SAH dynamically dominates nearly 50–80% of the variance of observed AfroASMP (Supplementary Fig. S2a), which is larger than other monsoon circulations (Supplementary Fig. S2b–g). Specifically, when the SAH changes are enhanced by 1 standard deviation, ascending motion will prevail over North China (NC), North India (Ind), and North Africa (Afo), and the corresponding linear precipitation will increase by approximately 30–40 mm, above 70 mm, and 30–50 mm, respectively. Conversely, rainfall is expected to reduce by around 40–60 mm over the Yangtze River valley (YRV) and 10–20 mm over the northern Tibetan Plateau (NTP) due to the descending motion (Fig. 1). In addition to the linear influence, the SAH–monsoon linkage exhibits pronounced nonlinear behavior (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Significant nonlinear responses are detected over the YRV, Ind, and Afo, indicating regionally asymmetric precipitation sensitivities under extreme SAH states. Specifically, precipitation responses to SAH variability weaken over the YRV and Ind but intensify over Afo when the SAH becomes extreme. Furthermore, the relationship between the SAH and AfroASMP is consistently reproduced in climate models. Across 30 CMIP6 models, the leading mode of AfroASMP explains approximately 30% of the inter-model variance in both historical and future simulations under different emission scenarios, showing a coherent increase across the core monsoon regions (Supplementary Fig. S4a–d). This spread is closely linked to variations in the simulated SAH across models, in both historical and future periods (Supplementary Fig. S4a1–d1). The strong modeled association between the SAH and AfroASMP mirrors the observed relationship, suggesting that the SAH can serve as an effective emergent constraint for reducing uncertainty in future monsoon projections.

Given the strong influence of the SAH on monsoon rainfall, we assess the role of SAH in modulating future precipitation distributions (Fig. 2b). Probability density functions of the raw projected precipitation and that without SAH influence are analyzed (see methods). The raw projections (dashed curves), which include the SAH signal, suggest a future shift towards wetter conditions. In contrast, when the SAH contribution is removed, the curves shift to the left (solid curves). These results indicate that a stronger SAH favors enhanced monsoon precipitation.

Moreover, the distribution of precipitation projections narrows when the SAH signal is excluded, suggesting that SAH contributes substantially to the spread in future rainfall estimates. Quantitatively, accounting for SAH influence reduces projection uncertainty by more than 20% across all three scenarios, with uncertainty measured as the standard deviation of projected precipitation. This relationship holds across five monsoon sub-regions, though the magnitude of uncertainty reduction varies by region (Supplementary Fig. S6).

To better understand the source of projection uncertainty, we further evaluate SAH simulations in CMIP6 models (Fig. 2c). During the historical period (1950–2014), modeled SAH intensity, represented by eddy geopotential height (SAHI, see “Methods”), exhibits large inter-model spread relative to observations, and the multi-model ensemble mean (MME) systematically underestimates the observed amplitude. Future SAH projections (2015–2045) show high variability with only a modest trend. Substantial disagreement across models and scenarios persists, reinforcing the need to correct or constrain SAH simulations in order to improve confidence in AfroASMP projections.

Physically constrained AfroASMP projections based on the South Asian High

To reduce model-related uncertainty in projecting the SAH, we applied a multi-model weighting scheme35 that accounts for both model performance and independence in simulating the historical SAH. Models were assigned differential weights based on their ability to reproduce observed SAH characteristics, and these weights were used to constrain future SAH projections derived from CMIP6 simulations (see Methods for details). Similar weighting approaches have been successfully applied in previous studies to constrain projections of key climate variables, including global temperature and sea ice extent7,36,37.

Based on individual model performance in simulating the SAH and their mutual independence, we derived a weighting scheme (see “Methods” for details), which was first evaluated using historical SAH simulations from CMIP6 models. Climatologically, the scheme yielded a modest improvement in root-mean-square error (RMSE), with slightly higher performance ratios for individual models relative to the weighted multi-model estimate than to the unweighted ensemble (Fig. 3a). By contrast, the weighting scheme showed clear advantages in capturing the long-term behavior of the SAH. It substantially reduced the RMSE of both the trend and the interdecadal variability amplitude (Fig. 3b, c), with the weighted-to-unweighted ensemble ratios falling below 1.0—indicating enhanced fidelity in representing temporal evolution. Additionally, the simulated SAH time series from the weighted multi-model ensemble (MME), the five highest-skill models, and the five lowest-skill models are compared. The weighted MME and the highest-skill models closely reproduce the observed SAH variability in ERA5 (Supplementary Fig. S7a), whereas the lowest-skill models show substantial deviations. These performance differences largely arise from inter-model contrasts in the representation of upper-tropospheric thermal structure over the SAH region (Supplementary Fig. S7b). Because the strength and position of the SAH are tightly linked to the vertical integration of atmospheric temperature38,39, biases in simulated thermal conditions directly translate into errors in SAH intensity. These results support the application of the weighting scheme in constraining near-term SAH projections for the twenty-first century.

Figure 3d compares the weighted and unweighted projections of SAH changes under future global warming. The unweighted ensemble suggests an increased likelihood of the SAH entering a negative phase in the near term, particularly under higher emission scenarios (light shading). The weighted projections exhibit a similar distribution but with enhanced amplitude (dark shading), which is critical for capturing the associated changes in Afro-Asian monsoon precipitation.

To further assess the robustness of the weighting scheme, we employed a perfect-model approach. In this framework, simulations from each model are alternately treated as the “truth”, and the remaining 29 models are corrected accordingly. The performance of the weighting scheme is then evaluated based on its ability to reduce errors relative to this reference (see “Methods”). Results show that the bias of the weighted SAH relative to the assumed “truth” is approximately half that of the unweighted estimates (Supplementary Fig. S8), demonstrating the robustness and effectiveness of the approach. Given its ability to improve both the trend and amplitude of SAH simulations, the weighted projection is adopted to constrain future AfroASMP.

Building on the physical linkage between the SAH and monsoon rainfall, we quantified the SAH’s contribution to AfroASMP by reconstructing rainfall at each grid point using a linear regression approach. The reconstructed AfroASMP explains more than 50% of the observed variance across much of the Afro-Asian monsoon region (Fig. 4a), with explained variance exceeding 90% in parts of Ind and NTP. In addition, the reconstructed precipitation indices for the five monsoon sub-regions exhibit significant correlations with observations (Blue lines in supplementary Fig. S9). Constrained projections of AfroASMP, based on the weighted SAH, are shown in Fig. 4b–f (see “Methods” for details on the constraint approach). In the original CMIP6 projections (represented by dots), AfroASMP increases markedly across the entire monsoon region under all three emission scenarios, with nearly all model outputs indicating positive anomalies. In contrast, the constrained projections reveal notably reduced rainfall estimates across all regions and scenarios relative to the original CMIP6 ensemble. Across most monsoon regions, projected precipitation changes remain within ±20 mm (boxes in Fig. 4b–f), indicating a generally limited increase in flood risk. A clear regional contrast emerges. In NC and Afo, precipitation consistently decreases under all three emission scenarios (Fig. 4b, f), suggesting a continuation of historically dry conditions over the next three decades. In contrast, Ind and the NTP exhibit increasing precipitation, with the lightly magnitude of wetting amplifying from low- to high-emission scenarios. The YRV shows comparatively weak responses, with only modest changes across scenarios (Fig. 4c). Overall, SAH-constrained AfroASMP projections highlight robust regional differentiation, effectively moderating the potentially exaggerated flood risks implied by raw CMIP6 simulations.

To quantify future changes in AfroASMP, we applied a probability density function (PDF) analysis. Across the five monsoon sub-regions, the SAH-constrained projections indicate a reduction in mean precipitation of approximately 10–50 mm (Fig. 5), with the smallest decrease over the YRV ( ~ 10 mm) and the largest over Ind (~50 mm). These precipitation responses are dynamically consistent with SAH-induced circulation adjustments in the lower troposphere. Relative to the unweighted projections, the weighted 850-hPa winds exhibit anomalous northerlies over East China, northerly flow over northern India, and easterly anomalies over North Africa (Supplementary Fig. 10). We further assessed the probabilities of abnormally high and low precipitation events, defined as anomalies exceeding one standard deviation from the historical observation. The frequency (Numbers in Fig. 5) and intensity (dots in Fig. 5) of these events can generally characterize potential flood and drought risks (see Methods for measurement of abnormal changes in precipitation). In the unconstrained projections, the probability of abnormally high precipitation (AHP) even can exceed 50% in most monsoon region, while the probability of abnormally low precipitation (ALP) falls below 5%. After applying the SAH-based constraint, the probability and intensity of AHP decrease by approximately 19–62% and 10–50 mm, respectively, across most regions and scenarios. In contrast, the probability of ALP increases by up to 16%, while changes in its intensity remain relatively modest.

Marked regional contrasts emerge in the constrained projections. NC, Ind, Afo, and NTP exhibit consistent reductions of more than 30% in frequency of AHP, accompanied by intensity decreases of up to 50 mm across all three scenarios (Fig. 5a, c, d, e). By comparison, the YRV shows less decrease in frequency and intensity (Fig. 5b). For ALP, pronounced increases greater than 10% are confined to NC and Afo. Overall, under the low-emission pathway, projected changes in monsoon precipitation remain modest, with probabilities of both abnormally wet and dry events generally below 10% (Fig. 5a1–e1). However, under intermediate- and high-emission scenarios, NC, and Afo will face increased risks of ALP (Fig. 5a2, a3, d2, d3), indicating drier conditions, while the NTP tend toward wetter extremes (Fig. 5e2, e3).

Discussion

This study focuses on future projections of AfroASMP constrained by the SAH under three emission scenarios. While our constraint-based approach provides improved estimates of regional precipitation risk, the underlying physical mechanisms linking the SAH and monsoon rainfall in the near term remain insufficiently understood.

Previous studies have shown that the future evolution of the SAH and monsoon precipitation is influenced by a range of interacting factors, including aerosol forcing, internal variability, and greenhouse gas emissions31,32,38,40,41. In particular, decadal variations in the SAH are strongly modulated by aerosol emissions and internal climate variability, with greenhouse gases playing a comparatively minor role31,38. Over near-term timescales, internal variability is likely to dominate decadal climate fluctuations42–44. Among the key drivers, the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO) has been shown to influence the SAH via air–sea interactions spanning the North Pacific, Indian Ocean, and North Atlantic45. Given this, our constrained assessments of SAH-related monsoon precipitation may be partially modulated by IPO dynamics, which remain challenging to predict. Further investigation is needed to unravel the mechanisms through which internal variability—and the IPO in particular—shapes SAH variability and its downstream impacts on regional precipitation. Addressing these uncertainties will be essential for improving near-term projections of monsoon hydroclimate and for enhancing the reliability of constraint-based approaches.

Given that the SAH–monsoon precipitation relationship is predominantly governed by its linear component, a comparison between quadratic and linear regression methods is presented in Supplementary Fig. S9. The precipitation reconstructed using the quadratic regression exhibits improved agreement with observations relative to the linear approach. While these improvements are modest over NC, YRV, and NTP, they are more pronounced over Ind and Afo, particularly for anomalous values (Supplementary Fig. S9). This is consistent with the nonlinear features identified in Supplementary Fig. S3. In future projections, the quadratic regression–based constraint yields slightly lower probabilities of AHP and higher probabilities of ALP over the NTP and Ind, with the opposite tendency over NC and Afo (Supplementary Fig. S11). However, these differences remain relatively small across most regions and become notable only over Africa, where the maximum change reaches approximately 12%. Overall, although the linear relationship accounts for the dominant contribution, the influence of nonlinear processes is non-negligible and may play a regionally important role.

Additionally, the perfect-model framework adopted in this study treats individual model simulations as “truth,” and therefore does not explicitly account for systematic discrepancies between simulations and historical observations. As a result, it may not fully represent real-world conditions and is inherently subject to shared structural biases across models, as well as the assumption that model errors remain stationary over time. To minimize the influence of these limitations, the weighting scheme adopted in this study explicitly accounts for model independence (Eq. 2), thereby reducing the impact of closely related models. In addition, the evaluation results indicate that the CMIP6 models have a certain degree of persistence in their simulation regarding the SAH from historical to projections (Supplementary Fig. S12). Nevertheless, even with these considerations, shared structural biases across models cannot be fully eliminated. Furthermore, the constrained projections rely on the assumption that model errors remain approximately stationary between the historical and future periods. Any substantial changes in error characteristics under future climate conditions may therefore limit the applicability of the weighting framework.

Our findings highlight the potential of SAH-based constraints to improve the reliability of regional monsoon precipitation projections. By reducing uncertainties in both mean and abnormal precipitation, particularly in regions like India, North China, and North Africa, this approach helps correct possible overestimations of flood risk in raw model outputs. The constrained projections also reveal distinct regional responses to different emission scenarios, with low-emission pathways mitigating both dry and wet extremes. These results underscore the added value of circulation-based constraints in supporting more targeted climate adaptation strategies in monsoon-affected regions.

This study focuses on the near-term period of 2015–2045, during which internal climate variability exerts a dominant influence on the climate system. This is consistent with our objective of constraining near-term precipitation changes through the SAH–precipitation relationship from the perspective of internal variability. The findings contribute to a deeper understanding of precipitation variations in the Asian‑African monsoon region and provide a physically grounded basis for water resource management and climate adaptation planning. Nonetheless, because this analysis is limited to the next three decades, its implications for long-term responses to emission mitigation remain constrained, motivating further investigation in future work.

Data and CMIP6 models

The monthly geopotential height and precipitation are derived from the ERA546 and CRU47 datasets. Thirty CMIP6 models are also used in this study48,49, the details of which are provided in Supplementary Table S1. These models provide complete simulations for the historical (1950–2014) and ScenarioMIP (2015–2045) experiments of CMIP6. Only the first realization for each model and each scenario was used. The Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) low (SSP1-2.6), intermediate (SSP2-4.5), and high (SSP5-8.5) radiative forcing scenarios were selected. The globally averaged radiative forcing of SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 by 2100 is approximately 2.6 W/m2, 4.5 W/m2, and 8.5 W/m2, respectively. All the datasets were interpolated to global 2.0° × 2.0° grids.

Methods

Definition of the SAH, monsoon regions, and their relationship

Following Zhang et al.25, the summer (June–July–August) SAH in this study is defined as the averaged eddy geopotential height over the SAH region (15°–45°N, 40°–110°E). The eddy geopotential height (H’) is obtained by subtracting the mean geopotential height over the region of 0°–40°N and 0°–360° from the geopotential height at each grid point. This H’ is more suitable to reflect the decadal variations of SAH and its influence on the climate25,29,31.

Only the decadal variations are the focus in this study, and thus all the data were obtained via the 9-year running-average method. Given that the SAH-monsoon coupling encompasses both the linear and nonlinear dynamics, a quadratic regression method is employed to characterize the relationship between SAH and AfroASMP. The Monte Carlo method represents a robust statistical framework for quantifying the significance of interdecadal signals within climate research. Its utility lies in its capacity to probabilistically simulate complex statistical distributions, making it optimally suited for evaluating the interdecadal SAH–monsoon relationship given the inherent constraints of the available sample size. While the efficacy of this approach can be sensitive to data non-stationarity and the underlying noise structure, these factors are mitigated by the documented stability of the SAH–AfroASMP linkage identified in this study (Supplementary Fig. S13). Consequently, we performed a Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations to robustly constrain the statistical significance of our findings.

Multi-model weighting scheme

The original projection of CMIP6 models in simulating the SAH has large uncertainty, greatly reducing the reliability of the SAH projection. Thus, this study applied a newly developed multi-model weighting scheme to post-induce the original CMIP6 projection, reducing the deviation and uncertainty. This method was developed by Knutti et al.36, based on the research of Sanderson et al.52–54. It can fully consider the performance of each CMIP6 model and the independence between them in simulating the SAH, and thus assign unequal weights to each model accordingly. The better the performance and independence of a model, the greater the corresponding weight. Based on the different weights of these models, the deviations of historical simulations from observation can be effectively corrected. Finally, a new and reliable projection is obtained by applying the weights of each model to their respective projection in the future. This method has been used to constrain many climate variabilities, such as global temperature, sea ice, newly formed sea ice, and so on7,36,37.

The weight of each model is the product of two weights36. One is the skill weight, which is determined by the performance of model simulations. The other is the independence skill, determined by the similarity of a model to other models. Here, we use a distance measurement to evaluate the deviation of a model from observation and other models, which is the root-mean-square error (RMSE) method7. Thus, the smaller the distances are, the larger the weights of the skill or independence will be. This perfect model test is used in two places in this paper. Measurement of abnormal changes in precipitation

In this study, we adopt a ± 1 standard deviation threshold to characterize anomalously high and low precipitation years. This approach is widely used in climate variability studies to identify substantial departures from the climatological mean. It should be noted that a ± 1 standard deviation threshold does not strictly represent extreme precipitation events, which are commonly defined using percentile-based thresholds (e.g., 90th or 95th percentiles).

However, the present analysis focuses on summer precipitation derived from monthly data, resulting in a relatively limited sample size (65 years). Under such conditions, high-percentile thresholds may lead to statistically unstable estimates. In contrast, the ±1 standard deviation threshold provides a more robust metric for identifying significant anomalies. Moreover, in monsoon regions, the 90th percentile threshold is often comparable in magnitude to one standard deviation (Supplementary Fig. S16), supporting its use as a proxy for extreme variability. Therefore, the ±1 standard deviation threshold offers a practical and statistically robust measure to capture wet and dry anomalies in this study, while acknowledging its limitations in representing true extremes.
