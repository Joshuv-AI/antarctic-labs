System-bath model for quantum chemistry
Dmitry S. Golubev 1, Reza G. Shirazi 1, Vladimir V. Rybkin 1, Benedikt M. Schoenauer 1, Peter Schmitteckert 1, Michael Marthaler 1

Abstract
We propose an approximate mapping of a molecular Hamiltonian to a Hamiltonian of qubits, which allows for high accuracy quantum chemistry calculations of vertical excitation energies of some molecules. The mapping is based on separating of a very small active space of only two orbitals and on modeling the electronic excitations in the remaining orbitals by a set of qubits or, equivalently, by a set of oscillators. This approach is inspired by the Random Phase Approximation (RPA), in which the excitations of electron gas are described by bosonic degrees of freedom. As a result, the Hamiltonian of the molecule is reduced to that of a system-bath model. The ”system” part of the Hamiltonian describes the two molecular orbitals – the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) – which are populated by two electrons. Two qubits are sufficient to encode the Hamiltonian of such a system. The ”bath” consists of oscillators or, equivalently, of two level systems with each of them corresponding to an electron excitation from a doubly occupied orbital below the Fermi level to an empty orbital above the Fermi level. We hope that this mapping can inspire new approaches and algorithms aimed at calculating excitation energies of molecules on near term quantum computers.

This reformulation directly addresses two major obstacles that arise when attempting to run quantum chemistry on quantum hardware.
First, the molecular electronic Hamiltonian is dominated by fermionic two-body interactions with four orbital indices,
leading after fermion-to-qubit mappings to very large numbers of Pauli terms and deep circuits.
Second, small active-space models often neglect (or only partially capture) dynamical correlation effects due to the remaining orbitals,
even though such effects can be essential for quantitative description of excitation energies and singlet–triplet splittings.

At the same time, a large body of work in quantum algorithms and quantum simulation focuses on efficient gate-based treatments of
spin–boson-type models and closely related system–bath Hamiltonians.
Our approach leverages this situation: it maps a relevant quantum-chemistry target (low-lying excitation energies of molecules) onto a Hamiltonian family

Our model is based on separating the molecular orbital space into a minimal “system” subspace and a large “environment” subspace.

II
Hamiltonian in simple terms

The system-bath Hamiltonian resulting from the mapping of a molecular Hamiltonian is
rather complicated, it will be presented in its complete form in Sec.
IV
.
In this section we briefly describe its structure
and its derivation in simple terms.

The starting point of our construction is the standard second-quantized molecular Hamiltonian,
which contains one-electron terms and two-electron repulsion integrals with four orbital indices.
Direct fermion-to-qubit mappings for such a Hamiltonian typically generate a large number of Pauli terms and deep circuits.

To arrive at a more hardware-friendly structure, we partition the orbital space into a minimal “system” subspace and an “environment” subspace.
In this work the system consists of the HOMO and LUMO orbitals populated by two electrons (indices
1,2
),
while all remaining orbitals form the environment.
This yields the exact decomposition

the environment Hamiltonian [Eq. (

and

the interaction [Eqs. (
15

)].

Averaging the interaction over the environment occupations (Hartree–Fock reference for the environment) produces an effective system Hamiltonian

(4)

[Eq. (
23

\tilde{V}
via

III.1
Hamiltonian of a molecule

The non-relativistic Hamiltonian of a molecule has the following general form

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\sigma}\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\;\Psi_{\sigma}^{\dagger}({\bm{r}})\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}}{2m}+U_{\rm nucl}({\bm{r}})\right]\Psi_{\sigma}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}=t_{kk}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\rm db}}(2h_{kk\alpha\alpha}-h_{k\alpha k\alpha}).

(39)

This definition applies to both doubly occupied and empty orbitals. The energy difference

\Delta_{m\alpha}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{m\alpha}=\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{\alpha}+U_{m}+U_{\alpha}-2J_{m\alpha}+K_{m\alpha}.

(40)

The frequencies
\hbar\omega_{m\alpha}
and the level splittings
\Delta_{m\alpha}
for
the transitions involving HOMO/LUMO orbitals, shown by vertical arrows in the right side of both panels
of Fig.
1
, are given in the Appendix, see Eqs. (
101
,
102
,
104
,
105
)
for the molecules with the singlet ground state of the system Hamiltonian and Eqs. (
) for the molecules with
the triplet ground state of
H_{\rm sys}
.
To obtain the frequencies of the normal modes
\Omega_{m\alpha}
, one needs to diagonalize the matrix
\hat{M}
, which
is derived from the Hamiltonian of the coupled bath oscillators and fully determines the properties
of the bath in the RPA approximation.
For a molecule with the singlet ground state of the system Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sys}
this matrix
has the following matrix elements

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{m\alpha,n\beta}=\omega^{2}_{m\alpha}\delta_{m\alpha,n\beta}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{4\sqrt{\omega_{m\alpha}\omega_{n\beta}}}{\hbar}r_{m\alpha}h_{m\alpha,n\beta}r_{n\beta}[1-\delta_{m\alpha,n\beta}].

(41)

Here the parameters
r_{m\alpha}
are defined as

\displaystyle r_{m\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{m\alpha}^{2}+K_{m\alpha}^{2}}+\Delta_{m\alpha}-K_{m\alpha}}{\sqrt{K_{m\alpha}^{2}+\left(\sqrt{\Delta_{m\alpha}^{2}+K_{m\alpha}^{2}}+\Delta_{m\alpha}\right)^{2}}}.

(42)

For molecules with the triplet ground state of
H_{\rm sys}
the last term of the matrix (
41
), describing
the potential energy of the interacting environment oscillators, should be modified in accordance with the form of
the Hamiltonian (
139
) given in Appendix
D.2
.
The normal mode frequencies
\Omega_{m\alpha}
are obtained from the equation

\displaystyle\det[\hat{M}_{m\alpha,n\beta}-\Omega^{2}\delta_{m\alpha,n\beta}]=0.

(43)

Each pair of indexes
m\alpha
or
n\beta
in Eqs. (
41
,
43
) should be viewed as a single index.
Thus, the matrix
\hat{M}
has the dimensions
N_{\rm RPA}\times N_{\rm RPA}
, where the total number of
the RPA oscillators in the environment,
N_{\rm RPA}
, equals to the number of possible pairs
m\alpha
.
For a molecule with the singlet ground state of
H_{\rm sys}
this number is given by

\displaystyle N_{\rm RPA}=(N_{\rm db}+1)\times(N_{\rm empt}+1)-1,

(44)

and for a molecule with the triplet ground state of the system Hamiltonian the total size of the environment is

\displaystyle N_{\rm RPA}=(N_{\rm db}+2)\times(N_{\rm empt}+2)-4.

(45)

Here, as before,
N_{\rm db}
is the number of the doubly occupied orbitals,
N_{\rm empt}
is the number of the empty orbitals
so that the total number of orbitals is
N=N_{\rm db}+N_{\rm empt}+2
with the number 2 accounting for the HOMO/LUMO orbitals.
The physical meaning of the frequencies
\Omega_{m\alpha}
is the following:
\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}

are approximately equal to the vertical excitation energies of the singlet states of the molecule if it
has the singlet ground state, and
\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}
are
the vertical excitation energies of the triplet states if the molecule has the triplet ground state.

Finally, the part of the Hamiltonian responsible for the interaction between the HOMO/LUMO orbitals and the environment (
15
)
can be approximated as

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha}(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}+b_{m\alpha})\bigg[g_{12}^{m\alpha}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

(46)

\displaystyle+\,g_{11}^{m\alpha}+g_{22}^{m\alpha}+\frac{g_{11}^{m\alpha}-g_{22}^{m\alpha}}{2}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{q=1}^{2}\sum_{m\alpha}\bigg[\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q1}^{m\alpha}+g_{2q}^{n\beta}g_{q2}^{n\beta}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q2}^{m\alpha}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q1}^{m\alpha}-g_{2q}^{m\alpha}g_{q2}^{m\alpha}}{2\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg].

The coupling constants between the system and the environment are

\displaystyle g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{n\beta}=\sum_{m\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{2\omega_{m\alpha}}{\Omega_{n\beta}}}h_{p_{1}p_{2}m\alpha}r_{m\alpha}\hat{S}_{m\alpha,n\beta},

(47)

where the matrix with the elements
\hat{S}_{m\alpha,n\beta}
is composed of the column eigenvectors of the matrix
\hat{M}
(
41
).
The physical meaning of various interaction terms in Eq. (
46
) is the following:
the terms containing
g_{12}^{m\alpha}
describe the fluctuations of the hopping amplitudes between the HOMO/LUMO orbitals,
which may be approximately expressed as
\tilde{t}_{1,2}+g_{12}^{m\alpha}(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}+b_{m\alpha})
;
likewise the terms containing
g_{11}^{m\alpha}-g_{22}^{m\alpha}
describe the fluctuations of the energy splitting
between the HOMO/LUMO orbitals, which may be expressed as
\Delta_{12}-(g_{11}^{m\alpha}-g_{22}^{m\alpha})(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}+b_{m\alpha})
;
and, finally, the last three lines of the interaction Hamiltonian (
46
) constitute the counter-term, which restores
the normal ordering of the fermionic operators in the Lamb shift, see Appendix
F

for details.

From the Hamiltonian (
34
) we obtain the ground state energy of the environment in the form

\displaystyle E_{\rm gr}^{\rm env}=E_{\rm HF}^{\rm env}+\sum_{m\alpha}\left(E_{m\alpha}^{\rm corr}+\frac{\hbar(\Omega_{m\alpha}-\omega_{m\alpha})}{2}\right).

(48)

It coincides with the energy obtained in the RPA approximation
Furche
;
RPA
, as expected.

The Hamiltonian (
27
,
34
,
46
) is similar to that considered
by Scott and Booth in Ref.
Scott1
and earlier by us in Ref.
RPA
.
Here we have introduced the following novel features compared to Ref.
RPA
: (i) we have added new oscillators
to the environment, which describe the transitions between the HOMO/LUMO and the environment orbitals,
(ii) the frequencies of the environment oscillators (
) are evaluated more accurately than in Ref.
RPA
,
where simple Hartee-Fock approximation has been used for this purpose, and (iii) we have introduced the
counter-term in the interaction Hamiltonian, which allows us to go beyond the static screening model.

IV
Hamiltonian of a molecule in terms of qubit operators

In this section we present our main result expressing the Hamiltonian of a molecule
in terms of qubit operators. We will use this form of the Hamiltonian for subsequent numerical simulations.

For the majority of molecules the interaction constants
g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{m\alpha}
are very small and the oscillators
of the environment are only weakly excited. Therefore the oscillators can be replaced by two level systems, or qubits, without significant loss
of accuracy. Hence, one can express the Hamiltonian of a molecule (
9
) in the approximate form

\displaystyle H=H_{\rm sys}+H_{\rm env}+\tilde{V},

(49)

where the system Hamiltonian is given by Eq. (
27
) as before,

\displaystyle H_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\frac{U_{1}+U_{2}+J_{12}}{2}+\,\frac{U_{1}+U_{2}-J_{12}}{2}\sigma_{1}^{z}

(50)

\displaystyle-\,\frac{\Delta_{12}}{2}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+K_{12}\sigma_{1}^{z}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\tilde{t}_{1}}{2}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y}+\sigma_{1}^{x}-\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\tilde{t}_{2}}{2}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y}-\sigma_{1}^{x}+\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x}),

the environment Hamiltonian
has the form

\displaystyle H_{\rm env}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E^{\rm env}_{\rm HF}+\sum_{m\alpha}\left[E^{m\alpha}_{\rm corr}-\frac{\hbar\omega_{m\alpha}}{2}\right]

(51)

\displaystyle+\,\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}\left(\sigma^{+}_{m\alpha}\sigma^{-}_{m\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

and the interaction term becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\alpha}\sigma^{x}_{m\alpha}\bigg[g_{12}^{m\alpha}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

(52)

\displaystyle+\,g_{11}^{m\alpha}+g_{22}^{m\alpha}+\frac{g_{11}^{m\alpha}-g_{22}^{m\alpha}}{2}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{q=1}^{2}\sum_{m\alpha}\bigg[\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q1}^{m\alpha}+g_{2q}^{m\alpha}g_{q2}^{m\alpha}}{\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q2}^{m\alpha}}{\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{m\alpha}g_{q1}^{m\alpha}-g_{2q}^{m\alpha}g_{q2}^{m\alpha}}{2\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg].

Here the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{x}_{m\alpha},

\sigma^{\pm}_{m\alpha}=(\sigma_{m\alpha}\pm i\sigma_{m\alpha}^{y})/2
describe the
environment qubits, which replace the environment oscillators considered in the previous section.

V
Static screening approximation

Accurate quantum chemistry simulations require keeping very large number of oscillators
or qubits in the environment. To achieve chemical accuracy even for a relatively small molecule,
one typically needs to retain from 2000 to 10000 qubits. Numerically solving the Hamiltonian
(
49
) with so many qubits is quite challenging.
Fortunately, most of the coupling constants (
47
) are very small,
which allows one to use the perturbation theory. Such perturbation theory leads to the static screening approximation
proposed in Ref.
RPA
. The basic idea behind this approximation is simple: the electron repulsion integrals,
which determine the system parameters (
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
),
should be modified by replacing the unscreened Coulomb potential in the corresponding integrals (
11
) with its screened
version. For example, in the simplest case of homogeneous electron gas this amounts to the following replacement:
e^{2}/r\to e^{2}\exp(-r/\lambda_{\rm TF})/r
,
where
\lambda_{\rm TF}
is the Thomas-Fermi screening length. The static screening approximation should work best
for diradical molecules, for which the energy splitting between the HOMO and the LUMO orbitals is small compared
to the frequencies of the environment oscillators,
\Delta_{12}\ll\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}
.
However, this approximation works reasonably well even for molecules with
\Delta_{12}\sim\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}
.

Within the static screening approximation the Hamiltonian of a molecule takes the form

\displaystyle H=\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}+H_{\rm env},

(53)

i.e. the system and the environment become decoupled. The Hamiltonian of the environment retains
its original form (
51
), while the parameters of the system Hamiltonian get renormalized.
After such renormalization, the system Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\frac{\tilde{U}_{1}+\tilde{U}_{2}+\tilde{J}_{12}}{2}+\,\frac{\tilde{U}_{1}+\tilde{U}_{2}-\tilde{J}_{12}}{2}\sigma_{1}^{z}

(54)

\displaystyle-\,\frac{\varepsilon_{2}+\tilde{U}_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}-\tilde{U}_{1}}{2}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\tilde{K}_{12}\sigma_{1}^{z}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\tilde{\tau}_{1}}{2}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y}+\sigma_{1}^{x}-\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\tilde{\tau}_{2}}{2}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y}-\sigma_{1}^{x}+\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{x}),

where the renormalized parameters are

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{(g^{(k)}_{jj})^{2}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{12}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2g^{(k)}_{11}g^{(k)}_{22}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{12}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2(g^{(k)}_{12})^{2}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2g^{(k)}_{12}g^{(k)}_{jj}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}}.

(55)

Here in the sums we have replaced each pair of indexes
m\alpha
, referring to the transition
\alpha\to m
, by a single index
k
,
which enumerates all normal modes of the environment and the corresponding qubits.
The details of the derivation are presented in Appendix F, the parameters (
55
) are obtained
by comparing the original form of the system Hamiltonian (
23
) with the renormalized form (
152
).
Diagonalizing the renormalized Hamiltonian (
54
), which is a
4\times 4
matrix,
one can easily obtain the lowest excitation energy of a molecule.
To obtain higher excitation energies, one should diagonalize the full Hamiltonian (
53
),
which can be easily done because the system and the environment are decoupled.
In particular, the lowest vertical excitation energies of the singlet states are equal to the frequencies of the normal
modes,
\Delta E_{\rm sg}^{(k)}=\hbar\Omega_{k}
, if the ground state of a molecule
is a singlet. For triplet molecules the frequencies
\hbar\Omega_{k}

correspond to the lowest triplet excitations.

VI
Mixed approximation

Although the static approximation described in the previous section already provides rather
good accuracy for many molecules, one should go beyond it if one wants to achieve
the chemical accuracy of 1 kcal/mol = 0.043 eV.
To approach such accuracy without
running into computational problems, we introduce mixed approximation.
Namely, we treat a manageable number of the strongest coupled qubits of the environment exactly
and apply the static screening approximation to all remaining environment qubits.
To measure the coupling strength, for every
environment qubit we calculate the parameter

\displaystyle L_{k}=\frac{\left(g^{(k)}_{12}\right)^{2}+\left(g^{(k)}_{11}-g^{(k)}_{22}\right)^{2}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}}.

(56)

We rank the qubits according to the value of
L_{k}

and select
N_{q}
strongest coupled qubits with the largest
L_{k}
.
Next, we approximate the Hamiltonian (
49
) as

\displaystyle H=\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}+H_{\rm env}+\tilde{V},

(57)

where the system Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}
has the form (
54
)
with the renormalized parameters determined only by weakly coupled oscillators with the indexes in the range
N_{q}+1\leq k\leq N_{\rm RPA}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{j}-\sum_{k=N_{q}+1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{(g^{(k)}_{jj})^{2}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{12}-\sum_{k=N_{q}+1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2g^{(k)}_{11}g^{(k)}_{22}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},\;\;

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{12}-\sum_{k=N_{q}+1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2(g^{(k)}_{12})^{2}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}-\sum_{k=N_{q}+1}^{N_{\rm RPA}}\frac{2g^{(k)}_{12}g^{(k)}_{jj}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}}.

(58)

The environment Hamiltonian has the unchanged form (
51
)
and the interaction term includes only
N_{q}
strongest coupled environment qubits,

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}}\sigma^{x}_{k}\bigg[g_{12}^{(k)}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

(59)

\displaystyle+\,g_{11}^{(k)}+g_{22}^{(k)}+\frac{g_{11}^{(k)}-g_{22}^{(k)}}{2}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}}\sum_{q=1}^{2}\bigg[\frac{g_{1q}^{(k)}g_{q1}^{(k)}+g_{2q}^{(k)}g_{q2}^{(k)}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}}

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{(k)}g_{q2}^{(k)}}{\hbar\Omega_{k}}(\sigma_{1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\otimes\sigma_{2}^{y})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{g_{1q}^{(k)}g_{q1}^{(k)}-g_{2q}^{(k)}g_{q2}^{(k)}}{2\hbar\Omega_{k}}(1+\sigma_{1}^{z})\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigg].

In conclusion, we have developed an approximate system-bath model suitable for quantum chemistry simulations.
It is particularly convenient for quantum computing algorithms because the
Hamiltonian of a molecule is expressed in terms of qubit operators.
The ”system” consists of the two HOMO/LUMO orbitals populated by two electrons, it is encoded
by two qubits. The ”bath” consists of large number of either oscillators or qubits, with each
of them corresponding to an electron excitation from a doubly occupied orbital below the Fermi level to an empty orbital above it.
Oscillator and qubit baths are numerically equivalent due to very weak coupling between
them and the system. We have demonstrated that with this model
one can achieve chemical accuracy of 1 kcal/mol for the lowest vertical excitation
energies of selected molecules.

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}=1}^{2}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}=1}^{N_{\rm env}}\frac{h_{p_{1}k_{1}p_{2}k_{2}}}{2}(c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma_{1}}c_{k_{1}\sigma_{1}}+c^{\dagger}_{k_{1}\sigma_{1}}c_{p_{1}\sigma_{1}})

\displaystyle\times\,(c^{\dagger}_{p_{2}\sigma_{2}}c_{k_{2}\sigma_{2}}+c^{\dagger}_{k_{2}\sigma_{2}}c_{p_{2}\sigma_{2}}).

(96)

This expression looks very similar to the interaction term between the environment orbitals (

now two of the indexes,

Here we assume that the ground state of the system Hamiltonian

is the singlet state

Then, as in the previous section, we can approximately express the excitation operator

\displaystyle V_{\rm ex}+V_{\rm pair}-\langle V_{\rm ex}+V_{\rm pair}\rangle_{\rm env}

\displaystyle\approx\,\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}}r_{1m_{1}}h_{1m_{1}1m_{2}}r_{1m2}(a^{\dagger}_{m_{1}1}+a_{m_{1}1})(a^{\dagger}_{m_{2}1}+a_{m_{2}1})

\displaystyle H^{\rm osc}_{m1,2\alpha}

\displaystyle\sum_{m}(E_{\rm corr}^{m1}+\hbar\omega_{m1}a^{\dagger}_{m1}a_{m1})

C.2
Summary: Hamiltonian of a molecule with the singlet ground state of

Let us now summarize the results of this section. We have shown that the Hamiltonian of a molecule with the singlet ground state
of the system Hamiltonian (
23

\displaystyle H=H_{\rm sys}+H_{\rm env}+\tilde{V}.

Here the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle H_{\rm env}=E^{\rm env}_{\rm HF}+\sum_{m\alpha}\left(E^{m\alpha}_{\rm corr}+\hbar\omega_{m\alpha}a^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}a_{m\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{m\alpha\not=n\beta}r_{m\alpha}h_{m\alpha n\beta}r_{n\beta}(a^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}+a_{m\alpha})(a^{\dagger}_{n\beta}+a_{n\beta}),

(120)

but with the indexes

,
\alpha,\beta
varying in slightly wider intervals
than they did in the Hamiltonian (
94
). Namely, now
the indexes
\alpha,\beta
span over all doubly occupied orbitals and the HOMO orbital,
while the indexes
m,n
go through all empty environment orbitals and the LUMO orbital.
The frequencies
\omega_{m\alpha}
, which correspond to the transitions between the

), and
the frequencies for the transitions from or to the HOMO/LUMO orbitals are defined in Eqs. (

104

The environment Hamiltonian (
120

V_{3}

and the counter term required for restoring the normal ordering of the fermionic operators of the HOMO/LUMO orbitals

\displaystyle N_{\rm RPA}=(N_{\rm db}+1)(N_{\rm emp}+1)-1.

Appendix D
Hamiltonian of a molecule with the triplet ground state of
H_{\rm sys}

In this section we consider molecules with the triplet ground state of the
system Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sys}
.
We emphasize that
the ground state of the full molecular Hamiltonian

H_{\rm sys}+H_{\rm env}+\tilde{V}

may become a singlet if the coupling between the HOMO/LUMO and
the environment orbitals is sufficiently strong to change the symmetry of the ground state.

D.1
Approximating interaction between HOMO/LUMO and the environment orbitals

As in the previous section, we approximate the interaction terms
V_{\rm ex},V_{\rm pair},V_{1},V_{3}

by introducing additional oscillators in the environment. The transitions
corresponding to the environment oscillators of the triplet molecule are show in the right
panel of Fig.
1
.

In this section we assume that the ground state of the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sys}
is the triplet state
\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}
(
68
).
Acting on this wave-function by the operator
\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{k\sigma}c_{1\sigma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{k\sigma}c_{1\sigma}\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}=\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{k\sigma}c_{1\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}|0\rangle=c^{\dagger}_{k\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}|0\rangle.

The resulting wave-function
c^{\dagger}_{k\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}|0\rangle
differs from zero only if the orbital
k
is empty.
Therefore, considering the
2\times 2
sub-space defined by
the functions
\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}
and
c^{\dagger}_{m\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}|0\rangle
we can approximate

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{m\sigma}c_{1\sigma}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sigma^{+}_{m1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}(c^{\dagger}_{m\sigma}c_{1\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{m\sigma})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sigma^{+}_{m1}+\sigma^{-}_{m1}.

(123)

where, as before, the index
m
refers to an empty state.
The energy change, corresponding to the excitation
1\to m
, is

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{m1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}+\varepsilon_{2}+J_{m2}-K_{m2}-(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+J_{12}-K_{12})

(124)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{1}+J_{m2}-K_{m2}-J_{12}+K_{12}.

Now we assume that the environment orbital is doubly occupied and has the index
\alpha
. In this case

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{\alpha\sigma}\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}=\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{\alpha\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\downarrow}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\,c^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c_{\alpha\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\downarrow}|0\rangle=-c^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\,c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}|0\rangle.

(125)

We have obtained a triplet state, in which
the orbtials 2 and
\alpha
are singly occupied and the orbital 1 became doubly occupied. Hence,
in the
2\times 2
Hilbert space defined by the wave-functions
\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}
and

c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}|0\rangle

we can approximate

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{\alpha\sigma}\approx\sigma^{+}_{1\alpha},

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}(c^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{\alpha\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\sigma}c_{1\sigma})\approx\sigma^{+}_{1\alpha}+\sigma^{-}_{1\alpha}.

(126)

To obtain the energy change corresponding to the single electron excitation
\alpha\to 1
, we find the
energies of the initial state
\Psi_{1}^{\rm tr}

\displaystyle E_{i}^{\rm tr}=E_{\rm HF}^{\rm env}+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+J_{12}-K_{12},

(127)

and the energy of the final state
c^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha\uparrow}|0\rangle

\displaystyle E_{f}^{\rm tr}=E^{\rm HF}_{2\alpha}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2}+J_{2\alpha}-K_{2\alpha}.

(128)

Here
E_{\rm HF}^{\rm env}
is given by Eq. (
35
) and

E^{\rm HF}_{2\alpha}
is the Hartree-Fock energy with the orbitals
\alpha
and
2
excluded and
the orbital 1 doubly occupied. This energy can be expressed in the form similar to Eq. (
35
),
but with modified occupation numbers of the orbitals
\tilde{n}_{k}
,

\displaystyle E_{2\alpha}^{\rm HF}=2\sum_{k}t_{k}\tilde{n}_{k}+\sum_{kk^{\prime}}(2h_{kkk^{\prime}k^{\prime}}-h_{kk^{\prime}kk^{\prime}})\tilde{n}_{k}\tilde{n}_{k^{\prime}}.

(129)

Here
\tilde{n}_{k}=1
for all doubly occupied orbitals of the environment except
\alpha
, for which
\tilde{n}_{\alpha}=0
, and
\tilde{n}_{1}=1
;
for all other orbitals
\tilde{n}_{k}=0
. Next, the energies
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2}

are defined in the same way as the energies
\varepsilon_{\alpha},\varepsilon_{2}
(
24
,
28
) with the

,

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha}-2U_{\alpha}+2J_{1\alpha}-K_{1\alpha},

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}-2J_{2\alpha}+K_{2\alpha}+2J_{12}-K_{12}.

(130)

Now we can find the excitation energy of the transition
\alpha\to 1

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E^{\rm HF}_{2\alpha}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}+J_{2\alpha}-K_{2\alpha}

(131)

\displaystyle-\,E^{\rm HF}_{12}-(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+J_{12}-K_{12}).

\displaystyle E^{\rm HF}_{2\alpha}-E^{\rm HF}_{12}

\displaystyle=\,2(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{\alpha})+2U_{1}+2U_{\alpha}-4J_{1\alpha}+2K_{1\alpha},

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{1\alpha}=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{\alpha}+2U_{1}-2J_{1\alpha}+K_{1\alpha}-J_{2\alpha}+J_{12}.

(133)

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{m1}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{1}+J_{m2}-K_{m2}-J_{12}+K_{12},

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{\alpha}+2U_{1}-2J_{1\alpha}+K_{1\alpha}-J_{2\alpha}+J_{12},

\displaystyle V_{\rm ex}+V_{\rm pair}-\langle V_{\rm ex}+V_{\rm pair}\rangle_{\rm env}\approx\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}=1}^{2}

Appendix F
Static screening approximation and the counter term

The frequencies of the environment oscillators usually exceed the
energy splitting between the HOMO and the LUMO orbitals,
\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}\gg\Delta_{12}
.
Therefore, one can use the static screening approximation introduced in Ref.
RPA
.
In this approximation, the effect of the environment is absorbed
in the Lamb shift correction
BP
to the system Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}=H_{\rm sys}+\langle\tilde{V}\rangle_{\rm env}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\langle\delta\tilde{V}(0)\delta\tilde{V}(-t)\rangle_{\rm env},

(146)

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{V}=\tilde{V}-\langle\tilde{V}\rangle_{\rm env}.\hskip 147.95424pt

(147)

The second term in Eq. (
146
) is the Lamb shift
BP
and the average value
\langle\tilde{V}\rangle_{\rm env}

is the counter-term, which is required to ensure the normal ordering of the fermionic operators in the exact system Hamiltonian.
Writing down the Lamb shift for the interaction Hamiltonian (
145
), we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\langle\delta\tilde{V}(0)\delta\tilde{V}(-t)\rangle_{\rm env}=

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}=1}^{2}\sum_{m\alpha,n\beta}g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{m\alpha}g_{p_{3}p_{4}}^{n\beta}

\displaystyle\times\,c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}(0)c_{p_{2}\sigma}(0)c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}(-t)c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}(-t)

\displaystyle\times\,\langle(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}(0)+b_{m\alpha}(0))(b^{\dagger}_{n\beta}(-t)+b_{n\beta}(-t))\rangle.

The static screening approximation relies on the assumption that the time integral in this expression converges
fast and one can make Markovian approximation replacing
c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}(-t)c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}(-t)\to c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}(0)c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}(0)
.
As we mentioned above, this approximation is sufficient if
\hbar\Omega_{m\alpha}\gg\Delta_{12}
for
all oscillators of the environment. After that, finding the Lamb shift reduces to the evaluation of the following
time integral:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\langle(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}(0)+b_{m\alpha}(0))(b^{\dagger}_{n\beta}(-t)+b_{n\beta}(-t))\rangle

\displaystyle=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\left\langle(b^{\dagger}_{m\alpha}+b_{m\alpha})\left(e^{-i\Omega_{n\beta}t}b^{\dagger}_{n\beta}+e^{i\Omega_{n\beta}t}b_{n\beta}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-i\Omega_{m\alpha}t}\delta_{m\alpha,n\beta}=\frac{\delta_{m\alpha,n\beta}}{i\Omega_{m\alpha}}.

(148)

Here we have assumed that the environment oscillators are in their ground state.
We also emphasize that the same result holds for the qubit environment.

Combining these expressions,
we obtain the renormalized Hamiltonian (
146
) in the form

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}=H_{\rm sys}+\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}=1}^{2}\sum_{q=1}^{2}\sum_{n\beta}\frac{g_{p_{1}q}^{n\beta}g_{qp_{2}}^{n\beta}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}c_{p_{2}\sigma}

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}=1}^{2}\sum_{n\beta}\frac{g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{n\beta}g_{p_{3}p_{4}}^{n\beta}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}c_{p_{2}\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}.

(149)

Performing the normal ordering of the operators in the second line, we notice that the counter-term cancels out,
which results in the normal ordered renormalized Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}=H_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}=1}^{2}\sum_{n\beta}\frac{g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{n\beta}g_{p_{3}p_{4}}^{n\beta}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}}c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{p_{2}\sigma}.

(150)

Thus, we have demonstrated that the counter term, i.e. the second line of Eq. (
145
), restores the normal ordering of the fermionic operators in the renormalized Hamiltonian.
This ordering is correct because all operators are normally ordered in the original Hamiltonian (
9
).

Introducing the screened electron repulsion integrals

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}=h_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}-\sum_{n\beta}\frac{2g_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{n\beta}g_{p_{3}p_{4}}^{n\beta}}{\hbar\Omega_{n\beta}},

(151)

we can express the renormalized Hamiltonian (
150
) in the form similar to that of the original two orbital Hamiltonian (
23
),

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm sys}=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}=1}^{2}\tilde{t}_{p_{1}p_{2}}c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}c_{p_{2}\sigma}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}=1}^{2}\tilde{h}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}c^{\dagger}_{p_{1}\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{p_{3}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{p_{4}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{p_{2}\sigma}.

(152)

The renormalized parameters (
55
) can be directly extracted from this Hamiltonian. For that purpose,
one should use the parameter definitions (
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
) and perform
the replacement
h_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}\to\tilde{h}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}
in them.

The screened Coulomb integrals (
151
) can be computed without diagonalizing the matrix
\hat{M}
(
41
),
it is sufficient to invert it, which is less computationally demanding.
Indeed, substituting the coupling constants (
47

151

sums, we obtain the screened integrals in the form

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}=h_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}-\frac{4}{\hbar}\sum_{m_{1}\alpha_{1},m_{2}\alpha_{2}}h_{p_{1}p_{2}m_{1}\alpha_{1}}r_{m_{1}\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle\times\,\sqrt{\omega_{m_{1}\alpha_{1}}}\big[\hat{M}^{-1}\big]_{m_{1}\alpha_{1},m_{2}\alpha_{2}}\sqrt{\omega_{m_{2}\alpha_{2}}}r_{m_{2}\alpha_{2}}h_{m_{2}\alpha_{2}p_{3}p_{4}}.

(153)

The same transformations can be performed in the counter term appearing as the second term in the first line
of Eq. (
149

121

We also note that the screened integrals (

RPA
