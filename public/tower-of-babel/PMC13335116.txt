TEAD1 condensates are transcriptionally inactive storage sites on the pericentromeric heterochromatin in cancer cells
Authors: Yiran Wang, Jindayi Liang, Kimberly S Lange, Justin Demmerle, Eleanor A Liu, Ethan Black, Britney Jiayu He, Christopher J Ricketts, Shawn R Yoshida, Shasha Chong, W Marston Linehan, Jennifer M Kavran, Chongzhi Zang, Danfeng Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTEA domain transcription factor 1 (TEAD1), a Hippo pathway transcription factor important in cellular homeostasis and development, is increasingly implicated in cancer biology. Here we reveal an unexpected role for TEAD1 in organizing nuclear condensates, independent of active transcription. Using high-resolution imaging, ChIP–seq, RNA sequencing and proximity-based proteomics, we demonstrate that in patient-derived renal cell carcinoma cells, TEAD1 forms micrometre-sized condensates by binding to heterochromatic pericentromeric regions using its DNA-binding domain. TEAD-specific MCAT motifs selectively enrich and cluster in the pericentromeric region to specifically seed TEAD1 condensates. TEAD1 condensates do not activate transcription but instead serve as depots for excess TEAD1, and disruption of TEAD1 condensates leads to increases in YAP and TEAD target gene expression. This organization of TEAD1 contrasts with that observed in other genomic regions of both renal cell carcinoma and normal kidney cells, in which TEAD1 associates with markers of active transcription. Our findings provide a mechanistic framework for the dual regulatory roles of TEAD1 and offer new insights into its contribution to transcriptional dysregulation and tumour progression.Subject terms: Nuclear organization, Centromeres

Main

Key nuclear processes such as transcription, RNA splicing and DNA repair occur in membraneless compartments known as biomolecular condensates1–3. These condensates are broadly defined as dynamic assemblies formed through weak interactions between proteins and nucleic acids1,2,4. Specifically, transcriptional condensates are formed by transcription-related factors5. Although they have garnered increased attention, their mechanisms of formation and function remain poorly understood. Liquid–liquid phase separation is the dominant model explaining their assembly6,7. However, alternative mechanisms—such as low-valency interactions with spatially clustered DNA-binding sites—have also been proposed8,9. Moreover, although transcriptional condensates are generally associated with promoting gene expression10–12, some have been shown to repress transcription13,14. Thus, a deeper understanding of how transcriptional condensates form and function in biologically relevant contexts is essential, particularly for the development of strategies to target them in diseases like neurodegeneration and cancer15,16.

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a common type of kidney cancer. Although there have been considerable improvements in disease-free survival among patients with RCC, effective management of metastatic disease continues to be a substantial challenge in clinical practice. Moreover, a clear understanding of the molecular mechanism of RCC progression is still much needed17. Genetic alterations in RCC frequently occur in the Hippo pathway18, a deeply conserved signalling network that regulates cell growth19. The Hippo pathway includes key proteins such as STE20-like kinase 1 (MST1) and MST2, large tumour suppressor kinase 1 (LATS1) and LATS2, scaffold proteins such as Salvador homologue 1 (SAV1) and neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), which can be activated through cell–cell contacts20. The main role of the Hippo pathway is to phosphorylate and repress its downstream transcriptional coactivators Yes-associated protein (YAP) or its paralogue transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) by sequestrating them in the cytoplasm21–23. Therefore, when the Hippo pathway is ‘off’ or mutated such as in RCC (most mutations occur in NF2 or SAV1)18,24, YAP and TAZ translocate into the nucleus, bind to the transcription factors TEAD1–TEAD4 and activate genes that promote cell proliferation and survival25. However, where and how components of the Hippo pathway are activated inside the cell is not well understood.

Recently, many constituents of the Hippo pathway have been found to form biomolecular condensates. The two transcription coactivators YAP and TAZ were first to be discovered to form condensates that activate transcription4,10,26. Subsequently, upstream Hippo pathway kinases and adaptor proteins were also observed to form condensates, and the effects of these condensates on signal transduction varied27,28. Although the transcription factors TEAD1–TEAD4 are mostly considered to be client proteins that are incorporated into existing YAP and TAZ condensates4,10,26, a recent study reported that TEAD condensates could turn into active hubs after YAP or TAZ overexpression29. However, the overexpression system used to activate transcription limited our understanding of the physiological roles of TEAD condensates. Furthermore, the function of condensates that make up the Hippo pathway components in various cancers is also poorly understood.

In this study, we focus on transcription condensates formed by TEAD1 in Hippo-mutated RCC. We found that different from most other transcriptional condensates, TEAD1 condensates localize to heterochromatin in pericentromeric regions and do not mediate active transcription. Our combined chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses and proteomics data suggest that TEAD1 condensates may be storage sites that buffer excess TEAD1, such as in Hippo-mutated kidney cancer cells. These results reveal an interesting alternative function for transcriptional condensates and new ways that cells can harness condensates in homeostasis and disease.

Results

TEAD1 forms large condensates in cancer cells

We aimed to determine whether endogenous YAP and TEAD form biomolecular condensates in cancer cells, especially in those with high YAP or TEAD expression. Therefore, we examined a previously established diverse panel of patient-derived RCC cells: the UOK cell lines30. Among these cell lines, UOK121, UOK122, UOK139 and UOK140 are clear-cell RCC lines, whereas UOK275 and UOK342 are papillary RCC lines. A non-cancerous kidney tubule cell line, HK2, was used as a control. As phase separation and condensate formation are tightly coupled to protein expression levels11, we first measured the levels of YAP and TEAD1 in these cell lines. HK2, UOK121, UOK342, UOK122 and UOK140 expressed high levels of YAP (Fig. 1a,b), among which, UOK121 and UOK342 also expressed high levels of TEAD1 (Fig. 1a,b). Examination of these cell lines for YAP–TEAD1 condensate formation, we found that although YAP was diffusely localized to the nucleus and formed small condensates in different cell lines (Extended Data Fig. 1h), TEAD1 formed prominent micrometre-sized condensates only in UOK121 and UOK342 cells, which express high levels of TEAD1 (Fig. 1c). We observed at least one, and up to five, TEAD1 condensates per nucleus in the majority of UOK121 and UOK342 cells (Fig. 1d). Notably, most of these TEAD1 condensates were either close to the nuclear lamina or the nucleolus (Fig. 1e,f). These condensates were not artefacts of immunofluorescence, as TEAD1 knockdown by RNA interference (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b) eliminated the formation of TEAD1 condensates (Extended Data Fig. 1c). They were also not aggregates, as they did not stain positive for the amyloid marker AmyTracker26 (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Interestingly, TEAD1 condensates seemed to be a general feature of cancer cells. In a panel of melanoma, breast cancer, cervical cancer and osteosarcoma cell lines, TEAD1 condensates were readily observed in most of them, whereas in the respective noncancerous cells, none were found (Extended Data Fig. 2a–e). Among other members of the TEAD family, TEAD2 and TEAD3 did not form condensates, whereas TEAD4 formed condensates, although less prominently, in UOK121 and UOK342 cell lines (Extended Data Fig. 1e). These results may be explained by the lower expression of TEAD2 and TEAD3 than TEAD1 and TEAD4 in different cell lines (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). We have previously found that TEAD1 cannot form condensates by itself but can incorporate into YAP condensates and stabilize YAP condensate formation10,26. Interestingly, we here found that TEAD1 formed condensates that do not enrich endogenous YAP (Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). We were intrigued by these TEAD1 condensates and went on to examine how they form and function.

To probe the biophysical properties of TEAD1 condensates, we used fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP). Specifically, we used full-condensate FRAP to examine the diffusion of TEAD1 molecules between TEAD1 condensates and the nucleoplasm, and half-condensate FRAP to examine the dynamics of TEAD1 inside the condensates. TEAD1 condensates recovered fluorescence rapidly after full-condensate FRAP (t1/2 = 3.44 ± 0.067 s, mobile fraction = 0.838), which indicated that TEAD1 exchanges quickly between the nucleoplasm and the condensates (Fig. 1g,h (left)). In the half-condensate FRAP experiments, the bleached area also recovered fluorescence rapidly (Fig. 1g,h (right, black line)), which was accompanied by a 25% dip in the unbleached area immediately after bleaching (Fig. 1h, right, green line). These findings indicate that TEAD1 molecules preferentially mix within TEAD1 condensates, a result consistent with a mechanism driven by liquid–liquid phase separation8. Notably, treatment with 1,6-hexanediol (1,6HD), an aliphatic alcohol1,2,31, did not dissolve the TEAD1 condensates in the nucleus nor did it alter their number (Fig. 1i,j). Together, these results suggest that TEAD1 condensates are liquid-like compartments stabilized by interactions that are not primarily hydrophobic.

TEAD1 condensates do not mediate active transcription

The TEAD family of transcription factors usually mediate active gene expression. To investigate whether TEAD1 condensates also mediate active transcription, we examined their association with the following markers: RNA polymerase II phosphor-serine2 (RNA PolII-pSer2), a marker of transcription elongation32; histone 3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac), a histone modification denoting active enhancers33; and bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), a transcriptional coactivator found at super-enhancers34,35. To our surprise, TEAD1 condensates did not colocalize with any of these active transcription markers but instead excluded them (Fig. 2a,b). Consistently, there was no active transcription from large TEAD1 condensates (LTCs), as evidenced by a lack of 5-ethynyl uridine staining in nascent RNA labelling experiments, unlike what was seen for the nucleolus, an active ribosomal RNA transcription centre (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Furthermore, TEAD1 condensates did not colocalize with the canonical YAP–TEAD target gene CYR61 (also known as CCN1)36,37 when visualized by simultaneous immunofluorescence against TEAD1 and nascent RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) against CYR61 (Fig. 2e–g). These findings indicate that TEAD1 condensates do not mediate active transcription at the YAP–TEAD1 canonical target gene. Moreover, TEAD1 condensates did not colocalize with other known nuclear membraneless organelles such as Cajal bodies, nuclear speckles or PML nuclear bodies (Extended Data Fig. 3b–e). TEAD1 condensates therefore represent a unique kind of condensate that contains a transcription factor yet does not mediate active transcription. Interestingly, TEAD1 not only formed LTCs but also formed many small TEAD1 condensates (STCs). Compared with LTCs (1.31 ± 0.48 µm3 in size and around 3 per cell), STCs were only 0.030 ± 0.015 µm3 in size and were present at an average of around 169 per cell (Fig. 2h). The STCs resembled canonical transcriptional condensates. That is, they colocalized with the coactivator YAP and mediated active transcription, as evidenced by their colocalization with the YAP–TEAD1 target gene transcript CYR61 (Fig. 2i,j) and their much shorter distance to CYR61 than the LTCs (Fig. 2k,l). Therefore, TEAD1 can form two types of condensates that differ in size and function. We therefore focused on characterizing the LTCs, as they differed from the canonical transcription condensates. From here on, we refer to them as TEAD1 condensates for simplicity.

TEAD1 condensates localize to the pericentromeric heterochromatin region

TEAD1 condensates do not associate with markers of active transcription (Fig. 2a,b) and are close to the nuclear lamina and the nucleolus (Fig. 1e,f), regions typically enriched in heterochromatin. Therefore, we went on to test whether TEAD1 condensates localize to heterochromatin. Histone 3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) is an epigenetic marker characteristic of heterochromatin38. Staining of TEAD1 and H3K9me3 showed that TEAD1 condensates colocalized with a H3K9me3 domain in condensate-positive kidney cancer and other cancer cell lines (Fig. 3a–d and Extended Data Fig. 2f). No co-occupancy between TEAD1 and H3K9me3 was observed in condensate-negative UOK122 and HK2 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d). Furthermore, in UOK121 cells, TEAD1 condensates colocalized with heterochromatin protein 1α (HP1α; Extended Data Fig. 4e–h), a protein responsible for heterochromatin assembly39. Polycomb bodies organize heterochromatin regions to repress gene expression40,41. TEAD1 condensates did not colocalize with markers of Polycomb bodies (Extended Data Fig. 4i,j), which indicated that TEAD1 condensates localize at unique heterochromatin regions distinct from Polycomb bodies. Instead, TEAD1 condensates were always adjacent to the centromere and showed a bimodal distribution around the centromere together with H3K9me3 (Fig. 3e–h and Extended Data Fig. 4k–n). This result suggests that TEAD1 condensates specifically bind to pericentromeric heterochromatin, a region crucial for centromere integrity and proper segregation of chromosomes during mitosis42,43. This finding was further supported by the much closer distance of TEAD1 condensates (0.2 µm) to the centromere histone marker centromere-specific protein A (CENP-A)42 than to the telomere marker telomeric repeat binding factor 2 (TRF2)44 (1 µm; Fig. 3i–l). These results suggest that TEAD1 condensates specifically localize to pericentromeric heterochromatin.

The DNA-binding domain of TEAD1 is essential for TEAD1 condensate formation

The localization of LTCs to the pericentromeric region suggests that TEAD1 condensate formation may be seeded by DNA binding. To test this possibility, we generated variants of TEAD1 with impaired DNA binding. The DNA-binding domain of TEAD1 (the TEA domain) is composed of a highly conserved homeodomain region containing three α-helices: helix 1 (H1), helix 2 (H2) and helix 3 (H3)45 (Fig. 4a). To disrupt DNA binding by TEAD1, we constructed TEAD1 variants that either lacked the DNA-binding domain (Δ55–122) or bore previously validated site-specific substitutions in the DNA-binding domain: either F43P and L47P (TEAD1(H1Mut)) or Q89P and S92P (TEAD1(H3Mut))46 (Fig. 4a). When transiently expressed in both LTC-positive and LTC-negative cells, compared with wild-type (WT) TEAD1 labelled with eGFP (eGFP–TEAD1(WT)), variants with impaired DNA binding formed fewer condensates (Fig. 4b–e and Extended Data Fig. 5a,b) under similar or even higher expression levels (Extended Data Fig. 5c–g). Furthermore, the eGFP signals of these mutants did not colocalize with heterochromatin (Fig. 4f–k and Extended Data Fig. 5h–m). By contrast, alteration of other TEAD1 regions, such as the amino-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR), the YAP-binding site (Y406)47 or the protein kinase A phosphorylation site (S102)48, did not disrupt LTCs nor their association with heterochromatin (Fig. 4a–e,l–p). These data indicate that the DNA-binding domain of TEAD1 is essential for TEAD1 condensate formation on heterochromatin.

TEAD1 and YAP co-occupy distal enhancer sites to upregulate gene expression

We wanted to understand the functions of TEAD1 in regions both outside and inside TEAD1 condensates. Therefore, we set out to determine the binding sites of TEAD1 on the genome and investigate their relationship to gene expression. To achieve this, we performed RNA-seq and ChIP–seq using antibodies against endogenous TEAD1, YAP and H3K27ac in TEAD1 condensate-positive (UOK121 and UOK342) and condensate-negative (UOK122 and HK2) cell lines. TEAD1 and YAP are known to co-occupy distal enhancer sites to upregulate gene expression in various stem cells and cancer cells49–51. Consistent with these results, in all cell lines, most TEAD1–YAP co-occupied peaks mapped to either distal intergenic (41.9%) or intronic regions (46.7%), whereas few (5.2%) were found at promoters (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Specifically, over 50% of TEAD1–YAP-bound regions were 50-kb away from transcription start sites (TSSs) in UOK121 and UOK342 cells (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). Alignment of these distal TEAD1-bound sites with H3K27ac peaks revealed a bimodal distribution of H3K27ac centred on TEAD1–YAP peaks (Fig. 5a). These findings indicate that most TEAD1 binds to distal enhancer sites. These regions were also enriched for gene ontology (GO) terms such as actin cytoskeleton organization and cell junction assembly (Extended Data Fig. 6d), which are typical TEAD1–YAP-regulated pathways. As expected, TEAD1 and YAP peaks colocalized at canonical TEAD1–YAP target genes such as CTGF and CYR61 (Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). When we combined ChIP–seq and RNA-seq data and performed binding and expression target analysis (BETA)52, the results showed that most YAP–TEAD1 co-bound regions exhibited upregulated gene expression (Extended Data Fig. 6g,h). As TEAD1 is a sequence-dependent transcription factor, we performed MEME–ChIP to examine its binding motif. As expected, the TEAD-family-specific MCAT motif53 was the most enriched binding motif in regions bound by TEAD1 (Fig. 5b).

TEAD1 condensates localize to clustered MCAT motifs at pericentromeric heterochromatin

Having established that TEAD1 binds MCAT motifs to activate canonical YAP–TEAD1 target genes, we next examined where TEAD1 condensates form in the genome. We found a striking enrichment of MCAT motifs in pericentromeric regions, where these motifs were also highly clustered. This finding was consistent with our imaging data showing that TEAD1 condensates assemble at pericentromeric heterochromatin in a DNA-binding–dependent manner (Fig. 5c–e,g). By contrast, a length-matched AP-1 motif showed neither enrichment nor clustering in these regions (Fig. 5d,f,h), which indicated that this pattern is specific to TEAD1-binding sites. These results suggest that clustered MCAT motifs at pericentromeric heterochromatin provide a genomic scaffold for the nucleation of TEAD1 condensates. Consistent with this model, additional ChIP–seq analyses of H3K9me3 marks revealed that 25–35% of the total TEAD1-binding sites intersected with H3K9me3 regions. Moreover, these TEAD1–H3K9me3 co-bound regions coincided with MCAT-rich pericentromeric regions (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 7a–d). These findings indicate that the binding of TEAD1 to clustered MCAT motifs in heterochromatin drives the formation of TEAD1 condensates.

To define the mechanism of TEAD1 condensate formation, we investigated whether and how TEAD1 interactions with DNA altered TEAD1 solubility and condensate formation. We monitored condensate formation of full-length, purified TEAD1 (Extended Data Fig. 7e) incubated with a panel of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) containing differing numbers of MCAT motifs: 0, 1 or 4 tandem repeats (denoted 0×MCAT, 1×MCAT and 4×MCAT, respectively) (Fig. 5j,k). Although TEAD1 formed condensates in the presence of all the DNA variants screened, the number of condensates differed, which suggested that interactions with DNA influence TEAD1 solubility. TEAD1 formed the most condensates in the presence of 4×MCAT DNA (for example, 3.4-fold higher than 0×MCAT and 13.7-fold higher than 1×MCAT). In the presence of a single MCAT motif, TEAD1 formed fewer condensates than either 4×MCAT or 0×MCAT. This altered solubility profile supports a model in which TEAD1 condensation is less likely in conditions that support weak or no DNA binding (for example, 0×MCAT or 1×MCAT), driven mostly by weak TEAD1 self-associations. And in conditions with high-affinity DNA binding (for example, 4×MCAT or pericentromeric regions), condensation is further enhanced by both multivalent interactions (both protein–protein and protein–DNA) and an increase in the effective local concentration of TEAD1 (refs. 45,54) (Fig. 5l). These cooperative effects drive phase separation of TEAD1–dsDNA complexes beyond the intrinsic phase separation capacity of TEAD1. The effect of multivalent DNA interactions provides a biophysical rationale for the formation of LTCs in pericentromeric regions.

TEAD1 condensates serve as depots to buffer excess TEAD1

To further evaluate whether TEAD1 condensates are associated with transcriptional regulation, we performed differential expression analysis of RNA-seq data and compared condensate-positive kidney cancer cell lines (UOK121 and UOK342) with the condensate-negative HK2 kidney cell line55 (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). We next used BETA to determine whether differentially expressed genes were associated with TEAD1–H3K9me3 co-bound genomic regions. TEAD1–H3K9me3 co-binding sites were over 500-kb away from the nearest TSS (Extended Data Fig. 7h,i), and genes proximal to these sites did not show significant enrichment among either upregulated or downregulated transcripts (Extended Data Fig. 7j,k). Consistent with this lack of transcriptional association, increasing the number of TEAD1 condensates from zero to three did not alter overall nuclear TEAD1 abundance or expression of the canonical TEAD target gene CYR61 (Fig. 6a–d). Together with the observation that LTCs are spatially separated from active transcription markers and transcription sites (Fig. 2), these data suggest that pericentromeric TEAD1 condensates are unlikely to directly drive transcription and may instead function as nuclear depots for TEAD1 in kidney cancer cells.

In Hippo-mutated cancer cells, depots may buffer TEAD1 overexpression so that downstream gene expression remains stable. Indeed, single-particle tracking experiments showed that TEAD1 dissociated later and stayed longer in LTCs than in the nucleoplasm (Fig. 6e–h). This result suggests that LTCs trap TEAD1 proteins within its structure. To definitively show that LTCs serve as depots, we sought for ways to disrupt TEAD1 condensates without mutating the TEAD1 DNA-binding domain and measured the consequent effects on TEAD1 target gene expression. Hyperosmotic stress mediated by sorbitol or NaCl rapidly dissociated LTCs, whereas hypo-osmotic stress did not (Fig. 6i,j). Consistent with LTCs serving as depots in TEAD1 condensate-positive cells (UOK121 and UOK342), their dissociation and thereby the release of TEAD1 to the nucleoplasm under hyperosmotic stress were accompanied by an increase in TEAD1 target gene expression. By contrast, this effect was not observed in condensate-negative cells (HK2 and UOK122; Fig. 6k–m). In summary, LTCs are seeded specifically in the pericentromeric region by the clustered MCAT sequences, which serve important roles to buffer downstream gene expression.

TEAD1 condensates selectively incorporate protein components

To further understand the functions of TEAD1 condensates, we set out to identify protein components of TEAD1 condensates using proximity labelling mass spectrometry56,57 with UOK342 cells stably expressing miniTurbo-TEAD1-HaloTag (WT hereafter; Fig. 7a). In this system, miniTurbo is a compact biotin ligase that rapidly labels TEAD1-surrounding proteins with biotin56, and HaloTag is used for visualizing TEAD1 localization with Halo dyes. We used two additional cell lines as controls: UOK342 cells expressing miniTurbo-TEAD1(H3Mut)-HaloTag (H3Mut hereafter), which does not form condensates; and UOK342 cells expressing miniTurbo-HaloTag-NLS (NLS hereafter), which localizes diffusely to the nucleus using a nuclear localization sequence (Fig. 7a,b). We first used imaging to confirm efficient biotinylation with miniTurbo after the addition of exogenous biotin in all three cell lines (Fig. 7b). We then enriched the biotinylated proteins from whole-cell lysate using streptavidin beads and confirmed that protein biotinylation increased after the addition of biotin and that all three constructs were expressed at similar levels (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). Using mass spectrometry with tandem mass tag (TMT) labelling, we identified a total of 7,580 proteins in the WT, H3Mut and NLS samples combined (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01; Supplementary Table. 4). Comparing the combined dataset of WT samples and H3Mut samples with the NLS control, we first identified nonspecific nuclear proteins (Fig. 7c, blue). Then, we compared the differential enrichment of proteins between the WT and H3Mut groups and identified 186 proteins enriched in the WT samples and 409 proteins enriched in the H3Mut samples (Fig. 7d). Among the 186 proteins enriched in the WT samples, 0% were nonspecific nuclear proteins identified in Fig. 7c, whereas this percentage increased to 56% in proteins enriched in the H3Mut samples. These results demonstrate the specificity of our mass spectrometry approach for detecting TEAD1 condensate components (Fig. 7e). GO terms of the unique proteins in the WT samples revealed nuclear proteins associated with DNA binding and transcriptional regulation, consistent with the nuclear functions of TEAD1 (Fig. 7f and Extended Data Fig. 8c). Using co-immunofluorescence, we confirmed the colocalization of endogenous replication protein A2 (RPA2, part of the trimeric RPA complex for maintaining genome stability) with TEAD1 condensates in UOK121 and UOK342 cells (Fig. 7g,h). RPA2 is not essential for TEAD1 condensate formation, as knocking it down did not affect TEAD1 condensate number or size (Fig. 7i,j and Extended Data Fig. 8d–g). Instead, RPA2 may be a client that partitions into TEAD1 condensates, as the fluorescence of RPA2 recovered much faster than TEAD1 after photobleaching58,59 (Fig. 7k). These findings indicate that in addition to storing TEAD1, TEAD1 condensates may selectively incorporate proteins to maintain chromatin structure.

Discussion

In this study, we made an interesting discovery that TEAD1 forms two types of condensates that differ in both size and function: (1) small, transcriptionally active TEAD1 condensates that are the canonical condensates that colocalize with YAP; and (2) large, transcriptionally silent TEAD1 condensates that localize to heterochromatin and function as depots to store excess TEAD1 protein at a relatively harmless genomic location. Cells need to maintain optimal concentrations of proteins for homeostasis. Too little protein may delay entry into mitosis60,61, whereas too much may promote protein aggregation and/or hyperactivation of downstream signalling62,63. Moreover, aberrant activation of oncogenes in non-transformed cells often induces oncogene-induced senescence involving cell cycle arrest64–66. Multiple cellular processes contribute to protein homeostasis, including regulation of transcription, translation and protein degradation systems. We propose that protein condensation of TEAD1 represents another way to regulate TEAD1 concentrations and is used to both buffer and limit the damaging effects caused by excess TEAD1 that may confer survival advantages to RCC cells. In RCC cells, YAP–TAZ and TEAD1 can activate genes that mediate ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death pathway67,68. Preventing excessive TEAD1 activity by forming inactive TEAD1 condensates could represent a previously unknown way to protect cancer cells from ferroptosis. However, not all phenomena in cancer cells promote cancer. TEAD1 condensates may instead represent a futile attempt of these cells to prevent tumorigenesis by decreasing YAP–TEAD-mediated cell proliferation. Although we successfully used hyperosmotic stress to disrupt TEAD1 condensates, future studies will investigate physiological modulation of TEAD1 condensates to examine their roles in cellular fitness and cancer progression.

Intriguingly, LTCs do not form at random locations in the nucleus but instead localize to pericentromeric heterochromatin regions. These areas have long been considered transcriptionally inactive domains but are critical for maintaining centromere integrity and proper segregation of chromosomes during mitosis42,43. Although most transcription factors are absent from those areas, a few can be found there. Heat shock transcription factor 1 (HSF1) binds to the pericentromeric region of human chromosome 9 during heat shock, transcribes satellite III repeats and forms nuclear stress granules containing HSF1 (refs. 69,70). These HSF1 granules are thought to sequester active transcription complexes, such as mammalian CREB-binding protein, to shut down global transcription during heat shock71. Another transcription factor that localizes to the pericentromeric region is GAGA factor, which binds to GA/CT repeats and establishes chromatin boundaries in cooperation with Polycomb proteins and the inner nuclear membrane72,73. Ikaros is a protein that localizes to the centromeric heterochromatin of lymphocytes, forms condensates and represses nearby genes74. Here we propose a novel role of the pericentromeric region, which is to serve as a reservoir for excess TEAD1 in a DNA-binding-dependent manner. Indeed, when we overexpressed TEAD1 in other condensate-negative cell lines, we observed that TEAD1 condensates formed at heterochromatin and were likely to be pericentromeric (Extended Data Fig. 5h,l,m), which indicates that this is a general mechanism used by cells to store TEAD1. This result raises the question of whether other transcription factors can localize specifically to pericentromeric or other heterochromatic regions and form condensates. If so, the functions of these condensates will be of interest.

Recently, condensate-targeting therapies have emerged as a promising way to treat previously untreatable diseases such as neurodegeneration and cancer15,75–77. Our data on TEAD1 condensates in RCC reveal that not all the condensates observed in disease may drive that disease. Furthermore, a single transcription factor can also form condensates of different sizes and functions in different cell types15. Therefore, careful studies of condensate morphology and function are paramount for designing precise condensate-targeting treatments.

Image analyses

TEAD1 condensate nuclear localization quantification

Immunofluorescence staining of UOK121 and UOK342 cell lines was performed. 3D images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 with an Airyscan 2 detector. Z stacks were captured at 0.15 µm intervals and 3D Airyscan processing was applied. TEAD1 condensate localization was analysed in 3D using Zeiss Zen software. Nucleoli were identified on the basis of nucleolin staining, and perinuclear regions were defined as areas within 1 µm of the nuclear envelope, as delineated by Hoechst staining. TEAD1 condensates were considered colocalized with the nucleolus if they overlapped with nucleolin-positive regions in 3D space. Similarly, TEAD1 condensates were classified as perinuclear if they were located within 1 µm of the nuclear envelope. Colocalization was visually confirmed in all three dimensions. The percentage of TEAD1 condensates localized to the nucleolus, perinuclear regions, both or neither was calculated for each cell line. For representative images, maximum projections of the z stack were generated in Fiji.

TEAD1 condensate quantification

Images were taken of fixed cells stained with Hoechst dye and TEAD antibody. Cellprofiler (v.4.2.8) was used to identify both cell counts and the appearance of TEAD condensates. Using Hoechst as a nuclear marker, we used Cellprofiler to identify the nuclei as objects. Using the TEAD antibody, Cellprofiler thresholded the TEAD condensates on the basis of their size and fluorescence intensity. The objects were manually screened for false positives, and any object that had been thresholded inaccurately was removed from the dataset before final quantification. To determine the appearance rate of TEAD condensates, we divided the number of cells that positively generated at least one TEAD condensate by the total number of nuclei detected.

Colocalization analysis

A square region measuring 3 μm by 3 μm was delineated around the focal point of TEAD1 condensates. A negative control square was also randomly selected in the nucleoplasm where TEAD1 staining appeared diffused. The colocalization analysis was conducted using Fiji software81 with the BIOP-JACoP plugin82. Statistical evaluation was performed using unpaired t-tests in GraphPad prism. To calculate the enrichment of TEAD1 and YAP signals at CYR61 intron RNA FISH images, we used a 2.12 × 2.12 μm region of interest (ROI) box to centre around RNA FISH signals in 2D immunofluorescence images using ImageJ and duplicated TEAD, YAP and CYR61 channels. The duplicated smaller images were then combined into stacks of each channel, and the averaged images are derived using the Z-project-> Average function. Enrichment of averaged signals across the centre point was generated using the Plot Profile function in ImageJ. Similar analyses were done except for centring at the LTC or centromeric DNA FISH signals.

Live-cell imaging, drug treatment and FRAP

UOK342 HaloTag-TEAD1 CRISPR knock-in cells were plated into a 24-well glass-bottom plate (Cellvis, P96-1.5H-N) for drug treatment and imaging the following day. Before drug treatment, the cells were labelled with 10 nM JFX554 HaloTag ligand for 30 min. The medium was replaced with FluoroBrite DMEM complete medium (Gibco, A1896701) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 26140079), 2 mM GlutaMAX-l (Gibco, 35050061) and 1× NEAA (Gibco, 11140050). Live-cell images of individual cells were taken before and after treatment with 1% 1,6HD (Sigma-Aldrich, 88571) or DMSO (Corning, 01023017) for 30 min with a 2-min interval.

UOK342 cells stably expressing eGFP–TEAD1 were plated into an 8-well chambered cover glass plate (Cellvis, C8-1.5H-N) for FRAP experiments. Fluorescence bleaching was done on nuclear TEAD1 condensates using bleach mode in Zen software. The ROI was selected on either the entire condensate or part of it using a rectangular box of approximately 1 × 1 μm in half-FRAP and 2 × 2 μm in full-FRAP. At least 20 iterations of bleaching were done with a 488 nm Argon laser at 100% power. Around 140 rounds of imaging were performed before and after bleaching until the fluorescence signal plateaued, with an interval of 410 ms. FRAP analysis was performed as described in previous studies84. In brief, for each time point, raw fluorescence intensity (I) values from the bleached ROI, an unbleached ROI, a background ROI and a reference region were measured. For full-bleach FRAP, bleached intensities (FRAPFull bleach) were first computed by standard normalization: FRAPFull bleach = (I − IBackground)/(IReference − IBackground). For half-bleach FRAP, bleach (IB) and non-bleached (INB) intensities were further normalized in 4 steps sequentially (I-IV) to correct for an artefactual intensity drop in the non-bleached trace immediately after bleaching. Both traces were offset by a constant equal to the difference between the first two post-bleach non-bleach points (II·B and II·NB), which produced adjusted signals. Signals were then scaled by the relative ROI areas to account for differing region sizes, III⋅B/NB=II⋅B/NBArea1Area1+Area2. Next, traces were normalized to the pre-bleach and immediate post-bleach levels to obtain IIII·B/NB. The immobile fraction was estimated from the terminal plateau by averaging the last three time points and comparing the non-bleached and bleached signals, and the recovery curve was corrected accordingly to generate the final normalized non-bleach trace and bleached trace (IIV·B/NB). The t1/2 was calculated using frapplot85, with fit formula for one diffusion component.

ChIP–seq

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed as per previous studies87. In general, 108 cells were used per each ChIP–seq experiment. HK2 and UOK cells were first treated with 1% formaldehyde in UOK medium, followed by the addition of 0.125 M glycine to halt crosslinking. The cells, washed in ice-cold PBS, were then lysed in cell lysis buffer, with subsequent nutation at 4 °C. Cells were treated by Dounce homogenization on ice, followed by centrifugation at 500g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were resuspended using nuclear lysis buffer. Nuclear lysates were then sheared using a Covaris E220 to achieve an average chromatin length of 300–400 bp. The protein–chromatin complexes were captured using antibodies specific to TEAD1, H3K9me3, YAP1, H3K27ac and mouse IgG, and the mixtures were incubated overnight on a nutator at 4 °C. The antibody–chromatin complexes were later captured using magnetic protein G beads, washed and eluted using elution buffer. After reversal of formaldehyde crosslinks, DNA was purified with ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator (Zymo Research, D5205) and library preparation was performed using NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumima (NEB, 10129566).

ChIP–seq data analysis

ChIP–seq data were processed using the following pipeline. Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were quality-checked using FastQC. For identifying TEAD1–YAP co-occupied regions, YAP and H3K27ac reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (v.2.3.5.3) with default parameters. The resulting BAM files were used to generate coverage tracks using bamCoverage from the deepTools suite88, with normalization of counts per million (CPM). Gene tracks were visualized using IGV (v.2.16.2).

Peaks for YAP and H3K27ac ChIP–seq were identified using MACS2, with default narrow peak settings and corresponding input controls (HK2 and UOK121 1% input) used to reduce background signal. A FDR cutoff of q < 0.01 was applied for peak selection. To identify putative co-regulatory TEAD1–YAP regions, overlapping peaks between TEAD1 and YAP datasets were identified using the bedintersect function in BEDTools. The resulting intersected peak regions were used for motif enrichment analysis via MEME–ChIP from the MEME Suite. GO enrichment analysis of intersected peak regions was done using DAVID86.

To investigate the genomic context of TEAD1 condensates, sequenced reads were mapped with Bowtie2 using repetitive mapping (-k 50). SICER2 (ref. 89) was used for peak calling to identify broad enrichment regions characteristic of H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin. Peaks for TEAD1 and H3K9me3 ChIP–seq were identified using SICER2 with broad peak parameters (-w 1000 rt 1 –fdr 0.05 -g 3000 –e 1000), and input controls with overlapped mapping (-k 50) ChIP–seq peaks were selected. To identify TEAD1–H3K9me3 regions, overlapping peaks between the two were identified using the bedintersect function in BEDTools, and centromere regions were eliminated using the bedsubtract function. To compare these co-bound regions across cell lines, we used computeMatrix90 to quantify signal intensities at TEAD1-binding regions across all four cell lines. Heatmaps of TEAD1 signals were generated using plotHeatmap to visualize TEAD1-binding profiles in each cell line in TEAD1–H3K9me3 co-bound regions in condensate-positive cell lines (UOK121 and UOK342).

Motif discovery and spacing calculation

To quantify whether motif occurrences are enriched near centromeres, we analysed genome-wide motif hit coordinates. BED files for MCAT (a TEA transcription factor binding motif) and AP-1 (a JUN–FOS transcription factor binding motif) were pre-built using FIMO91. Only primary hg38 chromosomes (chromosome 1–chromosome 22, chromosome X and, chromosome Y) were retained, and each motif hit was presented by its genomic midpoint. Centromere annotations were collapsed per chromosome by taking the minimum start and maximum end. For each chromosome, we defined a pericentromeric interval as the centromere region expanded by ±4 Mb (bounded by chromosome ends). We then partitioned each chromosome into consecutive 1 Mb bins and counted motif midpoints per bin using a histogram-based approach; counts were converted to densities (hits per Mb) for each bin. Motif density distributions between pericentromeric and non-pericentromeric bins were compared using a one-tailed Welch’s t-test. To calculate the ECDF in each chromosome, motif midpoints were de-duplicated to remove identical positions, sorted and the distances between consecutive midpoints were computed (bp) using the first difference. Each distance was assigned a genomic position at the midpoint of the corresponding gap.

BETA

To evaluate the regulatory function of TEAD1, we performed BETA using the BETA basic module52. ChIP–seq peak regions for TEAD1 were used as input along with RNA-seq differential expression results from UOK121 and UOK342 cells. Differentially expressed genes were identified on the basis of log2 fold change thresholds and were categorized as upregulated, downregulated or unchanged. The distance between TEAD1 peaks and target genes was defined using regions identified by the intersection of TEAD1 and H3K9me3 ChIP–seq peaks (via BEDtools), which reflected chromatin context-specific binding. BETA computes regulatory potential scores for each gene on the basis of binding site proximity and density and assesses whether TEAD1 functions primarily as an activator or repressor using a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to compare cumulative distributions of gene categories.

The cells were then incubated with Hoechst 33342 at room temperature for 5 min and mounted on glass slides with Vectashield and sealed with nail polish. Confirmation of protein biotinylation by western blotting

Cells were plated on 10 cm Petri dishes and transfected the next day at 70% confluency using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (L3000015, Thermo Fisher), followed by 24 h of incubation. Cells were treated with 500 µM biotin in fresh growth medium for 10 min at 37 °C, washed with ice-cold DPBS for 3 times and collected using cell scrapers and centrifuged at 300g, 4 °C for 5 min. Whole-cell lysates were collected in 400 µl RIPA buffer containing 50 mM Tris pH 8 (T60050-1000.0, Research Products International), 140 mM NaCl (S23025-3000.0, Research Products International), 1% Igepal CA-630 (56741-50ML-F, Sigma Aldrich), 0.1% sodium deoxycholate (30970-25G, Sigma Aldrich), 0.1% w/v SDS (L3771, Sigma-Aldrich) and complete mini protease inhibitor cocktail (11836170001, Sigma Aldrich) for 20 min on ice and clarified by centrifugation at 13,000g, 4 °C for 10 min. Protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay. In brief, 175 µg protein from the lysates was enriched for biotinylation using Pierce streptavidin magnetic beads (88816, Thermo Fisher) and incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotator. The bead slurry was washed twice with RIPA buffer, once with 1.0 M KCL, once with 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer and twice with RIPA buffer. About 10 µg of protein from input, post-immunoprecipitation supernatant and pull-down samples were loaded onto a gel for immunoblotting. Biotinylated proteins were stained using streptavitin-IRDye680 (926-68079, Licor). Other antibodies used were anti-TEAD1 antibody (610923, DB Biosciences) and anti-β-actin antibodies (AC004, ABClonal).

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis with miniTurbo

UOK342 cells stably expressing miniTurbo-TEAD1-HaloTag, miniTurbo-TEAD1(H3Mut)-HaloTag and miniTurbo-HaloTag-NLS were cultured overnight in 15 cm Petri dishes and treated with 500 µM biotin for 10 min at 37 °C. Cells were collected and the nuclear protein extract was collected using a Nuclear Extract kit (Active Motif, 40010). Around 600 µg of nuclear protein lysate was collected. Next, 60 µl magnetic beads (washed twice with RIPA buffer) was added. The mixture was topped up with additional RIPA buffer to 1 ml. Samples were incubated on a rotator overnight at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, streptavidin beads were collected on the side of the 1.5 ml tube using a magnet. The lysate solution was transferred into new tubes, and the beads were washed twice with 1 ml RIPA lysis buffer, once with 1 ml of 1.0 M KCl, once with 1 ml of 50 mM TEAB buffer and twice with Tris-HCl pH 7.5.

TCA–acetone precipitation

Nuclear lysates were buffered to pH 8.0 with 100 mM TEAB buffer in 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes, then reduced using 25 µl of 50 mM dithiothreitol for 1 h at 60 °C. Samples were chilled on ice then alkylated using 25 µl of 50 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min in the dark. Samples were then diluted on ice in 8× volume of 10% trichloroacetic acid in acetone w/v stored at –20 °C. Samples were vortexed and incubated for at least 4 h at –20 °C. Samples were pelleted by centrifugation at 16,000g for 10 min at 0 °C. The supernatant was carefully removed to leave the pelleted protein on the walls of the tube. A volume of 500 µl of neat acetone (stored at –20 °C) was added to the pelleted protein and vortexed. This was then incubated for at least 10 min at –20 °C before centrifugation at 16,000g once again and the supernatant carefully removed. The remaining acetone was allowed to evaporate for 10 min before the addition of trypsin (Pierce) in 100 mM TEAB at a ratio of 1:50 enzyme to protein and allowed to digest overnight at 37 °C. Digested peptides were lyophilized and reconstituted in 500 µl RIPA buffer and incubated with 50 µl Pierce streptavidin magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C on a rotator. The bead slurry was washed as described in the western blotting section, followed by two washes with ultrapure water. The washed beads were air-dried at 4 °C for 6 h and stored at –20 °C.

Eluting peptides from streptavidin linked magnetic beads

Biotinylated peptides bound to streptavidin linked magnetic beads were separated from the final wash buffer by placing the Eppendorf tubes on a magnetic holder. After removing and setting aside the final wash buffer, the following steps were performed in a fume hood. Neat HFIP (200 µl Millipore/Sigma, 105228) was added to each tube and put on a shaker at 1,000 rpm for 10 min. The bead slurry was then placed on a magnetic holder to recover the HFIP-eluted peptides from the magnetic beads. HFIP extraction was performed twice and both peptide elutions were combined in a new Eppendorf tube. The combined extractions were evaporated to dryness in a Speedvac and resuspended in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Samples were buffer exchanged using an Oasis HLB (Waters) with two washes of 0.1% TFA and eluted with 0.1% TFA, 60% acetonitrile into new tubes and dried by vacuum centrifugation before mass spectrometry analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Tryptic peptides were analysed by reverse-phase chromatography tandem mass spectrometry on a Vantage NEO UPLC interfaced with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated using a 2–90% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid gradient over 90 min at 300 nl min−1 using an in-house packed 75-µm ID by 15 cm column packed with ReproSIL-Pur-120-C18-AQ (2.4 µm, 120 Å bulk phase, Dr Maisch). Survey scans of precursor ions were acquired from 375 to 1,500 m/z at a resolution setting of 120,000. Precursor ions were isolated in data-dependent mode with a 3 s cycle time using a 1.5 dalton isolation width and a 45 s dynamic exclusion. Peptides were fragmented at a normalized HCD collision energy of 30 with a resolution setting of 30,000.

Data analysis and candidate protein selection

Mass spectrometry data for all six files were processed together using Proteome Discoverer (v.3.1.1.93, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Sequest with Match Between Runs enabled against the UniProt Homo sapiens database. The TEAD constructs were inserted at the end of the Human FASTA file. Search criteria included trypsin as the enzyme with two allowed missed cleavages, a 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance and a 0.02 Da fragment mass tolerance. Modifications included fixed carbamidomethylation on C, oxidation on M, deamidation on N or Q and biotin on K as variable modifications. Peptide identifications were filtered at the 1% FDR level using the Percolator Node in Proteome Discoverer. Abundance ratio and adjusted P values were generated and visualized by volcano plots showing differentially enriched proteins. String Analysis was generated based on the pulled candidate list. Protein–protein interaction networks and GO enrichment were rendered with settings at high confidence (0.700). Clusters composed of fewer than four proteins were removed. See Supplementary Table 4 for proteomic source data. The mass spectrometry proteomic data have been deposited into the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifiers PXD072858 and PXD072858.

Single-particle tracking

Single-particle tracking (SPT) of TEAD1-Halo molecules was performed on a Nikon Ti2-Eclipse Motorized TIRF system with a ×100/NA 1.49 oil-immersion objective, a Hamamatsu ORCA Fusion BT CMOS camera system and a Perfect Focus System to combat axial focus drift. A Tokai Hit system was used to keep the humidified incubation chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Nikon NIS-Elements was used to control the microscope hardware. UOK342 TEAD1-Halo cells were plated in an 8-well chambered cover glass plate (Cellvis, C8-1.5H-N) 48 h before imaging. On the day of imaging, cells were stained with 10 nM JFX554 Halo ligand for 30 min in FluoroBrite DMEM complete medium (Gibco, A1896701) supplemented with 10% (Gibco, 26140079), 2 mM GlutaMAX-l (Gibco, 35050061) and 1× NEAA (Gibco, 11140050). Images used for SPT were obtained at 16-bit (no binning), 300 ms exposure and 25% 561 nm laser (LUN-F XL) for 2,000 frames at an image size of 512 × 512 pixels (0.065 μm per pixel resolution). Before imaging, a snapshot of the same field of view was taken using epifluorescence (10 ms of exposure, 25% 550 nm channel D-LEDI) for segmenting the nucleus and thresholding a mask of TEAD1 condensates during analysis. Four independent replicates were performed with 8 cells imaged per replicate. To correct for photobleaching of the fluorophore during subsequent data analysis, individual H2B molecules were also imaged as they are stably bound on chromatin. UOK342 cells were plated and transiently transfected as described above with PB-EF1-Halo-H2B 16 h before staining with JFX554 Halo ligand and imaging. At least 5 cells were imaged per replicate under the same parameters.

SPT analysis

A previously published protocol92 was used for SPT analysis to determine the dissociation kinetics and residence time of long-binding TEAD1 molecules inside and outside TEAD1 condensates. The steps and parameters used are briefly described here. The nuclear region in each image stack was first segmented using the epifluorescence snapshot as a mask. We used a custom-written ImageJ macro using Trainable Weka Segmentation to create a binary mask of the nucleus from the snapshot, which was then manually verified. The segmented image stacks were then converted to TIFF format and used in subsequent analyses. Localization and tracking of single molecules were performed using SLIMfast, a GUI implementation of a previously described MTT algorithm93, with the following parameters: localization error = 10-6, deflation loops = 0, detection box = 9 pixels, lag time = 300 ms, max. expected diffusion coefficient (μm2 s−1) = 1, max. no. competitors = 1. ImageJ macros and Matlab code that have previously been reported94 were used to threshold and create a binary mask of TEAD1 condensates from the same nuclear snapshot and to subsequently categorize trajectories as being inside a condensate, which was defined as spending over 50% of its lifetime in the masked area. A custom-written ImageJ macro was also used to rotate and place the condensate mask randomly in the nucleus so that it remained in the same area as the condensate but did not overlap with it. Trajectories that were inside the original condensate or inside the randomly placed mask in the nucleoplasm were then compiled by replicate and fit to a bi-exponential decay model to extract the measured dissociation rate constant (kobserved) and residence time (1/k). To correct for photobleaching, the same analysis was performed for Halo-H2B molecules in the whole nucleus (without categorization into a condensate), which are largely immobile. Because they were transiently transfected, we observed a proportion of H2B molecules that behaved unbound and were highly dynamic. As a result, we filtered out tracks that were fewer than 4 frames long and used the remaining trajectories to calculate k of the H2B molecules (kpb). We subtracted kpb from kobserved to get the true koff of TEAD1 molecules. Photobleaching correction was done per replicate, whereby each replicate was corrected by the kpb of Halo-H2B movies taken on the same day. The corrected residence time was calculated by 1/koff. The koff and residence times were averaged and GraphPad Prism was used to perform two-tailed paired t-tests between in-condensate and masked nucleoplasm trajectories.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were repeated as stated in the figures, legends and Methods. Statistical details are presented in the figure legends and as detailed below. Comparisons were performed in GraphPad Prism (v.10.1.1). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes were similar to those reported in previous publications26,95. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. For experiments quantifying the per cent of condensate-positive cells, fields of view were randomly selected using the Hoechst channel. Spatial analysis including RNA FISH, 3D image quantification, colocalization analysis and SPT were not randomized, and cells with similar intensity and with LTCs were selected and imaged. For in vitro condensate experiment data analyses, clear outliers were removed from the dataset. Of the remaining data points per condition, the first six images were analysed and plotted to ensure equal representation of each condition. For drug treatment assays, time-lapse images of cells moving out of focus during the imaging period were carefully removed. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Data collection and analyses were not performed blinded to the conditions of the experiments.
