The Enforcement and Feasibility of Hate Speech Moderation on Twitter Corresponding authors. E-mail: manuel.tonneau@oii.ox.ac.uk ; paul.rottger@oii.ox.ac.uk ; sfraiberger@worldbank.org .
Manuel Tonneau, Dylan Thurgood, Diyi Liu, Niyati Malhotra, Victor Orozco-Olvera, Ralph Schroeder, Scott A. Hale, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Paul Röttger, Samuel P. Fraiberger

Abstract
Online hate speech is associated with substantial social harms, yet it remains unclear how consistently platforms enforce hate speech policies or whether enforcement is feasible at scale. We address these questions through a global audit of hate speech moderation on Twitter (now X). Using a complete 24-hour snapshot of public tweets, we construct representative samples comprising 540,000 tweets annotated for hate speech by trained annotators across eight major languages. Five months after posting, 80% of hateful tweets remain online, including explicitly violent hate speech. Such tweets are no more likely to be removed than non-hateful tweets, with neither severity nor visibility increasing the likelihood of removal. We then examine whether these enforcement gaps reflect technical limits of large-scale moderation systems. While fully automated detection systems cannot reliably identify hate speech without generating large numbers of false positives, they effectively prioritize likely violations for human review. Simulations of a human–AI moderation pipeline indicate that substantially reducing user exposure to hate speech is economically feasible at a cost below existing regulatory penalties. These results suggest that the persistence of online hate cannot be explained by technical constraints alone but also reflects institutional choices in the allocation of moderation resources.

Digital platforms mediate speech for billions of people worldwide, shaping how information circulates across economic, political, and social life

. Hate speech remains a persistent feature of this environment: global surveys suggest that nearly two-thirds of internet users encounter it online

In this context, platforms face sustained pressure from policymakers

, two fundamental questions remain: how extensively hate speech policies are enforced in practice, and whether stronger enforcement is feasible at a global scale.

Empirical evidence on the enforcement of hate speech policies remains limited by structural barriers to auditing moderation systems at scale. Platforms provide partial and sometimes misleading information about moderation practices

or suspended users
[

drawn from narrow, non-representative user groups. Existing work is also concentrated on English-language and U.S.-based contexts, while large-scale analyses are scarce and primarily focus on platform responses to misinformation during major events, including the COVID-19 pandemic
[
13

, the 2020 U.S. election

, and the Capitol attack

These limitations also complicate the evaluation of whether stronger enforcement is feasible, which requires reliable estimates of detection performance and moderation capacity. Automated detection systems are widely deployed, yet their real-world performance is rarely evaluated on representative data. Platform models are proprietary, while academic models are typically tested on curated datasets that poorly reflect the prevalence and ambiguity of hate speech in practice
[

]
. Recently disclosed moderator staffing levels (e.g.,
[
60

) reveal workforce size but not whether these resources are sufficient relative to the volume of harmful content. Without representative evidence on model performance and staffing adequacy, it remains unclear whether persistent online hate reflects technical constraints or institutional priorities.

To address these gaps, we conducted the first global audit of both the enforcement and feasibility of hate speech moderation on a major social media platform. We focused on Twitter prior to its late 2022 acquisition, when sustained public data access enabled large-scale external auditing. We used a complete snapshot of public tweets posted worldwide over a 24-hour period on September 21, 2022, collected 10 minutes after publication
[
42
]
. From this dataset, we drew representative samples stratified across eight major platform languages and four English-dominant countries, capturing both linguistic and geographic variation (Fig.
1
). We then conducted large-scale manual annotation to identify content characteristics, including whether tweets contain hate speech as defined by Twitter’s Hateful Conduct policy at the time (
Materials and Methods
). The resulting dataset contains 1.6 million labels for 540,000 tweets and is, to our knowledge, the largest manual annotation effort in hate speech research to date.

Using this dataset, we analyzed moderation along two dimensions. First, we examined enforcement by querying tweets and accounts five months after posting to measure content removal and user suspension rates. Second, we assessed feasibility by evaluating automated hate speech detection systems and simulating human–AI moderation pipelines to estimate achievable reductions in exposure to hate speech. Together, these analyses provide a unified assessment of how hate speech policies were enforced and whether persistent online hate reflects technical constraints or institutional choices in allocating moderation resources.

Results

We estimate the prevalence of hate speech using the annotated sample from our 24-hour snapshot. Hate speech constitutes a small fraction of all tweets, with 0.42% labeled as hateful and 0.03% as violent hate speech on average across languages (see
S2.5
). However, even low prevalence translates into large absolute volumes. Extrapolating these estimates to the full 24-hour platform dataset, the 0.42% rate corresponds to 224,963 hateful tweets per day (95% CI: [202,195, 249,684]) and 11,071 violent hateful tweets per day (95% CI: [7,657, 15,205]). These volumes imply routine exposure: based on typical usage patterns, users would, on average, encounter approximately one hateful tweet per day and one violent hateful tweet every 17 days in a chronological feed (
S2.6
). Exposure is likely higher in feeds optimized for engagement, which amplify divisive content
[
36
]
.

We next analyze how hate speech policies were enforced in practice, using data re-queried five months after initial collection. Enforcement of hate speech policies is limited: five months after posting, only 20.2% of tweets labeled as hateful were removed on average across languages (Fig.
2
A; see Table
S5
for per-language breakdown). However, this measure includes tweets removed for any reason, such as user deletion or account deactivation, and cannot be fully attributed to platform moderation. When restricting removals to user suspensions, which are unambiguously attributable to the platform and lead to the removal of all posts by suspended authors, the removal rate drops to 9.2%, with only 9.0% of users who posted hate speech suspended.

Strikingly, hateful tweets are no more likely to be removed than non-hateful tweets, including when they contain violent hate speech (

,
p=0.84
) (Fig.
2
B). This finding contradicts the platform’s stated policy that enforcement should increase with severity: even tweets containing explicit calls for physical harm, extermination, or mass violence against target groups are not more likely to be removed (see
S3.5
for examples). In contrast, other policy categories such as scams (
\beta=0.39
,
p<0.001
) and adult material (

,
p<0.001
) are significantly more likely to be removed. These patterns hold at the country level and when user suspensions are used as the outcome (

).

Moderation outcomes also vary systematically across languages (see
S3.3
). Relative to English-speaking users who posted hate, Arabic users are 12 percentage points less likely to be suspended (
p=0.006
), whereas Indonesian users are 11 percentage points more likely to be suspended (
p=0.037
). Within English-speaking countries, Nigerian users who posted hate are 7 percentage points less likely to be suspended than their U.S.-based counterparts (
p=0.03
). Although we cannot rule out moderation actions occurring in the ten minutes between posting and collection, keyword searches for severe hate speech identified many tweets containing explicit violations that remained publicly accessible at the time of collection, suggesting limited enforcement prior to collection (see
S3.4
for details).

While most hate speech remains online, its impact depends on whether it is seen. In our data, 66.9% of hateful tweets (95% CI: [63.2, 70.4]) received no engagement within the first ten minutes after posting, despite evidence that it can drive engagement
[
44
]
. This suggests that a substantial share of hate speech has limited visibility, raising the question of whether moderation targets content with the greatest potential exposure. Using early engagement as a proxy for exposure, we find that higher engagement is associated with a lower likelihood of removal (
\beta=-0.05
,
p=0.01
) (Fig.
2
C). This indicates that moderation enforcement is not concentrated where potential exposure is greatest. This pattern also holds at the country level (see
S3.3
).

Beyond removal and suspension, Twitter’s policies also reference visibility reduction as a potential action in response to policy violations
[
55
]
, which cannot be directly observed without access to view data. If such “downranking” were applied systematically, for example, by excluding violent hate from feeds while leaving it visible on profiles, we would expect tweets containing violent hate speech to receive less engagement than comparable tweets without it. Instead, we find no evidence of reduced engagement: tweets containing violent hate speech do not receive less engagement, with estimated effects that are positive and statistically indistinguishable from zero in both country- and language-level analyses (
\beta=0.71
,
p=0.12
;
\beta=0.21
,
p=0.66
). These results provide no evidence of systematic visibility reduction and suggest that any filtering in place was unlikely to have meaningfully limited the reach of the most severe content.

Taken together, these results indicate limited enforcement and a prioritization pattern that diverges from Twitter’s stated hate speech policy. Removal likelihood does not increase for hateful versus non-hateful content, violent hate is not treated more stringently than non-violent hate, and hateful tweets receiving more early engagement are less likely to be removed. In contrast, other content categories such as scams and adult material are removed more aggressively. Enforcement is therefore not effectively targeted toward the most harmful or widely viewed hate speech.

Automated moderation

We next examine whether higher moderation rates could be achieved using AI-based hate speech detection models. We first evaluate a fully automated moderation setting in which any tweet flagged as hateful by an AI model would be moderated. Our evaluation uses publicly available models for each language covered by our representative datasets. Candidate models are selected based on popularity, and the best model per language is used for analysis (
Materials and Methods
). We include supervised models from widely used open-source systems that output continuous hate scores, as well as the large language model GPT-5.1 evaluated in a zero-shot setting, which produces discrete predictions.

Across all languages, fully automated moderation would either over-moderate large amounts of non-hateful content or fail to moderate a substantial share of hate speech. Indeed, no classification threshold achieves both high precision and high recall (Fig.
3
A) and increasing recall substantially reduces precision, while improving precision sharply lowers recall. GPT-5.1 exhibits a similar pattern, achieving high recall only at the cost of very low precision. Model performance is particularly poor for non-European languages, while performance for English, though generally better resourced, is worse than in several other European languages (
S4
).

To understand the sources of these errors, we examined tweets assigned the highest hate scores by the best supervised learning models (Fig.
3
B), focusing on non-hateful tweets misclassified as hateful. The primary driver of low precision is the prevalence of offensive content at the top of the score distribution, i.e., tweets that do not target protected groups but contain profane or aggressive language that superficially resembles hate speech. On average, offensive content constitutes 38% of the top 0.5% tweets ranked by hate score across languages, often outnumbering true hate speech at the top of the distribution. Qualitative inspection (
S4.3
) further shows that models frequently misclassify tweets that mention or quote hate speech rather than express it, as well as statements that would be hateful only under specific contextual interpretations or minor typographical changes. Consequently, many non-hateful tweets receive high hate scores, limiting achievable precision even at conservative classification thresholds. Relaxing thresholds to increase recall rapidly produced large numbers of false positives.

Despite these limitations, supervised models consistently rank hateful content near the top of the score distribution (Fig.
3
C). Across languages, approximately half of hateful tweets fall within the top 3.2% of scored content. These results indicate that while current AI systems cannot reliably distinguish hate from non-hate at any single threshold, they effectively concentrate likely violations within a small fraction of highly-scored content, making them suitable for moderation triage.

with the best publicly available models, only 24.5% of exposure to hate speech is reduced on average across languages. Under the assumptions of our simulation, existing moderation capacity is therefore insufficient to substantially reduce exposure. Performance also varies substantially across the eight languages. German content reaches high levels of exposure reduction with roughly 1,000 moderators, whereas Arabic requires at least an order of magnitude more moderators to achieve comparable levels. These differences reflect variation in both content volume and detection performance. Because moderation resources are not allocated in proportion to these differences, exposure reductions vary widely: under reported staffing levels, English, French, and German achieve meaningful reductions in exposure to hate speech, whereas Arabic, Spanish, and Portuguese remain effectively unmoderated.

To assess feasibility, we translate moderation effort into financial terms and estimate the costs required to achieve different levels of exposure reduction, expressed as a share of Twitter’s 2022 global revenue (Fig.
4
C). Assuming an hourly wage of USD 20, adjusted for differences in purchasing power across countries (
Materials and Methods
), reducing 80% of exposure to hate speech across our eight languages can be achieved at a cost of 2.9% (95% CI: [1.5, 4.5]) of global revenue. This remains well below regulatory penalties for failing to moderate harmful content. Under the European Union’s Digital Services Act and the United Kingdom’s Online Safety Act, platforms face fines of up to 6% and 10% of global revenue, respectively, for systemic failures to mitigate illegal or harmful content. These results indicate that substantially reducing exposure to hate speech through AI-assisted human moderation is financially feasible at a cost below maximum statutory penalties.

Discussion

Twitter’s enforcement of hate speech policies is limited and misaligned with its stated priorities. Most hateful content remains online five months after posting, and hateful tweets are no more likely to be removed than non-hateful ones. Although our estimated removal rate exceeds the only previously available benchmark of 5% for Facebook in 2021
[
21
]
, enforcement remains low relative to stated commitments and contrasts with Twitter’s own transparency reports
[
56
]
. Enforcement does not increase with severity or visibility, which is inconsistent with harm-based prioritization.

We also document systematic cross-lingual and cross-geographic disparities in enforcement. Some contexts (e.g., Arabic and Nigeria) exhibit lower suspension rates, while others (e.g., Indonesia) show higher rates relative to English and the U.S. These differences are mirrored in our capacity simulations: languages that require more reviewer effort to achieve comparable moderation outcomes tend to have fewer moderators allocated. This mismatch between moderation needs and resources is consistent with evidence of chronic underinvestment in Arabic-language moderation
[
52
,
37
,
18
,
2
]
. Cross-national regulatory differences may further contribute, as regimes such as Indonesia’s MR5 impose stricter compliance obligations
[
27
,
49
,
34
]
. While we do not establish causality, these patterns point to uneven platform governance across contexts.

Our analysis underscores both the potential and the limitations of AI-based moderation. In real-world settings, classification performance is low, in contrast to results reported on curated benchmarks
[
5
,
59
]
, including for state-of-the-art AI models such as GPT-5.1. These systems cannot be reliably deployed in isolation: they either over-moderate benign content or miss substantial amounts of hate. Prior work attributes these limitations to the difficulty of distinguishing offensive from hateful language
[
17
]
and identifying the use versus mention of hate speech
[
24
,
31
]
. We find that these challenges are further amplified in real-world settings, where merely offensive but non-hateful content is far more prevalent. While improvements in training data and model design may help, classification performance remains constrained by semantic overlap and ambiguity at the boundary between hate and non-hate, where even human annotators only moderately agree.

Nevertheless, supervised learning models exhibit strong ranking ability, consistently concentrating hateful content near the top of their score distributions. This makes them effective triage tools for directing human attention to the most harmful content, where consensus on enforcement is stronger
[
48
,
40
]
, consistent with human–AI moderation systems already employed by major platforms
[
6
]
. Simulating such systems under conservative assumptions, we find that existing moderation resources are insufficient to achieve high moderation rates. While fully moderating hateful content would be prohibitively costly, preventing 80% of engagement would require resources equivalent to about 2.9% of global revenue. Because our simulation relies on conservative assumptions, this estimate should be interpreted as an upper bound. In practice, required resources are likely lower due to moderator learning and access to richer contextual signals
[
1
]
. Importantly, these costs remain below statutory penalties under frameworks such as the EU Digital Services Act and the UK Online Safety Act. Such penalties are not hypothetical: Twitter/X was fined 120 million euros by the European Commission under the DSA in December 2025, underscoring the real financial risks of inadequate enforcement
[
20
]
.

These results point to broader constraints shaping platform moderation. Expanding moderation capacity entails substantial financial commitments, particularly because divisive and polarizing content often drives engagement
[
44
,
9
]
, creating tension between user protection and profit maximization. Recent shifts toward looser moderation, often justified in the name of protecting free expression
[
29
]
, alongside greater reliance on AI automation
[
30
,
38
]
, reflect this tension. Yet our findings suggest that replacing human review with AI alone increases errors, whereas a more effective approach is to use AI for triage while retaining human decision-making. Under this design, residual errors stem from human judgment and can be minimized through multiple independent reviews coupled with targeted training and clear decision guidelines
[
31
,
15
]
. Error rates are likely to be especially low for more extreme forms of hate speech, where annotator agreement is highest. Stronger enforcement also need not imply broader censorship, as platforms could rely on measures such as visibility reduction to limit exposure while preserving open expression.

These findings also carry implications for policymakers. The mismatch between stated policy priorities and actual enforcement underscores the role of regulation in shaping platform incentives. By quantifying the resources required to improve enforcement, our results help calibrate regulatory penalties and show that these costs fall below statutory maxima. More broadly, they highlight the urgency of timely and independent assessments of sociotechnical risks. As researcher access to platform data continues to contract, maintaining oversight mechanisms is essential for transparency and accountability
[
7
,
54
]
and for identifying harms before they scale
[
41
]
.

This study has several limitations that highlight directions for future research. First, our analysis focuses on text-based hate speech, whereas visual and audiovisual content may require substantially different moderation approaches. Second, our estimates are based on a single day of platform activity; both hate prevalence and enforcement vary over time, particularly as generative AI increases content volume
[
35
]
. Third, our estimates assume timely moderation shortly after posting, which may require more reactive workflows in practice. In addition, our comparison between estimated moderation costs and statutory fines under the EU Digital Services Act and the UK Online Safety Act is approximate. Because our simulations include most major Twitter languages, the estimated costs should be interpreted as conservative upper bounds on the total resources required for EU- or UK-level compliance.

Despite these limitations, our results provide a benchmark for evaluating moderation systems under real-world conditions and across diverse linguistic contexts. The persistence of hate speech cannot be explained by technical constraints alone, but reflects how platforms allocate moderation resources under competing incentives. Substantial reductions in exposure are both technically and financially feasible, but require sustained investment in human-centered moderation systems.

Materials and Methods

All methods were approved by the University of Oxford Research Ethics Committee (reference: OII_C1A_23_013).

Data collection and sampling

We analyzed a global snapshot of all tweets (N=375 million) posted on Twitter (now X) during a 24-hour period beginning on September 21, 2022, collected approximately ten minutes after they were posted as described in
42
. At the time of collection, the platform’s Hateful Conduct Policy stated that hateful conduct could result in tweet removal, visibility filtering, or account suspension
[
55
]
. From this global dataset, we selected eight languages—Arabic, English, French, German, Indonesian, Portuguese, Spanish, and Turkish—to capture broad geographic and linguistic coverage. For each language, we drew two samples: a random sample of 30,000 tweets and an engagement-weighted sample of 15,000 tweets, where the probability of selection was proportional to total early engagement (likes, replies, retweets, quote tweets) within the ten-minute collection window. To assess geographic variation independent of language, we additionally selected four countries where the main language at the time on Twitter was English—the United States, India, Nigeria, and Kenya—and drew analogous random and engagement-weighted samples for each country. Across all languages and countries, the final dataset comprises 540,000 tweets (
S1
for details).

Annotation protocol

All tweets were annotated by trained native speakers using a prescriptive protocol
[
46
]
aligned with Twitter’s Hateful Conduct Policy in effect at the time of data collection
[
55
]
. Each tweet was assigned to one of three mutually exclusive categories:
hate
,
offensive
, or
neutral
. Hate speech was defined as content that promotes or incites violence against, or directly attacks, a protected group, including groups defined by race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, gender, or sexual orientation. Offensive content was defined as abusive, profane, or insulting language that does not target a protected group and is therefore not prohibited under the policy. Neutral content included all remaining tweets that were neither hateful nor offensive.
Each tweet was independently labeled by three annotators, with final labels determined by majority vote. Inter-annotator agreement for the three-way classification was moderate (Fleiss’
\kappa=0.50
). All tweets marked as hateful by at least one annotator were subsequently reviewed by the first author to ensure consistent alignment with the policy definition. Tweets with a majority hate label were then further classified as
violent
or
non-violent
hate, where violent hate includes explicit calls for physical harm, extermination, or direct threats. Two independent annotators conducted this secondary classification and achieved substantial agreement (Cohen’s
\kappa=0.68
).
On about half of the full sample, annotators additionally labeled whether tweets contained scam or adult content, allowing comparison of hate speech enforcement with other policy-relevant content categories. Annotation guidelines, agreement statistics, and additional details are provided in
S2
.

Enforcement measurement

Five months after the initial data collection, in February 2023, we assessed whether each tweet and its posting account remained accessible by querying the Twitter Compliance API. A tweet was classified as “removed” if it was no longer retrievable because (i) the posting account had been suspended, (ii) the account had been deactivated, or (iii) the tweet itself had been deleted. Although the API provides user-level removal reasons (e.g., suspension), it does not distinguish between platform-initiated and user-initiated tweet deletion. Using our annotated representative samples, we then computed removal rates across languages and countries and estimated associations between tweet characteristics and removal or suspension likelihood using an OLS linear probability model. Because tweets were collected approximately ten minutes after posting, moderation actions occurring within that initial window cannot be directly observed. However, keyword-based searches for severe hate speech within the collection window revealed numerous explicit violations that remained publicly accessible at the time of capture, suggesting that large-scale immediate removal was unlikely. Additionally, removals were assessed five months after posting, spanning a platform ownership transition. While account reinstatements under the subsequent administration could mechanically reduce observed removal rates, most enforcement likely occurred within the first weeks after posting, when the pre-acquisition moderation team remained in place. Additional details on measurement, descriptive statistics, regression specifications, and robustness analyses are provided in Section
S3
.

AI detection performance

We evaluated publicly available hate speech detection systems across languages, including open-source supervised models, the Perspective API, and the decoder-based large language model GPT-5.1 evaluated in a zero-shot setting. For each language, we selected the five most widely used open-source supervised hate speech classifiers available on Hugging Face, as determined by cumulative download counts at the time of evaluation. Perspective API was included as a widely deployed commercial toxicity detection system, and GPT-5.1 as a state-of-the-art decoder-based model.
All models were evaluated fully out-of-sample on our annotated representative datasets without additional fine-tuning. For supervised models (including Perspective API), performance was assessed using average precision (AP), which is robust to class imbalance and reflects ranking quality. For GPT-5.1, which produces discrete class predictions rather than continuous scores, we reported precision and recall. For each language, we selected the supervised model with the highest AP for subsequent analyses. To better understand systematic misclassifications, we conducted a qualitative analysis of high-confidence false positives among the top 0.5% of model scores, manually coding their content characteristics. Full model details and evaluation procedures are provided in
S4
.

Simulation of human–AI collaborative moderation

We simulated a two-stage human–AI moderation pipeline, focusing on the language level. In the first stage, all tweets were assigned a continuous hatefulness score by the best-performing supervised learning model for the corresponding language, as identified by highest average precision on our representative evaluation set. In the second stage, tweets were ranked in descending order of model score and the top
p
% were reviewed by human moderators. The share
p
was endogenously determined by operational parameters, including total daily tweet volume in the language, the number of moderators assigned to that language, daily working hours per moderator, reviewing speed (tweets per hour), and the number of independent reviewers per tweet.
We adopted conservative assumptions designed to minimize human error and moderator overload. In our baseline specification, each tweet was independently reviewed by three moderators and each moderator reviewed 100 tweets per hour. These assumptions likely overstate moderation effort relative to current operational practice and therefore yield upper-bound estimates of resources needed to achieve a certain moderation rate.
We reported two primary outcome measures.
Coverage
is defined as the share of all hateful tweets that are surfaced for review and, in principle, moderated.
Avoided hate engagement
is defined as the share of total engagement with hateful tweets that would be prevented through timely moderation, where engagement is measured as total interactions accrued within ten minutes of posting. For this metric, tweets were ranked using a combined score that incorporates both predicted hatefulness and early engagement in order to prioritize content with the greatest potential exposure.
To approximate moderation capacity, we used moderator counts reported in Twitter’s October 2023 Digital Services Act transparency filing
[
61
]
, which provide the earliest publicly available language-level staffing data. Given documented workforce reductions after September 2022, we treated these figures as conservative lower-bound estimates of moderation capacity at the time of posting. Because not all moderation activity concerns hate speech, we estimated the effective number of moderators handling hate-related content by applying the share of manual moderation decisions attributed to hate speech (31% in 2024
[
60
]
) to the reported total moderator counts for each language. This adjusted figure was used as the baseline workforce in our simulations.
To contextualize resource requirements, we estimated the annual financial cost of achieving target levels of avoided hate engagement as a share of 2022 total global revenue. Cost calculations assumed a purchasing-power-adjusted hourly wage of 20 USD and continuous platform operation (8 hours per day, 365 days per year), reflecting the fact that content is posted every day of the year.
A formal description of the simulation framework, parameter choices and justification, workforce estimation procedures, additional coverage results, and sensitivity analyses is provided in Section
S5
.

S1
Data and Sampling

S1.1
Full-platform dataset

We used the TwitterDay dataset
[
42
]
, comprising all public tweets posted globally during a 24-hour period beginning September 21, 2022. Tweets were collected approximately ten minutes after posting using the Academic API. The dataset consists of 375 million original tweets and retweets and provides a complete snapshot of public platform activity during that period.

S1.2
Sampling

Unit of analysis

Sampling was conducted on original tweets only; retweets were excluded in order to avoid duplication of content and to ensure that each observation represents a unique authored statement.

Linguistic and geographic scope

We focused on eight widely used platform languages: Arabic, English, French, German, Indonesian, Portuguese, Spanish, and Turkish.
Language selection was guided by two criteria. First, we aimed to capture substantial global platform coverage across regions. Second, we restricted attention to languages for which at least one publicly available academic hate speech dataset existed at the time of analysis (August 2024), as catalogued by
https://hatespeechdata.com/
. This ensured the availability of benchmark detection models for feasibility analysis. Because enforcement and moderation practices may differ across countries even when the primary language is shared, we additionally sampled tweets from four English-speaking countries spanning multiple continents: the United States, India, Nigeria, and Kenya. This allows us to examine geographic variation holding language constant.

Language inference

Tweet language was inferred using the language label provided by the Twitter API for each tweet. This label is generated by Twitter’s internal language detection system. The distribution of languages in the full 24-hour TwitterDay dataset is reported in Table

Country inference

User country was inferred by geocoding the self-reported profile location field using the Google Geocoding API. This approach follows established procedures in prior work
[
28
]
.
Because not all users provide a profile location, and because not all location strings can be successfully mapped to a country, country inference is available for only approximately 25% of users in the full dataset. As a result, country-based samples are less representative than language-based samples, which cover all tweets.

Sampling strategy and weighting

For each language and country, we drew two distinct samples:

1.

A random sample of 30,000 tweets.

2.

An engagement-weighted sample of 15,000 tweets, where sampling probability was proportional to total early engagement.

Total engagement was defined as the sum of likes, retweets, replies, and quote tweets measured ten minutes after posting. This short time window corresponds to the data collection procedure of the TwitterDay dataset and serves as a proxy for early exposure.

Random samples were used for prevalence estimation, enforcement analysis, AI standalone performance and moderation coverage estimation in the human-AI moderation setup. Engagement-weighted samples were used to study the relationship between engagement and removal and compute avoided engagement with hate in the human-AI moderation setup.

In total, the sampled dataset comprises 540,000 tweets across languages and countries.

S2
Annotation

S2.1
Annotation team

The annotation team consisted of 36 annotators, with three annotators assigned to each language–country group. Annotators were recruited through targeted outreach and screened for native or near-native proficiency in the target language, prior annotation experience, and familiarity with local sociolinguistic context. To capture within-language variation, we aimed to include annotators from different national or regional backgrounds where applicable (e.g., different dialect communities within the same language). The team was diverse in gender, age (primarily 18–39), and educational background (undergraduate to doctoral degrees). Compensation ranged from 5 to 24 US dollars per hour depending on country, education, and experience.

S2.2
Annotation framework

Policy alignment

Tweets were annotated using a prescriptive framework aligned with Twitter’s Hateful Conduct Policy in effect at the time of data collection
[
55
]
. The annotation protocol operationalizes the platform’s policy definitions in a structured coding scheme designed to distinguish hate speech from non-hateful content.

Primary classification

Each tweet was assigned to one of three mutually exclusive categories:

1.

Hate:
Content that promotes or incites violence against, or directly attacks, a protected group, defined by race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, gender, or sexual orientation.

2.

Offensive:
Abusive, profane, or insulting language that does not target a protected group.

3.

Neutral:
All remaining content.

Violence

Tweets labeled as hate were further categorized as
violent
or
non-violent
. Violent hate includes explicit calls for physical harm, extermination, or direct threats against protected groups. Non-violent hate includes degrading, dehumanizing, or exclusionary attacks that do not contain explicit calls for violence.

Additional content categories

On a subset of 300,000 tweets, corresponding to one third of random samples and all engagement-weighted samples, annotators additionally labeled the presence of other policy-relevant content types to enable comparison of hate speech enforcement with enforcement of other sensitive categories. Specifically, they annotated:

•

Scam:
Content that attempts to deceive users for financial or personal gain, including fraudulent offers, impersonation, or phishing attempts.

•

Adult content:
Sexually explicit, pornographic, or strongly suggestive material.

Annotation design

All tweets were independently labeled by three native speakers of the respective language for the primary three-way classification (hate, offensive, neutral) and for the additional content categories. Tweets labeled as hate were subsequently classified as violent or non-violent by two independent annotators. Annotations were conducted on textual content only. The full annotator-facing codebook, including detailed decision rules and examples, is available at
https://github.com/manueltonneau/twitter-hate-moderation
.

Attention checks

To ensure annotation quality, we inserted attention checks into each annotation chunk of size 2,500. For each chunk, four positive examples were randomly sampled: two from existing hate speech datasets for the primary annotation task
[
50
]
and two from the auxiliary categories generated using GPT-5.1. These items were manually verified by the first author prior to insertion and placed at random ranks within the chunk. After completing each chunk, the first author reviewed the annotator’s responses to the attention check items. If the annotator failed to correctly identify the inserted positives, the entire chunk was returned for re-annotation. This process was repeated until all attention checks in the chunk were correctly passed.

S2.6
Exposure and platform-scale estimation

Platform-scale extrapolation

Let
\hat{p}_{\ell}
denote the estimated prevalence of hateful tweets in the unweighted random sample for language
\ell
, and let
T_{\ell}
denote the total number of original tweets posted in that language during the same 24-hour snapshot. We estimated the total number of hateful tweets in language
\ell
as

\hat{H}_{\ell}=\hat{p}_{\ell}\times T_{\ell}.

The platform-wide total across all included languages is then

\hat{H}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\hat{H}_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\hat{p}_{\ell}\times T_{\ell}\right),

where
L
denotes the number of languages.

To quantify sampling uncertainty, we bootstrapped within each language (1,000 resamples at the tweet level). For each replicate, we resampled tweets into three mutually exclusive categories (violent hateful, non-violent hateful, and non-hateful) and recomputed both overall hateful prevalence and the violent-within-hate share, thereby jointly propagating uncertainty in both quantities. For bootstrap replicate
b\in\{1,\dots,1000\}
, we computed

\hat{H}^{(b)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\hat{p}_{\ell}^{(b)}\times T_{\ell}\right).

The language-level tweet totals
T_{\ell}
were treated as fixed and measured without error. We reported the point estimate together with a 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval,

\hat{H}_{CI}=\left[\hat{H}_{2.5},\,\hat{H}_{97.5}\right],

where
\hat{H}_{2.5}
and
\hat{H}_{97.5}
denote the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution
\{\hat{H}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{1000}
. These intervals capture sampling uncertainty in the snapshot but do not reflect day-to-day temporal variation in hate volume.

User exposure approximation

Let
F
denote the number of tweets viewed per day under a chronological feed. The average user spends approximately 34 minutes per day on the platform
[
47
]
, which we conservatively approximated to about 200 tweets read per day.

The pooled prevalence
\hat{p}
is computed as the tweet-volume-weighted average across languages, i.e.,

\hat{p}=\frac{\hat{H}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}T_{\ell}}.

Expected daily exposure to hateful tweets is given by

E[\text{hateful tweets per day}]=\hat{p}\times F.

Using
F=200
, we obtain

E[\text{hate}]=0.0042\times 200=0.84,

E[\text{violent hate}]=0.0003\times 200=0.06.

These calculations assume chronological ranking and random exposure proportional to overall prevalence and likely represent a lower bound under engagement-based feeds that have been shown to preferentially amplify divisive content
[
36
]
.

S3
Enforcement measurement

S3.1
Removal definition

Enforcement outcomes were measured using Twitter’s Compliance API in February 2023, approximately five months after the original tweets were posted. For each tweet in the annotated sample, we queried its availability and, when applicable, the status of the associated user account.

A tweet was classified as
removed
if it was no longer retrievable via the API at re-query. The Compliance API reports non-retrievability reasons at the user level, including
suspended
, and
deactivated
account status. User-level
suspended
status is interpreted as a platform-initiated enforcement action that renders all tweets from the account inaccessible. User-level
deactivated
statuses reflect a user-controlled action of deactivating one’s own account, which also make tweets inaccessible via the API. The API does not provide a reason when an individual tweet is unavailable while the account remains otherwise active.

Accordingly, our primary removal measure, defined as any tweet not retrievable at re-query, captures all observable forms of inaccessibility and should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound on platform-initiated enforcement. To provide a more conservative estimate of clearly platform-initiated action, we separately report removal attributable to user suspension only. Suspension-based measures therefore constitute a lower bound on unambiguous platform enforcement.

S3.3
Regression specification and tables

We estimated linear probability models (LPMs) to assess associations between hate speech and enforcement outcomes. The primary tweet-level specification is:

\Pr(\text{Removed}_{i}=1)=\alpha+\beta_{1}\text{ViolentHate}_{i}+\beta_{2}\text{NonViolentHate}_{i}+\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\mathbf{X}_{i}
includes log-transformed retweet count, the Twitter “sensitive” flag, reply status, verified author status, and additional policy-relevant content categories (scam, adult content). Neutral content serves as the reference category.

To examine cross-linguistic and cross-country disparities, we estimated interaction models between hate indicators and language or country indicators, using English and the United States as reference categories, respectively. Changing leadership and reinstated accounts

Tweets in our dataset were posted in September 2022, before Elon Musk’s acquisition of Twitter in late October 2022, while enforcement was measured five months later in February 2023. Because the pre-acquisition moderation team remained in place for approximately one month before the ownership change, and because the subsequent leadership reduced moderation capacity and relaxed enforcement rules, most enforcement actions were likely taken before the transition. However, one concern is that the post-acquisition leadership reinstated a number of previously suspended accounts
[
14
]
, which could reduce observed removal rates if tweets from formerly suspended authors became accessible again.

To assess this, we cross-referenced all author IDs in our dataset against a list of accounts known to have had their suspensions reversed following the acquisition
[
14
]
. We identified 31 overlapping authors (0.008% of the 399,470 unique authors in our data), responsible for 69 tweets, none of which were annotated as hateful. We re-estimated all regression models — tweet-level removal (language and country, random samples), engagement (language and country, engagement-weighted samples), and the engagement
\times
removal interaction — after excluding these 69 tweets. Estimates, standard errors, and significance levels are identical to the full-sample results across all models. Account reinstatements therefore pose no threat to our conclusions.

Keyword search for pre-collection enforcement

Because tweets were collected approximately ten minutes after posting, enforcement occurring within this window cannot be directly observed. To assess the likelihood of immediate pre-collection moderation, we conducted a keyword search for nine highly explicit violent hate terms across the full TwitterDay dataset. The keywords were:
kill all jews
,
gas the jews
,
death to jews
,
kill all n****rs
,

(German: “gas the Jews”), and

Examples include a tweet reading “f**k n****rs kill all n****rs”, a Spanish-language tweet reading “hitler es god, muerte a los judios, putos negros”, and an English-language tweet invoking religious eschatology to call approvingly for the killing of Jews.

S4.2
Evaluation setup

Classification performance

All models were evaluated fully out-of-sample on our annotated unweighted representative datasets. No additional fine-tuning, retraining, or parameter adjustment was performed. Also, no additional preprocessing beyond standard tokenization required by each model was applied. Supervised models were used with their publicly released pre-trained weights.

GPT-5.1 was evaluated in a zero-shot setting using the standardized classification prompt below. Temperature was set to 1.0, and each tweet was classified independently. Because GPT-5.1 produces discrete class predictions rather than continuous scores, we reported precision and recall rather than AP.

To assess ranking behavior, we computed the percentile rank of each hateful tweet within the full score distribution for its language. S5
Human-AI moderation simulation

S5.1
Formal framework of the human–AI moderation simulation

We model a two-stage human–AI moderation pipeline operating at the language level.
For each language
l
, let:

•

V_{l}
denote the total number of tweets posted per day,

•

H_{l}
denote the number of hateful tweets per day,

•

M_{l}
denote the number of moderators assigned,

•

r
denote the review rate (tweets reviewed per moderator per hour),

•

h
denote the number of working hours per moderator per day,

•

k
denote the number of independent reviewers per tweet.

The total number of tweets that can be reviewed per day in language
l
is:

N^{\text{review}}_{l}=\frac{M_{l}\cdot r\cdot h}{k}.

Given daily tweet volume
V_{l}
, the fraction of tweets that can be reviewed is:

p_{l}=\frac{N^{\text{review}}_{l}}{V_{l}}.

Tweets are ranked in descending order of predicted hatefulness score
\hat{s}_{i}
. For engagement reduction simulations, tweets are ranked by a combined score:

S_{i}=\alpha\hat{h}_{i}+(1-\alpha)\log(1+e_{i}),

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
determines the relative weight assigned to predicted hatefulness
\hat{h}_{i}
and early engagement
e_{i}
.

The top
p_{l}
fraction of tweets are selected for human review.

Coverage.

Coverage is defined as the proportion of all hateful tweets that are surfaced for review:

\text{Coverage}_{l}=\frac{\#\{i\in H_{l}:i\text{ ranked in top }p_{l}\}}{H_{l}}.

Avoided hate engagement.

Let
e_{i}
denote early engagement (measured within ten minutes of posting).
Engagement reduction is defined as:

\text{Engagement Reduction}_{l}=\frac{\sum_{i\in H_{l}^{\text{reviewed}}}e_{i}}{\sum_{i\in H_{l}}e_{i}},

where
H_{l}^{\text{reviewed}}
denotes the subset of hateful tweets selected for review.

Under the assumption that reviewed hateful tweets are successfully moderated prior to further exposure, this quantity represents the share of hate-related engagement that would be prevented through moderation.

S5.2
Parameter justification

We detail below the choice of each operational parameter. Sensitivity of the main results to these assumptions is examined in
S5.4
.

Review rate.

We assumed each moderator reviewed 100 tweets per hour.
8
reports that platform moderators process 600–800 items per day, implying roughly 75–100 items per hour over an 8-hour shift. Because our simulation focused exclusively on text-based content, which is faster to evaluate than images or video, we adopted the upper end of this range (100/hour).

Reviewers per tweet.

We assumed three independent reviewers per tweet. This conservative choice was motivated by the moderate inter-annotator agreement we observed in our annotations (Fleiss’
\kappa=0.50
), consistent with the well-documented ambiguity at the hate/offensive boundary. Three independent reviews substantially reduced the probability that a borderline tweet is incorrectly actioned due to a single reviewer’s judgment. This assumption was conservative relative to current practice at large platforms, where many moderation decisions are taken by a single reviewer or algorithmically.

Share of workforce dedicated to hate speech.

Not all moderation effort concerns hate speech. We estimated the effective share of each language’s moderator workforce dedicated to hate-related content by applying the proportion of manual moderation decisions attributed to hate speech reported in Twitter/X’s 2024 transparency report (31%,
60
) to the total per-language moderator counts from the October 2023 DSA filing
[
61
]
.

Comparison to regulatory penalties.

Revenue was expressed as a share of Twitter’s 2022 global annual revenue of $5.27 billion. We used 2022 as the reference year because it corresponds to the period of data collection and pre-dates the platform’s acquisition and subsequent restructuring.

The EU Digital Services Act (DSA) permits fines of up to 6% of a platform’s global annual turnover for systemic failures to mitigate risks from illegal or harmful content. The UK Online Safety Act permits fines of up to 10% of global annual turnover. Our central estimate of 2.9% of annual revenue to achieve 80% avoided hate engagement across all eight language communities therefore falls below both statutory maxima.

This comparison is approximate in two respects. First, DSA and OSA fines are assessed on
global
turnover, whereas compliance obligations apply only to EU- and UK-based users, respectively. Because our simulations cover all major Twitter language communities, the resources required for EU- or UK-level compliance alone would be lower, making the 2.9% figure a conservative upper bound for regulatory-compliance purposes. Second, fine amounts are statutory maxima; actual penalties depend on regulatory discretion and may be lower. Nonetheless, the 120 million euro fine imposed on Twitter/X by the European Commission under the DSA in December 2025
[
20
]
illustrates that these penalties are actively enforced.
