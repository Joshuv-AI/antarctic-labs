Screening of atherosclerosis diagnostic markers based on lymphangiogenesis-related genes and analysis of immunological characteristics
Authors: Ning Zhu, Junjie Bi, Guoxin Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerosis (AS) was a major cause of cardiovascular disease, and traditional diagnostic methods often fail to detect AS promptly and accurately. Formation of new lymphatic vessels, or lymphangiogenesis, is a crucial process in the development of many diseases. However, the value of lymphangiogenesis-related genes (LRGs) as potential diagnostic markers for AS remained incompletely elucidated.MethodsThis study analyzed AS transcriptome data from comprehensive gene expression omnibus databases. A systematic screening for potential AS biomarkers was performed using the integration of three machine learning techniques. Single-gene enrichment analysis explored the potential biological processes of diagnostic genes in AS. Diagnostic gene features served as the basis for constructing a nomogram, whose predictive efficacy was then assessed and confirmed. Immune infiltration analysis was employed to investigate the immune microenvironment characteristics of AS. To screen for potential drugs targeting diagnostic genes, drug prediction was performed using the DSigDB database. Separately, consensus clustering analysis of AS samples revealed molecular subtypes that differ in biological features.ResultsThis study identified three key LRGs as diagnostic biomarkers for AS and constructed a diagnostic model. The model demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in both training and validation sets. Further enrichment analysis indicated that all three diagnostic genes participate in immune-related biological processes. Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed higher infiltration levels of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils in the AS group. Butein and pimaric acid emerged as potential therapeutic agents targeting these diagnostic genes. Additionally, AS was successfully classified into three molecular subtypes, each exhibiting distinct molecular mechanisms and immune characteristics.ConclusionThis study established a diagnostic model centered on lymphangiogenesis to elucidate the complex immune response characteristics in AS and their associated molecular mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04462-6.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, lymphangiogenesis, Diagnostic biomarker, Immune characteristics

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic disease affecting medium- and large-sized arteries, primarily characterized by lipid deposition, immune cell infiltration, and inflammatory responses [1, 2]. Its progression typically involves early lipid streaks, gradually developing into fibrous plaques and atherosclerotic plaques, followed by processes such as intraplaque hemorrhage, rupture, and thrombus formation [3, 4]. Within AS lesions, thrombosis, platelet aggregation, and unstable plaque rupture can cause arterial narrowing or blockage, subsequently triggering cardiovascular disease [5, 6]. Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of global mortality and morbidity, with AS serving as its underlying foundation [7, 8]. Therefore, early AS identification and enhanced risk factor management not only improve vascular function and structure but also significantly elevate patients’ quality of life [9, 10].

The lymphatic system, as a key component in maintaining homeostasis and immune balance, functions to absorb dietary fats, clear tissue edema, and transport immune cells [11]. In healthy adults, the number and structure of lymphatic vessels in most tissues and organs remain relatively stable under physiological conditions [12]. However, under pathological stimuli such as inflammation, lipid metabolism disorders, and mechanical stress, lymphangiogenesis can be activated and participate in disease progression [13]. Research indicates that lymphangiogenesis—the process of forming new lymphatic vessels—plays a critical role in various diseases (such as tumors and cardiovascular diseases) [14–16]. Reports indicate that the extravascular lymphatic system serves as the primary pathway for cholesterol clearance from AS vessels, and improving lymphatic function promotes AS reversal [17, 18]. Conversely, in mouse AS models, blocking lymphatic drainage or inhibiting VEGFR-3-mediated lymphangiogenesis leads to increased CD3 + T cell infiltration in plaque intima and adventitia, accelerating AS progression [19]. In summary, lymphangiogenesis plays a crucial regulatory role in the pathological process of AS. However, whether lymphangiogenesis-related genes (LRGs) can serve as diagnostic biomarkers for AS remains a scientific question worthy of further investigation.

Against this backdrop, after using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to find AS-related modules, we systematically screened potential diagnostic biomarkers via three machine learning methods and built a nomogram to predict AS occurrence. Consensus clustering identified molecular subtypes. Gene enrichment and immune infiltration analyses explored LRG roles in AS pathogenesis, progression, and diagnosis. This study thus links lymphangiogenesis to AS, offering insights for diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

1. Data source

Transcriptome data for AS (GSE100927 and GSE43292) were downloaded from the gene expression omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. Among these, GSE100927 (AS: 69, control group: 35) served as the training set; while GSE43292 (AS: 32, control group: 32) served as the validation set. Additionally, 660 LRGs were obtained from the study by Yang et al. [18].

2. Screening key AS co‑expression modules with WGCNA

In the training set, differential expression analysis between control and AS samples was carried out utilizing the limma package, with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1 as cutoffs. This set of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was then subjected to WGCNA. Employing a soft threshold power of β = 12, a gene co-expression network exhibiting scale-free topology was generated, and identification of key AS-associated modules was achieved by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between module eigengenes and sample traits.

3. Screening of AS diagnostic genes

To identify diagnostic biomarkers for AS, this study first integrated DEGs, WGCNA key module genes, and LRGs as a candidate diagnostic gene set for AS. Subsequently, three machine learning methods—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis (glmnet package; with 10‑fold cross‑validation, alpha set to 1, λ.min used as the criterion for selecting the optimal λ value), support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE, e1071 package), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, xgboost package)—were applied to this gene set for feature selection. The final AS diagnostic gene set was obtained by integrating the screening results from these methods.

4. Constructing and testing a diagnostic model for AS

Construction of a diagnostic nomogram (rmda package) based on the key genes enabled the prediction of AS probability. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) served to validate the model’s predictive performance and clinical utility, while ROC analysis (pROC package) was carried out on the training and validation sets. Genes with an area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.7 in both sets were identified as candidate diagnostic biomarkers, suggesting a baseline level of discriminative ability.

5. Gene enrichment analysis

The potential roles of the diagnostic genes in AS were investigated via single-gene enrichment analysis (GSEA package). Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (clusterProfiler package) were applied to DEGs specific to each AS subtype (adjusted p-value < 0.05, |logFC| > 0.585 [20–22]) to clarify their functional differences.

6. Integrated multi-algorithm analysis of AS immune characteristics

We employed CIBERSORT, MCP-Counter (IOBR package), and ssGSEA (GSVA package) algorithms to profile immune cell infiltration in the AS and control groups, thereby investigating the immune characteristics of AS. Associations linking diagnostic gene expression to immune infiltration features were subsequently quantified via Pearson correlation tests.

7. Predicting potential regulatory networks of biomarkers

Multiple databases were used in this study to predict regulatory networks: miRNet (https://www.mirnet.ca/) for miRNA targets; NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) for upstream transcription factors (TFs); and DSigDB (http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) for protein-drug interactions. The final integrative visualization was generated using Cytoscape software (v3.10).

8. Identification of AS molecular subtypes

Distinct AS subtypes were identified by performing consensus clustering analysis (ConsensusClusterPlus package) on the training set based on diagnostic gene expression. Subtype stability was evaluated by cumulative distribution function (CDF) together with delta area curve analysis, and principal component analysis (PCA) additionally confirmed how well the subtypes could be distinguished.

9. Data analysis

R software (v4.4.0) was applied to data analysis and visualization. Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests were utilized to determine statistical differences between groups. The correlations were quantified using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients, with p < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

1. WGCNA‑based filtering of key module genes associated with AS

To identify diagnostic biomarkers for AS, this study first performed differential expression analysis between the AS group and the control group in the training set (adjusted p-value < 0.05, |logFC| > 1), yielding a total of 418 DEGs (Fig. 1A). WGCNA was applied to the DEGs. First, a weighted co-expression network was built using a soft threshold power of β = 12 (Fig. 1B). A hierarchical clustering tree was then constructed to detect gene modules (Fig. 1C). Following the merging of highly similar modules, the red module was found to be most strongly associated with AS (cor = 0.72) based on correlation analysis (Fig. 1D). Genes with higher scores within this module exhibited tighter biological associations with AS, comprising 1,292 key module genes (Fig. 1E). A total of 13 candidate diagnostic genes for AS were identified by combining DEGs, LRGs, and WGCNA-derived key module genes (Fig. 1F). Enrichment analysis of this gene set was performed to explore their potential roles in AS (Fig. 1G). GO results indicated these genes participate in cell adhesion processes, such as positive regulation of cell adhesion and cell adhesion mediated by integrin. Enriched pathways identified by KEGG included the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathway and Lipid and AS, among others.

2. Screening of AS diagnostic genes and functional analysis

Three machine learning methods were employed for feature selection from the 13 candidate genes to further identify potential diagnostic genes for AS. Using the minimum Lambda value from LASSO (Fig. 2A-B), Gain > 0 from XGBoost (Fig. 2C), and the minimum error value from SVM-RFE (Fig. 2D), 10, 7, and 5 key genes were identified, respectively. Integrating the three analyses yielded three AS diagnostic genes: metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), elastin microfibril interfacer 2 (EMILIN2), TNF (Fig. 2E). Compared to the control group, these genes showed significantly upregulated expression in the AS group (Fig. 2F). Single-gene enrichment analysis was conducted to assess the potential roles of these diagnostic genes in AS (Fig. 2G-I). Results revealed that all three genes participate in immune-related biological processes, including adaptive immune response, immune effector processes, and leukocyte mediated immunity.

3. Construction and validation of the AS diagnostic model

A nomogram model for personalized AS risk prediction was developed using the diagnostic gene features (Fig. 3A). It integrated expression data from EMILIN2, MMP9, and TNF to graphically map gene expression onto a probability scale. Validation via calibration curves and DCA confirmed the model’s robust diagnostic performance (Figs. 3B-C). According to ROC curve analysis, the AUC was 0.974, and each diagnostic factor had high predictive accuracy when used alone (Fig. 3D). Model performance was further evaluated with an independent validation set, resulting in an AUC of 0.773 (Fig. 3E), indicating robust diagnostic capability.

4. Integrated multi-algorithm analysis of AS immune characteristics

To elucidate the immune characteristics of AS, this study conducted a systematic analysis of immune cell infiltration. Compared with the control group, the AS group had significantly higher infiltration levels of most immune cell types/functions, as revealed by ssGSEA (Fig. 4A). Results from CIBERSORT algorithm analysis indicated higher infiltration levels of memory B cells, M0 macrophages, and follicular helper T cells in the AS group (Fig. 4B). The MCP-Counter algorithm further demonstrated elevated infiltration of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils in the AS group (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that the expression levels of diagnostic genes EMILIN2, MMP9, and TNF positively correlated with the infiltration levels of immune cells such as Macrophages, pDCs, and T helper cells, while negatively correlating with the infiltration level of Type II IFN Response (Fig. 4D).

5. Constructing TF-mRNA and miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks

This study utilized the NetworkAnalysts database to predict upstream TFs for diagnostic genes and constructed TF-mRNA regulatory networks (Fig. 5A). Results indicated that diagnostic genes were regulated by common TFs, such as FOXC1 and SRF, which could regulate three diagnostic genes. Additionally, unique TFs were identified, such as FOS, CEBPB, and JUN, which exclusively regulated MMP9. Using the miRNet online database, we further predicted potential miRNAs for the diagnostic genes (Fig. 5B). Results showed that hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-let-7a-5p, and hsa-miR-34a-5p could simultaneously regulate all three diagnostic genes. Additionally, drug prediction based on the DSigDB database screened potential therapeutic agents targeting diagnostic genes (Supplementary Table S1). The top five drugs ranked by p-value included: butein, pimaric acid, probucol, IMD-0354, and 3,14-Dihydroxybufa-20,22-dienolide (Table 1).

6. Identification of AS molecular subtypes

Based on diagnostic gene expression, consensus clustering identified three molecular subtypes of AS (Fig. 6A). Assessment of clustering stability using CDF and delta area curves confirmed the robustness of this subdivision (Fig. 6B), and PCA further verified clear separations among the subtypes (Fig. 6C). Notably, the three diagnostic genes exhibited the highest expression levels in subtype 3 (Fig. 6D). To investigate immune feature differences among AS subgroups, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Results revealed significantly higher infiltration levels of immune cells such as Plasma cells, Monocytes, and Macrophages M2 in subtype1 compared to the other two subtypes (Fig. 6E). ssGSEA analysis indicated higher infiltration levels of immune cells/functions such as CD8 + T cells, Neutrophils, and Tregs in subtype3 (Fig. 6F). Pairwise differential expression analysis (adjusted p-value < 0.05, |logFC |> 0.585) and intersection analysis identified 19 common genes (Fig. 6G). According to GO analysis, these genes predominantly participate in biological processes including MHC class II‑mediated antigen processing/presentation of exogenous peptide antigen, MHC class II protein complex assembly, and peptide antigen assembly with MHC class II protein complex (Fig. 6H). KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of these genes in pathways including hematopoietic cell lineage, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 6I). In summary, this study successfully identified three AS molecular subtypes with distinct immune characteristics and molecular mechanisms.

Discussion

AS is one of the primary pathological foundations of cardiovascular disease; however, its early lesions are often difficult to identify promptly and accurately through conventional diagnostic methods [23]. Lymphatic vessels are widely distributed throughout multiple organs and tissues, playing crucial roles in maintaining tissue fluid homeostasis, regulating immune function, and facilitating lipid transport [24, 25]. Lymphangiogenesis exerts significant regulatory effects in the pathological process of AS [17, 18]. Using multiple bioinformatics approaches, this study screened and validated three LRGs (EMILIN2, MMP9, TNF) as potential diagnostic biomarkers for AS. A predictive nomogram was built and validated in an independent dataset. In addition, we observed notable immune cell infiltration differences in AS and identified three subtypes with distinct immune/molecular features. A preliminary regulatory network and potential target drugs for diagnostic genes were also explored.

This study identified three key LRGs as diagnostic genes for AS. Among them, MMP9 exerts multiple pro-inflammatory and pro-lesional effects in the AS process [26, 27]. On one hand, MMP9 promotes inflammatory cell infiltration by degrading extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins. On the other hand, it disrupts the ECM surrounding vascular smooth muscle cells, inducing a shift from a quiescent contractile phenotype to a migratory proliferative phenotype. This transformation leads to foam cell formation, accelerating atherosclerotic progression [28]. Clinical studies further confirm that elevated MMP9 levels are an independent predictor of coronary atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability, positively correlated with lipid-rich necrotic core size and plaque rupture [29, 30]. MMP9 is also associated with the vulnerability of intracranial atherosclerotic plaques [31]. Furthermore, Lingguizhugan decoction (LD) enhances ventricular remodeling after myocardial infarction by promoting lymphangiogenesis and modulating the IL-17R/MMP-9/LYVE-1 signaling pathway [32]. Whether LD can alleviate AS progression through similar mechanisms warrants further investigation. TNF is a key inflammatory driver in AS progression. It activates endothelial cell inflammation via TNFR1 and TNFR2 receptors. Endothelial cell injury induces expression of chemokines and adhesion molecules, which bind to ligands on leukocytes (e.g., PSGL1), promoting leukocyte migration into the vascular wall. This enhances plaque inflammation and subsequently triggers plaque rupture [33–35]. In AS mouse models, Hydroxysafflor yellow not only reduces plaque formation, hepatic steatosis, and inflammation-associated lymphangiogenesis but also effectively decreases TNF-α expression, thereby exerting protective effects against AS [36]. Notably, Salvia miltiorrhiza alleviates coronary artery disease by reducing key pro-inflammatory factors such as MMP9 and TNF-α [37]. Whether Salvia miltiorrhiza mitigates AS progression through similar mechanisms warrants further investigation. EMILIN2, an ECM component, plays a crucial role in angiogenesis [38, 39]. Although direct reports on EMILIN2 in AS are lacking, our single-gene enrichment analysis revealed that EMILIN2 participates alongside two other diagnostic genes (TNF and MMP9) in AS immune processes, such as leukocyte mediated immunity and immune response regulating cell surface. This suggests that MMP9, TNF, and EMILIN2 may exhibit synergistic effects within the AS immune regulatory network. However, it should be noted that MMP9, TNF, and EMILIN2 are not specific markers for AS. They are also abnormally expressed in various systemic inflammatory diseases and malignant tumors [40–44], which poses a potential challenge to their diagnostic specificity for AS. Therefore, in clinical practice, relying solely on elevated levels of these three genes may lead to false-positive diagnoses, especially when patients have coexisting inflammatory or neoplastic conditions. To differentiate AS signals from other inflammatory interference signals, the following strategies are recommended: (1) combine with imaging examinations (e.g., coronary CT angiography, intravascular ultrasound) to confirm the presence of plaques [45, 46]; (2) integrate patient clinical history and laboratory exclusion indicators; (3) adopt a multi-marker combined diagnostic model rather than relying on a single gene or protein level [47, 48]. Future studies should include independent validation cohorts with systemic inflammatory diseases/tumor controls to quantify the differential diagnostic specificity of this model. In summary, these three genes highly expressed in AS show promise as novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for AS.

AS is a chronic inflammatory disease involving infiltration of multiple immune cells into the inner layer of the vascular wall [46, 49]. Immune infiltration analysis in this study revealed significantly higher infiltration levels of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils in AS compared to the control group. This aligns with previous reports indicating that NK cells promote AS through perforin and granzyme B production, which accelerate plaque progression and necrotic core enlargement [50]. Neutrophils primarily function following plaque rupture. They interact with activated platelets via P-selectin signaling, forming neutrophil extracellular traps and accelerating thrombosis [51, 52]. CD8 + T cells can induce apoptosis in target cells by releasing cytotoxic perforin and granzyme. Once activated, CD8 + T cells also massively produce the proinflammatory cytokine interferon-γ, thereby promoting AS formation [53]. Furthermore, CD137 (a TNF family member) stimulation drives effector CD8 + T cells to accumulate in regions of low shear stress or hemodynamic disturbance. These T cells secrete chemokines, recruiting additional CD8 + T cells into the plaque, thereby exacerbating the immune-inflammatory microenvironment and tissue damage within the plaque [54]. Whether the TNF highly expressed in AS identified in this study promotes malignant progression of AS through a similar mechanism warrants further investigation. To summarize, targeting the activation state of immune cells in AS could be a possible treatment strategy.

Current treatments for AS, such as statins or proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, aim to control levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol in the blood [55]. However, despite effectively lowering LDL cholesterol to guideline-recommended levels, these therapies reduce major adverse cardiovascular events by less than 50% [49, 56]. Therefore, identifying safer and more effective therapeutic agents is critically important. This study, based on drug screening analysis using the DSigDB database, identified butein and pimaric acid as the most significantly associated potential therapeutic agents with diagnostic genes (p = 8.25E-07). Pimaric acid exhibits potent anti-atherosclerotic activity, inhibiting MMP9 production and cell migration in TNF-α-induced human arterial smooth muscle cells [57]. Butein improves chronic stress-induced AS by targeting anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor pathways [58]. Notably, correlation analysis in this study revealed positive correlations between the expression levels of three diagnostic genes and the infiltration levels of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils. Whether pimaric acid and butein promote lymphangiogenesis and ultimately slow the malignant progression of AS by regulating the activity of these immune cells and the aforementioned diagnostic genes warrants further investigation.

In the three molecular subtypes of AS identified in this study, subtype 3 exhibited the highest overall expression levels of diagnostic genes and showed a relatively high degree of immune cell infiltration (e.g., CD8 + T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages). Previous studies have reported that macrophage death can promote the formation of pro-thrombotic necrotic cores, which are associated with unstable plaques [59]. CD8 + T cells may also contribute to the instability of advanced lesions by influencing smooth muscle cell phenotype [60, 61]. Further analysis revealed that subtype DEGs were mainly enriched in antigen processing and presentation and MHC class II pathways. It has been suggested that macrophages activate T cell immune responses by enhancing MHC class II-mediated antigen presentation, potentially participating in plaque progression [62]. Collectively, we hypothesize that subtype 3 may represent an advanced or even vulnerable plaque stage, where high-level immune infiltration is closely associated with plaque instability.

This study systematically revealed the potential immunological characteristics and molecular mechanisms of LRGs in AS by employing multiple bioinformatics analysis methods, including machine learning approaches, based on chip data from the GEO database. Diagnostic biomarkers identified using machine learning models such as SVM-RFE and XGBoost demonstrated high diagnostic value, achieving AUC values of 0.974 and 0.773 in the training and validation sets, respectively. Potential therapeutic drugs predicted by DSigDB (e.g., butein and PIMARIC ACID) provide new directions for interventions targeting these diagnostic genes. However, this study has several limitations. First, the identified diagnostic biomarkers and predicted candidate drugs lack validation in independent multicenter clinical cohorts. Future studies should include control samples from systemic inflammatory diseases and tumor to quantify the model’s diagnostic specificity, and conduct prospective studies to evaluate its prognostic value. Second, the interactions between LRGs and CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils in lymphangiogenesis require further investigation using in vitro co-culture systems and AS animal models, combined with overexpression/knockdown and signaling pathway blockade experiments to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Third, subtype 3 (possibly corresponding to the vulnerable plaque stage) initially inferred in this study needs precise spatial localization and functional validation through multiplex immunohistochemistry of human arterial tissues, correlative analysis of imaging and pathology, as well as single-cell/spatial transcriptomics.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified EMILIN2, MMP9, and TNF as potential diagnostic biomarkers for AS and constructed a personalized diagnostic model with robust predictive performance. Furthermore, it characterized AS-specific immune infiltration patterns and defined molecular subtypes.
