Host metabolism can produce many indoles and phenols independently of the microbiome
Authors: Jenna E. AbuSalim, Kellen Olszewski, Salma Youssef, Sarah J. Mitchell, Craig J. Hunter, Jessica Little, Ashley Sidebottom, Jacob A. Boyer, Steve D. Knutson, Laith Z. Samarah, Michael R. MacArthur, Alessa L. Henneberg, Rolf-Peter Ryseck, Christiane A. Opitz, David W. C. MacMillan, Mohamed S. Donia, Eric G. Pamer, Sabrina Imam, Christopher J. Lehmann, Olatoyosi Odenike, Joshua D. Rabinowitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indole and phenol metabolites are typically thought to be products of bacterial digestion of tryptophan (indoles) and phenylalanine or tyrosine (phenols). Interest in controlling gut microbial production of these metabolites has continually grown because they have important physiological impacts, with indoles agonizing aryl hydrocarbon receptor signalling and phenols being associated with healthy body weight. Although there is a growing body of research on which bacteria produce these metabolites, the host contribution to their circulating pools has not been characterized. Here, through stable isotope tracing in cell culture, mice and rats, we show that mammalian cells can make aryl-pyruvates, aryl-lactates, aryl-acetates and aryl-carboxylic acids independently of the microbiome. We demonstrate that circulating levels of these metabolites in mice and human patients are robust to perturbations of the microbiome. By contrast, bacterial metabolism is required to synthesize aryl-propionates and free indole, phenol and p-cresol. Overall, these results suggest that host metabolism is a major contributor to circulating indole and phenol metabolite pools.

Main

Indole and phenol metabolites have major impacts on host physiology1–3. Indole metabolites are ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, which is a key mediator of gut immune tolerance and epithelial homoeostasis2–4. Phenylalanine-derived phenol metabolites, such as cinnamic acid, correlate with healthy body weight and can affect glucose and lipid handling1, while tyrosine-derived metabolites, such as phenol sulfate or p-cresol sulfate, are uraemic toxins5,6. Supplementation with phenylalanine-derived or tryptophan-derived metabolites shows promise for the treatment of various diseases. In rodent models, providing indole metabolites alleviates the disease severity of inflammatory bowel disease7–9, while supplementing with cinnamic acid has protective effects against obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease1,10.

As indole and phenol metabolites have important physiologic impacts, there is growing interest in identifying which bacteria are responsible for their production11. A recent study showed that Clostridium species have a metabolic pathway that can reduce aryl amino acids to their respective propionates12. Other species, such as Lactobacillus johnsonii, have also been shown to produce indole-3-pyruvate and phenylpyruvate as well as downstream metabolites3,13,14. Changes in indole and phenol metabolite concentrations are often correlated with changes in microbiome composition2,15.

Although this work highlights a potential role of gut microbial health in controlling indole and phenol metabolite concentrations, microbiome compositional changes are also known to affect host metabolism16. Recently, it was shown that kynurenine, a tryptophan metabolite made by the host, can be induced when mice are colonized with Achromobacter pulmonis17. Additionally, the gut microbiome can even affect glucose homoeostasis through various mechanisms, including by producing short-chain fatty acids that can fuel hepatic gluconeogenesis16,18. IL4I1, a monocyte-specific enzyme, has the capacity to produce indole-3-pyruvate and phenylpyruvate, the transamination products of tryptophan and phenylalanine19–21. This suggests that there may be some contribution of host metabolism to circulating indole and potentially phenol pools. Additionally, recent work showed comparable levels of some indole metabolites between germ-free and conventional mice22. Whether the presence of these metabolites in germ-free mice reflects host metabolism or uptake from diet remains to be determined.

Isotope tracing, such as infusing 13C-labelled substrates, provides a gold-standard method for determining metabolite sources23. Here, we systematically evaluate the sources of circulating indole and phenol metabolites based on: (1) concentrations in germ-free mice; (2) production from a 2 h infusion of 13C-labelled amino acids (this short infusion feeds host cells but not the gut microbiome); (3) production from a 36 h infusion of 13C-labelled amino acids (this long infusion feeds both the host directly and the microbiome indirectly by secreted host protein); (4) modulation by antibiotics of labelling from the long infusions; (5) production from microbial digestion of a 13C-protein diet; (6) 13C-tracing in mammalian cell culture; and (7) cell and mouse-tissue lysate biochemistry. Through these studies, we trace mammalian cell metabolism of aryl amino acids to their respective aryl-pyruvates, aryl-lactates, aryl-acetates and aryl-carboxylic acids (but not aryl-propionates and indole, phenol and p-cresol). We find that the microbiome-derived, but not host-derived, indole and phenol metabolites decline in human patients treated with antibiotics. Therefore, many important indole and phenol metabolites that were thought to be microbiome-derived are substantially synthesized by the host.

Results

Germ-free mice have indole metabolites

We began by measuring indole metabolites in serum from germ-free or control (specific-pathogen-free) mice (Fig. 1a). Standard chow (PicoLab Rodent Diet 20, 5053) has an abundance of microbiome metabolites, including many of the indole species of interest. Compared to chow, purified diets had lower levels of the indole metabolites (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Consistent with increase dietary intake of these metabolites, chow-fed mice had significantly higher levels of indole-3-lactate and indole-3-propionate than mice fed with a purified diet (Extended Data Fig. 1b). Faeces were collected 10 days after initiation of the purified diet to confirm sterility of the germ-free mice, and serum was collected and analysed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Sterility was confirmed through anaerobic and aerobic cultures of dietary and faecal pellets. Some indole metabolites, such as indole-acrylic acid and free indole, were below the detection limit in serum from all mice. Others were readily detected. In serum from germ-free mice, indole-3-propionate was significantly depleted in comparison to control mice, and indoxyl sulfate was absent (Fig. 1b). Indole-3-propionic acid was present in the purified diet (Extended Data Fig. 1a), which may explain why there is a depletion of circulating concentrations but not total ablation as seen with indoxyl sulfate. Notably, other measured indole metabolites (indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-acetate, indole-3-carboxylic acid and indole-3-lactate) were not changed.

Many circulating indole metabolites are host-derived

To trace production of the indole metabolites, we infused minimally perturbative quantities of 13C-tryptophan into the systemic circulation of jugular-vein-catheterized mice for 2 h (Fig. 1c). As shown in prior work by our group24, circulating amino acids do not readily cross into the gut lumen, making the infused 13C-tryptophan inaccessible over this duration to gut microbes24. Consistent with this expectation, there was no detectable 13C-tryptophan in the faeces at 2 h despite 20% enrichment in circulating tryptophan (Fig. 1d). There was also no labelling observed in circulating indole-3-propionate or indoxyl sulfate (Fig. 1d). The remaining indole metabolites, however, were all substantially labelled by the infused 13C-tryptophan in both the circulation and tissues, suggesting that they are rapidly produced from circulating tryptophan by host cells (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). Based on the combination of these tracing data and the concentration data in germ-free mice, we propose that indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-lactate, indole-3-acetate and indole-3-carboxylic acid are substantially host-derived, while indole-3-propionate and indoxyl sulfate production requires microbiome metabolism.

We next sought to observe 13C-labelling in the microbiome-derived indoles. For this purpose, we again infused 13C-tryptophan, but for a much longer duration (Fig. 1e). Previous work by our group showed that bacteria in the gut microbiome use labelled amino acids that are infused for 36 h24, as the infused amino acid reaches the gastrointestinal lumen through host secretion of proteins such as mucins. Faecal tryptophan labelling was indeed observed at 36 h (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Serum measurements at 36 h revealed 13C-labelling into all indole metabolites, including indole-3-propionate and indoxyl sulfate, supporting a source of the microbiome-derived indoles being bacterial processing of tryptophan derived from secreted host protein (Extended Data Fig. 1f). In mice treated for 14 days with broad-spectrum antibiotics (ampicillin, neomycin, vancomycin and metronidazole), luminal tryptophan labelling was retained (Extended Data Fig. 1e), but labelling of indole-3-propionate and indoxyl sulfate was largely ablated (Fig. 1f). Antibiotic efficacy was confirmed by a decrease in both alpha diversity of the microbial composition (Extended Data Fig. 1g) and overall bacterial load (Extended Data Fig. 1h). Crucially, there was no change in labelling for the remaining indoles, supporting their production by the host.

In addition to secreted host proteins, dietary protein is a major source of amino acids feeding the gut microbiota. To assess whether there is a contribution to circulating pools of indoles from dietary amino acids, we gavaged mice with 13C-tryptophan. As tryptophan is a free amino acid, it was readily absorbed by the small intestine into the systemic circulation by 15 min, while the caecum did not show detectable 13C-enrichment until 30 min following the gavage (Extended Data Fig. 1i). Consistent with our 13C-tryptophan infusion experiments, all host-derived indoles were highly enriched by 15 min after gavage (Extended Data Fig. 1i). By contrast, there was very little enrichment of the microbiome-derived indole-3-propionate or indoxyl sulfate, even 2 h following the gavage. To more robustly provide the gut microbiome with 13C-amino acids from diet, we fed mice a purified diet containing 13C-algal protein, which is resistant to small intestinal digestion25 (Fig. 1g). Serum and caecal contents were collected 24 h after initiation of the 13C-enriched diet. Enrichment of the indole metabolites in serum was normalized to 13C-tryptophan enrichment in the caecal content (13%; Extended Data Fig. 1j). After 24 h, there was substantial labelling into indole-3-propionate and indoxyl sulfate (80% and 100% normalized labelling, respectively; Fig. 1h). Opposingly, none of the host-derived indole metabolites were enriched to a greater extent than circulating tryptophan (all less than 3% enriched), consistent with production by host tissues instead of the gut microbiome (Fig. 1h).

Our classification of metabolites as host-derived does not negate the known capacity of the microbiome to make these biochemicals. Rather, it reflects a capacity of the host, independent of the microbiome, to produce the metabolites in physiologically relevant quantities. Based on this definition, we conclude that four of the measured indole metabolites made from tryptophan are host-derived (indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-lactate, indole-3-acetate and indole-3-carboxylic acid) and two are microbiome-dependent (indole-3-propionate and indoxyl sulfate).

The host also produces phenylalanine catabolites

Similar to indole metabolites, phenol metabolites from phenylalanine and tyrosine are commonly considered microbiome-derived15. Other than changes in the aryl side group between tryptophan, phenylalanine and tyrosine, the enzymatic transformations needed to produce the phenols from phenylalanine or tyrosine mimic the transformations needed to produce indoles from tryptophan. Chow contained high levels of several phenols, which were present at lower levels in purified diet (phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate and benzoic acid; Extended Data Fig. 2a). Chow-fed mice also had significantly higher circulating levels of 3-phenylpropionate, consistent with its increased concentration in the chow diet (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Additional phenol metabolites such as phenylacetylglycine and hippuric acid are readily detected in both chow and serum from chow-fed mice but were depleted to near the limit of detection in mice fed a purified diet (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b), suggesting that diet is a major feedstock for these metabolites.

To explore whether there is host production of the other phenylalanine-derived metabolites, we analysed serum by LC–MS from purified diet-fed germ-free and control mice (Fig. 2a). Phenylpyruvate (the transamination product of phenylalanine), phenyllactate (the subsequent reduced product) and cinnamic acid (the associated alpha–beta unsaturated metabolite) were not changed between germ-free and control mice (Fig. 2b). Phenylacetate was below detectable levels in all the serum samples. Similar to tracing indole metabolite production using 13C-tryptophan, we used stable isotope labelling to measure the production of the above phenols from 13C-phenylalanine (Fig. 2c). In a minimally perturbative jugular vein infusion of 13C-phenylalanine for 2 h, there was no detectable 13C-phenylalanine in the faeces. Examining circulating and tissue labelling, the phenylalanine-derived phenols whose levels were maintained in germ-free mice (phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate and cinnamic acid) were robustly labelled (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Phenol metabolites that were significantly decreased in the germ-free mice, by contrast, were labelled modestly (benzoic acid, 20%) or not at all (3-phenylpropionate) (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 2d).

To more fully map the sources of these metabolites, we infused the 13C-phenylalanine tracer for 36 h. By 36 h into the infusion, the circulating 13C-phenylalanine enrichment reached 25% and the faecal enrichment reached 7% (Extended Data Fig. 2e). As with the 2 h infusion, there was full enrichment of phenylpyruvate and 80% enrichment of phenyllactate and cinnamic acid (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Even after 36 h of a 13C-phenylalanine infusion, however, benzoic acid was still only enriched by 30% and no labelled 3-phenylpropionate was detected. Consistent with phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate, cinnamic acid and benzoic acid being substantially host-derived, their labelling from 36 h 13C-phenylalanine infusion was maintained in antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 2e,g,h).

The missing labelling for benzoic acid and 3-phenylpropionate implies that there is another upstream precursor for these metabolites, possibly from dietary contamination or bacterial digestion of dietary protein. To assess the contribution from orally delivered amino acids, we gavaged 13C-phenylalanine. At 15 min after the gavage, there was substantial labelling of phenylalanine in serum (55%) but minimal enrichment in the caecum (3%). Consistent with being host-derived, phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate, cinnamic acid and benzoic acid all had circulating enrichment higher than the caecal phenylalanine enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 2i), but there was still no labelling into 3-phenylpropionate. To deliver the 13C-phenylalanine more robustly to the caecum, we fed mice diets with 13C-algal protein for 24 h (Fig. 2g). Caecal enrichment of phenylalanine reached 42%, while serum phenylalanine enrichment was 12% (Extended Data Fig. 2j). The 3-phenylpropionate was extensively labelled, equivalent to caecal phenylalanine (Fig. 2h). The remaining phenylalanine-derived metabolites (phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate, cinnamic acid and benzoic acid) were labelled less than serum phenylalanine, further supporting their systemic concentrations being substantially maintained by host metabolism independent of the microbiome.

Host metabolism is responsible for circulating hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate

Tyrosine is structurally identical to phenylalanine except for a hydroxyl group on the aryl ring. As such, we reasoned that tyrosine undergoes host-mediated transformation to hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate. Additionally, phenol and p-cresol are metabolites that are understood to come from microbial digestion of tyrosine down to the aryl group or aryl group plus one remaining methyl group11,26. These metabolites are then rapidly sulfated by the host26,27. The dietary content of phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate was higher for the tested purified diet than for chow (Extended Data Fig. 3a), but mice fed a purified diet had similar or lower circulating concentrations of phenol or p-cresol sulfate to chow-fed mice, pointing to these metabolites being made (either by the host or microbiome) rather than simply coming from diet (Extended Data Fig. 3b).

To understand the host contribution to tyrosine-derived metabolites, we measured serum from purified diet-fed germ-free and control mice (Fig. 3a). Hydroxyphenylacetate, hydroxycinnamic acid, free phenol and free p-cresol were below detectable levels in all the serum samples. We next traced host production of tyrosine-derived metabolites. The insolubility of 13C-tyrosine in saline at neutral pH limited the concentration at which tyrosine could be infused. At 2 h after infusion with 13C-tyrosine, the serum enrichment reached only 1% (Extended Data Fig. 3c). As tyrosine can be made from phenylalanine, we measured 13C-enrichment of tyrosine after a 2 h infusion of 13C-phenylalanine. The 13C-tyrosine enrichment from a phenylalanine infusion reached 2%. In both male and female mice, hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate were enriched by the tracer by 2 h into the infusion (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 3c,d). No labelled phenol sulfate or p-cresol sulfate was detected. This suggests that hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate are substantially host-derived, whereas the carbon skeletons of phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate are microbiome-derived (with the sulfation presumably done by the host).

To achieve labelling of phenol and p-cresol sulfate, we infused 13C-tyrosine for 36 h. By 36 h after infusion, the circulating 13C-tyrosine enrichment reached 4% and faecal enrichment was 2% (Extended Data Fig. 3d). All tyrosine-derived metabolites were enriched by 36 h (Extended Data Fig. 3e). To differentiate labelling by host metabolism versus microbiome metabolism, we infused 13C-tyrosine in antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 3e). Consistent with the assignment of hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate as host-derived, there was no change in enrichment between control and antibiotic-treated mice for these two metabolites (Fig. 3f). There was, however, a significant decrease with antibiotics in 13C-enrichment of phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate, consistent with production of these metabolites requiring the microbiome. Oral gavage with 13C-tyrosine aligned with these assignments (Extended Data Fig. 3i).

To deliver 13C-tyrosine preferentially to the microbiome instead of host circulation, we fed mice a diet with 13C-digestion-resistant algal protein for 24 h (Fig. 3g). When normalized to caecal 13C-tyrosine labelling (Extended Data Fig. 3j), circulating phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate had enrichment reaching about 50% (Fig. 3h). Hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate had relative enrichment matching or below circulating 13C-tyrosine enrichment (26%), further supporting phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate, but not hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate, originating from microbial metabolism.

Indole and phenol metabolites are also made by host metabolism in rats

To assess the generality of the above findings, we carried out short (2 h) 13C-tryptophan and 13C-phenylalanine tracer studies in jugular-vein-catheterized Sprague Dawley rats (Extended Data Fig. 4a). As in mice, these tracers appear in the circulation but not faecal contents over this timescale (Extended Data Fig. 4b–d). All of the metabolites that were assigned as host-derived in mice (indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-lactate, indole-3-acetate, indole-3-carboxylic acid, phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate, cinnamic acid, benzoic acid, hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate) became substantially labelled, whereas those that were assigned as microbiome-dependent (indole-3-propionate, indoxyl sulfate, 3-phenylpropionate, phenol sulfate and p-cresol sulfate) did not. These data suggest that mice and rats share conserved metabolic pathways for the production of indole and phenol metabolites.

Overall concentrations of indole and phenol metabolites are similar in faeces and circulation

As the gut microbiome has been thought to be required for the production of indole and phenol metabolites, many studies measure faecal concentrations of these metabolites7,28–30. We measured, from mice on a purified diet, absolute indole and phenol metabolite concentrations from blood, faeces and urine. The most abundant metabolites overall were p-cresol sulfate, phenylpyruvate, hydroxyphenyllactate and indole-3-pyruvate (Extended Data Fig. 5). For a majority of metabolites, urinary concentrations significantly exceed serum ones, consistent with these metabolites being filtered but not reabsorbed. The opposite pattern was observed for p-cresol sulfate, consistent with its high plasma protein binding31,32. Serum and faecal concentrations differed significantly for only five metabolites: two indoles were serum-enriched, and three phenols were faeces-enriched. The faeces-enriched phenols included hydroxyphenyllactate, whose circulating levels are substantially host-derived. This example highlights the potential for extensive local gut microbiome production of metabolites also made by the host.

Human cell lines can produce indole and phenol metabolites

To this end, we cultured colorectal cancer cells (HCT116) and traced the metabolism of tryptophan, phenylalanine and tyrosine (Fig. 4a). Similarly, we cultured HCT116 cells in media containing either 13C-phenylalanine or 13C-tyrosine. The cells produced phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate and cinnamic acid from the added 13C-phenylalanine, further supporting these being host-derived metabolites. The microbiome metabolite 3-phenylpropionate was, as expected, not made from the added tracer (Fig. 4c). Benzoic acid, which comes at least partially from host metabolism in mice, was not produced by HCT116 cells. From 13C-tyrosine, HCT116 cells produced labelled hydroxyphenylpyruvate and hydroxyphenyllactate but not phenol or p-cresol sulfate (Fig. 4d). The HCT116 cells were confirmed to be negative for Mycoplasma contamination (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). Production of these metabolites was also observed in various other common cell lines (Extended Data Fig. 6c–q). Overall, these cultured mammalian cells exhibited a subset of the in vivo transformations observed in rodents, with cells in culture converting aryl amino acids to their respective aryl-pyruvates, aryl-lactates and cinnamic acid.

The metabolic pathways from aryl amino acids to the indole and phenol metabolites could logically proceed initially through transamination to the respective pyruvate analogues. To assess whether transaminase inhibition could block production of the downstream products, we treated HCT116 cells with the pan-transaminase inhibitor aminooxyacetic acid and traced production of indoles and phenols through the addition of 13C-aryl amino acids (Fig. 4e). Aminooxyacetic acid was cytostatic at the given dose (500 µM). Upon treatment with aminooxyacetic acid, production of indole and phenol metabolites by HCT116 cells decreased (Fig. 4f–h). Therefore, isolated mammalian cells can autonomously produce many indole and phenol metabolites through transamination followed by subsequent metabolic reactions.

Characterization of the reactions in cell and tissue lysates

To further characterize the mammalian synthetic pathway to indoles and phenols, we performed biochemical assays in crude lysates from HCT116 cells and from mouse liver. The conversion of the amino acid to aryl-pyruvate is probably through a transaminase, which typically requires the cofactor pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) as well as a nitrogen acceptor such as alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG). With the addition of PLP and α-KG, both types of lysates had robust transaminase activity, converting tryptophan to indole-3-pyruvate and phenylalanine to phenylpyruvate (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). Both lysates also reduced the aryl-pyruvates to aryl-lactates with the addition of NADH (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig. 7d,e) and converted phenylpyruvate to cinnamic acid (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 7f). Interestingly, when phenyllactate was added, no cinnamic acid was formed, suggesting that cinnamic acid production proceeds directly from phenylpyruvate and not through phenyllactate (Extended Data Fig. 7f). Furthermore, when indole-3-pyruvate was added to the lysates (without any further cofactor requirement), the liver lysates, but not cell lysates, produced indole-3-acetate and indole-3-carboxylic acid. Similarly, liver lysates converted phenypyruvate into benzoic acid (Fig. 4k). Therefore, liver lysates can make all the host-derived indole and phenol metabolites.

GOT1 produces indole-3-pyruvate and phenylpyruvate

To understand what enzymes may be responsible for the conversion of aryl amino acids to their downstream products, we started by assessing IL4I1 activity, an enzyme initially discovered as being induced by IL-4 in B cells, but since found to be expressed and excreted predominantly by monocyte lineage cells. IL4I1 reportedly converts phenylalanine to phenylpyruvate and tryptophan to indole-3-pyruvate in vitro19–21. When comparing plasma concentrations from Il4i1−/− and wild-type mice, there were no significant changes in any of the host-derived indole or phenol metabolites, suggesting that IL4I1 is not required for these host pathways (Extended Data Fig. 8a–d).

To find additional host enzymes capable of tryptophan or phenylalanine transamination, we tracked these activities in mouse liver lysates as they were fractionated sequentially by size exclusion (SEC), anion exchange and cation exchange chromatography. The resulting fractions were assayed for indole-3-pyruvate and phenylpyruvate production, with the most active fraction from each step selected for further fractionation. Both activities co-eluted consistently through SEC, anion exchange and cation exchange (Fig. 5a–f). To find candidate enzymes responsible for the activity observed in these lysates, we performed proteomics on fractions exhibiting a wide range of activities from the final cation exchange separation and correlated the abundance of all measured proteins with phenylalanine transaminase enzymatic activity. Out of the top 20 best-correlated proteins (all Spearman correlation > 0.96), keratin 79 (KRT79) had the highest abundance in the most active fraction assayed (Fig. 5g). Glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase 1 (GOT1), one of the most abundant mammalian transaminases, was the next highest abundance. Given that KRT79 does not have known enzymatic activity and GOT1 is a well-characterized amino acid transaminase, we selected GOT1 for further biochemical characterization. With the addition of PLP and α-KG, recombinant purified GOT1 efficiently converted both tryptophan and phenylalanine to their respective aryl-pyruvates (Fig. 5h). The above data differentiate indole and phenol metabolites into host-derived and microbiome-dependent classes. Discussion

We demonstrate, by isotope tracing, that many physiologically important circulating indole and phenol metabolites are derived from endogenous metabolism in mice, rats and cultured human cells. We conclude that there is a conserved metabolic pathway for all three aryl amino acids in mammalian cells that can convert tryptophan, phenylalanine and tyrosine to their respective aryl-pyruvates and aryl-lactates (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Metabolism of aryl amino acids to indole and phenol metabolites.Mammals encode the metabolic capacity to transaminate tryptophan, phenylalanine and tyrosine to their respective aryl-pyruvates and reduce to aryl-lactates. Additionally, there is a mammalian metabolic pathway that can convert phenylalanine to cinnamic acid. Cinnamic acid appears to be made from phenylpyruvate directly, as there was no production of cinnamic acid from added phenyllactate. Although benzoic acid was not made in cell culture, it was produced in the mouse liver lysates and labelled from 13C-phenylalanine over 2 h in vivo, which suggests that benzoic acid is at least partially derived from host metabolism.

Host metabolism is also capable of making indole-3-acetate and indole-3-carboxylic acid from tryptophan. Although the responsible mammalian enzymes are not known, indole-3-acetate was produced in mouse liver lysates from indole-3-pyruvate, the precursor of indole-3-acetate in bacteria and plants35,36. The pathway to indole-3-carboxylic acid remains an open question in both mammals and bacteria37.

Furthermore, there is probably a similar metabolic pathway in humans, given that the indole and phenol metabolites that are host-derived in rodents are not affected by antibiotic-mediated depletion of the gut microbiome in humans. This conclusion is based on two studies. One is a retrospective analysis of metabolomics data from a cohort of 169 patients, of whom only 13 had antibiotics detected in circulation. The other involves three patients with cancer who went on or off broad-spectrum antibiotics. Although the findings of these studies are consistent, studies with larger patient populations are merited to validate the hypothesis that host metabolism is sufficient to maintain the circulating levels of these metabolites.

Not all indole and phenol metabolites can be made through host metabolic pathways. Bacterial metabolism is required for fully freeing the aryl group into indole, phenol or p-cresol. Additionally, the gut microbiome is necessary for reducing the amino acid all the way to indole-3-propionate or 3-phenylpropionate.

Indole-3-lactate and indole-3-acetate are frequently studied metabolites as they mediate important anti-inflammatory effects, with supplementation, in mouse models, ameliorating diseases ranging from inflammatory bowel disease to extreme nearsightedness2,7,38,39. Changes in the levels of these indoles have been correlated with microbiome composition changes in patient populations or mouse models. These correlations are classically interpreted as evidence that the microbiome metabolism is responsible for the production of the metabolites. Our data argue for caution in this interpretation. Although it is clear that the microbiome has the capacity to produce all the indole and phenol metabolites12,28,40, and introducing bacteria with the ability to produce metabolites such as indole-3-lactate has been shown to increase circulating levels28,41, we show that the contribution of host metabolism also needs to be considered in these correlations. For example, correlations may arise owing to diet or disease state affecting both microbial composition and host indole metabolite production or through crosstalk between host and microbiome metabolism.

Overall, an understanding of the source of indole and phenol metabolites is the fundamental step both to developing tools to control their circulating concentrations and to understanding the physiological factors controlling their levels and functions. Our data argue for host metabolism being a major source for physiologically important circulating metabolites such as indole-3-lactate, indole-3-acetate and cinnamic acid.

Methods

Mouse and rat studies

All mouse and rat studies were completed in compliance with protocols approved by the Princeton University Animal Care and Use Committee. Unless otherwise indicated, all mice were C57BL/6NCrl mice (Charles River Laboratories, strain 027). Sprague Dawley jugular-vein-catheterized rats were used (Charles River Laboratories, strain 001). Both mice and rats were 8–12 weeks old and fed a standard purified diet (Research Diets, D11112201). Mice were group-housed on a normal light–dark cycle (08:00–20:00 h), and rats were individually housed on a reverse light–dark cycle (20:30–08:30 h). The rats were acclimated to human handling for 2 weeks before experiments began. All rodents had ad libitum access to food and water.

Germ-free mouse model

C57BL/6N wild-type female germ-free or Murine Pathogen Free mice (Taconic, strain B6-F GF or MPF) were housed in individually vented cages (Allentown Sentry Sealed Positive Pressure system) under a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle. All mice were 9–10 weeks old, group-housed and had free access to water and doubly irradiated standard purified diet (D11112201-1.5Vii). Contamination quality checks were performed on mouse faecal pellets, cage samples and research diet pellets using aerobic and anaerobic cultures. Samples were collected 10 days after initiation of the purified diet.

Human subject details

Patients with a diagnosis of AML who were receiving treatment at the University of Chicago Medicine were recruited to the protocol (IRB23-1365). Written informed consent was obtained, and there was no compensation for participation. Serial collection of serum and stool was performed while hospitalized. Patient samples with examples of microbiome diversity loss and gain were selected to determine whether key metabolites correlate with microbiome composition. All serum–faecal sample pairs were taken within 24 h of each other. Information requests regarding the samples can be directed to Christopher J. Lehmann.

In vivo sample collection and metabolite extraction

Serum samples were collected by tail bleed. Blood was placed on ice immediately following collection, centrifuged at 4 °C, 14,000g for 10 min and moved to another tube to be stored at −80 °C. Faeces were collected fresh, immediately placed on dry ice and stored at −80 °C until processing.

Serum samples were extracted with methanol (40× dilution) and incubated for 10 min on ice. Samples were then centrifuged at 15,000g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a mass spectrometry vial. Faecal samples were collected fresh and placed on dry ice. Faecal and dietary samples were ground at liquid nitrogen temperature with a cryomill (Restch). The resulting powder was extracted with 40:40:20 methanol:acetonitrile:water (40× dilution) for 10 min on ice and centrifuged at 15,000g for 30 min. The resulting extract was moved to LC–MS vials (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 200046; caps, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 501313).

Metabolite measurement

An Orbitrap Exploris 240 or Q Exactive Plus hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) was used. For LC, an XBridge BEH Amide column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.5 μM particle size; Waters) was used with gradient solvent A (95%: 5% H2O: acetonitrile with 20 mM ammonium acetate, 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.4) and solvent B (100% acetonitrile). The gradient ran as follows: 0 min, 90% B; 2 min, 90% B; 3 min, 75% B; 7 min, 75% B; 8 min, 70% B, 9 min, 70% B; 10 min, 50% B; 12 min, 50% B; 13 min, 25% B; 14 min, 25% B; 16 min, 0% B, 20.5 min, 0% B; 21 min, 90% B; and 25 min, 90% B with a flow rate of 150 μl min−1. Injection volume was 10 μl and column temperature was 25 °C. A negative-ion mode was used for the MS scans with a resolution of 140,000 at m/z 200. Automatic gain control target was 5 × 106, and scan range was m/z 75−1,000. Xcalibur (v.4.3; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to collect raw data.

All LC–MS data were analysed using El-Maven (v.0.12.1)42. All 13C-labelling data were corrected for natural 13C abundance using the Accucor package43 (https://github.com/XiaoyangSu/AccuCor). All reported measurements are based on relative ion counts.

Metabolite assignment by LC–MS/MS

LC–MS/MS spectra were collected for sample metabolites and standards using the targeted MS2 scan function on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 at higher-energy collisional dissociation energy of 30 eV. Mass resolution of 15,000, m/z isolation window of 2, automated gain control of 5 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 200 ms were used. Unlabelled standards were from Sigma-Aldrich: tryptophan, phenylalanine, tyrosine, indole-3-pyruvate, indole-3-lactate, indole-3-carboxylic acid, indole-3-propionate, indoxyl sulfate, phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate, cinnamic acid, 3-phenylpropionate, hydroxyphenylpyruvate, hydroxyphenyllactate and phenol sulfate. D5-Benzoic acid (DLM-122), D7-Indole-3-acetic acid (DLM-8040) and D7-p-cresol sulfate (DLM-9786) were from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. LC–MS/MS spectral data for compounds and standards can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

13C-amino acid compounds

All amino acids were from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (13C9-phenylalanine, CLM-2250-H; 13C9-tyrosine, CNLM-439-H; and 13C11-tryptophan, CLM-4290-H). Upon suspicion of contamination of the 13C-tryptophan with 13C-indole-3-acetate, the 13C-tryptophan was further purified by Lotus Separations using a Phenomenex Luna NH2 column (100 Å, 5 µm, 150 mm (length) × 21 mm (internal diameter, i.d.)). Isocratic conditions with 60% A (20 mM ammonium acetate, 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, 95:5 water:acetonitrile, pH 9.6) and 40% B (acetonitrile) at 45 °C and a flow rate of 15 ml min−1 were used.

Jugular vein and carotid artery catheterization

For jugular-vein-catheterized mice, using aseptic surgical techniques, a vascular access button (Instech, VABM1B/25, 25 gauge, one-channel button) was implanted under the back skin and connected to a catheter (Instech, C20PV-MJV1301, 2 French, 10 cm) placed in the right jugular vein. Jugular-vein-catheterized rats were purchased from Charles River Laboratories, with externalized catheters secured by a wound clip. Mice and rats were allowed at least 5 days to recover from surgery before any tracer infusion. Catheters were flushed with sterile saline and refilled with sterile heparin glycerol locking solution (SAI, HGS) at least once per week.

2 h 13C-amino acid infusions

Mice were fed ad libitum for all infusions. At 18:00 h, mice were attached to the infusion line with a swivel and tether (Instech, swivel, SMCLA; line, KVABM1T/25) and acclimated for 1 h. With an infusion pump (SyringePump, NE-1000), the infusate was advanced at a rate of 0.1 μl min−1 per gram mouse weight starting at 19:00 h. Infusates were 5 mM 13C-tryptophan, 2 mM 13C-tyrosine or 15 mM 13C-phenylalanine in sterile saline. For rat infusions, infusion lines were connected by a blunt needle at 07:00 h (during light phase). The lines included a swivel and tether (Instech, swivel, CM375KRP; line, KVABR1T/22 modified by removal of the vascular access button connector). Infusions were started at 08:00 h at a rate of 0.5 μl min−1 per gram weight using the SyringePump pump. Infusates were 5 mM 13C-tryptophan and 30 mM 13C-phenylalanine. After 2 h of infusions, serum was collected by tail snip and faeces were collected fresh. All collected samples were stored at −80 °C.

36 h infusion with and without antibiotic treatment

The antibiotic cocktail (1 g l−1 ampicillin, 1 g l−1 neomycin, 1 g l−1 metronidazole, 0.5 g l−1 vancomycin and 0.5% aspartame) was administered through drinking water. Infusion of the 13C-tracers (5 mM 13C-tryptophan, 2 mM 13C-tyrosine, 15 mM 13C-phenylalanine) was initiated on day 12 with or without antibiotics at 19:00 h, and mice were maintained on antibiotics throughout the infusion. Serum and faeces were collected before infusion started and 2, 12 and 36 h after initiation of the infusion. Mice were fed ad libitum throughout the infusion.

Alpha diversity measurements in antibiotic-treated mice

Bacterial DNA was extracted from faecal samples with the Power Soil DNA Isolation kit (QIAGEN). The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 27F (AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG) and 1492R (CGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT) and LongAmp Taq 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs, M0287L). Thermocycler conditions were 94 °C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 65 °C for 90 s, followed by 10 min hold at 65 °C. The amplified V4 region DNA was isolated by AMPure XP beads. Correct amplicon size was confirmed by gel electrophoresis. Amplicons were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Life Technologies, Q32854) and concentrations were standardized to 15 ng µl−1. Library preparations were prepared using the Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 (SQK-NBD114.96). Sequencing was completed using full-length 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing using an Oxford Nanopore MinION sequencer. Species-level taxonomic annotation was assigned to reads with Emu (v.3.4.5) using default settings and database44–46. Phyloseq47 was used for alpha diversity (Shannon index) analysis.

Bacterial load quantification

Bacterial density was estimated using universal 16S qPCR with primers 515F (GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT) and 806R (GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA). Reaction mixture included 1 μl of template DNA, 12.5 μl PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix Reaction Mix (QuantaBio), 6 μl nuclease-free H2O and 2.25 μl of each primer. Thermocycler conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 40 s and 72 °C for 30 s. Reactions were performed in triplicate. Bacterial DNA concentrations were normalized to whole-genome equivalents based on a mean genome size of 4.50 Mbp, then multiplied by the total template DNA volume (50 μl). DNA concentrations were normalized by the grams of faeces used for DNA isolation.

Gavage with 13C-amino acids

Mice were fasted for 7 h before a gavage (10 ml kg−1) with 15 mM 13C9-phenylalanine, 5 mM 13C11-tryptophan and 2 mM 13C9-15N-tyrosine. Serum and caecal content were collected at 15, 45, 90 or 180 min following the gavage.

Feeding with 13C-digestion-resistant algal protein

Mice were fed a diet with 20% overall protein (10% 13C-algal protein (Sigma-Aldrich, 642878) and 10% rice protein isolate (NAKED Supplements)). Rice protein was used as both rice and algal protein are digestion-resistant. Blood from the portal vein and caecal contents were collected 24 h after diet initiation (08:00 h). For portal vein collection, mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane, and the portal vein was exposed by carefully displacing the intestines. The portal vein was nicked with dissection scissors, and pooled blood was collected from the peritoneal cavity by pipette and transferred to an Eppendorf tube on ice.

Cell culture

All cell lines were obtained from ATCC. Stock cells were maintained in either DMEM (HCT116, CCL-247; A549, CCL-185; HepG2, HB-8065; and HEK293, CRL-1573) or RPMI (THP-1, TIB-202; Jurkat, CRL-2899) with 10% complete FBS. All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were grown to approximately 70–80% confluence. Comparable cell density was confirmed visually and by cell count with a Countess II Hemocytometer. Cells were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the PlasmoTest Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Invivogen).

13C-tracing in cell culture

All cell lines were plated into six-well plates. Then, 24 h after plating, the media was aspirated and switched to media containing 10% dialyzed FBS and one of the 13C-aryl amino acids added (DMEM: 16 mg ml−1 13C-tryptophan, 66 mg ml−1 13C-phenylalaine or 80 mg ml−1 13C-tyrosine; RPMI: 5 mg ml−1 13C-tryptophan, 15 mg ml−1 13C-phenylalaine or 29 mg ml−1 13C-tyrosine). Cells were collected by scraping in 500 μl ice-cold 80% methanol after media aspiration. To inhibit transaminases, HCT116 cells were treated with 500 μM aminooxyacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, C13408) or sterile water in DMEM with dialyzed media for 24 h.

Il4i1−/− plasma samples

Plasma samples from three female (22–23 weeks old) and two male (16 weeks old) whole body Il4i1-knockout mice and three female (22–23 weeks old) and two male (16 weeks old) wild-type mice were provided by C. Opitz. All mice were fed ad libitum with irradiated extruded chow pellets (KLIBA, 3437), and at 14 days of age, they also began receiving autoclaved oat flakes. Plasma samples were stored at −80 °C and prepared for MS analysis in the same way as serum samples.

Tissue lysates

Freshly collected mouse tissues were homogenized using a KIMBLE Dounce tissue grinder set (Sigma-Aldrich, DWK885300-0000) in a 1:4 ratio of tissue to assay buffer (100 mM Tris NaCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 1× Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-Free (Thermo Scientific)). After homogenization, the sample was centrifuged at 4,000g for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted further 1:1 with assay buffer containing the relevant substrates and cofactors, and a baseline sample was taken immediately (and diluted by 500× in ice-cold methanol).

Cell lysates

HCT116 cells at 50–70% confluence were trypsinized, pelleted and washed twice in ice-cold PBS before resuspending in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8, 1 mM beta-mercaptoethanol and 1× complete mini protease inhibitor cocktail with ethylene-diaminetetraacetic acid (Thermo Fisher, 78429)). Pelleted cells were lysed with Emulsiflex (Avestin). Lysed cells were spun for 60 min at 4 °C at 10,000 rpm and allocated into 100 μl aliquots with 20% glycerol, then snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen.

In vitro activity assays

For all in vitro transaminase reactions, including isolated GOT1 (from porcine heart; Sigma-Aldrich 9000-97-9), activity was assessed by the addition of either phenylalanine or tryptophan (2 mM) with PLP (200 µM) and α-KG (20 mM). For dehydrogenase and downstream reaction conditions, 2 mM indole-3-pyruvate or phenylpyruvate was added to lysates, and, except for indole-3-acetate and indole-3-carboxylic acid producing reactions, 20 mM NADH was added to the reaction mixtures. All lysates were placed at 37 °C and protected from light. At the designated times, samples from the lysates were taken and diluted 500× in ice-cold methanol. Reactions were run in duplicate, and metabolite concentrations were quantified by LC–MS using internal spike-in standards.

Protein fractionation

Protein fractionation was completed on an ӒKTA pure chromatography system at 4 °C at a flow rate of 1 ml min−1. For SEC, a HiPrep 16/60 Sephacryl S 200 HR column with a column volume (CV) of 120 ml was equilibrated with 2 CVs of running buffer before the application of 1 ml of sample. Proteins were eluted isocratically over 1.5 CVs of running buffer, and 5 ml fractions were collected. The running buffer composition was 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 1 mM TCEP and 10% glycerol.

For anion exchange chromatography, a HiTrap Capto Q ImpRes column (CV of 1 ml) was equilibrated with 5 CVs of 2.5% buffer B before application of 1 ml of sample. The gradient conditions were as follows: 2.5% buffer B, 5 CVs; ramp to 100% buffer B over 20 CVs; 100% buffer B, 5 CVs; 2.5% buffer B, 5 CVs. Next, 1 ml fractions were collected starting at the ramp step. Buffer A was 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and buffer B was 1 M NaCl, 25 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol.

For cation exchange chromatography, a HiTrap Capto SP ImpRes column (CV of 1 ml) was used under the same chromatography conditions as for anion exchange, except for the buffer composition. Buffer A was 25 mM Bis-Tris (pH 6.0), 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol and buffer B was 1 M NaCl, 25 mM Bis-Tris (pH 6.0), 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol.

For sequential fractionation, the fraction of interest from the first chromatographic run was exchanged into the initial buffer composition for the second run using a HiTrap Desalting column (CV of 5 ml). The column was equilibrated with 1.5 CVs of the target buffer before application of 1 ml of the sample. Protein was eluted isocratically in 1.5 CVs of the target buffer, and 0.5 ml fractions were collected. Fractions containing >90% of the protein according to the absorbance readings at 280 nm were pooled for the second round of fractionation. SEC fractions were first concentrated from 5 ml to 1 ml using Amicon Ultra-15 spin filter tubes with a 10 kDa cutoff.

Proteomics

Fraction 6 from the anion column and fractions 1, 3, 5 and 7 following the cation column were used for proteomic analysis. For each tested fraction, a 10 µg sample was diluted with RIPA buffer (125 µl). DTT was added to a final concentration of 10 mM, and lysate was heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Then, iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 15 mM and tubes were incubated in the dark with rotation. After 30 min, an additional 10 mM DTT was added (final DTT concentration of 20 mM) and incubated at room temperature with rotation. After 15 min, 2 μl of a prepared 50 μg μl−1 Sera-Mag bead stock was added, followed by 450 µl ethanol, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 5 min with shaking at 1,000 rpm. Beads were then pelleted on a magnetic rack and supernatant was discarded. The beads were washed three times with 200 µl 80% ethanol before being resuspended in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. 0.5 µg trypsin was added to each tube and sonicated for 30 s in a water bath, followed by incubation for 18 h at 37 °C with rotation. Then, 1 µl Optima LC–MS formic acid was added, and supernatants were passed through a 0.22 µm spin filter column (CoStar, 98231-UT-1). The resulting sample was transferred to an LC–MS vial.

Label-free, data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics was performed on Bruker TimsTOF Pro 2 connected to a nanoElute LC. A 1 µl aliquot of each sample was injected onto a trap column (C18 Pepmap, 5 µM particle size, 5 mm length, 300 µM i.d.), followed by separation by an analytical column (C18 ReproSil AQ, 1.9 µM particle size, 100 mm length, 75 µM i.d.). Peptides were eluted by an acetonitrile/water gradient at a column temperature of 40 °C (buffer A, 0.1% formic acid/water; buffer B, 0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile; flow rate of 0.5 µl min−1; gradient: start at 2% B, increase to 35% B over 20 min, then increase to 95% B over 0.5 min, hold at 95% for 2.25 min). Scans were performed in positive ion, diaPASEF mode over a m/z range of 100–1,700 and ramp time of 100 ms, accumulation time of 100 ms and duty cycle of 100%, ramp rate of 9.43 Hz, MS averaging set to 1. Absolute thresholds were set to 5,000 for mobility peaks and 10 for MS peaks; data were collected with Bruker Compass HyStar v.6.2. Resulting raw data (.d files) were processed using DIA-NN v.1.8.1 with the following parameters: trypsin/P digestion, three missed cleavages, three max. variable modifications, N-term M excision, Ox(M), Ac(N-term) and C-carbamidomethylation, peptide length range of 7–30, precursor charge range 1–4, m/z range 300–1,800, fragment ion range 200–1,800, mass accuracy and MS accuracy both set to ten, precursor false discovery rate set to 1%. Within the DIA-NN algorithm, the following settings were applied: ‘Use isotopologues’, ‘MBR’ (match between runs), ‘No shared spectra’ and ‘Heuristic protein inference’. A spectral library generated in DIA-NN from all known Mus musculus proteins was used (in silico spectral library, generated in DIA-NN using FASTA of Uniprot M. musculus proteome UP000000589, options selected were ‘FASTA digest for library-free search/library generation’ and ‘Deep learning-based spectra, RTs and IMs prediction’; other parameters same as described above). After processing, matrix.pg files were analysed in Perseus (v.2.0.7.0)48; intensities are inputted as ‘main’ while the other descriptors are listed as ‘categorical’. Data are presented as raw ion counts in each chromatography fraction obtained during purification.

Boston Children’s patient dataset

Patient sample data were obtained from a study completed at Boston Children’s Hospital33. The metabolomics dataset from this study was deposited in MetaboLights33,34. A missing value imputation was performed, allowing for samples in which the metabolite was below detectable limits to be filled with the minimally detected ion count for that metabolite. Out of this dataset, patients with antibiotics were selected based on antibiotics detected in serum.

Metabolomics and microbiome sequencing of samples from patients with AML

For metabolic analysis details, methods can be accessed under MassIVE MSV000099027. In brief, for extraction, four volumes of extraction solvent (100% methanol spiked with 500 nM (acetyl-)D3-melatonin (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, DLM-7101-0 as internal standard)) were added to one volume (200 µl) of plasma. Samples were incubated at −80 °C for at least 1 h to overnight. Indole metabolites were analysed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II liquid chromatography system coupled to an Agilent 6470 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, operating in positive mode, with an Agilent Jet Stream Electrospray Ionization source. Data analysis was performed using MassHunter Quant software (v.B.10, Agilent Technologies) and confirmed by comparison with authentic standards (Supplementary Table 2). For phenol metabolites, plasma samples and standards were analysed on a Thermo Fisher liquid chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap IQ-X mass spectrometer, operating in positive mode. Raw data files were analysed in Thermo Freestyle software. Peak integration using the ICIS algorithm in Freestyle was done on extracted ion chromatograms for the m/z of each compound within 3 ppm of the m/z in each standard compound injection (Supplementary Table 3). A missing value imputation was performed, allowing for samples in which the metabolite was below detectable limits to be filled with the minimally detected ion count for that metabolite.

For sequencing, shotgun DNA sequencing was used for the faecal samples from patients with AML. A bead beater (BioSpec Product) was used for mechanical disruption, followed by sample purification with QIAamp mini spin columns (Qiagen). Purified DNA was quantified by a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, and an Illumina HiSeq platform was used for sequencing. A total of 7–8 million paired-end DNA sequencing reads (read length of 150 bp) were produced per sample. Adaptors were trimmed from the raw reads, and quality was assessed using Trimmomatic (v.0.39)49. Human genome reads were removed by kneaddata (https://github.com/biobakery/kneaddata, v.0.7.10), and taxonomy of the resultant high-quality reads was profiled with metaplan4 (ref. 50). MEGAHITS (v.1.2.9)51 was used to assemble microbial reads with genes called by prodigal (https://github.com/hyattpd/Prodigal, v.2.6.3).

Statistical analysis and figure production

A P value of <0.05 was used to determine statistical significance using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments, and there was no randomization. Unless otherwise stated, data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. No animals were excluded unless the infusions did not reach their desired endpoint, either through technical error or death of the mouse. Figures were made with GraphPad Prism (v.10.2.3), ChemDraw (v.23.1.1) and NIH BioArt (https://bioart.niaid.nih.gov).
