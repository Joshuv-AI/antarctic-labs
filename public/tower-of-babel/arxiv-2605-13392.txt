Tighter relaxations for MAP-MRF optimization via Singleton Arc Consistency
Asaf Lev-Ran, Pavel Arkhipov, Vladimir Kolmogorov

Abstract
We consider the MAP-MRF inference task, that is, minimizing a function of discrete variables represented as a sum of unary and pairwise terms. A prominent approach for tackling this NP-hard problem in practice is to solve its natural LP relaxation and then iteratively tighten the relaxation by adding clusters . Based on some theoretical observations, we propose a new technique for identifying such clusters. It works by running the Singleton Arc Consistency algorithm in a certain CSP instance. Experimental results indicate that the new tightening technique outperforms the previous approach by [ SCL12 ] that searches for frustrated cycles . Our code will be made available at https://github.com/vnk-ist/MAP-MRF/ .



1
Introduction

This paper focuses on the problem of minimizing functions of discrete variables represented as a sum of unary and pairwise terms:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x),\qquad\quad f[\theta](x)=\sum_{v\in V}\theta_{v}(x_{v})+\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\theta_{uv}(x_{u},x_{v})

(1)

The problem is specified by a graph
G=(V,E)
, a discrete set of labels
\mathcal{X}_{v}
for each node
v\in V
,
unary costs
\theta_{v}:\mathcal{X}_{v}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for each
v\in V
, and pairwise costs
\theta_{uv}:\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

for each
(u,v)\in E
.
The set of labelings is defined as
\mathcal{X}=\prod_{v\in V}\mathcal{X}_{v}
.

Under a probabilistic interpretation, this problem is known as

MAP inference in MRFs or CRFs
, i.e., maximum a posteriori inference in
Markov random fields or conditional random fields, in the computer vision and
machine learning communities
[
BKR11
]
. In the constraint
programming and constraint satisfaction communities, problem (
1
) is known as
Weighted CSPs
or

Cost Function Networks

[
CdGSF+10
]
.

MAP-MRF optimization was a prominent approach in computer vision before the
deep-learning era, especially for problems that could be formulated as discrete
labelings with spatial smoothness, such as image segmentation, stereo, restoration,
and object labeling
[
SZS+08
,
BKR11
]
.
More recently, MRF/CRF ideas are often used as structured components coupled to
learned representations rather than as standalone hand-engineered models. Examples
include learned potentials and neural message passing for stereo matching

[
GWL24
]
, learned MRF layers over discrete image tokens for
efficient text-to-image generation
[
JGR+24
]
,
SAM-based area matching formulated as an MRF energy minimization problem solved
by graph cuts
[
ZZ24
]
, neuralized MRFs for interaction-aware human
trajectory prediction
[
FHL25
]
, and voxel-adaptive message
passing for deformable CT registration
[
ZZW+25
]
.

Applications outside computer vision include sequence labeling tasks such as
named entity recognition, part-of-speech tagging, and chunking

[
MH16
,
LBS+16
,
WQHS21
]
, protein
design
[
TAA+13
,
AAB+14
]
, and recent deep
protein-design methods that incorporate MRF-style components

[
RYBZ24
,
RZZ24
]
. Related pairwise MAP formulations
also arise in Markov-logic knowledge-base reasoning
[
FLCS23
]
,
while the binary case is widely studied in operations research as QUBO or
UBQP
[
KHG+14
,
GKHD22
]
.

LP relaxations and tightening
Although problem (
1
) is NP-hard in general,
it has been demonstrated that many practical instances can be solved to optimality.
A prominent approach is
cluster-based tightening

[
SMG+12
]

which starts by solving an LP relaxation of (
1
) and then iteratively tightens
the relaxation by adding clusters of nodes. The success of this approach depends crucially on which
clusters are added. Early approaches chose clusters from a fixed dictionary, e.g. the set of all triplets
(3-cycles in the graph)
[
SMG+12
,
KP08
,
BNK11
]
.

[
SCL12
]
showed how to avoid fixed dictionaries by
proposing an algorithm for computing “frustrated cycles”, while showing that after adding these cycles the dual objective can be improved.

The main contribution of this paper is a new algorithm for computing clusters.
It is based on running a
Singleton Arc Consistency
(SAC) algorithm on a certain CSP instance.
We prove several properties of this algorithm. Roughly speaking, they can be formulated
as follows: (1) If SAC produces a cluster then after adding this cluster either the dual objective can be improved or some fractional vertices are cut off from the local polytope.
(2) If the instance has at least one frustrated cycle then SAC will find a cluster.
For more formal statements we refer to Sections
3
and
5
.

Our experimental results show our SAC tightening procedure outperforms the method in
[
SCL12
]

by consistently finding the same or better bounds.

Other related work

[
SJ07
]
proposed to tighten relaxations of MAP-MRF problems
by adding cutting planes that generalize
odd-cycle inequalities
for MAX-CUT.
Their separation algorithm works in the primal domain,
and requires a (fractional) primal solution of the relaxation. In contrast,
we focus on tightening techniques that use only dual solutions (“reparameterization”),
since relaxations of large-scale problems are often solved via message-passing techniques
that work only in the dual domain.

Tightening techniques require introducing high-order terms, which can become expensive when the number of labels
is large.
[
SGJ08
]
address this issue by proposing to use
coarse partitions
on labels
for high-order terms. Note that this approach is orthogonal to the question studied in this paper.

[
Wer09
]
presented an approach
based on the language of Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs), by showing
that all unsatisfiable CSP subproblems could potentially tighten the relaxation.
However, no concrete algorithm for identifying such subproblems was proposed.

In this work we use a particular CSP algorithm, namely Singleton Arc Consistency (SAC).
This algorithm was recently used in
[
DWdG23
]

for identifying
superreparameterizations
that improve the lower bound.
At each iteration they replace current vector
\theta
with another vector
{\overline{\theta}}

that satisfies
f[{\overline{\theta}}](x)\leq f[\theta](x)
for all labelings
x
,
and guarantees that
{\overline{\theta}}
gives a better lower bound than
\theta
.
This approach is not comparable with the tightening techniques that we consider:
it can be computationally less costly since it does not introduce any high-order terms,
but it may also decrease the minimum
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x)

(which would never be “undone” in later iterations).

Note that a simpler Arc Consistency (AC) algorithm has been used for approximately solving the standard LP relaxation of (
1
) (without tightening). In particular, an AC-based algorithm for establishing the
Virtual Arc Consistency
has been given in
[
CdGS+08
,
CdGSF+10
]
, and is similar to the
Augmenting DAG
algorithm
[
Sch76
,
Wer09
]
.

There are also alternative tightening approaches based on more expensive computational tools such as semidefinite programming relaxations, see e.g.
[
DKS22
]
.
We limit the scope of this work to LP relaxations, which arguably can scale to larger problems.

A survey of tightening techniques for MAP-MRF optimization can be found in
[
KKP19
]
.

2
Background

The cluster-based tightening approach
[
SMG+12
]
starts with the objective function in (
1
)
and then repeats the following procedure:

•

Add some zero-valued higher-order terms to the current objective, leading to the following optimization problem:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x),\qquad\quad f[\theta](x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{F}}\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})

(2)

Here
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{V}
is a set of non-empty subsets of
V
, called
factors
.
For a factor
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
we denote
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny\tt~def~}}}{{=}}}\prod\limits_{v\in\alpha}\mathcal{X}_{v}
,
and for a labeling
x\in\mathcal{X}
we let
x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}

be the restriction of
x
to
\alpha
.
By construction,
\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})=0
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F},x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
with
|\alpha|\geq 3
.

•

Approximately solve a natural LP relaxation of (
2
), or rather its dual.

Note that although discrete optimization problems (
1
) and (
2
) are equivalent,
their LP relaxations are not: the lower bound given by the relaxation of (
2
) is the same or larger than that of (
1
).
Below we describe these two steps in more detail.

2.1
LP relaxation

A class of relaxations of (
2
) has been presented in
[
Wer09
]
.
Each relaxation is specified by a directed acyclic graph
(\mathcal{F},J)

where
J
is a set of pairs of the form
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{F}
and
\beta\subset\alpha
.
Define the
J
-based local polytope

\mathcal{L}(J)
as follows:

\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{L}(J)=\left\{\mu\geq 0\;\begin{picture}\put(3.0,-20.0){\line{0}{1}{44.0}}\end{picture}\;\mbox{\begin{tabular}[]{ll}\;\;\; $\;\sum\limits_{x_{\alpha}}\hskip 14.0pt\mu_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})=1$&$\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{F}\!\!\!$\\
$\sum\limits_{x_{\alpha}:x_{\alpha}\sim x_{\beta}}\mu_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})=\mu_{\beta}(x_{\beta})$&$\forall(\alpha,\beta)\in J,x_{\beta}\!\!\!$\end{tabular}}\right\}\hskip-5.0pt

(3)

Here notation
x_{\alpha}\sim x_{\beta}
for factors
\alpha,\beta\subseteq V

means that
x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
and
x_{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{\beta}

are labelings that agree on the overlap
\gamma=\alpha\cap\beta
, i.e.
(x_{\alpha})|_{\gamma}=(x_{\beta})|_{\gamma}
.
A relaxation of (
2
) is now given by

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}(J)}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{F},x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}}\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\mu(x_{\alpha})

(4)

In this work we will tighten the relaxation only with triplets, i.e. only add subsets
\alpha\subseteq V
with
|\alpha|=3
.
If such
\alpha
contains a pair of nodes
u,v
with
\{u,v\}\notin E
then we also add such factor
\{u,v\}
to
\mathcal{F}

(with zero costs). Set
J
will always be the Hasse diagram of the poset on
\mathcal{F}
:

J=\{(\alpha,\beta)\>:\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{F},\beta\subset\alpha,|\alpha|=|\beta|+1\}
.
Note that when adding a new factor we check whether it already exists; if yes, then we do not duplicate it.

Reparameterizations and the dual problem

It is straightforward to check that the following transformation does not change the function
f[\theta]
:
pick edge
(\alpha,\beta)\in J
, labeling
x_{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{\beta}
, value
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
and then update
\theta_{\beta}(x_{\beta})\rightarrow\theta_{\beta}(x_{\beta})+\delta
and
\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\rightarrow\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})-\delta

for all
x_{\alpha}\sim x_{\beta}
. Such operation is called a
reparameterization
.
The set of all reparameterizations can be described by a vector
\lambda

with components
\lambda_{\alpha\beta}(x_{\beta})
for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in J
,
x_{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{\beta}
using the following rule:

{\overline{\theta}}_{\beta}(x_{\beta})=\theta_{\beta}(x_{\beta})+\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in J}\lambda_{\alpha\beta}(x_{\beta})-\sum_{(\beta,\gamma)\in J}\lambda_{\beta\gamma}(x_{\gamma})

(5)

where
x_{\gamma}
in the last term is the restriction of
x_{\beta}
to
\gamma
.
Vector
\lambda_{\alpha\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}_{\beta}}
is also known as a
message
from
\alpha
to
\beta
.
We will write
{\overline{\theta}}=\theta^{\lambda}
for vector
{\overline{\theta}}
obtained from
\theta,\lambda
as in (
5
).
Clearly, we have
f[\theta^{\lambda}](x)=f[\theta](x)
for any
\theta,\lambda
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

For each vector
\theta
the following expression gives a lower bound on
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x)
:

\Phi(\theta)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{F}}\min_{x_{\alpha}}\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})

(6)

The dual problem to (
4
) is equivalent to
maximizing this bound over messages
\lambda
(see
[
Wer09
]
):

\max_{\lambda}\Phi(\theta^{\lambda})

(7)

Message passing algorithms
Solving (
4
) or its dual (
7
) exactly
using generic LP solvers can be too expensive in practice for large-scale problems.
A large number of approximate techniques have been proposed for this problem.
A prominent family are
message passing
algorithms which maximize the bound
\Phi(\theta^{\lambda})

via a block-coordinate ascent
[
Kol06
,
GJ07
,
Wer09
,
SMG+12
,
Kol14
,
SKS17
]
.
In this work we use the SRMP algorithm
[
Kol14
]
.
Note that for pairwise MAP-MRF problems it is equivalent to TRW-S
[
Kol06
]
.

2.2
Cluster pursuit via frustrated cycles

Next, we discuss which clusters to add. As described in the introduction,
the technique which is most relevant to our paper is
[
SCL12
]
.
It works as follows. First, it selects a set
\Pi_{v}
for each node
v\in V
,
where each
\pi=(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})\in\Pi_{v}
is a partition of
\mathcal{X}_{v}
into two non-empty sets:
\mathcal{X}_{v}=\pi^{+}\sqcup\pi^{-}
.
Let
\Pi
be the union of partitions
\Pi_{v}
over
v\in V
(which we assume to be disjoint).
Now consider partitions
\pi_{u}\in\Pi_{u}
,
\pi_{v}\in\Pi_{v}
.
If
\{u,v\}\in E
then define

w(\pi_{u},\pi_{v})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}\\
\llbracket a\in\pi_{u}^{+}\rrbracket\>\neq\>\llbracket b\in\pi_{v}^{+}\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\theta_{uv}(a,b)-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}\\
\llbracket a\in\pi_{u}^{+}\rrbracket\>=\>\llbracket b\in\pi_{v}^{+}\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\theta_{uv}(a,b)

where
\theta
is the current reparameterization, and
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
is the Iverson bracket notation. Otherwise set
w(\pi_{u},\pi_{v})=0
.
Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Pi,\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph with the edges

\mathcal{E}=\{\{\pi_{u},\pi_{v}\}\>:\>|w(\pi_{u},\pi_{v})|>\varepsilon\}
where
\varepsilon
is a fixed positive constant.
Call edge
\{\pi_{u},\pi_{v}\}\in\mathcal{E}

positive
if
w(\pi_{u},\pi_{v})>\varepsilon
, and
negative
if

w(\pi_{u},\pi_{v})<-\varepsilon
. Now suppose that
\mathcal{G}
has a cycle
\mathcal{C}
that contains an odd number of negative edges;
such a cycle is called
frustrated
. Let
C
be the cycle in
G
obtained from
\mathcal{C}
by replacing each node
\pi_{v}\in\Pi_{v}
of the cycle with
v
.
It can be seen that for any labeling
x\in\mathcal{X}
there exists an edge
\{u,v\}\in C

such that
\theta_{uv}(x_{u},x_{v})>\min\limits_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}}\theta_{uv}(a,b)+\varepsilon
. Motivated by this observation,
[
SCL12
]

propose to tighten the relaxation using cycle
C
. This can be achieved by first triangulating
C
and then
adding each triplet as a high-order term. As
[
SCL12
]
observe,
after this operation there exists a reparameterization that increases the lower bound by at least
\varepsilon
.

[
SCL12
]
also show that frustrated cycles in
\mathcal{G}
can be found in linear time.
This defines an algorithm for tightening relaxations.
We discuss further details of this algorithm in Section
5
.

2.3
CSPs and Singleton Arc Consistency

A
Constraint Satisfaction Problem
instance is given by a set of nodes
V
, a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}=\prod_{v\in V}\mathcal{X}_{v}
,
a set of factors
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{V}
, and relations
\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
.
We will denote the instance as a tuple
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})
, or just as
\mathcal{Y}
when
V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F}
are clear from the context.
We will write
\mathcal{Y}\preceq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
if
\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
.
A
solution
to
(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})
is a labeling
x\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying
x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
.
The instance is called
non-empty
if
\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}\neq\varnothing
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
, and
satisfiable

if it has at least one solution.

Definition 1
.

Consider CSP instance
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})
and graph
(\mathcal{F},J)
.
Instance
\mathcal{I}
is called
J
-arc-consistent if every
(\alpha,\beta)\in J

satisfies
\{x_{\beta}\in\mathcal{X}_{\beta}\>:\>x_{\alpha}\sim x_{\beta}{\mbox{~}for~\!~some~}x_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}\}=\mathcal{Y}_{\beta}
.
It is
J
-consistent if there exists non-empty instance
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\preceq\mathcal{Y}
which is
J
-arc-consistent.

For a vector
\theta
and value
\varepsilon\geq 0
we define the CSP instance
CSP_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})
by setting

\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}=\{x_{\alpha}\>:\>\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\leq\min_{y_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}}\theta_{\alpha}(y_{\alpha})+\varepsilon\}
.
The following fact is well-known
[
Wer09
,
CdGSF+10
,
Kol14
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Consider instance
\mathcal{I}=CSP_{0}(\theta)
for a vector
\theta
.

(a)
If
\mathcal{I}
is satisfiable then
\Phi(\theta)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x)
,
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
is a solution of
\mathcal{I}
if and only if
x\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f[\theta](x)
.

(b)
Reparameterization
\mathcal{I}
is
J
-consistent if and only if
\theta
is a local maximum of a block-coordinate
ascent algorithm such as SRMP, i.e. no sequence of operations of SRMP can increase
\Phi(\theta)
.

2.4
2-CSPs

We say that instance
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})
is a
k
-CSP
if
|\alpha|\leq k
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
.
We will be mainly working with the 2-CSP instance
CSP_{\varepsilon}^{\leq 2}(\theta)

obtained from
CSP_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
by removing all factors
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\geq 3
.
In this section we discuss some algorithms for 2-CSPs.
We will assume that
\mathcal{F}=V\cup E
where
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
is a set of edges.
We say that it is
arc-consistent
if it is
J
-arc-consistent
for the set of edges
J=\{ev\>:\>e\in E,v\in e\}
.

Arc consistency (AC) algorithm

Let us consider the following problem: given instance
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},V\cup E,\mathcal{Y})
,
compute a maximal instance
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
which is arc-consistent. Clearly, this can be done by
repeating the following two operations while possible.

1.

Pick pair
(a,b)\in\mathcal{Y}_{uv}
for
\{u,v\}\in E
s.t.
a\notin\mathcal{Y}_{u}
.
Remove
(a,b)
from
\mathcal{Y}_{uv}
.

2.

Pick label
a\in\mathcal{Y}_{u}
for
\{u,v\}\in E
s.t.
(a,b)\notin\mathcal{Y}_{uv}
for all
b\in\mathcal{X}_{v}
.
Remove
a
from
\mathcal{Y}_{u}
.

These operations can be implemented in
O(|E|\cdot(\max_{v}|\mathcal{X}_{v}|)^{2})
time
[
BRYZ05
]
.
Furthermore, if it produces an empty CSP (i.e. if the domain of one of the variables becomes empty) then
it is possible to find a
minimal
sequence of such operations in the same time.

Singleton arc consistency (SAC)

Instance
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},V\cup E,\mathcal{Y})
is called
singleton arc consistent

if the following holds for each
v\in V
and
a\in\mathcal{Y}_{v}
:
fixing the label of
v
to
a
(i.e. replacing
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
with
\{a\}
) and then running the arc consistency algorithm gives a non-empty instance
[
DB97
]
.
Such consistency can be achieved by repeating the following operation: pick node
v\in V
and label
a\in\mathcal{Y}_{v}
,
fix the label of
v
to
a
, run the AC algorithm; if it results in an empty CSP then remove
a
from
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
. Efficient implementations of such an algorithm have been studied in
[
BE04
]
.

3
SAC tightening theory

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the current set of factors, and
\theta
be the current reparameterization.
Suppose that fixing node
r\in V
to label
s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}
in the CSP instance
\mathcal{I}=(V,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Y})=CSP_{\varepsilon}^{\leq 2}(\theta)
and then running the arc consistency (AC) algorithm makes the CSP instance empty. From the results of
[
Wer09
]
we can conclude that after adding factor
\alpha
to the relaxation the lower bound can be strictly increased, where

\alpha
is the set of all nodes that were touched by the AC algorithm. In this section we will refine this result. Based on this refinement, in the next section we will then propose a specific algorithm for tightening. The proofs are given in the appendix.

Throughout this section, let
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,K}
be the sequence of labels that were removed during AC, with
a_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{v_{i}}
. Let
u_{i}
be the vertex that caused the deletion of
a_{i}
, i.e.
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}\in E
, and for no
b\in R_{u_{i}}
we had
(b,a_{i})\in R_{u_{i}v_{i}}
. The value of
\varepsilon
is fixed throughout the section. The AC run can terminate either with the deletion of
s
(in this case,
a_{K}=s
) or with the deletion of the last label in some other vertex
v\neq r
. In the latter case, we artificially extend the sequence
a_{i}
with
s
. Thus, we now always have
a_{K}=s
,
v_{K}=r
. Consider the relaxation obtained from the current one by adding triplets
\{r,u_{i},v_{i}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,K
to
\mathcal{F}
,
as described in Section
2
.
Assume that vector
\theta
is set to zero for newly added factors, and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the new set of factors.
We can prove that our tightening is indeed a strict tightening, in the sense of the theorem stated below.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a reparameterization
{\overline{\theta}}
of
\theta
such that
\min_{x_{\alpha}}{\overline{\theta}}_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\geq\min_{x_{\alpha}}\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, and
{\overline{\theta}}_{r}(s)>\theta_{r}(s)
.

In the above theorem, we saw that there exists a reparameterization that increases
{\overline{\theta}}_{r}(s)
by a sufficiently small positive number. The construction that we used in the proof might be wasteful. Let us discuss by how much we can improve
{\overline{\theta}}_{r}(s)
in the general case, if we use the same reparameterization defined by the AC run, but fine-tune the amounts for the reparameterization.

We need to define an auxiliary directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
. The vertex set of this graph is the set of all deleted labels:
\mathcal{V}=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. The directed edges consist of labels
(a_{i},a_{j})
for
j>i
if
a_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{u_{j}}
. Notice that
\mathcal{G}
is a directed acyclic graph, since the ordering of the labels
\{a_{i}\}
in the chronological order of their deletion is also a topological ordering in
\mathcal{G}
.

Let us define another auxiliary directed graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
by applying a sequence of transformations to
\mathcal{G}
. Every node of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is going to be a subset of labels of some vertex of the original graph
G
. Start with
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}
. In the beginning, all the nodes of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
are single-element sets of labels. While possible, apply the following operation: contract two nodes
A,B
into a single node
A\cup B
, if the following three properties hold. (1):
A
and
B
are subsets of the same label set
\mathcal{X}_{v}

. Moreover, in case

).
∎

Proof of Corollary
5
.

In the setting of this corollary, the graph
\mathcal{G}
winds around the cycle
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
. We will define a suitable construction of the graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and then apply Theorem
4
.

Let us construct
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
as follows: for each vertex
w_{i}
, contract all the nodes
a
of
\mathcal{G}
that satisfy
a\in\mathcal{X}_{w_{i}}
. This is a valid construction of the graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, since we never introduce a cycle, and all the edges that are incoming to
a\in\mathcal{X}_{w_{i}}

\mathcal{X}_{w_{i-1}}
.

The constructed
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a path. Therefore,
B(A)=1

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
6

Define
\mathcal{I}=CSP_{\varepsilon}^{\leq 2}(\theta)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}\}
be a frustrated cycle in
\mathcal{G}
, with
\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{v_{i}}
. Denote
r=v_{1}
,
and consider label
s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}
. We have
s\in\pi_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}

where either
\sigma_{1}=+
or
\sigma_{1}=-
.
Let us fix the label of
r
to
s
,
i.e. update
\mathcal{Y}_{r}\leftarrow\{s\}
. It suffices to show that running AC on
\mathcal{Y}
will remove
s
from
\mathcal{Y}_{r}
.
For
i\in[k+1]
define
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{1}
if the path
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{i})
contains an even number of negative edges, and
\sigma_{i}=-\sigma_{1}

otherwise (where we assume that
\pi_{k+1}=\pi_{1}
).
We will prove by induction on
i

that after a certain number of AC steps we will have
\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}\subseteq\pi_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}
.
For
i=0
the claim holds since in the beginning
\mathcal{Y}_{v_{1}}=\{s\}\subseteq\pi_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}
.
Suppose that it holds for
i\in[k]
; let us prove it for
i+1
. We will only consider the case when edge

\{\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\}
is positive in
\mathcal{G}
(and hence
\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}=\sigma
);
for negative edges the argument is analogous.
Since
\{\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1}\}
is positive, for all
(a,b)\in\pi_{i}^{\sigma}\times\pi_{i+1}^{-\sigma}
we must have
\theta_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}(a,b)>\min_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}\theta_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})+\varepsilon
and so
(a,b)\notin\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}v_{i+1}}
.
Also,
\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}\subseteq\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
by the induction hypothesis.
Therefore, the AC algorithm will remove all labels from
\pi^{-\sigma}_{i+1}
. The claim follows.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is a frustrated cycle, we must have
\sigma_{k+1}=-\sigma_{1}
, and so
\mathcal{Y}_{r}\subseteq\pi_{k+1}^{-\sigma_{1}}=\pi_{1}^{-\sigma_{1}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{v}-\{s\}
after some number of AC steps. We also have
\mathcal{Y}_{r}\subseteq\{s\}
, and hence
\mathcal{Y}_{r}=\varnothing
.

We saw that fixing every label
s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}
leads to an AC contradiction. This implies that adding all the triplets generated by SAC tightening will admit a reparametrization that strictly increases the lower bound. Indeed, for every label
s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}
, we can run the reparametrization constructed in the proof of Theorem
4
, that increases the value
{\overline{\theta}}_{r}(s)
. Let
\lambda^{s}
be the corresponding reparametrization vector. Taking any reparametrization from the interior of the convex hull of
\{\lambda^{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}}
yields a strict increase of the lower bound. The costs
{\overline{\theta}}_{r}(s)
are increased for every
s\in\mathcal{Y}_{r}
, and the constraint
\min_{x_{\alpha}}{\overline{\theta}}_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})\geq\min_{x_{\alpha}}\theta_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha})
holds for every term
\alpha
, since these constraints are linear in
\lambda
and hold for every reparametrization vector
\lambda^{s}
.
