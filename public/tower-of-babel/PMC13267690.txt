X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ single crystal as a deformation-quantitative indicator sensitive to applied strain and load
Authors: Yohichi Kohzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA peak (330 K peak) dependent on strains appears around 330 K on the thermoluminescence (TL) glow curve for X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ crystals, but the details of the mechanism are not clear, based on experimental results. Here, the deformation luminescence model of the 330 K peak is explored for the crystals. Bleaching with F-light, 330 K peak intensity (I330K peak) decreases with the bleaching time as the variation of TL intensity (IF-center) due to F-centers with it. The 330 K peak relates to the F-center near the dislocation segment. The results analyzed in terms of I330K peak and IF-center versus absorbance peak at wavelength 560 nm indicate that the captured electrons are more easily released from F-centers near the dislocation segment rather than from the isolated F-centers. Under the optical or thermal stimulus, the electrons at F-centers near the dislocation are partially released and easily migrate to trapped holes at Eu3+ ions through the dislocation line.Subject areas: condensed matter physics, materials science, optical materials

Introduction

A type of light emission due to mechanical action (e.g., elastic or plastic deformation, internal defects, microcrack, and so forth) is called mechano-luminescence (ML). ML is divided into two fields: deformation luminescence (DL) and fractoluminescence (FL). The former appears in the process of plastic deformation, and the latter at the fracture of the material. ML is observed for many kinds of materials (mineral, metal, ceramic, inorganic glass, polymer, and alkali halide crystal). ML has been investigated for the purpose of developing a sensor detecting dynamic and full surface stress distribution of materials.1,2,3

Luminescence occurs for X-ray-irradiated alkali halide crystals during plastic deformation.4 Various ML (DL and FL) models have been reported for the irradiated alkali halides so far.5 KCl:Eu2+ has an excellent sensitivity as an X-ray imaging sensor utilizing an optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) phenomenon in comparison with KCl.6 It is well known that the rare-earth dopants, Eu2+ ions, are fluorescent and cause strong luminescence. KCl:Eu2+ exhibits excellent storage performance and is a reusable material for radiation therapy dosimetry.7,8 Co-doped KCl single crystal (i.e., KCl:Eu2+ and Ce3+) acts as a potential thermoluminescence (TL) and OSL dosimeter due to its high sensitivity to ionizing radiation because of the enhanced intensity of TSL (thermally stimulated luminescence) and OSL as against single doping (i.e., KCl:Eu2+ and KCl:Ce3+).9 It has been also reported that the occurrence of different states of dopants Eu2+ due to the heat treatment influences the absorption and fluorescence optical properties for KCl-KBr-RbBr:Eu2+ crystals.10 Furthermore, it was recently found that BaFBr0.85I0.15:Eu2+ demonstrates reusable and excellent dosimetry such as KCl:Eu2+.11 The TL intensity of KCl:Eu2+ phosphor is at least three orders of magnitude larger than that of KCl under the same reading conditions when KCl:Eu2+ and KCl crystals were exposed to the same X-ray radiation dose.6 The dopants, Eu2+ ions in the crystal, have intense luminescence. Irradiating KCl:Eu2+ (0.04 mol % in melt) single crystal with the X-ray at room temperature, electron-hole pairs are made and the formation of F (trapped electron)- and Fz (F-center near Eu2+ ion-vacancy dipole)- centers are ascertained on the TL glow curves and the optical absorption (OA) spectra of the KCl:Eu2+ crystal after the irradiation.12 F- and Fz-centers are directly involved as the trapping sites for the electrons in the TL and OSL processes. As other defects, there is the M-center composed of two neighboring chlorine vacancies and a trapped electron.13,14 A small absorption peak due to M-centers is also observed at 825 nm on the OA spectra of the X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ crystal.12 Additionally, it has been reported that VK (self-trapped-hole)- or VF (VK-center near a cation vacancy)- centers are in the KCl crystal irradiated with X-ray at 295 K. However, the VK- or VF-centers are not taken into consideration here, since these centers are formed at about 230–240 K.15 In this paper, all experiments were not carried out at such low temperatures.

It was found that a peak appears around 330 K on the TL glow curve under the influence of compressive deformation, and it was investigated in detail in a previous paper.16 The peak is termed the 330 K peak in this paper. On the basis of results on the heat treatment process of KCl:Eu2+ crystal, it has also been reported that the TL intensity of 330 K peak depends on the density of dislocations in addition to the color centers on its peak in the crystal.17

In this work, the 330 K peak is mainly investigated for the crystals in detail to obtain further insight into the DL on dislocations in the crystal. The result will inform us of a useful method to know the mechanical information, such as plastic deformation and stress loading, without touching the crystal, because the mechanical information can be converted to the optical information (DL observation). This paper offers a multifaceted exploration (TL glow-curve analysis, OA, F-light bleach, and so forth) to demonstrate the 330 K peak as a deformation-quantitative indicator for the crystal deformed intermittently or continuously after X-ray irradiation. Here, the probable DL model of the 330 K peak is further provided through analyzing the experimental data.

Ionic crystals are predominantly deformed by single glide24 and plastic deformation proceeds with the variation of dislocation structure within the temperature at which the diffusion of additives plays no role in the process, as follows (see, e.g., ref.22,25). In stage I of early plastic deformation, fresh dislocations are generated by multiplication and are nearly parallel to each other. Those are uniformly distributed on the primary slip plane. The density of dislocations is not so high in stage I. As the deformation progresses into stage II, the nearly parallel dislocations are getting disordered and then begin to tangle. Some dislocation dipoles and dislocation walls are generated in this stage. With increasing strain, their structures become cell structures, and further, their size is reduced in the process of deformation. In stage III of the final deformation region, some dislocations are annihilated when dislocations of opposite character meet. As a result, the gradually lower density of dislocations occurs with increasing strain in stage III and leads to a decrease in the strain-hardening rate of the crystal.

The emission mechanism of the 330 K peak appearance due to optical or thermal stimulus

It is well known that ML emission during plastic deformation of colored alkali halide crystals is generated by the mechanical interaction between moving dislocations and F-centers, which is due to the recombination of dislocation-captured electrons with dislocation-captured holes.30,31,32 The similarity between the ML and TL spectra is expected because both ML and TL emissions arise from excited halide ions at the hole centers.31 Chandra et al.30,31,32 have reported that the perturbation potential near the core of moving dislocations in alkali halide crystals causes the formation of a dislocation acceptor band, and the electrons released from F-centers may be transferred to the dislocation acceptor band. The electrons from the F-centers are captured by the dislocation segments to the states of the dislocated acceptor band lying just above the interaction from the F-center level. In X-ray- or γ-ray-irradiated alkali halide crystals (KCl, KI, KBr, NaCl, LiF), it has been reported that the energy gap between the bottom of the dislocation acceptor band and the ground state of the interacting F-centers is of the order of 0.10 eV, and further, the dislocation acceptor band lies a few electron volts below the conduction band. This model is found to be suitable for the colored alkali halide crystals.30 Accordingly, it is considered on the basis of the above reports that the color centers for the new peak (i.e., 330 K peak) on the TL glow curve are related to the F-centers lying in the expansion region of the dislocation segments in the KCl:Eu2+ crystal. Figure 11 shows the emission mechanism due to the optical or thermal stimulus for X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ crystal after plastic deformation. By exposing KCl:Eu2+ crystal to the X-ray irradiation after compressive deformation, free electrons and holes are formed in the crystal. Some electrons are excited from Eu2+ ions to the conduction band. And then, the electrons are partly trapped at anion vacancies (i.e., chlorine vacancies in here), which are resultantly produced as F- and Fz-centers in the crystal. Eu2+ ions are often paired with vacancies in the crystal, since Eu2+ ions strongly attract the cation vacancies to conserve the electrical neutrality.33 Fz-center is so-called F-center in the vicinity of the Eu2+ ion-vacancy dipole. While some holes are also trapped at Eu2+ ions and result in Eu3+ ions in the crystal. The electron captured at the F-center lying in the expansion region of a dislocation segment (see the dotted circle in Figure 11) is released by the stimulus, such as optical irradiation (the wavelength of about 560 nm) or heating at a constant rate until around 330 K, because the electron is in a metastable state. And further, the electron begins to migrate along the dislocation line under the Coulomb attraction, which is generated by the Eu3+ ion near the dislocation. As the dislocation-captured electron recombines with the trapped hole at the Eu3+ ion, the Eu3+ ion leads to the excited Eu2+ ion. And then, the transformation of Eu2+ ion from excited state into ground state occurs with the characteristic light emission of the wavelength of 420 nm14 due to the 4f65d→4f7, since an OSL peak is measured at 420 nm for X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ crystals and its emission is assigned to the 4f65d→4f7 transition of Eu2+ ions.34,35 These recombination processes are represented in Figure 12B. The energy level is also illustrated in the figure after the stimulus, where the electron migration is indicated by the red dashed arrows. It is assumed that the trap depth for the F-center is not significantly affected even near a dislocation segment in the crystal.

As described on the basis of Figure 10 in the previous section, the logarithmic variation of I330K peak with Abs560, Δlog(I330K peak)/Δlog(Abs560), is slightly more remarkable in contrast to that of IF-center with it, Δlog(IF-center)/Δlog(Abs560). Bleaching with the F-light, the concentration of F-centers near or situated away from the dislocation is reduced in the crystal. This leads to the experimental result that Abs560, which is related to the absorption amount of light at the wavelength of about 560 nm, becomes small after long F-bleaching time in Figure 10. In addition, both the I330K peak and the IF-center also reduce with the bleaching time in the figure. The decrement of F-center concentration causes the more and more diminutive TL intensities due to the color centers over time. The effect of F-bleach on the annihilation of some color centers is greater for the 330 K peak than for the F-center, which is away from the dislocation, as a result of the slightly different gradient of the linear-logarithmic relationship between Abs560 and TL intensity due to the color centers in Figure 10. This suggests that the captured electrons are more easily released from F-centers lying in the expansion region of dislocation segments rather than from the isolated F-centers, which are sited away from the dislocation, by the F-light bleaching of the KCl:Eu2+ crystal. The above mention supports the phenomenon that the energy level of the dislocation acceptor band is slightly lower than that of the conduction band, as shown in the energy level diagram (i.e., Figure 12B) represented for the crystal. As additional proof of it, the 330 K peak temperature is lower than 450 K of the F-center peak on the TL glow curve in Figures 2A and 6A. Through the above-mentioned excitation and emission mechanisms, the 330 K peak appears on the TL glow curve when the thermal stimulus was added to the X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ crystal after the compression. In the process of plastic deformation until the linear hardening region (stage II of strain hardening), the density of dislocations becomes high with strain (see, e.g., ref.22,25). This leads to the phenomenon that the electrons captured at F-centers near dislocation segments increase numerically with the strain of the KCl:Eu2+ crystal. Therefore, the 330 K peak height becomes large depending on strain, until stage II, as can be seen from the 330 K peak versus strain in Figure 6D.

In conclusion, it has been succeeded in clearing that the peak appears newly around 330 K on the TL glow curve and its TL intensity is affected by the variations of strain and strain-hardening rate, which are related to the dislocation density, for the KCl:Eu2+ (0.02 mol % in melt) single crystals deformed intermittently or continuously after the X-ray irradiation. The 330 K peak depends on the F-centers lying in the expansion region of dislocation segments. The phenomenon that the dislocation becomes dense with the strain until the stage II of strain hardening causes the increment of dislocation-captured electrons in the crystal. This is considered to lead to a higher 330 K peak with the strain. At the beginning of plastic deformation (i.e., easy glide and linear hardening regions), the crystal is sensitive to small variations in applied load and strain.

Only by heating the X-ray-irradiated KCl:Eu2+ (0.02 mol % in melt) single crystal at the rate of 20 K min−1 until around 330 K, it is contactlessly possible to determine whether load or strain was applied to the crystal.

Limitations of the study

The 330 K peak intensity is variable, depending on the density of dislocations in the crystal. This result will provide us with a useful method to investigate the deformation structure concerned with dislocations in solid materials, contactlessly. It is additionally possible to determine whether a load was applied to the material by heating. A new stress sensor has not yet been made on the basis of the 330 K peak on the TL glow curve for the crystal now. However, the 330 K peak has the ability to be applied to the non-contact stress sensor.

Method details

Preparation of samples

KCl:Eu2+ single crystal was grown from the melt of reagent grade powder of KCl and EuCl36H2O in a sealed test tube by the vertical Bridgeman method under vacuum. The concentration of doped Eu2+ was 0.02 mol % in the melt. Single crystal samples were prepared by cleaving out of the grown ingot, followed by annealing at 1033 K for 15 h and slowly cooling at the rate of 40 K h−1 to room temperature by a digital program temperature controller (Chino KP1000, display accuracy: ±0.1% of measurement range; control period: 0.1 s) in order to remove internal strains as far as possible. The size of samples is 2 × 3 × 6 mm3. Furthermore, the samples were held at 623 K for 30 min and then were quenched to room temperature for the purpose of dispersing the dopant ions in a sample. Subsequently, all the samples were exposed to X-ray from a W-target operated at 40 kV and 30 mA by an X-ray diffractometer (Shimadzu XD-610). The distance between the center of radiation source and the W-target is 0.1 m. The X-ray irradiation was conducted for 10 min at room temperature.

Compression tests

The samples were plastically deformed by two compressive methods, one intermittent (each strain is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 18, 22, and 26%) and the other continuous until the strain of 27%. The compression was carried out in the <100> direction along the longest axis at the cross-head speed controlled to 50 μm min−1 (i.e., shear strain rate of 2.8 × 10−4 s−1), of which is an error of less than ±0.15%, using a testing machine (Instron 4465) at room temperature.

Measuring systems of TL intensity and absorption spectrum

As thermofluorescent material is heated at a constant rate, luminescence is caused at a certain temperature and its peak is observed on a TL glow curve. The TL intensity was observed at 300 to 540 K, using a photo-multiplier (Hamamatsu R928) with a sensitive region from 185 to 900 nm through a lens. The sample set on the original sample holder was heated at the rate of 20 K min−1, which was performed by a thermocouple glued to the heater and a temperature controller (Chino JP1131-R00, temperature setting accuracy: ±0.1%). The infrared rays from the heater were excluded by using the filter. Avoiding the unwanted effect of environmental light, this was carried out in the dark box. Luminescence intensity was recorded in a personal computer and then the data were processed.

The absorption spectra of samples were measured before and after F-bleach (excitation bandpass:5 nm) with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi U-3010, measurement wavelength range: 220–730 nm; wavelength accuracy: ±2 nm; light source: xenon lamp).
