On The Convergence of the Discretized Linear Static State-Based Peridynamic Equations
Lukas Pflug, Michael Stingl, Max Zetzmann

Abstract
In this paper, the convergence of the solutions for a discretized linear state-based static peridynamic system to the corresponding continuous solution is analytically proven. To obtain an implementable model, we further apply one-point-quadrature to the terms in the discrete equations. The resulting system coincides with the commonly used meshfree discretization using a regular lattice, including the possibility of using partial area algorithms to improve the numerical behavior. We again prove convergence, this time for fixed choices of a weighting function commonly used in literature and stronger assumptions on the input data. We note however, that these assumptions are not significantly restrictive for practical purposes. In particular, they still allow discontinuities in the material parameters and external body forces.



Keywords—
peridynamics, state-based, meshfree, convergence

Introduction

Peridynamics is a relatively new approach to modeling a solid body in continuum mechanics first introduced by Silling in
[
28
]
. It uses a non-local integral equation instead of partial differential equations that, in contrast to the classical theory, does not rely on any spatial derivatives of the displacement
\mathbf{u}
, making peridynamics naturally relevant for fracture-modeling (See e.g.
[
26
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
8
]
). It has also inspired similar models for fracture analysis (See
[
16
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
, as well as the model introduced in
[
9
]
used for fracture in
[
7
]
). Further applications include Topology Optimization with pre-imposed cracks (see
[
13
]
).

The original peridynamic model as introduced in
[
28
]
is the so-called
bond-based
model, which, while being simple to implement and numerically efficient, has the disadvantage of only being able to replicate a fixed Poisson’s ratio. This shortcoming motivated the formulation of a generalized, so-called
state-based
model. This generalized formulation allows for the modeling of materials with arbitrary bulk and shear moduli. A particularly important instance of such a state-based model is stated in
[
25
]
, 5.3 Example 3. One should note that the bond-based model is a special case of the state-based model. Therefore, all results in this paper are also applicable without any further effort to bond-based models. If desired, a more detailed and complete overview of peridynamics can be found in
[
27
]
and
[
2
]
.

This paper focuses on the linearization of this state-based model, and aims to prove the convergence of the solutions for the discretized equations to the continuous solution.
Numerical analysis on the convergence and convergence behavior in this setting has been carried out before in
[
20
]
,
[
23
]
and
[
3
]
. In the context of the latter, we are dealing with the so-called
m
-convergence in this paper, meaning the horizon of the model will remain the same as the discretization becomes finer.

This paper aims to prove the convergence rigorously. However, proving a rate for the convergence is not part of the scope of this paper. We will do so in two different ways: A general setting allowing more general input data and in turn requiring the exact evaluation of multiple integrals in the constituents of the equation, and in a numerical setting using one-point quadrature on these integrals, but in turn requiring the specific choice of the weighting function
\omega
given by
\zeta\mapsto\rho(\zeta)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|\zeta\|)
,
\rho(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{\|\zeta\|}
. A remark generalizing the choice for the weighting function to include more regular functions
\rho
will also be stated at the end. This choice for the weighting function is frequently found in literature, e.g. in Silling’s works on the discretization of peridynamics
[
26
]
and the PMB (prototype microelastic brittle) material defined in it. In contrast to this PMB model however, we do not yet consider fracture in this paper.
See also
[
22
]
, which studies the effect of different choices of the weighting function on the non-local constitutive behavior. The discretization scheme we use for the numerical model coincides with the spatial discretization described in
[
26
]
on a regular lattice, extended to state-based models and is frequently encountered in numerical simulations.

One should note that similar rigorous convergence results for the dynamical problem given by the peridynamics-inspired non-linear model described in
[
16
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
have already been shown in
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
. Specifically, in
[
10
]
, an approximation using linear continuous interpolation on triangular or tetrahedral elements was used in the context of a finite element scheme, and it was assumed that the right hand side and the initial values are in
H^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}
. Furthermore, the material parameters were assumed to be constant.

In
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
, a spatially piecewise constant approximation was chosen and the regularity of the input data and right-hand side was assumed to be Hölder continuous, while the material parameters remained constant. We note that the model used in
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
10
]
differs from and does not include the model considered in this paper.

Other results for the linear bond-based model include
[
32
]
, which proved the convergence using Fourier analysis. This approach, however, imposes significant restrictions on the geometry of
\Omega
- a finite interval was considered in one dimension, and a box in two dimensions. On those domains, convergence was proven for a generic inner approximation in the context of a Galerkin–Ritz scheme.

Most notably, none of the above papers rigorously proved the convergence of the discrete solutions when one-point quadrature was additionally applied to the discrete equations. This is relevant since most practical computations make use of quadrature to simplify the integrals involved in the underlying non-local peridynamic model. Instead, if quadrature was addressed, the convergence of the discretized solutions using one-point quadrature was only numerically compared with the bounds and rate of the solutions without one-point quadrature (See
[
12
]
, Section 6).

This paper aims to fill this gap between the commonly used mesh-free discretization with one-point quadrature and the continuous formulation for the state-based linear model. To the best of our knowledge, there has not yet been a rigorous proof for the convergence of the mesh-free discretization of the static linear state-based model - whether or not one-point quadrature is used - that further does not assume continuity of the material parameters or right-hand side, nor impose significant restrictions on the domain
\Omega
.

The structure of this paper is as follows: after introducing the model used in this paper in Section 1, showing the convergence of the discrete solutions without one-point quadrature will be the subject of Section 2. Therein, the main result, Theorem
2
, will be proven under the Assumptions

, showing the convergence of the discrete solutions to the continuous solution in the

Section 3 will introduce the numerical model in (

), which results from the discrete model in Section 2 by applying one-point quadrature. To improve the practical convergence, we also include partial area weights in the numerical formulation as suggested in

under the Assumptions

. For this, we need stronger assumptions on both the material parameters and the right-hand side. We assume boundedness and almost everywhere local

1.1
The Linear State-Based Model

Let

denote the space dimension. Following
[
18
]
, the variational linear static state-based problem is given using the bilinear form

\text{Find }u\in\mathcal{V}\text{ such that }\forall v\in\mathcal{V}:B(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}b(x)\cdot v(x)\ \mathbf{d}x,

(1)

for an open and bounded reference configuration
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the external body forces
b:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\mathcal{V}
is a subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
disjoint with

\Pi:=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\exists c\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\ni A=-A^{\top}:f(x)=Ax+c,\forall x\in\Omega\}.

The subspace

realizes the homogeneous Dirichlet conditions imposed on the problem. For example, for a given open subset
\Theta\subset\Omega
on which the solution should be

takes the form

\mathcal{V}:=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:u_{|\Theta}\equiv 0\}.

Choices for

of this form are by construction disjoint with
\Pi
. This method of imposing boundary conditions has known drawbacks. Specifically, it causes unwanted artifacts at the interface between
\Theta
and the rest of the body. Suggestions for improved approaches can be found in
[
31
]
. We will stick to this method nonetheless because of its simplicity as well as its compatibility with the theory in
[
18
]
.

To formulate the bilinear form
B
in (
1
), we require the following non-local operators stemming from
[
4
]
.

\displaystyle(x)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\otimes\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right)\overline{\omega}(x,x^{\prime})\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime},

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}^{*}u](x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=-(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\otimes\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right).

These can be understood as non-local divergence and gradient operators justified by convergence results stated in

Choosing a weighting function

, the bilinear form in (

\displaystyle B(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\left(k(x)-\frac{\alpha(x)m(x)}{d^{2}}\right)\text{Tr}([\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\overline{\omega}}u](x))\text{Tr}([\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\overline{\omega}}v](x))

For the well-posedness of the continuous problem and the first convergence result, we further assume the following.

Assumptions 1.1

(
Assumptions for the Continuous Model
)

A1

Integrability, Non-Negativity and Symmetry of the Weighting Function
:

\omega(\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with

A2

There exist

.

A3

\alpha,k:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

.

]
proves the well-posedness under even more general assumptions on

2
Convergence of an Analytical Model

To obtain the discrete equations, a piecewise constant approximation is chosen where
\Omega
is being partitioned as follows.

Definition 2.1

(
Subdivision of
\Omega
into
(\Omega_{i})_{i\in I^{\kappa}}
)

1.

Subdivide
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into half-open cubes/squares
(B_{i})_{i\in Z^{\kappa}}
of side-length
\kappa
and midpoints
x_{i}
on a uniform grid
\kappa\mathbb{Z}^{d}

is therefore defined to be
[-\tfrac{\kappa}{2},\tfrac{\kappa}{2}[^{d}

2.

Define the index family corresponding to all such cubes

that intersect

I^{\kappa}\subsetneq Z^{\kappa}
.

3.

For all

, define
\Omega_{i}
as the intersection of

with
\Omega
.

With this subdivision, we define the following operators to simplify the notation.

Definition 2.2

(
Piecewise Constant Projection Operators
)

Let
\chi_{B}(x)
denote the characteristic function for a set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{|I^{\kappa}|}\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}

(3)

For these operators we can show the following.

Lemma 2.3

(
Approximation by Piecewise Constant Functions
)
Both
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}
are bounded linear operators fulfilling
\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}\circ\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{d|I^{\kappa}|}}
, and the

\|({\mathrm{id}_{L^{2}}}-\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}){u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

(4)

for all
{u}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Proof.

The identity
\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}\circ\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}=\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{d|I^{\kappa}|}}
follows directly from the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
. Their boundedness easily follows from Jensen’s Inequality. Similarly, the boundedness of

follows from

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left\|\frac{1}{V_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}u(x^{\prime})\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i}V_{i}\frac{1}{V_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x\leq\|{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}.

Using this, we follow

]

The claim then follows from Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|({\mathrm{id}_{L^{2}}}-\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}){f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\|({\mathrm{id}_{L^{2}}}-\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}){f}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{2}))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\sup_{\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leq\sqrt{d}\kappa}|{f}_{i}(x^{\prime})-{f}_{i}(x)|^{2}\right)\xrightarrow[f_{i}\in C^{0}_{0}(\Omega)]{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

∎

,

\operatorname{vol}(S^{\kappa})\leq\operatorname{vol}\left(\bigcup_{f\in F}\bigcup_{i\in L_{f}}B_{i}\right)\leq\operatorname{vol}\left(\bigcup_{f\in F}W_{f}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{<}}\epsilon.

The last inequality follows from (
10
).
∎

With this, we obtain the main contribution of this chapter, the density result, as follows.

Proposition 2.6

(
Density of the Discrete Admissible Sets
)

For every sequence
\kappa_{n}\searrow 0
, the set

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{V}_{\kappa_{n}}

is (strongly) dense in
\mathcal{V}
with respect to the norm of
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{V}
. We already know that due to Lemma
2
, there is a sequence of piecewise constant functions
v^{\prime}_{\kappa}:=\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}(v)\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
such that
v^{\prime}_{\kappa}\rightarrow v
as
\kappa\searrow 0
. The index
n
is again omitted unless relevant. One can modify this sequence to lie in
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
by restricting
v^{\prime}_{\kappa}
to
\cup_{i\in J^{\kappa}}\Omega_{i}=:SQ_{J^{\kappa}}
, thereby defining
v_{\kappa}:={v^{\prime}_{\kappa}}_{|SQ_{J^{\kappa}}}
. Recall that
J^{\kappa}\subset I^{\kappa}
was the set of indices
i\in I^{\kappa}
for which
\operatorname{vol}(\Omega_{i}\cap\Theta)=0
.

We now split the group of the
\Omega_{i}
for
i\in I^{\kappa}\backslash J^{\kappa}
into those completely inside of
\overline{\Theta}
and those intersecting both
\Omega\backslash\overline{\Theta}
and
\Theta
. This leads to the following definitions,

\displaystyle\Omega\supset SQ_{K^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in K^{\kappa}}\Omega_{i},\,\,K^{\kappa}:=\{i\in I^{\kappa}\backslash J^{\kappa}:\Omega_{i}\not\subset\overline{\Theta}\},

\displaystyle\Omega\supset SQ_{R^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle:=\Omega\backslash(SQ_{K^{\kappa}}\cup SQ_{J^{\kappa}})=N\dot{\cup}\bigcup_{i\in R^{\kappa}}\Omega_{i},\,\,R^{\kappa}:=\{i\in I^{\kappa}:\Omega_{i}\subset\overline{\Theta}\},

where
N\subset\Omega
is some set of measure zero.
Then, one obtains

\displaystyle\|v_{\kappa}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}(v)-v\|_{L^{2}(SQ_{J^{\kappa}})^{d}}^{2}+\|v_{\kappa}-v\|_{L^{2}(SQ_{K^{\kappa}})^{d}}^{2}+\|v_{\kappa}-v\|_{L^{2}(SQ_{R^{\kappa}})^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}(v)-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}}_{\xrightarrow{\text{L}\ref{lemm:PropProjOps}}0}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(SQ_{K^{\kappa}})^{d}}^{2}

since
v_{\kappa}\equiv 0
on
SQ_{K^{\kappa}}\cup SQ_{R^{\kappa}}
and
v\equiv 0
on
SQ_{R^{\kappa}}
because of
v\in\mathcal{V}
.

Because of the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral, one now only has to show that

\operatorname{vol}(SQ_{K^{\kappa}})\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

This is where we will need assumption A6 in
1.1
, which gives us a more concrete description of
SQ_{K^{\kappa}}
as

SQ_{K^{\kappa}}\subset\bigcup_{i:B_{i}\cap\partial\Theta\not=0}B_{i}=:S^{\kappa}.

This follows from the openness of
\Theta
and the path-connectedness of the half-open cubes
B_{i}
.
More specifically, for any
\Omega_{i}
with
i\in K^{\kappa}
, one has

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}\cap\Theta

\displaystyle=B_{i}\cap\Theta\not=\varnothing

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}\cap(\Omega\backslash\overline{\Theta})

\displaystyle=B_{i}\cap(\Omega\backslash\overline{\Theta})\not=\varnothing.

Now, a typical connectedness argument implies that
B_{i}\cap\partial\Theta\not=\varnothing
. So that
\Omega_{i}\subset B_{i}\subset S^{\kappa}
. The last part of the proof now uses that
\partial\Theta
is by assumption a compact set of measure zero to apply Proposition
2
.∎

With the density proven, the rest of the convergence follows from Galerkin theory as follows.

Theorem 2.7

(
The Convergence of the Discrete Solutions
)
Let the Assumptions
1.1
hold. Then, let
\kappa_{n}\searrow 0
. Let
u_{\kappa_{n}}
be the unique solution to (
5
) for each
\kappa_{n}
and
u
the unique solution to (
1
), then

\|u-u_{\kappa_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa_{n}\searrow 0}0.

Proof.

This proof uses the fact that the bilinear form
B
can also be represented as a self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, such that

B(u,v)=\langle-\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}=\langle u,-\mathcal{L}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

for all
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
(See
[
18
]
, Lemma 5). The proof then begins by showing a boundedness statement for the
u_{\kappa}
. For convenience, we drop the index
n
from the
\kappa_{n}
when it is not needed. Using the coercitivity of the bilinear form as in (
9
), one has

0\leq c\|u_{\kappa}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq B(u_{\kappa},u_{\kappa})=\langle b,u_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\|u_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

leading to
\frac{1}{c}\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\geq\|u_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}
being uniformly bounded.

Applying the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, one obtains a weakly-
L^{2}
convergent subsequence
u_{\kappa_{n_{k}}}
, renamed to just
{u}_{\kappa_{n}}
for simplicity, with some weak limit
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
. Due to the weak closedness of
\mathcal{V}
, we have
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
.

The next step is proving
u^{\prime}=u
by showing that
u^{\prime}
solves (
1
), thereby proving that the entire original sequence weakly converges to
u
. Let
v\in\mathcal{V}
, then, by Proposition
2
, there is a sequence of
(v_{\kappa_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that
v_{\kappa_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrow}}v
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
and
v_{\kappa_{n}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\kappa_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, since
\mathcal{L}
is a bounded operator and therefore
\mathcal{L}v_{\kappa_{n}}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}v
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
,

\displaystyle B(u_{\kappa},v_{\kappa})

\displaystyle=\langle u_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}v_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}\langle u^{\prime},-\mathcal{L}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

This immediately yields

B(u^{\prime},v)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}B(u_{\kappa_{n}},v_{\kappa_{n}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sol}}{{=}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\langle b,v_{\kappa_{n}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}=\langle b,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

The only thing left to show now is that the series also converges to
u
in the
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
norm. This is done by using the fact that
\mathcal{V}_{\kappa}\subset\mathcal{V}
and therefore
B(u,u_{\kappa})=\langle u_{\kappa},b\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}=B(u_{\kappa},u_{\kappa})
to obtain

\displaystyle c\|u-u_{\kappa}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq B(u-u_{\kappa},u-u_{\kappa})=B(u,u-u_{\kappa})-B(u_{\kappa},u)+B(u_{\kappa},u_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}\langle u-u_{\kappa},b\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

We used the fact that
u
solves (
1
) in the last equality.
∎

3
Convergence of a Numerical Model

The above framework relies on the exact computation of the
\vec{B}^{kl}_{ij}
and
\vec{b}_{i}^{k}
factors involved in (
6
). In practice, the exact computation of these factors is impractical, giving rise to the need to develop a numerically feasible model for which one can obtain a convergence result similar to Theorem
2
in Section 2. To this end, we now give explicit formulas for the constituents of the analytical discretization to which we want to apply one point quadrature. To make the use of one point quadrature possible, we will have to rewrite the integrals in the bilinear form into a sum of integrals on vanishing domains as
\kappa\rightarrow 0
.

The right hand side does not need any further treatment, as it is given by

\vec{b}_{i}^{k}=\int_{\Omega_{i}}e_{k}^{\top}b(x)\ \mathbf{d}x.

So, to deal with the
\vec{B}^{kl}_{ij}
terms from (
6
), first writing out the terms in the definition of
B
in (
2
) , one has

\displaystyle B(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(k(x)-\frac{\alpha(x)m(x)}{d^{2}}\right)\int_{\Omega}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))^{\top}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right)\overline{\omega}(x,x^{\prime})\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}

(12)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\Omega}(v(x^{\prime})-v(x))^{\top}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right)\overline{\omega}(x,x^{\prime})\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x)\int_{\Omega}\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))^{\top}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times(v(x^{\prime})-v(x))^{\top}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x.

We then define

\tau(x):=\frac{k(x)d^{2}}{m(x)^{2}}-\frac{\alpha(x)}{m(x)},

with which (
12
) can further be rewritten to

\displaystyle B(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\Omega}(v(x^{\prime})-v(x))^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x)\int_{\Omega}\frac{\omega(x^{\prime}-x)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)(v(x^{\prime})-v(x))^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x.

Then, plugging in
u=\chi_{\Omega_{i}}e_{k}
and
v=\chi_{\Omega_{j}}e_{l}
yields that

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{ij}^{kl}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega}(\chi_{\Omega_{i}}(x^{\prime})-\chi_{\Omega_{i}}(x))e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int_{\Omega}(\chi_{\Omega_{j}}(x^{\prime})-\chi_{\Omega_{j}}(x))e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x)\int_{\Omega}\frac{\omega(x^{\prime}-x)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}(\chi_{\Omega_{i}}(x^{\prime})-\chi_{\Omega_{i}}(x))e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times(\chi_{\Omega_{j}}(x^{\prime})-\chi_{\Omega_{j}}(x))e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x,

for
i\not=j
we then obtain

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}}-e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega_{j}}e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{j}}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{j}}-e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega\backslash(\Omega_{j}\cup\Omega_{i})}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega_{j}}e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{j}}(\alpha(x)+\alpha(x^{\prime}))\frac{\omega(x^{\prime}-x)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

and for
i=j
we have

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}}e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega_{i}}e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}}(\alpha(x)+\alpha(x^{\prime}))\frac{\omega(x^{\prime}-x)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x.

(13)

Summarizing this form and rewriting integrals over
\Omega\backslash\Omega_{i}
or
\Omega\backslash(\Omega_{i}\cup\Omega_{j})
as sums over all relevant
\Omega_{k}
making up the integration domain, one would then only have to evaluate the following integrals for
i,j,m\in I^{\kappa},1\leq k,l\leq d
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{2}_{ijm;kl}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\tau(x)\int_{\Omega_{j}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\int_{\Omega_{m}}e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\omega(x^{\prime}-x)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{1}_{ij;kl}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{j}}(\alpha(x)+\alpha(x^{\prime}))\frac{\omega(x^{\prime}-x)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}e_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)e_{l}^{\top}\left(x^{\prime}-x\right)\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}\ \mathbf{d}x.

Using only these integrals, the bilinear form is then given by

\displaystyle\vec{B}^{kl}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{m\not\in\{i,j\}}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{mij;kl}-\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{imj;kl}-\sum_{m\not=j}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{jim;kl}-\mathcal{I}^{1}_{ij;kl}&\text{for }i\not=j\\
\displaystyle\sum_{n\not=i}\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{inm;kl}+\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{mii;kl}+\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{1}_{im;kl}&\text{for }i=j.\end{cases}

(15)

In this chapter we will study the numerical version of the model considered above assuming
\omega(\zeta)=\rho(\zeta)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|\zeta\|)
with
\rho(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\|\zeta\|}
for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some fixed
\delta>0
. This numerical version is obtained by applying a one-point quadrature rule to the integrals in (
14
) and then using (
15
) to obtain the numerical bilinear form
B^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}
.
This approach to discretize the peridynamic equations is not new, and was originally introduced in
[
26
]
on bond-based models; it was merely brought into the usual variational framework here after being canonically extended to state-based models. One should note that
[
26
]
only mathematically proved the convergence for one-dimensional models and twice continuously differentiable material parameters and displacements.

For consistency between the numerical models and the continuous models, as well as the continuous PD model and the classical theory, we will substitute
\alpha(x)
with
\frac{l(x)}{m(x)}
for a function

.

First, we write the integrals in (
14
) in matrix form
\mathcal{I}^{1}_{ijm}:=(\mathcal{I}^{1}_{ijm;kl})_{1\leq k,l\leq d}
to simplify the notation. We then apply a one-point quadrature rule to obtain, using the notation
V_{i}:=\operatorname{vol}(\Omega_{i})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{ijm}:=V_{i}V_{j}V_{m}\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})\rho(x_{j}-x_{i})\rho(x_{m}-x_{i})\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)\left(x_{m}-x_{i}\right)^{\top}w_{ij}w_{im},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{1,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{ij}:=V_{i}V_{j}\rho(x_{j}-x_{i})(\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})+\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{j}))\frac{\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)^{\top}}{\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|}w_{ij},

with, for
x\in\Omega_{i}
,

\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x):=\frac{k(x_{i})d^{2}-l(x_{i})}{m^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})^{2}},\,\,\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x)=\frac{l(x_{i})}{m^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})},\,\,m^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x):=\sum_{j}w_{ij}V_{j}\rho(x_{j}-x_{i})\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}.

The
w_{ij}
terms now replace the
\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|)
terms and allow the usage of partial area algorithms for better convergence behavior (See
[
23
]
for an in-depth analysis on partial area/volume weights and convergence behavior). There will be requirements for these terms later in Assumptions
3.1
justifying this substitution.

Due to our choice to evaluate

,

and

at the mid-points

of the

\Omega
as the

.

Remark 3.2

As outlined in Remark
2
, to obtain the bond-based model from the state-based model on the continuous level, one would have to set
\alpha(x):=\tfrac{d^{2}k(x)}{m(x)}
. Doing so would require the exact evaluation of
m
in all points
x_{i}
if this model was discretized using
\alpha(x_{i})
in the above numerical integrals instead of
\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})
. Another significant drawback is that it would not imply

if

had been used instead, as in general

. This would mean that the discretized bond-based model would contain state-based terms.

With the substitution

however, the
m(x)
is also numerically approximated. So with
l(x)=d^{2}k(x)

\alpha=\frac{c_{d}\mu(x)}{m(x)}
(See
[
24
]
, Section 15) for a constant
c_{d}
depending on the dimension

and
\mu(x)
being the shear modulus from the classical theory, modeling materials from classical theory in this discrete peridynamic setting does not require the exact evaluation of
m(x)
either.

The right hand side of the continuous equation can also be numerically approximated as

\vec{b}^{k\,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{i}=b(x_{i})^{\top}e_{k}

(20)

defining one possible choice for a vector

.

given by

and

elsewhere.

To solve this system, note that it can also be reduced to

\text{Find }\tilde{u}_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{d|J^{\kappa}|}\text{ s.t. }\tilde{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\tilde{u}_{\kappa}=\tilde{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}

(22)

just like (

and

is omitted from the notation when not needed.

The matrix
\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}
is interpreted as a
(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})^{|I^{\kappa}|\times|I^{\kappa}|}
matrix and given just like in (
15
) by

\displaystyle\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{m\not\in\{i,j\}}\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{mij}-\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{imj}-\sum_{m\not=j}\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{jim}-\mathcal{I}^{1,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{ij}&\text{for }i\not=j\\
\displaystyle\sum_{n\not=i}\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{inm}+\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{2,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{mii}+\sum_{m\not=i}\mathcal{I}^{1,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{im}&\text{for }i=j.\end{cases}

(23)

The goal of this chapter will be to show that the solutions
\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{d|I^{\kappa}|}
of (
21
), embedded into
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
via
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
, converge to the continuous solution
u
of (
1
), that is,

\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\xrightarrow[L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}]{\kappa\searrow 0}u.

For this we will need assumptions on the input data which will be specified next.

3.1
The Convergence Proof

For the numerical convergence proof, we assume the following.

Assumptions 3.3

(
Assumptions on the Model for the Numerical Discretization
)

We assume
\kappa<\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}
and further,

A1

The Choice for the Weighting Function
: We set
\forall\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\omega(\zeta)=\rho(\zeta)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|\zeta\|)
for some
\delta>0
and
\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\|z\|}
.

A2

Regularity of the Material Parameters
:
l,k:{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a.e. locally
L,K
-Lipschitz continuous for some
L,K>0
respectively, and
0<l_{0}\leqq l\leqq l_{1}
as well as
0<k_{0}\leqq k\leqq k_{1}
for some constants
0<l_{0},l_{1},k_{0},k_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.

A3

Convergence of the Right-Hand Side
: The choice of the numerical right hand side
\vec{b}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d|I|^{\kappa}}
has to fulfill

\|b-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow 0.

A4

Limitations for the Partial Area Weights
: The
w_{ij}
terms fulfill the following requirements for all
i,j\in I^{\kappa}
.

(a)

If
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq\delta+\sqrt{d}\frac{\kappa}{2}
, then
w_{ij}=0
.

(b)

If
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq\delta-\sqrt{d}\frac{\kappa}{2}
, then
w_{ij}=1
.

(c)

0\leq w_{ij}\leq 1
.

(d)

w_{ij}=w_{ji}
.

A5

Regularity of the Domain
:
\Omega
is a bounded and connected Lipschitz domain, in particular, it satisfies an interior cone condition for some angle
\theta>0
and radius
h>0
.

A6

Regularity of the Dirichlet Boundary Condition
:
\Theta
is open, non-empty with
\operatorname{vol}(\partial\Theta)=0
.

The main idea behind the assumptions involving the a.e. local
L
-Lipschitz continuity is to allow bounded jumps (in the sense of discontinuities) on a set of measure zero, while keeping sufficient control over the function everywhere else, uniform with respect to
x
. Besides the material parameters, this can also be applied to the external body forces
b
. This is shown in Corollary
3.1
, which will prove that choosing
b
to be a.e. locally
L
-Lipschitz is a sufficient condition for
\vec{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}:=(b(x_{i}))_{i\in I^{\kappa}}
to fulfill the assumptions above.

We begin with the following fundamental lemma that allows extending the local Lipschitz continuity to a global one on convex sets that don’t intersect the set where the function behaves undesirably.

Lemma 3.4

(
Globalization of a.e. Local
L
-Lipschitz Continuity
)

Let
f:{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, a.e. locally
F
-Lipschitz continuous function with
F>0
, and

\mathrm{LFS}^{F}(f):=\{x\in{\Omega}:f\text{ fails to be locally }F\text{-Lipschitz in }x\}.

Then, for all convex sets
B\subset{\Omega}
with
B\cap\mathrm{LFS}^{F}(f)=\varnothing
, we have
f_{|B}\in C^{0,1}(B)^{n}
with the same Lipschitz constant
F
.

Proof.

By assumption, for any
x,x^{\prime}\in B
, the line segment between
x
and
x^{\prime}
lies in
B
. Since this line segment is also compact, it can be covered by finitely many neighborhoods on which
f
is
F
-Lipschitz. Appropriately subdividing the line segment between
x
and
x^{\prime}
and applying the
F
-Lipschitz bound on each segment then yields the claim after summing the bounds back together.
∎

The next proposition states a convergence result for these functions.

Proposition 3.5

(
Convergence of Midpoint Approximation
)
Let
u:{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded, almost everywhere locally
L
-Lipschitz function, then

\|\operatorname{num}_{\kappa}(u)-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0,

with

L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\ni\operatorname{num}_{\kappa}(u):=\sum_{i\in I^{\kappa}}\chi_{\Omega_{i}}u(x_{i}).

Proof.

We first split
I^{\kappa}
into two sets as follows,

\hat{I}:=\{i\in I^{\kappa}:\overline{B_{i}}\cap(\partial\Omega\cup\mathrm{LFS}^{L}({u}))\not=\varnothing\},\,\,\,\hat{II}:=I^{\kappa}\backslash\hat{I}.

One then has, using
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
to denote the supremum norm,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{num}_{\kappa}(u)-u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I^{\kappa}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)-u(x_{i})\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\hat{I}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)-u(x_{i})\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x+\sum_{i\in\hat{II}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)-u(x_{i})\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}4\|u\|_{\infty}^{2}\operatorname{vol}\left(\bigcup_{i\in\hat{I}}B_{i}\right)+\sum_{i\in\hat{II}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)-u(x_{i})\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x.

Invoking Proposition
2
now yields

\operatorname{vol}\left(\bigcup_{i\in\hat{I}}B_{i}\right)=\operatorname{vol}\left(\bigcup_{i:\overline{B_{i}}\cap S\not=\varnothing}B_{i}\right)\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0,

since
S:=(\partial\Omega\cup\mathrm{LFS}^{L}({u}))
is by definition a compact zero set.

Further,
i\in\hat{II}
implies that
\Omega_{i}=B_{i}
as
B_{i}
is path connected and
\Omega
is open, so that one could apply a connectedness argument to show

\Omega_{i}\not=B_{i}\Longrightarrow\varnothing\not=\overline{B_{i}}\cap\partial\Omega\subset\overline{B_{i}}\cap(\partial\Omega\cup\mathrm{LFS}^{L}({u}))\Longrightarrow i\not\in\hat{II}.

Due to Lemma
3.1
, one obtains that
u
has to be L-Lipschitz continuous on
\Omega_{i}=B_{i}
due to
\overline{B_{i}}\cap\mathrm{LFS}^{L}({u})=\varnothing
, ultimately yielding

\sum_{i\in\hat{II}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\|u(x)-u(x_{i})\|^{2}\ \mathbf{d}x\leq L^{2}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)d\kappa^{2}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0,

finishing the proof.
∎

\vec{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}=:(b(x_{i}))_{i\in I^{\kappa}}
as defined in (

.

Remark 3.7

. In fact, all that A4 requires through the constraints (a) and (b) is that elements that geometrically fully lie outside or inside the neighborhood of
x_{i}
due to the distance of
x_{j}
to
x_{i}
have to have exact weights, which we used in Lemma
3.1
. These conditions are fulfilled by many practically used weights since testing the distance

is already part of the algorithm to calculate the weights, or is otherwise feasible. For example, additionally to the weights in Remark

and those suggested in
[

]
, the PD-LAMMPS algorithm outlined in

The next steps to prove the convergence of this numerical model are structured as follows. We will first rewrite the numerical bilinear form into two equivalent forms to show point-wise convergence of the induced linear operator as well as coercitivity. These reformulations are lengthy, yet in essence no different than in the continuous case (See
[
18
]
, proof of Lemma 5, Proposition 1).
We begin with the operator form
\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
, motivated by the continuous equivalent

B(u,v)=\langle u,-\mathcal{L}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\,\,\longrightarrow\,\,\vec{u}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{v}=:\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},

then we will use the potential form
W^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
, motivated by

B(u,u)=2\int_{\Omega}W(u,x)\ \mathbf{d}x\,\,\longrightarrow\,\,\vec{u}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{u}=:2\sum_{i}V_{i}W^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}(\vec{u},x_{i}).

The first step is then to show that, for some
C>0
,

\forall v\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}v\xrightarrow[L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}]{\kappa\searrow 0}\mathcal{L}v,\,\,\text{ and }\|\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|\leqq C\text{ independent of }\kappa,

(24)

which will be done in Corollary
3.1
.
The second step will be to prove that for
\kappa>0
small enough and for all
\vec{u}\in\vec{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}
,

c^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i}V_{i}W^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}(\vec{u},x_{i})

(25)

for a
c^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}>0
independent of
0<\kappa<<1
. This will be proven later in Corollary
3.1
.

The latter property is based on the proof of the coercitivity of the continuous bilinear form (See
[
18
]
, Proposition 1 and Proposition 2).
These two requirements are sufficient for us to now prove the convergence.

Theorem 3.8

(
Convergence of the Numerical Discretization
)
Let
\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
be the solutions to the numerical discrete systems (
21
), then under Assumptions
3.1
, as well as (
24
) and (
25
),

\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\xrightarrow[L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}]{\kappa\searrow 0}u.

Proof.

Let
\kappa>0
be small enough for (
25
) to hold, as well as

\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}-b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|b\|

made possible by
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\rightarrow b
as assumed in A3 of Assumptions
3.1
. Then, using (
25
) and the fact that
\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}
is the solution of (
21
),

\displaystyle c^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}2\sum_{i}V_{i}W^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa},x_{i})=\vec{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{\kappa},\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq 1.5\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},

implying that the sequence
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
. By applying Banach-Alaoglu, one obtains a subsequence, written like the original sequence for convenience, and a weak limit
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
. This weak limit must lie in
\mathcal{V}
due to the weak closedness of
\mathcal{V}
.

We will now show
u^{\prime}=u
. Let
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Using Proposition
2
, there exist
\vec{v}_{\kappa}\in\vec{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\rightarrow v
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, then,

M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{\kappa},\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}\langle b,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},

as well as, using (
24
),

M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Further, again with (
24
),

\|\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}-\mathcal{L}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\|(\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}-\mathcal{L})v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}+\|\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|\|v-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0

leads to

\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{v}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}\langle u^{\prime},-\mathcal{L}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

In summary,

\langle u^{\prime},-\mathcal{L}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}=\langle b,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, therefore
u^{\prime}=u
.
Further, the uniqueness of
u
implies that the whole sequence
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
is weakly convergent to
u
.

We now show that the sequence
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
also strongly converges to
u
with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
norm. Let
\vec{s}_{\kappa}\in\vec{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
obtained just like in Proposition
2
.
We then have the inequality

\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}+\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}},

where we know the second summand vanishes. Turning our attention to the first, for
\kappa>0
small enough, one has using (
25
),

\displaystyle c^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\leq(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}})^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})+\vec{s}_{\kappa}^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{s}_{\kappa}-\vec{s}_{\kappa}^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})+\vec{s}_{\kappa}^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{s}_{\kappa}-M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}.

Where in the last line, we used that
\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}
solves (
21
).

Note that
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}\rightarrow u
,
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\rightharpoonup u
and
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\rightarrow b
, which leads to

M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{\kappa},\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(\vec{u}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}-\vec{s}_{\kappa})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0,

as well as

M\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\top}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{b}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{\kappa},\vec{s}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}\langle b,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

So what’s left to show is

\vec{s}_{\kappa}^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{s}_{\kappa}\rightarrow\langle b,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}=B(u,u).

First, we see that

\displaystyle\vec{s}_{\kappa}^{\top}\vec{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}\vec{s}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}+\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}(u-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Note that due to (
24
), there is a
C>0
such that
\|\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}\|\leq C
, leading to

|\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}(u-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}|\leq C\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

This leaves us with only having to show

\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa},-\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\rightarrow B(u,u)=\langle u,-\mathcal{L}u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}.

Due the strong convergence of the
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\vec{s}_{\kappa}
to
u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, this follows again from (
24
).
∎

All that is left to prove now is (
24
) and (
25
).
We begin with (
24
). First, the continuous operator can be split as follows (See
[
18
]
, Lemma 5),

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}u](x):=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{C}(x^{\prime},x)(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(x^{\prime},x):=k_{1}(x^{\prime},x)+k_{2}(x^{\prime},x),

\displaystyle k_{1}(x^{\prime},x):=(\alpha(x)+\alpha(x^{\prime}))\rho(x^{\prime}-x)\frac{\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|x^{\prime}-x\|)}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}}(x^{\prime}-x)(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} k_{2}(x^{\prime},x):=\int_{\Omega}&\tau(p)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|p-x\|)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|x^{\prime}-p\|)\rho(x-p)\rho(p-x^{\prime})(x-p)(p-x^{\prime})^{\top}\\
-&\tau(x^{\prime})\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|x^{\prime}-x\|)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|p-x^{\prime}\|)\rho(x-x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime}-p)(x-x^{\prime})(x^{\prime}-p)^{\top}\\
+&\tau(x)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|p-x\|)\chi_{[0,\delta)}(\|x^{\prime}-x\|)\rho(x-p)\rho(x-x^{\prime})(x-p)(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}\ \mathbf{d}p\\
\end{aligned}

We now split the integral in
k_{2}
into three terms corresponding to the summands in the integrand, therefore splitting
k_{2}(x^{\prime},x)
into
k_{2}^{p}(x^{\prime},x)
,
k_{2}^{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},x)
and
k_{2}^{x}(x^{\prime},x)
. Then, we split
\mathcal{L}
into four operators by splitting the integral in
\mathcal{L}
at the sum defining
\mathbf{C}
, to finally obtain

\displaystyle(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}k_{1}(x^{\prime},x)(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime},

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{2}^{x}u](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}k_{2}^{x}(x^{\prime},x)(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime},

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{2}^{x^{\prime}}u](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}k_{2}^{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},x)(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime},

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{2}^{p}u](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}k_{2}^{p}(x^{\prime},x)(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\ \mathbf{d}x^{\prime}.

The numerical operator will now be split in a similar way and the convergence and uniform boundedness claims will then be shown for each operator individually. The proofs for each operator are usually very similar and therefore partially omitted.

For notational simplicity we define
V_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}:=V_{j}w_{ij}
.

Lemma 3.9

(
Splitting the Numerical Linear Operator
)
The operator
\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}
can be split into

\mathcal{L}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}_{\kappa}=\mathcal{L}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{1}+\mathcal{L}^{x,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{2}+\mathcal{L}^{x^{\prime},{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{2}+\mathcal{L}^{p,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{2},

with, for

and

,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{1}u](x):=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\sum_{j\not=i}\Big[V_{i}V_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\rho(x_{j}-x_{i})(\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})+\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{j}))\tfrac{\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)^{\top}}{\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|}([\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{j}-[\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}^{x,{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}}_{2}u](x):=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\not=i\\
m\not=i\end{subarray}}\Big[V_{i}V_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}V_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{num}}(x_{i})\rho(x_{m}-x_{i})\rho(x_{j}-x_{i})

\displaystyle\phantom{=========================}\times\left(x_{m}-x_{i}\right)\left(x_{j}-x_{i}\right)^{\top}([\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{j}-[\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\phantom{=========================}\times\left(x_{i}-x_{j}\right)\left(x_{j}-x_{m}\right)^{\top}([\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{j}-[\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}u]_{i})\Big]

Lemma 3.11

(
Uniform Bound for the Integrals over the Numerical Weighting Function
)
Let

and define

. Then there is a constant

depending only on

where

\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x):=\begin{cases}0&x\in[-\tfrac{\kappa}{2},\tfrac{\kappa}{2})^{d}\\
\rho(x_{i})&x\in B_{i}\subset B_{R_{\kappa}}(0)\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

The domain
B_{R_{\kappa}}(0)
is partitioned into two sets,
A
and
B
, defined as

A:=B_{R_{\kappa}}(0)\cap\bigcup_{i\in Z^{\kappa}}B_{i},\,\,Z^{\kappa}:=\{i\in I^{\kappa}:x_{i}\text{ has a zero coordinate}\},\,\,\,B:=B_{R_{\kappa}}(0)\backslash A.

We now show

\displaystyle\int_{A}\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x)\ \mathbf{d}x\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0,

\displaystyle\int_{B}\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x)\ \mathbf{d}x\leq\int_{B_{R+2\delta}(0)}\frac{1}{\|x\|}\ \mathbf{d}x<\infty.

Due to the piecewise constant nature of
\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x)
, one can rewrite the integral over
A
as a sum. For
d=3
, this leads to

\displaystyle\int_{A}\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x)\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}6\kappa^{3}\sum_{R_{\kappa}/\kappa\geq i\geq 1}\frac{1}{\kappa i}+12\kappa^{3}\sum_{R_{\kappa}/\kappa\geq i\geq 1}\sum_{R_{\kappa}/\kappa\geq j\geq 1}\frac{1}{\kappa\sqrt{i^{2}+j^{2}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}6\kappa^{2}(1+\tfrac{2(R+2\delta)}{\kappa})\sum_{R_{\kappa}/\kappa\geq i\geq 1}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}6(\delta/\sqrt{d}+2(R+2\delta))\kappa\left(\log\left(\tfrac{R+2\delta}{\kappa}\right)+1\right)\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

The last inequality was implied by Proposition
3.1

Similarly, we obtain for

\displaystyle\int_{A}\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}(x)\ \mathbf{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}4\kappa^{2}\sum_{R_{\kappa}/\kappa\geq i\geq 1}\frac{1}{\kappa i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}4\kappa\left(\log\left(\tfrac{R+2\delta}{\kappa}\right)+1\right)\xrightarrow{\kappa\searrow 0}0.

For the integral over
B
, one has for all

0\leq\mathrm{step}^{\kappa}((x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{\top})\leq\Bigg(\sum_{d\geq i\geq 1}(x_{i}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\operatorname{sgn}(x_{i}))^{2}\Bigg)^{-1/2}=:F^{\kappa}((x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{\top}).

Then, since

is just a modified version of

Lemma 3.12

(

)
Let

The operators
