High-level convexity for products of squared Euclidean distance functions
Tudor Micu, Cornel Pintea, George C. Turcaş

Abstract
. We study smooth functions on Euclidean space whose Hessian is positive definite outside a bounded set, with emphasis on products of squared distance functions. More precisely, we first prove a simple convexity principle: if the superlevel region f − 1 ​ ( [ c , ∞ ) ) f^{-1}([c,\infty)) is contained in the Hessian-positive region of f f , then the sublevel set { f ≤ c } \{f\leq c\} is convex. We apply this to finite products F P ​ ( x ) = ∏ p ∈ P ‖ x − p ‖ 2 F_{P}(x)=\prod_{p\in P}\left\lVert x-p\right\rVert^{2} , proving that their Hessian-positive complements are bounded. For the two-centre product F p , q ​ ( x ) = ‖ x − p ‖ 2 ​ ‖ x − q ‖ 2 F_{p,q}(x)=\left\lVert x-p\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert x-q\right\rVert^{2} in dimension n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , we compute the Hessian-positive region and the exact value h max ​ ( F p , q ) = ‖ p − q ‖ 4 4 . h_{\max}(F_{p,q})=\frac{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert^{4}}{4}. 1.
Introduction

The set where the Hessian of a smooth function is positive definite could be regarded as a tool for discussing the convexity of the function near infinity. From a broader convex-analysis viewpoint, this also touches the Fenchel problem of level sets, namely the problem of recognising convexity from the geometry of level families
[
7
]
. In this short note, we
use this tool to study sublevel sets, continuing the line of high-level-set and
product results in

[
5
,
1
,
2
]
, and
apply it to products of squared Euclidean distances. The main class of examples is

F_{P}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert x-p_{i}\right\rVert^{2},

where
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, repetitions among the centres are allowed and
m\geq 1
.

The related literature studies functions with bounded Hessian-positive
complement and their high level sets, as well as products that preserve bounded
Hessian-positive complement. In particular, Brojbeanu and Pintea studied
products with bounded
\operatorname{Hess}^{+}
complements

[
1
,
2
]
. The present article
isolates the finite products of squared Euclidean distances and proves the
boundedness of their Hessian-positive complements directly, for all finite
configurations, by a leading-term argument. The final truncation-level
statement uses Pintea’s truncation framework
[
6
]
; the
finite-product theorem and the two-centre Hessian computation are proved
directly in the present work.

The note has three main contributions. First, Proposition

2.1
gives a direct dimension-free convexity
principle: if the high superlevel region of a
C^{2}
function is contained in
its Hessian-positive region, then the corresponding sublevel set is convex.
This isolates the fixed-level convexity mechanism behind earlier high-level
results. In contrast with the classical global first-/second-order criteria for quasi- and pseudo-convexity of
C^{2}
functions, Proposition
2.1
is a level-wise sufficient criterion that only requires Hessian positivity on the relevant high-value region
[
3
]
. Brojbeanu and Pintea prove such a high-level theorem for coercive
functions with bounded critical set and bounded Hessian-positive complement,
with convexity in the planar case
[
2
, Theorem 3.7]
.
Pintea proves compact connected regular high levels in all dimensions

[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
, and the three-dimensional
ovaloid/convex-boundary conclusion under bounded Hessian-positive complement

[
5
, Theorem 3.4 and Corollary 3.6]
. Our criterion does
not use planar curvature or three-dimensional ovaloid theory; it applies in
any dimension at every level satisfying

f^{-1}([c,\infty))\subset\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
. Proposition

2.2
and Corollary

2.3
record the resulting high-level
consequences.

Second, Theorem
3.1
gives the direct
leading-term proof that every nonempty finite product of squared distances has
bounded Hessian-positive complement. The corresponding high-level consequences
for this class are stated in Corollary
3.3
.
Third, and as the main model theorem, we compute the two-centre product
exactly.

For this model, writing

c=(p+q)/2
,
u=(p-q)/2
,
a=\left\lVert u\right\rVert
, and
y=x-c
, we compute the
normal form, gradient, Hessian, critical set, Hessian-positive region, and
compact Hessian-positive complement; see Lemma
4.1
,
Proposition
4.2
, Theorem

4.3
, and Corollary

4.4
. The boundary identity in Theorem

5.1
gives, for every
n\geq 2
,

h_{\max}(F_{p,q})=4a^{4}=\frac{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert^{4}}{4}.

The same value is the sharp infimum level for convexity of sublevel sets: we
prove convexity for
\lambda>4a^{4}
in Corollary

5.2
and nonconvexity for

0\leq\lambda<4a^{4}
in Theorem
5.3
. No endpoint
assertion for the sublevel
\{F_{p,q}\leq 4a^{4}\}
is needed. In the sense of

[
6
]
, Corollary
5.5
gives
the exact quasiconvexity and convexity truncation levels. For arbitrary finite
configurations we prove that
h_{\max}
is finite and attained whenever the
Hessian-positive complement is nonempty, but we do not claim a closed formula;
that remains a quantitative problem.

2.
Convexity from the Hessian-positive region

For a
C^{2}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, write

\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Hf(x)\text{ is positive definite}\}.

We say that
f
is norm-coercive if
f(x)\to+\infty
as
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\to\infty
.
When

K_{f}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)

is bounded, it is compact because it is closed, and we put

h_{\max}(f)=\sup_{x\in K_{f}}f(x),

with the convention
\sup\emptyset=-\infty
. Thus, if
K_{f}
is nonempty,

h_{\max}(f)
is a maximum.

The first observation we present is a convexity criterion. The proof does not require coercivity or any information about critical points. We just observe that a segment leaving a sublevel set would force an interior maximum along that segment, and such a maximum is incompatible with positive definiteness of the Hessian.

Proposition 2.1

(Convexity criterion)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{2}
. If

f^{-1}([c,\infty))\subseteq\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f),

then the sublevel set
E_{c}=\{x:f(x)\leq c\}
is convex.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in E_{c}
and suppose the segment
\gamma(t)=(1-t)a+tb
leaves
E_{c}
.
Then
\varphi=f\circ\gamma
has an interior maximum
t_{0}
with

\varphi(t_{0})>c
. Hence
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})\leq 0
. But

\gamma(t_{0})\in\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
, so

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})=(b-a)^{T}Hf(\gamma(t_{0}))(b-a)>0,

contradiction.
∎

The above criterion separates the convexity from the usual compactness and regularity issues. To turn it into a high-level statement, we also need to know that critical points cannot occur arbitrarily
far out
. This is already forced by the boundedness of the Hessian-positive complement, as we now show.

Proposition 2.2

(Bounded critical set)
.

If
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
is bounded, then the critical set
C(f)=\{x:\nabla f(x)=0\}
is bounded.

Proof.

Choose
R\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)\subset B(0,R)
.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that
C(f)
is unbounded. Then there exists a sequence
(x_{k})
of critical points with
\left\lVert x_{k}\right\rVert\to\infty
. Passing to a subsequence, we
may assume that
r_{k}=\left\lVert x_{k}\right\rVert
is strictly increasing and further that

u_{k}=x_{k}/r_{k}
converges in the unit sphere.

Fix
0<\delta<1
. Choose
k
large enough so that

r_{k}\sqrt{1-\delta/2}>R.

Then choose
l>k
so large that

\left\langle u_{k},u_{l}\right\rangle\geq 1-\delta.

Put
x=x_{k}
,
y=x_{l}
, and
z(t)=(1-t)x+ty
. Since
r_{l}\geq r_{k}
, for

0\leq t\leq 1
we have

\left\lVert z(t)\right\rVert^{2}\geq r_{k}^{2}\bigl((1-t)^{2}+t^{2}+2t(1-t)(1-\delta)\bigr)=r_{k}^{2}\bigl(1-2\delta t(1-t)\bigr)\geq r_{k}^{2}(1-\delta/2)>R^{2}.

Hence
z(t)\in\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
for every
t\in[0,1]
.

Let
g(t)=f(z(t))
. Then

g^{\prime\prime}(t)=(y-x)^{T}Hf(z(t))(y-x)>0

on
[0,1]
, so
g^{\prime}
is strictly increasing. But
x
and
y
are critical
points, and therefore

g^{\prime}(0)=\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle=0\text{ and }g^{\prime}(1)=\left\langle\nabla f(y),y-x\right\rangle=0,

a contradiction.
∎

We now combine the two elementary results above with coercivity. Above the height of the Hessian-positive complement, Proposition
2.1
gives convexity; above the critical values, the corresponding levels are regular.

Corollary 2.3

(High-level consequences)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{2}
and norm-coercive, and suppose that

K_{f}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
is bounded. Set

\gamma(f)=\max_{x\in C(f)}f(x).

Then, for every

c>\max\{h_{\max}(f),\gamma(f)\},

the sublevel set
E_{c}=\{f\leq c\}
is compact and convex, and the level set

M_{c}=f^{-1}(c)
is a compact regular
C^{2}
hypersurface. If
n\geq 2
, then

M_{c}
is connected and equals the boundary of a compact convex body.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
,
C(f)
is bounded. It is closed,
and since
f
is norm-coercive it is nonempty because
f
attains a global
minimum. Hence
\gamma(f)
is finite.

The set
E_{c}
is closed and bounded, hence compact. Since
c>h_{\max}(f)
, every
point with
f(x)\geq c
lies in
\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(f)
. Proposition

2.1
therefore implies that
E_{c}
is convex.

Since
c>\gamma(f)
, the level set
M_{c}
contains no critical point. Thus
c
is a
regular value and
M_{c}
is a
C^{2}
embedded hypersurface. The global minimum
value of
f
is strictly below
c
, and coercivity gives points with value above

c
along every sufficiently long ray; hence
M_{c}
is nonempty and compact.
Moreover
M_{c}=\partial E_{c}
: points with
f<c
are interior to
E_{c}
, and at a
regular point of the level
f=c
there are nearby points on both sides of the
level.

It remains only to record connectedness when
n\geq 2
. Let

x_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(E_{c})
. For
v\in S^{n-1}
, put

\rho(v)=\max\{t\geq 0:x_{0}+tv\in E_{c}\}.

Compactness gives existence of the maximum, and convexity shows that

x_{0}+\rho(v)v
is the unique point where the ray
x_{0}+\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}v
meets

\partial E_{c}
. The map
v\mapsto\rho(v)
is continuous: if
v_{j}\to v
and

\rho(v_{j})\to L
along a subsequence, then
x_{0}+Lv\in E_{c}
, so

L\leq\rho(v)
; conversely, for every
t<\rho(v)
the point
x_{0}+tv
lies in

\operatorname{int}(E_{c})
, hence
x_{0}+tv_{j}\in E_{c}
for all large
j
, giving

\liminf_{j}\rho(v_{j})\geq t
and then
\liminf_{j}\rho(v_{j})\geq\rho(v)
. Thus the
radial map
v\mapsto x_{0}+\rho(v)v
is continuous and onto
\partial E_{c}
.
Since
S^{n-1}
is connected for
n\geq 2
,
\partial E_{c}
is connected.
∎

3.
Finite products of squared distances

Let
n\geq 1
and let
P=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
be a nonempty finite list of points in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Repeated centres are allowed. Set

F_{P}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert x-p_{i}\right\rVert^{2}.

We first show that these have bounded Hessian-positive complements. The proof is purely asymptotic:
at infinity
,
F_{P}
has a certain leading term whose Hessian dominates all lower degree contributions.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every nonempty finite list
P
as above,

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{P})

is bounded.

Proof.

If
m=1
, then
HF_{P}=2I
everywhere, so the complement is empty. Assume

m\geq 2
.

Write

F_{P}(x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m}+Q(x),\text{ where }\deg Q\leq 2m-1.

The Hessian of the leading term is

H\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m}=2m\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m-2}I+4m(m-1)\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m-4}xx^{T}.

Thus for every unit vector
v
,

v^{T}H\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m}v\geq 2m\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m-2}.

Since every entry of
HQ
has degree at most
2m-3
, there is a constant

C>0
such that

\left\lVert HQ(x)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C(1+\left\lVert x\right\rVert)^{2m-3}

for all
x
. Hence, uniformly over all unit vectors
v
,

v^{T}HF_{P}(x)v\geq 2m\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m-2}-C(1+\left\lVert x\right\rVert)^{2m-3}.

This lower bound is positive for
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
sufficiently large. Thus
HF_{P}

is positive definite outside a ball, so the complement of
\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{P})
is
bounded.
∎

The argument above shows boundedness but does not yet reflect the geometry of the configuration. Recentering at the centroid removes the degree
2m-1
term and makes the first configuration-dependent correction visible.

Proposition 3.2

(Centroid expansion)
.

Let

\bar{p}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i},\text{ and }p_{i}=\bar{p}+u_{i},

so that
\sum_{i}u_{i}=0
. Then

F_{P}(\bar{p}+y)=\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m}+\widetilde{Q}(y),\text{ where }\deg\widetilde{Q}\leq 2m-2.

If
m\geq 2
, then the homogeneous term of degree
2m-2
is governed by the
second-moment matrix
M_{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}u_{i}^{T}
, More precisely, if we denote its trace by
\sigma_{P}=\operatorname{tr}(M_{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert^{2}
, then

F_{P}(\bar{p}+y)=\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-4}\bigl(\sigma_{P}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}-2y^{T}M_{P}y\bigr)+R(y),

where
\deg R\leq 2m-3
.

Proof.

For
x=\bar{p}+y
we have

\left\lVert x-p_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert y-u_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}-2\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle+\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert^{2}.

Thus

F_{P}(\bar{p}+y)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}-2\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle+\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\bigr).

The degree
2m
term is
\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m}
. The homogeneous term of degree

2m-1
comes from choosing the linear term in exactly one factor and

\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}
in all the others; it is

-2\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle=-2\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-2}\left\langle y,\sum_{i}u_{i}\right\rangle=0.

This proves the first assertion.

Assume now that
m\geq 2
. The homogeneous terms of degree
2m-2
have two
sources. Choosing the constant term in one factor gives

\sigma_{P}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-2}
. Choosing the linear terms in two distinct factors
gives

4\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-4}\sum_{i<j}\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle y,u_{j}\right\rangle.

Since
\sum_{i}\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle=0
, we have

\sum_{i<j}\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle y,u_{j}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left\langle y,u_{i}\right\rangle^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}M_{P}y.

Therefore the full homogeneous term of degree
2m-2
is

\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2m-4}\bigl(\sigma_{P}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}-2y^{T}M_{P}y\bigr).

All remaining terms have degree at most
2m-3
.
∎

We can now return from the asymptotic computations to the level-set geometry. Since
F_{P}
is coercive and its Hessian-positive complement is bounded, the general results of the previous section apply directly.

Corollary 3.3
.

For every nonempty finite list
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the critical set
C(F_{P})
is
bounded and
h_{\max}(F_{P})
is finite and attained if

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{P})
is nonempty. Moreover, all sufficiently high
sublevel sets of
F_{P}
are compact and convex. For
n\geq 2
, all sufficiently
high levels are compact connected regular hypersurfaces bounding compact convex
bodies.

Proof.

The boundedness of the critical set follows from Theorem

3.1
and Proposition

2.2
. The same theorem also shows that the
Hessian-positive complement is compact if it is nonempty, so
h_{\max}(F_{P})
is
finite and attained.

Finally,
F_{P}
is norm-coercive, since

F_{P}(x)/\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2m}\to 1
as
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\to\infty
. Applying Corollary

2.3
gives the stated high-level conclusions.
∎

4.
The two-centre product

Let
p\neq q
and consider the function

F_{p,q}(x)=\left\lVert x-p\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert x-q\right\rVert^{2}.

Set

c=\frac{p+q}{2},\quad u=\frac{p-q}{2},\quad a=\left\lVert u\right\rVert,\quad y=x-c.

Then

F_{p,q}(x)=(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})^{2}-4\left\langle y,u\right\rangle^{2}.

Looking at this form, one can guess why the two-centre case is explicitly computable:
apart from the radial term, only the direction
u
plays a role. The
quantity controlling the Hessian-positive region will be

D(y)=3\|y\|^{4}+4\langle y,u\rangle^{2}-2a^{2}\|y\|^{2}-a^{4}.

Lemma 4.1
.

One has

\nabla F_{p,q}(x)=4(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})y-8\left\langle y,u\right\rangle u

and

HF_{p,q}(x)=4(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})I+8(yy^{T}-uu^{T}).

Proof.

Since
p=c+u
and
q=c-u
, we have

F_{p,q}(x)=\left\lVert y-u\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert y+u\right\rVert^{2}=\bigl(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2}-2\left\langle y,u\right\rangle\bigr)\bigl(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2}+2\left\langle y,u\right\rangle\bigr),

which gives the displayed normal form. Differentiating with respect to
y

gives the gradient formula, and a second differentiation gives the Hessian
formula. The translation
x\mapsto y=x-c
does not change these derivatives.
∎

The gradient formula already determines the critical set. Any component of

y
orthogonal to
u
is multiplied by the positive factor

\|y\|^{2}+a^{2}
, so a critical point must lie on the line through the two
centres.

Proposition 4.2
.

(see also
[
1
, Example 2.1]
)
The critical set of
F_{p,q}
is

C(F_{p,q})=\{p,c,q\},

and the corresponding critical values are
0,a^{4},0
.

Proof.

If
\nabla F_{p,q}(x)=0
, then

(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})y=2\left\langle y,u\right\rangle u.

The component of
y
orthogonal to
u
must vanish, since

\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2}>0
. Hence
y=tu
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Substitution gives

(t^{2}+1)t=2t,

so
t\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Thus
x=c+tu
is one of
p,c,q
, and direct evaluation
gives the stated critical values.
∎

For the computation of
h_{\max}
, the critical points alone are not
enough; we need the full Hessian-positive region. The next result is a generalization to arbitrary dimension of
[
1
, Remark 3.2]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
n\geq 2
,

HF_{p,q}(x)\succ 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{4}+4\left\langle y,u\right\rangle^{2}-2a^{2}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}>a^{4}.

Proof.

Let
e_{1}=u/a
. If
y
is not parallel to
u
, choose a unit vector
e_{2}

so that
y\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
. If
y
is parallel to
u
,
choose any unit vector
e_{2}\perp e_{1}
. Write
y=se_{1}+te_{2}
, with
t=0

in the parallel case.

On
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}^{\perp}
, the two rank-one terms in the
Hessian vanish, so
HF_{p,q}
acts by the positive scalar

4(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})
. On
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
, the Hessian block is

4\begin{pmatrix}3s^{2}+t^{2}-a^{2}&2st\\
2st&s^{2}+3t^{2}+a^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The determinant of the inner matrix is

3(s^{2}+t^{2})^{2}+4a^{2}s^{2}-2a^{2}(s^{2}+t^{2})-a^{4}=D(y),

because
\left\langle y,u\right\rangle=as
. The trace of the full
2\times 2
block is

16(s^{2}+t^{2})=16\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}
. Thus, if
D(y)>0
, then
y\neq 0
and this trace is
positive; for a real symmetric
2\times 2
matrix this is equivalent to
positive definiteness of the block. Conversely, a positive definite block has
positive determinant, hence
D(y)>0
. Combining this with the positive
orthogonal directions proves the criterion. The same block decomposition gives
the useful determinant identity

\det HF_{p,q}(x)=4^{n}(\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+a^{2})^{n-2}D(y),

but the positive-definiteness criterion above uses the block decomposition, not
determinant positivity alone in higher dimension.
∎

The same inequality also gives a useful geometric bound. Points in the
Hessian-positive complement cannot lie farther from the midpoint
c
than
the two centres themselves.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
n\geq 2
,

K:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})=\{x:D(x-c)\leq 0\}

is compact and satisfies
K\subseteq\overline{B}(c,a)
.

Proof.

If
x\in K
and
r=\left\lVert y\right\rVert
, then

3r^{4}+4\left\langle y,u\right\rangle^{2}-2a^{2}r^{2}\leq a^{4}.

Dropping the nonnegative middle term gives

3r^{4}-2a^{2}r^{2}-a^{4}\leq 0.

With
z=r^{2}
, this is

3z^{2}-2a^{2}z-a^{4}\leq 0,

whose roots are
-a^{2}/3
and
a^{2}
. Since
z\geq 0
, we get
r\leq a
. The set

K=\{D\leq 0\}
is closed, so it is compact.
∎

5.
Exact
h_{\max}
and sharpness

We now maximize
F_{p,q}
on the Hessian-positive complement.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
p\neq q
and
n\geq 2
,

h_{\max}(F_{p,q})=4a^{4}=\frac{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert^{4}}{4}.

Proof.

Let
K=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})
. By Corollary

4.4
,
K
is compact, so
F_{p,q}|_{K}

attains its maximum.

First suppose that a maximizer lies in the interior of
K
. Then it is an
unconstrained local maximum of
F_{p,q}
, hence a critical point. By
Proposition
4.2
, the only critical points are

p,c,q
. The points
p
and
q
lie in
\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})
, since

D(\pm u)=4a^{4}>0
, while
c\in K
, since
D(0)=-a^{4}
, and

F_{p,q}(c)=a^{4}
. Hence every interior critical maximum on
K
has value at
most
a^{4}
.

Now suppose that a maximizer lies on
\partial K
. Then
D(y)=0
, i.e.

3\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{4}+4\left\langle y,u\right\rangle^{2}-2a^{2}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}=a^{4}.

Substitution into the normal form gives the boundary identity

F_{p,q}(x)=4\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{4}.

Since
K\subseteq\overline{B}(c,a)
, every boundary value is at most
4a^{4}
.
Equality is attained whenever
\left\lVert y\right\rVert=a
and
\left\langle y,u\right\rangle=0
, which is possible
because
n\geq 2
. Therefore

h_{\max}(F_{p,q})=4a^{4}=\frac{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert^{4}}{4}.

∎

Once
h_{\max}
is known, convexity above this value follows directly from
the criterion of Section 2.

Corollary 5.2
.

For every
\lambda>4a^{4}
, the sublevel set
\{F_{p,q}\leq\lambda\}
is convex.

Proof.

If
F_{p,q}(x)\geq\lambda>h_{\max}(F_{p,q})
, then
x\in\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})
.
Thus
F_{p,q}^{-1}([\lambda,\infty))\subseteq\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})
, and
Proposition
2.1
applies.
∎

It remains to show that the constant cannot be lowered. The construction
below exhibits, for every level below
4a^{4}
, two points in the sublevel
set whose midpoint lies outside it.

Theorem 5.3

(Sharpness below the threshold)
.

For
n\geq 2
and every
0\leq\lambda<4a^{4}
, the sublevel set

\{F_{p,q}\leq\lambda\}
is not convex.

Proof.

Put
\rho^{2}=\lambda/(4a^{2})
. Since
\lambda<4a^{4}
, we have
0\leq\rho<a
.
Choose
w\perp u
with
\left\lVert w\right\rVert=\rho
, and set
e=u/a
. Define

y_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{a^{2}-\rho^{2}}\,e+w.

Then
\left\lVert y_{\pm}\right\rVert=a
and

\left\langle y_{\pm},u\right\rangle=\pm a\sqrt{a^{2}-\rho^{2}}.

Using the normal form,

F_{p,q}(c+y_{\pm})=(2a^{2})^{2}-4a^{2}(a^{2}-\rho^{2})=4a^{2}\rho^{2}=\lambda.

Thus
c+y_{+}
and
c+y_{-}
lie in the sublevel set. Their midpoint is
c+w
,
and
\left\langle w,u\right\rangle=0
, so

F_{p,q}(c+w)=(a^{2}+\rho^{2})^{2}.

Therefore

F_{p,q}(c+w)-\lambda=\left(a^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4a^{2}}\right)^{2}-\lambda=\frac{(4a^{4}-\lambda)^{2}}{16a^{4}}>0.

The midpoint is not in the sublevel set. Hence the sublevel set is not convex.
For
\lambda=0
, this is the same construction with
w=0
and endpoints

p,q
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Corollary
5.2
and Theorem

5.3
show that
4a^{4}
is the sharp infimum level for
convexity of the sublevel sets. We do not use or assert convexity at the
endpoint
\lambda=4a^{4}
.

Finally, we translate this sharp level statement into the truncation
terminology of
[
6
]
. For a real number
\tau
, write

T_{\tau}(f)=\max\{\tau,f\}
, and denote by
sql(f)
and
scl(f)
the
smallest quasiconvexity and convexity truncation levels, respectively.. The truncation levels are taken over

\tau\geq\inf f
, as defined in
[
6
, Section 2]
.

Corollary 5.5
.

For
n\geq 2
,

\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q})=\operatorname{scl}(F_{p,q})=h_{\max}(F_{p,q})=\frac{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert^{4}}{4}.

Proof.

Let
h=4a^{4}
. Corollary
5.2
shows that
for every
\tau>h
, all sublevel sets
\{F_{p,q}\leq r\}
with
r\geq\tau

are convex. The sublevel sets of
T_{\tau}(F_{p,q})
below
\tau
are empty.
Hence
T_{\tau}(F_{p,q})
is quasiconvex for every
\tau>h
, and

\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q})\leq h
.

Conversely,
\inf F_{p,q}=0
. Thus the only truncation levels below
h
allowed
by the definition satisfy
0\leq\tau<h
. For such a
\tau
, choose
r
with

\tau\leq r<h
. By Theorem
5.3
, the sublevel set

\{F_{p,q}\leq r\}
is not convex. Since
r\geq\tau
, this is also a sublevel set
of
T_{\tau}(F_{p,q})
, so
T_{\tau}(F_{p,q})
is not quasiconvex. Thus

\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q})\geq h
, and

\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q})=h
.

The critical set of
F_{p,q}
is finite by Proposition

4.2
, and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\operatorname{Hess}^{+}(F_{p,q})
is
compact by Corollary
4.4
. Therefore

[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
applies and gives

\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q})\leq\operatorname{scl}(F_{p,q})\leq\max\{\operatorname{sql}(F_{p,q}),h_{\max}(F_{p,q})\}=h.

Hence
\operatorname{scl}(F_{p,q})=h
as well.
∎

6.
Cassini comparison and open problems

For
p=(-a,0)
and
q=(a,0)
in the plane,

F_{p,q}(x,y)=((x+a)^{2}+y^{2})((x-a)^{2}+y^{2})=f_{a}(x,y)+a^{4},

where

f_{a}(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}-2a^{2}(x^{2}-y^{2}).

Thus the classical Cassini polynomial
f_{a}
differs from the squared-distance
product by the constant
a^{4}
. This shifted quartic is the planar example
used in
[
4
,
6
]
. The shift matters for every threshold
statement: the product value at the midpoint is
a^{4}
, while the exact
Hessian-complement maximum for the product is
4a^{4}
.

The preceding finite-product theorem gives bounded Hessian-positive complement
for arbitrary nonempty finite configurations and hence guarantees that

h_{\max}(F_{P})
is finite and attained whenever this complement is nonempty.
Determining it explicitly for general configurations with more than two centres
is not addressed here. Even in the plane, the problem becomes a polynomial
optimization problem on a compact set defined by polynomial inequalities;
special symmetric configurations may admit formulas, but no configuration-free
expression is asserted in this article.
