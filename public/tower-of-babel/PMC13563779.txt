Doctor-nurse integrated management is associated with improved clinical outcomes in AECOPD patients with multimorbidity: A propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study
Authors: Li Wang, Yaping Dou, Kun Li, Yajie Wang, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) are frequently complicated by multimorbidity, particularly cardiovascular and metabolic disorders such as heart failure and diabetes. These coexisting conditions create therapeutic conflicts and increase the risk of poor outcomes. Effective management strategies addressing both pulmonary and associated disorders in this high-risk population are urgently needed. This study aimed to evaluate whether a Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management (DNIM) model is associated with better clinical outcomes compared to routine care in AECOPD patients with multimorbidity.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary hospital in China, screening electronic medical records of patients admitted for AECOPD with confirmed multimorbidity (defined as Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥ 2 or documented cardiac/metabolic comorbidities) between January 2022 and January 2025. Patients were allocated to either a DNIM group, receiving structured collaborative care including joint ward rounds, integrated care plans addressing both pulmonary and comorbid conditions, and collaborative discharge planning, or a routine care (RC) group. Propensity score matching (1:1) was employed to balance baseline characteristics. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality or readmission for cardiopulmonary events within one year. Secondary outcomes included length of stay (LOS), improvement in COPD Assessment Test (CAT) scores, and in-hospital complications.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 104 patients (52 pairs) with well-balanced baseline characteristics were included. The DNIM group demonstrated a significantly lower associated risk of the one-year composite endpoint compared to the RC group (Hazard Ratio [HR] 0.58, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 0.35–0.96, P = 0.034). Patients in the DNIM group also had a shorter mean length of stay (8.4 ± 2.1 vs. 11.2 ± 3.4 days, P < 0.001) and greater improvement in CAT scores (ΔCAT − 6.2 ± 2.5 vs. -4.1 ± 2.2, P < 0.01). Furthermore, the DNIM model was linked to fewer in-hospital complications (11.5% vs. 28.8%, P = 0.028), particularly hyperglycemia and acute heart failure.ConclusionThe Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management model was associated with a better one-year prognosis, reduced length of stay, and decreased in-hospital complications in AECOPD patients with multimorbidity. This integrated approach addresses the complex interplay between COPD and its associated comorbidities, supporting its implementation in respiratory clinical practice to enhance patient-centered outcomes.

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, presenting a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) are critical events in the natural history of the disease, often driving disease progression and accounting for a significant proportion of healthcare costs [1–3]. However, in clinical reality, AECOPD is rarely an isolated condition, particularly among the elderly demographic. It is estimated that over 70% of COPD patients suffer from at least one chronic comorbidity, a phenomenon known as multimorbidity [4]. Common comorbidities include cardiovascular diseases (e.g., heart failure, ischemic heart disease), metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes mellitus), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [5, 6].

The presence of multimorbidity creates a “syndemic” effect where diseases interact biologically and socially to worsen patient outcomes [7]. For instance, the systemic inflammation characteristic of AECOPD can precipitate acute heart failure, while the use of systemic corticosteroids—a cornerstone of AECOPD management—can induce severe hyperglycemia in diabetic patients [8, 9]. This complexity introduces significant therapeutic conflicts. Treating the lung alone while neglecting the heart or metabolic status often leads to iatrogenic complications, prolonged hospitalization, and increased readmission rates [10]. Therefore, the management of AECOPD with multimorbidity requires a delicate balance that transcends organ-specific guidelines.

Despite this complexity, traditional healthcare delivery in many settings remains fragmented and “siloed.” Physicians typically focus on diagnostic decision-making and prescribing medication, while nurses are responsible for execution and basic care [11]. For example, a physician might prescribe high-dose nebulized bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids for acute bronchospasm, potentially overlooking the risk of precipitating tachycardia and fluid retention in a patient with co-existing heart failure, unless a nurse promptly identifies and communicates early signs of worsening cardiac decompensation, a critical oversight known to increase mortality risk [12]. Evidence suggests that fragmented care contributes to medication errors, delayed recognition of clinical deterioration, and patient dissatisfaction [13]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for organizational innovation in the management of these complex patients.

The Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management (DNIM) model has emerged as a promising solution to bridge this gap. Unlike traditional teamwork, DNIM emphasizes a structured, synchronous collaboration where doctors and nurses conduct joint ward rounds, formulate integrated care plans, and share decision-making responsibilities regarding discharge and rehabilitation [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of collaborative care models in chronic disease management, such as diabetes [15]. In the context of respiratory diseases, integrated care has shown potential to improve inhaler technique adherence and quality of life [16]. However, existing literature largely focuses on general COPD populations or isolated comorbidity management. Data specifically targeting the vulnerable sub-population of AECOPD patients with complex multimorbidity—who theoretically stand to benefit most from holistic oversight—remains scarce [17, 18].

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the impact of the DNIM model on the prognosis of AECOPD patients with multimorbidity. We hypothesized that by fostering real-time communication and holistic assessment, the DNIM model would be associated with a reduced incidence of adverse events and improved recovery efficiency compared to routine care. To mitigate the selection bias inherent in retrospective observational studies, we employed Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to ensure a rigorous comparison. This study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies [19].

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis.

Participants

We screened the electronic medical records of patients admitted between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Primary diagnosis of AECOPD according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2024 criteria [20]; (2) Age ≥ 60 years; (3) Confirmed multimorbidity, defined as a Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score ≥ 2 [21] or the documented presence of at least one of the following key comorbidities: chronic heart failure (CHF) (NYHA class II-IV or NT-proBNP > 125 pg/mL), diabetes mellitus (HbA1c > 6.5%), coronary artery disease (CAD), or chronic kidney disease (CKD) (stage 3–5). Exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients with primary psychiatric disorders or severe cognitive impairment preventing cooperation; (2) Presence of terminal malignancy with a life expectancy of less than 6 months; (3) Incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up within the first month post-discharge.

Grouping and intervention

Based on the implementation timeline of the integrated management protocol in our department, patients were divided into two groups: the Routine Care (RC) group (admitted before the full implementation of DNIM or managed by non-DNIM teams) and the Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management (DNIM) group. The integrated management protocol for AECOPD patients was gradually implemented starting in June 2023 to address the high readmission rates and care fragmentation previously observed in multimorbid cases. Prior to this, standard routine care was the norm. Physicians providing care in both RC and DNIM groups possessed equivalent qualifications (board-certified respiratory attendings), with the primary difference being the structural pattern of interprofessional collaboration. Because group allocation was based on this timeline, a before-after design is inherent.

Routine Care (RC) group

Patients received standard medical treatment and nursing care. Physicians conducted ward rounds in the morning to prescribe medications and order tests. Nurses executed these orders and performed routine nursing assessments separately. Communication primarily occurred through written orders or brief verbal exchanges regarding specific acute events.

DNIM group

Patients were managed by a designated integrated team comprising senior respiratory physicians, specialized nurses, and clinical pharmacists, a model informed by principles of integrated disease management [22]. The core components of the DNIM model included: (1) Joint Ward Rounds: Physicians, clinical pharmacists, and nurses assessed patients together daily every morning. This allowed for immediate reconciliation of medical goals with nursing observations. (2) Integrated Care Plans: Customized standardized pathways addressed both COPD and comorbidities. For instance, for diabetic patients on systemic glucocorticoids, a dynamic insulin adjustment protocol was co-managed by the team to prevent hyperglycemia. (3) Collaborative Discharge Planning: Discharge education was conducted jointly, covering inhaler techniques, dietary restrictions for heart failure/diabetes, and symptom self-monitoring strategies. Furthermore, the DNIM model extended into the post-discharge phase, where nurses and pharmacists conducted structured telephone follow-ups monthly up to 90 days.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health record system. Baseline demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and GOLD stage. Clinical variables included forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) % predicted, CCI score, and baseline laboratory markers (e.g., N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP], hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], C-reactive protein [CRP]). The follow-up period extended for one year post-discharge, with data obtained through outpatient visits and telephone interviews. Missing data for baseline covariates (< 5%) were addressed using multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE).

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was a composite outcome of all-cause mortality or readmission for cardiopulmonary events (AECOPD, acute heart failure, acute coronary syndrome) within one year of discharge. Readmission events were tracked via the hospital’s electronic health records and verified during structured follow-up calls. Secondary endpoints included: (1) Length of Stay (LOS); (2) Hospitalization costs, calculated based on total direct medical billing from admission to discharge; (3) Improvement in health status, measured by the change in COPD Assessment Test (CAT) scores and modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale from admission to discharge; (4) Incidence of in-hospital complications, specifically acute kidney injury (AKI), hyperglycemia (blood glucose > 10 mmol/L requiring intervention), and electrolyte disturbances.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

To minimize selection bias inherent in observational studies, we employed Propensity Score Matching (PSM) [23]. A logistic regression model was used to calculate the propensity score for each patient based on covariates including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, CCI score, FEV1%, and presence of specific comorbidities (CHF, Diabetes). We performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper width of 0.02. Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) were calculated, with an SMD < 0.1 indicating negligible imbalance.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves based on the matched cohort were generated to visualize the time to the primary composite endpoint, and differences were analyzed using the Log-rank test. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to determine independent predictors. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Given the total number of composite events (n = 35), variables for the multivariable model were purposefully restricted to the most clinically relevant factors to minimize the risk of overfitting, adhering as closely as possible to standard statistical guidelines for event-per-variable ratios. Subgroup analyses were performed based on age and key comorbidities. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

The screening took place in a 1500-bed tertiary hospital with approximately 1500 annual respiratory admissions. A total of 450 patients admitted with AECOPD were initially screened, representing the subset meeting our broad diagnostic and age criteria over the study period. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 120 patients with multimorbidity were eligible for the study. Among them, 60 were in the RC group and 60 in the DNIM group. This specific yield of 60 patients in each group was the natural consequence of applying our strict multimorbidity inclusion criteria to two consecutive and equally prolonged clinical periods (the historical period for RC and the concurrent period for DNIM), without any predetermined quota. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Before matching, there were slight differences in age and CCI scores between the groups. After 1:1 PSM, 52 pairs (n = 104) were successfully matched. As shown in Table 1, all baseline characteristics, including age (72.4 vs. 71.9 years), smoking status, lung function, and prevalence of heart failure and diabetes, were well-balanced between the two groups (all SMD < 0.1, P > 0.05). The distribution of propensity scores before and after matching is visualized in Fig. 2, demonstrating excellent overlap and match quality.

Primary outcome: prognosis analysis

During the one-year follow-up, the DNIM group experienced significantly fewer adverse events compared to the RC group. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 3) revealed that the cumulative freedom from the composite endpoint (readmission or death) was significantly higher in the DNIM group (76.9%) compared to the RC group (55.8%) (Log-rank P = 0.034). Specifically, among the composite events, the DNIM group had 1 mortality (1.9%) and 11 readmissions (21.2%), while the RC group had 3 mortalities (5.8%) and 20 readmissions (38.5%). The rate of readmission due to acute heart failure decompensation was markedly lower in the DNIM group (9.6% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.04), suggesting better management of fluid status and cardiac load.

Secondary outcomes: efficiency and symptom improvement

The DNIM model demonstrated superior clinical efficiency (Table 2). The mean Length of Stay (LOS) was significantly shorter in the DNIM group compared to the RC group (8.4 ± 2.1 days vs. 11.2 ± 3.4 days, P < 0.001). Consequently, the average hospitalization cost was also reduced in the DNIM group (12,450 ± 1,800 vs. 15,600 ± 2,500 RMB, P < 0.001).

Regarding symptom improvement, both groups showed reductions in CAT scores and mMRC scales from admission to discharge. However, the magnitude of improvement was significantly greater in the DNIM group. As illustrated in Fig. 4; Table 2, the reduction in CAT score (ΔCAT) was − 6.2 ± 2.5 points in the DNIM group versus − 4.1 ± 2.2 points in the RC group (P = 0.002). Similarly, the DNIM group exhibited a more pronounced relief in dyspnea (ΔmMRC − 1.2 ± 0.5 vs. -0.6 ± 0.4, P < 0.001), indicating a more rapid and comprehensive recovery of health status.

Safety and complications

In-hospital safety analysis revealed a significant reduction in complications in the DNIM group. The overall incidence of monitored complications (hyperglycemia, AKI, acute heart failure, electrolyte disturbance) was 11.5% (6/52) in the DNIM group versus 28.8% (15/52) in the RC group (P = 0.028). Specifically, hyperglycemic events requiring rescue insulin were observed in only 2 patients in the DNIM group compared to 8 in the RC group, highlighting the effectiveness of the integrated glucose management protocol during steroid therapy (Fig. 5). Incidence of AKI (3.8% vs. 7.7%, P = 0.40) and electrolyte disturbances (1.9% vs. 7.7%, P = 0.17) also showed favorable trends in the DNIM group.

Subgroup and multivariate analysis

Subgroup analysis (Fig. 6) demonstrated that the protective effect of DNIM was consistent across most subgroups. Notably, the benefit appeared more pronounced in patients with co-existing Heart Failure (HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.29–0.91) and those aged > 75 years (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31–0.98). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 3), after adjusting for age, BMI, lung function, and CCI score, the DNIM model remained an independent protective factor associated with better prognosis (while the crude HR from the matched cohort was 0.58, the multivariable adjusted HR was 0.54, 95% CI 0.33–0.89, P = 0.015).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study, utilizing propensity score matching, provides compelling evidence that the Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management (DNIM) model is associated with significantly improved prognosis in AECOPD patients with multimorbidity. Our findings indicate that compared to routine care, DNIM was linked to a reduction in the one-year risk of readmission or death by approximately 42%, shortened hospital length of stay by nearly 3 days, and was associated with a lower incidence of in-hospital complications such as hyperglycemia and acute heart failure. These results highlight the critical value of a synchronized, holistic approach in managing the “syndemic” complexity of modern respiratory patients.

The core strength of the DNIM model lies in bridging the “know-do” gap. In traditional care, while physicians may recognize the theoretical need to balance fluid intake in a heart failure patient, the execution often falters due to delayed feedback from nursing staff regarding urine output or subtle signs of edema, a challenge that has been highlighted in studies on interprofessional ward rounds [24]. Our study confirms that the “Joint Ward Rounds” component of DNIM facilitates real-time information exchange, which aligns with evidence that structured interdisciplinary bedside rounds significantly improve interprofessional communication and workflow efficiency [25]. This integration of the decision-maker (physician) and the executor (nurse) at the bedside allows for dynamic adjustments of therapy—such as titrating diuretics or insulin—and is supported by research showing that multidisciplinary rounds are associated with reduced mortality in cardiovascular patients [26]. Such timely, collaborative adjustments likely contributed to the observed reduction in acute heart failure and hyperglycemic events, as structured inter-disciplinary rounds have been shown to lower the rate of adverse events [27].

A unique contribution of our study is the specific focus on multimorbidity. Previous research has often excluded patients with severe comorbidities to obtain a “clean” COPD cohort, yet these are precisely the patients who consume the most healthcare resources [28]. Our subgroup analysis revealed that patients with co-existing heart failure derived the most benefit from DNIM. However, these subgroup findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating due to the small sample size. This is biologically plausible, as the heart-lung interaction requires meticulous fluid management that routine care often struggles to provide, a challenge addressed by integrated care bundles [29]. Similarly, the reduction in hyperglycemic events supports the efficacy of integrated care plans for diabetic COPD patients, addressing the well-known adverse effect of systemic corticosteroids within structured management pathways [30]. This corroborates the established view that integrated care pathways are superior for managing complex chronic conditions [30].

Furthermore, the economic implications of our findings are substantial. The reduction in length of stay and subsequent readmissions translates to significant cost savings, a critical outcome within value-based healthcare frameworks where models that concurrently improve quality and reduce costs are highly prioritized [31]. It is worth noting that the overall LOS in our cohort is longer than typically observed in Western countries, which is partly attributable to differences in healthcare systems, as recovery periods often managed in community settings in the West are routinely completed within acute care hospitals in China. The improvement in CAT and mMRC scores also suggests that patients in the DNIM group achieved a better functional status at discharge, which is a known predictor of lower readmission risk, as demonstrated in studies of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease where CAT score was integral to readmission prediction models [32].

Despite these promising results, our study has limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, causal inference is limited, although PSM was used to mitigate selection bias. Second, the before-after allocation inherently introduces temporal bias. Overall hospital care protocols, equipment, and medical resources might have naturally evolved during the transition period, contributing to unmeasured confounding that PSM cannot entirely eliminate. Third, the sample size (n = 120) is relatively modest, which may limit the power of some subgroup analyses. Additionally, although we restricted the number of covariates in our multivariable Cox regression to minimize overfitting, the event-per-variable ratio was slightly below the optimal threshold of 10, indicating that these specific HR estimates should be interpreted with caution. Future multi-center randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to validate these findings and explore the cost-effectiveness of DNIM on a larger scale, a common recommendation following the development of novel predictive or interventional models in healthcare [33].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Doctor-Nurse Integrated Management model shows significant promise in managing AECOPD patients with multimorbidity. By fostering collaboration and holistic assessment, DNIM is associated with fewer adverse events, minimizes in-hospital complications, and links to better recovery efficiency. This model represents a paradigm shift from disease-centered to patient-centered care and should be considered for widespread implementation in respiratory wards managing complex elderly populations.
