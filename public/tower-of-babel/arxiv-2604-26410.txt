Longitudinal Outcomes Truncated by Death: Causal Estimands and Bayesian Estimators
Juliette Ortholand PhD, Young Lee PhD, Marie-Abele C Bind PhD

Abstract
Defining a causal estimand for a longitudinal outcome truncated by death is challenging, because the outcome may be undefined at the end of follow-up. Although a range of estimands and several estimators have been proposed, guidance on the underlying causal assumptions and on the contexts in which each estimand is most appropriate remains limited. We propose a framework to clarify the challenges of defining causal estimands in a longitudinal setting with censoring due to death. Within this framework, we review existing estimands and make explicit the assumptions required for their identification and estimation. We develop Bayesian estimators for each estimand and compare their behavior in a simulation study. Finally, we illustrate the proposed approach using data from a randomized controlled trial in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. We show that the main difficulty arises from the lack of a natural notion of ordering and distance for outcomes truncated by death. This leads to an inherently multifactorial problem. In this context, the stratified average causal effect, combined with restricted mean survival time, provides a more complete characterisation of treatment effects.

1
Introduction

Quality of life (QOL) and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), typically measured longitudinally, are key endpoints in clinical analysis and are increasingly regarded as essential even in settings with high mortality
[
38
]
. In such contexts, however, a non-negligible proportion of patients may die before the end of follow-up, rendering the longitudinal outcome ill-defined. In randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this feature complicates the formulation of a scientifically meaningful causal estimand for assessing treatment effects on such outcomes.

Some causal estimands have been proposed to address this issue, e.g.,
[
16
,
41
,
11
]
, but their use in practice remains limited, partly owing to the absence of a framework for comparing and interpreting them.
In parallel, a range of estimators has been developed to tackle this problem, notably based on landmarking
[
4
]
and joint modeling approaches
[
33
]
. However, despite the central role of estimands emphasized in the ICH E9 addendum
[
1
]
and in recent work advocating a shift in focus away from modeling alone
[
8
]
, these methods are typically not accompanied by an explicit formulation of the causal estimand they estimate. Consistently with this, a recent review of methods for handling death in RCTs summarized several analytical strategies, yet without framing them in terms of causal estimands
[
38
]
.

In this context, our contribution is twofold. First, we propose a general causal framework to formalize the challenges arising from truncation due to death when defining causal estimands for longitudinal outcomes. Within this framework, we review several existing estimands and assess their interpretability.
Second, for each selected estimand, we make explicit the assumptions required for the estimation of an unbiased estimate, and develop Bayesian estimators to investigate their statistical behavior. After validating these methods through simulation, we illustrate their application using data from a randomized controlled trial in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), a disease for which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires trials to assess treatment effects on both overall survival and longitudinal outcome capturing functional abilities
[
15
]
.

2
Motivating Example and Data Structure

2.1
Treatment Effect for Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

The motivating example considered in this paper is the estimation of a treatment effect on a longitudinal functional score in ALS.
ALS is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive loss of motor function and eventual death from respiratory failure
[
37
]
. The median survival time from disease onset is approximately 3–4 years
[
37
]
. Functional ability in daily activities, intended to capture patients’ functional decline, is most commonly assessed using the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRSr)
[
36
]
, a longitudinal score ranging from 48 to 0, with lower scores indicating more severe disease.

Longitudinal outcomes truncated by death pose a central challenge in ALS controlled trials, motivating the development of several methodological approaches. In its 2019 guidance, the FDA recommended “an analysis method that combines survival and function into a single overall measure.”
[
5
]
. In response, several composite estimands tailored to ALS have been proposed, including the Combined Assessment of Function and Survival (CAFS)
[
7
]
and the Patient-Ranked Order Of Function (PROOF)
[
39
]
. These clinically motivated estimands are grounded in more general rank-based estimands, such as the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney estimand (further discussed in Section
4.4.2
), for which a causal interpretation is not straightforward.
More recently, a model-based estimand has also been proposed
[
31
]
. However, this approach departs from European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommendations
[
1
]
and from general principles of causal inference, in that it defines the estimand through strong modeling assumptions rather than directly in terms of potential outcomes.

3
Estimands

Patients enrolled in randomized controlled trials are rarely representative of the target population. Accordingly, we focus on finite-sample estimands and do not consider generalization to a superpopulation, which would require additional assumptions and careful analysis
[
10
]
. In this section, we review classical causal estimands, discuss the challenges arising from truncation due to death, and outline the main approaches proposed to address them.

3.1
Classic Estimands

On the one hand, in the absence of death truncation, a commonly used causal estimand for longitudinal outcomes is the Average Causal Effect (ACE), defined at time
t
as:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{ACE}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)\right\}.

(1)

On the other hand, in survival analysis, two fundamental quantities are the hazard function and the survival function. For unit
i
under treatment level
W
, the hazard at time
t
is defined as the instantaneous event rate at time
t
, conditional on survival up to time
t
, while the survival function gives the probability of remaining event-free beyond time
t
. Formally,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{\Pr\!\left(t\leq T_{i}(W)<t+dt\,\middle|\,T_{i}(W)\geq t\right)}{dt},

(2)

\displaystyle S_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(T_{i}(W)>t\right).

(3)

These quantities form the foundation for defining causal estimands in time-to-event settings.
A commonly used hazard-based estimand is the hazard ratio:
\tau^{HR}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{i,t}(1)}{\lambda_{i,t}(0)}\right\}
. However, owing to the complexity of its causal interpretation and estimation, hazard-ratio-based estimands should be interpreted with caution
[
14
]
. For survival-based estimands, the Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) is widely used
[
43
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle\tau^{RMST}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\int^{t}_{0}S_{i,u}(1)-S_{i,u}(0)du\right\}=\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{T_{i,t}(1)-T_{i,t}(0)\right\}

(4)

The RMST admits a clear causal and clinical interpretation and is therefore adopted as the primary survival estimand in this study.

3.2
Challenges with Death Truncation

The central difficulty induced by truncation due to death is that the longitudinal potential outcome is no longer defined after death has occurred. As a consequence, two fundamental questions typically addressed in randomized controlled trials become difficult to answer: (i)
"Are treated patients in better health?"
and (ii)
"By how much are they better?"
.

In the absence of truncation by death, the first question can be addressed using the natural ordering of the longitudinal scale: for example, an ALSFRSr score of
47/48
is unambiguously better than
40/48
. However, when death occurs, one must compare outcomes for living and deceased patients, a situation for which no intrinsic ordering exists. This issue is particularly acute in chronic and fatal diseases, where quality of life is a key outcome and where, in some settings, end-of-life decisions such as euthanasia may be available. Thus, the first challenge posed by truncation by death is the lack of a natural ordering between survival and longitudinal health outcomes.

The second question,
“By how much are patients better?”
, relies on settings without truncation on the natural metric of the longitudinal outcome (e.g.,
47-40=7
points of ALSFRSr score). Under truncation by death, however, one must compare quantities measured on fundamentally different scales: score unit versus survival time. There is no intrinsic metric that allows these two quantities to be meaningfully combined, so truncation by death also removes the natural notion of distance of the longitudinal outcome.

In this article, we estimate and interpret the causal estimands proposed for handling truncation by death with respect to these two requirements: the existence of a meaningful ordering and of a meaningful distance.

3.3
Subpopulation estimands

One approach consists of identifying a subpopulation of patients for whom the natural metric of the longitudinal outcome remains well-defined.

3.3.1
Inappropriate naive effect

A naive approach to recovering the natural ordering and metric is to compute the average causal effect over the subset of individuals observed to survive up to time
t
:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{NAE}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\{\text{observed survivors at }t\}}\left\{Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)\right\}.

(5)

However, although the observed outcome is well defined for such individuals, the corresponding counterfactual outcome under the alternative treatment may not be, due to truncation by death, and hence the estimand itself is not well defined. Moreover, this approach conditions on a post-randomization variable—survival up to time
t
—thereby breaking randomization and inducing selection bias
[
18
]
.

3.3.2
Principal stratification

In the presence of death truncation, each patient can be classified into one of four principal strata
[
16
]
, denoted
G_{i,t}
:

•

Always-survivor (
\mathrm{LL}_{t}
): lives regardless of treatment assignment,

•

Protected (
\mathrm{DL}_{t}
): dies under
W_{i}=0
, but lives under
W_{i}=1
,

•

Harmed (
\mathrm{LD}_{t}
): lives under
W_{i}=0
, but dies under
W_{i}=1
,

•

Never-survivor (
\mathrm{DD}_{t}
): dies regardless of treatment assignment.

The principle of principal stratification
[
16
]
is to restrict the analysis to always-survivors, for whom the natural ordering and metric of the longitudinal outcome are preserved. The average causal effect within this stratum, known as the Stratified Average Causal Effect (SACE), is defined as:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{SACE}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\frac{1}{N_{LL}}\displaystyle\sum_{i,G_{i,t}\in\mathrm{LL}_{t}}\Big\{Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)\Big\}

(6)

SACE can be estimate at each time
t
, but its estimation requires identification of the always-survivor stratum.

3.4
Composite Causal Estimand

To define an estimand over the entire population, it is necessary to specify an ordering of outcomes (“better health”) across the four principal strata. This requires resolving the inherent trade-off between longitudinal outcomes and survival by aggregating them into a single composite outcome. In particular, an additional assumption is needed to order death relative to the longitudinal outcome. A common choice is to regard death as the worst possible outcome.
Under this assumption, a composite potential outcome can be defined as

\displaystyle Z_{i,t}(W_{i})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(Y_{i,t}(W_{i}),\,T_{i,t}(W_{i}),\,D_{i,t}\Bigr),

(7)

with ordering as described in Table
2
. A corresponding metric can then be constructed. For always-survivors, this metric coincides with that of the longitudinal outcome, i.e.
Z_{i,t}(1)-Z_{i,t}(0)=Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)
. For the remaining strata, to avoid combining quantities with incompatible units, the metric is defined as taking infinite values, with the sign chosen to reflect the ordering of outcomes.

More complex composite outcomes may be developed in collaboration with patients and clinicians, for example, following the approach of Van Eijk et al.
[
39
]
. The composite outcome must be specified prior to the definition of the causal estimand, and its construction should be made explicit, together with the assumptions required to justify the corresponding ordering and metric.

3.4.1
Pairwise Comparison

A natural approach, given that the metric is not finite over the entire population, is to base the estimand solely on the ordering of the composite outcome. The most direct choice is then a Pairwise Comparison (PC) estimand, defined as the probability that a randomly selected individual has a better composite outcome under treatment, namely:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big\{\mathbb{1}\left(Z_{i,t}(1)>Z_{i,t}(0)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\left(Z_{i,t}(1)=Z_{i,t}(0)\right)\Big\}

(8)

PC estimand is often associated, and estimated thanks to another causal estimand, the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney (WMW) estimand:

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{WMW}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\frac{1}{\text{Card}\left(\{i,W_{i}=1\}\right)}\frac{1}{\text{Card}\left(\{j,W_{j}=0\}\right)}\displaystyle\sum_{i,W_{i}=1}\displaystyle\sum_{j,W_{j}=0}\Big\{\mathbb{1}\left(Z_{i,t}(1)>Z_{j,t}(0)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\left(Z_{i,t}(1)=Z_{j,t}(0)\right)\Big\}

However, as noted by Fay et al.
[
13
]
, this estimand differs from the PC estimand, and its causal interpretation is more involved, which may lead to misleading conclusions
[
17
]
. Ensuring agreement between these estimands requires strong assumptions, such as a constant treatment effect
[
13
]
. Despite this limitation, it has been applied in ALS through the PROOF estimand
[
39
]
.
More generally, several estimands have been proposed to circumvent the need for a well-defined metric.
However, they rely on statistical quantities that are not fully well defined, including the win ratio
[
29
]
, the net benefit
[
11
]
, and the CAFS
[
7
]
.

Given its clear statistical definition and straightforward causal interpretation, we argue that the pairwise comparison estimand provides the most appropriate choice when constructing an estimand based solely on the ordering of a composite outcome.

3.4.2
Survival incorporated median

A second approach is to leverage the metric of the composite outcome to define an estimand. The average causal effect cannot be directly computed because the metric may take infinite values; however, certain percentiles of the distribution can be well-defined. Based on this idea, Xiang et al.
[
41
]
introduced the Survival-Incorporated Median (SIM):

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathrm{SIM}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &[Z_{i,t}(1)-Z_{i,t}(0)]_{\frac{N+1}{2}}&&\text{ for $N$ odd}\\
&\frac{1}{2}\left([Z_{i,t}(1)-Z_{i,t}(0)]_{\frac{N+1}{2}}+[Z_{i,t}(1)-Z_{i,t}(0)]_{\frac{N+1}{2}+1}\right)&&\text{ for $N$ even}\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

While this estimand provides an interpretable measure of the “magnitude of better health,” its use requires determining a priori whether the median—or any chosen percentile—will be well-defined (different from infinity) for the population during the trial.

3.5
Hypothetical estimands

A final approach that preserves the native ordering and metric of the longitudinal outcome consists in extending the outcome beyond death up to the end of follow-up, under a hypothetical intervention that prevents death without affecting the longitudinal trajectory
[
26
,
27
]
. Prominent examples of such hypothetical estimands include the controlled direct effect, originally introduced by Robins and Greenland
[
34
]
and further developed by Young et al.
[
42
]
, and are typically estimated using inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting.
Although such estimands may be meaningful in certain settings, in the context of chronic disease—where the same underlying biological process drives both functional decline and death—the assumption that death can be intervened upon without affecting the longitudinal outcome is highly implausible. Consequently, these estimands generally lack a coherent clinical interpretation and are not well-suited to the research questions considered in this paper.

4
Estimation

For estimation, we adopt the Bayesian model-based framework of Imbens and Rubin
[
21
]
, specifying separate models for each potential outcome. Using their formalism, the individual causal effect can be expressed as

Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)=(2W_{i}-1)*(Y^{obs}_{i,t}-Y^{mis}_{i,t})
.
At first sight, this approach may appear to introduce additional assumptions relative to alternative methods. However, it has been shown that it instead renders explicit assumptions that would otherwise remain implicit
[
21
]
. In this section, we present the modeling choices for the survival and longitudinal potential outcomes with the treatment effect estimators. We further examine how our assumptions relate to those commonly invoked for the identification and estimation of each causal estimand.
The resulting estimators are implemented using the
pymc
library
[
3
,
6
]
, with posterior inference performed via the No-U-Turn sampler
[
20
]
. The corresponding code is publicly available in the
Truncation By Death Estimates
repository at
https://github.com/JulietteOrtholand/TBDEstimates
,
together with a simple illustrative example.

4.1
Estimator for survival

For the time-to-event outcomes, we model the survival function

\displaystyle S_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(T_{i,t}(W)\geq t\right)

(10)

through the hazard function

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{\Pr\!\left(t\leq T_{i,t}(W)<t+dt\right)}{dt\,S_{i,t}(W)}.

(11)

We adopt a Bayesian piecewise-constant proportional hazards model
[
35
,
2
]
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0,t}(W)\exp(\alpha(W)^{T}X_{i})

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,t}(W)=\lambda^{j}(W)

for

\displaystyle t\in[\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}]

with the priors:
\alpha(W)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\alpha(W)},\sigma_{\alpha(W)}\right)
and
\lambda^{j}(W)\sim\Gamma\left(\mu_{\lambda^{j}(W)},\sigma_{\lambda^{j}(W)}\right)
.
This model is equivalent to a Poisson regression formulation applied over discrete time intervals, a representation commonly used for estimation
[
23
,
19
]
. Specifically, let
d_{i,j}
denote an indicator equal to 1 if individual
i
dies in the interval
(\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}]
and 0 otherwise:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{pmatrix}d_{i,j}(0)\\
d_{i,j}(1)\end{pmatrix}\,\right|\,\theta_{t}^{S}

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{j}(0)\,\exp\!\bigl(\alpha(0)^{\top}X_{i}\bigr)\,(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})\\
\lambda^{j}(1)\,\exp\!\bigl(\alpha(1)^{\top}X_{i}\bigr)\,(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

To identify the missing potential survival outcomes, we use the following assumptions:

•

Stable Unit Treatment Value (SUTVA):

D^{obs}_{i,t}=D_{i,t}(1)\times W_{i}+D_{i,t}(0)\times(1-W_{i})
, implying no interference between patients and a well-defined version of each treatment, which is a plausible assumption in an RCT.

•

No Unmeasured Confounders (NUC):

(D_{t}(1),D_{t}(0))\perp W
, implying that treatment allocation is independent of the survival potential outcomes. This condition is ensured by randomization in the RCT.

•

Positivity:

0<P(W=w|X=x)<1
for all
(w,x)
, so that each covariate stratum has a strictly positive probability of receiving either treatment, which is reasonable in a sufficiently large RCT.

Under these assumptions, the missing and observed events for
t\in[\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j}]
satisfy:

\displaystyle d^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,j}|d^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,j},W,\theta^{S}\sim\mathcal{P}\left(\left(W_{i}\lambda^{j}(1)\exp(\alpha(1)^{T}X_{i})+(1-W_{i})\lambda^{j}(0)\exp(\alpha(0)^{T}X_{i})\right)\times(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})\right)

\displaystyle d^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,j}|W,\theta^{S}\sim\mathcal{P}\left(\left(W_{i}\lambda^{j}(1)\exp(\alpha(1)^{T}X_{i})+(1-W_{i})\lambda^{j}(0)\exp(\alpha(0)^{T}X_{i})\right)\times(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})\right)

From the hazard, the survival probability can be computed with
S_{i,t}(W)=\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i,u}(W)\text{d}u\Big)
.

4.2
Estimator for longitudinal outcome

Longitudinal outcomes are only defined when the patient is alive, which in the case of observed potential outcomes, means that
D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}=0
so that
Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}
is defined. Yet, the definition of
Y^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
depends on
D^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
, which is unfortunately not observed. Principal ignorability, as clearly stated by Wang et al.
[
40
]
, assumes that all baseline covariates X are measured such that
D^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\perp Y^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}|X
, meaning that once we know
X
for a patient, knowing
D^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
would not provide further information on
Y^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
. This is a strong assumption, requiring both substantive knowledge of the disease and the availability of appropriate measured covariates.

We model longitudinal potential outcomes as linear functions of baseline covariates:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{pmatrix}Y_{i,t}(0)\\
Y_{i,t}(1)\end{pmatrix}\,\right|\theta_{t}^{Y}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{t}^{0}(0)+X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{t}^{1}(0)\\
\beta_{t}^{0}(1)+X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{t}^{1}(1)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&\sigma\end{pmatrix}\right),\qquad i\in\mathrm{LL}_{t}.

(13)

With priors

\displaystyle\beta_{t}^{k}(W)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{\beta_{t}^{k}(W)},\,\sigma_{\beta_{t}^{k}(W)}\right),\qquad k\in\{0,1\},

(14)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\sim\left|\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,\sigma_{\sigma}\right)\right|,

(15)

and

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{Y}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\beta_{t}^{0}(0),\,\beta_{t}^{0}(1),\,\beta_{t}^{1}(0),\,\beta_{t}^{1}(1)\Bigr).

(16)

In addition to Principal Ignorability, to identify the missing potential longitudinal outcomes, we use the following assumptions:

•

Stable Unit Treatment Value (SUTVA):

Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}|W_{i}=W_{i}\times Y_{i,t}(1)+(1-W_{i})\times Y_{i,t}(0)
, implying no interference between units and a well-defined version of each treatment, which is a plausible assumption in an RCT.

•

No Unmeasured Confounders (NUC):

Y_{t}(1),Y_{t}(0)\perp W
, implying that treatment allocation is independent of the survival potential outcomes. This condition is ensured by randomization in the RCT.

•

Positivity:

0<P(W=w|X=x)<1
for all
(w,x)
, so that each covariate stratum has a strictly positive probability of receiving either treatment, which is reasonable in a sufficiently large RCT.

Under these assumptions, the missing and observed potential longitudinal outcomes satisfy:

\displaystyle Y^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}|Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t},W,\theta^{Y}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(W_{i}(\beta^{0}_{t}(1)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(1))+(1-W_{i})(\beta^{0}_{t}(0)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(0)),\sigma_{t}\right)

\displaystyle Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}|W,\theta^{Y}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(W_{i}(\beta^{0}_{t}(1)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(1))+(1-W_{i})(\beta^{0}_{t}(0)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(0)),\sigma_{t}\right)

As the always-survivor stratum is not directly observable, we can only estimate the probability that a given individual belongs to this stratum at time
t
, defined as
p_{i,t}=\mathbb{1}\left(D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}=0\text{ \& }T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}>t\right)S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
. We incorporate this uncertainty by weighting the likelihood contribution of each individual by
p_{i,t}
. Specifically, the weighted log-likelihood for the longitudinal model at time
t
is given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i,t}\left(-\log\left(\sigma_{t}\sqrt{2\pi}\right)-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(W_{i}(\beta^{0}_{t}(1)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(1))+(1-W_{i})(\beta^{0}_{t}(0)+X_{i}^{T}\beta^{1}_{t}(0))-Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\right)^{2}\right)

Readers familiar with the joint modeling literature may recognize similarities with the likelihood used in joint models of longitudinal and survival data
[
33
]
. However, it is important to emphasize that our approach is fundamentally a two-step procedure: the survival model is first estimated, and the resulting survival probabilities are then used to weight the longitudinal likelihood. This ordering reflects the causal structure of the problem, as survival determines whether the longitudinal outcome is defined, but not vice versa. The approach could also be related to Inverse-Probability-of-Censoring Weighting (IPCW)
[
42
]
, except that the weights are not inverted and the target estimand corresponds to principal stratification rather than a hypothetical estimand.

4.3
Additional estimators

We have defined a composite outcome
Z_{i,t}
that lies in a three-dimensional space and whose ordering is specified in Table
2
. We now define an estimator of its associated metric,
Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
, that accounts for uncertainty regarding the principal stratum to which each patient belongs:

\displaystyle(2W_{i}-1)*(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{ if }T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}>t\left\{\begin{aligned} &(2W_{i}-1)*(Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})&&T^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}>t\text{ with probability }S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\\
&(2W_{i}-1)*(+\infty)&&T^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\leq t\text{ with probability }1-S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\end{aligned}\right.\\
&\text{ if }T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\leq t\left\{\begin{aligned} &(2W_{i}-1)*(-\infty)&&T^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}>T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\text{ with probability }S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}}\\
&(2W_{i}-1)*(+\infty)&&T^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\leq T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\text{ with probability }1-S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}}\end{aligned}\right.\end{aligned}\right.

4.4
Treatment effect estimators

4.4.1
Principal Stratification

We estimate the SACE (defined in equation
6
) using the following estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{SACE}_{fs}(t)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{i,D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}=0,T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\geq t}S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}}\displaystyle\sum_{i,D^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}=0,T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}\geq t}S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}\times(2W_{i}-1)\times(Y^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})

The main difficulty in estimating the SACE is the identification of the always-survivor stratum. In addition to principal ignorability, a commonly invoked simplifying assumption is monotonicity, which posits that treatment cannot reduce survival, i.e.,
S_{i,t}(0)<S_{i,t}(1)
. Under this assumption, only
S_{i,t}(0)
must be estimated to identify always survivors. This assumption is stronger than the assumptions required for estimating
S^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
in our framework.

4.4.2
Pairwise Comparison

We estimate the pairwise comparison estimand (defined in equation
8
) using the following estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{PC}_{fs}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(P((2W_{i}-1)(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})>0)+\frac{P((2W_{i}-1)(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})=0)}{2}\right)

To estimate the pairwise comparison estimand
\tau^{\mathrm{PC}}_{\mathrm{sp}}(t)
, a key difficulty arises from its nonlinear dependence on the potential outcomes. Although this estimand is often approximated using a Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney–type estimator, such procedures rely on strong assumptions—most notably constant treatment effects—to admit a valid causal interpretation, as discussed in Section
3.4.1
. More recently, flexible matching-based approaches have been proposed
[
12
]
; however, these methods implicitly assume that counterfactual outcomes are known or can be imputed without uncertainty, which is a stronger assumption than our model-based framework.

4.4.3
Survival Incorporated Median

We estimate the SIM (defined in equation
9
) using a weighted median estimator to integrate the uncertainty on the stratum as described in section
4.3
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{SIM}}_{\mathrm{fs}}(t)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &[(2W_{i}-1)\times(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})]_{\frac{N+1}{2}}&&\text{ for $N$ odd}\\
&\frac{1}{2}\left([(2W_{i}-1)\times(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})]_{\frac{N+1}{2}}+[(2W_{i}-1)\times(Z^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\widehat{Z}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t})]_{\frac{N+1}{2}+1}\right)&&\text{ for $N$ even}\end{aligned}\right.

To compute the SIM, the difficulty lies on the fact that the median is not linear. Xiang et al.
[
41
]
approach requires an assumption to force median linearity, which only occurs if all the elements of both sum elements are in ascending (or descending) order, meaning: (i) the order defined between the strata and then (ii) the order inside each stratum. For strata order, principal ignorability and monotonicity are used, yet the assumption to guarantee order inside each stratum is not discussed. By using the model-based approach
[
21
]
, we avoid such assumptions.

4.4.4
Restricted Mean Survival Time

We estimate the RMST (defined in equation
4
) using the following estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{RMST}_{fs}(t)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{(2W_{i}-1)\times\left(T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{i,t}-\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,u}\right)du\right\}

Here we estimate the RMST by modeling the survival, but another approach, harder to interpret, has been developed using the pseudo-values modeling
[
28
]
.

5
Simulation study

The simulation study was designed to assess the validity of the proposed estimators and to examine their behavior across a range of scenarios. Its design followed the ADEMP framework
[
25
]
. The experiments can be reproduced with the publicly available code:
https://github.com/JulietteOrtholand/truncation_by_death/tree/main
.

5.1
Method

We consider a single baseline covariate
X_{i}
observed for each individual. The longitudinal outcome is generated under a linear progression model, and survival times follow an accelerated failure time structure:

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}(W)

\displaystyle=((a^{0}_{0}+a^{0}_{X}X_{i})+W\times(a^{1}_{0}+a^{1}_{X}X_{i}))*t+\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\text{ with }\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma\right)

(17)

\displaystyle-\log(T_{i,\infty}(W))

\displaystyle=-\log\left((\theta^{0}_{0}+\theta^{0}_{X}X_{i})+W\times(\theta^{1}_{0}+\theta^{1}_{X}X_{i})\right)+\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle\text{ with }\gamma_{i}\sim\mathcal{G}\left(0,\frac{1}{\rho}\right)

(18)

So that
T_{i,\infty}(W)\sim\mathcal{W}\left((\theta^{0}_{0}+\theta^{0}_{X}X_{i})+W\times(\theta^{1}_{0}+\theta^{1}_{X}X_{i}),\rho\right)
. We simulated an RCT with a follow-up of 15 months and measurement times
t=3,6,9,12,15
.

Four scenarios were considered: (i) no treatment effect and no censoring; (ii) no treatment effect with censoring; (iii) a beneficial treatment effect on both survival and the longitudinal outcome; and (iv) opposing treatment effects on survival and the longitudinal outcome. The corresponding parameter values were summarized in Table
4
. For each scenario, 100 datasets were generated.

For each scenario and each individual, we estimate
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
and
\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{mis}}_{i,t}
at
t\in\{3,6,9,12,15\}
. Model estimation is performed using identical prior distributions under treatment and control, thereby encoding the absence of prior information on treatment effects. Weakly informative priors are specified for covariate effects in the survival model,
\alpha(W)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and in the longitudinal model,
\beta^{1}_{t}(W)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,100)
, as well as for the residual variability,
\sigma\sim|\mathcal{N}(0,100)|
. The prior
\beta^{0}_{t}(W)\sim\mathcal{N}(-2,3)
reflects the simulated longitudinal slope.
The simulated median survival lies between 18 and 22 months under a Weibull distribution (Table
4
). Assuming a constant hazard over time and writing
S_{i,t}(W)=\exp\bigl(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i,u}(W)\,\mathrm{d}u\bigr)
, this corresponds to
\lambda^{j}(W)\in[0.032,0.038]
. Accordingly, we specify
\lambda^{j}(W)\sim\Gamma(0.035,0.1)
. Additional experiments with less well-calibrated priors are reported in Appendix
B
.
We then compute the finite-sample estimators defined in section
4.4
: principal stratification (
\widehat{\tau}^{PS}_{fs}(t)
), pairwise comparison (
\widehat{\tau}^{PC}_{fs}(t)
), survival-incorporated median (
\widehat{\tau}^{SIM}_{fs}(t)
), and restricted mean potential survival times (
\widehat{\tau}^{RMST}_{fs}(t)
).
Performance is assessed in terms of bias and coverage.

5.2
Results

Figure
1
displays the posterior medians and associated credibility intervals of the estimators, together with the true medians under each of the four scenarios.

The RMST estimator performs well across all scenarios, yielding accurate estimates and coverage that improves as the number of observed deaths increases (Table
5
).

The SACE estimator exhibits increasing uncertainty as the number of deaths rises, reflected in wider credibility intervals (Figure
1
). When considered jointly with the RMST, it provides a complete picture in the mixed scenario, where the treatment has a beneficial effect on the longitudinal outcome but an adverse effect on survival.

The SIM estimate is not defined over the entire follow-up in the beneficial and mixed scenarios, owing to a high number of deaths in the protected stratum in the former and in the harmed stratum in the latter. Due to uncertainty in the estimation of the always-survivor stratum, treatment effects are estimated at certain time points (e.g. 9 months), even when they are not defined in the simulated data. As with the SACE, the associated credibility intervals widen as the number of deaths increases.

The PC estimator is sensitive to the prior specification of no treatment effect. In the beneficial scenario, however, it still indicates a positive treatment effect. In the mixed scenario, it reflects the evolving balance between longitudinal and survival effects: at early time points, when few deaths have occurred, the estimator exceeds 0.5, indicating a beneficial effect on the longitudinal outcome; as mortality increases under treatment, the estimator decreases below 0.5, indicating a detrimental effect.

are reported in Appendix, see Tables

and

6
Application

6.1
Method

We analyze data from a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in patients with ALS, estimating the efficacy of olesoxime in 512 individuals
[
24
]
. The trial showed no effect on survival based on the log-rank test, and only a modest improvement in ALSFRSr score at 9 months in favor of olesoxime, which was not sustained at 18 months, assessed using an inappropriate naive effect (see Section
3.3.1
).

We compute the estimators defined in Section
4
at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 18 months. Identification relies on the assumptions of SUTVA, no unmeasured confounding, positivity, and principal ignorability. To assess the plausibility of these assumptions, we report measures of reconstruction performance.
For the longitudinal outcome, reconstruction accuracy for missing potential outcomes is estimated using the mean absolute error (MAE). For survival, reconstruction performance is assessed using the cumulative dynamic area under the ROC curve (cdAUC), integrated from 2 to 17 months at monthly intervals as a measure of discrimination, and the integrated Brier score (IBS), which captures calibration of reconstructed survival probabilities. Survival metrics are computed using the
sksurv
Python package
[
30
]
.

As in the simulation study, we specify identical priors under treatment and control, reflecting the absence of prior information on treatment effects, together with weakly informative priors for covariate effects and for the residual variability of the longitudinal model (see Section
5.1
). For the longitudinal slope, we use
\beta^{0}_{t}(W)\sim\mathcal{N}(-1,3)
, consistent with the disease progression rate of approximately 0.92 units per month reported in randomized trials
[
9
]
.
Patients typically die 3–4 years after symptom onset
[
37
]
, and were enrolled on average 1.5 years after onset (Table
7
), corresponding to an expected survival of 18 to 30 months from trial baseline. Following the approach used in the simulation study (see Section
5.1
), we specify
\lambda^{j}(W)\sim\Gamma(0.035,0.1)
.

6.2
Results

Baseline characteristics were similar between treatment arms with respect to site of onset, sex, baseline ALSFRSr score, age at symptom onset, and time from symptom onset to baseline (Table
7
). Overall, 23.97% of patients had died by the end of follow-up (Figure
4
; Table
8
).

The integrated Brier score (IBS) had a centered 95% credibility interval of
[0.087,0.092]
, while the cumulative dynamic area under the ROC curve (cdAUC) had a 95% credibility interval of
[0.594,0.671]
, indicating adequate calibration but only moderate discrimination. Bias and absolute bias in the reconstruction increase over time, in line with the increasing number of deaths (Table
8
).

Figure
2
summarizes the estimated treatment effects. For both the RMST and SACE estimands, the posterior medians suggest a beneficial treatment effect, although the corresponding 95% credibility intervals include the null. Consistent with this pattern, the SIM and PC estimands yield more pronounced effects, particularly at 12 months, with 95% credibility intervals of
[0.53,0.60]
for the PC and
[0.78,2.54]
for the SIM (Table
8
).

7
Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a unified framework to define, interpret, and compare causal estimands for longitudinal outcomes truncated by death, together with Bayesian estimators for their estimation.

We showed that the main difficulty arises from the absence of a natural notion of ordering and distance for outcomes truncated by death. This setting is inherently multifactorial, as estimands either provide disaggregated information on survival and the longitudinal outcome or aggregate these dimensions into a single measure. Stratified Average Causal Esimand, combined with Restricted Mean Survival Time, enables a decomposition of these objectives at the cost of yielding multiple estimands. By contrast, scalar estimands require additional assumptions to define an ordering between survival and the longitudinal outcome. Among these, the Pairwise Comparison estimand is particularly appealing, as it corresponds to a probability of a better outcome, is well-defined mathematically, and remains identifiable across scenarios, in contrast to the Survival-Incorporated Median.

Identification of the proposed estimands relies, in addition to standard causal assumptions (SUTVA, no unmeasured confounding, and positivity), on the principal ignorability assumption. This is a strong assumption, requiring both substantive knowledge of the disease and the availability of appropriate measured covariates. We assessed its plausibility in the ALS setting with discrimination and calibration metrics of survival reconstruction.

We developed Bayesian estimators that robustly estimate uncertainty, and compare their performance in a simulation study. The results confirm that the pairwise comparison estimand captures the trade-off between survival and longitudinal outcomes in settings with opposing treatment effects, whereas principal stratification and restricted mean survival time provide a more complete but inherently multidimensional characterization. We have pointed to the long-lasting role of prior as posterior uncertainty does not vanish asymptotically in this setting, highlighting the persistent influence of prior specifications, as discussed for principal stratification by Richardson et al.
[
32
]
. Our implementation relies on relatively simple parametric models, which could be extended in future work.
We illustrated the proposed methods using data from a randomized controlled trial in ALS, where longitudinal outcomes are truncated by death, thereby providing a concrete example of their practical application.
Code for the proposed estimators is available at
https://github.com/JulietteOrtholand/TBDEstimates
, together with a simple example to facilitate their use.

Although our focus is on truncation by death as a specific intercurrent event, the underlying issues—particularly the need to define ordering and distance—also arise in other settings, such as treatment non-adherence, where the proposed framework may provide useful insights.

In summary, we provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing longitudinal outcomes truncated by death. We recommend the joint use of the Stratified Average Causal Effect (SACE) and Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) to disentangle survival and longitudinal components and to reflect the inherently multifactorial nature of the problem. More broadly, this work aims to support more coherent and interpretable analyses of randomized controlled trials in ALS and other life-threatening diseases.
