A sharp bound on the integrality gap in the 3 3 -set cover problem
Eli Berger, Ron Holzman

Abstract
Given a hypergraph with edges of size at most 3 3 , the 3 3 -set cover problem asks to determine the minimum size of a family of edges which covers the vertex set. As the problem is NP-hard, it is natural to consider its fractional (linear programming) relaxation, which provides a lower bound on the value of the optimal solution. The ratio between the actual value and that of the fractional relaxation is called the integrality gap. A classic bound of Lovász implies that the integrality gap in this problem is at most 11 / 6 11/6 . This has been improved to 5 / 3 5/3 by Fujito and Okumura. Here we prove that the integrality gap is at most 3 / 2 3/2 , which is best possible. A corollary of this result is that the vertex set of any 3 3 -uniform, regular hypergraph on n n vertices can be covered by n / 2 n/2 (or fewer) edges. This solves the k = 3 k=3 case of a problem of de A. Moreira and Kohayakawa. As another application, we derive a certain variant of the Gale-Shapley stable marriage theorem for triples.

Introduction

A
hypergraph

H
is a pair
(V,E)
, where
V=V(H)
is a non-empty finite set of
vertices
and
E=E(H)
is a family of subsets of
V
called
edges
. We always assume that the edges are non-empty and every vertex belongs to at least one edge, so that
V=\bigcup E
. We often write edges without braces and commas, e.g.,
123
instead of
\{1,2,3\}
.

The
rank
of
H
is
\max_{e\in E(H)}|e|
. If
|e|=k
for every
e\in E(H)
, we say that
H
is
k
-
uniform
. The
degree
of a vertex
v
is
|\{e\in E(H):v\in e\}|
. If every
v\in V(H)
has the same degree
d
, we say that
H
is
d
-
regular
, or just
regular
without specifying the degree.

An
edge cover
in
H
is a subfamily
E^{\prime}\subseteq E(H)
that covers the entire vertex set, i.e.,
V=\bigcup E^{\prime}
. We will be interested in edge covers that are as small as possible. The
edge covering number
of
H
is

\rho(H)=\min\{|E^{\prime}|:E^{\prime}\textrm{ is an edge cover in }H\}.

The combinatorial optimization problem of determining
\rho(H)
is usually called the (unweighted) set cover (or covering) problem. When restricted to hypergraphs of rank
k
, it is called the
k
-set cover problem. For
k=2
, i.e., in the case of graphs, computing the edge covering number is easy: it is equivalent to finding the maximum size of a matching. But already for
k=3
, the
3
-set cover problem is known to be NP-hard (e.g., by a reduction from the
3
-dimensional matching problem on Karp’s list in
[
10
]
). There is a large body of combinatorial opimization literature devoted to approximating the edge covering number; see
[
3
]
for a survey.

An efficient lower bound on the edge covering number is obtained by considering its linear programming relaxation. A
fractional edge cover
in
H
is a function
f:E(H)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that covers
V(H)
in the sense that
\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)\geq 1
for every vertex
v\in V(H)
. Its size
|f|
is
\sum_{e\in E(H)}f(e)
. The
fractional edge covering number
of
H
is

\rho^{*}(H)=\min\{|f|:f\textrm{ is a fractional edge cover in }H\}.

An edge cover is a
\{0,1\}
-valued fractional edge cover, hence
\rho(H)\geq\rho^{*}(H)
. The ratio
\rho(H)/\rho^{*}(H)
, measuring the relative distance between the integral solution and its fractional counterpart, is called the edge-cover integrality gap of
H
. An upper bound on the integrality gap over a class of hypergraphs allows to control the quality of the
\rho^{*}(H)
approximation to
\rho(H)
for
H
in that class. The following classic upper bound due to Lovász
[
12
]
has had many applications in graph and hypergraph theory, combinatorial optimization, additive number theory, and more. (The original formulation of Lovász’s bound was in terms of the vertex-cover integrality gap for hypergraphs of a given maximum degree; the form we give here is equivalent by interchanging the roles of vertices and edges while keeping the incidence relation.)

Theorem 1.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
H
be a hypergraph of rank
k
. Then

\frac{\rho(H)}{\rho^{*}(H)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{i}\leq 1+\ln k.

Examples for large
k
are known that show that Lovász’s bound cannot be improved by a multiplicative constant (we cite below, in more precise form, such a result). However, Fujito and Okumura
[
6
]
have improved the bound by an additive constant:

\frac{\rho(H)}{\rho^{*}(H)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{i}-\frac{1}{6}\textrm{ \,\, for hypergraphs }H\textrm{ of rank }k\geq 2.

This improvement is significant for small values of
k
. For
k=2
it improves the bound from
\frac{3}{2}
to
\frac{4}{3}
, which is best possible. Indeed, the triangle graph
K_{3}
has
\rho(K_{3})=2
and
\rho^{*}(K_{3})=\frac{3}{2}
. For
k=3
it improves the bound from
\frac{11}{6}
to
\frac{5}{3}
. As far as we know, the
\frac{5}{3}
bound is the best one known prior to this work. Here we focus on the
k=3
case, and establish the following best possible improvement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H
be a hypergraph of rank
3
. Then

\frac{\rho(H)}{\rho^{*}(H)}\leq\frac{3}{2}.

Before showing the sharpness of this bound, we state two increasingly special cases of Theorem
1.2
. A fractional edge cover
f:E(H)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
exact
if it satisfies the covering constraints as equalities:
\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)=1
for all
v\in V(H)
. Suppose that
H
is a
3
-uniform hypergraph with
|V|=n
, and
f
is a fractional edge cover in
H
. Then

|f|=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{v\in V(H)}\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)\geq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{v\in V(H)}1=\frac{n}{3},

and equality holds if and only if
f
is exact. Thus, if
H
has an exact fractional edge cover, then
\rho^{*}(H)=\frac{n}{3}
. Therefore, Theorem
1.2
immediately gives the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
H
be a
3
-uniform hypergraph with
|V(H)|=n
that has an exact fractional edge cover. Then
\rho(H)\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

Next, suppose that
H
is a
3
-uniform,
d
-regular hypergraph. Then
f:E(H)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by
f(e)=\frac{1}{d}
for all
e\in E(H)
is an exact fractional edge cover in
H
. We get the following special case of Corollary
1.3
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
H
be a
3
-uniform, regular hypergraph with
|V(H)|=n
. Then
\rho(H)\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

We proceed to give several examples of
3
-uniform, regular hypergraphs that attain equality in Corollary
1.4
. This will show that Theorem
1.2
and its two corollaries are sharp.

Example 1.5

(
K^{(3)}_{4}
)
.

The complete
3
-uniform hypergraph on
4
vertices is also
3
-regular. Two edges are needed to cover all vertices. Thus
\rho(K^{(3)}_{4})=2=\frac{4}{2}
.

Example 1.6

(
TPP(2)
)
.

The truncated projective plane of order
2
has
6
vertices and
4
edges:
135
,
146
,
236
,
245
. It is
2
-regular, and three edges are needed to cover all vertices. Thus
\rho(TPP(2))=3=\frac{6}{2}
. Note that this hypergraph is
3
-partite (with parts
\{1,2\}
,
\{3,4\}
,
\{5,6\}
), showing that the upper bound is sharp even in the
3
-partite case.

Examples satisfying
\rho(H)=\frac{n}{2}
for arbitrarily large
n
can be built from any of the above small examples by taking disjoint copies. The following construction gives arbitrarily large connected examples of equality.

Example 1.7
.

Given an even
n\geq 4
, we take a set
V
of
n
vertices and partition it into pairs
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots P_{k}
, where
k=\frac{n}{2}
and the pairs are arranged cyclically (so
P_{k+1}=P_{1}
). We take as edges in
E
all triples of the form
P_{i}\cup\{x\}
where
x\in P_{i+1}
. Then
H=(V,E)
is
3
-uniform and
3
-regular (in the case
n=4
we get back Example
1.5
). An edge cover of size
k
can be formed, for example, by taking one triple containing
P_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
. We claim that
H
has no smaller edge cover. Indeed, let
E^{\prime}
be an edge cover. We associate with
E^{\prime}
a cyclic string of length
k
, where the
i
-th entry is
0
,
1
, or
2
indicating the number of triples in
E^{\prime}
that contain
P_{i}
. The predecessor (in the cyclic sense) of any
0
entry must be a
2
in order to satisfy the covering constraints. Hence there must be at least as many
2
entries as there are
0
entries, implying that the sum of entries is at least
k
. Thus
|E^{\prime}|\geq k
and so
\rho(H)=k=\frac{n}{2}
.

In a related paper, de A. Moreira and Kohayakawa
[
4
]
studied the ratio
\rho(H)/\rho^{*}(H)
for hypergraphs that are uniform and regular. They defined the function

f(k)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{H}\frac{\rho(H)}{\rho^{*}(H)},

where the maximum is taken over all
k
-uniform, regular hypergraphs on
n
vertices. Based on a probabilistic construction for the lower bound and on Lovász’s upper bound, they proved that for any large enough fixed
k

\ln k-12\ln\ln k\leq f(k)\leq 1+\ln k.

They showed that
f(2)=\frac{4}{3}
, and posed the open problem of determining
f(k)
for all
k\geq 3
. Our Corollary
1.4
together with the above examples of equality solve the case
k=3
of their problem:
f(3)=\frac{3}{2}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is quite intricate, and relies on the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition of graphs and some structural properties of factor-critical graphs. Those are recalled or developed in Section 2, and the proof itself appears in Section 3. It is worth mentioning that the proof of Fujito and Okumura’s
[
6
]
previous improvement to Lovász’s bound, establishing the
\frac{5}{3}
upper bound for
k=3
, also used the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition. However, we apply it to a different graph associated with the hypergraph of rank
3
, and use the decomposition in other ways than they did.

In Section 4 we present an application of Theorem
1.2
to a Gale-Shapley type stable marriage problem, where we consider triples instead of couples.

2
Preliminaries

Although Theorem
1.2
is about hypergraphs of rank
3
, our proof will mostly be in terms of graphs that are associated with such hypergraphs. Here we introduce some terminology and notation for graphs, and recall or develop the main tools that we will use in the proof.

A
graph

G=(V,E)
is a
2
-uniform hypergraph. A
matching
in
G
is a set
M\subseteq E
of pairwise disjoint edges. The set of vertices covered by the matching
M
is
\bigcup M\subseteq V
. A
maximum
matching
M
is one that maximizes
|M|
among all matchings in
G
. When
|V|
is even, a
perfect
matching
M
is one with
\bigcup M=V
. When
|V|
is odd, a
near-perfect
matching
M
is one with
|\bigcup M|=|V|-1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, and let
U\subseteq V
. The
induced subgraph
of
G
on
U
is the graph
G[U]=(U,E[U])
where
E[U]
consists of all edges in
E
having both endpoints in
U
. When
U=V\setminus X
, we also write
G[U]
as
G-X
. The
contraction
of
X
in
G
is the graph
G/X
obtained from
G-X
by adding a new vertex
v_{X}
and an edge
uv_{X}
for every
u\in U
such that
uv\in E
for at least one
v\in X
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
O
be an odd cycle in
G
. We say that
O
is
induced
if it has no chords. If
O
is not induced, an
odd shortening
of
O
is a cycle
O^{\prime}
obtained from
O
by partitioning its edge set into odd paths
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{k}
, where
k\geq 3
and each
Q_{i}
is either an edge of
O
or its endpoints form a chord, and replacing each of the latter
Q_{i}
by the corresponding chord. Thus, the length of
O^{\prime}
is
k
, which is necessarily odd. We will need the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
O
be an odd cycle in
G
. If
O
is not induced, then it has an odd shortening
O^{\prime}
which is induced.

Proof.

Clearly,
O
has an odd shortening: if
uv
is a chord, we can use a partition
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{k}
consisting of the
u-v
path on
O
that is odd and single edges. Let
O^{\prime}
be an odd shortening with
k
as small as possible. Then
O^{\prime}
is induced. Indeed, if
xy
is a chord of
O^{\prime}
, then replacing the
x-y
path on
O^{\prime}
that is odd by the chord
xy
yields a shorter odd shortening of
O
.
∎

2.1
Factor-critical graphs

A graph
G
with an odd number of vertices is
factor-critical
if for every
v\in V(G)
there exists a near-perfect matching
M
in
G
such that
\bigcup M=V(G)\setminus\{v\}
. Factor-critical graphs will play a central role in our proof of Theorem
1.2
. We will need the following lemma, showing that this property can be preserved under contraction.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a factor-critical graph with
|V(G)|>1
. Then
G
has an induced odd cycle
O
such that
G/V(O)
is factor-critical.

Proof.

We recall that an odd ear decomposition of a graph
G
is its presentation as
P_{0}+P_{1}+\cdots+P_{r}
, where
P_{0}
is a single vertex, and each
P_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
, is either an odd path that has only its two endpoints in common with
P_{0}+\cdots+P_{i-1}
or an odd cycle that has exactly one vertex in common with
P_{0}+\cdots+P_{i-1}
. Lovász
[
11
]
proved that a graph is factor-critical if and only if it has an odd ear decomposition.

Now, let
G
be factor-critical with
|V(G)|>1
, and let
P_{0}+P_{1}+\cdots+P_{r}
be an odd ear decomposition of
G
. Since
P_{0}
is a single vertex,
P_{1}
must be an odd cycle through that vertex. We may assume that
P_{1}
is induced. Indeed, if it is not then by Lemma
2.1
it has an odd shortening
P^{\prime}_{1}
which is induced. Say that
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{\ell}
are the corresponding odd paths on
P_{1}
of length at least
3
in the formation of
P^{\prime}_{1}
. Then we can get another odd ear decomposition of
G
as follows. We start with an arbitrary vertex on
P^{\prime}_{1}
, then add
P^{\prime}_{1}
, followed by the odd paths
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{\ell}
. At this point, we have the entire cycle
P_{1}
and its chords that appear in
P^{\prime}_{1}
. From here on, we add
P_{2},P_{3},\ldots,P_{r}
while skipping those chords of
P_{1}
that already appeared in
P^{\prime}_{1}
.

So, assume that
P_{1}
is induced. Upon contracting
V(P_{1})
into a single vertex
v_{P_{1}}
, the graph becomes
v_{P_{1}}+P_{2}+\cdots+P_{r}
, and each
P_{i}
,
i=2,\ldots,r
, is still of one of the two allowed forms (though odd paths may become odd cycles if the contraction collapses their endpoints into a single vertex). Thus
G/V(P_{1})
has an odd ear decomposition as well, and is therefore factor-critical as required.
∎

The next lemma allows us to decompose a factor-critical graph into an odd cycle of smaller factor-critical graphs.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a graph with
|V(G)|>1
. Then
G
is factor-critical if and only if for some positive integer
k
, there exists a partition
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{2k+1}
of
V(G)
so that:

(a)

G[U_{i}]
is factor-critical for all
i=1,2,\ldots,2k+1
.

(b)

For
1\leq i\neq j\leq 2k+1
,
G
has at least one edge between
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
if and only if
i,j
are cyclically adjacent (i.e.,
|i-j|=1
or
\{i,j\}=\{1,2k+1\}
).

Proof.

Assume first that a partition
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{2k+1}
of
V(G)
satisfying (a) and (b) is given. We have to show that for every
v\in V(G)
there exists a perfect matching in
G-\{v\}
. Assume w.l.o.g. that
v=v_{1}\in U_{1}
. For
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, let
v_{2i}v_{2i+1}
be an edge of
G
with endpoints
v_{2i}\in U_{2i}
,
v_{2i+1}\in U_{2i+1}
(which exists by (b)). For
j=1,2,\ldots,2k+1
, let
M_{j}
be a perfect matching in
G[U_{j}]-\{v_{j}\}
(which exists by (a)). These matchings, together with the edges
v_{2i}v_{2i+1}
, cover all the vertices except
v_{1}
.

To prove the opposite direction, we use induction on
|V(G)|
. By Lemma
2.2
, we can find in
G
an induced odd cycle
O
such that
G/V(O)
is factor-critical. If
G/V(O)
is a single vertex, this means that
G=O
. In this case we can partition
V(G)=V(O)
into singletons to satisfy the requirements in the lemma.

Thus, we may assume that
G/V(O)
has more than one vertex. By the induction hypothesis,
G/V(O)
admits a partition
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{2k+1}
that satisfies the requirements. Let
v_{O}
be the new vertex created by the contraction of
V(O)
, and assume w.l.o.g. that
v_{O}\in U_{1}
. Let
U^{*}_{1}=\bigl(U_{1}\setminus\{v_{O}\}\bigr)\cup V(O)
. We claim that
U^{*}_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{2k+1}
is a partition of
V(G)
that satisfies (a) and (b) with respect to
G
. The only part that requires an argument is the fact that
G[U^{*}_{1}]
is factor-critical.

We need to show that for every
v\in U^{*}_{1}
, there is a matching
M
in
G
such that
\bigcup M=U^{*}_{1}\setminus\{v\}
. There are two cases to check. If
v\in V(O)
then we take a perfect matching in
O-\{v\}
and a perfect matching in
G[U_{1}]-\{v_{O}\}
(which exists because
\bigl(G/V(O)\bigr)[U_{1}]
is factor-critical). Together they give the required matching
M
. Now suppose that
v\in U_{1}\setminus\{v_{O}\}
. Then there is a perfect matching
M_{1}
in
\bigl(G/V(O)\bigr)[U_{1}]-\{v\}
, and one of its edges is of the form
uv_{O}
where
u\in U_{1}\setminus\{v,v_{O}\}
. By the definition of contraction,
G
has an edge
uw
for some
w\in V(O)
. Replacing
uv_{O}
in
M_{1}
by
uw
, and adding a perfect matching in
O-\{w\}
, we get the required matching
M
.
∎

2.2
The Gallai-Edmonds decomposition

Gallai
[
8
,
9
]
and Edmonds
[
5
]
introduced a canonical decomposition that applies to any graph and can be used to describe the family of maximum matchings in it. We recall here the definition of this decomposition and some of its properties (see
[
13
, Theorem 3.2.1]
).

Let
G
be a graph. We call a vertex
v\in V(G)

dispensable
in
G
if there is a maximum matching
M
in
G
with
v\notin\bigcup M
. We decompose
V(G)
into three (possibly empty) parts as follows:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{v\in V(G):v\textrm{ is dispensable in }G\}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in V(G)\setminus D:u\textrm{ is adjacent to at least one vertex in }D\}

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(G)\setminus(D\cup A)

If
D\neq\emptyset
, we further decompose
D
into the vertex sets
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{r}
of the connected components of
G[D]
. If
D=\emptyset
set
r=0
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
8
,
9
,
5
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{r}
,
A
,
C
be the vertex sets defined above. Then:

(a)

G[D_{i}]
is factor-critical for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
.

(b)

There exists a matching in
G
of size
|A|
, that matches the vertices in
A
to vertices in pairwise distinct
D_{j}
’s. Moreover, given any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
, such a matching exists that avoids
D_{i}
.

(c)

G[C]
has a perfect matching.

(d)

The family of all maximum matchings
M
in
G
can be constructed as follows. Choose any matching
M_{A}
as described in part (b). For every
D_{j}
that has a vertex covered by
M_{A}
, choose a near-perfect matching in
G[D_{j}]
that avoids that vertex. For every other
D_{i}
choose an arbitrary near-perfect matching in
G[D_{i}]
. Add to the above any perfect matching in
G[C]
. This gives a maximum matching
M
in
G
that covers all but
r-|A|
vertices, one in each
D_{i}
that is not touched by
M_{A}
.

We now list, for later use, a few immediate consequences of Theorem
2.4
. In each of them, we refer to the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition of
G
as defined above.

Corollary 2.5
.

If
v\in D_{i}
then any neighbor of
v
in
G
is either in
D_{i}
or in
A
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose
D\neq\emptyset
. Consider the bipartite graph
\Gamma_{G}
with
r
vertices on one side (corresponding to
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{r}
) and the vertices of
A
on the other side, where the
D_{j}
-vertex is adjacent to
u\in A
if and only if at least one vertex in
D_{j}
is adjacent to
u
in
G
. Then
\Gamma_{G}
has a matching that covers the
A
side, and this matching can be chosen so that it avoids any given
D_{i}
-vertex. In particular,
|A|<r
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Suppose
v\in D_{i}
and
M
is a maximum matching in
G
with
v\notin\bigcup M
. Then
M
contains a perfect matching in
G[D_{i}]-\{v\}
.

In addition to what we proved in the previous subsection, we will need another fact about the behavior of factor-critical graphs under contraction. Its proof uses the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
G
be a factor-critical graph, and let
X\subseteq V(G)
be such that
G-X
has a perfect matching. Then
G/X
is factor-critical.

Proof.

Let
D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{r}
,
A
,
C
be the Gallai-Edmonds decomposition of
G/X
. Because
G-X
has a perfect matching, so does
(G/X)-\{v_{X}\}
. It follows that
v_{X}
is dispensable in
G/X
, and therefore
v_{X}\in D
. Assume w.l.o.g. that
v_{X}\in D_{1}
. Now let
D^{*}_{1}=\bigl(D_{1}\setminus\{v_{X}\}\bigr)\cup X
, and consider the partition
D^{*}_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{r},A,C
of
V(G)
. By Corollary
2.5
, vertices in

2.3
Matchings in bipartite graphs

We recall here some facts about matchings in bipartite graphs. Let
G
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
(X,Y)
. For
S\subseteq X
we denote by
N(S)\subseteq Y
the set of vertices having at least one neighbor in
S
. We say that a set
U\subseteq V(G)
is
matchable
if there exists a matching
M
in
G
with
U\subseteq\bigcup M
. Hall’s theorem says that
X
is matchable if and only if
|N(S)|\geq|S|
for every
S\subseteq X
. We will need the following well-known generalization of this theorem, attributed to Ore
[
15
]
. Define the
deficiency
of
G
with respect to
X
to be

\mathrm{def}_{X}(G)=\max\{|S|-|N(S)|:S\subseteq X\}.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
(X,Y)
. Then
X
has a matchable subset
X^{\prime}
of size
|X^{\prime}|=|X|-\mathrm{def}_{X}(G)
.

We will also use a result of Mendelsohn and Dulmage
[
14
]
allowing to combine matchability of subsets of different sides.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
(X,Y)
. If each of the sets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
is matchable, then
X^{\prime}\cup Y^{\prime}
is matchable.

4
A Gale-Shapley type theorem for triples

The well-known stable matching theorem of Gale and Shapley
[
7
]
can be formulated in terms of bipartite graphs as follows. Consider a bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
, where every vertex
v
has a linear order
\succeq_{v}
on the set
E_{v}
of edges incident to
v
. Here
vx\succeq_{v}vy
means that
v
prefers to be matched to
x
than to
y
(or
x=y
). A matching
M
in
G
dominates an edge
e=uv\in E
if either
u
or
v
has an edge incident to it in
M
that it weakly prefers to
e
. A matching is stable if it dominates every edge in
E
. The stable matching theorem says that every bipartite graph, with any system of preferences as above, has a stable matching. This theorem and related ones have been much studied and widely applied in practice, e.g., to job markets, school assignments, and matching of organ donors to patients.

One direction of study is to allow the sets that can be formed (the edges) to be larger, not just couples. This is modeled by a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and a
preference system

\bigl(\succeq_{v}\bigr)_{v\in V}
, where each
\succeq_{v}
is a linear order on
E_{v}
, the set of edges in
E
that contain
v
. A set of edges
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}\subseteq E

dominates
an edge
e\in E
at a vertex
v\in e
if for some
1\leq i\leq m
we have
v\in e_{i}
and
e_{i}\succeq_{v}e
. We say that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
is
stable
if it dominates every edge
e\in E
at some
v\in e
.

When
H
is a bipartite graph, this coincides with the standard model described above, and a stable matching is guaranteed to exist. Beyond bipartite graphs, however, this is no longer true. Simple examples without a stable matching are known already for non-bipartite graphs (“the roommates problem”
[
7
]
) and for
3
-partite hypergraphs (“threesome matchings”
[
2
]
).

One way to regain existence is to relax the notion of matching to its fractional version. A
fractional matching
in
H=(V,E)
is a function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that satisfies
\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)\leq 1
for every vertex
v\in V
. Note that these inequalities are opposite to those required for a fractional edge cover. We say that a vertex
v
is
saturated
by
f
if
\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)=1
. Given a preference system for
H
, a fractional matching
f
in
H

dominates
an edge
e\in E
at a vertex
v\in e
if

\sum\{f(e^{\prime}):e^{\prime}\in E_{v},e^{\prime}\succeq_{v}e\}=1.

Observe that this requires
v
to be saturated by
f
, and all edges
e^{\prime}\in E_{v}
with
f(e^{\prime})>0
to be weakly preferred to
e
by
v
. We say that
f
is
stable
if it dominates every edge
e\in E
at some
v\in e
. Note that if
f
is
\{0,1\}
-valued, this means that the
1
-valued edges form a stable matching. The following theorem of Aharoni and Fleiner
[
1
]
guarantees the existence of a stable fractional matching (in the special case of graphs, this is implicit in an earlier result of Tan
[
16
]
).

Theorem 4.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

For any hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and preference system
\bigl(\succeq_{v}\bigr)_{v\in V}
there exists a stable fractional matching.

Here we propose another relaxation of the notion of stable matching. Instead of looking at matchings, we consider arbitrary sets of edges, and want to guarantee the existence of a stable one whose size is small (in terms of the number of vertices and the edge size). We will obtain such a result for
3
-uniform hypergraphs by combining Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
1.3
.

More precisely, we need a slight generalization of Corollary
1.3
, which may be of independent interest. In the corollary, we assumed the existence of an exact fractional edge cover, which is the same as a fractional matching which saturates all the vertices. The following proposition is an adaptation of the corollary that handles fractional matchings which may not saturate all the vertices.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
H=(V,E)
be a
3
-uniform hypergraph with
|V|=n
. Let
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a fractional matching, and let
S
be the set of vertices saturated by
f
. Then there exists a set of edges
E^{\prime}\subseteq\{e\in E:f(e)>0\}
of size
|E^{\prime}|\leq\frac{n}{2}
such that
S\subseteq\bigcup E^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First, we remove from
E
any edges
e
with
f(e)=0
. Next we describe, in three steps, a finite process in which we add new triples to
E
which are disjoint from
S
and assign weights to them, or increase the weights of existing such edges. At the end of the process, we will be able to apply Corollary
1.3
to the resulting hypergraph.

Step 1
. As long as there are at least
3
unsaturated vertices in
V
, we pick three of them
u,v,w
, add
uvw
to
E
if it is not an edge already, and assign or increase its weight so that at least one of
u,v,w
becomes saturated while the weight at the others does not exceed
1
.

Step 2
. Let
U
be the set of vertices that remain unsaturated at the end of Step 1. Then
|U|\leq 2
. For each
u\in U
, let
\alpha_{u}\leq 1
be the additional weight that
u
needs in order to be saturated. We add to
V
a set
W=\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}\}
of
4
new vertices. For each
u\in U
, we add new edges
uw_{1}w_{2}
and
uw_{3}w_{4}
with weight
\frac{\alpha_{u}}{2}
each. Now all vertices in
V
are saturated, and the weight at each
w_{i}
is
\sum_{u\in U}\frac{\alpha_{u}}{2}\leq 1
.

Step 3
. We add the
4
triples contained in
W
as new edges with weight
\frac{1}{3}(1-\sum_{u\in U}\frac{\alpha_{u}}{2})
each. Now all vertices in
V\cup W
are saturated.

Applying Corollary
1.3
to the resulting hypergraph, we find a set
E^{\prime\prime}
of edges (original or new) that cover
V\cup W
, such that
|E^{\prime\prime}|\leq\frac{n+4}{2}
. Because original edges do not contribute to covering
W
, there must be at least two new edges in
E^{\prime\prime}
. We remove the new edges from
E^{\prime\prime}
and obtain
E^{\prime}\subseteq\{e\in E:f(e)>0\}
of size
|E^{\prime}|\leq\frac{n}{2}
. We have
S\subseteq\bigcup E^{\prime}
because the new edges are disjoint from
S
.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.3
.

For any
3
-uniform hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and preference system
\bigl(\succeq_{v}\bigr)_{v\in V}
there exists a stable set of edges
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}\subseteq E
with
m\leq\frac{|V|}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a stable fractional matching, whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem
4.1
. Let
S
be the set of vertices saturated by
f
. Applying Proposition
4.2
, we can find a set of edges
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}\subseteq E
with
m\leq\frac{|V|}{2}
such that
S\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}
and
f(e_{i})>0
for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. We claim that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
is stable. Indeed, let
e\in E
. As
f
is stable, it dominates
e
at some
v\in e
. By definition, this means that
v\in S
and all edges
e^{\prime}\in E_{v}
with
f(e^{\prime})>0
satisfy
e^{\prime}\succeq_{v}e
. Choosing
e_{i}
such that
v\in e_{i}
, we have
e_{i}\succeq_{v}e
, which shows that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
dominates
e
at
v
. As
e
was arbitrary, this proves that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
is stable.
∎

The following example shows that Theorem
4.3
is sharp.

Example 4.4

(
K^{(3)}_{4}
with cyclic preferences)
.

Let
H
be the complete
3
-uniform hypergraph on
4
vertices. We write the vertex set as
V=\{0,1,2,3\}
and use addition modulo
4
. The preferences of each vertex
i
are:

V\setminus\{i+1\}\succeq_{i}V\setminus\{i+2\}\succeq_{i}V\setminus\{i+3\}.

No single edge forms a stable set. Indeed, the edge
V\setminus\{i\}
does not dominate the edge
V\setminus\{i+3\}
, because the vertices
i+1
and
i+2
both prefer
V\setminus\{i+3\}
to
V\setminus\{i\}
. Thus, the smallest size of a stable set of edges is
2
. More generally, we can take
k
disjoint copies of this example. Then we have
4k
vertices, and a stable set of edges must contain at least two edges from each copy, hence a total of at least
2k
edges.

We remark that the analog of Theorem
4.3
for graphs holds with the sharp bound
m\leq\frac{2}{3}|V|
. This can be deduced quite easily from Tan
[
16
]
. We omit the details.
