MAGEC2 promotes tumorigenesis in multiple myeloma through USP16-mediated deubiquitination and stabilization of c-Myc
Authors: Zihao Liu, Tingting Hu, Jie Min, Chen Wang, Yuhao Cao, Mengjie Guo, Jinjun Qian, Chunyan Gu, Ye Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple myeloma (MM) is an incurable malignancy characterized by clonal plasma cell proliferation, in which dysregulated ubiquitination plays a key pathogenic role. Using bufalin as a molecular probe, we identified melanoma-associated antigen C2 (MAGEC2) as a direct binding target and investigated its unconventional function beyond canonical E3 ligase modulation. MAGEC2 expression and clinical relevance were analyzed in patient cohorts. Functional assays (MTT, Western blot, CCK-8, flow cytometry) and mechanistic studies (co-immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, ubiquitination assays) were performed in vitro and in an adoptive B-cell transfer mouse model. High MAGEC2 expression was significantly associated with poor overall survival in MM patients. Functionally, MAGEC2 promoted malignant proliferation and induced resistance to adriamycin and bortezomib. Mechanistically, in addition to its known role in enhancing E3 ligase activity, MAGEC2 directly bound and increased the deubiquitinase activity of USP16, leading to c-Myc stabilization via reduced ubiquitination. Interactions among MAGEC2, USP16, and c-Myc were confirmed both in vitro and in vivo, establishing a MAGEC2-USP16-c-Myc axis. MAGEC2 overexpression also exacerbated bone lesions in vivo. Collectively, these findings identify MAGEC2 as both a prognostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target in MM, and highlight its role as a multifunctional regulator of the ubiquitin system.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00782-4.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, MAGEC2, USP16, c-Myc, Ubiquitination

To the Editor

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) plays a significant role in the treatment of hematologic malignancies through its multi-target mechanisms [1, 2]. Bufalin, a bioactive compound derived from toad venom, exhibits potent anti-tumor effects but is limited by cardiotoxicity [3, 4]. In this study, we focused on bufalin as a molecular probe and identified MAGEC2 as a direct binding target in multiple myeloma (MM). The MAGE family of proteins is known to enhance E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [5], but the mechanisms underlying their upstream regulation remain unclear. Therefore, our study aimed to investigate whether MAGEC2 functions differently from conventional E3 ligase interactions and acts as a multifunctional regulator of the ubiquitin system. Dysregulated ubiquitination is a key factor in the pathogenesis of MM, driving proliferation and drug resistance [6]. By uncovering the non-canonical role of MAGEC2, our study aims to establish it as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target to overcome malignant progression in MM.

Increased expression of MAGEC2 is associated with poor survival and aggressive proliferation in MM

To investigate the functional role of bufalin, MTT assays confirmed that bufalin significantly inhibited MM cell viability (Fig. 1A). Using bufalin as a probe, we identified MAGEC2 as a direct molecular target (Fig. 1B), and microscale thermophoresis (MST) verified their strong, direct interaction with a binding affinity of 36.416 µM (Fig. 1C). To further explore the clinical implications of MAGEC2, we performed survival analysis using the KM-Plotter database, which includes a large cohort of MM patients treated with conventional induction chemotherapy, high-dose melphalan, and autologous stem cell transplantation. Our analysis revealed that among 806 patients with low MAGEC2 expression and 610 with high MAGEC2 expression, high MAGEC2 expression was associated with significantly reduced overall survival (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). Additionally, a pan-cancer analysis further showed frequent MAGEC2 upregulation across multiple malignancies, further supporting its pro-oncogenic role (Fig. 1E). Despite these associations, the specific biological function and mechanistic role of MAGEC2 in MM pathogenesis remain unclear.

To address this gap, we conducted a comprehensive functional characterization of MAGEC2 in MM. We established and confirmed stable MAGEC2-overexpressing (MAGEC2-OE) ARP1 and H929 cell lines through Western blot (WB) analysis (Fig. 1F). Our CCK-8 assays demonstrated that MAGEC2 overexpression significantly enhanced proliferation in both ARP1 and H929 cells (Fig. 1G). Flow cytometry analysis of cell cycle distribution also revealed that MAGEC2 overexpression prolonged the G2/M phase in both cell lines (Fig. 1H). In MM treatment, Adriamycin (ADR) and bortezomib (BTZ) are commonly used drugs [7]. MTT assays showed that MAGEC2 overexpression conferred increased resistance to ADR and BTZ in ARP1 and H929 cells, suggesting a role for MAGEC2 in mediating drug resistance (Fig. 1I). Conversely, we established stable MAGEC2-knockdown (MAGEC2-KD) cell lines in ARP1 and H929 backgrounds, with efficient depletion of MAGEC2 protein validated by WB (Fig. 1J). CCK-8 assays revealed that MAGEC2-KD effectively suppressed proliferation in both cell lines (Fig. 1K), while flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis demonstrated an increase in apoptotic rates following MAGEC2-KD in ARP1 and H929 cells (Fig. 1L), highlighting its critical role in cell survival [8].

MAGEC2 promotes malignant proliferation of MM by enhancing USP16 deubiquitinase activity to stabilize c-Myc

Previous studies have shown that MAGEC2 exerts oncogenic functions by enhancing the E3 ligase activity of RING-domain proteins, such as TRIM28, which promotes p53 degradation [9]. To identify additional downstream effectors, we performed ubiquitination-focused mass spectrometry and identified ribosomal protein s27A (RPS27A) and USP16 as the top candidates (Fig. 2A). To determine which protein serves as a more suitable downstream effector of MAGEC2, we conducted an integrated analysis using clinical databases. We collected peripheral blood samples from 22 normal plasma (NP) individuals, 44 patients with monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), and 351 patients with multiple myeloma (MM). Interestingly, the clinical signal abundance of RPS27A was higher in healthy controls compared to the MGUS and MM groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, high expression of RPS27A was associated with better overall survival in MM patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Although RPS27A ranked highly in our screen, its identity as a ubiquitous ribosomal structural protein [10] and its association with favorable clinical outcomes in MM patients suggest that it is unlikely to be a specific, direct effector in MAGEC2-driven oncogenesis.

In contrast, USP16 was consistently upregulated across multiple tumor types (Fig. 2D). Using the same cohort of MM patients from the KM-Plotter database, USP16 overexpression was associated with a poorer overall survival rate in MM (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). This expression pattern, which aligns with a poor prognosis and suggests a potential oncogenic function, led us to select USP16 as a primary candidate downstream target of MAGEC2 for further investigation. As USP16 is a deubiquitinase, it may target multiple substrates. Through an integrated analysis of protein interaction databases and the existing literature, we identified a potential interaction between c-Myc and USP16 [11]. Previous studies have also confirmed that c-Myc is a critical oncogenic driver in MM. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that c-Myc may be a deubiquitination substrate of USP16 in MM (Fig. 2F). To verify this hypothesis, we performed Co-IP and confirmed interactions among MAGEC2, USP16, and c-Myc (Fig. 2G). The WB results further indicated that overexpression of MAGEC2 increased the protein levels of USP16 and c-Myc (Fig. 2H), whereas MAGEC2 knockdown in ARP1 and H929 cells reduced their levels (Fig. 2I).

The results of ubiquitination assays showed that overexpression of MAGEC2 increased c-Myc deubiquitination (Fig. 2J). Additionally, cycloheximide chase assays revealed that both USP16 and c-Myc had extended half-lives (Fig. 2K). To investigate if MAGEC2 suppresses c-Myc ubiquitination by enhancing USP16’s deubiquitinase activity, we used small interfering RNA (siRNA) to silence USP16 expression in MAGEC2-overexpressing cells. The ubiquitination experiment confirmed our hypothesis that silencing USP16 reduces c-Myc deubiquitination levels (Fig. 2L), thereby decreasing c-Myc protein stability (Fig. 2M). We then purified recombinant MAGEC2, USP16, and c-Myc proteins and conducted surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis, which showed that USP16 directly interacts with both MAGEC2 and c-Myc with high affinity in vitro (Fig. 2N). To extend our cellular observations in vivo, we established adoptive B-cell transfer models via tail vein injection of splenocyte suspensions from MYCflox/flox·Cre+ mice into NOD-SCID recipients, co-administered with concentrated empty vector (EV) or MAGEC2-OE lentivirus. Survival curve analysis showed that mice with high MAGEC2 expression had significantly shorter overall survival compared to those in the EV group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2O). We also used Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) imaging to examine tibias from the EV and MAGEC2 groups and found that the MAGEC2-OE group had lower bone volume fraction (BV/TV) (P < 0.001) and bone mineral density (BMD) (P < 0.05), indicating more severe bone lesions (Fig. 2P).

In conclusion, our study identifies MAGEC2 as a target of bufalin that drives MM progression by enhancing USP16-mediated c-Myc deubiquitination. This pathway promotes proliferation, drug resistance, and bone lesions, highlighting MAGEC2 as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, and emphasizing the use of natural compound-based probes for target discovery [12].
