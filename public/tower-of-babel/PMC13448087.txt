Spatially explicit temperature optima improve climate impact assessment of global crop productivity
Authors: Han Wang, Zhao Zhang, Xinyu Wu, Jialu Xu, Jichong Han, Huimin Zhuang, Shaokun Li, Jie Song, Fei Cheng, Shilong Piao, Fulu Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate impact assessments on crop productivity typically rely on globally constant temperature optima, overlooking physiological acclimation across spatial temperature gradients. This limits predictive accuracy under progressive climate warming. Here, we present 0.05-degree resolution global maps of temperature optima for rice (mean ± spatial Standard Deviation: 28.63 ± 2.65 °C), soybean (27.34 ± 2.34 °C), maize (26.12 ± 3.46 °C), and wheat (24.73 ± 3.41 °C), derived from satellite-based proxies of gross primary productivity and validated against eddy covariance flux observations. Our analysis reveals pervasive spatial heterogeneity in temperature optima within species, peaking in subtropical regions. This thermal acclimation is shaped by local temperature, water availability, and solar radiation, highlighting a phenotypic plasticity in crop thermal responses. As warming accelerates, growing-season days exceeding local temperature optima are increasing, contracting the thermal safe space, particularly in tropical and subtropical zones. This poses significant risks to maize and rice productivity. Integrating spatially explicit temperature optima into climate impact models improves global crop productivity projections by approximately 16 ± 2%, and by up to 22 ± 4% for temperate crops. These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating spatially explicit thermal responses into Earth system models to refine agricultural projections and inform targeted adaptation strategies.

Introduction

Crop development, growth, physiology, and yield formation are highly sensitive to climate change1–4. Globally, climate change has already significantly affected crop phenology and yields5, and is projected to have more intensified impacts in the future6–8. Accurately assessing climate impacts on crop physiology and productivity is critical for developing effective adaptation strategies9–12. The direction of these impacts—whether positive or negative—largely depends on whether temperatures remain below or exceed the crop optimal temperature threshold (Topt)13–16. Topt for crop productivity at the ecosystem level can be defined as daily maximum air temperature (Tmax) at which crop Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) reaches its peak value and beyond which GPP start to decline with Tmax17–23. Crop productivity typically increases with rising temperatures up to Topt19,24–26, and yields may benefit from climate warming within this temperature range27–32. However, temperatures exceeding Topt impair photosynthesis by reducing Rubisco activity and disrupting chlorophyll fluorescence, ultimately leading to yield losses33–36.

Process-based crop models are routinely applied to project the impacts of climate change on crop productivity, yet their simulations are often hampered by uncertainties in model inputs, structures and parameters37,38. Among these, the temperature response functions and Topt parameters are key sources of uncertainty24. Most current models (e.g., ORYZA, DSSAT, APSIM) adopt globally constant Topt values for each crop39–41 and standardized temperature response curves to estimate climate impacts on crop productivity42,43. However, recent inter-model comparisons reveal substantial divergence in these functions, leading to inconsistent projections—even regarding the direction of climate change impacts on productivity5,24,44–47. Such inconsistencies largely stem from neglecting the spatial heterogeneity of crop thermal responses and their acclimation to local climate48–50. For example, Chang et al.51 demonstrated that applying inappropriate Topt values, such as static biome-level Topt, can distort temperature response functions and lead to biased GPP estimates. Therefore, accurately estimating spatially explicit Topt values for different crops is critical for improving climate impact assessments and informing effective adaptation.

The relationship between crop productivity and temperature follows a parabolic, downward-opening curve, where Topt defines the vertex corresponding to maximum productivity52,53 (Fig. 1). Recent large-scale studies have derived Topt for global vegetation using symmetric parabolic models, reflecting a climatic equilibrium shaped by long-term natural selection17–19,54. In these frameworks, crops are often coarsely aggregated with natural vegetation, neglecting the profound impact of modern agricultural management—such as irrigation, fertilization, and cultivar turnover. Unlike natural ecosystems, cropping systems are intensively managed according to environments, which can partially decouple crop physiological optima from background climate constraints. We therefore argue that crop Topt should be conceptualized as a realized agronomic optimum rather than a purely potential physiological trait. Furthermore, at the biophysical level, the relationship between crop productivity and temperature is fundamentally non-linear and asymmetric, governed by the kinetics of enzyme activation and deactivation24. Photosynthesis exhibits a steeper decline above Topt due to enzyme heat-lability, a process better described by asymmetric formulations55,56. Symmetric models may overestimate high-temperature tolerance and mispresent crop sensitivity to temperature. And the lookup tables on the maximum, optimal, and minimum temperature parameters for various crops21–23 may fail to capture thermal acclimation—the shift of Topt with local temperature56. Methodological framework.

GPPdev is the deviation of GPP estimates using spatially explicit Topt vs. constant Topt. Tss (temperature safe space) defines the difference between Tmax and Topt. Fstress(frequency of thermal stress) quantifies the proportion of days with Tmax above Topt in the growing season.

Here, we aim to investigate the global Topt distributions and thermal acclimations for four staple crops (maize, soybean, wheat, and rice) from 2001 to 2023, and refine the climate impact assessment of global crop productivity using the spatially explicit Topt. The specific objectives are to: (1) derive the global spatially explicit Topt for four crops using three global satellite-derived GPP proxies including GOSIF-GPP57, GOSIF58, NIRv59 and ground-truth eddy covariance measurements with the Wang–Engel (WE) curvilinear temperature response function24, and investigate the thermal acclimations; (2) identify the primary drivers of spatial Topt variability using an interpretable Random Forest framework accounting for climatic, edaphic, and management factors; (3)assess crop heat stress exposure under historical and future climates by analyzing the temperature safe space (the difference between growing season Tmax and Topt) and the frequency of thermal stress (the proportion of growing-season days exceeding Topt); and (4) quantify the systematic biases in climate impact assessments caused by the conventional assumption of spatially constant thermal responses (Fig. 1).

Drivers of global variations in Topt

To unravel the drivers of Topt spatial variability, we employ random forest models incorporating 17 predictors across climate, soil properties, and management practices, which explained over ~92% of the global variance. The growing-season average Tmax emerged consistently as the dominant predictor for maize, soybean, and rice (Fig. 4a). Partial dependence analyses reveal a positive monotonic relationship (maize: slope = 0.71 °C/ °C, 95% CI = [0.70, 0.72], t(1998) = 125.06; soybean: slope = 0.67, 95% CI = [0.66, 0.68], t(1998) = 155.06; wheat: slope = 0.18, 95% CI = [0.17, 0.19], t(1998) = 36.72; rice: slope = 0.65, 95% CI = [0.64, 0.65], t(1998) = 121.07; all p < 0.001): as the climatic background is warmer, the crop’s Topt shifts higher. This provides robust evidence of thermal acclimation at the global scale, with crops adjusting their thermal niche to track the prevailing climate (Fig. S6). Beyond temperature, the drivers diverge by crop type, reflecting distinct physiological constraints. For wheat, solar radiation (Rad) is the primary predictor, likely because the growing season occurs during cooler, light-limited months. Water constraints are non-linear (Fig. 4b). For maize (slope = 0.70, 95% CI = [0.59, 0.81], t(171) = 12.84, p < 0.001), rice (slope = 1.00, 95% CI = [0.86, 1.14], t(116) = 14.23, p < 0.001), and soybean (slope = 1.11, 95% CI = [0.85, 1.37], t(101) = 8.59, p < 0.001), vapor pressure deficit (VPD) maintains a consistent positive association with Topt even under high evaporative demand ( > 2 kPa), reflecting robust heat-drought adaptation. In contrast, this positive trend reverses under severe dryness (VPD > 2 kPa) for wheat (slope = -0.32, 95% CI = [-0.69, 0.05], t(39) = -1.75, p = 0.087), indicating a specific vulnerability to stomatal closure. Conversely, the Aridity Index (AI) shows a negative association(maize: slope = -0.52, 95% CI = [-0.56, -0.49], t(1998) = -33.38; soybean: slope = -0.54, 95% CI = [-0.56, -0.52], t(1998) = -62.42; wheat: slope = -1.40, 95% CI = [-1.46, -1.34], t(1998) = -47.54; rice: slope = -0.11, 95% CI = [-0.12, -0.10], t(1998) = -22.44; all p < 0.001), implying that crops in wetter climates (higher AI) exhibit lower Topt due to reduced adaptive pressure compared to those in arid zones. Rice, however, shows a unique sensitivity to Precipitation (Prcp), highlighting the impact of waterlogging stress on thermal regulation in paddy systems60. Notably, soil potassium (K2O) emerges as a potential non-climatic driver for maize. This result may reflect an underlying nutrient-mediated mechanism for stomatal heat regulation61,62. Analysis of crop thermal acclimation (δTopt /δTmax, Topt sensitivity to Tmax) (Fig. S7) reveals a positive linear response to VPD for wheat (slope = 0.12, 95% CI = [0.09, 0.15], t(46) = 6.99, p < 0.001), indicating active physiological adjustments to cope with high atmospheric dryness. In contrast, soybean and rice show physiological tipping points at relatively low VPD levels ( ~ 0.96 kPa and ~0.56 kPa, respectively), beyond which acclimation capacity declines significantly (soybean: slope = -0.28, 95% CI = [-0.35, -0.20], t(15) = -7.78; rice: slope = -0.11, 95% CI = [−0.16, -0.05], t(44) = -3.81; all p < 0.001). High radiation generally enhances thermal acclimation in wheat (slope = 0.001, 95% CI = [0.000, 0.001], t(67) = 3.17, p = 0.002), maize (slope = 0.04, 95% CI = [0.03, 0.05], t(53) = 9.40, p < 0.001) and soybean (slope = 0.01, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.03], t(36) = 2.27, p = 0.03), likely due to increased energy availability for physiological adjustments. In contrast, rice remains relatively insensitive to radiation changes (p = 0.14-0.53). Maize show an initial positive acclimation response to increasing precipitation (slope = 0.0002, 95% CI = [0.0001, 0.0002], t(41) = 4.03, p < 0.001). While wheat and soybean both exhibit a hump-shaped response with different inflection points ( ~ 3,450 mm vs. ~1,200 mm), indicating that wheat tolerates substantially higher precipitation before thermal acclimation is suppressed. Rice exhibit a negative response (slope = -0.0001, 95% CI = [-0.0002, -0.0001], t(47) = -3.45, p = 0.001), suggesting that excessive precipitation may dampen the thermal acclimation signals in the paddy system. For AI, wheat exhibits a contrasting pattern compared to the other three crops, with thermal acclimation recovering beyond AI ~ 1.10.

Global patterns of thermal safe space and the frequency of thermal stress

Global Topt patterns reveal the critical impacts of climate change on thermal safe space (Tss) and the frequency of thermal stress (Fstress). Between 2001 and 2023, Tss and Fstress maintained spatially consistent patterns, as Tmax reached or surpassed optimal levels across primary agricultural regions in Asia, Africa, and South America (Fig. 5a, c). Notably, temperature stress occurred on >45% of growing season days, highlighting a progressive Tss deficit in subtropical and tropical crop zones. Integrating our spatially explicit Topt maps with CMIP6 climate projections, we established a robust biological baseline for assessing future thermal risks. Compared to the 2001–2023 baseline, the average Tmax during crop growing season is projected to increase by 2.56 ± 1.01 °C under SSP126, 3.62 ± 1.03 °C under SSP245, and 5.67 ± 1.22 °C under SSP585 by 2081–2100 (Fig. S8). The dynamic interactions between projected regional warming and our spatially explicit Topt drive distinct vulnerability trajectories. Specifically, wheat maintains a sufficient Tss (>4.23 °C) in all scenarios, though its safe space diminishes progressively from current baseline of 9.64 °C. In contrast, maize, rice, and soybean undergo a critical transition from safe to stressed conditions. Under baseline climate conditions, these crops operate within a safe thermal range, with a Tss between 1.13 °C and 2.04 °C. However, under moderate warming scenarios (SSP126 and SSP245), this safety margin collapses. Maize and Rice enter a state of mild-to-moderate thermal stress (Tss -1.55 °C ~ -2.70 °C), with the Fstress surging to 70–81%. Soybean similarly shifts into negative territory (Tss: -0.71 °C to -1.80 °C). Under the SSP585 scenario, all three crops face severe thermal failure. Tss plunges below -3.86 °C (-4.70 °C for maize), while heat exposure intensifies with 84–89% of the growing season exceeding the local Topt. This indicates that without significant adaptation, these staple crops will be exposed to supra-optimal temperatures for nearly their entire growth season, posing immense risks to global food productivity and resilience. This stress intensification aligns with regional patterns (Fig. 5c), where current heat stress frequency peaks in African rice (61%), European maize (47%), South American soybean (48%), and Asian wheat (23%). These regions are as the primary hotspots for future heat risk.

To rigorously assess the impact of heat stress on crop productivity, we perform a bin-averaged regression analysis between cumulative GPP and Fstress for the major producing regions of each continent (Fig. 5d; Table S4). The results reveal distinct crop- and region-specific responses. For example, maize’s cumulative GPP demonstrates consistent vulnerability to heat stress across all major regions. The negative impact is particularly pronounced in North America (slope = -11.70 GgC/%, 95% CI = [-15.60, -7.82], t(48) = -6.06, R2 = 0.43, p < 0.001) and Asia (slope = -1.59 GgC/%, 95% CI = [-2.25, -0.93], t(49) = -4.85, R2 = 0.33, p < 0.001), confirming that increased thermal exposure significantly suppresses productivity. Soybean exhibits a divergent pattern: productivity is significantly negatively correlated with Fstress in South America (slope = -7.10 GgC/%, 95% CI = [-8.55, -5.65], t(48) = -9.85, R2 = 0.67, p < 0.001), whereas Asia shows a significant positive relationship (slope = +1.61 GgC/%, 95% CI = [0.89, 2.32], t(49) = 4.49, R2 = 0.29, p < 0.001). In North America, the trend is positive but lack statistical significance (slope = 3.87 GgC/%, 95% CI = [-0.85, 8.58], t(29) = 1.68, p = 0.104, R2 = 0.09). In contrast to summer crops, wheat shows a widespread positive response to thermal exposure across all regions, especially in China (slope = 30.27, 95% CI = [27.05, 33.48], t(35) = 19.12, R2 = 0.91, p < 0.001) and Germany (slope = 19.34 GgC/%, 95% CI = [17.03, 21.65], t(30) = 17.11, R2 = 0.91, p < 0.001) (Fig. S9). This suggests that for this cool-season crop, current warming trends may alleviate cold constraints or extend the growing season rather than imposing net thermal penalties20. Unexpectedly, rice in Asia exhibits a robust positive correlation (slope = +5.01 GgC/%, 95% CI = [4.49, 5.53], t(49) = 19.40, R2 = 0.89, p < 0.001), indicating high resilience in these intensively managed systems, likely due to the buffering effects of aquatic environments.

Difference between using constant and spatially explicit Topt for GPP estimates

To translate the derived Topt maps into climate impact assessments, we utilized the Wang-Engel formulation (Eq. 3) to calculate the cumulative GPP deviation (GPPdev) and the Relative Improvement in Accuracy (RIA) at 0.05° resolution. Given that spatially explicit Topt accounts for local thermal adaptation and is physiologically more realistic, we treat its derived GPP as the reference baseline. Deviations from this baseline caused by using traditional constant Topt values are hereafter referred to as overestimations (positive GPPdev) or underestimations (negative GPPdev).

Specifically, under historical climatic conditions, GPP derived by constant Topt is overestimated for maize (GPPdev = +0.030 TgC) but underestimated for wheat (-0.056TgC), soybean (-0.034 TgC), and rice (-0.180 TgC) (Fig. 6a, c). Future climate warming (from SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5) fundamentally alters and exacerbates these discrepancies. Maize exhibits the most dramatic shift to a severe underestimation, with GPPdev from -0.005 TgC under SSP1-2.6 to -0.096 TgC under SSP5-8.5. This indicates that that model errors escalate significantly with climate warming (Fig. 6a). Soybean (-0.016 to -0.049 TgC) and wheat (-0.042 to -0.057 TgC) both show a steady intensification of negative bias. In contrast, rice maintains a relatively stable and slightly positive deviation ( + 0.006 to +0.008 TgC) across all climate scenarios. Spatially explicit patterns of GPPdev and RIA reveal a distinct latitudinal divergence (Fig. 6a; Fig. S10a). The most severe GPP underestimations (the largest negative GPPdev) are concentrated in temperate regions, particular across major agricultural countries such as the United States, Ukraine, and China (Fig. 6a–c; Fig. S10a). These deficits become most pronounced under the SSP585 scenario, where the bias is largest for maize (−0.131 TgC), followed by wheat (−0.081 TgC), soybean (−0.077 TgC), and rice (−0.064 TgC). This robustly demonstrates that using constant Topt most severely undervalue the productivity potential of mid-latitude breadbaskets in a warming world. In contrast, the hotspot of model sensitivity shifts dramatically toward the equator. During the historical period, temperate regions exhibit the highest potential for relative accuracy improvement (Fig. S10a, b). However, under SSP585, the Tropics emerge as the new epicenter of sensitivity, with RIA projected to reach 51% for tropical wheat and 41% for tropical maize. Consequently, spatially explicit parameterization will be disproportionately critical for accurate monitoring and predicting crop productivity in these heat-stressed environments.

Discussion

The Topt maps derived from multiple GPP proxies reveal consistent spatial patterns across all four crops, with subtropical regions exhibiting the highest Topt values, followed by tropical and temperate regions. This distribution contrasts with that for terrestrial ecosystem17,19,54, which typically shows higher Topt following warmer climatic zones. Our derived Topt patterns reflect a combination of physiological and agronomic drivers. At the leaf level, thermal acclimation mechanisms—particularly enhanced Rubisco activity and electron transport capacity20,63—partially explain the Topt patterns. However, the subtropical peak highlights the regulating role of agricultural management and physiological limits. First, management plays a critical role. Major subtropical breadbaskets (e.g., the US Corn Belt and North China Plain) are characterized by intensive irrigation and high nutrient inputs (e.g., potassium, as identified in our driver analysis)64,65. Irrigation modifies land-atmosphere interactions, cooling land surface and mitigating heat extremes. These practices alleviate water and nutrient limitations, effectively decoupling crops from the natural temperature-VPD coupling65–67. By sustaining transpiration cooling and stomatal function, these inputs enable subtropical crops to realize higher thermal optima than resource-limited tropical systems61,68. Second, tropical crops face incomplete acclimation and cumulative stress. Our data show that the thermal acclimation rate (slope of Topt ~Tmax) is consistently less than 1.0 (Fig. S6), indicating that physiological adjustments lag behind ambient heat in the tropics. Moreover, without the buffering effect of intensive management, prolonged exposure to heat stress ( > Topt) reduces photosynthetic efficiency26,69,70, explaining the paradoxically lower Topt values observed in tropical relative to subtropical regions. Among staple crops, rice exhibits the highest Topt, a result of unique genotypic traits, such as prevalence of heat-tolerant cultivars, integrated with environmental and management factors including phenology, irrigation intensity, and land-climate interactions71. Our analysis reveals that, prior to reaching their thermal tipping points, temperate crops exhibit the highest metabolic elasticity ( ~ 7% gain per °C increase in Topt). This confirms that humid and cool regions retain the greatest capacity to enhance carbon uptake through thermal adaptation18,72.

Our analysis identifies growing-season mean Tmax as the primary driver for most crops, confirming that thermal acclimation to the prevailing climate is the dominant adaptation strategy17,73–75. However, we acknowledge that the dominance of Tmax likely reflects both direct thermal effects and its structural coupling with atmospheric water demand (VPD). This adaptation is critically modulated by energy, water, and nutrient constraints. For wheat, Rad supersedes temperature as the dominant driver. This reflects light limitation during its cooler growing season, a finding reinforced by its positive acclimation response to radiation, as high Rad is required to activate photosynthetic enzymes and support the metabolic costs of thermal acclimation76,77. Water limitation acts as a potent adaptive pressure. Generally, higher atmospheric demand (higher VPD) and drier climates (lower AI) are associated with elevated Topt, suggesting that crops in water-limited environments develop higher heat tolerance78. However, the capacity to sustain this acclimation varies: while maize maintains robust gains and wheat demonstrates exceptionally high moisture tolerance, soybean and rice exhibit early acclimation-tipping points at relatively low VPD levels, indicating higher physiological vulnerability to combined heat-drought stress66. For maize, the significant role of K2O is consistent with a potential nutrient-mediated mechanism in stomatal regulation. This specific sensitivity aligns with recent findings demonstrating that maize has a disproportionately high physiological demand for potassium compared to other major cereals like wheat and rice, which may render it uniquely vulnerable to K-limitation79. Notably, our analysis showed that K₂O ranked higher in importance than N (a proxy for general productivity)80,81, and K2O showed negligible correlation with irrigation (r = 0.08, 95% CI [0.07, 0.08], t(229,496) = 38.12, p < 0.001). We hypothesize that this statistical selectivity could reflect potassium’s unique physiological role in maintaining stomatal turgor and sustaining transpiration cooling under heat stress61,82, though this correlational finding warrants further experimental verification. Given the correlational nature of our modeling, these findings imply that breeding for heat resilience could benefit from moving beyond thermal traits alone; future strategies may leverage enhanced radiation use efficiency (for wheat), drought tolerance (for soybean and rice), and nutrient use efficiency (for maize) to expand the physiological thermal safe space.

Our analysis reveals a critical divergence in climate resilience, challenging the common perception of universal heat risks. Maize demonstrates systemic vulnerability, characterized by a strong negative GPP sensitivity to warming—most pronounced in North America—that aligns with projected yield declines83–85. This necessitates urgent, local management interventions, ranging from the deployment of heat-tolerant hybrids to the optimization of irrigation infrastructure78. Similarly, South American soybean faces intensifying threats, demanding effective adaptive strategies to mitigate heat stress. In contrast, wheat emerges as the most climate-resilient crop. Its consistent positive response across Europe and China suggests that moderate warming benefits this cool-season crop by alleviating cold constraints, extending growing seasons, and leveraging CO2 fertilization effects86. Notably, our wheat category aggregates both spring and winter varieties, showing a wide range of Topt. The positive response to warming across these regions likely reflects the dominance of winter wheat, which benefits from alleviated cold constraints. Unexpectedly, Asian rice also exhibits robust resilience, this is likely attributable to the microclimatic buffering of aquatic paddy environments, intensive management, and varieties renewal, which decouple canopy temperature from ambient thermal stress87. These genotype-environment-management interactions underscore that effective adaptation requires localized solutions. Strategic integration of advanced breeding with management optimization, such as precision irrigation and optimal sowing dates, holds significant potential for enhancing adaptive capacity in vulnerable maize systems. Meanwhile, management in wheat and rice systems should focus on maximizing the benefits of ameliorating thermal resources88.

Crop models typically utilize global constant Topt values to project climate change impacts on global productivity89. However, our analysis indicates that applying these constant thresholds introduces systematic biases relative to the spatially explicit Topt. Specifically, under historical conditions, it overestimates the cumulative GPP of maize while underestimating it for rice, wheat, and soybean. These biases are most pronounced in temperate regions, where spatially explicit Topt values are generally lower than the conventionally used thresholds. Crucially, these estimation errors escalate significantly under future high-emission scenarios. In major agricultural producers, including America, China, and Ukraine, these discrepancies could lead to significant maladaptation strategies. For example, overestimating GPP can lead to unsustainable water allocation in drought-prone regions90. Similarly, underestimating GPP obscures the true nutrient requirements of rapidly growing crops, potentially leading to insufficient fertilizer application and exacerbating nutrient stress91. Furthermore, misestimating crop productivity may distort commodity forecasts, triggering price volatility that disproportionately affects food-insecure populations92. Integrating high-resolution, spatially explicit Topt values is therefore essential to reduce climate impact assessment uncertainties and guide more precise agricultural management strategies.

Understanding Topt dynamics under progressive climate warming is essential for building agricultural resilience. Previous studies showed Topt was increasing for approximately 30% areas of global vegetation due to thermal acclimation19,54. Our results reveal that across four staple crops, maximum GPP and Topt-Tmax coupling (thermal sensitivity) increased significantly over ~26% and ~57% of croplands, respectively (Fig. S11). In contrast, upward shifts in Topt were rare ( ~ 0.3%) and Topt trends were predominantly non-significant (Fig. S12). This stagnation is likely associated with the observed trends in growing-season Tmax which showed little to no linear increase in major production zones during this period (Fig. S8). This suggests that crops have not yet been forced to acclimate by a non-significant change in Tmax. However, future projections indicate that rapid warming will eventually exceed the buffering threshold. Using our conservative projection approaches (spatial pattern stability and historical gradient-based acclimation93), we estimate a 6% reduction in Fstress compared to static models (Fig. S13). This highlights a critical opportunity: since natural acclimation has been slow, targeted breeding to actively shift Topt could meaningfully mitigate future productivity losses, particularly under high-emission scenarios. The divergence between the observed historical stagnation and the required future adaptation underscores the urgent need for long-term monitoring to guide genetic interventions in a timely manner.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, our validation relies on flux tower observations that are concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere. Although we incorporate available datasets from the tropics and Southern Hemisphere and assume climatic analogy, expanding observation networks in the Southern Hemisphere is essential to refining the estimates. Second, the 0.05° resolution of current satellite products inherently limits our ability to capture sub-grid heterogeneity in highly fragmented landscapes. We have mitigated the background noise effectively by employing a rigorous 30-meter cropland mask, strict phenological masking, and 90th percentile extraction. Nevertheless, the Topt estimates could contain mixed vegetation signals, and this scale mismatch could introduce variance during site-level validation (e.g., Fig. 2). Third, a direct, mechanistic separation of management intensity from climate constraints remains a significant challenge at the global scale due to the lack of high-resolution, time-series agronomic management records. Therefore, our findings rely on statistical inference to provide a macro-scale perspective on these complex interactions. Fourth, it is essential to distinguish that our Topt estimates are derived from GPP (gross photosynthetic assimilation) rather than NPP (net biomass accumulation) or crop yield. Physiologically, autotrophic respiration rates increase exponentially with temperature, often exhibiting greater thermal sensitivity than photosynthesis94,95. Consequently, the temperature optimum for net carbon gain or yield may be lower than the Topt for GPP identified in this study, which represents the physiological upper limit for carbon assimilation. Future studies incorporating explicit crop respiration modules are necessary to complement this work. Finally, our CMIP6-based future assessments should be interpreted as potential vulnerability trajectories based on current physiological traits—a baseline thermal exposure assessment—rather than definitive future outcomes. These projections are subject to inherent climate model uncertainties and do not explicitly account for dynamic feedbacks such as CO2 fertilization (which may alleviate heat stress, particularly in C3 crops) or phenological shifts (e.g., earlier planting), or unprecedented future advancements in agricultural technology, crop breeding, and dynamic management adaptations.

In conclusion, utilizing three GPP proxies, we systematically analyze the spatial patterns and drivers of Topt and evaluate Tss and Fstress across global cropping systems under both current (2001-2023) and future climates (2081-2100). Our findings demonstrate that Topt is not a static constant but varies according to temperature regimes and environmental drivers, particularly Rad and VPD. Incorporating high-resolution spatially explicit Topt into crop models can significantly enhance the representation of photosynthetic processes and the accuracy of carbon sequestration estimates. Crucially, this study provides a quantitative assessment of biases introduced by the use of global constant versus spatially explicit Topt values in climate impact studies. These insights have profound implications for refining global climate change assessments and for designing targeted, local adaptation strategies. The study has important practical implications; for example, the results can guide farmers in cultivar selection and judicious management, support policymakers in developing effective adaptation strategies, and benefit insurance companies by helping them more precisely predict crop risk from heat stress.

Methods

Eddy covariance measurements

We use half-hourly averaged air temperature and GPP data from Fluxnet 2015, Fluxnet CH4, AmeriFlux96, OzFlux97, European Fluxes Database Cluster (https://www.europe-fluxdata.eu/) and ChinaFlux98, all of which have undergone a standardized set of quality control and gap filling99. These half-hourly average temperatures were subsequently used to determine the maximum daily temperature (Tmax) to align with satellite data. All the half-hourly data are aggregated into 8 days for further estimates of the optimal climate condition for crop productivity. In total, 34 sites covering 335 site-years of data are used (Table S5; Fig. S14). The data records range in length from 3 to 20 years. The crops at these sites include maize at 20 sites covering 171 site-years, soybean at 8 sites covering 36 site-years, wheat at 17 sites covering 105 site-years, and rice at 5 sites covering 23 site-years. The 335 site-years of data are selected based on two criteria: (i) sites must have at least 3 site-years of observations data, and (ii) individual site-years must contain more than 90% of the required half-hourly data.

Proxy data for Crop GPP

Previous studies have suggested that satellite-based near-infrared reflectance (NIRv), GOSIF, and GOSIF-GPP products can serve as a proxy for GPP in remote sensing39,100. SIF is a type of glow emitted by plants under sunlight, originating from chlorophyll molecules in leaves and spanning wavelengths between 600 and 800 nm. SIF is considered a more direct indicator of photosynthesis (GPP). The GOSIF dataset57, features high spatial (0.05°) and temporal (8-day) resolution with an extended data record from 2000 to the present. GOSIF is developed using machine learning methods (specifically, a stereoscopic model) based on discrete OCO-2 SIF soundings, MODIS remote sensing data, and MERRA-2 meteorological reanalysis data. GOSIF-GPP is a global GPP product mapped at a 0.05° spatial resolution and 8-day time step, based on the global SIF product (GOSIF) from OCO-2 and the linear relationship between SIF and GPP57.

We use 8-day NIRV for 2001–2023, calculated from the bidirectional reflectance distribution function-corrected surface reflectance with a spatial resolution of 1 km, provided by the MODIS MCD43A4. NIRv is defined as the product of the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and NIR reflectance (NIR) and has been found to effectively represent canopy structure and, to some extent, photosynthetic capacity. As a proxy indicator of photosynthesis, NIRv is considered to be highly correlated with solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF)59,101.

These GPP proxy datasets are then aggregated into 8-day gridded data for the period 2001–2023, with a spatial resolution of 0.05°. We extract and average values within a 3×3-pixel window around each site from the remote sensing data and calculated the standard deviation of the nine values within the window, thereby linking site-scale observations with raster-scale satellite data. We select 3×3-pixel window to mitigate geolocation uncertainty and point-spread-function effects inherent in satellite products. A comparison indicates that using 3×3-pixel window performs better than using 1×1-pixel window in validation (Fig. S15). We validated three GPP proxies against site-level data strictly during the crop growing seasons. While all proxies demonstrated reasonable performance (mean site-level r > 0.7), GOSIF and GOSIF-GPP emerged as the most robust. Specifically, validation against flux tower observations reveals that over 73% of the sites exhibited a correlation greater than 0.7 for these two datasets (Table S6). We use GOSIF to calculate the optimal climate values for the productivity of different crops (Fig. S4).

For subsequent GPP estimation, we employ a linear mixed-effects model (LME) to quantify the relationship between GOSIF and GPP across multiple growing seasons for different crops102. The model accounts for both fixed effects (the linear relationship between GOSIF and GPP) and random effects (spatial variability in site locations), facilitating the conversion of GOSIF values into GPP estimates. Climate data

We use meteorological data from ERA5-Land. ERA5-Land represents the land component of the ERA5 reanalysis data and offers more accurate land parameters with a higher resolution of 0.1°, allowing for better characterization of land conditions. ERA5-Land is forced solely by ERA5 meteorological fields in the lower atmosphere, combined with an additional lapse rate correction, making the computation of ERA5-Land more cost-effective103,104. We extract daily time series of maximum temperature and mean temperature for each pixel from the meteorological dataset. The daily data in 2001-2023 are averaged into 8-day intervals to match the spatial and temporal resolution of the GPP proxy remote sensing series.

Precipitation and potential evapotranspiration (PET) data from ERA5-Land are used to calculate aridity index105. PET is part of a newly developed global daily product (dPET), with a spatial resolution of 0.1°. It is calculated using meteorological variables from the ERA5-Land dataset106. The CMIP6 models (ACCESS-ESM1-5, CMCC-ESM2, CanESM5, FGOALS-g3, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, and MRI-ESM2-0) provide daily maximum temperature data every 8 days. We apply bias correction to the data from each model107. The correlation between the data for 2001–2014 from the models and ERA5-land data is significantly strong (mean r = 0.81) (Fig. S17). The mean value of the eight models is used to represent the Tmax for each 8-day period. Finally, the data for 2081–2100 under the SSP126, SSP245, and SSP585 scenarios are applied for projections under future climate scenarios. We used bilinear interpolation to downscale all meteorological variables from 0.1° to 0.05°108.

Identifying temperature optima of crop GPP

Topt values can be derived in over 89% of pixels with crop cultivation (Table S9). We provided latitudinal summaries of all three WE-derived cardinal temperatures (Tmin, Topt, and Tmax) for each crop, grouped by 10° absolute latitude bands (Table S10).

Drivers of global variation in Topt

We employ Random Forest models to identify the primary drivers of spatial variability of Topt114. These factors are selected due to their relevance to crop production dynamics. A suite of 17 predictor variables, selected for their established relevance to crop production dynamics (Table S11), is initially considered. To mitigate overfitting, we perform recursive feature elimination115. This iterative process ranks variables by their importance and systematically remove the least contributive predictors. Model performance is evaluated at each step using R2 value based on out-of-bag observations; variables are only retained if their inclusion improves the R2 value by at least 0.002. This procedure continues until the three most influential predictors are identified. To optimize the Random Forest models for each crop, we implement a rigorous grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation via the H2O machine learning platform. The optimization objective focuses on minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) across the validation folds. We identify the optimal hyperparameters—specifically the number of variables per node split (maize: 8, soybean: 8, wheat: 8, rice: 8) and the number of trees (maize: 500, soybean: 500, wheat: 500, rice: 300)—to train the final ranger models for SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis. The final predictor sets vary by crop, reflecting their specific acclimation dynamics.

Furthermore, we integrate the selected Random Forest model with SHAP to quantify the sensitivity of outputs to input variables and enhance feature importance analysis. Rooted in collaborative game theory, Shapley values evaluate each predictor’s impact by calculating its average marginal contribution across all possible feature combinations. This method ensures a fair distribution of credit to each feature based on its unique influence on model predictions. By utilizing SHAP dependence analysis, we isolate the specific influence of individual variables on the response, effectively uncovering complex, non-linear relationships independent of other predictors.

We integrate diverse environmental datasets to investigate how varying climatic conditions influence the thermal acclimation of major crops. Using the impact of VPD on Topt as an example, we partition Tmax and VPD into discrete intervals and calculate the mean Topt within each to visualize their joint statistical distribution. We define acclimation as the spatial sensitivity of the Topt; for instance, thermal acclimation is quantified as the sensitivity of Topt to Tmax. To assess this relationship, we employ a bootstrap resampling technique (1000 iterations) within each VPD interval to derive robust acclimation slopes. Only slopes that achieve statistical significance and fall within three standard deviations of the mean are retained. Finally, we utilize the R package (segmented) to perform piecewise regression, identifying critical breakpoints where shifts in thermal acclimation significantly occur across the VPD gradient.

Calculating the temperature safe space and frequency of stressed days

We calculate the temperature safe space (Tss) and the frequency of thermal stress (Fstress) for four major crops over the past two decades. Tss is defined as the deviation between the multi-year growing season mean temperature and the identified Topt. Fstress represents the proportion of days within the growing season where temperatures exceed the Topt relative to the total number of observation days. We map the global distributions of Tss and Fstress under the current climate and three future climate scenarios: SSP126, SSP245, and SSP585.

We calculate annual Topt at the pixel level and conduct a comprehensive trend analysis (Fig. S12). Our results indicate that most studied regions exhibit no significant increasing trend in Topt. Given this lack of clear temporal dynamics, we assume that the spatial distribution of Topt for the final two decades of this century (2081–2100) will remain consistent with the baseline (2001–2020).

Impacts of constant and spatially explicit Topt on GPP estimates

We first use 8-day Tmax data over the period 2001–2020 to represent the baseline climate and the period 2081–2100 to represent the future climate scenario. Then these temperature data are input into the response curves of Tmax and GPP established previously for each pixel. Next, we apply the constant Topt and our derived spatially explicit Topt values as Topt parameter in the response curves of Tmax and GPP, respectively. The constant Topt values are obtained from a lookup table established in previous studies21–23 (i.e., wheat 3.5, 21.0, 35.4 °C for Tmin Topt Tmax, rice 11.3, 26.3, 40.1 °C, maize 7.7, 30.5, 40.2 °C, soybean 6.0, 26.0, 39.0 °C). Finally, we calculate cumulative GPP for each crop during the growing seasons under various climate scenarios for each pixel, comparing estimates using constant versus spatially explicit Topt values. Grid-cell GPP is scaled by the respective crop planting area to ensure spatial representativeness100. We define the discrepancy between the two estimates as the bias inherent in traditional crop models that rely on static, constant Topt values. Because spatially explicit Topt accounts for local thermal adaptation and is therefore physiologically more realistic than a constant parameter, we treat the GPP estimates with spatially explicit Topt as the reference baseline for comparison. Accordingly, we use overestimation or underestimation to describe instances where the traditional crop model produces values higher or lower, respectively, than this adaptive baseline.
