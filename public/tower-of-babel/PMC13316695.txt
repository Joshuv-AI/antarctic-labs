Ultrasound-assisted water based sustainable extraction of potato peel extract for the development of potato starch based active edible films for the preservation of fresh-cut potatoes
Authors: Kiran Batool, Imran Pasha, Samran Khalid, Kashmala Chaudhary, Ahmed Fathy Ghazal, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing accumulation of plastic packaging waste and the limited shelf life of fresh-cut produce have created a need for sustainable packaging materials capable of simultaneously reducing environmental impacts and improving preservation of fresh-cut produce. To address this challenge, in this study we formulated biodegradable active films using potato starch (PS) added with potato peel extract (PPE) obtained via sustainable ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) with water (pulsed mode: 5 s on/5 s off; 300 W; 30 kHz; 25 min; 45 °C). PPE was incorporated into PS films at concentrations of 2–10% (w/w), and the resulting films were examined for mechanical, physical, optical, barrier, antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. SEM analysis demonstrated smoother, more compact surfaces with higher PPE content, while FTIR confirmed enhanced hydrogen bonding without altering the starch backbone. Films thickness increased from 0.01 ± 0.01 mm (T0) to 0.31 ± 0.01 mm (T5), whereas moisture content, water solubility, and water vapor permeability decreased significantly, indicating improved structural integrity and barrier performance. Color parameters and transparency showed dose-dependent changes due to phenolic compounds, while mechanical strength slightly decreased at higher extract concentrations. Antioxidant activity increased progressively, with TPC reaching 18.71 mg GAE/g, TFC 3.92 mg QE/g, and DPPH scavenging 40.81%. Films exhibited dose-dependent antimicrobial activity against S. aureus (2.09–19.13 mm), E. coli (1.91–31.34 mm), and S. enterica (1.45–21.61 mm). Biodegradability tests showed complete degradation of low-extract containing films by 15 days, while higher PPE containing films degraded nearly completely, contrasting with non-degradable polyethylene controls. Application on fresh-cut potatoes demonstrated that films with higher PPE concentrations (T4–T5) reduced visible browning and delayed spoilage-associated visual changes during 7 days of ambient storage. Overall, the results highlight the potential of using food crop-derived starch as a packaging material and food waste (potato peel) to develop active edible biodegradable films via sustainable technologies, combining environmental benefits with enhanced preservation of fresh-cut products.Keywords: Sustainable packaging; Non-thermal extraction; Fresh-cut produce; Antioxidant properties, Antimicrobial activity; Agro-food waste valorization

Introduction

Packaging plays a critical role in food preservation by ensuring containment, protection, and information delivery throughout storage and distribution (Mkhari et al., 2025). However, since the 1950s, petroleum-based plastics have dominated food packaging applications due to their low cost, durability, and design flexibility. Despite these advantages, their non-biodegradable nature has resulted in persistent environmental accumulation, microplastic contamination, and associated public health concerns (Asgher et al., 2020; Ncube et al., 2020). Currently, approximately 0.43 billion metric tons of plastic are produced annually worldwide, with nearly 36% allocated to food packaging applications (Barone et al., 2025; Khalid, Zahid, et al., 2024). The continuous increase in plastic production, coupled with global population growth, poses serious environmental sustainability challenges and directly conflicts with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) outlined by the United Nations (Shah et al., 2024).

In response, significant research efforts have focused on developing biodegradable biopolymer-based packaging materials as sustainable alternatives to conventional plastics (Shivangi et al., 2021). Beyond basic protective functions, modern food packaging systems are increasingly designed to actively enhance food quality and shelf stability (Khalid, Ali, et al., 2024; Modi et al., 2021; Nilsen-Nygaard et al., 2021). Within this context, edible films and coatings have emerged as promising eco-friendly solutions (Niu et al., 2024). These materials are commonly produced from naturally derived polymers such as polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids (Zhao et al., 2021). However, despite their biodegradability and safety, biopolymer-based edible films often exhibit limitations including poor mechanical strength, high moisture sensitivity, and limited intrinsic antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Huda et al., 2025). In parallel with these developments, active packaging systems have emerged as an advanced strategy to enhance food preservation performance. Unlike conventional passive materials, active packaging incorporates functional agents such as antioxidants and antimicrobials that interact with the food or its surrounding environment to retard oxidation, inhibit microbial growth, and extend shelf life (Khalid, Ali, et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2023). Incorporation of natural bioactive compounds into biodegradable polymer matrices represents a sustainable strategy to enhance film performance while maintaining environmental compatibility.

Among biodegradable polymers, potato starch (PS) has gained considerable attention due to its abundance, film-forming ability, edibility, and biodegradability (Fatima et al., 2024; Yassaroh et al., 2025). Nevertheless, native PS films generally suffer from structural fragility, moisture sensitivity, and limited functional activity, restricting their practical application in food systems The incorporation of plant-derived bioactive compounds has been proposed as an effective approach to improve mechanical, barrier, and bioactive properties of starch-based films (Rawat et al., 2024). Simultaneously, growing concerns regarding food waste have stimulated interest in valorizing agro-industrial by-products (Boateng & Clark, 2024; Rangaraj et al., 2021). Potato peel, a major by-product of potato processing industries, is generated in substantial quantities (70,000 to 140,000 tons) globally and is frequently discarded, contributing to environmental burden (Jimenez et al., 2023; Javed et al., 2019). Importantly, potato peels contain significantly higher concentrations of phenolic compounds compared to potato flesh, including chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, and caffeic acid, which exhibit strong antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Torres & Domínguez, 2020). Therefore, recovery and utilization of these bioactive compounds provide an opportunity to develop value-added functional materials while supporting circular economy principles (Salimi et al., 2025).

Efficient and sustainable extraction of bioactive compounds from potato peels is essential for their application in food systems. Conventional solvent extraction methods often involve organic solvents, extended processing times, and high energy consumption, limiting their environmental compatibility (Chanioti & Tzia, 2018). Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as an environmentally friendly non-thermal technology that enhances extraction efficiency through acoustic cavitation, leading to cell wall disruption and improved mass transfer (Pereira et al., 2025; Raza et al., 2025). It uses high-frequency sound waves (between 20 kHz and 100 kHz), which produce cavitation effects that disrupt the cell wall, allowing more compounds to move into the solvent and improving extraction efficiency (Alves Filho et al., 2021; Chaudhary, Khalid, Almasoud, et al., 2025; Chaudhary, Khalid, Alomar, et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2021). When combined with water as a green solvent, UAE offers a safe, residue-free, and food-compatible approach for recovering phenolic compounds. Moreover, its ability to penetrate plant tissues under the ultrasonic cavitation effect greatly improves extraction efficiency (Meena et al., 2024).

Several previous studies have investigated the incorporation of potato peel-derived compounds into starch-based films; however, important differences exist in extraction strategies, extract composition, polymer matrices, and targeted applications. Gebrechristos et al. (2020) prepared potato peel extracts (PPE) using ethanol and hot-water extraction methods and incorporated the extracts into PS films primarily to improve antioxidant properties and physicochemical characteristics of the films. Similarly, Lopes et al. (2021) recovered potato peel phenolics through Soxhlet extraction using n-hexane and ethanol at elevated temperatures and incorporated the extracts into starch-based films mainly to enhance antioxidant functionality. Moradi et al. (2023) employed UAE of potato peel using 75% ethanol and subsequently incorporated the extracts onto plasma-treated films to develop antimicrobial surfaces. More recently, Li et al. (2026) utilized UAE with ethanol to recover potato peel polyphenols and incorporated them into chemically modified starch-cellulose nanofiber composite films for structural and preservation-related applications. Although these studies demonstrated the potential of potato peel-derived bioactives in biodegradable packaging systems, most relied on organic solvents, chemically modified matrices, surface-coating strategies, or focused primarily on film characterization rather than direct same-commodity preservation applications.

Driven by the increasing demand for sustainable active packaging systems, the present study investigated a sustainable approach involving water-based UAE of PPE and its direct incorporation into PS-based edible films for fresh-cut potato preservation. Unlike conventional thermal or solvent-based extraction approaches, water-based UAE facilitates cavitation-assisted cell disruption, which can enhance the release and retention of phenolic compounds while minimizing thermal degradation and avoiding residual organic solvents. Preservation of these phenolic compounds is particularly important because their hydroxyl-rich structures contribute not only to antioxidant functionality but also to intermolecular interactions within the starch matrix. Specifically, phenolic compounds may interact with starch chains through hydrogen bonding, potentially improving extract compatibility, matrix homogeneity, and structural cohesion of the resulting films. These interactions may subsequently contribute to improved barrier properties, antioxidant functionality, and inhibition of oxidative browning reactions in fresh-cut potatoes during storage. Unlike previous studies primarily focused on general film characterization or unrelated food systems, the present work integrates potato peel waste recovery, green extraction technology, PS-based edible films development, and fresh-cut potato preservation within the same-commodity circular valorization framework. Therefore, we hypothesized that PPE obtained via UAE with water could be effectively incorporated into PS-based edible films to develop biodegradable active packaging materials. We further hypothesized that increasing PPE concentration would improve films functional performance and enhance the visual quality stability of fresh-cut potatoes during storage, particularly by delaying browning development and maintaining color attributes.

Materials and methodology

Raw materials procurement

Fully mature, fresh, and healthy potatoes (Solanum tuberosum L., cultivar: Desiree) were obtained at commercial harvest maturity from local farms in Faisalabad, Pakistan. Tubers were selected based on uniform size, smooth skin, firm texture, and absence of mechanical damage or visible disease to ensure suitability for PS extraction and preparation of fresh-cut potatoes. Potato peels generated during manual peeling were collected immediately and used for the preparation of PPE. All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Extraction of potato starch (PS)

PS was extracted using a physical water-based method, following the procedure described by Neeraj Siddiqui et al. (2021), as shown in Fig. 1. Fresh potatoes were washed, peeled and finely sliced. A total of 300 g of sliced potatoes was blended in a high-speed blender (HR3752/00, Amsterdam, Netherlands) together with 1 L of distilled water for 4 min, after which the blended mixture was passed through muslin cloth for filtration. The solid residue left on the muslin cloth was rinsed several times with distilled water. The collected filtrate was allowed to stand undisturbed for 2-3 h to allow starch sedimentation. The sediments were washed three to four times and sieved through muslin cloth until the rinsing water became completely clear. The purified starch was dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C, then ground, sieved, and stored in a sealed zipper bag. The extracted PS was derived from Solanum tuberosum (cv. Desiree), a cultivar reported to contain approximately 20–25% amylose, with the remainder being amylopectin, as documented in previous studies on PS of the same botanical origin (Kaur et al., 2025). The starch purity was ensured through repeated washing and sedimentation steps, minimizing residual proteins, lipids, and fibers, consistent with previously reported native PS preparations. The molecular weight distribution of amylose and amylopectin in native PS has been reported in the literature to be in the range of 105–106 Da for amylose and 107–108 Da for amylopectin, typically determined using size-exclusion chromatography or light scattering techniques (Kim & Kim, 2021). These characteristics are representative of native PS and were considered applicable to the PS used in this study.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) with water of potato peel extracts (PPE)

The collected potato peels were thoroughly rinsed under running water to eliminate dirt and any unwanted residues. The peels were cut into small pieces and immersed in a 0.2% sodium benzoate solution for about 10 min to minimize enzymatic browning. Subsequently, the peels were packed in a zip-lock bag and frozen at -80 °C for 24 h as a preparatory step for freeze-drying. Freeze-drying was carried out using a laboratory freeze dryer (STF-125; Stalwart Instruments, USA) operated at -74.5 °C for 8-12 h. The resulting freeze-dried peels were pulverized into a fine powder using a high-speed universal crusher (1200 W, 28000 rpm speed, WKS-20B, Zhengzhou, China). The freeze-dried peel powder was sealed in a zipper bag and stored at -20 °C in a freezer until further use. The UAE process was performed by using the procedure followed in our previous study (Chaudhary, Khalid, Almasoud, et al., 2025 and b). The suspension was made by combining the potato peel powder with distilled water as the solvent. A solute-to-solvent ratio of 1:30 was used, meaning 25 g of potato peel powder was mixed with 750 mL of distilled water. UAE was performed using a probe-type ultrasonicator (SCIENTZ-750F, Ningbo, China) set to a pulsating cycle (5 s on, 5 s off) at 300 W and 30 kHz for 25 min. Due to heat generation during ultrasonication (US), the beaker was partially immersed in an ice bath to dissipate excess heat, and the temperature was continuously monitored using a digital thermometer to maintain 40 ± 2 °C throughout the extraction. The resulting mixture obtained after the UAE with water process was filtered using Whatman No. 42 filter paper to separate out residual peel particles. The filtrate was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min using a refrigerated benchtop centrifuge (416KS, Sigma, Osterode, Germany), after which the supernatant was collected for analysis and stored under refrigeration for later use in film preparation (Fig. 1).

Characterization of potato peel extract (PPE)

The bioactive profile of PPE was assessed following the methods of García et al. (2020) with minor adjustments. Total phenolic content (TPC) was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu assay, while total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined through the aluminum chloride method. Antioxidant activity was evaluated following the procedure of Moradi et al. (2024) using DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay measured with a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Preparation of active edible films

PS based active edible films were prepared using solvent-casting method following the method of Nikmanesh et al. (2023) with slight modifications (Fig. 1). A total of 5 g of PS (5% w/v) was dispersed in 94 mL of distilled water and heated on a hot-plate magnetic stirrer at 80 °C with constant stirring (400 rpm) for 30 min to ensure complete gelatinization. When the PS solution reached a temperature of 40 °C, glycerol (1 g, 1% w/v of total solution, equivalent to 20% w/w of starch) was incorporated as a plasticizer to reduce intermolecular interactions among starch chains, enhance their mobility, and consequently improve the flexibility and stretchability of the films. For the production of PS-based active edible films, the PPE prepared using UAE was incorporated at five different concentrations (2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, 10%) by replacing the equivalent volume of distilled water in formulations with PPE. Thus, a total of five treatments (T1, T2, T3, T4 and T5) each having equal quantity of mass were produced along with control T0 without addition of PPE. The resulting mixtures were degassed by brief vacuum treatment to minimize entrapped air. The film forming mixtures then poured on to the levelled petri dishes and dried at 25 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity for 24 h in dust free environment. Then carefully peeled off the films from plates and conditioned at 25 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity for 48 h prior to characterization.

Characterization of active edible films

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface microstructure of the prepared PS based active edible films incorporated with PPE was examined using a scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM-6100, Tokyo, Japan). Before analysis, the films were trimmed into small sections (around 5 × 5 mm2) and affixed onto aluminum stubs using double-sided conductive tape. The samples were subsequently sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to improve surface conductivity. Micrographs were obtained using an accelerating voltage of 15 kV in low-vacuum mode to observe surface morphology and structural integrity.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The chemical composition of the films was examined using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Agilent Cary 630, Santa Clara, USA) fitted with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) unit. Film specimens (∼1 × 1 cm2) were positioned directly onto the ATR crystal and gently pressed using a pressure anvil to ensure proper contact. Spectra were collected in transmittance mode across a wavenumber range of 4000–650 cm-1.

Thickness

Films thickness was determined using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo, Tokyo, Japan) with the least count of 0.001 mm following the method of Otero-Herrera et al. (2025). Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated, and zero error was checked. The average thickness of the films was determined by measuring five different points on each film.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the films, including tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB), were determined using a (TST-101., Shandong, China) according to the method of Castañón Vilca et al. (2023). The film pieces were divided into dimensions of 150 × 15 mm for the determination of mechanical strength. The film samples were positioned between the machine clamps, ensuring proper horizontal and vertical alignment. The instrument speed was adjusted to 25 mm/min. Once the process for determining the tensile properties began, it led to the stretching and breaking of the films from the middle. The readings of TS and EAB were displayed on the screen.

The moisture content of the films was measured using a hot air oven according to the methods of Pérez-Córdoba et al. (2024). WVP of the films was assessed following the gravimetric method described by Khedri et al. (2021) with slight modifications. Square film pieces (2 × 2 cm2) were fixed over the openings of test tubes (1.5 cm diameters), each containing 15 mL distilled water. Color parameters

The colorimetric properties of the films were evaluated using a portable colorimeter (Konica Minolta CR-5, Tokyo, Japan) in reflectance mode in accordance with the specified method of da Nóbrega Santos et al. (2022) to determine the color values of films; they were placed against a white paper that served as background. To avoid errors in the measurements, the instrument was placed in a static position. By using this method, the L*, a* and b* values were determined. Here, L* represented the lightness or brightness (L* = 100 denoted white and L* = 0 indicated black), while a* values - meant green and + meant red. Similarly, for b* values - meant blue and + meant yellow.

Transparency

A UV–Visible spectrophotometer (UV-1800 Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to assess films' transparency according to the procedure outlined by Singh et al. (2020), with slight alterations. The film samples were cut into rectangular strips measuring 2 cm × 6 cm and placed on the inner surface of a quartz cuvette. A cuvette cell without any film was used as the reference. The absorbance of each sample was then measured at a wavelength of 600 nm.

Antioxidant properties

Total phenolic content (TPC)

TPC of the films was evaluated using the procedure described by Moghadam et al. (2020) using Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Film samples (125 mg) were soaked in 15 mL of distilled water and agitated at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting extracts were then centrifuged at 4,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was used for further analysis. For TPC determination, 0.1 mL of the extract was combined with 0.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (previously diluted 1:10 with distilled water) and 6 mL of distilled water. After allowing the mixture to stand for 8 min, 1.5 mL of 10% (w/v) sodium carbonate was added, and the volume was made up to 10 mL with distilled water. The prepared solution was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 h, followed by measurement of absorbance at 765 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

TFC of the film was assessed utilizing the method of Sutharsan et al. (2023), the same aqueous film extract prepared for TPC determination (125 mg film in 15 mL distilled water) was used for TFC analysis. In each test tube, 1.25 mL of distilled water was combined with 0.25 mL of the extract, after which 0.75 mL of a 5% (w/v) sodium nitrite solution was added. The mixture was kept at room temperature for 6 min, followed by the addition of 150 μL of 10% (w/v) aluminum chloride. After another 5 min, 0.5 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide was introduced, and the total volume was brought up to 2.5 mL with distilled water. Absorbance was read at 510 nm, and TFC was determined using a quercetin calibration curve prepared from standard solutions of known concentrations.

DPPH radical scavenging activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity of the film was evaluated following the methodology of Bhatia et al. (2025) with minor alterations. Film extracts (125 mg in 15 mL distilled water, 24 h) were centrifuged and clarified prior to assay. A 1.0 mL aliquot of 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol was mixed with 1.0 mL of film extract, and methanol was added to a final reaction volume of 3.0 mL. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was read at 517 nm.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the films against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Salmonella enterica was evaluated using the disc diffusion (inhibition zone) method following Chavez-Marquez et al. (2023). Bacterial strains were cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in lysogeny broth and tryptic soy broth and adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard (∼1–2 × 108 CFU/mL). Solidified Mueller–Hinton agar plates were inoculated by evenly spreading 0.1 mL of each bacterial suspension and allowed to absorb for 5–10 min at room temperature. Film discs (10 mm diameter) were aseptically placed onto the agar surface (two discs per plate). The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Antimicrobial activity was assessed by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones surrounding each disc with a sliding caliper.

Films biodegradability assessment

The biodegradability of films was assessed by using the soil burial method as described by Taktak and Kaya (2025). Film samples (2 × 2 cm2) were buried at a depth of 6 cm in pots filled with natural loamy soil obtained from Faisalabad, Punjab, Pakistan. The soil properties were as follows (pH ∼7.5, organic matter content ∼1.5%, moisture content ∼65% of field capacity, and temperature ∼ 25 ± 2 °C). Soil moisture was maintained by spraying water twice daily. Samples were gently retrieved every 5 days, cleaned to remove any attached soil, and assessed for biodegradation by monitoring visual changes.

Application of active edible films on fresh-cut potatoes

The preservation efficiency of the developed PS-based active edible films was evaluated on fresh-cut potato cubes. Fresh, mature potatoes were peeled and uniformly cut into cubes. One cube was left unwrapped as a control, while the remaining cubes were wrapped with different film formulations (T0–T5). All samples were stored in a controlled environmental chamber maintained at 25 ± 1 °C temperature and 60 ± 5% relative humidity for seven days. Temperature and relative humidity were continuously monitored using a calibrated digital thermo-hygrometer to ensure minimal environmental fluctuations during storage. Prior to the experiment, the thermo-hygrometer was verified using standard laboratory calibration procedures to ensure accuracy of temperature and humidity measurements. Storage conditions were checked regularly throughout the experimental period, and the monitored values remained within 24.3–25.8 °C and 57–63% relative humidity, indicating stable environmental conditions during storage. During the storage period, samples were periodically examined independently by three researchers for qualitative visual changes, specifically visible browning, surface dehydration, and spoilage-associated appearance changes, to comparatively assess the preservation performance of each film formulation. No formal sensory panel evaluation, sensory scoring system, inter-observer statistical agreement analysis, or instrumental odor/color measurements were performed, as the storage study was intended only as a preliminary qualitative proof-of-concept assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to determine significant differences among means, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to analyze the relationship and differences between PS-based active edible films incorporated with different PPE concentrations (T0, T1, T2, T3, T4, and T5) using Simca Statistical Software (Version 14.1, Simca, MKS Umetrics AB). Pearson's correlation matrix was used to evaluate the correlation between the physical, optical, mechanical, barrier, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties of PS-based active edible films, and the addition of PPE concentrations. All experiments were conducted in triplicate and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Characterization of potato peel extract (PPE)

Bioactive extracts derived from natural materials play a key role in developing active packaging. PPE is rich in various functional compounds, such as phenolic acids (including chlorogenic, gallic, and caffeic acids), flavonoids, glycoalkaloids like α-solanine and α-chaconine, and carotenoids, which collectively contribute to its strong antioxidant and antimicrobial effects (Jimenez-Champi et al., 2023). The strong antioxidant and antimicrobial properties of phenolics and flavonoids originate from their capacity to release hydrogen atoms and scavenge free radicals (Singh, Kim, & Lee, 2022). PPE obtained via UAE using water as a solvent showed higher TPC and TFC values, 8.72 ± 0.04 mg GAE/g and 3.59 ± 0.07 mg QE/g, respectively. These findings emphasize the strong antioxidant potential of PPE as well as the importance of selecting an appropriate extraction method and solvent. Similar extraction trends were observed in previous studies, with 2.19 mg of phenolic compounds per g of dry PPE achieved through UAE with water as a solvent (Mortazavi & Khodabakhshian, 2025) and 3.68 mg QE/g for TFC reported by Teklehaymanot et al. (2024). The high extraction yield is likely due to the effect of ultrasonication, by weakening the cell walls and increasing solvent infiltration, it promotes the extraction of phenolic compounds that are otherwise bound within the plant tissue (Xu et al., 2022). Studies of Santos et al. (2024) and Giri et al. (2024) also align with our findings, demonstrating the remarkable effectiveness of UAE when employing environmentally friendly solvents. They observed a TPC of 6.87 mg GAE/g in dragon fruit peels and TFC 1.97 mg QE/g dry weight from persimmon fruit peel, confirming the reproducibility and efficiency of UAE. Moreover, the TPC yield achieved via UAE was greater than that achieved through conventional solvent extraction (6.26 mg GAE/g), as reported by Wang et al. (2020), indicating that this approach achieved higher efficiency and greater sustainability than conventional extraction techniques.

DPPH scavenging activity of PPE was recorded as 83.41 ± 0.18%, reflecting its high phenolic and flavonoid content. This demonstrates a strong radical-scavenging ability, confirming the effective antioxidant potential of the PPE obtained through ultrasonic extraction. The data correspond closely with what has been observed by Saeed et al. (2022), who reported a DPPH scavenging activity of 82.16% in PPE. Similarly, Kumari et al. (2017) also got a similar value of DPPH of 85.1% for the extract obtained from the peels of the Lady Rosetta cultivar of potato peel through UAE.

Characterization of active edible films

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology and structural details of the films were analyzed using SEM, with results presented in Fig. 2(A). SEM provided the detailed insight into the surface texture, porosity, and homogeneity of films at different magnifications. Low magnification image (500 μm) highlighted the macro-defects and pore distribution, while higher magnifications (50 μm) exhibited micro-roughness, particle domains, and surface continuity. In the control film (T0), large pores and surface irregularities were clearly visible at 500 μm, indicating a less compact and non-uniform matrix. However, as PPE was progressively incorporated from T1 to T5, the films surface became progressively smoother and more uniform, as can be seen in 50 μm images. The films with higher extract concentrations (T4 to T5) exhibited minimal cracks and pores, which suggests that the compatibility was improved and there was a better dispersion of phenolic compounds within the starch matrix. The smoother and denser surface of films with higher PPE concentrations is likely the result of interactions between starch and the phenolic and flavonoid constituents present in the extract via hydrogen bonding, which improves compatibility and reduces pores and cracks (Santhosh et al., 2024). These observations are consistent with the outcomes of Chen et al. (2023), where higher concentrations of tea polyphenols in starch-based films promoted surface cross-linking, resulting in more compact and uniform film morphology. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2024) reported that SEM micrographs of composite films prepared from sweet-PS and polyphenol extracts from sweet-potato peel showed that increasing polyphenol content resulted in denser, less-porous surfaces, along with a noticeable reduction in visible cracks.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy allows the investigation of molecular bonds and interactions in biopolymer films by detecting the absorption of infrared radiation at characteristic frequencies (Mileti et al., 2024). In this research, the FTIR spectra of films from T0 to T5 were recorded to examine the structural interaction between PS and PPE, as shown in Fig. 2(B). The control film (T0) showed a broad absorption band at approximately 3276–3278 cm-1, associated with the stretching of hydroxyl (-OH) groups, suggesting hydrogen bonding between starch molecules. Peaks observed around 2924–2927 cm-1 are linked to C—H stretching of CH₂ groups, whereas the band at 1647–1653 cm-1 corresponds to bending vibrations of water or carbonyl stretching from amide or phenolic compounds. A clear band near 1202–1207 cm-1 indicates C-O-C stretching, and the sharp peak at 995–997 cm-1 is characteristic of C—O vibrations in the polysaccharide backbone. Thickness

Film thickness plays a crucial role in determining mechanical strength, protective capabilities, and overall performance in food-related uses (Lingait et al., 2024). Thicker films generally offer improved mechanical strength and barrier properties; however, they may compromise flexibility and transparency, which are crucial for product visibility and consumer acceptance. Conversely, thinner films enhance transparency and flexibility but may exhibit reduced mechanical integrity and barrier efficiency. Therefore, the optimal film thickness should balance these properties to ensure sufficient environmental protection while maintaining product palatability and desirable mouthfeel (Khalid, Zahid, et al., 2024). Film thickness varied between 0.01 ± 0.01 mm and 0.31 ± 0.01 mm, with the control film (T0) having the thinnest measurement (0.01 ± 0.01 mm) and T5 displaying the greatest thickness (0.31 ± 0.01 mm) (Fig. 3(A)). The primary factor controlling film thickness is the solid content of the formulation, mainly starch in this study. Because the starch concentration did not change, the thicker films observed with added extracts are probably a result of higher solid content and increased viscosity in the solution used to cast the films. The thickness of the films produced in this study falls within the ranges previously reported for enriched PS films. Guo et al. (2022), observed thickness values between 0.09 and 0.21 mm in plasma-treated PS films containing cinnamon essential oil. Likewise, Pérez-Córdoba et al. (2024) noted that incorporating copaiba leaf extract resulted in PS films with thicknesses of 0.10–0.19 mm, with the measurements increasing in relation to the amount of extract added.

Mechanical properties

Packaging materials developed from biopolymers are widely recognized as a more sustainable option compared to traditional plastics, yet their mechanical properties require further improvement for successful applications in the food industry (Taktak & Kaya, 2025). The film's durability against handling, tension, and stretching during storage and transit is evaluated through metrics such as TS and EAB. TS describes the maximum force a film can withstand before it ruptures, showing how well it resists deformation under load (Shah et al., 2023). In contrast, EAB expresses the extent to which a film can be stretched relative to its initial length before tearing, serving as an indicator of its elasticity and overall flexibility (Elvira López-Caballero et al., 2024). Fig. 3(B) illustrates that the control film (T0) displayed the greatest TS among all formulations (4.39 ± 0.19 MPa), which gradually decreased with the increasing concentrations of PPE and reached the lowest value of 3.29 ± 0.06 MPa in T5. This decline could result from the disturbance of intermolecular forces among starch molecules, caused by the incorporation of extract, which interferes with the compactness of the polymer matrix (Kola & Carvalho, 2023). Similarly, EAB also showed a decreasing trend, where the control film (T0) had the maximum value (62.56 ± 0.34%) while the lowest flexibility was observed in T5 (54.29 ± 0.13) Fig. 3(C). The decrease in EAB indicates that higher extract concentrations restricted the starch chain mobility, which resulted in a reduced capacity of the film to stretch before failure (Tian et al., 2024). These findings suggest that although the PPE-enriched films exhibited enhanced bioactive potential, a higher concentration of extract adversely affected both TS and EAB. Comparable trends have been observed by other researchers. For instance, Aydin and Yildiz (2022) observed that addition of Hibiscus sabdariffa L. extract into corn starch-based nanocomposite films reduced the TS from 80.88 MPa (control) to 65.51 MPa at the highest extract concentration. Likewise, in chitosan/gelatin/starch-based films, the addition of up to 30% pineapple peel extract reduced the TS from 8.15 MPa to 2.89 MPa and EAB from 89% to 32%. This decrease was attributed to sugars (e.g., glucose and fructose) in the extract, which function as plasticizers and consequently lower the mechanical strength of the films (Gürler, 2023). Similarly, Erma et al. (2022) reported that incorporating young coconut leaf extract into chitosan films caused the EAB to drop from 6.02% to 3.22%.

Moisture content

Moisture content reflects the water present in the film matrix and plays a crucial role in determining the film's stability, structural integrity, mechanical properties, barrier efficiency, and antimicrobial effectiveness (Shanbhag et al., 2023). Excessive moisture in films can promote microbiological growth, cause matrix plasticization and reduce tensile strength (Braun et al., 2021). Therefore, reducing moisture content enhances performance and shelf stability during storage, particularly for applications involving food packaging. In this study, a higher moisture content (23.44 ± 0.33%) was observed in control PS film (T0), this occurs because starch naturally attracts and retains water due to its hydrophilic characteristics. Conversely, moisture content showed a statistically significant variation (p < 0.05) among films containing different concentrations of PPE from (2%-10%). The moisture content decreased from 23.44 ± 0.33% in T0 to 15.38 ± 0.16% in T5, containing the highest PPE concentration (10%) (Fig. 3(D)). This decrease probably happened because of the phenolic compounds present in the extract that enhanced the hydrophobicity of the polymeric matrix, thereby limiting water retention. The polyphenol-starch interactions form a denser matrix within the film's structure, improved molecular packing and reduced water absorption capacity (Shahidi & Athiyappan, 2025). The present results reflect trends reported in earlier work, where Khaledian et al. (2024) reported that incorporating anthocyanins extracted from turnip peel into PS based nanofiber mat significantly reduced the moisture content (4.11-6.31%), considerably lower than the control containing only PS. In another study, Freitas et al. (2023) reported a comparable decrease in moisture content (from 8.3% to 7.8%) when rice straw extract and cellulose fibers were incorporated into corn starch-based films. The authors suggested that the films experienced a reduction in active water adsorption sites due to enhanced internal hydrogen bonding within the matrix. These findings collectively support the present results, indicating that incorporation of PPE not only improved the functional bioactivity of PS films but also improved the structural integrality and resistance to moisture absorption.

Water solubility

Water solubility represents the segment of the film material that can dissolve in water and is commonly employed to determine the resistance of the films to moisture and their stability in a watery medium (Rangaraj et al., 2021). It is an important parameter for evaluating durability of biodegradable films under humid circumstances. Lower water solubility reflects higher water resistance, which helps to retain the stable structure of films while handling and storing. Moreover, reduced water solubility enhances the moisture barrier capacity of films, thereby extending the storage life of packaged goods (Singh, Bangar, et al., 2022). In the present study, the control film (T0) exhibited the highest solubility value (20.89 ± 0.82%), whereas the incorporation of PPE significantly (p < 0.05) decreased the solubility, reaching the lowest value of 12.74 ± 0.11% in T5 (Fig. 3(E)). This was probably because the bioactive compounds like phenolics and flavonoids present in the extract and their interaction with starch network, that probably reduce their affinity for water (Gebrechristos et al., 2020). It is likely that these compounds established hydrogen bonds with starch, limiting the availability of hydrophilic sites for water interaction. A comparable pattern was reported by Leon-Bejarano et al. (2020) where the solubility of modified starch films decreased from 20.27% to 17.08% with increasing concentrations of nut by-product extracts. Similarly, Miao et al. (2021) reported that incorporating tea polyphenols reduced the solubility of PS and corn starch films to 15.15% and 16.13% respectively. The researchers reported that polyphenols interacting with starch limited the number of hydroxyl sites that could bind water, which led to increased water resistance. Consistent with these observations, Torche et al. (2025) also demonstrated a slight but noticeable decline in solubility from 25.2 to 24.1% upon the inclusion of onion peel powder to cassava starch-based biocomposite films. These observations support our results, indicating that the addition of phenolic-rich plant extracts can strengthen starch-based biodegradable films by improving their water repellency and structural stability.

Water vapour permeability (WVP)

WVP coefficient of a film represents the rate of moisture transmission through the material under specific temperature conditions. The ability of a material to allow substances to pass through depends on its molecular structure, the characteristics of the permeating agent, and the surrounding temperature conditions (Shivangi et al., 2021). Lower WVP values imply superior moisture barrier properties essential for food preservation by preventing undesired moisture absorption or loss (Santhosh et al., 2024). Shown in the Fig. 3(F), the incorporation of varying concentrations of PPE significantly (p < 0.05) affected the WVP of the resulting films. The control film (T0), prepared without PPE, exhibited the highest WVP value (0.82 ± 0.01 g mm/m2·h·kPa). With the progressive increase in PPE (from T1 to T5), the films demonstrated a consistent decline in WVP, ranging from 0.65 ± 0.01 g mm/m2·h·kPa to 0.31 ± 0.01 g mm/m2·h·kPa, respectively. This decline in permeability can be linked to the bioactive compounds in PPE, such as phenolics and flavonoids, which may serve as natural agents for cross-linking. By strengthening the hydrogen bonds within the starch network, these compounds help form a tighter, more organized film matrix that hinders the movement of water vapor (Pan et al., 2024). Similar trends have been reported in starch-based films enriched with papaya leaf extract, WVP dropped notably from 5.75 × 10-8 to 2.52 × 10-8 g·m-1·s-1·Pa-1 (Yin Feng et al., 2025). Similarly, Luo et al. (2022) also demonstrated a decrease in WVP from 17.32 to 9.07 × 10-11 g∙m-1∙s-1∙Pa-1 in PS films upon the addition of tea polyphenols and MgO nanoparticles. Reductions in WVP have also been documented in cassava starch films containing basil and green tea extracts (Medina-Jaramillo et al., 2017). Conversely, some studies have reported opposite effects. Bodana et al. (2024) found that WVP increased in jackfruit seed starch films as the concentration of pomegranate peel extract rose, ranging from 1.31 × 10-6 to 2.82 × 10-6 g·h-1·Pa-1. Nandi and Guha (2024) observed that incorporating polyphenols extracted from Piper betle L. waste into starch-based films led to an increase in WVP, ranging from 1.89 × 10-7 to 2.84 × 10-7 g·mm/m2·Pa·s as the polyphenol concentration was raised. Taken together, the results suggest that the impact of plant extracts on WVP varies according to the type of extract and its interaction with the polymer network. In this study, the observed steady decrease in WVP demonstrates that PPE significantly enhanced the moisture barrier of PS films, improving their applicability as materials for food packaging.

Color parameters

Color is a very important parameter of edible films because it directly influences customers' perception and product acceptance. Variations in lightness (L*) or hue (a*, b*) influence visual transparency, the impression of cleanliness, and overall acceptance of wrapped foods at the marketplace (Rangaraj et al., 2021). Generally, brighter and transparent films are preferred over darker ones. However, a slight color shift is expected when active extracts are incorporated, as these extracts usually carry pigments and phenolic compounds. The addition of PPE into PS-based films caused highly significant (p < 0.05) changes in all three color coordinates (L*, a* and b*) as shown in Fig. 4. The L* decreased gradually from 91.63 ± 0.07 in T0 to 72.67 ± 0.68 in T5, indicating a clear darkening of films with increasing PPE concentration. Concurrently, a* value increased from 0.15 ± 0.02 in T0 to 1.49 ± 0.01 in T5, reflecting a small but consistent shift towards red, while b* value rose from 1.72 ± 0.01 in T0 to 6.11 ± 0.51 in T5, showing a marked increase in yellowness. These variations suggest that even small additions of PPE can significantly alter the visual brightness and hue of the films. Comparable color modifications have been reported in previous studies where plant-derived extracts were incorporated into biopolymer films. For instance, de Fernandes et al. (2025) reported a similar decline in L* values from 95.01 ± 0.64 in the control to 88.95 ± 1.00 at the highest concentration of cashew apple bioactive compounds. Likewise, Mileti et al. (2024) observed a progressive reduction in L* value from 89.7 ± 0.1 in the control film to 50.8 ± 0.5 in the film containing the highest concentration of PPE. This decrease in lightness indicates that the incorporation of potato peel components imparted a darker appearance to the films, likely due to the presence of natural pigments and phenolic compounds. Similarly, Chen et al. (2023) reported a shift in a* value from -0.23 ± 0.01 in control to 1.56 ± 0.03 with increasing tea polyphenols concentration in PS-based films. Furthermore, Lafeuillee and Maharaj (2025) demonstrated an increase in b* values from -1.09 ± 0.01 to 4.93 ± 0.16 as the concentration of Panadol leaf extract rose, consistent with the yellowness enhancement observed in the present study.

Transparency

The transparency of packaging films is a key optical characteristic, as it reflects how much ultraviolet and visible light can transmit through the material (Shanbhag et al., 2023). As indicated in Fig. 4(D), absorbance measurements at 600 nm revealed that the control film (T0) exhibited the highest transparency (91.18 ± 0.17%), which progressively decreased with higher concentrations of PPE, reaching a minimum of 49.31 ± 0.12% in T5. This trend demonstrates a clear dose-dependent reduction in UV light transmission, suggesting that adding PPE significantly (p < 0.05) increased the films' opacity. The transparency decreased due to the presence of phenolic-rich compounds and natural pigments the extract contains which can absorb and scatter the light when embedded into the starch matrix. Comparable findings have been reported by Zhang et al. (2024), where the transparency of sweet PS films declined sharply from 92.04% in the control to 19.41% upon incorporating higher concentrations of sweet potato peel polyphenols at 600 nm. This demonstrates a similar light-blocking effect of phenolic-rich materials within starch matrices. Likewise, Nikmanesh et al. (2023) recorded a marked decrease in transparency, from 75.24% to 17.87% as the Viola odorata extract concentration increased, highlighting that polyphenolic compounds tend to reduce optical clarity through enhanced light absorption and scattering. Although reduced transparency may not be ideal for applications requiring clear or visually transparent films, it can be advantageous for packaging light-sensitive foods by minimizing photooxidation and preserving product quality during storage (Huda et al., 2025).

Antioxidant properties

TPC represents the level of phenolic compounds, which are secondary plant metabolites with key biological functions, notably serving as antioxidants that counteract free radical activity and protect cells from oxidative stress. Therefore, extracts with high TPC provide both antioxidant and antimicrobial effects and enhanced functional properties of edible films (Singh, Bangar, et al., 2022). A clear and consistent upward trend in TPC was observed in films with increasing concentrations of PPE. The control film (T0) had no measurable TPC, whereas TPC values rose significantly (p < 0.05) from 10.74 ± 0.09 in T1 to 18.71 ± 0.029 mg GAE/g in T5 (Fig. 5(A)). This notable increase appears to result from the even dispersion and strong retention of phenolics within the starch matrix, demonstrating that PPE was effectively incorporated into the film. Comparable findings were reported by Gebrechristos et al. (2020), who observed a TPC of 15.21 mg GAE/g in starch-based films incorporated with 10% PPE obtained using ethanol extraction. In comparison, the films developed in the present study achieved higher phenolic retention (18.71 ± 0.02 mg GAE/g at 10% PPE) when extracted through UAE, suggesting that this technique facilitates better recovery and stability of phenolics during film formation. Likewise, Esfahani et al. (2022) documented a steady rise in TPC values (4-12 mg GAE/g) as the concentration of pomegranate peel extract increased from 2% to 8%, demonstrating that phenolic-enriched films exhibit enhanced antioxidant functionality regardless of the plant source.

As a major category of polyphenols, flavonoids exhibit strong antioxidant effects, which significantly contribute to the functional performance and protective capabilities of active edible films (Singh, Kim, & Lee, 2022). Quantifying TFC is essential for evaluating a film's capacity to neutralize reactive oxygen species and to inhibit lipid peroxidation in packaged foods (Matloob et al., 2023). The TFC values presented in (Fig. 5(B)) showed a continuous increase from 0.81 ± 0.05 mg QE/g in T1 to 3.92 ± 0.04 mg QE/g in T5, confirming the dose-dependent enrichment of flavonoids through PPE incorporation. The pronounced increase from T0 (0 mg QE/g) to T1 (0.81 ± 0.05 mg QE/g) highlights the strong flavonoid potential of PPE even at the lowest inclusion level (2%), while the more moderate increment between T4 (3.32 ± 0.04 mg QE/g) and T5 (3.92 ± 0.04 mg QE/g) suggests a saturation effect, possibly caused by matrix entrapment or limited binding capacity of flavonoids within the starch network (Wang et al., 2023). Consistent results have been documented by Erma et al. (2022), which reported similar TFC up to 3.9 mg QE/g upon the addition of coconut leaf extract, confirming that phenolic-rich natural sources effectively elevate flavonoid content in edible films. Similarly, Huang et al. (2024), achieved a TPC of 2.63 mg QE/g upon incorporating the Premna microphylla Turcz leaves extract, further validating that phenolic and flavonoid compounds can be efficiently retained in polysaccharide-based film matrices.

To evaluate the films' antioxidant properties, the DPPH radical scavenging assay was employed, providing insight into their capacity to reduce free radical activity. The assay is based on the ability of antioxidants in the film to convert the stable violet-colored DPPH radical into a colorless or yellow form (Ezati et al., 2022). As anticipated, the control film (T0) showed an absence of antioxidant activity, whereas films containing PPE displayed significantly (p < 0.05) higher antioxidant potential, with DPPH inhibition values increasing progressively from 32.55 ± 0.08% (T1) to 40.81 ± 0.13% (T5) (Fig. 5(C)). This gradual enhancement underscores the positive influence of PPE concentration on the free radical scavenging capacity of the films. Consistent results have been documented in related research. Miao et al. (2021), recorded an increase in DPPH activity, achieving 86% scavenging in films containing tea polyphenols compared to only 1.5% in the control. Similarly, Esfahani et al. (2022) observed a DPPH increase from 24% to 70% in cassava starch-based films as pomegranate peel extract concentration rose from 2 to 8%. Furthermore, Gürler (2023) reported a significant increase in DPPH scavenging activity, rising from 61% in the control films to between 75% and 94% as the concentration of pineapple peel extract was raised from 10% to 30% in biopolymer based films. Overall, these results show that adding PPE resulted in a substantial rise in the films' phenolic and flavonoid levels, thereby boosting their antioxidant capacity. The dose-dependent rise in TPC, TFC, and DPPH confirms the strong antioxidant nature of PPE and its stable interaction within the starch matrix.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of PPE-incorporated PS-based films was evaluated against Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica) bacteria by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones presented in Fig. 6. These strains were selected as they represent common foodborne spoilage and pathogenic microbes frequently associated with food contamination. For the control film (T0), antimicrobial activity was very low, with measured inhibition zones of 2.09 ± 0.15 mm against S. aureus, 1.91 ± 0.13 mm against E. coli, and 1.45 ± 0.19 mm against S. enterica. Incorporation of PPE resulted in a clear dose-dependent enhancement of antimicrobial properties across all tested strains. In the case of S. aureus, the inhibition zone expanded progressively with increasing PPE concentrations, reaching a maximum of 19.13 ± 0.07 mm in T5. For E. coli, the zone measured 31.34 ± 0.10 mm, and for S. enterica, it was 21.61 ± 0.20 mm. These observations demonstrate that the incorporation of PPE markedly (p < 0.05) boosts the antimicrobial properties of PS films, underscoring their potential in active packaging of food products. The overall antimicrobial effectiveness of the films followed the order E. coli > S. enterica > S. aureus. Differences in bacterial response are probably due to structural distinctions in their cell walls. The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli and S. enterica, composed largely of lipopolysaccharides. They can be compromised by phenolic compounds, leading to increased membrane permeability and cell leakage. Conversely, the thicker peptidoglycan layer in Gram-positive S. aureus provides a stronger defense, reducing the ability of phenolics to penetrate and exert antimicrobial effects, which explains the comparatively smaller inhibition observed (Lobiuc et al., 2023; Takó et al., 2020). Furthermore, the concentration-dependent increase in inhibition zone diameters also indicates that higher PPE levels enhanced the diffusion and sustained release of phenolics from the film matrix, improving antimicrobial efficacy (Thi Nguyen et al., 2022). These results are consistent with those reported by Castañón Vilca et al. (2023), where PS films enriched with natural compounds such as glycerol, propolis, and montmorillonite exhibited a 17 mm inhibition zone against S. aureus. In a comparable study, starch nanocomposite films enriched with essential oils demonstrated antimicrobial activity, producing inhibition zones of 18-40 mm for E. coli and 28-34 mm for S. aureus (Raigond et al., 2019). Likewise, Ramakrishnan et al. (2023) demonstrated that adding grapefruit seed extract to alginate-CMC-starch films produced inhibition zones of 18 mm for S. aureus and 10 mm for E. coli, whereas the control film exhibited no antimicrobial effect. Syafiq et al. (2020) reported that starch-based films incorporated with cinnamon essential oil exhibited inhibition zones against S. enterica ranging from 1.9 mm to 15.5 mm, further confirming the potential of plant-derived extracts as natural antimicrobial agents. Another recent study reported that incorporation of plant extracts along with in-situ synthesized silver nanoparticles into chitosan/PVA films significantly improved antimicrobial efficacy and preservation performance in meat systems (Hunashyal et al., 2025). Overall, the results show that adding PPE significantly enhanced the films' antimicrobial activity, mainly due to its phenolic compounds that work together to inhibit and suppress bacterial growth.

Biodegradability

A preliminary qualitative biodegradability test was conducted to determine the environmental sustainability of the prepared plain PS film (control) and PS-based active edible films containing PPE (2–10%), using commercial polyethylene film as a non-biodegradable control. The biodegradability of the developed films was evaluated through controlled soil burial tests. The overall experimental workflow, including film preparation, cutting, and subsequent burial under standardized soil conditions. This visual sequence shows how the films were exposed to natural microbial and environmental factors to assess their degradation behavior (Fig. 7(A)). The film samples could not be weighed during the course of the analysis because the attached soil particles might have caused the inaccurate measurements. Fig. 7(B) illustrates the film samples before burial and after 5, 10, 15 and 20 days of soil degradation. By Day 5, noticeable physical changes such as surface roughening, edge disintegration, and minor fragmentation were evident in all PS-based films, whereas the polyethylene control remained intact. These early alterations indicate the initiation of microbial and enzymatic degradation of the starch matrix, which serves as a carbon and energy source for soil microorganisms (Bher et al., 2022). By Day 10, biodegradation became more pronounced, particularly in T0, T1, and T2, which exhibited substantial fragmentation and softening. Films with higher PPE concentrations (T3–T5) degraded at a comparatively slower rate. The comparatively delayed breakdown of the films is likely linked to the presence of phenolic and flavonoid constituents in the PPE, which may enhance intermolecular interactions like hydrogen bonding or cross-linking within the polymer network, creating a denser structure that resists microbial breakdown (Singh et al., 2022). After 15 days, T0, T1, and T2 showed nearly complete visible disintegration, while T3–T5 exhibited only small residual fragments. However, by Day 20, T3, T4 and T5 also underwent complete visible structural disintegration under soil burial conditions, whereas the polyethylene control remained visually unchanged throughout the experimental period. These observations qualitatively indicate the biodegradable nature of the developed PS-based films under the tested soil burial conditions, with PPE concentration slightly influencing the degradation behavior. The comparatively slower degradation observed in films containing higher PPE concentrations may be associated with enhanced intermolecular interactions between phenolic compounds and the polymer matrix, potentially resulting in a relatively denser structure that temporarily resists microbial breakdown. Similar observations were made by Ramakrishnan et al. (2023), who assessed the breakdown of starch–cellulose composite films enriched with grapefruit seed extract after placing them in soil for a 21-day period. The researchers observed steady weight loss and visible disintegration of films at 7, 14 and 21 days, confirming their effective biodegradability. On the contrary, Nandi and Guha (2024) observed significant biodegradation of starch films containing Piper betle L. waste extract after 21 days of burial in soil environment. The study revealed that films containing the extract degraded more rapidly than the control, indicating that the addition of natural extract can accelerate the biodegradation process. In another related work, Lingait et al. (2024), examined biodegradability of pectin/PVA films reinforced with natural biopolymer sporopollenin and achieved a complete degradation within 30 days of soil burial. These findings collectively support that the addition of plant-derived bioactive components influence degradation behavior of polymeric films, aligning well with the results of the present study. Application of active edible films on fresh-cut potatoes

The application of the developed films was evaluated on fresh-cut potatoes to qualitatively assess their ability to maintain visual quality and delay visible enzymatic browning during seven days of ambient storage (25 °C, 60% RH). Storage conditions remained stable throughout the experimental period, as confirmed by continuous thermo-hygrometer monitoring. The experimental outcomes are displayed in Fig. 8. The unwrapped potatoes showed rapid deterioration, with noticeable surface dehydration and browning from day 1 to onward and became completely spoiled by day 3. This deterioration was mainly caused by enzymatic oxidation catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD), which convert phenolic substrates into brown-colored quinones, leading to visible browning and loss of sensory attributes (Tilley et al., 2023). In contrast, T0 without PPE, slightly delayed these changes but still exhibited visual browning, textural breakdown and softening after day 4, which indicates its limited protective ability. However, incorporation of PPE (2–10%) into the starch matrix appeared to delay visible spoilage progression and maintain more acceptable visual appearance of the potato samples during storage. The films with higher concentrations of extract (particularly T4 and T5) showed reduced visible surface browning and fewer noticeable spoilage-associated changes in comparison with the unwrapped control throughout the storage period. The observed effect is likely due to the elevated phenolic and flavonoid content in PPE, which acted as antioxidant agents by scavenging reactive species and binding metal ions that normally stimulate PPO and POD activity, thereby reducing melanin formation and delaying browning reaction (Pan et al., 2020). Moreover, the presence of phenolic compounds may also contribute to improved resistance against spoilage development due to their reported antimicrobial potential, further contributing to the extended freshness and firmness of the fresh-cut potatoes. Consequently, the fabricated active films demonstrated strong potential as biodegradable and functional packaging materials, showed reduced visible browning and spoilage in minimally processed foods, and thus supporting both shelf-life extension and sustainable food preservation. Comparable results have been reported in earlier studies. Sun et al. (2024) found that soy protein isolate films embedded with quercetin-grafted dialdehyde effectively delayed enzymatic browning, maintained texture and minimized weight loss, effectively preserving fresh-cut potato slices throughout a 9-day storage period. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2024) showed that PS films, enzymatically modified and combined with L-cysteine, chitosan, and citric acid, were able to markedly slow browning in cut potato pieces by diminishing PPO activity and reducing the browning effect throughout storage. Together, these studies support that natural bioactives in biodegradable films showed enhance freshness by reducing oxidation and browning.

Although the developed films demonstrated preservation performance under ambient laboratory storage, practical industrial application would require further evaluation under commercial conditions such as refrigerated storage (4 ± 1 °C) and modified atmosphere packaging (MAP). Under refrigeration, reduced enzymatic activity and microbial growth could potentially enhance the effectiveness of PPE-based active films, while MAP environments may further complement browning control by limiting oxygen availability. However, several practical challenges should be considered for real processing operations, including film adhesion and wrapping efficiency on moist fresh-cut potato surfaces, mechanical stability during handling and transportation, and maintaining film integrity under high-humidity storage conditions. In addition, large-scale manufacturing would require optimization of film casting or extrusion processes, drying time, thickness uniformity, and compatibility with automated packaging systems. Therefore, future work should focus on validating film performance in refrigerated and MAP-based supply chain simulations, as well as assessing scalability and operational feasibility in industrial fresh-cut processing environments.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The research addressed the pressing challenge of reducing plastic use in food packaging by developing a biodegradable PS-based active edible film, added with PPE obtained using green UAE with water for prolonging the storage stability of fresh-cut produce. The experimental findings revealed that these active edible films showed reduced moisture content and water solubility, improved impermeability to moisture, better mechanical properties, enhanced antioxidant and antimicrobial properties with fast biodegradability, making them highly appropriate for use in food-packaging systems. Among the different formulations tested, T5 treatment containing the highest amount of extract (10% PPE) showed the most favorable performance among all formulations, despite having a marginally reduced EAB caused by decreased flexibility of the polymer network. The active edible films were applied to fresh-cut potatoes to qualitatively evaluate the preservation potential imparted by bioactive compounds present in PPE under ambient storage conditions. Films containing higher PPE concentrations visually delayed browning and spoilage-associated appearance changes during storage, indicating the potential applicability of PPE-incorporated PS-based films as biodegradable active packaging materials for minimally processed foods. The present study also highlights a sustainable same-commodity valorization approach by utilizing potato processing waste for the development of functional edible packaging systems, thereby contributing to reduced dependence on synthetic plastic materials. Despite these promising findings, the study has several limitations. The preservation assessment was primarily qualitative and conducted under controlled laboratory conditions using a single food model system. Therefore, comprehensive instrumental validation involving colorimetric analysis, texture evaluation, microbial assessment, and detailed shelf-life determination under commercial storage conditions remains necessary. In addition, future studies should further investigate refrigerated and modified atmosphere storage systems, large-scale processing feasibility, and industrial applicability of the developed films. Sensory attributes and consumer acceptance of the edible film were not assessed. Biodegradation was monitored only through a short-term soil burial test, without evaluation under diverse environmental conditions. Moreover, extraction optimization used only water as a solvent, and the influence of different potato peel sources or cultivars was not explored, which may affect consistency for large-scale production. Upcoming research ought to evaluate the effectiveness of these films on different categories of fresh-cut, high-moisture, and ready-to-eat food products to validate broader applicability. Sensory evaluation, consumer acceptability, migration studies, tests through advanced techniques, and market feasibility should be conducted to ensure safety, palatability, and regulatory compliance. Further work should also explore different green extraction strategies, combinations of bioactive compounds, and alternative biopolymers to enhance film functionality. Long-term biodegradation under different environmental conditions and shelf-life modeling in real supply-chain settings should also be investigated to support commercial scalability. In conclusion, this research provides a promising framework for developing novel biodegradable active edible films that support current sustainability initiatives and contribute to lowering plastic waste. A key insight from this work is that the primary food material can be transformed into a biodegradable packaging matrix, while its processing residues can be repurposed as functional additives that improve the protection and visual quality acceptability of that same product. This approach also showcases how advanced, green, and sustainable technologies can transform agricultural byproducts into functional packaging solutions, offering both environmental and industrial benefits.
