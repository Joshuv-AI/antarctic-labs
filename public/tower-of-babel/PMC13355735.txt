Regulatory science for AI-based software as a medical device in computational pathology and biomarker-driven drug development
Authors: Yael Liebes-Peer, Shlomo Czeisler, Rachel Broderick, Manuela Vecsler, Yair Heller, Chaim Linhart, Joseph Mossel, Jochen K. Lennerz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI)-enabled software is increasingly integrated into digital and computational pathology, driving new regulatory and quality management requirements that extend beyond traditional laboratory practice and classical medical-device oversight. Practical, experience-based guidance on balancing development agility with global regulatory readiness remains limited for biomarker developers and translational pathologists navigating the convergence of AI governance, cybersecurity, and regulated clinical deployment.MethodsWe reviewed our multi-year regulatory, quality, information security management program, and software lifecycle artifacts associated with AI-based diagnostic software development across multiple jurisdictions. Documentation, change control processes, and internal coordination mechanisms were analyzed to identify structural patterns supporting parallel progress in regulatory submissions, product releases, and assurance infrastructure.ResultsOur assessment consistently showed four transferable operational determinants to maintain iterative development while preserving regulatory readiness across jurisdictions: (1) Regulatory submissions and approvals (e.g., IVDR, FDA clearance); (2) product releases and lifecycle control; (3) quality and assurance infrastructure, including quality management system (QMS) certifications (e.g., ISO 13485, MDSAP); and (4) cybersecurity and information security certifications (e.g., ISO 27001, HITRUST, and C5). Together, these determinants enabled coordination of regulatory, release, and quality milestones in parallel, reducing friction at later submission stages and supporting synchronized readiness across jurisdictions.ConclusionsThis technical note presents a transferable framework for managing AI-based pathology software development in regulated environments. High-quality deployment requires more than model performance alone; it depends on technical, regulatory, and operational maturity across many dimensions, including change control, documentation, security-aligned quality systems, post-market surveillance, technical support, and workflow integration. The presented framework is directly relevant to computational scientists, pathologists, and laboratory/medical directors tasked with evaluating AI systems by supporting informed evaluation and adoption decisions, including procurement considerations, in clinical and biopharma settings.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled software systems are rapidly transitioning from exploratory tools in digital pathology to clinical-grade systems deployed in diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive workflows.1 This transition has been accompanied by a sharp increase in regulatory oversight, particularly for software as a medical device (SaMD),2 including AI-based diagnostic support systems.3 Whereas frameworks for laboratory oversight such as CLIA are familiar to pathologists and laboratory directors, they address only a subset of the regulatory landscape relevant to modern computational pathology. In contrast, global device regulation, spanning the European Union In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR),4, 5 the EU AI Act,6 and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) oversight of AI-enabled medical devices,7, 8 and internationally harmonized frameworks such as those developed by the International Medical Device Regulators Forum9 and the Good Machine Learning Practice10 principles jointly developed by the FDA, MHRA, and Health Canada, introduces novel, partially overlapping requirements.9, 11 These overlapping frameworks create new requirements that are often unfamiliar to pathology developers and clinical leaders.

Beyond analytical validation, current expectations increasingly include lifecycle governance,7 cybersecurity,3, 12 post-market monitoring,13 and controlled software modification.7 In Europe, IVDR has substantially increased evidentiary and quality management requirements for diagnostic software,4, 5, 14 whereas the EU AI Act introduces horizontal obligations related to risk management, transparency, and post-deployment monitoring.15, 16 In the United States, FDA oversight has evolved to address adaptive AI systems through concepts such as Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs),17 alongside increasing attention to cybersecurity, software updates, and real-world performance monitoring.2 These requirements intersect with information security management systems (ISMS),3, 12, 18, 19 privacy frameworks, quality management system (QMS) requirements, and software lifecycle standards. Together, they create a complex regulatory environment that extends well beyond conventional translational laboratory compliance in biopharma biomarker development and classical medical-device oversight, instead reflecting a convergence of AI governance, cybersecurity, and software lifecycle controls.12 In this context, AI-enabled pathology biomarkers increasingly inform therapeutic selection, trial eligibility, and stratification decisions, placing regulatory-ready governance mechanisms directly on the critical path of drug and treatment selection rather than downstream diagnostic confirmation.20, 21 These same gaps increasingly surface during procurement and request-for-proposal (RFP) processes, where laboratory directors and institutional decision-makers must evaluate not only algorithmic claims but also the regulatory, quality, and operational maturity of AI solutions. In this context, concrete, experience-based resources describing how AI tools in digital and computational pathology can be supported across biopharma, diagnostics, and drug development contexts remain scarce.

Here, we reviewed the multi-year experience of a regulatory team operating across multiple jurisdictions and regulatory frameworks. The primary objective of this work is to articulate the underlying operational logic required to translate AI models into clinically deployable, regulated SaMD. We examined regulatory deliverables, documentation timelines, and quality management artifacts, with particular attention to how development agility was maintained while achieving submission-ready documentation. Through this analysis, we identified a set of functional determinants enabling parallel regulatory progress across regions, with regulatory and ISMS-based governance operating synergistically. The relevance of this work lies in providing software developers, computational scientists, and medical directors with a practical framework for understanding how staged documentation and selected quality management elements can be applied to AI systems in digital pathology, without presupposing prior expertise in global medical device regulation.

Methods

Source material and review process

We conducted a structured review of regulatory submission documents, internal quality management artifacts, and versioned software documentation generated over multiple development and certification cycles. The reviewed materials spanned pre- and post-market phases, from early proof-of-concept development through certified product stages, and included documentation commonly assessed during partner due diligence and procurement processes. The review also encompassed regulatory documents and content supporting AI-enabled biomarker development activities, including companion diagnostic–adjacent workflows, clinical trial support, and post-deployment evidence from both biopharma drug development settings and routine clinical practice, reflecting real-world performance and integration.

Selection criteria and thematic synthesis

Materials were included when directly related to regulatory submission, certification, lifecycle governance, or evidence-generation activities between 2020 and 2026. Materials unrelated to these domains or duplicative administrative records were excluded. Recurring determinants were identified through iterative thematic review and two authors reviewed source materials and grouped recurring themes. Final determinants were refined through author consensus based on recurrence across regulatory, quality, and lifecycle domains. In parallel, we reviewed internal software-based tracking tools used to log development milestones, regulatory events, and quality management activities, which were subsequently synthesized into timeline visualizations resembling Gantt charts. To support readers without formal training in regulatory science, we provide an alphabetized glossary defining key regulatory, quality, and lifecycle terms used throughout this manuscript in plain, implementation-oriented language (S-Table 1).

Publications

In addition to regulatory and quality artifacts, we reviewed our internal publication tracker to extract peer-reviewed outputs associated with AI-based diagnostic development.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 For each publication, we recorded the month and year of publication, journal, and principal scientific or methodological focus (S-Table 2). We reviewed the total number of conference abstracts by calendar year as a proxy for scientific dissemination activity over time. For visualization, these data were summarized descriptively and aligned temporally with regulatory and quality milestones to contextualize the evolution of evidence generation alongside product and regulatory development. In addition, publications were assigned to development stages based on claim intent, study design, and operational context rather than publication venue or reported performance. The classification reflects whether evidence supports exploratory research, use-directed development, investigational evaluation, regulatory approval, or real-world clinical deployment (defined as development stages, see results).

Analytical approach

The analysis focused on identifying recurring documentation patterns, sequencing of regulatory activities, and change management mechanisms that enabled parallel regulatory progress across jurisdictions. Emphasis was placed on what documentation was generated at different stages of product maturation, rather than on jurisdiction-specific regulatory outcomes. The goal was not to evaluate regulatory success per se, but to characterize the structure and timing of documentation and quality controls that supported regulatory readiness.

As part of the analysis, we reviewed a broad set of artifacts spanning regulatory submission materials, quality management documentation, internal policies, and software-supported coordination mechanisms, including issue tracking, version control, document management, and project coordination platforms. These materials encompassed both organization-specific processes and widely adopted tooling used in software development and regulated environments. The objective of this review was not exhaustive cataloging, but comparative assessment of how different mechanisms supported (or impeded) development agility and regulatory traceability across product lifecycle stages.

Staged documentation process

For the purposes of this report, a “staged documentation process” is defined as a structured approach in which documentation depth, formality, and traceability increase in step with product maturity and regulatory intent. Early stages emphasize minimal, high-level documentation sufficient to support technical feasibility and internal decision-making, whereas later stages progressively introduce formal design controls, risk management, validation evidence, and regulatory-grade documentation. This approach contrasts with traditional “upfront compliance” models, in which full regulatory documentation is required at early development stages, often at the expense of development agility.

Team composition and operational model

Regulatory and quality activities were supported by an internal professional core team spanning regulatory affairs, quality management, and software lifecycle oversight. This core team was responsible for maintaining continuity across development stages, coordinating internal stakeholders, and ensuring traceability across documentation artifacts. External consultant support was used sparingly and in a targeted manner. Consultants supported defined, time-limited activities supporting specific compliance and registration activities in different jurisdictions; the internal team maintained all technical expertise, regulatory interpretation, and project governance.

Results

Milestones across domains

Our assessment consistently showed four transferable operational determinants across jurisdictions: (1) Regulatory submissions and approvals (e.g., IVDR, FDA clearance); (2) product releases and lifecycle control; (3) quality and assurance infrastructure, including QMS certifications (e.g., ISO 13485, MDSAP); and (4) cybersecurity and information security certifications (e.g., ISO 27001, HITRUST, and C5).

Regulatory submissions entail exhaustive review processes by governing authorities that are both resource-intensive and independent of a company's internal product development cycles. To achieve IVDR certification, we navigated a landscape of limited EU guidance for AI-enabled devices by extrapolating requirements and engaging in preliminary alignment with the Notified Body. The subsequent review spanned several months and involved reviewers of different disciplines, including clinical experts, prior to CE Marking. In the United States, the regulatory journey progressed from pre-submission through Breakthrough Device designation, three validation studies, and a completed 510(k) submission, culminating in FDA clearance.31 The formal FDA review involved extensive review and iterative interaction. These summary data are provided to reflect on the complexity of aligning clinical evidence, documentation, and regulatory expectations for an AI-enabled medical device. Furthermore, the regulatory agencies or other external factors can introduce significant delays; for instance, TGA (Australia) review timelines were extended by over a year due to pandemic-related prioritization in 2023.

Evolution of peer-reviewed publications and abstracts

Review of dissemination activities over time showed a sustained increase in peer-reviewed publications alongside a more variable pattern of conference abstracts. Between 2019 and 2026, dissemination comprised 90 scientific outputs, including 81 conference abstracts and 9 peer-reviewed journal articles.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Early phases of development were characterized by a higher relative number of abstracts, with abstract activity peaking in 2023 (n = 23) and 2024 (n = 19), reflecting exploratory work, community engagement, and the need for early visibility during rapid methodological iteration. Peer-reviewed publications emerged later and accumulated gradually, with one article each in 2020 and 2022, followed by three publications in 2024 and three in 2025, corresponding to maturation of the technology, availability of larger validation datasets, and increased emphasis on durable, citable clinical evidence. When mapped to lifecycle stages (Fig. 2), the early abstracts clustered in Stages II–III, whereas peer-reviewed validation studies emerged predominantly in Stages III–IV. Real-world evidence publications were comparatively fewer, suggesting that late-stage evidence generation remains a bottleneck in the field. This shift also reflects practical considerations, as well as a strategic prioritization of archival peer-reviewed literature as products approached regulated deployment and broader clinical adoption. Evidence evolution across lifecycle stages

Fig. 2 conceptualizes product maturation from exploratory development to routine clinical deployment. Early dissemination outputs were concentrated in Stages II–III exploratory and investigational phases, whereas peer-reviewed validation studies emerged primarily during Stages III–IV maturation. Real-world evidence studies appeared during deployment-stage use, highlighting the relative scarcity of late-stage evidence generation.

In procurement and RFP evaluations, these determinants consistently aligned with the questions raised when assessing AI-enabled pathology solutions. The relative emphasis placed on individual elements varied substantially across partners, reflecting differing priorities and risk perceptions. Importantly, scientific evidence for AI SaMD may be appropriately requested at different stages of product maturation, ranging from early analytical performance metrics to regulatory-grade validation evidence, to real-world clinical performance indicators, each providing distinct and complementary value. Whereas algorithmic bias, performance metrics, and training data composition were frequently foregrounded, attention to foundational elements such as change control discipline, documentation traceability, cybersecurity posture, data protection, and regulatory clearance status and experience in large-scale deployments with evidence of real-world performance and clinical benefit assessments was more variable. The framework described here makes these often-implicit maturity expectations explicit, accessible, and amenable to systematic evaluation.

In parallel, partner-facing integration and deployment readiness emerged as a practical extension of these coordinated milestones. For biopharma collaborations, regulatory-aligned baselines are intentionally coupled to defined deployment configurations (e.g., cloud tenancy models, data ingress/egress controls, versioned APIs, and audit-ready interfaces), enabling predictable integration into sponsor trial infrastructure and analytics pipelines. Treating the very task of integration as part of the lifecycle (rather than a post-hoc engineering task) reduces friction during onboarding and supports synchronized deployment across clinical trial, translational, and regulated diagnostic contexts.

Our assessment consistently identified four recurrent and transferable operational determinants that shaped the ability to sustain iterative AI development while preserving regulatory readiness across jurisdictions: (1) regulatory submissions and approvals (e.g., IVDR, FDA clearance); (2) product releases and lifecycle control; (3) quality and assurance infrastructure, including QMS certifications (e.g., ISO 13485, MDSAP); and (4) cybersecurity and information security certifications (e.g., ISO 27001, HITRUST, and C5). Together, these determinants enabled predictable handling of frequent software updates without unnecessary regulatory escalation, while allowing documentation depth, formality, and traceability to increase in step with product maturation and regulatory intent (referred to here as staged documentation). Targeted application of QMS and information security management elements provided cross-cutting governance controls that supported alignment across regulatory, quality, and development activities. Collectively, these determinants were repeatedly associated with reduced friction during submission processes and improved coordination across product lifecycle stages and jurisdictions.

Change control classification as a foundational control mechanism

Change control classification emerged as a critical and non-trivial determinant for AI-based diagnostic software. Unlike traditional devices, AI systems undergo frequent updates that may affect performance, usability, data handling, or infrastructure without necessarily altering intended use or risk classification. Clear differentiation between major releases, routine updates, and hot fixes was essential to ensure proportionate regulatory oversight (Table 1). Several changes proved difficult to classify in practice, requiring internal expertise and accumulated know-how. In addition, security-related changes posed classification challenges, ranging from routine code maintenance and dependency updates to the introduction of infrastructural security controls and patches aimed at mitigating potential vulnerabilities during ongoing operation. Resolution relied on predefined classification criteria linking the nature of the change to its potential impact on intended use, risk, and clinical performance, combined with documented rationale and escalation pathways when classification was ambiguous. Staged documentation aligned with product maturation

The staged documentation framework provided a complementary mechanism to change control by aligning documentation depth with product maturity and regulatory intent (Fig. 2), explicitly incorporating the evolving clinical use and evidence context across development stages. Early stages prioritized minimal, high-level documentation sufficient to support feasibility assessment and internal decision-making, while deferring full regulatory formality until design stabilization. Importantly, the staged documentation and change control framework enabled clear differentiation between exploratory biomarker use (e.g., hypothesis generation or trial support) and decision-grade applications influencing treatment selection or regulatory submissions, without requiring premature regulatory escalation. As products transitioned toward pre-submission readiness, documentation expanded to include formal design input, output, and controls, risk management files, verification and validation plans and tests, including human factors (usability) and clinical studies, and traceability matrices, submission files, and others. This approach proved advantageous in avoiding premature documentation burden during exploratory phases while still enabling a rapid transition to submission-ready artifacts once development converged. For example, early algorithm iterations were documented through concise system descriptions and exploratory test reports, which later served as inputs for formal requirements and validation documentation rather than being discarded or retroactively reconstructed.

Integrated governance architecture: Quality management, cybersecurity, and information security

Among QMS elements, cybersecurity and information security management emerged as particularly salient for AI-based diagnostic software. Fig. 3 illustrates how quality management and information security operate as linked management systems rather than isolated functions. Shared governance elements include document control, CAPA, supplier oversight, audits, and continual improvement. First, we want to share a high-level representation of the QMS as implemented in practice, illustrating how quality policy, management responsibility, resource management, design control, product realization, and measurement and improvement form an integrated governance loop (Fig. 3a). The cyclical structure emphasizes continual feedback between governance, execution, monitoring, and improvement. We want to make explicit that information security management resides within the QMS: not as a peripheral technical add-on, but as a cross-cutting control layer that interfaces with design control, execution, and post-market feedback. The implementation of cybersecurity and information security controls also serve as a complementary layer to the QMS requirements, providing both risk mitigation as well as monitoring capabilities to track compliance in various aspects such as disaster-recovery, access control mechanisms, and vulnerability remediation. This perspective reflects the operational reality that cybersecurity requirements shape upstream design decisions and downstream change management, rather than being addressed solely during deployment or incident response. Discussion

Here, we present a technical note that contributes a structured, experience-derived perspective on how regulatory, product, and quality milestones can be coordinated across domains in a way that is transferable beyond a single organization or setting. Rather than focusing on jurisdiction-specific outcomes, the contribution lies in identifying operationally actionable elements that generalize across regulatory environments: explicit separation of milestones into regulatory submissions, product releases, and assurance infrastructure; structured change control classification to manage frequent software iteration; staged documentation aligned with product maturation to balance agility and traceability; and selective use of QMS elements, with particular emphasis on cybersecurity and information security. Together, these elements form a pragmatic framework for maintaining development velocity while preserving submission readiness and post-market compliance and for supporting informed evaluation and adoption decisions by clinical and biopharma stakeholders. Importantly, this work situates AI model performance within a broader framework of technical, regulatory, and operational maturity that is required to enable trustworthy deployment and governance of AI-enabled pathology products in clinical and biopharma contexts.

Relation to existing regulatory science literature

Prior work has described the growing evidence requirements for AI products in pathology, broader regulatory expectations for AI-SaMD, deployment barriers in digital pathology, and emerging efforts toward harmonized computational pathology reporting and interoperability frameworks.1, 2, 18, 22, 34 Our contribution complements these reports by focusing less on model performance or policy description and more on operational mechanisms that enable sustained compliant development, namely staged documentation, change classification, and integrated QMS–ISMS governance.

The novelty of this work lies in situating these regulatory and quality mechanisms within the context of modern biopharma drug development, where computational pathology increasingly supports biomarker discovery, validation, and deployment. Unlike traditional diagnostics developed independently of therapeutic timelines, AI-based pathology biomarkers must evolve in synchrony with drug development milestones, reinforcing the need for governance mechanisms that allow rapid iteration without destabilizing trial or treatment decisions.20, 21 AI-enabled pathology tools now now support patient stratification, companion diagnostics, trial enrichment, and real-world evidence generation across the drug lifecycle. Consequently, regulatory readiness has become a critical enabler of biomarker-driven drug development.

A practical challenge underlying this transition is resource asymmetry between exploratory AI development and regulatory-grade deployment. Early-stage AI efforts often operate with limited personnel, constrained documentation infrastructure, and rapidly evolving technical assumptions, whereas later-stage regulated deployment requires durable governance structures, validation evidence, traceability, cybersecurity controls, and sustained operational support. The staged documentation and lifecycle framework described here can therefore also be understood as a mechanism for managing this asymmetry, allowing organizations to progressively increase formalization and governance in parallel with clinical responsibility, regulatory intent, and external exposure.

In parallel, the role of the pathologist is evolving from endpoint interpreter to diagnostic architect, responsible not only for analytical validity but also for integration, lifecycle oversight, and risk management of AI-supported systems. These governance mechanisms also support pathologist-in-the-loop oversight, ensuring that AI-derived biomarkers remain interpretable, auditable, and clinically contextualized at the point of treatment decision-making.11, 20, 21, 26 In this context, regulatory frameworks are not merely compliance mechanisms but enabling infrastructures that allow AI-based biomarkers and diagnostic algorithms to progress from exploratory discovery and trial-support applications into validated companion diagnostic-adjacent tools and, where appropriate, regulated clinical diagnostics within oncology drug development programs and ultimately regulated clinical diagnostics. For example, the current edition of the IVDR emphasizes fixed conformity assessment pathways, whereas the FDA PCCP guidance introduces a structured mechanism for pre-authorized evolution, highlighting divergent approaches to lifecycle adaptability. Of note, a proposal to amend the IVDR (COM/2025/1023) is under discussion and includes a PCCP approach. Thus, regulatory frameworks (and their evolving nature) are mechanisms to ensure appropriate balances between safety, risk, and innovation, supported by explicit control and governance structures.

From a biomarker perspective, these governance mechanisms translate directly into integration and deployment confidence.11, 22, 27 Clear change classification, staged documentation, and embedded security controls allow sponsors to integrate AI-based pathology tools into trial operations and companion diagnostic–adjacent workflows without destabilizing validated pipelines or triggering unplanned regulatory escalation. In practice, this enables deployment models that are reproducible across studies, geographies, and partners; an increasingly critical requirement as AI-enabled biomarkers move from exploratory (research-) use into decision-grade applications (e.g., within drug development programs or trial support in academic environments).2, 7, 13, 17, 35

In procurement and collaboration contexts, the relative emphasis placed on individual governance elements varies substantially, reflecting context-specific priorities, risk tolerance, and use cases.2, 12, 22 Whereas some collaborations appropriately foreground, e.g., algorithmic bias, analytical validity, or dataset representativeness,7, 11, 16, 26 other dimensions such as cybersecurity posture,12 data protection,6, 19 or lifecycle governance7 may receive comparatively less scrutiny. In practice, focusing strongly on a single dimension can obscure cross-domain dependencies; for example, advances in bias mitigation or performance optimization may introduce downstream cyber-risks if not accompanied by commensurate attention to security and data governance. The shared framework described here provides a structured way to contextualize such partner-specific priorities within a balanced view of technical, regulatory, and operational maturity, reducing the likelihood that strengths in one area are offset by unrecognized gaps in another.

Recent regulatory science literature has outlined complementary approaches to enabling faster delivery in regulated environments, including adoption maturity models for risk-based quality management in clinical trials,35, 36 and systematic frameworks for agile development under regulatory constraints.37 Building on these concepts, the present work contributes a biomarker and digital pathology-specific operationalization by detailing: (i) structured change control classification as a mechanism to manage frequent (AI software) updates, (ii) staged documentation aligned with product maturation to balance agility and regulatory traceability, and (iii) targeted application of QMS elements with a specific focus on cybersecurity and information security as cross-cutting controls.

Relation to existing maturity and agile development models

Existing maturity models emphasize process capability or trial-quality systems, whereas agile development frameworks focus on execution under regulatory constraints.35, 36, 37 The present framework adapts these concepts to computational pathology by integrating evidence generation, software change control, documentation staging, and deployment governance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it reflects the retrospective experience of a single organization and may not capture practices used elsewhere. The framework presented here should therefore be understood as introductory and intentionally selective, highlighting specific leverage points (i.e., change control, staged documentation, and cybersecurity-focused QMS elements) that can serve as starting points rather than comprehensive solutions. For example, despite its importance, the comparative analysis of ISO13485 and 27001 (Fig. 3b) has, to our knowledge, not been covered in the field of digital pathology.3, 12, 18, 19, 38, 39 Second, the analysis is descriptive and does not prospectively validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. Third, no formal comparator group or quantitative cost/time benchmark was available. Fourth, regulatory requirements evolve rapidly and jurisdiction-specific interpretations may change over time. Fifth, several important implementation domains—including reimbursement,12 clinical evidence generation,1, 9 health economics,6, 12, 15 human factors engineering,7, 35 and user training—fall outside the scope of this report. Accordingly, the framework should be interpreted as practical and hypothesis-generating rather than prospectively validated. Future work should evaluate how these governance mechanisms can be operationalized through pathology informatics tooling and assessed prospectively across multi-site biomarker development programs.

Currently, operating across multiple regulatory environments remains largely restricted to biopharma-driven development and clinical laboratories with an international footprint. However, as AI-based pathology solutions increasingly rely on cloud-based or federated deployments rather than local software implementations, the relevance of multiple regulatory environments is likely to emerge even for nationally operating institutions.6, 7, 18, 35, 36 One concrete example is the European Health Data Space (EHDS) which has been initiated and is currently being implemented.40, 41 This suggests that frameworks for staged documentation, change classification, and integrated quality controls may become broadly relevant beyond early adopters.37 Future work should explore how these elements can be integrated with existing clinical and research infrastructures, and how regulatory literacy (and related evaluation competency) can be more systematically embedded within computational pathology training and practice.

In summary, this technical report does not propose a prescriptive regulatory blueprint, but rather offers a focused, experience-informed framework illustrating how selected regulatory and quality mechanisms can be operationalized to support AI development in digital pathology. By emphasizing transferable structures rather than jurisdiction-specific detail, the intent is to support developers, pathologists, and medical directors in navigating the increasingly intertwined domains of innovation, regulation, and clinical deployment. Taken together, these determinants function as an operational roadmap for sustaining compliant AI development in digital pathology.
