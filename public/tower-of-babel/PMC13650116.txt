Effects of breast cancer risk factors on the tumor microenvironment: a morphological deep-learning analysis of three prospective cohorts
Authors: Clara Bodelon, Mohamed Amgad, James M Hodge, Maha A T Elsebaie, Mariah Landry, Cheng Peng, Rulla M Tamimi, Peter Kraft, Lauren E McCullough, Mark E Sherman, Mia M Gaudet, Alpa V Patel, Lee A D Cooper, Lauren R Teras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence suggests a critical role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in breast cancer (BC) development and outcomes, yet factors that modify the TME are poorly understood. We investigated the relationship between BC etiological factors and the tumor and TME using 110 histological features of the epithelium, stroma, and immune infiltration, computationally quantified in 3724 H&E slides from three prospective cohort studies. Age, race, hormonal, and lifestyle factors were associated with features of the breast TME. Menopausal hormone therapy was associated with epithelial and stromal features found in less aggressive tumors, while higher body mass index (BMI) was associated with two histological features not captured by grade, and both were associated with poor prognosis. These two features mediated the BMI and BC-specific mortality association by 18.1%. Our findings provide novel insights into the role of etiological factors on the TME including modifiable factors that have implications for prevention and outcomes.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is composed of cellular and non-cellular elements that interact with breast cancer (BC) cells to influence BC development and prognosis1–3. Genetics, lifestyle, and hormone-related factors are well-known to influence BC risk4, and have been suggested to alter the breast tumor and TME (TTME)2, although data to support this has been limited. Previous studies that investigated associations between etiological factors and the breast TTME suffered from small sample sizes, in part due to the time-consuming and labor-intensive work to manually quantify features of the TME5, or had limited characterization of the TTME. For example, most previous studies to date have used bulk tissue without preservation of specific morphology or spatial relationships6,7 or investigated broad categories of the stroma, such as abundance or stromal cellularity8. However, examining finer categories of the TTME may provide important information as different stromal cell types or the stromal extracellular matrix induce different pro- and anti-tumor responses5,9,10, and BC risk factors may affect these components differently. For instance, while tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have been associated with better prognosis in some breast cancers11, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are thought to be drivers of cancer progression and metastases10,12. Understanding whether BC risk factors are associated with morphology, abundance or activation of TILs or CAFs, or other features of the breast TTME, may highlight opportunities to develop preventive and therapeutic interventions that target the TTME.

Using data from 3724 BC patients who were participants in three population-based cohort studies with detailed prospectively-collected survey and clinical data, we investigated the relationship between BC etiological factors and a recent histological characterization of the TTME which included the abundance, morphology, and contextual relationships of epithelial, stromal, and immune elements extracted using deep-learning-based computer analysis on whole-slide images (WSIs) of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides (Supplemental Data 1)13. We further examined the mediation effect of the BC etiological factors with features of the TTME on BC-specific mortality (BCSM).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 3724 BC patients were included in this analysis. Median age at diagnosis was 66 years (interquartile ranges [IQR]: 58, 73; Table 1 and Supplemental Table 1). Approximately 1.0% of the women self-identified as Asian, 2.1% as Black, 1.6% as Hispanic and 94.4% as White. Of the women included in this analysis, 84.9% reported being postmenopausal prior to their breast diagnosis, 41.9% were never users of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) and 28.6% were current users. There were 54.8% of women who had a body mass index (BMI) equal or greater than 25 kg/m2. Most tumors were estrogen receptor (ER) positive (79.3%; Supplemental Table 2), and either localized (42.8%) or regional (49.0%). The features of the breast TTME included in this analysis were not strongly correlated with each other; over 85% of the pairs had correlations smaller than 0.25 (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Associations of demographic characteristics with breast TTME features

Increasing age at diagnosis was associated with multiple features of TTME (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05; Fig. 1A). In particular, age was significantly associated with lower CAF density (β=−0.06, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.08 to −0.04) and CAF clustering around epithelial cells (β=−0.05, 95% CI: −0.07 to −0.03). Increased levels of these two features were found to be associated with worse BCSM (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.10–1.45 and HR:1.19, 95% CI: 1.05–1.36, respectively; Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3). Older age was also associated with lower TIL density (β=−0.03, 95% CI: −0.06 to −0.01) and lower number of TILs to the stroma ratio in the peri-tumoral stroma (256uM)(β=−0.05, 95% CI: −0.07 to −0.02). Compared to women who self-identified as White, the remaining participants had significantly greater epithelial cell density (β = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.08–0.34), epithelial-to-stroma ratio (β = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.10–0.35) and increased variability of the staining of the extracellular matrix (ECM)(standard deviation: β = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.10–0.38; entropy: β = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.09–0.37).

Associations of hormonal factors with breast TTME features

Among the hormonal factors examined in this study (Fig. 1A), current use of MHT had the greatest number of significant associations with features of the TTME. These included decreased epithelial cell density (β=−0.14, 95% CI: −0.21 to −0.07), increased low-grade nuclei per epithelial nest (epithelial nests with fewer atypical cells)(β = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.05–0.19), decreased CAF activation (by means of not acquiring epithelialized morphology)(β= −0.15, 95% CI: −0.21 to −0.09), decreased clustering of CAFs around epithelial cells (β=-0.15, 95% CI:−0.22 to −0.08), and greater circularity of TIL clusters (β = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.05–0.21). However, none of these features were associated with worse BCSM (Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3). Given that features of the TTME are related to clinical tumor characteristics, and these are more widely available and studied, we assessed the associations between BC risk factors and clinical tumor characteristics in our population (Fig. 2) to put the associations between BC risk factors and features of the TTME in the context of associations with clinical tumor characteristics. Current MHT was associated with lower grade tumors (Odds Ratio [OR] = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.91) but it was not associated with BCSM (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.62–1.11).

Older age at menopause was associated with several breast features, including increased variation (i.e., standard deviation) of chromatin clumping within epithelial nuclei (β = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.03–0.09). This feature was predictive of increased BCSM (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03–1.38; Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3).

Associations of lifestyle factors with features of the breast TTME

We examined the association between lifestyle factors and epithelial features of the TTME (Fig. 1A). Higher BMI was significantly associated with increased epithelial cell density (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.03–0.08), increased epithelial nuclear size (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.08), greater nuclear boundary complexity (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.08) and chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.08). Increased chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei was associated with worse BCSM (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03–1.38; Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3). In contrast, increased physical activity was significantly associated with decreased epithelial nuclear size (β=−0.11, 95% CI: −0.19 to −0.04), although this association was no longer significant after adjusting for BMI, suggesting that it was not independently associated. BMI was also significantly related to several features of the stroma and epithelial-stroma interaction. Importantly, higher BMI was associated with a greater global density of CAFs (β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.08), which is a predictor of BC-specific mortality (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.10–1.46; Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3).

Given that higher BMI was associated with higher grade (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20 per 5 kg/m2; Fig. 2), and worse BCSM (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.34 per 5 kg/m2), especially under obesity (BMI≥30) conditions (Grade: 25≤BMI < 30 vs BMI < 25: OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.87–1.22; BMI≥30 vs BMI < 25: OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.15–1.66)(BCSM: 25≤BMI < 30 vs BMI < 25: HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 0.98–1.80; BMI≥30 vs BMI < 25: HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.11–2.16), we examined whether features previously found to be significantly associated with BMI were also associated by categories of overweight (25≤BMI < 30) and obesity (Fig. 3). Overweight and obese BMIs were significantly associated with greater tumor cell density, entropy of ECM staining, and white spaces at the epithelial nest margin which represent retraction artifacts and/or lymphovascular invasion. Nuclear related features and CAF density were significantly associated only with obesity.

Chromatin clumping and CAF density partially explain the effect of BMI on BCSM

Women with higher BMI had significantly greater chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei and greater density of CAFs, both of which were significantly associated with worse BCSM (Fig. 1B and Supplemental Table 3). We investigated whether the association between BMI and BCSM could be explained (or mediated) through these two features of the TTME (Fig. 4).

Directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing the relationships between body mass index (BMI), features of the tumor and tumor microenvironment (TTME), and breast cancer-specific mortality (BCSM).

We found that the percent mediated by chromatin clumping was 8.4% (95% CI: 2.49–20.8%) and by CAF density was 12.8 (95% CI: 6.1–29.6%). The percentage mediated by both features was 18.1% (95% CI: 9.7–43.4%). A similar analysis with grade as the mediator resulted in 14.0% (95% CI: 3.2–25.0%). By adding the chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei and CAFs to grade the mediating effect was 25.0% (95% CI: 12.7–53.4%). Hormonal factors were not associated with BCSM, and therefore, mediation analysis was not conducted.

Relation of etiological factors with features of the TTME by ER status

We assessed the relationship between etiological factors and features of the TME in groups defined by ERs status (Supplemental Fig. 2). In patients with ER positive tumors (N = 2952, 79.3%), patterns of associations were similar as those described for overall breast cancer, although due to the smaller sample size, many of the associations were only significant at the FDR < 0.1 level. In patients with ER negative tumors (622 [16.7%] with 591 [95.0%] in self-identified White participants), significant associations (FDR < 0.05) were observed for age and race. We found that tumors from self-identified White women were associated with decreased TIL density around epithelial cells.

Discussion

Our comprehensive analysis of BC etiological factors and quantitative histological features of the breast TTME revealed that age, race, hormonal, and lifestyle factors were associated with epithelial and stromal features, TILs, and interactions among these features. This analysis leveraged our recently published set of 109 quantitative features extracted from WSIs of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides using deep-learning that measure distinct biological phenomena and processes of the TTME including the abundance, morphology, and contextual relationships of epithelial, stromal, and immune elements13. We also included the total cell density on the slide for a total of 110 features. We found that two modifiable risk factors, BMI and use of MHT, were associated with multiple epithelial and stromal features. Since women with BC and high BMI have greater risk of BCSM14, we investigated how much of the effect of BMI on BCSM could be explained (or mediated) through features of the TTME, and found an 18.1% mediating effect by just two prognostic features, chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei and density of CAFs. These results provide insights into etiologic and prognostic mechanisms of the TTME beyond current clinical characterization of tumors and suggest the possibility of using standard histologic H&E images for identifying women with unfavorable features of the TTME and risk factors, who may benefit from specific therapeutic interventions15.

The relationship between obesity and increased risk of BCSM is well-established14, but the mechanisms underlying this risk are poorly understood. Obese patients tend to have tumors with higher grade and stage16. Where grade is determined entirely by histologic features of the epithelium, stage is based on tumor size and spread of the BC to regional lymph nodes or more distant metastases. Visual assessment of H&E-stained slides of the tumor epithelium is the standard practice for determining histologic grade. However, some histological features of the tumor epithelium, such as chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei, are not well captured by grade13, despite being prognostic of BCSM. Also, features of the stroma, such as CAF density, are not considered in grading. We found that these two features alone mediated the effect of BMI on BCSM by 18.1% and by adding grade to these features the mediating rose to 25.0%. Different mechanisms may explain the increased levels of chromatin clumping of epithelial nuclei and CAF density with increased BMI. Epigenetic changes, including chromatin changes, are associated with obesity17. Obesity also results in a greater quantity of adipose tissue in the breast18 that can secrete growth factors, hormones, and adipokines to produce a proinflammatory environment19. This proinflammatory environment has been found to alter the composition and function of the breast TME20,21. Our prior work compared TTME features with RNA sequencing data in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) to identify molecular correlates of TTME features13. Pathway analysis of significantly correlated genes revealed that CAF density is strongly associated with upregulated epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) pathways, that have both been implicated in EMT. Other features of CAF nuclear morphology were correlated with a myofibroblast CAF phenotype that is associated with immunosuppression13,22,23.

Hormonal factors are important in BC etiology, although most of these factors have not been found to be related to BCSM24. In particular, current use of MHT, whether estrogen alone or progesterone plus estrogen, has not been found to increase the risk of overall or BCSM24,25. Previous studies have found that use of MHT is associated with less advanced clinical characteristics of the tumors26. However, clinical tumor characteristics are a composite of multiple histological features of the tumor epithelium and do not include the stroma. We looked at individual histological features of the TTME and found that MHT was associated with lower cell density, lower CAF activation, and less abnormal characteristics of cells, which are also features of less aggressive tumor behavior. Estrogen in MHT may lower CAF activation via a TGF-beta mediated response27–29, which results in a downregulated EMT pathways and better prognostic tumors30. Our findings extend associations of MHT use to the stromal and immune features and provide biological insights into the tissue elements that may be affected by MHT use.

Older age at menopause was also associated with increased variation of chromatin clumping within epithelial nuclei, a prognostic feature of BMSC that is not well captured by histologic grading. Older age at menopause is thought to reflect longer exposure of the breast tissues to reproductive hormones, that may stimulate cell proliferation and result in abnormal aggregation of chromatin in some epithelial cell nuclei.

Demographic risk factors such as age and race were also associated with multiple features of the TTME. Older age was associated with lower CAF density or CAF clustering around epithelial cells. Lower levels of these features were found to be associated with decreased BCSM, and this is consistent with the fact that older patients often have tumors with less aggressive clinical characteristics compared with younger patients31. Older age was also associated with lower abundance of TILs; however, TILs have not been found to improve prognosis in hormone receptor-positive tumors32, which are commonly found in older women. Compared to self-identified White women, the remaining participants had increased variability of features of the extracellular matrix (ECM), reflecting the composition and density of the ECM. During cancer progression, the normal deposition and density of the ECM are disrupted, leading to a stiffer ECM, which correlates with increased disease severity33.

Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease that can be grouped into prognostically relevant clinical subtypes based on ER status34. These subtypes have been found to have different etiologies35–37. The cellular constitution and activation of elements of the TME may also differ depending on the parenchymal characteristics of the cancer cells, and we, therefore, assessed the relationship between etiological factors and features of the TME in groups defined by ER status. The associations in patients with ER-positive tumors were similar to those described for overall breast cancer, with age, hormonal, and lifestyle factors associated with epithelial, stromal, and interaction factors. This agrees with what we found; that tumors from self-identified White patients, which were ER negative, had decreased TIL density around epithelial cells. The sample size of ER-negative tumors in our study was small.

Our study is the largest to investigate associations between well-established breast etiological factors with a comprehensive set of histologic features measured by computational analysis. Our computational approach was applied to standard clinical H&E slides and provides consistent assessment of the breast TTME morphology, is less prone to interobserver variability compared to visual semi-quantitative evaluation and does not require additional clinical material or assays, making it suitable for the characterization in large epidemiological studies. We were able to formally test for mediators of BC etiological factors with BCSM. This is an important step to understand the mechanisms underlying BC etiological factors on BCSM. A unique aspect of our study is the assessment of features capturing distinct biological phenomena of the tumor epithelium, stroma, and TILs, allowing us to disentangle the underlying tumor biology and their relationship with BC risk factors. This differs from studying associations of BC etiological factors with clinical characteristics, which are a composite of multiple biological elements. Established associations between risk factors and tumor characteristics provide a biological framework for interpreting how etiologic factors may influence tumor development through the TTME. These associations are biologically relevant because many clinical tumor characteristics (such as grade, ER status, and stage) reflect underlying cellular composition, stromal remodeling, immune infiltration, and tumor–stroma interactions—processes that are captured by TTME features. Also, as noted previously13, the computational assessment of epithelial elements used in our analysis outperformed manual grading. An advantage of the digital pathology approach was the ability to study CAFs. Factors that alter CAFs are poorly understood in part due to lack of standardized methods for detecting them in tissues12. We found that both MHT and BMI were associated with morphological alterations and changes in density of CAFs. Despite these strengths, the findings from this analysis should be considered with certain limitations. Specifically, the population of patients was mostly White, which limited the ability to examine some associations across multiple racial and ethnic populations. They were mostly diagnosed with ER-positive disease and were older than 50 years. The distribution of features in the TME may differ in groups defined by race or ER status or in younger patients. H&Es could not be obtained from all the breast cancer patients from the parent cohorts to create digital images; however, we combined several cohorts to try to improve the representation of breast cancer patients. The population included in our analysis broadly represents the population of breast cancer patients, with 70% of women diagnosed over the age of 60, approximately 90% of tumors having localized or regional stage, and 80% of tumor being ER positive38.

In conclusion, we provide novel evidence on how established BC etiological factors, including modifiable risk factors, affect the TTME, providing mechanistic insights into disease etiology and outcomes.

Methods

Study population

BC patients were participants in three population-based prospective cohort studies with detailed BC etiological and clinical data: the Cancer Prevention Study II (CPS-II)39, the Cancer Prevention Study 3 (CPS-3)40 and the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial41. The CPS-II Nutrition Cohort is a prospective cohort study of 184,194 cancer-free participants (97,786 women) recruited in 1992–1993 from 21 states39. At the time of recruitment, participants completed a baseline demographic, lifestyle, and medical history questionnaire. Follow-up questionnaires were sent to cohort members every 2 years starting in 1997 to update exposure information and to ascertain newly diagnosed cancers. Self-reported cancers were verified through abstraction of medical records or linkage with state cancer registries. Information on clinical characteristics was captured from medical records to match data from cancer registries. Patients’ characteristics used in this analysis were extracted from the survey prior, but closest to, their cancer diagnosis. CPS-II cancer incidence follow-up is complete through 2017. Deaths were identified by linkage with the National Death Index through 31 December 2020. At the time of each mailed survey, participants were informed that their identifying information would be used to link with cancer registries and death indexes. Women diagnosed with breast cancer gave written informed consent to obtain medical records, tissue specimens and untreated formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. The Cancer Prevention Study II (CPS-II) Nutrition Cohort study was approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board. The IRB allowed participants’ completion of questionnaires to be considered as implied consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The CPS-3 prospective cohort includes 303,682 participants (198,632 women) who were cancer-free, aged 30–65 years, and lived in 35 states or Puerto Rico during recruitment between 2006 and 201340. Eligibility criteria included being aged 30–65 years and having no prior personal history of cancer. Participants returned a baseline survey and triennial follow-up surveys beginning in 2015. Patients’ characteristics used in this analysis were extracted from the survey prior, but closest to, their cancer diagnosis. Written informed consent was received from participants at study enrollment. Participants were linked to state cancer registries to identify all reportable cancer diagnoses. CPS-3 cancer incidence for this analysis is complete through 2018 and linkage with the National Death Index through December 31, 2020. Women diagnosed with breast cancer gave an additional written informed consent to obtain medical records, tissue specimens, FFPE tissue blocks, or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Emory University, and those of participating registries as required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The PLCO is a population-based multicenter randomized screening trial of people age 55 to 74 years at baseline with no history of prostate, lung, colorectal, or ovarian cancer between November 1993 and July 2001 (NCT00339495)41. At study entry, participants completed a self-administered baseline questionnaire to obtain demographic information, risk factor data, and medical history. Incident breast cancer cases were ascertained through annual study update forms mailed to participants and confirmed by review of medical records and cancer registries. Cancer data was collected up to December 31, 2009, and mortality data was collected from death certificates through 2015. Untreated FFPE tissue blocks were obtained from pathology laboratories of the participating centers. Women gave written informed consent, and protocols were approved by review boards at the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and individual centers. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Tissue collection and whole slide images (WSI)

In CPS-II and CPS-3, participants who reported cancer diagnosis were sent a consent form to obtain their medical record and information of the facility where their surgery took place and tissues stored39,40. Of the 4403 and 2037 consent forms that were mailed to breast cancer patients in CPS-II and CPS-3, respectively, 3576 (81.2%) and 1089 (53.5%) written consents had been returned at the time of initiation of this project (accrual of cases and consents in CPS-3 is on-going). Requests were sent to source pathology laboratories to obtain tissues for these patients, and the most representative tissue blocks and/or tissues slides were received for 1729 (48.6%) and 998 (91.6%) of the patients, respectively. Some pathology laboratories only sent a single H&E stained slide per patient, which was included in this analysis. If more than one slide (or block) was sent, a pathologist at the Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN) identified the most representative H&E slide (or block), which was used in the analysis. CPS-II and CPS-3 H&E slides were scanned at Emory University using the NanoZoomer 2.0-HT slide scanner by Hamamatsu Photonics at a 40×magnification to create WSIs13.

The information on tissue collection for the PLCO has been previously published42 and additional information is available at https://cdas.cancer.gov/learn/plco/images/pathology/. Briefly, the PLCO pathology breast tissue collection started in 2006. Subjects who had a pathologically confirmed breast cancer diagnosis (N = 4351) and who had signed the PLCO etiologic studies consent form (ESC) were eligible for selection. Patients with tumor size 0.5 cm or greater were selected from all eligible subjects (N = 1852); those who were included in the Cancer Genetic Markers of Susceptibility (CGEMS) project43 or had invasive cancers were preferentially selected42. Pathology reports of selected participants were reviewed by qualified PLCO trial staff and a consulting pathologist to select 1 to 3 representative tissue blocks from each patient: 2 blocks containing tumors of the dominant histologic features and 1 block containing adjacent normal tissue. One 4-micron H&E stained section per block was prepared and reviewed by a pathologist. H&E slides were imaged at 40x magnification with standard brightfield settings utilizing the Aperio AT2 whole slide scanner (Leica) at the Cancer Genomics Research Laboratory (CGR) at the NCI. A total of 1012 patients with invasive breast cancers had WSIs available. Scanned images from patients with ER, PR, and HER2 status available were provided by the NCI staff for our previous analysis (N = 461)13. They were also included in this analysis to increase the representation of our analytical population and the sample size.

Quantification of histological features

Details of the tissue segmentation of the WSIs and feature extraction were previously described13. Briefly, tissue regions and cell nuclei in the slide were delineated using a segmentation convolutional neural network (CNN) model, “MuTILs”13,44. The MuTILs architecture is composed of two U-Net CNN models that work in parallel to segment regions and nuclei at resolutions of 1 μm and 0.5 μm per pixel (MPP), respectively. These regions and nuclei were then used to extract several morphological and contextual features as described in Amgad et al.13. The features were aggregated to obtain patient-level data from tissue-region-level, cell-level, region of interest-level, and slide-level data. These values were normalized to the amount of tissue analyzed. Each patient sample was described by a set of 110 patient-level histological features (Supplemental Data 1). They include global features such as overall cell density, overall cancer cell density, overall TIL density within the WSI. They also included region of interest morphology features, nuclear morphology, TIL activation, epithelial nuclear atypia, CAF epithelialization, cytoplasmic texture and staining, local cell density, local cell clustering, region composition, region neighborhood composition, acellular stromal matrix and collagen, and interactions between them13. Histological features were classified under five major themes: epithelial features, stromal features, TIL features, necrosis abundance, and features capturing various interactions between different cells.

Statistical analysis

Each histological feature was standardized by dividing its original value by the standard deviation. Linear regression was used to assess the association (β) and 95% CI between sixteen well-established BC etiological factors (predictors) and the standardized TTME features (outcome). BC etiological factors were grouped into demographic, hormonal, lifestyle, and non-modifiable factors. Associations were adjusted for age, cohort study, estrogen receptor (ER) status, and stage. Grade was not included in the models because it may lie downstream of TTME morphological features, and adjustment for it could induce collider bias45,46. Subgroup analysis stratified by ER status was also performed. Cox proportional regression models were performed to estimate HRs and 95% CI for the associations between the standardized TTME features and BC-specific survival adjusted for the same variables as above. Associations between BC etiological factors and clinical characteristics of the tumors were estimated using logistic regression. Statistical significance was defined by the FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to account for multiple testing.

We explored whether the association between BMI and BCSM could be partially explained by features of the TTME using the method proposed by VanderWeele and Vansteelandt47. Mediating associations were assessed separately for each of the mediators considered (features of the TTME). Two models were specified to estimate effects and HRs for mediation, adjusting for the exposure (BMI). In the outcome model, the risk of BCSM was regressed on both the mediator and the exposure using a Cox proportional hazards regression model. Each mediator was also regressed on the exposure. The total effect was calculated from a multivariable Cox model assessing BMI and risk of BMSC. Given that an interaction between BMI and features of the TTME were not observed, we did not include an interaction between exposure and mediator in the models. All models were adjusted for age and study, as noted in Fig. 4. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation). Mediation analysis was perform using the cmest function in the CMAverse R package.
