Effect of Mindfulness Training on the Clinical Pregnancy Rate in Women Undergoing Frozen Embryo Transfer: Study Protocol for a Single-Center, Open-Label, Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Songping Yi, Junyan Zhang, Aimin Li, Na Zhou, Jing Li, Ruxue Cao, Yuan Gao, Xiaolei Liu, Jiajia Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effect of mindfulness training in addition to essential psychological counseling on the clinical pregnancy rate in women undergoing frozen embryo transfer (FET), and to explore the potential mediating role of neuropsychological improvements.Methods and AnalysisThis study will be conducted as a single-center, parallel-group, open-label, exploratory randomized controlled trial. Eligible women aged 18–45 years undergoing FET who present with psychological distress (mild to moderate anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, poor sleep quality, or psychosocial stress overload) will be recruited. Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the Mindfulness Training Group (MTG), which delivers structured formal mindfulness practice, or the Essential Psychological Counseling Group (EPCG), which provides supportive psychoeducation and emotional guidance without mindfulness-based training. The primary endpoint will be the clinical pregnancy rate. Secondary endpoints include changes in anxiety symptoms, depressive symptom, sleep quality, and psychosocial stress; the live birth rate will be assessed exploratorily. Mediation analyses will be performed to assess whether neuropsychological improvements explain the relationship between mindfulness training and clinical pregnancy. A total of 434 participants will be enrolled, allowing for a 20% dropout rate.Trial RegistrationThis trial is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2600116827).Keywords: mindfulness, psychological distress, clinical pregnancy, frozen embryo transfer, randomized controlled trial

Background

Against the backdrop of the growing global burden of infertility, assisted reproductive technology (ART) has become an essential therapeutic approach. Frozen embryo transfer (FET) is widely applied in contemporary ART practice because of its procedural flexibility, favorable safety profile, and stable reproductive outcomes. However, despite continuous technical refinements, improvements in clinical pregnancy rates following FET remain limited, highlighting persistent challenges in treatment optimization.1

Current evidence suggests that endometrial receptivity after embryo transfer is a critical determinant of FET success, and growing attention has therefore been directed toward factors that may influence this process, including psychological stress in women undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF).2 During treatment, patients commonly experience cumulative stress arising from medical procedures, psychosocial pressures related to infertility, and uncertainty surrounding pregnancy outcomes.3,4 Prolonged or excessive stress has been shown to interfere with reproductive function through integrated neuroendocrine and immune-related processes, thereby potentially compromising reproductive physiology and implantation conditions.5,6 Clinical and epidemiological studies have consistently reported associations between elevated psychological distress and adverse reproductive outcomes, including increased risk of miscarriage and reduced likelihood of achieving clinical pregnancy.7–9 In parallel, sleep disturbances, which frequently coexist with psychological stress during fertility treatment, have also been linked to altered reproductive outcomes, further supporting the relevance of neuropsychological factors in the reproductive microenvironment.10

Mindfulness training, as an evidence-based psychological intervention, has been increasingly explored for its potential to improve health outcomes under conditions of sustained stress. Mindfulness-based interventions are associated with small to moderate improvements in stress and psychological outcomes, some of which appear to be sustained over time.11 In addition to stress regulation, emerging research indicates that mindfulness-based interventions may be associated with broader biological adaptations relevant to reproductive health, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely characterized.12 Together, these effects may be relevant to outcomes of assisted reproductive treatment. Within reproductive medicine, mindfulness interventions have been proposed as a supportive strategy capable of modulating stress responses and improving overall psychological well-being during fertility treatment.13

Although a systematic review reported an overall improvement in pregnancy rates associated with psychological interventions across infertile populations, regardless of intervention characteristics, the included trials were not specifically designed for women with clinically defined neuropsychological distress undergoing assisted reproductive treatment.14 Against this background, the present study is designed to evaluate the impact of mindfulness training on clinical pregnancy rates in women undergoing IVF with FET who exhibit neuropsychological distress. In addition, this randomized controlled trial is designed to evaluate whether mindfulness training can improve the clinical pregnancy rate (CPR) in women with neuropsychological distress or allostatic overload undergoing FET, potentially through concurrent improvements in anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, sleep quality, and psychosocial stress.

Methods

Study Design and Grouping

This study is a single-center, parallel, open-label, exploratory randomized controlled trial, conducted in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.15

Eligible participants will be randomly assigned to the Mindfulness Training Group (MTG), which will receive mindfulness training in addition to Essential Psychological Counseling Group (EPCG), which will receive Essential Psychological Counseling only (Figure 1).

Neuropsychological Assessments

In this study, anxiety symptoms will be assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7),16,17 depressive symptoms will be assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9),16,18,19 sleep will be using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI),20 and stress is assessed using the PsychoSocial Index (PSI).21,22 The Chinese versions of these questionnaires are available.23–26

Study Phases and Corresponding Objectives

The screening phase is designed to identify eligible participants with neuropsychological distress. After provision of screening informed consent, baseline neuropsychological assessments will be conducted to determine eligibility, followed by trial informed consent for participants who meet the inclusion criteria before randomization (Figure 1).

The core phase includes a standardized 4-week intervention period for all randomized participants, followed by clinical pregnancy assessment according to routine clinical practice. Because the timing of ultrasound confirmation of clinical pregnancy may vary across individuals, the final outcome ascertainment may occur approximately 5–6 weeks after embryo transfer (Figure 1).

The extension phase focuses on longer-term outcomes beyond early pregnancy. Participants who achieve clinical pregnancy will be followed to assess live birth outcomes and sustained changes in psychosocial stress, enabling exploratory evaluation of longer-term intervention effects over approximately 33–35 weeks after embryo transfer (Figure 1).

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of the core phase is the proportion of participants achieving clinical pregnancy in each group. The secondary endpoints include changes in GAD-7, PHQ-9, and PSQI from baseline to the end of the core phase, as well as changes in PSI from baseline to the end of the extension phase. The exploratory endpoints include the live birth rate. Exploratory analyses will further assess the direct effect of mindfulness training on clinical pregnancy and its potential mediating effects through improvements in anxiety, depression, and sleep quality.

Study Procedures

Screening and Neuropsychological Assessment

Potential participants scheduled for frozen embryo transfer will first be invited to sign a Screening Informed Consent (SIC), indicating their agreement to undergo screening procedures. After providing SIC, participants will complete baseline neuropsychological assessments as part of the screening process, including GAD-7, PHQ-9, PSQI, and PSI. These assessments will be administered face-to-face by scanning a QR code on a smartphone, and data will be uploaded to a secure backend system for evaluation. All assessments will be conducted by trained physicians or nurses and led by a qualified neurologist with subspecialty expertise in neuropsychology.

Participants who meet the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria based on the screening assessments will then be invited to sign the Trial Informed Consent (TIC) for participation in the randomized controlled trial. Only participants who have provided TIC will proceed to randomization. Eligible and consented participants will subsequently be enrolled and randomized to the assigned intervention groups.

Randomization and Blinding

Allocation

Randomization will be conducted by an independent statistical party. Participants will be enrolled by the investigators and then assigned sequentially according to the predetermined random sequence.

If an individual withdraws informed consent immediately after allocation and before receiving any study intervention or completing any post-randomization assessment, no further study procedures will be performed. Such cases will be documented as post-randomization withdrawal before intervention initiation. The randomization record will be retained, and any replacement or additional recruitment, if required to maintain the planned sample size, will use the next allocation in the prespecified randomization sequence rather than invalidating or reusing the original allocation.

Concealment

To ensure standardization of the grouping procedure and to minimize the risk of selection bias, allocation concealment will be strictly maintained. The complete randomization list will be stored in an encrypted electronic file and will be independently preserved by the statistical party. After each participant completes screening and eligibility is confirmed, the research team will notify the statistical party. According to the predetermined randomization sequence, the statistical party will then verify the allocation and return the group assignment to the investigator. During the entire process, recruitment and assessment personnel will remain blinded to upcoming allocations, thereby ensuring effective allocation concealment, reducing potential selection bias, and maintaining the internal validity of the trial. Any modification of the randomization scheme is strictly prohibited.

Blinding

Because the interventions involve mindfulness training (MT) and essential psychological counseling (EPC), blinding is not feasible in this study.

Avoidance of Contamination

In the outpatient setting, participants undergoing IVF may exchange information with each other. To minimize the risk of information contamination between groups, standard post-IVF outpatient visits will be scheduled on different days for participants assigned to different interventions. These follow-up visits are part of the standard care required after IVF and will be arranged accordingly. This arrangement does not alter the standard medical care procedures.

Essential Psychological Counseling

As a standardized background intervention, all randomized participants in both study groups will receive the same EPC program throughout the core phase. Participants will access the stress-reduction training program by scanning a QR code via WeChat, which will direct them to the designated online platform. The EPC program consists of self-guided stress-reduction modules designed to support emotional regulation, stress coping, and psychological adaptation during frozen embryo transfer. Participants may freely select training modules according to their personal preferences, without restrictions on module selection, training duration, session frequency, or overall schedule. The program includes structured modules across three progressive levels (introductory, intermediate, and advanced), including mindful listening, body scan, stretching, mindful walking, awareness of thoughts and emotions, imagery-based meditation (mountain, sea, lake), loving-kindness practice, and sitting meditation (Table 2). All modules will be accompanied by voice guidance and soothing background music.

The mindfulness method is based on the literature30 with appropriate adaptations, as follows: Weekly centralized sessions covering various formal meditation techniques, including seated meditation, body sweep/body scan, mindful yoga movements, and walking meditation. Each session will include approximately 30 minutes of live guided instruction and supervised practice. Outside of class, participants are also required to integrate these mindfulness practices into daily life and persist with home practice. In most studies, the total practice time is approximately 28 hours, distributed across these 8 sessions; additionally, participants are required to perform daily independent guided practice using provided 30-minute audio CDs, which is recorded. Participants will also be encouraged to continue self-directed home practice using recorded materials between guided sessions and to integrate mindfulness practices into daily life. The present study adopts a flexible remotely delivered format to improve feasibility and adherence during the relatively limited clinical window between embryo transfer and clinical pregnancy assessment. In addition, the online platform will automatically record participants’ engagement with recorded materials, including access frequency and practice duration, to facilitate monitoring of intervention adherence throughout the study period.

Data Collection and Management

Before Randomization

Before randomization, all participants will undergo baseline neuropsychological assessments using the GAD-7, PHQ-9, PSQI, and PSI, which will serve both for eligibility screening and as baseline measurements.

After Randomization

Baseline Characteristics

After randomization, baseline characteristics will be recorded for each participant, including age, body mass index (BMI), years of education, duration of infertility, smoking and alcohol use, comorbidities (eg, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease), and parity.

Baseline Laboratory Tests

Serum levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH, μIU/mL), luteinizing hormone (LH, μIU/mL), estradiol (E2, pg/mL), progesterone (P, ng/mL), testosterone (T, ng/mL), and prolactin (PRL, ng/mL) will be measured using commercial kits (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics) with an automatic chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer. In addition, thyroid function, fasting blood glucose, liver function, renal function, and blood lipid profiles will be assessed.

Baseline Imaging and Instrumental Examinations

Routine clinical examinations, including electrocardiogram and chest X-ray, will be performed. Transvaginal ultrasound will be used to assess endometrial thickness.

Follow-Up Assessments

Biochemical pregnancy will be assessed by serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) at 2 weeks after embryo transfer. Clinical pregnancy will be confirmed by the presence of gestational sacs on ultrasound at 4 weeks. Neuropsychological assessments, including GAD-7, PHQ-9, and PSQI, will be repeated at the end of week 4.

Reporting Summary and Data Preservation

All study data will be collected, managed, and archived in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Source documents, electronic case report forms, informed consent forms, and laboratory records will be securely stored and made available for monitoring, auditing, and regulatory inspection. All study records will be retained for at least 5 years after study completion.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

Continuous variables will be described as the mean with standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, or as the median with interquartile range (IQR) presented as the 1st quartile to the 3rd quartile (Q1-Q3) for non-normally distributed data. The Student’s t-test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney test was employed for data with non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were represented as counts and percentages. The statistical significance of categorical variables was assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical Inference

Primary Endpoint

For the primary endpoint, multivariable logistic regression will be performed if covariate adjustment is required. Variables with p ≤ 0.10 in univariate analyses will be considered as candidate variables for the initial multivariable model.31,32 A backward stepwise selection procedure will then be applied, and variables will be retained in the final model if they remain statistically relevant during model selection. In addition, clinically important baseline variables with established associations with reproductive outcomes, such as age, body mass index, and duration of infertility, may be retained regardless of their univariate p-values to ensure clinical interpretability of the model. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be reported. All p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Secondary Endpoint

The secondary endpoints including the changes of GAD-7, PHQ-9, PSQI from baseline to the end of the core phase, and PSI changes from the baseline to the end of extension phase. Between-group differences will be expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR), depending on the data distribution. These analysis will not be adjusted for covariates in multivariate analyses, nor will they be included in considerations of α inflation. The resulting p-values will be reported as nominal and will not be interpreted as statistical inference.

Exploratory Endpoints

The exploratory endpoints include evaluation of the direct effect of mindfulness training on the CPR and its potential indirect effects mediated through changes in anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, sleep quality, and allostatic overload. Causal mediation analysis based on the counterfactual framework will be applied to decompose the total effect into natural direct and indirect effects, with appropriate adjustment for relevant covariates.

Analysis Datasets

This study will perform statistical analyses using both the Intention-To-Treat (ITT) dataset and the Per-Protocol (PP) dataset. The ITT dataset analysis will include participants who change groups or discontinue participation during the study, with their endpoint considered as no clinical pregnancy. Participants lost to follow-up will also be regarded as having no clinical pregnancy. The ITT dataset will provide the primary results of the study, while the PP dataset analysis, conducted based on the groups to which participants actually belonged and limited to those who completed the study, will serve as a sensitivity analysis to corroborate the ITT findings.

Sample Size Consideration

The CPR in the MTG was estimated at 55% based on findings from a representative randomized trial.33 Considering the documented adverse effects of neuropsychological distress on reproductive outcomes, the CPR in the EPCG was conservatively estimated at 40%. The α level was set at 0.05 (two-sided), with a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.2). Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, approximately 217 participants were required per group, resulting in a planned total sample size of 434 participants corresponds to 217 participants in each study group.

Discussion

Previous studies have suggested that mindfulness-based interventions may influence stress-related neuroendocrine regulation, including hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity, which may be relevant to reproductive physiology and endometrial receptivity.34,35 However, whether a relatively short-term intervention delivered during the limited peri-transfer window can translate into measurable improvements in reproductive outcomes remains incompletely understood. The present trial therefore further explores the potential relationship between mindfulness-associated neuropsychological improvement and reproductive outcomes in women undergoing frozen embryo transfer.

This study is designed to evaluate the potential effect of mindfulness training on clinical pregnancy outcomes in women undergoing frozen embryo transfer who present with psychological distress or allostatic overload. Unlike previous mindfulness-related studies in assisted reproduction, which have primarily focused on general psychological symptoms or self-reported well-being in unselected infertility populations, the present trial specifically targets women with psychological distress or allostatic overload, evaluates clinical pregnancy as the primary reproductive endpoint, and further explores potential neuropsychological mediation pathways under real-world clinical conditions. By integrating reproductive outcomes with standardized neuropsychological assessments and planned mediation analyses, this trial may provide new evidence regarding whether improvements in anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, sleep quality, and psychosocial stress are associated with clinical pregnancy outcomes in this specific population.

As a pragmatic behavioral intervention conducted under real-world clinical conditions, this study also has several inherent limitations. Because of the behavioral nature of mindfulness training, participant blinding is not feasible, which may influence self-reported psychological outcomes through expectancy effects and other non-specific intervention effects, although the primary reproductive endpoint is based on objective clinical assessment. In addition, participants in the MTG may receive greater structured interaction and guided engagement through instructor-guided online mindfulness sessions beyond the mindfulness-specific components of the intervention. Therefore, if between-group differences are observed, it may not be possible to completely distinguish mindfulness-specific effects from non-specific effects related to intervention engagement and interpersonal interaction. Furthermore, although follow-up visits for participants assigned to different interventions will be scheduled on separate days to reduce the risk of information exchange, residual contamination between groups cannot be completely excluded, particularly in the context of digital communication and social media among patients undergoing IVF treatment. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, and future studies using attention-matched control designs may further help isolate mindfulness-specific effects.

Conclusion

This trial is expected to provide clinically relevant evidence for integrating structured psychological interventions into routine assisted reproductive care. Specifically, this study will evaluate whether the addition of structured mindfulness training to standardized background psychological support is associated with improved clinical pregnancy outcomes during the core phase and subsequent live birth outcomes during extension-phase follow-up, while also exploring potential improvements in psychological well-being during treatment. By addressing the current lack of high-quality randomized evidence in this area, this study may help bridge an important gap in the existing literature and inform future clinical practice.
