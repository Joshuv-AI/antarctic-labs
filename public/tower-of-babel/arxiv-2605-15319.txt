Extended abstract: Canonical join complex and cubical coordinates for all framing lattices
Jonah Berggren, Clément Chenevière

Abstract
This document is an extended abstract for two articles in preparation. Recently, framing lattices were introduced to generalize many classical lattices such as the Tamari lattice and the weak order on 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} . We define bricks and brick cliques as a combinatorial model for join-irreducible elements and canonical join representations in all framing lattices, generalizing noncrossing arc diagrams of (Reading, 2015) for the weak order on 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} . Our model captures the natural bijection between join and meet canonical representations, as well as duality upon reflections of the framed graph. The proof is bijective, with an explicit reconstruction algorithm in two steps. A useful intermediate construction in our bijective proof is our new definition of cornered cliques. These enable us to define cubical coordinates on a framing lattice, generalizing bracket vectors for the Tamari lattice and providing an efficient comparison criterion.

Introduction

Flow polytopes model the space of
flows
on a directed acyclic graph, and are a fundamental object of combinatorial optimization with relations to many fields such as representation theory
[
BV08
,
Bel+24
]
, Grothendieck polynomials
[
LMSD19
]
, and algebraic geometry
[
EM16
]
.
Danilov, Karzanov, and Koshevoy
[
DKK12
]
defined special
framing triangulations
of flow polytopes given a choice of a local order on all edges of internal vertices, called a framing of the graph. Simplices of the triangulation indexed combinatorially by
maximal cliques
of the framed graph – i.e., collections of routes which are pairwise noncrossing with respect to the framing. The maximal cliques (hence, the maximal simplices of these framing triangulations) were given the structure of a
framing lattice
by von Bell and Ceballos
[
BC24
]
. Framing lattices are polygonal, semidistributive, and congruence uniform
[
BC24
]
, and they serve as a common generalization of many classical lattices such as the weak order on the symmetric group
[
GD+25
]
and the Tamari lattice
[
Bel+23
]
.

It follows from semidistributivity that a framing lattice admits a
canonical join complex
, which is the simplicial complex of canonical join representations of elements of the lattice
[
Bar19
]
. For some semidistributive lattices, such as the weak order on the symmetric group and the Tamari lattice, Reading introduced noncrossing arc diagrams as a combinatorial model for the canonical join complex
[
Rea15
]
. In this abstract, we generalize the model of Reading to all framing lattices by introducing collections of noncrossing
bricks
, henceforth referred to as
brick cliques
, as a combinatorial model for canonical join representations.
Covering relations in the framing lattice are naturally labeled by bricks, and the collection of labels of lower covers of a maximal clique forms its canonical join representation.
Conversely, we build an explicit algorithm to reconstruct the maximal clique associated to a brick clique.

Moreover, we introduce
left-cornered cliques
(dually, right-cornered cliques) as an intermediate object in our bijection between brick cliques and maximal cliques.
Hence, elements of the framing lattice may be equivalently described by maximal cliques, left-cornered cliques, or brick cliques.
Through a (co)rank map on the left-cornered clique representations, we obtain
cubical coordinates
on the framing lattice which are analogues of bracket vectors for the Tamari lattice
[
HT72
]
, as comparison in the framing lattice translates to componentwise comparison in these coordinates. In the process, we attach to each maximal clique of routes a set of left-clockwise bricks, which play a role analogue to inversions for the weak order.

2
Preliminaries on framing lattices

We start by recalling some background on framing lattices, and we refer to
[
BC24
]
and the references therein for a more detailed account.
A
flow graph

G
is a directed acyclic graph with vertex set
V(G)=[n]
and a multiset
E(G)
of edges, all directed from a smaller to a larger vertex. A path from a source to a sink is called a
route
.

A
framing

F
of
G
is a choice at each internal vertex
v
of total orders on incoming and on outgoing edges at
v
, denoted respectively
<_{\text{in}(v),F}
and
<_{\text{out}(v),F}
. The pair
\Gamma=(G,F)
is called a
framed graph
. An example of framed graph is displayed at the top of
Figure

2
.
Following
[
BC24
]
, we draw a framed graph so that incoming (resp. outgoing) edges are increasing in the framing from top to bottom, in which case we may omit the framing.

When two routes share an internal vertex
v
, we can view their suffix after
v
as words on edges, and use the framing on outgoing edges to compare them in the lexicographic order. This provides a preorder on all routes passing through
v
called the
outgoing preorder at
v
and denoted
\leq^{+}_{v,F}
. The
incoming preorder at
v

\leq^{-}_{v,F}
is defined similarly by comparing prefixes.
We can also define an
outgoing order

\leq^{+}_{F}
as an order on all routes, where two routes starting with different source edges are incomparable, and compared in the corresponding outgoing preorder otherwise. An
incoming order

\leq^{-}_{F}
is defined similarly, and we often omit
F
from the notation when it is clear from the context.

If two routes
p
and
q
share an internal vertex
v
,
p
is
clockwise
to
q
at
v
if
p<^{-}_{v}q
and
p>^{+}_{v}q
. Two routes are
coherent at
v
if neither is clockwise to the other at
v
, and
coherent
if coherent at every internal vertex
v
.
If
p
and
q
are incoherent at
v
, their maximal common subpath containing
v
is called a
subroute of conflict
. With the drawing conventions, incoherences appear visually as crossings.

A
maximal clique

\Delta
is a maximal collection of pairwise coherent routes. All maximal cliques have the same number of routes, namely
\lvert E(G)\rvert-\lvert V(G)\rvert+s(G)+t(G)
, where
s(G)
(resp.
t(G)
) is the number of sources (resp. sinks) of
G
. Two cliques are adjacent when they share all but one route.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
BC24
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be two adjacent maximal cliques, and
r
and
r^{\prime}
the routes of
\Delta\setminus\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime}\setminus\Delta
. The routes
r
and
r^{\prime}
have a unique subroute of conflict
\sigma
.
Moreover, writing
r=r_{1}\sigma r_{2}
and
r^{\prime}=r^{\prime}_{1}\sigma r^{\prime}_{2}
, the routes
r_{1}\sigma r^{\prime}_{2}
and
r^{\prime}_{1}\sigma r_{2}
are both in
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}
.

We can build a directed graph on maximal cliques with edges between adjacent cliques. Using the notations of
Proposition

2.1
, the edge is oriented from
\Delta
to
\Delta^{\prime}
if
r
is clockwise to
r^{\prime}
at their subroute of conflict, and replacing
r
with
r^{\prime}
is called a
counterclockwise rotation
. von Bell and Ceballos proved that this directed graph is the Hasse diagram of a semidistributive lattice called the
framing lattice

\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}

[
BC24
]
.

The
up-down reflection
of the framed graph
\Gamma
is the framed graph
\Gamma^{*}=(G,F^{*})
, where the underlying graph is unchanged but the outgoing and incoming orders at each vertex are reversed. Similarly, the
left-right reflection
of
\Gamma
is the framed graph
\Gamma^{T}=(G^{T},F^{T})
, where
G^{T}
is the graph obtained from
G
by reversing all edges, and the incoming and outgoing orders at each vertex have been exchanged. Up-down and left-right reflections correspond to the horizontal and vertical reflections of our pictures, respectively. Both of these operations dualize the framing lattice.

Example 2.2
.

The weak order on the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
can be realized as a framing lattice on the oruga graph, which has vertex set
[0,n]
and two edges
(i-1,i)
for all
i
. The framing is such that the outgoing order at vertex
i
matches the incoming order at vertex
i+1
.
Routes on the oruga graph can be seen as binary words where going down is a
0
and going up is a
1
. Given a permutation with one-line notation
i_{1}\dots i_{n}
, we associate the collection
\{p_{0},\dots,p_{k}\}
of binary words, where
p_{k}
has
k
ones, at positions
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
.
An example for
n=3
can be seen in
Figure

2
, where on the left, the permutations are displayed as cliques of coherent routes and with their one-line notation.

3
Bricks and canonical join complex

Our first contribution is the definition of left and right corners, which we will allow to be the start and end, respectively, of generalized paths of
\Gamma
.
A
left corner
of
\Gamma
is a triple
{\bf{c}}=({\bf{c}}^{\bullet},{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow},{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow})
where
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
is an internal vertex of
G
and
{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow},{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow}
are two incoming edges of
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
, consecutive in the framing. A
right corner

{\bf{c}}=({\bf{c}}^{\bullet},{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow},{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow})
is defined similarly with consecutive outgoing edges of
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
.
Note that
{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow}
is larger than
{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow}
in the framing, so it is drawn below
{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow}
, and arrows in superscript point towards the corner.

We generalize paths by letting paths of
G
start or end with respectively left or right corners.
More formally, a
generalized path

\overline{\sigma}
of
\Gamma
is a composition
L(\overline{\sigma})\sigma R(\overline{\sigma})
where
\sigma
is a path from
v
to
v^{\prime}
,
L(\overline{\sigma})
is either
v
or a left corner with
\left(L(\overline{\sigma})\right)^{\bullet}=v
, and
R(\overline{\sigma})
is either
v^{\prime}
or a right corner with
\left(R(\overline{\sigma})\right)^{\bullet}=v^{\prime}
. When
L(\overline{\sigma})
is a left corner and
R(\overline{\sigma})
is a vertex, we call
\overline{\sigma}
a
left-cornered path
, and a
left-cornered route
if furthermore
R(\overline{\sigma})
is a sink. Right-cornered paths and routes are defined similarly. We call
\overline{\sigma}
a
brick
if
L(\overline{\sigma})
and
R(\overline{\sigma})
are corners. Routes, cornered routes and bricks are called
generalized routes
.

We say that a generalized path
\overline{\sigma}

passes through
a vertex
v
if
v
is a vertex of
\sigma
.
We extend the framing order
\leq_{\text{in}(v),F}
to a total order on incoming edges and left corners of
v
by setting
{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow}<_{\text{in}(v),F}{\bf{c}}<_{\text{in}(v),F}{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow}
, and similarly for
\leq_{\text{out}(v),F}
.
We also extend naturally the outgoing and incoming preorders at
v
to all generalized routes passing through
v
.

If
\overline{\sigma}
and
\overline{\tau}
are two generalized routes passing through
v
, we say that
\overline{\sigma}
is
clockwise
to
\overline{\tau}
at
v
if
\overline{\sigma}<^{-}_{v}\overline{\tau}
and
\overline{\sigma}>^{+}_{v}\overline{\tau}
. They are
weakly coherent
(or noncrossing) if neither is clockwise to the other at any vertex, and they are
coherent
if they additionally do not share a left or a right corner.
Two generalized routes are incoherent when they have a crossing, as pictured on
Figure

3
.
A
left-cornered clique
is a maximal collection of pairwise coherent left-cornered routes.
We easily see that left-cornered cliques have one left-cornered route starting at each left corner.

Right-cornered cliques
are defined similarly. A
brick clique
is a (possibly empty) collection of pairwise coherent bricks.
The
brick complex
is the simplicial complex of brick cliques.
In the rest of this section, we prove that the brick complex is isomorphic to the canonical join complex of
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
. We first recall a few theoretical results on semidistributive lattices, for which we refer to
[
Bar19
]
.

Figure 3:
The bricks on the left are incoherent, while those on the right are coherent.

An element of a finite lattice is called
join-irreducible
(resp. meet-irreducible) if it covers (resp. is covered by) a unique element.
A lattice is distributive if the join and meet operations distribute over each other. Semidistributivity is a weakening of distributivity.

A key consequence of semidistributivity is that every element admits a “minimal” expression as the join of join-irreducible elements, called its
canonical join representation
.
There is moreover a canonical way to label covering relations of the lattice with join irreducible elements in such a way that the canonical join representation of an element is the set of labels of its lower covers.
The
canonical join complex

\mathop{CJC}(L)
of
L
is the simplicial complex whose faces are canonical join representations.
Canonical meet representation and the canonical meet complex
\mathop{CMC}(L)
are defined analogously, and for a semidistributive lattice

\mathop{CJC}(L)
and
\mathop{CMC}(L)
are isomorphic and flag
[
Bar19
]
.

We first extend
Proposition

4.8
to label covering relations of the
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
with bricks, and define two maps
\DownBricks
and
\UpBricks
from maximal cliques to brick cliques.

Lemma 3.1
.

In the situation of
Proposition

2.1
, the last (resp. first) edges of paths
r_{1}
and
r^{\prime}_{1}
(resp.
r_{2}
and
r^{\prime}_{2}
) are consecutive in the framing and define a left corner
{\bf{c}}_{1}
(resp. right corner
{\bf{c}}_{2}
). We label the covering relation with the brick
{\bf{c}}_{1}\sigma{\bf{c}}_{2}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Two bricks labeling lower (resp. upper) covering relations of a clique
\Delta
are coherent.

Theorem 3.3
.

The map
\DownBricks
that sends a maximal clique
\Delta
to the set of bricks labeling its lower covering relations is a bijection from maximal cliques of routes to brick cliques.

The map
\UpBricks
defined similarly with upper covering relations of
\Delta
is also a bijection.

Corollary 3.4
.

Join-irreducible (resp. meet-irreducible) elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
are in bijection with bricks. The brick complex is isomorphic to
\mathop{CJC}(\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma})
(resp.
\mathop{CMC}(\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma})
) via this bijection.

In order to prove
Theorem

3.3
, we define the inverse of the map
\DownBricks
through an explicit reconstruction algorithm in two steps.
We define a first map
\Phi_{L}
from brick cliques to left-cornered cliques, then a second one
\Psi_{L}
to maximal cliques. The left-right reflection of
\Gamma
defines a ‘right’ version
\Phi_{R}
and
\Psi_{R}
of the reconstruction maps, and the up-down reflection give a ‘dual’ version of the reconstruction, inverse to
\DownBricks
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
T
be a brick clique and
{\bf{c}}
a left corner of
\Gamma
.
We define the
reconstruction

\Phi_{L}(T,{\bf{c}})
by growing a left-cornered path starting with
\overline{\sigma}_{0}={\bf{c}}
.
Iteratively define
\overline{\sigma}_{i+1}=\overline{\sigma}_{i}e_{i+1}
, where
e_{i+1}
is the largest outgoing edge of
v_{i}=R(\overline{\sigma}_{i})
such that for all bricks
\overline{\tau}
of
T
with
v_{i}\in\overline{\tau}
, if
\overline{\sigma}_{i}\leq^{-}_{v_{i}}\overline{\tau}
then also
e_{i+1}\leq^{+}_{v_{i}}\overline{\tau}
.
This process concludes with a left-cornered route
\overline{\sigma}_{k}
, which we call
\Phi_{L}(T,{\bf{c}})
.

By construction,
\Phi_{L}(T,{\bf{c}})
is the largest left-cornered route in
\leq^{+}_{\bf{c}}
that is weakly coherent with
T
, and with
\Phi_{L}(T,{\bf{c}})>^{-}_{{\bf{c}}^{*}}\overline{\tau}_{\bf{c}}
for the brick
\overline{\tau}_{\bf{c}}\in T
with left corner
{\bf{c}}
if any.
The
first reconstruction map

\Phi_{L}
sends a brick clique
T
to the collection
\{\Phi_{L}(T,{\bf{c}})\}

{\bf{c}}
runs over all left corners of

The second step of the reconstruction is very similar to the first, except that we begin with a sink edge or left-cornered route and iteratively pick the smallest possible incoming edge to extend our reconstructed path to the left.

Let

We now sketch the proof of
Theorem

3.3
, focusing on the map
\DownBricks
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Firstly, if
\Delta
is a maximal clique then
\DownBricks(\Delta)
is a brick clique by
Lemma

3.2
. If
T
is a brick clique, then
\Psi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L}(T)
is a collection of pairwise compatible routes. Moreover, since
\lvert E(G)\rvert-\lvert V(G)\rvert+s(G)+t(G)
is equal to the number of left corners and sink edges,
\Psi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L}(T)
is a maximal clique.

By construction, the routes of
\Psi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L}(T)
which can be rotated clockwise are exactly those reconstructed from left corners of bricks of

. Moreover, these bricks of

are exactly those labeling these lower covers, so that
\DownBricks(\Psi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L}(T))=T
.

Given a brick clique

, the route reconstructed from a left corner (resp. sink edge)

is by construction the most clockwise route containing
{\bf{d}}^{\uparrow}
(resp.
{\bf{d}}
), not clockwise to any brick of

but counterclockwise to the brick
\overline{\tau}_{\bf{d}}\in T
with

if any.
For each route

, there is a left corner or sink edge

such that

satisfies the above property with respect to

and

.

4
Cornered routes and cubical coordinates

We now further explore left-cornered cliques, which are the intermediate object appearing in the reconstruction algorithm.
We define explicitely the inverse map of the second reconstruction step.
Taking coranks in the outgoing order, we define left cubical coordinates on the framing lattices, named as such because they embed the Hasse diagram with edges in the direction of the cube.
In the process, we introduce left-incoherent bricks, which play a role analogue to left inversions for the weak order on the symmetric group.
We prove that comparison in the framing lattice corresponds to componentwise comparison of cubical coordinates, generalizing bracket vectors for the Tamari lattice and providing an efficient comparison criterion.

We start by defining upper and lower cornering routes of a corner. Since all routes within a clique are coherent, its subset of routes going through a vertex is totally ordered.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\bf{c}}=({\bf{c}}^{\bullet},{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow},{\bf{c}}^{\downarrow})
be a left corner of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
a maximal clique of routes. The
upper cornering route

r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
of
\Delta
at corner
{\bf{c}}
is the smallest route in
\Delta
using the edge
{\bf{c}}^{\uparrow}
. The
lower cornering route

r^{\downarrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
is defined symmetrically.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\bf{c}}
be a left corner of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
a maximal clique of routes. The routes
r^{\downarrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
and
r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
coincide after
{\bf{c}}
and are consecutive within
\Delta
in the incoming order.
In particular, a route
p
of
\Delta
can not be the lower (resp. upper) cornering route of
\Delta
at two different left corners.

Since
r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
and
r^{\downarrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
coincide after
{\bf{c}}
, they define a unique left-cornered route with corner
{\bf{c}}
, which we call the
left-cornered route

\overline{\sigma}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)

of
\Delta
at corner
{\bf{c}}
. We write
\Sigma_{L}(\Delta)
for the collection of all left-cornered routes of
\Delta
. We omit the proof of the following result, which is very similar to the one of
Theorem

3.3
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The set
\Sigma_{L}(\Delta)
forms a left-cornered clique. The
left-cornering map

\Sigma_{L}
is the inverse map of the second reconstruction step
\Psi_{L}
defined in
Definition

3.7
.

Recall that the outgoing order at
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
is a total order on all left-cornered routes with left corner
{\bf{c}}
.
We define the
left cubical coordinate

\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}})
of
\Delta
at corner
{\bf{c}}
as the corank of
\overline{\sigma}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
in the outgoing order.
The name is justified by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\Delta\lessdot\Delta^{\prime}
be a covering relation in the framing lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
.
The left cubical coordinates of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
differ at a single corner
{\bf{c}}
, with
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}})<\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime},{\bf{c}})
.

In order to prove
Theorem

4.4
, we introduce the notion of left-clockwise bricks, as analogues of inversions for the symmetric group. A brick
\overline{\sigma}
is
left-clockwise
to a route
p
if
\overline{\sigma}
is clockwise to
p
at its left corner
L(\overline{\sigma})
, i.e. if we have
\overline{\sigma}<_{v}^{-}p
and
\overline{\sigma}>_{v}^{+}p
for
v=L(\overline{\sigma})^{\bullet}
.
The brick is left-clockwise to a clique
\Delta
if it is left-clockwise to a route of
\Delta
. We have the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

For a brick
\overline{\sigma}
with left corner
{\bf{c}}
and a maximal clique
\Delta
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\overline{\sigma}
is left-clockwise to
\Delta
;

2.

\overline{\sigma}
is larger than the left-cornered route
\overline{\sigma}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
of
\Delta
in the outgoing order at
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
;

3.

\overline{\sigma}
is left-clockwise to the upper cornering route
r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
of
\Delta
at corner
{\bf{c}}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The set of bricks with left corner
{\bf{c}}
that are left-clockwise to a clique
\Delta
is an upper ideal in the outgoing order at
{\bf{c}}^{\bullet}
, and their number is equal to
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}})
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.4
.

Let
\overline{\sigma}
be the brick labeling a covering relation
\Delta\lessdot\Delta^{\prime}
, with left corner
L(\overline{\sigma})={\bf{c}}
.
Let
r
(resp.
r^{\prime}
) be the route of
\Delta\setminus\Delta^{\prime}
(resp.
\Delta^{\prime}\setminus\Delta
).
The lower cornering route of
\Delta
at
{\bf{c}}
is
r=r^{\downarrow}_{{\bf{c}}}(\Delta)
, and
\overline{\sigma}
is counterclockwise to
r
but clockwise to
r^{\prime}
, so by
Lemma

4.5
,
\overline{\sigma}
is left-clockwise to
\Delta^{\prime}
but not
\Delta
. By
Lemma

4.6
, we have
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}})<\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime},{\bf{c}})
.

Let
\overline{\tau}
be a brick with left corner
{\bf{c}}^{\prime}\neq{\bf{c}}
.
If
\overline{\tau}
is left-clockwise to
\Delta
, then by
Lemma

4.5
, it is incoherent with
p:=r^{\downarrow}_{{\bf{c}}^{\prime}}(\Delta)
. Since
r=r^{\downarrow}_{{\bf{c}}}(\Delta)
,
r\neq p
by
Lemma

4.2
, so that
p\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
\overline{\tau}
is left-clockwise to
\Delta^{\prime}
.
Conversely, reusing notations of
Proposition

2.1
,
\Delta
contains the two routes
r_{1}\sigma r_{2}^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}_{1}\sigma r_{2}
.
If
\overline{\tau}
is left-clockwise to
r^{\prime}
, it is left-clockwise to either
r_{1}\sigma r_{2}^{\prime}
or
r^{\prime}_{1}\sigma r_{2}
, so
\overline{\tau}
is left-clockwise to
\Delta
.
Hence,
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}}^{\prime})=\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime},{\bf{c}}^{\prime})
by
Lemma

4.6
.
∎

The motivating example is that the Tamari lattice (and all type
A
Cambrian lattices) can be realized as a framing lattice of the so-called caracol graph,
which has vertex set
[0,n+2]
and edges
(i,i+1)
for all
i
, as well as edges
(0,i)
and
(i,n+2)
for
2\leq i\leq n
. The framing that produces the Tamari lattice is the one where edges
(i,i+1)
are always the smallest in the outgoing and incoming orders
[
Bel+23
,
BC24
]
.

Example 4.7
.

For the Tamari lattice on the framed caracol graph,
the left cubical coordinates recover precisely the classical bracket vectors of
[
HT72
]
.

Not only do these cubical coordinates provide a nice embedding of framing lattices, but they also give an easy comparison criterion, as bracket vectors for the Tamari lattice.

Proposition 4.8

(
[
BC24
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be two maximal cliques of routes. We have
\Delta\not\leq\Delta^{\prime}
in the framing lattice if and only if a route of
\Delta^{\prime}
is clockwise to a route of
\Delta
at a vertex
v
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be two maximal cliques of routes. We have
\Delta\leq\Delta^{\prime}
in the framing lattice if and only if
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta)\leq\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime})
componentwise.

Proof.

If
\Delta\leq\Delta^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
, there is a sequence of covering relations from
\Delta
to
\Delta^{\prime}
, and by
Theorem

4.4
we conclude that
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta)\leq\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime})
componentwise.

Reciprocally, if
\Delta\not\leq\Delta^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
, by
Proposition

4.8
there is a route
r^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\prime}
clockwise to a route
r\in\Delta
at a vertex
v
. Taking
v
minimal with this property,
r
and
r^{\prime}
are not using the same incoming edge of
v
. Pick any left corner
{\bf{c}}
of
v
with
r^{\prime}<^{-}_{v}{\bf{c}}<^{-}_{v}r
.
Since
r^{\prime}
is clockwise to
r
at
v
, we have
r<^{+}_{v}r^{\prime}
.
By
Definition

4.1
,
r\geq^{+}_{v}r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)
and
r^{\prime}\leq^{+}_{v}r^{\downarrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta^{\prime})
, so that
r^{\uparrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta)<^{+}_{v}r^{\downarrow}_{\bf{c}}(\Delta^{\prime})
. Hence,
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta,{\bf{c}})>\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta^{\prime},{\bf{c}})
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Combining
Theorems

4.9
and
4.6
, we obtain yet another description of comparison in the framing lattice as inclusion of sets of left-clockwise bricks, hence our claim that they play a role analogue to inversions for the weak order. However, the meet operation does not correspond to intersection of sets of left-clockwise bricks.

Example 4.11
.

Recall from
Example

2.2
that the weak order on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
can be realized as a framing lattice on the oruga graph,
which has one left corner for each integer
i\in[n-1]
.
For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the left-cornered path
\overline{\sigma}_{i}(\Delta_{\pi})
uses the top edge between
j
and
j+1
if and only if
(i,j+1)
is an inversion of
\pi
.
The left cubical coordinate
\mathop{CC}_{L}(\Delta_{\pi},i)
is equal to
\sum_{(i,j)\in\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}2^{n-j}
, where the sum runs over inversions
(i,j)
of
\pi
with
i<j
.

The Lehmer code, which can be obtained by replacing
2^{n-j}
by
1
in the above formula, also produces a cubical embedding of the weak order in the sense of
Theorem

4.4
, but componentwise comparison does not recover the weak order as in
Theorem

4.9
.

In this section, we focused on left-cornered cliques, but of course all our constructions and results extend symmetrically to right-cornered cliques, allowing us to define right cubical coordinates, which give a different embedding of the framing lattice.
As a corollary of
Theorem

4.9
, we obtain that the order dimension of a framing lattice is at most the number of left (resp. right) corners of
\Gamma
—this bound is tight for the weak order.

We also remark that
Proposition

4.3
extends under up-down reflection.
Interestingly, the left-cornering map
\Sigma_{L}
is invariant under up-down reflection.
Hence,
\Psi_{L}
and
\Psi_{L}^{*}
are inverse to the same, so they coincide as maps on maximal cliques, even though for a given corner
{\bf{c}}
, we always have
\Psi_{L}(LT,{\bf{c}})\neq\Psi_{L}^{*}(LT,{\bf{c}})
.

5
Concluding remarks

We conclude this extended abstract with a few further directions of ongoing research.

Question 1
.

Refine our description of the canonical join complex of a framing lattice into a model for the canonical complex, generalizing semi-noncrossing arc bidiagrams
[
AP23
]
.

Question 2
.

Study rowmotion on framing lattices using the map

\Psi_{L}\circ\Phi_{L}\circ\text{Upbricks}
.

Question 3
.

Find a combinatorial description of the forcing order on the canonical join complex in terms of bricks, and use it to understand quotients of framing lattices.
