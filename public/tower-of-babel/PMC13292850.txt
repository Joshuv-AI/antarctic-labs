Albumin-to-Globulin Ratio as a Predictor of Disease Severity in Emergency Department Patients Presenting with Flank Pain
Authors: Ibrahim Untan, Volkan Sabur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlank pain is a common emergency department (ED) presentation with a wide differential diagnosis. The albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR) reflects both nutritional status and systemic inflammation, but its utility in pre-intervention ED patients with flank pain has not been systematically evaluated.MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, we included 291 consecutive adults who presented to the ED with flank pain and underwent urinary ultrasonography between August 2022 and May 2023. Severe presentation was defined as ≥2 of the following: hydronephrosis, acute kidney injury (creatinine >1.2 mg/dL), elevated CRP (>50 mg/L), or leukocytosis (WBC >12×103/μL). Discriminative performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Multivariable logistic regression was performed with adjustment for age, sex, urolithiasis, and log-NLR, deliberately excluding CRP, creatinine, and WBC to avoid incorporation bias. Incremental value beyond routinely available markers was assessed by comparing nested models containing CRP and creatinine, with and without AGR.ResultsAmong 291 patients (mean age 47.5 ± 23.5 years, 46% male), 59 (20.3%) had severe presentations. AGR was significantly lower in the severe group (1.26 ± 0.35 vs 1.55 ± 0.31, p < 0.001), with moderate discriminative ability (AUC 0.729; optimal cutoff ≤1.35; sensitivity 62.7%, specificity 77.2%). In multivariable analysis, AGR remained an independent predictor; each 0.1-unit decrease was associated with 18% higher odds of severe presentation (adjusted OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.04–1.34, p = 0.010). However, AGR did not improve discrimination beyond a model containing CRP and creatinine (ΔAUC = 0.000, p = 0.957). AGR did not differ between patients with and without urolithiasis (p = 0.14).ConclusionAGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation in ED patients with flank pain, but its moderate sensitivity and absence of incremental value beyond CRP and creatinine suggest it should be used as an adjunctive marker rather than a stand-alone screening tool. Prospective validation is needed before integration into ED triage algorithms.Keywords: albumin-globulin ratio, flank pain, emergency department, urolithiasis, disease severity, inflammatory markers, biomarker, risk stratification

Introduction

Flank pain is one of the most common presenting complaints in emergency departments (ED), accounting for a significant proportion of visits worldwide.1 While urolithiasis is a leading cause, the differential diagnosis includes a wide spectrum of conditions ranging from benign musculoskeletal pain to life-threatening emergencies such as infected hydronephrosis, acute kidney injury (AKI), and urosepsis.2 Rapid and accurate risk stratification is essential for appropriate triage, resource allocation, and clinical decision-making.

Severity in this context encompasses two distinct but overlapping dimensions: mechanical severity, reflected in obstructing stones or hydronephrosis on imaging, and systemic severity, reflected in acute kidney injury, infection, and the host inflammatory response. Current ED approaches to risk stratification rely primarily on clinical presentation, imaging findings, and conventional inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) count, complemented in some settings by composite scores or the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR).3,4 While CRP is a highly sensitive acute-phase reactant, it captures only one dimension of the host response, providing limited information on the patient’s underlying nutritional reserve and immune state. A marker that simultaneously reflects acute-phase response and baseline protein reserve could therefore complement CRP and other established markers, particularly in the early ED window when imaging or culture results are pending.

The albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR) has emerged as a promising biomarker across diverse clinical settings. Albumin, a negative acute-phase reactant, decreases during inflammation through reduced hepatic synthesis, increased capillary permeability, and enhanced catabolism.5–7 Conversely, globulins—including immunoglobulins and other acute-phase proteins—increase as part of the host inflammatory and immune response. Although albumin is traditionally regarded as a long-term marker of nutritional status, in the acute ED setting changes in serum albumin and AGR are predominantly driven by the negative acute-phase response rather than by chronic nutritional decline, with measurable shifts occurring within hours of an inflammatory insult.7,8 The AGR therefore integrates two complementary biological signals—the acute-phase response and baseline protein reserve—and may provide information that is not captured by CRP alone.8–10

Previous studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of AGR in predicting postoperative sepsis after ureteroscopic procedures, outcomes in chronic kidney disease, and survival in various malignancies.5,6,10 A preoperative AGR below 1.2 has been reported as an independent predictor of sepsis after flexible ureteroscopy.6 However, prior urological work on AGR has been almost exclusively conducted in selected, post-intervention populations—patients already scheduled for or recovering from ureteroscopic lithotripsy—where the clinical question is risk of postoperative complications. The performance of AGR in the unselected, pre-intervention ED population presenting with flank pain, where the clinical question is initial risk stratification rather than perioperative outcome, has not been systematically evaluated. This distinction matters clinically because the differential diagnosis, baseline characteristics, and decision context differ substantially between ED triage and perioperative assessment.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the value of AGR for risk stratification—rather than etiological diagnosis—in ED patients presenting with flank pain, with severity defined as a composite of systemic and mechanical features. Secondary objectives were to determine whether AGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation after adjustment for age and other covariates, to compare its performance with other routinely available markers, to assess whether AGR provides incremental discriminative value beyond CRP and creatinine, and to evaluate its ability to predict specific complications including acute kidney injury and infection.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Kirsehir Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary care center in central Türkiye. The study protocol was approved by the Kirşehir Ahi Evran University Faculty of Medicine Health Sciences Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2026–04/46, Date: 24/02/2026) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines (see Supplementary File 1).

Study Population

We retrospectively reviewed the records of all patients who presented to the ED with flank pain and underwent urinary ultrasonography (USG) between August 2022 and May 2023. Adult patients (≥18 years) presenting with flank pain as the chief complaint and with complete laboratory data, including serum albumin and globulin levels, were eligible for inclusion.11 Patients with known chronic kidney disease (stage 4–5), active malignancy, or chronic liver disease were excluded, as were those with missing laboratory data.

Data Collection

Demographic data, clinical characteristics, and laboratory parameters were extracted from electronic medical records. Laboratory investigations included complete blood count (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, platelet count), serum biochemistry (creatinine, total protein, albumin, globulin), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein). The albumin-to-globulin ratio was calculated as serum albumin (g/dL) divided by serum globulin (g/dL). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as neutrophil percentage divided by lymphocyte percentage.

Ultrasonography findings were recorded, including the presence of urolithiasis (renal or ureteral stones), hydronephrosis (graded 1–4), renal cysts, angiomyolipoma, and bladder tumors. Stone size and location were documented when applicable.

Outcome Definitions

The primary outcome was severe presentation, defined as the presence of two or more of the following: hydronephrosis of any grade on USG, acute kidney injury (serum creatinine >1.2 mg/dL), elevated inflammatory markers suggesting infection (CRP >50 mg/L), or leukocytosis (WBC >12×103/μL). A severity score ranging from 0 to 4 was calculated based on the number of criteria met. Secondary outcomes included the presence of urolithiasis on USG and individual components of the severity score.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test due to non-normal distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations between continuous variables and the severity score.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of AGR and other markers for predicting severe presentation. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Optimal cutoff values were determined using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity – 1). Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9) with SciPy and scikit-learn libraries. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariable Analysis

To evaluate whether AGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation rather than a proxy for age or other covariates, we performed multivariable logistic regression. The dependent variable was severe presentation (severity score ≥2). Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and to avoid incorporation bias: age (continuous), sex, presence of urolithiasis on USG, and the natural logarithm of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (log-NLR). CRP, creatinine, and WBC were deliberately excluded from the multivariable model because these variables were components of the severity outcome definition; their inclusion would constitute incorporation bias and inflate apparent model performance. Odds ratios for AGR are reported per 0.1-unit decrease to reflect a clinically interpretable change on the AGR scale. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values <5 considered acceptable.

To assess the incremental discriminative value of AGR beyond routinely available markers, we compared two nested logistic regression models: a base model containing log-CRP, log-creatinine, age, and sex; and a full model adding AGR to these covariates. Models were compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), the likelihood ratio test, continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (cNRI), and the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI). The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was calculated to confirm adequate sample size for the multivariable model.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

A total of 291 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The mean age was 47.5 ± 23.5 years, and 134 (46.0%) were male. Urolithiasis was detected in 83 patients (28.5%), hydronephrosis in 62 patients (21.3%), and renal cysts in 83 patients (28.5%). The remaining patients had either normal USG findings or incidental findings unrelated to acute pathology.

Regarding severity markers, acute kidney injury was present in 56 patients (19.2%), elevated CRP suggesting infection in 58 patients (19.9%), and leukocytosis in 81 patients (27.8%). Fifty-nine patients (20.3%) met the criteria for severe presentation (severity score ≥2).

As expected, inflammatory markers were significantly elevated in the severe group, including CRP (105.7 ± 87.3 vs 12.5 ± 25.1 mg/L, p < 0.001), WBC (13.6 ± 4.8 vs 9.5 ± 3.0 ×103/μL, p < 0.001), and NLR (7.38 ± 7.28 vs 3.66 ± 2.38, p < 0.001). Serum creatinine was also significantly higher in the severe group (2.37 ± 2.71 vs 0.93 ± 0.99 mg/dL, p < 0.001).

Importantly, albumin was significantly lower (3.82 ± 0.69 vs 4.31 ± 0.43 g/dL, p < 0.001) and globulin was significantly higher (3.18 ± 0.64 vs 2.86 ± 0.43 g/dL, p < 0.001) in the severe group. Consequently, the AGR was significantly lower in patients with severe presentation compared to non-severe presentation (1.26 ± 0.35 vs 1.55 ± 0.31, p < 0.001) (Figure 1).

AGR and Urolithiasis

The AGR did not differ significantly between patients with and without urolithiasis on USG (1.53 ± 0.36 vs 1.48 ± 0.33, p = 0.14). This finding indicates that AGR is not useful as a diagnostic marker for the presence of kidney or ureteral stones in patients presenting with flank pain.

Correlation with Severity Score

AGR showed a significant negative correlation with the severity score (Spearman’s r = −0.285, p < 0.001). The mean AGR decreased progressively with increasing severity score: 1.57 ± 0.29 for score 0, 1.53 ± 0.34 for score 1, 1.34 ± 0.31 for score 2, 1.21 ± 0.39 for score 3, and 0.99 ± 0.32 for score 4 (Figure 2). For comparison, CRP (r = 0.470), creatinine (r = 0.508), WBC (r = 0.499), and NLR (r = 0.451) all showed positive correlations with severity score (all p < 0.001) (Figure 3).

Table 2 summarizes the ROC analysis results for various markers in predicting severe presentation (Figure 4). CRP demonstrated the highest discriminative ability (AUC 0.859), followed by creatinine (AUC 0.855), WBC (AUC 0.788), NLR (AUC 0.776), and AGR (AUC 0.729). The optimal AGR cutoff of ≤1.35 yielded a sensitivity of 62.7% and specificity of 77.2%.

AGR and Specific Complications

When analyzing individual components of severity, AGR was significantly lower in patients with acute kidney injury (1.31 ± 0.38 vs 1.54 ± 0.32, p < 0.001) and in patients with infection/elevated CRP (1.19 ± 0.33 vs 1.57 ± 0.30, p < 0.001). However, AGR did not differ significantly based on the presence of hydronephrosis alone (p = 0.73) or leukocytosis alone (p = 0.09) (Figure 5).

Independent Predictive Value of AGR and Incremental Performance

To determine whether AGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation, we performed multivariable logistic regression. After adjustment for age, sex, presence of urolithiasis, and log-NLR, AGR remained an independent predictor of severe presentation. Each 0.1-unit decrease in AGR was associated with 18% higher odds of severe presentation (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.18, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.04–1.34, p = 0.010). Age (aOR per year 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05, p = 0.002), urolithiasis (aOR 5.74, 95% CI 2.32–14.21, p < 0.001), and log-NLR (aOR 6.41, 95% CI 3.40–12.10, p < 0.001) were also independently associated with severe presentation, whereas sex was not (p = 0.168). The full model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.891, pseudo-R2 0.386). All variance inflation factors were below 1.5, indicating no relevant multicollinearity. With 59 events and five covariates, the events-per-variable ratio was 11.8, satisfying conventional sample size requirements for logistic regression. To assess whether AGR provides incremental discriminative value beyond routinely available markers, we compared a base model containing log-CRP, log-creatinine, age, and sex (AUC 0.911) with a full model that additionally included AGR (AUC 0.911). The addition of AGR did not significantly improve model discrimination (ΔAUC = 0.000; likelihood ratio test p = 0.957). Continuous NRI was −0.138, and IDI was 0.0001, confirming that AGR does not add substantial discriminative information when CRP and creatinine are already available. These findings indicate that AGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation but does not provide additional discriminative value beyond established markers; its clinical role is therefore best understood as an adjunctive marker.

Discussion

This study suggests that the albumin-to-globulin ratio is a useful biomarker for predicting disease severity in ED patients presenting with flank pain. Patients with severe presentations had significantly lower AGR values, and an AGR cutoff of ≤1.35 showed moderate discriminative ability for identifying these high-risk patients. Importantly, our findings also indicate that AGR does not predict the presence of urolithiasis, suggesting its value lies in assessing systemic severity rather than diagnosing specific urinary tract pathology.

The pathophysiological basis for the association between low AGR and disease severity is well-established. Albumin is a negative acute-phase reactant whose synthesis decreases during inflammation through cytokine-mediated mechanisms; in particular, IL-6 and TNF-α downregulate hepatic albumin production.7 Additionally, increased capillary permeability during systemic inflammation leads to albumin extravasation into the interstitial compartment.8 Conversely, globulins, which include immunoglobulins and various acute-phase proteins such as α-1 antitrypsin and haptoglobin, increase as part of the host inflammatory and immune response. The AGR thus provides an integrated assessment of both nutritional status and inflammatory burden.12,13

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the prognostic value of AGR in urological settings. Preoperative AGR <1.2, with an odds ratio of 2.81, has been reported as an independent predictor of sepsis after flexible ureteroscopy.6 AGR, with an optimal cutoff of 1.43, has also been shown to predict febrile urinary tract infection after ureteroscopic lithotripsy.5 The slightly higher cutoff identified in our study (1.35) may reflect differences in patient populations, as our cohort included pre-intervention ED patients rather than post-surgical patients.

The observation that AGR specifically predicts acute kidney injury and infection, but not hydronephrosis alone, has important clinical implications. Hydronephrosis, while concerning, may represent a compensated state when not accompanied by renal impairment or infection. In contrast, AKI and infection indicate systemic decompensation that is reflected in altered protein metabolism. This suggests that AGR may be particularly useful for identifying patients who require urgent intervention beyond simple analgesia and observation.

Compared with other inflammatory markers, AGR demonstrated moderate but consistent discriminative ability (AUC 0.729). CRP and creatinine showed higher AUCs (0.859 and 0.855, respectively); however, the apparent superiority of these markers is at least partly explained by incorporation bias, since both were components of the composite severity definition. NLR, which was not part of the severity definition, achieved an AUC of 0.776. To address this limitation directly, we performed a multivariable analysis that excluded the components of the severity definition. After adjustment for age, sex, urolithiasis, and log-NLR, AGR remained an independent predictor of severe presentation, with each 0.1-unit decrease associated with 18% higher odds of severe presentation (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.04–1.34, p = 0.010). This result indicates that the association between low AGR and severe presentation is not merely a reflection of advanced age or other baseline characteristics, but reflects the acute inflammatory and nutritional state captured by the ratio.

Although AGR is an independent predictor of severe presentation, the question of whether it adds incremental value over routinely available markers is equally important from a clinical standpoint. To address this, we compared a base model containing CRP, creatinine, age, and sex with a full model that additionally included AGR. Adding AGR to a model already containing CRP and creatinine did not improve discrimination (ΔAUC = 0.000; likelihood ratio test p = 0.957), and continuous NRI was negative. These findings indicate that, when CRP and creatinine results are already available, AGR does not provide additional discriminative information for identifying severe presentation. The clinical implication is therefore nuanced: AGR is an independent predictor in its own right, but its value is unlikely to lie in displacing or supplementing CRP and creatinine when these are available simultaneously. Rather, AGR should be regarded as an adjunctive marker, reflecting the same underlying biology of acute-phase response and nutritional reserve through a different and complementary lens.4

Several aspects of clinical applicability deserve emphasis. First, the moderate sensitivity of AGR at the optimal cutoff (62.7%) means that approximately one in three patients with severe presentation would be missed if AGR were used as a stand-alone screening tool. AGR should therefore not be used as a rule-out test in the ED and should never override clinical judgment, imaging findings, or established markers such as CRP and creatinine. Second, because AGR is derived from the routine comprehensive metabolic panel and requires no additional cost or testing, it can be interpreted alongside other parameters as part of an integrated assessment of acute-phase response and baseline reserve, particularly in resource-limited settings or when results of additional inflammatory markers are pending or unavailable.14 Third, the absence of an association between AGR and urolithiasis or isolated hydronephrosis reinforces that AGR should be used to assess systemic severity, not the underlying mechanical cause of flank pain.

The finding that AGR does not predict urolithiasis is noteworthy. This suggests that kidney stone formation, in itself, does not significantly alter systemic protein metabolism in the acute setting. The clinical implication is that AGR should be used for severity assessment rather than diagnostic purposes in patients with suspected urolithiasis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single-center design limits our ability to establish causality and may affect generalizability to other settings. Second, the severity definition was composite and incorporated CRP, creatinine, WBC, and hydronephrosis; comparisons of the discriminative performance of these individual markers against the composite outcome are therefore subject to incorporation bias, and we have addressed this directly through multivariable modeling that excluded these components and through transparent acknowledgment in the interpretation of ROC analyses. Third, the composite severity definition may not capture all clinically relevant outcomes, such as need for hospitalization, urgent urological intervention, or longer-term outcomes including 30-day readmission or mortality, which a cross-sectional design cannot evaluate. Fourth, although patients with chronic liver disease, advanced chronic kidney disease, and active malignancy were excluded, we did not systematically exclude patients with chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disorders that may independently lower AGR; such pre-existing conditions could contribute to the observed association and warrant attention in future prospective work. Fifth, the precise interval between symptom onset and blood sampling was not consistently recorded, which is relevant given that acute-phase protein shifts are time-dependent.15 Sixth, ultrasonography was performed and reported by the on-call radiology team rather than by a single dedicated reader, which may introduce inter-observer variability in the grading of hydronephrosis. Seventh, urine culture results were not available, limiting the assessment of infectious complications. Finally, although the events-per-variable ratio of 11.8 satisfies conventional requirements for logistic regression, the relatively small severe group (n = 59) limits the precision of effect estimates and precludes detailed subgroup analyses; prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate and refine the cutoff identified here.

Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings and to determine whether AGR can be incorporated into clinical decision algorithms for ED patients with flank pain. Studies examining the combination of AGR with other markers, and its potential to predict specific outcomes such as need for hospital admission or urgent urological intervention, would also be valuable.

Conclusions

The albumin-to-globulin ratio is an independent predictor of severe presentation in ED patients with flank pain after adjustment for age, sex, urolithiasis, and log-NLR. An AGR cutoff of ≤1.35 demonstrates moderate discriminative ability, particularly for acute kidney injury and infection. However, AGR does not predict the presence of urolithiasis and does not improve discrimination beyond models that already include CRP and creatinine. Together with its moderate sensitivity (62.7%), these findings indicate that AGR is best regarded as an adjunctive marker that may support, but should not replace, established markers and clinical judgment in the assessment of ED patients with flank pain. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the proposed cutoff and to determine whether integration of AGR into structured triage algorithms improves clinical outcomes.
