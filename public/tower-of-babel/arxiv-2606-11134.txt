A Resurgent Analytic Framework for Indicial Umbral Calculus via Mellin–Barnes and Borel–Laplace Theories
Roberto Ricci

Abstract
Indicial umbral calculus offers an effective operational framework for manipulating transcendental functions, yet its analytic foundations have long remained only partially understood. In this work, we provide a rigorous analytic realisation of the theory grounded in Mellin–Barnes integrals, Borel–Laplace summation, and resurgent analysis. By elevating umbral operators from formal algebraic symbols to continuous linear functionals, we establish a topological duality akin to Gelfand–Shilov theory. Formal substitutions are thereby replaced by well-defined, continuous pairings between geometric kernels and suitably topologised admissible ground states. Within this framework, divergent umbral evaluations acquire a precise meaning: they emerge as sectorial asymptotic expansions of analytic functions reconstructed exactly by Mellin–Barnes integrals. The associated Stokes phenomena are natively encoded by jump functions in the spectral variable. This leads to a central spectral transmutation law relating entire and rational kernels directly through Gamma regularisation, proving that classical algebraic umbral identities are merely local expansions of a global analytic correspondence. The construction is systematically extended to general umbral functionals via the Pólya representation of entire functions of exponential type. Explicit examples—including Hankel contours, genuinely Barnes-type integrals, and Lerch transcendents—demonstrate the exact geometric resolution of formal algebraic obstructions. Ultimately, this approach embeds indicial umbral calculus within a unified functional-analytic and resurgent framework, where formal series, analytic continuation, and topological spectral data are intrinsically linked.



1
Introduction

Umbral methods, in their various forms
[
14
,
19
]
, have a long tradition dating back to the 19th century
[
18
]
. However, a mathematically sound formulation of these methods only emerged in the second half of the 20th century, when Roman and Rota provided a rigorous foundation for umbral calculus as the action of an algebra of linear functionals on the space of polynomials
[
25
]
.

In more recent years, a different variant has developed under the name of
indicial umbral calculus
. Initially inspired by Steffensen’s poweroids
[
27
]
and the quasi-monomial formalism of Dattoli and Torre
[
12
]
, this approach has evolved into an operational calculus capable of handling a wide range of transcendental functions
[
1
]
, with notable applications to special functions (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
9
]
).

Indicial umbral calculus is rooted in the theory of special functions and is characterised by an analytic, rather than algebraic, perspective. Its basic mechanism consists in establishing formal identities between transcendental functions and simpler operatorial expressions—the
umbrae
—which act on suitable
ground states
by evaluation.

These formal identities allow one to perform nontrivial manipulations involving transcendental functions by operating on their umbrae as if they were ordinary functions, postponing the operatorial interpretation to the final step.

Despite its effectiveness, indicial umbral calculus has long lacked a rigorous analytic justification. A first mathematically sound formulation was recently proposed by the author
[
24
]
, based on formal series and Borel–Laplace summation, and building on ideas developed by Dattoli and collaborators on the role of the integral Borel transform in umbral methods
[
8
,
10
,
11
]
.

However, despite this progress, the analytic structure underlying indicial umbral calculus remains only partially understood. In particular, the interpretation of umbral evaluations as actual analytic objects, and the precise role of divergence and summability, require a framework going significantly beyond formal series manipulations.

The present work addresses this issue by providing a rigorous analytic realisation of indicial umbral calculus. We extend the formal framework developed in previous work into a setting grounded in Mellin–Barnes integrals
[
22
]
and Borel–Laplace theory
[
2
]
. The guiding principle is to elevate umbral operators from formal algebraic symbols to continuous linear functionals acting on a suitably topologised space of admissible test functions. By doing so, we establish a topological duality akin to the Gelfand–Shilov theory of generalised functions
[
16
]
, where formal substitutions are replaced by well-defined, continuous pairings between geometric kernels and admissible ground states.

Within this approach, divergent umbral evaluations acquire a precise meaning: they arise as asymptotic expansions of analytic functions reconstructed by Mellin–Barnes integrals. The associated Stokes phenomena are naturally encoded by jump functions in the spectral variable. This embeds the resulting analytic structure seamlessly into the broader framework of resurgent analysis
[
5
,
21
,
20
]
. A central outcome of this construction is a spectral transmutation law relating entire and rational kernels, which explains classical algebraic umbral identities as local expansions of a global analytic correspondence.

More precisely, the analytic realisation developed in this work achieves the following goals:

•

it provides a precise interpretation of the umbral action as a topological pairing between a kernel and a ground state, replacing purely formal substitutions with rigorous contour integral representations;

•

it clarifies the origin of the divergent series that arise from formal umbral evaluations, showing that they correspond to specific sectorial asymptotic expansions in the complex plane;

•

it identifies the underlying analytic objects as functions reconstructed by Mellin–Barnes integrals, thereby giving a concrete representation of these objects beyond their formal expansions and relating them to classical integral transforms;

•

it establishes a direct connection with Borel–Laplace summation, whereby the umbral kernels are interpreted as bilateral Laplace transforms of Borel-plane data, and the analytic pairing natively realises the corresponding summation procedure;

•

it embeds the formalism into the framework of resurgent analysis, in which the jump functions encode the Stokes phenomena of the associated analytic functions, and the umbral identities reflect structural relations between different resurgent representations.

Ultimately, this framework aims to elevate indicial umbral calculus from a formal heuristic to a rigorous analytic theory. By replacing discrete algebraic manipulations with continuous contour geometries, it provides exact, well-defined evaluations precisely in those regimes where the classical formal methods diverge or face analytic obstructions. We hope this perspective not only clarifies the foundations of umbral methods but also highlights their natural connection to modern asymptotic analysis.

We denote by

\mathbb{R}
. By
\mathbb{C}^{*}

\tilde{\mathbb{C}}

\mathbb{D}_{z}(R)

R

and
\mathbb{D}^{*}_{z}(R)

\mathbb{D}(R)

\mathbb{D}^{*}(R)

z=0
.

Throughout this text, we will operate within two complementary analytic regimes: the additive Borel–Laplace framework and the multiplicative Mellin–Barnes framework.

In the additive regime, we use the pair of complex variables
t\in\mathbb{C}_{t}
and
u\in\mathbb{C}_{u}
as duals of each other and refer to
\mathbb{C}_{t}
as the Laplace plane and
\mathbb{C}_{u}
as the Borel plane. The Laplace transform will always be applied to a function of
u
(Borel functional) and the formal Borel transform will always be applied to a (possibly convergent) formal series without constant term in
t^{-1}
.
In particular, we adopt the following naming conventions: the identifier of a formal power series without constant term in
t^{-1}
will be marked with a tilde (
\tilde{\Delta}
), and the convergent series in
u
(germ at the origin of the Borel plane) obtained as its formal Borel transform will be identified by the same identifier marked with a hat (
\hat{\Delta}
). The same hatted identifier will be used both for a Borel germ at the origin and for its analytic continuation in an unbounded domain of the Borel plane. The nude identifier (
\Delta
) will be reserved for the integral Laplace transform of the analytically continued function.

In the multiplicative regime governing the analytic pairing, we introduce the multiplicative Borel variable
z\in\mathbb{C}_{z}
(parametrised as
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
). Crucially, the variable
t\in\mathbb{C}_{t}
serves a dual topological purpose: it acts simultaneously as the additive Laplace variable and as the
Mellin spectral variable
dual to
z
. This enacts the core identity
z^{-t}=\operatorname{e}^{-ut}
, which seamlessly bridges the Mellin and Laplace representations. When the pairing involves the spectral scaling parameter
\zeta
, it acts via the scaled variable
w=\zeta z=\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}
, naturally extending this bridge to
w^{-t}=\zeta^{-t}\operatorname{e}^{-ut}
.

To structure the Mellin–Barnes topological duality, we adopt the following nomenclature for the pairing operators: the admissible test functions evaluated in the spectral plane are denoted by
\varphi(t)
(the
ground states
), while their inverse Mellin transforms acting as complex measures in the multiplicative plane are denoted by
W_{\varphi}(z)
(the
Mellin weights
). The umbral operators, which act in the multiplicative plane via the scaled kernels
\hat{K}(z)=\hat{\Delta}(\zeta z)
, encode the resurgent Stokes data in the spectral plane through their corresponding
jump functions

J_{\hat{\Delta}}(t;\zeta)

2
Preliminaries

In this section we summarise the definitions and main results concerning the theory of Borel–Laplace summation of divergent series of given Gevrey order that will be used in the rest of the article. Most of the theorems are reported without proofs, for which we remand the reader to the bibliography.

2.1
The multiplicative algebra
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]

Consider the vector space of formal nonpositive power series with complex coefficients in the Laplace indeterminate
t
, denoted
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]

[
21
]
.
This space forms an algebra under multiplication, defined coefficientwise by the usual Cauchy product
[
4
]
, and becomes a differential algebra with the additional structure provided by the natural derivation with respect to the indeterminate,
\partial\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}t}
, satisfying the usual Leibniz rule.

We define on
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
the valuation function, returning the index of the first non-zero coefficient of its argument.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
\phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}t^{-n}\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, the valuation function
\operatorname{val}:\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined by:

\operatorname{val}(\phi)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,c_{n}\neq 0\right\},\qquad\operatorname{val}(0)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\infty}

.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that
\operatorname{val}(\phi)=0
if and only if
\phi
has nonzero constant term and
\operatorname{val}(\partial\phi)\geq\operatorname{val}(\phi)+1
, i.e.
\partial
is a valuation-increasing operator.

The valuation function enables to introduce a metric structure on
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The function
\operatorname{d}:\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\times\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by

\operatorname{d}(\varphi,\chi)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=2^{-\operatorname{val}(\varphi-\chi)}\text{for any $\varphi,\,\chi\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]$}

,
satisfies the axioms characterising a distance function on
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]

[
3
]
.

The topology induced by
\operatorname{d}
, known as Krull topology
[
21
]
, transforms the algebra
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
into a complete metric space. Informally, any map
f:\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\to\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
is continuous in the Krull topology if, for any
\phi\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, each coefficient of
f(\phi)
depends on a finite number of coefficients of
\phi
.

The Krull topology enables to define the notion of formal convergence.

Proposition 2.4

(Cauchy sequences)
.

The sequence of formal series
\left\{\phi_{p}\right\}_{p=0}^{\infty}\,
, with
\phi_{p}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}^{(p)}t^{-n-1}\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, is Cauchy and converges formally to
\phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
if and only if, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an integer
\mu(n)
such that the tail subsequence of
n
-th coefficients
\left\{c^{(p)}_{n}\right\}_{p=\mu(n)}^{\infty}
is constant, i.e.
c^{(p)}_{n}=c_{n}
for
p\geq\mu(n)
.

Prop.
2.4
can be used to establish a criterion of convergence for a series of formal series, identified with the sequence of its partial sums.

Proposition 2.5

(Formal convergence of a series of formal series)
.

Given the series
\sum_{p\geq 0}\phi_{p}\,
, with
\phi_{p}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}^{(p)}t^{-n-1}\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, if there exists a sequence of integers
\left\{\nu_{p}\right\}_{p=0}^{\infty}\,
satisfying the condition
\lim_{p\to\infty}\nu_{p}=\infty
such that
\phi_{p}\in t^{-\nu_{p}}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, i.e.
c_{n}^{(p)}=0
for
0\leq n<\nu_{p}
, then the series converges formally to
\phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}\in\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
with coefficients given by

c_{n}=\sum_{p\in M_{n}}c^{(p)}_{n}\text{*}{M_{n}=\{p\,|\,\nu_{p}\leq n\}}

.

Remark 2.6
.

It is important to stress that formal convergence and analytic convergence are totally unrelated concepts. Formal convergence has to do with rules ensuring that the result of certain manipulations of formal series is itself a well-formed formal series.

In the following, we will be mainly concerned with the subalgebra of formal series without constant term, which is a maximal ideal of
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
. In view of following developments, we reserve to this subalgebra a special denomination and distinguish its elements with a superimposed tilde.

Definition 2.7

(Laplace space)
.

We call Laplace space the subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
formed by formal series without constant term, namely:

t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\left\{\tilde{\phi}(t)=t^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}t^{-n}\,\middle|\,c_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}}

.
Accordingly, we refer to
t
as the Laplace indeterminate.

Remark 2.8
.

The Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
is a non-unital multiplicative algebra under the Cauchy product.

2.1.1
Gevrey classification of elements of
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]

The Gevrey classification
[
13
,
23
]
is of fundamental importance for the considerations that will be made in the following. Although the notion of Gevrey order applies to any formal series, we specialise the definitions to the Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
.

Definition 2.9

(Gevrey order)
.

A formal series
\tilde{\phi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
is Gevrey of order
s\geq 0
(
s
-Gevrey for short) if some constants
C,A>0
exist such that

|c_{n}|\leq CA^{n}(n!)^{s}\text{for any $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

(1)

.

Remark 2.10
.

It follows directly from the definition that
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}
is
s
-Gevrey if and only if the series
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}/(n!)^{s}
is convergent in the analytic sense.

Proposition 2.11

(Nesting property)
.

For each
s\geq 0
,
s
-Gevrey formal series in
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
form a subalgebra, denoted
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}
.

For any
s,s^{\prime}
satisfying
0<s<s^{\prime}<\infty
the following chain of algebra inclusions holds:

t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}\equiv{t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{0}\subset t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}\subset t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s^{\prime}}\subset t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{\infty}\equiv t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]}

(2)

.

Remark 2.12
.

This chain defines a natural filtration of
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
by increasing divergence order: the larger the Gevrey order, the faster the factorial growth of the coefficients.

Remark 2.13
.

It follows from the nesting property that any
s
-Gevrey series is also
s^{\prime}
-Gevrey for any
s^{\prime}>s
. A formal series is said to be exactly
s
-Gevrey if
s
is the lowest value for which
eq.

1
is satisfied.

Remark 2.14
.

The Gevrey order
s=0
identifies the subalgebra of analytically convergent series, denoted with the special symbol
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}
. It is sometimes convenient to extend the definition of Gevrey order to include negative values of
s
. For
s<0
, the condition
eq.

1
is understood as a decay estimate
|c_{n}|\leq CA^{n}/(n!)^{|s|}
.

2.2
Formal Borel transform

Definition 2.15

(Formal Borel transform)
.

The formal Borel transform of
\tilde{\phi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\,t^{-n-1}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
is the formal series
\hat{\phi}
in the indeterminate
u
defined by:

\hat{\phi}(u)={(\mathcal{B}\,\tilde{\phi})(u)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}\,u^{n}}

(3)

.

Equation

3
can be interpreted as the action on
\tilde{\phi}
of the Borel linear operator
\mathcal{B}:t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\to\mathbb{C}[[u]]
.

Proposition 2.16
.

The linear operator
\mathcal{B}:t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\to\mathbb{C}[[u]]
realises a vector space isomorphism. Its inverse is defined by:

\tilde{\phi}(t)={(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\hat{\phi})(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\,n!\,t^{-n-1},\qquad\text{for any }\,\hat{\phi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}u^{n}\in\mathbb{C}[[u]]}

(4)

.

2.3
The convolutive algebra
\mathbb{C}[[u]]

The isomorphism realised by the formal Borel transform enables to establish a natural duality between the Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
and
\mathbb{C}[[u]]
, the space of formal series in the Borel indeterminate
u
. In the following we better clarify the nature of this duality relationship.

Definition 2.17

(Borel space)
.

We call Borel space the image under the formal Borel transform of the Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, i.e. the vector space of formal nonnegative power series with complex coefficients in the Borel indeterminate
u
, denoted
\mathbb{C}[[u]]
:

\mathbb{C}[[u]]\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\left\{\hat{\phi}(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}u^{n}\,\middle|\,c_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}}

.

We assume for this space the same metric and topological structures already defined coefficientwise for
\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
. On the contrary, it is convenient to introduce in
\mathbb{C}[[u]]
a different algebraic product, defined as the pushforward by
\mathcal{B}
of the Cauchy product.

Definition 2.18

(Convolution product)
.

Given
\hat{\phi},\,\hat{\chi}\in\mathbb{C}[[u]]
, their convolution product is defined by

\hat{\phi}*\hat{\chi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{B}\left((\mathcal{B}^{-1}\,\hat{\phi})\,(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\,\hat{\chi})\right)

.
Coefficientwise, if
\hat{\phi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}u^{n}/n!
and
\hat{\chi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}u^{n}/n!
, then:

\hat{\phi}*\hat{\chi}={\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{c_{n}}{(n+1)!}\,u^{n+1},\qquad\text{where }\;c_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}a_{m}b_{n-m}}

.

Remark 2.19
.

The Borel space
\mathbb{C}[[u]]
is a non-unital convolutive algebra under the the convolution product.

Remark 2.20
.

The convolution product in
C[[u]]
is bilinear, commutative and associative. These properties are derived from the corresponding properties of the Cauchy product restricted to
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
. Since the latter has no unit element, the convolution product has no unit element either, hence
C[[u]]
is non-unital.

In the present work we will be mainly concerned with the 0-Gevrey and 1-Gevrey subalgebras of the Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
and with their images under Borel transform. The following theorems clarify that, in this case, the Borel space reduces to
\mathbb{C}\{u\}
.

Theorem 2.21
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
. Then
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}
, i.e. it is 0-Gevrey hence convergent, if and only if its formal Borel transform
\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{B}\,\tilde{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
and converges to an entire function of exponential type
\tau
, i.e. there exists constants
A>0
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq A\operatorname{e}^{\tau\left\lvert u\right\rvert}
for
\left\lvert u\right\rvert\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.22
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
. Then
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
, i.e. it is 1-Gevrey, if and only if its formal Borel transform is convergent, i.e.
\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{B}\,\tilde{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
.

Remark 2.23
.

It is important to stress that theor.
2.22
does not exclude the possibility that the Borel transform of a divergent formal series
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
converges to an entire function in the Borel plane. Such function, though, cannot be of exponential type in all directions in the Borel plane, since in this case
\tilde{\phi}
would be convergent as a consequence of theor.
2.21
, hence exactly 0-Gevrey, thus contradicting the initial assuption that
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
.

Remark 2.24
.

Theor.
2.22
admits an immediate extension to formal series in the Laplace space of any Gevrey order. In particular, for any
s>0
,
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}
if and only if
\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{B}_{1/s}\,\tilde{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
.

The convolution product of convergent formal series in the Borel indeterminate admits an analytic realisation. Note that, as specified in the introduction, we use the same symbol for a convergent series and the function it converges to.

Lemma 2.25
.

Let
\hat{\phi},\,\hat{\chi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
and let
R>0
be smaller then their respective radii of convergence. Then the function:

(\hat{\phi}*\hat{\chi})(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\mathrm{d}v\,\hat{\phi}(v)\,\hat{\chi}(u-v)

is holomorphic in
\mathbb{D}(R)=\left\{u\in\mathbb{C}_{u}\,|\,\left\lvert u\right\rvert<R\right\}
and coincides there with the sum of the series
\hat{\phi}*\hat{\chi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{c_{n}}{(n+1)!}\,u^{n+1}
.

2.4
Laplace transform

The Laplace transform is usually defined as an improper integral along the positive real semiaxis for functions of a real variable with at most exponential growth along that semiaxis. This definition is not sufficiently comprehensive for our purposes, so we give in the following a more general definition, applicable to suitable functions of the complex variable
u
, which we interpret as analytic continuations of holomorphic germs at the origin of the Borel plane.

The material presented in this section is mostly taken from
[
21
]
and
[
20
]
.

Definition 2.26

(Half-strip in direction
\theta
)
.

Given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, a half-strip in the direction
\theta
is an open subset of the Borel plane of the form

S_{\delta}(\theta)={\left\{u\in\mathbb{C}_{u}\,\middle|\,\operatorname{dist}\bigl(u,\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\bigr)<\delta\right\}}\text{*}{\delta>0}

.

Definition 2.27

(Exponential type along a direction)
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})
the set of holomorphic germs
\hat{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
which admit analytic continuation to a half-strip
S_{\delta}(\theta)
for some
\delta>0
, and for which there exist constants
A>0
,
R>0
and
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq A\operatorname{e}^{\tau^{\prime}\left\lvert u\right\rvert}\text{$u\in S_{\delta}(\theta),\ \left\lvert u\right\rvert\geq R$}

(5)

.

The quantity
\tau
is defined as the infimum of the set of
\tau^{\prime}
for which
eq.

5
holds, and is called the exponential type of
\hat{\phi}
along the direction
\theta
. We also set:

\mathcal{H}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\bigcup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})

.

Remark 2.28
.

The growth condition in
eq.

5
is only required for large
\left\lvert u\right\rvert
; near the origin it is automatically satisfied since
\hat{\phi}
is holomorphic.

Remark 2.29
.

We denote by
\mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}))
the set of formal series
\tilde{\phi}
such that their formal Borel transform
\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{B}\,\tilde{\phi}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. The set
\mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathcal{H}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}))
is defined analogously.

Definition 2.30

(Directional Laplace transform)
.

Given a function
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, its Laplace transform in the direction
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the function

\phi^{\theta}(t)={(\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}\mathrm{d}u\,\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)}

(6)

,
holomorphic in the half-plane of
\mathbb{C}_{t}

\Pi_{\tau}^{\theta}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\left\{t\in\mathbb{C}_{t}\,|\,\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\theta})>\tau\right\}}

.

Proposition 2.31

(Inverse Laplace a.k.a. integral Borel transform)
.

The inverse of
eq.

6
is given by

\hat{\phi}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{B_{c}^{\theta}}\mathrm{d}t\,\operatorname{e}^{ut}\,\phi^{\theta}(t),

(7)

where
B_{c}^{\theta}
is the oriented line

B_{c}^{\theta}={\left\{t=c\operatorname{e}^{-i\theta}+is\operatorname{e}^{-i\theta}\,\middle|\,s\in\mathbb{R}\right\}}

,
with
c>\tau
, oriented in the direction of increasing
s
.

Equation

7
is also known as integral Borel transform.

It is of fundamental interest for the following considerations the case where the Borel germ at the origin
\hat{\phi}
can be continued to a function holomorphic not just in a half-strip, but rather in an open infinite sector of the Borel plane.

Definition 2.32

(Open sector of aperture
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert
and radius
\rho
)
.

Let
\Theta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\rho>0
. Define
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}
.
We denote by

S(\Theta,\rho)={\left\{u\in\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{u}\,\middle|\,\theta_{1}<\arg u<\theta_{2},\ |u|<\rho\right\}\subset\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{u}}

the corresponding open sector of aperture
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert
and radius
\rho
. When
\rho=\infty
, we simply write
S(\Theta)
.

Definition 2.33
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
f_{\tau}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
a locally bounded function, a.k.a.
indicator function
.
For any locally bounded function
A:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau},A)
the set of holomorphic germs
\hat{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}\{u\}
which admit analytic continuation to the sector
S(\Theta)
and satisfy the growth condition

\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq A(\theta)\operatorname{e}^{f_{\tau}(\theta)\left\lvert u\right\rvert}\text{*}{u\in S(\Theta),\ \left\lvert u\right\rvert\geq R}

for some
R>0
.

We denote by
\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
the set of
\hat{\phi}
for which a locally bounded function
A
exists such that
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau},A)
, and by
\mathcal{H}(\Theta)
the set of
\hat{\phi}
for which a locally bounded indicator function
f_{\tau}
exists such that
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
.

Remark 2.34
.

We recall that a function is locally bounded in an open interval
\Theta
if it is bounded in any compact subinterval of
\Theta
.

Lemma 2.35
.

For every
\theta\in\Theta
there exists a real number
c=c(\theta)
such that

\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})\subset\mathcal{H}_{c}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}).

A possible choice is
c(\theta)=\sup_{\vartheta\in I}f_{\tau}(\vartheta)
for any open interval
I\subset\Theta
containing
\theta
and with compact closure in
\Theta
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\Theta
and let
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
. By definition, there exists a locally bounded function

A:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

such that
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau},A)
. Thus
\hat{\phi}
admits analytic continuation to the sector
S(\Theta)
and there exists
R>0
such that

\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq A(\arg u)\,\operatorname{e}^{f_{\tau}(\arg u)\left\lvert u\right\rvert}\text{*}{u\in S(\Theta),\ \left\lvert u\right\rvert\geq R}

.
Since
\Theta
is open and
\theta\in\Theta
, one can choose an open interval
I=(\theta-\varepsilon,\theta+\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon>0
and compact closure
\overline{I}\subset\Theta
.
Because
A
and
f_{\tau}
are locally bounded, they are bounded on
\overline{I}
. Hence there exist constants
M>0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

A(\vartheta)\leq{M,\qquad f_{\tau}(\vartheta)\leq c}\text{*}{\vartheta\in\overline{I}}

.

Now choose
\delta>0
small enough so that, for all sufficiently large
u\in S_{\delta}(\theta)
, one has
\arg u\in\overline{I}
.
Then, for such
u
,

\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq{A(\arg u)\,\operatorname{e}^{f_{\tau}(\arg u)\left\lvert u\right\rvert}\leq M\operatorname{e}^{c\left\lvert u\right\rvert}}

.

Thus
\hat{\phi}
admits analytic continuation to the half-strip
S_{\delta}(\theta)
and satisfies there an exponential bound of type
c
for
\left\lvert u\right\rvert
sufficiently large. Therefore

\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{c}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}),

which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2.36
.

The Lemma shows that every
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
admits analytic continuation to a half-strip around any direction
\theta\in\Theta
, and therefore the directional Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\,\hat{\phi}
is well defined.
It is important, however, to distinguish two different roles played by the growth bounds. The constant
c(\theta)
in lemma
2.35
is obtained from a uniform bound for
f_{\tau}
in a neighbourhood of
\theta
, and is used to embed the sectorial class
\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
into a half-strip class. By contrast, the convergence of the Laplace integral along the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
only depends on the growth of
\hat{\phi}
on that ray and is therefore governed by the pointwise value
f_{\tau}(\theta)
. Consequently, for any
\theta\in\Theta
,
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\,\hat{\phi}
is holomorphic in the half-plane
\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}
.

Lemma 2.37
.

Let
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
and suppose that
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
satisfy
0<\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}<\pi
.
Then the restrictions of
\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{1}}\hat{\phi}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{2}}\hat{\phi}
to the open subset
\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{1})}^{\theta_{1}}\cap\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{2})}^{\theta_{2}}
coincide.

Proof.

Let
t\in\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{1})}^{\theta_{1}}\cap\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{2})}^{\theta_{2}}
. Then one has
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{1}})>\tau(\theta_{1})
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{2}})>\tau(\theta_{2})
.

Since
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
, it admits analytic continuation to the sector
S(\Theta)
. By Cauchy’s theorem, the integral along the contour formed by these two rays and the circular arcs of radius
\varepsilon
and
R
(with appropriate orientations) is zero.
This implies that

\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\mathrm{d}r\,\operatorname{e}^{-tr\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{1}}}\hat{\phi}(r\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{1}})\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{1}}-\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\mathrm{d}r\,\operatorname{e}^{-tr\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{2}}}\hat{\phi}(r\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{2}})\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{2}}

is equal to the sum of the integrals over the two circular arcs.

The integral over the inner arc tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
because
\hat{\phi}
is holomorphic near the origin.

For the outer arc, let

c\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sup_{\vartheta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}f_{\tau}(\vartheta)

.
Since
t\in\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{1})}^{\theta_{1}}\cap\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{2})}^{\theta_{2}}
and
\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}<\pi
, one has

\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\vartheta})>c\text{*}{\vartheta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}

.
By continuity, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\vartheta})\geq c+\delta\text{*}{\vartheta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}

.
Since
\hat{\phi}
has exponential type bounded by
c
in that sector, there exist constants
A>0
and
R_{0}>0
such that

\left\lvert\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert\leq A\operatorname{e}^{c\left\lvert u\right\rvert}\text{*}{u\in S(\Theta),\ \left\lvert u\right\rvert\geq R_{0}}

.
Along the outer arc
u=R\operatorname{e}^{i\vartheta}
, one then has

\left\lvert\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\right\rvert{\leq A\exp\left(-R\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\operatorname{e}^{i\vartheta})+cR\right)\leq A\operatorname{e}^{-\delta R}}

so the integral over the outer arc tends to
0
as
R\to+\infty
.
Passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
and
R\to+\infty
, one obtains

(\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{1}}\hat{\phi})(t)=(\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{2}}\hat{\phi})(t)

.
∎

Remark 2.38
.

For geometrical reasons, the intersection
\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{1})}^{\theta_{1}}\cap\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta_{2})}^{\theta_{2}}
is non-empty whenever
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
satisfy
\left\lvert\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\right\rvert<\pi
.

Remark 2.39
.

Lemma

2.37
allows one to extend the directional Laplace transform from a single direction to an interval of directions, at least when the aperture of the interval is not larger than
\pi
.

Definition 2.40

(Sectorial Laplace transform)
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval with
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert\leq\pi
, and let
f_{\tau}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally bounded indicator function. Define

\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})=\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}

.
The sectorial Laplace transform of
\hat{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
is the function
\mathcal{L}^{\Theta}\hat{\phi}
, holomorphic in
\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
, defined by

(\mathcal{L}^{\Theta}\hat{\phi})(t)=(\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\hat{\phi})(t)\text{*}{\theta\in\Theta,\ t\in\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}}

.

Remark 2.41
.

The previous lemma ensures that the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{\Theta}\hat{\phi}
is independent of the choice of
\theta
such that
t\in\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}
.

Remark 2.42
.

The domain
\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
is an open sectorial neighbourhood of infinity of aperture
\pi+\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert
(see
fig.

3
).

(a)
The Borel plane
\mathbb{C}_{u}
showing a discrete lattice of singularities in the left half-plane. An admissible sector
\Theta_{k}
is bounded by the rays passing through the poles
u_{k-1}
and
u_{k}
.
(b)
The corresponding domain of validity
\mathcal{D}(\Theta_{k},f_{\tau})
in the spectral plane

, obtained as the union of convergence half-planes. By depicting an exact concave sector originating at

, the figure visually represents the purely sectorial case where the exponential growth bound vanishes (
f_{\tau}(\theta)=0
).

Remark 2.43

(Sectors of aperture larger than
\pi
)
.

The previous construction extends without modification to the case
\pi<\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert<2\pi
, provided one works on
\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{t}
, the Riemann surface of the logarithm. In this case, the half-planes
\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}
and the domain
\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
are naturally lifted to
\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{t}
, and the sectorial Laplace transform is defined by analytic continuation along admissible paths in the lifted domain. We denote the lifted counterparts of
\Pi_{f_{\tau}(\theta)}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
with a superimposed tilde symbol.

Remark 2.44

(The case
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert\geq 2\pi
)
.

If
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert\geq 2\pi
, the analytic continuation becomes global on the Borel plane. One is then naturally led to the class of entire functions of exponential type in the usual sense, and the dependence on the direction
\theta
disappears.

2.4.1
Stokes phenomenon and lateral Laplace transforms

The directional Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\hat{\phi}
ceases to be directly meaningful whenever the analytic continuation of
\hat{\phi}
has singularities along the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Definition 2.45

(Singular direction)
.

A direction
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is called singular for
\hat{\phi}
if the analytic continuation of
\hat{\phi}
has a singularity along the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Definition 2.46

(Lateral Laplace transforms)
.

Let
\theta
be a singular direction. The lateral Laplace transforms of
\hat{\phi}
at
\theta
are defined by

\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{\pm}}\,\hat{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{L}^{\theta\pm\varepsilon}\,\hat{\phi},

whenever the limits exist.

Definition 2.47

(Stokes discontinuity)
.

The difference

\mathfrak{S}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{+}}\,\hat{\phi}-\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{-}}\,\hat{\phi}

is called the Stokes discontinuity of
\hat{\phi}
in the singular direction
\theta
.

Remark 2.48
.

If
\hat{\phi}
is analytic in a sector containing the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, then
\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{+}}\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{-}}\hat{\phi}
and therefore
\mathfrak{S}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi}=0
.

Definition 2.49

(Borel-plane discontinuity across a singular ray)
.

Suppose that
\hat{\phi}
admits analytic continuation to a sector

S\bigl((\theta-\eta,\theta+\eta)\bigr)\setminus\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}

for some
\eta>0
, and that for each
r>0
the lateral limits

\hat{\phi}_{\pm}\left(r\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\right)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\hat{\phi}\left(r\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta\pm\varepsilon)}\right)

exist.

The function

(\operatorname{disc}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})\left(u\right)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\hat{\phi}_{+}\left(u\right)-\hat{\phi}_{-}\left(u\right)\text{*}{u\in\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}}

is called the Borel-plane discontinuity, or lateral discontinuity, of
\hat{\phi}
across the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

The definition extends in the sense of distributions when the limits do not exist pointwise.

Remark 2.50

(Borel-plane discontinuity versus Mellin–Barnes jump kernel)
.

The object
\operatorname{disc}_{\theta}\hat{\phi}
is a discontinuity in the Borel variable
u
. It should not be confused with the Mellin–Barnes jump kernel used later in the analytic umbral pairing. The former lives on a singular ray in the Borel plane, whereas the latter lives in the dual Mellin–Laplace variable and is obtained by applying the appropriate Mellin–Barnes transform to the Borel data.

Proposition 2.51

(Borel-plane representation of the Stokes discontinuity)
.

Under the assumptions of
definition

2.49
, one has formally

(\mathfrak{S}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}\mathrm{d}u\,\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\,(\operatorname{disc}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(u),

whenever the integral is well defined.

Proposition 2.52

(Stokes discontinuity generated by simple poles)
.

Suppose that
\hat{\phi}
admits analytic continuation to a punctured neighbourhood of the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and has there no singularities except a finite number of simple poles

\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m}\in\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Assume moreover that the lateral Laplace transforms
\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{\pm}}\hat{\phi}
exist.
Then

(\mathfrak{S}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(t)={-2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\hat{\phi}(u)\right)\,\operatorname{e}^{-t\omega_{j}}}

(8)

.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
R>0
be such that all poles
\omega_{j}
lie in the truncated annular sector

\left\{u\in\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{u}\,\middle|\,\varepsilon<|u|<R,\ \theta-\eta<\arg u<\theta+\eta\right\}

for some sufficiently small
\eta>0
, and no other singularities of
\hat{\phi}
are present there.

Consider the clockwise oriented closed contour consisting of:

1.

the segment of the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta+\eta)}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
from
\varepsilon
to
R
,

2.

the circular arc of radius
R
from angle
\theta+\eta
to angle
\theta-\eta
,

3.

the segment of the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta-\eta)}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
from
R
back to
\varepsilon
,

4.

the circular arc of radius
\varepsilon
from angle
\theta-\eta
to angle
\theta+\eta
.

Applying the residue theorem one obtains that

\int_{\varepsilon\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta+\eta)}}^{R\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta+\eta)}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{\varepsilon\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta-\eta)}}^{R\operatorname{e}^{i(\theta-\eta)}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

is equal to

-2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\right)

,
up to the contributions of the two circular arcs.

Under the assumed existence of the lateral Laplace transforms, the large and small arcs tend to zero respectively as
R\to+\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Passing to the limit
\eta\to 0^{+}
then yields

(\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{+}}\hat{\phi})(t)-(\mathcal{L}^{\theta^{-}}\hat{\phi})(t)=-2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\right).

Since
\operatorname{e}^{-tu}
is holomorphic at each pole,

\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\phi}(u)\right)=\operatorname{e}^{-t\omega_{j}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\hat{\phi}(u)\right),

which gives
eq.

8
.
∎

Remark 2.53

(Borel-plane discontinuity for simple poles)
.

Under the assumptions of
proposition

2.52
, the Borel-plane discontinuity of
\hat{\phi}
across the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a discrete measure supported at the poles
\omega_{j}
. More precisely,

(\operatorname{disc}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(u)=-\,2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\hat{\phi}(u)\right)\,\delta(u-\omega_{j}),

in the sense of distributions along the ray.

The Stokes discontinuity is therefore obtained by Laplace transform of this Borel-plane discontinuity, which yields
eq.

8
.

Remark 2.54
.

The same argument applies to a discrete infinite family of poles along
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, provided the resulting series of exponentially small contributions converges normally in the relevant domain of the Laplace variable
t
.

Corollary 2.55

(Stokes discontinuity for a lattice of poles)
.

Suppose that
\hat{\phi}
has a sequence of simple poles along the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of the form

\omega_{j}=\omega_{0}+j\omega\text{*}{j\in\mathbb{N}}

,
with
\omega_{0},\,\omega\in\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and that the corresponding residues satisfy a growth condition ensuring convergence.

Then the Stokes discontinuity is given by

(\mathfrak{S}_{\theta}\,\hat{\phi})(t)=-\,2\pi i\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega_{j}}\left(\hat{\phi}(u)\right)\,\operatorname{e}^{-t\omega_{j}}

(9)

,
where the series converges normally in any domain where
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(t\omega_{0})>0
.

2.4.2
Elements of resurgence theory

To fix the terminology used in the analysis of rational umbral Borel functionals, we provide a brief synthesis of the core concepts of resurgence theory, following the framework outlined in
[
15
,
21
]
.

Definition 2.56

(Endless continuability)
.

A holomorphic germ at the origin in the Borel plane
\hat{\phi}
is said to be
endlessly continuable
if for any
L>0
, there exists a finite set of points
\Omega_{L}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\hat{\phi}
can be analytically continued along any path of length
L
starting from the origin and avoiding
\Omega_{L}
.

Definition 2.57

(Resurgent Borel germ)
.

A
resurgent Borel germ
is an endlessly continuable germ whose growth at infinity along any non-singular ray is at most exponential. We denote the space of such germs by
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
.

Remark 2.58
.

A resurgent Borel germ
\hat{\phi}
is characterised by two properties:

1.

it admits analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}_{u}\setminus\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a discrete set;

2.

along every non-singular direction, it has at most exponential growth.

Definition 2.59

(Resurgent series and transform)
.

A formal power series
\tilde{\phi}(t)=t^{-1}\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n}
is a
resurgent series
if its formal Borel transform
\hat{\phi}(u)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}u^{n}
is a resurgent Borel germ. Its
resurgent Laplace transforms
are the sectorial transforms
\mathcal{L}^{\Theta}\hat{\phi}
defined in
section

2.4.1
.

Remark 2.60

(Alien derivatives in the simple-pole case)
.

In the simple-pole situation relevant to the rational umbral class introduced later, the alien derivative at a singular point
\omega
may be identified with the singular part of the Borel germ at
\omega
. More precisely, if
\hat{\phi}
has a simple pole at
u=\omega
, then

\Delta_{\omega}\,\hat{\phi}=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{\,u=\omega}(\hat{\phi})\,\delta,

where
\delta
denotes the Dirac mass at the origin, i.e. the Borel transform of the constant
1
.

In this case, the corresponding contribution to the Stokes discontinuity in the direction
\theta=\arg\omega
is

\operatorname{e}^{-\omega t}((\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\circ\Delta_{\omega})\,\hat{\phi})(t)=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{\,u=\omega}(\hat{\phi})\,\operatorname{e}^{-\omega t},

which is precisely the elementary jump term appearing in proposition
2.52
.

2.5
Gevrey asymptotics and Borel–Laplace summability

We recall the notion of Gevrey asymptotic expansion and its relation with the generalised Borel–Laplace summation procedure.

2.5.1
Gevrey asymptotics

Definition 2.61

(Sectorial neighbourhood of infinity)
.

Let
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}
and
R>0
. We denote by

S_{\infty}(\Theta,R)={\left\{t\in\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{t}\,\middle|\,\theta_{1}<\arg t<\theta_{2},\ |t|>R\right\}}

the corresponding sectorial neighbourhood of infinity. When
R=0
, we simply write
S_{\infty}(\Theta)
.

Definition 2.62

(Gevrey asymptotic expansion)
.

Let
s>0
and let
S_{\infty}(\Theta,R)
be a sectorial neighbourhood of infinity in
\tilde{\mathbb{C}}_{t}
. Let
\tilde{\phi}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{-n-1}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
.

A function
\phi
holomorphic in
S_{\infty}(\Theta,R)
is said to admit
\tilde{\phi}
as Gevrey asymptotic expansion of order
s
in
S_{\infty}(\Theta,R)
if, for every compact subsector
W\subset S_{\infty}(\Theta,R)
, there exist constants
A,C>0
such that

\left|\phi(t)-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}c_{n}t^{-n-1}\right|\leq CA^{N}(N!)^{s}|t|^{-N-1}

for all
t\in W
and all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this case, we write

\phi\sim_{s}\tilde{\phi}\quad\text{in }S_{\infty}(\Theta,R).

Remark 2.63
.

If
\phi
admits a Gevrey asymptotic expansion of order
s
, then
\tilde{\phi}
is
s
-Gevrey.

Proposition 2.64

(Non-uniqueness for small sectors)
.

If
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert<s\pi
, then every
s
-Gevrey series admits at least one asymptotic realisation in
S_{\infty}(\Theta,\rho)
, but this realisation is not unique.

The ambiguity is given by exponentially small functions of the form
\operatorname{e}^{-at^{s}}
with
a>0
.

Proposition 2.65

(Uniqueness for large sectors)
.

If
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert>s\pi
, then a function admitting a given Gevrey asymptotic expansion is unique, whenever it exists.

2.5.2
Borel–Laplace summability

Definition 2.66

(Borel–Laplace summability in a direction)
.

Let
s>0
. A formal series
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}
is said to be
1/s
-Borel–Laplace summable (
1/s
-summable for short) in the direction
\theta
if its formal Borel transform
\hat{\phi}={\mathcal{B}_{1/s}\,\tilde{\phi}}
admits analytic continuation to a half-strip
S_{\delta}(\theta)
and belongs there to
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.67
.

It is trivial to verify that the
1/s
-Borel transform of an
s
-Gevrey formal series
\tilde{\phi}
always defines a holomorphic germ at the origin.

Definition 2.68

(Borel–Laplace sum)
.

If
\tilde{\phi}
is
1/s
-summable in the direction
\theta
, its Borel–Laplace sum is defined by

\phi^{\theta}={\mathcal{S}_{1/s}^{\theta}\tilde{\phi}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{L}_{1/s}^{\theta}\hat{\phi}}

.

Theorem 2.69
.

If
\tilde{\phi}
is
1/s
-summable in the direction
\theta
, then there exists
c>0
such that the function
\phi^{\theta}
is holomorphic in
\Pi_{c}^{\theta}
and satisfies

\phi^{\theta}\sim_{s}\tilde{\phi}\quad\text{in }\,\Pi_{c}^{\theta}

.

Definition 2.70

(Borel–Laplace summability in an interval)
.

Let
s>0
and let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. A formal series
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}
is said to be
1/s
-Borel–Laplace summable in
\Theta
if its formal Borel transform
\hat{\phi}={\mathcal{B}_{1/s}\,\tilde{\phi}}
admits analytic continuation to the open sector
S(\Theta)
and belongs there to
\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
for some locally bounded function
f_{\tau}
.

Theorem 2.71

(Borel–Laplace summation)
.

If
\tilde{\phi}
is
1/s
-summable in an interval
\Theta
, then:

1.

for each
\theta\in\Theta
, the function
\phi^{\theta}=\mathcal{L}_{1/s}^{\theta}\,\hat{\phi}
is well defined;

2.

the functions
\phi^{\theta}
can be combined to define a function
\phi^{\Theta}=\mathcal{L}_{1/s}^{\Theta}\,\hat{\phi}
holomorphic in the sectorial neighbourhood of infinity
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
;

3.

one has

\phi^{\Theta}\sim_{s}\tilde{\phi}\quad\text{in }\,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(\Theta,f_{\tau})

.

Remark 2.72
.

When
\theta
crosses a singular direction of
\hat{\phi}
, the corresponding sums in contiguous sectors differ by exponentially small terms, according to the Stokes phenomenon.

Definition 2.73

(
1/s
-Borel–Laplace summation operator)
.

For
\tilde{\phi}
summable in
\Theta
, we define

\mathcal{S}_{1/s}^{\Theta}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{L}_{1/s}^{\Theta}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{B}_{1/s}

,
where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the analytic continuation operator from the space of holomorphic germs at the origin to the sector
S(\Theta)
.

Remark 2.74
.

The Borel–Laplace summation operator
\mathcal{S}_{1/s}^{\Theta}
is linear and preserves algebraic operations (product, derivation, etc.). In particular, it maps formal solutions of analytic equations to genuine solutions.

Theorem 2.75

(Asymptotic uniqueness and Borel–Laplace summation in an interval)
.

Let
s>0
and let
\tilde{\phi}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{s}
. Assume that its Borel transform
\hat{\phi}=\mathcal{B}_{1/s}\,\tilde{\phi}
admits analytic continuation to a sector
S(\Theta)
and belongs there to
\mathcal{H}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
for some locally bounded function
f_{\tau}
.

Then the corresponding Borel–Laplace sum

\phi^{\Theta}=\mathcal{L}_{1/s}^{\Theta}\hat{\phi}

is holomorphic in a sectorial neighbourhood of infinity of aperture
\pi s+\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert
and satisfies there

\phi^{\Theta}\sim_{s}\tilde{\phi}.

In particular, if
\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert>0
, then
\pi s+\left\lvert\Theta\right\rvert>\pi s
, hence
\phi^{\Theta}
is the unique holomorphic function admitting
\tilde{\phi}
as
s
-Gevrey asymptotic expansion in that domain.

Remark 2.76

(Critical case)
.

The genuinely critical case corresponds to Borel–Laplace summation in a single direction. In that case the Laplace domain has aperture exactly
\pi s
, and asymptotic uniqueness may fail even though the Borel–Laplace construction still produces a canonical sum.

2.6
Mellin transforms, rotated rays, and Mellin–Barnes integrals

The Mellin transform
[
29
]
plays a fundamental role in development of the analytic umbral framework. We recall the Mellin-transform conventions used throughout the paper.
Because the analytic umbral framework relies on evaluating functions of the multiplicative Borel variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
, the Mellin transform along specified complex directions forms the backbone of the spectral representation.

Definition 2.77

(Mellin transform along a rotated ray)
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
f
be a function defined on the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The Mellin transform of
f
along this ray is defined by

\mathcal{M}_{\theta}[f](t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}f(w)\,w^{t-1}\,\mathrm{d}w,

where the power
w^{t-1}
is evaluated using the branch of the logarithm determined by
w=r\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}
and
\log w=\ln r+i\theta
for
r>0
. Equivalently,

\mathcal{M}_{\theta}[f](t)=\operatorname{e}^{i\theta t}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r\operatorname{e}^{i\theta})\,r^{t-1}\,\mathrm{d}r,

whenever the integral converges.

In particular, if
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
and
\theta=\operatorname{Arg}\mu
is a chosen principal argument, the Mellin transform along the ray
\mu\mathbb{R}_{+}
is understood in the preceding sense. The set of values
t
for which the integral converges usually forms a vertical strip
a<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t<b
, known as the fundamental strip.

Example 2.78

(Elementary Mellin kernels)
.

With these conventions, one has, initially in domains of convergence and subsequently by meromorphic continuation:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{\zeta x}x^{t-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=(-\zeta)^{-t}\Gamma(t),

provided the branch of
(-\zeta)^{-t}
is fixed and the integration direction is chosen so that the exponential decays. Similarly, for the rational kernel:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{t-1}}{1-\zeta x}\,\mathrm{d}x=(-\zeta)^{-t}\pi\csc(\pi t).

Proposition 2.79

(Mellin-admissible classes)
.

In the context of umbral Borel functionals, we will frequently require the Mellin transformability of specific classes of functions. The following classes are Mellin-admissible along suitable contours:

(i)

Rational functions
\hat{K}(z)
with poles strictly away from the contour of integration;

(ii)

Entire functions of exponential type
\hat{K}(z)=F(\zeta z)
whose indicator diagram is contained in an open half-plane, guaranteeing the existence of a ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{+}
along which the function exhibits uniform exponential decay.

The Hankel contour..
While the Mellin transform typically acts along rays, inverse Gamma factors naturally require contour representations.

Definition 2.80

(Hankel contour)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a Hankel contour encircling the negative real axis counterclockwise. Unless otherwise stated, powers
w^{-s}
on
\mathcal{H}
are computed with the branch cut along the negative real axis.

Proposition 2.81

(Hankel representation of the reciprocal Gamma function)
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{C}
, one has

\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\operatorname{e}^{w}w^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}w.

Mellin–Barnes integrals..
The inverse operation to the Mellin transform, and the core analytic mechanism of the umbral pairing, is the Mellin–Barnes integral.

Definition 2.82

(Mellin–Barnes integral)
.

A Mellin–Barnes integral is a contour integral of the form

F(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\Phi(t)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

,
where
\Phi(t)
is a meromorphic function of the Mellin variable
t
, and
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is an oriented vertical contour (or a suitable deformation thereof) chosen so that the integral converges absolutely and safely separates the relevant families of poles of
\Phi
.

Remark 2.83

(Residue expansions and analytic continuation)
.

The Mellin–Barnes representation is a global analytic tool. If the contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
can be displaced to the left or to the right without crossing singularities other than the poles of
\Phi
, Cauchy’s theorem expresses the integral as the sum of the corresponding residues. Different choices of closing the contour yield different asymptotic realisations of the same underlying analytic object, seamlessly bridging local series expansions and global analytic continuation.

3
Umbral Borel functionals and Laplace series

In sections
2.1
and
2.3
we introduced the convenient conventional denominations of
algebraic Laplace space
for the multiplicative algebra
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
and of
algebraic Borel space
for the convolutive algebra
\mathbb{C}[[u]]
. The two spaces are Borel-dual, in the sense that the formal Borel transform realises an algebra isomorphism
\mathcal{B}:t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\sim$}}\mathbb{C}[[u]]
that maps the product of two Laplace series into the convolution of the corresponding Borel series.

In particular, with theorem
2.22
we established that the Borel-dual of
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
, the subspace of 1-Gevrey divergent Laplace series, is
\mathbb{C}\{u\}
, the subspace of convergent Borel series, that is the space of holomorphic germs at the origin.

In this section, we identify
umbral Laplace and Borel spaces
as particular resurgent subspaces of respectively
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
and
\mathbb{C}\{u\}
. We begin by isolating a distinguished class of formal series in the Laplace space
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
, naturally associated with the analytic umbral framework outlined in this work.

The construction is based on a class of analytic functionals in the Borel plane, whose structure is governed by compositions of the form
z\mapsto F(\zeta z)
, evaluated along the multiplicative Borel variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
.

Remark 3.1

(Borel and umbral variables)
.

Throughout this section we distinguish between the
additive Borel variable

u
, the associated
multiplicative Borel variable

z=\operatorname{e}^{u},

and the
umbral variable

w={\zeta z=\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}

.
The passage from
u
to
z
corresponds to the transition from an additive to a multiplicative structure, underlying the passage from Laplace to Mellin transforms. The analytic functionals considered in this work are naturally expressed as functions of
w
, while their dependence on
u
is induced through this composition.

Definition 3.2

(Umbral Borel functionals)
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. We call
umbral Borel functional
any function of the form

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=F(w)\text{*}{w=\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u},}

where
w\mapsto F(w)
is holomorphic at
w=\zeta
and either

(i)

a polynomial;

(ii)

or admits a finite decomposition

F(w)=R(w)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}E_{j}(w),

with
R
rational (possibly zero) and each
E_{j}
entire of exponential type such that the associated function
z\mapsto E_{j}(\zeta z)
is Mellin-transformable along a line
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{j}}\mathbb{R}
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{z}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Polynomial functionals are naturally included in this class, although they do not admit a Mellin transform in the classical sense.

Indeed, the elementary monomial umbral Borel functional
u\mapsto\operatorname{e}^{u}
corresponds to the polynomial
F(w)=w
, and provides the analytic representative of the elementary indicial umbral operator
\mathfrak{u}
. More generally, polynomials generate finite linear combinations of exponentials in the Borel plane and form the algebraic backbone of the umbral construction.

Remark 3.4
.

The above class contains in particular:

•

rational umbral Borel functionals;

•

exponential umbral Borel functionals;

•

trigonometric and hyperbolic umbral Borel functionals, after decomposition into exponential components;

•

finite linear combinations of entire components, each admitting at least one direction of exponential decay after composition with
z\mapsto\zeta z
.

Remark 3.5
.

An entire function of exponential type admitting a Pólya representation,

F(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\,\mathcal{P}_{F}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

is admissible whenever its indicator diagram — i.e. the convex hull of the set of singularities of its Borel transform — is contained in an open half-plane. In this case, the composed kernel
z\mapsto F(\zeta z)
admits a direction of exponential decay.

In the general case, such functions can be treated by decomposing them into finitely many admissible components.

Remark 3.6

(Constraint on the parameter
\zeta
)
.

The requirement that
u\mapsto\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)
be holomorphic at
u=0
imposes a constraint on the parameter
\zeta
.
Indeed, this condition is equivalent to requiring that
F
be holomorphic at the point
w=\zeta
. In particular, if
F
has singularities at
\{a_{j}\}
, then one must have

\zeta\notin\{a_{j}\}.

Definition 3.7

(Umbral Laplace series)
.

We call
umbral Laplace series
any formal series
\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
such that

\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}

and its formal Borel transform
\hat{\Delta}=\mathcal{B}\,\tilde{\Delta}
admits analytic continuation to an umbral Borel functional.

Remark 3.8
.

We recall that, due to the nesting property
eq.

2
, the subalgebra
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
includes analytically convergent series.

3.1
Structure and classification of umbral Laplace series

We now analyze the analytic structure of umbral Borel functionals and the corresponding umbral Laplace series. The behavior of the latter is entirely determined by the properties of the composed functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=F(w)\text{*}{w=\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}.}

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be entire. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the function
u\mapsto F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
is entire of exponential type in the whole Borel plane;

(ii)

F
is a polynomial.

Proof.

If
F
is a polynomial, then
F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
is a finite linear combination of exponentials, hence entire of exponential type.

Conversely, assume
F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
is of exponential type
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exist constants
A,R>0
such that

|F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})|\leq A\operatorname{e}^{\tau|u|}\text{*}{|u|\geq R.}

Writing
F(w)=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}w^{m}
, we have

F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}a_{m}\zeta^{m}\operatorname{e}^{mu}.

Let
u=x+iy
with
x>R+2\pi
. Then

|x+iy|\geq x-|y|>R\text{*}{0\leq y\leq 2\pi},

so the exponential-type estimate applies. Integrating over
y
gives

|a_{m}||\zeta|^{m}\operatorname{e}^{mx}\leq A\operatorname{e}^{\tau(x+2\pi)}.

Rearranging to isolate the coefficients yields

|a_{m}|\leq A|\zeta|^{-m}\operatorname{e}^{2\pi\tau}\operatorname{e}^{-(m-\tau)x}.

If
m>\tau
, letting
x\to+\infty
yields
a_{m}=0
. Thus
F
is a polynomial.
∎

Remark 3.10

(Dependence on
u
and
w
)
.

Although umbral Borel functionals are defined as compositions

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=F(w)\text{*}{w=\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u},}

their analytic classification is exclusively governed by the dependence on the Borel variable
u
.

The passage from
w
to
u
transforms algebraic properties of
w\mapsto F(w)
into analytic properties of
u\mapsto\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)
. In particular, polynomial, entire, and polar structures in the
w
-plane correspond respectively to exponential type, anisotropic growth, and lattice singularities in the
u
-plane.

Theorem 3.11

(Classification of umbral Laplace series)
.

Let
\tilde{\Delta}
be an umbral Laplace series with Borel transform
u\mapsto\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
. Then exactly one of the following mutually exclusive cases occurs:

(i)

Polynomial case.

F
is a polynomial. Then
\hat{\Delta}
is a finite linear combination of exponentials and

\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}.

(ii)

Entire non-polynomial case.

F
is entire and non-polynomial. Then
\hat{\Delta}
is entire in the Borel plane with anisotropic growth, and

\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}\setminus t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\},

that is,
\tilde{\Delta}
is strictly 1-Gevrey divergent.

(iii)

Polar case.

F
has a finite number of poles
\{a_{j}\}_{j\in J}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then
\hat{\Delta}
is meromorphic in the Borel plane with a lattice of singularities at

u_{j,k}=\operatorname{Log}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{\zeta}\right)+2\pi ik\text{*}{j\in J,\ k\in\mathbb{Z},}

and

\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}\setminus t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\},

that is,
\tilde{\Delta}
is strictly 1-Gevrey divergent.

Proof.

By definition,
\hat{\Delta}
is holomorphic at the origin, so
\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
via theorem
2.22
.

In case (i),
F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
is entire of exponential type. By theorem
2.21
, its Laplace inverse
\tilde{\Delta}
is convergent, so
\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}
.

In case (ii),
\hat{\Delta}
is entire. However, if it were of exponential type in the whole Borel plane, lemma
3.9
would imply
F
is a polynomial, contradicting the assumption. Hence
\hat{\Delta}
exhibits anisotropic growth and is not of exponential type globally, meaning
\tilde{\Delta}
cannot be convergent.

In case (iii), the solutions to
\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}=a_{j}
immediately yield the stated logarithmic lattice of singularities. The presence of these finite singularities prevents
\hat{\Delta}
from being entire, thus
\tilde{\Delta}
is strictly 1-Gevrey divergent.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

By definition, umbral Laplace series belong to the 1-Gevrey class. Hence divergence in the non-polynomial cases is to be understood strictly in the 1-Gevrey sense. It arises from two distinct mechanisms: anisotropic growth in the entire case, and the presence of singularities in the polar case.

Proposition 3.13

(Nontriviality of umbral Laplace series)
.

The class of umbral Laplace series is non-empty and contains 1-Gevrey divergent elements.

Proof.

Consider the umbral Borel functional
\hat{\Delta}(u;1)=\exp(\operatorname{e}^{u})
, corresponding to the entire non-polynomial function
F(w)=\operatorname{e}^{w}
. Since
\hat{\Delta}(u;1)
defines a convergent germ in
\mathbb{C}\{u\}
, theorem
2.22
implies its formal Laplace inverse
\tilde{\Delta}
exists and belongs to
t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}
.

However, along the positive real axis,
|\hat{\Delta}(u;1)|=\exp(\operatorname{e}^{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}u})
grows super-exponentially. By lemma
3.9
, it is not of exponential type globally, excluding the possibility that
\tilde{\Delta}
is convergent. Therefore,
\tilde{\Delta}\in t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]_{1}\setminus t^{-1}\mathbb{C}\{t^{-1}\}
, confirming the existence of genuinely 1-Gevrey divergent elements.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

The previous proposition shows that the analytic umbral framework contains genuinely divergent, yet 1-Gevrey, Laplace series. In particular, the entire non-polynomial case is not a reformulation of the convergent polynomial case, but a genuinely asymptotic extension thereof.

Remark 3.15
.

The choice
\zeta=1
in proposition
3.13
is only made for simplicity. More generally, for any
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
the functional

u\mapsto\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})

is entire in the Borel plane but, unless the corresponding
F
is polynomial, it is never of exponential type as a function of the Borel variable
u
in the whole Borel plane. In particular, sectorial decay for suitable values of
\zeta
does not remove the 1-Gevrey divergence of the associated umbral Laplace series.

3.2
Parametric extensions

We now extend the basic construction by allowing affine rescalings in the additive Borel variable and multiplicative twists in the umbral variable.

Definition 3.16

(Parametric umbral Borel functionals)
.

Let

w=\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u},

where

{\beta,\nu\in\mathbb{C},\qquad\zeta,\mu,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}}

.
We call
parametric umbral Borel functional
any function of the form

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}F(w),

where
w\mapsto F(w)
is holomorphic at
w=\zeta^{\alpha}
and satisfies condition (i) or (ii) of
definition

3.2
.

Remark 3.17
.

The multiplicative prefactor
\zeta^{\beta}
and the exponential twist
\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}
do not alter the qualitative analytic classification established in theorem
3.11
. They only modify the corresponding growth rates and, in the Mellin picture, induce a multiplicative twist and an affine shift of the Mellin variable.

Remark 3.18
.

The basic class discussed above corresponds to the special choice

{\beta=\nu=0,\qquad\alpha=\mu=1}

.
In order not to burden the notation, the explicit dependence of
\hat{\Delta}
on all or some of the parameters will often be omitted when no confusion can arise.

Remark 3.19
.

The parametric class contains the elementary functional
\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}
and is stable under multiplication by exponentials
\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}
.
If
F
has poles at
\{a_{j}\}_{j\in J}
, then the singularities of the corresponding parametric functional are located at

u_{j,k}=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\operatorname{Log}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{\zeta^{\alpha}}\right)+2\pi ik\right)\text{*}{j\in J,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}.}

Remark 3.20

(Gevrey invariance)
.

By definition, umbral Borel functionals give rise to holomorphic germs at
u=0
. Therefore, the associated parametric umbral Laplace series always belong to the 1-Gevrey class.

The parameters
\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu
only modify the global analytic structure of the Borel functional, such as its growth, singular loci, and sectorial behaviour.

In particular, the parameter
\mu
rescales the singular lattice, rotates the distinguished decay directions, and changes the Mellin/Laplace representations. Thus
\mu
is invisible to the local Gevrey classification, but decisive for the global resurgent structure.

3.3
Laplace diagnostics and emergence of Mellin kernels

Although the analytic umbral pairing will ultimately be formulated in Mellin–Barnes terms, it is instructive to analyse the Laplace transformability of umbral Borel functionals. This provides a diagnostic tool revealing both the limitations of the Laplace framework and the emergence of canonical Mellin-type spectral objects.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\cdots)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}F\!\left(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}\right)

be a parametric umbral Borel functional. One may attempt to define directional Laplace transforms

\Delta^{\theta}(t;\cdots)=\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\Delta}(u;\cdots)\,\mathrm{d}u,

whenever the integrand has at most exponential growth along the ray
\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proposition 3.21

(Directional Laplace transform)
.

Let
\hat{\Delta}
be a parametric umbral Borel functional and let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
If, for fixed values of the parameters, one has

\hat{\Delta}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}(\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0})

for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, then the directional Laplace transform

\Delta^{\theta}(t;\cdots)=\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\Delta}(u;\cdots)\,\mathrm{d}u

is well defined and holomorphic in the half-plane
\Pi_{\tau}^{\theta}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Definition

2.30
.
∎

Remark 3.22

(Sectorial dependence)
.

In general, the existence of
\Delta^{\theta}
depends critically on the direction
\theta
, and different admissible directions may yield different analytic branches. Thus the Laplace description is inherently sectorial and non-canonical, necessitating a transition to a more global spectral representation.

3.3.1
Polynomial case: elementary kernels and distributional Mellin transform

We begin with the case where
F
is a polynomial. In this situation the corresponding umbral Borel functional is a finite linear combination of exponentials in the additive Borel variable, and the Laplace transform can be computed explicitly.

The fundamental building block is the elementary functional

\hat{\Delta}(u)=\operatorname{e}^{u},

corresponding to the choice
F(w)=w
with
\zeta=1
.

For any direction
\theta
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}((t-1)\operatorname{e}^{i\theta})>0
, one has

\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\operatorname{e}^{u}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{t-1}.

The directional Laplace transforms therefore combine to a single rational function

\Delta(t)=\frac{1}{t-1},

holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}_{t}\setminus\{1\}
.

For a polynomial of the general form

\hat{\Delta}(u)=\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}\sum_{m=0}^{N}a_{m}\operatorname{e}^{\mu mu},

one obtains the meromorphic transform

\Delta(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{a_{m}}{t-(\mu m+\nu)}.

Remark 3.23

(Absence of Stokes phenomenon)
.

In the polynomial case, the Laplace transform is independent of the direction and defines a single-valued meromorphic function. Thus no Stokes phenomenon occurs. This sharply distinguishes the polynomial case from both the entire non-polynomial and the rational regimes.

Remark 3.24

(Hyperfunctional interpretation)
.

The Borel functional
\operatorname{e}^{u}
admits a natural interpretation as an analytic functional of compact support. In the framework of Sternin–Shatalov
[
28
]
, the function
(t-1)^{-1}

is interpreted as the Cauchy transform of a hyperfunction supported at
t=1
. More generally, finite linear combinations of exponentials correspond to analytic functionals supported on finite sets.

Passing to the multiplicative Borel variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
, the kernel
\operatorname{e}^{u}
corresponds to the function
F(z)=z
. The associated Mellin transform is not defined in the classical sense, but admits a natural interpretation at the distributional level.

Indeed, for a test function
\phi
one has

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\operatorname{e}^{u}\phi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(\log z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

which shows that the kernel acts as evaluation at
z=1
in the multiplicative variable.

Remark 3.25

(Delta behaviour)
.

In this sense, the elementary umbral Borel functional
\operatorname{e}^{u}
behaves as a Dirac delta distribution supported at
z=1
in the multiplicative Borel variable. This distributional collapse explains why formal umbral calculus works flawlessly for polynomials without requiring the full Mellin-Barnes machinery: the spectral integral trivially evaluates to the operator
\mathfrak{u}
.

The polynomial case therefore provides the algebraic backbone of the theory. It yields convergent umbral Laplace series and admits a simple hyperfunctional interpretation, but does not exhibit genuine resurgent phenomena. The latter arise only beyond this regime.

3.3.2
Entire non-polynomial case: sectorial Laplace transforms and jump kernels

Assume that
F
is entire and non-polynomial, and consider the umbral Borel functional
\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta,\mu)=F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})
.
While
\hat{\Delta}
is entire in the additive Borel variable
u
, it typically exhibits anisotropic growth at infinity. The obstruction to global Laplace transformability is therefore due to this super-exponential growth in specific directions.

Remark 3.26

(Mittag–Leffler representation)
.

Let
F(w)=\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n}w^{n}
.
Formally integrating termwise along any admissible direction yields the local Mittag-Leffler representation

\Delta^{\theta}(t;\zeta,\mu)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{n}\zeta^{n}}{t-\mu n}.

This shows that the Laplace transform admits a meromorphic continuation with simple poles located at
t_{n}=\mu n
and residues
F_{n}\,\zeta^{n}
.
More precisely, for any interval
\Theta
of admissible directions, one can write

\Delta^{\Theta}(t;\zeta,\mu)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{F_{n}\zeta^{n}}{t-\mu n}+H_{N}^{\Theta}(t;\zeta,\mu),

where
H_{N}^{\Theta}
is holomorphic in the domain
\mathcal{D}(\Theta,f_{\tau})
.

Example 3.27

(The umbral Borel functional
\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
)
.

We examine the model example

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),

which captures the essential features of the whole class of entire Borel functionals.

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\zeta<0
, then for any
\theta\in\Theta^{+}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
the integrand decays sufficiently fast along the ray
u=re^{i\theta}
. The resulting sectorial transform, coinciding with the analytic continuation of the directional transform
\Delta^{0}(t;\zeta)
from the initial half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t>0
, evaluates to the incomplete gamma function:

\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}(t;\zeta)=(-\zeta)^{t}\Gamma(-t,-\zeta)\text{*}{\;t\in\mathbb{C}_{t}}

.

Conversely, for any fixed value of
\zeta
, the directional Laplace transform is well defined for any
\theta\in\Theta^{-}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)
. The resulting sectorial transform, coinciding with the analytic continuation of
\Delta^{\pi}(t;\zeta)
from the initial half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t<0
, captures the integration toward
u\to-\infty
:

\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)=-(-\zeta)^{t}\gamma(-t,-\zeta)=-(-\zeta)^{t}\left[\Gamma(-t)-\Gamma(-t,-\zeta)\right]\text{*}{\;t\in\mathbb{C}_{t}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

,
where
\gamma(-t,-\zeta)
denotes the lower incomplete gamma function.

In the intersection of the respective domains the two sectorial transforms differ by the
jump function

J(t;\zeta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}(t;\zeta)-\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)=(-\zeta)^{t}\Gamma(-t)}

.
This function is in fact meromorphic, due to the discrete pole structure generated by the asymptotic expansion at
u\to-\infty
.

Differently from the directional transforms, the jump function is a canonical construction, which can be identified with the bilateral Laplace transform along the real axis. After the change of variables
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
, it takes the form of a Mellin-type integral

J(t;\zeta)={\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{-t-1}\operatorname{e}^{\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z=(-\zeta)^{t}\Gamma(-t)}

.

Remark 3.28
.

The same mechanism persists for the general parametric exponential functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}\exp\bigl(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}\bigr).

Indeed, the transformations

u\mapsto{\frac{u}{\mu},\qquad t\mapsto\frac{t-\nu}{\mu},\qquad\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{\alpha}}

reduce the analysis to the previous case. In particular, the sectorial domains are rotated by
\operatorname{Arg}\mu
, the pole structure is rescaled by
\mu
, and the jump function becomes

J(t;\zeta)=\frac{1}{\mu}\zeta^{\,\beta-\alpha}(-\zeta^{\alpha})^{\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}\,\Gamma\left(-\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right),

with the same domain structure up to affine transformation.

Remark 3.29

(Mittag–Leffler representations and the jump function)
.

The pole structure of the bilateral Laplace transform is determined by the asymptotic expansion of the functional in the decay sector. For
\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
, one has, as
u\to-\infty
,

\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\operatorname{e}^{nu},

which yields formally the Mittag–Leffler expansion

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!(t-n)}.

In particular, since
\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}(t;\zeta)
is an entire function of
t
, the meromorphic principal part is carried entirely by
-\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)
:

-\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!(t-n)}\pmod{\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{C}_{t})}.

Consequently, the jump function

J(t;\zeta)=\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}(t;\zeta)-\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)

is a meromorphic function of
t
that exactly reproduces this principal part.
In this sense, the jump isolates the purely resurgent component of the Laplace transform, encoding the algebraic pole structure generated by the Mittag–Leffler expansion.

Remark 3.30

(The Meromorphicity of the jump kernel)
.

It is mathematically significant that
J(t;\zeta)
is a meromorphic function of
t
. While the sectorial Laplace transform
\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}
is inherently meromorphic – possessing poles that reflect the functional’s behaviour as
u\to-\infty
– the sectorial transform
\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}
is entire. Their difference distills the resurgent Stokes data into a globally canonical spectral object. Its simple poles at
t\in\mathbb{N}
provide the fundamental analytic representative required to extract the formal umbral coefficients
1/n!
via contour pairing.

Remark 3.31

(Paradigmatic character)
.

The preceding example is representative of the general behaviour of entire umbral Borel functionals.
Even in the absence of finite singularities in the Borel plane, the comparison of sectorial Laplace transforms produces a nontrivial, meromorphic jump kernel, which is naturally expressed as a Mellin-type integral. This kernel provides the robust spectral fingerprint required for the Mellin–Barnes pairing introduced in the following section.

Remark 3.32

(Ramanujan’s Master Theorem and Mellin–Barnes kernels)
.

The fact that the entire class produces a meromorphic Mellin–Barnes kernel is a structural manifestation of Ramanujan’s Master Theorem (
[
17
]
; see also
[
6
]
for a formal umbral derivation of the theorem). Recall that Ramanujan’s theorem asserts, under suitable analytic and growth assumptions, that if a function admits an expansion of the form

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(n)}{n!}(-z)^{n},

then its Mellin transform is given by

\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\Gamma(s)\phi(-s),

initially in a fundamental strip and then by meromorphic continuation.

In the umbral framework, passing to the multiplicative plane
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
transforms the paradigmatic entire functional into
\hat{K}(z)=\exp(\zeta z)
.

Expanding this function in Ramanujan’s alternating format gives

\exp(\zeta z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\zeta)^{n}}{n!}(-z)^{n},

so that the interpolating sequence is
\phi(n)=(-\zeta)^{n}
.

The bilateral jump kernel
J(t;\zeta)
is precisely the Mellin transform evaluated at the dual spectral variable
s=-t
. Applying Ramanujan’s Master Theorem therefore gives

J_{\hat{\Delta}}(t;\zeta)={\Gamma(-t)\phi(t)=(-\zeta)^{t}\Gamma(-t)}

,
with the power
(-\zeta)^{t}
understood on the chosen branch.

Thus Ramanujan’s Master Theorem explains why the Mellin representative of an entire umbral Borel functional is meromorphic in the dual spectral variable. From the resurgent viewpoint, this meromorphic kernel is the Mellin–Barnes manifestation of the singular behaviour of the entire functional at infinity. The analytic umbral pairing uses this classical Mellin interpolation mechanism to convert Borel-side data into a dual spectral kernel acting on analytic test germs.

3.3.3
Polar case: finite singularities, sectorial transforms and Stokes jumps

Assume that
F
is rational. Then the corresponding umbral Borel functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\cdots)=F(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})

is meromorphic in the Borel plane and develops a logarithmic lattice of singularities. In contrast with the entire case, the obstruction to Laplace transformability is now due to the presence of isolated singularities at finite distance from the origin.

By partial fraction decomposition, it suffices to consider the elementary rational functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\cdots)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}}{1-a\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}}.

The singularities are located at

u_{k}=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\operatorname{Log}\left(\frac{1}{a\zeta^{\alpha}}\right)+2\pi ik\right)\text{*}{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

,
and form a logarithmic lattice. The singular directions, corresponding to
\theta_{k}=\operatorname{Arg}u_{k}
, partition the Borel plane into sectors on which directional Laplace transforms can be defined.

Rather than analysing the problem in full generality, it is more instructive to examine a paradigmatic example, enabling to present all the relevant elements in explicit form.

Example 3.33

(The umbral Borel functional
1/(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
)
.

Consider the rational Borel functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}\text{*}{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

,
with principal determinations throughout.

The singularities are located at

u_{k}={\operatorname{Log}(1/\zeta)+2\pi ik=-\ln\,|\zeta|+i(-\operatorname{Arg}\zeta+2\pi k)\text{*}{k\in\mathbb{Z}}}

and form a logarithmic lattice. The condition
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
ensures that there are no poles on the real axis. Each residue is
\operatorname{Res}_{\,u=u_{k}}\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=-1
.

The corresponding singular directions
\theta_{k}=\operatorname{Arg}u_{k}
partition the Borel plane into admissible sectors.

In the region
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}u<-\ln\,|\zeta|
, one has the geometric expansion

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\operatorname{e}^{nu},

while for
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}u>-\ln\,|\zeta|
one has

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-n-1}\operatorname{e}^{-(n+1)u}.

These expansions yield, by formal termwise Laplace transform, Mittag–Leffler representations of the meromorphic components of sectorial Laplace transforms, with poles respectively at
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in-\mathbb{N}-1
.

In the special case
\ln\,|\zeta|=0
, there are only two admissible intervals, namely

\Theta^{+}={\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right),\qquad\Theta^{-}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)}

,
while for
\ln\,|\zeta|\neq 0
one obtains a countable family of intervals on one side and a single interval of aperture
\pi
on the other. In either case, we select the
distinguished pair of intervals

\Theta^{+}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{-},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\Theta^{+}\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{-}_{0},

where
\Theta^{+}_{0}
denotes the interval of aperture less that
\pi
containing
\theta=0
and
\Theta^{-}_{0}
the one containing
\theta=\pi
.

For each couple of intervals, the Stokes jump between the corresponding two sectorial transforms is obtained by Cauchy integration. As the integration contour rotates from
\theta=0
to
\theta=\pi
, it captures the infinite sequence of simple poles located at
u_{k}
for
k\geq 0
. The resulting jump is identical in both configurations and equates precisely to the sum of these Stokes residues:

J(t;\zeta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}(t;\zeta)-\Delta^{\Theta_{0}^{-}}(t;\zeta)=\Delta^{\Theta^{+}_{0}}(t;\zeta)-\Delta^{\Theta^{-}}(t;\zeta)}=-2\pi i\sum_{k\geq 0}\operatorname{Res}_{u=u_{k}}\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)\,\operatorname{e}^{-tu_{k}}.

Using the explicit form of the residues, this becomes

J(t;\zeta)=2\pi i\,\zeta^{t}\sum_{k\geq 0}\operatorname{e}^{-2\pi itk},

which can be summed geometrically (for
\operatorname{Im}t<0
), yielding the jump function

J(t;\zeta)=-(-\zeta)^{t}\pi\csc(\pi t),

initially defined in the strip
-1<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t<0
and extended meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.

The jump function admits the Mellin representation

J(t;\zeta)={\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-tu}}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{-t-1}}{1-\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z}

This identifies the jump as the bilateral Laplace transform along the real line, i.e. – after passing to the multiplicative variable
z
– the Mellin transform of the Borel functional. By the substitution
x=-\zeta z
, the integral evaluates directly to
-(-\zeta)^{t}\pi\csc(\pi t)
.

Remark 3.34

(Connection to the Euler functional
1/(\eta+\operatorname{e}^{u})
)
.

In applications involving divergent Euler-type series, one frequently encounters the shifted functional
\hat{\Delta}_{\text{Euler}}(u;\eta)=1/(\eta+\operatorname{e}^{u})
. This is trivially recovered from the paradigmatic form by setting
\zeta=-1/\eta
and scaling:

\frac{1}{\eta+\operatorname{e}^{u}}=\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{1}{1-(-\eta^{-1})\operatorname{e}^{u}}.

Applying this scaling to the jump function yields

J_{\text{Euler}}(t;\eta)=\frac{1}{\eta}J(t;-\eta^{-1})=-\eta^{-t-1}\pi\csc(\pi t),

which carries the exact same resurgent structure.

The analysis of the previous example extends directly to the general elementary rational functional depending on additional parameters.

Proposition 3.35

(General parametric jump formula)
.

Consider the elementary polar umbral Borel functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\dots)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}}{1-a\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}}\text{*}{\mu,\zeta,\alpha,a\in\mathbb{C}^{*},\quad\beta,\nu\in\mathbb{C}}

and assume that no pole of
\hat{\Delta}
lies on the oriented line defined by the condition
\mu u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then the corresponding jump function is represented by the bilateral Laplace integral

J(t;\dots)=\int_{\operatorname{e}^{-i\operatorname{Arg}\mu}\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\Delta}(u;\dots)\,\mathrm{d}u,

initially convergent in the strip
0<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{\nu-t}{\mu}\right)<1
.
Moreover, one has

J(t;\dots)=-\frac{\zeta^{\beta}}{\mu}\left(-a\zeta^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}\pi\csc\left(\pi\,\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right)

,
which extends meromorphically to the whole
t
-plane, with simple poles at

t_{k}=\nu+\mu k\text{*}{k\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Proof.

Introducing the multiplicative variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}
, the bilateral Laplace transform takes the canonical Mellin form

J(t;\dots)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}}{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{-\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}-1}}{1-a\zeta^{\alpha}z}\,\mathrm{d}z

.
Under the substitution
x=-a\zeta^{\alpha}z
, this becomes

\frac{\zeta^{\beta}}{\mu}\left(-a\zeta^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{-\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}-1}}{1+x}\,\mathrm{d}x

,
converging whenever
0<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{\nu-t}{\mu}\right)<1
.
The classical identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-1}}{1+x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\pi\csc(\pi s)\text{*}{0<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}s<1}

applied with
s=-\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}
directly yields the stated jump function. The meromorphic continuation and the location of the poles are an immediate consequence of the properties of the cosecant function.
∎

Remark 3.36

(Jump function as cumulative discontinuity)
.

The macroscopic jump function
J(t;\dots)
is structurally tied to the local discontinuity operator
\operatorname{disc}_{\theta}
introduced earlier. As the sectorial Laplace contour rotates from
\theta=0
to
\theta=\pi
, it crosses the countable set of singular rays
\theta_{k}\in(0,\pi)
.

By Cauchy’s theorem, the total sectorial difference is the sum of the Laplace transforms of the individual directional discontinuities:

J(t;\dots)={\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{k}}\!\left[\operatorname{disc}_{\theta_{k}}\hat{\Delta}\right]\!(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{0}^{\infty\operatorname{e}^{i\theta_{k}}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\operatorname{disc}_{\theta_{k}}\hat{\Delta}(u;\dots)\,\mathrm{d}u}

.
Because the local discontinuity is given by the hyperfunctional evaluation
\operatorname{disc}_{\theta_{k}}\hat{\Delta}=-2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{\,u_{k}}(\hat{\Delta})\,\delta(u-u_{k})
, this integral immediately evaluates to the geometric series of Stokes residues computed above. The jump function is therefore the global spectral synthesis of the local resurgent discontinuities.

Remark 3.37

(Structural interpretation)
.

The previous computation shows that the Mellin character of the jump is intrinsic to the exponential parametrisation
z=\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}
. After the scaling
w=\mu u
, the bilateral Laplace transform is converted directly into a Mellin integral in the multiplicative Borel variable.

In particular, the additive logarithmic lattice of singularities in the Borel plane gives rise, through the jump function, to a canonical meromorphic Mellin kernel in the spectral
t
-plane. This mechanism is the polar counterpart of the Mellin structure already observed in the entire case.

Remark 3.38

(Spectral synthesis)
.

The jump function
J(t;\dots)
enforces the spectral transition from the Laplace to the Mellin framework through two primary mechanisms:

1.

Cancellation of local algebraic poles:

The individual sectorial Laplace transforms
\Delta^{\Theta}
are meromorphic, carrying local poles induced by the logarithmic lattice in the Borel plane and by the asymptotic expansions at
u\to\pm\infty
. Since the principal parts at these poles are identical for both
\Delta^{\Theta^{+}}
and
\Delta^{\Theta^{-}_{0}}
, the jump function perfectly cancels these algebraic singularities, distilling the
purely resurgent
difference.

2.

Canonical Mellin representative:

The identification of
J(t;\dots)
with the bilateral Laplace transform proves that the jump is the
canonical spectral representative
of the functional. While individual directional transforms are multivalued and tied to the orientation of the logarithmic lattice,
J(t;\dots)
provides a single global entity. In the multiplicative variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
, this corresponds precisely to the Mellin transform.

3.4
Resurgent structure of umbral Borel functionals

Umbral Borel functionals naturally fit into the framework of resurgence theory introduced in
section

2.4.2
. We now make this relationship explicit.

Proposition 3.39

(Resurgence of the umbral class)
.

Let
\hat{\Delta}(u;\dots)
be an umbral Borel functional associated with a function
F(w)
as in
definition

3.2
. Then
\hat{\Delta}
is a resurgent Borel germ in the sense of
definition

2.57
.

Proof.

By construction,
\hat{\Delta}
is holomorphic at
u=0
. We distinguish the two admissible cases.

If
F
is rational, then
\hat{\Delta}(u)
is meromorphic in
\mathbb{C}_{u}
, with singular set given by a logarithmic lattice

\Omega={\left\{\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\operatorname{Log}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{\zeta^{\alpha}}\right)+2\pi ik\right)\;\middle|\;j\in J,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}}

,
which is discrete. Hence
\hat{\Delta}
is endlessly continuable.

Moreover, along any ray avoiding
\Omega
,
\hat{\Delta}
has at most exponential growth, since it is a rational function of
\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}
. Therefore
\hat{\Delta}
is a resurgent Borel germ.

If
F
is entire of exponential type and non-polynomial, then
\hat{\Delta}(u)=F(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})
is entire in
\mathbb{C}_{u}
, hence trivially endlessly continuable.

Its growth is anisotropic, but along any direction
\theta
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\mu\operatorname{e}^{i\theta})<0
, one has
\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}\to 0
as
|u|\to\infty
, and therefore
\hat{\Delta}(u)
remains bounded. Furthermore, because
F
satisfies the Mellin-transformability condition of
definition

3.2
(ii) (its indicator diagram is contained in an open half-plane), there exists a distinguished sector where the composition
F(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})
exhibits uniform exponential decay. These directions form a nonempty set of admissible directions, and along any non-singular ray the growth remains at most exponential. Hence
\hat{\Delta}
is again a resurgent Borel germ.
∎

Remark 3.40

(Logarithmic periodicity)
.

In the polar case, the singular lattice is
\frac{2\pi i}{\mu}
-periodic. The corresponding residues satisfy

\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega+\frac{2\pi i}{\mu}k}\,\hat{\Delta}(u)=\operatorname{Res}_{u=\omega}\,\hat{\Delta}(u)\operatorname{e}^{\frac{2\pi i}{\mu}\nu k},

reflecting the exponential twist
\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}
.

Remark 3.41

(Polar case and classical resurgence)
.

In the polar case, the resurgent structure is entirely encoded by the logarithmic lattice of singularities in the Borel plane. The corresponding Stokes discontinuities are given by finite or countable sums of exponential terms

2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{\,u=\omega}\hat{\Delta}(u)\,\operatorname{e}^{-\omega t},

in agreement with the description of alien derivatives in the simple-pole case given in remark
2.60
.

In particular, the jump functions computed in proposition
3.35
provide explicit representatives of the Stokes automorphisms associated with the singular directions of
\hat{\Delta}
.

Remark 3.42

(Entire case and resurgence at infinity)
.

In the entire non-polynomial case, the Borel functional
\hat{\Delta}
has no finite singularities, and the classical Stokes phenomenon is absent at finite distance.

However, after the change of variable
z=\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}
, the functional becomes
F(z)
, which typically has a singularity at
z=\infty
. The Mellin transform appearing in the jump functions of the entire case can then be interpreted as a Stokes phenomenon associated with this singularity at infinity.

In this sense, the entire case exhibits a form of
resurgence at infinity
, complementary to the lattice-driven resurgence of the polar case.

Remark 3.43

(Unified resurgent picture)
.

The umbral framework thus provides a unified class of resurgent Borel germs, in which:

•

polar functionals give rise to discrete resurgent structures governed by logarithmic lattices;

•

entire functionals give rise to continuous, Mellin-type structures associated with the point at infinity.

In both cases, the corresponding jump functions encode the global analytic information of the Borel germ: in the polar case through discrete Stokes contributions attached to the logarithmic lattice, and in the entire case through Mellin-type representations associated with the point at infinity.

These jump functions provide canonical analytic representatives of the resurgent data in the spectral Mellin plane.

3.5
From sectorial Laplace transforms to the canonical jump kernel

The preceding analysis shows that the Laplace framework is intrinsically sectorial and non-canonical. In the entire case, sectoriality is related to anisotropic growth, while in the rational case it is due to the presence of singularities in the Borel plane, producing genuine Stokes discontinuities.

In both situations, the globally meaningful object is not a sectorial Laplace transform, but the Mellin-type kernel obtained from the corresponding bilateral Laplace transform, i.e. the jump function.

This observation motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.44

(Jump function as canonical spectral representative)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}F(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})

be a parametric umbral Borel functional as in
definition

3.16
.

The associated jump function is defined as the bilateral Laplace transform

J(t;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\int_{\operatorname{e}^{-i\operatorname{Arg}\mu}\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\,\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)\,\mathrm{d}u.

In the polynomial case, the integral is understood in distributional sense.

Remark 3.45

(Well-posedness and decomposition)
.

The jump function is well defined for the class of umbral Borel functionals considered in this work.

Indeed, by construction, the defining function
F
decomposes into a finite sum of rational and entire components

F=R+\sum_{j}E_{j},

and the corresponding Borel functional decomposes accordingly.

The bilateral Laplace transform may therefore be evaluated separately for each term:

•

for rational components, convergence follows from the exponential decay away from the logarithmic lattice; the jump is given by an explicit Stokes residue summation, yielding a function meromorphic in
t
;

•

for entire components of exponential type, convergence holds along the oriented line
\operatorname{e}^{-i\operatorname{Arg}\mu}\mathbb{R}
, yielding a meromorphic function of
t
.

Remark 3.46

(Intrinsic Mellin structure)
.

Under the change of variables
z=\operatorname{e}^{u}
, the jump function takes the canonical Mellin form

J(t;\dots)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z;\dots)\,z^{-t}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z},

where

\hat{K}(z;\dots)=\zeta^{\beta}z^{\nu}F(\zeta^{\alpha}z^{\mu})

.

In particular, the jump function is, by construction, a Mellin transform (possibly in the distributional sense).

Jump functions associated with umbral Borel functionals are the canonical spectral representatives entering the Mellin–Barnes formulation of the analytic umbral pairing developed in the next section.

4
Spectral duality

In the following we establish a fundamental structural property of umbral Borel functionals: rational and a distinguished subclass of entire functionals—including finite linear combinations of exponential functionals—are not independent entities, but rather exhibit a remarkable spectral duality. We first prove this property in a simple model case.

Theorem 4.1

(Spectral regularisation identity)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}_{\exp}(u;\zeta)={\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\qquad\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}}(u;\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}}

.
Then their jump functions satisfy

J_{\exp}(t;\zeta)=\frac{J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\zeta)}{\Gamma(1+t)}.

Proof.

The jump functions are explicitly given by

J_{\exp}(t;\zeta)={(-\zeta)^{t}\,\Gamma(-t),\qquad J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\zeta)=-(-\zeta)^{t}\,\pi\csc(\pi t)}

.
Using Euler’s reflection formula

\Gamma(-t)\,\Gamma(1+t)=-\pi\csc(\pi t),

the result immediately follows.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Structural meaning)
.

The previous theorem shows that exponential umbral functionals are not independent of the rational class, but can be obtained from it by a Gamma-regularisation twist.

In particular, the discrete lattice structure encoded by the factor
\csc(\pi t)
in the rational case persists in the exponential case, but is algebraically modulated by the division by
\Gamma(1+t)
, which shifts the continuous contribution to the singularity at infinity.

This provides a unified interpretation of the rational and entire umbral classes within a single resurgent framework.

Remark 4.3

(Resurgent interpretation of the regularisation identity)
.

The spectral identity

J_{\exp}(t;\zeta)=\frac{J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\zeta)}{\Gamma(1+t)}

has a genuine analytic meaning in resurgence theory.

On the rational side, the jump kernel
J_{\mathrm{rat}}
encodes the discrete logarithmic lattice of Borel singularities of the kernel
(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
. Its poles in the spectral variable
t
are the Mellin image of this lattice structure and correspond to the local Stokes data carried by the simple alien derivatives at the singular points.

On the entire side, the jump kernel
J_{\exp}
corresponds to the kernel
\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}
, which has no finite singularities in the Borel plane. Its Stokes content is therefore attached to the irregular behaviour at infinity, and becomes visible only after passage to the Mellin variable.

The theorem shows that these two resurgent regimes are analytically equivalent at the level of spectral kernels: the entire jump is obtained directly from the rational jump by spectral division by the explicit Gamma factor
\Gamma(1+t)
. In this sense, the discrete pole-lattice regime and the entire regime are not separate constructions, but two Mellin-dual realisations of the same spectral structure.

The regularisation identity of
theorem

4.1
is invariant under affine transformations of the Mellin variable. In particular, it extends without change of principle to the parametric exponential and elementary rational kernels. This is made precise by the following definitions and theorem.

Definition 4.4

(Spectral duality of jump kernels)
.

Let
J_{1}(t;\dots)
and
J_{2}(t;\dots)
be the jump functions corresponding to the parametric Borel functionals
\hat{\Delta}_{1}(u;\dots)
and
\hat{\Delta}_{2}(u;\dots)
.

They are said to be in
spectral duality
if, in the normalised Mellin variable

s=\frac{t-\nu}{\mu},

one has

J_{1}(t;\dots)=\frac{J_{2}(t;\dots)}{\Gamma\!\left(1+s\right)}.

Definition 4.5

(Rational spectral duals)
.

Let
\hat{\Delta}_{E}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}E(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})
be an entire umbral Borel functional in the sense of
definition

3.16
. Its jump kernel
J_{E}(t;\dots)
is well defined by construction.

If there exists a rational umbral Borel functional with jump kernel
J_{R}
such that
J_{E}
and
J_{R}
are in spectral duality in the sense of
definition

4.4
, then
J_{R}
is called a
rational spectral dual
of
J_{E}
.

Theorem 4.6

(Existence of spectral duals in the parametric class)
.

The parametric exponential umbral Borel functional

\hat{\Delta}_{\exp}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}\exp(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})

admits a rational spectral dual, namely

\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}}{1-\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}}.

Proof.

The jump function associated with the parametric exponential kernel is

J_{\exp}(t;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}}{\mu}(-\zeta^{\alpha})^{\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}\Gamma\left(-\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right),

while the jump function of the parametric rational kernel is

J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=-\frac{\pi\,\zeta^{\beta}}{\mu}(-\zeta^{\alpha})^{\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}\csc\left(\pi\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right).

The reflection formula

\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(1+s)={-\pi\csc(\pi s),\quad\text{with }s=\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}}

directly yields

J_{\exp}(t;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\frac{J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right)}.

Hence the two jump kernels are in spectral duality in the sense of definitions
4.4
and
4.5
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The condition of
definition

4.5
is satisfied in particular by finite linear combinations of exponential-polynomial components

w^{m}\operatorname{e}^{aw}\text{*}{m\in\mathbb{N},\ a\in\mathbb{C}^{*}},

since their jump functions are finite linear combinations of Gamma-type factors, and the Gamma-regularised kernels are meromorphic of lattice type. In particular, they correspond to parametric rational umbral Borel functionals with finitely many poles.

4.1
Pólya realisation of spectral duality

The previously established spectral duality admits a natural extension to a broader class of entire umbral Borel functionals through the Pólya representation of entire functions of exponential type. This provides a structural mechanism lifting the elementary duality

\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{1-\lambda w}

to general functionals by superposition.

Definition 4.8

(Pólya representation)
.

Let
E
be an entire function of exponential type. A
Pólya representation
of
E
is a representation of the form

E(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

where
\gamma
is a contour in
\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{P}_{E}
is an analytic functional (possibly distributional) of finite exponential type.

The contour
\gamma
is chosen so that the integral converges and reproduces the Taylor coefficients of
E
via

a_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\lambda^{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Remark 4.9

(Interpretation of the Pólya density and contour)
.

The Pólya density
\mathcal{P}_{E}
can be interpreted as a distributional realisation of the formal Borel transform of
E
. Indeed, if

E(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}w^{n},

then formally

\mathcal{B}E(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}\,\lambda^{n},

and the Pólya representation recovers
E
as a Laplace-type transform of this Borel data.

In general,
\mathcal{P}_{E}
is not a function but an analytic functional whose singular support encodes the exponential growth of
E
.

The contour
\gamma
is chosen so as to enclose the conjugate indicator diagram of
E
, i.e. the convex compact set describing the exponential type of
E
. In particular, if
E
has exponential type bounded by a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, then
\gamma
can be taken as a contour surrounding
K
, and
\mathcal{P}_{E}
is supported in
K
.

Remark 4.10
.

The representation
4.8
is classical in the theory of entire functions of exponential type and analytic functionals, see e.g.
[
26
]
. In general,
\mathcal{P}_{E}
is not unique and may be a distribution supported on a suitable set in the complex plane.

Definition 4.11

(Pólya–Mellin admissible entire functionals)
.

An entire umbral Borel functional

\hat{\Delta}_{E}(u;\zeta)=E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})

is said to be
Pólya–Mellin admissible
if:

(i)

E
admits a Pólya representation as in definition
4.8
;

(ii)

there exists a line
L_{\theta}=\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\subset\mathbb{C}_{z}
such that the function
z\mapsto E(\zeta z)
is Mellin-transformable along
L_{\theta}
, i.e.

\int_{L_{\theta}}z^{t-1}E(\zeta z)\,\mathrm{d}z

converges for
t
in a vertical strip.

Remark 4.12

(Growth constraints from Mellin-transformability)
.

The Mellin-transformability condition imposes restrictions on the Pólya representation. Writing

E(\zeta z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda\zeta z}\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

the convergence of the Mellin transform along a line
L_{\theta}=\operatorname{e}^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
requires that

\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\lambda\zeta z)<0

for
z\in L_{\theta}
and
\lambda\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{P}_{E})
.

Thus the support of
\mathcal{P}_{E}
must lie in a sector compatible with the chosen Mellin direction. Equivalently, the indicator diagram of
E
must be contained in a half-plane dual to the Mellin contour.

This condition ensures that the Pólya integral and the Mellin transform can be interchanged.

Definition 4.13

(Pólya-resolvent dual)
.

Let
E
be Pólya–Mellin admissible. Its
Pólya-resolvent dual
is the function

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda w}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

whenever the integral converges.

Theorem 4.14

(Spectral duality via Pólya representation)
.

Let
E
be Pólya–Mellin admissible and let
R_{E}
be its Pólya-resolvent dual. Then the corresponding jump functions satisfy

J_{E}(t;\zeta)=\frac{J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)}{\Gamma(1+t)}.

Proof.

By
definition

4.8
,

E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

By linearity, the jump function is

J_{E}(t;\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}J_{\exp}(t;\lambda\zeta)\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Similarly,

J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\lambda\zeta)\,\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

The elementary duality

J_{\exp}(t;\lambda\zeta)=\frac{J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\lambda\zeta)}{\Gamma(1+t)}

can therefore be integrated against
\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)
, yielding the result.
∎

Remark 4.15

(Classical umbral kernels as distributional Pólya functionals)
.

The usual exponential kernels correspond to Dirac-type Pólya densities. For instance,

E(w)=\operatorname{e}^{aw}

corresponds to

\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)=2\pi i\,\delta(\lambda-a),

and yields

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{1-aw}.

More generally, exponential-polynomial functions correspond to derivatives of Dirac distributions and produce higher-order rational functions.

Remark 4.16

(The Pólya-resolvent dual)
.

The Pólya-resolvent dual
R_{E}(w)
constructed in
theorem

4.14
is not, in general, a simple rational function, but rather a Borel-summed resolvent. Its finite singularities in the
w
-plane are determined by the contour
\gamma
, which encloses the conjugate indicator diagram of
E(w)
. The theorem demonstrates that the mapping
\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\mapsto(1-\lambda w)^{-1}
acts as a desingularisation map: it translates the transcendental growth of
E(w)
at infinity into explicit finite analytic singularities for
R_{E}(w)
.

Remark 4.17

(Universal spectral transmutation)
.

From a resurgent perspective,
theorem

4.14
establishes a universal spectral transmutation. It proves that the continuous spectrum of any entire function of exponential type (encoded by its Pólya indicator diagram) is spectrally dual to the discrete/branch spectrum of its resolvent counterpart. The algebraic operation of regularisation by
\Gamma(1+t)^{-1}
is therefore identified as the canonical spectral operator that intertwines the geometry of continuous growth with the arithmetic of discrete lattice jumps.

Example 4.18

(Discrete Pólya support)
.

Let

E(w)=\operatorname{e}^{aw}+\operatorname{e}^{bw}.

Pólya representation..
The Pólya density is

\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)=2\pi i\,\bigl[\delta(\lambda-a)+\delta(\lambda-b)\bigr].

Resolvent dual..
Substituting into
Definition

4.13
yields

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{1-aw}+\frac{1}{1-bw}.

Interpretation..
Finite discrete Pólya support produces a rational resolvent dual with simple poles at the support points.

Example 4.19

(Continuous Pólya support)
.

Let

E(w)={\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\frac{\sinh w}{w}}

Pólya representation..
The Pólya density is

\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)=\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(\lambda),

where
\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}
denotes the indicator function of the subset
[-1,1]
.

Resolvent dual..
The resolvent dual is

R_{E}(w)={\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{1-\lambda w}=\frac{1}{2w}\log\left(\frac{1+w}{1-w}\right)}

Interpretation..
Continuous Pólya support produces a logarithmic resolvent dual with branch points determined by the support interval. This shows that the Pólya realisation extends the rational spectral duality from finite discrete Pólya support to genuinely continuous spectral distributions.

Anticipating the analytic pairing.

The ultimate operational consequence of spectral duality is that it descends to the level of explicit umbral evaluations. Although the formal analytic pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
will be rigorously defined and studied in
section

5
, it is instructive to anticipate its action here. As we will see, the pairing of an umbral functional with a test germ
\varphi(t)
can be realised via an integral against a corresponding Mellin kernel
W_{\varphi}(x)
. The following two examples demonstrate how Pólya spectral duality manifests perfectly in these explicit computations.

Example 4.20

(Explicit Pólya pairing)
.

Let

E(w)={\operatorname{e}^{aw}+\operatorname{e}^{bw},\qquad\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)}

Pólya representation..
The Pólya density is

\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)=2\pi i\bigl(\delta(\lambda-a)+\delta(\lambda-b)\bigr).

Analytic pairing..
Using linearity,

\langle\hat{\Delta}_{E},\varphi\rangle=\langle\operatorname{e}^{a\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle+\langle\operatorname{e}^{b\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle.

Each term is explicitly computable via the Mellin kernel

W_{\varphi}(x)=x\operatorname{e}^{-x},

giving

\langle\operatorname{e}^{c\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle={\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{c\zeta x}\,\operatorname{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{1-c\zeta}\text{*}{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(1-c\zeta)>0}}

.

Result..
Therefore

\langle\hat{\Delta}_{E},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{1-a\zeta}+\frac{1}{1-b\zeta},

which coincides with the pairing of the rational dual

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{1-aw}+\frac{1}{1-bw}.

Interpretation..
This example shows explicitly that the Pólya superposition commutes with the umbral pairing and that spectral duality reduces the entire functional to a rational one at the level of the analytic result.

Example 4.21

(A non-discrete Pólya kernel)
.

Let

E(w)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\operatorname{e}^{\lambda w}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Then

E(w)=\frac{\sinh w}{w},

with the value at
w=0
understood by analytic continuation. Thus
E
is entire of exponential type
1
, but its Pólya kernel is genuinely continuous.

Pólya-resolvent dual..
The associated resolvent dual is

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{1-\lambda w}.

For
w\notin(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)
, this gives

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{2w}\log\left(\frac{1+w}{1-w}\right).

This is not rational: it is a logarithmic resolvent kernel with branch points at
w=\pm 1
.

Pairing with
\Gamma(1+t)
..
Let

\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t).

Since

W_{\varphi}(x)=x\operatorname{e}^{-x},

the Mellin realisation of the pairing gives

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}E(\zeta x)\,\operatorname{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Substituting
E(w)=\sinh w/w
, one obtains

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\zeta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-x}\frac{\sinh(\zeta x)}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-x}\frac{\sinh(\zeta x)}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right),

one finds

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\zeta}\log\left(\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right).

Spectral-duality justification..
By definition of the resolvent dual, one has

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda w}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

For the elementary rational kernel
1/(1-\lambda\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
, the unreflected analytic pairing with
\varphi(t)=1
evaluates precisely to the function itself via Mellin inversion:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{\mathrm{rat}}(t;\lambda\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{1-\lambda\zeta}.

By the spectral duality identity
J_{E}(t;\zeta)=J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)/\Gamma(1+t)
, the analytic pairing with
\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)
yields

\langle\hat{\Delta}_{E},\Gamma(1+t)\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{E}(t;\zeta)\Gamma(1+t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By linearity and exchange of integrals, this is:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=R_{E}(\zeta).

Hence the analytic pairing coincides identically with the resolvent dual evaluated at
w=\zeta
.

Interpretation..
The analytic pairing coincides with the Pólya-resolvent dual evaluated at
w=\zeta
:

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle=R_{E}(\zeta).

Thus the continuous Pólya kernel produces a non-rational spectral dual with logarithmic branch points. This shows that the Pólya realisation extends the rational spectral duality from finite discrete Pólya support to genuinely continuous spectral distributions.

Example 4.22

(Bessel function of the first kind)
.

Let
E(w)
be the zeroth-order Bessel function,

E(w)=J_{0}(w).

This is an entire function of exponential type
1
.

Pólya representation and Resolvent dual..
Using the classical integral representation of the Bessel function, the Pólya indicator diagram is the continuous segment
[-i,i]
on the imaginary axis. Integrating the corresponding Pólya density against the elementary pole yields the resolvent dual:

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}.

Similar to the previous example, the continuous support generates a non-rational dual with branch cuts at
w=\pm i
.

Jump kernels..
The entire jump kernel
J_{E}
is the Mellin transform of
J_{0}(\zeta z)
, which yields a characteristic ratio of Gamma functions:

J_{E}(t;\zeta)=2^{-t-1}\zeta^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t/2)}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}.

The polar jump kernel
J_{R}
is the Mellin transform of
(1+(\zeta z)^{2})^{-1/2}
. By substitution, this evaluates via the Beta function to:

J_{R}(t;\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t/2)\Gamma\left(\frac{1+t}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

Spectral-duality justification..
According to the universal spectral regularisation identity, we must have
J_{E}(t;\zeta)\Gamma(1+t)=J_{R}(t;\zeta)
. Multiplying the entire jump by
\Gamma(1+t)
yields:

J_{E}(t;\zeta)\Gamma(1+t)=2^{-t-1}\zeta^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t/2)}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}\Gamma(1+t).

Applying the Legendre duplication formula
\Gamma(1+t)=\frac{2^{t}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+t}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(1+\frac{t}{2}\right)
, the
\Gamma(1+t/2)
term perfectly cancels, leaving:

J_{E}(t;\zeta)\Gamma(1+t)=\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t/2)\Gamma\left(\frac{1+t}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}=J_{R}(t;\zeta).

Interpretation..
This example demonstrates that the framework rigorously handles higher transcendental functions. Furthermore, it reveals a profound mechanism: the spectral regularisation division mapping the polar jump to the entire jump is exacted mechanically via the Legendre duplication formula.

Example 4.23

(Wright function and Mittag-Leffler pairing)
.

Let
E(w)
be the Wright generalized Bessel function,

E(w)={W_{\alpha,\,\beta}(w)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{r}}{r!\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)}\text{*}{\alpha,\beta>0}}

This is an entire function of order
\rho=1/(1+\alpha)<1
. Because it is of minimal exponential type, its Pólya indicator diagram collapses to the origin, representing a boundary case for the resolvent dual which manifests as a transcendental fractional resolvent rather than an elementary branch cut.

Jump kernels..
Despite this, the macroscopic Stokes jump is perfectly well-defined via Ramanujan’s Master Theorem. Setting
w=\zeta z
, the alternating sequence is
\phi(r)=1/\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)
. Evaluating the Mellin transform at the spectral dual variable
s=-t
yields the corresponding meromorphic jump kernel:

J_{E}(t;\zeta)=(-\zeta)^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t)}{\Gamma(\alpha t+\beta)}.

By the universal regularisation identity, its polar spectral dual is immediately:

J_{R}(t;\zeta)={(-\zeta)^{t}\frac{\Gamma(-t)\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(\alpha t+\beta)}=-(-\zeta)^{t}\frac{\pi\csc(\pi t)}{\Gamma(\alpha t+\beta)}}

.

Interpretation and anticipating the pairing..
This functional is of paramount importance in fractional calculus. Anticipating the formal umbral pairing with the test function
\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)
, the action of the pairing exactly annihilates the factorial in the denominator of
W_{\alpha,\beta}
, yielding:

\left\langle W_{\alpha,\,\beta}(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle={\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{r}}{\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)}=E_{\alpha,\,\beta}(\zeta)}

.
Thus, within this framework, the Wright function is identified as the exact umbral Borel pre-image of the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Example 4.24

(Mittag-Leffler functional and the spectral ladder)
.

Conversely, let
E(w)
be the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function,

E(w)={E_{\alpha,\,\beta}(w)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{r}}{\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)}}

.

Growth and admissibility constraints..
The order of growth is
\rho=1/\alpha
. Consequently,
E_{\alpha,,\beta}(w)
is an admissible entire Borel functional strictly when
\alpha\geq 1
.

Jump kernels and the resolvent ladder..
Assuming
\alpha\geq 1
, the sequence for Ramanujan’s Master Theorem is
\phi(r)=r!/\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)
. The associated meromorphic jump kernel evaluates to:

J_{E}(t;\zeta)=-(-\zeta)^{t}\frac{\pi\csc(\pi t)}{\Gamma(\alpha t+\beta)}.

Notice that this entire jump is identical to the polar jump of the Wright function. However, the Pólya-resolvent dual of the Mittag-Leffler function shifts the factorial weight up again:

J_{R}(t;\zeta)={J_{E}(t;\zeta)\Gamma(1+t)=(-\zeta)^{t}\frac{\pi^{2}\csc(\pi t)^{2}}{\Gamma(-t)\Gamma(\alpha t+\beta)}}

This demonstrates that the Pólya-resolvent mapping is an asymmetric spectral ladder: it maps the Wright function to the Mittag-Leffler function, and the Mittag-Leffler function to a higher-order factorial series
\sum n!w^{n}/\Gamma(\alpha n+\beta)
.

Analytic pairing..
To step backward down the ladder and recover the Wright function, one must use the analytic pairing with the inverse test function
\varphi(t)=1/\Gamma(1+t)
, which exacts the inverse operation of division by
n!
:

\left\langle E_{\alpha,\,\beta}(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle={\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{r}}{r!\Gamma(\alpha r+\beta)}=W_{\alpha,\,\beta}(\zeta)}

.

Remark 4.25

(Termination of the spectral ladder)
.

The asymmetry of the Pólya-resolvent mapping reveals a fundamental analytic limitation: the spectral ladder cannot be climbed indefinitely. Because each application of the resolvent dual multiplies the Taylor coefficients by
n!
, the order of growth of the resultant functional progressively degrades.

Starting from the Wright function (
m=0
) and stepping upward, the order of growth at the
m
-th step is
\rho=1/(\alpha-m)
. Unless the fractional parameter
\alpha
is continually forced to higher values, the ladder rapidly breaks analyticity. For instance, if
\alpha=1
, the first step yields the standard exponential (admissible entire), the second step yields the geometric series
\sum w^{n}=(1-w)^{-1}
(polar class, finite radius of convergence), and the third step collapses into Euler’s divergent series
\sum n!w^{n}
, which has a zero radius of convergence. The umbral framework thus naturally bounds itself, transitioning from entire functions, to meromorphic germs, to purely formal asymptotic series.

5
The Analytic Umbral Pairing

Classical umbral calculus is fundamentally algebraic, defining umbral operators strictly as formal linear functionals on the vector space of polynomials. In this section, we elevate this algebraic operation to a topological duality between asymptotically matched function spaces.

The previous sections demonstrated that the global analytic content of an umbral Borel functional is perfectly encoded by its jump kernel
J(t)
in the spectral plane. Because these jump kernels typically exhibit exponential vertical growth (e.g., via the discrete lattice regularisation
\csc(\pi t)
), their integration requires a specialized test space of functions to absorb this growth.

Therefore, we define the analytic umbral pairing
\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle
directly as a Mellin–Barnes contour integral. In this framework, the “ground states”
\varphi(t)
serve as a test space of continuous functions with super-exponential vertical decay (driven by Gamma ratios), while the umbral jump kernels act as the continuous linear functionals operating upon them. The pairing is thus treated as a conditional functional duality: it is rigorously defined strictly when the exponential decay of the chosen ground state majorizes the specific vertical growth of the umbral operator.

5.1
Admissible ground state functions

Definition 5.1

(Branched admissible ground states)
.

A branched admissible ground state is a function of the form

\varphi(t)=C\,\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\Gamma(a_{j}t+b_{j})^{m_{j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{q}\Gamma(\alpha_{k}t+\beta_{k})^{n_{k}}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}(t+\lambda_{\ell})^{-\sigma_{\ell}},

where

C\in{\mathbb{C},\qquad a_{j},\alpha_{k},b_{j},\beta_{k},\lambda_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C},\qquad m_{j},n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}}

,
the affine arguments

a_{j}t+b_{j},\qquad\alpha_{k}t+\beta_{k}

are not identically zero, and a branch is fixed for each factor
(t+\lambda_{\ell})^{-\sigma_{\ell}}
.

Definition 5.2

(Admissible ground states)
.

An admissible ground state is a branched admissible ground state with

r=0.

Equivalently,

\varphi(t)=C\,\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\Gamma(a_{j}t+b_{j})^{m_{j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{q}\Gamma(\alpha_{k}t+\beta_{k})^{n_{k}}}.

Remark 5.3

(Scope of admissible ground states and logarithmic factors)
.

While the restricted algebraic form of
definition

5.1
may initially appear narrow, it is purposefully designed to capture almost all non-pathological special functions while guaranteeing the global convergence of the Mellin–Barnes contour. Specifically, finite ratios of Gamma functions constitute the exact spectral signatures of the generalized hypergeometric class (including Meijer
G
-functions and Fox
H
-functions). Consequently, the admissible ground states natively span the umbral test spaces for virtually all solutions to linear differential equations with polynomial coefficients, including fractional and integral operators parameterized by the algebraic branch factors
(t+\lambda_{\ell})^{-\sigma_{\ell}}
.

This structural restriction is an analytic necessity rather than a severe limitation. The required asymptotic decay of the ground state along vertical lines (
\sim\operatorname{e}^{-\pi|\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}t|/2}
), intrinsic to the Gamma function, precisely balances the exponential growth of the umbral jump kernels (such as
\csc(\pi t)
). Functions excluded by this definition—such as doubly-periodic elliptic functions (which generate 2D pole lattices) or functions with super-exponential Gaussian decay—are structurally incompatible with the 1D separating contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
or violate Mellin inversion criteria.

Finally, the framework elegantly accommodates logarithmic behavior without requiring explicit
\log(t)
insertions. Logarithmic phenomena in the physical or Borel variables correspond directly to multiple poles in the spectral
t
-plane. Because the definition permits arbitrary integer exponents
m_{j},n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
on the constituent Gamma functions, the collision of affine pole sequences naturally generates higher-order singularities. Evaluation of the Barnes integral at an
m
-th order pole classically yields the required
\log^{m-1}
terms, ensuring that logarithmic test functions and distributions are fully embedded within the meromorphic structure of the Gamma ratios.

5.2
Umbral pairing as a Mellin–Barnes integral

Definition 5.4

(Admissible pairing data)
.

Let
\hat{\Delta}
be an umbral Borel functional and let

J(t;\dots)={\int_{\operatorname{e}^{-i\operatorname{Arg}\mu}\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{e}^{-tu}\hat{\Delta}(u;\dots)\,\mathrm{d}u=\mathcal{M}_{-\operatorname{Arg}\mu}[\hat{\Delta}](-t)}

denote the corresponding jump function as in
definition

3.44
. Let
\varphi
be a branched admissible ground state.

We say that
(\hat{\Delta},\varphi)
is admissible for umbral pairing if there exists a Barnes contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
such that:

(i)

\mathcal{C}_{t}
avoids the poles and branch cuts of both
J(t;\dots)
and
\varphi(t)
;

(ii)

\mathcal{C}_{t}
strictly separates the left-extending singularity sequences of the integrand from the right-extending singularity sequences, keeping the former entirely to the left of the contour and the latter entirely to its right;

(iii)

the integral

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\,\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

converges absolutely.

Remark 5.5

(Partitioning of bilateral pole sequences)
.

While the left-extending poles generally originate from the ground state
\varphi(t)
and the right-extending poles from the jump kernel
J(t)
, certain umbral operators generate bilateral singularity sequences. For example, the discrete lattice regularisation naturally introduces terms such as
\csc(\pi t)
, which possess poles at all integers
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. In such cases, the contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
must partition the sequence, keeping the non-negative integers
t\geq 0
to the right to correctly evaluate the umbral series, while pushing the negative integers
t\leq-1
to the left.

Definition 5.6

(Analytic umbral pairing)
.

Let
(\hat{\Delta},\varphi)
be admissible in the sense of
Definition

5.4
. The analytic umbral pairing is defined by

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\,\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

.

Remark 5.7

(Stratification of the function spaces)
.

The pair-wise admissibility condition in
definition

5.4
resolves a potential circularity in defining the function spaces. Because the umbral jump kernels exhibit varying degrees of exponential vertical growth, there is no single, monolithic test space that can absorb all possible umbral operators.

Instead, both the space of jump kernels and the space of admissible ground states are
stratified
by their asymptotic behavior along the imaginary axis. An umbral functional
\hat{\Delta}
belonging to a specific exponential growth stratum acts as a rigorous, continuous linear functional exclusively on the subspace of ground states whose exponential decay stratum strictly majorizes it. Therefore, checking if
(\hat{\Delta},\varphi)
forms an ”admissible couple” is equivalent to verifying that the two functions are drawn from compatible topological strata.

Remark 5.8

(Contour invariance)
.

The Mellin–Barnes contour contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
runs from
-i\infty
to
+i\infty
, possibly deformed to avoid branch cuts, and separates the singularities of
J
from those of
\varphi
.
While it is defined topologically rather than rigidly, the value of the integral is strictly unique. By Cauchy’s integral theorem, any continuous deformation of
\mathcal{C}_{t}
leaves the pairing invariant, provided the deformation does not cross any singularities of the integrand. Thus, the condition of separating the left and right singularity families uniquely determines the value of the umbral pairing.

Remark 5.9

(Polynomial case and distributional Mellin kernels)
.

If
F
is polynomial, then
\hat{\Delta}(u)
is a finite linear combination of exponentials
\operatorname{e}^{\mu nu}
, and the jump function reduces formally to a finite linear combination of Dirac distributions:

J(t;\dots)=\sum_{n}c_{n}\,\delta(t-n).

In this case, the Mellin representation is to be understood in this distributional sense, and the umbral pairing reduces to

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\sum_{n}c_{n}\,\varphi(n).

This expression coincides with the value obtained by a classical Cauchy integral selecting the residues of the integrand at the points
t=n
. The distributional formulation, therefore, only provides a compact encoding of an entirely classical residue computation.

Proposition 5.10

(Reduction to a Mellin integral)
.

Let
(\hat{\Delta},\varphi)
be admissible and let
J(t;\dots)
be the associated jump function.
Assume that:

(i)

there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
J(t;\dots)\,\varphi(t)
is holomorphic on the vertical line
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t=c
;

(ii)

there exist constants
A,B>0
such that along this line

|J(c+is;\dots)|\leq A(1+|s|)^{B};

(iii)

the ground state satisfies a vertical decay estimate

|\varphi(c+is)|\leq C(1+|s|)^{-2-B-\epsilon}\text{for some $\epsilon>0$}

;

(iv)

the Mellin inversion

W_{\varphi}(z)={\mathcal{M}^{-1}[\varphi](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\varphi(t)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t}

holds pointwise for
x>0
.

Then the umbral pairing admits a formulation in terms of the Mellin–Parseval integral

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z;\dots)\,W_{\varphi}(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}

.

Proof.

By
definition

5.6
,

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\,\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is a vertical line
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t=c
.

As shown in remark
3.46
, the jump function admits the Mellin representation

J(t;\dots)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z;\dots)\,z^{-t}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}.

Substituting this expression yields

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z;\dots)\,z^{-t}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}\right)\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By assumptions (ii)–(iii), the integrand satisfies

|\hat{K}(z;\dots)\,z^{-t}\,\varphi(t)|\leq C_{z}(1+|t|)^{-2-\epsilon},

uniformly for
t
on the contour, so that the integral is absolutely convergent. Hence Fubini’s theorem applies and the order of integration may be exchanged:

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z;\dots)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\varphi(t)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{zz}.

By assumption (iv), the inner integral coincides with
W_{\varphi}(x)
, yielding the result.
∎

Remark 5.11

(Gamma-ratio ground states and guaranteed Mellin inversion)
.

While proposition
5.10
is stated with general analytic assumptions, these conditions are intrinsically satisfied by the space of admissible ground states. By Stirling’s formula, finite ratios of Gamma functions exhibit super-polynomial (exponential) decay along vertical lines, strictly satisfying assumption (iii). Furthermore, this extreme vertical decay unconditionally guarantees the validity of the Mellin inversion in assumption (iv) via classical Fourier–Mellin theory. Consequently, every admissible ground state
\varphi(t)
rigorously maps to a well-defined function
W_{\varphi}(z)
(specifically, within the Fox
H
-function class or its fractional extensions), ensuring that the Mellin–Parseval reduction universally applies to this test space.

Remark 5.12

(Topological duality and asymptotic matching)
.

The Mellin–Parseval reduction in
proposition

5.10
reveals the deep functional-analytic structure of the umbral pairing. Rather than a purely formal algebraic operation, the pairing
\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle
constitutes a rigorous topological duality between asymptotically matched function spaces.

The Gamma-ratio ground states serve as the test space, providing the necessary exponential vertical decay, while the umbral jump kernels act as the continuous linear functionals (the dual space) operating upon them. Because specific jump kernels exhibit varying degrees of exponential growth, they do not pair unconditionally with all admissible ground states. The convergence requirement in
definition

5.4
(iii) ensures that the dual space is strictly bounded by the topology of the chosen test space: the Gamma-induced decay of
\varphi(t)
must strictly majorize the growth of
J(t)
. When this condition is met, the Mellin–Parseval identity establishes a continuous isomorphism between the distributional pairing in the complex spectral space and the convolution pairing in the physical space.

Furthermore, if the umbral parameter
\mu
is complex, its phase induces an asymmetric exponential growth along the vertical spectral axis; the decay stratum of the chosen ground state must be robust enough to majorize this phase-induced growth in both directions.

5.3
The Hankel reduction for entire ground states

The vertical decay assumptions in
proposition

5.10
naturally exclude entire ground states such as
\varphi(t)=1/\Gamma(1+t)
, which grow exponentially along the imaginary axis and therefore do not admit a standard vertical Mellin inversion to the physical real line. For such states, the umbral pairing is evaluated by shifting the integration to a complex loop contour.

Proposition 5.13

(Reduction to a complex contour integral)
.

Let
(\hat{\Delta},\varphi)
be admissible and let
J(t;\dots)
be the associated jump function. Assume that:

(i)

the ground state
\varphi(t)
is an entire function exhibiting exponential vertical growth;

(ii)

\varphi(t)
admits a complex integral representation

\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\Phi(z)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}z

,
where
\mathcal{H}
is a loop contour in the complex
z
-plane and
\Phi(z)
is holomorphic along and inside the region bounded by
\mathcal{H}
;

(iii)

the joint integral converges absolutely, allowing the exchange of the
t
and
z
contours.

Then the umbral pairing admits a formulation in terms of the contour integral

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\hat{K}(z^{-1};\dots)\,\Phi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

.

Proof.

By
definition

5.6
, the pairing is given by

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\,\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

.
Substituting the contour representation of
\varphi(t)
yields

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\Phi(z)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)\mathrm{d}t

.

By assumption (iii), Fubini’s theorem allows the order of integration to be exchanged:

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\Phi(z)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J(t;\dots)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}z

.

The inner integral over
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is exactly the inverse Mellin transform of
J(t;\dots)
evaluated at
z^{-1}
. As established, this inversion reconstructs the analytically continued global kernel
\hat{K}
in the multiplicative Borel plane, yielding
\hat{K}(z^{-1};\dots)
and proving the result.
∎

Remark 5.14

(Classical umbral sequences and generalized Hankel contours)
.

Proposition
5.13
rigorously encompasses the most fundamental ground state of formal indicial umbral calculus,
\varphi(t)=1/\Gamma(1+t)
, which corresponds to the standard monomial sequence
x^{n}/n!
. In this case,
\mathcal{H}
is the classical Hankel contour originating at
-\infty
below the real axis, encircling the origin counterclockwise, and returning to
-\infty
above the real axis, and
\Phi(z)=\operatorname{e}^{z}z^{-1}
.

Furthermore, this proposition extends to any ground state defined by a product of reciprocal Gamma functions,
\varphi(t)=1/\prod\Gamma(a_{i}t+b_{i})
. While such functions violently violate the vertical decay required for the Mellin–Parseval reduction, they are natively generated by integrating multi-parameter Wright functions (or Fox
H
-functions with empty numerator parameters) over generalized Hankel loop contours, seamlessly absorbing them into the topological duality framework.

\Gamma(1+t)
at negative integers
t=-n\leq-1
produces double poles, yielding logarithmic terms. One recovers the expansion

e^{\eta}E_{1}(\eta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}-\gamma-\log\eta}{n!}\,\eta^{n}

,
where
H_{n}
are the harmonic numbers.

The Mellin–Barnes integral thus reconstructs the full analytic function, while its evaluation by residues isolates the asymptotic expansions of this function in different regimes.

Remark 5.16
.

The function
e^{\eta}E_{1}(\eta)
may also be obtained by Borel–Laplace summation of the divergent Euler series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}n!\,\eta^{-n-1}

,
since the latter is precisely the asymptotic expansion of
e^{\eta}E_{1}(\eta)
at infinity. The Mellin–Barnes pairing therefore reconstructs the same global analytic object as Borel summation, but does so directly from the jump kernel.

Remark 5.17
.

This example shows that the umbral pairing does not merely reproduce a formal series: it canonically reconstructs the analytic function having that series as asymptotic expansion. In the present rational case, the global analytic information is encoded by the jump function
J(t;\eta)
, while the Barnes contour selects different local expansions by residue calculus.

Example 5.18

(Pairing of
\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
with
\Gamma(1+at)
)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})\text{*}{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

and let

\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+at)\text{*}{a>0}

.

Jump function..
The associated jump function is
J(t;\zeta)=\Gamma(-t)\,(-\zeta)^{t}
(cfr. example
3.27
).

Mellin–Barnes representation of the pairing..
Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}
be a vertical line
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t=c
with
c<0
. Then

f_{a}(\zeta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\left\langle\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+at)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\Gamma(-t)\,\Gamma(1+at)(-\zeta)^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t}

.

Reduction to a Mellin integral..
The inverse Mellin transform of
\Gamma(1+at)
is

W_{a}(z)={\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}z^{-t}\,\Gamma(1+at)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{a}\,z^{1/a}\,\operatorname{e}^{-z^{1/a}}\text{*}{z>0}}

,
whence

f_{a}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{\zeta z}\,\,z^{1/a}\,\operatorname{e}^{-z^{1/a}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}

.
Setting
z=y^{a}
one obtains

f_{a}(\zeta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-y+\zeta y^{a}}\,\mathrm{d}y

.

Mittag–Leffler and formal umbral pairing..
Shifting the Barnes contour to the right across the poles of
\Gamma(-t)
gives the residue expansion

f_{a}(\zeta)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+an)}{n!}\,\zeta^{n}

.
Since
\Gamma(1+at)
is regular at every
t=n\in\mathbb{N}
, this coincides with the formal umbral series

\exp(\zeta\mathfrak{u})\,[\Gamma(1+at)]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+an)}{n!}\,\zeta^{n}

.

Dependence on the parameter
a
..
By Stirling’s formula:

•

if
0<a<1
, the series is convergent;

•

if
a=1
, it has radius of convergence
1
;

•

if
a>1
, it is Gevrey-
(a-1)
divergent.

In particular:

•

for
a=\frac{1}{2}
,

f_{1/2}(\zeta)={\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-y+\zeta\sqrt{y}}\,\mathrm{d}y=1+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\zeta}{2}\,\operatorname{e}^{\zeta^{2}/4}\,\operatorname{erfc}\left(-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)}

,
and the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+n/2)}{n!}\,\zeta^{n}

is convergent. Thus the analytic pairing, the Mittag–Leffler pairing and the formal umbral pairing all coincide.

•

for
a=1
,

f_{1}(\zeta)={\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-(1-\zeta)y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{1-\zeta}}

,
and again the analytic, Mittag–Leffler and formal pairings coincide in the disk of convergence.

•

for
a=2
,

f_{2}(\zeta)={\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-y+\zeta y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{-\zeta}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\zeta}\right)\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-\zeta}}\right)}

,
while the common Mittag–Leffler and formal expansion is

f_{2}(\zeta)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n)!}{n!}\,\zeta^{n}

,
which is Gevrey-1 divergent.

Thus, for all
a>0
, the Mittag–Leffler pairing coincides with the formal umbral pairing. What changes with
a
is whether this common expansion converges or is merely asymptotic to the analytic pairing.

Remark 5.19

(Contribution from infinity and flat scale at
a=2
)
.

In the case
a=2
, the exact expression exhibits the exponential scale
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\zeta}\right)
which comes from the saddle point of the exponent in the integral representation

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,\operatorname{e}^{-y+\zeta y^{2}}

.
Indeed, the critical point
y_{*}=\frac{1}{2\zeta}
satisfies
\zeta y_{*}^{2}-y_{*}=-\frac{1}{4\zeta}
.

This scale does not appear as a separate additive correction to the Mittag–Leffler expansion. Rather, it is encoded in the exact analytic function and governs its exponentially improved asymptotics and Stokes behaviour. In particular, the Mittag–Leffler pairing and the formal umbral pairing still coincide, but only as a common Gevrey-1 asymptotic expansion of the analytic pairing. When Borel–Laplace resummed, the Borel transform of this series has a branch point at
u=\tfrac{1}{4}
, and the corresponding Laplace transform produces the exponential scale
\exp(-1/(4\zeta))
as a Stokes contribution. Thus the flat term appearing in the closed form of the analytic pairing is already encoded in the Borel singularity structure of the Mittag–Leffler or formal umbral series.

Example 5.20

(Pairing of
\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
with
\Gamma(1-t)
)
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and consider

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)={\exp(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\qquad\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1-t)}

.

Mellin–Barnes representation of the pairing..
The associated jump function is the same as in the previous example. Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}
be a vertical Barnes contour
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}t=c
with
c<0
. Then

f_{-1}(\zeta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\Gamma(-t)\,\Gamma(1-t)\,(-\zeta)^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t

.

Reduction to a Mellin integral..
The inverse Mellin transform of
\Gamma(1-t)
is

W_{-1}(z)=\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-1/z}}{z}\text{*}{z>0}

.
Since
\Gamma(1-t)
decays exponentially along vertical lines, the hypotheses of proposition
5.10
are satisfied and one eventually obtains

f_{-1}(\zeta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-y+\zeta/y}\,\mathrm{d}y

.

Identification of the analytic function..
For
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\zeta<0
, the integral converges absolutely. The result is

f_{-1}(\zeta)=2\sqrt{-\zeta}\,K_{1}\!\left(2\sqrt{-\zeta}\right)

,
with principal branches, where
K_{1}
is the 1-order modified Bessel function of the second kind.

Behaviour at the origin..
Since

f_{-1}(\zeta)=1-\zeta\log(-\zeta)+(1-2\gamma)\zeta+\mathcal{O}\left(\zeta^{2}\log\zeta\right)\text{*}{\zeta\to 0}

,
the analytic pairing is regular at
\zeta=0
, but its local expansion contains logarithmic terms and is not a power series.

Mittag–Leffler pairing..
Shifting the Barnes contour to the right across the colliding poles of
\Gamma(-t)
and
\Gamma(1-t)
at

gives the residue expansion

f_{-1}(\zeta)\sim 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!(n-1)!}\,(\gamma-H_{n-1})\,\zeta^{n}

.
This series defines an entire function which coincides with the holomorphic part of the local expansion of the analytic pairing at the origin, but does not reproduce the full analytic function, where logarithmic contributions are present.

Formal umbral pairing..
The umbral prescription would formally give

\exp(\zeta\mathfrak{u})\,[\Gamma(1-t)]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\,\Gamma(1-n)

,
but this expression is meaningless, since
\Gamma(1-n)
has poles for every integer
n\geq 1
.

Thus, in this example, the analytic pairing exists and is explicitly computable, providing a genuine extension of the formal umbral pairing, which is not defined as a formal series and therefore falls outside the scope of standard resummation methods.

5.5
Interpretation of the analytic pairing

Having observed the mechanisms of the analytic pairing in specific test cases, we now formalize its relationship with the formal indicial umbral framework. The following theorem demonstrates that the analytic pairing acts as a true analytic extension of the formal theory: it reproduces the formal umbral series exactly when the latter is mathematically justified. Crucially, rather than merely identifying the obstructions that cause the formal method to fail, the Mellin–Barnes framework naturally overcomes them, yielding a rigorous and well-defined exact result precisely where the formal evaluation breaks down.

Theorem 5.21

(Recovery of the formal umbral pairing)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=F(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})

be an umbral Borel functional with
F
holomorphic at the origin,

F(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n}z^{n}

,
and let
\varphi
be a branched admissible ground state.

Assume that:

(i)

the jump function
J(t;\zeta)
is meromorphic in
t
and admits a Mittag–Leffler expansion of the form

J(t;\zeta)=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{n}\,\zeta^{n}}{t-n}+H(t;\zeta)

,
where
H(t;\zeta)
is holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the interpolation lattice
t\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

the function
\varphi(t)
is holomorphic at the interpolation lattice
t\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

the Mellin–Barnes contour
\mathcal{C}_{t}
can be displaced to the right across the poles
t\in\mathbb{N}
without encountering additional singularities, and the contribution of the displaced contour is negligible in the corresponding asymptotic regime.

Then the analytic umbral pairing admits the asymptotic expansion

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n}\,\zeta^{n}\,\varphi(n)

,
which coincides exactly with the formal umbral pairing.

Remark 5.22

(Obstructions to recovery)
.

The hypotheses of theorem
5.21
fail in two distinct situations illustrated by the previous examples.

(i)

If the integrand
J(t;\zeta)\,\varphi(t)
develops higher-order poles at the interpolation lattice (for instance, if
\varphi(t)
ceases to be holomorphic and contributes colliding poles), the residue computation produces logarithmic contributions. In this case, the analytic pairing cannot be completely recovered from the formal umbral series.

(ii)

If the jump kernel contains contributions arising from saddle points at infinity, the analytic pairing may contain exponentially small terms which are invisible to the residue expansion. In this case, the formal umbral series captures only the asymptotic part of the pairing.

Thus the formal umbral pairing is recovered precisely when the Mellin–Barnes integrand has only simple poles and no additional contributions from infinity.

Remark 5.23

(Meaning of the asymptotic symbol)
.

The symbol

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(\zeta)

denotes an asymptotic expansion obtained by displacement of the Mellin–Barnes contour.

More precisely, let

I(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{s}}G(s;\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}s

,
where
G(s;\zeta)
is meromorphic in
s
, and let
\{s_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of poles of
G
.

Assume that the contour
\mathcal{C}_{s}
can be displaced so as to cross the poles
s_{0},\dots,s_{N-1}
without encountering other singularities, yielding

I(\zeta)=\pm\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{n}}G(s;\zeta)+R_{N}(\zeta)

,
where
R_{N}(\zeta)
is given by the integral over the displaced contour, and the sign depends on the orientation of the displacement (clockwise or counter-clockwise).

Then

I(\zeta)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{n}}G(s;\zeta)

means that, for every
N\geq 0
, such a decomposition holds and the remainder
R_{N}(\zeta)
is asymptotically negligible in the regime under consideration.

In particular, the expansion is determined by the polar structure of
G(s;\zeta)
relative to the chosen displacement direction. Additional contributions may arise from the behaviour of
G
at infinity and are not captured by the residue expansion.

5.6
Spectral transmutation law

The spectral duality established in
section

4
at the level of jump kernels descends naturally to the level of analytic pairings.

Indeed, by definition, the umbral pairing is realised as a Mellin–Barnes integral of the product of the jump kernel and the Mellin transform of the ground state. In particular, for an admissible ground state
\varphi
, one has schematically

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{\hat{\Delta}}(t;\zeta)\,\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

.

If two functionals are related by spectral duality,

J_{1}(t;\zeta)=\frac{J_{2}(t;\zeta)}{\Gamma(1+t)}

,
then substitution into the pairing integral yields a transformed pairing in which the ground state is directly modified by a reciprocal Gamma factor, while the spectral variable and the integration contour remain unchanged.

This mechanism defines the
spectral transmutation law
for umbral pairings, which will be developed in this section. In particular, the Pólya realisation shows that this transmutation acts linearly on the spectral data, reducing entire functionals to their resolvent duals at the level of analytic evaluation.

Corollary 5.24

(Spectral transmutation identity)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}_{\exp}(u;\zeta)={\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\qquad\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}}(u;\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}}

and let
\varphi
be a branched admissible ground state such that the pairings below are defined. Then

\left\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\exp},\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}},\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle

.

Proof.

This follows directly from theorem
4.1
by substitution of the dual jump kernels into the Mellin–Barnes representation of the pairing.
∎

Corollary 5.25

(Parametric spectral transmutation law)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}_{\exp}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}\exp(\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u})

,

\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}}(u;\zeta,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\nu)=\frac{\zeta^{\beta}\operatorname{e}^{\nu u}}{1-\zeta^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{\mu u}}

,
and let
\varphi
be a branched admissible ground state such that the pairings below are defined. Then

\left\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\exp},\varphi(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}},\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma\!\left(1+\frac{t-\nu}{\mu}\right)}\right\rangle

.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of theorem
4.6
, obtained by substituting the parametric jump kernels directly into the Mellin–Barnes pairing.
∎

Remark 5.26

(Spectral transmutation and resurgent reconstruction)
.

The previous corollaries show that the Mellin–Barnes pairing realises a genuine transmutation principle between rational and entire umbral functionals.

At the spectral level, the two functionals are related by the identity for the jump kernels. Since the jump function encodes the full resurgent content of the Borel transform, this identity expresses an equivalence between two distinct realisations of the same analytic data.

On the rational side, the jump kernel describes the discrete lattice of Borel singularities and the associated local Stokes data. The corresponding Mellin–Barnes pairing reconstructs the analytic function by summing the contributions of these singularities.

On the entire side, the umbral Borel functional has no finite singularities, and its resurgent content is concentrated at infinity. The pairing then reconstructs the same analytic object from a global spectral contribution associated with the irregular behaviour at infinity.

The spectral transmutation law shows that these two reconstruction mechanisms are analytically equivalent: the discrete Stokes data of the rational case and the global contribution from infinity in the entire case are related simply by Gamma regularisation.

Corollary 5.27

(Pólya spectral transmutation)
.

Let
E
be a Pólya–Mellin admissible entire function and let
R_{E}
be its Pólya-resolvent dual. Let
\varphi
be a branched admissible ground state such that the pairings below are defined. Then

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle R_{E}(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle

.

Proof.

This follows from theorem
4.14
by substitution into the Mellin–Barnes pairing and exchange of the Pólya and Mellin integrals.
∎

Remark 5.28

(Transmutation as a Gevrey-descent operator on test functions)
.

The spectral transmutation law can be interpreted as the action of an operator on admissible test functions.

Define

(\mathcal{T}\varphi)(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}

.

Then the transmutation identity for the pairing can be written in the compact form

\langle\hat{\Delta}_{1},\varphi\rangle=\langle\hat{\Delta}_{2},\mathcal{T}\varphi\rangle

,
whenever the jump kernels of
\hat{\Delta}_{1}
and
\hat{\Delta}_{2}
are in spectral duality.

In particular, spectral transmutation does not act on the pairing as such, but rather on the admissible test function, leaving the analytic value invariant.

Analytically, the operator
\mathcal{T}
acts as a spectral Borel reduction (or Gevrey descent) on the test space. By dividing by
\Gamma(1+t)
, it strictly improves the vertical exponential decay of the ground state. Consequently, it maps a formal evaluation with
s
-Gevrey divergence into an equivalent dual evaluation with
(s-1)
-Gevrey divergence, systematically regularising the asymptotic pairing.

Example 5.29

(Spectral transmutation: discrete case)
.

Let

E(w)={\operatorname{e}^{aw}+\operatorname{e}^{bw},\qquad\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)}

.

Original pairing..
By linearity,

\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\rangle=\langle\operatorname{e}^{a\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle+\langle\operatorname{e}^{b\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle

.
Using
W_{\varphi}(z)=z\operatorname{e}^{-z}
,

\langle\operatorname{e}^{c\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle={\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{c\zeta z}\operatorname{e}^{-z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{1-c\zeta}}

,
we obtain

\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\rangle={\frac{1}{1-a\zeta}+\frac{1}{1-b\zeta}=R_{E}(\zeta)}

.

Transmuted pairing..
By corollary
5.27
, the ground state transmutes to
\mathcal{T}\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)/\Gamma(1+t)=1
. Thus,

\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\rangle=\left\langle R_{E}(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),1\right\rangle

.
Since
R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{1-aw}+\frac{1}{1-bw}
, and the inverse Mellin transform of the constant
1
acts as a point evaluation at
z=1
in the multiplicative Borel plane, the transmuted pairing trivially reconstructs the same function:

\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\rangle=R_{E}(\zeta)

.

Interpretation..
The entire functional is transmuted into its rational dual. The Gamma-regularisation completely neutralises the ground state, reducing the complex pairing to a direct evaluation of the resolvent dual.

Example 5.30

(Spectral transmutation: continuous case)
.

Let

E(w)={\frac{\sinh w}{w},\qquad\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)}

.

Original pairing..
One has

\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\rangle={\int_{0}^{\infty}E(\zeta z)\,\operatorname{e}^{-z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{\zeta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z}\frac{\sinh(\zeta z)}{z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2\zeta}\log\left(\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)}

.

Spectral-duality justification..
By definition of the resolvent dual, one has

R_{E}(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda w}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

.
For the elementary resolvent kernel, Mellin inversion directly gives the dual identity. By linearity and exchange of integrals:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}J_{R_{E}}(t;\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}t={\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{E}(\lambda)}{1-\lambda\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=R_{E}(\zeta)}

.

Interpretation..
As in the discrete case, the transmutation reduces the ground state to
1
. The analytic pairing perfectly coincides with the Pólya-resolvent dual evaluated at
w=\zeta
:

\left\langle E(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\Gamma(1+t)\right\rangle=R_{E}(\zeta)

.
Thus the continuous Pólya kernel produces a non-rational spectral dual with logarithmic branch points, and the pairing realises this duality exactly without further integration.

It is immediate to realise that a similar transmutation law holds also at the level of formal umbral pairings. In its most elementary manifestation, it takes the form:

\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\mathfrak{u}}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\mathfrak{u}}\left[\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right]

.

We clarify in the following proposition that this ”umbral transmutation law” is not an independent formal rule, but strictly the primary (local) expansion of the global spectral transmutation identity.

Proposition 5.31

(Umbral transmutation as primary expansion of spectral duality)
.

The formal umbral identity

\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\mathfrak{u}}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\mathfrak{u}}\left[\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right]

is obtained exactly as the primary expansion at
\zeta=0
of the analytic spectral transmutation law.

Proof.

Start from the analytic identity established in corollary
5.24
:

\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\exp},\varphi\rangle=\left\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}},\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle

.
Expanding the left-hand side around
\zeta=0
by shifting the contour to the right yields the classical residue sequence

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\varphi(n)

,
which is precisely the definition of the formal pairing
\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\mathfrak{u}}[\varphi]
.

Since the transmutation equality holds analytically, the same formal expansion must be generated by expanding the right-hand side. Shifting the contour for the rational kernel yields the geometric sequence of residues applied to the transmuted ground state:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{\varphi(n)}{\Gamma(1+n)}

,
which is precisely the definition of
\frac{1}{1-\zeta\mathfrak{u}}\left[\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right]
.
∎

Remark 5.32

(Global vs. primary transmutation)
.

The previous proposition shows that the classical umbral transmutation law is recovered trivially as the primary expansion at
\zeta=0
of the analytic spectral transmutation identity. In this sense, the algebraic umbral rule reflects only the local contribution of the Mellin–Barnes integral arising from the interpolation lattice
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

The full spectral transmutation law, however, is substantially stronger. It is a global identity at the level of analytic pairings. As such, it guarantees equivalence not only for the primary expansion at the origin, but across the entire analytic structure of the paired functions, capturing their behaviour at infinity, their Stokes data, and their singularity structure in the
\zeta
-plane.

In particular, while the umbral transmutation merely captures the cancellation of factorial growth at the level of formal coefficients, the spectral transmutation encodes the full resurgent correspondence between entire and rational kernels. The classical algebraic rule should therefore be regarded as nothing more than a local, perturbative shadow of the global analytic duality.

6
Worked examples and analytic realisations

In this section we illustrate the general theory developed above through a collection of explicit examples. The aim is twofold. On the one hand, we show how the Mellin–Barnes pairing produces concrete analytic functions from admissible umbral data. On the other hand, we make explicit the mechanism of spectral transmutation, by exhibiting in each case the equivalence between entire and rational representations of the same analytic object.

The examples are chosen so as to highlight different aspects of the theory. We first consider ground states admitting elementary Mellin kernels, leading to explicit integral representations. We then treat genuinely Barnes-type cases, where the analytic structure is governed by the interplay between pole lattices and Gamma factors. Finally, we include examples arising from Pólya representations, illustrating the passage from discrete to continuous spectral data.

In all cases, the analytic pairing provides a unified framework in which asymptotic expansions, local singular behaviour, and global analytic structure are simultaneously accessible.

6.1
Elementary Mellin kernels

For some particular branched admissible ground state functions
\varphi
, the Mellin kernel
W_{\varphi}
, defined by the inverse Mellin transform

W_{\varphi}(z)={(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\varphi)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\varphi(t)\,z^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t}

,
reduces to an elementary function on
(0,\infty)
. The analytic pairing with an umbral Borel functional is represented by a Mellin integral, which in these cases can often be evaluated in closed form.

We examine in the following some examples involving ground state functions of this kind, by emphasising the resulting Mellin integral representation, its analytic interpretation and the relationship with the corresponding formal umbral result.

Example 6.1

(The Gamma ground state)
.

Let

\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)

.

Kernel..
The Mellin kernel is

W_{\varphi}(z)=z\operatorname{e}^{-z}

.

Pairing..
For the rational functional

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}

,
the multiplicative kernel is
\hat{K}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta z}
. Assuming initially
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(1/\zeta)<0
, one obtains

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\right\rangle={\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-z}}{1-\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z=-\frac{1}{\zeta}\operatorname{e}^{-1/\zeta}E_{1}\left(-\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)}

.

Interpretation and Transmutation..
This integral produces a function with a branch cut and a 1-Gevrey divergent Euler asymptotic expansion
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\zeta^{n}
at the origin, exactly coinciding with the formal umbral pairing.

By the spectral transmutation law
\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}},\psi\rangle=\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\exp},\psi(t)\Gamma(1+t)\rangle
, this rational pairing is analytically equivalent to the entire pairing:

\left\langle\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\Gamma(1+t)^{2}\right\rangle

.
Indeed, the formal series of this transmuted entire pairing is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}(\Gamma(1+n))^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\zeta^{n}

,
proving that transmutation perfectly preserves the local formal series while the analytic framework guarantees both representations reconstruct the identical, globally resummed exponential integral.

Example 6.2

(The Gaussian ground state)
.

Let

\varphi(t)=\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}

.

Mellin kernel..
The Mellin kernel is

W_{\varphi}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,z\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}

.
Thus, for a multiplicative kernel
\hat{K}(z)=\hat{\Delta}(\zeta z)
, the pairing is

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{K}(z)\,\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}\,\mathrm{d}z

.

Pairing with
\operatorname{e}^{i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}
..
Here

\hat{K}(z)=\operatorname{e}^{i\zeta z}

.
Therefore

\langle\operatorname{e}^{i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle={\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4+i\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(-i\zeta)=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\left(1+i\,\operatorname{erfi}(\zeta)\right)}

.
The corresponding formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\zeta)^{n}}{n!}\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\zeta)^{n}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}

,
which is convergent to the same entire function.

Pairing with
\cos(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
..
Here
\hat{K}(z)=\cos(\zeta z)
. Therefore

\left\langle\cos(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\right\rangle={\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}\cos(\zeta z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}}

.
The corresponding formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\zeta^{2n}}{(2n)!}\frac{\Gamma(1+2n)}{\Gamma(1+n)}={\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\zeta^{2n}}{n!}=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}}

.

Pairing with
\sin(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})
..
Here
\hat{K}(z)=\sin(\zeta z)
. Therefore

\left\langle\sin(\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}),\varphi\right\rangle={\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}\sin(\zeta z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{erfi}(\zeta)}

.
The corresponding formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\zeta^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\frac{\Gamma(1+2n+1)}{\Gamma(1+(2n+1)/2)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\zeta^{2n+1}}{\Gamma\!\left(n+\tfrac{3}{2}\right)}

,
which is convergent and coincides with the Taylor expansion of
\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{erfi}(\zeta)
.

Pairing with
(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{2u})^{-1}
..
Here

\hat{K}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta z^{2}}

.
Assuming the principal branch and initially
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\zeta<0
, one obtains

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{2u}},\varphi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}}{1-\zeta z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{-\zeta}}\,\operatorname{e}^{-1/(4\zeta)}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-\zeta}}\right)

.
The formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+2n)}{\Gamma(1+n)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{(2n)!}{n!}

.
This series is divergent, of Gevrey order
1
, and the analytic pairing above gives its Borel–Laplace sum in the corresponding sector.

Pairing with
(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
..
Here

\hat{K}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta z}

.
Assuming initially
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(1/\zeta)<0
, one obtains

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-z^{2}/4}}{1-\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z

.
By substituting
x=z/2
, this integral evaluates in closed form to

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{\zeta\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-1/(4\zeta^{2})}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\operatorname{erfi}\left(-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Ei}\left(\frac{1}{4\zeta^{2}}\right)\right]=-\frac{1}{\zeta}D\left(-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\right)+\frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{\pi}}\operatorname{e}^{-1/(4\zeta^{2})}\operatorname{Ei}\left(\frac{1}{4\zeta^{2}}\right)

,
with the usual branch conventions, where

D(x)={e^{-x^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{s^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{-x^{2}}\operatorname{erfi}(x)}

is Dawson’s integral and
\operatorname{Ei}(x)
is the exponential integral.
The corresponding naive umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{n!}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}

.
This series is divergent, of Gevrey order
1/2
. The exact evaluation above provides its canonical Borel–Laplace resummation, and its asymptotic expansion at the origin perfectly reproduces the full formal umbral series, with Dawson’s integral generating the even parity sector and the exponential integral generating the odd parity sector.

Interpretation and Transmutation..
The same Gaussian ground state produces three different analytic regimes. The entire kernels give convergent umbral series, while the rational kernels give divergent series of different Gevrey orders.

Crucially, spectral transmutation allows us to map between these formalisms exactly. By the identity
\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\exp},\varphi\rangle=\langle\hat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{rat}},\frac{\varphi(t)}{\Gamma(1+t)}\rangle
, transmuting the entire pairing
\langle\operatorname{e}^{i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle
yields the rational pairing:

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}\right\rangle

.
The formal series of this transmuted rational pairing is precisely
\sum(i\zeta)^{n}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}
, proving that the analytic transmutation operator perfectly preserves the exact formal summation across the duality.

Example 6.3

(The Beta-type ground state)
.

Let

\varphi(t)=\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(t+b)}\text{*}{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}b>1}

.

Mellin kernel..
The Mellin kernel is

W_{\varphi}(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b-1)}\,z(1-z)^{b-2}\text{*}{0<z<1}

.
Thus, for a multiplicative kernel
\hat{K}(z)=\hat{\Delta}(\zeta z)
, the pairing is

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{K}(z)(1-z)^{b-2}\,\mathrm{d}z

.

Pairing with
\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}}
..
Here
\hat{K}(z)=\operatorname{e}^{\zeta z}
. Therefore

\left\langle\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{e}^{\zeta z}(1-z)^{b-2}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b)}{}_{1}F_{1}(1;b;\zeta)

.
The corresponding formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n!}\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(n+b)}={\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{\Gamma(n+b)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b)}{}_{1}F_{1}(1;b;\zeta)}

,
so in this case the formal series is convergent and coincides with the analytic pairing.

Pairing with
(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
..
Here

\hat{K}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta z}

.
Thus

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-z)^{b-2}}{1-\zeta z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b)}{}_{2}F_{1}(1,1;b;\zeta)

.
The corresponding formal umbral series is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(n+b)}={\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{n!}{\Gamma(n+b)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b)}{}_{2}F_{1}(1,1;b;\zeta)}

.
This series has radius of convergence
1
, while the Euler integral gives its analytic continuation away from the cut produced by the pole of
(1-\zeta z)^{-1}
on the interval
1<z<\infty
.

The case
b=2
..
For
b=2
, the kernel becomes constant on
(0,1)
:

W_{\varphi}(z)=z

.
The two preceding formulas reduce to

\left\langle\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{1+t}\right\rangle=\frac{\operatorname{e}^{\zeta}-1}{\zeta}

,
and

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{1+t}\right\rangle=-\frac{\log(1-\zeta)}{\zeta}

.

Interpretation and Transmutation..
The Beta-type ground state has compact Mellin support. Consequently, the analytic pairing is governed by Euler-type integrals on
(0,1)
.

The mechanism of spectral transmutation relates the resulting functions explicitly. Transmuting the entire pairing
\langle\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle
divides the ground state by
\Gamma(1+t)
, yielding the rational pairing
\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(t+b)}\rangle
. As expected, this transmuted rational formal series exactly evaluates to the confluent
{}_{1}F_{1}
function.
Conversely, transmuting the rational pairing
\langle\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\varphi\rangle
multiplies the ground state by
\Gamma(1+t)
, yielding the entire pairing
\langle\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{\Gamma(1+t)^{2}}{\Gamma(t+b)}\rangle
. Evaluating this transmuted formal series rigorously reproduces the exact Gauss
{}_{2}F_{1}
function.

6.2
The inverse Gamma ground state and its Hankel realisation

The ground state
\varphi(t)=\Gamma(1+t)^{-1}
plays a distinguished role in classical umbral calculus. It acts as a formal regulariser, compensating for the factorial growth of sequences and mapping formally divergent series to entire functions.

As established in
section

5.3
, this ground state exhibits vertical exponential growth and therefore violates the standard real-line Mellin–Parseval reduction. Instead, proposition
5.13
demonstrates that its analytic pairing is rigorously evaluated by substituting the classical Hankel contour integral
[
30
]

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\operatorname{e}^{w}w^{-1-t}\,\mathrm{d}w
, which transforms the pairing into a complex contour integral in the Borel plane.

We explicitly evaluate this Hankel realisation below, highlighting how the resulting contour calculus reconstructs familiar entire functions through residue theory and branch-cut jumps.

Example 6.4

(The inverse Gamma ground state via Hankel representation)
.

Let

\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}

.

Hankel realisation of the pairing..
By proposition
5.13
, the analytic pairing reduces to

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\hat{K}(w^{-1})\,\operatorname{e}^{w}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w}

,
whenever the joint integral is absolutely convergent.

Pairing with
(1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
..
Setting the multiplicative kernel to
\hat{K}(z)=1/(1+\zeta^{2}z)
, one obtains

\cos_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)={\left\langle\frac{1}{1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{w}}{1+\zeta^{2}w^{-1}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w}}

.
Equivalently, clearing the denominator yields

\cos_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{w}}{w+\zeta^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w

.
Deforming the Hankel contour to enclose the simple pole and taking the residue at
w=-\zeta^{2}
gives

\cos_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}

.
This is the definition of the Gaussian cosine (see
[
7
]
and
[
24
]
for a discussion of Gaussian trigonometric functions).

Pairing with
\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}(1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
..
Setting
\hat{K}(z)=\zeta z^{1/2}/(1+\zeta^{2}z)
, one obtains

\sin_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)={\left\langle\frac{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}}{1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\zeta w^{-1/2}\operatorname{e}^{w}}{1+\zeta^{2}w^{-1}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w}}

.
Equivalently,

\sin_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{w}w^{-1/2}}{w+\zeta^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}w

.
Note that, unlike the previous case, the presence of
w^{-1/2}
introduces a branch cut along
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
, so that the evaluation involves the jump of the integrand across the Hankel contour rather than a simple sum of residues.

Writing the Hankel contour as the difference of the two boundary values on the cut
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
, with
w=-x
and
x>0
, one obtains for
a>0
:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{w}w^{-1/2}}{w+a}\,\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-x}x^{-1/2}}{a-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2}{\pi}\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{-y^{2}}}{a-y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y

.
Reducing the kernel to a Hilbert transform, one finds

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{w}w^{-1/2}}{w+a}\,\mathrm{d}w=a^{-1/2}\operatorname{e}^{-a}\operatorname{erfi}\!\left(\sqrt{a}\right)

.
Setting
a=\zeta^{2}
, this directly yields the Gaussian sine, namely

\sin_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{erfi}(\zeta)

.

Pairing with
(1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2})^{-1}
..
Using the algebraic decomposition

\frac{1}{1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}}=\frac{1}{1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u}}+i\,\frac{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}}{1+\zeta^{2}\operatorname{e}^{u}}

,
one obtains by linearity

\operatorname{e}_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)={\left\langle\frac{1}{1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle=\cos_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)+i\sin_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)}

,
and therefore

\operatorname{e}_{\mathrm{G}}(\zeta)={\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\left(1+i\operatorname{erfi}(\zeta)\right)=\operatorname{e}^{-\zeta^{2}}\operatorname{erfc}(-i\zeta)}

,
which is the definition of the Gaussian exponential.

Interpretation..
The inverse Gamma ground state naturally realises the pairing as a Hankel contour integral. The resulting analytic functions precisely coincide with those obtained from the Gaussian ground state paired with entire kernels (example
6.2
).

This coincidence is not accidental; it is the exact manifestation of a profound algebraic interplay between
Mellin rescaling
and
spectral transmutation
.

First, the fractional umbral shift
\operatorname{e}^{u/2}
appearing in the rational kernel induces a Mellin rescaling (a dilation of the spectral variable). This maps the fractional rational functional to a standard rational functional evaluated against a rescaled ground state:

\left\langle\frac{1}{1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u/2}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)}\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{1}{1-i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}\right\rangle

.
Second, applying the spectral transmutation law (
corollary

5.24
) to this rescaled rational pairing multiplies the ground state by the regularising factor
\Gamma(1+t)
, explicitly yielding the dual entire pairing:

\left\langle\operatorname{e}^{i\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(1+t/2)}\right\rangle

.
This exactly reconstructs the Gaussian ground state and its corresponding exponential pairing from
example

6.2
.

Thus, this example provides a rigorous analytic demonstration of how the umbral framework unifies distinct functional families: a fractional Mellin rescaling combined with spectral transmutation naturally maps the simple inverse Gamma ground state directly into the complex, fractional-gamma structure of the Gaussian pairings.

6.3
Genuinely Barnes pairings

In the previous examples, the analytic pairing could be reduced to explicit integral representations on
(0,\infty)
or to closed-form expressions in terms of classical special functions. This is no longer the case for more general ground states, where no elementary Mellin kernel exists and the pairing is intrinsically of Mellin–Barnes type.

In this regime, the Mellin–Barnes integral itself must be regarded as the primary analytic object. The formal umbral evaluation does not produce this function, but only one of its local expansions, typically the primary expansion at
\zeta=0
, which may be convergent or merely asymptotic of Gevrey type.

A distinctive feature of the Mellin–Barnes representation is that it simultaneously encodes the global analytic structure of the pairing. In particular, by deforming the contour, one can extract not only the primary expansion at the origin, but also the specular expansion at
\zeta=\infty
, whose structure is governed by the pole multiplicities and zero lattices of the integrand. These features, including the possible emergence of logarithmic terms, are in general invisible to purely formal umbral manipulations.

The following example illustrates this genuinely Barnes behaviour.

Example 6.5
.

Let

\varphi(t)={\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(1+t/3)},\qquad\hat{K}(z)=\hat{\Delta}(\zeta z)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta z}}

.

Mellin–Barnes representation..
The analytic pairing is

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\zeta^{t}\,\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi t)}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{\Gamma(1+t/3)}\,\mathrm{d}t

.
Equivalently, using the reflection formula, we obtain the spectrally transmuted form

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\zeta^{t}\,\frac{\Gamma(-t)\,\Gamma(1+t)^{2}}{\Gamma(1+t/3)}\,\mathrm{d}t

.

Primary expansion at
\zeta=0
..
Closing the contour to the right, the simple poles of
\Gamma(-t)
at
t=n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, give

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{n!}{\Gamma(1+n/3)}\zeta^{n}

.
This coincides with the formal umbral evaluation

\frac{1}{1-\zeta\mathfrak{u}}[\varphi]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(1+n/3)}\zeta^{n}

.

Gevrey order..
By Stirling’s formula,

\frac{\Gamma(1+n)}{\Gamma(1+n/3)}=\mathcal{O}\!\left((n!)^{2/3}A^{n}\right)

for a suitable constant
A>0
. Hence the primary series is Gevrey-
2/3
. It is therefore formally divergent, but naturally adapted to exact Borel–Laplace resummation of order
2/3
.

Expansion at infinity..
Closing the contour to the left (see
fig.

4
), one encounters the negative-integer lattice

t=-1,-2,-3,\dots

.
At these points, the squared factor
\Gamma(1+t)^{2}
produces double poles, while the reciprocal term

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t/3)}

vanishes at
t=-3,-6,-9,\dots
.

Figure 4:

6.5
.

The integration contour
\mathcal{C}
at
\mathrm{Re}(t)=c\in(-1,0)
separates the real axis into two distinct singularity lattices. The blue circles at

denote the simple poles of the ground state distribution
\Gamma(-t)
. The red crosses denote the umbral functional singularities from
\Gamma(1+t)^{2}/\Gamma(1+t/3)
; bold crosses at

indicate double poles (yielding logarithmic terms), while thinner crosses at

indicate reduction to simple poles.

\mathcal{C}_{A}
) captures the primary Gevrey expansion at

, while closing to the left (
\mathcal{C}_{F}
) yields the trans-series expansion at infinity.

Thus, one order of the pole is cancelled precisely at the multiples of
3
.

Consequently, the expansion at infinity has the form

\langle\hat{K},\varphi\rangle\sim\sum_{n\geq 1}A_{n}\zeta^{-n}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
n\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}B_{n}\zeta^{-n}\log\zeta

.
The logarithmic terms arise exactly from the surviving double poles.

Comparison with the inverted formal expansion..
Formally, one may write

\frac{1}{1-\zeta\mathfrak{u}}=-\frac{\zeta^{-1}\mathfrak{u}^{-1}}{1-\zeta^{-1}\mathfrak{u}^{-1}}

.
This formal identity suggests a purely geometric expansion at infinity in powers of
\zeta^{-1}
. However, the algebraic manipulation only detects the simple coefficient-level structure. It is entirely blind to the colliding double-pole geometry of the Mellin–Barnes integrand, and therefore cannot account for the logarithmic branches emerging in the true expansion at infinity.

Interpretation..
This example is genuinely Barnes in nature. The analytic pairing is controlled not merely by the pole lattice of the rational kernel, but by the complex interaction between this sequence, the squared Gamma factor
\Gamma(1+t)^{2}
, and the periodic zero lattice of
\Gamma(1+t/3)^{-1}
.

The primary expansion at
\zeta=0
perfectly recovers the formal umbral series, establishing it rigorously as a divergent Gevrey-
2/3
asymptotic expansion. The expansion at infinity, instead, reveals a filtered logarithmic structure completely invisible to the formal coefficient calculus. The Mellin–Barnes contour acts as a spectral filter, cancelling one order of the logarithmic defect exactly every third step.

6.4
Deformations of Gamma-type ground states

We now turn our attention to genuinely branched admissible ground states. While the previous examples focused on meromorphic test functions characterized by isolated pole lattices, introducing continuous deformations or algebraic factors natively generates branch cuts in the Mellin spectral plane. As illustrated in the following examples, the Mellin–Barnes framework naturally accommodates these branched structures, seamlessly mapping them into corresponding branch cuts and non-trivial analytic continuations in the
\zeta
-plane.

Example 6.6

(The family
(1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u})^{-1}
vs.
\Gamma(1+t)^{-\mu}
)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)={\frac{1}{1-\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\qquad\varphi_{\mu}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+t)^{\,\mu}}}

,
with
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
.

Mellin–Barnes representation..
The analytic pairing is

\Phi_{\mu}(\zeta)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi_{\mu}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\zeta^{-t}\,\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi t)}\,\Gamma(1+t)^{-\mu}\,\mathrm{d}t}

.
Using the reflection formula,

\Phi_{\mu}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\zeta^{-t}\,\Gamma(-t)\,\Gamma(1-t)^{1-\mu}\,\mathrm{d}t

.

Primary expansion at
\zeta=0
..
Closing the contour to the right, the simple poles of
\Gamma(-t)
at
t=n
yield

\Phi_{\mu}(\zeta)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{(n!)^{\,\mu}}

,
which is the defining formal series of the Le Roy function.

Dependence on
\mu
..
The analytic behaviour depends on
\mu
as follows:

•

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\mu>0
, the Le Roy series is entire and gives the analytic object directly.

•

If
\mu=0
, one recovers the rational function
\Phi_{0}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta}
.

•

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\mu<0
, the series is divergent and becomes a Gevrey-type asymptotic expansion rather than a convergent definition.

•

If
\mu\notin\mathbb{Z}
, the Mellin–Barnes integrand is branched because of the fractional power
\Gamma(1-t)^{1-\mu}
.

Thus the same Mellin–Barnes representation interpolates continuously between an entire Le Roy function, the rational geometric series, and genuinely branched or divergent regimes.

Special values..

{dgroup*}

\Phi_{0}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{1-\zeta}

,

\Phi_{1}(\zeta)=\operatorname{e}^{\zeta}

,

\Phi_{2}(\zeta)=I_{0}(2\sqrt{\zeta})

.

Spectral structure..
The factor
\Gamma(1-t)^{1-\mu}
controls the singular set on the right half-plane:

•

for
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
, one obtains isolated poles (possibly multiple);

•

for
\mu\notin\mathbb{Z}
, one obtains branch points.

Interpretation..
This family interpolates continuously between rational, exponential, and Bessel-type formalisms. The deformation parameter
\mu
acts directly on the Mellin–Barnes integrand, modifying the topological nature of the spectral singularities while preserving a unified complex integral representation.

Example 6.7

(Exponential kernel and the Lerch transcendent)
.

Let

\hat{\Delta}(u;\zeta)={\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\operatorname{e}^{u}},\qquad\varphi(t)=\frac{\Gamma(1+t)}{(t+\alpha)^{\sigma}}}

,
with
\alpha>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\sigma>0
.

Mellin realisation and convolution..
The ground state is the product of the factorial state
\Gamma(1+t)
and the algebraic state
(t+\alpha)^{-\sigma}
. Hence its Mellin weight is the multiplicative convolution of

W_{1}(z)=z\operatorname{e}^{-z}

and

W_{2}(y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}y^{\alpha}(-\log y)^{\sigma-1}\text{*}{0<y<1}

.
Thus

W_{\varphi}(z)=\frac{z}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{e}^{-z/y}\,y^{\alpha-2}(-\log y)^{\sigma-1}\,\mathrm{d}y

.

Analytic pairing..
The analytic pairing is

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{\zeta z}W_{\varphi}(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}

.
Substituting the convolution formula and applying Fubini’s theorem, initially for
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\zeta<1
, gives

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha-2}(-\log y)^{\sigma-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z(1/y-\zeta)}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)\,\mathrm{d}y

.
The inner integral evaluates to

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-z(1/y-\zeta)}\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{y}{1-\zeta y}

.
Therefore

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{\alpha-1}(-\log y)^{\sigma-1}}{1-\zeta y}\,\mathrm{d}y

.

Lerch expansion..
For
|\zeta|<1

\frac{1}{1-\zeta y}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}y^{n}

and integrating termwise gives

\langle\hat{\Delta},\varphi\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{(n+\alpha)^{\sigma}}

.
Thus the analytic pairing perfectly recovers the Lerch transcendent in its disk of convergence.

Equivalently, the formal umbral evaluation gives the same series:

\operatorname{e}^{\zeta\mathfrak{u}}[\varphi]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{n}}{(n+\alpha)^{\sigma}}

The integral representation

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{\alpha-1}(-\log y)^{\sigma-1}}{1-\zeta y}\,\mathrm{d}y

provides the canonical analytic continuation of the Lerch transcendent to the cut plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty)
.

This example shows how the exponential kernel and the factorial part of the ground state combine to produce the ordinary Lerch generating function. The exponential kernel alone would formally produce diverging factorial weights, but the

7
Conclusion and outlook

We have proposed an analytic framework for umbral calculus based on Mellin–Barnes representations and sectorial Laplace theory, in which umbral operators are realised as analytic functionals acting through contour integrals. Within this setting, classical umbral identities acquire a precise analytic meaning, and divergent formal series are interpreted as asymptotic expansions of well-defined functions reconstructed by Mellin–Barnes integrals.

A central outcome is the identification of a
spectral transmutation law
, relating entire and rational kernels directly through Gamma regularisation in the Mellin spectral space. This law acts at the level of analytic pairings and encodes a global correspondence between different spectral realisations of the same object. The familiar algebraic umbral rules emerge seamlessly as local primary expansions of this identity, proving that the combinatorial cancellation mechanisms of the formal theory are merely local shadows of a deeper, global analytic structure.

From a broader perspective, this suggests that umbral calculus admits a natural interpretation within resurgent analysis. The jump kernels play the role of Borel transforms, and the Mellin–Barnes pairing reconstructs analytic functions from their spectral data. In this picture, the distinction between entire and rational kernels reflects two complementary regimes: one in which the resurgent content is concentrated at infinity, and one in which it is encoded by discrete singularities in the finite Borel plane. The spectral transmutation law then appears as the exact analytic mechanism exchanging these two descriptions.

Crucially, this framework embeds umbral calculus within a rigorous
topological duality
of functional spaces, in the spirit of Gelfand–Shilov theory. The branched admissible ground states natively form a topological vector space of analytic test functions, governed by strict constraints on vertical exponential growth and sectorial decay. The umbral operators, in turn, are realised as continuous linear functionals—analytic distributions—acting upon this test space, with the Mellin–Barnes integral providing the explicit pairing mechanism. In this light, spectral transmutation operates as a continuous mapping between distinct functional topologies (a Gevrey descent), proving that the algebraic regularisation of formal umbral series is fundamentally dictated by the functional-analytic topology of the dual spaces.

The examples considered here indicate that this framework extends naturally beyond the classical Gamma-ratio setting. In genuinely Barnes-type cases, the Mellin–Barnes integral defines the analytic object itself, while the formal umbral series provides only partial information, typically as a Gevrey-type asymptotic expansion in a specific sector. The appearance of branch points in the spectral variable further suggests that the theory should be systematically extended to accommodate algebraic and more general resurgent singularities.

These observations point toward a number of speculative but highly promising directions. One may expect a rigorous correspondence between umbral operators and resurgent functions, in which the spectral variable plays the role of the Borel coordinate and the transmutation law reflects the action of alien derivatives. The interpretation of the pairing as a topological duality further opens the door to a formulation in terms of analytic functionals modulo holomorphic contributions (hyperfunctions), providing a natural setting for the decomposition of kernels into sectorial components and bridge equations.

Finally, the extension of the present framework to parametric families and to operators associated with differential, difference, or functional equations may reveal deeper connections between umbral calculus and integrable structures. In this context, the spectral transmutation law could be viewed as a manifestation of a more general duality principle acting on the space of solutions.

Taken together, these considerations suggest that umbral calculus, when formulated analytically, is not merely a combinatorial or operational tool, but natively belongs to a broader functional-analytic and resurgent structure in which formal series, analytic continuation, and spectral data are intrinsically linked. The present work provides a foundational step in this direction, and it is hoped that further developments will make this connection fully explicit.
