Autoantigen-loaded Polymeric Microparticles associate with B cells and promote tolerogenic antigen presentation in a mouse model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis
Authors: Nicole Rose Lukesh, Brian G Barbery, Kierstin A Clark, Luis Ontiveros-Padilla, Stephen A Ehrenzeller, Ryan N Woodring, Sophie E Mendell, Denzel D Middleton, Md Jahirul Islam, Erik S Pena, Dylan A Hendy, Sean R Simpson, Elizabeth G Graham-Gurysh, Alexandra M Lopez, Connor T Murphy, Grace L Williamson, Sophia A Ly, Kevin E Shilling, Elisa Landoni, Rebeca T Stiepel, Delaney Sherwin, Qi Ke, Barbara Savoldo, Gianpietro Dotti, Yuliya Pylayeva-Gupta, Roland M Tisch, Eric M Bachelder, Kristy M Ainslie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlmost 1 million people in the United States suffer from multiple sclerosis (MS). Current therapies suppress protective immunity and are non-curative. Antigen-specific therapies can selectively suppress autoreactive cells, preserving protective immunity. B cell dysregulation, a major driver of MS, remains largely untargeted for antigen-specific tolerance. We hypothesize that targeting B cells with antigen-loaded microparticles will enhance therapeutic efficacy. Here, we demonstrate that antigen-loaded acetalated dextran microparticles (AMP) surface-associate with B cells and enhance IL-10 secretion and MHCII expression, promoting tolerogenic antigen presentation. Leveraging this property, we developed a therapy using myelin oligodendrocyte (MOG35-55) peptide-loaded AMPs associated with B cells (MOG-AMP-B). Administering MOG-AMP-Bs in a late therapeutic model of multiple sclerosis resulted in unprecedented recovery, reducing central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, downregulating activated dendritic cells and macrophages, and increasing regulatory T- and B cells. MOG-AMP-Bs did not reduce the ability of animals to respond to a viral infection, representing a highly effective antigen-specific therapy for restoring immune balance in autoimmunity.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Peptide vaccines, Multiple sclerosis, Autoimmunity

Introduction

The immune system protects against pathogens while maintaining tolerance towards self-tissues. Autoimmune diseases occur when self-tolerance is broken and T- and B cells specific for self-antigens trigger inflammation and tissue destruction. Regulatory T cells (Treg) and B cells (Breg), in part defined by IL-10 production1, maintain self-tolerance. Various Treg subsets produce IL-10, including Foxp3-expressing Tregs (Foxp3+Treg), as well as type 1 regulatory T cells (Tr1). Autoimmunity progresses when the Treg and/or Breg compartments are dysregulated, which favors the differentiation and expansion of self-reactive, pathogenic T- and/or B cells.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease targeting the central nervous system (CNS) with an unclear etiology. Much attention has focused on autoreactive T cells, which drive CNS inflammation and damage by recruiting various immune effectors and producing proinflammatory cytokines. This initiates a feedback loop of demyelination, inflammation, and lesion formation that impairs cognitive and motor functions2. Epstein-Barr virus, which infects B cells, has recently been linked to MS through molecular mimicry and immune dysregulation3. In MS, autoreactive B cells contribute by producing autoantibodies, presenting antigen, and releasing cytokines, to amplify and coordinate the CNS attack4–6. This underscores the importance of including B cell targeting in MS treatment design.

Current MS therapies focus on broad immunosuppression and symptom management7. Antibody-based therapies targeting B cell markers such as CD19 and CD20 (e.g., Ofatumumab, Ocrelizumab, Rituximab) have slowed MS progression, but are not disease-specific or curative8. Therapies administering self-antigens aim to selectively tolerize autoreactive T- and B cells and thereby leave protective immunity unperturbed9. Antigen-specific-tolerance can, in part, be reestablished by the induction and/or expansion of Tregs, which suppress pathogenic autoimmunity through secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β). Foxp3+Tregs additionally regulate immunity through multiple mechanisms including cell-cell contact-mediated death and IL-2 consumption via CD25 (IL-2 receptor)10. Bregs contribute by producing IL-10 and modulating antigen presentation to autoreactive T cells11. However, soluble self-antigen protein- or peptide-based therapies have had significant challenges to clinical translation, largely due to MS heterogeneity, lack of a universal autoantigen, and poor lymphoid tissue targeting. Reactivity to candidate autoantigens such as myelin basic protein (MBP), myelin oligodendrocyte peptide (MOG), and proteolipid protein (PLP) vary between patients and disease stages12,13. Many patients are diagnosed years after disease onset when self-tolerance is more difficult to restore.

Ex vivo antigen-specific cell therapies, such as tolerogenic dendritic cells (DC)14 and Treg therapies15, are promising but limited by cost and complexity. In contrast, engineered biomaterials can deliver antigens directly to antigen-presenting cells (APC) or act as artificial APCs in situ16. Many, including FDA-approved polymer poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid, primarily target phagocytic APCs such as macrophages and DCs17 to induce Tregs. While some biomaterials have prevented MS in preclinical models (Supplementary Table 1), disease reversal at clinically relevant timelines remains challenging.

Given the central role of B cells in MS pathogenesis and the success of B cell depletion therapies, antigen-specific B cell modulation is a promising MS strategy. Recent studies showed that dextran-coated iron oxide nanoparticles bind B cells via complement proteins and deliver antigen(s) to modulate the APC function of B cells18,19. However, challenges remain with cargo loading, release, and iron accumulation.

Acetalated dextran (Ace-DEX) is a biocompatible, acid-labile polymer with tunable degradation rates that has previously been used in vaccine platforms for infectious diseases and autoimmunity20–26. Ace-DEX can be fabricated into roughly 1 μm microparticles (AMPs) via spray drying, a scalable and clinically translatable method. We hypothesize that the dextran motifs on AMPs promote complement-mediated B cell engagement, enabling preferential targeting of B cells for antigen delivery. AMPs are also available for passive targeting and phagocytosis by other professional APCs due to their 1μm size.

Herein, we test a strategy that combines particle-based antigen delivery and B cell targeting using AMPs. This platform helps us explore the role of B cell mediated autoimmune pathogenesis, as the cells’ potential as tolerogenic targets remains underexplored with regards to particle-based therapies. To address this, we characterize AMP biodistribution and AMP-B cell interactions and assessed the efficacy of B cells treated with AMPs encapsulating the I-Ab-restricted MOG35-55 peptide (MOG-AMP-B), to suppress late-stage experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a murine MS model. Our data shows that AMPs preferentially bind to B cells (AMP-Bs), resulting in IL-10 production. AMP-Bs can induce the formation of Foxp3⁺Tregs and CTLA4⁺Lag3⁺ T cells. MOG-AMP-Bs effectively reverse paralysis and ameliorate clinical disease scores. Late-stage EAE is clinically relevant, as diagnosis often occurs years after onset due to misdiagnosis and symptom overlap with other diseases. Our findings demonstrate that administration of MOG-AMP-B is a robust, scalable therapeutic platform to selectively suppress ongoing autoimmunity.

Results

B cells interacted with AMPs at the injection site and trafficked to the draining lymph node

Fluorescently labeled AMPs were formulated via spray drying, yielding ~1 μm spherical particles (Fig. 1A, and Supplementary Fig. 1). To investigate biodistribution and cell interaction of the particles, fluorescently labeled AMPs were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) in the flanks of mice. Temporal analysis via IVIS showed that AMPs formed a concentrated depot at the injection site that persisted for at least 7 days (Fig. 1B, and Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on prior work, we selected days 3 and 7 for in-depth analysis because they capture the significant duration AMP+ cells at the injection site and the draining inguinal lymph nodes (iLN)27,28. In our studies, the iLNs were the most fluorescent organ (Fig. 1C, D, and Supplementary Fig. 3), suggesting AMP trafficking, which is significant as iLNs are abundant with B cells and critical for establishing an immune response.

To identify and assess changes in the immune cells associated with AMPs at the s.c. depot and draining iLNs, extended flow cytometry staining panels were used, allowing for deeper phenotypic characterization compared to prior work27,28 (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). In the s.c. depot, AMP injection resulted in no marked changes in CD45+ frequencies compared to PBS-treated mice, suggesting that AMPs are non-inflammatory. There was a strong CD45 + AMP+ signal (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C), which was comprised of ~15% B cells. Indeed, ~70% of B cells were AMP+ on days 3 and 7 post-treatment at the s.c. depot (Fig. 1E, and Supplementary Fig 6D–G, Supplementary Table 2). Neutrophils were also a significant cell type associated with AMPs, with ~80% of neutrophils AMP+ on days 3 and 7, respectively. A relatively high frequency ( ~ 60%) of conventional DC type 1 cells (cDC1), which are highly phagocytic and capable of cross-presentation, were also AMP+ (Supplementary Fig. 6H–K). By day 7, AMP + B cells had similar counts to AMP+ neutrophils and were greater than AMP+ cDC1s (Supplementary Fig. 6L–O). AMP + B cells were CD21/35+ (complement receptors 1 and 2), which we hypothesized would promote AMP interactions with B cells based on dextran iron-oxide nanoparticle literature (Supplementary Fig. 7)18,19. T cells had low AMP+ frequencies. AMP+ frequencies were sustained in the depot between days 3 and 7, except for slight decreases in conventional DC type 2 (cDC2) cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and intermediate monocytes (IMO).

AMP+ cells trafficked to iLNs and did not affect the relative frequencies of CD45+ cells compared to PBS-treated controls (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Fig 4-5,8; Supplementary Table 3). It is unlikely that AMP undergo passive drainage through the lymphatics due to their ~1 µm size. Instead, AMPs likely rely on cellular transport to reach the iLN24. The percentage of AMP+ cells increased between days 3 and 7 for all cell types in the iLNs (Fig. 1F). By frequency, cDC1 and cDC2 cells had the highest %AMP+ at 4-5%, however, both DCs were less than 0.5% of total cells in the iLN. Meanwhile, B cells composed ~10% of cells in the iLN, with 1% of B cells being AMP + . This resulted in increased AMP + B cell counts, which surpassed other AMP+ APCs, including cDC1s, cDC2s, and neutrophils (Fig. 1F, G, and Supplementary Fig. 8). AMP + B cells were primarily follicular B cells, shown by CD21/35 + CD23+ (Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast, ≤0.1% T cells were AMP+ on either day (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results suggest AMP s.c. injection has a high affinity for B cells, which traffic to the iLN.

AMPs associated with the surface of B cells via complement proteins

Since AMPs target B cells in vivo, we hypothesized that serum proteins adsorb to AMPs, forming a protein corona that mediates association. Confocal microscopy showed that AMPs surface-associated with splenic B cells (Fig. 2A-B, Supplementary Fig 11A). Meanwhile, DCs internalized AMPs as expected22,29,30 (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig 11B). In contrast, T cells showed no interaction (Supplementary Fig 11C).

To assess whether B cell association is unique to spray-dried AMPs or dextran motifs, we tested emulsion AMPs, spray-dried poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid microparticles (PMP), which lack dextran motifs, and dextran iron-oxide nanoparticles (Dextran I.O. NP) (Fig. 2D, and Supplementary Fig 12, Supplementary Fig. 13A). Emulsion AMPs showed minimal association, likely due to the polyvinyl alcohol excipient in them altering the protein corona. Dextran I.O. NPs exhibited low association, consistent with reports that heat-inactivated serum does not promote association18. PMPs showed modest association and were selected as the control for in-depth analysis. AMP association was concentration-dependent (Fig. 2E), which saturated within 2 h, and persisted for 48hrs (Supplementary Fig 13B), outperforming PMPs. B cell viability was unaffected compared to controls (Supplementary Fig 13C). After 48 h at 100 µg/mL, AMP-treated B cells produced significant IL-10 levels, a potent tolerogenic anti-inflammatory cytokine (Fig. 2F). IL-10 levels exceeding 300 pg/mL are often considered a Breg hallmark, especially from freshly isolated splenocytes without lipopolysaccharide or CpG stimulation31–33. PMPs induced lower and inconsistent IL-10 levels. Neither particle induced B cell production of IFNγ or TNF (Supplementary Fig. 13D, E). Similarly, human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMC) were incubated with AMPs or PMPs. AMPs robustly associated with hPBMC B cells (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B) and preferentially associated with B cells over other cell types (Fig. 2G).

Unlike T cells, B cells can capture carbohydrate antigens, such as dextran, through their surface immunoglobulin, or B cell receptor (BCR)34. Considering the striking preferential binding of AMPs to B cells over other immune cells, it could be hypothesized that the BCR, exclusively expressed on B cells, mediated the B cell-AMP interaction to some extent. In a 2 hr association assay, murine B cells that transgenically express hen egg lysozyme-specific (HEL) BCR (MD4 strain) associated with AMPs at the same frequency as wild-type C57BL/6 B cells, suggesting BCR specificity does not contribute to binding (Fig. 2H).

Given that dextran I.O. NPs associate with B cells through complement deposition in non-heat-inactivated serum18,19, we investigated whether a similar mechanism drove AMP-B cell interactions. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) contains complement proteins, lipoproteins, albumin, immunoglobulins, and coagulation factors35, which can adsorb to particles, forming a protein corona. Heat inactivation typically denatures complement proteins. AMP association increased when FBS was non-heat-inactivated (Fig. 2I), and association was FBS concentration-dependent (Supplementary Fig 13F). In contrast, PMP association remained four-fold lower, independent of FBS conditions (Supplementary Fig. 13G, H).

To identify AMP-associated proteins, AMPs were incubated in 10% heat-inactivated FBS, and their protein corona was analyzed by proteomics (Supplementary Table 4). Complement proteins, particularly regulatory complement proteins, were highly abundant (Fig. 2J). Given that complement proteins engage B cell surface receptors, we assessed whether AMP association was receptor-mediated. Blocking CD21/35 (complement receptor 1/2) reduced AMP association to baseline levels (Fig. 2K). This mechanism did not apply for PMPs (Supplementary Fig 13I). AMP association decreased in C3 knockout mouse serum (from Collaborative Cross strain CC026/GeniUnc mice), which lacks a functional complement cascade, reinforcing complement’s role (Fig. 2L).

Sushi domains, C4BPA (complement 4 binding protein alpha chain), and LOC510860 (complement 4 binding protein-like protein) were the most highly abundant complement proteins in AMPs’ corona, all of which are parts of complement 4 binding protein (C4BP) (Fig. 2J). C4BP binds CD40 differently from CD40L36, and CD40 stimulation is associated with B cell IL-10 production37. Accordingly, we tested C4BP's role in AMP association by supplementing C4BP in vitro. AMP association with B cells increased above baseline with the addition of 0.05 to 0.5 µg/mL of C4BP. However, AMP association decreased at 5 µg/mL of C4BP, indicating competitive inhibition (Fig. 2M). Despite C4BP’s interaction with CD40, B cells treated with AMPs loaded with OVA MHCII antigen (termed OVA-AMP-B) treatment did not alter CD80/CD86 expression (Supplementary Fig. 13J, K, and Supplementary Fig 14), unlike binding of an agonistic anti-CD40 antibody. Both OVA-AMP-Bs and anti-CD40 increased MHCII MFI (Supplementary Fig. 13L). Thus, OVA-AMP-Bs have a regulatory mechanism different from an anti-CD40 antibody, potentially due to C4BP. In summary, these data demonstrate that AMPs surface-associate with B cells, inducing IL-10 production through a protein corona enriched in regulatory complement proteins.

Peptide-AMP-treated B cells induced Bregs, which promoted Tregs and CTLA4+Lag3 + T cells

Since OVA-AMP-Bs (Supplementary Fig. 15) increased B cell MHCII expression without co-stimulation (Supplementary Fig 13J-L), we assessed the effects of OVA-AMP-Bs on T cell stimulation. Co-culture of OVA-AMP-Bs versus B cells pretreated with soluble OVA significantly increased CD4 + OTII T cell proliferation (Fig. 3A–C, and Supplementary Fig 16), marked by an increase in the frequency of peptide-activated CD62L-CD44⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 17A, B). Despite the AMPs not being internalized (Fig. 2B), the improved proliferative T cell response demonstrates that the concentration of AMPs on the B cell surface mediates diffusion of peptide as it is released from the particle28, making it available for internalization and loading onto MHCII for presentation.

Furthermore, OTII CD4 + T cells treated with OVA-AMP-Bs exhibited decreased secretion of IFNγ (Fig. 3D) and IL-2 (Supplementary Fig. 17C). OVA-AMP-Bs upregulated B cell MHCII (Fig. 3E), which correlated with an increased percentage of Foxp3+Tregs (Fig. 3F) and elevated expression of PD-1 by OTII CD4 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. 17D).

While the OVA-AMP-B system models interactions between naïve T cells and B cells, autoimmune diseases involve a more complex T cell environment, including naïve, effector, and antigen-experienced populations. Therefore, the autoimmune BDC2.5 TCR-transgenic NOD mouse model was used as a heterogeneous source for CD4 + T cell subsets specific for the mimetic p31 peptide (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Similar to OVA-AMP-Bs, p31-AMP-Bs induced robust BDC2.5 CD4 + T cell proliferation (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Fig. 17E, F). Phenotypic analysis revealed an enrichment of LAG3⁺ B cells (Fig. 3H), many of which were CD5⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 17G, H), suggesting a Breg subset. Similar to the OTII system, Treg frequencies remained around 2-4% (Supplementary Fig. 17I), however, further T cell phenotyping demonstrated that p31-AMP-Bs induced a striking increase in CTLA4⁺Lag3⁺ T cells (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Fig 17J-K). This was accompanied by a decreased IFNγ:IL-10 ratio (Fig. 3J). The combination of inhibitory receptors decreased IFNγ levels, and yet high proliferation may indicate a Tr1-like regulatory response. Since prior studies have shown antigen-specific MPs can induce tolerogenic phenotypes in DCs and macrophages38–44, we then compared the role of B cells as APCs to DCs (Supplementary Fig. 18).

In parallel, DC-T cell co-cultures were performed to compare AMP-B antigen presentation to other APCs in the OTII model. Unsurprisingly, DCs treated with OVA-AMPs or soluble peptide induced robust CD4 + T cell proliferation (Fig. 3K). Interestingly, AMP-Bs were able to reach or exceed the proliferative capacity of DCs, but this did not occur when pulsed with soluble peptide alone, highlighting the unique ability of AMPs to modulate B cell APC function (Fig. 3L). Similar trends were observed in Treg expansion, indicating that AMPs promote tolerogenic antigen presentation in B cells and other APCs (Fig. 3M). In comparison to AMP-treated DCs, AMP-Bs induced the largest CD25 expression on CD4 + T cells (Fig. 3N), LAG3 expression on CD4 + T cells (Supplementary Fig 19A), and culture supernatants revealed dramatically reduced IL-6 secretion in B cell groups (Supplementary Fig 19B). This demonstrates that AMPs confer tolerogenic function on APCs, but they uniquely stimulate regulatory T cell responses through association with B cells. To further explore the role of DCs, we evaluated whether DCs phagocytose AMPs from B cells. B cells were pre-treated with AMPs before co-culture with DCs. As DC numbers increased, AMP⁺ B cell frequency decreased, suggesting AMP transfer to DCs (Supplementary Fig. 20A, and Supplementary Fig. 21). AMP pre-treatment of B cells enhanced DC uptake compared to direct AMP administration (Supplementary Fig. 20B), highlighting the robustness of AMP delivery across immune cell types. Although DCs internalized AMPs, B cells did not internalize the particles (Fig. 2A), despite antigen uptake being required for T cell proliferation. To confirm that cell active membrane dynamics and MHCII recycling are necessary for presentation of antigen that has diffused out of the AMPs, Golgi transport (Supplementary Fig. 20C–F), actin-dependent uptake (Supplementary Fig. 20G–J), and dynamin-mediated uptake (Supplementary Fig. 20K–N), were blocked, resulting in reduced T cell proliferation and regulatory phenotype.

Our findings demonstrate that B cells internalize and process antigen released via diffusion from AMPs, rather than requiring complete microparticle uptake - an important distinction given that the AMPs are too large for direct internalization by B cells. AMP-B cell interactions facilitate localized peptide transfer, internalization, and subsequent antigen presentation, thereby enabling B cell APC function and driving antigen-specific T cell modulation.

B cell antigen presentation is typically less robust than DCs, making this proliferative response unusual. Enhanced B cell APC capacity is typically mediated by antigen-specific capture and processing via the BCR45,46. To understand if antigen-specific BCR mechanisms were involved in AMP-induced presentation, we performed a similar OTII B cell-T cell co-culture using either C57BL/6 B cells or MD4 B cells, which are transgenically modified to only express HEL-specific BCR. Regardless of BCR specificity, Treg expansion and proliferation remained robust at levels that were not statistically different between MD4 and C57BL/6 B groups (Supplementary Fig. 22). Moreover, we treated B cells with AMPs or anti-IgM antibodies to evaluate BCR engagement and signaling using phosphorylated ERK (pERK) as an indicator of BCR signaling47. Western blots from B cell lysates revealed that pERK expression was not upregulated compared to media control (Supplementary Fig. 23), confirming that AMPs modulate B cells independent of BCR engagement.

MOG-AMPs associated with B cells reduced late-stage EAE clinical scores

In vitro, AMPs associated with B cells, upregulated IL-10 secretion, and facilitated antigen presentation to CD4 + T cells, which increased the frequencies of Foxp3+Tregs and Lag3 + CTLA4 + T cells. Accordingly, we assessed the efficacy of B cells pretreated with spray-dried AMPs loaded with the encephalogenic peptide MOG35-55 (MOG-AMP-B) to suppress severe EAE (Supplementary Fig. 24). MOG-AMP-Bs were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) into EAE mice at peak clinical disease, with an average clinical score of 3 corresponding to full hind limb paralysis (Fig. 4A., full scoring criteria in Supplementary Table 5). Unlike our prior particle-only studies (Fig. 1) which used s.c. injection28, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection was selected to support better viability and distribution of a B cell-based therapeutic mechanism. AMP particles associated with B cells and modulated them into tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells, which promoted regulatory T cell responses. This active modulation distinguishes our strategy from many tolerance-inducing approaches that target DCs and uniquely harnesses the antigen-specific regulatory potential of B cells.

In a long-term EAE study, MOG-AMP-B treatment led to robust recovery in 83% of mice (n = 6) (Fig. 4A-B), reducing clinical scores to ≤0.5 by day 60 (study enrollment criteria in Supplementary Fig. 25) and leading to 100% survival (Supplementary Fig. 26). Treatment with AMP-Bs or MOG-AMPs administered i.p. or s.c. had no marked effect on EAE progression (Supplementary Fig 26). Similarly, treatment with MOG-PMP-Bs also had no therapeutic effect (Supplementary Fig. 26). For simplicity and due to a lack of survivors, animals from MOG-PMP-B (i.p.), MOG-AMPs (i.p.), and MOG-AMPs (s.c.), were combined into a group termed “MOG Controls” with individual clinical scores and descriptions of all controls detailed in Supplementary Fig. 26. MOG-AMP-B treatment led to such a dramatic recovery (Fig. 4B), that an additional scoring criterion was implemented, 0.25: animals have full hind limb function and movement in their entire tail, but less than healthy (non-EAE) controls (Supplementary Table 5). MOG-AMP-B animals held themselves on the food grate, whereas those scoring ≥ 1 took food from the cage floor instead.

At day 60, flow cytometry and antigen recall assays revealed sustained immune reprogramming, despite inherent survivor bias in the control groups. This late in the EAE model, spinal cord infiltration becomes limited, but CNS damage remains48,49. RT-qPCR of unstimulated splenocytes revealed elevated levels of IL-10 and IFN-β, indicating an anti-inflammatory response (Fig. 4C, and Supplementary Fig. 27A). IFN-β mitigates EAE by modulating antigen presentation, limiting T cell activation, and enhancing regulatory pathways. To observe if these factors led to functional tolerogenic education, splenocytes were restimulated with MOG35-55. Cytokine ELISAs showed that MOG-AMP-B restimulated splenocytes had reduced IL-17a and IFNγ (Fig. 4D, and Supplementary Fig. 27B). Most significantly, there was reduced IL-6 production. There were trends toward increased IL-2 and IL-10 in the restimulated splenocytes, which are anti-inflammatory in tandem. Significant generation of TNF, IL-1β, IL-12p40, IL-12p70, and IL-4 were not observed (Supplementary Fig. 28).

In the brains of MOG-AMP-B–treated mice, flow cytometry revealed that IL-10–producing CD4⁺ T cells were significantly elevated (Fig. 4E, and Supplementary Fig. 29), while inflammatory CD4⁺ cytokines such as IFNγ and IL-17A were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 30A, B). These cytokine patterns were not observed in the spleen, iLNs, or cervical lymph nodes (cLN) (Supplementary Fig. 30C–E). Additionally, CD8⁺ T cell activation, measured by CD44⁺ frequency, decreased in the brain, while CD4⁺ T cell activation remained unchanged across all organs (Fig. 4F, and Supplementary Fig 31A–D). B cells in the brain of MOG-AMP-B–treated mice expressed lower levels of the co-stimulatory marker CD40 (Fig. 4G, H). In the spinal cord, B cells exhibited reduced expression of CD21/35 (Supplementary Fig. 32A), fewer functionally memory-like B cells (CD27⁺CD21⁺CD40⁺)50 (Supplementary Fig. 32B), and reduced CD43⁺ B cells (Supplementary Fig 32C), indicating reduced T–B interaction and activation. CD40 levels were also decreased in spinal cord B cells (Supplementary Fig. 32D; Supplementary Fig. 33), but not in lymph nodes or spleen (Supplementary Fig. 33C–E), indicating CNS-specific immune modulation.

The only peripheral immune change was in splenocytes stimulated ex vivo with PMA/ionomycin: while CD4⁺ T cell cytokines were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 30C), IFNγ-producing non–T cells, predominantly B cells, were significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 30F, G).

To reduce survivor bias and elucidate mechanistic differences in response treatment, we treated mice with MOG-AMP-B on days 15 (peak disease) and 22 post EAE-induction, and harvested tissues on day 27, a time point closer to peak disease (Fig. 4I). In this MOG35-55 EAE model, the spinal cord is the primary site of pathology51, so changes in the composition and phenotype of immune cells correlate with disease progression and resolution. At day 27, there is a decrease in the frequency of CD4 + T cells, B cells, and trends towards reduced CD11c+ cells in the spinal cord, while CD11b+ cells remained constant (Fig. 4J). Considering that this EAE model is CD4 + T cell driven, the reduction in this population frequency suggests that infiltration of CD4 + T effectors coincides with MOG-AMP-B therapeutic effect. In addition to a reduction in CD11c+ cells in the spinal cord, CD11c+ DCs upregulated MHCII expression in the PBS group but returned to the healthy control baseline in MOG-AMP-B mice (Fig. 4K), indicating a reduction in DC inflammation. Similar reduction in inflammatory cell phenotype was observed in the brain. The frequency of PDL1 + CD40 + CD45Hi cells in the brain increased in untreated mice and significantly decreased in response to treatment (Fig. 4L).

In the same study, we used the depleting anti-CD25 antibody PC61 to determine whether Foxp3⁺Tregs contributed to MOG-AMP-B remission of EAE. PC61 depletes Foxp3⁺Tregs within 24 hours52,53, with depletion lasting at least 12 days in vivo54. We administered PC61 on day 14 post-EAE induction, followed by MOG-AMP-B therapy on the same schedule. Despite Foxp3⁺ Treg depletion, all mice treated with MOG-AMP-Bs exhibited EAE remission, with ~87% of animals (n = 15) scoring ≤0.5 by day 27 (Fig. 4M, and Supplementary Fig 34). Clinical score trajectories were nearly identical between MOG-AMP-B, MOG-AMP-B + PC61, and MOG-AMP-B+isotype control groups. During peak depletion (days 15–19), clinical improvement was already evident, suggesting that early therapeutic effects are Foxp3⁺Treg-independent.

Flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig 35–36) revealed MOG-AMP-B-treated brains had decreased numbers of CD45⁺ cells (Supplementary Fig. 37A), CD45hi (Supplementary Fig. 37B), and CD11c⁺ DCs (Supplementary Fig. 37C, D), indicating decreased CNS infiltration. B cell frequency was reduced in the brains of mice treated with MOG-AMP-B, but not MOG-AMP-B + PC61 (Supplementary Fig. 37E). This may represent a compensatory mechanism where tolerogenic B cells persist longer in the absence of Foxp3⁺ Tregs as all brains from MOG-AMP-B treated mice had decreased inflammatory memory-like B cells (Supplementary Fig. 37F). MOG-AMP-Bs elicited increased MHCII on B cells (Fig. 4N), as well as decreased MHCII on CD11c⁺ DCs (Fig. 4O), consistent with targeted B cell engagement for antigen presentation. CD11b⁺CD11c⁻ myeloid cells trended toward a reduction in frequency and numbers (Supplementary Fig. 37G, H), accompanied by decreased inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) production, supporting diminished inflammation and myeloid activation (Supplementary Fig. 37I). In the spinal cord, MOG-AMP-B treatment combined with Foxp3⁺ Treg depletion (MOG-AMP-B + PC61) resulted in increased frequencies of anti-inflammatory CD206⁺CD11c⁺ cells, suggesting a possible compensatory regulatory mechanism triggered by Foxp3⁺ Treg loss (Supplementary Fig. 37J). Across all MOG-AMP-B-treated groups, CD11c⁺ cells in the spinal cord showed reduced iNOS and MHCII expression, reinforcing the CNS tolerogenic effect of MOG-AMP-Bs (Supplementary Fig. 37K, L).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that MOG-AMP-B therapy reprograms B cells to promote sustained CNS immune tolerance and clinical recovery in EAE, accompanied by reduced activation of myeloid cells, with early therapeutic effects evident even in the absence of Foxp3⁺ Tregs.

Mice were treated with an average clinical score of 2.5 (severe hind limb inhibition) (Fig. 5A). MOG-AMPs and B cells were fluorescently labeled for the following groups: MOG-AMPs alone, B cells alone, MOG-AMP-Bs, and PBS (Supplementary Fig. 38). MOG-AMP-Bs were administered and tracked in organs on days 15 and 17 after EAE induction (days 1 and 3 post-treatment) (Supplementary Fig. 39). While a small percentage of MOG-AMP-Bs were at the injection site, MOG-AMPs largely dissociated from B cells at the injection site and spleen by day 15 (Supplementary Fig. 40A, B), perhaps due to a dynamic protein corona19 and rapid in vivo MOG-AMP-B distribution. The MOG-AMP-B group had significantly fewer MHCIIhi DCs and MOG-AMP⁺ DCs by day 17 and trends towards fewer macrophages at the injection site (Supplementary Fig. 40C–F).

Discussion

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a debilitating autoimmune disease marked by CNS dysfunction and progressive disability. Current therapies are non-curative and broadly immunosuppressive, though B cell depletion has slowed disease progression. Antigen-specific therapies have shown promise for suppressing autoreactivity but often focus solely on Treg induction. Yet the demonstrated efficacy of B cell depletion therapies highlights the need for approaches that antigen-specifically reprogram B cells to restore immune homeostasis.

We developed an antigen-specific strategy utilizing spray-dried AMPs to target and re-educate B cells into tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells (APC). The preferential surface association of AMPs with B cells (Fig. 2A, B, and Supplementary Fig 11) in vitro (Fig. 2D, E) and in vivo (Fig. 1E–G) was mediated by serum complement proteins (Fig. 2J) that compose AMPs’ protein corona such as C3 (Fig. 2L) which engages CD21/35 (Fig. 2K) B cell complement receptors, and C4BP (Fig. 2M). C4BP is not only a key mediator of AMP-B cell association, but also a regulatory protein with tolerogenic effects in autoimmune models of colitis, lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis55–58. Consistent with the presence of regulatory proteins in the AMP corona, AMP association with B cells elicited IL-10 production (Fig. 2F), indicative of a Breg phenotype, positioning these cells as active mediators of immune tolerance rather than passive AMP or antigen carriers.

Peptide-AMP-treated B cells exhibited a distinct tolerogenic phenotype characterized by upregulated B cell MHCII expression (Fig. 3E, and Supplementary Fig 13L, Fig. 4N, Fig. 5I) without upregulation of other co-stimulatory molecules (Supplementary Fig 13J, K). This unique state enabled antigen presentation that favored Foxp3⁺Treg (Fig. 3F) and CTLA4⁺Lag3⁺ T cell (Fig. 3I) induction and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Fig. 3D,J). In contrast, BMDCs internalized AMPs (Fig. 2C), and when loaded with a peptide MHCII antigen, they did not stimulate T cells to the same extent as B cells, and they secreted higher levels of pro-inflammatory IL-6 (Fig. 3K–N, S19), highlighting the specificity of the B cell-driven tolerogenic antigen presentation mechanism.

Applying this antigen-based platform to the EAE, MOG-AMP-Bs effectively reversed paralysis and ameliorated clinical disease scores, surpassing previous literature outcomes (Fig. 4B, M, Supplementary Table 1). This strategy resembles “monocyte-backpacks,” polymer disks adhered to monocytes that previously reduced EAE severity59. Unlike monocyte-backpacks, MOG-AMP-Bs require no chemical modification or frustrated phagocytosis60. Importantly, both MOG-AMPs and B cell delivery were essential for EAE remission. This therapeutic effect correlated with reduced IL-6 secretion in antigen recall responses (Fig. 4D), decreased CNS-activated and memory B cells (Fig. 4G–L), and increased CNS IL-10–producing CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 4E). Depletion of Foxp3⁺Tregs did not abrogate clinical improvement, suggesting that early tolerance is mediated through Treg-independent pathways (Fig. 4M), but potentially by MOG-AMP educated MHCIIhi Bregs or Tr1-like T cells. This expands the potential therapeutic window to patients with a dysregulated Foxp3⁺Treg compartment, a common challenge in autoimmune conditions.

Infiltration of CD4 + T cells from EAE induction was reduced in the spinal cord following MOG-AMP-B treatment (Fig. 4J). MOG-AMP-Bs reshaped the antigen-presenting landscape in the CNS by enhancing MHCII expression on B cells (Fig. 4N, Fig. 5I) while diminishing MHCII expression on DCs (Fig. 4K,O) in the CNS, positioning B cells as tolerogenic APCs leading to disease resolution. The trafficking of MOG-AMP-B B cells from the injection site to the disease-related cLN (Fig. 5G) supports a role for B cells in promoting tolerance by modulating autoreactive T cell responses at key disease-related immunological sites. MOG-AMP-Bs facilitated MOG-AMP delivery to the spleen (Fig. 5B); this was not observed for administering MOG-AMPs without B cells. In the absence of B cell delivery, free MOG-AMPs instead attracted myeloid cells to the injection site (Supplementary Fig 40C–F), highlighting a distinct immune context and emphasizing the critical role of B cells in coordinating biodistribution and immune engagement.

The antigen-specific nature of this therapy preserved overall immune competence (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig 44), as demonstrated by maintained normal responses in an infectious disease model. This highlights the advantage of targeting B cells antigen-specifically without broadly suppressing protective immunity, a significant limitation of many current immunosuppressive therapies.

The robust and durable tolerance achieved by MOG-AMP-Bs suggests potential for translation to human autoimmune diseases like MS. Human peripheral blood B cells preferentially interact with AMPs (Fig. 2G-H). Unlike traditional cell-based therapies that require complex ex vivo manipulation, this approach benefits from the abundance of peripheral B cells and requires only a brief culture period. This could streamline clinical workflows and reduce some treatment cost barriers in comparison to other cellular strategies, such as CAR-T cells. In contrast to current FDA-approved B cell depletion therapies, our strategy reprograms B cells by harnessing their capacity for antigen presentation and immune regulation. The use of AMPs made by spray drying readily facilitates scale-up and enables adaptation to different disease-specific antigens, including encapsulating multiple autoantigens to adapt to patient-specific profiles.

While the immune environment in human MS is more heterogeneous than in the EAE model with multiple autoantigens and diverse patient profiles, therapeutic concepts engaging antigen-specific tolerogenic cells and biomaterial particle systems have already advanced to clinical trials supporting particle-based, multi-antigen-based, and cell-based therapies. Clinical trials for MS have included multi-antigen coupled PBMCs that reduced antigen-specific T cell responses61 and multi-antigen-loaded DCs that increased an antigen-specific Tr1 response (NCT0228367, NCT02618902, NCT02903537, NCT07020715)62,63. Others have advanced particle-based therapies to the clinic for autoimmune diseases, including CD206-targeting multi-antigen liposomes for MS64, gliadin peptide–loaded poly(dl-lactide-co-glycolic acid) emulsion NPs for celiac disease (NCT03486990, NCT03738475); both of which may face scale-up challenges compared to spray drying.

Therefore, MOG-AMP-B approach is translatable, but unlike these other therapies already in clinical trials, the peptide-AMP-B platform capitalizes on a simple and scalable spray drying process and the abundance and tolerogenic potential of B cells as APCs. By optimizing AMPs to tolerogenically engage endogenous B cells in vivo, future strategies could eliminate the need for ex vivo manipulation. Despite the striking reduction in clinical scores, much is still unknown regarding the AMP-B signaling pathways and downstream immune effects in vivo that lead to disease reversal. Future studies will focus on uncovering the mechanistic underpinnings of this delivery platform, potentially offering new avenues to enhance the therapeutic specificity. Still, this work demonstrates that B cell-targeted antigen delivery is a promising direction for achieving antigen-specific tolerance and advancing treatment options for MS and other autoimmune diseases.

Methods

Experimental design

The objective of these controlled pre-clinical experiments was to determine how AMPs interact with B cells and use that interaction to create a durable antigen-specific therapy to treat EAE. It was hypothesized that AMPs associate with B cells due to complement proteins and this interaction could be harnessed as a tolerogenic therapy. BALB/c mice were used to assess AMP trafficking to the iLNs from the s.c. depot. BALB/c mice were chosen for the increased depot signal during live animal imaging due to their light fur. OTII mouse B and T cells and BDC2.5 NOD mouse B and T cells were used to understand the role of B cells as APCs in response to antigen-specific OVA-AMP treatment. EAE mice were used to test the efficacy and trafficking of MOG-AMP-B treatment. EAE is a clinically relevant model for MS. We chose to enroll and treat EAE mice at peak disease to maintain a clinically relevant timeline wherein patients will present symptoms before diagnosis. In the experiments discussed in Fig. 4 and Fig. 6, all mice were age-matched and distributed evenly with similar cage averages of weight per mouse, % day weight per mouse, and score per mouse as discussed in Supplementary Fig 25. EAE mice were scored in a blinded manner. In the therapeutic EAE experiment, clinical scores were selected as the primary outcome, with an endpoint of day 60 to show treatment longevity as peak disease occurs around days 16–23. This timeframe translates to a scale of years when considering human treatment. All procedures involving animals were approved by UNC-Chapel Hill’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Experimental and control mice were co-housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with 12-hour light/dark cycles at 70 °F and 50% humidity. All mice used for EAE studies were bred by vendor (Taconic Farms, Hudson, NY), Stock# B6-Fand purchased at the desired age and sex. Female mice were selected for all in vivo studies because of the higher incidence and severity of autoimmune responses in women and female mice. At humane or study endpoints, mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation and cervical dislocation in accordance with UNC IACUC ethical standards. Sample sizes were determined based on preliminary data and prior results to ensure statistical analyses could be performed. All in vitro assays were repeated on three independent occasions. Each data point shown is representative of a distinct sample. Each graph that does not show distinct points states the group size (number of distinct samples) and whether the standard deviation or standard error mean is shown.

Acetalated Dextran Synthesis

Acetalated dextran was synthesized as previously described65. In brief, glassware was soaked in 1.0 M sodium hydroxide (Sigma Cat# 367176) to remove endotoxin contamination before synthesis. 71 kDa dextran (Sigma Cat# D8821-100G) and pyridinium p-toluenesulfonate (Sigma Cat# 232238) catalyst were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO Sigma Cat# 276855. Under anhydrous conditions, dextran was reacted with 2-ethoxypropene (Matrix Scientific; Montgomery, AL Cat# 70378). After 15 min, tetrahydrofuran (Thermo Fisher Cat# AA44608AE) was added to increase reactant solubility. After 2 hr the reaction was quenched with triethylamine (TEA Thermo Fisher Cat# AAA12646AK). The product was precipitated in basic water, spun down by centrifugation (21,000 g for 10 min), and washed twice with basic water. The product was dried via Rotovap and lyophilized overnight. The next day, the product was washed 3x, dried and lyophilized. NMR analysis determined the cyclic acetal coverage to be 60%65.

Spray-Dried Microparticle Fabrication

AMPs were formed by dissolving acetalated dextran (2.5 mg/mL) in 99% ethanol (Thermo Fisher Cat# 04355223EA) and 1% DMSO. Depending on the cargo’s physiochemical properties, it was dissolved in ethanol or DMSO before adding it to the acetalated dextran/ethanol solution. The feed solution was spray-dried using a B-290 spray-dryer (Buchi, New Castle, DE) with 10 mL/min feed flow rate, 75 °C inlet temperature, and 55 mm Q-flow meter. PMPs were formed by dissolving 85:15 poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid (2.5 mg/mL) in acetonitrile (ACN Thermo Fisher Cat# A996-4) and adding water for a final composition of 95% ACN and 5% H2O. The cargo was added to ACN or H2O before adding to the poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid/ACN solution. The PMP spray had the same parameters except for a 45 °C inlet temperature. MPs were sprayed into a 50 mL tube, suspended in a sucrose solution, aliquoted, lyophilized, and stored at -20 °C. Cargo used: ovalbumin(323-339) (OVA) peptide (Biomatik; Ontario, Canada), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein(35-55) (MOG) peptide (CSBio; Menlo Park, CA), p31 peptide (YVRPLWVRME) (BioMatik, Ontario, Canada), and DiD perchlorate (MedChemExpress; Monmouth Junction, NJ; Cat# HY-D1028).

Microparticle characterization

MP morphology was assessed via SEM (Hitachis-4300 Cold Field Emission SEM; Ibaraki, Japan; UNC CHANL). Endotoxin was evaluated via the Pierce Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Cat# A39553). All MPs were below the 0.01 EU/mg threshold. Peptide loading was measured using a fluorescamine assay. AMPs and peptide standards were dissolved in 0.1 N HCl. Borate buffer (0.1 M) was added to adjust the pH for the fluorescamine reaction. PMPs and peptide standards were dissolved in ACN. The peptide concentration was measured by fluorescence, and blank MPs were subtracted as background. AF647-labeled dextran iron-oxide nanoparticles were purchased from Luna Nanotech (Ontario, Canada).

Cell culture

B and T cells used in vitro were obtained from 8-14-week-old C57BL/6 (Jackson Labs; Bar Harbor, ME) Stock# 000664, C57/OTII (bred in-house), BDC2.5 (bred in-house), or MD4 (bred in-house) murine spleens postmortem. Where applicable, CD4 + T cell or B cell isolation kits were used (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-104-454 and 130-104-443 Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). BMDCs were obtained from 8-12-week-old C57BL/6 (000664) femurs or BDC2.5 NOD (004460) mice with tibia and cultured similarly to previous reports66–69. BMDCs were isolated from the femur of mice and matured according to standard protocol70. In brief, cells were cultured in complete RPMI media (Thermo Fisher Cat #MT10041CV) with GM-CSF (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 31503100UG) for seven days. On the seventh day, cells were isolated, plated in 96-well plates (VWR 10861-564), and rested for 8 hr before treatment. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from de-identified human buffy coats (obtained from Gulf Coast Regional Blood Center, Houston TX) by density gradient centrifugation (Lymphoprep, Accurate Chemical and Scientific Corporation Cat# AN1001967) and resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 media. Similar studies with de-identified human samples have been previously reviewed by the UNC Office of Human Research Ethics, which has determined that it does not constitute human subjects research as defined under federal regulations [45 CFR 46.102 (d or f) and 21 CFR 56.102(c)(e)(l)] and does not require Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval. Unless stated otherwise, all media for cell culture experiments used were complete RPMI 1640 media: RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (VWR Cat# 97068085), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Cat# 15140122), 1% non-essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Cat# 11140050), and 1× β-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Cat# 21-985-023).

Confocal Microscopy

Chambered cover glass slides were coated with poly-D-lysine in water (75 µg/mL) at 37 °C. Slides were washed with water 3x before plating cells. B cells, T cells, and BMDCs were plated (100k/per well) and rested overnight. Cells were fixed with 3% PFA and imaged on the Leica SPX8 Falcon.

Proteomics

Samples were dried down via vacuum centrifugation, then resuspended to 1 µg/µl in 1 M urea, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. For each sample, 25 µg of protein lysate was reduced with 5 mM DTT at 37 °C for 45 min and alkylated with 15 mM IAA at room temperature for 45 min. Samples were then digested with 2 μL of 0.5 mAU/mL LysC for 2 hours at 37 °C, followed by an overnight digestion with 4 ug of trypsin incubated in a thermomixer at 37 °C, shaking at 400 rpm. The resulting peptides were acidified to 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (Thermo Fisher Cat# 11140050) and desalted using Pierce desalting spin columns (Cat# 89862) using manufacturer’s recommendations. Eluates were dried via vacuum centrifugation and resuspended in 2% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid prior to determining peptide concentration using the Pierce Quantitative Fluorometric Assay. All samples were normalized to 0.1 μg/μl and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

The peptide samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Easy nLC 1200 coupled to a QExactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples were injected into an Easy Spray PepMap C18 column (75 μm id × 25 cm, 2 μm particle size) (Thermo Fisher) and separated over a 2 hr method. The gradient for separation consisted of a 107-minute linear gradient from 5–48% mobile phase B (MPB) at a 250 nl/min flow rate, and a constant 100% MPB for 13 minutes, where mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 80% ACN. The QExactive HF was operated in data-dependent mode, where the 15 most intense precursors were selected for subsequent fragmentation. Resolution for the precursor scan (m/z 350–1700) was set to 60,000, while MS/MS scans resolution was set to 15,000. The normalized collision energy was set to 27% for HCD. Peptide match was set to preferred, and precursors with unknown charge or a charge state of 1 and ≥ 7 were excluded.

Raw data files were searched against the Uniprot reviewed and unreviewed Bos taurus database (containing 37,506 entries, downloaded May 2024), using the Sequest HT search engine node within Proteome Discoverer (v3.1, Thermo Fisher). Enzyme specificity was set to trypsin, up to two missed cleavage sites were allowed, methionine oxidation and N-terminus acetylation were set as variable modifications, and cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a static modification. Acetylation, methionine loss, and methionine loss + acetylation were set as variable modifications on the protein terminus. The Minora node was used to extract label-free quantification (LFQ) intensities. A 1% peptide-level false discovery rate (FDR) and a 5% protein-level FDR was used to filter all data. Match between runs was enabled. Proteins were filtered out if there was no quantification in at least one sample. Statistical analysis was performed in Perseus software (version 1.6.14.0). Imputation was performed by drawing random numbers drawn from a normal distribution downshifted 1.8 with a width of 0.3. P-values were calculated using a two-sided Student’s T-test.

In Vitro Co-Culture Assays

For B cell and OTII CD4 + T cell co-cultures: 5 × 104 - 1 × 105 B cells were plated in a 96-well U-bottom non-TC treated, rested for 30 min, and treated with 0.1 mg/mL AMPs or the corresponding loading of OVA (1.22 μg/mL) for 2 hr. After AMP treatment, 2.5 × 104 CTV-stained T cells were added with IL-2 (Biolegend Cat# 575402)(500 pg/mL) and TGF-β (SinoBiological Cat# 80116-RNAH)(125 pg/mL) in RPMI media. Similarly, 3.0 × 104 BMDCs from C57BL/6 mice were treated with 0.1 mg/mL AMPs or the corresponding loadings of OVA for 2 h then cultured with 5.0 × 104 CTV-stained T cells (to volume normalize the density to that of the B and T cell co-culture) for 72h. Cytokines were titrated for this experiment to support T cell viability and proliferation. After 72 h, cells were stained for flow, and supernatants were run on cytokine ELISAs (IL-2 and IFNγ). To assess antigen uptake, B- and T cells were pre-treated with the following inhibitors for 30 min at 37 °C prior to adding OVA-AMPs and culturing for 72hrs: Brefeldin A (Biolegend; San Diego, CA; Cat# 420601) at 0.1-1μg/mL, cytochalasin-D at 0.05-0.5 μm (Sigma, Cat# C8273), and Dynasore at 2-20 μm (Sigma Cat# 324410).

For B cell-BDC2.5 CD4 + T cell cocultures: 1.0 × 105 B cells were plated in a 96-well U-bottom, rested for 30 min, and treated with 0.01 – 0.1 mg/mL AMPs or the corresponding loadings of p31 (0.047 - 0.466 μg/mL) for 2 hr. After AMP treatment, 5.0 × 104 CTV-stained T cells were added in RPMI media for 72 h. Supernatants were measured via cytokine ELISAs (IL-10, IFNγ) according to manufacturer’s instructions (Biolegend; San Diego, CA, Cat# 431411 and 430801).

B cell and BMDC cocultures: 5.0 × 104 B cells were plated in 96-well F-bottom and treated with AMPs for 2 h. BMDCs were rested for 8 h prior to treatment with AMPs. Varied numbers (2 − 10 × 104) of BMDCs were added and incubated for 24 h. B cells and non-adherent BMDCs were transferred to a U-bottom. The remaining cells were lifted via 2.5 mM EDTA and transferred to the U-bottom for flow cytometry staining.

In Vivo AMP Trafficking Study

2 mgs of AMPs encapsulating DiD fluorophore were administered to 10-12-week-old BALB/c mice in each s.c. flank. Two cohorts of mice were assessed to observe AMP+ cells in the s.c. depot and iLNs on days 3 and 7: n = 6 AMP-treated and n = 4 PBS-treated per day. Each cohort had spleens, iLNs, livers, lungs, hearts, depots, and kidneys imaged on IVIS. The IVIS Lumina (PerkinElmer Inc.; Waltham, MA) was used to image AMP-treated and PBS-treated mice with excitation of 600 nm and emission of 640 nm (exposure time 0.5 s for exposed depots post-harvest and 1 s for live animal imaging and organ imaging). Depots and iLNs were processed into a single-cell suspension for flow cytometry. Day 7 mice were imaged daily on IVIS to track the depot size.

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis studies

EAE was induced in female 12–13-week-old murine pathogen-free C57BL/6 mice (B6-F) (Taconic Farms, Hudson, NY) Stock# B6-F. Induction kits from Hooke Laboratories (EK-2110 kit, MOG35-55 antigen, Lawrence, MS) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols. On day 0, mice were weighed and immunized twice s.c. along the center of the back with a complete Freund’s adjuvant and MOG emulsion. A 200 ng pertussis toxin dose administered i.p. was given 4 h later and on day 1. Daily clinical scores and weight assessments occurred from days 7–60. Clinical scoring criteria adapted from Hooke Laboratories are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Mice were euthanized immediately if they developed front limb paralysis or weight loss of 20%. Daily, hydration levels were monitored, and Dietgel Boost (Lab Supply, Durham, NC; Cat# 72-06-5022) was provided throughout the EAE studies.

MOG-AMP-Bs trafficking and efficacy were evaluated in a late therapeutic model. MOG-AMP-Bs (or MOG-PMP-B) were prepared by isolating splenic B cells of age-matched healthy C57BL/6 mice (B6-F). 3 × 107 B cells in 30 mL RPMI media were plated in 150x25mm non-TC-treated dishes, rested for 30 min, and treated with 0.1 mg/mL MPs for 2 hr. MOG-AMP-Bs were collected in a 50 mL tube, spun (350gx8min), and resuspended in PBS (1 × 107 cells/mL). Treatments were given in 500μL i.p., except for MOG-AMPs s.c., where mice received 2 × 100 µL injections in the flanks.

For the trafficking study, once animals had a clinical score, they were enrolled (average score=2.5). Animals were randomly distributed and treated on day 14. On days 15 and 17, a cohort was harvested and spleens, spinal cords, brains, peritoneal washes, cLNs, and iLNs were collected for flow cytometry.

For the efficacy study, animals were enrolled at peak disease. Each cage was distributed so that the average score of the mice (n = 3–5 per cage) was 3, the average % day 0 weight was 90%, and their average weight was 19 g (Supplementary Fig 25). Cages were randomly assigned to treatment groups (n = 5–6). The lab member that scored mice was blinded from the treatment groups. A healthy, non-EAE, age-matched control (n = 3) was included as a reference. Mice were treated on days 15, 22, and 29 and monitored until day 60. At day 60, spleens, spinal cords, brains, cLNs, and iLNs were collected for flow cytometry and antigen recall.

For the PC61 depletion study, mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 500 μg of anti-CD25 monoclonal antibody (clone PC61, Bio X Cell, Cat# PC-61.5.3) on day 14 post-induction, a timepoint chosen to coincide with peak disease severity. An isotype control group received 500 μg of rat IgG1 isotype control antibody (Bio X Cell, Cat# BE0083) on the same schedule. Mice received two doses MOG-AMP-B, administered i.p. on days 15 and 22 post-induction. At day 27, spleens, spinal cords, brains, cLNs, and iLNs were collected for flow cytometry.

Western Blots

B cells were purified from C57BL/6 background mice using Miltenyi negative isolation kits. 2 × 106 B cell were plated in a 96-well U bottom plate and treated with 100 µg/mL of AMPs, 5 µg/mL anti-IgM antibody (Jackson Immunoresearch; West Grove, PA, Cat# 115-006-075) or media for 10 minutes. Cells were lysed using 50 µl RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Cat# 89901) with 1x HALT Protease/Phosphatase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Cat# 78443) and vortexing. Lysate protein concentration was quantified using a microBCA assay (Thermo Fisher Cat# 23235). 15 μg of each sample was boiled for 5 min at 95 °C and western blots were performed to detect pERK and vinculin (loading control). ImageJ was used for densitometry analysis. The ROI tool was used to calculate the signal for vinculin and pERK. pERK normalized signal was calculated by dividing by the vinculin signal for each respective sample. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was bought from CST and developed with clarity enhanced chemiluminescence substrate from Biorad and imaged with a Biorad Chemidoc.

Antigen Recall

Splenocytes isolated from EAE late therapeutic mice were plated in 96-well U-bottom plates (1 × 106 cells/well) and stimulated with 10 µg/mL of MOG for 36 hr. Unstimulated samples from each mouse were prepared and their cytokine levels were subtracted from stimulated samples.

RT-qPCR

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) (PCR primers listed in Supplementary Table 6) was used to measure cellular gene expression as previously72. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, RNA was isolated using the RNeasy RNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen; Cat# 74106, Hilden, Germany). RNA was converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using Protoscript II reverse transcriptase (New England Biolabs (NEB); Cat# M0368X, Ipswich, MA) using the manufacturer’s recommended reaction conditions. Reverse transcription reactions contained 50 units Protoscript II, 20 units murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, Cat# 10079149), 5 nmol dNTPs (Thermo Fisher Cat# 18427013), and were primed with 100 pmol of oligo dT23VN (Eurofin Genomics; Louisville, KY). cDNA was measured using a NanoDrop, and samples were diluted to the same concentration with nuclease-free water before use in downstream assays. For RT-qPCR reactions, 4.5 μL cDNA was mixed with 5 μL PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix and 0.5uL 20X primer mix(Thermo Fisher Cat# A25742) (4 μM forward/reverse primers in nuclease-free water). Custom primers were synthesized by Eurofin Genomics (catalog numbers not available). Data were collected on a QuantStudio 6 Flex qPCR system (Applied Biosystems; Waltham, MA). The ΔΔCT method was used to calculate changes in gene expression.

Viral challenge and immune competency model

13-week-old mice were administered a full course of MOG-AMP-B treatment. or PBS (n = 10 mice per group with 3 treatments, each one week apart). Each treatment group was split into n = 5 and received a submandibular bleed biweekly on an alternating schedule 12 hours after treatment so that n = 5 mice were bled on days -7, 1, 8, and 15 (1-3 drops collected for flow cytometry and cytokine analysis). Blood was collected in tubes containing 5 mM EDTA in PBS. Mice were then intranasally challenged 16 h after the final MOG-AMP-B (and 4 hours after their bleed) with 1 K PFU (50 μl) of the mouse-adapted A/California/07/2009 H1N1 virus (CA/09). Mice were weighed daily for 14 days, where a body weight loss of 20 % was used as the survival endpoint.

Flow cytometry

Organs were processed into a single-cell suspension. Lymph nodes and spleens were processed via mechanical dissociation through a 70 μm filter. Spleens used ACK lysis buffer (VWR/Avantor; Radnor, PA; Cat# 10128-808) to remove red blood cells by incubation with 2 mL ACK buffer for 2 minutes then dilution with 10 mL complete RPMI media, which was spun down (500gx5min) and decanted, then reconstituted with complete RPMI media. Subcutaneous AMP depots were harvested according to prior reports73. Spinal cords were isolated by hydrostatic extrusion where a 27-gauge needle was inserted into the caudal vertebral canal and flushed with 5 mL of cold RPMI. Spinal cords and brains were processed into single cell suspension in the same manner: A 15 mL glass Dounce homogenizer was used for tissue grinding, with 10 strokes using the loose-fitting pestle followed by 10 strokes with the tight-fitting pestle. Homogenates were spun in a 30% Percoll gradient (20 min × 500 g, no brake). Pellets were run through a 70 μm filter. In some cases, especially for animals who had heavy damage, spinal cord retrieval was incomplete (multiple pieces retrieved) or by meninges or blood contamination; all samples were therefore processed uniformly and underwent red blood cell lysis using ACK lysis buffer as detailed with spleens. All panels, antibodies (Supplementary Table 7), dilutions, clones, gating strategies, and other methods are detailed per experiment in the supplement. All antibodies were titrated in-house prior to use. Cells were plated and washed with PBS containing 2% v/v FBS (FACS buffer), stained with viability dye for 10 min at room temperature, stained for surface markers, and fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde, washing twice between each step. For intracellular stains, cells were fixed and permeabilized by incubating with fix/perm buffer from the Foxp3 Transcription Factor kit (eBioscience, San Diego, CA; 00-5523-00) for 30 min on ice. After washing twice, cells were stained with intracellular markers, washed twice and run on the Attune NxT (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) or the LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) with analysis on FlowJo.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were run in GraphPad Prism. Each statistical test is detailed in the figure captions. For experiments with flow plots or confocal images, representative images were chosen. Significance was defined *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. For comparisons of two groups, a one-tailed or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons of more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s posthoc test was used. For comparisons of more than two groups with multiple time points, a two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Sidak’s posthoc test was used. Error bars represent mean ± standard deviation, except for EAE clinical scores (Figs. 4B, 6B) where mean ± standard error mean is shown. All statistical analyses are detailed in Supplementary Table 8 for main figures and Supplementary Table 9 for supplemental figures. These tables include the figure reference, statistical test, multiple comparisons test, test statistic, 95% confidence interval, effect size, degrees of freedom, and p-value.
