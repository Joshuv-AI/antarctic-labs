Development of the Short Form of the Specific Module of QLICD-BPH (V2.0) for Assessing Quality of Life with Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia by Classical Test Theory, Item Response Theory, and Generalizability Theory
Authors: Yuxi Liu, Zhengqin Xiao, Hairong He, Yuming Huang, Delin Yang, Mushi Ye, Chonghua Wan, Qikang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageThis paper focused on exploring the most suitable simplified version of the quality of life instrument for patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (short-form Quality of Life Instrument for Chronic Diseases–Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia) by different simplification methods based on classical test theory, item response theory, and generalizability theory.Keywords: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Quality of Life, Item response theory, Classical test theory, Generalizability theory, Short form

What does this study add?

This is the first study to explore the most suitable simplified version of the quality of life instrument for patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH; short-form Quality of Life Instrument for Chronic Diseases–Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia) by different simplification methods based on classical test theory, item response theory, and generalizability theory.

The seven-item Specific domain (SPD-7) from SPD-16, which was able to distinguish individuals with BPH traits, was deemed sufficient for use in the short form, showing good reliability and validity.

Clinical Relevance

Quality-of-life assessment is important in men with benign prostatic hyperplasia, but lengthy questionnaires may limit routine use in busy clinical settings and may increase response burden, especially in older patients. The SPD-7 offers a shorter and practical alternative for outpatient assessment, dynamic monitoring, and clinical research. By enabling rapid evaluation of BPH-related symptoms and their impact on quality of life, this tool may help clinicians better integrate patient-reported outcomes into treatment evaluation and follow-up. Further external validation in larger and more diverse populations is warranted before broad clinical implementation. Associate Editor: Professeur Véronique Phé.

Patient Summary

Hospitalized patients with a confirmed diagnosis of benign prostatic hyperplasia at any stage and undergoing treatments who had a certain level of education were enrolled to complete the survey.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common urinary system disease. Typical symptoms include increased frequency of urination, nocturia, urgency, hesitancy, and weak urine stream [1], [2]. Emerging evidence shows that BPH affects the quality of life (QOL) of patients by inducing psychiatric disturbances because of obvious clinical manifestations and long-term treatment [3], [4], [5]. The more severe the symptoms of BPH, the poorer the QOL. The QOL result will strengthen our understanding of the health of patients with BPH, including the perfect state of physical, psychological, and social adaptation. Furthermore, these findings can be applied in clinically research (such as evaluating treatment effect and determining treatment options), in order to take appropriate measures to improve patients' QOL and reduce the cost burden [6].

In order to measure the QOL of patients with BPH suitably in China, our team has developed a QOL scale, Quality of Life Instrument for Chronic Diseases–Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (QLICD-BPH), under the system of the QLICD [7], [8]. The QLICD system was developed based on a popular modular approach consisting of a general/core module (QLICD-GM) general module plus a specific module [9], [10], [11], [12]. The QLICD-GM can be used for all chronic diseases, and the specific module only for related diseases. For example, the QLICD-BPH for BPH is constructed by combining QLICD-GM with the specific module for BPH. The updated QLICD system includes a 28-item general module, QLICD-GM, and 34 specific modules, which form 34 specific scales of the psoriasis-specific module [9], chronic gastritis–specific module [10], and breast cancer–specific module [12], etc.

QLICD-BPH Version 2.0 (V2.0) consists of 44 items, of which 16 constitute the specific module (SPD-16) domain [7], [8]. The 16 items reflect clinical symptoms and side effects of BPH. This SPD-16 scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity through comprehensive psychological evaluation [7], [8]. Although the full-length SPD-16 scale can provide more measurement information, the relatively large number of items may limit its popularization and widespread use in clinical dynamic monitoring or outpatient screening, especially when combined with a common module. In addition, too many items may also lead to patient fatigue [13], thereby reducing the accuracy of measurement results.

As such, it is important to develop a new short form of the SPD-16 domain in the QLICD-BPH V2.0 to facilitate efficient assessment in clinical practice, monitor patients’ health status, improve the quality of data, and reduce the burden of response. This paper aims to discuss in detail how to combine classical test theory (CTT), generalization theory (GT), item response theory (IRT), and core panel methods to shorten the scale. We (1) shortened specific module facets to determine a short structural model through core group; (2) used GT method to analyze the remaining qualified items and determine the minimum (optimal) number of items of the short form; (3) applied CTT and IRT methods to analyze item quality and provide basis for item selection; and (4) selected the best performing items to be included in a short form by integrating GT, CTT, IRT, and core panel discussion results.

Patients and methods

Patient sample

In total, 141 patients with BPH who visited the Department of Urology, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University, were included. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a clinical diagnosis of BPH; (2) personnel who can read, understand, and fill in the questionnaire by themselves; and (3) patients who cooperate voluntarily. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with severe systemic disease or who had cognitive impairment such as dementia and (2) patients who refused to cooperate.

Measures

The SPD-16 consists of 16 items divided into four facets: stimulation and obstruction symptoms (BPH1–BPH4 and BPH10–BPH13), dripping symptoms (BPH7–BPH9), hematuria and psychological impact (BPH5–BPH6), and the impact of disease and treatment on life (BPH14–BPH16) [7], [8]. It reflects the frequency of symptoms and side effects in the past week [7], [8]. After the positive transformation of all items, the item responses are graded from one (“extraordinary”) to five (“none at all”). Scores are added and standardized to generate the total score, ranging from 0 to 100; higher scores represent a higher QOL of patients with BPH.

Statistical analysis

Structural determination

The structural model of the short form of the SPD-16 was based on a core panel discussion. The core panel comprised 8 clinical experts, 4 QOL experts, 2 statistics experts, and 11 master’s students in epidemiology and health statistics from China, aiming to develop consensus recommendations to determine a short form of the structural model. Based on the structural model of the original scale, in addition to considering the degree of response of facets, the panel also considered the overlap of facet information and clinical significance in order to obtain a preliminary short form with good responsiveness and nonoverlapping information.

Item number determination

The process of determining the number of items of the preliminary short version was based on GT. GT included a generalizability study (G-study) and a decision study (D-study). We used the GT analysis preliminary short form to evaluate all measurement variation sources and their interactions and then estimated the impact level of each variation source, respectively, to better control the measurement error and improve the accuracy of measuring patients’ QOL [14], [15]. Using GT, we could calculate two types of relative reliability coefficients: the dependability coefficient (φ) and the generalizability coefficient (G) [16]. Among them, the φ coefficient determines the reliability of absolute measurement (actual measured values), and the G coefficient determines the reliability of the rank order of measured values (rank of a person’s score among others) [16]. At the same time, we focused on the G coefficient and used its estimated D-study variance components to obtain the relative error (σδ2) and absolute error (σΔ2) values [17]. Four indicators were evaluated in different D-studies that only changed the number of preliminary short-form items. Because the minimum acceptable reliability level of internal examination is usually defined as >0.70, this study adopted the reliability coefficients >0.7 and the error coefficients <0.2 as the acceptable reliability standard of the scale [15]. The preliminary short form could meet the acceptable reliability and avoid being lengthy, so as to determine the minimum (optimal) number of items of the short form, that is, N items.

Item reduction

The unidimensionality of the preliminary short form was confirmed by using exploratory factor analysis [18]. Essential unidimensionality is acceptable if the first factor explained >20–40% of variance, along with a value of >3 of the ratio of the eigenvalues for the first factor to the second factor [19].

The process of item selection of the preliminary short form was based on the CTT, IRT, and core panel. CTT comprised item-total correlation, corrected item-total correlation, factor analysis, and Cronbach a values. IRT was performed using RStudio 4.0.5 with an unconstrained graded response model. Discrimination parameter (a), difficulty parameter (b), and average information were estimated for each item. Statistical results were retained if items met four or more of the following criteria. Then, the core panel screened N items with the best quality through comprehensive consideration, item wording, clinical significance, etc.

CTT and IRT item screening criteria were as follows:

The GT analysis was conducted using mGENOVA, and the software and manual can be downloaded from the website [14], [23]. The CTT analysis was conducted with SPSS 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All other data analyses were performed using RStudio 4.0.5 [24]. The graded response model (GRM) was fit using the ltm package [25]. Descriptive analyses were adopted to summarize the demographic data of patients.

Initial validation of the final short form

The Cronbach a was used to evaluate the internal consistency for the total scale. A coefficient of >0.7 indicated acceptable internal consistency and of >0.8 indicated good internal consistency [26], [27]. Item-total and item-item correlations were used to demonstrate homogeneity of the items as a basis of internal consistency [28]. Item-total correlations of >0.20 were considered acceptable [28]. Item-item correlations of between 0.3 and 0.7 are considered acceptable [29]. Item-item correlations >0.70 indicate item redundancy, whereas correlations <0.30 indicate the item is not related to other items in the scale [29].

Convergent validity was evaluated by the Pearson correlation coefficients between the facet scores of the final short form and the full form. At the same time, the concurrent validity was also assessed by calculating the correlation coefficient of the final short form and full form. It was expected that there would be a significant positive correlation between the final short-form scores and the full-form scores. It is generally believed that r ≥ 0.8 is an extremely strong correlation, 0.8 > r ≥ 0.7 is strong, and 0.7 > r ≥ 0.6 is medium [30], [31].

Results

Sample characteristics

In total, we recruited 141 patients from the hospital for this study. All the participants were married, with a median age of 72.30 ± 6.813 yr. Among the patients, 87 cases (61.70%) were mainly workers and cadres, and 122 cases were of Han nationality (86.5%). The education level was mainly primary school, followed by junior high school, senior high school, and technical secondary school, with 45 cases (31.9%), 31 cases (22.0%), and 31 cases (22.0%), respectively. The family economic situation was medium, with 101 cases (71.6%).

Item reduction

The results of SPD-11 showed that the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test value was 0.847, the Bartlett test of sphericity value was 617.88 (p < 0.001), and the ratio of eigenvalues was >3 (4.788/1.300). The first factor accounts for 43.438% of the total variance, which is much higher than the second factor (11.819%). Therefore, the essential unidimensionality of SPD-11 was satisfied. Table 3, Table 4 show the item reduction results of 11 items of SPD-11 using CTT, IRT, and core panel discussion. As shown in Table 3, three items (BPH1, BPH7, and BPH9) were deleted from the SPD-11 following statistical criteria provided in the Patient and methods section above. As shown in Table 4, items of SPD-11 were removed if they failed to meet four of the statistical criteria. The information generated by each item and the total scale of SPD-11 are also displayed graphically in Fig. 2, which confirms that the flat information curve of the three items implies low measurement accuracy. Then, BPH2 was removed according to the discussion of the core panel by comprehensively considering the semantic and clinical significance. This new, shorter, and 7-item version was then produced, which was named SPD-7.

Initial validation of the final short form

Cronbach a for SPD-7 was 0.850, indicating good internal consistency. The Cronbach a was slightly lower because of the small number of items, remaining close to the full-form Cronbach a of 0.881. The item-total correlations of the SPD-7 were acceptable, ranging from 0.600 to 0.802. The item-item correlations were significant and ranged between 0.30 and 0.70, except for the correlation between BPH4 and BPH11. The weak correlation between the two items was 0.27, but still close to 0.30. The mean item-item correlation was 0.445. Overall, SPD-7 had good reliability similar to that of SPD-16.

The coefficients of correlation between the scores of SPD-7 and SPD-16 were 0.98, 0.78, and 0.91 (p < 0.01) for facets of stimulation and obstruction symptoms, dripping symptoms, and the total, respectively, which indicated convergent validity of the SPD-7 was satisfactory. Furthermore, the scores on the SPD-7 maintained a high positive correlation with the SPD-16 (r = 0.910, p < 0. 01) [31], which proved good concurrent validity of SPD-7. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to develop a short form that can reflect the disease characteristics of patients with BPH and then combine it with the general module short form to form a complete short scale to evaluate the QOL with BPH. Therefore, only the facets in specific modules overlapping with the general module or having poor responsiveness were deleted and could not have a great impact on the overall results. For the number of items in short form, we applied GT to evaluate the reliability and error values as the number of items decreased. This results in seven items that provide acceptable reliability (>0.7) and smaller error (<0.2), which means fewer items are required to provide reliable estimates [15], [16], [17]. In addition, we deleted three items based on the combination of seven selection standards of statistical methods: item-total correlations, corrected item-total correlations, Cronbach a, factor load, discrimination parameter, threshold parameter, and average information. The correlation coefficient, factor load and the average information amount of these three items were lower than the predetermined standard, indicating that the homogeneity of the reliability may be reduced and the measurement accuracy may be reduced [20], [26], [27], [28]. Eventually, the final item selection for the short form was determined through the core panel on the statistical results by comprehensively considering the wording and clinical significance of the items. BPH2 asked, “ Have you had frequently used the toilet in a day?” Its wording “one day” is indeed consistent with what we observed in our previous test investigation, when some patients with BPH stated they experienced differences in urination patterns during the daytime and at night.

The results of this study provide support for the reliability and validity of the measurement performance of SPD-7. Cronbach a of SPD-7 was 0.850, between 0.8 and 0.9, indicating good internal consistency and nonredundancy of items [26], [28]. Moreover, SPD-7 was similar to the full-form SPD-16 (Cronbach a = 0.881), although the sensitivity of Cronbach a to the number of items would decrease with the number of items [32]. All item-total correlations (r ≥ 0.600, p < 0.01) were significantly and positively correlated with total score, supporting the homogeneity of the SPD-7 [30], [31]. The item-item correlations were also mostly within the acceptable range of 0.3–0.7. Although the correlation between BPH4 and BPH11 was slightly lower, their correlations with other items were still acceptable. Therefore, SPD-7 may be considered a reliable measure. For the convergent validity, the SPD-7 exhibited a strong correlation with each corresponding original facet (r ≥ 0.783) [30]. In addition, we found that the correlation between the summed score and the SPD-7 and SPD-16 was high (>0.90) [31], demonstrating that the short version could be a valid summary of its original measure.

Therefore, SPD-7 is a short, valid, and reliable measure tool for evaluating the QOL of patients with BPH. SPD-7 may be more popular than SPD-16 as it reduces participant burden, which is particularly important among the older patients with BPH who may have difficulty completing the self-report questionnaire because of limited physical functioning. By using the shorter scale of SPD-7, researchers may be able to reduce research time and improve data quality by reducing fatigue effects [33]. In addition, SPD-7 may also be a more appropriate option for outpatient evaluation or rapid dynamic monitoring.

There are many advantages of this study. We determined the appropriate number of items in a new form by estimating the dependability of scores with the number of items in different forms through GT, which has also been confirmed in other studies [34], [35]. Therefore, it may be more reasonable to determine the number of items in a short form that is different from what most studies determined by subjective means. Furthermore, we combined CTT and IRT to select items. The unique advantage of this model is that it maintains the accuracy of results in small samples through CTT and also can provide richer and more accurate performance descriptions at the item and scale levels through IRT [36], [37]. The use of both methods may yield more information than only using one of the methods [38]. In addition to quantitative analyses, clinical opinion and item wording were considered through core panel discussion to ensure the effectiveness of SPD-7 content.

This study also has limitations. The sample size is not very large, which may affect the research results to a certain extent. Furthermore, the respondents in this study were from a hospital inpatient population, which may affect the universality of the scale. Therefore, in the future, we will expand the sample size, replicate the research results in different populations, and further test the quality of SPD-7 through external data from independent samples.

Conclusions

This study indicates that the short-form SPD-7 has acceptable reliability and good validity in patients with BPH. The use of the SPD-7 is expected to reduce the burden of detection and will be further used as a rapid assessment tool for the QOL of BPH in clinical and epidemiological studies. Owing to the simplicity of SPD-7, its routine use is recommended in clinical practice.
