Real-World Antidepressant Treatment Persistence in Children and Adolescents with Major Depressive Disorder in Japan: A Retrospective Nationwide Claims-Based Study
Authors: Keisuke Nomoto, Ataru Inagaki, Keita Yamauchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeReal-world evidence on major depressive disorder (MDD) management in children and adolescents is limited in Japan. This study aimed to evaluate the antidepressant treatment persistence rate in this population.Patients and MethodsThis study evaluated the antidepressant treatment persistence in children and adolescents (aged 6–18 years) with MDD who had claims data from 6 months before to 1 year after the index date between 2012 and 2017 using a large-scale Japanese claims database. Treatment persistence was defined as the absence of a prescription gap beyond 30 days. Factors associated with it were examined using a Cox proportional hazards model (JMP ver.18).ResultsIn total, 1,582 patients on the index date were included. The treatment persistence rate for any antidepressant was 62.6% on the 30th postindex day, 46.1% on the 60th postindex day, 22.3% on the 180th postindex day, and 10.2% on the 365th postindex day. The most frequently prescribed initial antidepressant was sulpiride (30.0%). By drug class, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) were the most frequently prescribed (39.9%), followed by tricyclic or tetracyclic antidepressants (TCAs) (18.3%) and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (6.3%). The significant factors associated with treatment discontinuation with any antidepressant were treatment initiation with sulpiride, noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressants, serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitors, or TCAs, as well as consultations with nonmental health specialists.ConclusionAntidepressant treatment persistence rates in children and adolescents with MDD were low. However, the rate varied depending on the first prescribed antidepressant and mental health specialist involvement. Relatively high persistence was observed among those prescribed SSRIs or SNRIs as their initial antidepressant treatment and those who received clinical interventions provided by mental health specialists. Given the claims data and no clinical severity measures, these findings should be interpreted with caution.Keywords: antidepressant, depression, longitudinal study, pediatric, prescribed drug, treatment continuation

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) in childhood and adolescence is a major global public health issue. It can have a serious impact on emotional well-being, academic performance, social adaptation, and developmental progression.1,2 Additionally, pediatric MDD may worsen individual health and impose substantial burdens on both families and society, including increased healthcare utilization and broader socioeconomic costs.3 Moreover, depression during childhood and adolescence has been linked to increased risks of suicidality, long-term physical health problems, and impaired interpersonal functioning later in life.4 However, antidepressant use during adolescence is reportedly associated with potential physiological and psychological harms, including treatment-emergent suicidality, behavioral activation, and effects on ongoing neurodevelopment, although the long-term consequences remain unclear.5,6 These findings highlight the need for careful management by experienced mental health professionals.

Clinical guidelines recommend psychosocial interventions as the basis for the treatment of depression in childhood and adolescence, with pharmacotherapy where necessary.7–9 Antidepressant drugs can be useful in both mild and severe cases. The first choice drug therapy is fluoxetine, which is approved for use in children and adolescents aged ≥8 years by the US Food and Drug Administration.10 This is the most widely used antidepressant worldwide. The Japanese Society of Mood Disorders (JSMD)’ guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of depression recommend sertraline for children aged ≥6 years and escitalopram for patients aged ≥12 years.9 However, in Japan, no antidepressants, including these drugs, are approved for pediatric depression. Since their efficacy and safety have not been established in Japanese children, these recommendations are based on the findings of clinical studies conducted in other countries and the drugs’ approved status elsewhere.

MDD frequently recurs. To prevent this, ongoing treatment is recommended, even after remission. When treatment with antidepressants is effective, the guidelines of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry recommend that drug therapy be maintained for 6–12 months. Medication is tapered and discontinued if remission remains stable.11 Treatment persistence reflects not only adherence but also treatment tolerability, perceived effectiveness, and the quality of the patient–physician interaction. If antidepressant treatment is discontinued prematurely, unfavorable clinical trajectories, including symptom chronicity and treatment-resistant depression emergence, may occur.12 These concerns are particularly relevant in children and adolescents, for whom inadequate initial treatment may have lasting consequences.

Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) can require discontinuation of antidepressant therapy,13–15 and young people are less tolerant of ADRs, with a higher risk for treatment interruption than adults.16,17 A previous study on the treatment persistence rate in 6–18-year-olds receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) for depression in South Korea found that ≥50% discontinued treatment within 1 month and approximately 90% discontinued treatment within 1 year.18 These rates are high compared with those of adult patients reported in a Japanese study,19 although this was not a direct comparison. In adults, various factors, including age, sex, psychiatric comorbidities, and specialist mental health care, have been related to antidepressant treatment persistence and discontinuation.20–22 Together, these factors may influence routine treatment continuity. However, there has been a dearth of systematic analysis on antidepressant treatment interruption in adolescents. Furthermore, reports on factors associated with treatment persistence and on evidence across different antidepressant classes remain unclear. While the World Health Organization’s multidimensional adherence framework has been extensively used to study treatment persistence in adults,23 its applicability to children and adolescents is still poorly established. The influence of these factors on antidepressant treatment persistence in pediatric populations is an important knowledge gap addressed by the present study.

In this study, we aim to clarify the antidepressant treatment persistence rate in children and adolescents in Japan using a large-scale Japanese claims database. We also aim to explore the demographic and clinical factors associated with treatment persistence. This study could be the first to investigate class-specific antidepressant treatment persistence and associated clinical factors in children and adolescents with MDD, using a large-scale Japanese claims database. Our findings may contribute to the development of effective treatment strategies, including the choice of drug therapy, in the treatment of pediatric depression.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

This retrospective observational study used a large-scale, longitudinal Japanese health insurance claims database from MinaCare Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). The MinaCare database contains anonymized health insurance claims data from corporate health insurance associations on employees and their dependents. As of January 2021, the personal information of ≥6.3 million people has been registered. This study used fully anonymized data and ensured strict confidentiality in accordance with the Act on the Protection of Personal Information in Japan. Under the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects (Japan, 2023), this research did not require institutional review board approval and informed consent. Nonetheless, it was conducted in accordance with these guidelines and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

This analysis used monthly claims data from April 2010 to March 2018. This included the patient’s basic characteristics (anonymized ID, age, and sex), International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes,24 medical procedures, and prescription information, as well as claims information on drug dispensing and medical procedures. The dispensing claims data includes outpatient prescriptions dispensed at an outpatient insurance pharmacy, and the medical fee data includes medications prescribed to hospital inpatients or to outpatients from in-hospital pharmacies.

The index date was defined as the date of the first antidepressant prescription occurring between April 2012 and March 2017. The study target population was patients with MDD aged 6–18 years on the index date, with claims data from ≥6 months before to 1 year after the index date (investigation period). Patients with MDD were defined as those with an ICD-10 diagnosis of depression, denoted by the diagnostic code F32 (depressive episode) or F33 (recurrent depressive disorder), from 1 month before to 2 months after the index date (Figure 1).

Patients with out-of-hospital prescriptions for antidepressants were included in the analysis. Some of these also had in-hospital prescriptions during the investigation period, which made prescription days obscure. To ensure the accuracy of prescription days, these patients were excluded. Also excluded were patients aged ≤5 years and those diagnosed with manic episodes (ICD-10, F30), bipolar affective disorder (ICD-10, F31), or schizophrenia, schizotypal, and delusional disorders (ICD-10, F20-F29) on the index date or before. Patients who began treatment with multiple antidepressants were excluded, and only patients who started monotherapy were included.

With reference to previous studies,25,26 Today’s Drug Therapy in 2024,27 and the classifications of the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare,28 we classified drugs as antidepressants, antipsychotics, hypnotics, and anxiolytics (Supplementary Table 1). Sulpiride acts primarily as a dopaminergic receptor antagonist29,30 and is generally classified as an antipsychotic. However, in Japan, it is approved for treating depression or depressive states in adults31,32 and commonly prescribed for depression in routine clinical practice.9,19,33 Therefore, we classified sulpiride as an antidepressant in patients with an ICD-10 diagnosis of depression. If phenobarbital was given for comorbid epilepsy (ICD-10, G40) or status epilepticus (state) (ICD-10, G41), it was not included in our data as a hypnotic.

In case where calculable counseling fees for specific pediatric diseases, in addition to medical procedure fees in a psychiatry department, were billed, the intervention was defined as a mental health specialist consultation (Supplementary Table 2). Counseling fees for specific pediatric diseases can be calculated when a doctor or certified psychologist provides counseling required for treatment to patients aged <18 years in a medical institution specializing in pediatric care or psychosomatic medicine. In Japan, pediatricians and psychosomatic medicine physicians who treat children can also provide such counseling. Given that this definition relies on billing codes rather than physician specialty, it may not fully distinguish care provided by psychiatrists from that provided by pediatricians or psychosomatic medicine physicians, potentially limiting comparability with studies based on physician specialty classifications.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcomes included the antidepressant treatment persistence rate for any antidepressant, and the persistence rates for the first prescribed antidepressant 30, 60, 180, and 365 days postindex. Treatment discontinuation was defined as no antidepressant prescription within 30 days of administration of the previous prescription. A 30-day treatment gap was selected to define treatment discontinuation, consistent with prior claims-based studies of antidepressant persistence, to balance sensitivity to early discontinuation and allowance for routine refills.34,35 Conversely, treatment persistence was defined as treatment continuation beyond 30 days despite the concomitant use of other medications. When calculating the treatment persistence rates for the first prescribed antidepressant, we classed switching to another antidepressant(s) as treatment discontinuation. Our third primary outcome was factors associated with antidepressant treatment persistence (for any antidepressant and by initial antidepressant).

Statistics

All data were analyzed using the full dataset. We performed time-to-event analyses using a Cox proportional hazards model. Time-to-event was calculated as the period between the index date and the treatment discontinuation day. Patients without a treatment discontinuation date were censored on their last prescription date. Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for prespecified covariates available in the claims database, including sex, age, antidepressant class, concomitant use of antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and hypnotics, mental health specialist consultation, and concomitant cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT] use. Categorical variables were entered as nominal variables, with reference categories selected according to the order of values. However, this study did not incorporate disease severity because of the lack of relevant information in the claims database. Prior treatment in the 6 months before the index date was also not included, given that the participants were not yet diagnosed with MDD or prescribed with an antidepressant prescription during this period and that this study focused on treatment patterns following MDD diagnosis. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated.

The Wilson score method was used for calculating 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for persistence rates. Hazard ratios (HR), their 95% CIs, and p-values were calculated using the Wald method (prob > chi-squared), and the significance level was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the student edition of JMP, ver. 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

We excluded 88 of these due to treatment initiation with multiple antidepressants. Discussion

This study is the first to investigate real-world antidepressant treatment persistence in children and adolescents with MDD in Japan. By focusing on a 1-year follow-up period, the study captures early and mid-term treatment discontinuation, which is most frequently observed during antidepressant treatment. The persistence rate dropped throughout the treatment period, and 60 days after treatment initiation, more than half of the patients had discontinued all antidepressant treatment. A study in South Korea has similarly reported low treatment persistence among children and adolescents with depression. However, the study focused specifically on SSRIs.18 In contrast, the treatment persistence rate in adult patients with MDD is relatively high worldwide, including Japan,19 the USA,36 and Italy.37 The low persistence rate in children and adolescents may be due to ADRs16–18 or negative familial attitudes toward psychotropic medications, including excessive concern when safety or efficacy issues arise.38

We found sulpiride to be the most frequently prescribed initial antidepressant. This aligned with the results of a previous study that investigated antidepressant prescriptions to children and adolescents with depression using the same database as this study.39 In Japan, sulpiride has historically been used as an initial monotherapy for adult depression; however, it is considered a third-line option for moderate-to-severe depression in expert consensus.40 This practice contrasts with that in other countries, where sulpiride is more commonly used as an adjunctive treatment.41,42 Sulpiride may be favored in some cases because of its perceived safety relative to other antidepressants. The package inserts and existing literature on certain other antidepressants have reported an increased risk of suicidal behavior in young people.43–45 Such considerations may influence prescribing patterns. However, given the study’s observational design and data limitations, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as unmeasured potential confounding factors, including disease severity or treatment response, may also have influenced the prescribing patterns. Nevertheless, more than half of the patients had discontinued sulpiride therapy by the 30th postindex day. This had increased to around 75% on the 60th postindex day. In adults, although frequently used in Japan,19,33 sulpiride demonstrated relatively low treatment persistence.19 Thus, this antidepressant may play a unique role in Japanese clinical practice. Additionally, these findings may have clinical implications, including the possibility that some treatment discontinuations reflect clinical improvement or remission rather than treatment failure.

In contrast, comparatively high persistence rates were observed among those treated with SSRIs and SNRIs, which are commonly used as first-line antidepressants in adults.9,33,40 This finding may be related to their generally favorable tolerability and established use in clinical practice.46–48 Until recently, only certain specific SSRIs have been recommended for child and adolescent MDD.9 However, the 2025-revised guidelines do not mention any specific classes or names of antidepressants.49 Therefore, SNRIs may only be considered in some clinical situations, depending on the patient’s symptoms. Among the classes of antidepressants, SSRIs, especially sertraline and escitalopram, have been recommended by the Japanese guidelines as a first-line treatment. SNRIs are also a treatment option in those whose symptoms suggest norepinephrine involvement. Taken together, these observations suggest that differences in persistence across initial antidepressant treatments may influence early discontinuation patterns.

We identified multiple factors associated with treatment discontinuation. These were treatment initiation with sulpiride or drug classes NaSSA, SARI, or TCA, and consultations by nonmental healthcare specialists. Conversely, when patients received consultations with mental healthcare specialists, and when the initial prescribed treatment was an SSRI or SNRI, there were significantly higher rates of treatment persistence. Although not significant, CBT was associated with a numerically lower hazard of treatment discontinuation; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution because of the small sample size. Consistent with previous studies,50,51 we found that the combination of psychotherapy with drug therapy may be useful in supporting treatment persistence. Even without CBT, supportive psychotherapy provided by mental health specialist clinics in daily practice may also be useful. These specialists can also provide disease education to patients and their families to facilitate treatment continuation. This finding may reflect more structured follow-up, systematic psychoeducation, and more nuanced medication management provided in specialist settings. By incorporating psychoeducation into routine clinical practice or developing resources to help families understand the rationale for continued treatment, the translational relevance of these findings would be enhanced. These findings further underscore that appropriate follow-up and patient and family education are essential to support treatment continuity when clinically indicated. Importantly, low treatment persistence may have significant downstream clinical consequences. Some patients who discontinue treatment early may eventually experience recurrence, chronification, or a treatment-resistant course. This trajectory may be particularly relevant for patients experiencing first treatment episode during childhood or adolescence, representing an important but underrecognized pathway to treatment-resistant depression. Additionally, individual differences, such as affective temperaments, may contribute to lower treatment persistence. Temperamental characteristics, including cyclothymic or anxious traits, reportedly increase sensitivity to ADRs and reduce engagement with treatment, thereby influencing adherence and persistence.52

In this study, the proportional hazard assumption appeared to be reasonably satisfied. Thus, treatment discontinuation may not have been limited to the early treatment phase, where adverse events are often most prominent.

This study has several limitations. First, it was sponsored by a pharmaceutical company, and one of the authors is an employee of the sponsor. These limitations may introduce potential conflicts of interest and should be considered when interpreting the results. Nevertheless, the analyses were conducted using predefined methods, and the conclusions are based on the observed data and relevant published literature.

Second, the database used included only members of Japanese health insurance societies and their family members. It did not include self-employed individuals, employees of small and medium-sized enterprises, or recipients of public assistance. Therefore, selection bias may be present, and the findings may not be generalizable to the broader Japanese population.

Third, because our source was a medical claims database, the diagnostic names used would have been selected for their utility in assessing insurance claims rather than for their correspondence to the diagnoses used in clinical practice.

Fourth, mental health specialist consultation was defined according to counseling fee codes rather than physician specialty. Pediatricians and psychosomatic medicine physicians in Japan may also provide mental health counseling for children. Hence, this operational definition may limit comparability with studies classifying care strictly according to physician specialty.

Fifth, we did not include prior treatments at baseline among the factors that may affect treatment persistence. In addition, the lack of data on residual confounders including MDD duration, severity, and psychosocial background prevented us from assessing the effects of these factors in this study.

Sixth, our data source did not include reasons for treatment discontinuation or information on treatment efficacy. Thus, some treatment discontinuations may have reflected clinical improvement or remission, ADRs, lack of efficacy, or other external factors, rather than treatment failure, which could not be distinguished in this study. Furthermore, the small number of patients receiving CBT limited reliability in statistical inference. These should be considered in the interpretation of the results.

Lastly, the available data on the database used in this study was limited to the period up to March 2018, so our results may not reflect the conditions after that date. However, no new antidepressants have been approved since March 2018.

Conclusion

We found a low antidepressant treatment persistence rate in children and adolescents with MDD in Japan. However, the rate varied depending on the first prescribed antidepressant and mental health specialist involvement. Although sulpiride was the most frequently prescribed initial treatment, it exhibited the lowest treatment persistence. Thus, careful follow-up and patient and family education may be necessary when sulpiride is prescribed. Relatively high persistence was observed among patients initially prescribed SSRIs or SNRIs and those receiving care from mental health specialists. Therefore, supportive approaches, including patient and family psychoeducation and improved access to specialist care, may be useful in maintaining treatment persistence. These findings may also guide healthcare professionals when making clinical decisions regarding treatment continuity in children and adolescents with MDD.
