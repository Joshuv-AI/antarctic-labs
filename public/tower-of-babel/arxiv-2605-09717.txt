The general regularisation scheme applied to conditional density estimation
Gilles Germain

Abstract
The general regularisation scheme, a versatile approach for nonparametric estimation, has been successfully applied to regression, density ratio, and score estimation. In this paper, we introduce a unified framework encompassing these settings and extend it to conditional density estimation, deriving a new estimator with rigorously established convergence rates. We implement the Landweber regularisation, which is computationally more tractable than Tikhonov regularisation in this context. Numerical experiments demonstrate that our estimator matches or outperforms the Nadaraya-Watson estimator in various scenarios, including time series models.



1
Introduction

This paper investigates the use of kernel-based regularisation methods for conditional density estimation.
Regularisation aims at addressing ill-posed inverse problems (see e.g.
(
12
)
). Starting from
(
13
)
, it has proved to be a powerful tool in nonparametric regression and learning theory. Among the various choices of hypothesis spaces, reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) have become particularly prominent. The earliest regularisation methods studied in this context were the Tikhonov method, also known as least-squares regularisation
(
11
;
9
)
, and the Landweber iteration, which relies on early stopping gradient descent
(
45
)
.
The general regularisation scheme (GRS), originally developed in
(
3
)
and introduced in this context by
(
38
;
5
)
, provides an abstract framework that unifies a broad class of regularisation methods. It encompasses the Tikhonov and Landweber approaches, as well as spectral cut-off techniques, the
\nu
-method, and iterated Tikhonov regularisation (see e.g.
(
38
)
).
Other approaches, such as Ivanov and Morozov regularisation, do not seem compatible with GRS
(
35
;
34
)
.
Several strategies have been proposed for the data-driven selection of the regularisation parameter, including the balancing principle
(
10
;
30
)
and the quasi-optimality criterion
(
25
)
. Optimal convergence rates have been established for Tikhonov regularisation
(
8
)
and for GRS
(
30
;
37
)
. In
(
6
)
, these ideas were extended to inverse learning problems, where optimal lower bounds on convergence rates were derived. Further extensions include linear functional regression
(
21
;
28
)
, polynomial functional regression
(
22
)
, and distributed learning
(
20
)
. More recently, a growing body of work has compared implicit and explicit regularisation in linear regression (see, e.g.,
(
1
;
44
)
).
Beyond regression, GRS has been applied to other statistical tasks. Density ratio estimation was first addressed in
(
36
;
24
)
, with pointwise convergence guarantees established in
(
32
)
, while quantile regression was studied in
(
27
)
and score estimation in
(
43
;
46
)
.

In this work, we focus on applying the GRS framework to conditional density estimation (CDE). While regression aims at estimating the conditional mean of a response variable
Y
given covariates
X
, many applications—including risk and asset management
(
39
)
and renewable energy forecasting
(
41
)
—require a more complete description of the uncertainty associated with the mean response. One way to achieve this is by inferring the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
, which is the subject of CDE. A wide range of approaches has been proposed, such as local linear estimators
(
14
)
, finite mixture models
(
15
)
, tree-based methods
(
17
)
, neural networks
(
26
)
, and mixture density networks
(
19
)
. In this paper, we restrict attention to kernel-based methods. In a parametric setting,
(
16
)
employed kernel principal component analysis, while
(
2
)
proposed an estimator defined as the minimiser of the maximum mean discrepancy with respect to the empirical distribution of
(X,Y)
. In the nonparametric setting, the Nadaraya–Watson estimator relies on Bayes’ rule and kernel density estimators of the joint and marginal distributions of
(X,Y)
(see e.g.
(
23
)
), whereas
(
40
;
42
)
reconstruct conditional densities from kernel mean embeddings.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce an abstract statistical framework in which the GRS can be applied, and we review previously studied settings, including regression, density ratio estimation, and score estimation. We recall the main regularisation methods within this framework and establish consistency of the resulting estimator under simplified assumptions. For the Landweber iteration, we further propose selecting the learning rate via exact line search, which accelerates the convergence of the gradient descent. In Section
3
, we apply GRS to CDE
and compare the resulting estimator with existing kernel-based CDE on several numerical examples, including Gaussian mixture and time series models.

To summarise the novelties of this paper, we (i) propose a general statistical framework suitable for the application of GRS that unifies several existing settings,
(ii) apply GRS to CDE and derive a novel estimator with well-established convergence rates.

2
The general regularisation scheme

2.1
A general framework for GRS

Let
V
be a random vector with values in
\mathcal{V}
and
f_{*}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function related to
V
, where
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are closed Euclidean sets. For example,
f_{*}
could be the density of
V
or the conditional mean of one component of

V
given the others. Let
Z
be another random vector with values in
\mathcal{Z}
.
Our goal is to estimate
f_{*}
nonparametrically from a sample
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of i.i.d. observations of
V
and a sample
(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{z}}
of observations of
Z
.
We will consider two alternative settings:

(S1)

(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{z}}
is a set of i.i.d. observations of
Z
,

(S2)

Z=(X,U)
and
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{n_{u}}
are i.i.d. observations of
X
and
U
respectively. We set

z_{i}=\left(x_{\lceil\frac{i}{n_{u}}\rceil},u_{i-n_{u}\lfloor\frac{i-1}{n_{u}}\rfloor}\right)

(1)

for
i=1,\ldots,n_{z}
with
n_{z}=n_{u}n
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
k:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
L^{2}(Z)
the space of square-integrable functions with respect to
Z
and by
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{Z}=\mathbb{E}[f(Z)g(Z)]
and
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
the scalar products in
L^{2}(Z)
and
\mathcal{H}
respectively.
At this stage, we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

f_{*}\in L^{2}(Z)
,

(A2)

\mathcal{H}\subset L^{2}(Z)
,
\mathcal{H}
is separable,
k
is symmetric and
k\leq\kappa^{2}
for some
\kappa>0
,

(A3)

There exists a linear operator
\mathcal{T}:L^{2}(Z)\to L^{1}(V)
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[f(Z)f_{*}(Z)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{T}f(V)\right]
for all
f\in L^{2}(Z)
and
\mathcal{T}
admits an explicit expression,

(A4)

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{2}k(\cdot,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and some
C>0
where
\mathcal{T}_{2}k(z,v):=\mathcal{T}[z^{\prime}\mapsto k(z,z^{\prime})](v)
.

We set
\mathbb{D}(f,f_{*}):=\left\|f-f_{*}\right\|_{Z}
. Minimising
f\mapsto\mathbb{D}(f,f_{*})
over
L^{2}(Z)
is infeasible because its Fréchet gradient in
L^{2}(Z)
is
2(f-f_{*})
, which is not available. Instead, we seek to estimate the best approximation of
f_{*}
within
\mathcal{H}
that is, the minimal norm solution of

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left\|f-f_{*}\right\|_{Z},

(2)

which we denote by
f_{\mathcal{H}}
. A sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is that
Pf_{*}\in\mathcal{H}
where
P
is the projection on the closure of
\mathcal{H}
(see e.g.
(
5
)
). In the terminology of inverse problems theory,
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is the Moore-Penrose solution of
I_{k}f=f_{*}
where
I_{k}:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(Z)
is the inclusion operator from
\mathcal{H}
into
L^{2}(Z)
. We recall that the kernel operator
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(Z)\to\mathcal{H}
associated to
k
and
Z
is defined by

\mathcal{L}f(z)=\mathbb{E}\left[k(z,Z)f(Z)\right].

(3)

Since the Fréchet gradient of
f\mapsto\mathbb{D}(f,f_{*})
in
\mathcal{H}
is
\mathcal{L}f-\mathcal{L}f_{*}
, the first order optimality condition is
\mathcal{L}f_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}f_{*}
.
Moreover, we have by Assumption (A3) that

\mathcal{L}f_{*}(z)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{2}k(z,V)]=:b(z).

(4)

We thus have
\mathcal{L}f_{\mathcal{H}}=b
where
b
can be estimated using the observations of
V
. Therefore, estimating
f_{\mathcal{H}}
reduces to inverting
\mathcal{L}I_{k}
.
For this reason, it might also be of interest to consider the discrepancy

\mathbb{D}_{k}(f,f_{*}):=\left\|\mathcal{L}f-b\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.
We now present a few examples of objects related to
V
that can be estimated in this framework. They all correspond to Setting (S1).
We write
X\sim q
to mean that the random variable
X
has density function
q
.

2.1.1
Density estimation

Given a random vector
X\sim q_{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we estimate its density
q_{X}
on a subset
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, we have
V=X
and
f_{*}=q_{X}\big|_{\mathcal{Z}}
. We choose
Z\sim q_{Z}
as any random vector such that
q_{Z}
has full support
\mathcal{Z}
. Assumption (A3) holds with
\mathcal{T}f=fq_{Z}
because

\mathbb{E}[f(Z)q_{X}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)q_{Z}(X)]

for all
f\in L^{2}(Z)
. Taking
f=k(z,\cdot)
, we obtain

b(z)=\mathbb{E}[k(z,Z)q_{X}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[k(z,X)q_{Z}(X)]

and we compute

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{2}k(\cdot,x)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=q^{2}_{Z}(x)k(x,x).

If
q_{Z}
is a uniform density,
b
is called the kernel mean embedding of
X
. We deduce that Assumption (A4) holds with
C=\kappa^{2}\left\|q_{Z}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
. If
\mathcal{Z}
contains the support of
X
,
\mathbb{D}
is the integrated mean square error and
\mathbb{D}_{k}
is called the kernel discrepancy or the maximum mean discrepancy.
There is an extensive literature using
\mathbb{D}_{k}
to design estimators but mostly in a parametric framework (see e.g.
(
33
)
).

2.1.2
Density ratio estimation

Given two random vectors
X\sim q_{X}
and
Y\sim q_{Y}
such that the distribution of
X
is absolutely continuous with respect to the distribution of
Y
, we can estimate the density ratio
q_{X}/q_{Y}
from samples of
X
and
Y
. In this case, we have
V=X
,
f_{*}=q_{X}/q_{Y}
and we take
Z=Y
. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[f(Y)\frac{q_{X}(Y)}{q_{Y}(Y)}\right]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)]

for all
f\in L^{2}(Y)
. Again,
b=\mathbb{E}[k(\cdot,X)]
is the kernel mean embedding of
X
. Consequently, we can choose
\mathcal{T}f=f
and we deduce that
C=\kappa^{2}
.
The application of regularisation to density ratio estimation has been explored in
(
24
;
36
;
40
;
32
)
.

2.1.3
Score estimation

Some density estimation methods, such as maximum likelihood estimation, have the drawback that they require computing the integration constant of the density, which can be costly in high dimension.
One way of circumventing this issue is to estimate the score instead of the density, namely
\nabla\log q_{X}
. This score allows one to recover its associated density by integration. In this case, we have
V=X\sim q_{X}
,
f_{*}=\nabla\log q_{X}
and
Z=X
. Integration by parts yields

\mathbb{E}[f(X)\cdot\nabla\log q_{X}(X)]=-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{div}f(X)]

for all differentiable functions
f\in L^{2}(X)^{d}
where
u\cdot v
is the Euclidean scalar product of
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathrm{div}
is the divergence operator. Hence, we have
\mathcal{T}f=-\mathrm{div}f
. Here,
\mathbb{D}
is the Fisher divergence and
\mathbb{D}_{k}(f,f_{*})
coincides with the kernelised Stein discrepancy between
q_{X}
and the density associated to the score
f
. This discrepancy, which is closely related to Stein’s method, has received significant attention in the statistical literature (see e.g.
(
4
)
).
The combination of score estimation and regularisation has been explored by
(
43
;
46
)
. Since
f_{*}
is a vector valued function, this case does not completely suit our framework
but the generalisation is straightforward and can be found in
(
46
)
.

2.1.4
Regression

Given a vector of predictive variables
X
and an outcome variable
Y
such that
Y\in[-B,B]
for some
B>0
, we can estimate the conditional expectation of
Y
given
X
. We have
V=(X,Y)
,
f_{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
and we choose
Z=X
. In this case, Assumption (A1) always holds by definition of the conditional mean. We have by the tower property

\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mathbb{E}[Y|X]]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)Y]

for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
. Hence, we can choose
\mathcal{T}f(x,y)=f(x)y
and we deduce that
C=\kappa^{2}B^{2}
, but the assumption that
Y
is bounded can be weakened (see e.g. Equation 1 in
(
5
)
). The application of regularisation to regression have been studied in
(
11
;
9
;
8
;
5
;
38
;
6
)
.

2.2
Regularisation

The operator
\mathcal{L}I_{k}
is Hilbert-Schmidt and thus compact (see e.g. Equation (15) in
(
8
)
), which implies that its eigenvalues converge to 0. Consequently, the equation
\mathcal{L}f_{\mathcal{H}}=b
is ill-posed in the sense that small perturbations in
b
may lead to arbitrarily large variations in
f_{\mathcal{H}}
. For this reason, the inversion of
\mathcal{L}I_{k}
must be regularised, which we accomplish using the general regularisation scheme (GRS). In the remainder of this section, some technical details are deferred to Appendix
B
.
We recall the definition of a regulariser, also known as a filter function, which constitutes the core element of GRS.

Definition 2.1
.

The family of functions
g_{\lambda}:[0,\kappa^{2}]\to\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda\in(0,\kappa^{2})
, is called a regulariser if there exist
B,D,\gamma>0
such that
\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\kappa^{2}}\left|\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|<D
,
\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\kappa^{2}}\left|g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|<B/\lambda
and
\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\kappa^{2}}\left|1-\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|<\gamma
for all
0<\lambda\leq\kappa^{2}
.
The qualification of
g_{\lambda}
is the maximal
\nu>0
such that there exists
\gamma_{\nu}>0
satisfying for all
\lambda\in(0,\kappa^{2})

\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\kappa^{2}}\left|1-\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\sigma^{\nu}\leq\gamma_{\nu}\lambda^{\nu}.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be a compact self-adjoint operator with eigenvectors
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and eigenvalues
(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\subset[0,\kappa^{2}]
with
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators tells us that
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
can be completed to form a basis
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathcal{H}
. Given a regulariser
g_{\lambda}
, we define the operator
g_{\lambda}(A)
by

g_{\lambda}(A)f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{\lambda}(\sigma_{i})f_{i}e_{i}

where
f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}e_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
. The operator
g_{\lambda}(A)
can be viewed as a regularised inverse of
A
, as will be illustrated by the examples below.

In the sequel, we will use the same notation
\mathcal{L}
for the operators
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(Z)\to\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{L}I_{k}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, the domain should be clear from the context. The operator
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is compact, as mentioned above, and self-adjoint because

\left\langle\mathcal{L}f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{E}\left[f(Z)\left\langle k(\cdot,Z),g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]=\mathbb{E}[f(Z)g(Z)].

(5)

We denote the operator norm on
\mathcal{H}
by
\left\|\cdot\right\|:=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}
. One can show that
\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|\leq\kappa^{2}
and thus the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
are included in
[0,\kappa^{2}]
(see Appendix
B
).
Let
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample of
V
and let
(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{z}}
be observations of
Z
satisfying Setting (S1) or (S2). The sample versions of
\mathcal{L}
and
b
are defined by

\hat{\mathcal{L}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}:f\mapsto\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{z}}k(\cdot,z_{i})f(z_{i})

(6)

and

\hat{b}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}_{2}k(\cdot,v_{i}).

(7)

The GRS estimator of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is defined by

\hat{f}_{\lambda}=g_{\lambda}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})\hat{b}.

(8)

It can also be written in the following way

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=f_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{z}}\alpha_{i}k\left(\cdot,z_{i}\right)+\beta n\hat{b}

(9)

where
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
depend on the chosen regulariser.
Before introducing the Tikhonov and Landweber regularisers, we observe that Assumption (A3) implies

\left\|f-f_{*}\right\|^{2}_{Z}=\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{Z}-2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}f(V)]+\left\|f_{*}\right\|^{2}_{Z}.

(10)

Therefore, minimising
\left\|f-f_{*}\right\|_{Z}^{2}
is equivalent to minimising
D(f)=\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{Z}-2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}f(V)]
which can be estimated by

\hat{D}(f)=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{z}}f(z_{i})^{2}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}f(v_{i}).

(11)

2.2.1
Tikhonov regularisation

The Tikhonov regulariser is given by
g_{\lambda}(\sigma)=1/(\sigma+\lambda)
. In this case, we have
B=D=\gamma=1
,
\gamma_{\nu}=1
for
\nu\in[0,1]
and the qualification is equal to 1 (see e.g.
(
5
)
). Given an operator
A
, we have
g_{\lambda}(A)=(A+\lambda I)^{-1}
where
I
is the identity operator. One can show that
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
is the solution of
\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\hat{D}(f)+\lambda\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

(see e.g. Proposition 3 in
(
7
)
). Using the Representer Theorem (see e.g. Theorem A.2 in
(
43
)
), we obtain that
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
is given by Equation (
9
) with
f_{0}=0
,
\beta={2}/{(n\lambda)}
and

\alpha=-\frac{4}{\lambda}\left(2K+n_{z}\lambda I_{n_{z}}\right)^{-1}\hat{B}

(12)

where
K=\left[k(z_{i},z_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n_{z}}
,
I_{m}
is the identity matrix of dimension
m\times m
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\hat{B}=\left(\hat{b}\left(z_{i}\right)\right)_{i=1}^{n_{z}}

(see Appendix
B
for a proof).

2.2.2
Landweber iteration

The Landweber regularisator is given by

g_{t}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left\{1-\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\delta_{j}\sigma)\right\}

where
t=\lfloor 1/\lambda\rfloor
and
(\delta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,1/\kappa^{2}]
. In this case, we have
B=1/\kappa^{2}
,
D=\gamma=1
,
\gamma_{\nu}=1
for
\nu\in[0,1]
and
\gamma_{\nu}=\nu^{\nu}
for
\nu>1
(see
(
45
)
). Hence, the qualification is infinite. To recall, the Fréchet gradient of
F(f)=\left\|f-f_{*}\right\|^{2}_{Z}
on
\mathcal{H}
is
\nabla F(f)=2(\mathcal{L}f-b)
.
Minimising
\hat{D}(f)
by gradient descent with the sequence of step size
(\delta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
leads after
t
steps to

\hat{f}_{t}=\left\{I-\hat{\mathcal{L}}g_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})\right\}f_{0}+g_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})\hat{b}

where
I
is the identity operator (see Proposition 4.2 in
(
45
)
). Thus,
\hat{f}_{t}
is of the form (
8
) only if we take
f_{0}=0
. However, if
f_{0}=\mathcal{L}^{\nu}u
for some
\nu>0
and
u\in L^{2}(Z)
, we have

\left\|\left(I-\hat{\mathcal{L}}g_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})\right)f_{0}\right\|_{Z}\leq\left\|u\right\|_{Z}\gamma_{\nu}t^{-\nu}

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
since the qualification of
g_{t}
is infinite. We can express
\hat{f}_{t}
in the form of Equation (
9
) by taking
f_{0}
as the initial function of the gradient descent, setting
\beta^{(t)}=-2/n\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\delta_{i}
and defining
\alpha
through the following recurrence relation
\alpha^{(0)}=0
,

\alpha^{(t+1)}=\alpha^{(t)}-\frac{2\delta_{t}}{n_{z}}\left[F_{0}+K\alpha^{(t)}+n\hat{B}\beta^{(t)}\right]

where
F_{0}=(f_{0}(z_{i}))_{i=1}^{n_{z}}
.

The Landweber method has a numerical advantage compared with the Tikhonov one.
In both cases, the estimator must be computed for each value of the regularisation parameter, namely
\lambda
or
t
. The Tikhonov method requires inverting
2K+n_{z}\lambda I_{n_{z}}
for each value of
\lambda
. In contrast, the Landweber method only requires computing
K\alpha^{(t)}
for each value of
t
. Therefore, the Landweber method replaces matrix inversions by matrix multiplications, which reduces the computational burden.

2.2.3
Exact line search

A key issue in the Landweber method concerns the choice of the step sizes
(\delta_{i})
, also referred to as learning rates, in the gradient descent procedure. The Landweber method defines a valid regulariser if
\delta_{i}\leq 1/\kappa^{2}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
(see
(
45
)
). While excessively large step sizes may compromise convergence, overly small step sizes require a larger number of iterations and increase the computational cost.
While
\delta_{i}=1/\kappa^{2}
is a natural default choice, it may result in unnecessarily conservative step sizes. Exact line search offers a way to adaptively select step sizes and potentially achieve a better balance. Because the objective function
F
is quadratic, the optimal step size along the descent direction
-\nabla F(f)
—that is, the solution to
\min_{\delta>0}F(f-\delta\nabla F(f))
— has an explicit expression

\delta^{(1)}=\frac{\left\|\mathcal{L}f-b\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}}{2\left\|\mathcal{L}f-b\right\|^{2}_{Z}},

which can be estimated. Alternatively, one may consider minimizing the discrepancy
\mathbb{D}_{k}(f-\delta\nabla F(f),f_{*})^{2}
with respect to
\delta
. The corresponding optimal step size is

\delta^{(2)}=\frac{\left\|\mathcal{L}f-b\right\|^{2}_{Z}}{2\left\|\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}f-b)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}}=\frac{\left\|\mathcal{L}f-b\right\|^{2}_{Z}}{2\left\langle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}f-b),\mathcal{L}f-b\right\rangle_{Z}},

(13)

where the second equality follows from Equation (
5
).
One can show that

\delta^{(1)}\geq\delta^{(2)}\geq\frac{1}{2\|\mathcal{L}\|}

(14)

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
(see Appendix
B
).
However,
\delta^{(2)}
admits no upper bound, and therefore neither
\delta^{(1)}
nor
\delta^{(2)}
satisfies the condition
\delta_{i}\leq 1/\kappa^{2}
. As a result, we cannot guarantee that
g_{t}
defines a regulariser when step sizes are selected via exact line search, and the asymptotic results of Section
2.3
no longer apply in this setting. Showing that
g_{t}
is still a regulariser when exact line search is employed is beyond the scope of the present paper and is deferred to future research.

2.3
Asymptotic properties

We are interested in bounds of the form

\mathrm{P}\left[\left\|\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{L^{2}(Z)}<\epsilon(n)\log(1/\eta)\right]\geq 1-\eta

for some positive decreasing function
\epsilon
and all
\eta\in(0,1)
. To obtain such a result, we need to impose regularity assumptions on
f_{\mathcal{H}}
, the solution of (
2
). We call
\phi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
an index function if
\phi
is continuous, strictly increasing, and satisfies
\phi(0)=0
. We say that
\phi
is operator monotone if for all self-adjoint operators
U
and
V
on
\mathcal{H}
with spectra in
[0,T]
such that
U\leq V
it holds that
\phi(U)\leq\phi(V)
, where
U\leq V
means that
\left\langle(U-V)h,h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 0
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. In
(
31
)
, it is proved that any
f\in\mathcal{H}
can be written as
f=\phi(\mathcal{L})u
for some index function
\phi
and some
u\in\mathcal{H}
. A common practice in the literature is to assume that there exist
u\in\mathcal{H}
and an index function
\phi
, subject to additional assumptions, such that
f_{\mathcal{H}}=\phi(\mathcal{L})u
, which is referred to as a source condition (see e.g. Equation (11) in
(
5
)
). One option frequently used is to require that
\phi
can be written as the product of an operator monotone function and a Lipschitz continuous function. To make the discussion simpler, we will enforce the stronger but more readable hypothesis that
\phi(t)=t^{r}
for some
r>0
, which is called a Hölder source condition. In other words, we assume that

(A5)

there exist
r>0
and
u\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}^{r}u
.

Before stating the main theorem, we give two technical results (see Appendix
B
for the proofs).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let Assumptions (A2) and (A4) hold,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta\in(0,1)
. Then,
with probability at least
1-\eta
, it holds

\left\|\hat{b}-b\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{6C}{\sqrt{n}}\log\frac{2}{\eta}.

where
C
is given in Assumption (A4),
b
in Equation (
4
) and
\hat{b}
in Equation (
7
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let Assumption (A2) hold,
n_{z},n,n_{u}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta\in(0,1)
. Then, with probability at least
1-\eta
, it holds under (S1)

\left\|\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{L}\right\|\leq\frac{6\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{n_{z}}}\log\frac{2}{\eta}

and it holds under (S2)

\left\|\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{L}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{6\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{6\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{n_{u}}}\right)\log\frac{4}{\eta}

where
\kappa^{2}
is given in Assumption (A2),
\mathcal{L}
in Equation (
3
) and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
in Equation (
6
).

We now state the asymptotic properties of the GRS estimator, which are obtained by a straightforward adaptation of Theorem 10 in
(
5
)
(see Appendix
B
for a proof).

Theorem 2.4
.

Consider either Setting (S1) with
n_{z}=m
or (S2) with
n_{u}=m
. Let Assumptions (A1) to (A5) hold for some
r>0
and
u\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
\bar{r}
be the qualification of the regulariser
g_{\lambda}
and
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
be defined as in (
8
). Let
\eta\in(0,1)
, choose
\lambda=(n^{-1/2}+m^{-1/2})^{1/(r+1)}
and assume that

\lambda^{-r}>6\kappa^{2}\log(4/\eta)
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\eta
, if
r\in[0,\bar{r}]
it holds

\left\|\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)^{\frac{r}{r+1}}\log\left(\frac{4}{\eta}\right)

and if
r\in[0,\bar{r}-1/2]
it holds

\left\|\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{Z}\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)^{\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}}\log\left(\frac{4}{\eta}\right)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants that do not depend on
n
,
m
and
\eta
.

Remark 2.5
.

Setting
m=n
in Theorem
2.4
yields convergence rates of order
n^{-{r}/{(2r+2)}}
in
\mathcal{H}
and
n^{-{(2r+1)}/{(4r+4)}}
in
L^{2}(Z)
for
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
. These rates can be improved if we add assumptions on the capacity of the hypothesis space, usually measured by the effective dimension

\mathcal{N}(\lambda):=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathcal{L}+\lambda I)^{-1}\mathcal{L}\right),\quad\lambda>0.

For example, under the assumption that
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)\leq c\lambda^{-\beta}
for some
\beta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
, Corollary 5.1 in
(
30
)
states that

\left\|\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{1}n^{-\frac{r}{2r+1+\beta}}\left(\log\frac{6}{\eta}\right)^{2}

and

\left\|\hat{f}_{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{Z}\leq C_{2}n^{-\frac{2r+1}{4r+2+2\beta}}\left(\log\frac{6}{\eta}\right)^{3}

with
\lambda=n^{-1/(2r+1+\beta)}
.

3
Conditional density estimation

Now we apply the general regularisation scheme (GRS) to conditional density estimation (CDE). Let
X
be a predictive vector with values in a closed set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y
be an univariate outcome variable. We consider an univariate response
Y
to simplify discussion, but the generalisation to multivariate responses is straightforward. We denote by
q_{Y|X}
the conditional density of
Y
given
X
. We want to estimate
q_{Y|X=x}(u)
for all
(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}
is a closed set. We choose a random variable
U\sim q_{U}
, independent of
X
and
Y
, such that
q_{U}
is bounded and has full support
\mathcal{U}
. In the notation of Section
2
, we set
V=(X,Y)\sim q_{(X,Y)}
,
Z=(X,U)\sim q_{X}q_{U}
and
f_{*}(x,u)=q_{Y|X=x}(u)
for
(x,u)\in\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
. Assumption (A3) holds because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(X,U\right)f_{*}(X,U)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(X,Y\right)q_{U}(Y)\right]

(15)

where we used the fact that
q_{(X,Y)}=q_{Y|X}q_{X}
.
We can thus choose
\mathcal{T}f(x,y)=f(x,y)q_{U}(y)
and Assumption (A4) holds with
C=\kappa^{2}\left\|q_{U}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
.

Let
((x_{i},y_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
and
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{n_{u}}
be i.i.d. samples of
(X,Y)
and
U
respectively. We define
z_{i}
as in Equation (
1
) for
i=1,\ldots,n_{z}
with
n_{z}=n_{u}n
,
which places us in Setting (S2). Alternatively, the problem could be formulated under Setting (S1) by taking
z_{i}=(x_{i},u_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
but this would lead to a different estimator. We adopt Setting (S2) because, as explained in Appendix
A.1
,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
has smaller variance under this setting.
We recall that our GRS-based estimator is defined in Equation (
9
). As explained in Section
2.2.2
, the Landweber method has some numerical advantages compared to the Tikhonov one, which are even more acute in the case of CDE if we assume that the kernel is of the form

k((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=k_{X}(x,x^{\prime})k_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})

where
k_{X}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
k_{Y}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Indeed, under this assumption, the matrix product with
K
can be written as

K\left(f(z_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{n_{z}}=K_{X}\left[f(x_{i},u_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n,n_{u}}K_{U}

where
K_{X}=[k_{X}(x_{i},x_{l})]_{i,l=1}^{n}
and
K_{U}=[k_{Y}(u_{j},u_{l})]_{j,l=1}^{n_{u}}
. Hence, instead of multiplying by
K
(which requires
n^{2}n_{u}^{2}
multiplications and additions), we can multiply by
K_{X}
and
K_{U}
(which requires only
(n+n_{u})nn_{u}
). This simplification can also be reached with the Tikhonov method by observing that, since
X
and
U
are independent,
\mathcal{L}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{X}(x,X)\mathbb{E}[k_{Y}(y,U)f(X,U)|X]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{X}\mathcal{L}_{U}f(x,y)

with
\mathcal{L}_{X}g(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{X}(x,X)g(X)\right]
and
\mathcal{L}_{U}g(y)=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{Y}(y,U)g(U)\right]
. This allows to invert
\mathcal{L}_{X}
and
\mathcal{L}_{U}
separately but it introduces the drawback that we have to select two regularisation parameters, one for the inversion of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
and one for the inversion of
\mathcal{L}_{U}
. For this reason and those mentioned in Section
2.2.2
, we will implement the Landweber regularisation in our numerical experiments.

3.1
Kernel estimators

We will compare our estimator, that we call GRS, with three other kernel CDE which we briefly describe. Recall that GRS is based
on the following representation of the conditional density

q_{Y|X=x}(y)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{U}\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}b(x,y)

where

\displaystyle b(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{X}(x,X)k_{Y}(y,Y)q_{U}(Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{X}(x,X)k_{Y}(y,U)p_{Y|X}(U)\right]

is the kernel mean embedding of
(X,Y)
. The kernel conditional density operator estimator (CDO) of
(
40
)
uses another representation of the conditional density, namely

q_{Y|X=x}(y)=\mathcal{L}_{U}^{-1}\left[\mathcal{L}_{XY}\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}k_{X}(\cdot,x)\right](y)

where
\mathcal{L}_{XY}f(y)=\mathbb{E}[k_{Y}(y,Y)f(X)]
and in particular
b=\mathcal{L}_{XY}k_{X}
. It is very similar to GRS, differing only by the permutation of
\mathcal{L}_{XY}
and
\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}
. CDO can be written as

\hat{f}_{\mathrm{CDO}}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\tilde{w}_{j}(x)\ k_{Y}\left(y,u_{j}\right).

where
\tilde{w}(x)=1/n_{u}^{2}(K_{U}+\lambda_{2}I_{n_{u}})^{-2}K_{U,Y}w(x)
,
w(x)=(K_{X}+n\lambda_{1}I_{n})^{-1}K_{x,X}
,
K_{x,X}=(k(x,x_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
and
K_{U,Y}=[k_{Y}(u_{j},y_{i})]_{j,i=1}^{n_{u},n}
.
No convergence rate is provided for CDO in
(
40
)
.

The kernel mean density estimator (KMD) of
(
42
)
relies on the following representation of the conditional density

q_{Y|X=x}(y)=\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\frac{1}{q_{U}(y)}\mathcal{L}_{XY}\left[\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}k_{X}(\cdot,x)\right](y)

where
h_{Y}>0
is the bandwidth parameter of
k_{Y}
and satisfies
\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}k_{Y}(u,y)=\delta_{y}(u)
. KMD can be written as

\hat{f}_{\mathrm{KMD}}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)\ k_{Y}\left(y,y_{i}\right).

Consistency of KMD in the supremum norm is established in Lemma 2.2 of
(
42
)
but this result does not provide any convergence rate.
Since

\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\mathcal{L}_{U}f(y)=\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\mathbb{E}[k_{Y}(y,U)f(U)]=f(y)q_{U}(y),

we have

\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\mathcal{L}_{U}^{-1}f(y)=f(y)/q_{U}(y)
for all
f\in L^{2}(U)
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{U}\left[\mathcal{L}_{XY}\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}k_{X}(\cdot,x)\right](y)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{h_{Y}\to 0}\frac{1}{q_{U}(y)}\mathcal{L}_{XY}\left[\mathcal{L}_{X}^{-1}k_{X}(\cdot,x)\right](y)

which shows that KMD can be viewed as a limiting case of CDO as
h_{Y}\to 0
.

Finally, the Nadaraya-Watson estimator (NW) is obtained by replacing
q_{(X,Y)}
and
q_{X}
with their respective kernel density estimators in the identity
q_{Y|X}=q_{(X,Y)}/q_{X}
. This yields

\hat{f}_{\mathrm{NW}}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{k_{X}\left(x,x_{i}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{X}\left(x,x_{j}\right)}k_{Y}\left(y,y_{i}\right).

The convergence rate of NW with respect to the integrated mean square error (see Equation (
16
) below) is of order
n^{-2/3}
when
d=1
(see
(
23
)
).

Remark that only
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{NW}}(x,\cdot)
is guaranteed to be nonnegative and to integrate to one. The other three estimators can be normalised by applying the ReLU function and dividing by the integral with respect to
y
.

3.2
Numerical experiments

We now conduct some numerical experiments on synthetic data. We will compare the performances of GRS, NW and KDE. We do not take CDO into account since it is very similar to GRS and KMD and requires two regularisation parameters. We implement GRS with Landweber regularisation and the step sizes
\delta_{t}=1/\kappa^{2}
and
\delta_{t}=\delta^{(2)}
as described in Equation (
13
). We use for each estimator the Gaussian kernels

k_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}H^{-1}(x-x^{\prime})\right)

and

k_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{h_{Y}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|^{2}}{2h_{Y}^{2}}\right)

where
H=\mathrm{Diag}(h_{1}^{2},\ldots,h_{d}^{2})
,
h_{X}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
is the vector of input bandwidths and
h_{Y}
is the output bandwidth. Taking inspiration from the median heuristic (see
(
18
)
), we select
h_{X}
from the set
\left\{M_{X}p_{X}^{l}:l=-L_{X},\ldots,L_{X}\right\}
where
p_{X}>1
and
L_{X}\in\mathbb{N}
are parameters and
M_{X}=(M_{l})_{l=1}^{d}
with

M_{l}=\sqrt{\mathrm{med}(|x_{i,l}-x_{j,l}|^{2},1\leq i<j\leq n)/2}

for
l=1,\ldots,d
where
x_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,d})
.
The output bandwidth
h_{Y}
is selected from the set
\left\{M_{Y}p_{Y}^{l}:l=-L_{Y},\ldots,L_{Y}\right\}
where
p_{Y}>1
and
L_{Y}\in\mathbb{N}
are parameters and

M_{Y}=\sqrt{\mathrm{med}(|y_{i}-y_{j}|^{2},1\leq i<j\leq n)/2}.

The regularisation parameter
\lambda
is chosen in the set
\left\{p_{\lambda}^{-l}:l=0,1,\ldots,L_{\lambda}\right\}
where
p_{\lambda}>1
and
L_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}
are parameters. We take
q_{U}
as the uniform density on
\mathcal{U}
and we choose
\mathcal{U}
on a case-by-case basis. For the GRS estimator, we start the gradient descent from
f_{0}=q_{U}
and we do at most
T_{1}=40
iterations with
\delta_{t}=1/\kappa^{2}
and
T_{2}=10
iterations with
\delta_{t}=\delta^{(2)}
.
For each estimator, we proceed in the following way:

•

We construct an estimator for each value of
(h_{X},h_{Y},t,\lambda)
with a training sample of
(X,Y)
of size
n_{\mathrm{train}}
.

•

We select the optimal value of
(h_{X},h_{Y},t,\lambda)
as the minimiser of
\hat{D}(\hat{f})
(see Equation (
11
)) evaluated with a validation sample of
(X,Y)
of size
n_{\mathrm{val}}
. We recall that
\hat{D}(\hat{f})
is, up to an additive constant, an estimator of
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{f}(X,U)-q_{Y|X}(U)|^{2}]
.

•

We compute the integrated mean square error

\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{f})=\frac{1}{nn_{u}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\left|\hat{f}(x_{i},u_{j})-q_{Y|X=x_{i}}(u_{j})\right|^{2}

(16)

for the chosen value of
(h_{X},h_{Y},t,\lambda)
with a test sample of
(X,Y)
of size
n=n_{\mathrm{test}}
.

We repeat the experiment
n_{\mathrm{MC}}
times and we report in our tables the mean and standard deviation of MSE
(\hat{f})
over the
n_{\mathrm{MC}}
repetitions.

Conclusion

We propose an abstract framework that encompasses several existing settings and allows the systematic application of the general regularisation scheme. Building on this, we develop a new conditional density estimator that enjoys well-established convergence rates, in contrast to kernel-based alternatives such as CDO or KMD for which such guarantees are not available. We contend that Landweber iteration is computationally more tractable than Tikhonov regularisation, especially with separable kernels, and that exact line search offers an efficient strategy for selecting the learning rate. Through numerical experiments, we demonstrate that our estimator performs comparably to NW in settings where the latter is particularly well suited—such as Gaussian mixtures—and that it can surpass NW in specific scenarios, notably for time series data. Finally, a natural direction for future research is to establish that the asymptotic properties of GRS still hold when exact line search is employed.

A.1
Settings for CDE

In Section

, to estimate
\mathcal{L}f(z)=\mathbb{E}[k(z,Z)f(Z)]

. Given a function

Proof that
\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}=\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(Z)\to L^{2}(Z)}\leq\kappa^{2}
.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is an integral operator with bounded symmetric kernel
k
, it is compact and self-adjoint on
L^{2}(Z)
. Hence, the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators tells us that the eigenvectors
\left(\tilde{e}_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{L}
form an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(Z)
. Moreover, the scalar product on
\mathcal{H}
of two elements
f=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}f_{i}\tilde{e}_{i}
and
g=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}g_{i}\tilde{e}_{i}
is given by

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f_{i}g_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}

where
\left(\sigma_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
(see e.g.
(
29
)
). Hence, an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}
is given by
\left(e_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
e_{i}=\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}\tilde{e}_{i}
.
We can conclude that
\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(Z)\to L^{2}(Z)}=\sigma_{1}=\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}
where
\sigma_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(Z)\to L^{2}(Z)}
denotes the operator norm on
L^{2}(Z)
.
Finally, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Assumption (A2), one obtains that
\left\|\mathcal{L}\right\|_{L^{2}(Z)\to L^{2}(Z)}\leq\kappa^{2}
.
∎
