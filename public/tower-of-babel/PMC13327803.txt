Efficacy and safety of single-fraction radiosurgery in vestibular schwannoma: a 10-year experience
Authors: Kerem Tuna Tas, Maike Fischer, Philipp Lishewski, Fatima Frosan Sheikhzadeh, Edgar Smalec, Katrin Reimann, Klemens Zink, Hilke Vorwerk, Sebastian Adeberg, Ahmed Gawish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate long-term tumor control, safety, and prognostic factors following single-fraction stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) for vestibular schwannoma (VS).Materials and MethodsIn this retrospective single-institution study, 68 patients with VS were treated with photon-based single-fraction SRS (mean dose, 13.1 Gy; range, 12 to 15 Gy). Treatment planning was based on computed tomography–magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) fusion with organ-at-risk sparing and a planning target volume margin of 0–2 mm. The primary endpoint was local control (LC). Secondary endpoints included toxicity and predictors of treatment failure.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 68 months, LC was maintained in 95.6% of patients. Actuarial LC rates at 5 and 10 years were both 98.5%. MRI showed tumor shrinkage in 41.2% and stability in 57.4% of cases. Treatment was well tolerated. Acute adverse events were infrequent and mild. Localized alopecia occurred acutely in one patient (1.5%) and did not persist into late follow-up. Vertigo and tinnitus were analyzed as disease-related symptoms rather than treatment-related toxicities. Severe toxicity (grade ≥ 3) was rare (<3%). Cox regression revealed no statistically significant predictors of local failure, though trends suggested higher risk after prior surgery and with doses ≥14 Gy.ConclusionSingle-fraction SRS provides durable long-term LC of VS with a favorable toxicity profile, supporting its role as a primary treatment modality. Optimal outcomes appear to be associated with lower prescription doses (<14 Gy) and treatment of de novo tumors.Keywords: Vestibular schwannoma, Acoustic neuroma, Stereotactic radiosurgery, Radiation injuries, Treatment outcome

Introduction

Vestibular schwannoma (VS), also termed acoustic neuroma, is a benign tumor arising from Schwann cells of the vestibulocochlear nerve. Despite its non-malignant histology, the tumor’s location within the cerebellopontine angle can lead to considerable morbidity, including hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, and brainstem compression [1]. Management strategies for VS have evolved substantially over recent decades and currently encompass active surveillance, microsurgical resection, and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS). The choice of approach depends on tumor size, growth kinetics, and patient-specific factors such as age, comorbidities, and functional hearing status [2].

SRS has emerged as a standard treatment for small- to medium-sized VS, offering high local tumor control rates while minimizing invasiveness and preserving neurological function [3]. Long-term series consistently report tumor control rates exceeding 90% at 10 years, establishing SRS as a first-line alternative to microsurgery in appropriately selected patients [4]. Importantly, SRS is also associated with lower rates of cranial nerve morbidity and faster recovery compared with surgical resection [5,6].

Nevertheless, several clinically relevant questions remain. First, the optimal prescription dose continues to be debated. Early protocols using 16–20 Gy achieved high tumor control but carried substantial risks of hearing loss and cranial neuropathies [2,3,5,7]. Contemporary practice has shifted toward lower doses of 12–13 Gy to improve safety; however, concerns persist regarding long-term efficacy in larger or previously resected tumors [6,8]. Second, several potential prognostic factors—such as tumor size, prior surgery, and cochlear dose—have been proposed, although evidence regarding their independent clinical impact remains limited and sometimes inconsistent across studies [9]. Finally, late toxicity—including hearing decline, facial nerve palsy, and trigeminal neuropathy— continues to affect quality of life, underscoring the importance of careful long-term outcome evaluation [10,11].

Against this background, we report our single-institution experience with photon-based single-fraction SRS for VS with long-term follow-up. Our objectives were threefold: (1) to evaluate long-term local and out-of-field tumor control, (2) to characterize acute and late toxicity profiles, and (3) to explore potential clinical and dosimetric factors associated with treatment outcomes. By providing long-term real-world data from a contemporary linear accelerator (LINAC)–based SRS cohort, this study aims to complement existing literature and further characterize the safety and effectiveness of single-fraction SRS in patients with VS.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design and patient population

This retrospective, single-center study included 68 consecutive patients with VS who underwent SRS at the Department of Radiotherapy and Radiation Oncology of a tertiary referral center in Germany between 2005 and 2023. Clinical, radiological, and dosimetric data were obtained from electronic medical records and the radiotherapy (RT) planning database.

Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) radiologically confirmed sporadic VS, (2) treatment with single-fraction photon-based SRS, and (3) a minimum radiological follow-up of ≥12 months. Patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) were excluded. Baseline characteristics included age, sex, tumor laterality, prior treatment, and indication for SRS. SRS was delivered as primary treatment in 55 patients (80.9%), whereas 13 patients (19.1%) received SRS for residual disease following partial microsurgical resection.

2. Treatment planning and delivery

Patients were immobilized using a stereotactic thermoplastic mask. Thin-slice (1–2 mm) planning computed tomography scans were acquired and rigidly fused with contrast-enhanced T1- and T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The gross tumor volume was defined as the contrast-enhancing lesion on MRI. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by applying a margin of 0–2 mm to account for setup uncertainties. The mean PTV volume was 1.29 cm³ (range, 0.05 to 5.00 cm³).

The brainstem and ipsilateral cochlea were routinely contoured as organs at risk (OARs) in accordance with institutional and international dose-constraint guidelines. Particular attention was paid to cochlear dose exposure due to its clinical relevance for hearing preservation.

Treatment planning was performed using commercial planning systems (Eclipse, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA; Pinnacle, Philips Medical Systems, Fitchburg, WI, USA). Intensity-modulated stereotactic treatment plans were optimized to achieve at least 95% PTV coverage of the prescribed dose while respecting OAR dose constraints. The mean prescribed dose was 13.14 Gy (range, 12 to 15 Gy). Most patients (75%) received a prescription dose of <14 Gy, while 25% received ≥14 Gy. For exploratory analyses, patients were stratified according to prescription dose (<14 Gy vs. ≥14 Gy), reflecting institutional treatment practice and commonly used dose ranges reported in the literature for single-fraction SRS in vestibular schwannoma. The prescription dose was typically delivered to the 80%–90% isodose line in accordance with institutional SRS planning protocols. SRS was delivered using either a Varian TrueBeam linear accelerator (47.1%) or a conventional linear accelerator system (52.9%), both equipped with high-definition multileaf collimators and stereotactic image guidance. For exploratory purposes, treatment delivery platforms were categorized as TrueBeam versus conventional LINAC to evaluate whether different stereotactic delivery systems influenced treatment outcomes.

3. Dosimetric analysis

Dosimetric parameters were extracted from the RT planning system and included PTV volume as well as OAR volumes and dose metrics. For the brainstem and ipsilateral cochlea, mean, maximum, and minimum doses were recorded. Institutional dose constraints aimed to limit the maximum brainstem dose to ≤12 Gy and to minimize cochlear dose exposure whenever feasible, with particular attention to the mean cochlear dose during treatment planning.

Due to incomplete contour availability, brainstem volume data were available for 56 patients, while brainstem and cochlear dose parameters were available for 54 patients.

4. Follow-up and outcome measures

Patients underwent clinical and radiological follow-up starting 3–6 months after SRS and subsequently at intervals of 6–12 months. Follow-up evaluations included neurological examination, audiometric testing when applicable, and MRI imaging. Local control (LC) was defined as the absence of in-field progression (IFP), which was characterized by an increase in the maximum tumor diameter of ≥20% or unequivocal radiological progression. To differentiate true tumor progression from pseudoprogression, radiological progression was defined as sustained tumor enlargement on consecutive MRI examinations and/or the requirement for salvage treatment. Radiological response was categorized as stable disease or tumor shrinkage.

Treatment-related toxicities were documented and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Acute toxicities were defined as events occurring within 6 weeks after treatment, whereas late toxicities were defined as those occurring thereafter. Second primary tumors detected during follow-up were documented separately and were not considered VS progression events.

5. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient characteristics, treatment parameters, and outcomes. Kaplan-Meier estimates were generated for LC and overall survival (OS). Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate potential predictors of LC, including sex, tumor laterality, prior surgery, prescribed dose (<14 Gy vs. ≥14 Gy), and treatment platform. Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Given the limited number of local failure events, these analyses were considered exploratory. Dosimetric variables were not included in the Cox regression analyses due to incomplete data availability. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

2. Tumor control and survival outcomes

After a median radiological follow-up of 68 months (range, 12 to 184), excellent tumor control was observed. LC, defined as the absence of IFP, was achieved in 65 patients (95.6%). Only three patients (4.4%) developed IFP during follow-up, and subsequently required salvage treatment. One patient developed a meningioma outside the irradiated field during follow-up, which was considered a second primary tumor rather than VS progression.

The actuarial LC rates were 100% at 1 year and 98.5% at both 5 and 10 years (Fig. 2). Radiological assessment on follow-up MRI demonstrated stable disease in 39 patients (57.4%) and tumor shrinkage in 28 patients (41.2%). A summary of oncological outcomes is provided in Table 3. The mean IFP-free survival was 62.1 months. The mean OS was 75.7 months. No disease-related deaths were observed at the last follow-up.

3. Prognostic factors

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis did not identify any statistically significant predictors of local failure. Given the very small number of local failure events (n = 3), the results of these analyses should be interpreted cautiously.

Patients with a history of prior partial microsurgical resection showed an increased estimated hazard for recurrence (HR, 598.06), although this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.493). This extremely large HR likely reflects statistical instability related to the low number of events rather than a precise estimate of effect size.

Similarly, a prescribed dose of ≥14 Gy showed a numerically higher estimated risk of local failure compared with doses <14 Gy (HR, 3.83; p = 0.282).

No associations were identified between local failure and patient sex, tumor laterality, or treatment platform. Detailed results of the univariate analysis are summarized in Table 4.

4. Treatment-related toxicity

SRS was generally well tolerated. Acute toxicities occurring within 6 weeks after treatment were predominantly mild and included headache (8.8%), nausea (4.4%), fatigue (4.4%), and localized alopecia in one patient (1.5%) (Table 5).

Vertigo, tinnitus, and ataxia were considered disease-related symptoms of VS rather than treatment-related toxicities and were therefore analyzed separately.

Late toxicities, defined as those occurring beyond 6 weeks post-SRS, were infrequent and remained largely low-grade. No cases of persistent alopecia were observed during late follow-up. Facial nerve paresis was observed in nine patients (13.2%) at late follow-up.

Importantly, four patients (5.9%) presented with facial nerve paresis prior to SRS. Accordingly, only new-onset facial nerve paresis developing after treatment was considered treatment-related toxicity. Based on this definition, five patients (7.4%) developed new facial nerve paresis attributable to SRS.

None of these cases were associated with tumor progression at the time of symptom onset. The median time to onset of facial nerve paresis after treatment was approximately 8 months (range, 3 to 18). During follow-up, partial or complete clinical improvement was observed in several patients, indicating that these deficits were often transient.

No high-grade (CTCAE ≥ 3) facial nerve toxicity was observed. Severe adverse events (CTCAE grade ≥ 3) were rare, occurring in fewer than 3% of patients. One patient developed trigeminal neuralgia during follow-up (1.5%). The symptoms improved with conservative medical management during subsequent follow-up.

5. Evolution of disease-related symptoms after SRS

Disease-related symptoms, including vertigo, tinnitus, ataxia, and hearing impairment, were evaluated separately from treatment-related toxicities. These symptoms primarily reflect the natural history of VS rather than radiation-induced effects.

Hearing impairment was defined based on clinical documentation and available audiometric reports; standardized classifications such as Gardner–Robertson were not consistently available in this retrospective cohort.

A comparison of symptoms before and after treatment is presented in Table 6. Overall, no clinically meaningful long-term deterioration in disease-related symptoms was observed. Discussion and Conclusion

VS, although histologically benign, often cause significant morbidity due to their location in the cerebellopontine angle and proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Our study of 68 patients treated with single-fraction SRS adds long-term real-world data supporting the established efficacy and safety of this treatment modality. We observed a local control rate of 95.6% at a mean follow-up of 62.1 months, which is in line with contemporary series reporting 90–98% long-term tumor control after SRS [3,6,7]. A detailed comparison of our outcomes with major contemporary studies is provided in Table 8 [3,12-15]. These outcomes support SRS as a first-line option for small- to medium-sized tumors, particularly when the preservation of neurological function and quality of life (QoL) is prioritized [1,8,9].

Hearing preservation data in the present study were not systematically assessed using standardized audiometric classifications such as Gardner-Robertson or American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery.

The mechanisms underlying VS control after SRS are thought to involve both direct tumor cell DNA damage and radiation-induced vascular changes. High-dose single-fraction irradiation induces DNA double-strand breaks that overwhelm repair mechanisms in the slowly proliferating Schwann cells [2]. In addition, radiation-induced endothelial apoptosis and subsequent hyalinization of intratumoral vessels further contribute to tumor control [5]. In our series, 41.2% of tumors demonstrated radiographic shrinkage, consistent with previously reported regression rates of 30%–50% [6,10,16]. Importantly, transient tumor expansion (pseudoprogression) was not uncommon and has been well characterized in prior studies, typically resolving within 2–3 years [10,16]. Thus, early post-treatment enlargement should not be misinterpreted as treatment failure.

The optimal radiation dose for VS remains an area of active discussion. Our institutional practice of prescribing 12–15 Gy (mean, 13.14 Gy) reflects the global shift from earlier high-dose protocols (16–20 Gy) toward lower-dose regimens to reduce cranial nerve toxicity [6,17,18].

In our exploratory analyses, doses ≥14 Gy showed a numerically higher estimated risk of local failure, although this finding did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution given the very small number of failure events in our cohort (n = 3).

Our findings align with Hasegawa et al. [3], who reported excellent tumor control with 12–13 Gy and lower complication rates compared to higher-dose treatments. Current consensus suggests that doses <12 Gy may compromise tumor control in larger tumors [19,20], while doses above 13–14 Gy may increase toxicity without significant gain in efficacy [6,21].

Hearing preservation remains a critical outcome metric in VS management. In our cohort, the mean cochlear dose (8.87 Gy) exceeded the 4–14 Gy range reported to correlate with better hearing preservation [4,8,9]. This likely reflects the inclusion of earlier treatment plans predating routine cochlear dose constraints. Contemporary practice emphasizes maintaining a mean cochlear dose ≤4–5 Gy when hearing preservation is a goal [11,12,19,22]. Emerging strategies such as cochlear-sparing planning and hypofractionated regimens (e.g., 25 Gy in 5 fractions) have demonstrated promising results in improving hearing outcomes without compromising local control [11,19,22,23].

However, standardized audiometric classifications such as Gardner-Robertson or American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery were not systematically available in this retrospective cohort, which limits formal evaluation of hearing preservation outcomes and direct comparison with published series.

The management of VS after prior subtotal resection presents unique challenges. In our exploratory analysis, prior subtotal resection was associated with a markedly elevated estimated HR for local failure (HR, 598.057). Given that only three local failures occurred in the entire cohort, this extremely large HR should be interpreted cautiously and likely reflects statistical instability rather than a precise estimate of effect size. Nevertheless, this observation is broadly consistent with prior reports suggesting that residual tumor after surgery may exhibit altered radiosensitivity [17] or represent more aggressive tumor biology [24]. Postoperative changes also complicate target delineation and dose delivery [25]. In such cases, the literature supports either definitive upfront SRS for residual disease or a surgical strategy aiming for gross total resection when feasible [17,26].

The toxicity profile in our series was acceptable and compares favorably with surgical series. Localized alopecia was rare and transient, occurring acutely in only one patient (1.5%) and resolving without persistence into late follow-up.

When pre-existing facial nerve deficits were excluded, new-onset facial nerve paresis attributable to SRS was observed in only five patients (7.4%), which remains substantially lower than the 15%–40% rates typically reported after microsurgery [8,9,26], and trigeminal neuropathy occurred in only 1.5%, comparable to modern SRS outcomes [3,6,27]. Our results emphasize the importance of careful dose planning to minimize radiation exposure to the brainstem and cranial nerves [18,28].

Most tumors in our series either remained stable (57.4%) or regressed (41.2%) on follow-up imaging. Transient enlargement, reported in up to 30% of cases [10,16], underscores the importance of cautious interpretation of post-SRS imaging. Our mean follow-up of ~68 months was sufficient to differentiate pseudoprogression from true recurrence.

The benign and localized nature of sporadic VS was further supported by the absence of true out-of-field tumor progression [21,29]. One patient developed a meningioma outside the irradiated field, which was considered a second primary tumor rather than VS progression; nevertheless, such findings warrant continued clinical vigilance, particularly in younger patients, to exclude underlying genetic predisposition such as NF2 [24].

In our cohort, outcomes were comparable between TrueBeam and conventional LINAC platforms, suggesting that adherence to dosimetric principles is more critical than platform selection [20,30,31]. Nevertheless, newer delivery systems with improved image guidance and shorter treatment times may further optimize therapeutic ratios [18]. Our mean PTV dimension of 1.29 cm³ reflects modern standards, though some centers advocate for sub-millimeter margins enabled by high-resolution MRI and advanced immobilization techniques [32,33].

Although formal QoL measures were not assessed in this study, the low rate of significant toxicity suggests that most patients maintain good functional outcomes after SRS. This is consistent with prospective QoL studies showing superior balance and facial function outcomes compared with microsurgery [1,9,12]. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating standardized QoL and audiometric outcomes are needed to confirm these findings [1,9].

Advances in imaging (diffusion tensor imaging, tractography, and functional MRI) may further improve target delineation and cranial nerve preservation [33]. Radiogenomic profiling holds potential for individualized dose prescription and outcome prediction [34]. Additionally, hypofractionated regimens (3–5 fractions) may offer an improved therapeutic window for larger tumors or lesions abutting critical structures [11,20,23,35].

Our institutional experience provides additional long-term real-world evidence supporting single-fraction SRS as a safe and effective treatment for vestibular schwannoma, achieving durable tumor control with low treatment-related morbidity. Prognostic analyses in this cohort were exploratory and limited by the very small number of failure events. Continued technological advances, improved dose constraints, and prospective studies incorporating standardized functional and audiometric outcomes will be important to further refine patient selection and optimize treatment strategies.
