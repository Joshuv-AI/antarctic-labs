Leveraging CT-derived chronic imaging signatures for acute kidney injury evaluation
Authors: Kipyo Kim, Yoon Ho Choi, Hyun Gyu Lee, Byoungje Kim, Jin Hyuk Paek, Jung Ui Hong, Ro Woon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of baseline serum creatinine (SCr) remains an unmet clinical need in acute kidney injury (AKI) evaluation, as premorbid SCr values are often unavailable. This study aimed to develop and validate a baseline SCr prediction model using CT-derived imaging features to improve AKI diagnosis and staging. The study included patients with available baseline SCr who underwent abdominal CT at two tertiary hospitals. Kidney segmentation was performed using a fine-tuned Swin UNETR model, from which imaging features were extracted. We evaluated machine learning models incorporating imaging features for baseline SCr estimation and compared their estimates with those derived from the back-calculation method. Most selected imaging features were associated with the presence of preexisting chronic kidney disease. Across internal and external test sets, the tabular foundation models achieved the best performance (MAE, 0.154–0.158/0.168–0.174; RMSE, 0.253–0.261/0.220–0.231). AKI staging based on predicted baseline SCr showed substantially higher agreement with the ground truth than back-calculation (Cohen’s κ, 0.69/0.60 vs. 0.31/0.36), while markedly reducing over-staging (8.7%/8.5% vs. 45.2%/37.6%). Our externally validated, image-driven prediction model enabled accurate baseline SCr estimation and improved AKI classification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57869-5.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a major health burden, affecting more than 13 million individuals annually worldwide1. AKI is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and a greater risk of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD)2. AKI-related mortality rates are higher than those for major non-communicable diseases such as heart failure and breast cancer, reaching 50% in severe cases3.

AKI diagnosis and staging depend on baseline serum creatinine (SCr); therefore, inaccurate estimation of baseline SCr can lead to misclassification of AKI4. However, baseline SCr data are often missing. Conventional back-calculation methods assume a normal baseline glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Because AKI and CKD frequently coexist in practice, this assumption may overestimate AKI, highlighting the need for more reliable approaches5,6.

Detecting preexisting CKD during an AKI episode is challenging. Imaging-derived features may help guide clinical decision-making in this setting. Ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT) are routinely used in kidney disease workup, detecting not only tumors or stones but also CKD-related structural changes7,8. CKD progression involves changes in kidney volume and shape, which correlate with histopathological findings9–12. Previous studies have identified 3D kidney imaging features as potential predictors of CKD11,13. However, kidney imaging biomarkers, except in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, have not yet been translated into clinical use14,15. In clinical practice, potentially informative kidney imaging features are still rarely documented or quantified. Although CT images contain rich, reproducible 3D voxel data, CT-derived features of kidney disease remain largely unexplored.

Encouragingly, recent deep learning advances enable automated image segmentation and feature quantification, facilitating clinical application. Moreover, large-scale pretrained models have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance and extensive adaptability16. Such models offer an efficient and accurate way to incorporate image-derived features into clinical prediction. Therefore, we aimed to develop and validate a deep learning–based model that uses CT-derived imaging features to predict baseline SCr for AKI diagnosis and staging.

Methods

Patients

We enrolled patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent noncontrast abdominal CT scans at Inha University Hospital (INUH) between January 2015 and March 2019 and at Keimyung University Hospital (KMUH) between November 2020 and January 2022. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unavailable baseline SCr data, (2) poor CT image quality as assessed by the radiologists, (3) polycystic kidney disease, a solitary kidney, renal parenchyma distorted by hydronephrosis, large cysts, or tumors, and (4) a baseline SCr > 4 mg/dL or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2. Baseline SCr was defined as the lowest outpatient SCr measured under steady-state conditions (without evidence of acute illness) within 3 months before the CT scan. Only patients with at least two outpatient SCr measurements during this period were included. Steady state at the time of measurement was confirmed through independent review of medical records by two board-certified nephrologists. For comparison, baseline SCr was estimated by back-calculation using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation 17. AKI stages were defined using the KDIGO criteria8, and AKI was also classified into no AKI/CKD, AKI without CKD, AKI superimposed on CKD, and stable CKD. Demographic and laboratory data from within 24 h of the CT scan were obtained from the electronic health records of each hospital. CT data from INUH were randomly split into training (75%) and test (25%) sets. Data from KMUH were used as the external test set. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Inha University Hospital (IRB No. 2020–10-009) and Keimyung University Hospital (IRB No. 2022–01-016). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

CT image acquisition and preprocessing

All participants underwent abdominal CT scans as part of standard care. Since iodinated contrast agents are generally avoided in patients with CKD, only noncontrast CT images were used in this study. CT scans were performed on scanners manufactured by GE Healthcare (Optima CT660, LightSpeed VCT, and Revolution EVO) and Siemens (SOMATOM Definition Edge, Force, Drive, and Sensation 16), following each institution’s standard protocols. The detailed acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Segmentation and image preprocessing

Kidney segmentation was automatically performed using the Swin UNEt TRansformers (Swin UNETR) model implemented within MONAI, an open-source medical imaging platform developed by NVIDIA. Fine-tuning was performed on top of pretrained weights of the Swin UNETR18. CT cases excluded from the main analysis served as the dataset for fine-tuning. Ground truth masks were generated semiautomatically using AVIEW Research (Coreline Soft, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and then manually refined and finalized by the consensus of two board-certified radiologists. All the images and corresponding masks were resampled to isotropic voxels (1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm). The Dice similarity coefficient was used to evaluate the segmentation performance. Further details on the segmentation model are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Feature extraction

On the basis of previous literature and preliminary analyses, we extracted imaging features representing kidney geometry for subsequent analyses11,19. First, 3D morphometric features were obtained using the PyRadiomics package (version 3.1.0)20,21. After removing redundant features, the following features were selected: mesh volume, surface area, surface area-to-volume ratio (SVR), sphericity, maximum 3D diameter, maximum 2D diameter (column-wise and slice-wise), minor axis length, least axis length, flatness, and elongation. Additionally, we introduced several unexplored morphometric features of the kidney, including convex hull–derived features and the mean local thickness. The 3D local thickness was calculated at each point within the organ volume and then averaged22. The convex hulls were generated using the SciPy package, from which convexity and solidity were calculated23. All the extracted features were averaged between the right and left kidneys before analysis. The relationships among radiomic features were explored using a hierarchical clustering heatmap and a multidimensional scaling (MDS) plot. The list of features and their definitions are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Model development and validation

Prediction models were developed using selected imaging and clinical features to estimate baseline SCr levels and identify preexisting CKD. Predicted baseline SCr values from the proposed model were compared with back-calculated values. The presence of AKI was determined by comparing the predicted baseline SCr and SCr measured at the time of CT. Preexisting CKD was evaluated using eGFR thresholds of 45 and 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. We evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms, including ElasticNet, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, support vector machine, random forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. In addition, we implemented the tabular prior-data fitted network (TabPFN) model, a transformer-based foundation model, through three approaches: a pretrained model without further optimization, hyperparameter optimization, and post hoc ensemble (PHE) modeling16. Acute laboratory values may make the model depend on measurement timing. We therefore stratified clinical features into three sets by temporal stability. The first included demographic and comorbidity features (age, sex, BMI, diabetes, and hypertension). The second added relatively stable laboratory features, such as albumin and hemoglobin. The third added acutely changing laboratory features, such as serum creatinine and electrolytes. For each set, we tested whether adding imaging features significantly improved performance. All steps of the modeling pipeline were implemented in Python (version 3.9; Python Software Foundation). The hyperparameters of the conventional ML models were tuned using Optuna with fivefold cross-validation, and all models were evaluated on two independent test sets. (Supplementary Table S4). TabPFN modeling and inference workflows were conducted using the TabPFN (version 2.5) and TabPFN Extensions (version 0.3.0) packages. Feature importance was assessed using the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) package (version 0.50.0).

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics are summarized as medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. The regression model performance was evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and mean bias error (MBE). Metrics are reported with 95% confidence intervals and were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resampling based on the bias-corrected and accelerated method. Differences in performance metrics between models with and without imaging features were compared using the paired t-test. AKI stage prediction was evaluated using accuracy, Cohen’s κ, mean absolute stage error, and the proportion of overpredictions (predicted stage > observed stage) and underpredictions (predicted stage < observed stage).

CT-derived radiomic features of the kidneys

Fourteen CT-derived radiomic features were extracted and subsequently characterized. Correlation heatmap and MDS plot suggested three feature groupings, with one group comprising direct geometric measures (e.g., volume- and diameter-related features) and the other two consisting of derived descriptors such as sphericity, elongation, and convexity (Supplementary Fig. S2a and b). When compared across AKI and CKD statuses, most features differed primarily according to the presence of CKD (Supplementary Fig. S2c). These findings suggest that selected features capture chronic structural alterations of the kidney, supporting the rationale for their use for baseline SCr estimation.

Prediction model for baseline serum creatinine

For models using imaging and full clinical features, TabPFN consistently outperformed the conventional ML models across all metrics in both test sets (Table 2 and Fig. 2a). The TabPFN variants performed comparably, with the PHE model best in the external (KMUH) test set (MAE 0.168, RMSE 0.220, MAPE 0.163). Using imaging and demographic features only, performance declined modestly; nevertheless, the TabPFN models remained among the top-ranked externally (TabPFN-HPO: MAE 0.245, RMSE 0.318, MAPE 0.250; Supplementary Table S5). In the ablation study, adding imaging features consistently reduced prediction errors across all clinical feature sets and both test sets (TabPFN-PHE: Fig. 2b; all models: Supplementary Fig. S3). For the TabPFN-PHE model (using imaging and full clinical features), correlation coefficients between ground-truth and predicted baseline SCr were 0.839 (INUH) and 0.856 (KMUH), with Bland–Altman mean biases of 0.003 and 0.01 mg/dL, respectively (Fig. 2c). Corresponding results for the model using imaging and demographic features are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4. Reclassifying AKI based on prediction models

We compared AKI staging from the TabPFN-PHE model (imaging and full clinical features) with that from the back-calculation method in the test datasets (Fig. 3). The back-calculation method showed lower agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.31/0.36, INUH/KMUH; same order throughout) and substantial overestimation of AKI stage (45.2%/37.6%). In contrast, our model achieved higher agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.69/0.60) with markedly reduced overestimation (8.7%/8.5%). For AKI/CKD classification, the back-calculation method assumes normal baseline kidney function and therefore classifies all patients as not having CKD (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, the predicted baseline SCr–based classification achieved overall accuracies of 74.8% and 76.1% (INUH/KMUH) at a GFR threshold of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, remaining consistent at 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (77.4%/80.3%). For binary CKD classification, the TabPFN-PHE model outperformed the conventional ML models (ROC-AUC = 0.926/0.943 at the 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 cutoff). Detailed results, including those for the models using imaging and demographic features, are provided in Supplementary Figs. Model interpretation and explainability

In the SHAP summary plots, feature contributions to model output were visualized (Fig. 4c and d). For the model using imaging and full clinical features, solidity, maximum diameter (coronal view), and SVR were the most important imaging features, whereas serum creatinine (measured at CT scan), age, and sex were the key clinical contributors. For the model using imaging and demographic features, solidity, sex, age, maximum 3D diameter, diabetes mellitus, and SVR were the leading contributors (Supplementary Fig. S10c and d). Other clinical features and shape descriptors, such as elongation and flatness, showed relatively low SHAP values.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a baseline SCr prediction model utilizing CT-derived imaging features, thereby improving the accuracy of AKI diagnosis and staging. CT-derived 3D features effectively captured CKD-related characteristics in an explicit and quantifiable manner. By employing pretrained foundation models, we achieved clinically applicable performance.

Baseline SCr estimation relies on premorbid SCr data that are often unavailable in practice. Several imputation methods, such as the lowest SCr during hospitalization24, back-calculation from the eGFR25, and admission SCr 26, have been used. However, these methods may lead to the misclassification of AKI27. In particular, the commonly used back-calculation from an eGFR of 75 mL/min/1.73 m2 assumes an unrealistically normal GFR for all patients. Because preexisting CKD increases susceptibility to AKI, a substantial proportion of AKI patients have CKD at diagnosis. Previous studies have reported underlying CKD in approximately one-third of critically ill patients with AKI and in 45% of those with sepsis28–30. A large multicenter Chinese cohort reported preexisting CKD in 30.7% and 21.3% of community- and hospital-acquired AKI, respectively31. In the present study, approximately half of the patients had preexisting CKD, which is clinically plausible for model development. Notably, by reducing back-calculation overstaging, our model refined severity assessment. It better discerned the chronic components of injury, providing insights into renal reversibility and therapeutic decision-making. In practice, the proposed model can be integrated into the clinical workflow at the time of CT scans. Many acutely ill patients undergo abdominal CT at hospital admission, often in the absence of prior SCr data. Automated baseline SCr estimation can be generated and delivered to clinicians, enabling individualized AKI diagnosis and staging at the point of care. In turn, it may support earlier nephrology consultation and reduce unnecessary testing or treatment.

To date, few studies have explored baseline SCr estimation methods beyond simple imputation. Using a regression model based on age and total body water, Bernardi et al. reclassified AKI stages32. However, the exclusion of patients with CKD limits clinical applicability and generalizability. In a Japanese study of 119 living kidney donors and CKD patients, kidney volume–based regression estimated baseline SCr, showing moderate correlation with measured values (r = 0.51). However, the sample size was small and included mainly individuals with mild CKD33. A gradient boosting model trained on demographic data from the Mayo Clinic ICU achieved MAEs of 0.248 (internal) and 0.387 (external) 34. This outperformed back-calculation but remained insufficient for detecting AKI, defined as a ≥ 0.3 mg/dL rise in SCr. In contrast, our model (TabPFN-PHE) achieved lower MAEs—0.157 and 0.168—reaching clinically relevant performance. This improvement stems from diverse imaging features and an advanced deep-learning model with large-scale pretraining. The Swin UNETR used for segmentation was pretrained using self-supervised learning on 5,050 publicly available abdominal CT images18. Our fine-tuned model achieved a Dice score of 0.94 on the test split, indicating high-quality segmentation. In addition, the TabPFN model was pretrained on approximately 130 million synthetic tabular datasets. Through in-context learning, it achieved state-of-the-art benchmark performance16. In this study, the TabPFN model consistently outperformed the other machine learning models evaluated.

A key strength is our use of explicitly defined imaging features instead of deep-learning features, ensuring interpretability. Several imaging features in this study have not been explored in nephrology research. Solidity, the top contributing imaging feature, was defined as the ratio of the kidney volume to the convex hull volume. The convex hull is the smallest convex shape enclosing the kidney; the gap between the convex hull and the kidney corresponds to the medial concave space at the renal sinus. Because this gap may reflect renal sinus fat volume, lower solidity indicates greater sinus fat — linking the feature to CKD. Renal sinus fat volume correlates negatively with GFR, independent of kidney volume35,36. The SVR is also among the key features. Our previous work revealed that the SVR was more strongly correlated with the GFR than with the kidney volume11. The SVR has an inverse relationship with object size and reflects surface complexity. It therefore captures CKD-related surface changes. Mean local thickness was defined as the average radius of the largest inscribed spheres within the kidney volume. This measure reflects renal parenchymal thickness and is highly reproducible. Sphericity can be calculated from the volume and surface area, reflecting 3D roundness of the kidney. Because these geometric features are less likely to be affected by cross-site variability, they yielded robust external validation performance. They also offer mechanistic insights into CKD-related structural changes.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, CT images and clinical data were retrospectively collected. Although baseline SCr levels were determined by a thorough review of medical records and laboratory results, some misclassification bias may remain. Second, although the model performed consistently on external testing, its broader generalizability remains to be established. Both included cohorts consisted of Korean patients who underwent CT scans with a limited range of imaging configurations. Third, our CT-based features capture renal morphometry but not parenchymal texture or perirenal fat stranding related to AKI. Finally, patients with polycystic kidney disease or postrenal AKI were excluded from the study. In addition, the specific cause of CKD beyond comorbidities could not be determined for each patient. Given these considerations, future studies should validate generalizability across diverse imaging settings and populations. The incorporation of CT texture features or multimodal data could further enhance model performance and clinical utility.

In conclusion, we present a CT-based baseline SCr prediction model for AKI evaluation that integrates deep-learning methods with novel quantitative imaging features. This approach achieved clinically relevant accuracy and holds promise for improving AKI diagnosis and staging, particularly in patients with preexisting CKD commonly encountered in practice.
