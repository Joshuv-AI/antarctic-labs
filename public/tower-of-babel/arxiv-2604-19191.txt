Improved Anomaly Detection in Medical Images via Mean Shift Density Enhancement
Pritam Kar, Gouri Lakshmi S, Saptarshi Bej

Abstract
Anomaly detection in medical imaging is essential for identifying rare pathological conditions, particularly when annotated abnormal samples are limited. We propose a hybrid anomaly detection framework that integrates self-supervised representation learning with manifold-based density estimation, a combination that remains largely unexplored in this domain. Medical images are first embedded into a latent feature space using pretrained, potentially domain-specific, backbones. These representations are then refined via Mean Shift Density Enhancement (MSDE), an iterative manifold-shifting procedure that moves samples toward regions of higher likelihood. Anomaly scores are subsequently computed using Gaussian density estimation in a PCA-reduced latent space, where Mahalanobis distance measures deviation from the learned normal distribution. The framework follows a one-class learning paradigm and requires only normal samples for training. Extensive experiments on seven medical imaging datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance. MSDE achieves the highest AUC on four datasets and the highest Average Precision on five datasets, including near-perfect performance on brain tumor detection (0.981 AUC/AP). These results underscore the potential of the proposed framework as a scalable clinical decision-support tool for early disease detection, screening in low-label settings, and robust deployment across diverse imaging modalities.



1
Introduction

Anomaly detection in medical imaging plays a crucial role in assisting clinical diagnosis by identifying rare or abnormal patterns that may assist in diagnosis. In many real-world scenarios, obtaining large-scale annotated datasets of abnormal cases is challenging due to the rarity of certain pathologies, the high cost of expert annotation, and privacy constraints. As a result, one-class anomaly detection methods (also referred to as one-class classification (OCC) in
Cai et al. 2025
), which learn only from normal data, have gained significant attention in recent years.

Traditional approaches to anomaly detection in medical imaging can be broadly categorized into three groups.

1.

Reconstruction-based methods
, such as autoencoders
Baur et al. 2021
and generative models, attempt to reconstruct input images and identify anomalies through reconstruction errors
Cai et al. 2025
.

2.

Feature-based methods
rely on extracting discriminative representations using pretrained or self-supervised models and detecting deviations in feature space
Cai et al. 2025
.

3.

Density-based methods
model the distribution of normal data and assign anomaly scores based on the likelihood or distance from the learned distribution
Cai et al. 2025
.

Recent studies have shown that feature-based methods have proven to be the most successful among these three
Cai et al. 2025
. Recent advances in this category have enabled the extraction of robust and semantically meaningful representations from medical images without requiring manual annotations. Methods such as CutPaste
Li et al. 2021
and its variants
Sato et al. 2023
have demonstrated strong performance by learning representations through synthetic transformations.

However, we show that anomaly detection performance can be improved by enriching the latent feature space through density enhancement. Shifting latent image representations toward regions of higher data likelihood in the latent space exposes anomalous instances and thereby improves detectability. The proposed framework hybridizes feature-based and density-based anomaly detection by combining learned latent representations with probabilistic modeling of the data manifold.

In this work, we propose an anomaly detection framework that combines self-supervised feature extraction with a
Mean Shift Density Enhancement
(MSDE) approach. The proposed framework first embeds medical images into a high-dimensional feature space using pretrained neural network backbones. Thereafter, to enhance regions of higher data likelihood in the latent space exposing anomalous instances, we employ the MSDE algorithm.

Following this, we model the distribution of the MSDE-enhanced latent space features using a Gaussian Density Estimator in a PCA-reduced latent space. Anomaly scores are computed using the Mahalanobis distance
De Maesschalck et al. 2000
, which measures the deviation of test samples from the learned inlier distribution. This combination of representation learning, density enhancement, and probabilistic distance-based scoring provides a robust framework for one-class medical image anomaly detection.

The key novelty of this work is the introduction of density-based refinement as an intermediate step between feature extraction and anomaly scoring, a direction that has received little attention in medical image anomaly detection. While prior methods largely rely either on learned representations or direct density estimation, the proposed framework shows that explicitly restructuring the latent space toward regions of higher data likelihood can improve anomaly detection. By refining feature embeddings before final scoring, the method demonstrates that latent manifold enhancement is a powerful and previously underexplored principle for one-class medical image anomaly detection.

2
Related Work

2.1
Anomaly Detection in Medical Imaging

Anomaly detection in medical imaging has been extensively studied due to its importance in identifying rare pathological conditions. Early approaches primarily relied on supervised learning; however, their effectiveness is limited by the scarcity of labeled abnormal samples. Consequently, one-class and unsupervised methods have become increasingly popular.

Reconstruction-based methods, such as autoencoders
Baur et al. 2021
, variational autoencoders (VAEs)
Kingma and Welling 2013
;
Zimmerer et al. 2019
, and generative adversarial networks (GANs)
Schlegl et al. 2017
;
Schlegl et al. 2019
, learn to reconstruct normal images and detect anomalies based on reconstruction errors. While these approaches can capture global image structures, they often struggle to detect subtle or localized abnormalities
Meissen et al. 2022
. Feature-based methods address this limitation by leveraging deep neural networks to extract high-level representations and identify anomalies in the feature space
Bergmann et al. 2020
;
Defard et al. 2021
. These methods have demonstrated improved robustness, especially when combined with pretrained
Reiss et al. 2021
or self-supervised backbones
Sohn et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2021
.

2.2
Self-Supervised Representation Learning

Self-supervised learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning meaningful representations without requiring manual annotations. In the context of anomaly detection, methods such as CutPaste
Li et al. 2021
introduce synthetic transformations to generate pseudo-anomalies and train models to distinguish between original and altered samples. These approaches encourage the network to learn discriminative features that capture normal data characteristics.

Recent extensions of CutPaste, including domain-specific variants such as AnatPaste
Sato et al. 2023
, have shown promising results in medical imaging by incorporating anatomically meaningful transformations. Such methods enable the learning of representations that are better aligned with the underlying structure of medical data. However, most existing approaches focus primarily on representation learning and rely on simple distance-based or clustering-based techniques for anomaly detection.

2.3
Density-Based Anomaly Detection

Density-based methods model the distribution of normal data in the feature space and assign anomaly scores based on deviations from this distribution. A commonly used approach is the Gaussian Density Estimator (GDE), where the feature distribution is approximated using a multivariate Gaussian model. The Mahalanobis distance
De Maesschalck et al. 2000
is widely employed as an anomaly score, as it accounts for both the mean and covariance structure of the data.

Several works have demonstrated the effectiveness of Mahalanobis-based scoring for out-of-distribution and anomaly detection tasks. However, these methods typically assume that the feature space is well-structured and do not explicitly address the geometric properties of the data manifold.

2.4
Manifold Learning for Anomaly Detection

Manifold learning techniques aim to capture the intrinsic structure of high-dimensional data by modeling relationships between neighboring samples. Methods such as k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), graph-based manifold learning approaches
Belkin and Niyogi 2003
, and dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., PCA
Jolliffe 2016
, UMAP
McInnes et al. 2018
) have been used to better understand the data distribution.

In anomaly detection, incorporating manifold information can improve the separation between normal and abnormal samples
Ruff 2021
. However, most existing approaches either rely on static feature representations or apply manifold learning solely for visualization or dimensionality reduction. The idea of iteratively refining feature representations through manifold-based transformations remains relatively underexplored.

2.5
Gaps in Existing Literature

Despite significant advances in self-supervised learning and density-based anomaly detection, existing approaches exhibit several limitations. First, reconstruction-based methods often struggle with subtle anomalies that do not significantly affect reconstruction error
Cai et al. 2025
. Second, feature-based methods typically assume static, Euclidean feature spaces and do not account for the intrinsic manifold structure of medical data. Third, while manifold learning has been explored for visualization and dimensionality reduction, its integration into end-to-end anomaly detection pipelines remains underexplored.

The proposed MSDE framework addresses these gaps by explicitly modeling and refining the feature manifold through iterative, density-weighted shifting before applying density estimation. This approach bridges self-supervised representation learning with geometric manifold analysis, providing a principled mechanism for one-class anomaly detection in medical imaging.

2.6
Summary and Motivation

Despite significant progress in representation learning and density-based anomaly detection, existing methods often treat the feature space as fixed and do not fully exploit its underlying manifold structure. This limitation can lead to suboptimal modeling of normal data distributions, especially in complex medical imaging scenarios.

To address these challenges, we propose a unified framework that integrates self-supervised feature extraction with a manifold shift mechanism and density-based anomaly scoring. By explicitly modeling and refining the feature manifold, the proposed approach aims to improve the compactness of normal data and enhance anomaly detection performance.

3
Methodology

The proposed anomaly detection framework consists of three main stages: (i) feature extraction using deep neural network backbones, (ii) mean shift-based feature refinement, and (iii) density-based anomaly scoring. An overview of the pipeline is illustrated in Fig.
1
. The overall pipeline operates under a one-class learning setting, where only normal samples are used during training. This makes the proposed method particularly suitable for medical imaging applications, where abnormal samples are scarce or difficult to annotate. Below we discuss the three components of the pipeline in detail.

3.1
Feature Extraction Using Deep Neural Network Backbones

The first stage of the proposed pipeline involves extracting discriminative feature representations from medical images using deep convolutional neural networks. Following the experimental setup of MedIAnomaly
Cai et al. 2025
, we employ two feature extraction strategies depending on the dataset characteristics. Briefly, we employ either a generic ImageNet-pretrained backbone for broad cross-modality feature extraction or a domain-adapted self-supervised backbone when anatomical priors and modality-specific structure can provide more informative representations. More details can be found in Section
4.3
.

Given an input image
I_{i}
, the feature extraction process is formally expressed as:

\mathbf{x}_{i}=f_{\theta}(I_{i}),

(1)

where
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
denotes the feature extraction function parameterized by the ResNet18 encoder (either ImageNet-pretrained or AnatPaste-trained), and
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}
is the resulting 512-dimensional feature vector.

3.2
Mean Shift-based Feature Refinement

The extracted feature representations are subsequently refined using the proposed Mean Shift Density Enhancement (MSDE) algorithm, introduced in our earlier work
Kar et al. 2026
. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the latent feature embeddings, where
N
is the total number of data points (train+test). MSDE first estimates sample-specific density weights
w_{i}
from local neighborhood structure using a manifold-aware similarity graph. The embeddings are then iteratively updated through a weighted mean-shift operation,

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(t)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{j}},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}+\eta\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}\right),

(2)

where
\mathcal{N}_{i}
denotes the local neighborhood of sample
i
and
\eta
is the update step size. This process shifts latent space representations toward high-density regions in the latent space. Further details of the MSDE algorithm can be found in
Kar et al. 2026
.

To preserve geometric consistency between reference and query embeddings, MSDE is applied to the unified feature set

\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{all}}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{train}}\cup\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{test}}.

(3)

The manifold refinement is therefore performed in a common latent space, ensuring that local neighborhood structure is evaluated consistently for both training and test samples and reducing representation mismatch during anomaly scoring. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the MSDE-refined latent embeddings obtained after applying MSDE. Overall, as outlined in the execution flow of Algorithm
1
, the MSDE framework transforms the feature space into a refined manifold.

3.3
Density-based Anomaly Scoring

After obtaining the refined feature representations through the manifold shift process, anomaly detection is performed using a probabilistic approach. Specifically, we model a PCA-reduced version of the MSDE-enhanced latent data distribution using a Gaussian Density Estimator (GDE) and compute anomaly scores based on the Mahalanobis distance
De Maesschalck et al. 2000
.

Dimensionality Reduction and Gaussian Density Estimation.

To ensure numerical stability and avoid covariance matrix singularity due to high dimensionality, we apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to project the shifted features into a lower-dimensional space. The PCA transformation is fitted exclusively on the shifted normal training samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(where
n
is the total number of training data points), projecting from the original feature dimension
d
(e.g., 512) to
d^{\prime}=256
dimensions:

\mathbf{z}_{i}=\text{PCA}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}),

(4)

where the PCA retains the top 256 principal components.

In this reduced space, we estimate the parameters of a multivariate Gaussian distribution using only the transformed normal training samples. The mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}
are computed using standard expressions. To ensure numerical stability during matrix inversion, a Tikhonov regularization term is added to the diagonal:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}+\lambda\mathbf{I},

(5)

where
\lambda
is the regularization penalty and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix.

Mahalanobis Distance-Based Anomaly Scoring.

The anomaly quantification employs the Mahalanobis distance, which accounts for feature correlations and variance structure in the learned normal distribution. Unlike Euclidean distance, which assumes independence and equal variance across dimensions, the Mahalanobis distance incorporates the inverse covariance matrix to appropriately scale deviations based on the directional variance of the data.

Given a test sample with reduced representation
\mathbf{z}
, the anomaly score
s(\mathbf{z})
is computed as:

s(\mathbf{z})=\sqrt{(\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z})}.

(6)

This formulation ensures that deviations along directions with high variance in the normal training distribution (representing natural biological variability) are penalized less severely than deviations along low-variance directions (which are more indicative of anomalies). To enable robust comparison across different datasets and ensure numerical stability, the raw Mahalanobis scores are standardized and transformed using a sigmoid function:

s_{\text{final}}(\mathbf{z})=\sigma\left(\text{StandardScale}(s(\mathbf{z}))\right),

(7)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigmoid function and StandardScale performs z-score normalization. This transformation bounds all anomaly scores to the
[0,1]
interval, with higher values indicating greater likelihood of being anomalous.

Data Splitting and Preprocessing.

For all datasets, we strictly follow the data splits and preprocessing pipeline provided in the MedIAnomaly repository. The training set contains only normal samples, while the test set includes both normal and anomalous samples. All images are resized to a fixed resolution and normalized prior to feature extraction. Grayscale images are converted to three-channel format (RGB) to ensure compatibility with pre-trained convolutional neural network backbones.

This diverse collection of datasets enables a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method across different modalities, anatomical regions, and anomaly characteristics.

4.2
Selection of Baseline Models

To ensure a focused comparison, we select a subset of representative methods based on their superior performance in the original benchmark (see Table 10 in
Cai et al. 2025
), prioritizing models that achieve the highest number of top-ranking results (best, second-best, and third-best) across datasets, as well as those with publicly available implementations. Based on these selection criteria, we select the models AE-PL, AnatPaste, CutPaste-3way, CutPaste, ResNet152 and ResNet18. It is worth noting that certain methods with good performance reported in the original benchmark, such as MSC
Reiss and Hoshen 2023
and PANDA
Reiss et al. 2021
, could not be included in our benchmarking studies due to the absence of their implementations in the official repository.

4.3
Implementation Details

The proposed framework is implemented by extending the MedIAnomaly pipeline and integrating the MSDE module within its feature processing and scoring stages. This section summarizes the practical configuration used for all experiments.

Feature Embeddings.

We utilize pre-extracted feature representations provided by the MedIAnomaly setup. For chest X-ray datasets, embeddings are obtained using the AnatPaste self-supervised model, while for all other datasets, a ResNet18 backbone pretrained on ImageNet is employed. In both cases, each image is represented as a 512-dimensional feature vector. Feature extraction is performed offline, and the same embeddings are reused across all experiments to ensure consistency and computational efficiency.

Preprocessing and Manifold Refinement.

All feature vectors are standardized using statistics computed from the training set. The MSDE-based manifold refinement is then applied in the original feature space using a joint processing strategy over training and test samples. Nearest-neighbor relationships are computed using approximate search to ensure scalability to large datasets. The iterative refinement process is controlled by a fixed learning rate and a maximum number of iterations, with convergence determined via a shift tolerance criterion.

Dimensionality Reduction and Density Estimation.

After manifold refinement, dimensionality reduction is performed using PCA. The PCA model is fitted exclusively on the refined training features and reduces the dimensionality from 512 to 256. A Gaussian density model is then estimated using the transformed training data. To ensure numerical stability during covariance inversion, a diagonal regularization term with
\lambda=10^{-4}
is applied.

Anomaly Scoring and Normalization.

Anomaly scores are computed for each test sample using the Mahalanobis distance in the reduced feature space. The resulting scores are standardized and subsequently passed through a sigmoid function to obtain normalized scores in the range
[0,1]
, facilitating consistent comparison across datasets.

Evaluation Protocol.

Performance is evaluated using AUC-ROC and Average Precision (AP), following standard anomaly detection benchmarks
Cai et al. 2025
. All experiments are conducted under a one-class training setting using only normal samples. Hyperparameters of the MSDE module, including the number of neighbors, density threshold, learning rate, and convergence criteria, are reported in Table
2
.

All experiments are implemented in Python using PyTorch and standard scientific libraries, and executed on a GPU-enabled computing environment.

5
Results

In this section, we present the results of our Benchmarking experiment. We show that our pipeline consistently outperforms state-of-the-art models across both performance metrics.

Performance for Brain Tumor dataset.

As detailed in Table
3
, even without dataset-specific tuning, the MSDE pipeline delivers exceptional baseline performance. For the Brain Tumor dataset, MSDE achieves a score of 0.981 for both AUC and AP simultaneously. In the historical context of medical OCC, models almost universally suffer from a strict precision-recall trade-off a model optimized for high AUC (general ranking) typically sacrifices AP (precision against false positives). MSDE effectively addresses this gap, proving that a tightly compacted, Mahalanobis-scored manifold is inherently resistant to false-positive degradation.

Comparative Analysis Against State-of-the-Art Baselines.

Table
4
and Table
5
show that MSDE achieves state-of-the-art AUC performance on 4 out of the 7 evaluated datasets (RSNA, VinDr-CXR, Brain Tumor, Camelyon16), and achieves the highest AP on 5 out of the 7 datasets. We would like to point out that such cross-modality consistency is rarely observed among the models compared in the recent work of Cai
et al.

Cai et al. 2025
.

We observe from the work of Cai
et al.
that reconstruction models like AE-PL dominate specific tasks like retinal imagery (LAG), while generative diffusion models like AutoDDPM excel at highly textured histopathology (Camelyon16). MSDE however shows superior performance on both.

The comparison on the Camelyon16 gigapixel dataset is particularly noteworthy. AutoDDPM leverages computationally intensive diffusion resampling processes to achieve an AUC of 0.807 and an AP of 0.767. The MSDE framework, operating purely on extracted ResNet18 features via relatively lightweight geometric shifting, surpasses this diffusion baseline, achieving an AUC of 0.812 and an AP of 0.820.

Furthermore, it is also interesting to observe that the MSDE pipeline outperforms the sophisticated feature-reconstruction autoencoders. In the VinDr-CXR multi-pathology benchmark, the previous best metric was held by the Uncertainty Autoencoder (AE-U) with an AUC of 0.769. MSDE improves upon this with an AUC of 0.819.

The primary limitation of MSDE is observed on the ISIC 2018 dermoscopy dataset, where it underperforms compared to the fully optimized AnatPaste model (0.705 vs. 0.807 AUC). This behavior is consistent with the underlying assumptions of the feature extractor. AnatPaste is designed to exploit structured anatomical regions (like distinct lung segmentations), whereas dermoscopic images exhibit relatively homogeneous textures without well-defined internal structures. Consequently, the extracted features are less discriminative, and MSDE inherits this limitation, consistent with the well-known “garbage in, garbage out” principle.

Discussion

Qualitative Analysis: Feature Space Manifold.

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed Mean Shift Density Enhancement (MSDE) module, we qualitatively analyze the effect of manifold shifting on the feature space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to project the high-dimensional embeddings into a 2D visualization space. We compare the original feature distributions against those refined by MSDE across two distinct datasets and backbone architectures: the RSNA dataset (AnatPaste backbone) and the C16 dataset (ResNet18 backbone), as illustrated in Figure
2
.

(b) RSNA: Shifted

Comparison of UMAP embeddings for the RSNA (AnatPaste backbone) and C16 (ResNet18 backbone) datasets. Top row: RSNA before/after MSDE. Bottom row: C16 before/after MSDE. MSDE consistently collapses diffuse representations into compact topological hubs, isolating anomalies to the periphery across different backbones.

In the original feature spaces, normal and anomalous samples often exhibit significant overlap or lack distinct clustering, complicating the separation process. After applying MSDE, the feature distributions consistently become more structured, with normal samples collapsing into compact, high-density topological hubs. In contrast, anomalous samples remain dispersed or are effectively pushed to the periphery due to their inability to conform to the concentrated local neighborhoods of the normal data. This improved structural separation across varying backbones demonstrates that MSDE creates a manifold better suited for Mahalanobis distance-based scoring, thereby contributing to more robust and reliable anomaly detection.

Untapped value in pretrained latent representations.

The strong performance of MSDE without any retraining of the underlying backbone network indicates that pretrained latent representations often contain substantial unused discriminative information. In many anomaly detection pipelines, extracted features are treated as final inputs for downstream scoring methods. Our results suggest that these embeddings may still possess hidden geometric structure that is not fully exploited in their original form. By refining the latent manifold through density-guided shifting, MSDE is able to reveal and utilize this information more effectively. This finding implies that improvements in anomaly detection can arise not only from better encoders, but also from better post-processing of existing representations.

Broad adaptability through modular design.

A major practical advantage of MSDE is its modular design, which allows it to be integrated with a wide range of existing feature extractors. Because the method operates entirely after representation learning, it does not require modifications to upstream backbone architectures or retraining procedures. This makes it compatible with standard convolutional networks, self-supervised medical encoders, and emerging foundation models. As stronger pretrained models continue to appear, MSDE can be directly applied as an additional refinement stage. Such flexibility increases its long-term relevance and makes it attractive for deployment in real-world medical imaging systems with diverse modalities and evolving model ecosystems.

Computational Efficiency and Scalability.

MSDE operates on pre-extracted feature embeddings rather than raw images, avoiding expensive end-to-end retraining. The dominant computational cost arises from approximate nearest-neighbor graph construction and iterative neighborhood updates over
T
refinement steps. Using NN-Descent
Ono and Matsui 2023
, graph construction scales approximately as
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
in practice, while each refinement step requires
\mathcal{O}(NkD)
operations, where
N
is the number of samples,
D
the feature dimension, and
k
the neighborhood size. Since
T
is small (
T=8
in all experiments), the method remains computationally practical. After refinement, PCA and Gaussian scoring introduce comparatively minor overhead. Overall, MSDE is substantially lighter than reconstruction- or diffusion-based anomaly detection methods that require repeated image-space generation or network optimization.

Dependence on backbone feature quality.

Despite its strong overall performance, MSDE has an important limitation: it remains dependent on the quality of the initial latent embeddings. The method refines existing feature geometry but does not generate discriminative information that is absent from the original representation. Consequently, if the backbone fails to capture pathology-relevant structure or produces weak modality-specific features, the benefits of manifold refinement may be limited. This likely explains lower performance on certain datasets with challenging textures or less structured anatomical cues. Therefore, MSDE should be viewed as a geometry enhancement mechanism that complements, rather than replaces, advances in representation learning.

A new intermediate stage in anomaly pipelines.

Taken together, these findings establish latent space manifold refinement via density enhancement as a promising new intermediate stage between representation learning and anomaly scoring in medical imaging pipelines. Most existing methods follow a two-step paradigm in which features are first extracted and then directly scored using distances, densities, or reconstruction errors. MSDE introduces a third stage in which the latent space itself is optimized before final decision making. This additional step improves compatibility between learned representations and downstream anomaly models. More broadly, the results suggest that future progress in anomaly detection may come not only from better encoders or scorers, but also from better feature-space transformation strategies.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we utilized Mean Shift via Density Enhancement (MSDE) to develop a likelihood-enhancing framework for one-class anomaly detection in medical imaging. By combining self-supervised feature representations with density-based scoring, MSDE addresses a common limitation of conventional pipelines that treat latent feature spaces as fixed after feature extraction. Through an iterative neighborhood refinement process, the method shifts samples toward regions of higher local likelihood, producing feature distributions that are more amenable to Gaussian modeling and Mahalanobis distance-based anomaly scoring.

Experiments across seven diverse medical imaging datasets indicate that MSDE achieves competitive and frequently state-of-the-art performance, obtaining the highest AUC on four datasets and the highest Average Precision (AP) on five datasets. In particular, the method demonstrated strong joint ranking and precision performance on the Brain Tumor benchmark, where both AUC and AP reached 0.981. In addition, sensitivity analyses using fixed hyperparameters across datasets suggest that the framework remains practically robust without requiring extensive dataset-specific tuning.

From a computational perspective, MSDE operates on pre-extracted embeddings and relies primarily on approximate neighborhood search and lightweight iterative updates, making it substantially less demanding than reconstruction-heavy or diffusion-based approaches that require repeated image-space optimization or sampling. This makes the framework attractive for scalable deployment settings where efficiency is important.

Despite these encouraging results, the performance of MSDE remains dependent on the quality of the underlying feature extractor. As observed on the ISIC 2018 dermoscopy dataset, when initial embeddings provide limited separation between normal and abnormal patterns, the gains achievable through likelihood enhancement may be reduced. Thus, MSDE should be viewed as a complementary post-representation refinement strategy rather than a replacement for strong feature learning.

Future work will focus on integrating MSDE within end-to-end trainable pipelines, extending the framework to volumetric imaging modalities such as CT and MRI, exploring domain adaptation across institutions and scanners, and incorporating uncertainty estimation to support clinically reliable anomaly detection systems.

Appendix A
Hyperparameter Tuning and Model Robustness

To rigorously evaluate the sensitivity of the Mean Shift via Density Enhancement (MSDE) framework to its hyperparameters, we conducted an extensive optimization study using the Optuna framework
Akiba et al. 2019
. The primary objective of this analysis was to determine whether MSDE relies on brittle, dataset-specific tuning to achieve competitive performance, or if its manifold refinement process is inherently robust across diverse medical imaging modalities.

Zero-Leakage Validation Protocol.

Hyperparameter optimization in anomaly detection carries a high risk of data leakage, particularly when anomalous samples are scarce. To prevent this, we implemented a strict ”zero-leakage” validation protocol. For each dataset, we constructed an isolated validation set consisting of:

1.

Validation Normals:
20% of the normal training samples were held out.

2.

Validation Anomalies:
10% of the anomalous samples were permanently removed from the final test set and repurposed strictly for validation.

During the Optuna search, the MSDE model was fitted exclusively on the remaining 80% of the normal training data and evaluated on the validation set. The final test set remained completely unseen during the entire tuning process.

Search Space and Optimization.

The optimization process focused strictly on the five geometric hyperparameters governing the manifold shift process: the number of neighbors (
k\in[5,60]
), neighborhood sample density threshold (
T_{\text{nbd}}\in[3,80]
), learning rate (
\eta\in[0.01,0.5]
), maximum shift iterations (
T\in[3,12]
), and the shift convergence threshold (
\tau\in[10^{-4},0.05]
). The Gaussian Density Estimation (GDE) parameters, including the PCA dimensionality reduction (
d^{\prime}=256
), were kept fixed. The objective metric was set to maximize the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) over 80 trials per dataset

Results and Robustness Analysis.

Following the Optuna search, the model was retrained on the full set of normal training samples using the dataset-specific optimal hyperparameters and evaluated on the held-out test set.

Crucially, the performance metrics obtained using the optimized hyperparameters showed only marginal deviations from the baseline results obtained using the fixed, universal hyperparameters reported in Table
7
. For instance, datasets that achieved state-of-the-art performance under the universal configuration maintained nearly identical AUC and AP scores after extensive tuning.

This parity demonstrates a key strength of the proposed approach: the MSDE framework is highly robust to hyperparameter variations. The multi-scale empirical density weighting and iterative local shifting naturally adapt to the intrinsic topology of the data, negating the need for exhaustive, dataset-specific hyperparameter sweeps in practical clinical deployments.
