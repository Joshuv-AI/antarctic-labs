 The relative importance of each manufacturing criteria on product cost were
determined and are shown in Table 1. The criteria with the highest impact on cost was
the cycle time of cell production. Lower cycle times allow for more capacity using the
same space and equipment. First pass inspection reliability, or the percent probability of
being able to read a cell’s bar code for traceability, is also an important manufacturing
criteria. The ability to reliability read a cell ID reduces cycle time and avoids operator

 Page 2 of 17

intervention (reducing direct labor). Both cycle time and first pass inspection reliability
were both ranked as a “high” impact on product cost. Direct labor was determined to
have a medium impact on product cost. More automation and, subsequently, less direct
labor lower the cost of goods sold. Finally, the manufacturing capital and space were
both determined to have a low relative importance on product cost compared to the other
criteria. Both were determined by assuming a “cost of capital,” 12-year straight line
depreciation, and cost of real estate.

The quality function deployment (QFD) process determined the importance of
brainstormed functional equipment requirements. The most expensive equipment
systems were estimated to be the quality leak tester, screen printer, low-pressure high-
volume aerosol sprayer, and the unloading and transfer of cells from thermal spray to the
quality equipment. The importance of functional requirements for the equipment (Figures
1-4) was determined by using the manufacturing criteria and consensus of impact of
functional requirements on those criteria. The most important functional needs for the
equipment, in general, were: reliability of loading, unloading and aligning the parts;
equipment strategy for buffering and queuing parts; ability of equipment to communicate
with the SCADA and MES systems; and reliability of the equipment to read the bar code
of each part. The results from the criteria sensitivity analysis and QFD exercises provided
input into the manufacturing equipment procurement specifications.

 The concept included a network infrastructure using a secured communication
channel that built a bridge from the shop floor equipment to the server database and
respective applications. Process flow diagram, process equipment requirements, and
key quality control data were used to define the information technology (IT) equipment
(router, switches, wiring / cabling, shop floor N/W ports) and how they communicate. The
virtual local area network (LAN) network would be configured with the network ports and
IP addresses to host the data acquisition application, database servers, and PLCs.

 Page 7 of 17

 A master PLC would be used as a hub for local equipment PLCs on the shop floor.
The proof-of-concept for master PLC was tested on existing equipment to understand
specifications for future equipment controls. Specific considerations included: data
collected at each operation; compatibility between equipment and master PLC; safety
requirements; and accuracy of data collection. The control infrastructure was designed
to be modular to simplify expansion of processes. The data would be communicated
using protocol (Ethernet IP, TCP I/P) and the link between the master PLC, SCADA/MES
Application and Database servers would be established through the existing network
infrastructure.
 The initial analysis of the data collection requirements, communication protocols,
equipment integration and handshake controls suggested that Cummins’ manufacturing
execution system (CMES) would be a suitable solution. CMES is a plant level IT system
utilized within Cummins that has custom code built utilizing Apriso FlexNet (if needed,
additional modification needs to the platform would be identified during commissioning).
The CMES application fully supports automated workstations and has the capability to
manage bill-of-materials (BOM) verification, which would be important during SOEC stack
build.
 The data collection software application would be designed as a central application
for connecting the manufacturing processes with key quality control parameters and
collecting the data for each process quality gate. The application would run as central
application on server and be integrated with equipment controls, data collection by means
of PLC communication protocols. The application would collect the data in database
repository running on the server, thereby helping to generate the product genealogy
(history of key data and process parameters) against each parts serial ID.
 Finally, Ignition (Inductive Automation) was considered to monitor and record key
process parameters as a function of time. For example, the temperature, speed or
pressure of equipment can be monitored in real time to ensure processes were within
specification.

 Manufacturing traceability, and the ability to read serialized part identification was
identified as a key risk to achieving the manufacturing targets. The loss of cell traceability
in the context of highly automated and high-volume production can lead to loss of
productivity and reduced production line capacity as un-traceable or unidentifiable parts
will require operator attention and interventions. Therefore, 100% traceability throughout
the whole manufacturing process was targeted.

 Historically, 2D matrix code (machine readable) and alpha numerical code (human
readable) have been engraved using different methods such as dot-peening or laser
engraving on non-active areas of our cells, typically on features designed to hold the parts
during processing. However, this approach has translated into less-than-optimal
traceability due to the multiple challenges associated with the nature of the part, including
the metallic support composition, symmetry, shape, as well as the manufacturing
processes used in the fabrication of the cells. The baseline process resulted in read rates

Develop single-print glass process

 Previously we demonstrated that reducing the number of glass screen-prints from
two to one is feasible in producing print thickness within specification. Based on the
results, we moved on to a detailed evaluation of the glass print prepared and observed a
visual difference in the whiteness of prints between double-print (two prints) glass and
single-print (one print) glass (Fig. 1). The single-print glass is greyer than the double-print
glass. To identify the cause and possible risk, a microscope observation was conducted
on the prints (Fig. 2). The double-print glass contains pores of ~10 to 50 μm, which are
resulted from the evaporation of solvent from the paste/print. By comparison, the single-
print glass contains higher population of larger-size pores (~50 to 80 μm) because an
increased mass of solvent (twice that of double print) needs to evaporate for the same
period. The higher population of pores could have adverse effects on the sealing
performance of glass by i) potentially forming continuous path for gas transport and ii)
potentially causing a severe shrinkage of glass print during the stack heating process,

 Page 2 of 11

both of which leading to sealing leakage. To assess the potential risks, the sintering
shrinkage of glass prints was analyzed (Fig. 3), while the microstructure observation of
single-print after a stack heating process is under planning. Glass prints were subjected
to a stack heating process and the change in thickness before and after the process was
measured to calculate the shrinkage ratios.

 Figure 1. The comparison of glass prints: (A) Double-print glass and (B) Single-print
 glass. The single-print is greyer than the double-print glass.

 Figure 2. Microscopic observation of (A) double-print glass and (B) single-print
glass. The single-print glass contains higher population of larger-size pores (~50 to 80
μm) as compared to the double-print glass.

 Page 3 of 11

 Figure 3. Measurement of shrinkage of glass prints during a stack heating process:
(A) photos of glass print before and after the process and (B) thickness of glass prints
before and after the process along with the corresponding shrinkage ratios calculated.

 After a stack heating process, the color of print changed to grey because of the
sintering of glass powders forming a bulk glass layer. Single-print glass showed larger
shrinkage than double-print (40% vs 36%), presumably due to the higher population of
large-size pores to be filled up through sintering mechanism, which leads to a larger
reduction in thickness. Despite the larger shrinkage, since single-print glass displays
larger initial thicknesses than double-print (229 μm vs 213 μm), it still provides a final
thickness comparable to that of double-print after a stack heating process (139 μm vs 136
μm). This result indicates that single-print glass can provide sufficiently large thickness
for properly sealing cells without causing a sealing leakage. As plan for moving forward,
three action items are planned: i) Cross-sectional microstructure observation of glass print
after stack-heating process to identify the possible formation of continuous pore paths; ii)
Evaluation of the process repeatability of single-print glass in terms of print weight gains,
iii) SOEC stack test(s) to validate the sealing performance of single-print glass.

Develop high-speed thick film drying

 To integrate maintenance/emergency-stop scenarios into the design of drying
oven, a search for an oven with fast-heating capability was initiated. One candidate IR
(infrared) drying oven was found and a prototype oven is provided by ProTherm Ltd. The
prototype uses the same heating element of the final design but does not have a
controlled air flow. As shown in Fig. 4, the prototype drying oven can reach the target
temperature of ~ 200°C in 40 seconds, as compared to ~ 10 minutes of a conventional

 Page 4 of 11

dryer for screen printing. Information on the heating element of the oven is not shared for
protecting the intellectual property of the manufacturing company.

a time-temperature curve of the oven showing its fast-heating capability. The prototype
drying oven can reach the target temperature of ~ 200°C in 40 seconds.

requires the temperature profile established for the conventional drying oven to be
updated. It was found that time for drying anode prints may potentially be reduced from 3
min to 2 min 30 sec (Fig. 5).

 ii. Development of high speed thick film drying

 The temperature profile of the baseline drying process was first characterized and
is shown in Figure 5. The baseline infrared (IR) drying oven is composed of two zones
with a combined length of 9 feet. The part temperature reaches about 170°C in the oven
and, after 10 mins, exits the oven at approximately 140°C. The same profile is used for
all three thick film coatings in the project manufacturing scope, including the anode,
contact, and glass seal prints.

 The temperature and the conveyor speed were optimized to increase the speed of
drying process (or to reduce the time required to dry the coatings) by increasing the
heating rate. The objective was to fully dry the coating without defects, such as bubbles
resulting from evaporating the solvent too fast, or mudflap cracks typically resulting from
excessive shrinking or a weak binder. Through multiple series of experiments for different
prints, we succeeded in reducing the drying time from 10 minutes to 3 minutes. The
contact print temperature and time profile, for example, is shown in Figure 6 plotted with
the current baseline profile for comparison.

 After the high speed temperature profile was optimized and selected, emergency-
stop scenarios were taken into consideration for more robust line operation. For instance,
it is anticipated that the line will stop during manufacturing due to routine or unplanned
maintenance. Two scenarios were considered: scenario 1, a cell stays in oven for a
prolonged time (10 minutes) with the heater on; and scenario 2, a cell stays in oven for
10 min with heater off. Figure 7 shows the photos of the cells after testing the scenarios.
The cell that was exposed to scenario 1 showed evidence of binder burn-out, as particles
were friable and easily rubbed off. The cell exposed to scenario 2 had well particles that
were well adhered, demonstrating limited or no binder burn-out. These results suggest
that the drying oven design include two capabilities: 1) a low-power setting control, when
the emergency-stop happens, to suppress binder burn-out and 2) fast heating capability,
such that after the emergency-stop scenario ends the oven can return to target
temperature. These considerations were included in the oven specification as critical to
the quality of manufacturing.

 iii. Develop in-line thickness measurement method

 To directly measure the thickness of screen-prints during continuous
manufacturing, instead using indirect/cost-intensive weight-measuring method, the
feasibility of a laser-scan method (Keyence LJ-X8200 sensor unit) has been under
evaluation. In addition to that, another potential candidate method was identified using
laser photothermal radiometry (produced by Enovasense), of which the fundamental
measurement principle is shown in Figure 8. The method consists of a laser that
illuminates an object while creating heat at the surface. The heat diffuses through the
coating and back-diffuses at the interface between the coating and the substrate. The
back-diffusion emits infrared (IR) radiation at the surface and is detected by an IR
detector. By measuring the time delay between laser illumination and IR detection, the
thickness of print can be calculated. A set of specimens with varying thickness and
combination of print layers were prepared and provided to the company (Enovasense) to
evaluate the feasibility of the method for our application.

 b. Automation Development
 i.  End of Arm Tooling Down-selection

 Selection of End of Arm Tooling (EOAT) for handling of the SOEC substrates is a
critical task in the design of the stack building and line loading stations of the automated
manufacturing line. The configuration of the SOEC substrates is such that commercially
available, complete, ready-to-use EOAT solutions were not suitable, and a custom design
specific to the parts is required. Namely, the cell is thin and with limited areas where
contact between the tooling and part are allowable without risk of contaminating or
damaging the functional ceramic layers. Three EOAT approaches were considered:
mechanical gripping, magnetic part holding, and vacuum cup systems. Prototype tooling
for each type was fabricated and evaluated for part picking and positioning, as well as
other criteria such as cost, facility requirements, and duty cycle.

 Prototype evaluation was conducted using a UR-10e collaborative robot (Universal
Robots, Odense, Denmark), and representative prototype part magazines, stack base
manifolds, and tooling to simulate the required pick-and-place operations in the line
loading and stacking stations of the manufacturing line.

 For mechanical gripping, a basic pallet arrangement was considered, using
parallel-jaw pneumatic actuators to position and hold the part, and a single-axis grip finger
which provided additional support of the part. Figure 9 is an image of the mechanical
gripping EOAT prototype. Although parts could successfully be picked and placed with
this tool, there were additional demands on pick location precision. Parts needed to be
positioned within 2mm in X and Y directions for a successful and repeatable pick.
Improper pick location introduced the potential to damage the part and or tooling. For this
reason, further trials were suspended to focus on more compliant approaches to part
handling.

 Page 9 of 31

 Figure 9. The mechanical gripping EOAT prototype.

 Magnetic tooling was evaluated next. Magnetic holding devices for robot end of
arm tooling are readily available and fall into three general categories based on the
approach for engaging and dis-engaging the part. The ferritic stainless steel used for our
SOEC substrates is suitable for this approach of magnetic handling. It is important to
note that published data for load capacity for most magnetic gripping and work holding
devices is based on 20mm or thicker parts. For thinner parts such as the SOEC substrate,
the force is considerably less, and use of larger, higher-rated magnets is necessary.

 The first type of magnetic part holders are simply electromagnets. This type was
ruled out for our application due to the relatively low holding force, which results in use of
more magnets, and a heavier tool. This in turn increases the required robot payload. A
suitable solution of this type would be too heavy for testing on the UR-10e robot, and too
bulky to allow reasonable access into the part magazine with reasonable part spacing.

 The second type utilize a permanent magnet and pneumatic actuation to vary the
effective field at the part interface. These are low cost and highly effective for picking
parts made from magnetic alloys. Use of polymeric contact “boots” is common with this
type and could have limited contact to only areas of the part where damage or
contamination was not of concern. Unfortunately, the relatively large size of these type
of magnetic gripping devices reduced viability when considering picking parts from
magazines or rack systems with tight part spacing.

 The third magnetic-based EOAT was selected for additional evaluation, as it was
most suited to the spatial and holding force requirements of our application. This hybrid
type uses neodymium and AlNiCo magnets, and an electrical coil to reverse the polarity
of the AlNiCo magnet. In the released state, the magnetic circuit is completed between
the Neodymium magnet and the AlNiCo magnet. A pulse of current through the coil
reverses the AlNiCo magnet’s polarity, blocking the magnetic flux and forcing it to travel
through the gripped part. The result is a magnetic part holding system which is compact

                                        Page 10 of 31

and powerful, while drawing no electrical current except during polarization. This system
also maintains the added benefit of holding the part in the event of loss of power, resulting
in retaining the part on the robot in such events, with obvious benefits for safety and
reduced risk of part damage. It is important to note that there is a limitation to the duty
cycle of polarization for this type of gripper. This is dependent on the heat input from the
coil during re-polarization in combination with ambient conditions. This could potentially
be problematic for the line loading station, where timing between pick-and-place can be
less than 5 seconds in some scenarios. Water or air cooling can be applied to maintain
element temperature below 45oC, but this adds cost, complexity, and weight. Working
with Minuteman Empire Automation Systems (Rochester, NY, USA), and Schunk
(Lauffen/Neckar, DE), a suitable magnet configuration was identified and the feasibility of
picking a cell with this method was demonstrated. Based on these results, three EGM-
M-Q-10-1-FX magnets was procured and fitted to a suitable pallet and adapter for the
UR-10e robot. Figure 10 is an image of this configuration. This approach proved
successful in picking parts from tightly spaced (19 mm between parts) magazine racks,
and for accurately placing the parts (reproducibility of less than 1 mm). However, one
problem noted was interaction between the magnets and a stack of SOEC substrates.
This led to unwanted disturbance of the stack when placing subsequent parts. For this
reason, along with the higher cost, the decision was made to proceed with evaluating
alternative methods for picking parts.

 Figure 10. The mechanical gripping EOAT prototype.

 The final type of EOAT evaluated for SOEC stacking and handling was vacuum
cup-based systems. Oval vacuum cups from Piab AB (Danderyd, Sweden) were
procured based on preliminary considerations, namely the dimensions of these cups were

                                        Page 11 of 31

suitable in picking force while fitting within the allowable contact area. Non-rotation type
compliance adapters were added to allow the array of cups to conform to the curvature
of the SOEC substrates. This also enables the tooling to conform to variations in the
stack when placing parts. This is important, as the incremental increase in stack height
can vary per cell placed during the stacking operation. This discovery also led to a change
in approach for stacking, with force sensor feedback used to re-calculate part placement
height and orientation every 5-10 parts placed to the stack. Figure 11 shows the design
of the prototype vacuum EOAT, which will form the basis for the final EOAT to be use in
the Stacking and Line Loading stations. It should be noted that variations such as
modifying the tool to robot wrist angle, and adding a second part interface position are
planned, as these offer advantages in cycle time when used along with an optimized
station layout.

 Figure 11. The Vacuum-based EOAT prototype shown on a UR-10e robot.

 The vacuum cup EOAT solution required additional considerations to improve the
reproducibility of placement location. A robot tool center point (TCP) is needed to place
parts within 1 mm in the x and y directions onto the stack with greater than 3 mm variability
in pick location. Earlier trials with a Keyence CV-X452 (Keyence Electronics Corporation,
Osaka, Japan) machine vision system with 5 Mp Camera showed promise for using vision
correction of placement position. With this approach, the vision system takes an image
of the part with the robot in a known position. Next, the offset in rotation and position of
the part relative to a calibrated master image is used to generate the offset data for
placement of the part, in the x and y direction, as well as rotation correction about the part
z-axis

 Testing was conducted to estimate the possible accuracy of this vision correction
approach using the UR-10e collaborative robot, with the vision system camera attached
to the wrist, to simulate a pick position correction as is common for vision guided robotic
pick-and-place operations. The means of evaluation consisted of moving a robot
mounted camera in a simple 2-dimensional grid pattern relative to a SOEC Substrate,

                                        Page 12 of 31

and comparing the actual robot position, programmed position, and the vision system
offset data at each point. Use of the actual part in this case instead of a simple pointer is
important, as it allows the assessment to include the ability of the vision software to
identify reference features of the part in the evaluation.

 The primary finding from this testing was that the accuracy of the offset data
decreased as distance from the expected position increased. This testing was conducted
with relatively large offset distances, with positional variation of up to 75 mm in X and Y
directions, in order to assess the viability of vision methods for locating parts to pick, or
to correct placement positions. At relatively large offsets, the performance was not
promising, with up to 16.4 mm error in the Y-direction. Figure 12 plots the positional error
between vision part detection, and actual position, as a function of the distance from the
expected position. For smaller offsets between the expected position, error of less than
1 mm was noted in both directions. Figure 13 shows the vision detected positions for the
75 mm grid with 25 mm offset increments, and 10 iterations for each position. The greater
variability in the Y-direction error has yet to be understood or explained. The most
plausible explanation is that it arises from the software calibration method which
generates the distance to pixel correlation in X and Y, or to the ability of the software tool
to identify the exact edges of reference features. The curvature of the SOEC substrate
can also play a role due to the focal distance effect on the positional calibration.

 In the interest of reducing the cost and complexity of the robotic cells, and
minimizing cycle time, the team developed a simpler solution. Using pins affixed to the
gripper pallet to position the part positively in two dimensions before picking, then
offsetting to a known pick location, the need to adjust placement position can be
eliminated. The part shifting motion is accomplished in less than 2 seconds, so the cycle
time impact is likely less than that required for a machine vision correction approach.
Trials with this cost effective, simple EOAT were very successful, and allowed for building
of multiple 80-cell test stacks and allowed operation cycle times to be assessed with
improved accuracy and confidence.

 ii. Magazine Design

 High-volume automated manufacturing of SOEC stacks and cells requires a
combination of continuous and batch operations, between which it may be necessary to
accumulate buffers of parts to ensure continuous supply to down-stream operations. For
the scope of this project, this accumulation occurs at the beginning of the manufacturing
line. An approach common in the printed circuit board (PCB) industry for part
accumulation is the use of a magazine, a rack system with precise positioning of the part,
which is designed to allow automated loading and unloading. The advantage of this
approach is the density at which parts which can be stored, when compared to other
solutions such as vertical accumulators or accumulating conveyors.

 The magazine system evaluated for the beginning of the SOEC manufacturing line
was modified from a standard PCB design and produced by Hary Manufacturing
Incorporated (Lebanon, NJ, USA). The standard design was modified to provide open
bottom/top plates for access to load and unload with a 6-axis industrial or collaborative

                                         Page 14 of 31

robot, and dimensional changes to fit the SOEC substrates. Figure 14 shows the initial
design. The shelf width (6.4 mm) and spacing (19 mm) proved adequate to allow robotic
loading and un-loading. The basic concept was demonstrated to be suitable for the SOEC
application, however, several modifications were identified to improve reliability. First, the
need for a sensor array to detect parts out of position, or incorrectly oriented was
identified. With the asymmetrical nature of the SOEC substrate, reverse orientation in
the magazine could result in a collision when unloading, and damage to the part. An
array of three photo-electric sensors was demonstrated to verify the location and
orientation of parts in the magazine. The next modification required would be the addition
of doors on the front and back to retain parts during transport of the magazines. This
ensures that the parts do not shift out of the allowable position for unloading. Finally, it
will be necessary to fit precision aligning features to the base of the magazine, so the
position relative to the robot is adequately repeatable for picking of parts.

 The design of robotic manufacturing cells must first take into consideration the
safety implications of the process, parts, and other machines in the cell. The task-based
hazard analysis approach specified in the Robotics Industry Association (RIA) technical

approach and mitigation of all associated hazards identified. Additionally, the governing
standard for collaborative robots (ISO TS 15066) includes the workpiece as a factor which
must be considered when determining appropriate protective measures. In the case of
SOEC manufacturing, the thin profile and sharp corners of the SOEC substrate are such
that any power and force limit setting capable of moving the part and EOAT would also
be capable of causing injury if the corner or edge of the part were to strike the operator.

using a collaborative robot for the line loading and stacking station were limited, in terms
of its implications for the complexity and cost of the required safety guarding. This being
the case, the greater speed and payload of the industrial robot outweighed any remaining
benefit of the collaborative robot. This is especially true when considering the benefits of
using a dual-position EOAT configuration for improved cycle time, which more than
doubles the required payload, and increases the required robot reach for a given cell
layout. We have tentatively selected the IRB-4600 2.05m reach from ABB for both the
stacking and line loading station.

bolting of stack compression straps after sealing, kitting hardware for SOEC system

other collaborative robots may also be beneficial for some applications. As an example,
we were able to measure stack height using a force-search function of the UR-10e robot.

 c. Manufacturing Traceability
 i.  Part Traceability Background

 Maintaining traceability of solid oxide electrolyzer cells throughout their
manufacturing processes is critical to preserve the highest quality standards and process
control. Moreover, loss of cell traceability in the context of highly automated and high-
volume production can lead loss of productivity and reduced production line capacity as
un-traceable or unidentifiable parts will require special attention and interventions.
Therefore, 100% traceability throughout the whole manufacturing process is targeted.

 Historically, 2D matrix code (machine readable) and alpha numerical code (human
readable) have been engraved using different methods such as dot-peening or laser
engraving on non-active areas of our cells, typically on features designed to hold the parts
during processing. However, this approach has translated into less-than-optimal
traceability at our prototyping facility in Malta, NY, due to the multiple challenges
associated with the nature of the part, including the metallic support composition,
symmetry, shape, as well as the manufacturing processes used in the fabrication of the
cells that leads to degradation of the code as the cells are being processed. The current
process results in base read rates of 40-60% of the DataMatrix code with a Cognex
DataMan 8600 handheld scanners. Higher rates were possible only with extreme
difficulty when manipulating the hand-held scanner and using auxiliary lighting. Trials with
digital processing imaging and deep learning technologies have not improved the
capability. The current marking location frequently resulted in poor readability of 2d codes
due to poor and variable contrast caused by exposure to process conditions. Figure 18
illustrates some examples of degraded codes.

 d. Manufacturing Hardware and Data Acquisition Process Flow
 i.  Design Manufacturing Process Flow

 The manufacturing process flow is shown in Figure 19. The updated process flow
integrates the process and automation development results and discoveries previously
discussed. The manufacturing process flow defines and categorizes the data that will be
collected from each operation. This establishes the data handshake and PLC integration
boundaries with respect to each operation and sub-operation. The line is comprised of
the following operations:

  Line loading and QC Station (Op. 1100)
      Anode Print (Op. 1200)
  Contact Print (Op. 1300)
      Glass Print (Op. 1400)

                                        Page 22 of 31

  Dielectric Deposition (Op. 1500)
  Cell accumulation (Op. 1600)
  Stack building (Op. 1700)

 Figure 19. The refined operations and process flow.

 The parts are delivered to the manufacturing line after a proprietary electrolyte
thermal spray operation. In operation 1100, a line loading station utilizes a pick-and-place
robot. The part will be scanned with a 2D data matrix code reader and the part Serial ID
will be logged into the data acquisition system. The industrial robot at this station will also
transfer the part to quality control. In this station, the height and position of cell
characteristics will be measured, as well as the overall part flatness using Keyence LJ-X
series laser scanners. (Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Following this
measurement, the part will be transferred to a leak testing system, which measures the
electrolyte to metal-substrate seal quality. Data for the metrology measurements and
leak testing will be logged into the MES system and attributed to the serial number of
each part.

 The part is then transferred to the screen printing processes (operations 1200,
1300, and 1400). Three coatings are applied via ceramic paste, the anode, anode contact,
and the glass seal. Although the process parameters will be unique for each paste and
print, the general processes for these coatings will be similar: print, analyze, dry, analyze.
Each coating will be inspected for any inconsistency, contingency in the coating layer
through a machine vision inspection system. Initial trials indicate the Keyence CV-X492
vision system with 21MP camera and LumiTrax lighting system (Keyence Corporation,
Osaka, Japan) is suitable for the required inspection and measurement operations. The
coatings are then dried in a conveyor oven and then thickness is measured after drying.

                                         Page 23 of 31

As the positioning and dimension of the glass seal are critical to sealing of the stack, the
width and position of the glass print is also measured using a vision system.

 The part is sprayed with a dielectric coating (operation 1500) on the back side of
the part. This operation will require additional material handling to “flip” the part to expose
the opposite cell surface. After deposition, the part is then dried and inspected for voids,
uniformity, and position at an additional vision system station. After operation 1500, the
cell has completed the total manufacturing process and is a “completed” part.

 Completed parts are then transferred to the stacking station, using an indexing
accumulating conveyor. The industrial robot of the stacking station picks parts from this
conveyor and a query of the MES database is conducted concurrently, to determine the
proper disposition of the part. Parts with a “Failed” status are placed to a rack for
inspection and review. The balance of parts which meet all specifications for CTQ
parameters are placed to the production stack. Stack position and stack ID are recorded
to the MES system for each part serial number to ensure a traceability link to the stack
level.

 ii. Data Acquisition and Cell Disposition

 The data collection software application will be designed as central application for
defining manufacturing process flows, identify key quality data parameters by each
operation, and collect the data at each checkpoint in the process. The application will run
on the server and will be integrating with equipment controls and data collection by means
of PLC communication protocols. The application will collect the data in database
repository running on server. This will help with generating the Product genealogy
(history of key data, process parameters) against each Serial ID. Based on process
needs CMES SL – Delmia Apriso (Dassault Systems, Long Beach, CA) is the identified
software application to run the MES data acquisition for Product quality and traceability
and Ignition (Inductive Automation, Folsom, CA, USA) will be used as SCADA application
for process monitoring.

 Data to be collected has been categorized as detailed below:

 Critical to Quality (CTQ) – These are the parameters used to define the quality of
the cell and decides the ‘Pass’ or ‘Fail’ status for the part based on specification limits.
This data is attributed to the specific part serial number and is recorded in the CMES
system.

 Process Parameters – These are the process recipes that is setup for each
individual equipment. Monitoring and control of these process parameters is critical to get
the required quality for the part. This data is recorded periodically and captured in the
Ignition SCADA system.

                                         Page 24 of 31

 Input Parameters – These parameters are used to calculate CTQ parameters.
This data is recorded to the CMES system, for later look-up by processes where the input
data is needed for CTQ calculation.

 Traceability – The cell serial ID (2D code) is used as traceability measure and build
the part genealogy. Reading of the 2D code enables attribution of CTQ data to the correct
part with certainty. Part serial ID verification is used at process steps where CTQ and
Input Parameter data will be written to CMES

 The Part ‘Pass or ‘Fail’ status is determined by the value logged against the each
identified CTQ parameter by each operation. Each operation have CTQs identified with
necessary specification limit. After processing the part at respective operation, it will be
inspected for CTQ parameters. If the values are within specification limit the part is ‘Pass’,
otherwise it is ‘Failed’.

 The data against each Serial ID is logged in CMES SL and it also maintains ‘Pass’
or ‘Fail’ status against each part. Though the part is ‘Failed’, the part is continued through
the Manufacturing process. Except at the Stacking Operation station the part Serial ID
(2D code) is scanned, and that Serial ID is run down through the database to check for
any ‘Failed’ flag. If the part is ‘Failed’ in any of the previous manufacturing operations by
not being with CTQ parameters specification limits, then part is put under the ‘Scrap’ bin.
Failed parts are separated towards the end of the line and manufacturing line is not
interrupted due to any part failure in-between. This keeps the data sanity through
maintaining the sequence of the part throughout manufacturing process flow and efficient
way to prevent ‘Failed’ parts ending up in the stack assembly.

 These tasks will have a large impact on the ability to meet the manufacturing
targets. For example, reducing the number of glass prints from double to single would
reduce the amount of required equipment, such as a screen printer, a weighing station,
and a drying oven, which in turn would reduce capital investment and space necessary

method of film thickness could provide a means to replace the current cost-intensive

 The requirement for glass seal thickness is currently achieved by printing twice,
which requires two sets of a screen-printing system (two screen printers, three weighing
stations, two drying ovens, and two vision inspection cameras). Our approach is to, by
collaborating with a screen-manufacturing company, develop screens that could yield the
required print thickness using a single print. The key design point for the new screens is
increasing the thickness of screen mesh wires and emulsion, for which a schematic is
shown below.

 The current screens used for the double-print glass process are comprised of
mesh wires that are 160 μm thick and an emulsion of 80 μm thick to yield a combined

the bottom right-hand side on the Figure 1, is composed of mesh wires of 160 μm thick

 Currently, the thickness of films in SOECs, such as the anode, contact, and glass
seal, are estimated using the specific wet print weight (for example, 20 mg/cm2),
determined by measurement before and after screen printing. To ensure reproducibility,
each cell is measured, and defects are identified when the print weight is out of
specification. Although the weight-gain method is useful indication of the robustness of
overall screen-printing process and average coating thickness of a given layer, it does
not provide information about potential coating thickness variations across the part, or the
presence of localized defects. Also, the cost of weighing stations is expected to be
considerably higher, which adds risk to meeting the target capital investment and quality
control inspection rate. Thus, we are evaluating the risk and impact of using a smaller
number of weighing stations, in combination with materials and drying assumptions, to
lower capital investment. In addition, a task to evaluate the feasibility of developing new
methods to measure the thickness of films and detect defects in the coated layers has
been initiated, including using 2D or 3D laser scanning for thickness evaluation, and
visual inspection for defect detection.
 A list of possible defects and their potential impact on cell performance is being
generated and will help guide the development of the new visual inspection methods. For
example, a system is under development to have a function to detect defects in screen
prints, such as pin holes, scratches and misalignment using a vision inspection (Keyence
CV-X system with a CA-H500MX camera; see Figure 2 below).
 Separately, a laser-scan unit (using Keyence LJ-X8200 sensor heads) to scan the
surface height profile of cells is also under development. The laser-scan unit is already
being used routinely to quantify functional features on the metal-support. It was tested to
measure the thickness of glass seal prints on cells. However, the laser unit results in
measured values that are 40% lower than those obtained using a contact surface
profilometer (Mitutoyo SJ-410), demonstrated in Figure 3. The smaller values by laser
scan unit are speculated to arise from the triangulation measuring principle. Since the
glass powders in the print have local transparency to laser scan, to certain extent, the
values by laser scan may provide smaller values than the actual ones. Based on the
above speculation, it can be assumed that, after proper calibration between a laser scan
unit and a surface profilometer unit, the laser scan unit could replace the current cost-
intensive weighing stations for glass print. Current estimates suggest a 44% decrease in

cost for this quality control measurement. The feasibility of calibrating the laser-scan for
measuring the glass print will be pursued next quarter.

 On the other hand, using a laser scan method on the other films (anode and

Status:
 The relative importance of each manufacturing criteria on product cost were
determined. The criteria with the highest impact on cost is the cycle time of cell
production. Namely, the time at which each cell is produced, on average, with each
sequential piece of equipment. Lower cycle times allow for more capacity using the
same space and equipment. First pass inspection reliability, or the percent probability
of being able to read a cell’s bar code for traceability, is also an important manufacturing
criteria. The ability to reliability read a cell ID reduces cycle time and avoids operator
intervention (reducing direct labor). Both cycle time and first pass inspection reliability
were both ranked as a “high” impact on product cost. Direct labor was determined to
have a medium impact on product cost. More automation and, subsequently, less direct
labor lower the cost of goods sold. Finally, the manufacturing capital and space were
both determined to have a low relative importance on product cost compared to the
other criteria. Both were determined by assuming a “cost of capital,” 12-year straight
line depreciation, and cost of real estate.

 The quality function deployment (QFD) process determined the importance of
brainstormed functional equipment requirements. The four estimated most expensive
equipment systems were the quality leak tester, screen printer, low-pressure high-
volume aerosol sprayer, and the unloading and transfer of cells from thermal spray to
the quality equipment. The importance of functional requirements for the equipment
was determined by using the manufacturing criteria and consensus of impact of
functional requirements on those criteria. The most important functional needs for the
equipment, in general, were: reliability of loading, unloading and aligning the parts;
equipment strategy for buffering and queuing parts; ability of equipment to communicate
with the SCADA and MES systems; and reliability of the equipment to read the bar code
of each part.

Products:

  1. Serialization of Solid Oxide Electrochemical Device. Corresponding Patent /
 Application Number: 19/449,730. Inventors: Luc Leblanc, Todd Striker.
 Description: The invention teaches a method to maintain readable serialization of
 solid oxide electrochemical devices, such as SOFC and SOEC, throughout the
 manufacturing process. Key Technology Areas: solid oxide fuel cell, solid oxide
 electrolysis cell. Search Keywords: solid oxide fuel cell, solid oxide electrolysis
 cell, electrolysis, electrochemical device, serialization, barcode. Detailed
 Description: A serialization on a coating that is applied to a component of an
 electrochemical device. Business Reason for Pursuing: The article is the only
 method where the cell can be read through the manufacturing process in a reliable
 way. This will be necessary for cell traceability during high volume production such
 that quality control, process control, and quality assurance can assigned to each
 unique cell provide low cycle time and low cost. This ensures that each cell can be
 certiﬁed prior to stacking the sub-assembly for the system. Novel Features: An
 article providing a serialization on a coated component of an electrochemical
 device. The serialization is durable and readable through the manufacturing
 process.
