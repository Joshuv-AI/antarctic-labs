Backstepping Control of First-Order Hyperbolic Equations in Arbitrary Dimensions with Non-Trapping Characteristics
Mohamed Camil Belhadjoudja

Abstract
This paper presents a backstepping approach for the boundary control of first-order hyperbolic equations with spatially varying coefficients posed on domains of arbitrary dimension. The method is based on a change of variables induced by the characteristic flow of the time-invariant transport operator, transforming the original multidimensional system into a continuum of decoupled one-dimensional hyperbolic equations evolving along individual characteristic curves. A backstepping controller is then designed for each equation in the decomposition, and the resulting control laws are reassembled in the original coordinates to achieve finite-time stabilization of the full system. The framework relies on the existence of characteristic curves foliating the spatial domain, with uniformly bounded transit times (non-trapping).

Our change of variables is rooted in the method of characteristics
[
23
, Sections 2.1 and 3.2]
,
which is classically employed to solve multidimensional transport equations by
reducing them to ordinary differential equations (ODE)s whose solutions trace
the so-called characteristic curves. For
the evolution transport equation
\partial v/\partial t=a(x)\cdot\nabla v
,
these characteristic curves are defined in the space-time domain. Our
construction departs from this classical picture by discarding the time
variable in the very definition of characteristics: we consider the
characteristic curves of the
time-invariant
transport equation

a(x)\cdot\nabla v=0
. Each such curve is parametrized by two variables

(\sigma,\rho)
, where
\rho
identifies the curve and
\sigma
denotes the position along it. We prove that a general multidimensional
first-order hyperbolic equation of the form

\partial v/\partial t=a(x)\cdot\nabla v+(\text{destabilizing terms})

can be mapped, through the change of variables
x\leftrightarrow(\sigma,\rho)
, into a continuum of
one-dimensional first-order hyperbolic equations evolving along these
characteristic curves and expressed in the variables
(\sigma,t)
, with the
family indexed by
\rho
. The controller of
[
38
]
is then applied
to each PDE in the resulting decomposition. The sole assumption required to
conclude finite-time stability of the original multidimensional system from
that of the decomposed PDEs is that the transit time of characteristics —
the range of
\sigma
— be uniformly bounded across all characteristic
curves. This condition could fail, for example, when the velocity field generates limit cycles, vanishes in the interior of
the spatial domain, or becomes arbitrarily small along certain trajectories,
causing characteristics to stagnate.

It is worth emphasizing that the control of a continuum of hyperbolic
equations — also referred to as an ensemble of hyperbolic equations — is
not new, and this is emphatically not the contribution of the present paper.
Our contribution is to establish that the boundary control of a multidimensional
hyperbolic equation reduces, through our
change of variables, to the control of an ensemble of one-dimensional hyperbolic
equations. To the best of our knowledge, this relationship is identified here
for the first time. Prior works on the control of ensembles of hyperbolic
equations have been driven by entirely different motivations and have led to
structurally different ensembles. For instance,
[
33

34
,
35

design backstepping
controllers for ensembles of equations and subsequently discretize the
resulting kernel to obtain a controller for a large-scale finite coupled
hyperbolic system; there, the ensemble formulation is a computational tool for
managing complexity in large-scale systems. By contrast,
[
2
]
considers ensembles arising naturally from modeling: physical systems in which agents–vehicles, drivers, particles–are continuously parametrized, and whose collective dynamics are more faithfully captured by a continuum model. Beyond addressing, for the first time since the work of

[

equations, the present paper thus offers a new and fundamental motivation for
the emerging theory of control of ensembles (and ensembles of ensembles) of hyperbolic equations.

System description

This paper extends some of the results of
[
38
]
to arbitrary spatial dimensions
n\geq 1
. The equation considered in the aforementioned reference is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\lambda(x)v+g(x)v(0)

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{x}f(x,y)v(y)\,dy\qquad x\in(0,1),\ t>0,

(1)

subject to the boundary condition

\displaystyle v(1,t)=U(v(\cdot,t))\qquad t\geq 0.

(2)

Here, the function
v:[0,1]\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the state, the coefficients

,

, and

are assumed to be continuous, and
U(v(\cdot,t))\in\mathbb{R}
is the control variable.

When
g
and
f
are positive and sufficiently large, the equilibrium
\{v=0\}
for (

)–(
2
) is unstable under zero input

. One of the main contributions of
[

]
is the design of a finite-time stabilizing control law

In this section, we formulate a higher-dimensional analogue of the control system (
1
)–(
2
). Since the underlying equation is hyperbolic, particular care is required to ensure that the resulting system is well-posed. For example, the location of the control must reflect both the geometry of the domain and the direction of the
velocity field
, i.e.,
minus
the coefficient of the first-order spatial derivative term in (
1
). In one dimension, the velocity reduces to a scalar, so the control location (either at
x=0
or
x=1
) is determined solely by its sign.

2.1
Geometry, velocity, and characteristics

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, be a bounded open and connected set, that satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 1

\Omega
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}

[
23
, Page 626]
.

\bullet

Our system will be defined on
\Omega
.

Since
\Omega
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, it admits a unique unit outward normal
\nu:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\partial\Omega
is the boundary of
\Omega
.

A first-order hyperbolic equation on
\Omega
describes (among other phenomena) the transport of a quantity, such as a concentration, a density, or a temperature, through the domain
\Omega
with a given velocity field
-a
, where

\displaystyle a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\mapsto a(x)=(a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),...,a_{n}(x)).

The following regularity assumption is imposed on
a
.

Assumption 2

a
is lipschitz.

\bullet

The sign of
\nu\cdot a
determines whether the transported quantity enters the domain, leaves, or travels
tangentially along
\partial\Omega
: if
\nu(x)\cdot a(x)<0
, the velocity field points outward at
x

and the quantity is leaving
\Omega
(recall that the velocity field is by convention
-a
, not
a
); if
\nu(x)\cdot a(x)>0
, it points inward and
the quantity is entering
\Omega
; and if
\nu(x)\cdot a(x)=0
, the velocity is
tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
x
and the quantity does not cross the boundary there.
Accordingly, we define the following subsets of
\partial\Omega
:

•

Inflow boundary

\Gamma^{+}=\{x\in\partial\Omega\ :\ \nu(x)\cdot a(x)>0\},

•

Ouflow boundary

\Gamma^{-}=\{x\in\partial\Omega\ :\ \nu(x)\cdot a(x)<0\},

•

Tangential boundary

\Gamma^{o}=\{x\in\partial\Omega\ :\ \nu(x)\cdot a(x)=0\}.

Note that
\Gamma^{+}
,
\Gamma^{-}
, and
\Gamma^{o}
are mutually disjoint by construction and together cover the entire boundary:

\partial\Omega=\Gamma^{+}\cup\Gamma^{-}\cup\Gamma^{o}.

We impose the following standard assumption
[
43
]
.

Assumption 3
(Transversality)

\Gamma^{o}
has zero Hausdorff measure.

\bullet

We now define the characteristics associated to
a
. For each
x\in\bar{\Omega}
, where
\bar{\Omega}
denotes the closure of
\Omega
with respect to the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
, the characteristic curve
passing through
x
is the unique complete classical solution

s\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\phi(s;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi}{ds}=a(\phi),\quad\phi(0;x)=x,

(3)

whose existence and uniqueness follow from the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem, applicable
here by Assumption
2
. Its
entry time

\tau^{+}(x)
and

exit time

\tau^{-}(x)
are defined as

\displaystyle\tau^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\{s\geq 0\ :\ \phi(s;x)\in\Gamma^{+}\},

(4)

\displaystyle\tau^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{s\leq 0\ :\ \phi(s;x)\in\Gamma^{-}\},

(5)

whenever these quantities exist.

We impose the following key assumption on the characteristic curves. A similar condition appears in the theory of geometric optics, where the characteristic curves are the light rays (the solutions to the Eikonal equation), and where it is referred to as the
non-trapping condition
: every ray must exit the medium in finite time
[
41
]
.

Assumption 4
(Non-trapping characteristics)

For all
x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}
,
\tau^{+}(x)
and
\tau^{-}(x)
exist and are finite. Moreover,

\displaystyle T_{\max}=\sup_{x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}}\{\tau^{+}(x)-\tau^{-}(x)\}<+\infty.

\bullet

2.2
First-order hyperbolic equations on
\Omega

As an extension of (
1
)-(
2
) to higher dimensions, we consider on
\Omega
the first-order hyperbolic equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle~a(x)\cdot\nabla v+\lambda(x)v+g(x)v(\rho(x))

\displaystyle~+\int_{D(x)}f(x,y)v(y)\ d\ell(y),

(6)

where
v:\bar{\Omega}\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is the state,
\rho(x)
is the
recirculation point

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\phi(\tau^{-}(x);x),

(7)

D(x)
is the
downstream curve

\displaystyle D(x)=\{\phi(s;x)\ :\ \tau^{-}(x)\leq s\leq 0\},

(8)

and
d\ell(y)
is the arc length element along
D(x)
. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{D(x)}f(x,y)v(y)\ d\ell(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau^{-}(x)}^{0}f(x,\phi(s;x))v(\phi(s;x))|a(\phi(s;x))|\ ds.

(9)

The coefficients
\lambda,g:\bar{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f:\bar{\Omega}\times\bar{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 5

For each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
, the maps

\displaystyle\sigma\in[0,\tau^{+}(\rho)]

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda(\phi(\sigma;\rho)),

\displaystyle\sigma\in[0,\tau^{+}(\rho)]

\displaystyle\mapsto g(\phi(\sigma;\rho)),

\displaystyle(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in[0,\tau^{+}(\rho)]^{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto f(\phi(\sigma;\rho),\phi(\sigma^{\prime};\rho)),

are continuous.

\bullet

Remark 2.1
.

Note that since
\phi(\cdot;\rho)
is continuous for each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
, then Assumption
5
holds trivially if, e.g.,
\lambda
,
g
, and

are continuous on their domains of definition.

\bullet

Finally, we impose the boundary condition

\displaystyle v(z,t)=U(v(\cdot,t),z)\qquad z\in\Gamma^{+},\ t\geq 0,

(10)

where
U(v(\cdot,t),t)\in\mathbb{R}
is a control variable to be designed to stabilize

Remark 2.2
.

Instead of (
6

3
Concept of solutions and reduction to a continuum of one-dimensional first-order hyperbolic
equations

As mentioned above, the objective is to achieve finite-time stabilization of

\{v=0\}
. To state this objective precisely, we first need to define carefully
the concept of solutions under consideration. A natural first candidate is the
notion of classical solution.

Definition 3.1

(Classical solution)
.

Let
v_{o}\in C(\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o})
. Given a feedback law
U
, a
classical solution
to
(
6
)–(
10
) starting from

is any function

v\in C((\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o})\times[0,+\infty))\cap C^{1}(\Omega\times(0,+\infty))
that satisfies
(
6
) for all
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,+\infty)
,
(
10
) for all
(z,t)\in\Gamma^{+}\times[0,+\infty)
, and

v(x,0)=v_{o}(x)
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}
.

\bullet

Let us suppose for now that we are given a classical solution
v
to
(
6
)–(
10
). Roughly speaking, we will show that the restriction of
v
to each characteristic curve solves a one-dimensional equation of the form (
1
)–(
2
). The key idea is to first
straighten out
the characteristics of
(
6
): rather than working directly on
\Omega
with the spatial coordinates

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, we introduce a change of variables that replaces
x\in\Omega
by a
pair
(\sigma,\rho)
, where
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
identifies

which
characteristic curve passes through
x
, and
\sigma\geq 0
records

how long
the flow has been traveling along that curve from
\rho
to
x
. In these new coordinates, the transport term
a(x)\cdot\nabla v
in
(
6
) reduces to a simple partial derivative with respect to
\sigma
, and the
full
n
-dimensional system (
6
)–(
10
) decouples into a continuum of
one-dimensional systems of precisely the same form as (
1
)–(
2
),
each one evolving along a characteristic curve parameterized by
\rho
.

We now make this construction precise. For each
x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}
, we define the

time to exit

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=-\tau^{-}(x)\geq 0,

(11)

that is,
\sigma(x)
is the time the characteristic flow takes to travel from
x
to
\rho(x)=\phi(\tau^{-}(x);x)\in\Gamma^{-}
. In particular,
\sigma(x)=0
if and only if

x\in\Gamma^{-}
, and
more generally
\sigma(x)\in[0,T(x)]
, where
T(x)
is the transit time of the characteristic passing through
x
,
defined as
T(x)=\tau^{+}(x)-\tau^{-}(x)>0
.

Note that, since by Assumption
2
the ODE (
3
) admits unique
solutions, characteristic curves cannot intersect: if two solutions of (
3
)
coincide at any single time, they must coincide for all times. Therefore
each point
x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}
belongs to exactly one characteristic, and the
transit time is constant along that characteristic:
T(\phi(s;\rho))=T(\rho)=\tau^{+}(\rho)
for all
s\in[0,T(\rho)]
and all
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
.

Next, we introduce the
transformed domain

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}=\left\{(\sigma,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Gamma^{-}\ :\ 0\leq\sigma\leq T(\rho)\right\},

(12)

with
T(\rho)
the transit time of the characteristic passing through
\rho
, and the map

\displaystyle\Psi:\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}\to\hat{\Omega},\qquad x\mapsto\Psi(x)=\left(\sigma(x),\,\rho(x)\right).

(13)

The map
\Psi
is bijective. Indeed, given any
(\sigma,\rho)\in\hat{\Omega}
,
the point
x=\phi(\sigma;\rho)
satisfies
\rho(x)=\rho
and
\sigma(x)=\sigma
, so
\Psi
is surjective. Injectivity follows
from the non-intersection of characteristics: if
\Psi(x_{1})=\Psi(x_{2})
, then

x_{1}
and
x_{2}
lie on the same characteristic and reach the exit point
\rho(x_{1})=\rho(x_{2})

at the same time, hence
x_{1}=x_{2}
. The inverse of
\Psi
is therefore
\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)=\phi(\sigma;\rho)
.

We now use
\Psi
to rewrite (
6
)–(
10
) in the coordinates

(\sigma,\rho)\in\hat{\Omega}
. To this end, we introduce the transformed state

u:\hat{\Omega}\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle u(\sigma,\rho,t)=v\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho),\,t\right)=v\!\left(\phi(\sigma;\rho),\,t\right).

(14)

In particular,
u(0,\rho,t)=v(\rho,t)
is
the value of
v
at the exit point
\rho
, and
u(T(\rho),\rho,t)=v(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),t)
is the value of
v
at the corresponding entry point on

\Gamma^{+}
.

The following main result shows that
u
satisfies a continuum of equations of exactly the
same form as (
1
)–(
2
).

Theorem 3.2
.

The function
u
defined by (
14
) satisfies, for each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
and for all
\sigma\in(0,T(\rho))
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial u}{\partial\sigma}+\hat{\lambda}(\sigma,\rho)\,u(\sigma,\rho,t)+\hat{g}(\sigma,\rho)\,u(0,\rho,t)

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{\sigma}\hat{f}(\sigma,\rho,\sigma^{\prime})\,u(\sigma^{\prime},\rho,t)\ d\sigma^{\prime},

(15)

together with the boundary condition

\displaystyle u\!\left(T(\rho),\rho,t\right)=U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),\,t\right)\qquad t\geq 0,

(16)

where the transformed coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}(\sigma,\rho)

\displaystyle=\lambda\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right),

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\sigma,\rho)

\displaystyle=g\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right),

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\sigma,\rho,\sigma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho),\,\Psi^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime},\rho)\right)\left|a\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime},\rho)\right)\right|.

Conversely, if
u(\cdot,\rho,\cdot)
satisfies (
15
)–(
16
) for every

\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
, and if
u\circ\Psi\in C((\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o})\times[0,+\infty))\cap C^{1}(\Omega\times(0,+\infty))

is a classical solution of

6
)–(

Proof 3.3
.

We transform each term of (
6
) separately after the substitution

.

Step 1: time derivative.
Differentiating both sides of (

) with respect to

, and using the

does not depend on

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\sigma,\rho,t)=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho),t\right).

(17)

Step 2: transport term.
We wish to show that

a(x)\cdot\nabla v(x,t)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\sigma}(\sigma,\rho,t)

at
x=\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)
. To this end, we differentiate the function
s\mapsto v(\phi(s;x),t)
, to obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\,v(\phi(s;x),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\phi(s;x)}{ds}\cdot\nabla v(\phi(s;x),t)

\displaystyle=a(\phi(s;x))\cdot\nabla v(\phi(s;x),t).

Evaluating (

\displaystyle a(x)\cdot\nabla v(x,t)=\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}v(\phi(s;x),t).

(19)

We now set

in

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}v\!\left(\phi\!\left(s;\,\phi(\sigma;\rho)\right),t\right).

(20)

We simplify the argument of

using

\displaystyle\phi\!\left(s;\,\phi(\sigma;\rho)\right)=\phi(s+\sigma;\rho).

(21)

Substituting (
21
) into (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}v\!\left(\phi\!\left(s;\,\phi(\sigma;\rho)\right),t\right)=\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}v\!\left(\phi(s+\sigma;\rho),t\right).

By definition (

),
v(\phi(s+\sigma;\rho),t)=u(s+\sigma,\rho,t)
.
Substituting and differentiating at
s=0
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}u(s+\sigma,\rho,t)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\sigma}(\sigma,\rho,t).

(22)

Combining (
19
)–(
22
), we conclude

\displaystyle a\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right)\cdot\nabla v\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho),t\right)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\sigma}(\sigma,\rho,t).

(23)

Step 3: reaction term.
By the definition of
\hat{\lambda}
and the relation (
14
), we have
directly

\displaystyle\lambda\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right)v\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho),t\right)=\hat{\lambda}(\sigma,\rho)\,u(\sigma,\rho,t).

(24)

Step 4: recirculation term.
We need to transform
g(x)\,v(\rho(x),t)
at
x=\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)
.
We first identify the recirculation point. Since
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
is the exit point of the characteristic through

\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)
, we have

\displaystyle\rho\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right)=\rho.

(25)

Next, the value of

at
\rho
is, by (
14
) evaluated
at
\sigma=0
,

\displaystyle v(\rho,t)=v\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(0,\rho),t\right)=u(0,\rho,t).

(26)

Combining (

26
) with the definition of
\hat{g}
,

\displaystyle g\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right)v\!\left(\rho\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)\right),t\right)=\hat{g}(\sigma,\rho)\,u(0,\rho,t).

Step 5: nonlocal term.
We next transform the term

\displaystyle\int_{\tau^{-}(x)}^{0}f(x,\phi(s;x))\,v(\phi(s;x),t)\,|a(\phi(s;x))|\ ds.

(27)

We set
x=\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho)=\phi(\sigma;\rho)
, so that
\tau^{-}(x)=-\sigma
and the integration interval is
[-\sigma,0]
. Using (
21
), we have

\phi(s;x)=\phi(s;\phi(\sigma;\rho))=\phi(s+\sigma;\rho).

Then, we perform the change of variables
\sigma^{\prime}=s+\sigma
,
which maps
s\in[-\sigma,0]
to
\sigma^{\prime}\in[0,\sigma]
and gives

\phi(s;x)=\phi(\sigma^{\prime};\rho)=\Psi^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime},\rho),\ \ v(\phi(s;x),t)=u(\sigma^{\prime},\rho,t),

and
ds=d\sigma^{\prime}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\tau^{-}(x)}^{0}f(x,\phi(s;x))\,v(\phi(s;x),t)\,|a(\phi(s;x))|\ ds

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{\sigma}\hat{f}(\sigma,\rho,\sigma^{\prime})\,u(\sigma^{\prime},\rho,t)\ d\sigma^{\prime}.

(28)

Conclusion.
Summing the results of Steps 1–5 and using the fact that
v
satisfies (
6
),
we obtain (
15
). The boundary condition (
16
) follows directly from
(
10
) evaluated at
z=\phi(T(\rho);\rho)\in\Gamma^{+}
.

The converse is immediate by reversing each step and using the assumed regularity of
u\circ\Psi
.

)

.

Remark 3.6
.

By the converse

, any solution in the sense of Definition

is a classical solution. In other words, characteristic solutions that are regular enough must be classical solutions, and classical solutions are necessarily characteristic solutions, which justifies the proposed concept of solutions. It is worth noting that defining solutions to first-order hyperbolic equations through formulas derived from the method of characteristics is not new; see
[

, Definition 3.1]
,
[

, Definition 4.1]
and
[

, Definition 3.1]
for related approaches in the context of controllability and stability analysis of systems of one-dimensional hyperbolic equations. The novelty of our formulation lies in the fact that the resulting characterisation is expressed in terms of PDEs that are suitable for PDE-based control techniques, such as backstepping.

\bullet

Given the characterization in Theorem
3.2
and Definition

for all

and all

beyond a certain finite time, reduces to the finite-time stabilization of each PDE in the continuum representation. It is precisely here that Assumption

4
Backstepping controller

For each fixed
\rho
, we have a first-order hyperbolic equation on the interval
[0,T(\rho)]
, which is
precisely the class of systems studied in
[
38
]
. The idea is therefore to apply, for each fixed
\rho
, the backstepping controller of
[
38
]
.

First, we normalize the interval
[0,T(\rho)]
to
[0,1]
. To do so, we introduce the rescaled spatial and time variables

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{T(\rho)}\in[0,1],\qquad\bar{t}=\frac{t}{T(\rho)}\geq 0,

(29)

and define the normalized state

\displaystyle\bar{v}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})=u\!\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},\,\rho,\,T(\rho)\bar{t}\right).

(30)

To find the equation satisfied by
\bar{v}
, we substitute into (
15
). Since

u(\sigma,\rho,t)=\bar{v}(\sigma/T(\rho),\rho,t/T(\rho))
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{T(\rho)}\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{t}},\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{1}{T(\rho)}\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}.

For the nonlocal term, we change variables
\sigma^{\prime}=T(\rho)\bar{y}
in the
integral, giving
d\sigma^{\prime}=T(\rho)\,d\bar{y}
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\sigma,\rho,\sigma^{\prime})\,u(\sigma^{\prime},\rho,t)\,d\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=T(\rho)\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}\hat{f}\!\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},\rho,T(\rho)\bar{y}\right)\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y}.

Substituting into (
15
) and multiplying through by
T(\rho)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{t}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}+\bar{\lambda}(\bar{\sigma},\rho)\,\bar{v}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})+\bar{g}(\bar{\sigma},\rho)\,\bar{v}(0,\rho,\bar{t})

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}\bar{f}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})\,\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y},

(31)

where the rescaled coefficients are defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}(\bar{\sigma},\rho)

\displaystyle=T(\rho)\,\hat{\lambda}\!\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},\rho\right),

\displaystyle\bar{g}(\bar{\sigma},\rho)

\displaystyle=T(\rho)\,\hat{g}\!\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},\rho\right),

\displaystyle\bar{f}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})

\displaystyle=T(\rho)^{2}\,\hat{f}\!\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},\rho,T(\rho)\bar{y}\right).

The boundary condition (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle\bar{v}(1,\rho,\bar{t})=U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),\,T(\rho)\bar{t}\right).

(32)

Next, we absorb the reaction term into the nonlocal term. Concretely, we define

\displaystyle\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)=\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}\bar{\lambda}(\xi,\rho)\,d\xi,

(33)

and the new state

\displaystyle w(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})=e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)}\,\bar{v}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t}).

(34)

Note that
\Lambda(0,\rho)=0
, so
w(0,\rho,\bar{t})=\bar{v}(0,\rho,\bar{t})
.
To derive the equation for
w
, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=e^{\Lambda}\,\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{t}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\lambda}\,e^{\Lambda}\,\bar{v}+e^{\Lambda}\,\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}=\bar{\lambda}\,w+e^{\Lambda}\,\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}},

so that

e^{\Lambda}\,\frac{\partial\bar{v}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}-\bar{\lambda}\,w.

Multiplying (
31
) by
e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)}
and substituting,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{t}}=

\displaystyle~\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}-\bar{\lambda}\,w\right)+\bar{\lambda}\,w+\bar{g}\,e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)}\,\bar{v}(0,\rho,\bar{t})

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}\bar{f}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})\,e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)}\,\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y}.

The
\bar{\lambda}\,w
terms cancel. Using
\bar{v}(0,\rho,\bar{t})=w(0,\rho,\bar{t})
and

e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)}\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})=e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)-\Lambda(\bar{y},\rho)}w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{t}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}+G(\bar{\sigma},\rho)\,w(0,\rho,\bar{t})

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}F(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})\,w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y},

(35)

where

\displaystyle G(\bar{\sigma},\rho)

\displaystyle=\bar{g}(\bar{\sigma},\rho)\,e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)},

\displaystyle F(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})

\displaystyle=\bar{f}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{y})\,e^{\Lambda(\bar{\sigma},\rho)-\Lambda(\bar{y},\rho)}.

The boundary condition (
32
) becomes

\displaystyle w(1,\rho,\bar{t})=e^{\Lambda(1,\rho)}\,U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),\,T(\rho)\bar{t}\right).

(36)

Finally, we define the backstepping transformation

\displaystyle\bar{w}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})=w(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})-\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)\,w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y},

where the kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot;\rho)
(parametrized by
\rho
) is to be determined on the triangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\left\{(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y})\in[0,1]^{2}\ :\ 0\leq\bar{y}\leq\bar{\sigma}\leq 1\right\},

such that
\bar{w}
solves the
target system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial\bar{\sigma}},

(37)

\displaystyle\bar{w}(1,\rho,\bar{t})

\displaystyle=0.

(38)

The solution to the target system verifies

\displaystyle\bar{w}(\bar{\sigma},\rho,\bar{t})=0\qquad\forall\,\bar{\sigma}\in[0,1],\ \forall\,\bar{t}\geq 1.

(39)

The kernel equations are the same as in
[
38
]
, namely,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}+\frac{\partial k}{\partial\bar{y}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bar{y}}^{\bar{\sigma}}k(\bar{\sigma},\xi;\rho)\,F(\xi,\rho,\bar{y})\,d\xi-F,

(40)

\displaystyle k(\bar{\sigma},0;\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)\,G(\bar{y},\rho)\,d\bar{y}-G,

(41)

and they admit, for each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
, a unique solution
k(\cdot,\cdot;\rho)\in C^{1}(\mathcal{T})
.

To enforce
\bar{w}(1,\rho,\bar{t})=0
, it suffices to let

\displaystyle w(1,\rho,\bar{t})=\int_{0}^{1}k(1,\bar{y};\rho)\,w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y}.

(42)

Combining (
42
) with (
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{\Lambda(1,\rho)}\,U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),\,T(\rho)\bar{t}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}k(1,\bar{y};\rho)\,w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y}.

Substituting
w(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})=e^{\Lambda(\bar{y},\rho)}\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})
and dividing by

e^{\Lambda(1,\rho)}
,

\displaystyle U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),\,T(\rho)\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{1}k(1,\bar{y};\rho)\,e^{\Lambda(\bar{y},\rho)-\Lambda(1,\rho)}\,\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})\,d\bar{y}.

(43)

We now revert to the original variables. Setting
t=T(\rho)\bar{t}
and

\sigma=T(\rho)\bar{y}
in (
43
), so that

d\bar{y}=d\sigma/T(\rho)
and
\bar{v}(\bar{y},\rho,\bar{t})=u(\sigma,\rho,t)
by (
30
), we obtain

\displaystyle U\!\left(\phi(T(\rho);\rho),t\right)=\int_{0}^{T(\rho)}K(\sigma;\rho)\,u(\sigma,\rho,t)\,d\sigma,

(44)

where
K:[0,T(\rho)]\times\Gamma^{-}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle K(\sigma;\rho):=\frac{1}{T(\rho)}\,k\!\left(1,\frac{\sigma}{T(\rho)};\rho\right)\,e^{\Lambda(\sigma/T(\rho),\rho)-\Lambda(1,\rho)}.

Finally, we express (
44
) in terms of the original state
v
on

\Omega
. Let
z\in\Gamma^{+}
, and define the
upstream curve
of
z

as

\displaystyle C(z):=\left\{\phi(s;z)\ :\ \tau^{-}(z)\leq s\leq 0\right\},

(45)

i.e., the arc of the characteristic curve from the exit point
\rho(z)
to
z
,
parameterized by backward time from
z
. As
\sigma
runs from
0
to
T(\rho(z))
,
the point
y=\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho(z))=\phi(\sigma;\rho(z))
traces
C(z)
,
and the arc length element along
C(z)
is
d\ell(y)=|a(y)|\,d\sigma
. Using

u(\sigma,\rho(z),t)=v(\Psi^{-1}(\sigma,\rho(z)),t)=v(y,t)
and
d\sigma=d\ell(y)/|a(y)|
, the integral in (
44
) becomes

\displaystyle U(z,t)=\int_{C(z)}\mathcal{K}(z,y)\,v(y,t)\,d\ell(y),

(46)

where
\mathcal{K}:\{(z,y)\in\Gamma^{+}\times\bar{\Omega}:y\in C(z)\}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(z,y)=\frac{K(\sigma(y);\rho(z))}{|a(y)|}.

(47)

Finally, let the initial condition
v_{o}:\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\sigma\mapsto v_{o}(\phi(\sigma;\rho))
belongs to
\mathcal{C}([0,T(\rho)])\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(0,T(\rho))
for each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
. By the invertibility of the backstepping transformation for each
\rho
and the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
regularity of the corresponding inverse backstepping kernel
[
38
]
, each equation in the continuum admits a unique classical solution starting from
v_{o}(\phi(\cdot;\rho))
provided the compatibility condition
v_{o}(\phi(\sigma;\rho))=U(\phi(\sigma;\rho),0)
holds, and therefore the system (
6
)–(
10
) admits a unique characteristic solution starting from
v_{o}
.

We can now state the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the system (
6
)-(
10
) and let Assumptions
1
–
5
hold. Furthermore, let the control input be given by (
46
). Then, for any initial condition
v_{o}:\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\sigma\mapsto v_{o}(\phi(\sigma;\rho))
belongs to
\mathcal{C}([0,T(\rho)])\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(0,T(\rho))
for each
\rho\in\Gamma^{-}
, and verifying the compatibility condition
v_{o}(z)=U(v_{o},z)
on
\Gamma^{+}
, the system (
6
)-(
10
) admits a unique characteristic solution starting from
v_{o}
that satisfies

\displaystyle v(x,t)=0\qquad\forall\,x\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o},\ \forall\,t\geq T_{\max}.

5
Example

Let
\Omega
be the unit disk

\displaystyle\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ :\ |x|<1\},

whose unit outward normal is

\displaystyle\nu(x)=x\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

Furthermore, let

\displaystyle a=(1,1).

Then, by expanding the inner product
a\cdot\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma^{+}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\partial\Omega\ :\ x_{1}>-x_{2}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\partial\Omega\ :\ x_{1}<-x_{2}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{o}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\partial\Omega\ :\ x_{1}=-x_{2}\}.

Note that Assumptions
1
–
3
hold.

The characteristic curve passing through
x\in\bar{\Omega}
is the unique complete solution to

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi}{ds}=(1,1),\qquad\phi(0;x)=x.

That is,

\displaystyle\phi(s;x)=(s,s)+x\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

For each
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma^{o}
, the entry time of
\phi(\cdot;x)
is

\displaystyle\tau^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\{s\geq 0\ :\ (s+x_{1},s+x_{2})\in\Gamma^{+}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{s\geq 0\ :\ (s+x_{1})^{2}+(s+x_{2})^{2}=1

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\text{and}\ s+x_{1}>-(s+x_{2})\}.

The polynomial equation
(s+x_{1})^{2}+(s+x_{2})^{2}=1
admits two roots

\displaystyle s_{\pm}=\frac{-(x_{1}+x_{2})\pm\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}{2}.

Additionally, from the condition
s+x_{1}>-(s+x_{2})
, we must have

\displaystyle s>\frac{-(x_{1}+x_{2})}{2}.

We therefore select the nonnegative root
s_{+}
, and obtain

\displaystyle\tau^{+}(x)=\frac{-(x_{1}+x_{2})+\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}{2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\tau^{-}(x)=\frac{-(x_{1}+x_{2})-\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}{2}.

Hence, the transit time of
\phi(\cdot;x)
is given by

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=\tau^{+}(x)-\tau^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2-(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}.

As a result, Assumption
4
holds with

\displaystyle T_{\max}=T(0)=\sqrt{2}.

We consider on
\Omega
the PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{2}}+\gamma\exp^{b(x_{1}+x_{2})}v(\rho(x)),

(48)

where
\gamma,b>0
are constants. Note that Assumption
5
holds. Here,

\displaystyle\rho(x)=

\displaystyle~\phi(\tau^{-}(x);x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\tau^{-}(x),\tau^{-}(x))+x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\bigg(\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}-\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}{2},

\displaystyle~\qquad\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}-\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+2(1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}{2}\bigg).

To achieve stabilization in finite time
T_{\max}=\sqrt{2}
, we implement the control law in (
46
) for all
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\Gamma^{+}
. The integration path is

\displaystyle C(z)=\bigg\{(s,s)+z\ :

\displaystyle~\frac{-(z_{1}+z_{2})-\sqrt{(z_{1}+z_{2})^{2}+2(1-z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})}}{2}\leq s\leq 0\bigg\}.

It remains to calculate the kernel
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
47
). For each
(z,y)\in\Gamma^{+}\times\bar{\Omega}
such that
y\in C(z)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(z,y)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}K(\sigma(y);\rho(z)),

where
\sigma(y)=-\tau^{-}(y)
, and

\displaystyle K(\sigma(y);\rho(z))=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{T(\rho(z))}\,k\left(1,\frac{\sigma(y)}{T(\rho(z))};\rho(z)\right).

For each
\rho=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\in\Gamma^{-}
, the function
(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y})\mapsto k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)
verifies, on the interior of
\mathcal{T}=\{(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y})\in[0,1]^{2}\ :\ 0\leq\bar{y}\leq\bar{\sigma}\leq 1\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}+\frac{\partial k}{\partial\bar{y}}=0,

\displaystyle k(\bar{\sigma},0;\rho)=T(\rho)\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)g(\phi(T(\rho)\bar{y};\rho))d\bar{y}

\displaystyle~\qquad\qquad\quad-T(\rho)g\left(\phi\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma};\rho\right)\right),

where

\displaystyle g\left(\phi\left(T(\rho)\bar{\sigma};\rho\right)\right)=

\displaystyle~g\left((T(\rho)\bar{\sigma},T(\rho)\bar{\sigma})+\rho\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\gamma\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\exp^{2bT(\rho)\bar{\sigma}}.

The solution to the above equations has the form

\displaystyle k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)=\eta(\bar{\sigma}-\bar{y};\rho),

where
\eta
will be determined. One has

\displaystyle\eta(\bar{\sigma};\rho)

\displaystyle\quad=\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}}\eta(\bar{\sigma}-\bar{y};\rho)\exp^{2bT(\rho)\bar{y}}d\bar{y}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\exp^{2bT(\rho)\bar{\sigma}}.

Applying the Laplace transform in the
\bar{\sigma}
-variable, and denoting by
s\mapsto\hat{\eta}(s;\rho)
the Laplace transform of
\bar{\sigma}\mapsto\eta(\bar{\sigma};\rho)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}(s;\rho)=

\displaystyle~\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\frac{\hat{\eta}(s;\rho)}{s-2bT(\rho)}

\displaystyle~-\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\frac{1}{s-2bT(\rho)}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}(s;\rho)=-\frac{\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}}{s-\left(2b+\gamma\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\right)T(\rho)}.

Applying the inverse Laplace transform,

\displaystyle\eta(\bar{\sigma};\rho)=-\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\exp^{\left(2b+\gamma\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\right)T(\rho)\bar{\sigma}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle k(\bar{\sigma},\bar{y};\rho)

\displaystyle\quad=-\gamma T(\rho)\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\exp^{\left(2b+\gamma\exp^{b(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}\right)T(\rho)(\bar{\sigma}-\bar{y})}.

As a result, we have an explicit expression for
\mathcal{K}
.

We now evaluate our controller numerically. To accommodate general initial conditions

v_{o}
that are not compatible with the controller
U
, and
inspired by
[
19
]
, we modify the control law as

\displaystyle v(z,t)

\displaystyle=U_{\text{new}}(v(\cdot,t),z)

\displaystyle=U(v(\cdot,t),z)+\varepsilon(z,t)\quad z\in\Gamma^{+},\ t\geq 0,

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-m_{1}\operatorname{sign}(\varepsilon)|\varepsilon|^{m_{2}}\qquad m_{1}>0,\ m_{2}\in(0,1),

\displaystyle\varepsilon(z,0)

\displaystyle=-U(v_{o},z)+v_{o}(z).

With this modification, any
v_{o}
is compatible with
U_{\text{new}}
.
Moreover, since
\varepsilon
converges to zero in finite time
t_{o}>0
(which can be tuned
freely through the parameters
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
), it follows that
v(\cdot,t_{o})
is compatible
with
U
, thereby guaranteeing that
v(x,t)=0
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
and all

t\geq T_{\max}+t_{o}
.

The parameters are set to
\gamma=2
,
b=0.1
,
m_{1}=5
, and
m_{2}=0.5
.
The initial condition is chosen as

\displaystyle v_{o}(x)=1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2},\quad x\in\bar{\Omega},

yielding
T_{\max}+t_{o}\approx 2.5
. The kernel
\mathcal{K}
is depicted in
Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
The kernel
\mathcal{K}
for all

6
Conclusion

This paper extended the backstepping approach of
[
38
]
to first-order hyperbolic equations defined on
n
-dimensional
domains, for any
n\geq 1
, by decomposing the multidimensional equation into
a continuum of one-dimensional equations along characteristic curves and
applying the approach of
[
38
]
to each equation. In a forthcoming work, we reveal how the proposed framework can be extended to interconnections of multidimensional first-order hyperbolic equations with colinear velocities.

It is worth investigating if other control approaches developed in the one-dimensional setting might also extend to the multidimensional case through our reduction. For example, Lyapunov-based techniques constitute one of the most important approaches for the stability analysis of one-dimensional hyperbolic systems
[
15
,
7
,
8
,
27
,
28
,
22
,
42
,
16
]
. In the multidimensional setting, several important extensions have been proposed in
[
30
,
31
,
55
,
6
]
for establishing stability through Lyapunov functionals defined over the entire spatial domain. In light of the reduction established in Theorem
3.2
, one may attempt to construct a Lyapunov functional for each one-dimensional equation in the resulting continuum and then relate the corresponding stability properties back to those of the original multidimensional equation. Such an approach could, for example, yield bounds on the
\mathcal{C}^{0}
norm of the state in dimensions
\geq 2
without resorting to
H^{s}
Lyapunov functionals with
s\geq 2
, relying instead on a continuum of
H^{1}
Lyapunov functionals along each leaf. When considering a scalar multidimensional equation, or systems of equations with colinear transport velocities, only one foliation of the domain by characteristic curves needs to be accounted for. In more general settings, however, the Lyapunov analysis would have to accommodate the additional coupling induced by multiple, non-aligned foliations of the domain associated with distinct characteristic directions. Our reduction may also make it possible to extend spectral approaches developed for one-dimensional hyperbolic equations
[
29
,
21
,
10
]
to the multidimensional setting. This approach would avoid studying an eigenvalue problem directly on the full multidimensional domain, requiring instead only the analysis of the family of one-dimensional equations arising in the continuum reduction. The spectral properties of the original multidimensional system could then be recovered from the corresponding properties of these one-dimensional equations/systems.
