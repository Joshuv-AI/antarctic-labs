Non-Redundancy of Low-Arity Symmetric Boolean CSPs
Amatya Sharma, Santhoshini Velusamy

Abstract
Non-redundancy, introduced by Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos (AAAI 2020), has emerged as a central structural parameter for Constraint Satisfaction Problems ( CSPs ): it underlies kernelization and exact sparsification, and recent work shows that it also governs approximate sparsification and exact streaming complexity. The non-redundancy NRD measures the largest size of a CSP instance that admits no smaller subinstance with the same set of satisfying assignments. In this paper we study the non-redundancy ( 𝖭𝖱𝖣 n ​ ( R ) \mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R) ) for Boolean symmetric CSPs specified by a symmetric r r -ary relation R R , that is, a relation whose value depends only on Hamming weight. An instance of CSP ( R ) (R) consists of n n variables and m m constraints, each given by an r r -tuple of variables, and a constraint is satisfied if and only if the resulting tuple belongs to R R . This is a natural and broad class containing Max-Cut , Max- k k -SAT , etc. Our main result is a near-complete classification of the growth of 𝖭𝖱𝖣 n ​ ( R ) \mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R) for symmetric Boolean predicates of arity at most 5 5 . Using computational experiments together with algebraic upper- and lower-bound criteria, we determine the asymptotic growth of 𝖭𝖱𝖣 n ​ ( R ) \mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R) for every symmetric Boolean predicate of arity at most 4 4 . At arity 5 5 , the framework resolves all predicates except two. On the upper-bound side, we introduce a lifted, higher-degree version of the balancedness notion of Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse (Algorithmica 2020), and show that it yields general upper bounds on non-redundancy. More precisely, we define t t -balancedness and prove that it is equivalent to the existence of degree at most t t multilinear polynomials “capturing” R R , implying that 𝖭𝖱𝖣 n ​ ( R ) = O ⁡ ( n t ) \mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R)=O(n^{t}) for every t t -balanced relation R R . On the lower-bound side, we use Carbonnel’s (CP 2022) framework, which gives a criterion for when a predicate admits a special reduction from the k k -ary OR relation; since OR already has non-redundancy Ω ⁡ ( n k ) \Omega(n^{k}) , this yields corresponding lower bounds on 𝖭𝖱𝖣 n ​ ( R ) \mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R) . While these determine the non-redundancy of symmetric predicates up to arity 4 4 and nearly all predicates of arity 5 5 , we discover two exceptional predicates of arity 5 5 that are not characterized by this framework—we obtain an Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) \Omega(n^{2}) lower bound and an O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) upper bound. We further show that these remaining cases reduce to natural extremal set-system questions. Independent Work. Very recently, Brakensiek, Guruswami, and Putterman [ 14 ] used a similar experimental viewpoint to study arity- 4 4 Boolean predicates, and one of their remaining cases connects to the symmetric arity- 5 5 predicate with W ( R 023 ) = { 0 , 2 , 3 } (R_{023})=\{0,2,3\} that also appears here.

Symmetric Boolean
\mathsf{CSPs}
.

A relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
is symmetric if membership in
R
depends only on the Hamming weight of the input. Equivalently, there is a set
\operatorname{W}(R)\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,r\}
such that
R=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{r}:|x|\in\operatorname{W}(R)\}
.
Thus a symmetric predicate can be specified simply by its set of accepted weights, and throughout the paper we will freely identify a symmetric predicate with its weight set
\operatorname{W}(R)
.

Symmetric Boolean predicates already contain many natural and canonical problems. For example, the
r
-ary
\operatorname{OR}
predicate corresponds to
\operatorname{W}(\operatorname{OR}_{r})=\{1,\dots,r\}
and gives the usual
r
-
\mathsf{SAT}
constraints; the
r
-ary
Not-All-Equal
predicate corresponds to
\operatorname{W}(\mathsf{NAE}_{r})=\{1,\dots,r-1\}
and is closely related to hypergraph
2
-coloring and
\mathsf{NAE}
-
\mathsf{SAT}
; threshold predicates correspond to intervals of the form
\{t,t+1,\dots,r\}
and capture “at least
t
” type constraints; and parity predicates correspond to the even or odd weights and encode systems of linear equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. For these reasons, symmetric predicates form a broad yet structured class, and they have been studied explicitly in work on approximation, sparsification, sketching, and non-redundancy
[

Thus, we restrict our study to symmetric Boolean
\mathsf{CSPs}
in this paper. We ask how large can an instance be if every one of its constraints is non-redundant, that is, if no constraint can be removed without changing the satisfying set. This leads to the notion of non-redundancy.

Non-redundancy.

Given a
\mathsf{CSP}
instance, a constraint is
redundant
if deleting it does not change the set of satisfying assignments. An instance is
non-redundant
if every constraint is
non-redundant
in this sense. Following Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos
[
10
]
, who introduced this viewpoint in the context of learning
\mathsf{CSP}
instances with few membership queries, and Carbonnel’s
[
20
]
subsequent structural study, we define the non-redundancy dimension of a constraint language
\Gamma
by

\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\Gamma):=\max\{|I|:I\text{ is a non-redundant }\mathsf{CSP}(\Gamma)\text{ instance on }n\text{ variables}\}.

When
\Gamma=\{R\}
consists of a single relation, we also write
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
. Thus
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
asks a basic extremal question: how many
R
-constraints can be simultaneously non-redundant in an instance on
n
variables? At first sight, this is purely a combinatorial quantity. However, it turns out to be closely connected to several algorithmic and structural questions about
\mathsf{CSPs}
.

Connections of
\mathsf{NRD}
to other problems.

One such connection comes from
exact sparsification
. The goal is to reduce any
n
-variable instance in polynomial time to an equivalent subinstance of size
g(n)
, where equivalence means having exactly the same satisfying assignments. Exact sparsification is closely related to kernelization, where one preprocesses an instance to an equivalent instance whose size is bounded in terms of a chosen parameter. In our setting the parameter is the number
n
of variables, and exact sparsification asks for the stronger property that the output is an equivalent subinstance obtained by deleting constraints. On the positive side, several structured Boolean
\mathsf{CSPs}
admit exact sparsification by retaining only a small subset of constraints
[
40
,
22
,
57
]
. Non-redundant instances are the canonical obstruction to such subinstance sparsification: any equivalent subinstance must retain every constraint of a non-redundant input.

Another, more approximation-theoretic notion is
approximate sparsification
, where one seeks a weighted subinstance of small size, with weights on constraints, that approximately preserves the value of every assignment. This line of work originates in graph and hypergraph cut sparsification and has since developed into a broad theory for
\mathsf{CSPs}

[
55
,
33
,
19
,
49
,
48
]
. Very recently, Brakensiek and Guruswami
[
17
]
proved that, for every unweighted predicate
R
, the worst-case size of approximate sparsifiers is governed, up to polylogarithmic factors, by
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
; see also the recent follow-up on random
\mathsf{CSP}
sparsification
[
13
]
. Thus the same combinatorial parameter that obstructs exact sparsification also controls approximate sparsification.

Recently, there has been a similar connection in the
streaming
setting as well. In very recent work by Sharma and Velusamy
[
62
]
, it was shown that, up to logarithmic factors, the single-pass streaming complexity of deciding satisfiability and exact sparsification is characterized by non-redundancy. Taken together, these results show that
\mathsf{NRD}
is not merely a technical parameter arising in one corner of the literature, but rather a structural invariant that governs exact sparsification, approximate sparsification, and streaming decidability and streaming exact sparsification.

These connections motivate the problem studied in this paper:

Determine the growth of
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
for symmetric Boolean predicates
R
.

Our goal is both structural and computational: to identify algebraic criteria that certify polynomial upper and lower bounds on
\mathsf{NRD}_{n}(R)
, and to use these criteria to classify low-arity symmetric Boolean predicates.

Upper bounds via lifted balancedness.

On the upper-bound side, our main conceptual contribution is a lifted version of

Let

Lower bounds via universal cubes.

On the lower-bound side, we use Carbonnel’s

Robustness to instance-model choices.

Our classification concerns the exponent of
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
and is unaffected by
standard choices in the constraint model. In
Section

6
, we prove
that allowing unary maps on variables, constants, or repeated variables inside
constraints does not change the asymptotic non-redundancy, compared with the
model where each constraint is applied directly to a tuple of distinct variables.
The equivalence holds up to constant-factor changes in the number of variables.
The intuition is that, for Boolean predicates of fixed arity, literals,
constants, and repeated occurrences can be simulated by replacing each original
variable with a constant number of tagged copies. Hence our results are
independent of these modelling choices.

Preliminaries and notation

2.1
Basic notation

For a positive integer
n
, we write
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For a finite set
S
, we write
\{0,1\}^{S}
for the set of all Boolean vectors indexed by
S
, that is, all functions
x\colon S\to\{0,1\}
. When
S=[n]
, we identify
\{0,1\}^{[n]}
with the usual Boolean cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
For a vector
x\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, we write
|x|
for its Hamming weight, that is, the number of coordinates equal to
1
.
All asymptotic notation is with respect to the number
n
of variables. The hidden constants may depend on fixed parameters such as the arity of the relation under consideration.

Throughout, all rings are commutative rings with identity. When evaluating
polynomials over a ring
E
on Boolean inputs, we identify
0,1
with

0_{E},1_{E}
. Integer coefficients and signs are interpreted through the
canonical map
\mathbb{Z}\to E
,
k\mapsto k\cdot 1_{E}
. By a prime-power
ring, we mean the residue ring
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
, where
q
is a power of some prime
p
.

We will use polynomials in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
over such a ring
E
. Since these polynomials are only evaluated on Boolean inputs, every polynomial can be multilinearized using the identities
x_{i}^{2}=x_{i}
. Thus we may assume without loss of generality that all polynomials are multilinear, i.e., of the form
p(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[r]}\alpha_{S}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
with coefficients
\alpha_{S}\in E
. The degree of a multilinear polynomial is the maximum
|S|
such that
\alpha_{S}\neq 0
.

2.2
CSPs

A constraint satisfaction problem is specified by a set of relations over a domain. Fix a finite domain
D
and a set
\Gamma
of
r
-ary relations over
D
.
An instance of
\mathsf{CSP}(\Gamma)
consists of
n
variables
V:=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and a set
I
of
m
constraints. A constraint
C
is defined by a pair
\{R,\bar{x}\}
, where
R\in\Gamma
is an
r
-ary relation and
\bar{x}=(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}})\in V^{r}
is an ordered tuple of variables. Repetitions inside
\bar{x}
are allowed unless explicitly stated otherwise.
An assignment is a map
\sigma\colon V\to D
. Such an assignment satisfies the constraint
\{R,(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}})\}
if
(\sigma(x_{i_{1}}),\ldots,\sigma(x_{i_{r}}))\in R
. We write
\mathrm{Sat}(I)
for the set of satisfying assignments of an instance
I
.

Notice that above we assume that all relations in a fixed language
\Gamma
have the same arity
r
. This is without loss of generality for fixed finite languages: if
R\subseteq D^{k}
has arity
k<r
, we may replace it by the padded relation

R^{\prime}:=R\times D^{r-k}\subseteq D^{r}
, where the extra coordinates are dummy coordinates. A constraint using
R
can then be viewed as a constraint using
R^{\prime}
by adding arbitrary variables in the dummy positions.

Symmetric Boolean predicates and the
\operatorname{OR}
relation.

From now on, when discussing
Boolean relations
, the domain is
\{0,1\}
. We use the terms Boolean relation and
Boolean predicate
interchangeably. Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
be a Boolean relation. We say that
R
is
symmetric
if membership in
R
depends only on Hamming weight. Equivalently, there is a set
\operatorname{W}(R)\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,r\}
such that
R=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{r}:|x|\in\operatorname{W}(R)\}
. Throughout the paper, we identify a symmetric relation with its accepted-weight set
\operatorname{W}(R)
.

For
k\geq 2
, we write
\operatorname{OR}_{k}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k}
for the
k
-ary
\operatorname{OR}
relation, that is,
\operatorname{OR}_{k}:=\{0,1\}^{k}\setminus\{0^{k}\}
. Equivalently,
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\operatorname{OR}_{k}
if and only if at least one input bit is
1
. Equivalently,
\operatorname{W}(\operatorname{OR}_{k})=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Although one can define non-redundancy for arbitrary finite Boolean constraint
languages, it is enough, at the level of exponents, to understand singleton
languages
\Gamma=\{R\}
. Indeed, if
\Gamma=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{s}\}
is fixed and
the singleton exponents are known, then the exponent for
\Gamma
is the
maximum of the singleton exponents. The lower bound follows by using only one
relation, and the upper bound follows by sparsifying the constraints of each
relation separately. We therefore mostly work with a single symmetric relation

R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
.

Equivalence and non-redundancy.

Two instances
I
and
I^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if they have the same set of satisfying assignments, that is, if
\mathrm{Sat}(I)=\mathrm{Sat}(I^{\prime})
.
Let
I
be an instance and let
C
be a constraint of
I
. We say that
C
is
redundant
if
I\setminus\{C\}
is equivalent to
I
, that is, if deleting
C
does not change the set of satisfying assignments. We say that
I
is
non-redundant
if none of its constraints is redundant.
The main parameter studied in this paper is the non-redundancy of a language. For a finite language
\Gamma
, define
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\Gamma)
to be the maximum number of constraints in a non-redundant instance of
\mathsf{CSP}(\Gamma)
on
n
variables.
When the language consists of a single relation
R
, we write
\Gamma_{R}:=\{R\}
,
\mathsf{CSP}(R):=\mathsf{CSP}(\Gamma_{R})
, and
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R):=\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\Gamma_{R})
.

We call a relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}

non-trivial
if

\emptyset\neq R\subsetneq\{0,1\}^{r}
. The two trivial cases are easy to handle
separately: if
R=\{0,1\}^{r}
, then every
R
-constraint is always satisfied and
hence redundant, so
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=0
; if
R=\emptyset
, then any instance containing at
least one
R
-constraint is unsatisfiable, and it is easy to see that

\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=1
. Thus, except where stated otherwise, we restrict attention to
non-trivial relations.

We also note that every non-trivial relation already has linear non-redundancy. Indeed, if
a\in R
and
b\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
, then by partitioning the variables into
\lfloor n/r\rfloor
pairwise disjoint
r
-tuples and placing one copy of
R
on each block, we obtain a non-redundant instance with
\lfloor n/r\rfloor
constraints: to witness that a given block is essential, assign
b
on that block and
a
on every other block. Hence
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega(n)
for every non-trivial
R
.

Bit-flip symmetry.

For a Boolean relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
, let
\overline{R}:=\{1-x:x\in R\}
be its image under global bit-flip. If
R
is symmetric with accepted weights
W(R)
, then
\overline{R}
has accepted weights
r-W(R):=\{r-w:w\in W(R)\}
. The relations
R
and
\overline{R}
have the same non-redundancy: replacing every
R
-constraint by the corresponding
\overline{R}
-constraint and complementing all Boolean assignments gives a bijection between satisfying assignments, preserving equivalence of subinstances and non-redundancy. Hence
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\overline{R})
.

Literals, positive, and simple positive models.

We will use two closely related models of instances, and one useful restriction of the positive model.

1.

Literal model.
Fix a finite set
\mathcal{U}
of unary maps
g:D\to D
containing the identity map. Constraints may be applied to
literals
, i.e., unary substitutions of variables: a constraint has the form

\{R,(g_{1}(x_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g_{r}(x_{i_{r}}))\}
,
where
R\subseteq D^{r}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in\mathcal{U}
. Constant maps may be included in
\mathcal{U}
, so this model also allows constants. We call this the literal model because, in the Boolean case, taking
\mathcal{U}=\{\operatorname{id},\neg,0,1\}
gives exactly the usual variables, negated variables, and constants.

2.

Positive model.
Constraints are applied only to variables: a constraint has the form

\{R,(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}})\}
,
where the tuple
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}})
is ordered and repetitions are allowed. It is called
simple
if no constraint repeats a variable. Thus, in a simple positive instance, every constraint has distinct variables.

By
Theorem

6.1
, for fixed finite domains and fixed arity, the literal, positive, and simple positive models have the same asymptotic non-redundancy up to a constant-factor blow-up in the number of variables. We will therefore move between them when convenient, explicitly indicating the model when it matters. Unless stated otherwise,
\mathsf{CSP}(R)
and
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
refer to the literal model.

3
Computing non-redundancy of symmetric predicates of small arity

3.1
Experimental methodology

Our computational approach studies the lower-bound and upper-bound mechanisms separately.
For each symmetric Boolean relation
R
, we compute one algebraic witness for a lower bound on
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
and one algebraic witness for an upper bound on
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
. When the two exponents match, this yields the exact growth rate of the non-redundancy. The full proofs appear later in
Sections

5
and
4
; here we state the statements and the intuition behind the two tests.

3.1.1
Upper-bound criterion

The upper-bound criterion is based on polynomial capturing. Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and let
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
be a falsifying tuple. A polynomial
p
over a commutative ring
E
with identity
captures

f
with respect to
R
if
p(y)=0
for every
y\in R
, but
p(f)\neq 0
. Thus, a capturing polynomial separates one particular falsifying tuple from all satisfying tuples of
R
.

Capturing polynomials are useful for non-redundancy because of the sparsification theorem of Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse
[
22
]
: if every falsifying tuple
f\notin R
can be captured by a polynomial of degree at most
t
, then every instance of
\mathsf{CSP}(R)
on
n
variables has an equivalent subinstance with
O(n^{t})
constraints. Consequently, any non-redundant instance itself has size at most
O(n^{t})
. Therefore, to prove
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=O(n^{t})
, it is enough to certify that every falsifying tuple of
R
has a degree-
\leq t
capturing polynomial.

We now introduce the degree-
t
lift, whose purpose is to turn degree-
t
multilinear polynomials into linear polynomials. Fix
t\geq 1
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{r,t}:=\{S\subseteq[r]:1\leq|S|\leq t\}
. The degree-
t
lift maps each tuple
x\in\{0,1\}^{r}
to the vector
\nu_{t}(x)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{S}_{r,t}}
whose
S
-coordinate is
\nu_{t}(x)_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
. Thus a degree-
t
multilinear polynomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
is exactly a linear polynomial in the lifted coordinates, together with a constant term.

3.1
.

Consider
R=\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
, so
\operatorname{W}(R)=\{1,2\}
. The falsifying tuples are
000
and
111
. The polynomial

p(x)=(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}-1)(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}-2)

vanishes on all tuples of Hamming weight
1
or
2
, and is nonzero on tuples of Hamming weight
0
or
3
. Thus this degree-
2
polynomial captures both falsifying tuples.

For
t=2
, the lift is
\nu_{2}(x)=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}x_{3},x_{2}x_{3})
. The lifted relation
R^{(2)}
consists of the six vectors obtained from the accepting tuples:

\begin{array}[]{c|c}x&\nu_{2}(x)\\
\hline\cr 100&(1,0,0,0,0,0)\\
010&(0,1,0,0,0,0)\\
001&(0,0,1,0,0,0)\\
110&(1,1,0,1,0,0)\\
101&(1,0,1,0,1,0)\\
011&(0,1,1,0,0,1).\end{array}

The falsifying tuples lift to
\nu_{2}(000)=(0,0,0,0,0,0)
and
\nu_{2}(111)=(1,1,1,1,1,1)
. After multilinearization,
p
becomes

p(x)=2-2(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})+2(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3})
,
which is a linear expression in the lifted coordinates. This illustrates why degree-
t
capturing polynomials can be studied through linear conditions after the degree-
t
lift.

Instead of explicitly searching for capturing polynomials in the lifted space, we use an equivalent closure condition called
t
-balancedness
. Let
X_{t}:=\nu_{t}(\{0,1\}^{r})
be the lifted Boolean cube and let
R^{(t)}:=\nu_{t}(R)
be the lifted relation. We say that
R
is
t
-balanced
if
R^{(t)}
is closed under odd alternating sums inside
X_{t}
: for every odd integer
m
, every
a^{(1)},\ldots,a^{(m)}\in R^{(t)}
, and every
u:=a^{(1)}-a^{(2)}+\cdots-a^{(m)}\in X_{t}
, we also have
u\in R^{(t)}
.
The significance of this definition is the following theorem, proved in Section
4
.

See
1.2

Implementation.

We now describe how the upper-bound criterion is implemented. Fix a symmetric relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and an integer
t\geq 1
. We first form the degree-
t
lift
\nu_{t}(x)
for every tuple
x\in\{0,1\}^{r}
. This gives the lifted Boolean cube
X_{t}=\nu_{t}(\{0,1\}^{r})
and the lifted accepting set
R^{(t)}=\nu_{t}(R)
. The goal is to test whether
R^{(t)}
is balanced relative to
X_{t}
.

Directly checking the definition of
t
-balancedness would require searching over all odd alternating sums of tuples from
R^{(t)}
. Instead, we use the equivalent integer-span formulation proved in
Lemma

4.4
. For a lifted vector
v
, write
\operatorname{ext}(v):=(1,v)
. Then
R
is
t
-balanced if and only if, for every
b\in X_{t}\setminus R^{(t)}
, the vector
\operatorname{ext}(b)
does not lie in the integer span of the vectors
\{\operatorname{ext}(a):a\in R^{(t)}\}
.

Thus the computation reduces to a finite set of integer-span tests. We form the integer matrix
M_{R}^{(t)}
whose columns are the vectors
\operatorname{ext}(a)
for
a\in R^{(t)}
. For each
b\in X_{t}\setminus R^{(t)}
, we test whether the integer linear system
M_{R}^{(t)}z=\operatorname{ext}(b)
has a solution
z
over
\mathbb{Z}
. If such a solution exists for some
b
, then
R
is not
t
-balanced. If no such
b
lies in the integer span, then
R
is
t
-balanced.

We perform these integer-span tests using Smith normal form. Namely, we compute the Smith normal form of
M_{R}^{(t)}
and use it to decide membership in the integer span of its columns. This avoids explicitly enumerating alternating sums and instead uses the integer-span characterization of balancedness. For each symmetric relation
R
, we test
t=1,2,\dots,r
and record the smallest value
u(R)
for which the test succeeds. By the upper-bound criterion above, this certifies
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=O(n^{u(R)})
.

3.2
Continuing
Example

3.1
.

The previous example showed the polynomial side of the lift. We now use the same relation to illustrate the integer-span test actually used in the implementation.

The two rejected lifted points are
\nu_{2}(000)=(0,0,0,0,0,0)
and
\nu_{2}(111)=(1,1,1,1,1,1)
. To test
2
-balancedness, we check whether either rejected point lies in the integer span of the accepted lifted points after adding a leading
1
coordinate. For
b=\nu_{2}(000)
, membership would mean that there are integers
z_{100},z_{010},z_{001},z_{110},z_{101},z_{011}
such that

\sum_{a\in R^{(2)}}z_{a}\operatorname{ext}(a)=\operatorname{ext}(b)=(1,0,0,0,0,0,0).

Looking at the three degree-
2
coordinates forces
z_{110}=z_{101}=z_{011}=0
. Then the three degree-
1
coordinates force
z_{100}=z_{010}=z_{001}=0
, contradicting the leading-coordinate equation
\sum_{a}z_{a}=1
. Hence
\operatorname{ext}(\nu_{2}(000))
is not in the integer span.

Similarly, for
b=\nu_{2}(111)
, the three degree-
2
coordinates force
z_{110}=z_{101}=z_{011}=1
. The degree-
1
coordinates then force
z_{100}=z_{010}=z_{001}=-1
. But the leading-coordinate sum is then
-1-1-1+1+1+1=0
, not
1
. Hence
\operatorname{ext}(\nu_{2}(111))
is also not in the integer span. Therefore neither rejected lifted point lies in the integer span, so the integer-span test certifies that
\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
is
2
-balanced.

3.1.2
Lower bound criterion

The lower-bound criterion is based on defining the
k
-ary
\operatorname{OR}
predicate from
R
. The predicate
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
has non-redundancy
\Theta(n^{k})
. For example, the instance containing one positive
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
constraint on each
k
-subset of variables is non-redundant. Thus, if
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
is “definable” from
R
by a constant-size gadget, then non-redundant
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
-instances can be translated into non-redundant
R
-instances, giving
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega(n^{k})
.

3.3
.

The predicate
\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
defines
\operatorname{OR}_{2}
after pinning one coordinate to
0
: the constraint
\{\mathsf{NAE}_{3},(x,y,0)\}
is false exactly on
(x,y)=(0,0)
and true otherwise. Thus it behaves as
\{\operatorname{OR}_{2},(x,y)\}
. Since
\operatorname{OR}_{2}
has non-redundant instances of size
\Omega(n^{2})
, this gives an
\Omega(n^{2})
lower bound.

The formal version of this gadget notion is Carbonnel’s
[
20
]

fgpp-definability
. Informally, a relation
S
is fgpp-definable from
R
if every
S
-constraint can be expressed using a constant number of
R
-constraints, possibly with auxiliary variables and simple unary functional guards; see
Definition

5.1
. In particular, each occurrence of
S
can be replaced by a constant-size gadget over
R
in a way that preserves the non-redundancy lower bound. We denote this as
S\in\langle\{R\}\rangle_{\mathrm{fg}}
or in short
S\in\langle R\rangle_{\mathrm{fg}}
.

3.4
Lower-bound criterion; informal
Theorem

5.6
.

Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and let
2\leq k\leq r
. If
\operatorname{OR}_{k}\in\langle R\rangle_{\mathrm{fg}}
, then
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega(n^{k})
.

Implementation.

We do not search directly for an fgpp-definition of
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
from
R
. Instead, we use Carbonnel’s
[
20
]
equivalent preservation criterion:
\operatorname{OR}_{k}\in\langle R\rangle_{\mathrm{fg}}
if and only if a certain partial operation, called the universal
k
-cube operation, does not preserve
R
. We denote this partial operation by
f_{k}
. Here “preserve” has the usual polymorphism meaning: if we arrange tuples from
R
as rows of a matrix and apply
f_{k}
coordinatewise, then the resulting tuple should again lie in
R
, whenever
f_{k}
is defined on every column. Thus, to prove a lower bound, the code searches for a failure of preservation: input rows all lying in
R
whose coordinatewise image under
f_{k}
lies outside
R
.

Fix
k\geq 2
, and let
m=2^{k}-1
. Index the
m
input rows by the nonzero strings
v\in\{0,1\}^{k}\setminus\{0^{k}\}
. For each coordinate
i\in[k]
, define the column
c_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
by
(c_{i})_{v}=v_{i}
, where
v_{i}
is the
i
th bit of
v
. The universal
k
-cube partial operation, denoted
f_{k}
, is the partial operation of arity
m
whose domain is

\{0^{m},1^{m}\}\cup\{c_{i},1-c_{i}:i\in[k]\}
,
and whose values are given by

f_{k}(0^{m})=0
,
f_{k}(1^{m})=1
,
f_{k}(c_{i})=0
, and
f_{k}(1-c_{i})=1
for every
i\in[k]
. See
Example

3.5
.

Concretely, to test whether
f_{k}
preserves an
r
-ary relation
R
, choose
m
tuples from
R
, one for each nonzero string
v\in\{0,1\}^{k}\setminus\{0^{k}\}
, and place them as rows of an
m\times r
matrix. If every column lies in the domain of
f_{k}
, then applying
f_{k}
coordinatewise gives an output tuple in
\{0,1\}^{r}
. A failure of preservation is exactly the case where all input rows lie in
R
, but this output tuple lies outside
R
.

3.5
.

For example, when
k=2
, the input rows are indexed by
11,10,01
. The operation
f_{2}
has arity
3
and is defined on the following column types:

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\text{column}&f_{2}(\text{column})\\
\hline\cr 000&0\\
111&1\\
110&0\\
001&1\\
101&0\\
010&1.\end{array}

Here
110
and
101
are the two coordinate columns of the nonzero
2
-cube, and
001
and
010
are their complements.

As a concrete failure, take
R=\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
, so
\operatorname{W}(R)=\{1,2\}
. Consider the following three input rows, indexed by
11,10,01
:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc|c}&\text{coord. }1&\text{coord. }2&\text{coord. }3&\text{weight}\\
\hline\cr 11&1&1&0&2\\
10&1&0&0&1\\
01&0&1&0&1.\end{array}

All three rows belong to
\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
. Now look at the columns: they are
110
,
101
, and
000
. By the table above,
f_{2}
maps these columns to
0
,
0
, and
0
, respectively. Hence the coordinatewise output is
000
, which does not belong to
\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
. Therefore
f_{2}
does not preserve
\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
, and thus gives the lower bound
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\mathsf{NAE}_{3})=\Omega(n^{2})
.

For symmetric relations, the implementation can simplify this preservation test. Since membership in
R
depends only on Hamming weight, the actual positions of the column types do not matter; only their multiplicities matter. Thus, for fixed
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and
k
, the code enumerates all multiplicity vectors over the domain column types of
f_{k}
, with total multiplicity
r
. Each multiplicity vector determines the Hamming weights of all input rows and of the output row. The lower-bound criterion succeeds if every input-row weight lies in
\operatorname{W}(R)
, while the output-row weight lies outside
\operatorname{W}(R)
.

3.6
.

Consider again the case
k=2
. The column types in the domain of
f_{2}
are

000,111,110,001,101,010
, with outputs
0,1,0,1,0,1
, respectively. Suppose
R=\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
, so
\operatorname{W}(R)=\{1,2\}
. Since
R
has arity
3
, a multiplicity vector assigns three coordinates among these six column types.

Take the multiplicity vector that uses
110
once,
101
once, and
000
once, and uses all other column types zero times. The three input rows then have weights

2,1,1
: the row indexed by
11
sees the entries
1,1,0
, the row indexed by
10
sees
1,0,0
, and the row indexed by
01
sees
0,1,0
. All these weights lie in
\operatorname{W}(R)=\{1,2\}
. The output row has entries
0,0,0
, hence output weight
0
, which does not lie in
\operatorname{W}(R)
. Therefore this multiplicity vector witnesses failure of preservation, and the lower-bound criterion succeeds for
R=\mathsf{NAE}_{3}
and
k=2
.

For each symmetric relation
R
, we run this test for
k=2,\dots,r
and define
\ell(R)
to be the largest
k
for which the test succeeds, with the convention
\ell(R)=1
if no such
k
is found. By the lower-bound criterion above, this certifies
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega(n^{\ell(R)})
.

Enumerating all symmetric predicates.

The code used for the computational experiments is publicly available at

https://github.com/aaysharma/nrd_csps.git
.
Given an arity
r
, the code enumerates all symmetric Boolean
r
-ary relations, represented by their accepted-weight sets
\operatorname{W}(R)\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,r\}
. For each relation, it computes the upper-bound exponent
u(R)
certified by
t
-balancedness and the lower-bound exponent
\ell(R)
certified by the universal
k
-cube test.

We enumerate relations only up to the bit-flip symmetry defined in

Section

2
. For a symmetric arity-
r
relation with accepted weights

\operatorname{W}(R)
, this symmetry sends
\operatorname{W}(R)
to

r-\operatorname{W}(R):=\{r-w:w\in\operatorname{W}(R)\}
and preserves non-redundancy. Hence

\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(r-\operatorname{W}(R))
, so it suffices to list one representative from each
pair
\operatorname{W}(R)
and
r-\operatorname{W}(R)
. Modulo bit-flip symmetry, the number of relations is

2^{r}+2^{\lceil(r-1)/2\rceil}
.

3.3
The exceptional arity-

As shown in

, the only symmetric Boolean predicates of arity
5
not resolved by our current lower- and upper-bound criteria are

\operatorname{W}(R_{023})=\{0,2,3\}
and
\operatorname{W}(R_{124})=\{1,2,4\}
. For both predicates, the
\operatorname{OR}
-reduction lower-bound criterion certifies only an
\Omega(n^{2})
lower bound, while the
t
-balancedness upper-bound criterion gives an
O(n^{3})
upper bound. Thus these two predicates mark the first point in our classification where the two criteria fail to meet. Resolving them therefore requires going beyond at least one of the two techniques used in the rest of the paper. In this subsection, we describe our attempts to do so and explain how the remaining obstruction leads naturally to an extremal set-system problem. As proved in
Theorem

6.1
, we work with simple positive instances up to a constant-factor change in the number of variables.

Conditional
\mathsf{NRD}
.

It is useful to compare ordinary non-redundancy with a conditional version of the problem, as studied in
[
17
,
12
]
. Let
R\subseteq S\subseteq D^{r}
be relations over domain
D
. An
R
-instance
I
is
S
-conditionally non-redundant
if for every constraint
C=\{R,(\bar{x})\}\in I
, there exists an assignment
\sigma
such that
\sigma
satisfies every constraint in
I\setminus\{C\}
, while the tuple induced by
\bar{x}
under
\sigma
lies in
S\setminus R
. Equivalently,
\sigma
satisfies all other
R
-constraints and satisfies the relaxed constraint
\{S,(\bar{x})\}
, but falsifies
C
. We write
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R\mid S)
for the maximum number of constraints in an
S
-conditionally non-redundant
R
-instance on
n
variables.

The quantity
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R\mid S)
is useful because of the triangle inequality proved by
[
17
]
:

\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)\leq\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R\mid S)+\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(S).

For the predicate
R_{124}
, let

4.1
t
-balancedness and capturing polynomials

We begin by defining the relative notion of balancedness in a set.

4.1
Relative balancedness.

Let
A\subseteq X\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N}
. We say that
A
is
balanced relative to
X
if for every odd integer
m\geq 1
and every tuple
a^{(1)},a^{(2)},\dots,a^{(m)}\in A
, it holds that if the coordinatewise alternating sum
u:=a^{(1)}-a^{(2)}+a^{(3)}-\cdots-a^{(m-1)}+a^{(m)}
belongs to
X
, then
u\in A
.

We now define the degree-
t
lift.

4.2
t
-lift and
t
-balancedness
.

Fix integers
r\geq 1
and
t\geq 1
, and let

\mathcal{S}_{r,t}:=\{S\subseteq[r]:1\leq|S|\leq t\}
.
The degree-
t
lift is the map

\nu_{t}:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{S}_{r,t}}

defined by
\nu_{t}(x)_{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
.

For a relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
, let

X_{t}:=\nu_{t}(\{0,1\}^{r})
and
R^{(t)}:=\nu_{t}(R)
.
We say that
R
is
t
-balanced
if
R^{(t)}
is balanced relative to
X_{t}
.

Next, we formalize the polynomial notion used in the upper-bound criterion.

4.3
Capturing polynomial.

Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and let
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
.
A polynomial
p
over a ring
E

captures
f
with respect to
R
if

p(y)=0\quad\text{for all }y\in R,\qquad\text{and}\qquad p(f)\neq 0.

Equivalently, we may write
p\ \mathrm{captures}_{R}\ f
.

The next lemma reformulates relative balancedness as an integer-span condition.

4.4
.

Let
A\subseteq X\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N}
, and define
\mathrm{ext}(v):=(1,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{N+1}.

Then the following are equivalent.

1.

A
is balanced relative to
X
.

2.

For every
u\in X
, if
\mathrm{ext}(u)\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\{\mathrm{ext}(a):a\in A\}\bigr),

then
u\in A
.

Proof.

Let’s first prove the forward direction, i.e.,
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Assume that
A
is balanced relative to
X
, and let
u\in X
satisfy
\mathrm{ext}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\gamma_{j}\,\mathrm{ext}(a^{(j)})
for some
a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(s)}\in A
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Looking at the first coordinate gives
1=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\gamma_{j}
.
Now expand this integer combination as an odd alternating sum. I.e., for each
\gamma_{j}>0
,
take
\gamma_{j}
copies of
a^{(j)}
with sign
+
, and for each
\gamma_{j}<0
, take

-\gamma_{j}
copies of
a^{(j)}
with sign
-
. Since the total sum of coefficients is
1
,
the number of positively signed copies is exactly one more than the number of negatively
signed copies. After reordering, we have

\mathrm{ext}(u)=\mathrm{ext}(b^{(1)})-\mathrm{ext}(b^{(2)})+\mathrm{ext}(b^{(3)})-\cdots+\mathrm{ext}(b^{(m)})

for some odd
m
and some
b^{(1)},\dots,b^{(m)}\in A
. This implies
u=b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}+b^{(3)}-\cdots+b^{(m)}.
Since
u\in X
and
A
is balanced relative to
X
, it follows that
u\in A
.

For the reverse direction, let
u=a^{(1)}-a^{(2)}+a^{(3)}-\cdots+a^{(m)}
for some odd
m
and some
a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(m)}\in A
, and assume
u\in X
.
Then

\mathrm{ext}(u)=\mathrm{ext}(a^{(1)})-\mathrm{ext}(a^{(2)})+\mathrm{ext}(a^{(3)})-\cdots+\mathrm{ext}(a^{(m)}),

since the first coordinate on the right-hand side is
1
.
Hence
\mathrm{ext}(u)
belongs to the integer span of
\{\mathrm{ext}(a):a\in A\}
,
and so by assumption
u\in A
. Therefore
A
is balanced relative to
X
.
∎

We now prove the analogue, in the set
X
, of the balancedness theorem of
[
22
]
for ordinary Boolean relations.

4.5
.

Let
A\subseteq X\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N}
. Then the following are equivalent.

1.

A
is balanced relative to
X
.

2.

For every
u\in X\setminus A
, there exists a prime-power
q_{u}:=p^{a}
(for some prime
p
) and a linear polynomial

\ell_{u}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}z_{N}

over
\mathbb{Z}/q_{u}\mathbb{Z}
such that
\ell_{u}\ \mathrm{captures}_{A}\ u
.

Moreover, if
A
is not balanced relative to
X
, then there exists some

that is not capturable by any linear polynomial over any commutative ring with identity.

Proof.

Let’s first prove
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Assume that
A
is balanced relative to
X
, and
fix
u\in X\setminus A
. By
Lemma

4.4
, we have that
\mathrm{ext}(u)\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\{\mathrm{ext}(a):a\in A\}\bigr)

Let
M
be the matrix whose rows are the vectors
\mathrm{ext}(a)
for
a\in A
,
followed by
\mathrm{ext}(u)
as the final row.

Suppose for contradiction that no such linear capturing polynomial exists.
Then for every prime-power
q
and every nonzero

, the linear system

M\alpha=b_{c},\qquad b_{c}:=(0,\dots,0,c)^{T}

has no solution over
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
.
By
[
22
, Lemma 4.6]
, this implies that for every prime-power
q
,
the last row
\mathrm{ext}(u)
lies in the span of the previous rows modulo
q
.
Then by
[
22

, it follows that
\mathrm{ext}(u)\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\{\mathrm{ext}(a):a\in A\}\bigr),

contradicting

4.4

Therefore there exists a prime-power
q
and coefficients
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}

such that

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}a_{N}=0\qquad\text{for all }a\in A,

while

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}u_{N}\neq 0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}.

Thus

\ell_{u}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N}):=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}z_{N}

captures
u
with respect to
A
.

Next let’s prove the contrapositive of
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
, in the stronger form stated at the end of the theorem.
Assume that
A
is not balanced relative to
X
. Then there
exist an odd integer
m\geq 1
and tuples

such that

We claim that no linear polynomial over any commutative ring with identitycaptures
u
with respect to
A
.

Suppose for contradiction that some commutative ring
E
with identity and some linear polynomial

\ell(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{N}z_{N}

over
E
capture

. By our ring convention, Boolean coordinates and the signs
\pm 1
are
interpreted in

via the map
\mathbb{Z}\to E
,
k\mapsto k\cdot 1_{E}
.

Then
\ell(a)=0
for all
a\in A
, and
\ell(u)\neq 0
.
But our assumption gives

\ell(u)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\bigl(a^{(1)}_{i}-a^{(2)}_{i}+a^{(3)}_{i}-\cdots+a^{(m)}_{i}\bigr).

Since

is odd, the constant term also alternates correctly, and hence

\ell(u)=\ell(a^{(1)})-\ell(a^{(2)})+\ell(a^{(3)})-\cdots+\ell(a^{(m)}).

Since each

lies in

Hence

is not capturable over any commutative ring with identity.
∎

We now obtain the desired equivalence between
t
-balancedness
and degree-
\leq t
capturing.

4.6
.

Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
and let
t\geq 1
. Then the following are equivalent.

1.

R
is
t
-balanced
.

2.

For every
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
, there exists a prime-power
q_{f}
and a multilinear polynomial
p_{f}
over
\mathbb{Z}/q_{f}\mathbb{Z}
of degree at most

captures

with respect to

.

Moreover, if
R
is not
t
-balanced
, then there exists
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
that is not captured by any degree-
\leq t
multilinear polynomial over any commutative ring with identity.

Proof.

By definition,
R
is
t
-balanced
if and only if
R^{(t)}
is balanced relative to
X_{t}
.

We first prove
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Assume that
R
is
t
-balanced
and fix
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
. Since
t\geq 1
, we have that the lift
\nu:=\nu_{t}(f)
lies in
X_{t}\setminus R^{(t)}
(because it clearly is in
X_{t}
, and it cannot be in
R^{(t)}
since
f\notin R
).

Applying
Lemma

4.5
to the pair

R^{(t)}\subseteq X_{t}
, there exist a prime-power
q_{f}
and a linear polynomial
\ell_{\nu}(z)=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{r,t}}\alpha_{S}z_{S}

\ell_{\nu}(v)=0,

\ell_{\nu}(\nu)\neq 0

Define

\mathbb{Z}/q_{f}\mathbb{Z}

If

\nu_{t}(y)\in R^{(t)}

On the other hand,

Therefore

\mathrm{captures}_{R}

f
.

Now we prove
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
, in the stronger form stated at the end of the theorem. Assume that for every
f\in\{0,1\}^{r}\setminus R
,
there exists a degree-
\leq t

is balanced relative to

. Suppose not. Then, by

\nu\in X_{t}\setminus R^{(t)}
that is not capturable by any linear polynomial over any commutative ring with identity. Since the

. By assumption, there exists a commutative ring

\leq t

with respect to

If

5
Lower Bound

In this section we recall the lower-bound framework of Carbonnel
[
20
]
. The role of this section is to justify the lower-bound test used in
Section

3.1
. The framework has two ingredients. First, fgpp-definability formalizes when constraints of one relation can be replaced by constant-size gadgets over another relation while preserving non-redundancy lower bounds. Second, pattern partial polymorphisms give an algebraic characterization of fgpp-definability. For our purposes, the only pattern needed is Carbonnel’s universal
k
-cube pattern: if this pattern fails to preserve
R
, then
R
inherits the
\Omega(n^{k})
non-redundancy lower bound of
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
.

Before defining fgpp-definability formally, let us explain their role. In ordinary pp-definability (famously used by Schaefer
[
61
]
for
P
vs
NP
classification of problems), one expresses a target relation by a conjunction of constraints over a base language. Fgpp-definability extends this by allowing certain unary functional guards required for non-redundancy lower-bounds.

5.1
fgpp-definition.

Let
\Gamma
be a constraint language over a finite domain
D
, and let
R\subseteq D^{m}

be a relation. We say that
R
is
fgpp-definable
from
\Gamma
if there is a formula

R(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\equiv\exists y_{1},\dots,y_{q}\;\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{q}),

where
\psi
is a conjunction of constraints over
\Gamma
together with
functional guards

of the form
Q_{g}(u,v)
, where
g\colon D\to D
is a unary map and

Q_{g}:=\{(a,g(a)):a\in D\}.

We write
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{fg}
for the set of all relations fgpp-definable from
\Gamma
.

Next, we define the pattern partial polymorphisms. The intuition is that a

pattern
specifies a partial operation by listing a collection of allowed input-output
templates. Such operations are more structured than arbitrary partial polymorphisms, but still
rich enough to capture the definability questions relevant here.

5.2
Polymorphism pattern and pattern partial operation.

A
polymorphism pattern
of arity
s
is a set of pairs

((x_{1},\dots,x_{s}),x),

where
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
is a tuple of variables and
x
is one of the variables appearing in it.
Given a finite domain
D
, the
interpretation
of such a pattern
P
on
D
is the
partial operation
f_{P}^{D}
defined as follows:
its domain consists of all tuples of the form

(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{s}))

arising from a pair
((x_{1},\dots,x_{s}),x)\in P
and an assignment
\varphi
of the variables to
D
,
and on such a tuple we set

f_{P}^{D}(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{s})):=\varphi(x).

A partial operation obtained in this way is called a
pattern partial operation
.

We only consider patterns for which this rule is well-defined.

Next we recall what it means for such an operation to preserve a relation. This is the usual
coordinatewise preservation notion for partial polymorphisms, restricted to the operations that
come from patterns.

5.3
Pattern partial polymorphisms.

Let
R\subseteq D^{r}
be a relation and let
f
be a
k
-ary partial operation on
D
.
We say that
f
is a
partial polymorphism
of
R
if for every choice of tuples

t^{(1)},\dots,t^{(k)}\in R
such that
f
is defined coordinatewise on these tuples,
the tuple obtained by applying
f
coordinatewise also belongs to
R
.
If in addition
f
is a pattern partial operation, then we call it a

pattern partial polymorphism
of
R
.

For a constraint language
\Gamma
, we write
\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\Gamma)
for the set of all pattern partial polymorphisms of every relation in
\Gamma
.

The importance of
\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\Gamma)
is that it gives an exact characterization of fgpp-definability: definability on one side corresponds to inclusion of pattern partial
polymorphisms on the other.

5.4
Carbonnel
[
20
, Proposition 7]
.

Let
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
be finite constraint languages over the same finite domain. Then

\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\Gamma_{1})\subseteq\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\Gamma_{2})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\Gamma_{2}\subseteq\langle\Gamma_{1}\rangle_{fg}.

In particular, for a relation
R
and any relation
S
over the same domain,

S\in\langle\{R\}\rangle_{fg}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\{R\})\subseteq\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\{S\}).

To turn this into concrete lower bounds, Carbonnel defines one specific pattern, the
universal
k
-cube pattern. Its role is simple: failure of this pattern is exactly the
criterion that
R
can encode the
k
-ary
\operatorname{OR}
relation, which is the canonical source of
an
\Omega(n^{k})
lower bound.

5.5
Universal
k
-cube pattern
[
20
, Definition 13]
.

Fix
k\geq 2
. Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{2^{k}-1}
be the lexicographic ordering of the tuples in
\{x,y\}^{k}\setminus\{(y,\dots,y)\}

with respect to
y>x
. The
universal
k
-cube pattern

P_{k}^{u}
of arity
2^{k}-1
is the set of pairs
(t_{i},y)
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, where

t_{i}:=\bigl(c_{1}[i],c_{2}[i],\dots,c_{2^{k}-1}[i]\bigr).

Its interpretation on the Boolean domain is denoted by
f^{\{0,1\}}_{P_{k}^{u}}
.

Under the identification
x\mapsto 1
and
y\mapsto 0
, the Boolean interpretation

f^{\{0,1\}}_{P^{u}_{k}}
is exactly the universal
k
-cube operation
f_{k}
used in

Section

3.1
: its domain consists of
0^{m},1^{m},c_{i},1-c_{i}
for

i\in[k]
, where
m=2^{k}-1
, and its values are

f_{k}(0^{m})=0
,
f_{k}(1^{m})=1
,
f_{k}(c_{i})=0
, and
f_{k}(1-c_{i})=1
.

For example, when
k=3
, the left-hand sides of the pairs in
P_{3}^{u}
are exactly the
three columns of the
\operatorname{OR}_{3}
relation, and the right-hand side is the missing
tuple
(0,0,0)
, after the identification
x\mapsto 1
and
y\mapsto 0

[
20
, Example 14]
.

We can now state the lower-bound theorem we need. It says that if the universal
k
-cube
pattern fails on
R
, then
R
inherits the
\Omega(n^{k})
non-redundancy lower bound of

\operatorname{OR}_{k}
.

5.6
[
20
, Lemma 15]
.

Let
R
be a relation of arity
r
over a finite domain
D
, and let
2\leq k\leq r
. If

f^{D}_{P^{u}_{k}}\notin\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\{R\})
, then
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega(n^{k})
.

For us, this theorem provides a direct computational test: if we can certify that the
universal
k
-cube pattern does not preserve
R
, then we immediately obtain an

\Omega(n^{k})
lower bound on
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
.
Consequently,
if

\ell(R):=\max\Bigl(\{1\}\cup\{k\geq 2:f^{\{0,1\}}_{P^{u}_{k}}\notin\operatorname{p^{2}Pol}(\{R\})\}\Bigr).

then
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)=\Omega\bigl(n^{\,\ell(R)}\bigr)
.

6
Literals, Constants, and Repetitions

In this section we justify that allowing literals, constants, repeated variables, or only simple positive constraints changes non-redundancy only up to a constant-factor blow-up in the number of variables. The issue is that fgpp-definability naturally allow unary substitutions such as literals and constants, whereas the set-system discussion in
Section

3.3
uses simple positive constraints, i.e., constraints on distinct variables. We show that, for fixed finite domains and fixed arity, these models are equivalent up to a constant-factor blow-up in the number of variables. The proof is by a simple variable-splitting construction: for each original variable, we create copies indexed by the unary substitution and by the coordinate position in the constraint. This removes literals, constants, and repeated variables while preserving non-redundancy witnesses.

Let
D
be a fixed finite domain, let
\Gamma
be a fixed finite constraint language containing
r
-ary of a relation over
D
. We use three variants of non-redundancy in this section.

1.

First,
\operatorname{NRD}^{+}_{n}(\Gamma)
denotes the usual positive tuple model: constraints have the form
\{R,(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})\}
, where
R\in\Gamma
has arity
k
, and the tuple
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})
is ordered and repetitions are allowed.

2.

Second,
\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{simp}}_{n}(\Gamma)
denotes the simple positive model: constraints again have the form
\{R,(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})\}
, and the variables in the tuple are requred to be distinct.

3.

Third,
\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{lit}}_{n}(\Gamma)
denotes the literal model: constraints have the form
\{R,(g_{1}(x_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g_{k}(x_{i_{k}}))\}
, where
R\in\Gamma
and each
g_{j}
belongs to a fixed finite set
\mathcal{U}
of unary maps
D\to D
containing the identity map.

We treat instances as sets of constraints. Repeated identical constraints are irrelevant for non-redundancy, since duplicate copies are automatically redundant.

6.1
.

Let
D
be a domain of size
|D|=q
, let
\Gamma
be a fixed finite constraint language containing
r
-ary relations over
D
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a finite set of unary maps
D\to D
containing the identity map. Then, for every
n\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{simp}}_{n}(\Gamma)\leq\operatorname{NRD}^{+}_{n}(\Gamma)\leq\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{lit}}_{n}(\Gamma)\leq\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{simp}}_{O_{r,q}(n)}(\Gamma).

Consequently, for fixed
D,\Gamma
, and
\mathcal{U}
, the three models have the same asymptotic non-redundancy up to constant-factor changes in the number of variables.

Proof.

The first two inequalities are immediate. Every simple positive instance is a positive tuple instance, and every positive tuple instance is a literal model instance because
\mathcal{U}
contains the identity map.

It remains to prove
\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{lit}}_{n}(\Gamma)\leq\operatorname{NRD}^{\mathrm{simp}}_{O_{r,q}(n)}(\Gamma)
. Let
I
be a non-redundant literal model instance over variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Thus every constraint
C
of
I
has the form
C=\{R,(g_{1}(x_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g_{r}(x_{i_{r}}))\}
, where
R\in\Gamma
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in\mathcal{U}
.

We construct a simple positive
\mathsf{CSP}(\Gamma)
instance
I^{\mathrm{simp}}
. Its variables are
x_{i}^{g,j}
for
i\in[n]
,
g\in\mathcal{U}
, and
j\in[r]
. Upper bounding the size of
\mathcal{U}
by the set of all unary maps
D\to D
, gives
|\mathcal{U}|\leq|D|^{|D|}
. Thus
I^{\mathrm{simp}}
has
r\cdot|\mathcal{U}|\cdot n=O_{r,q}(n)
variables. We translate each constraint
C=\{R,(g_{1}(x_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g_{r}(x_{i_{r}}))\}
to the positive constraint

C^{\mathrm{simp}}=\{R,(x_{i_{1}}^{g_{1},1},x_{i_{2}}^{g_{2},2},\ldots,x_{i_{r}}^{g_{r},r})\}
.
This constraint is simple: even if the original constraint repeats a variable or a unary map, the coordinate index
j
is different in each position. The translated instance has the same number of constraints as
I
.

We now show that
I^{\mathrm{simp}}
is non-redundant. Fix a constraint
C
of
I
. Since
I
is non-redundant, there is an assignment
\alpha:\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\to D
that satisfies every constraint of
I
except
C
, and falsifies
C
. Define an assignment
\beta
to the variables of
I^{\mathrm{simp}}
by
\beta(x_{i}^{g,j})=g(\alpha(x_{i}))
.

By construction, for every constraint
C^{\prime}
of
I
, the translated constraint
(C^{\prime})^{\mathrm{simp}}
has under
\beta
exactly the same truth value that
C^{\prime}
has under
\alpha
. Therefore
\beta
satisfies every translated constraint except
C^{\mathrm{simp}}
, and falsifies
C^{\mathrm{simp}}
. Hence
C^{\mathrm{simp}}
is not implied by the other translated constraints.

Since this holds for every constraint
C
of
I
, the translated instance
I^{\mathrm{simp}}
is non-redundant. Therefore every non-redundant literal model instance on
n
variables yields a non-redundant simple positive instance on
O_{r,q}(n)
variables with the same number of constraints. This proves the final inequality.
∎

In the Boolean case, if we only need literals and constants, it suffices to take
\mathcal{U}=\{\mathrm{id},\neg,0,1\}
, so
|\mathcal{U}|=4
. Hence the blow-up from the literal-and-constant model to the simple positive model is at most
4rn
variables. In light of this theorem, throughout the paper we suppress these model
distinctions when discussing asymptotic non-redundancy.

7
Conclusion

We studied the non-redundancy of symmetric Boolean predicates. On the upper-bound side, we introduced
t
-balancedness
, a higher-degree extension of the balancedness framework of Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse
[
22
]
, and showed that it yields polynomial capturing representations and hence upper bounds on
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
. On the lower-bound side, we used Carbonnel’s
[
20
]
universal-cube criterion to certify reductions from
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
and obtain matching lower bounds. Combining these two criteria, we classified the asymptotic growth of
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)
for every symmetric Boolean predicate of arity at most
4
, and for all but two predicates of arity
5
.

The most immediate open problem is to resolve the two remaining arity-
5
predicates,

\operatorname{W}(R_{023})=\{0,2,3\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{W}(R_{124})=\{1,2,4\}.

For both predicates, our current criteria give only
\Omega(n^{2})\leq\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(R)\leq O(n^{3})
.
Closing this gap would complete the arity-
5
classification. It would also clarify whether the lifted balancedness upper bound or the universal-cube lower bound can be strengthened, or whether these predicates require genuinely different techniques.

A second direction is to understand non-redundancy beyond symmetric Boolean predicates. Symmetry makes the problem amenable to a finite weight-set analysis, but this structure disappears for general languages. A concrete benchmark is the ternary non-Boolean predicate
\mathrm{BCK}:=\{111,222,012,120,201\}\subseteq\{0,1,2\}^{3}
from Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos
[
10
]
, which has been highlighted in later work as a predicate whose non-redundancy is not explained by the standard universal-cube lower-bound machinery
[
12
]
. Understanding such examples may require new lower-bound mechanisms beyond reductions from
\operatorname{OR}_{k}
, and could be a step toward a structural characterization of the exponent governing
\operatorname{NRD}_{n}(\Gamma)
for arbitrary finite constraint languages.
