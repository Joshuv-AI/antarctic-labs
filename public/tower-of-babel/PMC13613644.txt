Assessment of the knowledge and awareness among general medical practitioners toward dental intervention in diagnosing and treating obstructive sleep apnea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Arti Agrawal, Sharayu Nimonkar, Vikram Belkhode, Surekha Godbole, Namita Zilpilwar, Raghav Munje, Pranali Nimonkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), one of the most common sleep disorders, is associated with a large amount of morbidity to both the heart and metabolic function. Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) remains the gold standard treatment for patients with OSA. In contrast, Dental treatments such as Mandibular Advancement Devices (MADs) are considered well-established alternatives for certain patients. Since General Practitioners (GPs) often initiate the diagnostic and therapeutic management of these patients, the extent of their understanding of dental and craniofacial management options remains unclear.Materials and methodsAn electronic cross-sectional survey was conducted among licensed Medical Practitioners from both the Allopathic and AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Homeopathy) streams. A 15-question questionnaire was used to assess four domains: (1) Aetiology, (2) Diagnosis, (3) Treatment, and (4) Referral Practices. Responses (“Agree,” “Disagree,” “Not aware”) were recorded and converted to a binary system for analysis, where Agree equals 1 and Disagree/Not aware equals 0. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests will help analyse the variation between domains at a statistically significant level (p < 0.05).ResultsDiagnostic awareness was moderate, with 57.6% identifying polysomnography (PSG) as the gold standard test. There was a deficiency in knowledge on the available treatment options: only half of them recognized MADs as efficient. The composite scoring provided a moderate knowledge score (6.72 ± 3.08) and significantly lower mean awareness score (1.79 ± 0.73). There was great heterogeneity between domains (χ2 = 127.31, df = 36, p = 0.0001) as familiarity with dental and craniofacial and referral-related aspects of OSA care was inconsistent.ConclusionGPs showed a greater level of awareness of general diagnostic concepts compared to craniofacial risk factors, oral appliance therapy, or interdisciplinary referral pathways. Specific educational programs and organized medical-dental partnership models should be established in order to enhance integrated management of OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is a disease that has become one of the most widespread chronic illnesses in the world today, as about one out of every ten adults is affected by the disease [1–3]. The rising incidence of OSA has been linked to the increased population of overweight/obese individuals and rising levels of cardiometabolic diseases [2].In addition, the recent rapid urbanisation has resulted in the development of an increasing number of undiagnosed cases of OSA, with an estimated 25 per cent or more of the population having untreated OSA [3]. The high prevalence of habitual snoring, witnessed apneas, and excessive daytime sleepiness is consistently reported in all the studies conducted to date [4, 5]. Without appropriate treatment, OSA leads to numerous adverse effects on the cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurocognitive health of the patient, highlighting the necessity of early recognition and prompt referral [6–8].

The gold-standard treatment for moderate to severe OSA is Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) [9]. CPAP provides adequate physiological effect for the treatment of moderate to severe OSA as long as patients adhere to their CPAP therapy [10–12]. Despite behavioural interventions and support follow-up programs that assist patients in improving their use of CPAP therapy, adherence to CPAP therapy in a real-world setting has not improved for many OSA patients [13, 14]. Because of these limitations, increasing the awareness amongst the primary care physicians regarding the alternative and/or supplemental solutions to treat OSA, such as Lifestyle modifications, Surgical management, Laser Therapy, and Oral devices such as tongue-retaining devices, Palatal Lifters, MADs, etc. may prove to be beneficial [15]. As a result, physicians are likely to face an increasingly diverse therapeutic environment as the primary point of contact for these patients with symptoms [16–18].

Oral appliance therapy, particularly MADs, is now well established as an evidence-based treatment for patients with mild to moderate OSA or for those who are intolerant to CPAP [19]. MADs enhance upper airway patency by anteriorly repositioning the mandible and have demonstrated favourable comfort and compliance compared with CPAP [20–22]. Despite this, misconceptions persist among physicians regarding indications, expected outcomes, and referral processes for dental sleep medicine interventions [23].

GPs play a crucial role in recognising and treating OSA [24]. Most GPs regularly assess patients with the symptoms of heavy snoring, uncontrolled high blood pressure, unexplained daytime sleepiness, or heart problems [25, 26]. However, studies show that the level of knowledge that GPs have regarding the role of dentists in managing OSA is significantly lacking [27]. There is also low awareness of the American Academy of Dental Sleep Medicine (AADSM’s) recognised training standards, which are new and are necessary to provide adequate training for professionals to deliver effective Apnea care [28, 29]. Hence, this study aims to assess and measure the knowledge level and awareness of the GPs about OSA, its possible causes, diagnosis, available treatment options & the need for a referral to a specialized Dentist [30].

Materials and methods

Ethical aspects

An approval for the study was received from the Institutional Ethical Committee (Ref. no- DMIHER(DU)/IEC/2025/757). The Cross-sectional study began only after receiving approval. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants prior to the study.

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess GPs knowledge and awareness regarding dental involvement in the diagnosis and management of OSA.

Study setting and duration

The data collection took place via an online survey sent out over a pre-set three-month timeframe via email.

Participants

The participants were licensed medical practitioners who were actively involved in clinical practice in allopathic (MBBS, MD, MS, DNB) and AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Homeopathy) streams. It represents a distinct stream of medical education and practice, operating under its own ministry (Ministry of Ayush) to provide holistic, preventive, and cost-effective treatment alongside modern medicine. As these practitioners often first come into contact with OSA patients in India during Primary care screening, their awareness regarding dental intervention in sleep apnea care holds significant importance, which in turn will affect the referral pathway.

Sample size justification

At least 100 subjects were considered to be sufficient to identify medium effect sizes in chi-square-based awareness comparisons (power = 0.80, α = 0.05). The obtained sample (n = 125) was large enough and comparable to prior awareness research in sleep medicine. One hundred and twenty-five of the total answers were evaluated.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study included licensed medical practitioners who were actively engaged in treating patients.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded incomplete questionnaire answers and duplicate responses.

Sampling technique

Voluntary sampling was based on convenience, with the addition of snowball recruitment through professional networks.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire was well framed and consisted of 15 items under four domains, namely aetiology (4 items), diagnosis (3 items), treatment (5 items), and referral practices (3 items) (Table 1). Each of the domains included questions that were aimed at measuring and comparing the level of knowledge of the GPs in reference to dental intervention. This was formulated on the basis of the studies conducted of a similar nature [11]. A panel of experts was constituted to review the questionnaire to analyse it with respect to dental intervention in OSA. The panel has also taken into account the face validity of the questionnaire as well as its design and structure. They evaluated the appropriateness of questions to estimate the relevant field. The alpha of Cronbach in the test questionnaire was 0.7, which concluded that the internal consistency of the questionnaire used in the study was good, and the test-retest and Kappa (0.80) were used to establish reliability. Pilot testing was used on 12 practitioners to ensure clarity and response. A prosthodontist, a dental sleep medicine specialist, and a sleep physician determined content validity.

The study questionnaire

Scoring

The analysis was split into three responses: “Agree, Disagree, Not aware.”

Agree = 1; Disagree/Not aware = 0.

Among the 15 questions, those numbered 1 to 12 were used to assess knowledge, and those numbered 13 to 15 were used to assess awareness. Composite scores were obtained with regard to: The Knowledge Score (aetiology + diagnosis + treatment; range 0–12), Awareness Score ( items related to referral; range 0–3). This dichotomous system of scoring is consistent with previous research in awareness that focused on recognition and a lack of knowledge [31].

Data collection

Institutional mailing lists and clinician networks were used to send the questionnaire through Google Forms. The responses were analyzed in a secure spreadsheet of those that were completed. Two discrete missing responses identified on Q11 and Q12 (0.8% each) were re-coded as system-Missing and left out in item-level percentage calculations.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS v27 was used to analyse the data. Item-wise responses were summarised using frequencies and percentages. There were manual checks on data to ensure that there were no incomplete or incorrect entries. Frequency distributions were compared to raw response sheets provided by the statistician.

Results

The outcome was derived by the analysis of 125 complete responses.

Awareness of etiological factors

There was a significant difference in awareness of craniofacial contributors to OSA. The aetiological factor that was most commonly identified was malocclusion (78.4%). The 60.8% of respondents recognized macroglossia. Knowledge about retrognathia was significantly less (40.8% agreement; 31.2% unaware). The highest knowledge gap was found to be in the high-arched palate, where 38.4% of the respondents said they did not know that it can be related to OSA. Generally, the respondents showed moderate knowledge of common craniofacial risk factors but had less knowledge of structural airway factors other than malocclusion.

Table 3 shows the distribution of responses to aetiological items. In general, there was an average aetiological awareness with a consistent discrepancy in craniofacial feature recognition.

Awareness of diagnostic modalities

There was a significant variability in the case of diagnostic awareness (Table 4). Most people (76.8%) stated that the neck circumference is a good screening measure. Only 57.6% of them identified PSG as the gold-standard diagnostic test correctly. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) was considered a supplementary anatomical evaluation tool by 64.0%, and almost a quarter of the respondents did not know its purpose. These findings are an indicator of greater knowledge of general clinical predictors as compared to advanced diagnostic tools in dental sleep medicine.

Table 4 provides an overall overview of item-level knowledge of diagnostic methods. There was a relatively high level of diagnostic knowledge, but the PSG familiarity was still suboptimal.

Awareness of treatment modalities

The awareness on the availability of treatment varied among items (Table 5). The 62.4% identified CPAP as the gold-standard treatment of severe OSA. A low awareness of MADs as an evidence-based intervention in mild-moderate OSA was found (50.4%). The level of awareness was low (34.4% agreement; 27.2% unaware) regarding orthognathic surgery. Knowledge of palatal lifters was also poorly represented (36.8% agreement). Only 58.4% of respondents recognized lifestyle modification as beneficial. Such results demonstrate the obvious gap in knowledge about dental and maxillofacial treatment plans. The palatal lifter and lifestyle modification items had two entries (0.8%) system-missing; these were not interpreted according to recoding guidelines. Treatment awareness had the lowest score, especially when it comes to dental and maxillofacial modalities.

Referral practices and interdisciplinary awareness

The weakest area was the referral-level awareness (Table 6). The majority of the respondents (73.6%) admitted that medical-dental collaboration is significant to OSA care; 91.2% stated that they lack knowledge about dental treatment options, which means that they have limited access to dental sleep medicine training pathways. Composite knowledge and awareness scores

The average composite knowledge score was 6.72 on a scale of 1–12 (with a Standard Deviation of 3.08); this indicates the majority of respondents showed some familiarity with the fundamental concepts, but the range of results varied greatly. Conversely, Respondents’ average Awareness score was only 1.79 on a scale of 1–3 (with a Standard Deviation of 0.73). Respondents’ low awareness score suggests that they were ill-equipped to recognize when dental interventions might be needed or to determine the appropriate pathway to obtain dental referrals in such situations. A correlation exists between knowledge and awareness (X^2 = 127.31, df = 36, p < 0.0001) based on a Chi-Square Analysis; however, the pattern of how respondents answered indicates that while an increase in factual knowledge does not reliably correlate with a higher likelihood of making referrals. Discussion

This study assessed general medical practitioners’ knowledge and awareness across aetiological, diagnostic, therapeutic, and referral domains related to OSA. The results demonstrate a regular pattern: a moderate level of factual knowledge and significantly poor awareness of dental and interdisciplinary aspects, and most importantly, those that are needed to provide integrated OSA care. The close correlation between Knowledge and Awareness scores (χ 2 = 127.31, df = 36, p < 0.0001) suggests that these deficits are systematic, not random. There were three different areas where practitioner performance was lowest. Craniofacial risk factors were generally not well recognised by most individuals. Individuals’ understanding of craniofacial conditions (e.g., retrognathia, narrow maxilla, or high palate) did not develop as a result of their understanding of the apparent signs of malocclusion [28–30]. This continues to support previous studies, such as that conducted by Smith et al., that demonstrate craniofacial morphology is generally not included in OSA assessments completed by practitioners of OSA [32]. The average composite mean score for craniofacial knowledge was 6.72 ± 3.08, representing a broad range of variability in individuals’ understanding of these craniofacial characteristics at the time of their first visit [33].

The level of awareness among practitioners regarding diagnostic testing for diagnosing OSA was moderate. Nearly all respondents reported being aware of neck circumference measurement as a useful screening tool; however, fewer than 60% correctly identified PSG as the gold standard for diagnosing OSA. Similarly, knowledge of CBCT and other types of diagnostic imaging was inconsistent, suggesting that dental radiographic knowledge was not being incorporated into diagnostic assessments of OSA [34–36].

Therapeutic knowledge was the weakest area apart from referrals. Only half of the respondents were aware that MAD would be effective treatments for mild to moderate OSA and that surgical management was rarely considered [37–40]. Referral knowledge demonstrated the most significant gaps, with an average awareness score of only 1.79 ± 0.73. More than 80 per cent of respondents had no knowledge of formal dental sleep medicine continuing education programs, including the AADSM Clinical Mastery Program. Limited understanding of provider qualifications and indications for referral creates a barrier to safe and appropriate multidisciplinary management [41, 42].

The lower diagnostic and craniofacial awareness in this cohort contrasts with earlier studies, as conducted by Barnes et al., which reported stronger recognition of PSG and craniofacial features [43]. There are three contextual explanations that are plausible. To start with, the MBBS undergraduate programmes expose students to little dental anatomy, craniofacial development, or functional airway assessment, compared to previous cohorts who were exposed to more anatomy-focused curricula. Second, Indian medical and dental systems are mainly parallel systems with limited interdisciplinary training and referral systems. Third, continuing professional development (CPD) in India tends to prioritise cardiometabolic updates; sleep medicine topics, particularly dental-sleep interfaces, receive limited focus. Prior studies that sampled specialists or conference-attending clinicians would therefore overestimate general practice awareness relative to the broader practitioner base captured in this study [44–48].

A significant barrier to optimal patient care regarding the craniofacial aspects of OSA, including diagnosis and referral for dental treatment and the use of oral appliances, is the lack of knowledge in these areas. Due to inadequate clinician training and a lack of understanding of the craniofacial aspects of OSA, multiple cases have been reported in the literature, leading to delayed diagnosis, misclassification of disease severity, and inability to identify appropriate patients for treatment. In addition to an insufficient understanding of the craniofacial aspect of OSA, many patients will not receive effective treatment options if the physician or dentist is unfamiliar with these treatment modalities, forcing the patients to undergo more invasive CPAP therapy, as well as the unnecessary burden of keeping these patients in a sleep laboratory for an excessive amount of time. The overall lack of awareness of referral requirements and required training contributes to delays in appropriate referrals, further reducing the quality of patient care.

Limitations of the present study

In the interpretation of the findings, there are a number of limitations involved in this study. Selecting convenience sampling can also have brought about selection bias, which could have contributed to the lack of representativeness of the study population. Though a digital platform was used to recruit participants, the sample might not be overall representative of the practitioners in the different geographic locations, especially those working in rural or underrepresented areas. Also, the use of a self-reported questionnaire makes response bias, such as recall bias and social desirability bias, possible and, consequently, affects the accuracy of the data reported. There was a lack of demographic variables collected, which limited the possibility of doing subgroup analysis or determining how the characteristics of the participants affected the results. Although the study sample (n = 125) was statistically satisfactory, a bigger and more multicentric research with a more heterogeneous and systematically chosen population would increase the generalizability and external validity of the results.

Further scope

The present study provides a structured evaluation of GPs knowledge and Awareness regarding dental intervention in OSA care. It provides a strong insight into the limited referral practice, which will help promote various educational programmes and the development of targeted continuing medical education (CME) programmes. It will form a basis for inculcating dental sleep medicine into the curriculum for undergraduate and postgraduate medical training. Inclusion of programs related to dental sleep medicine, similar to AADSM under the Indian Dental Association (IDA), will certainly help to improve the quality of sleep apnea care in India.

Conclusion

The survey results indicate that most GPs are unaware of the craniofacial factors associated with OSA and the role of dentists and their procedures in the diagnosis and treatment of this condition. GPs have limited knowledge about the various treatment options available for patients who are unable or unwilling to use CPAP machines due to this lack of understanding. Therefore, GPs are unsuccessful in making a proper referral to the correct type of dental sleep medicine specialist. Educating and training GPs, along with providing them with additional resources, will enable them to more effectively identify and refer patients who are suitable for dental sleep medicine specialists. Incorporating dental sleep medicine into the integrated clinical management of all patients diagnosed with OSA may also reduce delays between diagnosis and the commencement of treatment, creating a more holistic and comprehensive, patient-centred approach to managing OSA.
