Use of Glucagon‐Like Peptide 1 Receptor Agonists and the Associated Risk of Hospitalisation in Bipolar Disorder, From a Nationwide Cohort, 2009–2024
Authors: Heidi Taipale, Mark Taylor, Markku Lähteenvuo, Ellenor Mittendorfer‐Rutz, Antti Tanskanen, Jari Tiihonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDiabetes, obesity and bipolar disorder often co‐occur and may have shared pathophysiology. Glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) treat diabetes and obesity but their impact on bipolar disorder is unknown. In this era of stagnated pharmacotherapy, we examined psychiatric hospitalisation and absence from work due to sick leave as potential measures of relapse in people diagnosed with bipolar disorder who were also prescribed antidiabetic medications, including GLP‐1RA.MethodsThe study cohort was derived from the National Swedish Registers and included all people with a diagnosis of bipolar disorder who used any antidiabetic medication, between the years 2009 and 2024. GLP‐1RA individually and as a group were compared with non‐use of GLP‐1RA, and directly with other second‐line antidiabetic medications. A within‐individual design was utilised for all comparisons to reduce confounding. The main outcome was psychiatric hospitalisation for any reason, with secondary outcomes being psychiatric hospitalisation after a relapse of bipolar disorder, and sick leave due to psychiatric reasons. Within‐individual stratified Cox models with adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used.Results14,694 people were included in the study, of whom 5200 used GLP‐1RA. Semaglutide was associated with a 21% (aHR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.91) lower risk of psychiatric hospitalisation compared with non‐use of GLP‐1RA in that same individual. Liraglutide and dulaglutide were not specifically associated with a lower hospitalisation risk. Semaglutide was also linked to a 17% (aHR 0.83, 95% CI 0.69–0.99) lower risk of relapse of bipolar disorder. Absences from work due to sick leave were not significantly associated with the specific antidiabetic medications studied.ConclusionIn people with both bipolar disorder and diabetes and/or obesity, the use of semaglutide was linked to lower rates of psychiatric hospitalisation compared with time periods when GLP‐1RA were not used by that individual. Liraglutide and dulaglutide were not associated with reduced psychiatric hospitalisation, implying this is not a class effect. This finding with semaglutide should be further tested in a randomised controlled trial.

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) are licensed for the treatment of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and obesity in many countries [1]. The worldwide uptake of GLP‐1RA is increasing, including off‐label use and online prescribing; for example, in the Stockholm region of Sweden the prescription prevalence of GLP‐1RA has risen from 4.7 to 17.5 per 1000 people [2] between 2019 and 2023.

An estimated 14% of adults or ~830 million worldwide live with diabetes [3], with over 95% having T2DM [3]. Bipolar disorder, with a prevalence of 1%, affects ~40 million people worldwide [4] and primarily affects younger adults leading to potential lifelong disability [4]. For people with bipolar disorder, the rate of T2DM [5] and obesity [6] is roughly doubled, with a ~9.5% prevalence [5] of T2DM in bipolar. Knowing their shared biopsychosocial risk factors, it's likely that bipolar disorder and T2DM have a bidirectional relationship [7].

A recent review [8] suggested that GLP‐1RA may be of value in bipolar disorder due to direct brain effects including reduced neuroinflammation, as bipolar disorder has been linked with oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [9]. Thus far, however, there have not been any original research studies evaluating GLP‐1RA use in people with bipolar disorder.

In a large new observational study [10] we found that semaglutide and, to a lesser extent, liraglutide were associated with improvements in depression, anxiety and reduced self‐harm, which is consistent with the latest large meta‐analysis that concluded there were no major adverse psychiatric outcomes attributable to GLP‐1RA usage [11].

Here we evaluate the psychiatric outcomes of people taking antidiabetic medications, including GLP‐1RA, who also have a diagnosis of bipolar disorder, using the national Swedish case registers. We specifically focus on the risk of psychiatric hospitalisation in this group, given the clinical significance of this event for both the individual and the health service. As suggested [8], GLP‐1RA may offer novel therapeutic models in bipolar disorder in an era of stagnated pharmacotherapy.

Sweden has a decentralised government funded healthcare system, available to all people free of charge, with private or insurance funded healthcare being a rarity. Periods of GLP‐1RA use were compared in the same person to periods when they did not use GLP‐1RA, or when other second‐line antidiabetic medications were prescribed.

Aims of the Study

We aimed to study the risk of psychiatric hospitalisation and sick leave away from work associated with GLP‐1RA use in people with bipolar disorder who also take antidiabetic medications.

Methods

We involved a person with related lived experience in the study design and write‐up. Ethnicity data were not available.

Study Population

National Swedish electronic health‐registers were used to obtain and combine data through personal pseudonymised identification numbers. Informed consent is not required in Sweden for register‐based studies where there is no patient contact. The National Patient registry for in‐patient and specialist out‐patient visits as well as Microdata for Analysis of the Social Insurance (MiDAS) register containing data on sick leave (absence from work due to ill‐health) and disability pension diagnoses were used to identify study subjects.

The study cohort included people with a diagnosis of bipolar disorder (using the International Classification of Diseases, vol 10—ICD10—diagnostic codes, F30 and F31) who used any antidiabetic medication during the follow‐up, between the years 2009 and 2024. The period from 2009 was chosen due to the availability of GLP‐1RA in Sweden and ended in 2024 when data linkage ceased. People with a previous diagnosis of schizophrenia‐spectrum disorders (F20–F29) before bipolar disorder were excluded as clinically this represents a different population. Cohort entry was defined as the last of the following: first bipolar disorder diagnosis, start of first antidiabetic medication and 1 January 2009, when follow‐up for GLP‐1RA started. Subject follow‐up ceased due to death, emigration, developing schizophrenia or on the 31 December 2024.

Exposures

The PRE2DUP method [12] was used to construct individual drug use periods by modelling data from the National Prescribed Drug Register. The exposures analysed were the most frequently prescribed GLP‐1RA (Anatomic Chemical Therapeutic classification code A10BJ), namely semaglutide; liraglutide and dulaglutide. Lixisenatide and exenatide were not reported due to lack of use but they were included in the group category of all the GLP‐1RA, which was also constructed.

GLP‐1RA were also compared in a secondary analysis against the most used other second‐line antidiabetics, which were sodium‐glucose co‐transporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors empagliflozin (ATC A10BK03) and dapagliflozin (A10BK01) and dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor (DPP‐4) sitagliptin (A10BH01).

Outcomes

The main outcome was any psychiatric hospitalisation (F00–F99) during the study period. The main outcome was also analysed after excluding psychiatric hospitalisation due to substance use disorders (SUDs; F00–F09, F20–F99), as people with bipolar disorder can often have comorbid substance misuse [4, 6].

Secondary outcomes included hospitalisation specifically due to a relapse of bipolar disorder and sickness absence from work (either full‐time or part‐time sick leave of more than 14 days) due to psychiatric reasons (F00–F99). In this analysis of sick leave and time away from work, individuals who were on disability pension (N = 4168) at cohort entry were excluded as they are not at risk. This analysis ceased on 31 December 2022, when data linkage to MiDAS ended.

Statistical Analysis

We used a within‐individual design where each individual acts as their own control. Cox regression models with fixed effects were used to calculate the within‐individual risk of an outcome. Within‐individual design minimises selection bias and automatically eliminates the impact of time‐invariant characteristics (gender, baseline severity of illness or other comorbid conditions) by comparing specific treatments periods within the same subjects. Time‐varying covariates were adjusted for, and these were temporal order of exposures (GLP‐1RA use vs. GLP‐1RA non‐use periods in the main analysis), time since cohort entry and use of other medications namely mood stabilisers; antipsychotics, antidepressants, other antidiabetic medications; medications used for SUDs and benzodiazepines and related drugs.

In the main model, individual GLP‐1RA were compared with non‐use of GLP‐1RA within the same individuals. During both GLP‐1RA use and non‐use periods, the same individuals could also use other antidiabetics, which was then adjusted for in the analysis.

For the main outcome, we also conducted an analysis (see Supporting Information Data S1) omitting the first 30 days from exposure and non‐exposure periods to account for possible bias arising from preferred initiation when being more clinically stable versus not initiating/more likely discontinuing when psychiatric symptoms worsen, acknowledging that pharmacological effects carry over some time after discontinuation.

As a secondary comparison, use of GLP‐1RA was compared with other individual antidiabetic medications. These analyses were restricted to monotherapies of second‐line antidiabetics, namely monotherapy of liraglutide, semaglutide, empagliflozin, dapagliflozin or sitagliptin (adjustment was made for concurrent use of first‐line antidiabetics, such as metformin or insulin).

Results are presented as adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Data analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

The total cohort comprised 14,694 individuals, with the mean age at cohort entry being 53.9 years (SD 15.5) years. A majority (60.5% N = 8890) of the study cohort were female (Table 1). Sociodemographic or socioeconomic characteristics of people using GLP‐1RA (N = 5200, 68.0% female) did not differ from the total cohort, but they were more likely to be on sick leave at cohort entry. The mean follow‐up period for the cohort was 6.0 (SD 5.2) years.

GLP‐1RA were used by 5200 people (see Table 1, 35.4% of total cohort) during the follow‐up period, and of them, 3682 were prescribed semaglutide (70.8%) and 2047 (39.4%) were prescribed liraglutide, as each person might be prescribed more than one GLP‐1RA over the study period.

During the follow‐up period 5288 individuals experienced the main outcome of psychiatric hospitalisation due to any cause at least once (Figure 1). Semaglutide (aHR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.91) was associated with a decreased risk of psychiatric hospitalisation compared with time periods when the same individuals did not use GLP‐1RA.

Use of GLP‐1RA and risk of any psychiatric hospitalisation, and any psychiatric hospitalisation excluding substance use disorder (SUD)‐related hospitalisations in bipolar disorder.

When all GLP‐1RA were grouped together a similar reduction in risk of psychiatric hospitalisation was observed (Figure 1, aHR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.79–0.97).

Other GLP‐1RA were not associated with a significant decreased risk of psychiatric hospitalisation; however, they were also less commonly used, which reduced statistical power. When semaglutide was compared directly with any other GLP‐1RA used by the same individual, the aHR was 0.83 (0.71–0.98), suggesting a specific decreased risk for semaglutide among the 1274 people using both semaglutide and another GLP‐1RA during the follow‐up.

When the first 30 days of treatment were omitted from all exposures and non‐exposure periods, semaglutide was still associated with decreased risk of psychiatric hospitalisations (aHR 0.78, 95% CI 0.68–0.90) (Supporting Information Data S1).

When SUD‐related hospitalisations were removed from the main outcome (restricting to psychiatric hospitalisations not due to mainly SUDs), semaglutide was still associated with 20% reduced risk (0.80, 0.67–0.94).

Hospitalisation specifically due to a relapse of bipolar disorder was analysed separately, and this outcome occurred in 1634 people during the follow‐up period, with a similar pattern being found. Semaglutide was linked with a reduced risk of hospitalisation due to bipolar disorder (aHR = 0.83; 95% CI = 0.69–0.99) but liraglutide and dulaglutide were not (Figure 2). However, all GLP‐1RA as a group (n = 5200) were noted to be linked to reduced risk of hospitalisation due to bipolar relapse (aHR = 0.85, 95% CI = 0.74–0.97).

Use of GLP‐1RA and risk of hospitalisation due to a relapse of bipolar disorder specifically.

When liraglutide and semaglutide were compared head‐to‐head with the most prescribed second‐generation antidiabetic, empagliflozin, as a reference, only trends in reduced risk of hospitalisation were noted (see Figure 3) for semaglutide, linagliptin and dapagliflozin.

Sick leave due to psychiatric reasons was analysed in a sub‐cohort of all individuals who were not on disability pension at cohort entry (N = 10,526), of whom 2466 people experienced medically ratified sick leave during the study period (see Figure 4). Use of a specific GLP‐1RA or all GLP‐1RA together as a group was not significantly associated with a decreased risk of sick leave due to psychiatric reasons.

Discussion

We found (see Figure 1) that semaglutide use was associated with a 21% reduced risk of psychiatric hospitalisation compared with time periods when the same individual was not using GLP‐1RA, over the 15‐year study period of people with bipolar disorder and diabetes and/or obesity. Semaglutide use was also associated with a decreased risk of hospitalisation due to bipolar relapse.

When all GLP‐1RA were analysed together as a group (Figure 1), a significant 13% associated reduction in psychiatric hospitalisation rate was noted. However, specific GLP‐1RA other than semaglutide may lack numeric power in these analyses. Whilst some of the reduction in psychiatric hospital admissions in bipolar disorder may be due to diminished substance misuse [13], when the analysis was restricted to hospitalisation due to bipolar relapse alone, a similar pattern of results emerged, with semaglutide being linked to a 17% reduced risk and GLP‐1RA as a group being associated with a 15% reduction (Figure 2). It is conceivable that worsening psychiatric symptoms may have reduced the likelihood of an individual being prescribed semaglutide or continued in people who were already well engaged with care‐providers, but that possibility would not explain the differential effect observed between GLP‐1RA.

The findings on decreased risks of hospitalisation during GLP‐1RA use are clinically important as hospitalisation rates for bipolar disorder are high, with typical reported 1‐year rates of 3% [14] and a lifetime incidence for hospitalisation of 75% [15]. The mean length of stay in a psychiatric hospital in Sweden for bipolar disorder is around 21 days [16], with substantial economic and personal costs involved. Predictors of hospitalisation for bipolar disorder include the severity of illness and comorbid conditions such as SUD. We and others have previously shown [13, 17, 18] that GLP‐1RA can be beneficial for SUD, but when we excluded cases of comorbid SUD, the results linking semaglutide with reduced risk of hospitalisation remained, implying there may be a direct mood effect as well as an anticipated anti‐craving effect.

The low numbers of people with both diabetes and bipolar who were employed may explain the lack of significant correlation between being sick away from work (sick leave) and GLP‐1RA use (Figure 4). In a similar study with a larger sample size [10], we found that there was an association with significantly reduced sick leave or time off work due to anxiety or depression in people using GLP‐1RA. Association between GLP‐1RA use and work‐related outcomes should be examined in longer studies with larger samples, as being employed is important for people with bipolar disorder.

The psychiatric effects of GLP‐1RA could be due to a direct effect on brain function as well as via weight loss [1]. GLP‐1 hormone may reduce neuroinflammation through decreased oxidative stress and produce less reactive oxygen species and inflammatory cytokines [18]. GLP‐1 receptor stimulation has also been associated [19] with neuroprotective and neurotrophic properties in animal studies, and rat hippocampal neurons treated with GLP‐1RA were more resistant to glutamate excitotoxicity, leading to enhanced neural growth and differentiation [19, 20]. This possibly supports opinion [8] that GLP‐1RA may be beneficial in bipolar disorder. However, further research is needed and ideally GLP‐1RA should be investigated in randomised controlled trials among individuals with bipolar disorder.

Study Limitations

The main limitation here is that causality cannot be attributed in an observational study. Fluctuating bipolar symptoms were captured by adjusting time‐varying use of other psychotropic medications, and the severity of an individual's diabetes was also controlled for by adjusting time‐varying use of other antidiabetics, including metformin and insulins, although there are no individual‐level data on symptom severity or parameters such as weight change, HbA1C or BMI in these national registers.

Residual confounding, particularly by indication, may still occur as in all observational studies despite the within‐individual comparisons and adjustment for temporal order of exposures and concomitant medication use, covering both the severity of diabetes and the psychiatric condition. Statistical power was confined mainly to semaglutide, whereas results on other specific GLP‐1RA may have suffered from a lack of power.

The availability of GLP‐1RA varied during the study period, and drug shortages may have hindered initiation and continuation of semaglutide use, especially. However, this would dilute the estimates for semaglutide.

Our results may only be generalisable to similar healthcare systems to Sweden, where healthcare is provided to all residents with minimal out‐of‐pocket costs. The price of GLP‐1RA may hinder access for those who would benefit most from these medications in private healthcare systems, at least until more affordable generic versions become available. In this study, it is also possible that the cost of GLP‐1RA had some influence even though second‐line antidiabetic medications are all comparably expensive. As in many other countries, GLP‐1RA were reimbursed only for treatment of T2DM, which may suggest that the subgroup of GLP‐1RA users treated for obesity has a higher socioeconomic status.

Finally, our study period covered the COVID‐19 pandemic and it is possible that COVID‐19 skewed our results. However, previous Swedish studies on the impact of COVID‐19 noted a lower suicide rate and no major changes in sick leave [21, 22].

Conclusions

Using long‐term national observational data, we found that semaglutide was linked to significantly lower rates of psychiatric hospitalisation in people with bipolar disorder and diabetes and/or obesity, compared with time periods when they were not using GLP‐1RA. These findings support suggestions that GLP‐1RA have therapeutic potential for bipolar disorder, in an era of stagnated pharmacotherapy, and this finding could and should be tested in a randomised controlled trial.
