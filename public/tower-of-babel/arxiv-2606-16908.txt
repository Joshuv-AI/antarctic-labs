LESS Is More: Mutual-Stability Sampling for Diffusion Language Models
Amr Mohamed, Guokan Shang, Michalis Vazirgiannis

Abstract
Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) offer a promising alternative to autoregressive decoding by iteratively refining masked sequences, enabling parallel token updates and bidirectional conditioning. Their practical efficiency, however, is limited by sampling procedures that execute a fixed number of reverse denoising steps selected before decoding, spending computation on already-stable positions and sometimes committing unstable ones too early. We present LESS , a training-free, model-agnostic adaptive sampler that treats token commitment as an online stopping problem. LESS implements mutual-stability sampling through a joint stability rule that makes a masked position eligible for unmasking only when its top-1 prediction has high confidence, its top-1 token persists across recent reverse steps, and its predictive distribution is stable under top- K K inter-step Jensen–Shannon divergence. We evaluate LESS on Dream-7B, LLaDA-8B, and LLaDA-1.5-8B, covering full-sequence diffusion and semi-autoregressive blockwise sampling regimes, across seven benchmarks spanning general knowledge, math, and code. LESS improves average accuracy over strong training-free adaptive samplers while using 72.1 % 72.1\% fewer reverse steps than fixed-budget decoding. Since each reverse step requires a Transformer forward pass, these step-count reductions translate into fewer forward evaluations, lower measured wall-clock latency, and lower estimated inference compute.

Large language models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly, yet text generation remains dominated by autoregressive (AR) decoding, limiting opportunities for global refinement
(
3

34

36

. Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) have emerged as a compelling alternative: instead of generating one token at a time, they iteratively denoise masked sequences, enabling parallel token updates, bidirectional conditioning over partially generated text, and natural infilling
(
39
)
. This paradigm has matured quickly, with recent work training capable diffusion LMs from scratch
(
23
;

;

)
, adapting AR checkpoints to diffusion objectives
(

, and extending dLLMs to reasoning, planning, and code-generation
(

. Together with scaling evidence
(

, these advances position dLLMs as practical language modeling architectures.

Despite this promise, decoding efficiency remains a central bottleneck for dLLMs. Generation proceeds through a reverse-diffusion chain with many refinement steps, and standard samplers require the reverse-step budget and unmasking schedule to be chosen
a priori
, often conservatively to avoid quality loss across tasks
(
23
;
33
;
38

. This fixed schedule ignores position-wise convergence: empirical studies of adaptive dLLM decoding show that some token predictions stabilize well before the final reverse step
(
17
;
15
;
19
)
. As a result, some positions continue to be recomputed after their local predictive distributions have stabilized, while others may be unmasked before those distributions have settled. Since most dLLM samplers treat unmasked tokens as fixed, premature commitment is difficult to recover from: once an incorrect token is written, later reverse steps typically cannot revise it.

A growing body of work addresses this bottleneck from complementary directions. Training-time methods shorten the reverse process through distillation, curricula, or flow matching
(
26
;
5
;
20
)
, while systems methods reduce per-step latency through caching or speculative execution
(
18
;
13
;
1
;
7
)
. Training-free samplers instead adapt when positions are unmasked, using confidence, margin, or inter-step divergence signals from the model’s predictive distributions
(
17
;
15
;
31
;
19
)
. These methods show that predictions often stabilize before the final diffusion step, but leave open a fundamental question:
when is a masked position stable enough to stop refining and commit?

We address this question by framing adaptive unmasking as per-position online stopping. Prior training-free samplers provide partial criteria: confidence- and margin-based methods test whether the current prediction is locally decisive, while divergence-aware methods test whether consecutive predictive distributions are close
(
17
;
31
;
19
;
15
)
.
LESS
extends this line by treating stability as a joint property of the current prediction and its recent reverse-time trajectory. It implements mutual-stability sampling with a joint rule: a position is eligible for unmasking only when its top-1 prediction is confident, its top-1 token persists across recent reverse steps, and its top-
K
inter-step Jensen–Shannon divergence is small.

The top-
K
inter-step Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) is the key distributional signal in
LESS
. It provides a bounded, symmetric measure of how much a position’s predictive distribution changes between reverse steps. This matters as confidence alone cannot reveal ongoing distributional motion, and a fixed top-1 token can hide redistribution among plausible alternatives. Together, the three signals yield a training-free, model-agnostic joint stability rule computed from predictive distributions already produced during decoding.

We evaluate
LESS
on three dLLMs across seven math, code, and general-knowledge benchmarks.
LESS
improves the accuracy–efficiency trade-off on average over strong training-free adaptive samplers, with the most pronounced gains on math and code. Ablations show that confidence is the primary acceptance signal, while persistence and inter-step JSD provide complementary safeguards. FLOPs profiling and wall-clock measurements confirm that step reductions translate into fewer Transformer forward evaluations and lower decoding latency. Our contributions are:

We formulate dLLM token commitment as a per-position online stopping problem induced by irreversible unmasking.

•

We introduce top-

•

We present
LESS
, a training-free sampler that combines confidence, top-1 token persistence, and inter-step JSD in a joint stability rule.

•

We validate
LESS
across three dLLM families and seven benchmarks, showing improved average accuracy–efficiency trade-offs with measured compute savings.

Related Work

Masked diffusion language models.

Masked diffusion language models generate text by iteratively denoising partially masked sequences, exposing per-position predictive distributions at each reverse step. Recent dLLMs such as LLaDA, Dream, and LLaDA-1.5 support instruction following, reasoning, and code generation
(
23
;
33
;
38
)
. Related works study adaptation from AR checkpoints, scaling behavior, long-context extension, and code-specialized variants
(
8
;
22
;
9
;
29
)
. These efforts position dLLMs as a practical alternative to AR generation across diverse generation settings, but inference remains dominated by reverse denoising steps.

Efficient dLLM inference.

Prior acceleration methods reduce this cost in three complementary ways. Training-time methods shorten the reverse process through curricula, consistency-style distillation, or flow matching
(
26
;
5
;
20
)
; systems methods reduce per-step cost through diffusion-aware caching, speculative decoding, or related reuse mechanisms
(
18
;
13
;
1
;
7
)
; and inference-time adaptive samplers change
which
positions are unmasked and
when
.
LESS
belongs to the third class and is orthogonal to the first two: it reduces the number of reverse steps and can in principle be combined with faster per-step execution.

Adaptive token commitment.

Training-free adaptive samplers typically accelerate dLLM decoding by committing tokens once a local reliability criterion is satisfied. Confidence-based rules unmask positions whose top prediction or top-1/top-2 margin is large
(
17
;
31
;
19
)
; these methods are effective when confidence is well aligned with convergence, but confidence alone does not explicitly track recent changes in the predicted token or continued motion of the predictive distribution. Divergence-aware methods address this limitation by adding inter-step stability checks, such as token-level KL between consecutive per-position predictive distributions
(
15
)
, although KL-based criteria are directional and unbounded.
LESS
extends this line by treating adaptive unmasking as per-position online stopping: a position is accepted only when confidence, top-1 token persistence, and inter-step distributional stability agree. In particular,
LESS
uses a bounded, symmetric top-
K
JSD criterion and adds an explicit persistence gate for the predicted token identity. To our knowledge,
LESS
is the first training-free dLLM sampler to use JSD as an inter-step stability signal for adaptive token commitment.

3
Methods

We first define the masked-diffusion decoding setup, then present
LESS
, a training-free adaptive sampler that treats token-unmasking as a per-position online stopping problem.
LESS
implements mutual-stability sampling through a joint stability rule over top-1 confidence, top-1 token persistence, and top-
K
inter-step JSD.

3.1
Preliminaries: masked diffusion language models

Setup.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be the vocabulary and let
\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle
denote the mask token. Given a prompt
x_{\mathrm{prompt}}
, a masked diffusion language model generates a length-
L
continuation by denoising a sequence

x_{t}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle\})^{L},\quad t=T,T-1,\ldots,0,

where
x_{T}
is fully masked and
x_{0}
is fully decoded. At reverse step
t
, the model observes the prompt and current partially decoded sequence
x_{t}
, then produces logits

L_{t}=f_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{prompt}},x_{t},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times|\mathcal{V}|},

(1)

with per-position predictive distributions

p_{t,i}(\cdot)=\mathrm{softmax}(L_{t,i})\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}.

(2)

We write

\mathcal{M}_{t}\coloneqq\{i:x_{t,i}=\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle\}

(3)

for the still-masked positions.

Forward masking process.

During training, clean sequences
x_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
are corrupted by progressively replacing tokens with
\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle
. With step-dependent masking rate
\beta_{t}\in[0,1)
, the forward transition is

\!\begin{aligned} q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})&=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\Big[(1-\beta_{t})\delta(x_{t,i}=x_{t-1,i})\\
&\hskip 23.99997pt+\beta_{t}\,\delta(x_{t,i}=[\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle])\Big].\end{aligned}

(4)

Writing
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{s})
for the token survival probability after
t
steps,
the
t
-step marginal becomes

\!\begin{aligned} q(x_{t}\mid x_{0},t)&=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\Big[\bar{\alpha}_{t}\,\delta(x_{t,i}=x_{0,i})\\
&\hskip-1.00006pt+(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\,\delta(x_{t,i}=[\langle\mathrm{mask}\rangle])\Big].\end{aligned}

(5)

The mask token is absorbing in the forward process.

Reverse denoising process.

The learned reverse process provides clean-token predictions for positions that remain masked. For each
i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
, the model predictive distribution in Eq.
2
parameterizes

\!\begin{aligned} p_{\theta}(\widehat{x}_{t,i}\mid x_{\mathrm{prompt}},x_{t})=\\
&\hskip-50.00008pt\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(\widehat{x}_{t,i};p_{t,i}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathrm{prompt}},x_{t})\right).\end{aligned}

(6)

Positions that are already unmasked are carried forward deterministically:

\!\begin{aligned} p_{\theta}(x_{t-1,i}\mid x_{\mathrm{prompt}},x_{t})=\\
&\hskip-50.00008pt\delta(x_{t-1,i}=x_{t,i}),\quad i\notin\mathcal{M}_{t}.\end{aligned}

(7)

Thus, stochastic or argmax token selection is applied only to positions chosen for unmasking; all other positions are copied unchanged.

Partial unmasking.

In practice, a reverse step usually unmasks only a subset of masked positions. Let

\mathcal{U}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{t}

be the unmasking set at step
t
. The update is

x_{t-1,i}=\begin{cases}x_{t,i},&i\notin\mathcal{U}_{t},\\
\widehat{x}_{t,i},&i\in\mathcal{U}_{t},\end{cases}

(8)

where
\widehat{x}_{t,i}
is selected from
p_{t,i}
, either by argmax or by the model’s configured stochastic decoding rule. Because unmasked tokens are typically carried over in subsequent reverse steps, each unmasking decision can be viewed as a token commitment. Standard samplers choose
\mathcal{U}_{t}
using a fixed unmasking schedule, for example by unmasking a predetermined number of high-confidence positions at each step. This is the decision
LESS
replaces: rather than unmasking according to a static budget,
LESS
asks whether each position is stable enough to stop.

Training objective.

Masked diffusion language models are trained to reconstruct clean tokens at masked positions. With timesteps sampled uniformly, the standard masked-token objective is

\!\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}\{1{:}T\}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim q(\cdot\mid x_{0},t)}\\
&\left[-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}\log p_{t,i}\big(x_{0,i}\mid x_{\mathrm{prompt}},x_{t}\big)\right].\end{aligned}

(9)

This objective yields the per-position predictive distributions used by the reverse sampler, but it does not prescribe when a predicted token should be committed during inference.

3.2
Adaptive unmasking as online stopping

Because committed tokens are typically not revised, selecting
\mathcal{U}_{t}
is an irreversible token-commitment decision. LESS therefore treats adaptive unmasking as a per-position online stopping problem. For each position
i
, the sampler observes a trajectory of predictive distributions over reverse steps and chooses a stopping time that is adapted to the information available during decoding. Once
i
is unmasked, its selected token is carried forward by the reverse process and is not revised by later steps. The goal is to stop early enough to avoid recomputing positions whose local predictions have already stabilized, while avoiding premature commitments before the predictive trajectory has settled. LESS implements this stopping rule through three observable stability signals: current top-1 confidence, persistence of the top-1 token over recent observations, and inter-step distributional stability under top-
K
Jensen–Shannon divergence.

3.3
Stability signals

For each still-masked position
i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
, define the top-1 token

w_{t,i}\coloneqq\argmax_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t,i}(v),

(10)

Top-1 predictive confidence.

The confidence signal is the probability assigned to the current top-1 token:

\mathrm{conf}_{t,i}\coloneqq p_{t,i}(w_{t,i})=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t,i}(v).

(11)

Top-1 token persistence.

High confidence alone does not guarantee temporal stability: the top-1 token may have just changed.
LESS
therefore requires the current top-1 token to remain unchanged over a persistence window of length
P
:

\mathrm{persist}_{t,i}\coloneqq\prod_{k=1}^{P-1}\mathbf{1}\!\left[w_{t+k,i}=w_{t,i}\right].

(12)

For steps with insufficient history, we set
\mathrm{persist}_{t,i}=0
.

Top-
K
inter-step Jensen–Shannon divergence.

The third signal measures whether the local predictive distribution itself has stopped changing across reverse steps. For a masked position
i
,
LESS
compares
p_{t,i}
and
p_{t+1,i}
using a Jensen–Shannon divergence on a coarsened vocabulary. Let

A_{t,i}\coloneqq\mathrm{top}\text{-}K(p_{t,i})\cup\mathrm{top}\text{-}K(p_{t+1,i})

(13)

be the union of the consecutive top-
K
candidate sets. All remaining vocabulary mass is collapsed into a residual symbol
\bot
. Define

\phi_{t,i}(v)=\begin{cases}v,&v\in A_{t,i},\\
\bot,&v\notin A_{t,i},\end{cases}

(14)

and let

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{t,i}\coloneqq(\phi_{t,i})_{\#}p_{t,i},

(15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{t+1,i}\coloneqq(\phi_{t,i})_{\#}p_{t+1,i}

(16)

be the coarsened predictive distributions on
A_{t,i}\cup\{\bot\}
. With

m_{t,i}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\widetilde{p}_{t,i}+\widetilde{p}_{t+1,i}\right),

(17)

the inter-step JSD is

\!\begin{aligned} \mathrm{JSD}_{t,i}&\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{p}_{t,i}\,\|\,m_{t,i})\\
&+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{p}_{t+1,i}\,\|\,m_{t,i}).\end{aligned}

(18)

At the first reverse observation, where no previous predictive distribution is available, we set
\mathrm{JSD}_{t,i}=+\infty
. The signal tests whether the token-level belief at position
i
has become locally stationary under ongoing context updates. Since
p_{t,i}
and
p_{t+1,i}
are produced under different partially unmasked contexts, the comparison should not depend on which step is treated as the reference. One-way divergences such as
\mathrm{KL}(p_{t,i}\,\|\,p_{t+1,i})
are less suitable for this inter-step stability test because KL divergence is asymmetric and unbounded
(
12
;
28
;
25
)
. By contrast, JSD is symmetric and bounded:

0\leq\mathrm{JSD}_{t,i}\leq\log 2

(19)

under natural logarithms
(
24
;
30
)
. The bounded scale makes the threshold
d
easier to share across positions, timesteps, models, and tasks. The top-
K
projection focuses the comparison on tokens most likely to affect the top-1 decision, while the residual bin preserves total mass.

3.4
Joint stability rule

A masked position is eligible under the joint stability rule if and only if all three stability tests pass:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{accept}_{t,i}\;=\;&\bigl(\mathrm{conf}_{t,i}\geq c\bigr)\,\wedge\,\mathrm{persist}_{t,i}\quad\\
&{}\wedge\,\bigl(\mathrm{JSD}_{t,i}\leq d\bigr).\end{aligned}}

(20)

where
c
is the confidence threshold and
d
is the inter-step JSD threshold; larger
c
and smaller
d
make the rule more conservative. The conjunction is intentional: the three tests respectively check decisiveness, top-1 temporal stability, and distributional stationarity.
LESS
accepts a position only when all three agree.

3.5
Unmasking policy

The joint stability rule identifies eligible positions; the unmasking policy chooses among them. For full-sequence diffusion samplers, where positions may be selected across the full-generation span,
LESS
uses a frontier-first policy. Let

f_{t}\coloneqq\min\mathcal{M}_{t}

(21)

be the leftmost still-masked position. If
f_{t}
is eligible,
LESS
unmasks it; otherwise, it scans a look-ahead window of size
W
and unmasks the first eligible position:

\displaystyle j^{\star}=\min\{j:f_{t}<j\leq f_{t}+W,\;j\in\mathcal{M}_{t},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{accept}_{t,j}=1\}.

If no eligible position is found, the sampler skips the step. After
\Pi
consecutive skipped steps,
LESS
performs a forced-unmasking step using the underlying sampler’s default action. This fallback guarantees termination but is not counted as accepted by the joint stability rule. For semi-autoregressive blockwise samplers,
LESS
applies the same eligibility rule inside the active block and preserves the model’s blockwise decoding interface.

6
Ablations and Analysis

We analyze
LESS
along four axes: the contribution of each stability signal to robust unmasking, the extent to which decoding is governed by rule-accepted unmaskings rather than the forced-unmasking fallback, the role of the unmasking policy across decoding regimes, and whether reductions in reverse steps translate into compute savings. Full ablation results are provided in Appendix
E
.

6.1
Complementarity of the stability signals

We test whether the three signals in the joint stability rule provide complementary safeguards by comparing full
LESS
with single-signal and drop-one variants on Dream-7B and LLaDA-8B. Under the fixed global-threshold setting, no individual signal provides the same reliable operating point as the full rule. Confidence is the strongest standalone signal on average, while JSD-only, persistence-only, and margin-only exhibit substantially weaker aggregate performance across model families and benchmarks (Appendix Table
4
). This indicates that JSD and persistence are not intended to act as standalone unmasking criteria; rather, they serve as stabilizing gates when combined with confidence.

Drop-one variants show the same pattern in reverse. Removing confidence causes the largest degradation, confirming its role as the primary acceptance gate. Removing inter-step JSD or persistence is less damaging, but the resulting two-signal variants are less stable across models and benchmarks than the full rule (Appendix Table
5
). These results support the conjunctive design of
LESS
: confidence provides decisiveness, persistence guards against recent top-1 changes, and JSD guards against continuing distributional motion.

6.2
Rule-driven versus forced unmasking

The forced-unmasking fallback guarantees progress when no position passes the joint stability rule by forcing at least one required position to be unmasked after
\Pi
consecutive skipped steps. In the full-sequence Dream-7B setting, where the rule can directly govern unmasking decisions, the fallback is invoked on only
2
–
9\%
of reverse steps across audited benchmarks. Thus, most Dream-7B reverse steps are governed directly by the joint stability rule rather than by fallback progress. We also audit top-1 token changes over reverse time. As shown for Dream-7B in Figure 2, the top-1 flip rate is highest early and decays steadily, reaching at most
5\%
in the final quarter of the executed trajectories. This supports the temporal-stability assumption underlying
LESS
: per-position predictive distributions tend to stabilize over reverse time, but early commitments can occur before that stabilization is complete. Further details are provided in Appendix
E.6
.

6.3
Robustness across decoding regimes

To isolate the effect of the unmasking policy, we compare the frontier-first policy with a parallel-unmasking variant that commits all eligible positions at each reverse step. On Dream-7B, parallel unmasking lowers accuracy on three of four math/code benchmarks, with an average change of
-2.7
accuracy points and little aggregate change in step count. This is consistent with the interpretation that committing many eligible positions simultaneously can perturb subsequent denoising states before local context has fully settled. On LLaDA-8B, accuracy is unchanged because its released semi-autoregressive blockwise sampler already commits accepted positions in parallel within the active block. Additional budget and token-selection ablations show that
T=256
reaches the rule’s operating point for the evaluated settings: increasing to
T=512
produces no material aggregate improvement, reducing to
T=128
degrades harder settings, and nucleus sampling affects accuracy without changing the eligibility criterion at each reverse step. Further details are provided in Appendix
E
.

7
Discussion

As diffusion LLMs become increasingly practical alternatives to autoregressive generation, inference efficiency becomes central to their real-world applicability.
LESS
addresses this challenge by committing tokens only when their predictions are confident, temporally persistent, and distributionally stable. Our results show that this stability-gated commitment improves the aggregate accuracy–efficiency trade-off while reducing inference compute, with the most pronounced gains on math and code generation tasks.

Stability is more than confidence.

The central premise of
LESS
is that irreversible token commitment should depend on stability at the time of commitment, not solely confidence. A high-confidence predictive distribution can still be unreliable if the top-1 token has recently changed or if probability mass is still moving among plausible alternatives. The ablations in Section
6
support this decomposition: confidence provides the main selectivity signal, while top-1 token persistence and inter-step JSD improve robustness by guarding against recent top-1 flips and continuing distributional motion before local predictions have fully settled.

Why inter-step JSD is useful.

The top-
K
inter-step JSD provides a practical notion of distributional stability between consecutive token-level predictive distributions. Unlike one-way KL criteria, it does not require choosing a reference step. Its bounded scale gives the JSD threshold a stable numerical range across positions and timesteps, and its symmetry matches the sampler’s question: whether two consecutive beliefs are close enough to treat the current prediction as stable, even under changing partially decoded contexts. The top-
K
projection focuses this comparison on tokens that can plausibly affect the unmasking decision while retaining remaining mass through a residual bin, without requiring full-vocabulary computations.

Complementarity.

LESS
reduces the number of reverse denoising steps and is therefore complementary to methods that reduce per-step cost, such as diffusion-aware KV caching, speculative decoding, or systems-level reuse mechanisms. It is also complementary to training-time acceleration methods that shorten the reverse process through distillation or modified objectives. Because
LESS
uses only per-position predictive distributions already emitted during decoding, it acts as a drop-in inference-time sampling policy rather than a replacement for model-level or systems-level acceleration, and can be combined with such methods without changing the underlying model.

8
Conclusion

We introduced
LESS
, a training-free adaptive sampler for diffusion language models that treats token unmasking as per-position online stopping.
LESS
commits a masked position only when top-1 confidence, top-1 token persistence, and top-
K
inter-step Jensen–Shannon divergence jointly indicate stability. Across Dream-7B, LLaDA-8B, and LLaDA-1.5-8B on seven benchmarks,
LESS
improves the accuracy–efficiency trade-off on average over fixed-budget decoding and strong training-free adaptive samplers, with the largest gains on math and code. Averaged across the three model-family means, it uses
72.1\%
fewer reverse steps than fixed-budget decoding while also yielding average accuracy gains. Ablations confirm that the three signals provide complementary safeguards, and FLOPs profiling shows that step reductions translate into fewer Transformer forward passes, lower wall-clock time, and lower inference compute. These results support mutual stability as a practical inference-time criterion for robust early token commitment in efficient dLLM sampling.

Limitations

While
LESS
provides a training-free mechanism for accelerating diffusion language-model decoding, our study has several limitations.
LESS
exposes an explicit quality–efficiency trade-off through its stability thresholds, primarily the confidence threshold
c
and inter-step JSD threshold
d
. More conservative settings, with higher confidence requirements and lower JSD tolerance, reduce the risk of premature commitment but yield more moderate speedups. More permissive settings can accelerate decoding further but may accept unstable positions. In our experiments, this trade-off is resolved by selecting a fixed global configuration shared across the evaluated models and benchmarks. In practice, the optimal operating point may depend on the model, decoding horizon, task type, output length, and tolerance for quality degradation.

E.6
Fallback Usage and Top-1 Token Stability

We audit Dream-7B on 50 examples per benchmark at the per-setting tuned configuration. For each reverse step, we log whether the fallback is invoked, the number of positions unmasked, and the top-1 token at each position. The fallback is invoked only when no position is accepted by the joint stability rule at a given step; the forced-unmasking action then unmasks the frontier position.

Across GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and MBPP, the fallback is invoked on
2.1\%
,
3.9\%
,
8.9\%
, and
2.5\%
of reverse steps, respectively. Thus, most Dream-7B reverse steps are governed directly by the joint stability rule, and
LESS
acts primarily as a stability-gated stopping rule in the full-sequence setting. The per-step top-1 token flip rate on Dream-7B is highest early in decoding and falls to at most
5\%
in the final quarter of the trajectory across all audited benchmarks. This supports the temporal-stability assumption underlying
LESS
: per-position predictive distributions tend to stabilize over reverse time, but early commitments can occur before that stabilization is complete.
