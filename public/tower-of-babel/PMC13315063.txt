Tyrosine Kinase Receptors, Inhibition, and Potential Role in the Pharmacotherapy of Retinal Disorders
Authors: Pradeep Venkatesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past two decades, impressive gains in the restoration and maintenance of visual acuity have been made in patients with, otherwise blinding, chronic retinal diseases such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) and diabetic macular edema (DME) using intravitreal pharmacotherapy against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). However, there is serious economic burden, continued potential for risks, and potential drug resistance owing to the necessity of repeated injections so as to sustain the initial gains of therapy. Although efforts such as intravitreal treatment with dual pathway inhibitors and sustained drug release implants have been introduced to address such concerns, they do not seem to be the ideal approaches. Consequently researchers continue to explore more plausible solutions, among which tyrosine kinase inhibition seems to have a strong potential to reduce the treatment burden. In this review, the nature of protein kinases and tyrosine kinases, their receptors, and their downstream signaling effects are discussed, followed by the role of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK) and their inhibitors in the treatment of retinal diseases such as nAMD and DME.Keywords: Protein kinases, Receptor tyrosine kinase, Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, Aga-related macular degeneration, Diabetic macular edema

Key Summary Points

Current anti-VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) therapy for retinal disorders has limitations in the form of increased frequency of injection and associated continued risks.Newer approaches such as high dose aflibercept, dual inhibition of VEGF and Ang2, and sustained drug release implants provide only some benefits.Anti-VEGFs act externally on VEGF receptors. So, drugs with potential to act within the internal milieu of the cell and on signaling pathways may have the ability to reduce treatment burden by complementing the beneficial effects of anti-VEGF pharmacotherapy.Tyrosine kinase inhibitors act directly on receptor tyrosine kinases and thereby modify the effects of VEGF internally, including their downstream signaling effects and gene transcription. Hence, this category of drugs is being extensively studied in preclinical and clinical trials to determine their safety and efficacy in the treatment of retinal diseases such as nAMD, DME and a few others.This review provides a comprehensive summary of the structure and natural role of protein kinases, receptor tyrosine kinases, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and current status of some these drugs that have been studied or under evaluation for the management of chronic retinal disorders such as diabetic retinopathy, diabetic macular edema and nAMD.

Introduction

The identification of molecules involved in angiogenesis, vasculogenesis and vascular permeability was a momentous discovery that opened newer therapeutic avenues for the management of systemic malignancies, as well as important retinal disorders such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), proliferative vascular retinopathies, and macular edema secondary to retinal vascular derangement. Pharmacotherapy of these conditions over the past two decades, as demonstrated in a multitude of randomized studies, using anti-vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF), has resulted in a sea change in our ability to preserve and indeed improve visual outcomes. In order to maintain these benefits, however, current approaches have several limitations such as high psychological and financial burden from the necessity for repeated injections. Even with treat and extend regimens, it has been reported that, on an average, about 13–14 injections are needed over the first 2 years [1–4]. With the recent availability of longer-acting therapy such as faricimab and aflibercept 8 mg, this burden has reduced to 8–11 injections over the same period, in a significant majority of patients. In addition, there is cumulative risk of adverse events such as endophthalmitis, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal tear and detachment etc. and of reduced treatment compliance [5, 6]. In order to reduce these limitations various approaches are being explored, including sustained release intravitreal implants (Susvimo), high dose aflibercept (8 mg), dual VEGF/PDGF inhibition, and dual inhibition of VEGF and Ang2 (Angiopoietin-2) with faricimab [7, 8]. An additional approach to reduce the injection burden of anti-VEGF pharmacotherapy is through inhibition of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK), a class of protein kinases [9].

This article provides a broad overview of the rationale, structure, function and inhibition of the protein kinase family of cell receptors, with focus on RTKs, in relation to the management of some chronic and visually disabling retinal conditions.

The basis for targeting protein kinase receptors in disease management is the recent, more nuanced understanding of cell to cell communication, mechanisms through which messages from external stimuli get translated into internal messages and, thereby, to changes in the internal milieu of the cell [10]. Cell to cell communication has been known to be critical for efficient and effective functioning of tissues and organs. This communication is mediated by various kinds of receptors located on the plasma membrane as well as the nuclear membrane [11, 12]. Cell surface receptors are activated following their interaction with external stimuli/agents called ligands. Ligands can be of many types, e.g. lipids, proteins, polypeptides, carbohydrates, small organic molecules etc. [13]. Cell surface receptors play two important roles: firstly, they can receive and transmit signals from the environment to the cytoplasm and/or nucleus (signal transduction); secondly, receptors can also act as enzymes by themselves. In the latter capacity, binding to a compatible signal molecule activates the receptor’s inherent enzymatic activity [14]. In signal transduction, stimuli from extracellular agents are amplified in a stepwise and graded manner depending on the nature, strength, and specificity of the stimulus [15].

Cell surface receptors have been classified by the type of ligands they interact with, type of biological response they cause, and their primary structure [16]. G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR) are the largest group of cell surface receptors, followed by RTKs and nuclear hormone receptors (NHR) [17]. Unlike GPCRs, the last two receptor types mediate effects that are slower and long-lasting, and initiate a series of secondary cascade events that alter the intracellular milieu, as well as gene expression [18].

One of the principal methods through which signal transduction and enzymatic activation of cell receptors occurs is through protein phosphorylation. Phosphorylation is the transfer of phosphates to oxygen atoms, and occurs with the help of enzymes called transferases. Addition of phosphate groups to substrate proteins directs the localization, activity and overall function of several cellular proteins that are involved in the orchestration of almost all cellular processes. During the process of protein phosphorylation, signals from the extracellular environment are transmitted to the cytoplasm and/or nucleus, across the plasma membrane. When signals are transferred to the nucleus, gene expression could get altered through the influence of transcription factors [19]. In eukaryotes, protein phosphorylation, first discovered by Hunter and Sefton in 1980, is a key process in the post-translational regulatory control of many biological functions [20]. Protein phosphorylation is a dynamic and reversible process and is controlled, in addition to other factors, by a balance in the functioning of counteracting enzymes—protein kinases and the phosphatases. Other than protein phosphatases, protein kinase activity is affected by other means such as interaction with other proteins, and secondary protein–protein interactions [21].

Enzymes that are involved in the phosphorylation of protein substrates are called protein kinases. Protein kinases constitute one of the largest and functionally most diverse gene families, and about 518 of these are encoded by the human genome [22]. Protein kinases function as ‘molecular switches’ wherein phosphorylation of protein residues switches receptors into an active (on) or off (inactive) state. This form of control is an important mechanism in cellular regulation. Being key regulators of cell function, the action of protein kinases is most evident during events of the cell cycle and signal transduction. In addition, these enzymes are also involved in the response to injuries and in cellular adaptation [23]. Hence, it is not surprising that abnormalities in protein phosphorylation have been found to be associated with an ever-expanding gamut of pathologies such as malignancies, inflammatory disorders, neurodegeneration, cardiac dysfunction, infectious diseases and complications of diabetes. Protein kinases have also been implicated in diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and in restenosis of cardiac shunts, which occurs in almost 40% of all angioplasties [24]. Thereby, protein kinase inhibitors are now routinely employed in the treatment of various malignancies, rheumatological disorders, and others.

Classification

On the basis of structural similarity and function, protein kinases have been subdivided into groups, families and subfamilies. On the basis of the substrate that they phosphorylate, protein kinases are categorized into five major groups: serine-threonine group (ST-PKs), tyrosine group, histidine-specific group, dual specificity group (can phosphorylate both ST and tyrosine residues) and aspartic acid-glutamic acid group. Serine-threonine and tyrosine groups form the first and second largest group of protein kinases respectively, with 70% being serine-threonine protein kinases [25]. Examples of the ST-PK group are MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase), cyclin-dependent kinase, DNA-dependent protein kinase, protein kinase D etc. The tyrosine group of protein kinases may belong to one of four classes: receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), non-receptor tyrosine kinases (NRTKs), nuclear tyrosine kinases (NTK) and dual specificity protein kinases [26]. RTKs are receptors for polypeptide hormones and growth factors, while nuclear tyrosine receptors are for steroids and non-peptide hormones. RTKs are involved in transmembrane signaling while non-RTKs, which reside within the cytoplasm, are involved in signal transduction to the nucleus (cell cycle control, properties of transcription factors etc.).

In the human genome, about 90 tyrosine kinase genes have been discovered, of which 58 for RTKs, while the other 32 code for non-RTK or cytoplasmic tyrosine kinases [27]. The 58 RTKs have been grouped into 20 subfamilies, based on their amino acid sequence similarities and structural characteristics in their extracellular domains. The NRTKs, on the other hand, have been grouped into 11 subfamilies. Some important RTK subfamilies include type 1, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family; type 2, insulin receptor (IR) family; type 3, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) etc. family; type 4, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) family; type 5, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family; type 12, Tie (tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin and epidermal growth factor homology domains) family of angiopoietin receptors; type 16, DDR (collagen receptor family); type 17, reactive oxygen species (ROS) receptors etc.

EGFR was the first RTK to be identified. Other important RTK receptors include stem cell factor receptor (e.g. KIT), ErbB receptor family (consisting of EGFR, and HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2). Receptors that lack their own tyrosine kinase domains but act by associating with intracellular/cytoplasmic proteins that have tyrosine kinase activity are called tyrosine kinase-associated receptors [28]. Most of these are associated with the cytoplasmic kinase JAK (Janus kinase). JAK acts in conjunction with STAT (signal transducers and activators of transcription) proteins.

There are five structurally related VEGF ligands, VEGFA, VEGFB, VEGFC, VEGFD and PlGF (placenta growth factor), but only three VEGF receptors—VEGFR1 (Flt-1, fms such as tyrosine kinase-1), VEGFR2 (KDR, kinase insert domain-containing receptor) and VEGFR3. The major regulatory functions of VEGF include angiogenesis and vascular permeability (VEGFA), non-angiogenic growth (VEGFB), dual functions of angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis (VEGFC and VEGFD), and multifunctional roles of angiogenesis, vascular permeability, chemotaxis and proliferation (PlGF). VEGFR1, also known as FLT1, is normally expressed in the liver, heart, lung, kidney, brain and placenta. It has three ligands: VEGFA, VEGFB and PlGF. It plays an important role in vasculogenesis during embryonic development. VEGFR2, also known as KDR, is only expressed in endothelial cells. Its ligands are VEGFA, VEGFB and VEGFC. It is involved in angiogenesis, increased vascular permeability, and tumorigenesis. VEGFR3, also known as FLT4, is expressed mainly on the lymphatic vessels and is involved in lymphangiogenesis as well as tumorigenesis. Its ligands are VEGFC and VEGFD. VEGFR2 is the most important in mediating the biological effects of VEGF [29]. VEGFR binding to ligands also involves co-receptors called neuropilins [30]. Through their interactions with Ang1 and Ang2, Tie RTK receptors have critical functions in vascular biology [31]. Recently, discoidin domain receptors (DDR) have also been recognized as belonging to the RTK family. A unique element of these receptors is that they are activated by binding to collagen [32]. They have a role in epithelial–mesenchymal transition and hence may be involved in the pathogenesis of proliferative vitreoretinopathy.

Structure and Activation of RTKs

Structural mechanisms of RTK activation have been studied using crystallography, single molecule fluorescence experiments in living cells, mathematical modelling, electron microscopy, small angle x-ray scattering methods etc. [33, 34]. In addition, advances in proteomics such as SILAC (stable isotope labelling by amino acids in cell culture) and OTM (oligonucleotide-tagged multiplex assay) and functional genomics have enabled the study of entire RTK-induced cascade changes rather than individual components in isolation [35, 36]. All RTKs, except those of the IR family, are monomers. Molecules that activate RTKs are of a wide range, generated locally and at very low levels in the body [37]. Important molecules that activate RTKs are growth factors and hormones. Extracellular matrix proteins and surface receptors on adjacent cells can also activate RTKs. The ultimate effect of RTK activation is gene transcription.

Transmembrane proteins, including RTKs, are localized in specialized regions of the plasma membrane called lipid rafts [38, 39]. Structurally, protein kinases have three regions, the regulatory N-terminus, the three-dimensional catalytic domain, and the C-terminus. The catalytic domain forms a deep groove, and itself has the following subdomains: ATP binding site, substrate binding site, N-terminus subdomain and C-terminus subdomain. The ATP binding site can take up ‘on’ or ‘off’ conformations based on the active or inactive state of the enzyme and the linking to ATP. The non-catalytic subdomain is involved in recruitment of signaling molecules as well as attachment of relevant substrates. Despite their variation in gross structure and function, it has been found that the sequence and design of the catalytic domain are preserved through different species ranging from yeast, invertebrates and mammals. Although the catalytic domain structure is well preserved, there is variability in the regulation of catalysis [16, 40].

Functionally, in vivo, protein kinases select their site of action with precision according to their affinity for the aliphatic alcohols of serine and threonine or for the alcoholic moiety of tyrosine. This selection is also influenced by the adjacent secondary/tertiary structures. Protein kinase substrate specificity is also impacted by the cellular microenvironment and through control at the transcription level. All proteins phosphorylate the hydroxyl groups of specific amino acids, i.e. serine-threonine (majority) or tyrosine (in RTKs). Unlike serine-threonine protein kinases, the kinase activity in RTKs involves only a small number of substrates within the protein chains of the receptor itself.

Extracellular domains are generally ligand specific. The extracellular domain has an amino-terminal with a ligand binding site, transmembrane domain with an α-helix configuration, and an intracellular domain with tyrosine-rich carboxy C-terminal. While the extracellular domain is normally glycosylated and binds to specific ligands, the intracellular domain has a conserved protein tyrosine kinase core and associated regulatory sequences. The extracellular domain of RTK is a dynamic region and ligand specificity depends on distinct structural elements such as immunoglobulin-like domains, fibronectin type III-like repeats, EGF-like domains, and cysteine-rich regions [41]. The cytoplasmic domain also has some juxtamembrane and C-terminal regulatory proteins. The C-terminal region has the highest level of conservation and contains the catalytic domains. This region may sometimes be split into two domains by an insertion called the kinase insertion. Phosphotyrosine recruitment sites in RTKs are located at three regions: the C-terminal tail, juxtamembrane region, and kinase insert region [42].

RTKs can exist in two forms, namely as dimeric aggregates constitutively or as independent units that form a dimer following the binding of a ligand to their extracellular portion. Receptor dimerization is stabilized through other interactions between the receptors. Four mechanisms for RTK dimerization have been described: ligand-induced dimerization without receptor contact, ligand-induced dimerization with receptor contact, ligand-induced dimerization with receptor contact with accessory molecules, and receptor-induced dimerization [43]. Some RTKs may form dimers even in the absence of activating ligands, by staying in a dynamic equilibrium between monomers and dimers (e.g. insulin receptor) [44]. Under normal physiological conditions, RTK activity is well balanced through the process of autoinhibition, counteracting enzymes such as tyrosine phosphatases and other temporal and spatial regulatory mechanisms [45]. Cis-autoinhibtion may be achieved in multiple ways and is unique to a particular RTK [16]. These include inhibition by the activation loop, juxtamembrane inhibition and inhibition by the C-terminal tail. Ligand-induced dimerization and trans-phosphorylation leads to destabilization of these autoinhibitory interactions. Ligand binding usually forms a 1:2 (ligand/receptor) complex wherein one ligand binds to adjacent receptors. This dimer cross-linking leads to activation of the tyrosine kinase activity and hence to phosphorylation of multiple tyrosine residues within the cytoplasmic domain of the receptors. Since one RTK phosphorylates tyrosine residues of the other RTK to which it is linked during dimerization, the process is termed as cross-phosphorylation. During cross-phosphorylation, the dimerized RTKs phosphorylate each other multiple times to achieve signal amplification. Autophosphorylation is associated with a conformational change in protein orientation from cis configuration to trans configuration. It is this change that also allows for assembly of protein complexes and stimulation of various signaling pathways. For most RTKs, binding of stimulus to the receptor induces conformational change within the catalytic domain enabling simultaneous trans-autophosphorylation and release of the cis-inhibition.

Activated RTKs primarily catalyse the transfer of γ-phosphate of ATP to hydroxyl groups of tyrosines on target proteins. Catalysis is controlled by two loops, the catalytic loop and the activation loop, within the core of the kinase domain. The HRD motif is found in the catalytic loop and contains His-Arg-Asp; during catalysis, Asp (aspartic acid) on this motif creates a hydrogen bond with –OH on Tyr (tyrosine). The location and conformation of the activation loop determine whether the kinase is active or inactive. The DFG motif (usually with the sequence Asp-Phe-Gly) starts the activation loop [46]. After cross-phosphorylation, the cytoplasmic tails of the RTKs become suitable for attachment or docking of compatible intracellular proteins. Signaling molecules either bind directly to the phosphorylated tyrosine residues or indirectly through binding to docking proteins phosphorylated by RTKs. These docking proteins include IRS1 (insulin receptor substrate 1), FRS2 and Gab1 (Grb-associated binder) [47].

Tyrosine autophosphorylation sites are located on both the catalytic and non-catalytic region of the receptor. Those in the non-catalytic region serve as binding sites for SH2 (Src homology 2) or PTB (phosphotyrosine binding) domain of signaling proteins. These molecules are usually the ones involved in signal transduction, and are characterized by having an SH2 domain. SH2 signaling molecules can either bind directly or indirectly through docking proteins. Docking proteins act as assembly platforms for recruitment of further signaling molecules with SH2 or other domains [48]. Since more than one SH2-containing protein can bind simultaneously to an activated RTK, activation of multiple intracellular signaling pathways can occur at the same time. These newly assembled signaling complexes then pass on the signal to pre-existing intracellular proteins leading to activation of signaling pathways such as Ras/ERK pathway, PI3K/Akt pathway, phospholipase C pathway etc. Ras in turn triggers a phosphorylation cascade of other kinases such as MAP kinase. Signaling effects are usually autocrine or paracrine in nature. Since they phosphorylate several tyr residues, RTK receptors can activate various signal transduction pathways. Signaling proteins have their own intrinsic enzymatic activity and impact interaction between other molecules (proteins, phospholipids, nucleic acids). The total amount of signaling proteins stimulated by an activated RTK consists of proteins recruited directly by the receptor as well as those recruited by the docking proteins. In general, there are three mechanisms by which RTK activation leads to stimulation of signaling molecules: activation of membrane translocation and generation of second messengers, activation by conformational change and release of autoinhibtion, and activation of tyrosine phosphorylation within the signaling molecules [49]. Some of the important signaling pathways include Ras/MAPK, JAK2/STAT, PI-3K/AKT, phospholipase Cγ and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase etc.

More recently, after ligand stimulation, RTKs and some of their linked proteins have been found to be localized within the nucleus [50]. This observation has raised the possibility of direct links of activated RTKs with the nucleus, in addition to the well-understood process of signal transduction. Both ligand-dependent and mechanostimulatory pathways have been noted to result in rapid (within 2 min) translocation of VEGFR2 and its associated proteins [51]. Direct interactions with the nucleus are facilitated through STAT proteins. These proteins enter the nucleus directly and alter transcription after they become active on binding to phosphorylated tyrosines in the cytoplasm. Indirect interaction with the nucleus occurs through pathways such as such as MAP kinase cascade and protein kinase cascade, mentioned earlier.

As signal travels from the extracellular region, across the cell membrane and into the intracellular region, the message delivered becomes complex owing to cross-talk between intermediates in various signaling pathways. This could be assisted by adapter proteins which have no intrinsic enzymatic activity but connect to the activated receptor and serve other functions such as connecting RTK activation to other signaling cascades such as MAP kinase [52, 53]. Activation of RTKs also causes upregulation of nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and nitric oxide (NO) production [54]. Hence, activation of RTKs leads to a ripple effect inducing the activation of multiple signaling pathways and their effects.

While the understanding of the structure and mechanisms of activation has been well elucidated, the processes leading to specificity of signaling pathways remain unclear. For example, it is not known why stimulation of an RTK within fibroblasts results in cell proliferation, while the same RTK within a neuronal cell results in cell differentiation. Some of the proposed mechanisms include cellular compartmentalization, utilization of specific scaffold proteins, signal duration and amplitude, combinatorial control, and cellular context [55, 56].

To restore balance, active RTKs also need to be downregulated. This is done by attenuation and termination of RTK activity through various means such as inhibition by protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTP), antagonistic ligands, negative feedback through activation of juxtamembrane domain, receptor endocytosis and degradation, and other complex stimulatory and inhibitory feedback mechanisms [57]. Ligand binding also promotes internalization of the activated receptors so as to downregulate the signaling. This internalization may occur through endocytosis, vesicular trafficking, clathrin-dependent or caveolar pathways [58]. Clathrin-dependent internalization is the most common pathway for RTK downregulation [59]. More recently it has been found that VEGFR2 may be preferentially internalized through the caveolar pathway [60]. Incidentally, caveolae, which are 50–100-nm, flask-shaped invaginations, account for 95% of the cell surface vesicles within the vascular endothelial cells [61]. The modulation of RTK activity that occurs thereby also impacts downstream targets of RTKs such as eNOS (endothelial nitric oxide synthase) and VE-cadherin, as well as their transport to the cell nucleus. Internalization of RTKs into endosomes does not always result in their degradation. On the basis of certain internal cues, they may also be recycled back to the plasma membrane [62]. Trafficking of RTKs is determined by the type and concentration of the ligand, dimerization of the receptors, signaling by molecules such as ubiquitin and Dynamin-2, non-classical PKC control etc. Targeting of pathways that traffic RTKs is now being considered as a potential therapeutic option [45].

Function of Protein kinases

Serine threonine protein kinases are important in embryogenesis, cell differentiation, proliferation, cell death, cellular response to inflammatory cytokines, osmotic stress, heat shock protein etc., initiating and regulating the various phases of the cell cycle from G1, S, G2 to M phase, regulation of some intracellular functions such as Golgi apparatus membrane transport, calcium homeostasis, regulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP), transport of transferrin receptor, apoptosis etc. Non-receptor tyrosine kinases are involved in transcription and cell cycle regulation. They regulate signal transduction of cytokine receptors, growth factor receptors, adhesion (integrin)-mediated signaling, lymphocyte antigen receptors etc. Some of the important NRTKs include JAK that regulate cytokine receptor signal transduction, SRC kinases involved in cytokine receptor and integrin-mediated signaling, and the Sky/ZAP-70 family that coordinates lymphocyte antigen signal transduction.

Tyrosine phosphorylation, principally mediated by RTKs, is a highly regulated post-translational modification that is necessary for intercellular and intracellular communication. RTKs are single pass type I membrane receptors that are active during embryonic development as well as adult homeostasis [63, 64]. By transducing messages carried by the ligands, they play a critical role in cell cycle, cell division, cell growth, cell differentiation, cell migration, cell proliferation, cell adhesion, cell motility, cell survival, cell senescence, spatial orientation of tissues etc. They also regulate apoptosis [65]. RTKs also impact intercellular junctions between endothelial cells. These junctions are critical in maintaining homeostasis. RTKs achieve this by regulating the activity of VE-cadherin, a major component of the adherens junctions, of adjacent cells [66]. Cell migration is also promoted by activation of RTKs as it leads to dissolution of endothelial specific adhesion through endocytosis of VE-cadherin.

The VEGF family is potentially the most important growth factor family as it is involved in embryogenesis, vasculogenesis, angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis. Activities such as endothelial cell proliferation and relaxation and proliferation of vascular smooth muscle occur through cross-talk between activated VEGFR and FGFR, and IR and PDGFR, respectively [67]. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are also a class of RTKs which get activated by pathogenic molecules and send early ‘danger’ signals so as to enable the body to identify and destroy the pathogens [68]. As a result of their wide effect on cellular control and communication, RTKs have been recognized to play a major role in the pathogenesis of several malignancies, through various mechanisms [49]. These include constitutive receptor autocrine activation, receptor overexpression, gain of function gene mutation, genomic amplification, chromosomal translocation, kinase domain duplication, microRNAs, geographic and microenvironment dysregulation, tyrosine phosphatase dysregulation, altered endocytic/ trafficking gene etc. In patients with malignancies, one or more of these mechanisms result in hyperactive kinase activity and abnormal, ligand-independent cellular proliferation.

Inhibition of RTK- Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (RTKI)

Originally, protein phosphorylation was thought to have limited effects as in the control of glycogen metabolism. Several decades later, the importance of protein phosphorylation in disease pathogenesis was revealed by two independent investigators who found that the transforming factor of v-Src (Rous sarcoma virus), the first known oncogene, was a protein kinase and that protein kinase C (PKC) could be activated by tumour-promoting chemicals (phorbol esters) [69]. With the understanding that RTKs play a major role in various aspects of cell regulation, the natural next step was to target protein kinases using therapeutic agents. Currently, after GPCR targeting, inhibition of protein kinases has become the most important focus of drug targeting.

Biologically active compounds that block and specifically inhibit RTK activity are called receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (RTKIs). On the basis of the target, RTKIs can be of three types: specific inhibitors (e.g. rapamycin), non-specific inhibitors and multikinase inhibitors. They could also be categorized into small molecules and macromolecules (monoclonal antibodies, polypeptides, drug conjugates, and nucleic acids). TKIs have also been categorized as first generation (erlotinib and gefitinib), second generation, and third generation (osimertinib). They have also been classified as reversible and non-reversible TKIs. Drugs targeting RTKs may directly inhibit tyrosine activity, block activation of RTKs or act by chelating the cognate ligands. The large majority of TKIs currently available target the ATP-binding site [70]. The ATP binding pocket is between the two lobes of the kinase fold; being relatively less conserved, is the focus for designing drugs with selective effects. The first PKI to gain US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval, imatinib, used in treatment of Philadelphia chromosome-positive chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), acts by binding to the catalytic cleft. In addition to drugs, RTK activity is also known to be suppressed by certain viral infections.

Protein kinase inhibition during chemical agents gained momentum when it was discovered that the antifungal agent staurosporine, produced from certain Streptomyces bacteria, possessed properties of PKC inhibition. Staurosporine belongs to the category of bisindolyl maleimides. Subsequently, other agents in this category underwent evaluation by major pharmaceutical companies to assess their protein kinase inhibition potential [71]. The first synthetic protein kinase inhibitors were reported in 1980 by a team of Japanese researchers who replaced the naphthalene of naphthalene sulfonamide with an isoquinoline moiety; naphthalene sulfonamide had already been established as an inhibitor of calmodulin, a calcium binding protein. The first protein kinase inhibitor to be studied in clinical trials was fasudil hydrochloride, and it was approved in Japan in 1995 for treating of patients with cerebral vasospasm and other cerebral ischemic syndromes [72]. Subsequently, the RTK inhibitor PKC412 (midostaurin) was tested in clinical trials for treating certain cancers as well as ischemic retinopathy [72, 73]. Agents such as maleimides, with more specific and effective action, have been tested in diabetic retinopathy, inhibition of neural apoptosis and neurodegeneration. Although protein kinase inhibitors have been largely used in the treatment of various malignancies, there are a few that have been approved for managing various non-neoplastic conditions. Some of these include tofacitinib for rheumatoid arthritis, nintedanib for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and pegaptanib for macular degeneration [74, 75]. As of 2025, the FDA had approved 94 protein kinase inhibitors, of which 48 are RTK inhibitors. Some of the tyrosine kinases which have been targeted for therapeutic utility include the ErbB family (ErbB-1, ErbB-2 (also called HER2), ErbB-3 and ErbB-4), Src kinase family (c-Src, Yes, Fyn, Fgr, Lyn, Hck, Lck, Yrk, Fyk and Blk), Syk and ZAP-70 kinase family, Abl kinase family, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) kinase family (FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, FGFR4), and platelet-derived growth kinase family (PDGFRα, PDGFRβ, colony-stimulating factor receptor (CSFR), Steel growth factor receptor and fetal liver kinase-2).

There are several challenges to achieving human protein kinase inhibition, including the number of different kinases (which make specific targeting difficult), presence of other innumerable ATP-utilizing proteins and presence of high intracellular concentrations of ATP [76, 77]. The last of these can be as high as 8 mM in non-transformed cells and significantly higher in transformed cells [78]. In addition, protein kinases are known to use short synthetic peptides for their functioning, and inhibition of these is challenging because peptides have limited bioavailability. Incomplete characterization of the structure as well as biochemical roles also makes specific inhibition of protein kinases challenging. It is also not clear whether to target the inactive or active form of the enzyme. Some of the newer approaches to protein kinase inhibition include targeting one or more of the following: unique inactive conformation, global constellation of the residues within the ATP site, less conserved additional pockets, single residues, and non-catalytic domains [79, 80].

Drug resistance is an important consideration while using RTKI drugs and is a major therapeutic challenge. Mechanisms of drug resistance to RTKIs include on-target mutations, off-target mutations, epigenetic changes, autocrine activation, overexpression, abnormal fusions, activation of metabolic pathways, activation of vascular pathways, cell cycle aberrations, modulation of death pathways, metabolic reprogramming, histological transformation (e.g. EMT), conformational changes in RTK receptor, lysosomal sequestration of the RTKI drug, altered drug uptake and transport, and reprogramming of tumour microenvironment [81]. Some of the methods to overcome this resistance include early detection of resistance and switching to an alternative agent before it becomes fully established, altering pharmacokinetics through use of nanocarriers and liposomes, combining with other downstream signaling pathway modulators, combining with non-RTK pathway inhibitors, combining with immunochemotherapy etc. [82]. In the management of tumours, resistance to RTKIs is a well-established but poorly understood condition. Resistance to anti-VEGF treatment and reactivation of angiogenesis has been demonstrated in animal models of pancreatic cancer [83, 84]. This may be related to FGF and other factors. In patients with advanced CML, a single mutation in the Abl protein leads to resistance to imatinib [85].

Side Effects of RTKIs

Use of TKIs has been very effective in the management of several malignancies, but as they inhibit multiple kinases, there is an increased risk of adverse effects, both cardiac and extracardiac [86]. RTKI toxicity has been classified as on target (due to inhibition of target receptor) or off target (due to simultaneous inhibition of multiple unintended kinases). On-target side effects from excessive inhibition of intended RTK include hypertension, hypothyroidism, skin reactions, proteinuria etc. These side effects can be mild to severe. RTKIs have a high risk of cardiac toxicity and could can systemic hypertension, pulmonary hypertension, QT wave prolongation, arterial thrombosis, apical ballooning syndrome and fulminant heart failure. This is because VEGF inhibition has been found to increase the production of mitochondrial superoxide and reduce nitric oxide production [87]. Another frequent adverse effect of a majority of protein kinase inhibitors is hepatotoxicity [88]. The mechanism of its occurrence is poorly understood but is conjectured to be a direct effect of the drug itself, or secondary to an immunological effect against metabolic products of these drugs. Rare and fatal idiosyncratic hepatocellular or cholestatic liver damage has also been reported. Some agents such as imatinib and nilotinib may cause reactivation of hepatitis B.

TKIs used for treating systemic malignancies may also result in ocular side effects such as conjunctivitis, uveitis (BRAF inhibitors), retinal vein occlusion (BRAF and MEK inhibitors), and central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR; combination of BRAF and MEK therapy, and in 25% of patients treated with the FGFR inhibitor erdafitinib) [89, 90]. Bilateral rhegmatogenous retinal detachment during treatment with oral TKIs has been reported, but it is difficult to comprehend a direct causative relationship [91]. These risks, associated with systemic use of the drug, would not occur following local ocular administration.

RTKs and the Eye

Pathological formation of new blood vessels occurs in several ocular conditions, predominant being in the retina and optic nerve. Other structures that may develop pathological neovascularization include the cornea, iris, angle of the anterior chamber, and pars plana/vitreous base. The primary driver for pathological vascularization is hypoxia and ischemia, and the molecules mediating the tissue response include hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) and VEGF. VEGF mediates its effects by binding to VEGF receptors VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 on the endothelial cells (ECs). These receptors act through the RTK mechanism. In normal circumstances, ECs and pericytes share a common basement membrane (BM) and there is a close interdependence between them. Pericytes release VEGF, and affect ECs through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms. ECs release PDGF, which acts on PDGFR located on pericytes to promote their survival. In the presence of prolonged hypoxia there is remodelling of the basement membrane and poor integration with pericytes and ECs. This instability between the cells and BM leads to increased permeability, loss of pericytes, proliferation and migration of ECs.

Important RTKs that play a role in retinal biology include class 1 EGFR family, class 3 PDGFR family, class 4 VEGFR family, class 5 FGFR family, class 11 Tie receptor family and class 15 (ROS receptor family) [92]. VEGFs are a family of 40-kDa dimeric glycoproteins that include VEGFA–E and PlGF (placental growth factor). These factors bind to VEGFR1–3, which are all RTKs. Most important in angiogenesis is VEGFA and its receptors VEGFR1 and VEGFR2. PlGF acts synergistically with VEGF under pathological conditions, thereby promoting angiogenesis and vascular permeability. PDGF binds to PDGF receptors (α and β), while angiopoietins-1 and -2, bind to Tie receptors. Under physiological conditions Ang1 binds to Tie2 receptor and promotes vascular stability. Under pathological conditions such as hyperglycaemia, there is an increase in Ang2. This leads to destabilization of the blood vessels, increased response to VEGF, angiogenesis and vascular extravasation [93]. Inhibition of Ang2 occurs with faricimab and may thereby achieve better inhibition of neovascularization and vascular leakage.

Anti-VEGF pharmacotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of several disabling ocular conditions such as nAMD, diabetic macular edema (DME), proliferative vascular retinopathies etc. In addition, in patients with severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), wherein progression to PDR within 1 year occurs in 50–75% cases, the PANORAMA and Protocol W studies with aflibercept revealed significant reduction in the retinopathy score and reduced complications [94]. However, there is an overwhelming treatment burden and risk associated with this approach and so the ultimate risk of blindness persists. In addition, current anti-VEGF drugs are rapidly eliminated and hence if drug design and delivery can be achieved through zero-order kinetics, then they can be used once in 6/12 months [95]. It is believed that by complementing the effects of anti-VEGF pharmacotherapy, TKIs may have a role in reducing the injection burden. It is important to highlight that TKIs would not be effective as stand-alone agents to achieve complete control over VEGF-mediated diseases such as choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Instead, they would likely be effective in reducing the treatment burden by suppressing pathways of VEGF secretion, and thereby prolonging the treatment-free interval. While anti-VEGF antibodies block extracellular receptors, TKIs inhibit intracellular protein tyrosine phosphorylation and down-stream signaling. Hence, TKIs prevent formation of new vessels by blocking the VEGF signaling pathway [96]. TKIs may also open up the possibility of other mechanisms of action such as VEGFC and VEGFD inhibition (e.g. ongoing study using OPT-302, a VEGFC/D inhibitor, by Ophthea).

Protein kinase inhibitors that act on VEGF receptors include axitinib (VEGFR1–3), carbozantinib (VEGFR2), fruquintinib (VEGF), lenvatinib (VEGFR1–3), pazopanib (VEGFR1–3), regorafenib (VEGFR1–3), sorafenib (VEGFR1–3), tivozanib (VEGFR1–3), vandetanib (VEGFR2), nintedanib (VEGFR) and pegaptanib (VEGFR1–3) [97]. Combined VEGFR and PDGFR suppression has been found to have better results compared to mono-suppression of either receptor [98]. PKIs with combined activity against VEGF, fibroblast growth factor receptor and/or platelet-derived growth factor receptor include nintedanib (VEGFR, FGFR and PDGFR), sunitinib (PDGFR), ripretinib (PDGFR), lenvatinib (VEGFR, FGFR and PDGFR), erdafitinib (FGFR) and futibatinib (FGFR). JAK inhibitors include pacritinib, ruxolitinib, abrocitinib, baricitinib, deuruxolitinib, ritlecitinib, tofacitinib and upadacitinib [99]. Expression of spleen tyrosine kinase receptors has been found in 100% cases of retinoblastoma and fostamatinib is a PKI inhibitor that acts of this receptor [100, 101].

Before trials with TKIs, targeting of protein kinases was considered for the management of DME and diabetic retinopathy [102]. PKC isoforms β and δ have a role in regulating endothelial permeability, blood flow and VEGF signaling. PKC is an important intracellular signaling molecule belonging to the serine/threonine category of protein kinase enzymes. Their activity is increased in response to oxidative stress and exposure to DAG (diacylglycerol). Three of the four metabolic pathways that get activated by chronic hyperglycaemia (DAG pathway, glycation pathway, superoxide overproduction pathway) stimulate PKCβ activation [103]. Hence, inhibitors of PKC were tested in clinical trials for the management of patients with diabetic retinopathy and DME. PKC412, a non-specific PKC inhibitor, was the first agent tested for this purpose, but trials showed significant patient dropout from drug-related adverse effects, including elevated glucose levels [104]. Subsequently, a more specific inhibitor of the β1 and β2 isoforms of PKC, ruboxistaurin mesylate, was found to prevent and reverse effects of diabetic retinopathy in experimental models. A large multicentre clinical trial was then undertaken with two clinical arms: the PKC-diabetic retinopathy study (DRS) and PKC-diabetic macular edema study (DMES). Phase 3 study results from both PKC-DMES and PKC-DRS, with ruboxistaurin, did not show statistically significant effects on primary study outcomes [105]. Hence, focus has now shifted from PKC inhibition to RTK inhibition for the management of some retinal and non-retinal ophthalmic conditions. Although several categories of TKIs have received FDA approval for the treatment of several systemic disorders, the most important families that may have a useful role in managing ocular disorders include drugs targeting the VEGFR and PDGFR families. These include drugs having an effect on VEGFR such as axitinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, ponatinib, regorafenib, pazopanib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib, pralsetinib, FGFR family (erdafitinib), and JAK family (midostaurin, ruxolitinib, fedratinib). In addition, nintedanib, a TKI inhibitor, has been considered as a potential agent to reduce the risk proliferative vitreoretinopathy [106].

Retinal Pharmacotherapy with Tyrosine Kinases

A compound is considered to possess favourable drug-like properties if it has the following properties: aqueous solubility, chemical stability, metabolic stability, sufficient membrane permeability, receptor specificity, favourable ADME/Tox (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity) properties [107]. TKIs in general are lipophilic and possess limited bioavailability on oral administration [108]. For ophthalmic applications too, TKIs do not possess good drug-like properties. TKIs being small lipophilic molecules have the potential for topical delivery to treat posterior segment disorders. However, most are neutral or weakly basic in nature, thus limiting their aqueous solubility. Attempts have been made to increase drug availability of TKI eye drop formulations by combining with agents such as cyclodextrins (CDs) [109]. CDs are cyclic oligosaccharides with the following useful properties: they can form water-soluble complexes with many lipophilic molecules, thereby increasing their aqueous solubility, help achieve high drug concentrations and gradient to promote passive diffusion into intraocular tissues, and without affecting the intrinsic nature of the drug itself. CD has been used to form an inclusion complex with the TKI cediranib. CD has also been used earlier to improve water solubility of other TKIs such as lapatinib, erlotinib and gefitinib. Other methods such as use of riboflavin to prevent certain drugs (in eye drops) from thermal degradation have also been reported [110].

RTKIs have been studied for the management of some posterior as well as anterior segment ocular pathologies. Anterior segment pathologies include corneal vascularization and pterygium. Posterior segment conditions include nAMD, DME and severe non-proliferative retinopathy. Routes of TKI drug delivery that have been used for ocular applications include oral (e.g. sorafenib, pazopanib), topical delivery (e.g. pazopanib, PAN-90806, regorafenib, cediranib), intravitreal injectables (e.g. sunitinib), intravitreal implants (e.g. vorolanib, EYP-1901; axitinib, OTX-TKI) and suprachoroidal delivery (e.g. axitinib, CLS-AX). Attempts to treat patients with wet AMD using TKI inhibitors were initially made by administering some of these drugs, such as vorolanib, orally. Though some beneficial effects were noted, there were significant concerns with hepatic dysfunction and other gastrointestinal side effects. So, this is no longer the preferred route for TKI pharmacotherapy to manage retinal disorders.

It is well recognized that VEGFR and PDGFR are the predominant RTKs implicated in the evolution of wet AMD and other proliferative conditions of the retina. Of these, VEGFR has been the most targeted receptor for TKI pharmacotherapy. VEGFR inhibitors can be selective or non-selective in terms of target specificity and low, moderate or high in terms of potency of their action. For example, axitinib is a high-potency, selective inhibitor, whereas pazopanib is a moderate-potency, selective inhibitor. Amongst non-selective inhibitors, sorafenib has low potency, sunitinib has moderate potency and cabozantinib has high potency. The potency of action against each receptor (VEGFR1–3, PDGR, Tie2 and EGFR), effect on adjacent cells, inhibitory concentrations, and affinity for binding to adjacent structures are important determinants of the efficacy and safety of the drug [111]. The IC50 (half maximal inhibitory concentration) of axitinib, vorolanib and sunitinib for VEGFR2 is 0.2 ng/g, 52 ng/g and 43 ng/g respectively. For PDGFR it is 1.6, 260 and 160 respectively. One study, using assays, compared the anti-angiogenic effects of the TKIs vorolanib, sunitinib and axitinib to bevacizumab (positive control) [112]. All three TKIs were found to inhibit VEGFR2 more effectively than the positive control. In another screening experiment, comparing the three TKIs with aflibercept and OPT-302, only the TKIs were found to inhibit all three VEGF receptors 1–3; of these, axitinib had the strongest inhibitory effect on VEGFR2 [112]. This study also assessed the binding affinity of the three TKIs to melanin within iris, choroid and RPE. Chloroquine was used as a positive control. This binding may influence the pharmacokinetics and drug availability. Only sunitinib was found to bind to melanin. Of the three drugs, vorolanib was found to not impact functioning of VEGFR2 or binding to melanin [112]. The authors indicated that potent inhibition of VEGFR2 by axitinib may not be ideal because VEGFR2 is important for normal vascular stability [112]. Similarly, normal Tie2 function is necessary for vascular stability; however, axitinib shows higher inhibition of Tie2 compared to other TKIs. Amongst the three most studied TKIs for retinal diseases, axitinib shows the strongest inhibition, but its strong inhibition of physiologically important VEGFR2 and Tie2 are reasons for concern [112]. TKI inhibitors with combined inhibition of VEGFR and PDGFR have been proposed to have a role in managing patients who are resistant to anti-VEGF therapy [113, 114]. However, PDGF also increases VEGF upregulation and new vessel formation and hence care must be taken while using agents that block pericytes [115]. In addition, it has been reported that EGFR inhibition leads to delayed wound healing and side effects such as corneal thinning, erosion and ulceration [116]. Hence, TKIs that are evaluated for treatment of ocular conditions such as nAMD, DME etc. should possess a high affinity for VEGFR and no or minimal affinity for EGFR. The potential for drug resistance during ocular use of TKIs also exists.

Disorders in which TKI trials have been conducted and are also ongoing include DME, nAMD and NPDR. Most studies have revealed the safety and tolerability of TKIs, as well as their potential for reducing treatment burden while maintaining anatomic and functional outcomes. However, some TKIs have demonstrated dose-dependent adverse effects. Small-molecule TKIs that are in clinical trials include acrizanib (Novartis), axitinib (ocular therapeutics, Alcon, Clearside, Kala), cabozantinib, cediranib, dovitinib, dendranib (Ashvattha), nintedanib (Cloudbreak), pazopanib (GlaxoSmithKline), PAN-90806 (PanOptica), regorafenib (Bayer), sunitinib (CalciMedica and SCAI therapeutics) and vorolanib (EyePoint and Tyrogenex). Some of the other TKIs discussed in this review include anlotinib, R406, acrizanib and nintedanib. Extensively Studied TKIs

Axitinib TKI

Axitinib has been used in clinical trials in multiple forms: nanosuspension eye drops for treatment of ocular surface diseases, hydrogel implant for treating DR and nAMD, injectable suspension for treating AMD, and a formulation for suprachoroidal injection. The oral bioavailability is reasonable at 58%. Axitinib has a potency 10 times that of other TKIs and a high VEGFR/EGFR IC50 ratio [117, 118]. It inhibits all VEGFRs but is most potent in inhibiting VEGFR2. Unlike other TKIs, axitinib causes suppression of Tie2. Axitinib binds to the intracellular ATP binding site of VEGFR1, VEGFR2 and VEGFR3, thereby preventing phosphorylation of these TKIs and resultant suppression of downstream VEGF-mediated pathways. It suppresses new vessel formation in the cornea, retina and choroid.

Intravitreal Axitinib TKI

OTX-TKI (Axpaxli) is a highly selective TKI against both VEGF and PDGF. Axpaxli uses the Elutyx drug delivery platform that contains a programmable and bioresorbable hydrogel matrix. Axpaxli hydrogel is created using PEG polymers cross-linked with micronized axitinib enmeshed within the matrix using trilysine linkers [119]. The implant is injected using a 25G–27G needle and the drug is released through zero-order kinetics. Being highly selective, potency and biocompatibility are superior compared to other TKIs. The HELIOS phase 1 trial reported the safety and tolerability of axitinib intravitreal implant in patients with nAMD, DME and DR [120]. Axpaxli has been found to be efficacious in reducing treatment burden in early studies.

In patients with nAMD, two independent phase 1 trials have been undertaken and the drug is now undergoing phase 3 superiority study (SOL study). Investigators in the HELIOS phase 1 study reported that both central subfoveal thickness (CST) and best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) using a single axitinib 600 µg implant on day 0 plus aflibercept 2 mg at 4 weeks, or aflibercept 8 weeks, was comparable to that of using aflibercept 2 mg at 8-weekly intervals. Twenty-one patients were randomly assigned to the two groups in a 3:1 ratio (16 in the implant group and 5 in the aflibercept-alone group). At 12 months, 89% reduction in treatment burden was observed. No additional injections were needed in 73% and 33% of patients at 6 months and 12 months, respectively. Monthly anti-VEGF injection rate in the implant group was reported to be 0.08 injections in comparison to 0.78 injections before the treatment. BCVA changes from baseline were − 1.0 ± 6.0 letters and + 2.0 ± 7.2 letters in the implant and aflibercept-alone groups, respectively. Changes in CST were 20.0 ± 41.6 µm (implant group) and − 2.2 ± 8.5 µm (standard of care group) [121]. No severe adverse events such as retinal vasculitis, iritis or intraocular infection were noted. The SOL phase 3 study in wet AMD is using a reformulated drug with 450 µg more axitinib in the Form IV crystal polymorph structure. This is a randomized controlled trial involving 300 patients randomized in a 1:1 ratio to two arms of axitinib implant and single aflibercept 2 mg injection, followed by as-needed anti-VEGF injection [8].

The HELIOS study also evaluated the utility of the axitinib implant in patients with moderately severe to severe NPDR (a two-step change on DRSS) [9, 122]. In the phase 1 study in the USA (n = 21 patients), only one dose of the implant (600 µg) is being assessed. In the phase 1 study being undertaken in Australia (n = 28 patients), multiple arms are being assessed using 200 µg, 400 µg and 600 µg of the implant, either as monotherapy or in combination with anti-VEGF. Interim results from both studies indicate that the implant has the potential as a sustained maintenance therapy for 6–12 months. Up to 93% reduction in the treatment burden has been observed. In patients with diabetic retinopathy, compared to the sham group, overall 46% of patients showed some improvement in the DRSS score, of which 23% had a two-step improvement. In addition, no patients showed worsening in the implant group compared with worsening in 25% of patients in the sham group. Hence, disease stability was observed in patients with severe NPDR.

Suprachoroidal Axitinib

CLS-AX is a suspension of axitinib for suprachoroidal delivery. The drug is delivered using the Clearside (CLS) Biomedical SCS (suprachoroidal space) 30G microinjector, which has already been FDA approved for Xipere. Compartmentalization of the drug within the suprachoroidal space is claimed to improve safety, efficacy, bioavailability and durability. This form of delivery was first studied in a rabbit model wherein it yielded 11-fold greater exposure of the posterior eye surface to axitinib [118]. In addition, the drug was undetectable in plasma and aqueous humour over a 3-month period. The first clinical study using this route was the OASIS phase 1/2a and OASIS extension trials, in 27 patients with nAMD [123]. The drug is now undergoing phase 2b study (ODYSSEY trial). In phase 1/2a OASIS studies, four dose-escalating groups were assessed and the drug was found to be well tolerated. The four doses of the drug being studied are low dose (0.03 mg), low-mid dose (0.10 mg), high-mid dose (0.50 mg) and high dose (1.0 mg). A good safety profile has been reported alongside a 57% reduction in treatment burden at 6 months. Indications for rescue injection with aflibercept in the CLS-AX study were more than 10-letter loss of vision, increase in CMT of 75 µm or greater, and onset of new hemorrhage. The ODYSSEY trial is a phase 2b study in 60 patients with nAMD wherein CLS-AX is being compared with aflibercept injections [124, 125]. Phase 3 studies are under consideration after assessing safety and efficacy outcome of this trial. OASIS and Extension study of CLS-AX used three doses: 0.1 mg, 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg. The ODYSSEY phase 2b study uses the 1.0-mg dose only (compared with aflibercept in 2:1 ratio).

AsclepiX therapeutics is studying a microparticulate formulation (micronized crystals) of axitinib, AXT107, in 15 patients with nAMD, as part of a phase 1/2a trial. This formulation is claimed to target VEGFR2 and also activate Tie2 receptor (which helps in vascular stability).

Vorolanib TKI

Vorolanib inhibits VEGFR, PDGF and FGF, the strongest being against VEGFR2. It does not inhibit Tie2. In animal models, a single intravitreal injection of the drug was found to stay above the IC50 dose for about 9 months [126]. This drug may also have some neuroprotective effect and reduce the risk of fibrosis, as well. Neuroprotection has been claimed in animal retinal detachment model studies [127].

Intravitreal Vorolanib TKI

Vorolanib (EYP-1901, EyePoint) implant uses the biodegradable Durasert technology which allows 90% of the implant to be the drug itself. This enables delivery of a high loading dose of the drug. Unlike other carrier implants, Durasert implants do not have a polyimide covering. The entire implant is covered with polyvinyl alcohol. This design has been found to release the drug at a more consistent rate (zero-order kinetics) after the initial burst release (which allows rapid establishment of therapeutic levels of the drug). The implant is called Duravyu and is administered using 22G needle, as in-office intravitreal injection. Following the initial burst release, there is stable zero-order drug elution for about 9 months. Each implant is 1/5000th of the vitreous volume and up to three inserts have been administered as single intravitreal injection [128]. This drug has been studied in patients with nAMD and severe NPDR in the DAVIO and PAVIA trials, respectively. Dose escalation in DAVIO 1 trial was 440 µg, 1060 µg, 2060 µg and 3090 µg single injection. In DAVIO 2 and PAVIA trials only two doses were studied: 2060 µg and 3090 µg. In the DAVIO 2 study, the comparator was aflibercept; in PAVIA, it was sham injection. In the DAVIO 1 study injection burden was reduced by 75% at 6 months, thus indicating its potential to play the role of ‘treat to maintain’ drug in nAMD. Fifteen percent of the 17 patients did not respond to both standard of care and EYP-1901 in the trial [129]. No cases of implant migration and occlusive vasculitis were noted. The DAVIO 2 study was undertaken in 150 patients suffering from nAMD. The three arms in this study were low dose drug (2 mg, n = 48), high dose drug (3 mg, n = 48) and aflibercept alone (2 mg, n = 48) [130]. The low and high doses were found to be non-inferior to aflibercept 2 mg, after an initial loading dose. Visual acuity curves at 12 months were identical, while the central subfield retinal thickness was more stable in the implant group for about 14 months. With both implant doses, treatment burden was reduced by more than 85%, and about 64% were supplement free for over 6 months. However, follow-up evaluation at regular intervals is still recommended. No systemic and ocular side effects, including occlusive retinal vasculitis and implant migration, were observed. The implant is now undergoing phase 3 trials in nAMD (LUGANO, trial in US subjects; and LUCIA, global trial).

The PAVIA trial in patients with moderately severe to severe NPDR studied the efficacy and safety of the implant in being able to reduce the diabetic retinopathy score by two steps [131]. Low dose and high dose of the drug were compared with a single sham injection. At the 12 and 24 months follow-up, the study failed to achieve the primary end point. The VERONA trial is a phase 2 trial in patients with previously treated DME wherein the time to supplemental anti-VEGF is being determined. It is comparing two treatment arms, 2 mg and 3 mg (n = 20) of the implant with aflibercept (n = 5). Following positive results in this trial, phase 3 studies, COMO and CAPRI, have been planned.

Oral vorolanib has been studied in the X-82 trial (APEX study) for the treatment of nAMD but was withdrawn because of systemic side effects [132]. With the oral formulation of the drug, about 17% patients discontinued its use owing to side effects.

Sunitinib TKI

Sunitinib (GrayBug Vision’s GB-102) inhibits VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 as well as α and β forms of PDGF. Inhibition of VEGFR2 is a predominant effect. Other than VEGFR and PFGFR, sunitinib also inhibits stem cell growth factor receptor (KIT) and FLT3 [133]. As a result of its reversible binding to melanin in the RPE and choroid, sunitinib has been found to maintain pharmacologically active levels for 6 to 7 months in animal models [134]. Sunitinib has been found to reduce photoreceptor cell death, and have protective effects on retinal ganglion cells [135]. In animal models, sunitinib drops and intravitreal implants were seen to have similar efficacy [136]. Sunitinib has been tested as eye drops, intravitreal implant and as an intravitreal injectable, self-aggregating depot preparation.

Intravitreal Injection: Sunitinib has been formulated as an intravitreal injectable, self-aggregating depot preparation. Microparticles (mPEG-PLGA) of the drug get aggregated as a depot in the inferior vitreous. Active release of the drug occurs slowly from the depot. This has undergone phase 1 and phase 2 trials [111]. In the latter study, 56 patients with wet AMD were randomized into aflibercept and TKI arms. Sunitinib was randomized in two groups: of 1 mg/mg and 2 mg/mg at baseline, and 1 mg/mg at 6 months in both groups. Sham injections were given at the other 2-monthly intervals until month 10.2 mg/mg at 6 months, though planned earlier, was changed to 1 mg/mg owing to adverse effects. These AEs were explained by the non-selective nature of the drug. The results of this study were disappointing because the visual outcomes were much worse than aflibercept alone. In phase 1 studies (GB-102), self-limited migration of the particles of sunitinib into the anterior chamber was seen in 28% of patients [137]. ALTISSIMO, a phase 2b study of the drug, showed a decrease in injection frequency of 58%. In this phase, migration was below 10% following changes to the drug formulation [138]. However, even after extension study, the overall benefits were insufficient and so the development of GB-102 has been stopped by Graybug (which since been acquired by CalciMedica). Oral sunitinib has been found to be useful in some cases of peripapillary capillary hemangioblastomas in von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHL), but side effects were multiple [139].

Less Extensively Studied TKIs

D-4517.2 TKI

This drug belongs to a new class of TKIs called dendranibs. It uses globular shaped polyamidoamine dendrimers coated with hydroxyl groups. They are hence called hydroxyl dendrimers (HDs). Each of these is 4 nm in diameter and binds to 7–8 sunitinib molecules. They have been found in animal studies to cross the blood retinal barrier after systemic administration, and to selectively target and accumulate within areas of neovascularization and inflammation. Ashvattha Therapeutics is evaluating the safety and efficacy of a TKI molecule, D-4517.2. Initial studies, using oral and subcutaneous routes in a mouse model, showed a two-fold reduction in choroidal neovascular membrane (CNV) size [140]. In a phase 1 study on 12 healthy volunteers, the drug was found to be safe following subcutaneous injection of doses varying from 0.25 mg/kg to 1.0 mg/kg. TEJAS is a phase 2 ongoing study with this drug in patients with nAMD or DME using subcutaneous, once-a-month injection of the drug [141, 142].

PAN-90806 TKI (PanOptica)

This is a selective and potent inhibitor of VEGFR2. In a rabbit model the molecule was found to reach the posterior segment tissues, including retina [143]. Phase 1/2 study has been undertaken in patients with nAMD using once daily suspension of the drug at different doses (2 mg/ml, 6 mg/ml and 10 mg/ml). Preliminary results from phase 1/2 trials indicated clinical benefit in 88% patients and good safety profile. Despite notable safety and tolerability of the suspension, 50% of patients needed rescue therapy within 16 weeks. Hence, efficacy of this approach seems underwhelming. In addition, punctate corneal erosions were seen in several patients, but these were reversible [144, 145].

Pazopanib TKI

This drug targets all three VEGFRs and has been studied as eye drops as well as oral formulation [146]. In tablet form it has low bioavailability of 20%. In a study with topical pazopanib, 510 patients from different regions were given the eye drops 2–4 times in different doses (5 mg/ml and 10 mg/ml), placebo eye drops and ranibizumab injections (monthly) for 52 weeks. As-needed rescue therapy was given in all groups. Though the drops were well tolerated, no advantages in terms of visual acuity and reduction in the injection frequency were observed [147]. As it failed to meet the primary objectives, its further development was discontinued. In another study with oral pazopanib, 15 patients with nAMD were given 15 mg of the drug daily for 28 days. Six patients with high-risk CFH Y402H CC genotype needed rescue therapy. Nine patients showed 8-letter improvement and 50-µm reduction in CMT; seven patients had adverse effects [148].

Regorafenib TKI

This drug targets all three VEGFRs and has a low EGFR/VEGFR ratio and so may not be best suited to treat posterior segment diseases. Topical regorafenib (DREAM study) was evaluated in phase 2 trial in 51 patients with nAMD; 30 mg/ml solution of the drug was used three times daily. No visible gain in visual acuity was noted but adverse events and need for rescue injections were significant. Having failed to show benefits, this approach was discontinued [149].

Sorafenib TKI

Sorafenib is a potent inhibitor of both VEGFR1 (65 times higher than IC50) and VEGFR2 (18 times higher than IC50). There is only one report of two cases of nAMD [150]. Oral formulation of the drug, 200 mg administered thrice weekly, was used in both cases. It was seen to reduce injection frequency. One patient complained of acral dermatitis.

AIV007 (AiViva Biopharma) TKI

AIV007 is a phase 1 study in 18 patients with nAMD or DME using lenvatinib delivered via a periocular injectable proprietary JEL technology [151]. Following injection, the drug converts into a biodissolvable depot for long-term drug release.

Miscellaneous TKIs

Anlotinib is a TKI that inhibits several RTKs: VEGFR, PDGFR and FGFR. Authors first used cell lines of ECs and pericytes for demonstrating in vitro activity. In vivo studies were performed in oxygen-induced retinopathy model (OIR; ICR mouse pups) and laser-induced CNV model (mouse model) [152]. Using OIR, it was found to be well tolerated and inhibited the proliferation of endothelial cells. In the CNV model, when combined with ranibizumab injection it revealed a 26% reduction in size of the lesion.

Acrizanib was tested as a 2% eye drop suspension for the treatment of nAMD. Its development was discontinued as it failed to achieve clinical end points and also resulted in corneal haze (which was reversible) in every other patient [153]. The latter was found to be due to its higher affinity for EGFR and a low VEGFR/EGFR IC50 ratio. It has also been evaluated as an intravitreal injection [154].

R406 TKI: Spleen tyrosine kinase receptor has been identified in the majority of retinoblastomas (Rb). R406, a spleen TKI, was evaluated on a xenograft mouse model of Rb [155]. Multiple routes were tried: subconjunctival, topical drops, intravitreal, and intra-arterial injections. However, the drug failed to reach exposure required for retinoblastoma cell death in vitro. One previous study had shown that R406 can induce Rb cell death in culture [156]. Fostamatinib is an orally administered prodrug of RT406. It has been used in clinical trials to treat patients with autoimmune disorders [157]. Its role remains to be tested in retinoblastoma.

Nintedanib TKI: Apoptosis and necrosis are the two main mechanisms involved in cell death. The former is mediated by the caspase family of cysteine proteases and is a well-regulated process. The latter was earlier considered as a passive, unregulated form of cell death, but more recent evidence indicates that sometimes it is regulated by RIP (receptor interacting protein kinases) [158]. RIP kinases are serine/threonine kinases and it has been shown in experimental models of retinal detachment that their inhibition, alongside caspase inhibition, is necessary for photoreceptor protection [159]. Box 1 proteins are released by necrotic cells and not apoptotic cells. These proteins have been found to be elevated in the vitreous of patients with retinal detachment. Thus, programmed necrosis and not programmed cell death (apoptosis) seems to be an important mechanism in photoreceptor death after retinal detachment. This has also been noted in patients with inherited retinal disorders and light-induced cell injury [160]. In addition, following retinal detachment, RPE cells undergo type 2 epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Nintedanib is a small-molecule TKI that suppresses EMT in vitro. One report found nintedanib to have a strong potential for suppressing proliferative vitreoretinopathy, following in vitro studies [106, 161]. Nintedanib, which is also under evaluation for the management of corneal neovascularization and pterygium, has a low affinity for EGFR, and is considered to be less toxic to the cornea [162, 163].

p75NTR, with its low affinity for nerve growth factor, an RTK, is reported to have a role in development of the eyes [164]. Hepatocyte growth factor receptor (HGFR) is also known as c-MET or mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET) factor [165]. Because of this effect, it may be useful to study the role of EMT influencing TKIs in the prevention of proliferative vitreoretinopathy.

Cabozantinib has the highest VEGFR/EGFR IC50 ratio and so may have the potential to be effective in conditions such as nAMD [166]. Safety may be a concern as a case of corneal perforation during treatment with this drug has recently been reported [167]. Cediranib, a multitarget VEGFR signaling pathway inhibitor, was one of the TKIs tested earlier in topical form for treating nAMD, but has a low VEGFR/EGFR IC50 ratio [109]. Dovitinib also possess low VEGFR/EGFR IC50 ratio, and has been tested in topical form for the treatment of corneal neovascularization. Some novel RTKIs, yet to receive approval, that may be useful for ocular evaluation owing to their VEGFR targeting include apatinib (rivoceranib) and surufatinib.

Conclusion

Suboptimal outcomes with anti-VEGF in the real world are attributed to high treatment burden and hence poor treatment adherence. TKIs seem to have the promise of being able to reduce treatment burden and improve adherence to treatment. Though several studies are ongoing, the overall efficacy, safety and potential of TKIs in improving the anatomical and clinical outcomes of patients with chronic retinal conditions remains to be fully deciphered.
