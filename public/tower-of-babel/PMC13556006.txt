Hand-eye laterality and structural asymmetry between dominant and non-dominant eyes: a multi-modal study in 524 healthy volunteers
Authors: Pelin Kiyat, Melis Palamar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the relationship between ocular and hand dominance patterns in healthy volunteers and to evaluate structural differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes using anterior and posterior segment measurements.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 524 healthy volunteers (50% female, age 20–60). Ocular dominance was determined using the hole-in-card test and hand dominance with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Participants underwent Swept-Source Optical Coherence Tomography for retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell complex (GCC), subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), and choroidal vascularity index (CVI) measurements; Scheimpflug imaging for anterior segment parameters; and partial coherence interferometry for axial length determination.ResultsRight eye dominance was observed in 60.9% and left eye dominance in 39.1% of participants. A pronounced cross-lateralization pattern emerged: 93.6% of left eye dominant individuals exhibited right hand dominance, significantly higher than in right eye dominant participants (57.1% right-handed, p = 0.024; 42.9% left-handed, p = 0.016). After correction for multiple comparisons, dominant eyes demonstrated significantly greater central corneal thickness (p = 0.002). RNFL nasal quadrant thickness showed the strongest trend toward significance (p = 0.014). Axial length, additional RNFL quadrants, and GCC sectors demonstrated consistent trends toward significance. No differences were found in SFCT or CVI.ConclusionsDominant eyes demonstrated significantly greater central corneal thickness after correction for multiple comparisons, with nasal RNFL thickness showing the strongest trend toward significance. A differential lateralization pattern was observed between ocular dominance groups, suggesting a potential association between ocular and manual preferences; however, this finding warrants cautious interpretation given the population-level prevalence of right-handedness.Trial registrationThis cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of İzmir Democracy University, Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Training and Research Hospital (date: 26.03.2025; number: 2025/427) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Introduction

Ocular dominance is a neurophysiological phenomenon in which one eye is preferentially used for visual processing during binocular vision [1]. First documented by Giovanni Battista della Porta in 1593, this visual preference establishes a hierarchical relationship between the paired organs despite their apparent symmetry [2]. Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated enhanced visual cortex activation in response to dominant eye input, with individual variation attributed to differences in neural connectivity [3, 4].

Several studies have investigated structural differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes, with variable results. Hamurcu et al. [5] found greater retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in dominant eyes, while axial length investigations have yielded conflicting findings [6, 7] and corneal and choroidal parameters have similarly shown inconsistent outcomes [2, 8]. The relationship between ocular and hand dominance remains equally debated: although approximately 90% of the population is right-handed and 70% right eye-dominant, the nature of their association is controversial [9]. Hand-eye laterality is classified as uncrossed (ipsilateral) or crossed (contralateral), a distinction with functional relevance in both visuomotor tasks and sports performance [10, 11].

The integration of ocular dominance assessment into clinical ophthalmology has significant therapeutic implications. Refractive surgeries, presbyopic corrections, and cataract procedures show improved outcomes when treatment strategies incorporate dominance considerations [12, 13]. Designating the dominant eye for distance correction during monovision treatments underscores the clinical value of ocular dominance evaluation [13, 14].

To our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively integrated multi-modal structural assessment of both anterior and posterior segments with systematic evaluation of hand-eye laterality in a large cohort. While prior investigations have examined structural differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes [5, 15] or dominance-related choroidal vascular patterns [16, 17] and biometric asymmetry [6, 7, 18] in isolation, anterior segment characterization via Scheimpflug imaging which enables comprehensive evaluation of corneal and anterior chamber parameters [19] has not been incorporated into any prior ocular dominance study. The present study addresses this gap through a comprehensive, multi-modal assessment encompassing anterior segment parameters, posterior segment measurements, and axial biometry alongside systematic hand-eye laterality evaluation in a large cohort of healthy volunteers.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of İzmir Democracy University, Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Training and Research Hospital (date: 26.03.2025; number: 2025/427) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

A total of 524 consecutive healthy volunteers (50% female, 50% male) aged between 20 and 60 years were recruited from individuals who presented for routine ophthalmological examinations at the Department of Ophthalmology, İzmir Democracy University, Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Training and Research Hospital. Participants with history of ocular surgery or trauma, any ocular pathology including glaucoma, retinal disorders, corneal opacity, or media opacities, refractive errors greater than ± 1.0 diopters spherical equivalent in either eye (thereby limiting anisometropia to ≤ 2.0 diopters), intraocular pressure greater than 21 mmHg, best-corrected visual acuity less than 10/10 on the Snellen chart, use of any topical or systemic medications that could affect ocular measurements, presence of any systemic disease that could influence ocular parameters, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and inconsistent results in dominance testing were excluded. Of note, although the upper age limit of 60 years coincides with the conventional threshold for senile cataract onset, all participants underwent slit-lamp biomicroscopy and were required to have clear ocular media and best-corrected visual acuity of ≥ 10/10; individuals with any degree of lens opacification were excluded irrespective of age.

The hole-in-card test was employed to determine ocular dominance, as it is widely regarded as one of the most reliable and reproducible methods for assessing sighting dominance in clinical and research settings. Each participant held a cardboard test card with a central hole using both hands while viewing a target 5 m away through the hole with both eyes open. Each eye was then occluded alternately by the examiner. When the dominant eye was covered, the target disappeared from view through the hole; when the non-dominant eye was covered, the dominant eye maintained fixation on the target. Additionally, participants were instructed to bring the card toward their face while maintaining target alignment until the hole positioned itself over one eye, typically the dominant one. For reliability, the test was repeated three times, and consistent results were required for inclusion [2].

For hand dominance assessment, the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, which evaluates hand preference across nine common manual tasks—writing, throwing, scissors-use, toothbrush-use, using a knife without a fork, spoon-use, sweeping with a broom, lighting a match, and opening a box lid—was utilized. Participants indicated their preference using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Always left” (1) to “Always right” (5) for each activity. The handedness sum score was calculated by summing all nine values to determine overall hand preference. The laterality quotient was then derived from these responses, generating a score ranging from − 100 (indicating strong left-handedness) to + 100 (indicating strong right-handedness), with 0 representing ambidexterity. Participants with positive laterality quotients were classified as right-handed, those with negative values as left-handed [20].

All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmological examination including visual acuity assessment with Snellen chart, refraction testing, slit-lamp biomicroscopy for anterior and posterior segment evaluation, intraocular pressure measurement using Goldmann applanation tonometry, and detailed fundus examination.

Peripapillary RNFL and GCC measurements were obtained using SS-OCT (DRI-OCT Triton, Topcon, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) with the Optic Disc Cube scan protocol (12 mm × 9 mm). This 3D scanning protocol enabled comprehensive evaluation of the optic disc and peripapillary region. RNFL thickness was measured in four quadrants (superior, temporal, inferior, and nasal). GCC thickness, comprising the RNFL, ganglion cell layer, and inner plexiform layer, was automatically measured from the inner limiting membrane to the outer boundary of the inner plexiform layer and assessed in six sectors (superotemporal, superior, superonasal, inferonasal, inferior, and inferotemporal). Only images with a signal strength index ≥ 50 were included for analysis, and scans with motion artifacts or segmentation errors were excluded.

Subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) was measured using the same SS-OCT device (DRI-OCT Triton), which enables deep choroidal penetration due to its longer wavelength (1050 nm) without requiring enhanced depth imaging mode. SFCT was defined as the perpendicular distance from the outer boundary of the retinal pigment epithelium to the choroid-sclera interface at the subfoveal location. Two ophthalmologists (P.K. and M.P.) independently performed blinded manual measurements and subsequently calculated the average value. Measurements displaying a discrepancy within 10 micrometers of the mean were considered acceptable. In instances where the variance exceeded 10 micrometers, measurements were repeated. Interexaminer reproducibility was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient. To mitigate potential diurnal variations in choroidal thickness, all OCT scans were performed at the same time of day, between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM.

Choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was analyzed following the methodology established by Agrawal et al.16 using ImageJ software (Version 1.53a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Subfoveal OCT images were selected for binarization and segmentation. Using the polygon selection tool, the total choroidal area (TCA) was outlined from the outer edge of the retinal pigment epithelium to the choroidal-scleral boundary, spanning 1500 μm horizontally (750 μm bilateral to the foveal center). Selected regions were stored in the ROI manager. The images underwent 8-bit conversion, followed by application of Niblack autolocal thresholding to calculate mean pixel intensity and standard deviation values. The luminal area (LA), corresponding to the vascular compartment, was isolated via color thresholding and recorded in the ROI manager. Final vascular area quantification was achieved by combining both regions through an “AND” operation. CVI was derived by dividing LA by TCA.

Anterior segment parameters were evaluated by the Pentacam Scheimpflug camera system (Oculus Inc., Wetzlar, Germany), recording central corneal thickness (CCT), anterior chamber depth, anterior chamber volume, anterior chamber angle, and corneal volume. Measurements were taken in a dark room without pupillary dilation [21]. For axial length determination, the IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany), which utilizes 780 nm wavelength light and partial coherence interferometry to measure the distance from corneal vertex to retinal pigment epithelium, was used. Axial length-related magnification corrections were not applied; however, the strict inclusion criteria (refractive error within ± 1.0 diopters) and paired-eye comparison design minimized potential magnification-related variability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess normality of data distribution. For comparisons between dominant and non-dominant eyes, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were utilized based on normality of the data. Chi-square tests were employed to evaluate the relationship between ocular dominance and hand dominance patterns. Different hand preference ratios between eye dominance groups were compared using Chi-square tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For paired comparisons between dominant and non-dominant eyes across structural parameters, Bonferroni correction was additionally applied to control for multiple testing, with anatomically distinct measurement families treated separately: anterior segment and biometric parameters, RNFL quadrants, GCC sectors, and choroidal parameters. Findings that did not survive Bonferroni correction but achieved nominal significance (p < 0.05) are reported as showing a trend toward significance. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all other analyses. To assess potential refractive confounding, linear regression analysis was performed with interocular spherical equivalent difference as the independent variable and dominant/non-dominant eye differences in central corneal thickness and axial length as dependent variables. While a formal a priori power analysis was not performed prior to data collection, the sample size of 524 paired observations provides robust statistical power for the comparisons performed. For reference, a paired t-test with n = 524 achieves > 99% power to detect medium effect sizes and > 80% power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.15) at α = 0.05. Accordingly, the present cohort exceeds the requirements for detecting the clinically meaningful structural differences reported herein.

Results

The study included 524 healthy volunteers (50% female, 50% male) with a mean age of 38.2 ± 12.9 (20–60) years. Ocular dominance assessment revealed right eye dominance in 60.9% of participants and left eye dominance in 39.1%.

Analysis of the relationship between ocular and hand dominance demonstrated significant lateralization patterns. Among left eye dominant participants, 93.6% exhibited right hand dominance. In contrast, among right eye dominant volunteers, 57.1% showed right hand dominance while 42.9% displayed left hand dominance. Statistical analysis revealed that the prevalence of right hand dominance in left eye dominant individuals was significantly higher compared to both right hand dominance (p = 0.024) and left hand dominance (p = 0.016) in right eye dominant individuals, suggesting a differential lateralization pattern between ocular dominance groups that warrants further investigation.

Structural comparisons between dominant and non-dominant eyes revealed differences across multiple parameters. Following Bonferroni correction, dominant eyes demonstrated significantly greater CCT (p = 0.002) and a trend toward shorter axial length (p = 0.042) compared to non-dominant eyes (Table 1). RNFL nasal quadrant thickness demonstrated the strongest trend toward significance (p = 0.014), followed by inferior (p = 0.025) and superior quadrants (p = 0.049). GCC thickness in the superotemporal and inferonasal sectors similarly demonstrated a trend toward significance (p = 0.045 and p = 0.030, respectively). No significant differences were observed in SFCT or CVI between dominant and non-dominant eyes (Table 2). The mean interocular spherical equivalent difference was 0.26 ± 0.17 diopters. Regression analysis revealed no significant association between interocular spherical equivalent difference and CCT asymmetry (r = 0.019, p = 0.854) or axial length asymmetry (r = 0.014, p = 0.894), confirming that the structural differences observed are independent of interocular refractive error.

Discussion

The investigation of ocular and hand dominance patterns in 524 healthy volunteers revealed several significant findings that enhance the understanding of lateralization in visual processing. The prevalence of right eye dominance (60.9%) in our cohort aligns with previously reported distributions: Cheng et al. (63.6% in Taiwan) [6], Ito et al. (77.7% in Japan) [7], and Zhou et al. (67.8% in Shanghai, China) [22]. These variations likely reflect differences in ethnic demographics, testing methodologies, and population characteristics, with our findings showing values close to the established range for diverse populations.

This is the first study to comprehensively evaluate both hand-eye dominance patterns and detailed anterior-posterior segment measurements in such a large cohort. Previous investigations have typically examined these dimensions in isolation, either structural correlates of ocular dominance or its relationship with handedness, often with limited sample sizes and restricted anatomical scope. The present study offers two principal contributions that distinguish it from prior work: first, an integrated multi-modal assessment encompassing anterior segment parameters, posterior segment measurements, and axial biometry within a single cohort of 524 participants; and second, the demonstration of a differential lateralization pattern between ocular dominance groups, with 93.6% of left eye dominant individuals exhibiting right hand dominance. This finding challenges previous reports suggesting weak correlations between eye dominance and handedness. While Carey and Hutchinson [9] found no clear correlation using performance-based dominance strength measurements, the categorical classification approach used here, with a larger sample size (524 participants), revealed a distinctive lateralization pattern that warrants further investigation.

This observed lateralization pattern aligns with Chaumillon et al. findings [10] which demonstrated significantly faster reaction times in right-handed individuals when visual targets appeared in the contralateral hemifield to their dominant eye during Poffenberger paradigm testing. The present data showing that nearly all left eye dominant individuals naturally exhibit this configuration should be interpreted with caution given the inherent population-level skew toward right-handedness, and future studies incorporating baseline population correction are needed to confirm whether this reflects a true neurodevelopmental preference rather than a disadvantage, potentially explaining the functional advantages observed by Chaumillon et al.

The practical implications emerge in sports performance research where dominance patterns significantly impact visual-motor skills. Laborde et al. [11] demonstrated that archers with uncrossed eye-hand dominance patterns significantly outperformed those with crossed patterns, attributing this performance difference to archery’s heavy reliance on the geniculate-striate system for precision targeting. Conversely, Allen et al. [23] found no such performance advantage among elite golfers with either crossed or uncrossed dominance patterns, suggesting that the benefits of specific dominance configurations may be highly task-dependent.

Structural comparisons between dominant and non-dominant eyes revealed complex relationships that reconcile apparent contradictions in the literature. The finding of shorter axial length in dominant eyes aligns with Ito et al. [7] who observed longer axial lengths in non-dominant eyes of anisometropic patients (≥ 2.0 diopters). Conversely, Cheng et al. [6] reported longer axial lengths in dominant eyes within their anisometropic cohort. These discrepancies likely reflect fundamental differences in study populations, the participants herein had minimal refractive errors (± 1.0 diopters), while previous studies focused on significant anisometropia, alongside variations in biometric measurement techniques. The shorter axial length observed in dominant eyes in our cohort is unlikely to reflect differential near work exposure or presbyopic changes, given the predominantly pre-presbyopic age distribution, the stability of axial length in adults, and the exclusion of participants with significant refractive error. Of note, this difference demonstrated a trend toward significance after correction rather than formal statistical significance, and confirmation in future larger studies with pre-specified endpoints is warranted. Furthermore, regression analysis confirmed that the interocular spherical equivalent difference did not significantly predict axial length asymmetry (r = 0.014, p = 0.894), supporting the conclusion that the observed structural difference reflects a genuine biological feature of ocular dominance rather than a refractive confound.

Neural tissue distribution showed consistent patterns across studies. RNFL nasal quadrant thickness demonstrated the strongest trend toward significance after correction, while superior and inferior quadrants demonstrated a consistent trend toward significance. This pattern of peripapillary neural tissue asymmetry extends upon Hamurcu et al. [5] findings and suggests a genuine structural difference. The observed trends in superior and inferior quadrants may reflect population-specific characteristics or methodological sensitivity, and their biological relevance warrants further investigation.

Similarly, GCC thickness differences in the superotemporal and inferonasal sectors demonstrated a trend toward significance following Bonferroni correction. While Jimenez-Santos et al. [24] found central ganglion cell layer differences that lost significance after correction for multiple comparisons, the consistent directionality of GCC differences across multiple sectors in our cohort supports the hypothesis-generating value of these observations.

No significant differences were found between dominant and non-dominant eyes in SFCT and CVI, corroborating findings by Özcan et al. [8] and Nakaniida et al. [25] However, the novel finding of greater CCT in dominant eyes contrasts with Gundreddy et al. [2] who reported predominantly thinner CCT in dominant eyes despite noting inconsistent results. The larger sample size in the present study may have provided superior statistical power, while methodological differences including measurement techniques, timing, and environmental factors could explain these disparate findings. Additionally, regression analysis confirmed independence of CCT asymmetry from interocular refractive error (r = 0.019, p = 0.854), further supporting the biological basis of this finding.

Clinical implications emerge from Zhou et al. [22] work demonstrating that in myopic patients with marked visual acuity differences (≥ 0.2 logMAR), dominant eyes were less myopic with shorter axial lengths. The structural differences identified in the present study have practical implications across multiple clinical domains. In cataract surgery, particularly when planning monovision or selecting premium intraocular lenses, the finding of shorter axial length and greater corneal thickness in dominant eyes may influence IOL power calculations and refractive target selection. For refractive surgery candidates, the greater CCT observed in dominant eyes could affect ablation depth planning and residual stromal bed thickness considerations. In glaucoma practice, the RNFL and GCC thickness differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes warrant attention, as physiological asymmetry related to ocular dominance may be misinterpreted as early glaucomatous damage; clinicians should consider incorporating dominance assessment when evaluating asymmetric nerve fiber layer findings.

Regarding neural mechanisms, ocular dominance likely manifests at multiple visual pathway sites. While ocular dominance columns are well-studied in animal models, [26] technological limitations prevent their visualization in humans. The retinal-level structural differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes may represent downstream effects of cortical organization, though causality remains unclear.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design prevents determination of causality between structural differences and dominance. Additionally, axial length-related magnification corrections were not applied to OCT measurements. Although the strict refractive error criteria and minimal intraindividual axial length differences (0.15 mm) likely rendered this effect negligible, future studies may consider implementing magnification correction algorithms for enhanced precision. The population of healthy adults with minimal refractive error may limit generalizability to pediatric populations or those with significant ametropia. Furthermore, ocular dominance was determined using only the hole-in-card test. Although this is a widely accepted and reliable method for assessing sighting dominance, future studies may benefit from employing multiple dominance testing methods to capture different aspects of ocular preference. Additionally, structural comparisons were not stratified by hand-eye laterality type (ipsilateral versus contralateral dominance). Given that the majority of our cohort exhibited a crossed dominance configuration, future studies with larger subgroups of uncrossed dominance are warranted to determine whether laterality type independently influences structural ocular asymmetry. While central corneal thickness difference was statistically significant after correction, with nasal RNFL thickness demonstrating the strongest trend toward significance, the absolute magnitudes of the observed differences are modest and individual measurements may not independently alter clinical decisions in most routine cases. Future research should include longitudinal studies tracking dominance development from childhood and investigate whether individuals with cross-lateralized preferences show different patterns of interhemispheric communication.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates structural differences between dominant and non-dominant eyes, with findings that both support and extend previous research. After correction for multiple comparisons, greater central corneal thickness in dominant eyes was confirmed as statistically significant, while nasal RNFL thickness demonstrated the strongest trend toward significance. A differential lateralization pattern was observed between ocular dominance groups, suggesting a potential association between ocular and manual preferences; however, this finding warrants cautious interpretation given the population-level prevalence of right-handedness. These findings have important implications for clinical practice, especially in refractive surgery planning, and for sports science where precise visuomotor coordination is essential. Future research should include longitudinal studies tracking dominance development from childhood and investigate whether individuals with cross-lateralized preferences show different patterns of interhemispheric communication.
