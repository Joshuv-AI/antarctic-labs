Predictive Value of Combined MEWS and NIPDS Scores for 24-Hour Clinical Deterioration in Emergency Observation Patients: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Xiangnan Xu, Yunhan Nie, Ping Chang, Wei Dai, Fan Jiang, Yuxi Zhang, Lu Chen, Zhen Wang, Hongfang Ye, Xiaojing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the predictive value of the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) combined with the Nurse Intuition Patient Deterioration Scale (NIPDS) for 24-hour deterioration among emergency observation patients.Patients and MethodsThis single-center prospective cohort study enrolled 2,415 emergency observation patients at a tertiary care hospital in Nanjing between May and December 2025. Patients underwent serial assessments using MEWS and NIPDS at 0, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 24 hours post-admission. The primary outcome was unplanned transfer to the resuscitation room or ICU or death within 24 hours. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of MEWS alone and the combined score for 24-hour deterioration.ResultsAmong the 2,415 patients, 287 (11.9%) experienced unplanned transfer to the resuscitation room or ICU or died within 24 hours. The combined score showed significantly better predictive performance than MEWS alone (AUC=0.859 vs 0.688, P<0.001) and improved overall risk reclassification (NRI=0.309, 95% CI: 0.245–0.373), although event-specific reclassification was modest and did not reach statistical significance (NRI=0.056, 95% CI: −0.011 to 0.123; P=0.103). Calibration was good (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.287), and decision curve analysis indicated a higher net benefit within the 5%–40% threshold range. Bootstrap validation confirmed model stability (optimism=0.0008).ConclusionThe combined MEWS and NIPDS score improves overall risk stratification compared with MEWS alone; however, clinical implementation should consider that the incremental reclassification value was driven primarily by correct down-classification of patients who did not deteriorate, while maintaining comparable sensitivity for those who did.

Introduction

The Emergency Observation Unit (EOU) serves as a critical bridge between rapid acute assessment and definitive inpatient treatment.1 Patients admitted to the EOU often present with uncertain diagnoses and masked clinical manifestations. Deterioration in these patients typically manifests as progressive abnormalities in physiological parameters, hemodynamic instability, and alterations in subjective symptoms and objective signs.2 Timely identification of this dynamic process poses a significant challenge for emergency medical staff.3 Studies have reported that the incidence of delayed treatment due to failure to recognize deterioration can be as high as 59.4%.4

Although the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) reduces the risk of missed diagnosis by quantifying vital signs, its inherent threshold-dependent limitations may lead to diagnostic blind spots during the physiological compensation phase.5,6 Recent large-scale validations have quantified these performance ceilings: Edelson et al reported an AUC of only 0.757 (95% CI: 0.750–0.764) for MEWS in over 360,000 patients.7 Haegdorens et al further demonstrated that integrating NIPDS with NEWS significantly improved predictive performance in general ward patients, with an AUROC difference of 0.136 (p = 0.038), sensitivity improving from 0.300 to 0.900, and greater net benefit across threshold probabilities of 0–30%. The combined approach (NEWS ≥5 OR NIPDS ≥5) further achieved the greatest net benefit when the number needed to treat exceeded 7.8 To address this limitation, Nurse intuition—a nonlinear clinical judgment rooted in clinical experience—can compensate for the blind spots of objective scoring by capturing precursor signals such as behavior and facial expressions.9,10 NIPDS standardizes this subjective judgment and has demonstrated incremental predictive value in hospitalized patients.11 However, given the rapid turnover of EOU patients and limited monitoring resources, the applicability of NIPDS in this context remains unverified.

To date, no studies have evaluated the dynamic, time-dependent predictive performance of combining objective early warning scores with nurse intuition in emergency observation settings. Improving the prediction of 24-hour clinical deterioration in the EOU extends beyond statistical modeling; it supports risk stratification for potential escalation to the resuscitation room or ICU, thereby guiding subsequent clinical decision-making. Early and accurate risk stratification thereby supports efforts to reduce both missed deterioration and unnecessary escalation of care, ultimately improving patient safety. This study employed a prospective observational cohort design to evaluate the association of MEWS and NIPDS with disease progression and to investigate the predictive performance of their combined use at different clinical time points in the EOU, aiming to provide an evidence-based approach for early detection and intervention.

Materials and Methods

Design and Setting

This prospective cohort study enrolled 2,415 observation patients admitted to the EOU of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Nanjing between May and December 2025. The hospital’s Emergency Department (ED), which recorded over 270,000 visits in 2024, oversees an EOU comprising 50 beds and managing approximately 5,000 observation admissions annually. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.12

Tools

MEWS Developed by Subbe et al, MEWS is an adaptation of the Early Warning Scoring System (EWS).13 It comprises five vital sign parameters: body temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, and level of consciousness. The body temperature parameter is scored from 0 to 2 points, while each of the other parameters is scored from 0 to 3 points, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 14 points. These indicators enable rapid early assessment of a patient’s condition within a short period; a higher score indicates a greater risk of disease progression. Detailed scoring criteria for MEWS are provided in Supplementary Material 1. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.826.14

NIPDS Developed by Haegdorens et al in 2023, NIPDS is a revised version of the intuitive scoring tool originally proposed by Romero-Brufau et al11,15 The scale consists of nine items, including inability to accurately express oneself, feeling uncomfortable, abnormal facial expression changes, drowsiness, and restlessness. A 3-point Likert scale is employed, where 0 indicates “absent,” 1 indicates “present,” and 2 indicates “clearly present,” resulting in a total score range of 0–18. The complete NIPDS items and scoring criteria are shown in Supplementary Material 1. Nurses use this scale to quickly assess subtle patient manifestations through intuitive observation, predicting deterioration events in both internal medicine and surgical patients. A total score ≥5 suggests a higher likelihood of patient deterioration. The scale also demonstrates good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.816.16

Procedure

Since 2014, MEWS has been the standard tool for clinical assessment of patients admitted to the EOU. Upon admission, patients were stratified by MEWS score for tiered management: red (MEWS ≥ 6, monitored every 15 minutes, immediate resuscitation), yellow (MEWS 4–5 or single variable ≥ 3, monitored every 30–60 minutes, physician notification), green (MEWS 1–3, monitored every 4 hours), and no alert (MEWS 0, routine monitoring every 8 hours). Body temperature was measured using a tympanic thermometer (Pro 6000, Welch Allyn, USA), while pulse rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure were continuously monitored via a patient monitor (VS-600, Mindray, China). Data were manually entered into a bedside mobile terminal for automated MEWS calculation and integration with the Hospital Information System (HIS). NIPDS assessments were documented on paper forms concurrent with MEWS evaluations.

Training and Inter-Rater Reliability of NIPDS Assessors

The NIPDS scoring system was introduced as a quality improvement initiative, with unified training (didactic instruction plus case simulation) conducted in January 2025. All nurses who performed NIPDS assessments (n = 12) were selected from among the EOU bedside nursing staff and completed a standardized training session prior to study commencement. These nurses had an average emergency department tenure of 5–10 years. The training, conducted over a single 4-hour session, comprised: a 1-hour didactic introduction to the nine NIPDS items and their behavioral anchors; 2 hours of supervised bedside practice using the Chinese version of NIPDS on non-study patients; and a 1-hour inter-rater reliability test.

Inter-rater reliability was evaluated by having three trained nurses—randomly selected from the 12 eligible assessors—independently evaluate a convenience sample of 15 observation patients who were not part of the main cohort, using the NIPDS. The two-way mixed-effects model (consistency definition) revealed an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.854 (95% CI: 0.694–0.943), indicating good inter-rater reliability for individual measurements.

Outcomes

Unplanned Transfer to the Resuscitation Room or ICU: This refers to patients who, during observation, had no pre-established plan for transfer to a resuscitation room or ICU but required such transfer due to clinical deterioration, necessitating advanced-level monitoring and treatment, or due to death.17

Death is defined as the absence of spontaneous circulation and respiration after 30 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) during observation, accompanied by a straight-line electrocardiogram (ECG), and a comprehensive clinical assessment by physicians declaring clinical death.18,19

The primary outcome was assessed by tracking the 24-hour outcomes of enrolled patients under observation. For patients experiencing more than one endpoint, the first-occurring event determined the classification. Meeting either criterion was deemed as achieving the primary outcome.

Blinding of Outcome Assessors

The primary outcome was ascertained by a dedicated research assistant who was independent from the scoring process. Outcome data were extracted retrospectively from the electronic health record using a pre-specified case report form that included only patient identifiers and outcome indicators; scoring data were not queried during extraction. The assessor had no involvement in clinical care or scoring during the observation period.

Statistical Analysis

Data were organized using Excel, and all statistical analyses were performed using the pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels, and scikit-learn packages in Python (version 3.9). For baseline characteristics, normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using chi-square tests.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent predictive value of MEWS and NIPDS for the primary outcome. Both scores were entered as continuous variables. A mutually adjusted model was constructed with MEWS and NIPDS entered simultaneously as the only two independent variables; no additional demographic or clinical covariates were included, to maintain model parsimony given the event-to-variable ratio. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding P-values were reported.

The discriminative capacity of the MEWS score and its combined score was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis method. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated through 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The AUC values of the MEWS score and composite score were compared using the DeLong test.20 Model calibration was performed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a P-value > 0.05 indicating good calibration.21 Calibration curves were plotted to visually demonstrate the consistency between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes.

The incremental predictive value of the combined score compared to the use of the MEWS scale alone was quantified by calculating the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).22 NRI was calculated separately for events and non-events, with the total NRI value reported. The 95% CIs for NRI and IDI were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The clinical utility of the scoring system was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA) at different threshold probabilities.23 The formula for net benefit was: Net benefit = (true positives / sample size) - (false positives / sample size) × (threshold / (1-threshold)), where true positives, false positives, and sample size were defined according to standard DCA methodology.

The temporal trends in predictive performance were evaluated by assessing both scoring systems at multiple predefined time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 16 h, and 24 h). ROC curves were generated for each interval, and the corresponding AUC values were compared.

The prediction model underwent internal validation using a 500-iteration bootstrap resampling approach.24 An optimism-corrected AUC was derived by subtracting the mean optimism value from the original AUC, and the 95% confidence interval of the bootstrap AUC was reported. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical Application Value of Combined Scoring

The combined score significantly improved risk reclassification, with an NRI of 0.309 (95% CI: 0.245–0.373, P < 0.001) and an integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) of 0.230 (95% CI: 0.195–0.265, P < 0.001) (Table 4). Notably, the NRI was substantially higher for patients without the primary outcome (0.253) than for those with it (0.056). Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the combined score offered greater net benefit than MEWS alone across the entire threshold probability range (0%–100%). Temporal Trends in Predictive Value of MEWS and Combined Score

MEWS and NIPDS scores were assessed at predefined intervals: 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 16 h, and 24 h. Patient numbers varied across time points due to study protocols and patient disposition: 2,415 patients at 0 h; 232 patients at 2 h; 1,648 patients at 4 h; 1,791 patients at 8 h; 1,151 patients at 16 h; and 1,046 patients at 24 h. Notably, the 8-hour cohort (n = 1,791) exceeded the 4-hour cohort (n = 1,648) because patients with an initial MEWS of 0—who were not reassessed at 2 or 4 hours per protocol—were first evaluated at the 8-hour interval. The reassessment rate at 2 h was low (9.6%, n=232) because institutional protocol restricted 2-hour reassessment to high-risk patients (defined as those with an initial MEWS ≥4 or any single item score of ≥3). Consequently, this protocol likely introduced selection bias when evaluating clinical deterioration at this specific time point. Because the same patient could be assessed at multiple time points, the events reported in each time-specific subset represent overlapping rather than mutually exclusive counts; consequently, the row-wise sum of events exceeds the total of 287 unique primary outcomes.

Predictive performance varied over time (Table 5, Figures 5 and 6). At most time points, the AUC of the combined score exceeded that of MEWS alone. Specifically, at 0 h, the combined score achieved an AUC of 0.859 (95% CI: 0.840–0.877), compared to 0.688 (95% CI: 0.660–0.716) for MEWS alone. However, at 8 h, the predictive performance of both scores became comparable, with MEWS showing a numerically higher AUC (0.900) than the combined score (0.895; ΔAUC = −0.005). This minimal difference, likely reflecting overlapping confidence intervals and dynamic case mix, does not suggest a true reversal in relative performance. Discussion

This single-center prospective observational study demonstrated that combining MEWS with NIPDS improved the dynamic prediction of unplanned transfer to the resuscitation room/ICU or death in emergency observation patients (ΔAUC = 0.171). Although MEWS-based early warning systems are widely adopted in ED worldwide, recent studies have shown limited predictive performance of MEWS in specific applications.6 Edelson et al reported an AUC of only 0.757 (95% CI: 0.750–0.764) in a cohort study of over 360,000 patients.7 Similarly, Golsheikhi et al observed a sensitivity of merely 61.4% (AUC 0.729) in COVID-19 emergency patients, suggesting that MEWS may lack sufficient sensitivity in acute respiratory diseases, with a consequent risk of missed diagnoses.26

Given the limitations of MEWS in these scenarios, integrating nurses’ intuitive judgment may provide supplementary information for risk identification. This study expanded the application of NIPDS from general wards (internal medicine, surgery, geriatrics), as validated by Haegdorens et al, to the EOU. In their original study, NIPDS alone demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.900 and a specificity of 0.920.11 By contrast, our combined score showed a sensitivity of 0.791 and a specificity of 0.778 in emergency observation patients, possibly reflecting the higher patient turnover and more insidious disease progression characteristic of this population. Recently, Ma et al reported that NIPDS alone achieved an AUC of only 0.753 in emergency settings (cut-off score: 6), significantly lower than the 0.859 observed with our combined score.27 These findings suggest that relying solely on nurses’ intuition has limited predictive efficacy in high-turnover emergency environments, necessitating the integration of objective physiological indicators to enhance the prediction of clinical deterioration.

This phenomenon can be explained by the Endsley situational awareness model (Level 1: perception).28 The model illustrates the dynamic complementarity of early cue detection (Level 1 perception, corresponding to NIPDS capturing subtle behavioral changes) and subsequent quantitative confirmation (corresponding to MEWS detecting overt physiological decompensation). During the pre-decompensation phase, NIPDS identifies risks by capturing subtle behavioral fluctuations, thereby compensating for the false reassurance provided by MEWS when physiological indicators remain within compensatory ranges. Following decompensation, MEWS rapidly regains sensitivity through quantitative assessment, validating the temporally asymmetric complementary advantages of these two approaches. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that once overt physiological decompensation is established, MEWS alone may perform comparably to the combined score; however, the marginal difference at 8 h (ΔAUC = −0.005) should be interpreted cautiously given the overlapping confidence intervals and the dynamic case mix at that time point.

This temporally asymmetric synergy—NIPDS contributing most during the pre-decompensation phase (0–4 h) and MEWS regaining prominence once physiological collapse is established—should inform the timing of assessments in clinical protocols. These temporal patterns highlight the importance of selecting the appropriate scoring system based on the specific clinical context and timing of assessment.

The NRI of 0.309 (95% CI: 0.245–0.373) indicates a net improvement in risk classification of 30.9 percentage points across the entire cohort; however, the event-specific NRI was modest (0.056, 95% CI: −0.011 to 0.123; P = 0.103) and did not reach statistical significance, indicating that the net improvement was driven predominantly by correct down-classification of patients who did not deteriorate (NRI_non-event = 0.253). This magnitude was consistent with the findings from the cohort study conducted by Haegdorens et al in Belgium.8 The clinical relevance of structured nurse intuition in deterioration prediction has also been discussed in independent commentary.29 Notably, Chen et al reported an AUC of 0.907 (cut-off score: 4) when validating NIPDS alone in hospitalized patients in China, which is significantly higher than that observed in our emergency population.16 This suggests that the predictive efficacy of NIPDS may be scenario-dependent: in relatively stable inpatient wards, nurses have more time to observe patient behavior patterns, whereas in the high-turnover environment of EOU, the sensitivity of the scale may be relatively reduced. Hincapie-Osorno et al demonstrated that a “one-size-fits-all” assessment strategy significantly reduced predictive accuracy by ignoring lesion heterogeneity in emergency infectious patients (n = 1,661), confirming the necessity of developing hybrid early warning systems for specific populations.30 This study specifically targeted the high-risk population of emergency observation patients and evaluated predictive performance at multiple time points across six intervals, which demonstrated the accuracy and stability of the combined score in this specific clinical context.

Limitations

Still, this study has several limitations. First, according to our center’s MEWS protocol, only patients with MEWS ≥4 or individual item scores of 3 underwent reassessment at the 2-hour time point, while those with MEWS <4 in the compensated phase were excluded from protocol-driven reassessment. This resulted in an assessment rate of only 9.6% (n = 232), reflecting a protocol-driven selection mechanism that may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of performance estimates to lower-risk patients in the compensated phase. Second, although the combined score demonstrated an AUC of 0.988 at 24 hours, only seven events occurred; this small sample size rendered the estimate unstable, warranting cautious interpretation. Third, as a single-center observational study, all patients underwent dual scoring assessments without actual clinical decision-making interventions. Thus, whether the statistical superiority of the combined score translates into improved clinical outcomes (eg., reduced unplanned ICU admission or mortality) remains unknown and requires validation in multicenter prospective interventional or stepped-wedge cluster randomized trials. Fourth, although prior sample size calculations were not performed, the ratio of 287 outcome events to 2 predictive variables (events per variable [EPV] = 143.5) substantially exceeded the recommended threshold of 10, suggesting model stability and a low risk of overfitting; nonetheless, this does not substitute for a formal a priori sample size calculation.31 Fifth, although the NIPDS assessors were experienced EOU bedside nurses with an average emergency department tenure of 5–10 years and demonstrated good inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.854), we did not stratify analyses by professional experience or years of clinical tenure. As intuitive pattern recognition is inherently expertise-dependent, this represents an unmeasured source of variability that may have influenced NIPDS performance. Future studies should evaluate whether predictive accuracy varies across novice, proficient, and expert nurses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that integrated NIPDS with MEWS significantly enhanced the predictive accuracy for unplanned transfer to the resuscitation room/ICU or death among emergency observation patients. The combined scoring system exhibited robust discriminative ability, good calibration, and potential for clinical utility, although temporal performance varied across assessment intervals and the 24-hour estimate was derived from only seven events. While overall risk reclassification was improved (NRI=0.309), the modest event-specific NRI (0.056, P=0.103) suggests that the incremental value may be driven primarily by correct down-classification of patients who do not deteriorate; therefore, clinical implementation should consider this asymmetric reclassification pattern. However, as a single-center observational study without interventional validation, these findings should be generalized cautiously, and both the protocol-driven non-reassessment of 90.4% of patients at 2 h and the extremely small event number at 24 h (n=7) represent additional methodological limitations. Future research should prioritize external validation across multicenter diverse populations to elucidate the differential predictive value of the combined score for outcomes of varying severity. Additionally, developing automated computational tools integrated with electronic health records is essential to reduce documentation burdens on nursing staff, thereby facilitating seamless clinical translation of this scoring system.
