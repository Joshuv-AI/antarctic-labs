APOE Genotypes Modulate the Relationship of Hypertension With Alzheimer’s Disease: Associations and Clues of Peripheral Mechanisms
Authors: Hong-Yu Wu, Jia-Hui Hou, Liang-Yu Huang, Lan Tan, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGene-environment interplay contributes to the heterogeneous etiology of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Hypertension (HTN) is a major modifiable vascular risk factor, but whether its association with AD differs across APOE genotypes remains unclear. We examined the interaction between HTN and APOE genotypes in relation to incident AD and explored plasma proteomic correlates as potential biological clues.MethodsLongitudinal data from 318,923 UK Biobank participants without dementia were analyzed; participants had a mean age of 56.24 years, an APOE ε4 frequency of 28.29%, and a median follow-up period of 13 years. Cox proportional hazards models with competing-risk adjustment for death were used to assess additive and multiplicative interactions between HTN and APOE genotypes. Plasma proteomic data from 34,141 participants, covering 2790 proteins, were further analyzed using mediation and bioinformatics approaches.ResultsHTN showed a significant multiplicative interaction with APOE ε4 status for incident AD (p < .001). The association between HTN and AD risk was strongest among APOE ε2 carriers, followed by ε33 carriers and ε4 carriers, with hazard ratios of 1.570, 1.213, and 1.129, respectively. Among APOE ε2 carriers, prosaposin and ganglioside GM2 activator significantly mediated the HTN-AD association, with mediation proportions of 10.46% and 3.37%, respectively. These proteins were enriched in sphingolipid metabolism and lysosomal function.ConclusionsThe association between HTN and incident AD varies across APOE genotype strata, with the strongest relative association observed among APOE ε2 carriers. These findings suggest genotype-dependent heterogeneity in vascular contributions to AD risk. Proteomic results should be interpreted as exploratory biological clues and require further validation.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia, has emerged as a major global health challenge. Currently, approximately 55 million individuals are affected worldwide, and this number is projected to rise to nearly 152 million by 2050 (1). The onset of AD is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Among genetic determinants, the APOE ε4 genotype is recognized as the strongest genetic risk factor, conferring a 2- to 6-fold increased risk of AD, whereas the APOE ε2 genotype exerts a protective effect (2). Beyond genetic predisposition, modifiable environmental exposures also play a crucial role in AD pathogenesis. As a major cardiovascular risk factor, hypertension (HTN) has been identified as an important and potentially modifiable environmental contributor to AD (3, 4, 5, 6), although its precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the interplay between genetic and environmental factors in AD development. Such interactions can manifest as additive or multiplicative effects wherein different risk factors exert cumulative or synergistic impacts on disease risk (7, 8, 9, 10). A comprehensive understanding of these interactive patterns is crucial for improving AD prediction, prevention, and precision intervention strategies. Although both HTN and the APOE ε4 genotype are strongly associated with AD risk, it remains uncertain whether HTN interacts with the APOE genotypes to influence AD susceptibility. Kivipelto et al. (11) reported that the coexistence of HTN, the APOE ε4 genotype, and hypercholesterolemia synergistically increases AD risk. Peila et al. (12) found that HTN was significantly associated with increased AD risk only among non-APOE ε4 carriers. The overall evidence base is still weak and limited by variations in population characteristics, sample size, and statistical modeling.

In addition, the underlying peripheral mechanisms by which HTN and its interplay with APOE ε4 were linked to AD occurrence remain largely unclear. Although AD has long been regarded as a disease confined to the brain, emerging evidence indicates that peripheral factors, such as immune and metabolic dysfunctions, play pivotal roles in its onset and progression. The peripheral blood proteomics offer a promising avenue for uncovering these mechanisms. Studies have shown that peripheral immune responses, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances can impact brain health and exacerbate neurodegenerative processes (13,14). Therefore, investigating the peripheral blood proteome may help identify candidate peripheral biological correlates and hypothesis-generating pathways related to the complex association among HTN, APOE genotype, and AD.

To address these gaps, in the current study, we utilized the large-scale longitudinal dataset from the UK Biobank (UKB) to 1) test whether and how the association between HTN and AD risk was modulated by APOE genotypes and 2) explore plasma proteomic correlates and hypothesis-generating pathways related to the association of HTN with AD and its heterogeneity across APOE genotype strata.

Methods and Materials

Study Population

The data were derived from the UKB, a large-scale prospective cohort involving approximately 500,000 participants ages 40 to 69. Participants were recruited between 2006 and 2010 across 22 centers in the United Kingdom. At the baseline stage, participants provided information regarding their social demographics, lifestyle, environmental exposures, and health status through touchscreen questionnaires and nurse-led oral interviews, and they underwent multiple measurements of physical indicators. Blood samples have been processed and properly preserved for further research, including plasma proteomic analysis and whole-genome genotyping (15). All participants provided electronic signed consent for their data to be used in health-related research. The UKB received ethical approval from the National Health Service North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (Reference No. 11/NW/0382) (16). For this study, we excluded participants who lacked APOE genotype data or who had no information on the incidence of AD or mortality follow-up. After exclusions, 318,923 participants were retained for the main analyses. History of HTN

The definition of HTN was 1) a self-reported doctor’s diagnosis of HTN or 2) self-reported current use of antihypertensive medication. Participants who did not meet any of these criteria were considered as control participants.

APOE Genotyping

APOE genotypes were determined based on rs429358 and rs7412 (17,18). APOE ε4–positive status was defined as carrying at least 1 ε4 genotype (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, or ε4/ε4), while individuals with ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, or ε3/ε3 genotypes were categorized as APOE ε4 negative. To assess ε2 and ε4 genotype-specific effects, individuals with the ε2/ε4 haplotype (2.52%) were excluded from subsequent analyses. Finally, participants were grouped into 3 groups: APOE ε2 group (ε2/ε2 and ε2/ε3), APOE ε3 group (ε3/ε3), and APOE ε4 group (ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4).

AD Diagnosis

In this study, AD cases were identified through data linkage with hospital inpatient records and the death registration system. This electronic linkage encompasses all primary and secondary health events recorded in the health care systems of England, Scotland, and Wales. The diagnosis date is determined by the earliest date of AD-related coding across various sources. According to the ICD criteria, AD corresponds to code 331.0 in ICD-9 and codes F00 and G30 in ICD-10.

Blood Proteomics

Plasma supernatant was extracted, and multiplexed proteomic assays were performed on approximately 55,000 baseline plasma samples using the Olink Explore 3072 platform, which applies dual-barcoded antibody-based proximity extension assay technology. A total of 2941 plasma protein analytes, corresponding to 2790 distinct proteins, were quantified, and protein levels were reported as normalized protein expression (NPX) on a log2 scale. Any data points that failed to meet quality control criteria were treated as missing NPX values (19,20). Finally, a subsample of 34,141 participants was included for the proteomic analyses.

Covariates

Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related characteristics known to be associated with either HTN (21) or AD risk based on previous literature were included as covariates (22). Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), with higher scores indicating greater material deprivation (23). Age, sex, and educational level were self-reported. Alcohol consumption was quantified in standard units per week based on touchscreen questionnaire responses regarding the typical volume of each type of alcoholic drink consumed. Obesity was determined based on body mass index, which was calculated from objectively measured weight and standing height during the physical examination. Comorbid conditions included anxiety, depression, cancer, diabetes, stroke, hyperlipidemia, and smoking status and were ascertained from a combination of self-reports and medical records.

Statistical Analyses

The characteristics of participants enrolled in this study were summarized according to whether they developed AD during the follow-up period. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, non-normally distributed continuous variables as median and IQR, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the t test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

First, Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to quantify the association between HTN and incident AD, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using the Schoenfeld residual method, and no violations were observed. The multiplicative interaction term was added to assess whether the effect of HTN on AD risk differed according to APOE genotypes, while additive interaction assessed whether the combined risk of HTN and APOE ε4 was greater than the sum of individual risks. We calculated 3 metrics for additive interaction: relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI). RERI measures the extra risk that comes from the additive interaction between 2 factors, beyond what would be expected from just adding their individual effects. AP represents a proportion that shows what part of the total risk is caused by the additive interaction. SI assesses whether the combined effect of 2 factors is stronger or weaker than the sum of their individual effects (24). Subgroup analyses were further stratified by APOE ε2 (22,23), APOE ε33, and APOE ε4 (25,26) status. To examine the potential impact of different follow-up durations on effect estimates, participants were stratified by follow-up time (≤5 years, 5–10 years, and >10 years). To account for potential competing risk due to death, sensitivity analyses were performed using Fine and Gray’s competing risks models. Moreover, we calculated the population attributable risk (PAR) of HTN on AD across different APOE genotypes in the total population and in subgroups stratified by age and sex.

Second, to explore candidate peripheral biological correlates of the association between HTN and AD, as well as its heterogeneity across APOE genotype strata, plasma proteomic analyses combined with exploratory mediation and bioinformatic analyses were conducted. Specifically, multiple linear regression models and Cox proportional hazards models were separately used to identify proteins associated with both HTN and incident AD, with effect estimates in the same direction. To control for multiple testing, the false discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method via the fdrtool package. Exploratory mediation analyses were then performed using the mediator package (version 4.5.0) to identify candidate proteins showing statistically significant indirect associations between HTN and AD. These analyses were intended to generate biological hypotheses rather than to establish causal mediation. Candidate indirect associations were retained only if all the following criteria were met: 1) HTN was significantly associated with protein levels, 2) HTN was significantly associated with AD risk, 3) protein levels were significantly associated with AD risk, and 4) the association between HTN and AD changed significantly after inclusion of the protein in the model. Indirect effects were estimated using 1000 bootstrap iterations, and significance was determined at pFDR < .05. To explore genotype-specific candidate pathways, we separately constructed and compared protein profiles among APOE ε2 and APOE ε4 carriers.

Finally, these selected significant proteins were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) for functional annotation, including Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analyses. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed, followed by k-means clustering, and the cluster with the highest number of enriched proteins was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.2).

For all abovementioned analyses, 2 statistical models were set unless otherwise specified, including model 1, which adjusted for age, sex, TDI, educational level, and APOE ε4 status. Model 2 was further adjusted for alcohol dependency, anxiety, cancer, obesity, depression, diabetes, smoking, stroke, and hyperlipidemia. A 2-tailed p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

The HTN-AD Association Differs Across APOE Genotype Strata

HTN was associated with a 31.9% higher risk of AD (HR = 1.319, 95% CI [1.161 to 1.499], p < .001) (Figure 2A). The association remained significant across different groups by APOE genotypes (Figure 2B). There was a significant multiplicative interaction between HTN and APOE genotypes (p < .001), but no significant additive interaction was found (p > .05). In APOE ε2 carriers, AD risk was increased by 57% (HR = 1.570, 95% CI [1.101 to 2.239], p = .013), while in APOE ε4 carriers, the risk was elevated by only 12.9% (HR = 1.129, 95% CI [1.014 to 1.257], p = .027) (Figure 2B). These findings were largely unchanged in Fine and Gray competing-risk models (p < 2.0 × 10−16) (Figure 2C) and when more confounding factors were included. Subgroup analyses by different lag time indicated that significant associations remained only when the follow-up duration exceeded 10 years (HR = 1.178, 95% CI [1.059 to 1.311], p < .005) (Table S1). The PAR calculation also suggested that the contribution of HTN to AD in the general population is higher among APOE ε2 carriers (28.9%) than among APOE ε4 carriers (16.8%). Exploratory Proteomic Correlates of the HTN-APOE Genotype Interaction on AD Risk

The interaction analyses revealed that the association between HTN and AD was stronger among individuals who carry the APOE ε2 genotype across midlife and late life. Accordingly, we further explored whether plasma protein profiles related to the HTN-AD association differed between APOE ε2 and APOE ε4 carriers (Figure 4A). After FDR correction, 7 proteins in the APOE ε2 group were significantly associated with both HTN and AD, with consistent directions of effect (Supplements 6 and 7). Exploratory mediation analyses identified 2 proteins, PSAP (p < .05, 10.46%) and GM2A (p < 2 × 10−16, 3.37%), which showed statistically significant indirect associations between HTN and AD among APOE ε2 carriers (Figures 4B, C and Supplement 8). PPI analyses suggested that these proteins were biologically interconnected (p = .002) and enriched in 5 pathways, among which sphingolipid metabolism and glycosphingolipid metabolism showed the highest enrichment scores (Supplement 9). Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study, we found that HTN was associated with an increased risk of incident AD and that this association varied across APOE genotype strata. The relative association between HTN and AD was most pronounced among APOE ε2 carriers, followed by ε33 carriers and ε4 carriers. Statistically, this heterogeneity was supported by a significant multiplicative interaction, whereas additive interaction was not statistically significant. Therefore, our findings primarily indicate APOE genotype–dependent differences in the relative association between HTN and AD rather than definitive evidence of synergistic effects on the absolute risk scale. Exploratory plasma proteomic analyses further identified several peripheral protein signals, particularly PSAP, GM2A, and BRK1, that may provide biological clues to this observed heterogeneity.

Our results showed that HTN was associated with a higher risk of incident AD and that this association differed across APOE genotype strata. PAR analysis suggested a larger proportion of AD cases statistically attributable to HTN among APOE ε2 carriers than among APOE ε4 carriers; however, this estimate should be interpreted as a population-level statistical indicator rather than direct evidence that blood pressure management should be prioritized only in a specific genotype group. The stronger HTN-associated relative risk among APOE ε2 carriers may appear counterintuitive, given that APOE ε2 is generally considered protective against AD. One plausible interpretation is that this protection operates predominantly through amyloid-related pathways—such as reduced amyloid-β (Aβ) production or enhanced clearance (27)—which may be less relevant, or even overwhelmed, when chronic vascular stress is superimposed. Importantly, APOE ε2 has been associated with fibrinoid necrosis of vascular walls and severe cerebral small vessel disease (28,29), indicating that APOE ε2 carriers may harbor specific vascular vulnerabilities that become unmasked or amplified under HTN. Consequently, the observed differential interaction should not be interpreted as a reversal of APOE ε2’s general protective effect but rather as context-dependent vulnerability: When vascular pathways—rather than amyloid pathways—predominate in driving AD risk, the residual susceptibility of ε2 carriers may be disproportionately shaped by blood pressure–related cerebrovascular injury and small-vessel disease burden. This notion is further supported by evidence that APOE genotype–dependent effects on AD risk are highly context specific across different environmental exposures (7). Future studies are needed to determine whether HTN systematically unmasks genotype-specific vascular vulnerability in ε2 carriers and clarify the underlying biological basis.

Our exploratory proteomic analyses suggested that sphingolipid metabolism and lysosomal biology may represent candidate pathways related to the stronger HTN-AD association that we observed among APOE ε2 carriers. In this framework, PSAP and GM2A were identified as candidate protein signals showing statistically significant indirect associations between HTN and AD rather than as confirmed mechanistic mediators. As a multifunctional precursor protein, PSAP exerts protective effects on dopaminergic neurons by promoting sphingolipid catabolism, maintaining lipid homeostasis, and activating immune responses (30, 31, 32). PSAP encodes the activator protein saposin B, which participates in sphingolipid metabolism and influences lysosomal function (33). Concurrently, it can upregulate IL-6 and oligodendrocyte-derived factors through a GPR37-dependent pathway, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammatory responses and leading to dopaminergic neurodegeneration and associated behavioral deficits (34). Furthermore, PSAP deficiency may induce cerebral white matter lesions, consequently increasing the risk of various neurodegenerative diseases, including AD (25,35). GM2A plays a critical role in ganglioside degradation, maintenance of neuronal structure, and regulation of lysosomal activity (36, 37, 38). This protein exhibits a distinct bidirectional regulatory characteristic: On the one hand, elevated GM2A levels may enhance its neurotoxic effects, damage neuronal structure, and accelerate the progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD (37); on the other hand, insufficient GM2A levels lead to abnormal degradation of gangliosides and glycosphingolipids, further impairing neuronal structure and exacerbating neuroinflammation (39). The accumulation of GM2 ganglioside can promote the aggregation of Aβ and α-synuclein to form toxic oligomers and plaques by activating downstream signaling pathways (such as protein kinase C and oxidative stress factors) (40). Previous studies have reported elevated GM2A levels in the brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with AD, which is broadly consistent with our exploratory plasma-based findings. Lysosomes serve as crucial signaling hubs within cells, regulating various fundamental cellular processes including membrane repair, energy metabolism, and inflammatory responses (41, 42, 43, 44). Lysosomal dysfunction promotes the abnormal propagation of Aβ and tau proteins between glial cells and neurons, thereby increasing the risk of multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and Parkinson’s disease (26,45, 46, 47, 48). However, because the proteomic data were observational and cross-sectional, these proteins should be considered hypothesis-generating candidates rather than confirmed mediators or therapeutic targets.

Another hypothesis-generating observation was that BRK1, a core component of the WAVE complex, was associated with APOE ε2 status among the candidate protein signals. This finding raises the possibility that WAVE complex–related cytoskeletal regulation may be relevant to genotype-specific vascular vulnerability, although this interpretation requires further validation. As an essential core subunit of the WAVE complex, BRK1 selectively stabilizes SCAR/WAVE proteins (such as SCAR2), thereby regulating the Arp2/3 complex–mediated actin nucleation process (49,50). As a cellular activity–dependent actin regulatory factor, WAVE participates in regulating various nervous system functions including synapse formation, neurite outgrowth, and neuronal activity (51). Experimental evidence suggests a possible mechanism: AICD produced through the amyloidogenic pathway can bind to the WAVE1 promotor and inhibit its transcription and protein expression. Experimental studies have demonstrated that WAVE1 interacts with and colocalizes with APP in the Golgi apparatus, and knocking down WAVE1 expression suppresses Aβ production (52). This finding is supported by publicly available datasets, which show downregulated expression of WASF1 (the human WAVE1 gene) in the brain tissue of patients with AD (53, 54, 55). These observations support the plausibility of a BRK1/WAVE-related hypothesis, but they do not establish BRK1 as a causal mediator of the HTN-APOE ε2 interaction. Longitudinal proteomic analyses, brain-tissue validation, and experimental perturbation studies are needed to determine whether BRK1/WAVE-complex biology has a direct role in vascularly driven AD risk. This study has several methodological strengths. First, the large sample size and long-term follow-up of a prospective cohort provided substantial statistical power for evaluating the association between HTN, APOE genotypes, and incident AD. Second, we used competing-risk regression to account for death as a competing event and applied FDR correction to reduce the probability of false positive findings in high-dimensional proteomic analyses. Third, by integrating epidemiologic analyses with plasma proteomics, mediation analysis, and pathway enrichment, we generated a coherent set of hypotheses regarding peripheral biological correlates of genotype-dependent vascular risk. However, several limitations should be emphasized. First, the main interaction signal relied largely on the APOE ε2 subgroup. Although the ε2-specific association was supported by the primary Cox models, competing-risk analyses, and analyses restricted to longer follow-up duration, APOE ε2 carriers represented a smaller genotype stratum and had fewer AD events than the full cohort. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as a consistent signal within the current dataset and a strong rationale for external validation rather than definitive evidence for genotype-specific clinical recommendations. Second, HTN was ascertained using self-reported physician diagnosis and/or antihypertensive medication use. We did not have detailed information on measured blood pressure levels, HTN duration, blood pressure control status, antihypertensive medication class, or longitudinal blood pressure trajectories. Therefore, exposure misclassification is possible, and we could not determine whether the observed associations differed according to HTN severity, treatment pattern, or long-term blood pressure control. Accordingly, the genotype-specific PAR estimates should be interpreted cautiously as population-level statistical indicators rather than as direct evidence for genotype-specific blood pressure management strategies. Third, AD ascertainment relied on electronic health records and ICD codes without systematic biomarker confirmation. Although this approach is reasonable for a large population-based cohort, diagnostic misclassification might have occurred. In particular, overlap with mixed dementia or vascular dementia cannot be excluded, which is especially relevant in a study focused on HTN and vascular risk. Fourth, plasma proteomic measurements were obtained at a single baseline time point, and the proteomic analyses were observational. Therefore, the identified protein signals cannot establish causal mediation or definitive biological mechanisms linking HTN, APOE genotype, and AD. Finally, the interaction was statistically evident on the multiplicative scale but not on the additive scale, which limits direct inference regarding absolute excess risk or clinical synergy. Independent cohorts, longitudinal blood pressure and proteomic measurements, biomarker-confirmed AD outcomes, and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm the robustness and biological relevance of these subgroup and proteomic findings.

Conclusions

This study provides population-based evidence that the association between HTN and incident AD differs across APOE genotype strata, with the strongest relative association observed among APOE ε2 carriers. Exploratory plasma proteomic analyses identified PSAP-, GM2A-, and BRK1-related signals that may reflect peripheral biological correlates involving sphingolipid and lysosomal biology as well as WAVE-complex pathways. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating and require validation in independent cohorts and mechanistic models before they can inform genotype-specific prevention strategies.
