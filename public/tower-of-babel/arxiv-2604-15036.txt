Poor man’s Majorana bound states in quantum dot based Kitaev chain coupled to a photonic cavity
Francesco Buonemani, Álvaro Gómez-León, Marco Schirò, Olesia Dmytruk

Abstract
Quantum dot based platforms offer a promising route towards realizing the Kitaev chain Hamiltonian hosting Majorana bound states (MBSs). Poor man’s MBSs arise in a two-site Kitaev chain when the parameters of the system are fine-tuned to the sweet spot. Based on our previous work Gómez-León et al. 2025 , we consider a microscopic model for the Kitaev chain based on quantum dots with proximity effect embedded in a photonic cavity. We find that the photon coupling in the microscopic model yields an effective Hamiltonian where the cavity affects the pairing term. However, we demonstrate that even in this case, it is possible to screen particle interactions and reach the sweet spot condition for the emergence of the poor man’s MBSs. In particular, we find that attractive particle interactions can be canceled for the cavity prepared in the zero-photon state, while repulsive ones can be screened with a cavity prepared in the one-photon state. Furthermore, in case of a large number of photons in the cavity, we find that the hopping amplitudes are suppressed resulting in a degenerate spectrum. This motivates the use of quantum light for engineering poor man’s MBSs with cavity embedding.

III
Effective Hamiltonian

To find the sweet spot condition for poor man’s MBSs, we derive a low-energy, effective electronic Hamiltonian. The path to do so is the following:

we express the total Hamiltonian in the photon number basis

Then we perform the adiabatic elimination of the photon subspace in the large detuning regime,

This procedure allows us to obtain the low-energy electronic effective Hamiltonian that depends on the parameters of the cavity.

IV.3
Sweet spot for
g\sqrt{n}=\text{const}

In this section, we consider the limit of a large number of photons in the cavity, recovering the classical light limit.
Following Ref.
Sentef et al. 2020
, we fix
g\sqrt{n}=\text{const}
that corresponds to a large photon number
n\rightarrow\infty
and small light-matter coupling
g\rightarrow 0
.
Starting from the light-matter Hamiltonian (
1
), we perform a unitary transformation
\hat{U}(t)=\exp{\left(-i\omega_{c}\,a^{\dagger}a\,t\right)}
. This transformation moves the system description to the interaction picture with respect to
\omega_{c}\,a^{\dagger}a
. The transformed Hamiltonian
H_{\text{el-ph}}^{\text{sc}}\left(t\right)=\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\left(H_{\text{el-ph}}-i\partial_{t}\right)\hat{U}(t)
is then periodic in time, and one can perform a high-energy frequency expansion. Therefore, considering the limit
n\to\infty
at the lowest order in the expansion with
2g\sqrt{n}\equiv\lambda=\text{const}
, we find the renormalized parameters (see Appendix
D
for more details)

\displaystyle t\to tJ_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2},

(26)

\displaystyle\Delta\to\Delta J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2},

(27)

\displaystyle t_{\text{so}}\to t_{\text{so}}J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2},

(28)

where
J_{0}\left(x\right)
is the zero-th Bessel function of the first kind.
We observe that hopping and pairing amplitudes are renormalized in the same way.
The sweet spot conditions become

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(\lambda)=0,

(29)

\displaystyle t_{\text{so}}\Delta\,e^{-g^{2}/2}J_{0}(\lambda)=t\epsilon,

(30)

where

\displaystyle\gamma(\lambda)=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}e^{-g^{2}}J_{0}^{2}(\lambda)+\epsilon^{2}},

(31)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda)-V_{\text{Z}}+\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}(\lambda)\left(\gamma(\lambda)+V_{\text{Z}}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\left[(t_{\text{so}}\epsilon)^{2}-(t\,\Delta e^{-g^{2}/2}J_{0}(\lambda))^{2}\right].

(32)

We find that even in the limit of a large number of photons, the cavity embedding can influence the sweet spot for poor man’s MBSs. However, in this regime, the zero-order Bessel function goes to zero, suppressing the hopping amplitudes and making the energy spectrum degenerate. We stress that the degeneracy of the energy levels originates from the dressing of the superconducting pairing,
\Delta
, with the photonic operators through the Peierls phase. Indeed, without the term
\Delta\,e^{-g^{2}/2}J_{0}(\lambda)
in Eq. (
30
), we recover the sweet spot conditions for the isolated effective Kitaev chain. Therefore, we conclude that it is beneficial to work with a cavity prepared in a state with a small number of photons to realize isolated poor man’s MBSs without suppressing the hopping amplitudes in the quantum dot-based platform.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we theoretically demonstrated the emergence of the poor man’s MBSs in an interacting quantum dot-superconductor array from cavity embedding. Starting with a microscopic model for two spinful quantum dots with local superconducting pairing coupled to a single mode cavity, we derived the effective Hamiltonian description for the problem and a set of sweet spot conditions for poor man’s MBSs that also depend on the parameters of the cavity. Moreover, we found that cavity embedding engineers effective electron interactions that could be used to compensate for the intrinsic particle interactions in the quantum dot-superconductor array. In particular, the cavity prepared in its ground state generates repulsive electron interactions, while cavity prepared in the state with one photon gives rise to attractive electron interactions. Finally, a large number of photons in the cavity suppresses the electron hopping highlighting the importance of working with quantum light for controlling properties of quantum system.

A possible extension of this work is to study the effect of the photon losses and quasiparticle poisoning on poor man’s MBSs.

(59)

\displaystyle H_{\text{C,odd}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\frac{t_{m,n}(g)}{2\gamma(n)}\left[\left(\gamma(n)-\epsilon\right)\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}-\left(\gamma(n)+\epsilon\right)\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}\right]+\frac{t_{m,n}(-g)}{2\gamma(n)}\left[\left(\gamma(n)-\epsilon\right)\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}-\left(\gamma(n)+\epsilon\right)\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}\right]Y^{n,m}.

(60)

Noticing that the Hamiltonian commutes with the fermionic parity operator, we have split the contribution to the
H_{\text{C}}
in the even and odd sectors. Finally, we redefine
E_{\alpha}\rightarrow~E_{\alpha}~+~2\left[(t_{\text{so}}\,\epsilon)^{2}-(\Delta(n)\,t)^{2}\right]/\left[\gamma(n)^{2}\left(\gamma(n)+V_{\text{Z}}\right)\right]
to include the contribution to the on-site energy coming from the second order in the projection method expansion.

Appendix C
Derivation of an effective electronic Hamiltonian in a large detuning regime

To derive an effective electronic Hamiltonian from Eq. (
10
),
we adiabatically eliminate the
n\pm 1
photonic subspace using the projector’s method in the RWA approximation
Feshbach 1958
;
Gómez-León et al. 2025
. We define the projectors
P=\ket{n}\bra{n}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\text{el}}
and
Q=\ket{n-1}\bra{n-1}+\ket{n+1}\bra{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\text{el}}
, with
\mathbb{I}_{\text{el}}
identity for the fermionic Fock space.
The terms
\left[\Delta_{m,n}(g)+\Delta_{m,n}(-g)\right]
for
m=n\pm 1
sum up to zero, therefore
H_{\text{QD}}^{\text{OD}}
has zero contribution.

We can then analyze separately the electronic even and odd sector, defined by:

\displaystyle\text{Even states}\qquad\ket{00},\,\ket{11}=\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\ket{00}

(61)

\displaystyle\text{Odd states}\qquad\ket{10}=\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\ket{0},\,\ket{01}=\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\ket{0}.

(62)

In the even sector, the effective Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{\text{even}}^{D}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n}\Big[\frac{t_{\text{so}}\Delta(n)}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}(g^{2})e^{-g^{2}/2}(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}+\text{h.c.})

\displaystyle+n\omega_{c}\Big]Y^{n,n}+H^{D}_{\text{QD}},

(63)

\displaystyle H_{\text{even}}^{OD}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{t_{\text{so}}}{2\gamma(n)}\left(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}+\text{h.c.}\right)\Big(\Delta_{m,n}(g)

\displaystyle+\Delta_{m,n}(-g)\Big)Y^{m,n},

(64)

where
H_{\text{even}}^{D}
is diagonal in the photon number basis and plays the role of
H_{0}
in the projector method, and

H_{\text{even}}^{OD}
is the perturbation corresponding to
H_{1}
.

We note that the even subspace is coupled to the cavity, see Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
), resulting in the renormalization of the superconducting paring of the isolated QD Hamiltonian (
34
) as
\Delta\rightarrow\Delta_{m,n}(g)
.

The terms
\left[\Delta_{m,n}(g)+\Delta_{m,n}(-g)\right]
for
m=n\pm 1
sum up to zero. Therefore, the relevant contribution to the adiabatic elimination of the photonic subspaces comes only from
PH_{\text{eff},\text{E}}^{0}P
. Consequently, the effective electronic Hamiltonian in the even subspace after the adiabatic elimination becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{even}}(n)

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{t_{\text{so}}\Delta(n)}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}(g^{2})e^{-g^{2}/2}(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}^{\dagger})+n\omega_{c}\right]

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(n)\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}\right)+2\left(\epsilon-\gamma(n)\right)

(65)

Continuing the analysis for the odd electronic many-body subspace, we obtain for the Hamiltonian in the photonic number basis

\displaystyle H_{\text{odd}}^{\text{D}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n}\frac{\epsilon\,te^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}(g^{2})\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}+\text{h.c.}\right)Y^{n,n}+H^{D}_{\text{QD}}

(66)

\displaystyle H_{\text{odd}}^{OD}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\neq n}\Big\{\frac{t_{m,n}(g)}{2\gamma(n)}\Big[\left(\gamma(n)-\epsilon\right)\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\gamma(n)+\epsilon\right)\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}\Big]+\text{h.c.}\Big\}Y^{n,m}.

(67)

Here,
H_{\text{odd}}^{\text{D}}
is the diagonal part in the photon number basis,

H_{\text{odd}}^{OD}
is the off-diagonal part in the photon number basis.
To apply the projector method, we identify
H_{\text{odd}}^{\text{D}}\equiv H_{0}
as free Hamiltonian and
H_{\text{odd}}^{OD}\equiv H_{1}
as perturbation.

At the first order in the projection
PH_{\text{eff,O}}^{0}P
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{odd}}^{\text{1st}}(n)

\displaystyle=n\omega_{\text{c}}+E_{\alpha}(n)(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2})+2\left(\epsilon+\gamma(n)\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon\,te^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}(g^{2})\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}\right).

(68)

To analyze the second order, we specify the basis in the Fock space made by the tensor product of the photonic Fock space and the electronic Fock space. Since the projectors defined at the beginning are relative only to the photonic subspaces
n,\,n\pm 1
, a valid basis choice, associated with the relative eigenenergies, is the following one:

\displaystyle\ket{\sigma_{x}^{\pm},n}

\displaystyle=\ket{\sigma_{x}^{\pm}}\otimes\ket{n}

(69)

\displaystyle\ket{\sigma_{x}^{\pm},n\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\ket{\sigma_{x}^{\pm}}\otimes\ket{n\pm 1},

(70)

where
\ket{\sigma_{x}^{\pm}}=1/2\left(\ket{1,0}\pm\ket{0,1}\right)
.
In this basis, the second order in the projection using RWA reduces to:

\displaystyle-\sum_{a,b}\frac{PH_{1}QH_{1}QP}{E_{b}-E_{a}}=

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{1}}\bra{\tilde{1}}\left(\frac{\left|{\Braket{\tilde{4}|H_{\text{eff,O}}^{1}|\tilde{1}}}^{2}\right|}{\tilde{E}_{1}-\tilde{E_{4}}}+\frac{\left|\Braket{\tilde{6}|H_{\text{eff,O}}^{1}|\tilde{1}}^{2}\right|}{\tilde{E}_{1}-\tilde{E}_{6}}\right)

\displaystyle+\ket{\tilde{2}}\bra{\tilde{2}}\left(\frac{\left|{\Braket{\tilde{3}|H_{\text{eff,O}}^{1}|\tilde{2}}}^{2}\right|}{\tilde{E}_{2}-\tilde{E}_{3}}+\frac{\left|{\Braket{\tilde{5}|H_{\text{eff,O}}^{1}|\tilde{2}}}^{2}\right|}{\tilde{E}_{2}-\tilde{E}_{5}}\right),

where we can notice that the non-zero matrix elements are only the ones with the same electronic part that differ of
\pm 1
in the number of photons.
After a bit of algebra, the second-order Hamiltonian in the projection, written in terms of the electronic operators
\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2}
, yields

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{odd}}^{\text{2nd}}(n)=(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2})\left[\frac{n\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\tilde{\kappa}^{2}_{n+1}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}^{2}}\right]+

\displaystyle\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}+\text{h.c.}\right)\left[\frac{n\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{n}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\tilde{\kappa}^{2}_{n+1}\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}^{2}}\right],

(71)

where we define the following frequencies

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{t\epsilon}{\gamma(n)}e^{-g^{2}/2}\left[L_{n-1}(g^{2})+L_{n}(g^{2})\right],

(72)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{n}

\displaystyle=t\,g\,e^{-g^{2}/2}\,{}_{1}F_{1}(1-n,2,g^{2}).

(73)

Adding the effective electronic Hamiltonian in the even subspace
\tilde{H}_{\text{even}}(n)
(
65
), the first order effective electronic Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\text{odd}}^{\text{1st}}(n)
(
68
) and the second order effective electronic Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\text{odd}}^{\text{2nd}}(n)
(
71
) of the odd subspace, we arrive at

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{eff}}(n)=\tilde{H}_{\text{eff,E}}(n)+\tilde{H}_{\text{eff,O}}^{\text{1st}}(n)+\tilde{H}_{\text{eff,O}}^{\text{2nd}}(n)

(74)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{eff}}\left(n\right)=\tilde{U}\left(n\right)\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{2}c_{1}\right)-\tilde{\mu}\left(n\right)\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{2}\right)+\tilde{C}(n)

\displaystyle+\tilde{\Delta}\left(n\right)\left(c_{1}c_{2}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1}^{\dagger}\right)-\tilde{t}\left(n\right)\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1}\right),

(75)

where we write
\tilde{H}_{\text{eff}}(n)
using the mapping
\alpha_{1}\rightarrow c_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}\rightarrow c_{2}
. The coefficients in Eq. (

) are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{U}\left(n\right)=-2\left[\frac{n\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\tilde{\kappa}^{2}_{n+1}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}^{2}}\right],

(76)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\left(n\right)=-E_{\alpha}(n)-\frac{n\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\tilde{\kappa}^{2}_{n+1}\omega_{c}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}^{2}},

(77)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(n)=n\omega_{c}+2\left(\epsilon-\gamma(n)\right),

(78)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}\left(n\right)=\frac{t_{so}\Delta(n)e^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}\left(g^{2}\right),

(79)

\displaystyle\tilde{t}\left(n\right)=-\frac{n\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{n}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n}^{2}}+\frac{(n+1)\tilde{\kappa}^{2}_{n+1}\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}}{\omega_{c}^{2}-\tilde{\omega}_{n+1}^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon te^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(n)}L_{n}\left(g^{2}\right).

(80)

The effective Hamiltonian (

Appendix D
Derivation of an effective light-matter Hamiltonian in the limit of
g\sqrt{n}=\text{const}

In this section, we provide details on the derivation of the effective Hamiltonian in the limit of
g\sqrt{n}=\text{const}
. To do so, we follow the approach developed in Ref.
Sentef et al. 2020
. Starting with the total microscopic electron-photon Hamiltonian
H_{\text{total}}
given by Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
),
we note that
H_{\text{total}}
is already in the Floquet form. However, to apply the Van Vleck high frequency expansion
Eckardt and Anisimovas 2015
, we need to make the time dependence explicit. Therefore, we derive the Hamiltonian in the rotating frame by performing the unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}=e^{-i\omega_{\text{c}}a^{\dagger}a\,t}
,

H(t)=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}H\mathcal{U}-i\,\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\partial_{t}{\mathcal{U}},

(81)

resulting in

\displaystyle H_{\text{QD}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\left(\epsilon+V_{\text{Z}}\right)n_{i\uparrow}+\left(\epsilon-V_{\text{Z}}\right)n_{i\downarrow}\right]

\displaystyle+\Delta\left(e^{iA}d^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}+e^{-iA}d^{\dagger}_{2\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{2\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle H_{\text{C}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\,\in\left\{\uparrow,\downarrow\right\}}t\left(e^{iA}d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}d_{2\sigma}+\text{h.c.}\right)

\displaystyle+t_{\text{so}}\left[e^{iA}\left(d^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}d_{2\downarrow}-d^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}d_{2\uparrow}\right)+\text{h.c.}\right],

(83)

where
A=g(a^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{c}t}+ae^{-i\omega_{c}t})
.
Now it is straightforward to see that the total Hamiltonian is periodic, with a period
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{c}}
. Therefore, the first order in high frequency expansion is given by the time average of the Hamiltonian, which in our case reduces to calculating the time average of the Peierls phases. Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, we arrive at

\frac{\omega_{c}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\omega_{c}}{2\pi}}dte^{iA}=\int_{0}^{1}dxe^{{iga^{\dagger}}e^{2i\pi x}}e^{{iga}e^{-2i\pi x}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}},

(84)

where
x=\omega_{\text{c}}t/(2\pi)

Next, we expand the exponential

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{g^{2}}{2}}\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(ig)^{k+k^{\prime}}}{k!k^{\prime}!}{a^{\dagger}}^{k}{a^{\dagger}}^{k^{\prime}}e^{2i\pi x(k-k^{\prime})}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{g^{2}}{2}}\sum_{k}\frac{(ig)^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}{a^{\dagger}}^{k}{a^{\dagger}}^{k}.

(85)

and then evaluate it in a photon number state basis
\ket{n}
:

\braket{n|e^{iA}|n}=e^{-g^{2}/2}\sum_{n=0}^{n}\frac{(ig)^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.

(86)

Since we are interested in the limit for
n\rightarrow\infty
we can do the approximation
\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\approx n^{k}
and arrive at the following form

\braket{n|e^{iA}|n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}e^{-g^{2}/2}J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right),

(87)

where
J_{0}
is the zero-order Bessel function of the first kind and
\lambda=2g\sqrt{n}
. Hence, both
t_{\text{so}}
,
t
, and
\Delta
terms become renormalized as

\displaystyle t\to tJ_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}

(88)

\displaystyle\Delta\to\Delta J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}

(89)

\displaystyle t_{\text{so}}\to t_{\text{so}}J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2},

(90)

which leads to the following time-independent electronic Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\text{QD}}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\epsilon+V_{\text{Z}}\right)n_{i\uparrow}+\left(\epsilon-V_{\text{Z}}\right)n_{i\downarrow}

\displaystyle+\Delta J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}\left(d^{\dagger}_{i\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{i\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle H_{\text{C}}=\sum_{\sigma}tJ_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}\left(d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}d_{2\sigma}+\text{h.c.}\right)

\displaystyle+t_{\text{so}}J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}\left(d^{\dagger}_{1\uparrow}d_{2\downarrow}-d^{\dagger}_{1\downarrow}d_{2\uparrow}+\text{h.c.}\right).

(92)

We diagonalize the resulting renormalized Hamiltonian given by Eqs. (
91
) and (
92
)) by introducing the Bogoliubov operators

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma(\lambda)}}\left(\sqrt{\gamma(\lambda)-\epsilon}\,d^{\dagger}_{i\downarrow}+\sqrt{\gamma(\lambda)+\epsilon}\,d_{i\uparrow}\right),

(93)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma(\lambda)}}\left(\sqrt{\gamma(\lambda)-\epsilon}\,d^{\dagger}_{i\uparrow}-\sqrt{\gamma(\lambda)+\epsilon}\,d_{i\downarrow}\right),

(94)

and corresponding eigenvalues
E_{\alpha}(\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda)-V_{\text{Z}}
and
E_{\beta}(\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda)+V_{\text{Z}}
. Here,
\gamma(\lambda)=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}e^{-g^{2}}J_{0}^{2}(\lambda)+\epsilon^{2}}
.

Then, as in Appendix
B
, we can eliminate the high-energy electronic degree of freedom through the projector
P=~\left(1-\beta_{1}^{\dagger}\beta_{1}\right)\left(1-\beta_{2}^{\dagger}\beta_{2}\right)
. For the effective electronic Hamiltonian in the limit of
\lambda=\text{const}
, we obtain

\displaystyle H_{\text{QD}}(\lambda)=E_{\alpha}(\lambda)\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}\right)+2\left[\epsilon-\gamma(\lambda)\right],

(95)

\displaystyle H_{\text{even}}(\lambda)=-\frac{t_{\text{so}}\Delta(\lambda)e^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(\lambda)}L_{n}(g^{2})(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}+\text{h.c.})

(96)

\displaystyle H_{\text{odd}}(\lambda)=-\frac{\epsilon\,te^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(\lambda)}L_{n}(g^{2})\left(\alpha_{1}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{2}^{\dagger}\alpha_{2}\right),

(97)

where
\Delta(\lambda)=\Delta J_{0}\!\left(\lambda\right)e^{-g^{2}/2}
and

E_{\alpha}(\lambda)
=
\gamma(\lambda)-V_{\text{Z}}+2\left[(t_{\text{so}}\epsilon)^{2}-(t\,\Delta e^{-g^{2}/2}J_{0}(\lambda))^{2}\right]/\left[\gamma^{2}(\lambda)\left(\gamma(\lambda)+V_{\text{Z}}\right)\right]
.

Finally, we can bring the above Hamiltonian in the same form used in Eq. (
75
). Adopting the mapping
\alpha_{1}\rightarrow c_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}\rightarrow c_{2}
, with
c_{i}(c_{i}^{\dagger})
being spinless annihilation (creation) fermionic operator, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{eff}}\left(\lambda\right)=-\tilde{\mu}\left(\lambda\right)\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{2}\right)+\tilde{C}(\lambda)

\displaystyle+\tilde{\Delta}\left(\lambda\right)\left(c_{1}c_{2}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1}^{\dagger}\right)-\tilde{t}\left(\lambda\right)\left(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}+c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{1}\right).

(98)

Here,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\left(\lambda\right)=-E_{\alpha}(\lambda),

(99)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(\lambda)=2\left(\epsilon-\gamma(\lambda)\right),

(100)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}\left(\lambda\right)=\frac{t_{so}\Delta(\lambda)e^{-g^{2}/2}}{\gamma(\lambda)}L_{n}\left(g^{2}\right),

(101)

\displaystyle\tilde{t}\left(\lambda\right)=\frac{\epsilon t}{\gamma(\lambda)}e^{-g^{2}/2}L_{n}\left(g^{2}\right).

(102)

Diagonalizing Eq. (
98
), one finds the energy spectrum of the effective Hamiltonian in the large photon regime. We note that for
\lambda\gg 1
the zero-order Bessel function
J_{0}(\lambda)\approx 0
is suppressed leading to a degenerate energy spectrum.
