Abstract
Signed distance fields are often used in multiphysics simulations to track material interfaces. We
present a simple methodology based on the fast sweeping method to generate the exact signed
distance from triangular meshes and linear paths on Cartesian grids. The methodology propagates
the closest primitive to the boundary to the rest of the domain following the characteristics. A
local upwind criterion is used to decide between the new and existing closest primitive at each
grid point while capturing the correct sign of the global function. The methodology has optimal
computational complexity and runs efficiently in distributed-memory architectures. We include 2D
and 3D test cases along with a resolution study up to 0.512 trillion zones and 1, 000 computer cores.
The solution strategy can also be applied to other types of meshes or collections of primitives.

1 Introduction
Implicit functions are routinely used in computational physics to represent material interfaces or
track dynamic fronts since they can naturally deal with topology changes [1]. Many applications
also require derivative information such as vector normals [2, 3] or even local surface curvature [4]
which motivates the use of a subclass of implicit functions: the signed distance function (SDF).
 In continuum multiphysics simulations, it is common practice to use Computer-Aided Design
(CAD) software to build engineering components that are later exported as triangulated meshes—
e.g. stereolithography (STL) files. Different families of volumetric methods have been proposed
in the literature to generate the exact SDF from triangular meshes on an underlying discrete grid.
Hierarchical approaches are common in the computer graphics community where a tree of bounding
volumes is associated with every triangle [5, 6]. Alternatively, characteristic methods based on
Mauch’s Characteristic/Scan Conversion (CSC) algorithm [7] offer linear computational complexity
and they are amenable to implementation in graphics hardware [8, 9]. Another popular class of
methods calculates the unsigned distance field approximately by integrating the Eikonal equation
numerically [10–12]. However, a common difficulty with these approaches is the determination of
the sign of the SDF which requires specialized techniques such as ray casting or angle-weighted
pseudonormals [13].
 Additional challenges arise when the physical components to be modeled are generated using
additive manufacturing. The triangular mesh must be sliced and converted into sequential ma-
chine instructions (G-code) that define the final toolpath that the 3D printer follows. The final
geometry will contain a complicated internal lattice not captured by the STL file that often needs
to be explicitly modeled in solid mechanics and fluid dynamics applications—e.g., switchable high
explosives use their additive-manufactured lattices to activate/deactivate the detonability of the
charge [14]. If the machine toolpath is made of thousands or even millions of linear strand seg-
ments, the computational complexity of the SDF algorithm could lead to massive bottlenecks even
for coarse grids.
 In this work, we are interested in computing exact signed distance fields in rectilinear grids from
manifolds composed of large collections of simple geometry primitives—e.g., piecewise linear curves
from toolpaths and triangular meshes from STL files. The methodology presented is inspired by the
seminal work of Mauch [7] and Tsai [15] using closest points. It has linear computational complexity
and can run efficiently in large distributed-memory CPU architectures using a parallel version of
the fast sweeping method [16]. Pseudocode using the Message Passing Interface (MPI) is provided
to assist users with the implementation.

3 Methodology
 Consider the boundary of the object Γ to be made of a collection of individual primitives γ0 , γ1 , . . . , γM ,
 each with a known analytic signed distance function ψ0 , ψ1 , . . . , ψM . We require this set of primitives
 to be connected forming a closed surface with consistent normals if their individual dimensionality
 is n − 1, e.g., triangular meshes in 3D. If dim(γm ) < n − 1 such as piecewise-linear toolpaths or
 point clouds, we relax this requirement.
 As previously suggested by Steinhoff et al. [19] and Tsai [15], we aim to establish a mapping
 between each point ⃗x and its nearest primitive γm ∈ Γ. We refer to this mapping as the closest
 primitive transform, which corresponds to the Voronoi diagram in the special case where the prim-
 itives are points [20]. Since distance information propagates along straight characteristic paths,
 efficient algorithms such as the fast sweeping method (FSM) can be used to propagate the closest
 primitive γm away from Γ given the appropriate upwinding criteria.
 But the closest primitive at a point ⃗x is not always unique and for primitives with dim(γm ) =
 n − 1, this can lead to the appearance of spurious protrusions and cavities in the boundary even
 when Γ is closed. This can be better understood from Fig. 1 which shows two adjacent planar
 polygons γA and γB contained in planes PA and PB with outward-pointing normals n̂A and n̂B
 respectively. We also adopt the convention that these unit normals specify the sign of ψA and ψB .
 The characteristic lines defined by ∇ψ form a rarefaction fan centered at the γA –γB intersection.
 The region bounded between the C− and C+ characteristics has one single closest point ⃗x∗ but two
 possible closest primitives since ⃗x∗ is in both γA and γB . If we assign γA in the (C− , PB ) region—or
 γB in the (PA , C+ ) region—the signed distance will be negative and Γ will have a spurious protrusion.
 Similarly, a cavity would form if we revert the sign of the normals. In the interior region (ψ ≤ 0),
 the characteristic lines intersect forming a shock corresponding to the medial axis Γs . At each point
⃗xs ∈ Γs , the closest primitive field is multi-valued but it is well defined on each side. In this case,
 the choice of primitive along Γs can be made arbitrarily since both polygons γA and γB have the
 same ψ(⃗xs ) value.

3.1 Discretization and initialization
Let us consider a finite spatial domain discretized using a uniform Cartesian grid with dimensions
Nx × Ny × Nz and grid spacing ∆x = ∆y = ∆z. Using an MPI communicator with an attached
Cartesian topology, the computational domain is split across the available ranks to create spatial
subdomains [xL , xR ]×[yL , yR ]×[zL , zR ]. We are interested in evaluating ψ(⃗x) to machine precision at
xi = xL +i∆x, yj = yL +j∆y, zk = zL +k∆z with i, j, k ∈ Z+ given a collection of M primitives that
satisfy the requirements described previously. The primitives are read from a file, such as STL or
G-code, and stored in a 1D shared-memory array of length M (see MPI_Win_allocate_shared).
That is, each node with shared memory stores a single copy of the array but all the cores have access
to all the primitives. The motivation behind this approach rather than domain-decomposing the
array of primitives as well is that one does not know a priori the range of influence of each primitive
which can span across many spatial subdomains. On the other hand, having all the cores reading
the file and storing the array would cause a memory overflow for most practical problems.
 The next step is initializing the Nx × Ny × Nz solution arrays corresponding to the signed
distance function and the closest primitive field near the boundary Γ. The closest primitive field
is a 3D array of unsigned integers whose value maps to the index in the 1D shared-memory array
of primitives. Both the closest primitive field and the array of primitives provide the complete
solution but it is recommended to also store the signed distance during the calculation to avoid
repetitive floating-point operations. We use two types of initialization mechanisms depending on
the dimensionality of the primitives:
Line algorithm: For G-code files, we use the 3D extension of Bresenham’s line algorithm [21] to
initialize the computational grid. The line algorithm has linear complexity with the number of grid
points along the major axis. If the slicer and printer support helical arcs or splines, we decompose
each of these commands into a series of linear moves. A more sophisticated alternative would be to
use different primitives provided that a distance function is available for each.
Bounding box: For convex polygons such as triangles, we loop over the M triangles in the array
of primitives and calculate the indexes of the 3D box that encloses the polygon:

3.2 Parallel fast sweeping method
The key idea is to propagate the closest primitive and signed distance information from Γ to the
rest of the domain following the characteristics. The two main ingredients of the fast sweeping
method (FSM) are a consistent upwind criterion and Gauss-Seidel sweeps that cover all the possible
directions of information propagation [11].
 For the present methodology applied to polygon meshes, the upwind criterion is provided by
Eq. (5) where the γA is the existing polygon at (i,j,k) and γB corresponds to each of the polygons
in the stencil. To prevent issues with subgrid primitives, it is also recommended to check discrete
continuity of the signed distance field before accepting a new polygon. Algorithm 1 includes pseu-
docode for this upwind criterion to assist with implementation. This criterion determines the sign
of the signed distance function without the need for angle-weighted pseudo normals or ray tracing.
Computing the SDF to the plane as ϕ = n̂ · (⃗x − p⃗1 ) is typically required to obtain the SDF to the
primitive itself. Therefore, there are no additional floating point operations required but simply the
need to store the local ϕ as an intermediate variable. For primitives with dim(γm ) < n − 1 such as
lines and points, the upwind criterion can be reduced to simply choosing the polygon in the stencil
with the smallest distance at (xi , yj , zk ).
 In n dimensions, the characteristics are divided into 2n groups that are systematically covered by
alternating the looping directions. This is extremely simple in serial but solution causality requires
special care when the computational domain is decomposed across multiple processes. Figure 2
depicts an extension of the parallel FSM described by Tro et al. [16] using ghost points and blocking
MPI communication where the domain decomposition is performed in all three spatial coordinates.
To ensure that a problem in both serial and parallel requires the same number of sweeps, the sweep
in each subdomain can only start once it receives the information from the relevant neighboring
process. This enforces the causality principle on which the FSM is based but it also results in idle
processors until the information reaches the relevant subdomain.

coordinate direction as in Tro et al. [16]. However, such decomposition is not practical for most
multiphysics applications. Similar optimizations can be made regarding the sweeeping ordering
using the modulo operator. Alternative methodologies such as the hybrid massively parallel fast
sweeping method of Detrixhe and Gibou [22] could also be applied.

the grid. As the size of the primitives approaches ∆x, a small number of additional sweeps are
typically required to reach full convergence due to the discrete nature of the closest primitive field.
Note that once the size of the primitives drops below ∆x, some of these primitives will not appear
on the grid during the initialization step and the resulting SDF will only be exact for the reduced
triangular mesh but approximate for the original input one.

4 Results
We present a series of 2D and 3D test cases to demonstrate the methodology and evaluate its
performance. The first case shown in Fig. 3 is adapted from Mauch [7] and consists of ten polygons
with outward-pointing normals that form a closed surface. We initialize the signed distance and
closest primitive fields near the boundary and use the first polygon as the background value to avoid
unnecessary checks in the algorithm. As previously mentioned, the closest primitive field (polygon
ID) is an array of unsigned integers that marks the range of influence of each polygon. Figure 3
shows the evolution of this field after each directional sweep of the fast sweeping method. The
solution is locally updated using the upwind criterion and fully converges after three sweeps—the
fourth sweep is redundant in this particular problem but not in general.
 The next test cases are the Stanford bunny (336 triangles) and a turbocharger compression wheel
(96, 474 triangles) both given as triangle meshes in the STL file format. Figure 4 shows the signed
distance field with values ψ = 0, 5, 10, 15 mm generated from the triangle mesh directly (STL file)
and from the toolpath (G-code file) produced after slicing the triangle mesh. The G-code strand
diameter is 2 mm and the underlying Cartesian grid has ∆x = 0.25 mm spacing. This strand
diameter was simply chosen to assist with the visualization of the toolpath. For the turbocharger
example, there is a large disparity in triangle sizes with many smaller than the grid spacing.
 Fianlly, we use an icosahedron (20 triangles) to assess the algorithm efficiency and parallel

5 Conclusions
We describe a simple methodology to compute the exact signed distance function (SDF) from
triangular meshes and segmented paths on Cartesian grids. The method uses a parallel version
of the fast sweeping method to propagate the closest primitives near the boundary to the rest of
the domain. The primary advantage is that the methodology does not require accounting for the
faces, edges, and vertices but rather each polygon represents a single primitive. A local upwind
criterion is then used to decide between the new and existing closest primitive at each grid point.
This criterion captures the correct sign of the SDF by construction and avoids having to compute
angle-weighted pseudonormals or additional ray tracing steps. Since only the primitives that are
resolved on the grid are initialized, the method has optimal complexity to the number of grid points
and runs efficiently in distributed-memory CPU architectures. This also has the disadvantage that
the SDF becomes formally approximate rather than exact if the primitives become smaller than
the grid size. To demonstrate the capabilities of the methodology, we include 2D and 3D test
cases along with a resolution study up to 0.512 trillion zones and 1,000 computer cores. The main
limitation of the parallel FSM comes from load balancing since the information causality leads to
idle processors during sweeps. This affects the strong scaling of the method but allows for extremely
large computational grids that require distributed-memory architectures.
