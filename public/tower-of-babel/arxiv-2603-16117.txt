Ciphertext-Policy 𝖠𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{ABE} for 𝖭𝖢 1 \mathsf{NC}^{1} Circuits with Constant-Size Ciphertexts from Succinct 𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{LWE}
Jiaqi Liu, Yuanyi Zhang, Fang-Wei Fu

Abstract
We construct a lattice-based ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption ( 𝖢𝖯 ​ - ​ 𝖠𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE} ) scheme for 𝖭𝖢 1 \mathsf{NC}^{1} access policies with constant-size ciphertexts. Let λ \lambda be the security parameter. For an 𝖭𝖢 1 \mathsf{NC}^{1} circuit of depth d d and size s s on ℓ \ell -bit inputs, our scheme has the public-key and ciphertext sizes O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) (independent of d d ), and secret-key size O ⁡ ( ℓ ) O(\ell) , where the O ⁡ ( ⋅ ) O(\cdot) hides poly ⁡ ( λ ) \poly(\lambda) factors. As an application, we obtain a broadcast encryption scheme for N N users with ciphertext size poly ⁡ ( λ ) \poly(\lambda) independent of log ⁡ N \log N and key sizes poly ⁡ ( λ , log ⁡ N ) \poly(\lambda,\log N) . Our construction is selectively secure in the standard model under the poly ⁡ ( λ ) \poly(\lambda) -succinct 𝖫𝖶𝖤 \mathsf{LWE} assumption introduced by Wee (CRYPTO 2024).



I
Introduction

In a traditional public-key encryption scheme, only a user who possesses the secret key corresponding to the public key used for encryption can decrypt the ciphertext. As a generalization of public-key encryption,
attribute-based encryption
(
\mathsf{ABE}
) realizes fine-grained access control over encrypted data. There are two types of
\mathsf{ABE}
schemes:
ciphertext-policy

\mathsf{ABE}
(
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
) and
key-policy

\mathsf{ABE}
(
\mathsf{KP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
). In a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme, each secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathbf{x}}
is associated with an attribute
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, and each ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}_{f}
is associated with an access policy
f
that specifies which attribute vectors are authorized to decrypt it. In contrast, in a
\mathsf{KP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme, each secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{f}
is associated with an access policy
f
, and each ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}_{\mathbf{x}}
is associated with an attribute
\mathbf{x}
. In both cases, decryption succeeds only if the attribute satisfies the access policy, i.e.,
f(\mathbf{x})=0
; otherwise, the decryptor learns no information about the underlying plaintext.

The concept of
\mathsf{ABE}
was first proposed independently by Sahai and Waters
[
24
]
and by Goyal et al.
[
20
]
. Since then,
\mathsf{ABE}
has become a fundamental cryptographic primitive, and many concrete schemes have been proposed. A large fraction of existing
\mathsf{ABE}
constructions rely on assumptions over bilinear groups. However, due to known quantum attacks on bilinear-group-based assumptions, there has been substantial interest in constructing schemes from alternative assumptions. In particular, many subsequent
\mathsf{ABE}
schemes base their security on the
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption or its variants, which are believed to be resistant to quantum attacks. In
[
2
,
11
,
17
,
9
,
19
,
13
,
25
,
4
,
15
]
, a series of
\mathsf{KP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
schemes were proposed, all based on the
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption.

A generic transformation can convert a
\mathsf{KP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme into a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
one via a universal circuit. However, this transformation typically leads to significantly worse parameters. To optimize the parameters of
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme, Agrawal and Yamada
[
5
]
proposed a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for all
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
circuits with ciphertext size independent of the circuit size
s
, relying on the
\mathsf{LWE}
and generic bilinear groups. Datta et al.
[
16
]
proposed a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for all
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
circuits via linear secret sharing schemes, relying on the hardness of the
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption. Subsequently, there are many
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
schemes relying on the nonstandard
\mathsf{LWE}
-related assumptions. Wee
[
27
]
proposed a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme based on the evasive
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption and tensor
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption. Hsieh et al.
[
21
]
presented a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme based on the (structured) evasive
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption. Agrawal et al.
[
3
]
proposed a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for all Turing machines based on the circular evasive
\mathsf{LWE}
and tensor
\mathsf{LWE}
. Wee
[
28
,
29
]
presents
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for all circuits based on the weaker
\poly(\lambda,d)
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption with ciphertext size independent of the attribute length
\ell
.

I-A

Our results

We list our results as below:

1. In this work, we present a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for access policies represented by
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
circuits. For circuits on
\ell
-bit inputs with depth
d
and size
s
(hence
s=\poly(\ell)
and
d=\log(\ell)
) where
s,d,\ell
are fixed at setup. We construct a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme with parameters:

|\mathsf{pk}|=O(s),\quad|\mathsf{sk}|=O(\ell),\quad|\mathsf{ct}|=O(1),

where the notation
O(\cdot)
hides
\poly(\lambda)
factors. It proves to be selectively secure against unbounded collusions. The security relies on the
\poly(\lambda)
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
assumptions with sub-exponential modulus-to-noise ratio.

•

The scheme we construct is a
succinct

\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
. Namely, the ciphertext size in our scheme relies only on the security parameter
\lambda
and independent of the size
s
, the input length
\ell
, and the depth
d
, which is asymptotically optimal up to
\poly(\lambda)
factors.

•

The size of the public parameters scales with the circuit size
s
. Though it is seen as not competitive compared with previous schemes (many schemes have public key size
O(\ell)
, such as
[
14
,
27
,
21
]
), we observe that in the public parameters, all elements are sampled uniformly except for the public parameters used in the succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
instance (i.e.,
\mathsf{pp}=(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{T})
), which is of size
\poly(\lambda)
. Therefore, we can generate the purely uniform part with a pseudorandom generator instead. We can then compress the size of the public parameters into
O(1)
size.

•

The bound on the size of
\mathsf{sk}
is not tight. It depends on the number of attributes associated with the secret key rather than strictly on
\ell
. In scenarios where the users possesses only
O(1)
attributes, the secret-key size can be reduced to
O(1)
.

2.
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
for
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
circuits gives a broadcast encryption for
N=\ell
users as a special case. In the broadcast encryption, we can use a circuit of size
O(N\log N)
and depth
O(\log N)
to check the membership of the users. We obtain a broadcast encryption scheme for
N
users with parameters

|\mathsf{pk}|=N\cdot\poly(\lambda,\log N),\quad|\mathsf{sk}|=\poly(\lambda,\log N),\quad|\mathsf{ct}|=\poly(\lambda).

By using a pseudorandom generator to compress the public parameter, we can reduce
|\mathsf{pk}|
to
\poly(\lambda)
. Since
|\mathsf{pk}|+|\mathsf{ct}|+|\mathsf{sk}|=\poly(\lambda,\log N)
, we obtain an optimal broadcast encryption (under
\poly(\lambda)
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption).

3. In the proof of the selective security of our construction, we use the technique of using the trapdoor
\mathbf{T}
for
[\mathbf{I}_{\ell}\otimes\mathbf{B}\mid\mathbf{W}]
in the
\ell
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption to generate other trapdoors for the matrix of the form
[\mathbf{I}_{\ell^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbf{B}\mid\mathbf{W}^{\prime}]
, where
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is statistically close to uniform.

DKW21 scheme.
Let
\mathbb{U}
denote the attribute universe, let

, and identify

•

The public key is given by

, where
\mathbf{A}_{u},\mathbf{Q}_{u}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
and

•

The master secret key is given by

, where
\mathsf{td}_{\mathbf{A}_{u}}
is the trapdoor for the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{u}

\mathbf{A}_{u}

•

Given an attribute set

U

\hat{\mathbf{t}}\leftarrow\chi^{m-1}
, where
\chi
is some error distribution, set
\mathbf{t}=(1,\hat{\mathbf{t}}^{\top})^{\top}

\mathbf{k}_{u}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}_{u}^{-1}(-\mathbf{Q}_{u}\mathbf{t})
. Then the secret key is given by
(\{\mathbf{k}_{u}\}_{u\in U},\mathbf{t})
.

•

To encrypt a message
\mathsf{msg}\in\{0,1\}
under the access structure determined by matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\ell\times s_{\max}}
, the ciphertext is given as follows: Assume that
\rho
is an injective function that maps the row indices of
\mathbf{M}
to the attribute. First sample
\mathbf{s}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{s_{\max}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
. The ciphertext components are computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{1,i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\uwave{\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho(i)}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=M_{i,1}(\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{m-1})+\sum_{j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}}M_{i,j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}+\uwave{\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{\rho(i)}},

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=\uwave{\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}+\mathsf{msg}\cdot\lceil q/2\rfloor.

Then the ciphertext is given as
(\{\mathbf{c}_{1,i}\}_{i\in[\ell]},\{\mathbf{c}_{2,i}\}_{i\in[\ell]},c_{3})
.

•

Suppose that the secret key is associated with an authorized attribute set. Let
I
be the set of row indices of
\mathbf{M}
corresponding to the attributes associated with the secret key. Let
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\{0,1\}
be the reconstruction coefficients in the
\mathsf{LSSS}
scheme with respect to the matrix
\mathbf{M}
. The decryption algorithm proceeds as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}(\mathbf{c}_{1,i}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{\rho(i)}+\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\top}\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}(\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho(i)}\mathbf{k}_{\rho(i)})+\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}M_{i,1}(\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y},0,\ldots,0)\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in I,j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}}w_{i}M_{i,j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{t}+\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{\rho(i)}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}M_{i,1}\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y}+\sum_{i\in I,j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}}w_{i}M_{i,j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{t}

Since the reconstruction coefficients
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are such that
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}M_{i,1}=1
and
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}M_{i,j}=0
for
j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}
. Hence
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}(\mathbf{c}_{1,i}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{\rho(i)}+\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\top}\mathbf{t})\approx\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
, and we can recover
\mathsf{msg}
by subtracting it from
c_{3}
and decoding.

From the above description, the ciphertext size scales with
\ell
, i.e., the number of rows of the matrix
\mathbf{M}
. We expect the ciphertext size to be independent of
\ell
, and we use the matrix commitment technique in
[
29
]
to compress the ciphertext. Precisely, we replace the matrices
\{\mathbf{A}_{u}\}_{u\in\mathbb{U}}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
with a matrix
\mathbf{B}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
of the same size. Instead of generating
\mathbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{s_{\max}}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}

\mathbf{B}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{s_{\max}}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
. We modify the second component of the ciphertext as follows:

\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\prime}=M_{i,1}(\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{y},0,\ldots,0)+\sum_{j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}}M_{i,j}\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}+\uwave{\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{\rho(i)}}

for our commitment purposes (with this modification, the scheme remains selectively secure in the same model). To compress the
\{\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\prime}\}
, for
i\in[\ell]
, let

\mathbf{U}_{\rho(i)}\leftarrow M_{i,1}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{0})+\sum_{j\in\{2,\ldots,s_{\max}\}}M_{i,j}\mathbf{B}_{j}+\mathbf{Q}_{\rho(i)}

and for
u\in[N]\setminus\rho([\ell])
we sample
\mathbf{U}_{u}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
. For
i\in[\ell]
, we have
\mathbf{c}_{2,i}^{\prime}\approx\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\rho(i)}
. Then we commit to
[\mathbf{U}_{1}\mid\cdots\mathbf{U}_{N}]\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times mN}

\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}

\mathbf{C}
contains partial information about all
\mathbf{U}_{u}
.

II-B

Static Security

We prove that our scheme is selectively secure under the
2m^{2}
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
assumption in
V-B
. We achieve our goal by defining a sequence of hybrid games. The first hybrid game corresponds to the real game as defined in
16
, while the last hybrid game is independent of the messages, where the advantage of the adversary is exactly 0. By arguing each adjacent pair of the hybrid games is indistinguishable, the adversary in the real game can win the game with negligible advantage.

More precisely, in the reduction, we attempt to prove the ciphertext components
\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},c_{3}
are pseudorandom, since they resemble
\mathsf{LWE}
samples intuitively. However, in the key query phase, the challenger uses the trapdoors
\mathsf{td}_{\mathbf{B}}
for
\mathbf{B}
to generate corresponding secret keys, which may result in the additional information leakage of the
\mathsf{LWE}
samples. To overcome this problem, we notice that using the matrix
\mathbf{T}
in the public parameters in the
2m^{2}
-succinct
\mathsf{LWE}
instance, we can construct trapdoors for the matrix of the form

Discrete Gaussians.
Let

represent the centered
discrete Gaussian distribution
over
\mathbb{Z}
with standard deviation
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
(e.g.,
[
7

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\mod q

When

is applied to a matrix, it is understood as being applied independently to each column of the matrix.

The following lemma (e.g.,
[
23
]
) shows that for a given Gaussian width parameter

, the probability of a vector drawn from the discrete Gaussian distribution having norm greater than

is negligible.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\lambda
be a security parameter and
\sigma=\sigma(\lambda)
be a Gaussian width parameter. Then for all polynomials
n=n(\lambda)
, there exists a negligible function
\negl(\lambda)
such that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}

\Pr\left[\|\mathbf{v}\|>\sqrt{n}\sigma:\mathbf{v}\leftarrow D_{\mathbb{Z},\sigma}^{n}\right]=\negl(\lambda).

Smudging Lemma.
In the following, we present the standard smudging lemma, which formalizes the intuition that sufficiently large standard deviation can “smudge out” small perturbations, making the resulting distributions statistically indistinguishable.

Lemma 2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\lambda
be a security parameter, and let
e\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
|e|\leq B
. Suppose that
\sigma\geq B\cdot\lambda^{\omega(1)}
. Then, the following two distributions are statistically indistinguishable:

\{z:z\leftarrow D_{\mathbb{Z},\sigma}\}\,\text{ and }\,\{z+e:z\leftarrow D_{\mathbb{Z},\sigma}\}.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
\chi,\sigma\geq\chi_{0}
, where

is the polynomial given in Lemma

Proof.

First we consider the matrix

in two games. In Game

Since

outputs a matrix that is statistically close to a uniform one together with a

in both games is statistically indistinguishable from each other.

Next we consider the matrix

(6)

\mathsf{H}_{1}

\mathsf{H}_{0}

\mathsf{H}_{0}

\mathsf{LSSS}
and
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
for
\mathsf{LSSS}
-realizable Access Structures

In this section, we first introduce the notion of Linear Secret Sharing Scheme (
\mathsf{LSSS}
) which we use to describe the access structure in our construction, and the ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption (
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
).

Definition 13

(Access Structure,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
S
be a set and
2^{S}
be the power set of
S
, i.e., the collection of all subsets of
S
. An
access structure
on
S
is a set
\mathbb{A}\subseteq 2^{S}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
, consisting of some non-empty subsets of
S
. A subset
A\in 2^{S}
is called
authorized
if
A\in\mathbb{A}
, and
unauthorized
otherwise.

An access structure is called
monotone
if it satisfies the following condition: for all subsets
B,C\in 2^{S}
, if
B\in\mathbb{A}
and
B\subseteq C
, then
C\in\mathbb{A}
. In other words, adding more elements to an authorized subset does not invalidate its authorization.

In the following, we present a result given by
[
16
]
, which shows that there exists a non-monotone
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{LSSS}
for access structure represented by a Boolean formula.

Lemma 14

(
[
16
]
)
.

For any access structure
\mathbb{A}
which is described by a Boolean formula, there is a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for generating
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\ell\times d}
and
\rho:[\ell]\to[2n]
satisfying that

1.

(\mathbf{M},\rho)
yields a non-monotone
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{LSSS}
for
\mathbb{A}
, namely

•

For
S\subseteq[n]
, let
\hat{S}=S\cup\{i\in[n+1,\ldots,2n]\mid i-n\notin S\}\subseteq[2n]
. Let
\mathbf{M}_{\hat{S}}
be the submatrix that consists of all the rows of
\mathbf{M}
with row indices in
\rho^{-1}(\hat{S})
. For any authorized set of parties
S\subseteq[n]
, there is a linear combination of the rows of
\mathbf{M}_{\hat{S}}
that gives
(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}
. Moreover, the coefficients in this linear combination are from
\{0,1\}
.

•

For any unauthorized set of parties
S\subseteq[n]
, there is no linear combination of the rows of
\mathbf{M}_{\hat{S}}
that gives
(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}
. Moreover, there exists a vector
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}
, such that its first component
d_{1}=1
and
\mathbf{M}_{\hat{S}}\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{0}
.

2.

For any unauthorized set of parties
S\subseteq[n]
, all of the rows of
\mathbf{M}_{\hat{S}}
are linearly independent.

Here we present the notion of ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption (
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
) for
\mathsf{LSSS}
.

Definition 15

(
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
for
\mathsf{LSSS}
)
.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter, and
n=n(\lambda),q=q(\lambda)
be lattice parameters. A
ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption (
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
) scheme
for access structures captured by a
linear secret sharing scheme (
\mathsf{LSSS}
)
over some finite field
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is defined as four efficient algorithms
\Pi_{\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}}=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{Keygen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
. These algorithms proceed as follows:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},\mathbb{U})\rightarrow(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{msk})
: The setup algorithm takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and an attribute universe
\mathbb{U}
, and outputs the public parameters
\mathsf{pk}
and a master secret key
\mathsf{msk}
. We assume that
\mathsf{pk}
specifies the attribute universe
\mathbb{U}
.

•

\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{msk},U)\rightarrow\mathsf{sk}_{U}
: The key generation algorithm takes as input the master secret key
\mathsf{msk}
and an attribute set
U\subseteq\mathbb{U}
. It outputs a secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{U}
associated with attribute set
U
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{U}
implicitly contains the attribute set
U
.

•

\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{msg},(\mathbf{M},\rho))\rightarrow\mathsf{ct}
: The encryption algorithm takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pk}
, a message
\mathsf{msg}
, and an
\mathsf{LSSS}
access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
such that
\mathbf{M}
is a matrix over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and
\rho
is a row-labeling function that assigns each row of
\mathbf{M}
to an attribute in
\mathbb{U}
. It outputs a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
.

•

\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk},(\mathbf{M},\rho),\mathsf{ct})\rightarrow\mathsf{msg}^{\prime}/\perp
: The decryption algorithm takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pk}
, a secret key
\mathsf{sk}
, an access structure
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
, and a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
. It outputs a value
\mathsf{msg}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
when the attributes in
U
satisfy the
\mathsf{LSSS}
structure
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
, i.e., the vector
(1,0,\ldots,0)
lies in the span of the rows of
\mathbf{M}
whose labels are in
U
. Otherwise, the decryption outputs
\perp
, indicating the failure of the decryption.

Correctness.
A
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
scheme for
\mathsf{LSSS}
-realizable access structures is said to be
correct
if for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, every attribute universe
\mathbb{U}
, every message
\mathsf{msg}
, every
\mathsf{LSSS}
access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
, and every attribute set
U\subseteq\mathbb{U}
that satisfies the access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
, it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathsf{msg}^{\prime}=\mathsf{msg}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{msk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda},\mathbb{U});\\
\mathsf{sk}_{U}\leftarrow\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{msk},U);\\
\mathsf{ct}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{msg},(\mathbf{M},\rho));\\
\mathsf{msg}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk}_{U},(\mathbf{M},\rho),\mathsf{ct})\end{array}\right.\right]\geq 1-\negl(\lambda).

Selective Security.

Here we define the selective security of a
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}
for
\mathsf{LSSS}
-realizable access structures by a game between a challenger and an adversary. For a security parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, the game proceeds as follows:

•

Setup Phase:
The adversary first sends an
\mathsf{LSSS}
access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
. The challenger runs algorithm
(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{msk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}
and sends the public parameters
\mathsf{pk}
to the adversary.

•

Key Query Phase 1:
The adversary can make polynomially many secret-key queries to the challenger. Each secret-key query is of the form
U\subseteq\mathbb{U}
such that
U
does not satisfy the
\mathsf{LSSS}
access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
. The challenger then runs
\mathsf{sk}_{U}\leftarrow\mathsf{Keygen}(\mathsf{msk},U)
and sends
\mathsf{sk}_{U}
to the adversary.

•

Challenge Phase:
The challenger flips a coin
b\xleftarrow{\$}\{0,1\}
and encrypts the message
b
with respect to the
\mathsf{LSSS}
access policy
(\mathbf{M},\rho)
as
\mathsf{ct}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},b,(\mathbf{M},\rho))
. Then the challenger sends the ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
to the adversary.

•

Key Query Phase 2:
This phase is the same as Key Query Phase 1.

•

Guess Phase:
The adversary outputs a guess
b^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
for the value of
b
.

The
advantage
of the adversary
\mathcal{A}
in this game is defined by

\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}}(\lambda):=|\mathrm{Pr}[b=b^{\prime}]-1/2|.

(Selective security for
\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}

\mathsf{LSSS}

\mathsf{LSSS}

\mathsf{PPT}

\mathcal{A}

.

\mathsf{CP}\text{-}\mathsf{ABE}

Lemma 19
.

We have
\mathsf{H}_{0}\equiv\mathsf{H}_{1}
.

Proof.

The only difference between Games
\mathsf{H}_{0}
and
\mathsf{H}_{1}
is the way the
matrices
\mathbf{A}
,
\{\mathbf{U}_{u}\}_{u\in[N]}
and

\{\mathbf{Q}_{u}\}_{u\in[N]}
are generated. In Game
\mathsf{H}_{0}
, the matrices
\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{Q}_{u}\}_{u\in[N]}
are generated uniformly and independently at random. Then, for all
i\in[\ell]
, it sets
\mathbf{U}_{\rho(i)}\leftarrow M_{i,1}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{0})+\sum_{2\leq j\leq s_{\max}}M_{i,j}\mathbf{B}_{j}+\mathbf{Q}_{\rho(i)}
, and for all
u\in[N]\setminus\rho([\ell])
, it sets
\mathbf{U}_{u}\leftarrow\mathbf{Q}_{u}+\mathbf{D}_{u}
. In Game
\mathsf{H}_{1}
, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is obtained by first sampling

\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
and then setting

\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}
, where
\mathbf{C}
is a matrix independent of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
(
\mathbf{C}
is not sent to the adversary, and in both games it is computed as
\mathsf{Com^{mx}}(\mathsf{pp},\mathbf{U})
from the same distribution of
\mathbf{U}
). Then the challenger instead samples
\{\mathbf{U}_{u}\}_{u\in[N]}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times(m+1)}
and sets
\mathbf{Q}_{u}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}_{u}-(M_{\rho^{-1}(u),1}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{0})+\sum_{2\leq j\leq s_{\max}}M_{\rho^{-1}(u),j}\mathbf{B}_{j})
for all
u\in\rho([\ell])
and
\mathbf{Q}_{u}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}_{u}-\mathbf{D}_{u}
for all
u\in[N]\setminus\rho([\ell])
.

In Game
\mathsf{H}_{1}

the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is obtained as a fixed shift of the uniform matrix

\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
, so
\mathbf{A}
itself is uniform and independent of

\{\mathbf{U}_{u}\}
and
\{\mathbf{Q}_{u}\}
, exactly as in Game
\mathsf{H}_{0}
.
For each
u\in\rho([\ell])
, the pair

(\mathbf{Q}_{u},\mathbf{U}_{u})
in Game
\mathsf{H}_{0}
is uniformly
distributed over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times(m+1)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times(m+1)}
subject to

\mathbf{U}_{u}-\mathbf{Q}_{u}=M_{\rho^{-1}(u),1}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{0})+\sum_{2\leq j\leq s_{\max}}M_{\rho^{-1}(u),j}\mathbf{B}_{j},

and exactly the same relation holds in Game
\mathsf{H}_{1}
. Similarly, for
each
u\in[N]\setminus\rho([\ell])
, the triple

(\mathbf{Q}_{u},\mathbf{D}_{u},\mathbf{U}_{u})
is uniformly distributed subject to

\mathbf{U}_{u}=\mathbf{Q}_{u}+\mathbf{D}_{u}
in both games. Hence the joint distribution of

(\mathbf{A},\{\mathbf{Q}_{u}\},\{\mathbf{U}_{u}\})
is exactly the same in
Games
\mathsf{H}_{0}
and
\mathsf{H}_{1}
.
Observe that all the changes between Game
\mathsf{H}_{0}
and
\mathsf{H}_{1}
are merely syntactic. Since all subsequent uses of these matrices in the query phase and challenge phase are identical in both games, we conclude
that
\mathsf{H}_{0}\equiv\mathsf{H}_{1}
.
∎

\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{u}\leftarrow\mathsf{SamplePre}(\mathbf{B},\mathsf{td}_{\mathbf{B}},-\mathbf{d}_{u}^{\prime}-\mathbf{D}_{u}^{\prime}\hat{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{B}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u},\chi_{1}).

in the same way as above, we obtain that the distribution of
\mathbf{k}_{u}
is statistically indistinguishable from that of
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{k}_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}+\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{u}+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{Z}_{u}\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}-\mathbf{d}_{u}^{\prime}-\mathbf{D}_{u}^{\prime}\hat{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{B}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Z}_{u}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}-\mathbf{D}_{u}\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{B}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{u}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{U}_{u}\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{u}+\mathbf{Q}_{u})\mathbf{t},

which matches the key equation (

In summary, for every attribute

\mathbf{k}_{u}
in Game
\mathsf{H}_{3}
is statistically
indistinguishable from a sample from
D_{\mathbb{Z},\chi_{s}}^{m}
(and hence from that of

\mathbf{k}_{u}
in Game
\mathsf{H}_{2}
), and the key equation

\mathbf{B}\mathbf{k}_{u}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{u}+\mathbf{Q}_{u})\mathbf{t}
holds in both games.

Since the adversary makes only polynomially many secret-key queries,
a union bound shows that the joint distribution of all secret-key
answers in
\mathsf{H}_{2}
and
\mathsf{H}_{3}
is statistically indistinguishable.
All other parts of the two games are identical. Therefore,

\mathsf{H}_{2}\overset{s}{\approx}\mathsf{H}_{3}
.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Suppose that
\chi>\sqrt{m}\chi_{1}\cdot\lambda^{\omega(1)}

\mathsf{H}_{3}\overset{s}{\approx}\mathsf{H}_{4}

Proof.

This lemma follows directly from the noise-smudging lemma (Lemma
2
). The only difference between Games
\mathsf{H}_{3}
and
\mathsf{H}_{4}
lies in the way that the challenger generates the vector component
\mathbf{t}

\mathsf{H}_{3}

\mathsf{H}_{4}

(Lemma

\mathsf{H}_{4}

.
Therefore, the distributions of the secret keys in Games
\mathsf{H}_{3}

\mathsf{H}_{7}

\mathsf{H}_{6}
, samples

\mathsf{H}_{7}

By bounding the error term, we have

with overwhelming probabiliry. By the noise-smudging lemma, we obtain that

\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}

\mathbf{c}_{2}
generated in two games are statistically indistinguishable. An analogous argument applies to

between the two games, the full ciphertext distributions in

\mathsf{H}_{6}

\mathsf{H}_{7}

\mathsf{H}_{6}\overset{s}{\approx}\mathsf{H}_{7}
