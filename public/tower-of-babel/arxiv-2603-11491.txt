The Generators of a Colon Ideal with an Application to the Weak Lefschetz Property for Monomial Almost Complete Intersections in Three Variables
Matthew Davidson Booth, Adela Vraciu

Abstract
. Much progress has been made in classifying when the weak Lefschetz property holds for A = 𝔽 ⁡ [ x , y , z ] / I A=\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]/I where char ​ ( 𝔽 ) = 0 \text{char}(\mathbb{F})=0 and I = ( x 1 d 1 , y d 2 , z d 3 , x a 1 ​ y a 2 ​ z a 3 ) I=(x_{1}^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}}) is a monomial almost complete intersection. We connect this problem to the setting of two variables through a certain relation. In so doing, we are led to determine explicit formulas for the generators of the colon ideal ( x d 1 , y d 2 ) : ( x + y ) a 3 (x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a_{3}} . With these generators in hand, we construct a matrix and show that failure of WLP for A A is dictated by the vanishing of a certain polynomial (namely the determinant of our matrix) when A A is level. We further show in the level case that a conjecture first posed in [ 9 ] is true in a few new cases.

1.
Introduction

Let
P=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
where
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic zero, and let
I
be an Artinian homogenous ideal of
P
. Recall that
P/I
is said to enjoy the
weak Lefschetz property (WLP)
if the multiplication by a general linear form map
\times\ell:(P/I)_{d-1}\rightarrow(P/I)_{d}
has maximal rank at all degrees
d
. The first “landmark” result on the subject of WLP was given in
[
12
]
(see also
[
13
]
), where it is shown that Artinian monomial complete intersection ideals always enjoy WLP. (In fact, they are shown to have the
strong Lefschetz property, SLP
.) While arbitrary complete intersection ideals are not known to always have WLP, it is conjectured that they do. More recent work has given attention to the class of almost complete intersection ideals, which are on the whole more mysterious. Any almost complete intersection (and more generally any Artinian ideal) in two variables is known to have WLP from Proposition 4.4 in
[
7
]
, so the first case of interest is three-variable setting.

Let
P=\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]
and
I=(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})
. The natural question is as follows: what conditions on the exponents defining
I
characterize the presence of the WLP for
P/I
? Early results specific to this type of ideal are Corollary 7.3 in
[
2
]
and Theorem 3.3 in
[
1
]
, where the authors determine semistability of the syzygy bundle associated with the ideal and show how this semistability implies the presence or absence of the WLP. Accompanying this analysis are a handful of numerical criteria which appear in subsequent treatments where ideals of the prescribed form are the focal point; two prominent such places are section six in
[
9
]
and sections three and four in
[
4
]
. These last two sources form the primary point of departure for this paper.

Retaining the setup established thus far, if at least two of
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{3}
are zero, then
I
is a complete intersection (and so enjoys WLP). In the case where exactly one of
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, or
a_{3}
is zero, it was shown by Migliore, Miró-Roig, and Nagel in
[
9
]
(Lemma 6.6) that
P/I
has WLP as well. This leaves only the possibility that all of
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{3}
are nonzero for examination, and Theorem 4.10 of
[
4
]
takes a large step toward classifying when WLP holds or fails for such ideals.

For level algebras, i.e. those with
d_{1}-a_{1}=d_{2}-a_{2}=d_{3}-a_{3}
, the following was originally proposed as part (b) of Conjecture 6.8 in
[
9
]
. Letting
t
denote the common value of the differences
d_{i}-a_{i}
, this conjecture appears again as Conjecture 4.13 in
[
4
]
to incorporate the two known “rogue” cases
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},t)\in\big\{(2,9,13,9),\,(3,7,14,9)\big\}
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Consider the ideal
I=(x^{a_{1}+t},y^{a_{2}+t},z^{a_{3}+t},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]
where
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},t
satisfy the following three conditions:

(1)

0<a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq 2(a_{1}+a_{2}),\ 3\ \text{divides}\ a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3},\ \text{and}\ t\geq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}.

If
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},t)
is not
(2,9,13,9)
or
(3,7,14,9)
, then
\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]/I
fails WLP if and only if
t
is even,
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
is odd, and
a_{1}=a_{2}
or
a_{2}=a_{3}
.

The WLP is known to hold when the assumptions in the conjecture are not satisfied. Indeed, part (a) of Conjecture 6.8 in
[
9
]
says WLP holds when any of the three conditions in (
1
) does not hold, and this part of the conjecture is true by Lemma 6.6, Corollary 6.3, and Lemma 6.7 of the same. The failure of WLP for
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},t)
when
t
is even,
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
is odd, and
a_{1}=a_{2}
or
a_{2}=a_{3}
is given by Corollary 7.4 of
[
9
]
(and is recovered by part (b)(3)(V’) of Theorem 4.10 in
[
4
]
.)

The forward implication of the conjecture is still open, although some cases have been established (see for instance Proposition 4.14 and Corollary 4.16 of
[
4
]
). More precisely, the remaining open cases for level algebras are:

•

a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
is odd,
t
is even, and
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
;

•

a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
is even,
t
is odd, and
a_{1}<a_{2}
or
a_{2}<a_{3}
.

The goal of this paper is to make some progress toward resolving these open cases in Section 3.
The prelude to these results, however, is a theorem of interest all on its own. Given
P=\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]
and the ideal
I=(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})
with
0<a_{j}<d_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2,3\}
, consider the homomorphism which maps
z
to
-(x+y)
. The image of
I
in
\mathbb{F}[x,y]
under this map is the ideal
\big(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}(x+y)^{a_{3}}\big)
. A homogeneous relation with respect to this ideal then takes the form

Ax^{d_{1}}+By^{d_{2}}+C(x+y)^{d_{3}}+Dx^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}(x+y)^{a_{3}}=0\quad\text{where}\quad A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{F}[x,y].

The preceding line indicates that
C(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}+Dx^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a_{3}}
, and the generators of this colon ideal turn out to be useful in settling some of the open cases in
1.1
. Section 2 is dedicated to proving
2.2
, which provides explicit formulas for the generators of the aforementioned colon ideal. Knowledge of the generator’s degrees will allow us in Section 3 to prove
3.1
, which shows that the vanishing of a certain determinant (which is a polynomial in
t
) coincides with failure of WLP for
I=(x^{a_{1}+t},y^{a_{2}+t},z^{a_{3}+t},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})
when
t\geq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}
. Then Section 3 will conclude (see
3.6
) by showing that
1.1
is true when
t=\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}+1
provided
a_{3}=2(a_{1}+a_{2})-3a
and
0\leq a\leq 3
.

The subsequent sections have many parameters to keep track of, so we mention here that all notation we define holds precisely in the section in which it is defined. For instance, the parameter “
a
” will play the role of
a_{3}
in Section 2 but has a different meaning (which we specify) when it appears again in
3.6
.

2.
Generators of the Colon Ideal

Fix the polynomial ring
P=\mathbb{F}[x,y]
where
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic zero. The main result of this section is an explicit description of the generators for the colon ideal
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
. We define four elements of
P
which take center stage in what follows.

Notation 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, and let
a
be a nonnegative integer. For each nonnegative integer
n
, define the following elements of
P
:

(1)

When
a
is odd,

\displaystyle F_{1,d,a,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-\frac{a+1}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d-1-i}{\frac{a-1}{2}+n}\binom{\frac{a-1}{2}+i}{\frac{a-1}{2}}x^{d-\frac{a+1}{2}-n-i}y^{i},\ \text{whose degree is}\ \ d-\frac{a+1}{2}-n\,;

\displaystyle F_{2,d,a,n}

\displaystyle=y^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{d-\frac{a+3}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d-3-i}{\frac{a-3}{2}+n}\binom{\frac{a+1}{2}+i}{\frac{a+1}{2}}x^{d-\frac{a+3}{2}-n-i}y^{i},\ \text{whose degree is}\ \ d-\frac{a-1}{2}-n.

(2)

When
a
is even,

\displaystyle G_{1,d,a,n}

\displaystyle=y\sum_{i=0}^{d-\frac{a+2}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d-2-i}{\frac{a-2}{2}+n}\binom{\frac{a}{2}+i}{\frac{a}{2}}x^{d-\frac{a+2}{2}-n-i}\,y^{i},\ \text{whose degree is}\ \ d-\frac{a}{2}-n\,;

\displaystyle G_{2,d,a,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-\frac{a}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d-1-i}{\frac{a-2}{2}+n}\binom{\frac{a-2}{2}+i}{\frac{a-2}{2}}(i-1)x^{d-\frac{a}{2}-n-i}\,y^{i},\ \text{whose degree is}\ \ d-\frac{a}{2}-n.

Note that any terms with negative exponents have a binomial coefficient equal to zero. (Indeed, if we view
\binom{m}{r}
as a polynomial in
m
, then the condition
0\leq m<r
implies
m-j=0
for some
j\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}
.) Our goal in this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the ideal
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
where
2\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
and
1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
. Setting
k=d_{2}-d_{1}
, the aforementioned colon ideal has the following generators in
P
:

(1)

If
1\leq a\leq k
, then the generators are
x^{d_{1}}
and

H_{d_{1},a,k}:=y^{k-a+1}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{1}-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{1}+a-2-i}{a-1}x^{d_{1}-1-i}y^{i},\ \text{of degree}\ \deg(H_{d_{1},a,k})=d_{2}-a.

(2)

If
k+1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
, then:

(i)

When
a-k
is odd, the generators are
F_{1,d_{2},a-k,k}
and
F_{2,d_{2},a-k,k}
.

(ii)

When
a-k
is even, the generators are
G_{1,d_{2},a-k,k}
and
G_{2,d_{2},a-k,k}
.

Note that
(x+y)^{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
, and therefore the colon
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
is the whole of
P
for
a\geq d_{1}+d_{2}-1
.

Our approach is outlined as follows: we will collect some preliminary observations concerning the degrees of the colon ideal’s generators. After a key lemma (see
2.6
), we prove a version of the main theorem in which
d_{1}=d_{2}
. Finally, we develop a connection between the proposed generators (in the
d_{1}=d_{2}
case) and their partial derivatives to pave the way for proof of the main theorem.

Next, we explain why the colon ideal we study has two generators and derive a relation which the generator degrees satisfy.

Observation 2.3
.

Let
P=\mathbb{F}[x,y]
where
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic zero. Consider
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
where
2\leq d_{1},d_{2}
and
1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
. This ideal has two generators
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, and these generators satisfy the relation

\deg(Q_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})=d_{1}+d_{2}-a.

Proof.

This is just unpacking the Hilbert-Burch Theorem (see
[
3
]
or Theorem 20.15 from
[
5
]
) in our specific context. Let
I
be the ideal
\big(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},(x+y)^{a}\big)
where
2\leq d_{1},d_{2}
and
1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
. We have a resolution of
P/I
(over
P
):

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P/I}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

where
\pi
is the canonical projection. Viewing elements in
P^{3}
as
3\times 1
column vectors, the map
\varphi_{1}
can be represented as the
1\times 3
row matrix

\varphi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}x^{d_{1}}&y^{d_{2}}&(x+y)^{a}\end{bmatrix}.

Viewing elements in
P^{2}
as
2\times 1
column vectors, the map
\varphi_{2}
can be represented as a
3\times 2
matrix, say

\varphi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
B_{1}&B_{2}\\
Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{where each entry is an element of}\ P.

This matrix encodes the relations on the generators of
I
, which is to say
A_{i}x^{d_{1}}+B_{i}y^{d_{2}}+Q_{i}(x+y)^{a}=0_{P}
for
i=1,2
. It follows that the colon ideal
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
is the same as the ideal
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
. Indeed, if
Q\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
is arbitrary, then there exist elements
A,B\in P
such that

\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\\
Q\end{bmatrix}\in\text{Span}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}\\
B_{1}\\
Q_{1}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}\\
B_{2}\\
Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)\quad\implies\quad Q\in\text{Span}(Q_{1},Q_{2}).

Moreover, the
j^{\text{th}}
entry of
\varphi_{1}
is obtained from the
2\times 2
minor in
\varphi_{2}
by omitting the
j^{\text{th}}
row. Explicitly,

(2)

x^{d_{1}}=\det\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&B_{2}\\
Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad y^{d_{2}}=\det\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad(x+y)^{a}=\det\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
B_{1}&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Because
I
is a homogeneous ideal, the relations are also homogeneous. Therefore, from these determinants we deduce

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=\deg(B_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})=\deg(B_{2})+\deg(Q_{1}),

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=\deg(A_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})=\deg(A_{2})+\deg(Q_{1}),

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\deg(A_{1})+\deg(B_{2})=\deg(A_{2})+\deg(B_{1}).

Adding
\deg(A_{1})
to the first equation, adding
\deg(B_{2})
to the second equation, and adding
\deg(Q_{1})
or
\deg(Q_{2})
to the third equation gives rise to some equalities:

d_{1}+\deg(A_{1})=d_{2}+\deg(B_{2})=a+\deg(Q_{1})=a+\deg(Q_{2}).

Now we sum
d_{1}+\deg(A_{1})
and
d_{2}+\deg(B_{2})
. By the above, this is equal to the sum of
a+\deg(Q_{1})
and
a+\deg(Q_{2})
. Using the fact that
\deg(A_{1})+\deg(B_{2})=a
from the third determinant in (
2
), we arrive at the desired relation:

d_{1}+\deg(A_{1})+d_{2}+\deg(B_{2})=2a+\deg(Q_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})\ \implies\ \deg(Q_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})=d_{1}+d_{2}-a.

∎

For the next lemma, recall that the kernel of the endomorphism
\times(x+y)^{a}
on the quotient
P/(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
is the image of the colon ideal
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
. Thus, finding the first degree for which injectivity of
\times(x+y)^{a}
fails is equivalent to knowing the first degree in which a generator of the colon ideal lying outside
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
will be found.

Lemma 2.4
.

Form the quotient
R=P/(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
where
2\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
, and set
k=d_{2}-d_{1}
. Let
\ell
be a general linear form, and for any fixed positive integer
a
let
\times\ell^{a}:R_{t}\rightarrow R_{t+a}
be the map induced by “multiplication by
\ell^{a}
”. The first degree
t
where this map fails to be injective is given by

t=\begin{cases}d_{2}-a&\text{when}\ a\leq k,\\
d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil&\text{when}\ a\geq k+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that
2d_{2}-k-2
is the socle degree of
R
. As
R
is a complete intersection, Theorem 1 of
[
11
]
tells us the Hilbert function
\mathrm{HF}_{R}(t)
is symmetric about half the socle degree and moreover that
\mathrm{HF}_{R}(t)
strictly increases from
t=0
to
t=d_{2}-k-1
, remains constant from
t=d_{2}-k-1
to
t=d_{2}-1
, and strictly decreases from
t=d_{2}
to
t=2d_{2}-k-2
. We pair these opening remarks with the classic result of
[
12
]
(or Proposition 4.4 in
[
7
]
), which is that
R
enjoys SLP. The upshot is that
\times\ell^{a}:R_{t}\rightarrow R_{t+a}
fails to be injective precisely when

(3)

\text{HF}_{R}(t)>\text{HF}_{R}(t+a).

Our aim is to find the least nonnegative
t
for which (
3
) is satisfied. Notice that
t\leq d_{2}-a-1
will never meet this condition. Indeed,
t\leq d_{2}-a-1
is equivalent to
t+a\leq d_{2}-1
, and the Hilbert function is nondecreasing up to the latter degree. Thus,
t\geq d_{2}-a
is necessary for (
3
) to hold.

Suppose here that
a\leq k
. This setup implies
d_{2}-k-1<d_{2}-a\leq d_{2}-1
, and using the description of
\text{HF}_{R}(t)
from the first paragraph we obtain

\text{HF}_{R}(d_{2}-k-1)=\text{HF}_{R}(d_{2}-a)=\text{HF}_{R}(d_{2}-1)>\text{HF}_{R}(d_{2}).

This shows that the lower bound established in the previous paragraph actually satisfies (
3
), so
t=d_{2}-a
is the first degree where injectivity fails when
a\leq k
.

Now suppose that
a\geq k+1
. This setup implies
d_{2}-a-1\leq d_{2}-k-1
, meaning that (by exploiting the symmetry of the Hilbert function) the first degree where injectivity fails can be obtained by finding the least
t
such that
t+(t+a)
exceeds the socle degree:

2t+a>2d_{2}-k-2\quad\iff\quad t>d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil-1.

This immediately yields
t=d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil
as the first degree for which injectivity fails when
a\geq k+1
.
∎

Pairing the preceding results gives us the degrees of the generators for our colon ideal.

Observation 2.5
.

Let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
generate
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
where
2\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
and
1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
. Set
k=d_{2}-d_{1}
.

(1)

If
1\leq a\leq k
, then
\deg(Q_{1})=d_{1}
and
\deg(Q_{2})=d_{2}-a
.

(2)

If
k+1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
, then
\deg(Q_{1})=d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil
and
\deg(Q_{2})=d_{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

Let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
generate
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
. Since
k=d_{2}-d_{1}
, the relation from
2.3
reads in this setting as

(4)

\deg(Q_{1})+\deg(Q_{2})=2d_{2}-(a+k).

Suppose
1\leq a\leq k
holds. By
2.4
, at least one of the generators of
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
, say
Q_{2}
, has

\deg(Q_{2})=d_{2}-a.

In view of (
4
), we then obtain

\deg(Q_{1})+(d_{2}-a)=2d_{2}-(a+k)\quad\implies\quad\deg(Q_{1})=d_{2}-k,\ \ \text{i.e.}\ \ \deg(Q_{1})=d_{1}.

Now suppose
k+1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
holds.
2.4
says one of the generators of
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
, say
Q_{1}
, satisfies

\deg(Q_{1})=d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil.

In view of (
4
), we then obtain

\left(d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil\right)+\deg(Q_{2})=2d_{2}-(a+k)\quad\implies\quad\deg(Q_{2})=d_{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rfloor.

∎

The bulk of the proof of
2.7
is dedicated to showing that our proposed generators belong to the colon ideal at all. The following lemma tells us that their membership in the colon ideal is enough.

Lemma 2.6
.

Retain the setup of
2.5
. If
g,h\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
have
\deg(g)=\deg(Q_{1})
and
\deg(h)=\deg(Q_{2})
with
g\nmid h
, then in fact
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}=(g,h)
.

Proof.

We go by cases according to whether
\deg(Q_{1})<\deg(Q_{2})
or
\deg(Q_{1})=\deg(Q_{2})
. Suppose the former situation holds. Since
g\in(Q_{1},Q_{2})
, there exist elements
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in P
such that
g=\alpha_{1}Q_{1}+\alpha_{2}Q_{2}
. However,
\deg(g)=\deg(Q_{1})<\deg(Q_{2})
then implies
\alpha_{2}=0
and
\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
, i.e.
g
is a scalar multiple of
Q_{1}
. Hence, we may replace
Q_{1}
by
g
and generate the same ideal. Next,
h\in(g,Q_{2})
means there exist
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in P
such that
h=\beta_{1}g+\beta_{2}Q_{2}
. We have
\deg(h)=\deg(Q_{2})
, implying that
\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}
. In fact,
\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
because otherwise we obtain
h=\beta_{1}g
, a contradiction since
g
does not divide
h
. All this leads to the following realization:

h=\beta_{1}g+\beta_{2}Q_{2}\quad\implies\quad Q_{2}=(-\beta_{2})^{-1}\cdot(\beta_{1}g-h)\quad\implies\quad Q_{2}\in(g,h).

This completes the proof when
\deg(Q_{1})<\deg(Q_{2})
, so assume now that their degrees are equal. Observe that
g,h\in(Q_{1},Q_{2})
means there exist elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in P
such that

g=\gamma_{1}Q_{1}+\gamma_{2}Q_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad h=\delta_{1}Q_{1}+\delta_{2}Q_{2}\qquad\implies\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{2}\\
\delta_{1}&\delta_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}\\
Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g\\
h\end{bmatrix}.

In this case, notice that

\deg(g)=\deg(Q_{1})=\deg(Q_{2})=\deg(h)\quad\implies\quad\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{F}.

We claim that the matrix containing these field entries is invertible, for suppose instead that it is not. Then its rows are
P
-linearly dependent, so there exists
c\in P
(actually
c\in\mathbb{F}
) such that
\delta_{1}=c\gamma_{1}
and
\delta_{2}=c\gamma_{2}
. But this yields

h=c\gamma_{1}Q_{1}+c\gamma_{2}Q_{2}\quad\implies\quad h=c(\gamma_{1}Q_{1}+\gamma_{2}Q_{2})\quad\implies\quad h=c\cdot g.

In other words, we have
h
is a scalar multiple of
g
, contrary to the assumption
g\nmid h
. Therefore, the rows are linearly independent and so the matrix is invertible. We thence obtain

\left(\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{2}\\
\delta_{1}&\delta_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}g\\
h\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}\\
Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\implies\quad Q_{1},Q_{2}\in(g,h).

This establishes the lemma when
\deg(Q_{1})=\deg(Q_{2})
.
∎

,
G_{1,d_{2},a,0}
, and
G_{2,d_{2},a,0}
with respect to
x
satisfy the following:

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-1}{2}+j\right]\cdot F_{1,d_{2},a,n}\,;\qquad\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{2,d_{2},a,0}]=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-3}{2}+j\right]\cdot F_{2,d_{2},a,n}\,;

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[G_{1,d_{2},a,0}]=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-2}{2}+j\right]\cdot G_{1,d_{2},a,n}\,;\qquad\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[G_{2,d_{2},a,0}]=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-2}{2}+j\right]\cdot G_{2,d_{2},a,n}\,.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that the following is
n^{\text{th}}
partial derivative of
F_{1,d_{2},a,0}
with respect to
x
:

\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{2}-1-i}{\frac{a-1}{2}}\binom{\frac{a-1}{2}+i}{\frac{a-1}{2}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}-i-j\right)\right]x^{d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}-i-n}y^{i}.

Observe that
d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}=d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-1
. Now define the quantity
C
as follows:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\binom{d_{2}-1-i}{\frac{a-1}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-j-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(d_{2}-1-i)!}{\left(\frac{a-1}{2}\right)!\left(d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-1\right)!}\cdot\left[d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-1\right]\cdots\left[d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-n\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(d_{2}-1-i)!}{\left(\frac{a-1}{2}\right)!\left(d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-(n+1)\right)!}.

When we multiply
C
by the reciprocal of
\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+2\right)\cdots\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+n\right)
, we obtain

C\cdot\frac{1}{\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+2\right)\cdots\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+n\right)}=\frac{(d_{2}-1-i)!}{\left(\frac{a-1}{2}+n\right)!\left(d_{2}-\frac{a-1}{2}-i-n-1\right)!}=\binom{d_{2}-1-i}{\frac{a-1}{2}+n}.

Thus,

\frac{\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-1}{2}+j\right]}=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}}(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{2}-1-i}{\frac{a-1}{2}+n}\binom{\frac{a-1}{2}+i}{\frac{a-1}{2}}x^{d_{2}-\frac{a+1}{2}-n-i}y^{i},

\text{which is to say}\qquad\frac{\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-1}{2}+j\right]}=F_{1,d_{2},a,n}.

Multiplying both sides of the equation above by
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\frac{a-1}{2}+j\right]
achieves the formula asserted in the observation. In particular, note that
\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]
is a scalar multiple of
F_{1,d_{2},a,n}
. Therefore,

\left(\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}[F_{1,d_{2},a,0}]\right)=(F_{1,d_{2},a,n})\ \ \text{as ideals for all}\ n.

The assertions for the
n^{\text{th}}
partial derivatives of
F_{2,d_{2},a,0}
,
G_{1,d_{2},a,0}
, and
G_{2,d_{2},a,0}
with respect to
x
are obtained the same way
mutatis mutandis
.
∎

We need one more tool, namely that the partial derivative lies in an appropriate “shift” of the exponents in the colon ideal.

Lemma 2.9
.

Consider the ideal
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
where
2\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
and
a
is a nonnegative integer, and let
k=d_{2}-d_{1}
. Assume
a>k
. If
f\in(x^{d_{1}+k},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a-k}
, then
\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial x^{k}}[f]\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
.

Proof.

We shall prove something stronger, namely that

(21)

\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial x^{j}}[f]\in(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a-k+j}\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 0\leq j\leq k.

Going by induction on
j
(up to
k
), there’s nothing to prove for the base case
j=0
, so fix any
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Our inductive hypothesis is that
\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x^{j-1}}[f]\in(x^{d_{1}+k-(j-1)},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a-k+(j-1)}
. We are therefore justified in writing

\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x^{j-1}}[f]\cdot(x+y)^{a-k+j-1}=\widetilde{g}\cdot x^{d_{1}+k-j+1}+\widetilde{h}\cdot y^{d_{2}}\quad\text{for some}\ \widetilde{g},\widetilde{h}\in P.

Taking the partial derivative with respect to
x
on both sides yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{j}f}{\partial x^{j}}(x+y)^{a-k+j-1}+\,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{j-1}f}{\partial x^{j-1}}(a-k+j-1)(x+y)^{a-k+j-2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{g}}{\partial x}x^{d_{1}+k-j+1}+\widetilde{g}(d_{1}+k-j+1)x^{d_{1}+k-j}\right)+\frac{\partial\widetilde{h}}{\partial x}y^{d_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{g}}{\partial x}x+\widetilde{g}(d_{1}+k-j+1)\right)x^{d_{1}+k-j}+\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{h}}{\partial x}\right)y^{d_{2}}.

The upshot of the preceding is that
\frac{\partial^{j}f}{\partial x^{j}}\cdot(x+y)^{a-k+j-1}+\frac{\partial^{j-1}f}{\partial x^{j-1}}\cdot(a-k+j-1)(x+y)^{a-k+j-2}
lies in the ideal
(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}})
. We multiply this expression by
(x+y)
to reach

\frac{\partial^{j}f}{\partial x^{j}}\cdot(x+y)^{a-k+j}+\frac{\partial^{j-1}f}{\partial x^{j-1}}\cdot(a-k+j-1)(x+y)^{a-k+j-1}\in(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}}).

The second term above contains
\frac{\partial^{j-1}f}{\partial x^{j-1}}\cdot(x+y)^{a-k+j-1}
, which by induction belongs to
(x^{d_{1}+k-j+1},y^{d_{2}})
. (Note here that
a-k+j-1>0
because
0\leq k<a
and
j\geq 1
.) As this ideal is contained in
(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}})
, it follows immediately that

\frac{\partial^{j}f}{\partial x^{j}}\cdot(x+y)^{a-k+j}\in(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}}),\ \text{i.e.}\ \frac{\partial^{j}f}{\partial x^{j}}\in(x^{d_{1}+k-j},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a-k+j},\ \text{the content of \eqref{claim}}.

∎

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section, whose statement we recall here for the reader’s convenience:

See
2.2

Before embarking on the proof, we mention in passing that
H_{d_{1},a,k}
as given in part (1) of the theorem may alternatively be expressed as

H_{d_{1},a,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{2}-a}(-1)^{j}\binom{d_{2}-j-1}{a-1}x^{d_{2}-a-j}y^{j}.

This expression of
H_{d_{1},a,k}
is more akin to the formulas in
2.1
, but notice that its first terms will be multiples of
x^{d_{1}}
since
d_{1}=d_{2}-k\leq d_{2}-a
in part (1). On deletion of those terms, one immediately observes that
H_{d_{1},a,k}
is a multiple of some power of
y
, namely
y^{k-a+1}
.

To simplify the presentation in our proof of (1), we will suppress the subscripts
d_{1}
and
k
, simply writing
H_{a}
when proving the theorem.

Proof.

Assume first that
1\leq a\leq k
. By
2.5
, one generator of the colon ideal has degree
d_{1}
and the other has degree
d_{2}-a
. An appeal to
2.6
tells us
x^{d_{1}}
is the first generator of the colon ideal. To prove that
H_{a}
is the other generator, it suffices by
2.6
to show that
H_{a}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
.

Going by induction on
a
, take
a=1
. We show that
(x+y)H_{1}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
holds. To see this, compute the coefficient of
x^{d_{1}-i}y^{i}
in
(x+y)H_{1}
, which comes from the
i^{\text{th}}
and
(i-1)
terms in the expansion of
H_{1}
:

\displaystyle\text{cff}_{(x+y)H_{1}}=(-1)^{i+1}\binom{d_{1}-1-i}{0}+(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{1}-i}{0}

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle\text{cff}_{(x+y)H_{1}}=0\ \text{for all}\ i\in\{1,\dots,d_{1}-1\}.

Therefore,

(x+y)H_{1}=y^{d_{2}-d_{1}}\big[x^{d_{1}}+(-1)^{d_{1}-1}y^{d_{1}}\big]=x^{d_{1}}y^{d_{2}-d_{1}}+(-1)^{d_{1}-1}y^{d_{2}}\ \ \implies\ \ H_{1}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y).

We next take as our inductive hypothesis that
H_{a}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
for a fixed
a<k
and endeavor to show that
H_{a+1}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a+1}
. Observe here that if
(x+y)H_{a+1}=H_{a}
, then

(22)

(x+y)^{a+1}H_{a+1}=(x+y)^{a}H_{a}\quad\implies\quad H_{a+1}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a+1}.

Thus, we set our sights on proving
(x+y)H_{a+1}=H_{a}
to complete the inductive step. This comes down to determining the coefficient of
x^{d_{1}-i}y^{i}
in
(x+y)H_{a+1}
, which again arises from the and
i^{\text{th}}
and
(i-1)
terms in the expansion of
H_{a+1}
. With the aid of Pascal’s rule, we obtain

\text{cff}_{(x+y)H_{a+1}}=(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{1}+a-1-i}{a}+(-1)^{i-1}\binom{d_{1}+a-i}{a}=(-1)^{i-1}\binom{d_{1}+a-1-i}{a-1}.

The upshot of this observation is the realization that

\displaystyle(x+y)H_{a+1}

\displaystyle=y^{k-a}\left[\binom{d_{1}+a-1}{a}x^{d_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}-1}(-1)^{i-1}\binom{d_{1}+a-1-i}{a-1}x^{d_{1}-i}y^{i}+(-1)^{d_{1}-1}y^{d_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=y^{k-a}\left[\binom{d_{1}+a-1}{a}x^{d_{1}}+\sum_{i=0}^{d_{1}-2}(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{1}+a-2-i}{a-1}x^{d_{1}-1-i}y^{i+1}+(-1)^{d_{1}-1}y^{d_{1}}\right].

Notice that the first term is a multiple of
x^{d_{1}}
, so it lies in
(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
. We therefore ignore it and, after incorporating the last term into the summation and extracting a factor of
y
, reach

(x+y)H_{a+1}=y^{k-a+1}\sum_{i=0}^{d_{1}-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{d_{1}+a-2-i}{a-1}x^{d_{1}-1-i}y^{i},

\text{which says precisely that}\quad(x+y)H_{a+1}=H_{a}.

We conclude that (
22
) holds, completing the proof of assertion (1) in the theorem.

Now we assume that
k+1\leq a\leq d_{1}+d_{2}-2
. According to
2.7
, we have

(x^{d_{2}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a-k}=\begin{cases}(F_{1,d_{2},a-k,0},\,F_{2,d_{2},a-k,0})&\text{when}\ a-k\ \text{is odd},\\
(G_{1,d_{2},a-k,0},\,G_{2,d_{2},a-k,0})&\text{when}\ a-k\ \text{is even}.\end{cases}

Suppose
a-k
is odd.
2.8
and
2.9
yield
F_{1,d_{2},a-k,k},\,F_{2,d_{2},a-k,k}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}}):(x+y)^{a}
. Notice further that

\deg(F_{1,d_{2},a-k,k})=d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a-k}{2}\right\rceil-k=d_{2}-\left\lceil\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rceil,\ \text{and similarly}\ \deg(F_{2,d_{2},a-k,k})=d_{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{a+k}{2}\right\rfloor.

2.6
now guarantees that
F_{1,d_{2},a-k,k}
and
F_{2,d_{2},a-k,k}
generate the colon ideal. The argument proceeds identically (with the obvious necessary modifications) to show that
G_{1,d_{2},a-k,k}
and
G_{2,d_{2},a-k,k}
generate the colon ideal when
a-k
is even, whence the theorem is established.
∎

3.
Application to WLP in 3-Variable Monomial ACIs

In this section we consider the question of classifying the values of
(t,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
for which WLP holds for the level almost complete intersection ring

A=\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]/I
, where the ideal
I=(x^{t+a_{1}},y^{t+a_{2}},z^{t+a_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}}
). We assume this generating set is minimal and that
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}>0
. As an application of the results from the previous section, our main result in this section is the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

For fixed values of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
with
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
divisible by
3
and
a_{3}<2(a_{1}+a_{2})
, there exist polynomials
P_{1}(t)
and
P_{2}(t)
such that:

a.
For
t\geq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}
even, WLP fails if and only if
P_{1}(t)=0
.

b.
For
t\geq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}
odd, WLP fails if and only if
P_{2}(t)=0
.

In particular, for fixed
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
and fixed parity for
t
, either WLP fails for all values of
t
or fails for at most finitely many values.

We can write the polynomials in this theorem explicitly for small values of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
and prove that they do not have any “large” roots, meaning that WLP holds for all values of
t\geq\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}
(see
3.5
). Notice also that this theorem does not assume
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
; our result is independent of the exponent ordering.

In anticipation of the theorem’s proof, we have two preparatory lemmas:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
I=(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})\subset\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]
, and
L=x+y+z
.
For a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}[x,y,z]
, we denote the image of
f
under the homomorphism that sends
z
to
-(x+y)
by
\tilde{f}\in\mathbb{F}[x,y]
. Fix a degree
d
.

(a)

Assume that
FL=Ax^{d_{1}}+By^{d_{2}}+Cz^{d_{3}}+Dx^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}}
and
F\notin I
. Then
\tilde{D}\notin(x^{d_{1}-a_{1}},y^{d_{2}-a_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}})
.

(b)

Conversely, if there exist
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}[x,y]
such that

(23)

A^{\prime}x^{d_{1}}+B^{\prime}y^{d_{2}}+C^{\prime}(x+y)^{d_{3}}+D^{\prime}x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}(x+y)^{a_{3}}=0

is a homogeneous relation of degree
d
(meaning that each of the four terms is homogeneous of degree
d
), and

D^{\prime}\notin(x^{d_{1}-a_{1}},y^{d_{2}-a_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}})
, then there exists a homogeneous relation of degree
d
as in part (a) such that
F\notin I
.

Proof.

For (a), we argue by contrapositive. We have

\tilde{D}\in(x^{d_{1}-a_{1}},y^{d_{2}-a_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}})\quad\iff\quad D\in(x^{d_{1}-a_{1}},y^{d_{2}-a_{2}},z^{d_{3}-a_{3}},L).

Say
G
is such that
D-GL\in(x^{d_{1}-a_{1}},y^{d_{2}-a_{2}},z^{d_{3}-a_{3}})
. Then

(F+Gx^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}})L\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}}).

The degree assumption implies that
F+Gx^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}z^{a_{3}}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},z^{d_{3}})
since WLP holds for the complete intersection, whence we obtain
F\in I
.

Part (b) proceeds by contrapositive as well. Let

F:=C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{(x+y)^{d_{3}}-(-z)^{d_{3}}}{x+y+z}+D^{\prime}\cdot\frac{(x+y)^{a_{3}}-(-z)^{a_{3}}}{x+y+z}.

It is clear that
FL\in I
and
\deg(F)=d-1
. Further, we can rewrite

\displaystyle F=

\displaystyle\ C^{\prime}\big((x+y)^{d_{3}-1}+(-z)(x+y)^{d_{3}-2}+\cdots+(-z)^{d_{3}-1}\big)\,+

\displaystyle\ D^{\prime}x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}\big((x+y)^{a_{3}-1}+(-z)(x+y)^{a_{3}-2}+\cdots+(-z)^{a_{3}-1}\big).

Assume
F\in I
. Looking at the terms of
F
containing
z^{d_{3}-1}
, we see that
F\in I
implies
C^{\prime}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{1}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{1}})
. Next, look at the terms of
F
containing
z^{a_{3}-1}
; we see that
F\in I
implies
C^{\prime}(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}+D^{\prime}x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})
. Thus, we obtain

D^{\prime}x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})+(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}})\quad\text{and note that}

(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})+(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}})=(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}},(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{2}}).

This implies that there exists

such that

(D^{\prime}-(x+y)^{d_{3}-a_{3}}H)x^{a_{1}}y^{a_{1}}\in(x^{d_{1}},y^{d_{2}})

.
∎

.

3.1

Proof.

As mentioned in the proof of the preceding corollary, we know that WLP fails if and only if there exists a homogeneous relation

Assuming WLP fails,

We know that

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg}(F_{1})=\left\lfloor\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}-a_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor,\ \ \ \displaystyle\mathrm{deg}(F_{2})=\left\lceil\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}-a_{3}}{2}\right\rceil,

\mathrm{deg}(H_{1})=t+a-1-\left\lfloor\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}-a_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor=a-1-\left\lfloor\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}-a_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lceil\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{6}\right\rceil-1

\mathrm{deg}(H_{2})=\left\lfloor\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{6}\right\rfloor-1.

unknowns.
Equation (

we get an equation by setting the coefficient of that monomial on the left hand side equal to the coefficient of that monomial on the right hand side. More precisely, we get one equation for each of the following monomials:

\displaystyle t>\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}{3}-1

a_{1}+a_{2}

unknonwns.

We claim that the system given by (

\tilde{C}\neq 0
that satisfies
(\ref{sys})
. Indeed, if there is a nontrivial solution with
\tilde{C}=0
then we have

. This would mean that there exists a nonzero polynomial

of degree

such that

However, the smallest degree of a generator of

is

. The assumption that

implies that

. This is a contradiction.

According to
3.3
, we now have that failure of WLP is equivalent to existence of a nontrivial solution for the linear system of equations given by (

and

. We get two different polynomials according to the parity of

because the coefficients of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
involve
(-1)^{t}
.
∎

B:=\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-3}{2}}
.
Note
B=\frac{a_{3}-1}{a_{3}+3}A
.

Then

\mathcal{C}=(\mathrm{cff}(x^{3},\tau^{3}D)+\mathrm{cff}(y^{3},\tau^{3}D))B+(\mathrm{cff}(x^{2}y,\tau^{3}D)+\mathrm{cff}(xy^{2},\tau^{3}D))A=0\Leftrightarrow

(\mathrm{cff}(x^{3},\tau^{3}D)+\mathrm{cff}(y^{3},\tau^{3}D))(a_{3}-1)+(\mathrm{cff}(x^{2}y,\tau^{3}D)+\mathrm{cff}(xy^{2},\tau^{3}D))(a_{3}+3)=0

Substituting
a_{3}=2(a_{1}+a_{2})-9
and dividing by 2, we have

\mathcal{C}=0\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{G}(a_{1},a_{2}):=(a_{1}+a_{2}-5)(a_{1}^{3}-a_{2}^{3}-3a_{1}^{2}+3a_{2}^{2}+2a_{1}-2a_{2})+(a_{1}+a_{2}-3)(-3a_{1}^{2}a_{2}+3a_{1}a_{2}^{2})=0.

Note that
\mathcal{G}(a_{1},a_{2})
is divisible by
a_{1}-a_{2}
, so every
a_{1}=a_{2}
is a solution for
\mathcal{G}(a_{1},a_{2})=0
.
Dividing by
a_{1}-a_{2}
leaves

\mathcal{F}(a_{1},a_{2}):=a_{1}^{3}-a_{1}^{2}a_{2}-a_{1}a_{2}^{2}+a_{2}^{3}-8a_{1}^{2}-2a_{1}a_{2}-8a_{2}^{2}+17a_{1}+17a_{2}-10.

Using
S:=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
P:=a_{1}a_{2}
, we have

\mathcal{F}=S(S^{2}-3P)-SP-8(S^{2}-2P)-2P+17S-10

so
\mathcal{F}=0\Leftrightarrow S^{3}-8S^{2}+17S-10=4SP-14P
.
This implies
2S-7\,|\,S^{3}-8S^{2}+17S-10
. Modulo
2S-7
, we have
2S\equiv 7
, so

2S-7\,|\,8S^{3}-64S^{2}+17\cdot 8S-80=(2S)^{3}-16\cdot(2S)^{2}+68(2S)-80\equiv 7^{3}-16\cdot 7^{2}+68\cdot 7-80=-45.

It remains to check the divisors of 45 as possible values of
2S-7
.
As
a_{3}=2S-9\geq 1
, we have
2S-7\in\{3,5,9,15,45\}
, i.e.
S\in\{5,6,8,11,26\}
. The corresponding values of
P=(S^{3}-8S^{2}+17S-10)/(4S-10)
are given by
0
,
2
,
7
,
18
,
140
and the resulting
(a_{1},a_{2})
are:
(0,5),(\text{non-integer}),(1,7),(2,9),(\text{non-integer})
. In particular, there are no integer solutions with
a_{1}\geq 3
.

Now assume
a_{1}=2
. We must consider the coefficient of
x^{t-2}y^{t-1}
in
(\tau^{3}D)(x+y)^{a_{3}}
. This is

\mathcal{C}:=\text{cff}(x^{2},\tau^{3}D)\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-3}{2}}+\text{cff}(xy,\tau^{3}D)\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-1}{2}}+\text{cff}(y^{2},\tau^{3}D)\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}+1}{2}}.

Set
A=\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-1}{2}}=\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}+1}{2}}
, which makes the binomial coefficient in the first term expressible as
\frac{a_{3}-1}{a_{3}+3}A
. With this identification, we have
\mathcal{C}=0
if and only if

(a_{3}-1)\text{cff}(x^{2},\tau^{3}D)+(a_{3}+3)\text{cff}(xy,\tau^{3}D+(a_{3}+3)\text{cff}(y^{2},\tau^{3}D)=0.

As
a_{1}=2
, we have
a_{3}=2a_{2}-5
. Substituting these values into the relation described above, we obtain

-2a_{2}^{4}+24a_{2}^{3}-58a_{2}^{2}+36a_{2}=0\quad\implies\quad-2a_{2}(a_{2}-1)(a_{2}-2)(a_{2}-9)=0.

However,
a_{2}\leq 2
would imply
a_{3}<0
, an obvious impossibility. Thus, the only “viable” root is
a_{2}=9
, which gives rise to
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(2,9,13)
.

Finally, assume
a_{1}=1
. We examine the coefficient of
x^{t-3}y^{t-1}
in
(\tau^{3}D)(x+y)^{a_{3}}
. This is

\mathcal{C}:=\text{cff}(x,\tau^{3}D)\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-3}{2}}+\text{cff}(y,\tau^{3}D)\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-1}{2}}.

Keeping
A=\binom{a_{3}}{\frac{a_{3}-1}{2}}
, it follows that
\mathcal{C}=0
precisely when

(a_{3}-1)\text{cff}(x,\tau^{3}D)+(a_{3}+3)\text{cff}(y,\tau^{3}D)=0.

As
a_{1}=1
, we have
a_{3}=2a_{2}-7
. Substituting these values into the relation described above, we obtain

-2a_{2}^{4}+20a_{2}^{3}-46a_{2}^{2}+28a_{2}=0\quad\implies\quad-2a_{2}(a_{2}-1)(a_{2}-2)(a_{2}-7)=0.

However,
a_{2}\leq 3
would imply
a_{3}<0
, which is absurd. This leaves only the root
a_{2}=7
, corresponding to an
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
triple with two equal entries, namely
(1,7,7)
.
∎

We point out that the idea of the proof of
3.6
can be used to tackle larger values of
a
. For a fixed value of
a
, one has a polynomial
\mathcal{F}(a_{1},a_{2})
constructed as above, which is symmetric in
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. This can then be expressed a polynomial in the elementary symmetric functions
S=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
P=a_{1}a_{2}
. We have implemented the construction for the polynomial obtained in the case
a_{1}\geq a
in
Macaulay2
, using the package “SymmetricPolynomials,” and have observed experimentally that when
\mathcal{F}(a_{1},a_{2})
is expressed as a polynomial in
P
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}[S]
, the constant term is

\mathcal{P}(S):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\prod_{i=1}^{a-1}(S-i)\prod_{i=\frac{3a+1}{2}}^{2a-1}(S-i)&\mathrm{for}&a\ \mathrm{odd}\\
&&\\
\prod_{i=0}^{a-1}(S-i)\prod_{i=\frac{3a}{2}}^{2a-1}(S-i)&\mathrm{for}&a\ \mathrm{even}\\
\end{array}\right.

and all the other coefficients of powers of
P
are multiples of
2S-c(a)
, where
c(a)=3a-2
for
a
odd and
c(a)=3a-1
for
a
even. If
\mathcal{F}(a_{1},a_{2})=0
, it follows that
2S-c(a)
must divide
\mathcal{P}(S)
. Similar to the argument in the proof of
3.6
, this gives a finite list of possible values of
S
. For each possible value of
S
from this list,
P
must divide
\mathcal{P}(S)
. Therefore, we will have a finite list of values of
S
and
P
to consider, giving rise to a finite list of values of
a_{1},a_{2}
. Unfortunately, for values of
a\geq 7
the list of values that need to be checked is too large to be practical. Using
Macaulay2
, we were able to verify for values of
a
up to 6 that there are no positive integer solutions for
\mathcal{F}(a_{1},a_{2})=0
, except for the known exceptions when failure of WLP has been established. For larger values of
a
, we were not able to finish checking all possible values even with the help of
Macaulay2
(although we have verified all possible values of
S
up to 1000 for
a
up to 40).
