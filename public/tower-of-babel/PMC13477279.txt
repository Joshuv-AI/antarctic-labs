Associations of Combined Moderate and Vigorous Physical Activity With All‐Cause, Cardiovascular, and Cancer Mortality: A Cohort Study of 586 936 US Adults
Authors: Yongyu Huang, Qing Wang, Geng Li, Yuzhe Zhang, Mingyue Yin, Yanwei You, Ruyi Huang, Ming Ding, Zuosheng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough both moderate‐intensity physical activity (MPA) and vigorous‐intensity physical activity (VPA) are recommended, the optimal combination of intensities for maximal health benefits remains uncertain. This study examined the associations of MPA, VPA, and combined moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) with all‐cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality.MethodsWe analyzed data from 586 936 adults, aged ≥18 years, in the US National Health Interview Survey (1997–2018), linked to mortality through 2019. Participants were categorized as inactive, MPA only, VPA only, or MVPA. Among those engaging in MVPA, we further classified participants by activity frequency and the proportion of VPA. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for all‐cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality, adjusting for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Sensitivity analyses excluded individuals with baseline chronic diseases or deaths within the first 2 years.ResultsOver a median 10.0‐year follow‐up, 82 959 deaths occurred. Compared with inactivity, MPA only (HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.72–0.76]), VPA only (HR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.54–0.59]), and MVPA (HR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.48–0.52]) were associated with progressively lower all‐cause mortality. Similar patterns were observed for cardiovascular disease and cancer mortality. Across all MVPA volumes, individuals with >25% of MVPA from VPA had the lowest mortality risks. Findings were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsVPA was associated with greater mortality reduction than MPA, and combining both intensities conferred the strongest survival benefits. These results support current guidelines and highlight the importance of incorporating VPA, when feasible, into public health strategies.

Physical inactivity has become a significant global public health challenge, with prevalence rates continuing to increase. In 2022, an estimated one‐third (31.3%; 1.8 billion) of adults worldwide were physically inactive, marking a significant increase from 23.4% (900 million) in 2000. 1 In 2020, physical inactivity accounted for 7.2% of all‐cause deaths and 7.6% of deaths from cardiovascular disease (CVD) globally. The proportion of noncommunicable diseases attributable to physical inactivity ranged from 1.6% for hypertension to 8.1% for dementia. 2 If the global prevalence of insufficient physical activity remains unchanged, nearly 500 million new cases of preventable noncommunicable diseases could occur between 2020 and 2030, incurring health care costs of ≈$520 billion. 3 Substantial evidence underscores that increasing physical activity levels can prevent various chronic diseases and reduce all‐cause and cause‐specific mortality rates. 4 , 5 , 6 Therefore, promoting physical activity worldwide is essential to reduce premature mortality and alleviate the burden of noncommunicable diseases.

The 2018 Second Edition of the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans and the 2020 World Health Organization Physical Activity Guidelines both recommend that adults engage in 150 to 300 minutes of moderate‐intensity aerobic physical activity (MPA) per week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous‐intensity aerobic physical activity (VPA) per week, or an equivalent combination of both. 7 , 8 One minute of VPA is equivalent to 2 minutes of MPA based on energy expenditure. MPA corresponds to 3.0 to 5.9 metabolic equivalents of task, whereas VPA is defined as ≥6.0 metabolic equivalents of task. 9 , 10 Recent research has revealed that different physical activity intensities elicit distinct physiological responses and health benefits. 11 Studies by Yafeng et al showed that, for the same total volume of physical activity, a higher proportion of VPA within moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was associated with lower all‐cause mortality. 12 Similarly, a study from Copenhagen, Denmark, found that individuals who cycled at higher intensities had lower all‐cause and coronary heart disease mortality. 13 These studies suggest that higher‐intensity physical activity may confer greater health benefits; however, this does not imply that VPA can fully replace MPA. The Whitehall II cohort study previously identified a unique association between MPA and reduced mortality risk, independent of light (light‐intensity physical activity) and vigorous activities. 14 Furthermore, moderate‐intensity physical activities (MPAs) have demonstrated benefits in blood sugar control, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and blood pressure regulation. 15 , 16 , 17 However, it remains unclear whether MPA and VPA exert independent or combined effects on mortality risk, as has been observed for physical activity and muscle‐strengthening activity. 18 , 19 Moreover, both the relative benefits of MPA and VPA and their optimal combination remain uncertain.

Therefore, in this study, we examined the associations of MPA, VPA, and MVPA with all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, in a large nationally representative US cohort. We further investigated whether varying proportions of VPA within MVPA were associated with differences in mortality risk, and hypothesized that individuals engaging in a balanced combination of MPA and VPA would have the lowest mortality risk.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

This study uses data from publicly available versions of the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). All data and code that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Ethical approval for the conduct of the NHIS is maintained by the research ethics review board of the National Center for Health Statistics and the US Office of Management and Budget. This study was viewed as exempt from institutional review board approval because it used deidentified publicly available data by the National Center for Health Statistics.

Study Population

The NHIS, jointly conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Center for Health Statistics, is a nationally representative cross‐sectional survey targeting noninstitutionalized civilians in the United States. Since 1957, the survey has used a stratified, multistage sampling design each year, selecting ≈35 000 households from randomly chosen sample clusters. Additionally, one adult (aged ≥18 years) from each household is randomly selected to provide further information, including health status, health behaviors, and health care use. Comprehensive descriptions of NHIS design, methods, and weighting are available in published reports. 20 , 21 , 22

This study analyzed data from 671 696 adults across 22 NHIS cross‐sectional rounds conducted between 1997 and 2018. Participants were linked to the National Death Index using unique identifiers, enabling mortality tracking until December 31, 2019. Data collected before 1997 were excluded because of major revisions in the physical activity questionnaire that year. Participants with missing data on physical activity, mortality status, or covariates were excluded from the analysis. The final analysis sample consisted of 586 936 adults (Figure 1). Missing physical activity or covariate data primarily resulted from participant uncertainty, lack of awareness, or refusal to respond.

Using the weekly frequency and duration of both MPA and VPA, the total weekly duration for these activity types was calculated. According to the 2020 World Health Organization Physical Activity Guidelines and the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, second edition, 1 minute of VPA equals 2 minutes of MPA for MVPA calculation. 7 , 8

Based on the objectives of our study, participants were categorized into 4 groups: no PA (no reported physical activity of any intensity), MPA only (reported only low‐ to moderate‐intensity physical activity), VPA only (reported only vigorous‐intensity physical activity), and MVPA (reported both intensities). Furthermore, participants who reported physical activity were further stratified by frequency into 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6, and ≥7 times per week. Finally, intensity levels were defined according to the proportion of VPA within total MVPA: 0% (MPA only), 0% to ≤25%, >25% to ≤50%, >50% to ≤75%, >75% to <100%, and 100% (VPA only). For example, if a participant engaged in 30 minutes of VPA and 40 minutes of MPA per week, his/her total MVPA was calculated as 30 minutes of VPA×2 plus 40 minutes of MPA=100 MVPA minutes; the proportion of VPA was thus 60% (60/100).

Mortality Confirmation

Mortality status was determined via linkage to the National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Causes of death were classified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD‐10), codes. Detailed information on the National Death Index's methods and accuracy has been published elsewhere. 20 , 21 , 22 In this study, we examined the following mortality outcomes: all‐cause mortality, CVD mortality (codes I00‐I09, I11, I13, I20‐I51, and I60‐I69), and cancer mortality (codes C00‐C97).

Covariates

The covariates included in this study encompass personal factors, such as age, sex, region, race, marital status, and educational level. Lifestyle‐related variables include body mass index (BMI), smoking, and drinking, alongside chronic health conditions, such as hypertension, heart disease, stroke, cancer, and diabetes. Regions were categorized into 4 major US divisions: north, Midwest, south, and west. Race was classified as White, Black, or other (Including Asian and multi‐ethnic groups); marital status as married, divorced, separated or widowed, and never married; and educational level as below high school, high school graduate, and above high school. Chronic disease diagnoses were based on participants' self‐reports of whether a physician or other health care professional had informed them of having specific conditions. BMI was calculated using self‐reported height and weight and categorized as normal weight (<25 kg/m2), overweight (25–<30 kg/m2), or obesity (≥30 kg/m2). Smoking status was defined as having smoked ≥100 cigarettes in one's lifetime, and participants were categorized as never smokers or ever smokers. Drinking status was defined as never drink or drink based on self‐reported responses.

Statistical Analysis

χ2 Tests were used to compare categorical baseline characteristics across physical activity groups. Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs to assess associations of different types of physical activity participation with all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, using years of follow‐up as the time scale. No violations to the proportional hazards assumption were noted using Schoenfeld residual plots.

To evaluate the impact of potential confounding factors on the association between guideline‐recommended physical activity levels and mortality, 4 sequential Cox models were constructed: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, region, and race; model 3 additionally included marital status, educational level, BMI, smoking, and alcohol use; and model 4 further adjusted for chronic conditions (hypertension, heart disease, stroke, cancer, and diabetes).

Additional analyses were conducted to determine whether associations with mortality differed by total physical activity volume (0–149 minutes/week, 150–299 minutes/week, and ≥300 minutes/week), frequency, and intensity. Comparative analyses across groups with different weekly activity volumes, frequencies, and intensity levels were performed. We also explored the associations of mortality with MVPA across varying intensity subgroups. Finally, subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the study findings varied among populations stratified by potential covariates. All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design of NHIS, incorporating stratification, clustering, and sampling weights to ensure nationally representative estimates. Analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2, and 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

With advancing age, the proportion of individuals engaging in VPA or combined MVPA decreased, whereas MPA participation increased. Compared with individuals with no physical activity, individuals engaging in MVPA were more likely to be younger, male, White race, better educated, unmarried, current smokers or drinkers, and free of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, coronary heart disease, or stroke.

Associations Between Physical Activity Types and All‐Cause, CVD, and Cancer Mortality

Over a median follow‐up of 10.0 years, 82 959 adults (14.1%) died from various causes, including 25 893 (4.4%) from CVD and 19 468 (3.3%) from cancer. In the fully adjusted model (model 4), participants engaging in MPA only, VPA only, or MVPA had 26% (HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.72–0.76]), 44% (HR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.54–0.59]), and 50% (HR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.48–0.51]) lower risks of all‐cause mortality, respectively, compared with those with no physical activity. A similar pattern was observed for CVD mortality, with reductions of 31% (HR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.67–0.72]), 51% (HR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.46–0.53]), and 58% (HR, 0.42 [95% CI, 0.40–0.44]), respectively. Cancer mortality reductions were 23% (HR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.74–0.80]), 41% (HR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.55–0.64]), and 48% (HR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.50–0.55]), respectively (Table 2).

Subgroup Analysis

In subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, race, region, marital status, educational level, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, self‐rated health status, and chronic disease presence, the protective effects of physical activity on all‐cause mortality were more pronounced among older adults, women, White individuals, individuals with higher educational levels, those with normal BMI, nonsmokers, and drinkers. Notably, the mortality benefits of physical activity were more evident among individuals with preexisting chronic diseases and those reporting poorer self‐rated health. Additionally, consistent with the main findings of this study, MVPA was associated with further reductions in all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality risks compared with VPA or MPA alone across nearly all subgroup populations (Tables S1–S3).

Sensitivity Analysis

To ensure the robustness of the study results, 5 sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, participants with chronic diseases at baseline were excluded. Second, deaths occurring within the first 2 years of follow‐up were removed. Third, analyses excluding both participants with chronic diseases at baseline and deaths within the first 2 years of follow‐up were performed. Fourth, adults aged ≥70 years were excluded to reduce potential bias from functional limitations or subclinical disease. Fifth, instead of weighting VPA as twice MPA (ie, VPA=2×MPA) as in the main analysis, we recalculated MVPA using the original, unweighted minutes of MPA and VPA. Sixth, landmark analyses at 2, 5, and 10 years were performed by restricting the cohort to participants who survived beyond each landmark and reinitiating follow‐up thereafter. Across all 6 sensitivity analyses, MVPA was consistently associated with the lowest risks of all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality (Tables S4–S6).

DISCUSSION

This study used a large, nationally representative cohort from the United States to investigate the associations of different physical activity forms (MPA, VPA, and MVPA) and all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. We found that adults engaging in mixed MVPA activities had lower all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality rates compared with those participating in MPA only or VPA only. Additionally, subgroup analyses across varying total physical activity volumes, frequencies, intensities, and demographic characteristics, as well as sensitivity analyses excluding deaths within the first 2 years of follow‐up and participants with chronic conditions at baseline, consistently supported the primary findings of the study.

Comparison With Other Studies

To our knowledge, although numerous studies have examined associations between physical activity intensity and health outcomes, 12 , 23 none has compared single‐intensity activities (MPA and VPA) with mixed activities (MVPA) in relation to all‐cause and cause‐specific mortality.

A key finding of this study is that adults engaging in mixed MVPA types had the lowest all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality rates compared with those performing single‐intensity physical activities. Recent systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have also highlighted that objectively measured low‐intensity physical activity can reduce CVD mortality risk. 24 , 25 Additionally, such activities are significantly associated with cardiometabolic factors, including waist circumference, triglyceride levels, insulin sensitivity, and metabolic syndrome. 24 Another study using accelerometer‐based objective measurements found that many (but not all) beneficial associations of light‐intensity physical activity with cardiometabolic biomarkers, including waist circumference, CRP (C‐reactive protein), triglycerides, and insulin, were independent of MVPA. 26 The authors suggest that a potential explanation lies in the differences in muscle fiber recruitment between varying physical activity intensities. MVPA predominantly activates fast‐twitch glycolytic muscle fibers, whereas LPA primarily engages slow‐twitch oxidative muscle fibers. 27 Additionally, MPA and VPA exert differing impacts on fitness and body composition. 28 Research by Drenowatz et al found a positive association between VPA and cardiorespiratory fitness, whereas MPA was beneficially linked to body fat percentage. These findings support the view that MPA and VPA may provide health benefits through distinct yet complementary physiological pathways. Beyond increasing energy expenditure, there might be complementary mechanisms that contribute to improved health outcomes.

Furthermore, consistent with previous findings, we observed that engaging in VPA only resulted in lower all‐cause mortality compared with MPA only. 12 , 29 This trend was consistent across all age groups and among adults with or without chronic conditions, suggesting that VPA provides greater health benefits than MPA. Previous studies have proposed several potential explanations, such as high‐intensity activities inducing greater physiological adaptations that improve function and capacity. 10 , 11 These include increased cardiac output and capillary density, enhanced endothelial function, and reduced peripheral resistance. 30 , 31 Moreover, high‐intensity physical activity offers greater benefits in maximizing oxygen uptake, improving blood pressure and lipid profiles, and exerting anti‐inflammatory effects. 32 , 33

Clinical and Public Health Implications

A key finding of our study is that adults engaging in mixed forms of physical activity (MVPA) had higher survival rates than those performing MPA only or VPA only. Furthermore, our results indicate that, under comparable conditions, VPA may confer greater health benefits than MPA. These findings carry important clinical and public health implications. In interpreting our results, it is essential to emphasize that MPA remains an effective, achievable, and safe option for most adults, and should continue to serve as the primary recommended target in public health messaging. VPA may provide additional benefits when feasible, but it may not be accessible or appropriate for all populations. From a public health perspective, promoting a balanced approach that incorporates both MPA and VPA, where appropriate and safe, may yield the greatest survival benefits.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

This study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare the mortality outcomes of exclusive MPA or VPA participation versus combined MVPA. Second, the use of a large, nationally representative prospective cohort enhances the generalizability of our findings. Third, consistent survey methods were applied across 22 years (1997–2018), allowing assessment of multiple potential confounders, including personal characteristics, lifestyle factors, and chronic diseases. Additionally, extensive sensitivity analyses were conducted, which consistently validated the robustness of the findings.

Despite its notable strengths, this study has certain limitations. First, physical activity was assessed using self‐reported questionnaires rather than objective accelerometer‐based methods, which may introduce recall bias. In addition, physical activity was assessed only at baseline; therefore, age‐related changes in activity patterns during follow‐up could not be captured, potentially leading to nondifferential exposure misclassification. Second, chronic disease information was self‐reported rather than medically confirmed, and the severity of chronic conditions was not captured. Third, 84 760 participants (12.62%) were excluded because of missing exposure or covariate data, and if excluded individuals differed systematically from those included, selection bias may have occurred.

Another important limitation relates to the assessment of physical activity. NHIS collected only leisure‐time physical activity and did not capture occupational, domestic, or transportation‐related activity. Because participation in these activity domains varies substantially across socioeconomic strata, this omission may lead to differential misclassification of total physical activity. Individuals with higher socioeconomic status, who typically accumulate more leisure‐time activity, are more likely to be correctly classified as active, whereas those with lower socioeconomic status may engage in significant unmeasured occupational or transport‐related activity and thus may be misclassified as inactive. Such differential misclassification would likely bias the estimated associations away from the null, potentially exaggerating the apparent benefits of leisure‐time physical activity. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations with leisure‐time rather than total physical activity.

A further limitation concerns unmeasured confounding. Although we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to reduce the likelihood of reverse causation, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. In particular, we were unable to adjust for cardiorespiratory fitness, a strong predictor of mortality that is closely related to participation in vigorous‐intensity and combined moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity. Because the NHIS lacks objective cardiorespiratory fitness assessments (eg, Maximum oxygen uptake or graded exercise testing), confounding by baseline cardiorespiratory fitness may persist even after extensive adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Moreover, cardiorespiratory fitness may serve both as a confounder, reflecting long‐term behavioral patterns and genetic predisposition, and as a mediator through which physical activity influences mortality risk. Given this limitation, the observed relationships should be interpreted as observational rather than causal, although the prospective design, temporal sequence, and consistency across sensitivity analyses strengthen confidence in the robustness of our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Engaging in VPA only was associated with lower mortality risk than MPA only, whereas a balanced combination of both intensities was associated with the greatest survival advantage. These findings support the continued promotion of physical activity guidelines in clinical and public health contexts. Where feasible, increasing engagement in VPA may offer additional benefits, although the contributions of MPA remain important and should not be underestimated. Overall, a balanced approach incorporating both VPA and MPA may confer the most substantial health benefits.
