Korean red ginseng saponins ameliorate metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis in mice by inhibiting caspase-11 and NLRP3 inflammasomes
Authors: Eojin Kim, Hee Ryeong Lim, Deok-Hyeong Choi, Young-Su Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough Korean red ginseng (KRG) has been investigated in various liver disorders, its role in Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the pharmacological effects of the KRG saponin fraction (KRGSF) in MASH and to elucidate its underlying mechanisms in hepatocytes, macrophages, and an in vivo MASH animal model.MethodsC57BL/6 mice were fed a methionine/choline-deficient (MCD) diet to induce MASH and were orally administered KRGSF at varying doses for 8 weeks. HepG2 cells were treated with free fatty acids (FFA) to induce steatosis and inflammation. RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with either palmitic acid (PA) or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to model inflammatory responses.ResultsKRGSF ameliorated hepatic steatosis in MASH mice by downregulating lipogenic gene expression. It further attenuated liver injury, macrophage infiltration, and fibrosis, concomitant with reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production, pyroptosis, and serum ALT and AST levels. Mechanistically, KRGSF suppressed the expression of caspase-11 and NLRP3 and inhibited the proteolytic activation of gasdermin D (GSDMD) in MASH mice. In vitro, KRGSF decreased lipid accumulation and lipogenic gene expression in FFA-treated HepG2 cells, while also reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and lowering ALT and AST levels. Additionally, KRGSF inhibited pro-inflammatory cytokine production in PA- or LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that KRGSF exerts protective effects against MASH by mitigating hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and injury, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for the prevention and treatment of MASH.Keywords: KRG saponins, MASH, Inflammasome, Hepatocyte, Macrophage

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), formerly termed nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), represents a progressive and severe form of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), characterized by excessive lipid accumulation, hepatic inflammation, and hepatocellular injury. The prevalence and incidence of MASH continue to rise in parallel with the global increase in metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [1,2]. Clinically, MASH is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, as affected individuals are at heightened risk of progressive liver damage, fibrosis and cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver transplantation, and liver-related mortality [1,2]. It is estimated that approximately 30–35% of the global population is affected by MASLD, of whom 1.5–6.5% progress to MASH, a stage marked by persistent hepatic injury [3]. Despite the urgent need for effective therapeutic interventions, drug development for MASH has been particularly challenging, with many candidate therapies failing to demonstrate satisfactory clinical outcomes [4].

Ginseng has been widely utilized as a traditional herbal remedy in East Asia, particularly in Korea, Japan, and China, due to its diverse pharmacological properties [5]. This plant contains a variety of bioactive constituents, including steroidal saponins and non-saponin compounds such as polysaccharides, glycosides, phytosterols, flavonoids, sesquiterpenes, and essential oils [6]. Among these, ginsenosides, the major saponin components, are recognized as the primary bioactive molecules responsible for regulating multiple physiological processes. Extensive studies have demonstrated that ginseng saponins exert a wide range of pharmacological activities across various disease conditions [7]. Notably, these compounds possess potent anti-inflammatory effects, contributing to the suppression of inflammatory responses and the amelioration of diverse inflammatory disorders [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Moreover, studies further indicates that ginseng saponins modulate the activation of inflammasomes, key intracellular signaling platforms that regulate inflammatory responses to pathogenic and cellular stress signals [7,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Interestingly, recent accumulating evidence suggests that ginseng saponins confer protective effects in chronic liver diseases, including MASLD, alcohol-related liver injury, hepatic fibrosis, chronic viral hepatitis, and HCC [8,[17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, despite these advances, their therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms in MASH remain insufficiently characterized. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of ginseng saponins on MASH and to elucidate the associated molecular mechanisms using both in vivo mouse disease model and in vitro cellular systems, including hepatocytes and macrophages.

Materials and methods

Materials

C57BL/6J mice (female, 8-week-old) were purchased at ORIENT BIO (Seongnam, Korea). Methionine/choline-deficient (MCD) diet was purchased at Saeronbio (Uiwang, Korea). KRG saponin fraction (KRGSF) and the information of its constituent composition (Table 1) were provided by Korea Ginseng Cooperation (Daejon, Korea). HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells were purchased at American Type Culture Collection (Manassa, USA). Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, Dulbecco's Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin solution, mouse and human TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β ELISA kits, oleic acid (OA), palmitic acid (PA), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; E. coli O111:B4), and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased at Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Oil Red O Stain kit, and triglyceride kit were purchased at Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Easy-BLUE™ reagent was purchased from iNtRON Biotechnology (Seongnam, Korea). M-MLV reverse transcriptase (RT) and primers for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were purchased at Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). qPCRBIO SyGreen Mix Lo-ROX was purchased at PCR Biosystems (London, UK). Quanti-LDH™ PLUS Cytotoxicity assay Kit was purchased at BIOMAX (Seoul, Korea). CheKineTM Micro Alanine Aminotransferase and Aspartate Aminotransferase Activity kit were purchased at Abbkine (Georgia, USA). Antibodies specific for F4/80 (ab300421), caspase-11 (ab180673), NLRP3 (sc-151015), GSDMD (ab219800), and β-actin (sc-517582) were purchased at Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Enhanced chemiluminescence (ELC) reagent was purchased from ELPIS-Biotech (Daejeon, Korea).

MASH mouse model

Mice were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, including regulated temperature and humidity, with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. MASH was induced by administering an MCD diet for 8 weeks, during which mice received oral treatment with KRGSF. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: a normal diet, an MCD diet, and an MCD diet supplemented with KRGSF at doses of 50 or 100 mg/kg (Fig. 1A). All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyonggi University (Approval No. 2024-001).

C57BL/6 mice were fed an MCD diet and orally administered KRGSF (50 or 100 mg/kg) every 24 h for 8 weeks. (B) Representative liver images, Oil Red O staining of liver sections and quantitative analysis of Oil Red O-positive areas. (C) Serum triglyceride levels. Primary hepatocyte preparation, cell culture, and induction of steatosis and inflammation

Primary hepatocytes were prepared from experimental mice using a previously described method [21] and stored in −80°C until use. HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 and DMEM, respectively, each supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 mg/ml), and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To induce steatosis and inflammation, HepG2 cells were treated with free fatty acids (FFA; 0.66 mM OA and 0.33 mM PA). In RAW264.7 cells, inflammation was induced by treatment with either PA (150 μM) or LPS (1 μg/ml).

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses of liver tissues

Liver specimens were collected from mice at week 8 and promptly fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h. Subsequent histological and immunohistochemical evaluations were conducted by the Korea Non-clinical Technology Solution Center (Seongnam, Korea). Paraffin-embedded liver sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Sirius Red, and F4/80 staining, whereas Oil Red O staining was carried out on frozen liver sections. The stained areas corresponding to Oil Red O, Sirius Red, and F4/80 were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Oil Red O staining

HepG2 cells were fixed in 10% formalin for 1 h and then treated with 60% isopropanol for 5 min. The cells were stained with Oil Red O solution for 30 min, washed 3 times, and examined microscopically. For quantitative assessment of intracellular lipids, the retained dye was extracted using 100% isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm.

Triglyceride measurement

Triglyceride levels in mouse serum and HepG2 culture supernatants were determined following incubation with an enzymatic assay solution containing lipoprotein lipase and glycerol kinase in N,N-bis(2-hydroxyethyl)-2-aminoethanesulfonic acid buffer at 37°C for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from primary hepatocytes, HepG2, and RAW264.7 cells, followed by reverse transcription using M-MLV RT to generate cDNA. Gene expression levels of lipogenesis-related factors and pro-inflammatory cytokines were quantified by qPCR using gene-specific primers (Table 2).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations in primary hepatocytes, mouse serum, and both culture media and whole cell lysates from HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells were determined by ELISA, with measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) measurement

LDH activity in mouse serum and RAW264.7 cell culture media was evaluated using an LDH assay reagent, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

ALT and AST measurement

Mouse blood and HepG2 cell culture media were incubated in Reagent I at 37°C for 30 min, followed by the incubation in Reagent II at 37°C for 20 min. After the final incubation in Reagent III, ALT and AST levels were immediately determined by measuring absorbance at 505 nm.

Western blot

Primary hepatocytes isolated from mouse liver were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 150 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40; 0.5% sodium deoxycholate; 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors by incubating on ice for 1 h. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected as whole cell lysates. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with target-specific primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The target proteins were detected using ECL reagent.

MTT assay

HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of KRGSF for 24 h, after which cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm in accordance with previously established protocols [22].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from 3 to 5 independent experiments (n ≥ 3 per group). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests or the Mann-Whitney test, depending on data distribution. A p-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01).

Results

KRGSF alleviated hepatic steatosis in MCD diet-induced MASH mice

The hepatoprotective potential of KRGSF against MCD diet-induced hepatic steatosis was investigated in mice. KRGSF resulted in a dose-dependent attenuation of lipid accumulation and steatotic changes in the livers of MASH mice (Fig. 1B). Consistently, KRGSF significantly decreased hepatic triglyceride levels (Fig. 1C) and downregulated the gene expression of key lipogenic regulators, including PPRAR-γ, CEBPA, FAS, and SREBP-1c in a dose-responsive manner (Fig. 1D–G). Although MASH induction led to marked reductions in both body weight (Fig. 1H) and liver weight (Fig. 1I), these parameters were not significantly altered by KRGSF (Fig. 1H and I). Similarly, the liver index remained unaffected following KRGSF administration (Fig. 1J). Collectively, these findings indicate that KRGSF alleviates hepatic steatosis in MASH without influencing overall body weight, liver weight, and liver index.

KRGSF alleviated hepatic inflammation and injury in MCD diet-induced MASH mice

The protective effects of KRGSF on hepatic inflammation and injury induced by a MCD diet were further examined in mice. KRGSF led to a dose-dependent reduction in liver damage, macrophage accumulation, and fibrotic alterations in MASH livers (Fig. 2A). In addition, KRGSF significantly decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, in both serum (Fig. 2B–D) and hepatic tissues (Fig. 2E–G) of MASH mice. In line with these observations, the hepatic mRNA expression of these cytokines was markedly suppressed by KRGSF (Fig. 2H–J). Furthermore, circulating indicators of cellular injury, such as LDH (Fig. 2K), ALT (Fig. 2L), and AST (Fig. 2M), were significantly reduced following KRGSF administration. Overall, these results suggest that KRGSF effectively attenuates hepatic inflammation and tissue damage in MASH.

KRGSF inhibited caspase-11 and NLRP3 inflammasomes in MCD diet-induced MASH mice

The underlying mechanisms by which KRGSF ameliorates hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and injury were further investigated in MASH mice. KRGSF resulted in a dose-dependent suppression of caspase-11 (Fig. 3A) and NLRP3 (Fig. 3B) in liver tissues. Given that GSDMD functions as a key downstream effector of both caspase-11 and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, and that its proteolytic cleavage generates the active N-GSDMD responsible for pore formation and pyroptotic cell death [7], the impact of KRGSF on GSDMD processing was subsequently assessed. KRGSF markedly inhibited GSDMD cleavage and reduced N-GSDMD generation in a dose-dependent manner in the livers of MASH mice (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that KRGSF attenuates inflammasome activation by suppressing both caspase-11- and NLRP3-mediated signaling pathways in MASH liver tissue.

KRGSF attenuated FFA-induced cellular steatosis in hepatocytes

The effects of KRGSF on hepatic steatosis were evaluated in vitro using HepG2 hepatocytes. KRGSF exhibited cytotoxicity at concentrations exceeding 200 μg/ml in HepG2 cells (Fig. 4A); therefore, concentrations of 100 and 200 μg/ml were selected for subsequent experiments. KRGSF dose-dependently reduced triglyceride production (Fig. 4B) and intracellular lipid accumulation (Fig. 4C) in FFA-stimulated HepG2 cells. In agreement with the in vivo findings (Fig. 4D–G), KRGSF significantly downregulated the gene expression of the lipogenic regulators, including PPRAR-γ, CEBPA, FAS, and SREBP-1c, in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4D–G). Collectively, these results indicate that KRGSF effectively alleviates lipid accumulation and steatotic changes in hepatocytes.

KRGSF attenuated FFA-induced inflammatory responses in hepatocytes

The anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects of KRGSF were further examined in vitro using FFA-stimulated HepG2 hepatocytes. KRGSF markedly decreased the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, both at the intracellular level (Fig. 5A and B) and in the culture supernatant (Fig. 5D and E). Interestingly, IL-1β was undetectable in both cell lysates and culture media under these conditions, precluding assessment of KRGSF-mediated modulation of this cytokine (Fig. 5C–F). In line with these findings, KRGSF significantly suppressed the gene expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines in FFA-stimulated HepG2 cells (Fig. 5G–I). Furthermore, KRGSF reduced the levels of hepatocellular injury markers, including ALT (Fig. 5J) and AST (Fig. 5K), in FFA-stimulated HepG2 cells. Overall, these results demonstrate that KRGSF mitigates inflammatory responses and cellular injury in hepatocytes.

KRGSF attenuated PA- or LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages

The anti-inflammatory activity of KRGSF was finally evaluated in vitro using RAW264.7 macrophages stimulated with PA or LPS. KRGSF exhibited cytotoxic effects at concentrations over 100 μg/ml (Fig. 6A), and cytotoxicity was also observed at 50 μg/ml under PA stimulation. Accordingly, lower concentrations (12.5 and 25 μg/ml) were selected for subsequent analyses. KRGSF significantly decreased LDH release in PA-stimulated RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 6B) and dose-dependently inhibited both the production (Fig. 6C and D) and gene expression (Fig. 6F and G) of pro-inflammatory cytokines under PA stimulation in RAW264.7 cells. Although KRGSF suppressed IL-1β gene expression (Fig. 6H), IL-1β secretion was not detectable in PA-stimulated cells (Fig. 6E). Similarly, KRGSF markedly reduced these pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Fig. 6I–K) and downregulated their corresponding gene expression levels (Fig. 6L–N) in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. Collectively, these results demonstrate that KRGSF effectively attenuates PA- and LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages.

Discussion

MASH represents an advanced stage of MASLD and develops in close association with metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [1,2]. While both MASLD and MASH are metabolic in origin, MASH is distinguished by pronounced hepatic inflammation and inflammation-mediated tissue injury, features that are less evident in MASLD [1,2]. Inflammation constitutes a fundamental component of innate immunity in response to pathogenic insults and cellular stress signals [23] and is generally characterized by two sequential phases: a priming step that prepares the inflammatory machinery and a triggering step that activates the response [24,25]. Previous studies have indicated that ginseng saponins exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating both the priming and triggering steps of inflammatory signaling [7,9,13,26]. Although a growing body of evidence supports the beneficial properties of ginseng saponins in MASLD and related metabolic diseases [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]], their specific effects and underlying mechanisms in MASH, particularly with respect to the triggering step of inflammation, remain insufficiently defined. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the pharmacological effects of KRG saponins and elucidate their mechanisms of action using an MCD diet-induced MASH mouse model, along with complementary in vitro systems involving hepatocytes and macrophages.

KRG saponins attenuated hepatic steatosis in MASH mice by decreasing lipid accumulation and suppressing the expression of lipogenic genes in liver tissues, without causing significant changes in body or liver weight. These findings suggest that KRG saponins exert protective effects against hepatic steatosis without evident toxicity at the tested doses. Notably, KRG saponins did not reverse the reductions in body and liver weights induced by MCD diet and consequently had no significant impact on the liver index. This observation is in line with previous reports demonstrating that the MCD diet leads to substantial weight loss [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]], indicating potential limitations of this model in accurately recapitulating human MASH pathology and highlighting the need for more representative experimental models that more closely mimics the metabolic context of the disease, such as animal models induced by high-fat/high-sugar diet, high-fat/high-cholesterol diet, amylin diet [34,38,39]. Given that MASH is characterized by pronounced inflammation and inflammation-associated tissue injury [40], the anti-inflammatory properties of KRG saponins were further investigated. KRG saponins markedly alleviated hepatic inflammation and tissue damage in MASH mice, as evidenced by reduced macrophage infiltration, decreased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, attenuation of pyroptotic cell death, and suppression of hepatic fibrosis. In addition, serum levels of liver injury markers, including ALT and AST [41], were significantly lowered following KRG saponin administration. These results demonstrate that KRG saponins exert pharmacological effects in MASH by mitigating hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammatory responses, and inflammation-driven liver injury.

Given the accumulating evidence implicating caspase-11 and the NLRP3 inflammasome in the pathogenesis of MASH [42,43], we next investigated whether KRG saponins modulate these signaling pathways in vivo. KRG saponin treatment markedly reduced the expression of both caspase-11 and NLRP3, and inhibited the cleavage of GSDMD, a key downstream effector of inflammasome signaling that mediates pore formation and pyroptotic cell death in hepatic tissues of MASH mice. Activation of inflammasomes is known to promote GSDMD processing, which in turn facilitates the maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [24]. Consistent with our earlier findings demonstrating reduced cytokine production following KRG saponin treatment, these findings suggest that KRG saponins attenuate hepatic inflammatory responses by suppressing caspase-11 and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby limiting the production and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the livers of MASH mice. Although direct extrapolation of animal doses to human intake remains challenging because of interspecies differences in pharmacokinetic properties, the doses used in the present study fall within the range commonly employed in preclinical investigations evaluating the pharmacological efficacy of KRG-derived extracts and nutraceutical agents. Accordingly, these dosing regimens may provide a relevant experimental framework for assessing the potential therapeutic utility of KRGSF in nutraceutical and KRG-associated therapeutic applications.

The in vivo findings were further substantiated by in vitro experiments using FFA-stimulated HepG2 hepatocytes and RAW264.7 macrophages stimulated with PA or LPS. In HepG2 cells, KRG saponins effectively attenuated cellular steatosis, as evidenced by reduced lipid synthesis and accumulation, along with downregulation of key lipogenic genes, consistent with the observations obtained in MASH mouse models. Furthermore, KRG saponins attenuated inflammatory responses and hepatocellular injury in FFA-stimulated HepG2 cells, as evidenced by reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and decreased ALT and AST levels. Notably, IL-1β was not detectable at both the intracellular and secreted level under these conditions. Although KRG saponins dose-dependently suppressed IL-1β gene expression, its basal expression level remained minimal, which likely accounts for the absence of detectable secretion. These findings suggest that IL-1β may have a limited contribution to FFA-induced inflammatory responses in HepG2 hepatocytes relative to other cytokines. Macrophages are central players of inflammatory responses [44,45], therefore, the anti-inflammatory effects of KRG saponins were further assessed in RAW264.7 cells stimulated with PA, which induces lipid accumulation [46] or LPS, a strong inflammatory stimulus [47]. KRG saponins significantly suppressed the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines under both conditions, indicating their capacity to modulate macrophage-driven inflammatory responses. Interestingly, while PA-induced IL-1β gene expression was reduced by KRG saponins, IL-1β secretion remained undetectable. This may be attributed to the requirement of inflammasome activation during the triggering phase for IL-1β maturation and release [24], whereas PA stimulation primarily represents a priming signal.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the MCD diet-induced MASH model does not fully recapitulate human disease, as it is associated with marked weight loss, altered lipid metabolism, and an artificial mechanism of liver injury that may lead to increased mortality and nutrient deficiency-related toxicity [48]. Second, the optimal doses of KRG saponins for in vivo experiments remain to be determined. Third, the present study is limited to experimental animal model and in vitro cell systems; therefore, further validation in MASH patients is necessary.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that KRG saponins confer protective effects against hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and inflammation-associated liver injury in MASH. These beneficial effects are, at least in part, mediated through inhibition of caspase-11 noncanonical and NLRP3 canonical inflammasome pathways. Overall, these findings broaden the understanding of the pharmacological actions of KRG saponins by identifying novel mechanisms and highlight their potential as a natural therapeutic strategy for MASH.
