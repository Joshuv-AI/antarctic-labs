Caregiver experiences of social isolation and loneliness in chronic kidney disease: systematic review of qualitative studies
Authors: Noshin Farzana, Chandana Guha, Nibras Jasim, Amanda Sluiter, Allison Jaure, Karine Manera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis qualitative systematic review aimed to describe the experiences and perspectives of loneliness and social isolation among informal caregivers of people with chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsTerms for caregivers, qualitative research, loneliness and social isolation and CKD were entered into MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and PsycINFO and searched from inception to May 2025. Qualitative studies that described social isolation and loneliness among caregivers of people with CKD were included in this review. Study characteristics were extracted into Microsoft Excel. Qualitative data from each study were imported into HyperRESEARCH and analysed using thematic synthesis.ResultsWe included 19 articles involving 598 caregivers of people with CKD from 28 countries. Four major themes with subthemes were identified: confined by the patient’s needs (social withdrawal due to unrelenting demands, separated from communities due to treatment, torn between work and caregiving responsibilities, foregoing social outings with family and friends, restricted by patient’s diet, feeling protective against infection risk); limited care assistance exacerbating isolation (inadequate familial support, absence of respite care, lacking accessible guidance from health professionals); disrupting relationships and social roles (sacrificing social needs and identity, family conflicts worsening isolation, withdrawing to avoid stigma and ridicule, grappling with hopelessness, reluctance to share struggles); and coping with support resources (connecting with other families, seeking assistance from support services, finding support through faith).ConclusionsCaregivers of people with CKD experience restricted social participation and loss of social roles and identity, which can exacerbate feelings of loneliness and social isolation. Support services are needed to prevent and address social isolation and loneliness in CKD caregivers.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420250637194.

Introduction

Caring for a person with chronic kidney disease (CKD) involves navigating complex medical regimens, emotional challenges and significant lifestyle disruptions associated with the burden of the disease and care. Caregivers of people with CKD take on a range of responsibilities, including managing treatment and dialysis schedules, dietary restrictions, medication adherence, medical appointments and providing physical and emotional support to the patient throughout the illness trajectory.13 The demanding nature of caregiving responsibilities can lead to loneliness and social isolation among caregivers, negatively impacting their physical and mental health and diminishing their quality of life.25

Loneliness and social isolation are increasingly recognised as significant public health issues due to their association with poor physical and mental health outcomes.69 Loneliness refers to the subjective negative feeling that arises when there is a discrepancy between actual and desired social relationships, whereas social isolation is defined as an objective lack of interactions with others.6 7 Both loneliness and social isolation are common but neglected experiences among informal caregivers, defined as people who provide continuous unpaid care to someone in need within a pre-existing relationship, such as a family member, friend or neighbour.68 Due to the ageing global population and the higher burden of chronic diseases, the number of informal caregivers is increasing, indicating the need to thoroughly explore caregiver experiences to mitigate poor outcomes for both caregivers and patients.6 10 11

Experiences of loneliness and social isolation among caregivers of people with CKD have received little attention both in research and clinical practice,2 4 12 which is critical for developing holistic care approaches.2 Furthermore, qualitative exploration is necessary to acquire a deeper understanding of the multifaceted nature of loneliness and social isolation among caregivers of people with CKD.13 Systematically synthesising qualitative studies can help uncover the complex and nuanced experiences of loneliness and social isolation among caregivers of people with CKD.

This study aimed to describe the experiences and perspectives of informal caregivers of people with CKD on loneliness and social isolation. This will inform the development of targeted interventions and support services aimed at alleviating the burden of caregiving and improving caregiver well-being.

Materials and methods

This systematic review followed the Enhancing Transparency of Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research framework14 (online supplemental file 1) and has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO_2025_CRD420250637194).

Selection criteria

Qualitative studies that described the experiences and perspectives of loneliness and social isolation among informal caregivers of people with CKD were included in this review. An informal caregiver is defined as a person who provides continuous unpaid assistance to someone in need within a pre-existing relationship, such as a family member, friend or neighbour.15 16 Studies were eligible if they included caregivers of patients of any age (children or adults) and at any stage of CKD. For the purposes of this review, CKDs were defined to include conditions characterised by a chronic or relapsing course, including idiopathic nephrotic syndrome, in line with clinical practice recommendations of the International Pediatric Nephrology Association.17 Non-English articles were screened for eligibility and translated using online translation tools. Mixed-method studies were included if they reported relevant qualitative data. We excluded quantitative only studies, abstracts, study protocols and review articles. Studies only including patients’ perspectives of loneliness and isolation were excluded.

Data sources and searches

Comprehensive searches were conducted across databases including MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and PsycINFO (online supplemental file 2). We also searched reference lists of relevant articles and reviews. Two reviewers (NF, KM) independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full texts for eligibility. In case of any conflicts, a third reviewer (CG) resolved them.

Appraisal of transparency of reporting

To assess the transparency of reporting of studies, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) framework was used for articles that collected data through interviews and focus group discussions, containing particular standards for the research team, methodology, context, analysis and interpretation.18 Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers using the framework (NF, NJ). If there were any discrepancies, they were resolved through discussion by the third reviewer (KM).

Analytical approach

We analysed the data using thematic synthesis following the approach described by Thomas and Harden.19 All articles were imported into HyperRESEARCH software (ResearchWare V.3.7.3). Author NF conducted line-by-line coding of relevant participant quotations, author’s commentary and author’s interpretations from the Results and Discussion section of each study and inductively identified concepts related to loneliness and social isolation. To remain within the scope of our aim, other relevant topics or experiences such as mental health or caregiver burden were only included if the data (quotes) directly related to loneliness or social isolation. Similar concepts were grouped into themes and subthemes. Two reviewers (KM, CG) read, reviewed and discussed the preliminary analysis to ensure that the full range and depth of the data were included in the analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and caregivers were involved across all stages of this review from inception to completion. One caregiver (author CG) provided strategic direction and input (including defining the need/rationale, scope and purpose) and was involved in the analysis. One patient (AS) participated in the data collection and analysis and contributed to revisions of the manuscript.

Results

Literature search and study description

The database search yielded a total of 135 records. After removing duplicates, 96 records were screened for title and abstract, of which 64 were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria. The full texts of the remaining 32 studies were assessed for eligibility, and 19 studies were included in the final review after excluding 13 articles which did not meet the inclusion criteria. Confined by the patient’s needs

Social withdrawal due to unrelenting demands

Some caregivers felt their personal and social lives were hampered by their multiple caregiving responsibilities, such as administering medication according to treatment schedules, assisting with dialysis exchanges, accompanying patients to appointments, doing daily chores and providing psychological support. The caregiving duties left no time for meeting with friends—“Tough keeping outside friendships with care needed”.3 Some mentioned that the progression of CKD and increase in care burden worsened their sense of loneliness—“I see her health deteriorating and she needs help with everything… I have no contacts with others and feel left alone”.20 The uncertainties and risk of the patient developing treatment complications meant that caregivers would refrain from planning social activities—“you do not know what new issues that you have to deal with and may come up from one day to next”.20 Caregivers felt exhausted by their duties and wanted to “cut off [from others] and just go into my cave”.11

Separated from communities due to treatment

Some caregivers of patients who had to relocate due to limited access to treatment facilities, particularly those living in rural areas, felt forcibly disconnected from their friends—“We see each other very much less than we used to. Partly because we moved from our old house where we had lived for 20 years, because it was too far from the dialysis unit”.21 Caregivers who had to relocate for an extended period felt anxious about remaining at distance from family and friends—“Originally it was 6 weeks [at the transplant centre]. And then it turned into 4 months.”22

Torn between work and caregiving responsibilities

The competing demands of work and caregiving responsibilities increased the sense of social isolation and loneliness of some caregivers—“On my lunch break, I might go and run some errands for him [my father] and some things that I may have to do, which cuts into my [personal/social] time”.11 Some caregivers, particularly mothers, had to consider leaving their jobs to care for their patient and thus felt even more isolated.

Foregoing social outings with family and friends

To keep up with the demands of caregiving, some had to miss out on travel and social outings with family and friends—“Traveling and touring and going to park have been closed and all my recreation has become only around the house”.23 Spousal caregivers missed connecting with their partners through activities such as travelling—“I used to travel a lot with my wife… but I can’t do these activities anymore”.20

Restricted by patient’s diet

Caregivers often avoided social gatherings due to the dietary restrictions of the patient—“You have to eat at home, because you know you’re going to get there, you can't [eat] anything that’s there, so sometimes you give up on some things”.24 Caregivers reflected on how others did not consider the dietary needs of the patient, highlighting their feelings of exclusion—“Sometimes we want to go somewhere, the people at work are going to have a barbecue and so on, but then he can’t drink alcohol…you don’t have a diet soda, because nobody cares about that, you get there, people season the meat with coarse salt…”.24

Feeling protective against infection risk

Some caregivers voluntarily socially isolated themselves and their family member with CKD as a protective behaviour—“I’m frightened to take him anywhere, even to see family, because he’ll get sick, so I can’t really”.22 Parental caregivers also expressed that they took extra precautions and vigilance to ensure the child’s safety in public places by “always sterilizing his hand before touching anything”25 which left them in a socially awkward position from the “strange looks from others”25 and led to a sense of social isolation. The sense of isolation deepened during the COVID-19 pandemic “COVID-19 and its restrictions have led to our decreased relationship with family and relatives; well, every person has the right to protect themselves and their family”.26

Limited care assistance exacerbating isolation

Inadequate familial support

Some caregivers exemplified that the lack of familial assistance in caregiving exacerbated loneliness and led to “the trap of isolation”.20 Some caregivers highlighted that family members were “not only uninvolved, but also unsupportive”25 towards caregiving efforts, including mothers who felt their husbands were “largely absent” in the care of their sick child. Frustration due to the unequal distribution of caregiving responsibilities among adult siblings of older patients was also captured—“I need family support from my siblings. They usually come and go with minimal help…”,20 illustrating how primary caregivers are often left isolated and unsupported.

Absence of respite care

Some caregivers expressed their clear and pressing requirement “for a break once or twice a week”1 from caregiving responsibilities, whether to go on vacation or attend social gatherings. However, they were unable to take the desired break, as “finding someone willing [to assist] is almost impossible”.27 Caregivers reported asking for short-term assistance from family and friends but were declined as others were ‘too busy’, meaning they missed out on attending important family milestones such as graduation ceremonies. Some caregivers also reported missing their own healthcare appointments due to lack of support or replacement care for the patient—“I have to cancel my own appointments and when I have time, I have nobody available to back me up or help me”.20

Lacking accessible guidance from health professionals

Some caregivers felt that they lacked sufficient information or education of how to care for the patient, leading to them feeling overwhelmed and disconnected from the healthcare system—“This all causes extra stress, it’s just a stress, deciding if you need to contact the doctor or even the ambulance. I have no background in anything medical, I don’t understand why there is no-one who could speak to you even to say yes or no give you advice on this”.28 Caregivers of home dialysis patients felt they had limited connection with the renal unit29 when accessing information about the care of the patient, particularly when transitioning from the in-centre training environment to home-based dialysis care. Some caregivers also highlighted the feelings of being geographically isolated from transplant teams after initiating home dialysis.22 Participants also expressed how the “availability of the 24/7 telephone helpline”1 to connect with their renal care unit helped to alleviate the sense of isolation.

Disrupting relationships and social roles

Sacrificing social needs and identity

Some caregivers often found themselves prioritising the needs of the care recipient over their own social and personal needs—“I keep postponing my personal stuff for later”.20 Many spousal caregivers experienced loneliness from the changes in relationship dynamics with their partner—“We became lost as a couple as focus turns to being medical providers and caregivers. Losing identity as a couple and not being able to go out as a couple”.3 Female spousal caregivers felt they were “more isolated than their husbands”,21 and their social activity schedules received least priority—“the days the patient dialysed accommodated his service club commitments, while the wife’s activities fell on dialysis days”.21 Young caregivers also expressed how caregiving changed their social need and priorities—“I can’t go with my friends sometimes [because it’s] more important to be at home with my mom. People my age really don’t have the same concerns”.22 They also felt a sense of exclusion—“they have their lives kind of mapped out. I can’t say I’ve really had a plan since I was 17 as we don’t know how my mum is going to be”.22

Family conflicts worsening isolation

Some caregivers expressed how family conflicts, particularly those due to the patient’s sickness, added to their burden of social isolation—“My family didn’t understand, and there were a lot of family conflicts. In those six months, the child was hospitalised every month, so how could it not be tiring?”.25 Some caregivers preferred isolation over family conflict—“Yes, my life has been socially isolated. Some of my relatives are difficult, so I stay away from them”.20

Withdrawing to avoid stigma and ridicule

Some caregivers refrained themselves from joining social gatherings as they “did not wish to meet with others to focus further on dialysis [of the patient]”.29 Some parental caregivers from China reported concealing their child’s condition and leaving their social circle out of fear of encountering ‘ridicule and gossip’25 from others. Self-stigmatisation among the parents due to the impact of the disease also intensified their isolation and enhanced their ‘social burden’.25

Grappling hopelessness

Some caregivers expressed anguish over loneliness from caregiving role—“I feel lonely; loneliness is killing me. My social life is almost non-existent even with my mother present; I need someone to talk to”.20Suffering from depression was also common among caregivers which also made them socially isolated—“I knew I was depressed. I didn’t want anybody around me. I didn’t want to talk to anybody”.11

Reluctance to share struggles

Some caregivers revealed the unique emotional burden that they felt others could not understand, leading them to avoid sharing their struggles “because no one can understand this particular hell we are both in”.27 Some caregivers obscured their feelings due to fear of judgement or appearing vulnerable—“I was scared to open up… it would make me look vulnerable or depressed”.3

Coping with support resources

Connecting with other families

Some caregivers mentioned, “Being introduced to other families helped me a lot to not feel alone!”.3Some participants mentioned that “the caregivers’peers are an informal source of learning”23 to deal with the caregiving roles and offer mutual support, which helped them to feel less isolated.

Seeking assistance from support services

Some caregivers encouraged other caregivers to seek support—“because you could get very isolated in the process, if you don’t reach out, you know what I mean?”.1 There were a variety of support services considered by the caregivers to alleviate social isolation and loneliness, including ‘group therapy’,1 ‘nursing help’20 and ‘self-help groups’1 tailored to the needs of the caregivers. Some caregivers also highlighted that ‘nursing help’3 and ‘mental health support’3 should be started from the beginning of the caregiving journey to make them better equipped to deal with social isolation and loneliness.

Finding support through faith

Some caregivers reported how their religious beliefs and spiritual communities played a significant role in providing emotional strength, acceptance and social support—“I think my biggest help comes from the Lord”.11 Strong connections within religious communities provided a sense of comfort, belonging and support—“On the odd occasion I cannot get to church there are numerous phone calls to see if everything is ok, that just shows how close the church family is”.28

Discussion

Due to the overwhelming demand for care, the caregivers of patients with CKD struggled to find energy to engage in social activities, strike a balance between the professional and caregiving roles, or go out with family and friends, leading to social isolation and loneliness. Caregivers also avoided public gatherings to protect themselves and their patients from external threats such as infection and social stigma. As a result of social isolation and loneliness, most of the caregivers often experienced utter despair, compromised self-identity and changes in social behaviour and relationship dynamics, which were exacerbated by inadequate family support and respite. However, caregivers found solace in connecting with other families facing similar challenges, obtaining necessary support services and seeking spiritual support.

In this study we found that caregiver experiences of social isolation and loneliness varied by the caregiver’s relationship to the patient. Spousal caregivers felt lonely from missing activities with their partners and deprioritising social life. Parental caregivers avoided social contact due to stigma related to their child’s illness, while adult children caring for older parents faced isolation from unsupportive family members, particularly from their siblings. Home dialysis caregivers reported feeling disconnected from their healthcare team and inadequately trained. Moving residences to access treatment facilities distanced rural caregivers from their peers. Young caregivers often felt excluded due to sacrificing work or study commitments.

The common negative influences of social isolation and loneliness among caregivers highlighted in this study include neglecting their own health and suffering from depression. Evidence from caregivers of individuals with other chronic diseases similarly reported negative impacts of social isolation and loneliness on psychological well-being,6 30 31 cognitive abilities (eg, concentration, decision making ability)31 32 and self-esteem.31 Collectively, these interrelated effects not only compromise the overall well-being of the caregivers but also reduce caregiving quality.33

This study highlighted the need to improve various aspects of care to reduce social and care burden of caregivers to alleviate social isolation and loneliness. Improving access to CKD care in remote areas can help caregivers and patients avoid social isolation resulting from relocation. For caregivers supporting home dialysis patients, thorough training and regular communication with the renal unit can help to reduce the burden of isolation. The review also revealed the need for respite care to enable caregivers to maintain their social and personal activities. Special consideration is needed for younger caregivers to help balance caregiving with their social lives.

The review underscored how connections with other caregivers and support groups made it easier for caregivers to share their experiences, which has been identified as a potential strategy to alleviate isolation and loneliness for caregivers of patients with other chronic diseases.34 It also emphasised the importance of support services, such as counselling, peer connections, self-help groups, faith-based connections and caregiving training, to strengthen the resilience of caregivers.35 Caregivers also highlighted that accessing these resources at the time of diagnosis can be especially helpful to cope with social isolation and loneliness in an effective manner.

There have been several interventions developed to support psychosocial well-being for caregivers of patients with CKD.7 31 36 37 However, none of the interventions had been developed to directly target social isolation and loneliness. Moreover, there is limited information available on existing interventions for specific groups, such as younger caregivers. Further research is needed to develop culturally appropriate and context-specific interventions to prevent or reduce the burden of social isolation and loneliness among caregivers of people with CKD. It is also worth exploring how supporting peer connection can be incorporated into interventions that ameliorate isolation and loneliness among caregivers.

Strength and limitations

In this study, we performed a comprehensive search and systematic assessment of study reporting. The inclusion of non-English articles and articles from developing and developed countries contributed to the diversity of the findings and enhanced the transferability of the findings to other settings.38 However, we acknowledge some potential limitations. Only a few of the included studies focused specifically on loneliness and social isolation. Furthermore, the included studies did not define or distinguish the concepts of loneliness and social isolation or assess the levels of loneliness or social isolation among the participants. This limits our ability to analyse the concepts of loneliness and social isolation separately. There are qualitative methodologies and theoretical perspectives varied across the included studies, which may have an impact on interpretations and comparisons. Future studies that can accurately assess and explore these distinct concepts among caregivers of patients with CKD would be of interest.

Conclusions

As a result of relentless caregiving demands, caregivers of patients with CKD experience social isolation and loneliness in multidimensional ways. Considering the detrimental effects of loneliness and social isolation among caregivers of people with CKD, it is important to emphasise and create awareness of the physical and psychological well-being of caregivers. Comprehensive support plans and tailored interventions to address the specific emotional and social needs of caregivers with CKD would be beneficial in reducing social isolation and loneliness.
