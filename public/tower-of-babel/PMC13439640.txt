Catheter malposition leading to superior vena cava perforation: A rare but serious complication of hemodialysis access
Authors: Mavia Najam, Zara Muhammad Nayyar, Omar Khyatt, Haris Iftikhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Central venous catheterization through the internal jugular vein is a standard procedure for hemodialysis access in end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Although generally safe, serious and life-threatening complications can occur.Case presentation We present a rare case of superior vena cava (SVC) perforation during tunneled dialysis catheter (PermCath) insertion in a patient with chronic renal failure. The injury led to mediastinal hematoma, bilateral hemothoraces, and hemorrhagic shock, requiring emergency thoracotomy and surgical repair.Discussion Perforation of the SVC is an exceptionally rare but critical complication of central venous catheterization. Early symptoms are often nonspecific, making diagnosis challenging. Imaging modalities such as ultrasonography and urgent computed tomography (CT) are vital for detecting extravascular catheter placement and associated mediastinal bleeding.Conclusion Early recognition and coordinated management were crucial in stabilizing the patient. Following emergency thoracotomy and surgical repair, the patient achieved hemodynamic recovery and was successfully weaned from ventilatory support. This case highlights the importance of high clinical vigilance, appropriate imaging, and multidisciplinary coordination in the early recognition and management of catheter-related vascular injuries.

1. INTRODUCTION

Tunneled central venous catheters (CVCs), such as PermCath, are commonly used for long-term hemodialysis access in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), particularly when arteriovenous fistulas are unavailable or dysfunctional.1 The right internal jugular vein (RIJV) is often preferred because of its straight course into the superior vena cava (SVC) and lower risk of catheter malposition and thrombosis.2–4

Despite being a routine procedure, CVC insertion is associated with potentially serious complications, including arterial puncture, pneumothorax, hemothorax, cardiac tamponade, and vascular injury. Reported complication rates range from 3.3% to 19%, depending on patient characteristics, the site of insertion, and operator experience.5–7 SVC injury, although exceedingly rare, can lead to catastrophic complications such as mediastinal hematoma, hemothorax, hemorrhagic shock, and death.8,9

Risk factors for vascular injury include repeated catheterizations, central venous stenosis, and a prolonged hemodialysis history, all of which may predispose the SVC to injury or rupture during catheter insertion.3,10 Prompt recognition and management are essential to reduce mortality, and treatment options may include conservative measures, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS),11,12 open surgical thoracotomy repair, or endovascular intervention.13

We report a rare and life-threatening complication of PermCath insertion in a hemodialysis patient, requiring emergency thoracotomy and surgical repair.

The patient was managed in a public tertiary care center within Qatar’s advanced healthcare system.

2. CASE PRESENTATION

A 46-year-old female with a long-standing history of hypertension was diagnosed with ESRD five years ago. She initially initiated hemodialysis via a CVC, and subsequently, a left arteriovenous (AV) fistula was created in November 2020, which was then used for maintenance hemodialysis.

In June 2025, she presented to the Emergency Department with a blocked left AV fistula of more than two weeks’ duration. She was also due for her scheduled dialysis session on the same day. Two weeks earlier, while on vacation in her home country, a temporary femoral catheter had been inserted to facilitate dialysis. However, due to the temporary nature of the femoral line and the need for long-term vascular access, a decision was made to proceed with the insertion of a PermCath, followed by an AV fistulogram and thrombectomy. After receiving hemodialysis via the femoral catheter in the Emergency Department, the patient was admitted to the medical ward for elective PermCath insertion scheduled two days later.

The PermCath was inserted via the RIJV under fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 1), and the patient was transferred to the ward in a stable condition. In the ward, catheter flushing was attempted to initiate dialysis, but the flow was poor, raising suspicion of central venous stenosis. During the procedure, the patient experienced chest discomfort and shortness of breath, although her hemodynamic status remained stable.

The following day, she underwent an AV fistulogram with thrombectomy and stenting. Due to associated redness, swelling, and pain at the fistula site, it was decided to rest the fistula before resuming its use for dialysis.

As the patient continued to experience chest pain following PermCath insertion, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and a chest X-ray were performed. Thoracic ultrasound revealed a pleural effusion (Figure 2), while the chest X-ray showed a widened mediastinum (Figure 3). A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) thorax (Figure 4) confirmed an anterior mediastinal hematoma measuring 4.4×2.8×11.4 cm, bilateral hemothoraces, and malposition of the catheter tip, which was found outside the vessel in the anterior mediastinum.

Within six days of PermCath insertion, the patient’s hemoglobin gradually dropped from 12 g/dL to 6 g/dL (Figure 5). Initially, she remained hemodynamically stable (Figure 6); however, on day 6, she was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU) in hemorrhagic shock and received two units of packed red blood cell transfusions.

Subsequent interventions performed on the same day included thoracoscopy and angiography, which evacuated approximately 1 L of hemothorax; however, no active extravasation was identified. The catheter tip could not be visualized. The PermCath was left in situ to allow tract maturation. The patient remained intubated and was transferred back to the SICU with two chest drains and vasopressor support. Within a few hours after thoracoscopy in the SICU, her condition deteriorated, with increasing vasopressor requirements, a further decline in hemoglobin levels, and ongoing blood transfusion needs. Chest tube output exceeded 1.8 L of frank blood.

An emergency thoracotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, a large right-sided hemothorax (approximately 1 L) was evacuated. Bleeding from the apical lung parenchyma was controlled with stapling and suturing. A mediastinal hematoma was also evacuated. The catheter tip was found to have perforated the SVC. The catheter was withdrawn, and the SVC injury was repaired with 5-0 Prolene sutures, with no residual bleeding. A chest tube was placed in the right pleural cavity, and the thoracotomy was closed in layers. This surgical intervention took place eight days after the initial CVC insertion.

Following successful surgical repair of the SVC, the patient was extubated on the fourth day and gradually resumed oral intake. During her SICU course, she required reintubation four days after initial extubation due to increased oxygen requirements and underwent proning. She was extubated two days later and remained hemodynamically stable. On the 12th postoperative day, she was transferred to the ward in stable condition and continued to receive follow-up care from nephrology for ongoing hemodialysis.

3. DISCUSSION

Central venous catheterization, particularly via the RIJV, is a common and often essential procedure in patients with ESRD requiring hemodialysis. The RIJV is generally preferred because of its short and straight anatomical course to the SVC, which reduces the risk of catheter malposition compared to other access routes.9,14 Ultrasound guidance has further reduced the complications such as arterial puncture, pneumothorax, and catheter malposition, especially when performed by experienced providers.15 However, mechanical complications—though rare—can still occur and may be life-threatening if unrecognized or poorly managed.

This case illustrates a rare but serious complication: SVC perforation resulting in bilateral hemothorax and mediastinal hematoma following tunneled dialysis catheter placement. While most hemothorax cases are associated with arterial injuries, venous perforations—particularly those caused by dilators—are much less commonly reported and can have devastating consequences.2,3 The stiff texture and rigidity of the dilators can lead to more severe vascular trauma than needles or catheters, especially when force is applied against resistance during advancement.16

Despite using ultrasound guidance and limiting the procedure to a single puncture, this complication likely resulted from mechanical trauma caused by the forceful advancement of the dilator, which failed to follow the guidewire’s path. Prior central venous access, chronic hypertension, obesity, and anatomical variation—common among hemodialysis patients—may have increased the susceptibility to vascular injury.5,17 The perforation at the SVC or the brachiocephalic–SVC junction has the potential to cause rapid hemodynamic compromise or cardiac tamponade.

The patient’s symptoms—dyspnea, chest pain, and blood-stained catheter aspiration—prompted immediate imaging. Although chest X-ray and bedside ultrasound raised early concern, the CT scan played a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis. CT imaging is particularly useful for detecting catheter-related vascular perforations, hemothorax, and lung collapse with high sensitivity.3,18

Multidisciplinary collaboration—nephrology, vascular and thoracic surgeries, and critical care—was essential for coordinating the patient’s management. Emergency thoracotomy revealed that the catheter tip had breached the SVC wall, with associated bilateral hemothorax. Surgical management included catheter removal, direct SVC repair using nonabsorbable sutures, pleural cavity irrigation, and chest tube drainage. Postoperative care focused on preventing infection, monitoring for rebleeding, and ensuring respiratory recovery, especially given the potential for compromised lung function.19

This case reinforces several key learning points. First, pre-procedural planning, including neck ultrasound and anatomical risk assessment that helps identify venous patency, stenosis, anatomical variants, and technical challenges, is crucial. Second, the entire CVC placement process—not just needle insertion—should be guided by real-time imaging such as fluoroscopy to prevent and detect guidewire kinking or malposition. Although ultrasound and fluoroscopy are essential for confirming venous access and guiding catheter advancement, they do not reliably visualize the vessel wall, and catheter deviation or injury may still go undetected.20 Third, awareness of subtle signs such as resistance during dilator advancement, new onset of chest pain, or unexplained hypotension post-insertion should prompt urgent evaluation for vascular injury. Lastly, it must be emphasized that although the incidence of SVC perforation is reported to be as low as 0.5%, it remains a life-threatening complication, and early recognition is key to survival.21,22 Risk factors include left-sided catheterization, obesity, prior CVCs, and catheter stiffness or angulation. Even with blood aspiration or ultrasound guidance, malposition or erosion may still occur, emphasizing the limitations of conventional confirmation methods and the need for high suspicion in symptomatic cases.2,23

4. CONCLUSION

This case contributes to the existing literature by emphasizing the role of operator vigilance, imaging guidance, multidisciplinary teamwork, and surgical readiness in managing rare but high-stakes vascular complications. SVC perforation following CVC placement, though rare, can be life-threatening. This case underscores the importance of meticulous technique during dilator advancement, early recognition of warning signs, and an understanding that fluoroscopic guidance, while essential, does not eliminate the risk of vascular injury, thereby emphasizing the need for post-procedural vigilance. A high index of suspicion and prompt multidisciplinary interventions—including emergency thoracotomy and surgical SVC repair—were crucial in preventing fatal outcomes in our patient, who ultimately made a good clinical recovery. Operator awareness, especially in high-risk patients with prior catheterizations or vascular anomalies, is essential to ensure procedural safety and improve survival.
