Output Corridor Impulsive Control of First-order Continuous System with Non-local Attractivity Analysis
Alexander Medvedev, Anton V. Proskurnikov

Abstract
This paper addresses the design of an impulsive controller for a continuous scalar time-invariant linear plant that constitutes the simplest conceivable model of chemical kinetics. The model is ubiquitous in process control as well as pharmacometrics and readily generalizes to systems of Wiener structure. Given the impulsive nature of the feedback, the control problem formulation is particularly suited to discrete dosing applications in engineering and medicine, where both doses and inter-dose intervals are manipulated. Since the feedback controller acts at discrete time instants and employs both amplitude and frequency modulation, whereas the plant is continuous, the closed-loop system exhibits hybrid dynamics featuring complex nonlinear phenomena. The problem of confining the plant output to a predefined corridor of values is considered. The method at the heart of the proposed approach is to design a stable periodic solution, called a 1-cycle, whose one-dimensional orbit coincides with the predefined corridor. Conditions ensuring local and global attractivity of the 1-cycle are established. As a numerical illustration of the proposed approach, the problem of intravenous paracetamol dosing is considered.



I
INTRODUCTION

Impulsive feedback constitutes an attractive paradigm in applications where corrective action is executed seldom and a discrete (sampled-data) controller with synchronous sampling is excessive or infeasible. Open- and closed-loop impulsive control is widely used in, e.g., discrete dosing of chemicals and drugs, mechanical systems with impacts
[
9
]
, spacecraft control
[
15
]
, temperature regulation, and ecological and environmental systems
[
8
]
. More spectacular application examples are impulsive altitude regulation in hot air balloons by means of “burner duty cycle” and the actuation of pumped hydrofoils through load-unload body movement. Because the plant evolves in an open loop between impulses, impulsive control is most effective for systems whose dynamics are sufficiently slow over the inter-impulse intervals.

Since process control traditionally aims at stabilizing the measured output at a predefined setpoint, the literature on impulsive control has naturally emphasized stability and dissipativity properties, whereas the design of sustained periodic regimes has received less attention.
Periodic solutions are typically characterized by fixed points of a discrete (Poincaré or return) map
[
8
,
6
]
.
Then designing a feedback that sustains a desired periodic solution corresponds to the stabilization of a fixed point.

In process control, 90–95% of all plant models are linear of first order, possibly being augmented with a static nonlinearity. A similarly, in pharmacometry, pharmacokinetics (PK) of more than 90% of drugs are adequately described by a single-compartment model with elimination of first-order
[
3
]
. Naturally, the pharmacodynamics (PD) are nonlinear, and generally, well captured by Hill functions.

Studying scalar continuous dynamics under pulse-modulated feedback, which gives rise to a hybrid system of effective order two, significantly simplifies the mathematical setup without rendering the problem trivial. The nonlinear dynamics of such hybrid systems were investigated in
[
16
]
, where a wide range of complex phenomena were demonstrated, including high-period cycles and deterministic chaos. The model in
[
16
]
stems from studies of the so-called Impulsive Goodwin’s Oscillator (IGO), which was motivated by endocrine regulation models
[
1
]
and builds on the continuous Goodwin’s oscillator
[
7
]
, a widespread model in mathematical biology. The IGO model has been generalized to systems with an arbitrary number of compartments; however, the relevant works
[
2
,
13
]
mainly focus on the existence and local orbital stability of special periodic solutions (1-cycles). Low-order dynamics in the single-compartment case not only introduce particularities in the nonlinear dynamics analysis
[
16
]
but also open the perspective of solving some impulsive control design problems explicitly.

An example of such a problem, important for drug dosing, is the design of a controller that maintains the output of the plant within a predefined corridor. The standard approach to solving this problem for a general dosing system is model predictive control
[
12
]
. An alternative method, proposed in
[
10
]
, is based on exciting and stabilizing a special periodic solution of the impulsive system, called 1-cycle. The approach in
[
10
]
is based on the properties of the three-dimensional IGO (in particular, on the extreme points of its impulse response) and cannot be directly applied to the simplest single-compartment dynamics. Furthermore, in the general case, only local stability of the 1-cycle is guaranteed.

Contributions

The contributions of this paper are as follows. First, we formulate the output corridor control problem for scalar (single-compartment) kinetics and exploit its special structure to obtain an analytic expression for the periodic solution that tightly satisfies the corridor constraint – that is, the orbit endpoints coincide with the corridor bounds. Second, we derive conditions for the global attractivity of this solution. Third, we show that a proper design of the impulsive feedback guarantees super-exponential convergence in a neighborhood of the solution, which in practice is indistinguishable from finite-time convergence. Finally, we apply the results to paracetamol dosing and illustrate the theoretical findings by numerical simulations.

\displaystyle x(t_{n}^{+})

\displaystyle=x(t_{n}^{-})+\lambda_{n},\quad t_{n+1}=t_{n}+T_{n},

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=\Phi(x(t_{n}^{-})),\;\lambda_{n}=F(x(t_{n}^{-})),\quad n=0,1,\ldots

To explicitly restrict the domain in which the solutions of closed-loop system (

0<\Phi_{1}\leq\Phi(\cdot)\leq\Phi_{2},\quad 0<F_{1}\leq F(\cdot)\leq F_{2},

,
\Phi_{2}

The modulation functions are assumed to be locally Lipschitz, and hence differentiable almost everywhere and absolutely continuous on each finite interval. In the standard IGO design, the monotonicity conditions are imposed
[

]
requiring that

is

and
\Phi(\cdot)
is
non-decreasing

This negative feedback mechanism keeps the controlled output in the vicinity of the stationary solution while operating exclusively through positive signals. As shown below, this feedback property is not necessary for the stabilization of the periodic orbit, although it ensures some additional features of the closed-loop system. We thus present the theory separately for general controller (
2

The modulation functions

,

1
), (
2
). As discussed in Section

.

In the present study, the following control problem is addressed.

Output corridor impulsive control problem

Given the plant (
1
),
design the modulation functions

III
Background results

In this section, we collect several technical results used throughout the paper. Henceforth we assume that
F,\Phi
satisfy global bounds (
3
) but yet are not necessarily monotone.

Ultimate bounds on the solutions

The solution to (

\displaystyle x(t)=\e^{a(t-t_{n})}x_{n}\quad\text{whence}

\displaystyle x_{n}<x(t)<\e^{-a(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}x_{n}=\e^{-a\Phi(x_{n-1})}x_{n}.

(5)

As a consequence, the following ultimate bounds on the solutions can be derived.

Proposition 1
(
[
16
]
)

For any admissible initial conditions

, the solution to (
1
) under pulse-modulated feedback (
2
) is ultimately bounded from above and below

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\inf x(t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{F_{1}}{\e^{-a\Phi_{2}}-1},

(6)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup x(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F_{2}}{1-\e^{a\Phi_{1}}}.

(7)

As will be shown below in Lemma
2
, the estimates from Proposition
1
can be substantially tightened in the case of the one-dimensional IGO with monotone nonlinearities
[
16
]
. In view of Proposition
1
, the bounds for the modulation functions should be chosen compatible with the corridor (
4

\frac{F_{1}}{\e^{a\Phi_{2}}-1}\leq x_{\min}^{*}<x_{\max}^{*}\leq\frac{F_{2}}{1-\e^{-a\Phi_{1}}},

since otherwise the full corridor cannot be utilized.

Another immediate consequence of (
5
) is that, under controller (
2

t_{n}\to\infty
, and thus the corridor condition (
4
) at steady state is equivalent to the relation

x_{\min}^{*}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}x(t_{n}^{-})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}x(t_{n}^{+})\leq x_{\max}^{*}.

(8)

The return map and 1-cycles

The satisfaction of the corridor condition thus depends only on the sequence of sampled (pre-impulse) states

x_{n}\triangleq x(t_{n}^{-}),\quad n=0,1,\ldots

which, in accordance with (
1
), (
2
), obey the recursion

x_{n+1}=Q(x_{n}),

(9)

where the
return map
is defined as follows:

Q(x)\triangleq\e^{a\Phi(x)}(x+F(x)).

(10)

Recall that
\Phi
and
F
are continuous and satisfy (
3
), entailing that
Q(0)>0
and
Q(x)<x
for sufficiently large

.
Hence,

admits a fixed point (possibly, non-unique)

x_{*}=Q(x_{*})>0.

(11)

The fixed point gives rise to a periodic solution of system (
1
), (
2
) with
x_{n}=x(t_{n}^{-})\equiv x_{*}
,
x(t_{n}^{+})\equiv x_{*}+\lambda
and
t_{n+1}-t_{n}\equiv T

and
\lambda\triangleq F(x_{*})
.
Such a periodic solution to system (
1

. As discussed in
[

,
2
]

Note that the period

x_{*}=\frac{\lambda\e^{aT}}{1-\e^{aT}}.

On the other hand, for an arbitrary pair

, the state in (

\lambda=F(x_{*})
.
For brevity, we call the pair

the

The corridor-spanning 1-cycle

In the output corridor impulsive control problem, the parameters are calculated from the corridor bounds and specify one point (i.e., the fixed point in (
12
)) on the modulation functions
F,\Phi
, ensuring the desired steady-state behavior. The procedure follows immediately from the result below.

Proposition 2

Let closed-loop system (
1
), (
2
) exhibit a 1-cycle with the parameters
(T,\lambda)
. Then, over each period of this 1-cycle, the state lies between the values

\inf x(t)=x_{*}=\frac{\lambda\e^{aT}}{1-\e^{aT}},\quad\sup x(t)=x_{*}+\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{1-\e^{aT}}.

Proof:

The proof is straightforward by applying inequalities (
5
) to the 1-cycle with parameters
(T,\lambda)
and using the fact that
x_{n}=x_{*}
and
x(t_{n}^{+})=x_{n}+F(x_{n})=x_{*}+\lambda
, where
x_{*}
is specified by (
12
).
∎

Given corridor (
4
), a 1-cycle is called

if its orbit spans the corridor, that is, if its parameters satisfy the equations:

x_{\min}^{*}=\frac{\lambda\e^{aT}}{1-\e^{aT}},\quad x_{\max}^{*}=\frac{\lambda}{1-\e^{aT}}.

(13)

Obviously, equations (
13
) are uniquely solvable for any

and every pair
x_{\max}^{*}>x_{\min}^{*}>0

\displaystyle T=\frac{\ln x_{\min}^{*}-\ln x_{\max}^{*}}{a},\quad\lambda=x_{\max}^{*}-x_{\min}^{*}.

IV
Global and Local Stability of 1-cycle

In this section, we investigate the global and local attractivity properties of the corridor-spanning 1-cycle. Note that convergence to the 1-cycle is fully determined by the sequence of sampled states

Proposition 3

Consider a solution of closed-loop system (
1
), (
2
), and assume that
x_{n}\to x_{*}=Q(x_{*})

x_{*}
corresponds to the 1-cycle with parameters

. Then, the following statements hold as

(ii)

(iii)

for every intermediate time

1

Notice that, in this case,

x(t_{n}+\tau)\xrightarrow{}\e^{a\tau}(x_{*}+F(x_{*})).

In particular, if the 1-cycle is corridor-spanning, then the solution satisfies the corridor condition in (

Proof:

The proof of (i)–(iii) follows in a straightforward way from equations (

To prove the last statement, it suffices to notice that (

Global convergence via the contraction property

The following lemma is a straightforward consequence of the Banach contraction principle

Lemma 1

Let there exist a constant

|Q^{\prime}(x)|\leq q<1\quad\text{for almost all}\quad x\geq 0.

Then the 1-cycle in closed-loop system (

|x_{n}-x_{*}|=O(q^{n})

Proof:

Since

is absolutely continuous on any interval

|Q(x_{2})-Q(x_{1})|\leq\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}|Q^{\prime}(x)|\,dx\leq q(x_{2}-x_{1}),

that is,
Q
is a
q
-contraction on
[0,\infty)
. The Banach contraction principle entails that recursion (
9
) converges exponentially to
x_{*}

as
n\to\infty
; in particular,
x_{*}
is the unique fixed point on

. Now, in virtue of Proposition
3
every solution converges to the 1-cycle,
associated with

.
∎

Exponential and super-exponential local convergence

It is well known that the local exponential stability of the 1-cycle reduces to the local exponential stability of the fixed point

2

. It should be noted, however, that the general results for multidimensional case ensure only orbital stability and rely on differentiability of

Proposition 4

Assume that contraction condition (

is invariant under discrete-time dynamics (

x_{0}=x(t_{0}^{-})\in I

Proof:

The invariance is straightforward, since

entails that
|x-x_{*}|\leq\delta
, whence

|Q(x)-x_{*}|=|Q(x)-Q(x_{*})|\leq q|x-x_{*}|=q\delta<\delta.

Hence,
Q(x)\in I
, i.e.,

is invariant under

, and therefore
x_{n}\in I
for all
n
. By the Banach contraction principle,
x_{n}\to x_{*}
exponentially, i.e., the solution is attracted to the 1-cycle in accordance with Proposition

∎

Note that if

is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of
x_{*}
and
|Q^{\prime}(x_{*})|<1
, the condition of Proposition

can always be satisfied by choosing

Q^{\prime}(x)\to Q^{\prime}(x_{*})
as

The fastest convergence rate corresponds to the case where

is “flat” at the point

|Q(x)-x_{*}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\xi\in I}|Q^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|(x-x_{*})^{2}.

This motivated the following proposition, establishing ultrafast super-exponential

Choice of initial impulse time

In drug dosing applications (see Section
VI
), drug administration is typically initiated by a bolus dose calculated based on a combination of patient-specific factors (weight, age, organ function) and independent of patient response. Control algorithm (
2
) fires the first impulse at time
t_{0}
that can be regarded as an additional design parameter. For instance, if
x(0)>x_{*}
, the controller can wait until the solution of (
1
) reaches
x_{*}
. In reality, due to model and measurement uncertainty,
x(t)
is in a small neighborhood of
x_{*}
.

In the matter of fact, the distinction between local and global attractivity is negligible, as one can guarantee that
x_{0}=x(t_{0}^{-})
is close to
x_{*}
(see the Case 1 in Section
VI
). Disregarding measurement and modeling errors, one can formally guarantee finite-time (deadbeat) convergence to the 1-cycle.
However, medical regimens often impose a maximum time interval between consecutive doses (naturally captured by the bound
\Phi_{2}
in (
3
)), which the initial time
t_{0}
cannot exceed. Therefore, one cannot initiate the pulse-modulated feedback at the point
x_{*}
, and the convergence rate to the 1-cycle should be considered.

V
The Negative Feedback Case

In this section, we consider the one-dimensional IGO system introduced in
[
16
]
, characterized by a monotonically non-increasing
F
and a monotonically non-decreasing
\Phi
. As discussed previously, this choice of modulation functions implements negative feedback from the state to both the amplitude and frequency of the pulses.

This special case enjoys additional properties; in particular, it is possible to substantially tighten the bounds in Proposition
1
, which in some situations leads to an alternative global attractivity criterion.

A tightened estimate of the solution

Introduce the function

\Psi(x)\triangleq\frac{F(x)\e^{a\Phi(x)}}{1-\e^{a\Phi(x)}}=\frac{Q(x)-\e^{a\Phi(x)}x}{1-\e^{a\Phi(x)}}.

Since
F
is decreasing, while the assumptions

and
\Phi
increasing
imply that
\e^{a\Phi(x)}
is (monotonously) decreasing and
1-\e^{a\Phi(x)}>0
is increasing, it follows that
\Psi
is
decreasing on
[0,\infty)
. Hence, the composition
\Psi\circ\Psi
is continuous and increasing.

The set of solutions to the equation

x=\Psi(\Psi(x)),\qquad x\geq 0,

(16)

is closed. Moreover,
\Psi
maps
[0,\infty)
into a bounded interval:

\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[\Psi(\infty),\Psi(0)]\subseteq\left[\frac{F_{1}\e^{a\Phi_{2}}}{1-\e^{a\Phi_{2}}},\,\frac{F_{2}\e^{a\Phi_{1}}}{1-\e^{a\Phi_{1}}}\right],

where
F_{1},F_{2}
and
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
are the bounds introduced in (
3
), and
\Psi(\infty)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\Psi(x)
.
Hence, all solutions of (

Therefore, the solution set of (
16
) is compact, and in particular
the minimal and maximal solutions exist. Denote them by
m_{*}>0
and
M_{*}\geq m_{*}
,

The following lemma provides a simple iterative procedure for

\Phi(\cdot)
is non-decreasing and

The minimal and maximal solutions

\begin{gathered}m_{0}=0,\qquad m_{n}=(\Psi\circ\Psi)(m_{n-1}),\quad n\geq 1,\\

m_{*}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\leq M_{*}.

Consequently, every solution of the hybrid dynamics satisfies the ultimate bounds

m_{*}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}x(t)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}x(t)\leq\e^{-a\Phi(M_{*})}\,M_{*}.

Using induction on

\Psi

M_{n}=\Psi(m_{n})>m_{n}
.
Since

VI
Simulation example

An example illustrating the utility of the proposed control approach in drug dosing is provided in this section. It features a standard pain reliever drug – intravenous (IV) paracetamol – an effective analgesic for acute pain management, particularly in postoperative settings, offering a fast-acting alternative when oral route is not feasible.

VI-A

PKPD model

The PK of paracetamol can be approximated
[
11
]
by

\dot{x}=-kx,\quad x(0)>0,

(20)

where
x
is drug concentration in
\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{L}

is the administrated IV bolus dose. The effect of medication is measured on Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where zero means “no pain” and ten corresponds to “worst imaginable pain” and is described by the PD model

Fixed point design

We first find the corridor-spanning 1-cycle by solving equations (

Substituting

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln x_{\min}^{*}-\ln x_{\max}^{*}}{a}=$2.4755\text{\,}\mathrm{h}$,

Stability and convergence

A straightforward computation gives the derivative of the return map

Q^{\prime}(x)=\e^{a\Phi(x)}+\e^{a\Phi(x)}\begin{bmatrix}1&a(x+F(x))\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}F^{\prime}(x)\\

Case 1

\Phi_{2}

Notice that despite the coefficients

Case 2

This design corresponds to pure frequency modulation feedback as

Conclusions

The problem of controlling the output of a stable scalar continuous system into a predefined corridor of output values by means of amplitude- and frequency-modulated feedback is considered. The design of the controller is performed by means of constructing a fixed point of a discrete map and guaranteeing its stability. An approach to analyze non-local attractivity of the designed periodic attractor is proposed. Controller performance is illustrated by a simulated drug dosing application using a first-order PKPD model from the literature. Exact convergence of the controller to the predefined output corridor in finite time is demonstrated.
