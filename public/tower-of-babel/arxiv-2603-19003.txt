Uniqueness and locality of the ground state of the disordered Monomer-Dimer models on independently weighted Unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees
Mihyun Kang and Mike Liu

Abstract
. Consider a finite graph G = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E ⁡ ( G ) ) G=(V(G),E(G)) and two continuous weight distributions ω \omega and ξ \xi , for which we only assume that ξ \xi is lower bounded. Next, independently draw weights ( w ⁡ ( e ) ) e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) (w(e))_{e\in E(G)} with distribution ω \omega on edges and ( x ⁡ ( v ) ) v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) (x(v))_{v\in V(G)} with distribution ξ \xi on vertices. The ground state of the monomer-dimer model on the weighted graph G G is a collection of edges (dimers) and vertices (monomers) such that every vertex is included in at most one monomer or dimer, and such that the sum of weights on its dimers and monomers is maximised. Take ( G n , o n ) n ∈ ℕ (G_{n},o_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} to be a sequence of random rooted weighted graphs that converges locally to an independently weighted unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree ( 𝕋 , o ) (\mathbb{T},o) with vertex-weight distribution ξ \xi and edge-weight distribution ω \omega . By proving that the ground state of the monomer-dimer model on the tree ( 𝕋 , o ) (\mathbb{T},o) is almost surely unique and locally approximable, we prove that the ground state of the monomer-dimer model on ( G n , o n ) (G_{n},o_{n}) must converge locally to the ground state of the monomer-dimer model on ( 𝕋 , o ) (\mathbb{T},o) . This also implies a strong decorrelation property on monomer-dimer models on unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees.



1.
Introduction

In the field of study of disordered systems, physicists have made many remarkable conjectures with the replica method. In the case of
replica symmetric
models, the justification of such methods relies on the locality of an underlying message-passing cavity equation (see the monograph
[
10
]
by Marc Mézard and Andrea Montanari). In the context of the random assignment problem, several conjectures formalised by Marc Mézard and Giorgio Parisi
[
12
,
11
]
were later proven correct by David Aldous
[
1
,
2
]
. The fact that the underlying belief propagation procedure admits a unique measurable solution was later established by Antar Bandyopadhyay
[
3
]
. The soundness of the replica method for the random assignment problem for more general weight distributions
was then proven by Johan Wästlund
[
15
]
, and the uniqueness in law of the belief propagation procedure was obtained by Justin Salez
[
13
]
.

In the case of i.i.d. weighted sparse graphs, little is proven about the replica symmetric behaviour of maximum matchings. David Gamarnik, Tomasz Nowicki and Grzegorz Swirszcz
[
7
]
proved that correlation decay holds for maximum weight matchings on Erdős-Rényi and
d-
regular random graphs when the weights are exponentially distributed and gave a general criterion that links correlation decay to the uniqueness of solutions to an iterated operator corresponding to the stationary measure of the cavity equation.
A recent paper by Arnab Sen and Wai-Kit Lam
[
9
]
shows that correlation decay for the belief propagation procedure holds for graphs of degree bounded by
3
or trees of bounded degree when the weights are i.i.d. and exponentially distributed.

A main difficulty of establishing correlation decay in maximum matchings on sparse graphs comes from the fact that, as opposed to the dense regime, the space of weight distributions that one can allow is essentially every non-atomic integrable distribution. Analytical solutions are thus very difficult to compute outside of specific cases, such as the aforementioned exponential weight distribution. As far as we know, exponential weight distributions are the only case where one obtains explicit solutions to the cavity equation in the sparse regimes.
The space of random sparse trees is also much larger than the previously considered class of trees represented by the Poisson Weighted Infinite Trees with intensity of the form
t\mapsto t^{d}\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 0}
for
d>1
, whose underlying unweighted graph is essentially deterministic. Even when restricted to Unimodular-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees, this allows for an additional parameter for the degree distribution, which is infinite-dimensional.
As a result, correlation decay in sparse graphs has been left as an open problem (see
[
9
, Question 1.2]
).

In a recent paper by Nathanaël Enriquez, Laurent Ménard, Mike Liu, and Vianney Perchet
[
5
]
, the local weak convergence of maximum matching problems on random graphs converging locally weakly to an i.i.d. weighted unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with integrable weight distribution was proven. Their proof was based on a generalisation of Aldous’ original method in
[
1
,
2
]
and goes to great lengths in order to circumvent the inability to establish correlation decay of the maximum matching problem. The uniqueness in law of solutions of the cavity equation was established without answering the question of the almost sure uniqueness of the cavity messages on the tree itself.

In this paper, we achieve two different goals. We first generalise the study of maximum weight matching problems in sparse random graphs to the ground state of monomer-dimer models on i.i.d. weighted Unimodular-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees by allowing vertices to have any lower-bounded integrable weights and attributing to any matching the sum of weights of its unmatched vertices. This recovers maximum weight matchings by simply setting vertex weights to be identically
0
. Second, we give a short and concise proof that correlation decay occurs for the ground state of monomer-dimer models. This proof is based on constructing an analytical invariant over extremal families of messages that appeared in the criterion given in
[
7
]
. Let us finally note that the study of correlation decay has been carried out for ground states of monomer-dimer models on i.i.d. weighted
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for a broad class of weight distributions by Krishnan Kesav and Ray Gourab
[
8
]
. However, their setting is quite different from ours, as the unweighted graph studied in
[
8
]
is not random and also not a tree. The proof there also leans into heavy unimodular techniques and does not rely on message-passing arguments.

As a consequence, we strengthen the results of
[
5
]
and answer
[
9
, Question 1.2]
in a positive manner in the case of Unimodular-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees. Instead of weak convergence, we show that if a sequence of weighted random rooted graphs converges locally strongly to an i.i.d. weighted unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree, then the ground states of the monomer-dimer model on the weighted graphs must also converge locally strongly to the ground state of the monomer-dimer on the weighted tree that we identify thanks to the almost sure unique solution to the cavity equation on the weighted tree. We also lift the integrability requirement on the weights in the process. We thus state our first theorem below.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
be a unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with reproduction law
\pi
with vertex weights
(x(v))_{v\in V(\mathbb{T})}
with distribution
\xi
and edge weights
(w(e))_{e\in E(\mathbb{T})}
with distribution
\omega
.
Assume that:

•

The vertex-weight distribution
\xi
is lower bounded and continuous outside of a potential atom at its minimum.

•

The edge-weight distribution
\omega
is continuous.

•

The degree distribution
\pi
admits a second moment.

Then there almost surely exists a unique optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration
\mathbb{MD}
on
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
in the following sense:
Assume that
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of weighted random rooted graphs converging locally to
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
. Denote by
\mathrm{MD}_{n}
any optimal dimeric-monomeric configuration on
G_{n}
. Then
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},\mathrm{MD}_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to
(\mathbb{T},w,x,\mathbb{MD},o)
.

Using Skorokhod’s representation theorem, we get the equivalent proposition for local weak convergence.

Corollary 1
.

Let
(\mathbb{T},w,x,\mathbb{MD},o)
defined as in Theorem
1
.
Let
(G_{n},o_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence of rooted random graphs that converge locally weakly to
(\mathbb{T},o)
. Draw i.i.d. edge and vertex weights
w_{n}
and
x_{n}
on
G_{n}
according to distribution
\omega
and
\xi
. Once again, denote by
\mathrm{MD}_{n}
any optimal dimeric-monomeric configuration on
G_{n}
. Then
(G_{n},\mathrm{MD}_{n},o_{n})
converges locally weakly to
(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{MD},o)
.

When
\xi=\delta_{0}
, Corollary
1
is one of the main findings of
[
5
]
.
We also establish correlation decay for the ground state of monomer-dimer models themselves, which we formalise in the next theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of weighted random graphs. Let
\mathrm{MD}_{n}
be any optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
G_{n}
.

Take
o_{n},o_{n}^{\prime}
two vertices in
G_{n}
. Assume that:

(1)

The graph distance between
o_{n}
and
o_{n}^{\prime}
goes to infinity as
n\to\infty
.

(2)

The couple

\bigg((G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n}),(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n}^{\prime})\bigg)

jointly locally converges to two independent copies of
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
.

Then the couple

\bigg((G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},\mathrm{MD}_{n},o_{n}),(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},\mathrm{MD}_{n},o_{n}^{\prime})\bigg)

jointly locally converges to two independent copies of
(\mathbb{T},w,x,\mathbb{MD},o)
.

Remark 1.1
.

The additional conditions (1)–(2) in Theorem
2
are necessary to avoid pathological cases such as
G_{n}
being
n
copies of the same subcritical Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree.

Since the correlation between states of edges and vertices in the ground state of the monomer-dimer model decays, this also allows us to deduce laws of large numbers on these ground states as a corollary.

Corollary 2
.

Let us set ourselves in the context of local weak convergence of Corollary
1
.
Assume that the conditions of Theorem
2
hold for two uniformly chosen vertices
o_{n}
and
o_{n}^{\prime}
in
G_{n}
, with (2) replaced with local weak convergence. Let
\mathrm{MD}_{n}
be any optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
G_{n}
. Let
f
be any bounded local function on rooted marked graphs. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(G_{n})|}\left(\sum_{v\in V(G_{n})}f(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},\mathrm{MD}_{n},v)\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbb{T},w,x,\mathbb{MD},o)\right].

Remark 1.2
.

A law of large numbers can then be deduced for the total weight. Indeed, fix some constant
K>0
and set the localising function
\tau_{K}:t\mapsto\max(-K,\min(K,t))
. Apply Corollary
2
to the function

f_{K}:(G,w,x,\mathrm{MD},o)\mapsto\tau_{K}\left(x(o)\mathbbm{1}_{o\text{ is a monomer in }\mathrm{MD}}+\sum_{v\sim o}\frac{1}{2}w(o,v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{o,v\}\text{ is a dimer in }\mathrm{MD}}\right),

using monotone convergence theorem and uniform integrability to swap the order of integration, this becomes a law of large numbers on the average weight of the optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
G_{n}
, the renormalised total weight

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(G_{n})|}\left(\sum_{v\text{ is a monomer in }\mathrm{MD}_{n}}x_{n}(v)+\sum_{e\text{ is a dimer in }\mathrm{MD}_{n}}w_{n}(e)\right)

converges in probability to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x(o)\mathbbm{1}_{o\text{ is a monomer in }\mathbb{MD}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}w(o,v)\mathbbm{1}_{\{o,v\}\text{ is a dimer in }\mathbb{MD}}\right].

If we do not assume integrability of the weights, then the right hand side can be infinite and the convergence still holds, where convergence in probability to
+\infty
means that for any constants
C>0,\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n
large enough from which the probability that the random variable on the left is bigger than
C
is at least
1-\varepsilon
.
In Section
2
, we specify the notations and the precise definitions we use in this paper. The objective of Section
3
is to exhibit the message-passing procedure that builds the unique ground state of the monomer-dimer model on generically weighted finite trees. Section
4
gives the proof, whose main idea is derived from
[
7
]
, of our main theorems conditionally on the validity of Proposition
4.2
, which reflects the uniqueness in distribution of the iterated cavity equation. The heart and main contribution of the paper then lies in Section
5

2.
Preliminaries

We let
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathbb{N}_{\geq\ell}:=\{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots\}
and
[\ell]:=\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\}
.

Let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}
. For
e=\{u,v\}\in E(G)
, we say that
u
and
v
are adjacent, denoted by
u\sim v
, and that
u
and
v
are incident to
e
. We often write
v\in G
and
\{u,v\}\in G
instead of
v\in V(G)
and
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
. For a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
, we denote by
G\setminus G^{\prime}
the graph with vertex set
V(G)\setminus V(G^{\prime})
and edge set
E(G)\setminus E(G^{\prime})
. For
U\subseteq V(G)
, we let
G\setminus U
denote the graph with vertex set
V(G)\setminus U
and edge set
E(G)\cap\binom{V(G)\setminus U}{2}
.

A (possibly infinite) graph
G
is
locally finite
if every vertex of
G
has finite degree. A
rooted graph
is a pair
(G,o)
, where
G
is a locally finite graph and
o
is a vertex of
G
. Two rooted graphs
(G,o)
and
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
, which is denoted by
(G,o)\cong(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
, if there exists an isomorphism from
G
to
G^{\prime}
that takes
o
to
o^{\prime}
. Given
H\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and a rooted graph
(G,o)
, the ball
B_{H}(G,o)
of radius
H
with centre
o
is the subgraph of
G
induced on the vertices with distance at most
H
from
o
and it is considered as a rooted graph with root
o
. We also call
B_{H}(G,o)
the
H-
neighbourhood of
(G,o)
. Note that
B_{0}(G,o)=(\{o\},\emptyset)
.

For any rooted graph
G
, we can define a set of directed edges
\overset{\rightarrow}{E}(G)
by duplicating each edge. Formally,
\overset{\rightarrow}{E}(G):=\bigcup_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}\{(u,v),(v,u)\}
.

In a rooted tree
(T,o)
, we will say that a directed edge
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}\in\overset{\rightarrow}{E}(G)
is pointing outwards if there exists a directed path
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{0},...,\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{k}
such that the source of
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{0}
is
o
, and
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{k}=\overset{\rightarrow}{e}
. Inversely, we will say that the directed edge
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}
is pointing inwards if there exists a directed path
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{0},...,\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{k}
such that
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{0}=\overset{\rightarrow}{e}
and the endpoint of
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}_{k}
is
o
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a graph
G
, we can additionally equip vertices and edges with weights by considering two functions
x:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
and
w:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}
. We will then say that
(G,w,x)
is a weighted graph.
In order to alleviate notations, we will often write
w(u,v)
or
w(v,u)
to be the same value
w(\{u,v\})
.

On a rooted graph, we can then define a monomeric-dimeric configuration as a subset of non-adjacent edges.

Definition 2.2
.

For any graph
G
, we say that a subset of edges
\mathrm{MD}\subseteq E(G)
is a monomeric-dimeric configuration if for any vertex
v\in G
, there exists at most one vertex
u\in G
adjacent to
v
such that
\{u,v\}\in\mathrm{MD}
. We then call every element of
\mathrm{MD}
a dimer in
\mathrm{MD}
, and every vertex
v\in G
such that there exists no element of
\mathrm{MD}
incident to
v
a monomer in
\mathrm{MD}
.

We can now define optimality of monomeric-dimeric configurations on a finite weighted graph as a maximiser of the sum of weights on monomers and dimers

Definition 2.3
.

A monomeric-dimeric configuration
\mathrm{MD}
on a finite weighted graph
(G,w,x)
is said to be optimal if it maximises

\sum_{e\text{ is a dimer in }\mathrm{MD}}w(e)+\sum_{v\text{ is a monomer in }\mathrm{MD}}x(v).

We write
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G,w,x)
to be any arbitrarily chosen optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
(G,w,x)
.

Remark 2.4
.

The optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration also corresponds to the ground states of the weighted monomer-dimer model on
G
.

Next, we provide a definition of local convergence that is standard in the literature, see
[
14
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that a sequence of rooted graphs
(G_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to another rooted graph
(G,o)
if for any
H\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that for any
n\geq N
, we have
B_{H}(G_{n},o_{n})\cong B_{H}(G,o)
.
This convergence induces a metrisable topology called local topology on the space of rooted graphs, and a corresponding distance is

d_{\mathrm{loc}}\left((G,o),(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})\right)=\frac{1}{1+R}

where
R:=\inf\{H\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:B_{H}(G,o)\cong B_{H}(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})\}
.

For weighted graphs, we will mimic the definition by saying that
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to
(G,w,x,o)
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that for any
n\geq N
, we have

B_{H}(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})\cong B_{H}(G,w,x,o)
.

The weak convergence resulting from this topology on random rooted graphs is then called weak local convergence.

Remark 2.6
.

Since weights are continuous, it would be more natural to define local convergence by allowing some leeway on weights. For instance, we could instead state that for every
\varepsilon>0
and
H\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that for any
n\geq N
, we both have that

B_{H}(G_{n},o_{n})\cong B_{H}(G,o)
and that the sum of the difference of weights through the corresponding graph isomorphism is bounded above by
\varepsilon
. This results in a topology that we call
L^{1}-
local topology of weighted graphs. We voluntarily choose a more restrictive definition to avoid technicalities, as converting the proof with this less restrictive distance does not change the main idea of our proof.

Finally, we introduce the definition of a local function which only depends on a neighbourhood of the root.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}^{\star}
be the space of rooted weighted graphs. We say that a function

f:\mathcal{G}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}

is a local function if there exists
H\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{H}(G,w,x,o)\cong B_{H}(G^{\prime},w^{\prime},x^{\prime},o^{\prime})
implies
f(G,w,x,o)=f(G^{\prime},w^{\prime},x^{\prime},o^{\prime})
.

Definition
2.5
and Definition
2.7
can be extended to a rooted weighted graph equipped with a monomeric-dimeric configuration
(G,w,x,\mathrm{MD},o)
by identifying a monomeric-dimeric configuration with its membership function
e\in E(G)\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{e\text{ is a dimer in }\mathrm{MD}}
.

2.1.
Unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees

In this subsection, we introduce
Unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees (UBGW)
along with models of random graphs that converge locally in law to these trees. Weights on vertices and edges will always be drawn independently according to distributions
\xi
and
\omega
. These results being common folklore in the literature, we draw the presentation from
[
5
]
.

Let
\pi
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with finite expectation
E>0
. Let
\hat{\pi}
be the size-biased version of
\pi
, that is,
\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\hat{\pi}(k)=\frac{k}{E}\pi(k)
.

The vertex-rooted UBGW tree is the random tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
with the following law:

•

The number of children of vertices in
\mathbb{T}
are all independent.

•

The number of children of the root
o
is distributed according to
\pi
.

•

Every non-root vertex has a number of children distributed according to
\hat{\pi}
.

The random tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
is a vertex-rooted unimodular random graph. See Figure

for an illustration.

The most classical examples of random graphs converging to UBGW trees we consider are sparse Erdős–Rényi and configuration models:

•

Sparse Erdős–Rényi:
As introduced in the celebrated paper of Erdős and Rényi
[
6
]
, for
c>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the random graph
\mathcal{G}(N,\frac{c}{N})
is defined on the vertex set
[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
with independent edges between vertices with probability
\frac{c}{N}
. Once uniformly rooted, the sequence of these graphs
\left(\mathcal{G}(N,\frac{c}{N})\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges locally when
N
goes to
\infty
, to a UBGW tree with reproduction law Poisson with parameter
c
.

•

Configuration model:
This model was introduced by Bollobás in 1980
[
4
]
and can be defined as follows.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{N}
is even. We interpret
d_{i}
as the number of half-edges attached to vertex
i
. Then, the configuration model associated with the sequence
(d_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is the random multigraph with vertex set
[N]
obtained by a uniform perfect matching of these half-edges. If
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{N}
is odd, we change
d_{N}
into
d_{N}+1
and do the same construction. Now, let
\mathbf{d}^{(N)}
be a sequence of random variables defined on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{d}^{(N)}=(d_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,d_{N}^{(N)})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
. Furthermore, suppose that there exists
\pi
a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with finite first moment such that

\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{d_{j}^{(N)}=k}\underset{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\pi(\{k\}).

The sequence of random configuration graphs associated with the
\mathbf{d}^{(N)}
has asymptotically a positive probability to be simple. In addition, this sequence of random graphs, when uniformly rooted, converges locally in law to the UBGW random tree with offspring distribution
\pi
, see
[
14
]
for more details.

3.
Message-passing for monomer-dimer ground states

In this section, we present one of the main tools we use for studying optimal monomeric-dimeric configurations. In particular, there exists a message-passing algorithm that constructs the optimal solution in linear time on finite trees.

Let
(T,w,x)
be a finite weighted tree, and assume that there is a unique optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
(T,w,x)
.
Let us consider an edge
e=\{u,v\}
, the principle is to look at the difference between the unique optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration of
(T,w,x)
and the optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration under the constraint of
e
not being a dimer. Let us write
\mathrm{OPT}(T,w,x)
for the weight of the optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on the implicitly weighted
T
.

Let us write
T_{(v,u)}
and
T_{(u,v)}
to be the two connected components of
T\setminus{e}
containing respectively
u
and
v
. The key is that the total weight of the optimal configuration
forcing

e
to be a dimer can be written as

w(e)+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)}\setminus\{u\})+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)}\setminus\{v\}).

The total weight of the optimal configuration of
T\setminus e
can simply be written as:

\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)})+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)}).

From this, we deduce that
e
is a dimer in the optimal configuration
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(T,w,x)
if and only if the first quantity is bigger than the second one, in other words,

w(e)+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)}\setminus\{u\})+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)}\setminus\{v\})>\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)})+\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)})

which can be rearranged into contributions of the left and right parts:

(1)

\displaystyle w(e)>\bigg(\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)})-\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(v,u)}\setminus\{u\})\bigg)+\bigg(\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)})-\mathrm{OPT}(T_{(u,v)}\setminus\{v\})\bigg).

We thus set (refer to Figure
2
for an illustration):

(2)

From (

(4)

w(u,v)>Z(u,v)+Z(v,u).

The message

is exactly the gain of attaching
v
to the forest
T_{(u,v)}\setminus\{v\}
, which allows us to compute its values recursively on the tree (refer to Figure

First, if
v
is a monomer in
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(T_{(u,v)},w,x)

.

Next, assuming that
\{v,u^{\prime}\}
is a dimer in the optimal configuration
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(T_{(u,v)},w,x)
for some

w(v,u^{\prime})

u^{\prime}

w(v,u^{\prime})
and lost

from attaching
\{v\}

w(v,u^{\prime})-Z(v,u^{\prime})
.

Finally, the optimal configuration has to optimise among all the possibilities above. Using the convention that the maximum of an empty list is

, we get the recursion

(5)

Z(u,v)=\max\left(x(v),\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u^{\prime}\sim v\\
u^{\prime}\neq u\end{subarray}}\left(w(v,u^{\prime})-Z(v,u^{\prime})\right)\right).

These messages can thus be computed recursively starting from leaves, where they evaluate to the weight of the vertex leaf, and they construct the optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration in at most
2|E(T)|
iterations through Criterion (

).
The main task in this paper is then to extend this construction and show that there almost surely exists a unique family of messages

on the weighted rooted UBGW tree
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
.

4.
Uniqueness of messages on the limit tree and convergence

The main arguments in both settings can be broken down as follows:

(1)

First, for a given graph
G
,
o\in V(G)
and
H\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{H}(G,o)
is a tree, we can represent the influence of the optimal monomer-dimer configuration outside the ball
B_{H}(G,o)
by a well-chosen boundary condition on the messages on the ball. Namely, take a boundary vertex
u\in\partial B_{H}(G,o):=B_{H}(G,o)\setminus B_{H-1}(G,o)
and denote by
u^{\prime}
its unique neighbour in
B_{H}(G,o)
. If the vertex
u
is matched to an exterior vertex, we can set
Z(u^{\prime},u)=+\infty
, on the contrary,
u
is not matched to the exterior of
B_{H}(G,o)
, we can set
Z(u^{\prime},u)=x(u)
. Then, the restriction of the optimal monomer-dimer configuration to
B_{H}(G,o)
coincides with the monomer-dimer configuration induced by the messages inside
B_{H}(G,o)
defined by these boundary conditions.

(2)

Another general fact is that inside
B_{H}(G,o)
, the messages around the root vertex
o
are deterministically increasing with respect to the boundary conditions when
H
is even, and decreasing when
H
is odd. So as
H\rightarrow\infty
, we get that the messages are almost surely bounded by the limit of two extremal families one built by setting
Z(u^{\prime},u)=m
for
u\in\partial B_{H}(G,o)
and
u^{\prime}\in B_{H}(G,o)
and the other by setting
Z(u^{\prime},u)=+\infty
instead where
m\in\mathbb{R}
is the minimum of the vertex weight distribution
\xi
. The main difficulties, which were only overcome in special cases (mainly when vertex weights were zero and edge weights were exponentially distributed) in previous works
[
7
,
9
]
, thanks to an explicit formulation of the message distribution. The main contribution of this paper lies in an invariant trick given in Section
5
that allows us to show that the two extremal families are almost surely equal in i.i.d. weighted UBGW trees
(\mathbb{T},o)
.

(3)

As a conclusion, we have that as the depth
H
increases, any compatible family of messages inside the ball must converge to the unique family given by the common almost sure limit of the extremal families. This common limit then constructs the unique ground state of the monomer-dimer model on the limiting tree through Rule (
4
). It is also, by the first point, the universal limit of monomer-dimer models on any graph
(G,o)
close to the tree
(\mathbb{T},o)
.

Pick a sequence of rooted weighted random graphs
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})
such that
(G_{n},,w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})
converges locally to
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
.
This is equivalent to saying that almost surely, for any
H\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
N_{H}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>N_{H}
,

B_{H}(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})\cong B_{H}(\mathbb{T},w,x,o).

Our goal is then to show that there exists a locally approximable hence almost surely defined monomeric-dimeric configuration
\mathbb{MD}
on
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
such that for every
H_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\mathrm{MD}(G_{n})
is an optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n})
, then there almost surely exists
N_{H_{0}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N^{\prime}_{H_{0}}
,

(6)

\displaystyle B_{H_{0}}(G_{n},\mathrm{MD}(G_{n}),o_{n})\cong B_{H_{0}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{MD},o).

We will begin by defining
\mathbb{MD}
and show that it can be characterised as the monomeric-dimeric configuration given by the almost surely only family of messages on
(\mathbb{T},o)
verifying Recursion (
5
). Recall that
m\in\mathbb{R}
is the minimum of the vertex weight distribution
\xi
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Define the families of truncated decorated trees
\{\left(\mathbb{T}_{H},w,x,o,Z_{+}^{H},Z_{-}^{H}\right)\}_{H\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
as follows:
Sample
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
, set
\mathbb{T}_{H}=B_{H}(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
and
Z_{-}^{H}=m
for every edge of
\mathbb{T}_{H}
pointing towards the
H-
boundary. Then use Recursion (
5
) to define
Z_{-}^{H}
on the remainder of
\mathbb{T}_{H}
. We proceed similarly for
Z_{+}^{H}
by setting
Z_{+}^{H}=+\infty
on the boundary instead of
m
.
We can then extend both families on
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
by arbitrarily setting any value, say
0
, outside of
\mathbb{T}_{H}
.

Then
(\mathbb{T},o,w,x)
-almost surely, for any
\overset{\rightarrow}{e}\in\overset{\rightarrow}{E}(\mathbb{T})
,

Z_{+}^{H}(\overset{\rightarrow}{e})

and

Z_{-}^{H}(\overset{\rightarrow}{e})

converge to a common value
Z(\overset{\rightarrow}{e})
that is thus
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)-
measurable.

Furthermore,
(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
-almost surely, the only family of messages verifying Recursion (
5
) is this common limit
Z
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let us start by looking around the root. We will use the fact that the random variables
(Z_{-}^{2H})(o,v)
(resp.
(Z_{+}^{2H})(o,v))
are deterministically increasing (resp. decreasing) as
H
increases.
Indeed, for a fixed family of weights
(w_{l})_{l\geq 0}
and any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, if we pick two families
(a_{l})_{l\geq 0}
and
(a_{l}^{\prime})_{l\geq 0}
such that
a_{l}\leq a_{l}^{\prime}
for every
l
, then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

\max\left(x,\max_{l\leq N}(w_{l}-a_{l})\right)\geq\max\left(x,\max_{l\leq N}(w_{l}-a_{l}^{\prime})\right).

In terms of random variables, the following almost sure limits exist when looking at even depths:

\displaystyle Z_{-}^{\text{even}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{H\rightarrow+\infty}Z_{-}^{2H},

\displaystyle Z_{+}^{\text{even}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{H\rightarrow+\infty}Z_{+}^{2H}.

For the odd depths, the analogous limits also exist around the root:

\displaystyle Z_{-}^{\text{odd}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{H\rightarrow+\infty}Z_{-}^{2H+1},

\displaystyle Z_{+}^{\text{odd}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{H\rightarrow+\infty}Z_{+}^{2H+1}.

Furthermore, we obtain the following almost sure chain of inequalities around the root

\displaystyle Z_{-}^{2H}\leq Z_{+}^{2H+1}\leq Z_{-}^{2H+2}\leq Z_{+}^{2H+2}\leq Z_{-}^{2H+1}\leq Z_{+}^{2H}.

Going from the outer edge to the centre, the first inequalities are obtained by looking at the boundary of both variables at depth
2H
, the next inequalities by looking at their boundary at depth
2H+1
, and the centre inequality by looking at their boundary conditions at depth
2H+2
. Taking
H
to infinity implies that:

Z_{-}^{\text{even}}\leq Z_{+}^{\text{odd}}\leq Z_{-}^{\text{even}}\leq Z_{+}^{\text{even}}\leq Z_{-}^{\text{odd}}\leq Z_{+}^{\text{even}},

which condenses into

Z_{-}^{\text{even}}=Z_{+}^{\text{odd}}\leq Z_{+}^{\text{even}}=Z_{-}^{\text{odd}}.

To show the conclusion, we only need to show that the centre inequality is in fact an equality. For this purpose, it is sufficient to show that the distribution of the two variables on the left is the same as the distribution of the two variables on the right.
Furthermore for any
v\sim o
, we have that

Z_{+}^{2H+1}(o,v)=\max\bigg(x(v),\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\sim v\\
u\neq o\end{subarray}}\left(w(v,u)-Z^{2H+1}_{+}(v,u)\right)\bigg).

Now, the variable
Z_{+}^{2H}(o,v)
has the same distribution as
Z_{+}^{2H+1}(u,v)
for any
v\sim u,v\neq o
. Indeed, the subtree that
Z_{+}^{2H+1}(u,v)
uses has distribution
(\mathbb{T}_{2H},x,w,o)
by virtue of the underlying tree being an i.i.d. weighted UBGW and
(u,v)
also sees a boundary condition valued at
+\infty
.
Writing
\zeta_{+}^{2H+1}
to be the distribution of
Z_{+}^{2H+1}(o,v)
and
\zeta_{+}^{2H}
to be the distribution of
Z_{+}^{2H}(o,v)
,
we deduce that for any
H\in\mathbb{N}
, the following equality in law holds:

(7)

Z_{+}^{2H+1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\max\bigg(X,\max_{1\leq l\leq\hat{\pi}}(w_{l}-Z_{+}^{2H,(l)}\bigg)

where
X\sim\xi
,
\hat{N}\sim\hat{\pi}
,
(w_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
i.i.d. of law
\omega
and
(Z_{+}^{2H,(l)})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
i.i.d. of law
\zeta_{+}^{2H}
, all mutually independent, and
Z_{+}^{2H+1}\sim\zeta_{+}^{2H+1}
.
We thus deduce, taking
H\rightarrow+\infty
and applying a symmetrical argument to
Z_{-}^{H}
, the distributions of
Z_{-}^{\text{even}}
and
Z_{+}^{\text{even}}

\zeta_{-}
and
\zeta_{+}

If

is an i.i.d. sequence of distribution

,

is an i.i.d. sequence of distribution
\zeta_{+}
,
X
of distribution
\xi

\omega
and finally
\hat{N}

(8)

\displaystyle Z_{-}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\max\left(X,\max_{1\leq l\leq\hat{N}}(w_{l}-Z_{+}^{(l)})\right),

(9)

\displaystyle Z_{+}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\max\left(X,\max_{1\leq l\leq\hat{N}}(w_{l}-Z_{-}^{(l)})\right).

Let us denote by

the cumulative distribution functions corresponding to
\zeta_{+}

\zeta_{-}

(10)

\displaystyle h_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{X}(t)\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{-}(W-t)]\right),

\displaystyle=h_{X}(t)\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{+}(W-t)]\right).

Proposition 4.2
.

Take
h_{+},h_{-}
any pair of cumulative distribution functions verifying Equations (

h_{-}=h_{+}
.

We continue the proof of Proposition

by assuming

and hence

. Since the inequality

.

) on

. Since the values of this family on the

and

The next argument, contained in the lemma below, establishes the link between

and

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(G,w,x,o)
be a finite rooted weighted graph. Let
H\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
G_{H}:=B_{H}(G,w,x,o)
is a tree with a unique optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration. Recall that
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)
is any optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on
(G,w,x)
. Consider the decorated graph
(G_{H},o,Z_{M}^{H})
where
Z_{M}^{H}
is defined as follows:

(1)

Set
Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)=x(v)
if
v
is on the boundary and not matched by
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)
to the exterior of
G_{H}
. In other words,
Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)=x(v)
if

and
\{v,u^{\prime}\}
is not a dimer for
u^{\prime}\sim v,u^{\prime}\neq u
.

(2)

Set
Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)=+\infty
if
v
is on the boundary and matched by
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)
to the exterior of
G_{H}
.

(3)

Use Recursion (
5
) to define
Z_{M}^{H}
on the remainder of
(G_{H},o)
.

Then any edge
\{u,v\}\in G_{H}
is a dimer in
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)
if and only if

\displaystyle w(u,v)>Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)+Z_{M}^{H}(v,u).

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

The idea is that the optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration in
G_{H}
behaves as if every vertex on the boundary matched to the exterior in
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)
is removed and untouched otherwise. Setting
Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)=x(v)
has the same effect in
G_{H}
as if the vertex
v
were a leaf. Similarly, setting
Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)=+\infty
forbids the edge
\{u,v\}
to be a dimer. Indeed, we always have
+\infty>w(u,v)
and this removes any effect that the edge
\{u,v\}
has in the rest of
G_{H}
since the value appearing in Recursion
5
in the next generation’s from
\{u,v\}
is
w(u,v)-(+\infty)=-\infty
which is always smaller than
x(v)
. We know that the
Z
message formalism is exact on finite trees, so the rule

w(u,v)>Z_{M}^{H}(u,v)+Z_{M}^{H}(v,u)

constructs an optimal monomeric-dimeric configuration on

G^{H,\star}:=G_{H}\setminus\big\{v\in G:\left(\exists u\in G\setminus G_{H}\right)\wedge\big(\{u,v\}\text{ is a dimer in }\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G)\big)\big\}.

∎

would grow by an additional

incurred from the allowed variability of the weights in
B_{H_{0}+H}(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})
compared to
B_{H_{0}+H}(\mathbb{T},w,x,o)
. Indeed, the families
Z_{+}^{H_{0}+H}
and
Z_{-}^{H_{0}+H}
are
1-
Lipschitz with respect to every weight appearing in
B_{H_{0}+H}(G_{n},w_{n},x_{n},o_{n})
. The proof would thus write the same with
g_{\omega}(3\varepsilon^{\prime})
instead of
g_{\omega}(2\varepsilon^{\prime})
.

We now explain the adaptation to Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

This theorem is essentially proved the same way, except that instead of considering one ball, we consider two balls around
o_{n}
and
o_{n}^{\prime}
. By using the condition that the graph distance between
o_{n}
and
o_{n}^{\prime}
goes to infinity, for any
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find
N_{H,H^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that the two balls
B_{H}(G_{n},o_{n})
and
B_{H^{\prime}}(G_{n},o_{n}^{\prime})
do not intersect. We can then continue by writing that the influence of one ball on the other is always uniformly controlled by the extremal families in each ball, which maps to extremal families of two independent weighted unimodular Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees by applying the joint convergence hypothesis. The remainder follows in the same way.
∎

The deduction of Corollary
2
is standard once correlation decay is established, as it implies that the covariance between the states of two edges in
\mathrm{MD}_{\mathrm{opt}}(G_{n})
decays with their distance
d
at the speed with which
Z_{+}^{d}
and
Z_{-}^{d}
converge to their common limit. We thus omit the details.

5.
Invariance trick

In this section, we complete the proof by showing Proposition
4.2
.
Recall the system

(
10
)

\displaystyle h_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{X}(t)\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{-}(W-t)]\right),

(
11
)

\displaystyle h_{-}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{X}(t)\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{+}(W-t)]\right).

The trick is to introduce the following quantity that will allow us to define an invariant:

(13)

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{-}+Z_{+}<W\right)

where
Z_{+},Z_{-},W
are three independent random variables of distribution
\zeta_{+},\zeta_{-},\omega
.

Recall that
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
denote the cumulative distribution functions corresponding to
\zeta_{+}
and
\zeta_{-}
, respectively.
By conditioning either over the value of
Z_{-}
or over the value of
Z_{+}
, we get the following equality:

(14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{-}<W-t)\mathrm{d}h_{+}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{+}<W-t)\mathrm{d}h_{-}(t).

Rewritten in terms of expectations, this yields

(15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[h_{-}(W-t)]\mathrm{d}h_{+}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[h_{+}(W-t)]\mathrm{d}h_{-}(t).

We naturally have that
h_{+}=h_{-}=0
on
(-\infty,m)
, so using Equations (
10
)–(
11
) and that
h_{X}>0
on
(m,+\infty)
, we obtain

(16)

\int_{m}^{+\infty}\left(1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}\left(\frac{h_{+}(t)}{h_{X}(t)}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}h_{+}(t)=\int_{m}^{+\infty}\left(1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}\left(\frac{h_{-}(t)}{h_{X}(t)}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}h_{-}(t).

Set
F:u\mapsto(1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}(u))
that is decreasing on
[0,1]
. We can then rewrite the equality as:

(17)

\int_{m}^{+\infty}F\left(\frac{h_{+}(t)}{h_{X}(t)}\right)\mathrm{d}h_{+}(t)=\int_{m}^{+\infty}F\left(\frac{h_{-}(t)}{h_{X}(t)}\right)\mathrm{d}h_{-}(t).

This quantity, which can be written as a function of
\zeta_{+}
or
\zeta_{-}
, does not depend on which of the distributions we choose and is thus an invariant over solutions to System (
10
)–(
11
).
We will now use the fact
Z_{-}^{\text{even}}\leq Z_{+}^{\text{even}}
, which implies that we have
h_{+}\leq h_{-}
.

We will finish the proof by first considering the case where
\xi
is continuous on
[m,+\infty)
, then by allowing
\xi
to have an atom at
m
.

5.1.
Case
\xi
continuous

If
\xi
is continuous, then the right-hand side of Equations (
10
)–(
11
) are both continuous by virtue of both
\xi,\omega
being continuous, so
\zeta_{+}
and
\zeta_{-}
are also continuous.

We can then set the change of variable
h_{+}(t)=u,h_{-}(t)=u
in Equation (
17
) to obtain:

(18)

\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

As
F
is a decreasing function,
h_{X}
is non-decreasing and
h_{+}\leq h_{-}
(and hence
h_{+}^{-1}\geq h_{-}^{-1}
), we deduce that

F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\geq F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)

with equality if and only if

h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))=h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u)).

Since Equation (
18
) implies that the integrals of both sides are equal on
[0,1]
, we deduce that
h_{+}=h_{-}
on the support of
\xi
.

Using Equations (
10
)–(
11
), we deduce that for all
t\in\text{supp}(\xi)
,

\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{-}(W-t)]\right)=\hat{\phi}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{+}(W-t)]\right)

which implies

\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{-}(W-t)]=\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\omega}[h_{+}(W-t)].

Since
h_{-}\geq h_{+}
, this implies that
h_{-}=h_{+}
on
\{w-t:w\in\text{supp}(\omega),t\in\text{supp}(\xi)\}\cap[m,\infty)
.

Repeating this argument, we see that
h_{+}=h_{-}
on

\left\{\sum_{l=0}^{N}(-1)^{l}w_{l}+(-1)^{N+1}t:N\in\mathbb{N},\,\,\forall l\geq 0,w_{l}\in\text{supp}(\omega),\,\,\,t\in\text{supp}(\xi)\right\}\cap[m,+\infty)

which is precisely
[m,+\infty)
.

5.2.
Case
\xi
with a singular atom at its minimum

We now assume that
\xi
is continuous outside of an atom of mass
\alpha
located at the minimum
m
of its support.

Once again, Equations (
10
)–(
11
) show that
\zeta_{-},\zeta_{+}
are continuous outside of a potential atom located at
m
, let us call
\beta_{+}=h_{+}(0)
and
\beta_{-}=h_{-}(0)
. (Note that
\beta_{+}\leq\beta_{-}
because
h_{+}\leq h_{-}
.) Of course, if these atoms do not exist, then the Proposition is true by virtue of the previous case.

Define
\mu_{-}
(resp.
\mu_{+}
) to be the pushforward measure of
\mathrm{d}h_{-}
by
h_{-}
(resp.
\mathrm{d}h_{+}
by
h_{+}
), we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu_{+}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu_{-}(u).

As
h_{+}
and
h_{-}
are continuous on
(m,+\infty)
of respective image
(\beta_{+},1)
and
(\beta_{-},1)
, we have that
\mathrm{d}\mu_{+}(u)=\mathrm{d}u
on
(\beta_{+},1)
and similarly
\mathrm{d}\mu_{-}(u)=\mathrm{d}u
on
(\beta_{-},1)
. Taking into account the atom at
m
of respective mass
\beta_{+}
and
\beta_{-}
gives:

\displaystyle\int_{\beta_{+}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(\beta_{+}))}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}))}\right).

By definition
h_{+}^{-1}(\beta_{+}))=m,h_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}))=m
and
h_{X}(m)=\alpha
so that the equality becomes

(19)

\int_{\beta_{+}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right)=\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right).

Once again, we still use the inequality, now valid for
u\in[\beta_{-},1]

F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\geq F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)

with equality if and only if

h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))=h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u)).

Integrating this inequality yields

(20)

\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u\geq\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

Next, by using that for any
u\in[\beta_{+},\beta_{-}],h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))\geq h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(\beta_{+}))=\alpha
, we also have
\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\leq\frac{u}{\alpha}
, recalling that
F
is decreasing yields

F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\geq F\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right).

Integrating this inequality gives:

(21)

\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u\geq\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}F\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

Integrating the right hand side by part, and remembering that
F:u\mapsto 1-\hat{\phi}^{-1}(u)
hence
F^{\prime}:u\mapsto-(\hat{\phi}^{-1})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{-1}{\hat{\phi}^{\prime}(\hat{\phi}^{-1}(u))}<0
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}F\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\left[uF\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\right]_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}-\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}\frac{u}{\alpha}F^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right)-\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right)-\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}\frac{u}{\alpha}F^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\geq\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right)-\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right).

Putting this together with Inequality (
21
) gives

(22)

\int_{\beta_{+}}^{\beta_{-}}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u\geq\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right)-\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right).

Putting Inequality (
20
) and Inequality (
22
) together gives

(23)

\int_{\beta_{+}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u\geq\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right)-\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right),

which is equivalent to

\int_{\beta_{+}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{+}F\left(\frac{\beta_{+}}{\alpha}\right)\geq\int_{\beta_{-}}^{1}F\left(\frac{u}{h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1}(u))}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\beta_{-}F\left(\frac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha}\right)

with equality implying
h_{X}(h_{+}^{-1})=h_{X}(h_{-}^{-1})
.

But the previous deduced Equation (
19
) states precisely that equality occurs, we thus deduce that
h_{+}=h_{-}
on the support of
\xi
, the remainder of the proof follows verbatim from the continuous case.
