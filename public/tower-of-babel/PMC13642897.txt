High-resolution metabolomics of maternal polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE) exposure and preterm birth in the Atlanta African American Maternal-Child Cohort
Authors: Siddhi S. Desai, Anne L. Dunlop, Dana Boyd Barr, Paula-Dene C. Nesbeth, Susan S. Hoffman, Youran Tan, Mengyuan Ren, ViLinh T. Tran, Grace Lee, Parinya Panuwet, Stephanie M. Eick, P. Barry Ryan, Dean P. Jones, Donghai Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlack and African American women face a dual burden of elevated risk of preterm birth (PTB, <37 weeks of gestation) and exposure to polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE), endocrine-disrupting pollutants. Although prenatal PBDE levels have been linked to PTB, underlying mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsWe examined serum PBDE levels and metabolomic profiles in 177 pregnant participants (18–40 years old) from the Atlanta African American Maternal-Child Cohort (2014–2019). BDE-47, −100, and −99 were quantified in maternal serum collected at 8–14 weeks’ gestation using chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Untargeted metabolomics profiling was conducted on maternal serum samples collected during the same period via high-resolution mass spectrometry. Generalized linear models were used to conduct metabolome-wide association studies to identify features linking PBDE levels to PTB.ResultsHere we find that 46 (26%) of 177 participants experienced PTB. Twenty-six metabolites, which were confirmed with Level One evidence according to the Metabolomics Standard Initiatives, are associated with maternal PBDE levels and/or PTB. These metabolites suggest disruptions in thiamine-dependent pathways, cysteine and methionine metabolism, the carnitine shuttle, and glycerophospholipid metabolism.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that PBDE-associated perturbations in oxidative stress, inflammation, and energy metabolism may be mechanisms through which environmental exposures contribute to PTB risk in populations disproportionately exposed.

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB) (live birth at <37 weeks of gestation) is a leading cause of death among infants and children under five globally1,2. PTB arises from a complex interplay of biological, socioeconomical, and environmental factors, including maternal age, education, income, body mass index (BMI), chronic health conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, substance use, and stress exposures3–9. In the United States, there are striking racial disparities wherein Black and African American women experience the highest rates of PTB compared to all other groups10,11. These disparities are not explained by inherent biological differences but reflect disproportionate exposure to social stressors, structural inequities, and environmental hazards12.

Environmental exposures are increasingly recognized as important contributors to PTB risk13–16. African American women are disproportionately exposed to several environmental pollutants, including polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE), a class of persistent organic pollutants that are known endocrine disruptors17–19. Higher exposures in this group may reflect disparities in socioeconomic status and structural factors that influence housing conditions20,21. PBDEs were historically used in the manufacturing of a wide range of products, including furniture, clothing, and electronics, due to their flame-retardant qualities22. PBDEs are lipophilic, bio-accumulative, and linked to neurotoxicity in humans and endocrine disruption23–25. As a result of these public health concerns, penta and octaBDE formulations were voluntarily phased out of production by manufacturers in the United States, and the manufacture and import of decaBDE formulations have been prohibited22. Despite improvements in regulations of these compounds, human exposure risk remains due to the persistent nature of PBDE coupled with their previous extensive use, along with routes largely via ingestion and contact with house dust22,26–28.

Epidemiologic studies have identified links between maternal PBDE levels and risk of PTB29–32. However, the biological mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. High-resolution metabolomics (HRM) technology offers a powerful approach to explore these mechanisms by capturing small-molecule perturbations in biological samples, which may reflect both endogenous processes and exogenous exposures33,34. A few metabolomics investigations have reported prenatal PBDE level-associated metabolic changes, including pentose phosphate pathway and lipid metabolism changes associated with placental PBDE levels and newborn Apgar scores in a Chinese population35. Other studies have identified metabolomic perturbations associated with PTB33,36, but none have examined metabolic mechanisms linking PBDE levels with PTB. To address this critical knowledge gap, we leveraged HRM profiling and metabolome-wide association studies (MWAS) within the Atlanta African American Maternal-Child Cohort. Our objective was to identify metabolic perturbations associated with serum PBDE levels and PTB, providing insights into potential biological pathways through which environmental exposures may contribute to PTB risk in disproportionately affected populations.

We investigated relationships between maternal serum PBDE in early pregnancy, PTB, and early pregnancy serum metabolomics. We find that PBDE and PTB are associated with perturbations in thiamine and thiamine-dependent pathways, as well as lipid and amino acid metabolism. These findings suggest disruptions in oxidative stress, inflammation, and energy pathways, implicating these mechanisms as potential links between PBDE exposure and PTB.

Methods

Study population

Participants were drawn from the Atlanta African American Maternal-Child Cohort (2014–2019)37,38. Pregnant African American women were recruited during prenatal visits at two Atlanta hospitals (Emory University Hospital Midtown, a private hospital, and Grady Memorial Hospital, a public hospital). The two hospitals were selected to recruit women with diverse socioeconomic characteristics. Eligibility criteria included: self-identification as Black or African American, 18–40 years old, 8–14 weeks’ gestation at the first prenatal visit, verified singleton pregnancy, and no chronic medical conditions. Data were collected during early (8–14 weeks) and late pregnancy (24–30 weeks) prenatal visits, and included survey, biospecimen, and clinical data. Maternal venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes; serum was isolated and stored at −80 °C until measurement of PBDE. This analysis focused on data collected during early pregnancy due to limited PBDE and metabolomics data available at the second visit. We excluded those who experienced early term birth (≥37–<39 weeks; N = 32) or stillbirth (N = 1). The final analytic sample included 177 participants who experienced either PTB or full-term births (Fig. S1). The Emory University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant (IRB approval reference number: 68441).

PBDE exposure assessment

Six PBDE congers (BDE-47, BDE-85, BDE-99, BDE-100, BDE-153, BDE-154) were quantified in maternal serum samples collected at 8–14 week gestation using gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) in the Laboratory for Exposure Assessment and Method Development in Environmental Research (LEADER) at Emory University; details regarding analytic protocols have been previously published39.

Congeners detected in ≥70% of samples were included in analyses and included BDE-47, BDE-99, and BDE-100 (Table S1). BDE-85, BDE-153, and BDE-154 were excluded due to low detection rates ranging from 2 to 10%. PBDE concentrations were normalized using natural-log transformations as visual inspection of their distributions indicated right-skewness (Fig. S2). The limits of detection (LODs) were 2.5 pg/mL for BDE-47, 6.25 pg/mL for BDE-99, and 2.5 pg/mL for BDE-100. Birth outcome

Gestational age at birth in completed weeks was abstracted from medical records; the abstracted value was the ‘best obstetrical estimate’ that considers the date of delivery in relation to the estimated date of confinement41. Given enrollment between 8–14 weeks’ gestation, all participants received early pregnancy dating based on the first day of their last menstrual period and/or early ultrasound. Gestational age at birth was classified as either preterm (>20 and <37 weeks of gestation) or full term (≥39 weeks of gestation) to create a dichotomous PTB variable for analysis. Early term births (≥37 and <39 weeks of gestation) were excluded, given their clinical overlap with PTB42–44.

Covariates

Information on covariates were abstracted from questionnaire data and medical records and included maternal age (continuous); education level (less than high school, high school, college or above); substance use in pregnancy: alcohol (yes/no), marijuana (yes/no), and tobacco (yes/no); parity (prior birth or not), body mass index (BMI) at first prenatal visit (continuous), and infant sex (male/female). Maternal age, education level, prenatal BMI, parity, and tobacco use were selected a priori as potential confounders, and infant sex was included as a covariate based on a review of the literature and a directed acyclic graph (Fig. S3)45. We additionally adjusted for alcohol and marijuana use to control for potential effects on the maternal metabolome46,47. Given the lipophilic nature of PBDE, our primary analysis did not adjust for serum lipids to avoid obscuring potential lipid metabolism-mediated links with PTB48.

High resolution metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics profiling was conducted on serum collected at 8–14 weeks’ gestation. Serum was treated with acetonitrile (2:1) to precipitate proteins and analyzed in triplicate via liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). The samples were analyzed in both polar and non-polar analytical columns to improve the detection of metabolic features: hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) with positive electrospray ionization (ESI) and reverse-phase chromatography (C18) with negative ESI. Metabolic feature intensities were then extracted and aligned using apLCMS with modifications by xMSAnalyzer to improve data quality control and minimize batch effects49,50. For further quality control, metabolic features detected in less than 10% of samples were excluded from further analysis, and the remaining metabolic features were normalized via log10-transformations.

Pathway enrichment analysis and chemical annotation

Using the complete MWAS results and the R package metapone (version 1.12.0), we conducted pathway enrichment analysis to identify the functional biological activity of metabolic features associated with PTB or levels of any PBDE52. Metapone accounts for uncertainties in feature-to-metabolite mapping through a weighted testing approach and allows for joint analysis of data from both positive and negative ion modes. We utilized an unadjusted p-value < 0.05 as the significance threshold for input metabolites due to the exploratory study design. After metapone performed enrichment analysis to identify overrepresented biological pathways, we filtered pathways involving three or more significant metabolic features to reduce the likelihood of false positives and better capture pathway-level perturbations53,54.

Chemical annotation was conducted for metabolic features associated with any of the PBDE or PTB at unadjusted p-value < 0.05. The metabolic features were matched to known metabolites based on their mass-to-charge ratio (mass error threshold: 10 ppm) and retention time ± 30 seconds. To confirm the chemical identity of the annotated metabolites with Level One evidence according to the Metabolomics Standard Initiatives, we compared their mass-to-charge ratio, retention time, and ion dissociation patterns to analytical standards55.

Meet-in-the-middle approach

To identify potential metabolites and metabolic pathways linking PBDE exposure with the occurrence of PTB, we utilized a meet-in-the-middle approach56. For this analysis, we first conducted MWAS, pathway enrichment, and chemical annotation analyses separately for the three PBDE and PTB. We then identified overlapping pathways and metabolic features between the PBDE and PTB. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis repeated the MWAS with lipid adjustment, followed by pathway enrichment and chemical annotation. Another set of sensitivity analysis grouped early term births (≥37–<39 weeks) with full term births, given their clinical overlap with PTB, to assess robustness42–44. This was followed by MWAS, pathway enrichment, and chemical annotation. Additionally, after excluding participants who reported substance use during pregnancy (n = 77), we reexamined the associations of serum PBDE and PTB with metabolic features identified with Level One evidence in our primary analysis among the 100 remaining participants.

Results

Study Population and Serum PBDE Levels

Among 177 pregnant participants included in the final analytic sample, 46 (26%) delivered preterm (>20 and <37 weeks of gestation) and 131 (74%) delivered full term (≥39 weeks of gestation) (Table 1). The average maternal age was 24.7 years (standard deviation [SD] = 4.8 years), and the average prenatal BMI was 28.7 kg/m2 (SD = 7.3 kg/m2). Overall, 48.0% of the women had a college degree or higher, 53.1% gave birth to a male infant, and 54.8% had at least one prior pregnancy. Alcohol (9.6%) and tobacco use (16.4%) were less commonly reported in the month prior to the first clinical visit, whereas 35% of the participants reported using marijuana in the month prior to the first visit. Substance use rates were similar in the cohort at large. BDE-47, BDE-99, and BDE-100 were detected in 78% to 100% of samples, with serum geometric mean levels of 90.6 pg/mL (Geometric SD [GSD] = 2.1), 24.3 pg/mL (GSD = 2.4), and 14.7 pg/mL (GSD = 2.9) for BDE-47, BDE-99, and BDE-100, respectively (Table 2). Unadjusted serum PBDE concentrations in our study were consistent with those reported in a previous analysis of this cohort (2014–2017; N = 193)57, but lower than those found in a Northern California pregnancy cohort (2014–2016; N = 180), whereas lipid-adjusted concentrations were similar17. Higher levels of unadjusted serum PBDE in the Northern California cohort may reflect California’s historically strict flammability standards, which resulted in elevated PBDE levels in household furniture and dust58. The similarity in lipid-adjusted concentrations may also reflect differences in sample collection timing, as serum samples in the Northern California cohort were collected at mid-gestation, when circulating lipid levels are typically higher, compared with the first-trimester samples collected in our study59,60. Compared to lipid-adjusted serum PBDE concentrations reported for non-Hispanic Black participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2003–200461, those in our sample were moderately higher, potentially due to differences in study populations as well as pregnancy-related physiological changes.

Chemical annotation

A total of 26 unique metabolites were identified and confirmed with Level One evidence (p < 0.05). Sensitivity analysis

In the lipid-adjusted models, seven of the nine PBDE level-associated pathways and more than 90% of PBDE level-associated metabolites overlapped with those in the primary analysis (Table S3 & Supplementary Data 2). In the analysis grouping early term births with full term births, one of six pathways enriched with PBDE levels and less than 65% of metabolites annotated in association with PBDE levels overlapped with those in the primary analysis (Table S4 and Supplementary Data 3). For PTB, all four enriched pathways and 85% of annotated metabolites overlapped with those in the primary analysis (Table S4 and Supplementary Data 3). In the analysis excluding participants reporting substance use, associations of PBDEs and PTB with metabolites identified in the primary analysis were generally consistent: all metabolites retained the same direction of effect, and 13 of 26 unique metabolites remained statistically significant, likely reflecting reduced sample size (Supplementary Data 4).

Discussion

This study leveraged targeted PBDE exposure assessment and untargeted HRM to identify metabolites and metabolic pathways linking maternal serum PBDE levels and PTB in the Atlanta African American Maternal Child cohort. We observed that thiamine was linked with both BDE-100 levels and PTB, implicating thiamine metabolism as a link between PBDE levels and PTB. We observed associations with thiamine-dependent pathways, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism, suggesting that disruption of energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways may contribute to PBDE level-related PTB risk. To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating potential metabolites and biological pathways linking maternal PBDE levels and the occurrence of PTB.

Thiamine (vitamin B1), an essential vitamin, was positively associated with both BDE-100 levels and PTB. Thiamine pyrophosphokinase (TPK1) converts thiamine to thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), which is an essential cofactor in several metabolic reactions and plays a fundamental role in energy metabolism62. The positive association between thiamine and BDE-100 levels in our study may reflect a disruption in TPP production, potentially due to impaired TPK1 activity or altered thiamine utilization, leading to accumulation of free thiamine in serum. Reduced availability of TPP may perturb TPP-dependent metabolic pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway, which was associated with BDE-100 levels in our study63. The pentose phosphate pathway produces nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), which plays a critical role in the maintenance of glutathione, a key antioxidant, in its reduced form and is thus critical in combating oxidative stress64. Oxidative stress is considered a key factor in the complex etiology of PTB65. Thus, PBDE-induced alterations in the pentose phosphate pathway may impair oxidative stress response and subsequently contribute to increased risk of PTB.

Evidence suggests that disruptions in lipid metabolism and related metabolites may play a role in the occurrence of PTB66–68. Adding to the evidence, our study identified perturbations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and the carnitine shuttle as being associated with PTB, aligning with recent studies that have demonstrated evidence of alterations in lipid metabolites and pathways in association with PTB. In a lipidomics analysis of maternal plasma samples collected during the second and third trimesters, Aung et al. found significant associations between metabolites critical to glycerophospholipid metabolism and PTB66. Furthermore, Morillon et al. identified links between perturbations in second-trimester plasma glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids and PTB67. While there is limited published research assessing serum PBDE level effects on the human metabolome, a Chinese population-based birth cohort study reported links between placental PBDE levels and perturbations in lipid metabolism, including glycerophospholipid metabolism35. Our study found that two acylcarnitines, decanoylcarnitine and O-acetylcarnitine, were negatively associated with PTB and BDE-100 levels, respectively. Both metabolites are involved in the carnitine shuttle, which is essential for cellular energy production as it facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for beta-oxidation69. Reduced levels of serum acylcarnitines, as in our study, may indicate issues in the uptake of free fatty acids into the mitochondria or beta-oxidation, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of PTB. This hypothesis is further supported by the Elshenawy et al. study, which linked PTB with disrupted levels of placental acylcarnitines, including decanoylcarnitine and acetylcarnitine68. However, while serum acylcarnitines were reduced in our study, Elshenawy et al. observed elevations in placental acylcarnitine levels, suggesting tissue-specific differences in acylcarnitine metabolism.

Polyunsaturated fatty acid levels involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism were linked to BDE-100 levels or the occurrence of PTB in our study. Specifically, we found that docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) levels were negatively associated with BDE-100 levels, and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and linoleate levels were negatively associated with PTB. DHA and EPA are anti-inflammatory omega-3 fatty acids, and low levels have been linked with increased risk of PTB70–72. On the other hand, linoleate is an omega-6 fatty acid and is a precursor to arachidonic acid, which has pro-inflammatory derivatives73. Lower abundance of DHA and EPA in maternal serum may indicate increased use or fatty acid peroxidation due to PBDE-induced oxidative stress. Lower levels of linoleate in association with PTB could similarly indicate increased peroxidation or increased conversion to downstream, pro-inflammatory derivatives. While multiple studies have previously implicated altered glycerophospholipid metabolism in the occurrence of PTB67,74,75, our findings suggest that PBDE levels may play a role in inducing such disruptions.

Amino acid metabolism pathways appeared to play a significant role in the link between PBDE levels and PTB. Prior research has found links between PBDE exposure and alterations in amino acid pathways, including a placental metabolomics study in a human birth cohort, in vitro experiments in PC12 cells, and experimental studies in rats35,76,77. Further, the relevance of amino acid metabolism perturbations, and particularly cysteine and methionine metabolism, in PTB has been illustrated in several human and animal studies68,78–80. Among the amino acid pathway metabolites associated with PBDE levels and PTB in this study, four were related to cysteine and methionine metabolism: glycine, glutamine, 5-oxoproline, and methionine. In our study, glycine, glutamine, and 5-oxoproline levels were negatively associated with PBDE levels, and methionine levels were negatively associated with PTB. Cysteine and methionine metabolism play a crucial role in the regulation of oxidative stress through the synthesis of glutathione, wherein methionine, glycine, and glutamine are integral precursors81,82. Furthermore, low 5-oxoproline levels are an indicator of glycine, glutamine, and cysteine scarcity, and subsequently signal glutathione depletion83. PBDE exposure has been associated with increased oxidative stress in experimental and human studies84,85, which may deplete glutathione and its precursors. The inverse associations between methionine levels and PTB thus suggest an oxidative stress mechanism linking maternal PBDE levels and risk of PTB.

Our study had several strengths and limitations. Our analysis integrated targeted exposure assessment with untargeted HRM, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of the impact of maternal PBDE levels on metabolic perturbations and biological pathways. The meet-in-the middle approach employed in our study identified underlying biological mechanisms potentially linking maternal PBDE levels to PTB, and our study thus begins to fill a critical knowledge gap. Furthermore, this study leverages the well-characterized, prospective Atlanta African American Maternal-Child Cohort, providing much-needed insight into the contribution of environmental exposures to racial disparities in rates of adverse birth outcomes, which were carefully phenotyped based on medical record abstraction. However, while the cohort was prospectively established, this study only evaluated metabolomic perturbations at a single time point, making it difficult to assess perturbations across pregnancy in association with PBDE levels and PTB. While previous studies have established positive associations between PBDE levels and PTB29–32, our study did not find a significant association, likely due to limited statistical power given our relatively modest sample size (n = 177). Furthermore, despite adjustment for demographic and lifestyle factors, unmeasured or imperfectly measured variables may have introduced residual confounding in our results. For instance, non-fasting serum samples were used in this analysis; therefore, the metabolomic profile may reflect recent diet and supplement intake. Additionally, while dietary data have previously been reported for this cohort86, this data was only available for a subset of our study sample and was thus excluded to preserve sample size and limit model complexity. Future research could include longitudinal analyses of a larger study sample, examining individual PBDE congeners, PBDE mixtures, and metabolomics at multiple gestational time points to uncover changes in metabolites during pregnancy, particularly leading up to PTB.

Conclusions

Although several PBDEs have been phased out of use, exposure persists in the general population, and growing evidence links them to PTB and other adverse maternal and child health outcomes17,29,30,32,87,88. Our findings suggest that PBDE may perturb thiamine and thiamine-dependent pathways, with effects on oxidative stress and inflammation that increase risk for PTB. By identifying early pregnancy metabolic signatures of PTB, this work provides insight into potential mechanisms and provides a foundation for targeted interventions to reduce the impact of environmental exposures on populations disproportionately affected by PTB.
