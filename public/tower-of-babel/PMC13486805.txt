Antisense oligonucleotide treatment following viral delivery of artificial SOD1-targeting miRNA shows improved efficacy in SOD1-G93A mice

ABSTRACT
Adeno-associated virus (AAV) artificial microRNAs (amiRNAs) targeting superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) have been proposed as a therapeutic strategy for people living with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) who harbor toxic gain-of-function variants in the SOD1 gene. Clinical efforts have primarily focused on AAV delivery via the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), as blood-brain-barrier-crossing capsids are still being developed preclinically. However, intra-CSF delivery has been shown to be highly variable and increases the risk for AAV-related adverse events such as dorsal root ganglion (DRG) toxicity. Here, we show that immunosuppressants (IMS) following intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 in non-human primates (NHPs) prevented AAV-related DRG toxicity, and this benefit was maintained for over 300 days even after immunotherapy stopped at 90 days post-AAV treatment. In line with prior literature, we found intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 to be highly variable in adult mice and NHPs, requiring higher doses of virus to achieve efficacious endpoints. To address this concern, we developed a proof-of-principle study showing that AAV9-amiR-SOD1, in combination with a SOD1-targeting antisense oligonucleotide, provided an additive therapeutic benefit in SOD1-G93A mice compared with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 alone. This combinatorial approach lowered the viral load needed to reach efficacious endpoints, which could mitigate AAV-related adverse events alongside IMS.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder that is defined by the progressive loss of upper and lower motor neurons leading to paralysis and death ∼2–5 years after diagnosis.1 Approximately 2% of all ALS cases harbor toxic gain-of-function variants in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene.2,3 Rodent models that express mutant SOD1 display overt ALS-like phenotypes, including progressive motor neuron degeneration with concomitant motor deficits and eventual paralysis, designating mutant SOD1 as a causative factor in ALS disease progression.4,5,6

In a phase 3 clinical trial (VALOR) and open-label extension (OLE) in participants with SOD1-ALS, tofersen—an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) used to silence SOD1 mRNA expression, reduced the levels of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) SOD1 protein (indirect marker of target engagement) by 35% and plasma neurofilament light chain (NF-L; a marker of axonal injury and neurodegeneration) by 55% over 28 weeks, and earlier initiation of tofersen was associated with reductions of decline in measures of clinical function (ALS Functional Rating Scale Revised [ALSFRS-R]), respiratory function (percentage of predicated slow vital capacity [SVC]), and muscle strength (handheld dynamometry [HHD] megascore) compared to those who initiated tofersen 28 weeks later.7 These data provided clinical evidence that lowering SOD1 protein levels in people living with ALS harboring a toxic gain-of-function SOD1 variant is an effective therapeutic strategy to mitigate disease progression. Adeno-associated virus-based artificial microRNAs (miRNAs) targeting SOD1 (AAV-amiR-SOD1) have been proposed as an alternative to ASOs because their stable expression may offer a one-time treatment option rather than the repeated dosing required of ASOs.8

Several groups have shown that a single administration of AAV-amiR-SOD1 results in delayed development of motor deficits, decreased motor neuron death, and prolonged lifespan in SOD1-G93A mice.9,10,11,12 In non-human primates (NHPs), intra-CSF delivery of AAV-amiR-SOD1 was effective in reducing SOD1 levels in spinal motor neurons, but it has also been reported to show safety concerns related to dorsal root ganglion (DRG) toxicity.13,14 AAV-associated DRG toxicity is characterized by neuronal degeneration/necrosis, nerve-fiber damage both proximally and distally in peripheral nerves and spinal cord, mononuclear cell infiltration, and higher levels of blood plasma/serum and CSF neurofilament.13,15 This phenomenon has been observed in several animal models (mice, rats, rabbits, dogs, piglets, and NHPs) using several different types of AAV cargoes and serotypes, providing no clear evidence of an exact cause of AAV-based DRG toxicity attributable to AAV serotype or cargo design.13,15,16,17,18,19,20,21

In the clinic, intrathecal (i.t.) delivery of AAVrh10-amiR-SOD1 was reported to result in ∼90% reduction of SOD1 in the lumbosacral spinal cord of one patient with SOD1-ALS, but the patient also experienced sensory deficits (meningoradiculitis), suggestive of DRG toxicity.8 These sensory deficits were believed to be mitigated by an aggressive immunosuppressant (IMS) regimen in a second patient treated with AAVrh10-amiR-SOD1.8 This would align with recent reports in NHPs that intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 can lead to immunotoxicity within the DRGs, which can be relieved by IMS treatment.13,22 However, it is unknown whether IMS delays the onset of DRG toxicity or completely suppresses the effect long term.

Beyond concerns related to DRG toxicity, intra-CSF delivery of AAV-based gene therapies has been shown to be highly variable, with limited biodistribution throughout the CNS often most concentrated at the injection site.23 This lack of uniform distribution throughout the CNS often requires higher doses (and viral load) to reach efficacious endpoints at the risk of safety. Furthermore, AAV9—the serotype often used to target CNS disorders—has strong transduction rates in neuronal cell types, especially motor neurons in the spinal cord, but little transduction in other cell types when delivered directly into the CNS.24 Therefore, while AAV9 vectors can effectively transduce neurons in the CNS, this ignores the potential role of other cell types in disease. For example, in the case of SOD1-ALS, expression of mutant SOD1 in different glial cell types (i.e., microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes) has been shown to play an important role in motor neuron cell death and disease progression in several animal models.25,26,27,28 Therefore, intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 as a therapeutic approach may have limited efficacy due to its inability to distribute throughout the CNS and target a wide range of cell types.

Dual treatment strategies converging on the same target could offer an opportunity to overcome some of the limitations associated with monotherapies. For example, an ASO and a small molecule designed to correct SMN2 splicing have shown additive effects when used together versus when used alone in preclinical models of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).29 This indicates that dual therapies are a viable option even when the drug target is the same. This warrants further investigation into whether dual SOD1 knockdown strategies (i.e., ASO and AAV-amiR) could provide additional benefit.

Here, we examined intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 as a therapeutic approach for SOD1-ALS and explored the possibility that combining AAV9-amiR-SOD1 with a SOD1-targeting ASO would provide additive benefit. Consistent with previous literature, postnatal day 0 (P0) intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 can effectively mitigate motor deficits in SOD1-G93A mice. However, i.t. administration in adult SOD1-G93A mice, while still efficacious at higher doses, shows more variability in biodistribution, SOD1 knockdown, and motor recovery. Similarly, in NHPs, i.t. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 results in highly variable viral genome expression and SOD1 knockdown in motor neurons throughout the spinal column. NHPs did experience DRG toxicity when treated with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 alone, but co-administration of IMSs mitigated AAV-related DRG toxicity events. This benefit was observed ∼10 months post-AAV dosing even after the immunotherapy had concluded ∼3 months post-AAV dosing. Lastly, in SOD1-G93A mice, we showed that AAV9-amiR-SOD1 in combination with an ASO strategy has additive benefits in preventing the development of ALS-like phenotypes, suggesting these two therapies may be more effective in combination.

Results

AAV-amiR-SOD1 shows dose-dependent efficacy in SOD1-G93A mice

First, we wanted to assess the efficacy of our AAV9-amiR-SOD1 vector. We developed an AAV-amiR-SOD1 cargo containing a CAG promoter, a single amiRNA targeting SOD1, a woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE), and human growth hormone (hGH) poly(A) tail. This was packaged into AAV9 for CNS delivery (Figure 1A). Several studies have indicated that miRNA biogenesis is impacted in ALS,30,31,32,33 which may impact the efficacy of an amiRNA approach. To address this concern in the context of SOD1-ALS, we examined the ability of our vector to knock down SOD1 mRNA levels in human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived motor neurons from isogenic lines that were either wild type (WT) or harbored heterozygous variants in SOD1 (G93A or A4V). In both SOD1 mutant lines, we were able to successfully knock down SOD1 in a dose-dependent manner similar to what is observed in healthy control motor neurons (Figure 1B). Sequence-based off-targets were also assessed to determine the specificity of the amiRNA using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and miRNA-seq. Potential off-targets were determined by matching expressed sequences (guide and passenger) of the amiR to the human transcriptome. For a transcript to be considered an off-target, it had to match an amiR having at least 10 copies expressed in the miRNA-seq analysis with ≤3 mismatches. Of the 55 potential off-targets identified using this approach, 52 were detected in our experimental RNA-seq dataset evaluating AAV9-amiR-SOD1 treatment in iPSC-MNs. We observed no evidence of downregulation of potential off-targets in WT, SOD1-G93A, and SOD1-A4V cultures treated with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 50,000 or 100,000 (Tables S1–S3). While we observed downregulation of a small number of potential off-targets (6 in WT, 2 in SOD1-G93A, and 5 in SOD1-A4V) at higher MOI (1,000,000) for amiR-SOD1 relative to PBS controls, no impact on these potential off-target species (DDR2, ELK3, EVC, HEPH, HSPB8, OTOGL, and TEX26-AS1) was observed relative to AAV controls (AAV9-U6-sgMOG-CBA-NLS-eGFP or AAV9-CAG-amiR-NT) (Tables S1–S3 and S4). Together, these findings indicate that the downregulation of apparent off-targets detected relative to the PBS control group is more likely due to non-specific responses to AAV treatment rather than amiR-mediated degradation of non-SOD1 transcripts.

Having establishing that our vector can effectively and specifically reduce SOD1 in vitro under disease conditions, we next wanted to examine the efficacy of the amiRNA in vivo. SOD1-G93A mice were given a P0 i.c.v. injection of either artificial CSF (aCSF) or one of five doses of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 (1E10, 2E10, 4E10, 8E10, or 16E10 GC). Median survival was extended in a dose-dependent manner, with an extension of 44, 73, 85, 127, or 181 days in mice treated with 1E10, 2E10, 4E10, 8E10, or 16E10 GC of AAV9-amiR-SOD1, respectively, relative to the aCSF-treated group (Figure 1C). Furthermore, we show a dose-dependent decline in serum levels of phosphorylated neurofilament heavy (pNF-H), an established biomarker of axonal damage, and a dose-dependent improvement in rotarod performance (Figures 1D and 1E). Interestingly, while we saw the expected decline in grip strength and compound muscle action potential (CMAP) in aCSF-treated mice, there was no observed decline in either phenotype in mice treated at any dose level of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 (Figures 1F and 1G). Therefore, amiRNA strategies, if well developed, could be an effective therapeutic strategy for SOD1-ALS.

IT injections in adult mice show higher variability in biodistribution, SOD1 knockdown, and efficacy than P0 i.c.v. injections

It has been demonstrated that P0 i.c.v. or adult i.t. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 can successfully reduce SOD1 levels and rescue ALS-like phenotypes in SOD1-G93A mice.9,10,11,12 However, we wanted to contrast these routes of administration to assess how each would impact AAV9-amiR-SOD1 distribution and efficacy. Therefore, we administered SOD1-G93A mice with 5 doses of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 either P0 i.c.v. (1E10, 2E10, 4E10, 8E10, or 16E10 GC) or i.t. at 6 weeks of age (10E10, 20E10, 40E10, 80E10, or 160E10 GC) to examine differences in biodistribution and efficacy.

In SOD1-G93A mice following P0 i.c.v. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1, there was a dose-dependent increase in viral load in both the cortex (∼2.0E4–2.5E6 vg/μg of gDNA) and spinal cord (∼1.0E3–4.0E4 vg/μg of gDNA) (Figure 2A). We next examined the impacts of P0 i.c.v. administration of AAV-amiR-SOD1 on human SOD1 (huSOD1) mRNA expression in SOD1-G93A mouse spinal cords via fluorescence in situ hybridization (ISH) using RNAscope probes specifically recognizing huSOD1 and mouse choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) to measure target engagement in spinal motor neurons (Figure 2B). Relative to aCSF-treated SOD1-G93A control animals, we observed a significant and dose-dependent reduction in the huSOD1 transcript following P0 i.c.v. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1, ranging from ∼75% at the lowest dose (1E10 GC) to 95% at the highest dose (16E10 GC) (Figure 2C). Having demonstrated robust SOD1 knockdown and amelioration of motor neuron losses and muscle atrophy in SOD1-G93A mice with P0 i.c.v. delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1, we next sought to investigate whether i.t. delivery could confer similar benefits. Consistent with the i.t. route of administration, viral load was markedly greater in the spinal cord relative to the cerebral cortex (Figure 2G). While we observed a consistent increase in viral load in both the cerebral cortex and spinal cord with ascending dose, viral loads showed a greater degree of variability in the spinal cord relative to the cerebral cortex following i.t. administration, as well as relative to both regions post- i.c.v. (Figures 2A and 2G). While ranges evaluated for i.c.v. and i.t. administration resulted in nearly equivalent delivery of viral genomes to the spinal cord (1E3–1E5 vg/μg of gDNA) (Figures 2A and 2G), SOD1 mRNA expression was not as robustly reduced in motor neurons following i.t. AAV-amiR-SOD1 administration (Figures 2C and 2I). Significant lowering of SOD1 mRNA was observed only in the two highest i.t. dose groups (Figure 2I), which was consistent with the observation that significant rescue of motor neuron counts (Figure 2J) and muscle atrophy (Figures 2K and 2L) were restricted to these two treatment groups. While both P0 i.c.v. and adult i.t. dosing can achieve efficacious outcomes, i.t. administration in older animals resulted in higher variability in AAV9-amiR-SOD1 distribution, requiring relatively higher viral loads to achieve desired therapeutic effects.

IMSs reduce AAV-associated DRG toxicity events in NHPs treated with AAV9-amiR-SOD1

Previous literature has shown that IMS strategies can mitigate AAV-associated DRG toxicity in NHPs, including when AAVs harbor an amiR-SOD1 cargo.22 However, it is unclear whether DRG toxicity events will emerge after animals are removed from IMS treatment. To address this question, we examined CSF and serum neurofilament levels in NHPs treated with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 (9.33E13 GC) either with or without an IMS regimen. The IMS regimen included treatment with dexamethasone and tacrolimus, starting 3 days prior to AAV delivery and continued for ∼3 months post-treatment. Decreased cellularity in the thymus due to IMS treatment was confirmed at ∼3 months post AAV treatment, suggesting that IMSs resulted in T cell depletion as expected (Figure S1), which recovered ∼6 months post-AAV treatment or ∼3 months following the removal of the IMS regimen (data not shown). NF-L levels were periodically analyzed in each group over 10 months. NHPs treated with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 without an IMS regimen showed a sharp, transient rise in CSF neurofilament, with NF-L levels peaking on day 43 at levels up to ∼350-fold higher than baseline for multiple animals. A similar rise, albeit to a lesser extent, in serum NF-L was also observed in the AAV9-amiR-SOD1 group without an IMS regimen. The rise in both CSF and serum NF-L resolved ∼90 days post-treatment. In contrast, co-administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 with IMSs resulted in substantially smaller increases in CSF NF-L, with peak NF-L levels only reaching ∼50-fold higher than baseline levels (comparable to the procedure-related NF-L rise in the vehicle-treated group), suggesting a profound impact of IMSs in blocking NF-L increase. Such blockade persisted at any time point over the 10-month period, even after the IMS regimen ended (Figure 3A). Right DRGs from the cervical (C2, C4, and C6), thoracic (T2, T4, and T6), and lumbar (L2, L4, and L5/L6) regions were processed for light microscopic examination 301 days post-treatment with AAV. i.t. injections with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 in NHPs produced high variability in biodistribution, transduction, and downstream target engagement

We next examined the biodistribution of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 and its impact on SOD1 expression in NHP spinal cord sections following i.t. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1(3.35E13 or 5.95E13 GC) with and without IMSs via ISH-based detection of both the WPRE component of the AAV9-amiR-SOD1 construct and SOD1 alongside a ChAT marker for motor neurons (Figure 4A; Table 1). Independent of IMS treatment, we observed average transduction efficiencies of approximately 50%–70% in lumbar and thoracic spinal cord sections and ∼15%–20% in cervical spinal cord sections (Figure 4B), consistent with greater concentration of the viral vector closer to the lumbar puncture injection site. A significant correlation between the extent of viral transduction and expression was achieved, and the degree of SOD1-lowering per animal was observed across spinal cord sections, with the strongest correlations observed in lumbar and thoracic spinal cord sections (Figures 4D and 4E). In addition, amiR-SOD1 showed higher levels of expression in lower spinal regions compared to upper spinal regions (lumbar > thoracic > cervical), and WPRE expression and SOD1 knockdown correlated with amiR-SOD1 concentration in the lumbar region (Figure S2). Combo therapy of SOD1 targeting ASO with an AAV-amiR-SOD1 provides improved benefit in SOD1-G93A mice

Given the variability in biodistribution observed following intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-based gene therapies, we sought to determine whether adding ASO treatment following AAV9-amiR-SOD1 treatment would improve overall efficacy in SOD1-G93A mice. To model a clinical scenario in which suboptimal knockdown of SOD1 is achieved with AAV treatment and evaluate the potential for an additive effect of the ASO, we selected lower AAV9-amiR-SOD1 doses than those previously explored (Figure 1). P0 i.c.v. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 in SOD1-G93A mice was given at a dose of either 0.25E10, 0.5E10, 1.0E10, or 3.0E10 GC, which was followed by multiple doses of either a SOD1-targeting ASO or vehicle (Figure 5A). Similar to observations made in Figure 1C, AAV9-amiR-SOD1 when administered alone showed an increase in median survival, extending median survival by 13.5, 19.5, 51.5, and 39.5 days in animals treated with 0.25E10, 0.5E10, 1.0E10, and 3.0E10 GC, respectively, compared to vehicle-treated mice. In contrast, SOD1-G93A mice given an ASO plus AAV9-amiR-SOD1 combination therapy had median survival extensions of 41.5, 54, 49.5, and 91 days when receiving 0.25E10, 0.5E10, 1.0E10, or 3E10 GC of AAV, respectively (Figure 5B). AAV and ASO combo treatment was also beneficial in lowering neurofilaments, retaining CMAP function, and improving rotarod performance relative to the group treated with AAV alone. Furthermore, ASO plus a low dose of AAV (0.25E10 GC) performed similarly to high-dose AAV (3.0E10 GC) alone (Figures 5C–5E). This proof-of-concept experiment provides evidence that a combination of ASO with an AAV modality can have additive effects, allowing us to reduce the AAV viral load to meet efficacious endpoints, reduce risk of AAV-related toxicities, and overcome limitations of AAV biodistribution after intra-CSF delivery.

Discussion

Intra-CSF delivery of AAV-based amiRNAs has the potential to be an effective therapeutic approach for people living with SOD1-ALS, but challenges in safety and biodistribution have limited their implementation. Here, we highlighted some challenges in the delivery of AAV9 cargo, with a focus on AAV9-amiR-SOD1, and proposed ways to overcome these limitations. For example, AAV-associated DRG toxicity has been well reported in NHPs across several groups using a variety of cargo designs, including AAV9-amiR-SOD1, suggesting a common phenomenon that needs to be addressed.13,15,22 IMSs can mitigate AAV-related adverse events in the DRGs,22 and here, we further showed that this benefit persists long term even after NHPs are taken off the IMS regimen. In addition, we showed that intra-CSF delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 in adult mice and NHPs led to variable biodistribution and therefore required higher dosing to achieve efficacious outcomes, thereby increasing the risk of AAV-related adverse events. In a proof-of-principle study, using a combo strategy of ASO and AAV-based amiRNAs, we provided evidence that these two modalities provided an additive benefit in SOD1-G93A mice, which reduced the viral load needed to reach efficacious endpoints.

Previous studies have reported high variability in spinal motor neuron transduction and unequal distribution in the spinal cord sections following i.t. injections of AAV9-based vectors in NHPs.35,36,37 Bey et al. reported AAV9-GFP transduction efficiencies of ∼3%–45%, 1%–60%, and ∼5%–66% in cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spinal motor neurons, respectively.36 Another group reported that NHPs given a single i.t. injection of scAAV9-CBA-GFP showed ∼30%, ∼50%, and ∼70% motor neuron transduction efficiency in cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spinal regions, respectively.35 This aligns with the high variability of motor neuron transduction and reduced uptake of AAV9 in the cervical spinal region after i.t. administration of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 observed in this study. One proposed strategy to provide more even distribution of AAVs throughout the CNS following intra-CSF delivery is to use Trendelenburg tilting tables, which are commonly used in the clinic for proper spreading of anesthetics through the CSF.38 Infusion of AAV9 into the CSF while NHPs were tilted 15°–30° head down resulted in improved spreading of AAV vectors throughout the spinal cord and achieved higher cervical uptake with ∼55% motor neuron transduction.35 Overall, this study is in agreement with several other studies showing that intra-CSF delivery of AAV9 transduces only a subpopulation of motor neurons with unequal distribution throughout the spinal cord. This limits the effectiveness of drugs such as AAV9-amiR-SOD1.

One strategy we proposed was to supplement an AAV9-amiR-SOD1 with ASO treatment to improve efficacious outcome. In our proof-of-principle study in SOD1-G93A mice, a low dose of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 (0.25E10 GC) supplemented with ASO achieved similar efficacy with respect to survival, neurofilament reduction, and motor performance outcomes compared to a high dose of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 alone. McCampbell and colleagues have previously tested ASO monotherapy and shown improved disease outcomes in SOD1-G93A mice at multiple dose levels. We have replicated these findings in our study (data not shown). However, the data presented here provide evidence that ASO and AAV combo therapies could have the potential to improve efficacious outcomes. The additive effects of these therapies could be for two reasons: (1) since AAV9 primarily targets neurons and astrocytes and ASOs have a more homogenous distribution across cells within the region (neurons, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and astrocytes),39 the combo of these two strategies may allow for improved targeting of neurons and other cell types relevant to disease, and (2) ASOs mediate RNA silencing through the RNase-H-mediated degradation pathway, whereas amiRNAs engage with the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to initiate RNA degradation.40 Utilizing both pathways could have greater potency in gene silencing, providing superior benefit. ASOs as an add-on therapy to AAV9-amiR-SOD1 could result in more efficacious outcomes at lower viral loads, reducing the risk of AAV-related toxicities. However, determination of optimal dosing regimens to balance both efficacy and safety of this combination approach still needs to be addressed. Future experiments also assessing the potential advantages/disadvantages of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) in combination with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 should be performed and would provide an interesting insight into the hypothesis on whether having two therapies utilizing the same RNA degradation pathway (i.e., RISC-mediated silencing) is less or more effective than having two modalities targeting different RNA degradation pathways as described here.

With novel RNA-based technologies continuing to emerge, including mRNA, RNAi, ASO, RNA aptamers, and RNA activation, among others, exploring alternative combination approaches could offer an opportunity for different therapies to complement each other to improve efficacious outcomes in patients. For example, RNAi strategies targeting antisense C9orf72 RNA foci or splice-modifying ASOs targeting STMN2 or UNC13A are currently being explored as potential monotherapies for C9-ALS or broad ALS (including C9-ALS), respectively.41,42,43 If these therapies are proven effective individually, it is plausible that they may work better in combination in a C9-ALS population, as they target unique pathways related to neuronal function and degeneration. In addition, with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) showing the ability to package multiple mRNA and siRNA targets together,44 these therapies could be delivered as one rather than individually.

Advances in AAVs that can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and be delivered intravenously (i.v.) in an adult system have demonstrated improved biodistribution throughout the CNS. For example, developing AAV capsids that have preferential binding to the human transferrin receptor (TFR1) or CD98 heavy chain (CD98hc) have shown efficient CNS-wide gene delivery after tail vein delivery in mice expressing a humanized form of TFR1 or CD98, respectively.45,46 Recent studies on alkaline phosphatase (ALPL)-targeting capsids have also shown significantly improved biodistribution and motor neuron transduction in both mice and NHPs over AAV9 following i.v. delivery.47 However, while i.v. delivery has been shown to reduce AAV-related DRG toxicity severity in preclinical models, it does not completely eradicate the risk.15 Furthermore, i.v. delivery increases the risk for hepatotoxicity and, while mild in most clinical cases, can lead to liver failure and death.48 Therefore, while BBB capsids offer exciting new avenues to achieve superior biodistribution within the CNS, there is still a need to mitigate safety risks related to AAV delivery.

IMSs have been shown to be effective in mitigating AAV-related DRG toxicity in NHPs treated with different AAV transgene and RNAi cargo designs.22 This manuscript further shows that the benefits of using IMSs are maintained for at least 10 months in NHPs. It has been reported that DRG toxicity is likely a transient event that occurs within the first 12–13 weeks following the administration of an AAV, suggesting this is a crucial period for intervention to mitigate AAV-related DRG toxicity.49 Pathological events associated with AAV-related DRG toxicity show decreased severity and incidence at >12 weeks post-treatment compared with those observed at <12 weeks post-injection, which aligns with the transient rise in biofluid neurofilament levels, suggestive of resolution of this toxicity long-term.49 Here, we show that if DRG toxicity is not addressed, there could be long-term damage to the DRGs, as observed in NHPs who received the AAV9-amiR-SOD1 without IMS regimen. This may be important in the context of ALS, as it is unknown how this additional neurotoxicity on top of the neurodegenerative phenotype may impact disease progression, at least initially. In lieu of AAV capsids and/or cargoes that de-target the DRGs, co-administration of AAVs with the appropriate IMS could be an effective strategy to safely deliver the current generation of gene therapies.

Neurofilament has been a useful biomarker for assessing potential AAV-related DRG toxicity events in a preclinical setting, but its lack of specificity for DRG toxicity could limit its ability to be used in the clinic as an adverse drug-response biomarker. Rises in neurofilament associated with AAV delivery are easily detected in NHPs and other animal species that do not contain an underlying neurodegenerative phenotype. However, people living with SOD1-ALS already have high CSF/serum neurofilament levels, making it uncertain whether we would be able to detect a further rise in neurofilament suggestive of DRG toxicity after delivery of AAV9-amiR-SOD1. Additionally, if AAV9-amiR-SOD1 was effective in reducing motor neuron degeneration, we would expect a reduction in neurofilament levels as previously established,7 but this benefit may be masked by rises in neurofilament associated with DRG toxicity. Therefore, it is essential that we identify novel biomarkers of DRG toxicity that distinguish from disease-related biomarkers. miR-21-5p has been suggested as a potential alternative biomarker to neurofilament to inform on DRG toxicity, as it shows increased expression in DRGs in response to AAV-related DRG toxicity in NHPs, and furthermore, these increases are detectable in the CSF and correlate with increases in neurofilament following AAV treatment.13 However, further work needs to be done to determine the specificity of rises in miR-21-5p levels in response to AAV-associated DRG toxicity.

In summary, we highlighted that intra-CSF AAV delivery via lumbar puncture is highly variable in both mice and NHPs, necessitating higher AAV doses to achieve ideal efficacy at the risk of reduced safety. We focused on AAV9-amiR-SOD1 and highlighted that previously published IMS strategies have long-term benefits in preventing DRG toxicity and, furthermore, show that administering SOD1-targeting ASOs in conjunction with AAV9-amiR-SOD1 can significantly reduce the viral load needed to achieve efficacy comparable to AAV9-amiR-SOD1 alone. Here, we provide two practical measures that may prove beneficial to improving the safety and delivery of AAV-based gene therapies in the context of SOD1-ALS: immunosuppressive treatment and combination ASO treatment. Additional studies to evaluate the potential long-term benefits of immunosuppression-mediated mitigation of DRG toxicity and the safety of combined ASO and gene therapy treatments for SOD1-ALS (and other targets/indications) in NHP models are still needed and will provide critical information to aid in advancement of AAV-based gene therapies to the clinic.

Methods

Plasmid constructs

The transgene plasmid was composed of a single-stranded miR-SOD1 transgene flanked by two AAV inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) and driven by a CAG promoter. The sequence 3′ of the primary miRNA is composed of a woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE) and a hGH poly(A) sequence. The miRNA scaffold is based on the miR-E sequence.50 The nucleotide sequence for the amiRNA is listed as follows, with the guide sequence underlined; 5′- TGTTTGAATGAGGCTTCAGTACTTTACAGAATCGTTGCCTGCACATCTTGGAAACACTTGCTGGGATTACTTCGACTTCTTAACCCAACAGAAGGCTCGAGAAGGTATATTGCTGTTGACAGTGAGCGTCAGGATACATTTCTACAGCTTAGTGAAGCCACAGATGTAAGCTGTAGAAATGTATCCTGATGCCTACTGCCTCGGACTTCAAGGGGCTAGAATTCGAGCAATTATCTTGTTTACTAAAACTGAATACCTTGCTATCTCTTTGATACATTTTTACAAAGCTGAATTAAAATGGTATAAATTAAATCACTTT-3′.

The Rep/Cap expression plasmid contains an AAV2 Rep sequence inclusive of both the long (Rep78) and short (Rep52) major isoforms of Rep2. The capsid sequence is derived from Cap9.hu14 (WT) and contains all WT AAV9 coding sequences, including but not limited to VP1, VP2, VP3, and the assembly-activating protein (AAP). Finally, the p5 promoter, which typically drives the longest AAV transcript (Rep72 and Cap), was translocated from the 5′ end of the Rep gene to the 3′ end of the capsid gene. The helper plasmid encodes the adenovirus serotype 2 (Ad2) genes E1A, E2, E4, and VA RNA. All three plasmid constructs contain a kanamycin-resistant gene to allow for selection when transiently transfected into HEK293 cells.51

Production of AAV-amiR-SOD1 vectors

AAV-amiR-SOD1 vectors were produced by a suspension, triple transfection process at a 250 L scale. Suspension HEK293 cells were thawed and expanded in a series of shake flasks, scaled into a 50 L single-use bioreactor, and finally inoculated into a 250 L single-use production bioreactor. Rep/Cap, transgene, and helper plasmids were pre-complexed with a cationic polymer transfection reagent and then added to the 250 L production bioreactor to allow transfection of the plasmids into the suspension HEK293 cells. Lysis and endonuclease treatment of the culture and harvest occur 4 days after transient transfection.

The harvest material is clarified by depth filtration followed by 0.22-mm filtration. The clarified harvest material is affinity purified using AAV9 affinity chromatography followed by anion-exchange chromatography to separate empty and full AAV viral vector capsids. Using tangential flow filtration, the product is concentrated and buffer exchanged into a standard formulation buffer at neutral pH mimicking CSF. Purified AAV9-amiR-SOD1 viral vector product is sterile filtered to form the drug substance. The AAV9-amiR-SOD1 drug substance was diluted in formulation buffer to the required dosing concentrations for the mouse and NHP studies and sterile filtered into final test article vials.

Characterization of AAV-amiR-SOD1 drug substance and test article

The AAV9-amiR-SOD1 drug substance and test article were characterized in a panel of analytical assays to assess genome titer, genome and capsid identity, biological activity, percentages of full and empty capsid content, vector purity, percentage of aggregation, and correct formulation by assessing pH, osmolality, appearance, and poloxamer 188 concentration as well as to confirm low levels of endotoxin and bioburden. The analytical assessment of the AAV-amiR-SOD1 drug substance and test article demonstrated that the material met all established acceptance criteria.

iPSC lines

Lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) were obtained from an unaffected 40-year-old White male through Coriell (#ND07189) and reprogrammed using non-viral vectors, following the Epi5 Episomal iPSC Reprogramming Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A15960). Isogenic lines containing heterozygous SOD1 G93A (±) or SOD1 A4V (±) mutations were generated by CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, introducing point mutations into the endogenous SOD1 locus of human iPSCs. Single-cell clones were screened and validated by Sanger sequencing to confirm heterozygous incorporation of the desired alleles.

iPSC maintenance

iPSCs were thawed into T-75 flasks (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #156499) coated with human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-qualified Matrigel (Corning, #35477) and cultured in mTeSR Plus (STEMCELL Technologies, #100-0276) with 5 μM Y-27632 (MilliporeSigma, #Y0503). Following 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with mTeSR Plus. iPSC cultures were routinely passaged every 5–7 days using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, #05872), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

iPSC-motor neuron generation

iPSC-motor neurons were generated as previously described in Maury et al.52 Briefly, iPSCs were washed with DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14190144) and dissociated with ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, 05872) for 2–5 min at 37°C. Cell colonies (5–10 cells/colony) were resuspended in 1:1 mTeSR Plus medium containing 5 μM Y-27632 and N2B27 medium (1:1 DMEM/F-12 [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11320033] and neurobasal medium [Thermo Scientific, #21103049] supplemented with 1× N2 supplement [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #17502048], 1× B27 supplement [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #17504044], MEM NEAA [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11140050], 1% penicillin-streptomycin [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15140122], 0.1% b-mercaptoethanol [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #21985023], and 20 μM L-ascorbic acid [MilliporeSigma, #A4403]) and transferred to ultra-low attachment (ULA) T-75 U-flasks (Corning, #3814) to promote the formation of embryoid bodies (EBs). EB cultures were grown in the presence of 40 μM SB431542 (MilliporeSigma, #54317) and 3 μM CHIR99021 (Tocris, #252917069) from days 0 to 3, 0.2 μM LDN 193189 (Miltenyi Biotec, #130106540) from days 0 to 5, 0.5 μM SAG (Millipore, #566660) and 1 μM retinoic acid (MilliporeSigma, #302794) from days 2 to 11, and 10 μM DAPT (MilliporeSigma, #208255805) from days 9 to 13. On day 14, EBs were dissociated with Papain and DNase I (Worthington Biochemical, #LK003178, #LK003172) and washed twice with DPBS, and day in vitro 0 (DIV0) iPSC-motor neurons were resuspended in Dissociation Plating Media (neurobasal medium containing 1× N2 supplement, 1× B27 supplement, 20 μM L-ascorbic acid, 1% knockout serum replacement [Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10828010], 5 μM Y-27632, and 10 ng/mL BDNF [Tocris, #2837], GDNF [R&D Systems, #212-GD-050], and CNTF [R&D Systems, #257-NT-050]). Dissociated cells were plated at a density of 1.0–1.5 E5 cells/cm2 in poly-D-lysine (MilliporeSigma, #A-003-E)- and laminin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23017)-coated 12- or 6-well tissue culture plates. At DIV2, the medium was replaced with Feed Media (neurobasal medium containing 1× N2 supplement, 1× B27 supplement, 20 μM L-ascorbic acid, and 10 ng/mL BDNF, GDNF, and CNTF, and 1× CultureOne Supplement [Thermo Fisher Scientific, A3320201]). The medium was changed with 50% conditioned medium every 5–7 days.

iPSC-derived motor neuron transduction

iPSC-derived motor neurons were transduced at either 0, 50,000, 100,000, or 1,000,000 MOI on DIV5 and were maintained in motor neuron feed media and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 until DIV19. Cells were harvested at DIV19 using QIAzol (Qiagen, #79306), and RNA extraction was performed using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, #217004) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Experiments were performed across many plates; therefore, to account for plate-to-plate variability, each plate contained at least one PBS control group per genotype. This resulted in a higher number of PBS-treated motor neuron samples compared to other treatment groups.

RT-qPCR

Quant-IT RNA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #Q33140) was used to determine the total RNA concentration. RNA was converted to cDNA with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Applied Biosystems, #4368814). Real-time PCR was performed using the TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific #4444556) and the TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems). Primer/probes included GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1; VIC/MGB) and custom probes targeting SOD1 exon 4 (forward: 5′-TGGTGTGGCCGATGTGTCTA-3′, reverse: 5′-ATGATGCAATGGTCTCCTGAGA-3′, probe: 5′-6FAM-TGAAGATTCTGTGATCTCA-MGB/NFQ-3′). Changes in SOD1 levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCT method. Specifically, SOD1 Ct values were normalized to GAPDH (a housekeeping gene) Ct values to calculate ΔCT, and the average ΔCT for the PBS-treated group for each was used to normalize individual ΔCT values for each cell line to calculate ΔΔCT. 2–ΔΔCT was then used to calculate fold change.

RNA-seq data analysis

The FASTQ files were generated from the BCL files using bcl2fastq v.2.20 (Illumina). The RNA-seq analysis pipeline consisted of alignment with STAR v.2.5.2a against human genome version GRCh38 and Gencode gene model release 27.53,54 After alignment, quantification of gene expression was carried out with RSEM v.1.2.26.55 Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v.1.30.0.56 The function apeglm was used for LFC shrinkage to reduce noise and preserve large differences.57 We predicted potential off-targets by performing in silico alignment (Bowtie v.0.12.7) of expressed sequences (guide and passenger) of the amiR to the mature human transcriptome (Gencode v.21).58 A given guide or passenger strand sequence was considered to be expressed if >10 reads were detected in the smRNA library. For a transcript to be considered an in silico off-target, it must align with ≤3 mismatches to a given guide or passenger strand sequence and have a transcript support level (tsl) ≤ 2.

Mouse studies and procedures

C57BL/6J (stock #000664) mice and transgenic SOD1-G93A mice (B6.Cg-Tg(SOD1∗G93A)/1Gur/J) (stock # 004435) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratories. Animals were randomized to treatment groups in all studies without stratification by any other variable. Experimenters were blind to treatment. Mice were housed in a 12/12 h light/dark cycle in a temperature-controlled room at 22°C–24°C, with access to food pellets and water provided ad libitum. All animal procedures were performed following the Biogen Institution Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The i.c.v. procedure was adapted from Chen et al.12 On P0, neonatal mice were anesthetized via hypothermia and injected with 4 μL AAV-RNAi vectors diluted in PBS with 0.25% Fast Green using a 33G needle and 10 μL 45-bevel syringes (Hamilton). The injection site targeted the lateral ventricle located laterally from the sagittal suture, midway between lambda and bregma, to a depth of 2 mm. Pups were allowed to recover on a warm pad post-injection and returned to their home cage until weaning at 4 weeks of age. P0 i.c.v. injections were randomized and staggered for all treatment groups to minimize variation due to the timing of injection. Mice were only injected if they were within 24 h of age. At the end of the study, the spinal cord and cortex were collected at 10 weeks of age for bioanalytical and histological analysis. i.t. injections were performed on mice 5–6 weeks of age. These mice were anesthetized with isoflurane for administration of i.t. injections, as previously described.13 Once anesthetized, 10 μL of AAV or ASO buffered in PBS was administered through the i.t. space between the L3 and L5 vertebrae using a 29G, 0.3 mL syringe (Smiths Medical ASD, cat. #4429-3). Previously published “ASO 3” was used in this study as a tool ASO for combo therapy assessment.34 The ASO sequence is AGoToGTTTAATGTToToATC (unmodified phosphodiester linkages noted with “o”; MOE [2′-O-methoxyethylribose] modifications are underlined; cEt [(S)-2′,4′-constrained 2′-O-ethyl] modifications are italicized).

Rotarod and grip strength

Mice were acclimated to the testing room for approximately 30 min before motor function assessment. For assessment on the rotarod, mice were placed on a slowly rotating rod programmed to accelerate from 5 to 40 rpm (revolutions per minute) over the subsequent 5 min. Three trials were conducted for each mouse with a minimum of 5 min between them. The length of time an animal stayed on the rod before it fell off was recorded. If mice did not fall from the rod, they were removed at 5 min. For the assessment of grip strength, mice were individually removed from their home cage by the tail and allowed to grip a small bar with their forelimbs only, which was connected to a mechanical force-measuring device. The animal was then gently pulled horizontally away from the bar until it lost its grip. The process was repeated until at least 3 measurements were recorded on the TSE computer program.

CMAP

Experimenters were blind to genotype and treatment conditions. CMAP recordings were performed under isoflurane (1.5%–2.5%) anesthesia and with body temperature maintained at 37°C. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed in the absence of a pain withdrawal reflex upon mild pressure on the hindlimb. Disposable monopolar 28G needle electrodes (Teca 25 mm, 28G electrodes, Natus Medical) were used for stimulation and recording. The sciatic nerve was stimulated near the sciatic notch with constant-current monophasic square-wave pulses started at 1.0 mA and increased at 0.5 mA increments. Each stimulation was 0.1 ms in duration and delivered every 2 s. For CMAP recordings, the recording electrode was placed intramuscularly 1 mm deep in the tibialis anterior. Stimulation current was increased until the amplitude of CMAP had reached a maximum, known as the supramaximal stimulus (1.5–3.5 mA). Recordings were performed using a current level of 0.5 mA above this value. The amplitude of the maximum peak to minimum peak of the biphasic CMAP waveform was recorded after 0.8 ms of stimulation to exclude the latency from the initiation of stimulus to the initiation of the response. For each animal, the right and left leg amplitudes were averaged to obtain the CMAP value.

Survival study guideline

Daily body weight and paralysis were monitored in SOD1-G93A mice after 130–140 days of age or as soon as onset of the disease phenotype was observed. DietGel, hydrogel, and dry food pellets were supplemented daily. Subcutaneous fluids (1 mL) were provided if dehydration was observed. The parameters measured for a humane endpoint to euthanize an animal included a loss of more than 32% of peak body weight and the inability to return to a quadruped position within 15 s after being placed on the side.

Tissue viral load

AAV biodistribution was examined in the mouse cortex and spinal cord (cervical/thoracic regions) by examining the amount of viral DNA. DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen #69504) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Concentration of DNA was determined using a Nanodrop8000 spectrophotometer. Viral DNA was examined using qPCR with the TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems #4304437) and primer/probes designed to target the WPRE element in the viral construct. 20 ng of total DNA was added per reaction. Four replicates were run for each sample. A standard curve was developed to determine the viral copy number in each sample. The final viral load in each sample was determined by normalizing the WPRE copy number to micrograms of DNA within the qPCR reaction. WPRE primer/probe sequences were as follows: forward primer, 5′-TGTTGCTCCTTTTACGCTATGTG-3′; reverse primer, 5′-CGGGAAGCAATAGCATGATACA-3′; and probe, 5′-6FAM-ATACGCTGCTTTAATGC-MGB/NFQ-3′.

Neurofilament assessment

NHP and mouse whole blood was collected in BD Microtainer SST Clog Activator/Gel tubes (Becton Dickinson). NHP and mouse blood samples were centrifuged at 4,600 rpm to isolate serum. NHP CSF was also periodically collected. Blood serum and CSF samples were used to analyze levels of pNF-H in mice and NF-L in NHPs. Neurofilament-level measurements were quantified using the pNF-H kit (Protein Simple, SPCKB-PS-000519) or NF-L kit (Protein Simple, SPCKB-PS-002448) and the ELLA microfluid ELISA platform following the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse serum samples were tested at a 5-fold dilution. NHP serum samples were tested at a 2-fold dilution, and CSF samples were tested at an 8-fold dilution.

Muscle fiber analyses

For muscle fiber staining, the Gomori method was used for staining reticulin fibers. Image analysis was carried out in Visiopharm using custom algorithms to measure muscle fiber diameter, as previously described in LIMS 20028–20036; four sections were analyzed per animal (level 1 and level 2, L + R). Mean values for average lesser diameter were determined for each animal.

Motor neuron analyses

Spinal cords were collected, fixed in formalin, processed, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were collected at 4 different levels of the spinal cord. huSOD1 (Hs-SOD1-NoXMm-C1, ACD catalog no. 1147998-C1) and endogenous murine ChAT (Mm-Chat-C2, ACD catalog no. 408738-C2) transcripts were detected by ISH via RNAscope incorporating the following pretreatment conditions: (1) epitope removal 2: 15 min at 88°C; (2) protease III: 15 min at 40°C. Hs-SOD1-NoXMm-C1 probes were diluted 1:160 to account for very high huSOD1 expression levels in SOD1-G93A animals. Primary SOD1 (C1) and mouse ChAT (C2) probes were subsequently detected via application of Opal 750 dye at 1:250 and Opal 650 at 1:1,000, respectively. Samples were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were acquired using a 3DHistech Pannoramic SCAN II digital slide scanner equipped with a 40× objective. Image analysis was carried out in Visiopharm (v.2022.12). Ventral spinal horns were manually annotated, and ChAT+ motor neurons were identified using AI-assisted algorithms trained on a set of manually annotated motor neurons. ChAT-negative cells were identified by a DAPI+ nuclear signal and outlined by incorporating a modest cytoplasmic border (1.75–5 μm). Detection of SOD1 transcripts was carried out using custom spot detection algorithms to identify spots larger than 0.04 μm2 and determine the relative percentage of positive tissue area for individual cells and cell populations. Signal positivity cutoffs were set to exclude any signals overlapping with background-level non-specific signals in WT mice (no huSOD1 expression). Per animal, the SOD1+ relative tissue areas are expressed relative to median values measured in AAV-naïve SOD1-G93A animals (aCSF treated).

DRG histopathology in NHPs

3- to 5-year-old naive male and female Vietnamese cynomolgus monkeys were randomized into 3 treatment groups and housed in either pairs or trios of the same sex and dose group. Animals were housed and fed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011), with water available ad libitum and enrichment provided. On day 1, male weights ranged from 4.1 to 7.0 kg, and female weights ranged from 3.3 to 3.7 kg. Each treatment group received a single i.t. lumbar bolus injection. Two males and 3 females received the reference item (1 mM Na2HPO4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.4 mM CaCl2, 0.8 mM MgCl2, and 0.01% poloxamer 188 [pH 7.2]). Three males and 2 females were treated with AAV-miR-SOD1 at a 9.33E13 GC/dose. Two males and 3 females were treated with AAV-miR-SOD1 at 9.33E13 GC/dose plus an IMS regimen of decreasing dose levels of dexamethasone and tacrolimus administered by oral gavage initiated on day −3 and ending during week 13. On day 301, the animals were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital followed by exsanguination. The right DRG with the dorsal and ventral nerve roots from the cervical (C), thoracic (T), and lumbar (L) regions and the spinal cord were collected (C2, C4, and C6; T2, T4, and T6; and L2, L4, and L5/6). The tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffer formalin (NBF), embedded in paraffin blocks, and sectioned within 72 h post-necropsy. The DRGs with the dorsal and ventral spinal nerve roots were sectioned longitudinally. The sections were mounted on glass slides and stained using a standard H&E staining protocol. The tissues were evaluated by light microscopy examination. Grades of severity for lesions in nervous tissues were determined as unremarkable, minimal, mild, moderate, marked, or severe following the Society of Toxicology Pathology Scientific and Regulatory Committee points to consider.59

Target engagement studies and transduction in NHPs

Cynomolgus monkeys were group housed according to guidelines described in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011) under the supervision of a study director and clinical veterinarian. Animals were socially housed and provided enrichment items and food supplements, outside of study procedures. Meloxicam was administered subcutaneously prior to dosing/CSF collection and orally thereafter for 2 days. Animals were deprived of food for at least 6 h prior to dosing and anesthetized, intubated, and placed in lateral recumbency for the dosing procedure. AAV9-amiR-SOD1 was administered to cynomolgus monkeys by a single lumbar puncture i.t. bolus injection using an atraumatic spinal needle (B. Braun, 25G, 1 inch) in combination with oral IMSs (dexamethasone and tacrolimus). NHPs were examined 92 days following AAV treatment to determine transduction efficiency of AAV9-amiR-SOD1 (Table 1).

Scheduled euthanasia was carried out following intramuscular administration of a sedative cocktail (ketamine, dexmedetomidine, and glycopyrrolate) under veterinary supervision by intravenous injection. Sodium pentobarbital was then administered by intravenous injection, followed by exsanguination via axillary or femoral artery incision. Complete necropsy examination and tissue collection were performed following euthanasia.

NHP tissue samples were collected at the time of scheduled euthanasia (day 92). Spinal cord tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 1–2 days and then embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were stained on a Ventana Discovery ULTRA (Roche) autostainer using duplexed immunohistochemistry (IHC)-ISH assays to detect ChAT (Abcam catalog no. ab178850) alongside RNAscope ISH probes targeting WPRE (WPRE-O1, ACD catalog no. 450269) or NHP SOD1 (Mfa-SOD1, ACD catalog no. 472009). Tissue sections were pretreated with RNAscope VS Protease, followed by incubation with RNAscope probes (2 h at 43°C), amplification (AMP 7), and visualization with Discovery mRNA Fast Red (Roche, reference no. 253-4879). ChAT primary antibodies were diluted 1:600 from the stock concentration (stock = 2.325 mg/mL), and slides were incubated for 1 h at 37°C, followed by incubation with anti-Rabbit HQ (Roche, reference no. 760-4815) (24 min at 37°C) and anti-HQ HRP (24 min at 37°C) (Roche, reference no. 760–4820), and visualized with DAB (Roche, reference no. 760-4304) (8 min at 37°C). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, coverslipped, and imaged using a 3DHistech Pannoramic P250 digital slide scanner equipped with a 40× objective and imported into Visiopharm for subsequent analysis. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually annotated to surround Lamina IX of the ventral horn in each spinal cord tissue section. Motor neurons were identified using custom algorithms developed in Visiopharm to detect ChAT+ tissue areas. WPRE+ and SOD1+ dots were detected using custom algorithms. WPRE positivity cutoffs were set to exclude any signals overlapping with background-level non-specific signal in the reference group (group 1). Motor neuron transduction levels were reported as the proportion of WPRE+ motor neurons relative to total motor neurons identified per animal in lumbar, thoracic, and cervical spinal cord sections. SOD1 expression levels were reported as the relative SOD1+ tissue area in segmented motor neurons, normalized to the median of the untreated reference group (group 1) per spinal cord region.

amiR-SOD1 MSD assay

The MSD N-PLEX platform, a nucleic acid detection platform that uses MSD electrochemiluminescence detection technology, was used to measure amiR expression in tissue lysate samples. The bulk matrix used as diluent and the study samples were pretreated with an RNase-inactivating reagent. An amiR calibration curve was prepared by diluting the amiR calibrator in the inactivated bulk matrix diluent. Next, hybridization probes specific for amIR-SOD1 were briefly incubated with the lysate samples, calibrator samples, and diluent to form the amiR-probe complexes. The 96-well N-PLEX MSD plate, used to capture the fully formed amiR-probe complexes, was prepared for use by adding N-PLEX blocker to each well and incubating for 30 min at 37°C. At the end of the blocker incubation, the plate was washed three times with D-PBS wash solution. The calibrator, lysate, and diluent samples were then added to the blocked N-PLEX plate and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following another D-PBS wash step to remove any unbound samples and probes, 50 μL of the Sulfo-TAG detection reagent was added to each well, and the wells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Following a final wash step, 150 μL of MSD Gold Red Buffer B was added to each well, and the plate was read on an MSD instrument. The electrochemiluminescence signal generated is proportional to the amount of amiR in the sample. The amiR concentration in the unknown samples was back calculated utilizing the non-linear regression analysis of the calibrator curve samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s correction to adjust for multiple comparisons of means. In cases where normality was violated, Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA, followed by Dunn’s correction for multiple comparisons of medians, was used. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to determine the linear correlation between two datasets. Statistical analyses were performed on GraphPad Prism v.10.4.1.
