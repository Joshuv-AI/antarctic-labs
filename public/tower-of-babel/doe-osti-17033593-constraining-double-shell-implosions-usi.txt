  Constraining Double Shell Implosions using Diagnostics based on Charged-particle
 Transport

 Level 3 Milestone
 A. C. Hayes, Gerard Jungman, J.D. Martin
 Los Alamos National Laboratory, Los Alamos, NM, 87544 USA
                                                      (Dated: June 5, 2026)
 We consider alpha-particle and knock-on deuteron induced Radchem reactions, as well as reaction-
 in-flight neutrons, as mix diagnostics for double shell capsule designs involving tungsten shells. We
 use a set of 1-D simulations from the radiation hydrodynamic code xRAGE to gain insight on how
 these diagnostics vary with mix. For this, we couple the output from a set of 1-D xRAGE simulations
 to our recently developed deterministic charged-particle transport code, the SIT code. We find that
 increasing the mass of mixed W shell material into the DT fuel systematically reduces all of the mix
 diagnostics per unit DT yield. A more comprehensive study of the diagnostics as a function of mix
 will require examining a large set of mix scenarios, including smooth atomic mix, jetting and chunk
 mix.

 I. INTRODUCTION

At the National Ignition Facility (NIF), high-Z shell capsule designs are being pursued [1, 2] with the aim of achieving
ignition at lower radii of convergence than hot-spot ignition designs [3, 4]. A main role of the high-Z pusher is to
trap the radiation in the DT fuel, and in this way to achieve a volumetric burn, as opposed to the propagating burn
achieved in hot spot ignition. One such capsule design is the so-called double shell design [5, 6], in which the capsule
consists of an inner high-Z shell pusher, surrounding liquid DT fuel. The high-Z pusher is separated from an outer
low-Z ablator shell by a CH foam layer. A concern with such designs is the possible mixing of some of the high-Z
shell material into the DT fuel, which would lower the fuel temperature and quench the burn. Diagnosing such mix
relies on nuclear probes, because the trapping of the radiation inside the DT fuel renders x-ray mix diagnostics less
than useful.
To diagnose mix of the high-Z pusher into the DT fuel of double shell capsules we consider three diagnostics, alpha-
particle induced radiochemistry (Radchem), deuteron-induced Radchem and reaction-in-flight (RIF) neutrons. As
discussed in more detail below, all three diagnostics involve energetic charged-particles. These charged-particles are
being slowed by the plasma stopping power as they move though plasma. Their sensitivity to mix arises because
mixing of high-Z material into the fuel increases the plasma stopping power, which changes the number of charged-
particle Radchem and RIF neutron reactions taking place. In general, under typical double shell conditions, we find
that the number of each type of reaction is suppressed by the presence of mix, compared to unmixed situations. This
suppression, relative to a clean burning capsule, acts as a clear indications that mix has taken place.
The purpose of the present Milestone is to discuss what theoretical and computational research is needed to go
beyond the simple statement that mix has taken place and to examine how more detailed information on mix,
including estimates of mix amounts and distributions, might be extracted from these diagnostics. As discussed below,
this requires detailed studies on how these diagnostics vary with varying plasma parameters, such as electron density
and temperature profiles that determine plasma stopping powers and the spatial distributions of shell material. An
additional diagnostic that can help in distinguishing between mix scenarios is neutron imaging, and we also discuss how
neutron imaging can play an important role in extracting information on mix distributions from RIF and Radchem
measurements.

 II. THE DIAGNOSTICS

The double shell capsule design used in the present work is described in [6, 7], and consists of an inner 8.33 µg DT
fuel layer, surrounded by a 540 µg W layer, which is doped with 5(at)% 10 B. Outside of the W-layer there is a 40 µm
thick layer of Be, which acts as a tamper. Between the Be layer and the outer Al ablator is a CH foam layer 640 µm
thick, surrounded by the outer 175 µm thick Al ablator. A sketch of the capsule design is shown in Fig. 1.

 III. THE CHARGE-PARTICLE TRANSPORT CODE, THE SIT CODE

Rigorous treatments of charged-particle transport for arbitrary plasma configurations are very challenging and nu-
merical schemes often rely on Monte Carlo (MC) treatments of the problem. An MC method for estimating the
suprathermal charged-particle fluxes must include a sufficient number of sample particles to resolve the high energy
component of the spectrum which naturally falls off rapidly in intensity. This ultimately necessitates a substantial,
potentially prohibitive, increase in computational cost. We have therefore developed a very different approach to
charged-particle transport that does not suffer from statistics limitations, one that uses what is known as Simple
Integral Transport (SIT) design and framework. This formalism was developed by one of us (G. Jungman) to write a
practical charged-particle transport code, SIT, which can calculate charged particle transport deterministically. The
method is applicable to general geometries for systems through which charged particles are moving.
The SIT framework is based on a direct method for computation along beam lines, which are lines along which the

 3

charged particles can travel within the plasma of interest. The transport is treated as a continuous slowing-down
process through the plasma stopping power and, technically speaking, uses the method of characteristics applied to
the hyperbolic energy-dependent transport equation. In this approach, any problem setup is naturally divided into
two independent components; a geometric aspect and a transport aspect. For the geometric aspect, the problem is
decomposed into a user defined set of beam lines passing through the general 3D geometry that defines the plasma
geometry, and along which energetic charged particles can move. Once the beams defining the geometry of the
plasma have been set up, the transport problem is treated independently, so that the transport solver is independent
of geometry. This concept of two independent components, beams and transport, is fundamental to our SIT formalism
and code. The geometric aspects of the problem are analogous to aspects of computational tomography, particularly
attenuated x-rays [1, 2]. However, additional complications arise for charged particles in general plasmas that are
quite substantial and important compared to the methods used for tomography. These details are summarized in the
report [10]. We further summarized some of the formalism and implementation in a second report [11], where we also
gave some examples, using relatively simple plasma geometries, though the code can be applied to any geometry.
The code user has to supply the spatially- and energy-dependent source of alpha-particles and neutrons as input, as
well as the corresponding spatially-dependent density and temperature profiles. These can be determined from time-
dependent output from a 1-D, 2-D or 3-D radiation hydrodynamics code. Alternatively, they can be time-averaged
values determined from the analysis of an actual ICF experiment.

 IV. THE EFFECT OF INCREASING MIX ON DIAGNOSTICS

To determine how to analyze a given set of measurements of the diagnostics for an ICF shot, we first looked at the
expectations from a set of xRAGE [12] simulations. For this we used the 1-D simulations of Montgomery et al. [6]
which used the Besnard-Harlow-Rauenzahn (BHR) mix model [13–15]. The amount of mix of the high-Z shell material
into the DT gas was varied in the simulations by varying the mixing length parameter of the BHR model, S0 . As
described in ref. [7], the yield of the capsule varied from 1.12 MJ to 0.041 MJ, as S0 was varied from S0 = 0 (non-mix)
to S0 = 500 nm. Table I summarizes the five mix scenarios that we consider in the present work.

 V. ANALYSES TO CONVERT RIF AND RADCHEM DIAGNOSTICS TO MIX SCENARIOS

lead to very smooth variations of the mixed W layer into the DT fuel. In reality, more complicated mix scenarios
are likely, including jetting or chunk mix. To understand how the mix diagnostics would behave under these or other
scenarios, it will be necessary to calculate the diagnostics for a broad range of plasma conditions. The main effects
of mix on the diagnostics are (1) an increase in the plasma stopping power, which suppresses the alpha-particle and

These two effects operate in competition, and, for the cases we have considered, the stopping power effect tends to

To study these effects in detail and to understand what to expect for a broad range of mix scenarios, we propose to
vary the plasma parameters, starting with the temperature and density profiles that determine the stopping powers.
Within these variations in density and temperature profiles, we will examine the diagnostics assuming (1) smooth
shell distribution within the fuel, (2) jetting of shell material into the fuel and (3) chunky shell mixing.
An alternate mix scenario that needs to be studied is one in which there is a clean DT burn fuel region surrounded
by a very mixed fuel/shell layer that is too cold to show any appreciable burn. In this scenario, the neutron imaging
would show a fuel hotspot that is smaller than the actual fuel region. To detect such scenarios we propose to use the
neutron images and to calculate all of the diagnostics assuming that there is no DT fuel outside of the image. If this
is not the case, our RIF and Radchem predictions from the neutron image will run into contradictions, relative to the

 6

observed diagnostics. Thus, neutron imaging will be an essential part of our mix diagnostic analysis.
With calculations a sufficient number of mix scenarios, we should be in a position to rule in (or out) a broad set of mix
scenarios from consistency checks between the different diagnostics. Also, for any given shot we can feed formation
on the density and temperature profiles from experiment into the SIT code and calculate the expected diagnostics
assuming clean or mixed DT fuel. Varying the mix assumptions until the diagnostics are approximately reproduced
will help narrow down the mix possibilities.

 VI. SUMMARY

We used the newly developed deterministic charged-particle transport, the SIT code, to transport the alpha-particle
and KO deuteron and triton sources predicted in a series of 1-D xRAGE simulations. This allowed us to examine
the sensitivity of α-Radchem, KO deuteron-Radchem and RIF neutrons to mixing of the W shell into the DT fuel in
double shell capsules, within one set of mix scenarios. Mix affects Radchem in two competing ways. First, the integral
of the Radchem flux over the cross section of interest is suppressed because mix suppresses the fluxes by increasing
the plasma stopping power. Second, mix moves some of the shell material closer to the Radchem fluxes, increasing
the signals. We find that the first effect of suppressing the fluxes far outweighs any benefit obtained from moving
some of the shell into the DT gas. The RIF neutron spectra are suppressed and softened in energy by the increase in
stopping power.
We find that alpha-particle Radchem is by far the most sensitive diagnostic to mix, with the alpha signals being
suppressed by more than a factor of 20 in going from a clean calculation to the most mixed scenarios. In comparison,
the KO deuteron and RIF signals are suppressed by about a factor of 4. Thus, we suggest that a strong emphasis
be placed on the 13 N signals being measured using the RAGS facility at NIF. In particular, a detailed and full
understanding of the 13 N collection efficiency is essential.
To use these diagnostics to extract more detailed information on the nature of the mix taking place in a given
experiment, it will be necessary to run a series of simulations that make different assumptions on the nature of the
mix taking place. This will require examination of the Radchem and RIF diagnostics for different plasma parameters,
density and temperature profiles, and for different mix morphologies. Finally, we mention that a RIF neutron imaging
capability would allow us to extract more detailed information on mix that RIF spectra alone.
