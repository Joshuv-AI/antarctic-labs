Effects of preharvest 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) spraying on delaying peel-color change and expression of carotenogenic genes in CO2-deastringed persimmons
Authors: Kaixin Li, Wenjun Wang, Jing Huang, Li Wang, Bao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the changes in persimmon peel color induced by CO2 deastringency and preharvest 1-MCP spraying, we conducted a study with three experimental treatments: control, CO2 deastringency (CO2 group), and preharvest 1-MCP spray followed by CO2 deastringency (1-MCP/CO2 group). Relative to the control, the CO2 group exhibited considerably higher fruit respiration rate, ethylene production, peel-color index, and total carotenoid content, accompanied by a marked decrease in chlorophyll content. The 1-MCP/CO2 group maintained higher chlorophyll content, while all other indicators remained lower than those in the CO2 group. Among the carotenoid biosynthesis genes examined, DkGGPPS-1 predominated the control group, while DkGGPPS-2 was present in the CO2 and 1-MCP/CO2 groups. In the CO2 group, peak expression levels of DkPSY-1 and DkPSY-2 were 17.6–22.7 times the reference value; DkPDS, DkZ-ISO, and DkZDS were 5.5–9.3 times; DkLCYB was 1.9 times; and DkBCH was 5.5 times, whereas DkLCYE was markedly reduced. Alternatively, the control and 1-MCP/CO2 groups exhibited relatively stable expression. In the control group, DkNCED-1 was the main gene for making ABA. In the CO2 and 1-MCP/CO2 groups, DkNCED-1 and DkNCED-2 were the main genes, but 1-MCP could inhibit their expression. CO2 deastringency and 1-MCP treatments considerably enhanced and suppressed the expression of DkERF-1, respectively. Overall, CO2 deastringency and preharvest 1-MCP spraying influenced fruit respiration rate, ethylene production, total carotenoid and chlorophyll contents, and the expression of carotenoid biosynthesis genes, NCED, and ERF, thereby facilitating or delaying changes in persimmon peel color.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58754-x.

Introduction

Persimmon (Diospyros kaki) is one of the crucial deciduous fruit trees and is generally considered a high-quality source of nutrients and bioactive compounds beneficial to human health1,2. Persimmon cultivars are classified into two types based on whether they naturally lose astringency upon ripening on the tree: astringent persimmons and nonastringent persimmons (commonly known as sweet persimmons)3. Astringent persimmons cannot be eaten crisp when ripe owing to the high content of soluble tannins in the fruit; thus, they require postharvest deastringency treatment to reduce the soluble tannin content below the perception threshold (1.0 mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/g fresh weight, mgGAE/gFW)4 before they can be eaten crisp. One of the widely used commercial deastringency treatments today is a short-term constant temperature treatment with high concentrations of CO2, which has successfully promoted the postharvest commercialization of astringent persimmons5. However, in some cultivars, deastringency treatment can accelerate the ripening process of the deastringed fruit, leading to rapid softening and a quick color change from yellow-green to yellow-orange or red.

The color change in fruits primarily involves the metabolic regulation of various pigments, including carotenoids and chlorophyll. Carotenoids, as one of the essential pigments in plants, are closely related to plant growth and development and play crucial roles in photosynthesis, plant hormone metabolism, and response to environmental signals6. The bright colors found in many horticultural crops, such as yellow, orange, and red, are attributed to the accumulation of large amounts of carotenoids in cells. In addition, carotenoids positively contribute to human nutrition and health7.

The biosynthetic pathways of carotenoids in higher plants have become relatively clear7,8. The core carotenoid biosynthetic pathway includes of the following steps: condensation, desaturation/isomerization, hydroxylation, oxidation, and epoxidation to produce various carotenoids and xanthophylls7,8, with the primary process shown in Fig. 1. Geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) is the direct precursor for carotenoid synthesis, and it forms through catalysis by geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (GGPPS). The first committed step in the plant carotenoid biosynthetic pathway is the phytoene synthase (PSY)-catalyzed conversion of GGPP into 15-cis-phytoene. After sequential desaturation and isomerization, 15-cis-phytoene is converted to all-trans-lycopene. Lycopene represents the branch point of the carotenoid biosynthetic pathway, and it can be cyclized to form β-carotene in the β,β-branch and α-carotene in the β,ε-branch by lycopene β-cyclase (LCYB) and lycopene ε-cyclase (LCYE). Subsequent hydroxylation and epoxidation of these carotenes produce various xanthophylls. The synthesis of lutein and neoxanthin in the two branches finalizes the core biosynthetic pathway9. Violaxanthin and neoxanthin undergo multiple reactions to eventually generate the plant hormone abscisic acid (ABA)6,9. Within the carotenoid biosynthetic pathway, PSY is the most essential rate-limiting enzyme; it plays a key role in controlling the total amount of carotenoids9,10. LCYE and LCYB are the main rate-limiting enzymes for carotenoid cyclization; they catalyze the branching of lycopene, determining the content and composition of carotenoids in plant tissues9. For example, LCYE can be a target gene to alter metabolic flux and thereby facilitate the accumulation of β-carotene in crops9,11,12.

In addition to the accumulation of carotenoids, chlorophyll degradation is an essential factor that leads to color changes during fruit ripening. Many plants bear fruits containing large amounts of chlorophyll in their peel before ripening, but this chlorophyll gradually breaks down during subsequent ripening, and the green color of the early peel is masked by other pigments synthesized later13. For example, ethylene-induced degreening results from the coordinated action between chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid biosynthesis14.

Color change is one of the most prominent markers of postharvest ripening15. Carotenoids and chlorophyll are regulated in a complex manner throughout the life cycle of a plant. Among the regulatory mechanisms, transcriptional regulation of biosynthetic genes is the first-level and crucial control mechanism for carotenoid production in pigmented organelles, which involves factors such as developmental signals, light signals, and plant hormones8,9. Transcription factors are vital for the transcriptional regulation of carotenoid biosynthesis genes. In model plants and various crops, numerous transcription factors have been observed to regulate the transcription levels of structural genes in the carotenoid metabolic pathway6,8,9. During fruit ripening, plant hormones, such as ethylene, auxin, gibberellins, ABA, and brassinosteroids, regulate carotenoid biosynthesis, as they are usually closely associated with the fruit ripening process16. Of these hormones, ethylene plays a key role in climacteric fruit ripening, being the primary signaling molecule that controls most aspects of climacteric fruit maturation16–18. For example, ethylene response factors (ERFs), as an essential components of the ethylene signaling pathway and direct mediators of ethylene-responsive genes, have been shown to participate in multiple aspects of fruit ripening, such as ethylene biosynthesis, color change, and fruit softening17. Moreover, ERFs can integrate ethylene, ABA, jasmonic acid, and redox signals in plant as responses to multiple abiotic stresses and play a crucial role in carotenoid accumulation during color changes19,20. In climacteric fruits, ethylene becomes the primary regulatory factor18, and controlling its activity becomes crucial for maintaining or improving the postharvest quality of horticultural products, as exemplified by the use of 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP)21. 1-MCP can irreversibly bind to ethylene receptors and inhibit ethylene perception, thereby effectively blocking the effects of ethylene22. By modulating physiological processes regulated via ethylene signaling, 1-MCP delays the ripening and senescence of climacteric fruits and is widely applied to preserve their quality during storage. Numerous studies exist reporting the effects of 1-MCP on the ripening of climacteric fruits across different species and environmental conditions21,23.

Persimmon fruits are climacteric24. Numerous studies have examined the composition of carotenoids and the expression characteristics of related biosynthetic enzyme genes during persimmon fruit development and postharvest ripening, as they are associated with color change25–30. In addition, many studies have investigated postharvest 1‑MCP treatment for improving persimmon fruit quality31 and one report has described preharvest 1‑MCP spraying to enhance the postharvest quality of the “Rojo Brillante” variety32. However, compared with well-established fruit crops such as apples33, studies on preharvest applications in persimmons remain limited. To date, no studies have addressed the effect or mechanism of preharvest 1-MCP spraying on peel-color changes in CO2-deastringed persimmons.

Therefore, this study aims to (1) investigate the effects of preharvest 1-MCP spraying on physiological indicators of CO2-deastringed persimmon fruits, (2) analyze the expression characteristics of carotenoid biosynthetic enzyme genes, and (3) examine the effects of preharvest 1-MCP spraying on the expression of two key ABA biosynthesis genes and two ERF-encoding genes. This study provides insight into the mechanisms through which preharvest 1-MCP application influences peel-color changes in CO2-deastringed persimmons and offers a reference for understanding the regulatory roles of CO2 deastringency and preharvest 1-MCP application in persimmon peel coloration.

Results

Changes in respiration rate, ethylene production and soluble tannin content

Respiration rate and ethylene production

The changes in respiration rate (unit: mL/(kg·h)) and ethylene production (unit: µL/(kg·h)) are shown in Fig. 2a and b, respectively.

In the control group, the respiration rate gradually increased from 7.22 on day 0 and peaked to 14.48 on day 12 (Fig. 2a). Ethylene production increased from 0.31 on day 0 and peaked to 0.57 on day 12, after which it declined (Fig. 2b).

The CO2 group had a respiration rate of 13.85 on the day of treatment completion, which was 1.92 times that of the control group. A peak (22.95) was observed on day 2, which was 3.19 times that of the control group, significantly higher than the latter (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Ethylene production increased to 0.48 on day 1 and peaked to 0.70 on day 2, both were markedly higher than in the control group, representing increases of 60% and 80%, respectively (Fig. 2b).

In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, the respiration rate followed a similar trend to the CO2 group during the first 4 days but significantly lower than the latter (p < 0.05), and became lower than the control group on days 8 and 12 (Fig. 2a). Ethylene production peaked to 0.64 on day 3, which was significantly higher than the control group (p < 0.05) but lower than the CO2 group, and was lower than the control group after day 8 (Fig. 2b).

Soluble tannin content

In general, a soluble tannin content (unit: mgGAE/gFW) of 1.0 in persimmon fruit is the threshold for edibility4. As shown in Fig. 2c, in the control group, soluble tannin content gradually decreased from 8.38 on the day of harvest (0 days postharvest) to 6.31 on day 16. In the CO2 group, the content rapidly dropped to 1.27 on day 1 postharvest, when the deastringency process was completed and it further declined to 0.56 on day 2. In the 1‑MCP/CO2 group, the initial value was 8.41 and it decreased to 1.79 on day 1 postharvest, reaching 0.91 and 0.57 on days 2 and 3, respectively, during which deastringency was completed.

Changes in persimmon peel-carotenoid and chlorophyll contents, and color index

Total carotenoid content

The total carotenoid content (unit: µg/g) of persimmon peel increased under all treatments (Fig. 3a). In the control group, the content gradually increased from 44.91 on the harvest day to 54.98, 67.69, and 68.59 on days 8, 12, and 16, respectively. In the CO2 group, a significant increase was observed after treatment (p < 0.05), with values rising from 44.91 to 50.68 and then to 58.70, 69.41, and 79.66 over the next 3 days, at a rate significantly higher than observed for the other groups (p < 0.05), except for the first day with 1-MCP/CO2 treatment. In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, the content was 45.96 on the harvest day and it was significantly lower than the CO2 group on days 2, 3, and 4 (p < 0.05), but it increased to 63.85, 75.15, and 79.85 on days 8, 12, and 16, respectively.

Chlorophyll content

The chlorophyll content (unit: µg/g) of persimmon peel under all treatments showed a decreasing trend (Fig. 3b). In the control group, peel chlorophyll content was highest on the harvest day (70.64) and it gradually decreased to 10.14 by day 16. The CO2 group showed a relatively higher decline than the other two groups, dropping rapidly to 56.80 immediately after the deastringency treatment and further to 9.49 by day 4 (p < 0.05). By contrast, the 1-MCP/CO2 group maintained relatively high chlorophyll content throughout, with 73.63 on the harvest day. During and within 2 days after CO2 deastringency treatment, chlorophyll content became lower than that of the control group but significantly higher than that of the CO2 group (p < 0.05). From days 4 to 16, the content remained higher than the control group.

Color index and fruit photos

During the experiment, the color index of persimmon peel under different treatments increased (Fig. 3c). The fruits in the control group changed their color from light green to yellow-green, showing a distinct orange-yellow by day 8 (Fig. 4). The CO2 group exhibited significantly higher color index values than the other groups (p < 0.05) after CO2 deastringency treatment. A noticeable orange-yellow color was observed immediately after treatment; by day 3, almost no green was visible, and by day 4, the fruit appeared orange-red. The color index of the 1-MCP/CO2 group was significantly lower than that of the control and CO2 groups (p < 0.05), showing a light yellow-green on day 8 and a pronounced yellow on day 12.

DkGGPPS-1 and DkGGPPS-2

In the control group, DkGGPPS-1 (Fig. 5a) was significantly higher than the reference value on the 2nd–8th days (p < 0.05). DkGGPPS-2 (Fig. 5b) showed a fluctuating pattern, but there was no significant difference observed between the highest value and the reference value.

In the CO2 group, DkGGPPS-1 expression was significantly increased on the day treatment ended but gradually decreased over the following days. DkGGPPS-2 was continuously expressed at a significantly high level (p < 0.05). In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, DkGGPPS-1 was significantly higher than the reference value (p < 0.05) in the first 2 days, which later decreased, showing a fluctuating pattern. The expression level of DkGGPPS-2 was slightly below the reference value on the day of treatment, and it maintained a significantly high level, namely 1.40–1.67 times the reference value (p < 0.05) on the 2nd–8th days, and gradually decreased later.

DkPSY-1 and DkPSY-2

In the CO2 group, DkPSY-1 (Fig. 6a) and DkPSY-2 (Fig. 6b) increased sharply and significantly within 3 days after the treatment ended (p < 0.05), with the increase in DkPSY-2 exceeding that of DkPSY-1. Compared with the reference value, the expression levels of DkPSY-1 were 11.19, 17.55, and 8.60 times, and those of DkPSY-2 were 12.16, 22.65, and 16.85 times, respectively.

The change in trend of the two genes in the control group and the 1-MCP/CO2 group was basically the same and relatively small, and the peak appeared on the 12th day. The peaks of DkPSY-1 and DkPSY-2 in the control group were 2.52 and 2.28 times, respectively, and in the 1-MCP/CO2 group, they were 1.98 and 1.69 times, respectively.

DkPDS-1 and DkPDS-2

For the control group and the 1-MCP/CO2 group, the expression changes in the two genes were relatively gentle. DkPDS-1 (Fig. 6c) and DkPDS-2 (Fig. 6d) expression levels in the control group were relatively higher on the first day after harvest, but in the 1-MCP/CO2 group, it relatively decreased on the day of deastringency.

The expression levels of DkPDS-1 and DkPDS-2 in the CO2 group slightly decreased on the day of treatment but later increased significantly (p < 0.05). DkPDS-1 levels were 6.33, 7.32, and 7.84 times, while DkPDS-2 was 4.49, 5.11, and 5.51 times, which were slightly lower than those of DkPDS-1.

DkNCED-1

The CO2 group showed no significant difference from the reference value at the end of the deastringency treatment but later significantly increased over the subsequent 3 days (p < 0.05), reaching 16.57, 22.93, and 26.66 times the reference value, respectively (Fig. 8a). The 1-MCP/CO2 group showed a slight decrease on the day the deastringency treatment ended, with a significant increase to 8.36 and 5.05 times on day 2 and 3 (p < 0.05), respectively, which later gradually decreased.

DkNCED-2

In the CO2 group, the expression level was 8.03 times the reference value on the day the deastringency treatment ended (p < 0.05) and then rapidly decreased. The control group showed smaller changes in expression, with a decreasing trend. In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, expression increased 4.26 times on the day the deastringency treatment ended, which quickly declined to levels close to that of the control group (Fig. 8b).

Discussion

Preharvest spraying of 1-MCP can reduce the increase in respiration rate and ethylene production in persimmon fruits induced by postharvest CO2 deastringency treatment

1-MCP has become a research tool for studying the role of ethylene in fruit ripening and senescence and as a commercial technology for maintaining postharvest quality23. Compared with the control group, the results of this study reveal that CO2 deastringency treatment considerably increases the respiration rate and ethylene production in persimmon fruit, whereas preharvest spraying of 1-MCP can reduce these increases induced by CO2 treatment (Fig. 2a, b). Using meta-analysis, Zhang et al.21 reported that 1-MCP treatment strongly inhibits physiological and biochemical indicators such as ethylene production and respiration rate, with the most pronounced effect on endogenous ethylene production observed in tomatoes, kiwifruit, and persimmons. For persimmons, Harima et al.37 and our previous experiments combining CO2 deastringency with 1-MCP treatment36,38 showed similar results. Therefore, preharvest spraying of 1-MCP can markedly inhibit the rapid increases in respiration rate and ethylene production induced by deastringency treatment and is a viable approach for maintaining the quality of deastringed persimmons.

Preharvest spraying of 1-MCP can slow the reduction of soluble tannin content in persimmon fruit

Previous studies on the ‘Mopanshi’ persimmon variety have shown that 1-MCP treatment can slow the decrease in soluble tannins in fruits that remain nonastringent after harvest34. Our earlier experimental results indicated that when postharvest 1-MCP treatment was applied simultaneously with CO2 deastringency treatment, the deastringency process in the fruit was delayed by ~ 1 day compared with CO2 treatment alone35,36. Herein, the time for soluble tannin content in 1-MCP/CO2-treated fruits to drop below the edible threshold was delayed by ~ 1 day compared with CO2-treated fruits (Fig. 2c), consistent with previously reported results. This finding indicates that preharvest application of 1-MCP, similar to postharvest deastringency treatment combined with 1-MCP, can slow the decrease in soluble tannin content for a certain period without impairing the rapid deastringency of persimmon fruits.

CO2 deastringency treatment considerably accelerates changes in the peel-color index and carotenoid and chlorophyll contents, whereas preharvest spraying of 1-MCP can markedly delay these changes.

Color change is one of the most significant indicators of postripening15. Carotenoids impart yellow, orange, and red colors to fruit, and the carotenoid-to-chlorophyll ratio determines the color displayed by the fruit13. The ‘Mopanshi’ variety appears yellow at maturity, and during development, under natural conditions, its peel-color changes from green to green‑yellow and eventually to orange. With extended postharvest time, carotenoid and chlorophyll contents in the peel of different treatment groups markedly changed (Fig. 3a, b), accompanied by noticeable alterations in their peel color (Fig. 4). Compared with control and 1-MCP/CO2 groups, CO2 deastringency treatment significantly increased carotenoid content and significantly decreased chlorophyll content in persimmon peels, whereas 1-MCP treatment reduced the extent of these changes induced by deastringency (Fig. 3a, b, p < 0.05). Previous meta-analyses of 1-MCP treatments across different crops indicate that lycopene and carotenoid synthesis can decrease by > 36% following treatment, while chlorophyll retention is enhanced through delayed degradation in fruits21. For peppers and grapes, 1-MCP treatment can considerably alleviate chlorophyll degradation39,40. The results of this study are consistent with findings reported for other crops. Moreover, CO2 deastringency treatment can considerably accelerate the change in peel color from light green-yellow to orange-red, while fruits sprayed with 1-MCP still show considerably slow color changes even after CO2 deastringency treatment (Fig. 4). On days 4, 12, and 16, chlorophyll content of 1-MCP/CO2 fruit-peel becomes significantly higher than that in the control group on the corresponding observation date, (Fig. 3b, p < 0.05), which can partly explain the reason behind the lower color index than that of the control group during the observation period (Fig. 3c).

Relative expression levels of carotenoid metabolism–related genes in persimmon fruit peel under different treatments

GGPPS

GGPP is a precursor to carotenoid synthesis, and GGPPS catalyzing its synthesis is a key enzyme9, which has been reflected in a variety of crops. The expression levels of SlGGPPS3 in tomato fruits41 and DkGGPPS in persimmon fruits28 increase during fruit development to ensure carotenoid production and color change. DkGGPPS-1 and DkGGPPS-2 are the two DkGGPPS identified in the RNA-Seq results analysis, with the former in the control group and the latter in the CO2 and 1-MCP/CO2 groups significantly increased during the first 8 days after harvesting (Fig. 5a, b). Correspondingly, the color index and total carotenoid content of the peel increases (Fig. 3c and a) and the color of the peel rapidly changes, especially in the CO2 group (Fig. 4). The expression patterns of the two DkGGPPS genes in this study are consistent with the changes in pericarp color and with those observed in the aforementioned crops. Concurrently, our results indicate that in natural conditions and CO2-deastringency anaerobic environments, the genes that play a major role in providing precursors for carotenoid synthesis may be different.

Changes in gene expression in the core carotenoid synthesis pathway

There are two key nodes in the core carotenoid synthesis pathway. The first is the synthesis of phytoene from GGPP catalyzed by PSY, and its expression determines the total amount of carotenoids, which is the most important rate-limiting enzyme in the entire pathway8–10,41. PSY1 when expressed in tomato fruits leads to the accumulation of a large amount of lycopene when the transcription level considerably increases during ripening42. When PSY1 inactivates via a mutation or silences through virus-induced gene silencing, total carotenoid content considerably reduces43,44. Among the related genes studied in this study, DkPSY-1 and DkPSY-2 are the most significant in the CO2 group, which are ~ 9–23 times higher (Fig. 6a, b). DkPDS (Fig. 6c, d), DkZ-ISO (Fig. 6e), and DkZDS (Fig. 6f) expression levels significantly increases (p < 0.05) by ~ 3–9 times. The control group demonstrates changes similar to those of the 1-MCP/CO2 group, with increases far lower than those observed in the CO2 group. The peak expression of two DkPSY and DkZDS genes appears on the 8th–12th days in the two groups. However, DkPDS-2 slightly increases in the first 2 days after harvest in the control group, while DkZDS slightly increases on the 8th and 12th days of the 1-MCP/CO2 group compared with the control group. No considerable change is observed in the DkPDS-1 and DkZ-ISO levels. However, the changes observed in total carotenoid content (Fig. 3a) in this study show that the measured values of the CO2 group are far higher than those of the other two treatment groups on the same observation date, which should be a specific manifestation of the high expression of related genes. This is consistent with the reports of previous studies conducted on persimmon fruit26–28.

The second key node is to divide from lycopene downward into two branches; one is to direct the metabolic flux to the β,ε-branch to generate α-carotene under the catalysis of LCYE and LCYB, and the other clade is the β,β-branch, which is catalyzed by LCYB to produce β-carotene. Therefore, the expression characteristics of LCYE and LCYB determine the type and content of carotenoids in plant tissues9. If there is an expression imbalance between LCYE and LCYB, the accumulation of α-carotene and β-carotene can be disturbed45. Increasing the expression levels of LCYB in kiwifruit46 can considerably enhance the accumulation of carotenoids, especially of β-carotene. Moreover, LCYE gene can be targeted for altering metabolic flux11,12; for example, RNAi-mediated inhibition of LCYE expression in sweet potato considerably increases the content of β,β-type carotene12. By contrast, downregulation of LCYB gene results in less accumulation of β-carotene and lutein in transgenic tomato plants47. LCYB2 is the main carotenoid synthase in citrus that controls the large accumulation of downstream carotenoids48; coordinated changes in LCYE and LCYB are noted, which change the synthesis pathway of citrus fruit carotenoids49.

In the CO2 group of this study, there are differences noted in the expression of the two synthetic genes located at the branch point of lycopene: DkLCYE of the α-carotene branch significantly decreases to 0.45 in the first day and only 0.05 in the 4th day (Fig. 7a). However, DkLCYB of the β-carotene branch (Fig. 7b) significantly increases to 1.85 times on the 2nd day, shifting the synthesis pathway toward the β,β-branch, which noticeably decreases over the subsequent 2 days. The expression characteristics of these two genes can increase the levels of β-carotene and other carotenoids during the first 2–3 days due to the increase of DkLCYB, leading to a rapid yellowing of the peel. By days 3–4, as DkLCYE and DkLCYB expression decreases, the genes responsible for lycopene synthesis maintains high expression levels (Figs. 5a and b and 6a–f), resulting in relatively increased lycopene accumulation in the peel. Combined with the rapid decline in chlorophyll content (Fig. 3b), this changes the appearance of the peel to orange‑red (Fig. 4). Concurrently, DkBCH expression rapidly increases to 2.65–5.53 times within a few days after the CO2 treatment ends (Fig. 7c), consistent with the subsequent increase in carotenoid content (Fig. 3a). In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, DkLCYE expression shows a decreasing trend, with reductions considerably smaller than those in the CO2 group, suggesting the reducing effect of 1‑MCP. After day 4, the expression levels significantly decreased when compared with the control group. The changes in expression levels of DkLCYB and DkBCH do not markedly differ from those of the control group. In the control group, only DkLCYE shows a 1.45-fold peak on the 4th day, which is significantly higher than the 1-MCP/CO2 group on subsequent days.

Previous studies indicate that the primary carotenoid monomers in persimmon peel are lutein, β-cryptoxanthin, β-carotene, zeaxanthin, and lycopene25,26,28. Of these monomers, lutein primarily accumulates during the green stage, while β-cryptoxanthin and zeaxanthin accumulate primarily during the coloring stage26. The changes in expression levels of the three genes in these two treatment groups are relatively small, which can explain the gradual increase in the corresponding peel-color index and total carotenoid content. In the control group, DkLCYE maintains relatively high expression, ensuring the presence of a certain amount of lutein in the peel. As chlorophyll gradually decreases (Fig. 3b), the peel slowly turns yellow (Fig. 4), which is generally consistent with previously reported results.

Overall, under the natural conditions of the control group, the expression levels of genes such as DkPSY, DkPDS, DkZ-ISO, DkZDS, DkLCYB, and DkBCH change relatively less, while CO2 deastringency treatment markedly enhances their expression. Alternatively, preharvest spraying with 1-MCP markedly inhibits their expression, especially of DkPSY. Considering that the CO2 group facilitates an increase in the ethylene production in fruits, whereas 1-MCP preharvest spraying suppresses it (Fig. 2b), this suggests that ethylene regulates the expression of these carotenoid synthase genes. Notably, in the CO2 group, on the day the deastringency process completes, two PSY and ZDS genes are not suppressed by deastringency treatment (Fig. 6a, b and f), whereas DkPDS and DkZ-ISO genes experience inhibition (Fig. 6a, b and e). The reason behind this can be the anaerobic environment or the direct effect of CO2; further study is required for confirmation.

CO2 deastringency and 1-MCP treatment can markedly influence changes in DkNCED gene expression

ABA is an essential apocarotenoid formed by the oxidative cleavage of carotenoids, playing a crucial role in fruit ripening, stress responses, and transcriptional regulation. NCEDs are the key enzymes regulating its production6,50. The expression patterns of the two DkNCED genes vary among the different treatment groups in this study (Fig. 8a, b). In the CO2 group, the expression of DkNCED-1 is slightly lower at the end of the deastringency treatment but sharply increases over the following 3 days, while DkNCED-2 markedly increases at the end of the treatment but gradually declines over the subsequent 3 days. The expression of both DkNCED genes is markedly suppressed in the 1-MCP/CO2 group. In the control group, DkNCED-1 expression peaks between days 8 and 12 after harvest, while DkNCED-2 expression steadily declines following harvest. The results obtained from these three treatment groups indicate that both DkNCED genes may play a crucial role in ABA production in persimmon peel treated with the deastringency, but their expression is inhibited by 1-MCP; under natural conditions, DkNCED-1 is the primary contributor. Studies conducted on kiwifruit, apricot and persimmon indicate that during postharvest ripening, the increase or decrease in NCED gene expression corresponds to higher or lower ABA production, affecting fruit maturation and color changes51–53. For example, in tomato, transient overexpression of AcNCED1 accelerates fruit ripening and coloration51. In goji berries, the expression of NCED and PSY, PDS, and ZDS, is strongly correlated with carotenoid accumulation and color change during ripening54. CitNCED, involved in carotenoid degradation, contributes to carotenoid content and compositional differences among citrus varieties, and CitNCED2 expression notably increases along with higher ABA content in the flavedo and juice sacs49.

In the CO2 treatment group, DkNCED-1 expression (Fig. 8a) closely mirrors the expression of genes involved in core carotenoid synthesis (Figs. 5, 6 and 7), indicating a link between ABA synthesis and the rapid color changes observed during persimmon ripening. During the same period, the color index (Fig. 3c) and total carotenoid content (Fig. 3a) in the 1-MCP/CO2 group are notably lower than those in the CO2 group, likely owing to significant differences in NCED expression, resulting in varied ABA production. Considering the changes in ethylene production (Fig. 2b), variations in peel-color index and total carotenoids correspond to increases in NCED expression and ethylene production, both of which are suppressed or delayed by preharvest application of 1-MCP. Similar findings have been reported in persimmon, where ethylene and ABA exhibit comparable patterns during postharvest ripening53. CO2 deastringency treatment can induce the accumulation of ethylene and ABA, which is inhibited by combined CO2 + 1-MCP treatment55.

Changes in ERF gene expression

ERFs are transcription factors downstream of the ethylene signaling pathway, and they are encoded by a plant-specific gene family. Furthermore, ERFs influence fruit coloration in various crops by regulating carotenoid accumulation through the control of ethylene‑responsive gene expression17,19,20. The two ERFs in this study, which are associated with carotenoid changes, are identified through RNA-Seq analysis. Results demonstrate that in the CO2 group, DkERF-1 expression remains considerably increases to 10.71 times on the 4th day after the treatment (Fig. 9a), while DkERF-2 considerably increases on the day the treatment ends but later decreases (Fig. 9b). The control group shows a slight increase in the DkERF-1 and DkERF-2 expression levels (Fig. 9a, b). In the 1-MCP/CO2 group, DkERF-1 expression was similar to that of the control group. DkERF-2 expression considerably increases during the first 2 days, but levels remain lower than those in the corresponding CO2 group. After day 4, the levels decrease in the control group (Fig. 9a, b). This indicates that CO2 deastringency treatment can considerably increase the expression of the two ERF genes, especially of DkERF-1, whereas preharvest application of 1-MCP can considerably reduce their expression. Silencing SlERF6 in tomato considerably increases carotenoid and ethylene levels during fruit ripening20; SlAP2a can activate the expression of SlPSY1, SlCRTISO, SlBCH, and SlPDS1 via participating in the regulation of carotenoid biosynthesis19. In citrus, CsERF061 can activate promoters of multiple key carotenoid genes, contributing to pigment accumulation during postharvest fruit ripening48; the expression patterns of pomelo CitERF23, CitERF27, and CitERF32 are related to pigment content and color indices56.

Considering the expression characteristics of DkERF-1 in the CO2 group (Fig. 9a) along with the increase in ethylene (Fig. 2b) and carotenoid contents (Fig. 3a), we propose that DkERF-1 is upregulated by elevated ethylene, which in turn considerably increases the color index and total carotenoid content in the CO2 group, making it one of the regulatory factors showing a marked difference compared with the control and 1-MCP/CO2 groups. However, the specific target genes among the carotenoid metabolism genes regulated by DkERF-1 require further experimental confirmation. Based on the results of the two NCEDs and two ERFs, there are two interesting trends. First, the expression changes in DkERF-1 and DkERF-2 (Fig. 9) are similar to those of the two NCED genes (Fig. 8), but whether there is a direct interaction between them is yet to be confirmed via further experiments. Second, under the anaerobic environment of CO2 deastringency treatment, both types of genes exhibit distinct patterns: one shows a slight decrease at the end of the treatment followed by a sharp increase, while the other increases under anaerobic conditions but declines under aerobic conditions, suggesting a possible sequentially efficient expression phenomenon that requires verification.

Conclusion

Overall, herein, the measured indicators of fruits in the control group generally showed significant changes at ~ 8 days during postharvest storage. By contrast, in the CO2 group, marked changes appeared immediately after the deastringency treatment, manifested by a sharp increase in respiration rate and ethylene production, a rapid rise in total carotenoids, and a steep decline in chlorophyll content in the peel. The expression levels of most carotenoid biosynthesis–related genes in the peel (such as GGPPS, PSY, PDS, Z-ISO, ZDS, LCYB, and BCH) rapidly increased, whereas LCYE was inhibited. Moreover, the expression of NCED, a gene involved in the synthesis of ABA, one of the apocarotenoids, and ERF genes regulated by ethylene increased. Overall, these factors contributed to the rapid change in peel color. Preharvest application of 1-MCP notably inhibited the increases in physiological indicators and gene expression induced by CO2 deastringency while maintaining higher chlorophyll content and thereby delayed peel-color changes in persimmon fruit caused by the treatment.

Materials and method

Experimental materials

The variety used in this experiment was “Mopanshi,” the predominant persimmon cultivar in the Beijing area. The sample was collected from the persimmon orchard in Manshuihe Village, Qinglonghu Town, Fangshan District, Beijing. On September 20, 2023, six trees (15 years old) from each of the two plots separated into three buffer rows, were selected for field treatment. The trees were conventionally managed and were healthy. Plot 1 was sprayed with clean water, and the fruits were used as control and for conducting CO2 deastringency treatment experiments. Plot 2 was sprayed with a 1-MCP formulation, and the fruits were used in the experiment with preharvest 1-MCP spraying and postharvest CO2 deastringency treatment. 1-MCP (1.8%, liquid formulation; Harvista™, AF-701) was sprayed under the guidance of a technical personnel after observing effects in the preliminary experiment last year and completed using an aerosol sprayer (THANTEK CS-5030) with a 10X diluted spray solution and about 2.5 mL per fruit. Fruits with similar shapes, uniform sizes, and free of pests and diseases were harvested on September 29 and transported back to the laboratory for their respective experimental procedures.

Postharvest experimental treatments and sampling arrangement

The fruits harvested from the orchard on the same day were divided into three groups, with an average single-fruit weight of ~ 220 g, and they were treated as follows: (1) control group: one hundred persimmons sprayed with water before harvest were neatly placed in clean plastic baskets and stored in the dark at ~ 20 °C, (2) CO2 deastringency group (CO2 group): fifty persimmons sprayed with water before harvest were placed in sealable containers, the air was removed, and the containers were filled with 98% CO2. After sealing, the containers were placed in the dark at ~ 20 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the seals were opened and the fruits were stored under the same conditions as that of the control group, and (3) 1-MCP/CO2 group: one hundred persimmons treated with 1-MCP before harvest underwent deastringency treatment using the same method as applied to the CO2 group, and the fruits were stored under the same conditions as the control group.

CO2-group fruits would become completely soft in ~ 3 days after harvest, with hardness falling below 10 N. Therefore, during the first 4 days, all three treatments were sampled and measured daily, with one sample retained each day. For the control and 1-MCP/CO2 groups, sampling and measurements were performed every 4 days thereafter, up to day 16, with one sample retained at each time point. On each measurement day, the fruits were photographed to record external appearance and analyzed for assessing color index, respiration rate, and ethylene production. Each treatment included three replicates, with three fruits per replicate. During biochemical assays and qPCR experiments, each sample is tested in triplicate, and the entire experiment is repeated three batches. After taking these measurements, each replicate fruit was separated into peel and pulp (removing the core), quickly chopped into pieces, treated with liquid nitrogen, and then stored in a − 80 °C freezer for later use.

Determination of the respiration rate and ethylene production

Three persimmon fruits were weighed and placed in 5-L glass containers (three containers per treatment), each fitted with a septum. The containers were sealed, and concentrations of CO2 and ethylene in the headspace (1.0-mL samples) were determined after 2–3 h at 20 °C.

Respiration rate and ethylene production were determined by injecting a 1-mL sample into a 3420 A gas chromatograph (BeiFen-Riley Analytic Instruments Co., Beijing, China). The column box, injector, and detector were operated at 100 °C, 100 °C, and 380 °C, respectively. The carrier gas (N2) flow rates were 40 mL/min. Respiration rate and ethylene production were expressed in mL (CO2)/kg/h and µL/kg/h, respectively.

Fruit color index and total carotenoid and chlorophyll contents in fruit peels

Color index

A colorimeter (CR‑410, Konica Minolta, Japan) was used to measure the chromatic values of sampled fruits on the sun‑exposed side, shaded side, and fruit top, such as luminance (L*), red-green value (a*), and yellow-blue value (b*). The color index of a single fruit was calculated according to the color index formula 57. The average value of three fruits was taken as the mean for each replicate. The calculation formula is color index = (1,000a*)/(L* × b*).

Determination of total carotenoid and chlorophyll contents in fruits

The total content of peel pigments was measured with some modifications based on the lab manual58. Specifically, ~ 1 g of frozen fruit peel was ground in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, 5 mL of a precooled mixture of n-hexane and acetone (volume ratio 6:4) was added and ground further at 4 °C. The residue was thoroughly washed with the mixed solution until it became colorless, and the extract was incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 3 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred into a new tube. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 470, 645, and 663 nm using a UV spectrophotometer, with n-hexane and acetone (volume ratio 6:4) as the blank. The results were calculated according to the following formula and was expressed in µg/g. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Chlorophyll a (Ca) = [(12.7 × A663 − 2.69 × A645) × V]/W Chlorophyll b (Cb) = [(22.9 × A645 − 4.68 × A663) × V]/W Total Chlorophyll (CT) = [(20.2 × A645 − 8.02 × A663) × V]/W Total Carotenoid Content = [(1,000 × A470 − 2.05 × Ca − 114.8 × Cb) × V]/W/245, where V denotes the volume of the extract (mL); W signifies the FW of the peel (g); and A470, A645, and A663 represent the absorbance of the solution at 470, 645, and 663 nm, respectively.

Soluble tannin assay

The soluble tannin content was determined based on the method used by Li et al.38. Frozen samples from three fruits were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen, with three replications. Powdered pulp (1.5 g) was extracted twice using 15 mL of methanol: H2O (80:20, v/v) for 30 min at 20 °C. The supernatants from the two extractions were combined and adjusted to a final volume of 50 mL. The method for determining soluble tannin content is as follows: 150 µL of extract, 2,400 µL of nanopure water, and 150 µL of 0.25-N Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) were combined in a plastic vial and mixed well via vortexing. The mixture was allowed to react for 3 min, after which 300 µL of 1-N Na2CO3 solution was added and mixed thoroughly. The solution was incubated in the dark at 20 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 725 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2800 A, Shanghai Precision Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and the results were expressed in gallic acid equivalents (mgGAE/gFW) using a gallic acid (0–0.1 mg/mL) standard curve. It was additionally diluted if the absorbance value measured crossed the linear range of the standard curve.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from persimmon peel using the RN53 EASYspin Plus Plant RNA rapid extraction kit (Aidlab Bio-technologies, Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The yield of RNA was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA), and the integrity was evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis stained with ethidium bromide36.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

Quantification was performed via a two-step reaction process: reverse transcription (RT) and PCR. Each RT reaction consisted of 0.5-µg RNA, 2 µL of 5× TransScript All-in-One SuperMix for qPCR, and 0.5 µL of gDNA Remover, in a total volume of 10 µL. Reactions were performed in a GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems, USA) for 15 min at 42 °C and 5 s at 85 °C. The 10-µL RT reaction mix was diluted 10 times using nuclease-free water and held at − 20 °C. Real-time PCR was performed using the LightCycler® 480 Ⅱ Real-time PCR Instrument (Roche, Switzerland) using 10 µL of PCR reaction mixture that included 1 µL of cDNA, 5 µL of 2× PerfectStart™ Green qPCR SuperMix, 0.2 µL of forward primer, 0.2 µL of reverse primer, and 3.6 µL of nuclease-free water. The reactants were incubated in a 384-well optical plate (Roche) at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Each sample was run in triplicate for analysis. At the end of the PCR cycles, melting curve analysis was performed to validate the specific generation of the expected PCR product36. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1 and are synthesized by TsingKe Biotech for use in the quantification test. The expression levels of mRNAs were normalized to DkActin59,60 and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method61. The control group sample on the day of harvest was used as the reference and set to a value of 1.

Statistical analysis

All samples were carried out in triplicate and the all experiments was repeated three batches. Analysis were conducted using the general linear model program of IBM SPSS version 26 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All results were evaluated by general linear model analysis, the different treatments, days after harvest, and their interaction were used as fixed effects. The Least Significant Differences test was used to identify the significance of the analysis of the differences (p < 0.05). Graphpad Pism software was used for plotting. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.
