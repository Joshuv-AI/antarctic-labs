Scalp nerve blocks for external ventricular drainage placement in children
Authors: Hande Gurbuz, Elif Basaran Gundogdu, Derya Karasu, Nermin Kilicarslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExternal ventricular drainage (EVD) placement is usually performed in the operating room under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation in pediatric patients with elevated intracranial pressure. Scalp nerve blocks (SNB) with sedation can avoid the safety concerns and complications associated with general anesthesia, such as airway and hemodynamic imbalance. Moreover, SNB can offer a reduced analgesic need when compared with incision-site infiltration. This prospective observational study aimed to compare perioperative outcomes between SNB with sedation and general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration for EVD placement in pediatric patients.MethodsData from 63 EVD placement procedures performed in children under 8 years of age either under sedation with SNB (n = 29) or under general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration (n = 34) were analyzed. Outcomes included need for postoperative analgesics, total analgesic consumption in the first 24 h postoperatively, heart rate response to surgical incision, and the requirement for conversion to general anesthesia.ResultsCompared with incision-site infiltration, the incidence of heart rate response to surgical stimulus was significantly lower in patients with SNB (3.4% vs. 73.5%, p < 0.001). Postoperative analgesic requirement and total analgesic consumption within the first 24 h were also lower in the SNB group (both p < 0.001), with an absolute risk reduction of 47.4%. Compared with patients receiving SNB, those receiving incision-site infiltration had higher odds of postoperative analgesic requirement (OR 8.6, 95% CI 3.2–25.0, p < 0.001); however, this association was attenuated after adjustment for intraoperative fentanyl (OR 2.4, 95% CI 0.2–33.3, p = 0.49). No procedures required conversion to general anesthesia.ConclusionsSNB combined with sedation may represent a feasible alternative to general anesthesia for pediatric EVD placement. Lower rates of nociceptive responses and postoperative analgesic requirements, as well as lower intraoperative opioid use, were observed in patients receiving SNB, while spontaneous ventilation was preserved throughout the procedure.Keywords: Hydrocephalus, Nerve Block, Neuroanesthesia, Pediatric Anesthesia, Regional Anesthesia, Scalp

Introduction

Hydrocephalus refers to the abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid in the brain ventricles, resulting from an imbalance in cerebrospinal fluid production and absorption or an obstruction within the ventricular system or subarachnoid space limiting its flow [1]. In pediatric patients, hydrocephalus can either be congenital or acquired. Various factors can contribute to hydrocephalus, including subarachnoid hemorrhage, brain tumors, infections, or congenital diseases. In addition to these causes, malfunctioning prior shunts or drainage catheters can also result in acute hydrocephalus, which is a medical emergency [2].

Patients with hydrocephalus often require neurosurgical placement of a cerebrospinal fluid diversion or drainage system to reduce elevated intracranial pressure. Elevated intracranial pressure may impair cerebral blood flow and metabolism. External ventricular drainage (EVD) serves as a temporary solution until a decision is reached regarding the need for a permanent shunt. While EVD can be performed in settings other than the operating room (OR) for both adult and pediatric patients, it is recommended that the procedure be conducted in the OR to reduce peri-procedural complications [3]. In pediatric patients, the placement of EVD is usually performed in the OR under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation [4]. A recent report suggests that bedside placement of ventricular access devices in neonates can be performed with incision-site infiltration in the neonatal intensive care unit without increasing complication rates [5]. Regional anesthesia with sedation can avoid the safety concerns and complications associated with general anesthesia. Additionally, scalp nerve blocks offer advantages over incision-site infiltration anesthesia by covering the dermatomes of relevant nerves, encompassing a larger area than the locally infiltrated region. Additionally, scalp nerve blocks offer the benefit of being administered outside the surgical field.

In our hospital, pediatric EVDs are placed under OR conditions; however, endotracheal intubation and general anesthesia are not routine practices. Although scalp nerve blocks have been investigated in pediatric craniotomy and other neurosurgical procedures, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has specifically evaluated their use for pediatric EVD placement, where they may potentially serve as a primary anesthetic technique. This study aimed to compare perioperative outcomes between SNB with sedation and general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration in pediatric EVD placement. The primary outcome was postoperative analgesic requirement. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic response to surgical incision, total analgesic consumption within the first 24 postoperative hours, procedural comfort, and complications associated with scalp nerve blocks.

Materials and methods

In this observational study, data were prospectively collected after approval of the study protocol by the institutional ethics committee (date: 25.08.2021, decree: 2011-KAEK-25 2021/08–24) and subsequently analyzed retrospectively. The study was conducted in a tertiary-level hospital between August 2021 and August 2022. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06903598). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained for all participants from their parent or legal guardian.

Patient selection

Children under 8 years of age who underwent EVD placement in OR were assessed for eligibility for the study. Patients who were intubated before reaching the OR for any reason (such as pulmonary disease, low Glasgow Coma score, etc.) were excluded from the study. Furthermore, patients who underwent any cerebrospinal fluid diversion procedure other than EVD were also excluded. Some children underwent repeated procedures for EVD placement at different times; therefore, the data were collected on a case-by-case basis.

Perioperative procedure

During the study period, the anesthesia practice for pediatric EVD placement at our institution underwent a gradual transition. Prior to the integration of pediatric regional anesthesia techniques into routine clinical practice, all children undergoing EVD placement were managed exclusively under general anesthesia. Following the appointment of an anesthesiologist experienced in pediatric regional anesthesia, scalp nerve blocks were started to be used as an alternative anesthetic approach for this procedure. Therefore, both general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration and sedation combined with scalp nerve blocks were utilized during the study period, reflecting real-world clinical practice rather than a predefined protocol.

While anesthesia may be administered by different anesthesiologists, all surgeries were performed by a single surgeon. Each anesthesiologist was allowed to choose their preferred anesthesia method, and no interference with their clinical decision-making occurred. The steady-state heart rate was recorded immediately prior to surgical incision under stable anesthetic conditions, defined as the value obtained after sterile preparation and draping, just before the start of the incision. An anesthesiology nurse, unaware of the study protocol, documented any patient who exhibited at least a 10% increase in heart rate following incision, and the duration of anesthesia. A ≥ 10% increase from baseline was used as a sensitive, pragmatic surrogate for nociceptive response, selected a priori to detect earlier and more subtle hemodynamic changes under sedation compared with the 20% threshold commonly used to define clinically significant stress responses [6]. Anesthesia duration was defined as the time from initiation of sedation prior to block performance to the end of surgery in the scalp nerve block group, and from induction of anesthesia to extubation in the general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration group. Paracetamol (10 mg/kg) was administered intravenously prior to completion of the procedure. After surgery, the operating surgeon assessed the anesthesia technique with regard to surgical comfort.

General anesthesia along with surgical incision-site infiltration

After routine monitoring, induction of general anesthesia was achieved using propofol (2–3 mg/kg) or thiopental (3–4 mg/kg) until loss of the eyelash reflex, combined with fentanyl (1 µg/kg) and rocuronium (0.5 mg/kg) administered intravenously. If a laryngeal mask airway was used, no neuromuscular blocking agent was administered. Following endotracheal intubation or laryngeal mask placement, anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in a mixture of oxygen and air, titrated to 1 MAC. Prior to skin incision, the surgeon infiltrated the incision site with a maximum of 1 mL of 0.25% plain bupivacaine.

Scalp nerve blocks along with sedation

After routine monitoring, including electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry, and capnography, the procedure was carried out with supplemental oxygen delivered via face mask at 4 L/min. Then, scalp nerve blocks were then carried out using plain bupivacaine under combined sedation with midazolam (0.05–0.1 mg/kg) and propofol (1–2 mg/kg). Fentanyl was administered only to patients who exhibited a response to injection during block performance or demonstrated a ≥ 10% increase in heart rate in response to surgical incision. The procedure was completed under sedation with preservation of spontaneous ventilation. Additional bolus doses of propofol were administered as needed to ensure immobility (Ramsay Sedation Score 5–6), with the total dose not exceeding 3 mg/kg throughout the procedure.

The specific nerves to be blocked were selected according to the planned incision site and incision width (Fig. 1). Given that multiple scalp nerve injections were performed, the total local anesthetic volume was divided among the injection sites while remaining within the maximum recommended dose of bupivacaine. In addition, local anesthetic volumes were reduced due to the absence of epinephrine, the highly vascular nature of the scalp, and the use of multiple injection sites; moreover, lower volumes were used given the relatively smaller surgical extent of EVD placement compared with more extensive procedures such as craniosynostosis surgery, where higher volumes have been reported in the literature [7]. Block efficacy was assessed at the time of surgical incision based on the presence or absence of a heart rate response, according to the predefined criteria.

Scalp nerve blocks were performed as previously described [7, 8]. The injections were administered at the anatomical landmarks shown in Fig. 1. Local anesthetic concentration was adjusted according to age: 0.1% plain bupivacaine for neonates and infants younger than 6 months, and 0.2% plain bupivacaine for children older than 6 months. The injected volumes were as follows: a single-shot of 0.2–0.3 mL for the combined supratrochlear and supraorbital nerve block, 0.2–0.3 mL for the auriculotemporal nerve, 0.3–0.5 mL for the zygomaticotemporal nerve, 0.2–0.3 mL for the greater auricular nerve, and a single-shot with a maximum total volume of 1 mL for the combined greater and lesser occipital nerve block.

Postoperative follow-up

Following surgery, patients were transferred to the ward, neonatal intensive care unit, or pediatric intensive care unit, depending on their clinical condition. Postoperative analgesia was administered as required based on age-appropriate pain assessment tools: the Neonatal Pain, Agitation, and Sedation Scale for neonates and the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability scale for older children [9, 10]. When the pain score exceeded 3, intravenous paracetamol (10 mg/kg) was administered. Analgesic requirement and total analgesic consumption were recorded during the first 24 postoperative hours. Staff responsible for postoperative follow-up and analgesic administration were blinded to the anesthesia technique.

Sample size

Sample size was estimated a priori based on postoperative analgesic requirements of 10 children who had previously undergone EVD placement at our institution without scalp nerve blocks, of whom 7 required postoperative analgesia (G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4)). Assuming a 50% reduction in analgesic requirement with scalp nerve blocks, a total of 62 procedures were required to achieve 80% power with an alpha level of 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze nonparametric continuous variables, and Pearson chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact, where appropriate) was used to compare categorical variables. Numerical data are presented as median (minimum–maximum), and categorical data as numbers and percentages. Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and absolute risk differences were additionally calculated for major categorical outcomes. To account for repeated procedures within the same patient and the resulting within-subject correlation, multivariable analyses were performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with robust standard errors. Variables considered clinically relevant or potentially associated with postoperative analgesic requirement were included in the multivariable models. Model 1 was adjusted for age, Glasgow Coma Scale score, urgency/emergency status, and repeat procedures. Model 2 additionally included intraoperative fentanyl dose to evaluate its potential confounding effect on postoperative analgesic requirement. A sensitivity analysis restricted to first-time procedures was also performed using multivariable logistic regression under independent observation assumptions. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 42 children undergoing 67 EVD placement procedures were initially assessed for eligibility. Three children who were already intubated upon transfer to the OR from the intensive care unit were excluded. Additionally, one more case was excluded due to the need for interventions beyond EVD placement (removal of a dysfunctional ventriculoperitoneal shunt). In this case, the procedure was managed under sedation combined with a scalp nerve block (supratrochlear and supraorbital nerve blocks). An additional a transversus abdominis plane block was performed for the abdominal incision. After exclusions, the final analysis included 38 children and 63 EVD placement procedures, comprised of both first-time and repeated interventions.

The reasons for a temporary EVD placement included hydrocephalus due to an intracranial mass, intraventricular hemorrhage, infection, shunt malfunction, EVD malfunction, cerebral aneurism, Dandy-Walker syndrome, West syndrome, and Krabbe syndrome. The repeated EVD placement procedures included malposition of the EVD (1 case), obstruction of the EVD cannula (2 cases), malfunctioning shunt (5 cases), and replacing a prolonged EVD every 15 days (7 cases) (as recommended by the institutional infection control committee) [11]. Infections observed during the study period were related to preexisting shunt infections rather than first-time EVD placement.

Demographics and characteristics of the cases are presented in Table 1. The surgical procedures were carried out using a scalp nerve block without conversion to general anesthesia. The incidence of heart rate response to surgical incision was significantly lower in the scalp nerve block group compared with the incision-site infiltration group (3.4% vs. 73.5%, p < 0.001). In addition, the need for postoperative analgesics and total analgesic consumption within the first 24 h were significantly lower in patients receiving scalp nerve blocks (both p < 0.001). The absolute risk difference for postoperative analgesic requirement was 47.4%, and for heart rate response to incision was 70.1%, indicating a substantial clinical effect.

To account for potential confounding and clustering due to repeated procedures within the same patient, a multivariable analysis was performed using generalized estimating equations. In addition, intraoperative fentanyl dose was specifically evaluated given its potential influence on the primary outcome postoperative analgesic requirement. In the model adjusted for age, Glasgow Coma Scale score, urgency/emergency status, and repeat procedures (Model 1), patients receiving incision-site infiltration had higher odds of postoperative analgesic requirement than those receiving scalp nerve blocks (OR 8.6, 95% CI 3.2–25.0, p < 0.001). However, after additional adjustment for intraoperative fentanyl dose (Model 2), this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR 2.4, 95% CI 0.2–33.3, p = 0.49). No other covariates were significantly associated with postoperative analgesic requirement in either model (Table 2). In the general anesthesia group, all children received fentanyl during anesthesia induction, whereas in the scalp nerve block group, fentanyl was administered in three children to facilitate block performance and in one child due to a heart rate response following unanticipated extension of the surgical incision. In addition, a sensitivity analysis restricted to first-time procedures was performed to further assess the robustness of the findings under independent observations. Accordingly, scalp nerve block remained significantly associated with a lower likelihood of postoperative analgesic requirement (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.61, p = 0.014) (Table 3). There were no difficulties during intubation or ventilation in patients undergoing general anesthesia. In patients managed with scalp nerve blocks, sedative agents were carefully titrated to ensure immobility while preserving spontaneous ventilation, and avoiding apnea or oxygen desaturation.

Potential complications of scalp nerve blocks include inadvertent intravascular injection, hematoma formation, and local anesthetic systemic toxicity due to the close proximity of injection sites to vascular structures such as the occipital artery, superficial temporal artery, and supraorbital artery and vein, and rarely, direct needle-related nerve injury. No complications related to either the scalp nerve block or incision-site infiltration were observed during the 24-hour postoperative evaluation period. Furthermore, despite variable lengths of hospital stay related to the underlying disease, no delayed scalp nerve block–related complications were identified during the remainder of the hospital stay. According to the operating surgeon, surgical conditions and procedural comfort were comparable between the two anesthetic approaches.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that scalp nerve blocks combined with sedation were associated with favorable perioperative outcomes during pediatric EVD placement. Compared with general anesthesia with incision-site infiltration, lower rates of hemodynamic response to surgical incision and postoperative analgesic requirement were observed in patients receiving scalp nerve blocks. This observation may reflect improved attenuation of nociceptive input at the time of incision, since hemodynamic responses to surgical stimuli may still occur under general anesthesia despite unconsciousness when antinociception is insufficient. Lower intraoperative opioid requirements were also observed in the scalp nerve block group, suggesting a potential opioid-sparing effect. Moreover, all procedures performed with scalp nerve blocks were completed without conversion to general anesthesia. Taken together, these findings suggest that scalp nerve blocks combined with sedation may represent a practical alternative to general anesthesia for selected pediatric patients undergoing EVD placement in the OR.

The EVD placement procedures can be held in various environments, such as ORs, intensive care units, wards, or even emergency rooms [12]. Nonetheless, each option presents its own set of advantages and disadvantages. In an emergency, placing the EVD at the bedside or in the emergency room helps to avoid delays associated with OR preparation and mitigates risks involved in patient transport. However, numerous studies have shown that EVD placement outside of an OR setting is associated with complications [3]. Placement of EVD outside the OR is believed to carry a significant infection risk; however, several studies contend that there is no meaningful difference [13–20]. Additionally, the risk of hemorrhage and malposition is greater in non-OR settings compared to the OR [12, 13]. Therefore, it is recommended to prefer OR depending on the patient’s clinical situation. [11].

Since many studies on EVD placement have primarily focused on patients with low Glasgow Coma Score or those heavily sedated in intensive care units with secured airways, the anesthesia methods used for these patients have not been a concern, except in a few reports. While EVD can be easily placed with local incision-site infiltration with or without sedation in an adult patient with spontaneous breathing, it is quite challenging in awake and agitated children. Therefore, general anesthesia is a widely accepted conventional method for EVD placement in the OR for children [4, 21]. However, anesthesiologists often hesitate to administer general anesthesia in non-OR settings. Yet, a recent report emphasized that percutaneous tunneled EVD can be placed under general anesthesia in extremely low-birth-weight infants, even at the bedside outside the OR [22].

On the other hand, general anesthesia requiring endotracheal intubation is associated with hemodynamic changes and potential airway-related adverse events during induction and airway management, which may contribute to increased physiological stress in pediatric patients. Additionally, congenital hydrocephalus may accompany other airway abnormalities [21]. The enlarged head in children with chronic hydrocephalus also poses a risk of difficult airway and laryngoscopy challenges. Furthermore, unintentional extubation or kinking can happen under surgical drapes when the head is extended and rotated laterally for the surgical position. For similar reasons, a supraglottic airway is not the best choice. Therefore, regional anesthesia methods may be preferred over general anesthesia based on the child’s general condition. Likewise, a recent report indicated that bedside ventricular access devices can be placed with incision-site local anesthetic infiltration without the need for general anesthesia in newborns [5]. However, in this report, the newborns were swaddled to keep them still. Therefore, providing immobilization without anesthesia in older children may be more challenging.

The existing literature on EVD placement in children with regional anesthesia techniques is relatively scarce, and to our knowledge, no studies indicating the use of scalp nerve blocks in this procedure. Guidelines recommend using scalp nerve blocks and incision-site infiltration for postoperative analgesia in craniotomy [23]. However, meta-analyses show that scalp nerve blocks are more effective than incision-site infiltration for this purpose [24]. Additionally, scalp nerve blocks have also been successfully used in awake craniotomy for pediatric patients [25–28]. Also, a recent case series reported favorable outcomes with occipital nerve blocks and pulsed radiofrequency ablation in treating headaches for children with ventriculoperitoneal shunts [29]. When placing a tunneled EVD, a denser regional block than the incision-site infiltration may be necessary for subcutaneous tunneling. Moreover, in patients who have undergone repeated interventions, dense fibrotic scar tissue at previous wound sites may complicate the procedure, and in some cases, the incision line may need to be extended. In such instances, scalp nerve blocks provide anesthesia over a broader area than incision-site infiltration. Furthermore, administering scalp nerve blocks in an area outside the surgical field provides a benefit for preventing infections. Another challenge of EVD placement under sedation, similar to awake craniotomy in children, is the potential need for conversion to general anesthesia due to agitation, pain, respiratory depression, or acute clinical deterioration [26]. Based on these considerations, we successfully managed EVD placement procedures in children using scalp nerve blocks at our institution. Careful titration of sedative agents allowed the procedure to be completed under sedation with preserved spontaneous ventilation, avoiding conversion to general anesthesia and the potential airway and hemodynamic complications associated with endotracheal intubation, with the additional benefit of reduced intraoperative opioid requirements.

This study has several limitations. First, surgical-site and EVD-related infections could not be evaluated because repeat procedures were included. In addition, some patients required temporary EVD placement due to infection of a previous ventriculoperitoneal shunt. Therefore, the impact of scalp nerve blocks and incision-site infiltration on infection rates in a cohort limited to first-time EVD placements could not be assessed and should be addressed in future studies. Second, although data were prospectively collected, the analysis was performed retrospectively, which may introduce inherent bias. Moreover, the study period coincided with a transition in institutional anesthesia practice for pediatric EVD placement. Following the appointment of an anesthesiologist experienced in pediatric regional anesthesia, scalp nerve blocks were gradually integrated into routine clinical practice, whereas general anesthesia had previously been the standard approach. Consequently, the choice of anesthetic technique was not randomized and reflected real-world clinical decision-making rather than investigator convenience or a predefined protocol. While this approach mirrors daily practice, the lack of randomization may introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings rather than establishing causal relationships. Third, although anesthesiologists were not blinded to the study, both the anesthesiology nurses who recorded intraoperative parameters and the staff responsible for postoperative analgesic administration and data collection in the follow-up units were blinded to the anesthesia technique. Anesthesiologists selected the anesthesia technique based on their clinical judgment, experience with regional anesthesia, and the patient’s condition, which reflects routine clinical practice. Fourth, the response to surgical stimulus was assessed using heart rate changes following incision. Although hemodynamic parameters are commonly used surrogate markers of nociception, more objective pain monitoring technologies have recently become available. Nevertheless, the applicability of these devices in neonates and very young children remains limited. Finally, the single-center design and relatively limited sample size may further restrict the generalizability of the results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, scalp nerve blocks combined with sedation may represent a practical anesthetic approach for EVD placement in children. Lower rates of hemodynamic responses and postoperative analgesic requirements, as well as lower intraoperative opioid use, were observed in patients receiving scalp nerve blocks, and all procedures were completed without conversion to general anesthesia. By preserving spontaneous ventilation and avoiding airway instrumentation, scalp nerve blocks may represent a feasible alternative to general anesthesia in selected pediatric patients.
