A rapid, non-invasive blood-based reporter system for early selection of high-percentage germline chimeras in transgenic chicken production
Authors: Pan Li, Caiying Wang, Ying Bao, Fei Gao, Zhenyang Zhang, Linfang Wang, Chunyuan Zhang, Panxue Wu, Zhihao Jiang, Xuguang Du, Lizhi Lu, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of genetically modified chickens via primordial germ cells (PGCs) transplantation is hindered by the lack of efficient methods to assess germline chimerism in G0 founders. Currently, researchers face a binary choice between destructive sampling and prolonged blind rearing, as no non-invasive screening tool exists to assess chimerism prior to maturity. Conventional approaches rely on either invasive gonadal biopsy or time-consuming progeny testing, both of which significantly delay breeding timelines and increase costs. Here, we present a transformative, non-invasive screening strategy that enables longitudinal, real-time quantification of exogenous PGCs engraftment. We generated male chicken PGCs co-expressing a secreted Gaussian luciferase (Gluc) reporter and GFP via piggyBac transposition. Upon microinjection into recipient embryos, these PGCs successfully colonized the gonads and gave rise to germline-competent chimeras. Gluc activity in peripheral blood was positively associated with donor the abundance of donor PGCs in testes, enabling discrimination between male and female chimeras and revealing individual variation in chimerism degree. This secreted reporter system facilitated the identification of high-percentage male chimera pre-sexual maturity, with sustained or increased signal at 120 days, confirming stable transgene expression. Progeny testing demonstrated that higher blood Gluc activity was associated with greater germline transmission efficiency (75–81%). Moreover, Gluc-based screening identified more transgene-positive offspring than GFP fluorescence and demonstrated stable germline transmission of the reporter transgene to the G1 generation. By enabling early, non-destructive selection of optimal breeding founders, our method drastically reduces housing costs, resource use, and the time required to establish transgenic chicken lines. Collectively, these results suggest that blood Gluc activity could serve as a quantitative surrogate marker for donor PGC colonization and provide a rapid, non-invasive method for the early selection of high-percentage germline chimeras prior to sexual maturity during transgenic chicken production, thereby reducing housing costs and accelerating transgenic chicken line establishment.Keywords: Primordial germ cells (PGC), Gaussia luciferase, Germline chimera, Transgenic chicken

Introduction

Livestock production in general and domestic chicken production in particular plays a vital socio-economic role for people living in low-income countries of Africa and Asia (Mohamadinejad et al., 2024; Mohammadreza et al., 2025). Domestic chickens are one of the most widely distributed avian species worldwide, owing to their short generation interval and remarkable adaptability to diverse agroecologies environments (Khabiri et al., 2025; Mohammadabadi, 2017). The domestic chickens provide high-quality protein and income for the poor rural households and are the most widely kept livestock species in the world(Mohammadabadi et al., 2010, 2024). This is due to the presence of the valuable traits of chicken like disease resistance, adaptation to harsh environments, and ability to utilize poor quality feeds (Khabiri et al., 2023). Against this background, the development of efficient transgenic chicken breeding technology is of great practical significance for genetic improvement, disease-resistance breeding, and industrial upgrading of poultry.

The generation of gene-modified animals has become an essential tool in biotechnology, agriculture, and biomedical research. Currently, there are several approaches to generate gene-modified animals, including somatic cell nuclear transfer, pronuclear microinjection of zygotes, embryonic stem (ES) cell-mediated chimera formation, and primordial germ cell (PGC)-mediated chimera production. Among these methods, PGC-mediated germline transmission has emerged as the most reliable and efficient approach for generating genome-edited chickens (Kolluri et al., 2026). Unlike mammalian ES cells that can contribute to both somatic and germline tissues, chicken PGCs are unipotent cells that migrate specifically to the gonadal ridge during embryonic development, where they can differentiate into functional gametes (Nakamura et al., 2013). A significant challenge in PGC-mediated genome modification is the evaluation of chimeric status in G0 offspring. Unlike mouse chimeras generated through ES cell injection, where coat color markers allow immediate visual identification of chimerism, chicken PGC-derived chimeras show no external phenotypic indicators because the donor PGCs are restricted to the gonadal tissue. This tissue-specific engraftment pattern necessitates specialized detection methods. Currently, two primary approaches exist: (1) destructive sampling of G0 chicks to obtain gonadal tissue for analysis, which precludes the use of high-value individuals for breeding; or (2) waiting approximately 5-6 months for sexual maturation to collect semen or eggs for germline transmission analysis. Both methods present significant limitations in terms of time, cost, and animal welfare.

The prolonged rearing period required for conventional screening methods creates substantial challenges for transgenic chicken production programs. During the waiting period, large numbers of G0 chicks must be housed and maintained, incurring considerable costs for feed, housing, and labor. Furthermore, the uncertainty of chimeric status makes it difficult to prioritize high-quality individuals for breeding, potentially leading to the loss of valuable genetic modifications. Therefore, the development of an early, rapid, and non-invasive method for assessing PGC chimerism in G0 chicks is urgently needed to improve the efficiency and reduce the costs of avian transgenesis programs.

Secreted luciferase reporter systems offer a promising solution for non-invasive monitoring of biological processes in vivo. Gaussia luciferase (Gluc), derived from the marine copepod Gaussia princeps, is a highly sensitive and stable bioluminescent enzyme with a molecular weight of approximately 20 kDa (Tannous et al., 2005). The enzyme contains a signal peptide sequence that enables efficient secretion from cells into the culture medium or circulation (Tannous et al., 2005). Gluc catalyzes the oxidation of the substrate coelenterazine to produce bright blue light, and its signal can be detected in blood, urine, and conditioned culture media (Wurdinger et al., 2008). The short half-life of Gluc in circulation (approximately 20 minutes) makes it particularly suitable for real-time monitoring of dynamic biological processes (Tannous et al., 2005). The Gluc reporter system has been successfully applied for monitoring protein secretion rates, promoter activity, and cell proliferation in vivo (Sugawara et al., 2023). Compared to other secreted reporters such as secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP), Gluc offers significantly enhanced sensitivity, a broader linear range, and much faster assay time (Tannous, 2009). These advantages make Gluc an attractive candidate for developing non-invasive screening methods in poultry.

The piggyBac transposon system, isolated from the cabbage looper moth Trichoplusia ni, has proven to be an efficient tool for stable gene transfer in chicken cells (Macdonald et al., 2012; Park and Han, 2012). Unlike viral vectors that pose biosafety concerns and may cause transgene silencing, the piggyBac system enables stable integration of transgenes into the host genome with high efficiency. The transposon exhibits a cut-and-paste mechanism that integrates into TTAA sequences throughout the genome, accommodating relatively large transgenes without compromising transposition efficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated that piggyBac-mediated transgene insertion into chicken PGCs can achieve high rates of germline transmission, with donor PGC-derived offspring reaching up to 95.2% efficiency (Park and Han, 2012).

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that integrating the secreted Gluc reporter gene into chicken PGCs via the piggyBac system would enable non-invasive assessment of exogenous PGC chimerism through simple blood sampling. In this study, we established a comprehensive system for producing, monitoring, and screening G0 chimeric chicks carrying Gluc-expressing donor PGCs. We further investigated whether circulating Gluc activity could serve as a surrogate marker for donor PGC colonization in recipient gonads and predict subsequent germline transmission efficiency. Our results demonstrate that blood Gluc activity correlates with the degree of PGC chimerism and remains readily detectable throughout post-hatch development. Importantly, circulating Gluc activity measured in G0 chimeras was predictive of subsequent germline transmission performance, while the reporter cassette was stably inherited by G1 offspring. These findings establish a simple, sensitive, and non-destructive strategy for real-time assessment of germline chimerism and early identification of high-potential breeding founders. To our knowledge, this study represents the first application of a secreted luciferase-based blood reporter system for longitudinal monitoring of donor PGC contribution and prediction of breeding potential in transgenic chicken production, providing a practical tool for accelerating the generation of gene-modified poultry lines while reducing breeding costs and animal use.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

The fertilized Hy-Line variety White chicken eggs for PGC isolation were obtained from Beijing Guichuan Yashen Breeding Center. The fertilized White Leghorn eggs for embryonic vascular microinjection were obtained from Boehringer Ingelheim. The fertilized eggs were incubated in an artificial incubator maintained at 38°C with 60% humidity. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Experimental Committee of Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (26ZALAS09) and performed in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Isolation and culture of chicken PGCs

PGCs were isolated from the gonads of 7-day-old Hy-Line White chicken embryos. Briefly, embryonic gonads were dissected under a stereomicroscope, dissociated with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 8 min, and neutralized in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The dissociated cells were then firstly seeded in 24-well culture plates and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4-5 h to facilitate differential adhesion. The non-adherent PGCs were subsequently collected by gentle pipetting and plated onto mitomycin C-treated BRL feeder layers in fresh 24-well plates. The PGCs were cultured in mKO medium as previously described (Xie et al., 2019). Briefly, this medium was consisted of avian KO-DMEM supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2% chicken serum, 1 × EmbryoMax nucleosides, 1 × GlutaMax, 1 × non-essential amino acids, 0.1 mM beta-mercaptoethanol, 1.2 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin and streptomycin, 0.1mg/mL sodium heparin, 1 × B27, 25 ng/mL Activin A and 4 ng/mL FGF2. The self-made avian KO-DMEM was prepared as previously described (Kinoshita et al., 2024). Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator and passaged every 5-6 days onto mitomycin-inactivated BRL feeder layers.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence analysis, PGCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes at RT. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 minutes, cells were incubated with mouse anti-stage-specific embryonic antigen-1 (SSEA-1) primary antibody at a dilution of 1:50 (DSHB, IA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgM secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Plasmid construction

The piggyBac transposon vector carrying the EF1a-Gluc-IRES-GFP expression cassette was constructed as follows. The Gluc coding sequence was cloned downstream of the human elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1a) promoter, followed by an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) and the enhanced green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene via PCR and Gibson assembly. The entire expression cassette was flanked by the 5′ and 3′ terminal repeat sequences of the piggyBac transposon from pZGs.

Transfection and selection

PGCs isolated from the male embryonic gonads were used for transfection after approximately 30 days in culture. The PGCs were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min and resuspended in 5 mL PBS for counting. The PBS was removed after centrifuging again at 500 × g for 5 min. A total of 1 × 106 PGCs were resuspended in 100 μL pre-warmed nucleofection solution mixed with 9 μg of the pPB-EF1a-Gluc-IRES-GFP transposon vector and 3 μg of the CAG-PBase vector. This cell-DNA mixture was then transferred into an electroporation cuvette and electroporated using the Lonza electroporator 2b device with program B016. Following electroporation, cells were cultured for 7 days to allow stable integration of the transgene. GFP-positive PGCs were then sorted using a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) and expanded in culture for subsequent experiments.

Gaussia luciferase activity assay

Gluc activity in culture supernatants and blood samples was measured using a bioluminescence assay, as previously reported (Tannous, 2009). For cell culture samples,10 μL of supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of a 20 μM coelenterazine substrate solution in a 96-well white-walled plate, and luminescence was immediately recorded using a SpectraMax ID5 microplate reader with an integration time of 1 second. For blood sample collection, the whole blood was collected from the wing vein of G0 chicks into an anticoagulant. Following collection, 10 μL blood samples were directly transferred to a 96-well white-walled plate. The Gluc activity was measured immediately by adding 100 μL of a 100 μM coelenterazine substrate solution to each well, and luminescence was measured immediately after gentle mixing.

PGC microinjection and embryo transfer

Fertilized eggs from White Leghorn chickens were incubated at 38 °C with 60% humidity. At Hamburger-Hamilton stages 14-17 (52-54 hours of incubation), a small window (approximately 1 cm in diameter) was created in the eggshell over the embryo using a small blade and a pointed tweezer. The vitelline membrane was carefully torn to expose the dorsal aorta. Approximately 1,000-2,000 GFP-positive PGCs in 2 μL of PBS were microinjected into the dorsal aorta using a pulled sharp glass capillary needle connected to a microinjector. Following injection, the eggshell window was sealed with parafilm, and eggs were returned to the incubator and positioned vertically with the blunt end (air cell) upward. Hatching occurred at approximately 21 days of incubation.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data statistical analysis. Statistical analysis of biological replicates was performed by an unpaired two-tailed t-test. P < 0.05 was considered significant. Detailed information is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Establishment and characterization of chicken PGC lines

Firstly, we successfully established 18 PGC lines from the gonads of 20 Hy-Line White chicken embryos at 7 days of incubation using the mKO-F culture system, achieving an overall success rate of 90%. The isolated PGCs exhibited typical morphological characteristics, appearing as round, phase-bright cells with a characteristic cytoplasmic lipid vacuole, as previously reported (Fig. 1A). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that the cultured PGCs strongly expressed SSEA-1, a well-established marker for chicken primordial germ cells (Fig. 1B). The SSEA-1 signal was localized to the cell surface, consistent with its role as a stage-specific embryonic antigen. These results confirmed that the established cell lines maintained the characteristic phenotype of germline-competent PGCs.

Construction and validation of the Gluc-IRES-GFP reporter PGCs

To enable non-invasive monitoring of PGC chimerism, we constructed a piggyBac transposon vector containing the EF1α promoter driving expression of a Gluc-IRES-GFP bicistronic cassette (Fig. 1C). The EF1a promoter was chosen for its capability of stable long-term germline transmission of GFP transgenic rat (Jeon et al., 2024), while the IRES element allows simultaneous expression of both Gluc and GFP from a single transcript. The GFP marker enables visual tracking of transfected cells and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

Rooster-derived PGC lines were co-transfected with the pPB-EF1a-Gluc-IRES-GFP vector and a CAG-driven transposase expression plasmid using electroporation. After 7 days of culture, GFP-positive cells were sorted by FACS and subsequently recovered in culture to enrich the transgenic population. The sorted PGCs maintained typical morphology and showed stable GFP expression (Fig. 1D). To verify Gluc secretion, culture supernatants from wild-type and transfected PGCs were collected and assayed for luciferase activity. As shown in Fig. 1E, supernatants from Gluc-expressing PGCs (PGC-Gluc-IRES-GFP) exhibited high luminescence signals, whereas wild-type PGC supernatants showed background-level activity, confirming that the expressed Gluc protein is both active and secreted.

Production of G0 germline chimeric chickens via PGC transplantation

To evaluate the germline transmission competency of the Gluc-expressing PGCs, these expanded PGCs were microinjected into 101 White Leghorn recipient chicken embryos. Of these embryos, 22 (21.8%) successfully hatched. Of these 22 chicks, we raised 7 males and 11 females. Finally, five males and four females reached early sexual maturity. This hatching efficiency is comparable to previously reported values for PGC microinjection experiments in chickens (Kinoshita et al., 2024). To confirm that the injected PGCs successfully colonized the recipient gonads, we examined gonadal tissue from selected G0 chicks at 12 days post-hatching. Gonads from chimeric chicks were dissected and examined under a fluorescence microscope. As shown in Fig. 2B, abundant GFP-positive cells were observed in the testes of chimeric chicks, indicating successful colonization by the donor PGCs. In contrast, no GFP signal was detected in the gonads of the G0 hens (Fig. 2C) and the wild-type control chicks (Fig. 2A). These results demonstrate that the Gluc-expressing PGCs retained their normal migratory and colonization capabilities following transfection and culture.

Blood Gluc activity reflects donor PGC colonization in recipient gonads

To evaluate whether blood Gluc activity could serve as a surrogate marker for gonadal exogenous PGC chimerism, blood samples were collected from all 18 G0 chimeric chicks at 55 days post-hatching. Blood samples were prepared and assayed for Gluc activity using the bioluminescence assay. The results revealed a striking sex-specific pattern in Gluc activity (Fig. 2D). Among the 18 G0 chicks, hens exhibited minimal or undetectable Gluc activity in their blood, whereas all roosters showed robust Gluc activity, albeit with considerable variation among individuals. This sex difference is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that male donor PGCs display limited colonization efficiency in female recipient gonads. At the same time, male-to-male PGC transplantation results in efficient gonadal colonization and robust germline chimerism. The variation in Gluc activity among roosters likely reflects individual differences in the degree of gonadal colonization by donor PGCs. These findings suggest that quantitative measurement of blood Gluc activity may provide a non-invasive strategy for assessing exogenous PGC chimerism in G0 chicks.

To directly evaluate the relationship between circulating Gluc activity and donor PGC colonization in recipient gonads, we generated an additional cohort of G0 chimeric chicks and analyzed both blood Gluc activity and donor PGC contribution in the testes at 2 days post-hatch. Donor-derived PGCs were quantified as the percentage of GFP-positive cells by flow cytometry. Notably, blood Gluc activity showed a significant positive correlation with the proportion of GFP-positive testicular cells (R² = 0.6205, P = 0.0117) (Fig. 2F). This result demonstrates that circulating Gluc activity closely reflects the extent of donor PGC engraftment in recipient gonads, thereby validating blood Gluc activity as a reliable quantitative indicator of gonadal chimerism. It is worth noting that the relatively lower blood Gluc activity may be attributable to the limited number of Gluc-expressing cells present in newly hatched chicks at this early developmental stage. As donor-derived germ cells proliferate and expand during postnatal development, the circulating Gluc signal is expected to increase accordingly.

Blood Gluc activity correlates with long-term transgene stability

Furthermore, to assess the long-term in vivo expression stability of the PB transposon-mediated Gluc transgene, blood samples were collected from all nine surviving G0 chimeric chicks approximately 120 days after hatching and subsequently assayed for Gluc activity. As shown in Fig. 2E, the overall expression pattern of Gluc activity at 120 days was consistent with that observed at 55 days post-hatching, indicating sustained transgene expression over time. Notably, we found that the Gluc activity at 120 days post-hatching was approximately fivefold higher than that detected at 55 days. This increase likely reflects a corresponding expansion in the number of exogenous Gluc-positive transgenic PGCs within the recipient gonads, as the spermatogonia resume cell division at 10 weeks after hatching (Mathan et al., 2023). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the PB transposon system employed in this study supports long-term, stable expression of the Gluc transgene in chickens across extended developmental periods. Moreover, the secreted Gluc reporter enables non-invasive monitoring of exogenous PGC proliferation in recipient gonads through quantitative measurement of luciferase activity in peripheral blood.

Stable germline transmission and sensitive detection of transgenic offspring

To directly prove that this Gluc reporter system could be used as a breeding tool, we performed progeny testing to verify the relationship between the blood Gluc activity in G0 chimeric roosters and their corresponding germline transmission rates. As shown in Table 1, three mature roosters (Fig. 2G) generated via donor PGC transplantation produced stable donor-derived offspring (Fig. 2H and I), and the efficiency of germ-line transmission ranged from 75% to 81%. G0 chimeric roosters exhibiting higher blood Gluc activity generally produced a higher proportion of Gluc-positive offspring. Furthermore, the number of positive offspring detected by the Gluc assay was higher than that identified by GFP fluorescence observation, indicating that Gluc-based screening provides greater sensitivity for identifying transgene-positive progeny.

Importantly, robust Gluc activity remained detectable in these founders at 280 days post-hatch and was readily identified in their offspring, demonstrating stable expression and germline inheritance of the integrated reporter cassette. Together, these findings indicate that blood Gluc activity not only reflects donor PGC colonization in recipient gonads but also serves as a practical predictor of subsequent germline transmission efficiency. Therefore, early measurement of blood Gluc activity provides a valuable strategy for prioritizing high-potential breeding founders before sexual maturity, thereby improving breeding efficiency and accelerating the establishment of transgenic chicken lines.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and validated a non-invasive selection method based on secreted Gluc, enabling rapid identification of high-percentage chimeras from blood samples collected prior to sexual maturity. And the blood Gluc activity measured prior to sexual maturity was predictive of subsequent germline transmission efficiency, highlighting its potential utility as an early screening tool for identifying high-value breeding founders. The ability to identify high-percentage chimeras immediately after hatching represents a significant improvement over the conventional screening strategy. Existing approaches typically require either sacrificing valuable G0 individuals for direct gonadal analysis or waiting approximately 5-6 months until sexual maturity to assess germline transmission through test-crossing. In contrast, our non-invasive method enables early identification and selection of the best G0 candidates for breeding and allows elimination of non-chimeric or low-percentage individuals, thereby reducing housing costs, feed consumption, and labor requirements. Furthermore, early identification facilitates more efficient breeding program management, as high-percentage chimeras can be prioritized for mating as soon as they reach sexual maturity. Collectively, this selective method would improve the positive rate of G1 chicks and ultimately accelerate the overall timeline for transgenic chicken production.

While this Gluc-based approach offers clear advantages for PGC-mediated transgenesis, it is noteworthy that similar limitations also exist in ES cell-based strategies for generating gene-modified animals. Although coat color chimerism in G0 animals provides a convenient and widely used visual indicator of ES cell contribution to somatic tissues, it does not reliably identify true germline chimeras. In practice, a substantial fraction of coat color-positive G0 chimeras fail to transmit the targeted allele to the next generation, necessitating extensive and time-consuming test-crossing to confirm germline contribution. Unlike the unipotent PGCs, ES cells are multipotential stem cells that can differentiate into various types of cells, including the germ cells. Consequently, global or ubiquitous expression of the secreted Gluc reporter in ES cell-derived chimeras would primarily reflect overall somatic contribution rather than germline involvement. To overcome this limitation, it is necessary to conduct targeted integration of the secreted Gluc reporter into germ cell-specific gene sites, such as DAZL or VASA (DDX4), in ES cells to restrict reporter expression to the germ cell lineage. Such a design would enable blood Gluc activity to serve as a functional and non-invasive surrogate marker specifically for the germline contribution of ES cells.

Despite these promising applications, the present study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. A major limitation is the small sample size. The limited cohort precludes robust correlation analysis and weakens sex-based conclusions. While blood Gluc activity showed a positive association with testicular donor PGC colonization, these findings are preliminary proof-of-concept. Reliable regression analysis and threshold establishment require substantially larger samples. The undetectable Gluc in hens is consistent with sex-specific colonization patterns reported previously, but needs further confirmation in expanded cohorts. Further scaled-up validation is essential before practical application.

Although our blood-based Gluc detection system could identify the high-rate germline chimeras under laboratory conditions, further cost reduction and high-throughput optimization are required to expand its practical promotion in diverse poultry research laboratories and breeding enterprises. Current detection relies on commercial luminometers and expensive coelenterazine substrates, which increases overall testing costs and restricts large-scale batch screening of numerous candidate G0 chicks. Several feasible optimization directions can be implemented in subsequent research. First, the microplate readers could be substituted by low-cost portable miniature luminometers. On the other hand, the substrate formulas and reaction volume could be optimized to decrease reagent consumption per sample while maintaining assay sensitivity. Second, it is a promising avenue to develop lateral flow immunochromatographic test strips for Gluc qualitative and semi-quantitative blood detection in terms of high-throughput transformation. Such strip-based detection eliminates dependence on precision and expensive instruments, supporting rapid field batch testing. Collectively, these technical upgrades will greatly reduce equipment and testing thresholds, making this non-invasive screening method affordable for ordinary breeding labs and grassroots poultry farms, accelerating the popularization and industrial application of transgenic chicken breeding technology.

The current study established proof-of-concept in male donor-to-male recipient transplantation using White Leghorn chickens. Wider validation is needed to ensure broad application. First, female chimeras were not well studied in our study. The correlation between circulating Gluc level and gonadal chimerism in female chimeric chicks should be thoroughly analyzed to complete full-sex validation. Second, various chicken breeds with distinct genetic backgrounds will be examined to validate the system’s reliability across different genetic lines. Third, the relationship between the common physiological stressors (e.g., handling, health status) and the blood Gluc signal will be investigated. These results will facilitate the formulation of standardized sampling protocols and further enhance the practicality and translational value of this non-invasive screening technology.

An additional consideration for the routine application of this platform is the long-term stability and biosafety of the integrated reporter system. In our study, the sustained Gluc expression could be detected in chimeric roosters after reaching adulthood, demonstrating sustained expression of the piggyBac-integrated transgene throughout development. More importantly, Gluc activity was also detected in G1 offspring derived from these founders, providing direct evidence of stable germline transmission and inheritance of the reporter cassette. These findings indicate that the integrated Gluc transgene remains functional across generations and support the long-term reliability of this blood-based monitoring strategy. In addition, no obvious abnormalities in growth, survival, behavior, fertility, or sexual maturation were observed in Gluc-positive G0 chimeras and G1 offspring during the observation period. As a naturally secreted reporter protein widely used for in vivo monitoring, Gaussia luciferase is generally considered safe and has not been reported to adversely affect animal health. Together, these observations suggest that the reporter system is compatible with normal development and reproduction. Nevertheless, future studies involving larger populations and additional generations will further strengthen the assessment of long-term transgene stability, reproductive performance, and animal welfare.

In summary, we developed and validated a rapid, sensitive, and non-invasive blood-based reporter system for quantitative assessment of donor PGC contribution in chimeric chickens. Blood Gluc activity was significantly associated with donor-derived PGC colonization in recipient testes and remained stably detectable throughout development and after germline transmission. Importantly, circulating Gluc activity measured in G0 chimeras was predictive of subsequent germline transmission efficiency, enabling early identification of high-potential breeding founders prior to sexual maturity. Compared with conventional screening approaches, this strategy provides a simple and cost-effective means of monitoring germline chimerism while reducing the time and resources required for transgenic chicken production. We anticipate that this platform will facilitate the generation of gene-modified poultry lines and promote broader applications of avian transgenic technologies in agriculture, biomedicine, and basic research.
