Canada’s unregulated private health care market
Authors: Chad G. Ball, Sean Gregg, Edward J. Harvey
Published: 2026

On November 24, 2025, Alberta tabled the Health Statutes Amendment Act (No. 2), also known as Bill 11. It became law on December 18, 2025, and established a legal framework for physicians to engage in dual practice. Essentially, it allows physicians in Alberta to sign up for both private and public practice models.

Debate surrounding Alberta’s Bill 11 has been brisk and intense. Many health care coalitions from across Canada have framed it as a violation of the Canada Health Act. While there is often considerable nuance within these debates, at the broader level they have focused on whether Canada should permit any private health care at all. Supporters of Bill 11 describe an opportunity to improve access and increase capacity, whereas its critics warn of a gradual drift toward a 2-tier system that threatens citizens who can’t afford to pay.

Although public and accessible health care continues to be a central tenet for nearly all Canadians, tremendous frustration exists among patients who feel the system is already inaccessible in any real timely manner. An even more fundamental question that we should be asking: are we truly debating the introduction of private health care, or rather, are we discussing an attempt to regulate a phenomenon that already exists in Alberta and beyond? Certainly, Quebec has been seen as the vanguard of this movement — other provinces are just realizing what will potentially happen.

The premise that Canada currently operates as a purely public health care system is increasingly difficult to reconcile with reality. Across the country, patients routinely access care through alternative funding arrangements, workers’ compensation programs, federal insurance plans, interprovincial referral networks, medical tourism, and a growing number of uninsured services that exist at the margins of provincial health insurance frameworks. While these activities differ in their legal and ethical implications (and as a result, in their alignment with the Canada Health Act), they share a common feature: they create differential access to health care outside the conventional publicly funded pathway.

This reality is particularly apparent in the growing grey zone between insured and uninsured services. Consider the patient who waits months to see an orthopedic surgeon but can obtain a paid review of a magnetic resonance imaging scan within a few days. The orthopedic surgeon can reasonably argue that a detailed review of imaging, performed outside the context of an insured consultation, is not a publicly insured service and therefore cannot be billed to the provincial health plan. Yet, the practical value being purchased is not merely interpretation of an image. The patient is purchasing earlier access to specialist expertise. Other examples include patients who may pay for consultations regarding medical travel or fitness for surgery outside their province. Formally, these services may constitute uninsured professional advice. Functionally, however, they often serve as entry points into expedited diagnostic or surgical pathways. Whether these arrangements represent payment for a service or payment for access is precisely the question policy-makers have thus far avoided confronting.

The Canada Health Act was intended to address this concern. Through provisions prohibiting extra-billing and user charges for insured services, and through the principle of portability between cities and provinces, the Act sought to ensure that access to medically necessary care would not depend upon a patient’s ability to pay. The challenge facing modern health care systems is not that these principles have been abandoned, but that the complexity of enforcement has allowed increasing divergence between legislative intent and operational reality. Many contemporary arrangements appear to comply with the technical wording of existing regulations while simultaneously challenging the spirit of equitable access that those regulations were designed to protect. Examples of these scenarios are ample and include not only access to surgical care, but also access to primary care in the form of “membership-style” semi-private family practice offices that merge the delivery of public and private health care access on a more timely and as-needed basis.

This is perhaps most apparent in the increasingly sophisticated forms of jurisdictional arbitrage that have emerged across Canada. Provincial differences regarding physician opt-out provisions, private facilities, and uninsured services have created a landscape in which patients and providers routinely cross provincial boundaries in search of opportunities unavailable within their own jurisdictions. Surgeons may maintain public practices in one province while participating in private activity in another. Patients travel thousands of kilometres to access procedures that are unavailable, delayed, or prohibited at home. These arrangements create additional costs, fragmented follow-up care, and administrative complexity, while doing little to advance the broader objectives of accessibility or system efficiency. These scenarios are not new; they have existed for many, many years.

Another important concern remains that when provincial options and alternatives prove insufficient, patients increasingly look beyond Canada’s borders altogether. Clinics in the United States, Germany, Lithuania, Mexico, and elsewhere actively market health care services to Canadians frustrated by domestic wait times. In doing so, they capture not only health care expenditures, but also surgical volume, innovation, expertise, and economic activity that might otherwise remain within the Canadian system. The paradox is difficult to ignore. While Canadian policy-makers continue to debate whether private health care should exist domestically, private health care providers around the world are already serving Canadian patients. From both an economic and clinical point of view, this can be extremely challenging given patients who undergo procedures performed in other countries often return home to Canada with procedure-associated complications and confusion that must be managed within their publicly funded provincial health care system.

One of the central weaknesses inherent within the current approach is not that private health care activity occurs; rather, it is that much of it occurs without meaningful measurement, oversight, or policy design. Remarkably little data exist regarding the scale of interprovincial private care, medical tourism, privately funded consultations, or alternative access pathways. Consequently, the debate surrounding private health care is often driven more by ideology than evidence. Proponents promise improved access; opponents predict harm to the public system. Neither side can accurately nor adequately quantify phenomena that remain largely unmeasured.

This is where Alberta’s Bill 11 becomes worthy of serious discussion. The strongest and most optimistic argument for a regulated private-pay framework is not that it will necessarily improve access, nor that it represents a superior model of care. Rather, it offers the possibility and hope of transparency. In theory, activity occurring within a regulated framework could be measured, monitored, and modified. Governments could collect data regarding volumes, outcomes, physician participation, wait times, workforce effects, and patient migration. Regulatory levers could be adjusted in response to evidence. Safeguards could be strengthened when unintended consequences emerge. In contrast, the current environment offers little more than fragmented oversight of an equally fragmented reality. From this point of view, however, another question arises: can the government ensure a nuanced and motivated level of oversight and regulation when they have not been able to provide this previously within a system over which they have maintained nearly complete control?

Although we may disagree on the appropriate role of private health care within Canada, it should no longer be debatable that private health care already exists in many provinces. The choice before policy-makers is therefore not between a public system and a private system. It is really between a private market that remains largely informal, opaque, and reactive, and one that could potentially be transparent, accountable, and subject to engaged and deliberate governance. The question is whether our governments can adequately regulate either.
