Trends in dental care utilization and tooth loss among vulnerable adults in Nevada (2018–2024): a repeated cross-sectional analysis in the context of COVID-19 and medicaid coverage expansions
Authors: Olufemi Erinoso, Julia Gillen, Jannette Gomez, Lynn Short, Emily Firman, Cyndy Cendagorta, Brooke DerAshodian, Keith Benson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health disparities persist among adults with chronic conditions and structural barriers to care, including diabetes, disability, and residence in non-metropolitan areas. Recent Medicaid expansions in Nevada and COVID-19-related disruptions to dental service delivery provide important context for examining population-level trends in oral health care utilization and outcomes.MethodsWe used repeated cross-sectional data from the Nevada Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) for 2018, 2020, 2022, and 2024 (N = 11,552) to examine trends in past-year dental visits and permanent tooth loss among adults aged ≥ 18 years. Survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted prevalence by year and to assess associations with area of residence, diabetes, and disability status, adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, income, smoking status, and insurance coverage.ResultsThe prevalence of past-year dental visits declined from 67.1% in 2018 to 63.5% in 2024 (AOR:0.84, 95% CI: 0.74–0.95). In 2018, adults with diabetes had lower utilization than those without diabetes (53.9% vs. 69.0%), a difference that was attenuated in subsequent years (2020, 2022, and 2024). Adults with disabilities (vs. no disabilities) consistently had lower utilization in both 2018 (58.3% vs. 71.5%) and 2024 (57.9% vs. 67.3%). Metropolitan residents had higher utilization than non-metropolitan residents in 2018 (68.5% vs. 62.7%) and 2024 (66.1% vs. 56.1%). The prevalence of permanent tooth loss increased from 45.8% in 2018 to 49.0% in 2022 (AOR:1.17, 95% CI: 1.04–1.31). Permanent tooth loss was higher in all years for adults with diabetes, disability, and residing in non-metropolitan counties.ConclusionsBetween 2018 and 2024, dental service utilization in Nevada declined following the COVID-19 pandemic and did not fully rebound, while disparities by disability, diabetes, and geographic residence persisted. These findings highlight the need for integrated strategies that address both coverage and structural barriers to improve oral health equity in Nevada.

Introduction

Oral health is a fundamental component of overall health and qualityof life, and preventing tooth loss remains a priority of federal and state oral health initiatives [1, 2]. Despite national improvements, disparities persist among adults with increased biological and structural risks for oral disease [3]. For example, adults with diabetes experience higher prevalence of periodontal disease and tooth loss [4] due to impaired immune responses and inflammatory pathways [5, 6]. Similarly, adults with disabilities often experience barriers to establishing a dental home, including transportation challenges, limited disability-trained provider availability, and reduced insurance coverage [7, 8]. These intersecting biological vulnerabilities and structural barriers contribute to persistent inequities in oral health outcomes across the United States [3].

To address these disparities, several states have implemented policies to expand access to oral health services for high-risk adults [9–13]. Medicaid Sects. 1115 and 1915(c) waiver programs [14], in particular, have been used to broaden coverage for populations with chronic conditions or functional limitations. Nevada recently adopted two such waivers: a 2023 waiver to increase access to oral health treatment services for adults with disabilities, and a 2024 waiver extending coverage to adults with diabetes [9, 13, 15]. The 1115 Demonstration waiver provides expanded dental services to eligible Medicaid members with diabetes, while the 1915(c) waiver expands dental services to eligible Medicaid members who receive services from this Home and Community Based Services Waiver. Although these waivers represent important policy developments, population-level patterns of oral health service utilization among adults with diabetes and disabilities in Nevada have not been described in the period surrounding their implementation.

In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced significant disruptions to dental service delivery nationwide, including temporary practice closures, reduced appointment availability, and deferred preventive care [16–18]. Nevada experienced similar service interruptions, and little is known about how quickly oral health service use recovered in the years following the pandemic. Examining trends across pre- and post-pandemic periods may help contextualize whether utilization patterns rebounded, stabilized, or remained suppressed. Understanding trends in oral health care utilization alongside recent policy expansions and pandemic-related disruptions is essential for contextual interpretation and guiding future state-level planning and resource allocation.

Geographic access is an additional structural determinant of oral health which independently impacts access to oral health [19, 20], as adults living in non-metropolitan areas often face dental workforce shortages, longer travel distances, and fewer preventive and restorative service options compared with metropolitan residents [21]. These rural-urban disparities have been well documented nationally but remain understudied in state-specific analyses of oral health care utilization and outcomes in the post-pandemic period [21, 22].

Using Nevada Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from 2018 to 2024, this study examines population-level trends in dental care utilization and permanent tooth loss among adults. We further assess whether disparities by key risk factors including area of residence, diabetes, and disability changed over time. Diabetes and disability capture distinct but complementary pathways of vulnerability, biological susceptibility and structural barriers, making their joint examination important for understanding population-level oral health disparities. This study describes the trends in oral health outcomes from 2018 to 2024, and does not evaluate the causal impact of COVID-19 or recent Medicaid policy changes, with the goal of potentially informing ongoing oral health planning efforts in Nevada.

Methods

Study design and settings

The study uses a cross-sectional design and data were obtained from the Nevada Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) for the year 2018, 2020, 2022, and 2024 (N= 11,552). Survey years were selected based on biennial availability of oral health measures (i.e., dental visits and permanent tooth loss), with 2018 representing the most recent pre-pandemic baseline. Subsequent waves (2020, 2022, and 2024) span early and later pandemic periods [17]. The BRFSS is a repeated cross-sectional dataset that samples adults across all US states and the District of Columbia, the US Virgin Islands, Puerto Rico, and Guam on chronic health conditions, use of health services, and health-related risk behaviors. The study respondents are non-institutionalized civilian populations, and the mode of collection comprised in-person and computer-assisted interviews in English and Spanish. The BRFSS is a state-based, probability-based survey of non-institutionalized adults that uses telephone-based data collection (landline and cellular) and post-stratification weighting to produce estimates representative of the state population. Participation is voluntary, and weighting is used to reduce potential nonresponse bias. Data collection procedures are described in more detail on the CDC’s website noted in bibliograph [23].

Study population

The study sample comprised adults (18 years and above) who resided in Nevada during the data collection period. All measures in the study population were self-reported.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Research participants in the BRFSS survey provided informed consent prior to participation in the study procedures, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Data from the BRFSS is publicly available. The University of Nevada, Reno, Institutional Review Board deemed the following study exempt and not to be human subject research.

Study measures

Area of residence

Counties were classified as metropolitan or non-metropolitan using the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) urban–rural county classification scheme [24]. Metropolitan counties included those located within metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) ranging from large metropolitan areas with populations of 1 million or more to small metropolitan areas with populations greater than 50,000. Non-metropolitan counties included micropolitan statistical areas, defined by urban clusters with populations of 10,000–49,999, as well as non-core (rural) counties that are not part of a metropolitan or micropolitan statistical area and have populations below 50,000.

Diabetes status

Individuals indicated having ever received a diagnosis of diabetes. The measure excluded women with gestational diabetes or individuals who received a diagnosis of pre-diabetes or border-line diabetes.

Disability status

Disability status was assessed using six constructs that were not mutually exclusive: (a) visual impairment, (b) auditory impairment, (c) cognitive disability – defined as difficulty concentrating and remembering, (d) self-care disability – defined as difficulty bathing and dressing, (e) independent living disability – defined as difficulty doing things alone, and (f) ambulatory disability – defined as difficulty walking. Additionally, we created a separate binary variable – any disability - where individuals were grouped as having no disability vs. one or more disabilities [25]. While analyses of single versus multiple disabilities could provide additional insights, we focused on specific disability types (vision, hearing, cognitive, self-care, independent living, ambulatory) because these represent distinct functional limitations with targetable intervention implications. For example, adults with vision impairment may benefit from transportation services and accessible office signage, while those with ambulatory limitations may require specialized dental equipment and wheelchair-accessible facilities. This specificity allows for more actionable policy and programmatic recommendations.

Utilization of dental services

The measure was defined as having at least one dental visit in the past year. Response options to past year dental visit were ‘Yes or No’.

Permanent tooth loss

The measure was defined as having one or more permanent teeth removed due to gum disease or tooth decay. The definition excluded individuals who had lost a tooth/teeth due to injury or orthodontic treatment.

Covariates

Covariates were selected for their known association with oral health outcomes and dental service utilization [4, 19]. The study covariates were socio-demographic variables including age (18–44/45–64/65+), biological sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white/non-Hispanic black/non-Hispanic other or multiracial identity/Hispanic) as potential confounders affecting both exposure status and oral health outcomes. Annual household income (<$50,000 [ref] vs. $50,000 - <$100,000 vs. $100,000 + vs. undisclosed) was included to account for socioeconomic pathways linking structural barriers to dental access, and education was not included due to its high correlation with income and to preserve model parsimony. Insurance status (any health insurance [prepaid plans, or government plans such as Medicaid/Medicare, or Indian Health Services] vs. no insurance) was included as it represents a key mechanism through which vulnerable populations access dental care. Tobacco smoking status among established consumers (never/current/former) was included given its direct biological effects on periodontal disease and tooth loss.

Statistical analysis plan

Analysis involved combining Nevada BRFSS data from 2018, 2020, 2022, and 2024 into a single analytic dataset. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design using BRFSS sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units. Two dependent variables were examined: (1) having had a dental visit in the past year and (2) any permanent tooth loss. Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, annual household income, current smoking status, and insurance status (insured vs. uninsured). Independent variables of interest were residence status (metropolitan vs. non-metropolitan), diabetes status (yes vs. no), and disability status (any disability and specific disability types).

We first estimated adjusted prevalence of dental visits and permanent tooth loss for each independent variable in each survey year. To do so, we fitted separate survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models for each independent variable, including an interaction term with survey year (2018, 2020, 2022, and 2024). Adjusted prevalence estimates (predicted marginal probabilities) were obtained using the margins command in Stata and reported as percentages. Next, we examined the association between each independent variable and the two oral health dependent variables within each survey year. For these analyses, we fit year-stratified survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for all covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05. Observations with missing data on analytic variables were excluded using listwise deletion, and missingness accounted for < 5% of variables per year. (Table 3 in Appendix) We also added a supplementary (Table 4 in Appendix) summarizing key demographic characteristics of the study sample across survey years and census based estimates. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

Past year dental visit

Among adults in Navada, there was a decline in dental visits between 2018 and 2024. Compared with 2018, adults had significantly lower odds of dental visits in 2022 (62.5% vs. 67.1%; AOR:0.80, 95% CI: 0.71, 0.90) and 2024 (63.5% vs. 67.1%; AOR:0.84, 95% CI: 0.74, 0.95). (Fig. 1; Table 1)

Area of residence

Across survey years, metropolitan residence was associated with higher utilization of dental services compared with non-metropolitan residence, although the magnitude and statistical significance of this association varied over time. For example, in 2018 - Adults living in metropolitan counties had a significantly higher prevalence of past-year dental visits than those in non-metropolitan counties (68.5% vs. 62.7%), with 31% higher adjusted odds of a dental visit (AOR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.09–1.57); 2020 - This metropolitan advantage persisted and slightly increased during the early pandemic period (68.0% vs. 60.5%; AOR:1.43, 95% CI: 1.17–1.76), suggesting that urban residents were more likely to maintain access to dental care during COVID-related service disruptions. In 2022 - no statistically significant difference was observed between metropolitan and non-metropolitan residents (62.3% vs. 63.2%; AOR:0.96, 95% CI: 0.78–1.17), indicating a temporary convergence in utilization during the post-acute pandemic period, and in 2024 - the disparity re-emerged and widened, with metropolitan residents again demonstrating substantially higher utilization (66.1% vs. 56.1%), corresponding to 69% higher adjusted odds of a dental visit (AOR:1.69, 95% CI: 1.37–2.08). (Fig. 1; Table 1)

Diabetes

Adults with diabetes had a lower prevalence of dental visits than those without diabetes in all years; however, only the difference in 2018 was statistically significant (53.9% vs. 69.0%; AOR:0.54, 95% CI: 0.42, 0.69). Differences were attenuated and no longer statistically significant in later years, with the smallest difference observed in 2022 (62.1% vs. 63.0%; AOR:0.93, 95% CI: 0.72, 1.21). (Fig. 1; Table 1)

Disabilities

Adults reporting any disability consistently had lower dental visits than adults without disabilities: 2018 (58.3% vs. 71.5%; AOR:0.59, 95% CI:0.49, 0.70), 2020 (57.6% vs. 70.5%; AOR:0.53, 95% CI:0.43, 0.64), 2022 (58.2% vs. 65.0%; AOR:0.73, 95% CI:0.61, 0.87), 2024 (57.9% vs. 67.3%; AOR:0.57, 95% CI:0.47, 0.69). The disability group with the lowest prevalence varied by year, with vision disability in 2018 (53.8%), self-care disability in 2020 (46.9%), 2022 (51.8%), and independent-living in 2024 (49.7%). Among adults with cognitive disability, the prevalence of dental visits declined from 2018 to 2022. (Fig. 1; Table 1)

Permanent tooth loss

The prevalence of permanent tooth loss was relatively consistent across years, except for 2022, when prevalence was significantly higher than in 2018 (49.0% vs. 45.8%; AOR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.31). (Fig. 2; Table 2)

Area of residence

Non-metropolitan residence was consistently associated with higher prevalence and odds of permanent tooth loss compared with metropolitan residence, although this association weakened over time. For example, in 2018 - Adults in non-metropolitan counties had significantly higher prevalence of tooth loss (52.2% vs. 43.8%) and 52% higher adjusted odds compared with metropolitan residents (AOR:1.52, 95% CI: 1.25–1.83). 2020: The disparity persisted during the pandemic (49.0% vs. 44.1%, AOR:1.26; 95% CI: 1.03–1.56). In 2022, elevated odds of tooth loss among non-metropolitan residents remained significant (52.2% vs. 48.0%; AOR:1.26; 95% CI: 1.03–1.53), and in 2024, the difference was no longer statistically significant (49.8% vs. 47.5%; AOR:1.07, 95% CI: 0.87–1.32), reflecting partial convergence driven largely by increasing tooth loss prevalence among metropolitan residents, rather than marked improvement in rural populations. (Fig. 2; Table 2)

Diabetes

Adults with diabetes had significantly higher prevalence and odds of tooth loss in 2018, 2020, and 2022: 2018 (54.7% vs. 44.6%; AOR:1.51, 95% CI:1.16, 1.96), 2020 (54.5% vs. 43.5%; AOR:1.65, 95% CI:1.23, 2.22), and 2022 (58.9% vs. 46.9%; AOR:1.83, 95% CI:1.39, 2.42). Individuals with diabetes showed no significant temporal change in tooth loss between 2018 and 2024. (Fig. 2; Table 2)

Disabilities

Adults with disabilities consistently had higher prevalence of tooth loss than those without disabilities across all years: 2018 (52.2% vs. 42.9%; AOR:1.50, 95% CI:1.26, 1.79), 2020 (52.5% vs. 41.8%; AOR:1.63, 95% CI:1.34, 1.98), 2022 (54.9% vs. 45.8%; AOR:1.54, 95% CI:1.29, 1.84), and 2024 (54.6% vs. 43.8%; AOR:1.70, 95% CI:1.41, 2.05). Tooth loss among adults with disabilities did not significantly change over time. (Fig. 2; Table 2)

Discussion

Using repeated cross-sectional BRFSS data from 2018 to 2024, the current study examined population-level trends in dental service utilization and permanent tooth loss among adults in Nevada, with a focus on disparities by key risk factors and structural determinants, including diabetes, disability status, and area of residence. Three key findings emerge from this study: dental visit prevalence declined after 2018 and did not fully rebound by 2024; disparities in utilization and tooth loss by disability persisted across all survey years and in 2018 and 2020 for individuals with diabetes; and structural inequities in oral health outcomes remain substantial despite recent policy efforts, the effects of which are not yet observable in these data. These patterns reflect population-level trends across pre-pandemic (2018), early pandemic (2020), and later periods (2022–2024).

Overall dental visit prevalence declined from 2018 to subsequent survey years, with significantly lower adjusted prevalence observed in 2022 and 2024. These findings are consistent with nationwide reports of substantial COVID-19-related disruptions to dental care delivery, including practice closures, reduced appointment availability, and deferred preventive care [26–28]. Although some recovery in utilization was observed after 2020, utilization levels in 2024 remained below pre-pandemic levels, suggesting incomplete recovery several years after the acute pandemic period. Notably, permanent tooth loss did not decline over time and was significantly higher in 2022 than in 2018, underscoring the cumulative nature of tooth loss and raising the possibility that deferred or delayed care during the pandemic contributed to a short-term increase in tooth loss prevalence at the population level. Because BRFSS data are cross-sectional, causal attribution cannot be determined from these trends. Therefore, they reflect population-level shifts rather than individual trajectories, but they nonetheless highlight prolonged disruptions in access to routine dental care in Nevada.

Across all survey years, except 2022, metropolitan residence was consistently associated with higher dental service utilization, while non-metropolitan residence was associated with a higher cumulative burden of permanent tooth loss, underscoring rural-urban oral health inequities in Nevada, except 2024. Although disparities in dental visits narrowed temporarily in 2022, this convergence was not sustained, with utilization gaps re-emerging by 2024 as dental service demand appeared to normalize following pandemic-related disruptions. These patterns likely reflect enduring structural barriers in non-metropolitan areas, including dental workforce shortages and limited availability of preventive and restorative services [19, 29]. Similarly, while rural-urban differences in tooth loss were attenuated by 2024, this change was driven primarily by increasing tooth loss prevalence among metropolitan residents rather than meaningful improvements in rural populations. This pattern may reflect heterogeneity within metropolitan areas, where population density can coexist with provider shortages, as well as delayed care during the COVID-19 period leading to greater need for extractions afterward. However, these explanations are speculative and warrant further investigation. Given the cumulative and irreversible nature of permanent tooth loss, these findings suggest that long-standing differences in access to timely and comprehensive dental care continue to shape oral health outcomes across geographic settings, highlighting the need for targeted strategies to address rural access barriers and workforce limitations in Nevada.

The geographic patterns observed in this study parallel disparities observed among adults with disabilities and chronic conditions, suggesting that structural barriers to accessing dental care may compound across multiple dimensions of vulnerability. In all survey years, adults with disabilities consistently had lower prevalence of dental visits and higher prevalence of permanent tooth loss compared with adults without disabilities. Similar, though somewhat attenuated, patterns were observed among adults with diabetes. These disparities persisted despite adjustment for sociodemographic factors, smoking status, and insurance coverage, suggesting that barriers beyond insurance, such as transportation challenges, limited availability of disability-trained providers, and difficulties navigating dental care systems, may continue to shape oral health outcomes [29, 30]. Disability subtype analyses further revealed particularly low dental service utilization and high tooth loss among adults with ambulatory, independent living, and vision disabilities, groups that may face compounded functional and access barriers. Collectively, these findings indicate that disparities in oral health outcomes remain entrenched and have not meaningfully narrowed in the post-pandemic period.

Policy implications and future directions

Recent Medicaid Sects. 1115 and 1915(c) waivers expanding oral health coverage for adults with disabilities and diabetes in Nevada represent important policy developments aimed at reducing these inequities [14, 15]. However, this study was not designed to evaluate the causal impact of these policies, and the 2024 survey reflects only early implementation with limited post-policy observation time. Additionally, both dental waivers provide expanded services to a limited group of individuals, their population-level impact may not yet be observable in BRFSS data. Nonetheless, the persistence of disparities observed in this study suggests that coverage expansions alone may be insufficient to address structural barriers to care. Complementary strategies, such as increasing provider participation, expanding mobile and community-based dental services, improving transportation supports, and enhancing workforce training in disability-competent care, may be necessary to translate coverage into meaningful utilization and improved outcomes. Continued surveillance will be essential to monitor longer-term trends as these policies mature, and future studies using longitudinal or quasi-experimental designs are needed to more directly assess policy impacts on oral health equity.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, BRFSS data are cross-sectional and repeated at the population level, which precludes inference about individual-level changes in dental service utilization or tooth loss over time. Second, although recent Medicaid waivers expanding oral health coverage for adults with disabilities and diabetes provide important policy context, this analysis was not designed to assess the effect of these policies. Additionally, the short interval between policy implementation (2023–2024) and the 2024 survey limits assessment of early policy-related changes. Third, permanent tooth loss is a cumulative outcome that reflects long-term disease processes and access to care; therefore, short-term changes in service delivery or policy may not result in immediate changes in prevalence. Fourth, all measures were self-reported and subject to recall and social desirability bias. This study involved multiple statistical comparisons across subgroups, years, and outcomes, which increases the possibility of Type I error (false positive findings), and the limited number of survey waves restricts inference about continuous temporal trends. Finally, we did not apply formal corrections for multiple comparisons because our primary aim was descriptive estimation of population-level patterns rather than hypothesis testing.

Conclusion

The current study documents sustained declines in dental service utilization after 2018, spanning the COVID-19 period, and persistent disparities in both utilization and permanent tooth loss among adults with disabilities, diabetes, and those residing in non-metropolitan areas in Nevada. Despite recent policy efforts to expand oral health coverage, inequities remained substantial across the study period, highlighting the enduring influence of structural barriers to care. The cumulative nature of permanent tooth loss further underscores the need for timely, preventive, and accessible dental services to mitigate long-term oral health consequences. Together, these findings provide important population-level evidence to inform ongoing state oral health planning and underscore the need for integrated strategies that address both coverage and structural access to dental care.
