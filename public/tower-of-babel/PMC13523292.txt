Effect of resin cement shade and tooth-shaded background on the final color and optical properties of high-translucency lithium-based CAD/CAM ceramics
Authors: Merve Ünal, Aysıla Tekeli Şimşek, Berkay Enes Güngör, Bengisu Yıldırım
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of ceramic composition, resin cement shade, and tooth-shaded background on the optical properties and color differences of high translucent (HT) lithium-based ceramics.MethodsA total of 192 specimens were prepared from four CAD/CAM ceramics: advanced lithium disilicate, lithium disilicate, and two zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate ceramics. The relative translucency parameter (RTP) and opalescence (OP) were evaluated using a digital spectrophotometer. Specimens were evaluated using two resin cement shades (opaque and translucent) and two tooth-shaded backgrounds (A1 and A3). Color differences (ΔE₀₀) between baseline and after cementation measurements were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula. Data were analyzed using one-way and three-way ANOVA (p < 0.05).ResultsCeramic material significantly influenced RTP and OP values (p < 0.05). Three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of material and cement shade (p < 0.001), as well as a significant material × tooth-shaded background interaction (p = 0.013). Opaque cement resulted in significantly higher color differences than translucent cement across all materials and backgrounds (p < 0.05). Higher ΔE₀₀ values were found on the A3 background.ConclusionsThe final color and optical properties of HT lithium-based ceramics were influenced by ceramic composition and resin cement shade. These findings may help clinicians optimize cement shade and material selection for improved esthetic outcomes.

Introduction

Recent advances in the microstructure and manufacturing techniques of glass ceramics have increased their clinical use due to their favorable esthetic and mechanical properties, enabling their use across a wide range of clinical applications [1]. Lithium-based ceramics are particularly suitable for minimally invasive restorative procedures because of their beneficial mechanical strength and optical performance [2, 3]. However, new lithium-based ceramic materials have been developed to further improve both mechanical and optical performance [4]. Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate ceramics, produced by incorporating approximately 10 wt% zirconia into a glassy matrix, combine the beneficial properties of lithium disilicate and zirconia, offering increased mechanical properties while maintaining esthetic outcomes [5–7]. In addition, advanced lithium disilicate ceramics have been developed by incorporating virgilite-based lithium disilicate crystals in a glassy matrix, resulting in enhanced mechanical strength and improved esthetic properties [5, 8–10]. These structural modifications may also influence the optical behavior of the materials.

The optical properties of ceramic materials, such as translucency, contrast ratio, and opalescence, play an important role in determining the final color of restorations and achieving optical harmony with adjacent natural teeth [11, 12]. Translucency and opalescence directly influence the ability of ceramic restorations to mimic natural dentition. Because opalescence contributes to the vitality and enamel-like appearance of restorations, while translucency influences light transmission and the blending ability of restorations with surrounding dentition, evaluating these optical properties may improve esthetic predictability. Translucency is generally assessed using relative translucency parameter (RTP) and contrast ratio (CR) values [13, 14]. Opalescence (OP) represents a distinct optical phenomenon in which materials exhibit “bluish-white” under reflected light and “orange-brown” when light is transmitted through them [15–17]. Accurate color matching between ceramic restorations and surrounding dentition is a complex procedure, and mismatches may necessitate the fabrication of a new restoration [18–19]. Therefore, understanding the factors that influence the final color outcome of high translucent (HT) ceramic restorations is clinically important for achieving predictable esthetic results.

The final color outcome of translucent ceramic restorations is affected by multiple variables, such as the type and thickness of the material, the underlying tooth color, abutment properties, and the color of the bonding cement [4, 20–24]. These factors collectively have significant effects on the final color results of restorations. Previous studies have found that, under optimal conditions of ceramic thickness and favorable underlying tooth color, the effect of cement shade on the final restoration color is minimal [12, 21]. However, when ceramic thickness is reduced below 1.5 mm or when restorations are placed over dark-colored substrates or abutments, the color of the cement becomes increasingly significant [1, 18, 25, 26]. Because HT ceramics allow greater light transmission, their final color may be more susceptible to the optical influence of the underlying substrate and resin cement. In such cases, the shade of resin-based cement may complicate color matching during the bonding procedure and change the final appearance of translucent restorations [18]. Therefore, the use of more opaque ceramic materials or resin cements, or increased ceramic thickness, may be required to achieve satisfactory esthetic outcomes [11].

Accurate and objective color evaluation methods are required to assess the optical behavior and final color changes of ceramic restorations. Color in dental materials is frequently evaluated using the CIE Lab* color system, which allows accurate color representation within a three-dimensional color space. In this system, L* shows lightness, while a* and b* indicate the red–green and yellow–blue axes, respectively [25]. The CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) color difference formula was developed to improve the perceptual uniformity of the CIELab system and has demonstrated increased correlation between instrumental measurements and visual color perception, particularly for dental applications [18].

Limited information is available regarding the interaction among ceramic composition, resin cement shade, and background color in recently developed HT lithium-based ceramics. Although previous studies have investigated the effects of cement shade or substrate color on conventional lithium disilicate ceramics, limited information is available regarding the combined influence of ceramic composition, cement shade, and tooth-shaded backgrounds on the optical behavior of newly developed HT lithium-based ceramics, including advanced lithium disilicate and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate ceramics. In addition, limited information is available regarding the opalescence behavior of advanced lithium disilicate ceramics. The optical properties of these recently introduced materials have not been sufficiently compared with those of conventional lithium disilicate ceramics widely used in clinical practice. Understanding the optical behavior of these newly developed HT ceramics may help clinicians optimize material and cement selection for improved esthetic outcomes. Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to investigate the effects of ceramic composition, cement shade, and tooth-shaded backgrounds on the optical properties of HT lithium-based ceramics. Materials and methods

Sample sizes were determined through a power analysis (G*Power, version 3.1.3; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf) at a 95% confidence level, with a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80% (effect size: 0.25). This analysis determined a minimum of 12 specimens per subgroup. The workflow of the study is shown in Fig. 1. Four different CAD-CAM ceramics were selected: CEREC Tessera (Advanced lithium disilicate glass-ceramic, ALD, Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany), IPS e.max CAD (Lithium disilicate glass-ceramic, LDS, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein), Vita Suprinity (Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate glass ceramic, ZLS-VS, Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany), and Celtra Duo-HT (Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate glass ceramic, ZLS-CD, Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany). Rectangular specimens (1.0 ± 0.01 mm thickness) were prepared from each material (N = 192). The detailed compositions of the investigated ceramics are provided in Table 1.

Ceramic blocks were sectioned using a low-speed diamond saw (Microcut; Metkon, Bursa, Turkey) under continuous water cooling. All specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min. The samples were crystallized in a furnace (Programat P300; Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein) following the manufacturer’s instructions, as detailed in Table 1. One surface of each specimen was polished with 600-, 800-, 1000-, and 1200-grit silicon carbide papers under water cooling, and no glaze application was performed. This protocol was selected to standardize the surface treatment procedure among all specimens. The thickness was verified using a digital caliper (Mitutoyo Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Tooth-shaded backgrounds were fabricated from a nanohybrid composite resin (Filtek Z 250; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA). Shades A1 and A3 were used to simulate light and dark backgrounds, respectively. Composite blocks were prepared with dimensions of 7 × 12 × 5.0 ± 0.01 mm [21, 27]. The composite was polymerized in polyethylene molds with an LED light-curing unit (Bluephase; Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein, 1200 mW/cm²) for 20 s. The composite specimens were polished with 600- to 1000-grit wet silicon carbide papers [3, 21].

A total of 192 resin cement samples in two shades (opaque pearl and translucent; Allcem Core, Dentscare Ltda., Joinville, Brazil) were prepared at a thickness of 0.1 mm using polyester strips and glass plates under a 0.75 kgf static load [28, 29]. The cements were polymerized with a light-curing device for 20 s on each side. Complete polymerization was completed after 24 h.

A digital spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade V; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) was used in “Base Shade” function to measure the RTP parameter against standard black (L* = 0.5, a* = 14.6, b* = −21.5) and white (L* = 90.9, a* = 0.3, b* = 4.9) backgrounds. 21 Calibration was performed before each measurement according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed at the center of each specimen by the same researcher in a closed, windowless room under a standard D65 illuminator. Each specimen was measured three times, and the mean values were recorded. Baseline measurements were obtained without resin cement. To reduce light scattering, a glycerin gel (n ≈ 1.48) was applied both between the ceramic specimens (n ≈ 1.55) and the cement interfaces and between the cement and tooth-shaded background interfaces (n ≈ 1.50). After cement placement, a second set of measurements was performed. The CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀)21 formula was used to calculate color differences between baseline and post-cementation measurements. ΔL′, ΔC′, and ΔH′ represent differences in lightness, chroma, and hue, respectively. The rotation function (RT) accounts for the interaction between chroma and hue differences in the blue region, while weighting functions (SL, SC, SH) adjust the overall color changes. Parametric factors KL, KC, and KH were set to 1. The thresholds for perceptibility and acceptability were defined as ΔE₀₀ < 0.81 and ΔE₀₀ < 1.77, respectively [31, 32]. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variances were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Mean and standard deviation (SD) values were calculated for all variables. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the optical properties (OP, RTP, ΔL, Δa, and Δb values) among the ceramic materials. When significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s post hoc test. The effects of ceramic material, resin cement shade, and tooth-shaded background on ΔE₀₀, ΔL, Δa, and Δb values were analyzed using three-way ANOVA. The interaction effects among material, cement shade, and background color were also evaluated. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s post hoc test when significant differences were detected. Partial eta squared (η²) values were calculated to determine effect sizes. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

According to the one-way ANOVA results, ceramic material significantly influenced OP (p < 0.001), RTP (p = 0.001), ΔL (p < 0.001), Δa (p < 0.001), and Δb (p < 0.001) values (Table 2). The mean and standard deviation values of L*, a*, and b* coordinates of ceramic materials over black and white backgrounds are presented in Table 3. The mean and standard deviation values of OP, RTP, ΔL, Δa, and Δb are presented in Table 4. The ALD (11.93 ± 0.87) and ZLS-VS (12.62 ± 1.39) groups showed significantly higher OP values than the LDS (6.73 ± 0.46) and ZLS-CD (7.06 ± 0.18) groups (p < 0.001). The ALD (13.20 ± 0.78), LDS (13.23 ± 0.87), and ZLS-VS (13.61 ± 1.28) groups presented significantly higher RTP values than the ZLS-CD group (12.02 ± 0.78) (p = 0.021, 0.017, 0.001). No significant difference was found in RTP levels among the other materials (p > 0.05). For ΔL values, the LDS group showed significantly higher negative mean values than the other ceramic groups (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between the ALD, ZLS-VS, and ZLS-CD groups. For Δa values, the ALD group presented significantly higher negative mean values than the LDS, ZLS-VS, and ZLS-CD groups (p < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between the latter groups. Regarding Δb values, the ALD and ZLS-VS groups exhibited significantly higher negative mean values than the LDS and ZLS-CD groups (p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the ALD and ZLS-VS groups or between the LDS and ZLS-CD groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The first hypothesis, that ceramic composition influences OP, RTP, ΔL, Δa, and Δb values, was accepted. In the present study, ceramic material significantly affected OP, RTP, ΔL, Δa, and Δb values (p < 0.05).

According to previous studies, OP values for monolithic ceramics ranged between 5 and 13 [6, 15, 16], which was consistent with the findings of the present study. However, none of the tested materials exhibited OP values comparable to those of natural enamel. This may indicate that current HT ceramics still cannot completely reproduce the natural opalescent appearance of enamel. Regarding OP values, no statistically significant difference was observed between the ALD and ZLS-VS groups (p = 0.681). In contrast, the LDS and ZLS-CD groups exhibited significantly lower OP values than the ALD and ZLS-VS groups (p < 0.05), while also differing significantly from each other. Similar findings have been reported, with higher mean OP values for zirconia-reinforced lithium (di)silicate (Vita Suprinity) than for lithium disilicate (E.max CAD) [33] and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Celtra Duo) at a thickness of 1 mm [16, 34].

The higher OP values observed in the ALD and ZLS-VS groups may be associated with their higher negative Δb values, indicating greater color changes along the yellow–blue axis. Since opalescence is closely related to blue-orange light scattering behavior, differences in Δb values may reflect variations in the optical behavior of the ceramic materials. In addition, similar Δa values among the LDS, ZLS-VS, and ZLS-CD groups suggest comparable color changes along the red–green axis. However, the ALD group demonstrated significantly higher negative Δa values than the other groups, which may indicate greater shifts toward the green axis.

According to previous studies, inconsistent findings have been reported regarding the translucency of lithium-based ceramics [4, 5, 8, 9, 23]. Several studies demonstrated that advanced lithium disilicate (Tessera) and lithium disilicate (E.max CAD) exhibited similar translucency values [5, 8, 9, 23], which is consistent with the findings of the present study. However, some studies have reported higher TP values for lithium disilicate (E.max CAD) compared with advanced lithium disilicate (Tessera) [5, 9, 23]. Al-Johani et al. [7] reported significantly higher light transmittance values for zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Vita Suprinity) compared with lithium disilicate (E.max CAD), zirconia-reinforced lithium (di)silicate (Celtra Duo), and advanced lithium disilicate (Tessera). However, other studies reported lower TP values for zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate materials [4, 5, 8].

Similar to the findings of Al-Johani et al. [7], the ZLS-VS group exhibited the highest RTP values in the present study, although the differences were not statistically significant. In addition, the ALD, LDS, and ZLS-VS groups presented significantly higher RTP values than the ZLS-CD group (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the optical behavior and light transmission characteristics may differ according to ceramic material composition and microstructure. The lower RTP value observed in the ZLS-CD group may be related to reduced light transmission caused by material-specific differences in crystal morphology, crystalline phase distribution, and glass-matrix characteristics.

Although RTP values provide information regarding the overall translucency behavior of ceramic materials, evaluation of ΔL values may provide additional insight into how these materials respond to different background colors. The LDS group exhibited significantly higher mean ΔL values than the other groups (p < 0.05). Since ΔL reflects the lightness difference between black and white backgrounds, these findings indicate that ceramic materials responded differently to background color changes. The higher ΔL values observed in the LDS group may indicate greater sensitivity to background-related lightness changes, which could be associated with differences in the microstructure and optical behavior of the material.

Differences in chemical composition and crystalline structure of tested materials may explain the differences in the OP, RTP, ΔL, Δa, and Δb values. All ceramic materials evaluated in the present study were selected from high-translucency ceramics; nevertheless, their RTP and OP values were material-dependent, in agreement with previous studies [5, 8, 23, 24, 35]. Although lithium-based ceramics share the common characteristic of containing a high proportion of lithium-based crystals, variations in crystalline content, crystal morphology, crystal size, and glass-matrix composition may influence light transmission and scattering. The needle-shaped lithium disilicate crystals (1.0–1.5 μm, 70%) are larger than the more spheroidal zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate crystals (0.5–0.7 μm, 58%). Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate also contains dissolved zirconia (1.9%) in a glass matrix [7]. Advanced lithium disilicate ceramics contain rod-shaped submicron-dimension lithium disilicate crystals of 0.5 μm (95%) and virgilite crystals of 0.2–0.3 μm (5%) [5, 13].

When opaque cement was used, the mean ΔE₀₀ value of the LDS group was significantly lower than those of the ZLS-VS and ZLS-CD groups (p < 0.05). In contrast, with translucent cement, ZLS-VS presented significantly higher ΔE₀₀ values than the other groups (p > 0.05). The crystal structure of LDS may have altered light transmission behavior differently compared to the other ceramic groups, resulting in less color change in the presence of opaque cement. In contrast, the more translucent structure of ZLS-VS, when combined with translucent cement, may have reflected the effect of the dark underlying background more clearly than the other ceramics.

In contrast, over the A1 background, no significant differences were detected among the ceramic groups for either cement shade (p > 0.05). Opaque cement exhibited comparable color change between the A1 and A3 backgrounds in all ceramic groups (p > 0.05). When translucent cement was used, comparable color change values were observed between the A1 and A3 backgrounds in all groups (p > 0.05) except the ALD group (p = 0.010). This suggests that darker backgrounds alone may not substantially influence the final color; rather, their effect becomes more pronounced through interaction with ceramic composition and cement shade.

Various techniques have been evaluated for masking the translucent ceramics over dark backgrounds. Pilecco et al. [32] reported that applying opaque stains to the lithium disilicate glass ceramic improved color matching. Soares et al. [36] investigated the masking ability of resin composite layering and ceramic material on discolored substrates and reported that the resin composite layering, ceramic material, and interaction of both factors influenced the color change. Lehr et al. 33 reported comparable masking results with the combined application of opaquer and resin composite over dark backgrounds. The use of white and/or opaque cements is a simple method to mask the darker backgrounds. Using opaque cements represents a simple and clinically applicable method to enhance masking [20, 36, 37]. Cement shade had a significant effect on lithium-based ceramics such as lithium disilicate and zirconia-reinforced lithium (di)silicate, according to previous studies [18, 20, 25, 36]. On the other hand, Yamalı et al. [1] reported that the cement thickness (40-80-120 μm) and cement shade (yellow-clear-white) did not significantly affect the final color of 1.5-mm-thick lithium disilicate anterior crowns. The possible explanation for this difference may be attributed to the higher thickness of the crown. Pala et al. [12] demonstrated that the highest threshold thickness was achieved when a HT ceramic material was used in combination with a transparent cement and that the masking capacity was significantly influenced by the translucency of the ceramic. Yıldırım et al. [21] reported that opaque cement exhibited a statistically significant color change compared to translucent cement on an A1-colored background for lithium silicate (E.max CAD) and zirconia reinforced lithium silicate (Celtra Duo).

In all material and background combinations, the use of opaque cement resulted in significantly higher ΔE₀₀ values than translucent cement. The large effect size observed for cement shade indicates that resin cement optical properties may play a significant role in final color outcomes. Cement shade influenced the final color of thin ceramic specimens with different compositions, which was consistent with previous studies [11, 18, 20]. This finding may be related to opaque cement reducing light transmittance more than translucent cement, thereby masking the underlying background color more effectively and consequently leading to greater color change. The higher filler content of opaque cement may also contribute to this effect by limiting light transmission and increasing light reflection.

When the Δa and Δb values were evaluated, it was observed that darker backgrounds and the use of translucent cement generally shifted the color coordinates toward more negative values in several groups, whereas lighter backgrounds and opaque cement tended to shift them toward more positive values. Opaque cement may have reduced light transmission and increased light reflection, which may have contributed to a warmer optical appearance of the restorations. In contrast, translucent cement may have allowed greater light transmission through the ceramic material and increased the optical influence of the background color, resulting in reduced warm color expression and more negative color coordinate values in some groups. Similarly, lighter backgrounds may have helped preserve warm tones by reflecting more light, whereas darker backgrounds may have increased light absorption and contributed to a cooler optical appearance. Higher ΔL values were generally observed with opaque cement compared to translucent cement, although this effect was not consistent across all material and background combinations. This finding may be associated with the lower light transmittance and higher light reflectivity of opaque cement, which could increase the perceived lightness of the restorations.

Accordingly, the second hypothesis, that ceramic composition, cement shade, and tooth-shaded background influence the final color of the lithium-based ceramics, was partly accepted.

In the measurements with translucent cement, the LDS and ZLS-CD groups over the A1 background showed ΔE₀₀ values below the perceptibility threshold (PT, ΔE00 = 0.81), while over the A3 background, the ALD, LDS, and ZLS-CD groups also remained below the perceptibility threshold (PT, ΔE00 = 0.81). These results indicated that translucent cement induced minimal color change when used with LDS and ZLS-CD ceramics. Because translucent cements influenced the final color slightly, they can be used in clinical situations where no masking or color modification was required with LDS and ZLS-CD ceramics [12]. In agreement with previous studies [20, 22, 36, 37], color change was significantly higher with opaque cement than translucent cement over A3 backgrounds (p < 0.05), which could be related to higher masking ability of opaque cements. In the measurements with opaque cement, all ceramic groups over A1 and A3 backgrounds showed ΔE00 values above the perceptibility threshold (PT, ΔE00 = 0.81), indicating that the color differences could be visually noticed; however, below the acceptability threshold (AT, ΔE00 = 1.77). Therefore, the observed color changes were perceptible yet remained within clinically acceptable limits. While cement shade and background color may influence the final color without causing clinically unacceptable discoloration, these effects may have limited clinical impact under the tested conditions. In the present study, at a ceramic thickness of 1 mm, the masking ability of the ceramic materials may have limited the clinical impact of background color and cement shade differences. These effects may become more clinically pronounced in thinner ceramic restorations with higher translucency. Previous studies have [22, 26] reported that within the 1.0–2.0 mm thickness range, the effect of the cement color on the final color was limited. However, this thickness was within the clinically acceptable range and allowed evaluation of the effect of cement shade on the final restoration color. Previous studies have demonstrated that decreasing ceramic thickness increases translucency and enhances the influence of cement color. According to a study by Gomes et al. [19], although cementation materials caused only slight color changes in lithium disilicate ceramics, ceramic thickness influenced final restoration color in a clinically perceptible manner. Some previous studies reported a threshold for thickness lower than 1.5 mm and that cement shade influences the final color [20, 26]. Even though some results were statistically significant, all ΔE₀₀ values stayed below the clinical acceptability threshold. Therefore, the finding should be interpreted with caution since statistical significance does not always reflect clinical relevance.

In the present study, the ceramic specimens were only polished. Recent studies [38–40] have reported that mechanical polishing is an appropriate finishing procedure for dental ceramics, as it can reduce surface roughness and restore surface smoothness after milling or chairside adjustment. In some ceramic systems, polishing has been shown to provide surface characteristics comparable to glazing. However, selecting an appropriate polishing system for each material is essential. However, glazing is commonly recommended, particularly because it may infiltrate microcracks formed during milling and further reduce surface roughness, potentially influencing color outcomes. Glazing may also affect the optical behavior of ceramic materials by altering light reflection, scattering, and transmission. Smoother surfaces may reduce light scattering and promote more regular light reflection and transmission. This condition may be associated with lower OP values and higher RTP values. As a result, the L*, a*, and b* color coordinates, as well as the overall ΔE00 values, may also be affected. However, it should also be taken into consideration that glaze thickness, application technique, and firing conditions may influence the final color outcomes. Future studies should also compare the effects of polishing and glazing procedures on the optical properties and color outcomes of ceramic materials.

In the present study, the resin cement layer was prepared independently to standardize cement thickness and minimize variability related to cement flow and seating pressure. Since variations in cement thickness may influence optical measurements and color coordinate values, a standardized cement thickness was preferred for all specimens. However, polymerization shrinkage associated with resin-based materials may have affected the specimen dimensions to some extent. In addition, preparing the resin cement layer independently does not completely reproduce clinical intraoral cementation procedures. HF acid application was not performed; therefore, this may be considered a limitation of the present study. Therefore, the findings of the present study should be interpreted within the limitations of this in vitro design. Future studies should evaluate the effects of background substrate and restorative material type using clinically relevant cementation protocols.

One limitation of this study is that color measurements were obtained with a chairside spectrophotometer rather than a laboratory reference spectrophotometric system. Although laboratory-based devices may provide higher absolute measurement accuracy, the use of the same instrument for all specimens and measurement stages enabled standardized relative comparisons among the tested groups. Different outcomes may be observed with different ceramics, substructures, and cements of various shades, thicknesses, and brands. The use of flat specimens, standardized cement thickness, and in vitro conditions without aging also may limit the generalizability of the findings to clinical scenarios. Future studies are recommended to include clinical investigations using anatomical restorations with ceramics, substructures, and cements with different shades, thicknesses, and brands.
