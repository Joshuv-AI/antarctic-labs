In Silico Design and Computational Characterization of Novel Chimeric Multiepitope Antigens for Mpox Serosurveillance: An Immunoinformatics Approach

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe Monkeypox (Mpox) virus is a zoonotic Orthopoxvirus with a global outbreak that began in 2022, with more than 132,000 confirmed cases and 1500 deaths across over 128 countries. The pandemic preparedness pipeline emphasizes the importance of diagnostic surveillance of pathogens in at‐risk populations to monitor transmission and mitigate the impact on public health. Current PCR‐based diagnostics are limited in field‐applicability, creating an urgent need for deployable serosurveillance tools. This is the first study aimed at designing and computationally characterizing novel multiepitope antigens (MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2) for Mpox serodiagnostics using immunoinformatics approaches and combining these with molecular docking to the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM.MethodsLinear B‐epitopes from previously validated Mpox diagnostic proteins were predicted using the BepiPred‐3.0 and LBtope servers. Antigenic epitopes were assembled into two multiepitope constructs using GSGSG linkers. The three‐dimensional structures of both antigens were predicted by AlphaFold2, refined using FG‐MD and GalaxyRefine servers, and validated via ProSA‐web, ERRAT, and Ramachandran analysis. Protein‐protein docking with the Fab regions of human IgA, IgG, and IgM was performed using the ClusPro 2.0 server, and binding affinities were calculated using the PRODIGY server. Molecular dynamics was assessed via Normal Mode Analysis using the iMODS server.ResultsBoth antigens were predicted to be antigenic (VaxiJen scores: 0.61‐0.75; ANTIGENpro scores: 0.95) with favorable physico‐chemical properties (hydrophilic and thermostable). with desirable physicochemical properties. In addition, both antigens also demonstrated stable interactions with the Fab regions of selected immunoglobulins, with several residues interacting at the interfaces of all the docked complexes.ConclusionsThese preliminary findings demonstrate that MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 are promising diagnostic antigens for Mpox serosurveillance. Future work will focus on the expression and serological characterization of both antigens to determine their diagnostic parameters (sensitivity, specificity, and others).Keywords: immunoglobulins, immunoinformatics, Monkeypox virus, MP‐MEDA‐1, MP‐MEDA‐2, multiepitope antigen, serosurveillance

Introduction

Mpox (previously known as monkeypox) is a zoonotic disease caused by the Mpox virus (MPXV), with sporadic outbreaks reported primarily in West and Central Africa [1]. The predominant symptom of MPXV infection is skin rashes (often accompanied by anogenital or oropharyngeal/perioral mucosal lesions) [2]. However, fever, chills, headache, and lymphadenopathy are also commonly reported symptoms in patients [3]. The MPXV consists of 2 genetically distinct clades (clade I and clade II), with clade I (formerly called the Central African clade) infections associated with more severe disease and higher mortality [4, 5]. On the other hand, clade II (formerly called the West African clade) is known to cause less severe disease with lower case fatality rates (CFRs).

Since 2022, two successive Mpox outbreaks have raised unprecedented public health concerns [6]. The 2022 outbreak caused by clade IIb (a subclade of clade II) is estimated to have resulted in more than 100,000 cases and over 220 deaths in 122 countries [7]. Subsequently, in October 2023, a large‐scale mpox outbreak caused by clade I occurred in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [8], and as of 5 January 2025, more than 9500 laboratory‐confirmed cases were reported in the DR Congo, with an estimated CFR of 3.4% [9]. However, the full scope and transmission characteristics of both outbreaks are unknown due to likely underestimation of the number of people infected, limited genomic surveillance, and human movements throughout central Africa [10]. The recent successive outbreaks underscore the virus's pandemic potential and the critical need for robust surveillance infrastructure.

Current diagnostic limitations severely hamper efforts geared toward outbreak preparedness and response. While PCR‐based viral DNA detection remains the gold standard for Mpox diagnosis, the requirement for sophisticated laboratory infrastructure limits field deployment, particularly in resource‐limited endemic regions [11]. Furthermore, genomic surveillance faces several substantial challenges in Africa [12], where limited sequencing capacity allows viral circulation to remain undetected [13]. Additionally, the occurrence of asymptomatic cases (estimated to occur in 1.8%–6.5% of persons with Mpox infection) is reported to contribute to the underestimation of the disease burden and warrants the deployment of seroprevalence serological techniques [14], which enable real‐time population immunity assessment and the identification of circulating strains based on their immunological signatures in longitudinal surveys [15].

Although the detection of viral DNA by PCR is the gold standard to confirm MPXV infection, the sophistication of this technique limits its applicability in field settings [16]. Serological surveillance offers complementary advantages for Mpox monitoring [17]. Antibodies against MPXV can be detected in serum approximately 1–2 weeks post‐symptom onset and can aid in diagnostics under certain circumstances [18]. Nevertheless, different studies have reported the role of IgA, IgG, and IgM as serological markers associated with the acute and post‐acute phases of infection [19, 20, 21]. However, despite several attempts and the urgent need, there are no approved serological assays for routine Mpox surveillance.

Multi‐epitope chimeric antigens represent a promising diagnostic platform, combining multiple B‐cell epitopes into a single construct to enhance sensitivity and specificity. This approach has proven successful for the serodiagnosis of several diseases, including onchocerciasis [22, 23, 24], tuberculosis [25], and Chagas [26, 27]. Though recent computational studies have explored the design of multiepitope vaccine candidates for Mpox [28, 29], the development of multiepitope diagnostic antigens specifically optimized for antibody capture in serosurveillance applications remains unexplored.

This study employed an immunoinformatics approach for the computational design and characterization of two novel multi‐epitope diagnostic antigens (MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2) for Mpox serosurveillance. Linear B‐epitopes from five previously validated diagnostic proteins were integrated into chimeric constructs and comprehensively evaluated for their structural properties, antigenicity, and predicted interactions with human immunoglobulins. The designed antigens could be useful in the development of a One Health approach for Mpox serodiagnosis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Computational Framework

This computational study employed a multi‐step immunoinformatics pipeline to design and characterize multiepitope diagnostic antigens. All analyses utilized publicly available bioinformatics tools and servers with established validation metrics. No statistical hypothesis testing was performed, as validation relied on different scoring functions, structural quality metrics, and consensus predictions across multiple algorithms.

Protein Sequence Retrieval and Preliminary Characterization

Five MPVX proteins from strain Zaire‐96‐I‐16 (B21R, N3R, A30L, E8L, and B18R; UniProtKB accession numbers Q8V4Q7, Q8V4Q2, Q8V4U9, Q8V4Y0, and Q8V4R0, respectively), previously evaluated for diagnostic potential, were retrieved from the UniProtKB database in FASTA format for preliminary analysis as described in earlier studies [30, 31]. Signal peptide prediction was performed using SignalP 6.0 and TOPCONS servers, and transmembrane domain prediction was performed using DeepTMHMM 1.0 and TOPCONS. SignalP 6.0 employs machine‐learning protein language models trained on millions of unannotated sequences across all domains of life to predict all five signal peptide types [32]. DeepTMHMM 1.0 uses a deep learning language model‐based algorithm to accurately identify and predict the topology of both alpha‐helical and beta‐barrel proteins [33]. TOPCONS is a consensus‐based tool for membrane protein topology and signal peptide prediction, offering state‐of‐the‐art performance in topology analysis [34].

Linear B‐Epitope Prediction

Linear B‐cell epitope prediction was carried out using the BepiPred‐3.0 and LBtope web servers. BepiPred‐3.0 is a sequence‐based tool that leverages numerical representations derived from protein language model (LM) embeddings to enhance the accuracy of both linear and conformational B‐cell epitope predictions across multiple independent test sets [35]. Predictions for the protein sequences were performed using the default threshold of 0.1512. In contrast, LBtope employs machine learning techniques trained on a large dataset of experimentally validated B‐cell and non‐B‐cell epitopes. By incorporating diverse features such as binary profiles, dipeptide composition, and amino acid pair (AAP) profiles, LBtope achieves prediction accuracies ranging from approximately 54% to 86% [36].

Epitope Antigenicity Prediction

To design multiepitope chimeric diagnostic antigens for serological studies, the antigenicity of all predicted epitopes was assessed using the VaxiJen v2.0 server. VaxiJen v2.0 is an alignment‐independent method for antigen prediction, relying on the physicochemical properties of peptides and proteins. Its algorithm applies autocross‐covariance (ACC) transformation to convert input peptide sequences into uniform vectors representing principal amino acid features, enabling antigenicity prediction for bacterial, fungal, parasitic, viral, and tumor peptides/proteins [37]. For this analysis, the default threshold of 0.4 under the viral model was used to evaluate each predicted epitope.

Multiepitope Diagnostic Antigen Design

The selected antigenic linear B‐cell epitopes were assembled into two multiepitope diagnostic antigens, designated MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2, using GSGSG flexible linkers. Each construct included a 10×His tag at the N‐terminal region, followed by the HRV 3 C protease cleavage site (LEVLFQ/GP), with individual peptide sequences separated by GSGSG linkers. The GSGSG linker is widely reported to enhance epitope stability and recognition [38] and has been successfully applied in the design of multiepitope diagnostic antigens for various infectious diseases, including onchocerciasis [23, 24], Chagas disease [39], and hepatitis B [40].

Physicochemical Properties and Solubility Prediction

The physicochemical properties of the designed multiepitope diagnostic antigens were evaluated using the ProtParam web server. ProtParam provides key protein parameters such as molecular weight, aliphatic index, theoretical pI, instability index, amino acid composition, and atomic composition. Additionally, it predicts the estimated half‐life of the protein in Escherichia coli, yeast, and mammalian cells [41]. Protein solubility upon bacterial expression was assessed using the NetSolP‐1.0 and DeepSoluE servers. NetSolP‐1.0 employs deep‐learning protein language models to predict solubility and usability directly from sequence data, achieving state‐of‐the‐art performance and outperforming existing in‐silico tools [42]. Conversely, DeepSoluE uses a long short‐term memory (LSTM) network with hybrid features combining physicochemical properties and distributed amino acid representations to predict protein solubility [43].

Multiepitope Antigen Construct Antigenicity Prediction

The antigenicity of the designed multiepitope chimeric antigens was evaluated using VaxiJen v2.0 (as described above) and the ANTIGENpro server from the Scratch Proteomics Predictor platform. Similar to VaxiJen, ANTIGENpro is an alignment‐free, sequence‐based prediction tool trained on a large, non‐redundant dataset derived from protein microarrays, achieving an accuracy of approximately 82% [44].

Secondary Structure and Intrinsic Disorder Prediction

Secondary structure prediction for the designed chimeric antigens was performed using PSIPRED v4.0 and NetSurfP‐3.0. PSIPRED applies two feed‐forward neural networks to analyze outputs from position‐specific iterated BLAST (PSI‐BLAST), providing a simple yet highly accurate approach to secondary structure prediction [45]. The current version (v4.0) achieves a Q3 prediction accuracy of approximately 84.25% [46]. NetSurfP‐3.0, on the other hand, uses convolutional and long short‐term memory neural networks to predict secondary structure, solvent accessibility, disorder, and backbone geometry from protein sequences. By leveraging pretrained protein language models, it delivers cutting‐edge accuracy and improved runtime performance [47, 48].

Given that unfolded proteins represent an important class of structural antigens [30] recognized by antibodies [49], intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in the designed antigens were predicted using AIUPred and flDPnn2. AIUPred is an advanced version of the IUPred server that integrates deep learning into its energy estimation framework for improved performance [50, 51]. Conversely, flDPnn2 employs deep neural networks to provide fast and accurate predictions of intrinsic disorder directly from protein sequences [52].

Solvent Accessibility and Thermal Stability Prediction

Relative solvent accessibility (RSA) for both designed antigens was predicted using the E‐pRSA and DeepREx‐WS servers. E‐pRSA is a novel sequence‐based method that estimates RSA values by leveraging two complementary protein language models (ProtT5 and ESM2) to deliver fast and accurate predictions [53]. DeepREx‐WS, in contrast, provides a multidimensional characterization of exposed and buried residues using sequence information. It employs the DeepREx deep learning framework for two‐class solvent exposure prediction, trained and validated on high‐quality structures from the Protein Data Bank [54].

Since maintaining a cold chain is often critical for biologics used in infectious disease control, the thermal stability of the designed antigens was assessed using DeepSTABp and TemStaPro via the NeuroSnap platform. DeepSTABp predicts protein melting temperature through an end‐to‐end approach that combines transformer‐based protein language models for sequence embedding with advanced deep learning techniques [55]. TemStaPro, on the other hand, uses large‐scale protein language model embeddings to estimate stability above defined temperature thresholds [56].

Tertiary Structure Prediction, Refinement, and Validation

The 3D structures of the designed diagnostic antigens were predicted using ColabFold, an easy‐to‐use platform that integrates AlphaFold2 technology within the Google Colab environment [57]. ColabFold accelerates structure and complex prediction by combining MMseqs. 2's fast homology search with AlphaFold2 or RoseTTAFold [58]. It provides an intuitive interface along with pre‐ and post‐processing steps to simplify protein modeling for users [59].

The predicted structures were refined through a two‐step process. First, refinement was performed using the FG‐MD server, which applies a molecular dynamics‐based algorithm combining the AMBER99 force field with knowledge‐based hydrogen bonding and repulsive potentials. This approach has demonstrated strong performance in atomic‐level refinements in benchmark and CASP experiments [60]. Further refinement was carried out using GalaxyRefine, which rebuilds side chains, repacks them, and relaxes the overall structure via molecular dynamics simulation. GalaxyRefine was ranked among the best methods for improving local structure quality in CASP10 evaluations [61].

The quality of the refined 3D models (MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2) was assessed using ProSA‐web and ERRAT. ProSA detects potential errors in both experimental and theoretical protein structures [62], while ERRAT evaluates non‐bonded atomic interactions against high‐resolution crystallographic standards [63]. Finally, Ramachandran plot analysis was performed using PDBsum to determine residue distribution in allowed and disallowed regions.

Disulfide Engineering of the Multi‐Epitope Vaccine

Disulfide bridges are critical for protein folding, stability, and functions [64]. In this light, engineering new disulfide bonds in protein molecules can enhance overall structural stability or rigidity [65]. In this study, the Disulfide by Design 2.0 (DbD2) webserver was used to engineer disulfide bonds into the designed diagnostic antigens. The server uses an algorithm that focuses on native protein geometry for the prediction of disulfide bond formation [66]. The default parameters were used for the analyses (χ3 angle of −87° to +97°, ±30, and Cα–Cβ–Sγ angles of 114.6°, ±10. In addition, the prediction parameters included the intra‐chain, inter‐chain, and build Cβ for Gly.

Discontinuous B‐Cell Epitope Prediction

Following validation of the 3D structures, discontinuous B‐cell epitopes were predicted using the ElliPro server with default parameters (minimum score: 0.5; maximum distance: 6 Å). ElliPro applies a method that models protein structures as ellipsoids and calculates protrusion indices for residues located outside the ellipsoid. The tool was benchmarked against a dataset of discontinuous epitopes derived from antibody–protein complexes and demonstrated superior performance compared to six other structure‐based prediction methods, achieving an AUC of 0.732 when considering the top prediction for each protein [67].

Protein–Protein Docking, Binding Affinity, and Interaction Analyses

The Fab region structures of human IgA, IgG, and IgM (PDB IDs: 7K75, 7K76, and 1DEE, respectively) were retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Data Bank for protein–protein interaction analysis. The RCSB PDB provides annotated 3D structural data for macromolecules (proteins, nucleic acids) and associated small molecules, supporting global research and education [68, 69]. IgA, IgG, and IgM antibodies are key players in disease progression and have been widely studied in serological assay development [19, 20, 70].

The downloaded structures were cleaned by removing water molecules and extraneous components using PyMOL, a Python‐based molecular graphics tool widely used for 3D visualization, editing, ray tracing, and molecular animations [71]. Protein–protein docking between the designed multiepitope antigens and the selected immunoglobulins was performed using ClusPro 2.0 in “Antibody mode,” with Fab regions as receptors and antigens as ligands. ClusPro ranks among the top docking servers in CAPRI evaluations [72] and employs a three‐step protocol: (i) rigid‐body docking via PIPER, an FFT‐based algorithm sampling billions of conformations [73]; (ii) clustering of the 1000 lowest‐energy structures using RMSD to identify the most probable complexes; and (iii) energy minimization for refinement.

Binding affinities of the predicted complexes were estimated using PRODIGY, which applies a robust model based on intermolecular contacts and non‐interface surface features [74, 75], and AREA‐AFFINITY, which predicts affinities from interface and surface areas of protein–protein complexes [76]. Further interaction analysis was conducted using PDBsum, which generates 2D interaction maps from 3D coordinates [77], and PICKLUSTER in ChimeraX (v1.9), a plug‐in that clusters protein interfaces by distance [78]. Interface clustering was performed with a default cutoff of 5 and a pLDDT threshold of 50.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations

Identifying flexible regions in protein structures is crucial for understanding their biological functions [79]. Normal Mode Analysis (NMA) of the docked antigen–Ig Fab complexes was performed using the iMODS server. iMODS provides multiple representations of effective motions, including vector fields, affine‐model arrows, and modal animations, along with detailed outputs such as mobility profiles (NMA B‐factors), covariance maps, deformability, eigenvalues, and linking matrices [80]. Its predictions have shown strong agreement with targeted molecular dynamics and crystallographic anisotropic displacement studies [81]. This analysis was used to evaluate internal dihedral coordinates and cooperative functional movements of the complexes, predicting parameters such as B‐factors, deformability, eigenvalues, covariance, and elastic network models.

Additionally, solubility and aggregation propensity of each complex were assessed using AGGRESCAN3D v2.0. AGGRESCAN3D integrates 3D structural information to identify solvent‐exposed aggregation‐prone regions by projecting experimentally derived aggregation propensities onto the protein structure [82].

Codon Optimization and In‐Silico Cloning

Since bacterial expression is commonly preferred as the initial approach for protein production [83], the E. coli K12 strain was selected as the host for expressing the multiepitope diagnostic antigens. Reverse translation of protein sequences and codon optimization were performed using the Java Codon Adaptation Tool (JCat). JCat enables codon usage adaptation for any input sequence to match the selected host organism, thereby improving heterologous protein expression [84]. The tool uses an iterative algorithm based on precise mathematical formulations [85] and provides additional metrics such as GC content and codon adaptation index (CAI), both of which influence expression efficiency [86]. For in‐silico cloning, the optimized gene sequences were inserted between the NdeI and XhoI restriction sites in the pET30a+ vector using SnapGene software.

RNA Secondary Structure and Tertiary Structure Prediction

RNA secondary and tertiary structures are known to influence stability, function, protein expression, and protein–RNA interactions [81, 87, 88]. The codon‐optimized DNA sequences for MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 were transcribed into mRNA using the Transcription and Translation Tool. Secondary structure prediction of the mRNA sequences was performed using RNAfold with default parameters. RNAfold predicts minimum free energy (MFE) structures using the Zuker and Stiegler algorithm and calculates equilibrium base‐pairing probabilities via McCaskill's partition function approach [89]. Tertiary structure modeling was carried out using trRosettaRNA, which employs a deep learning‐based pipeline. The process begins with 1D and 2D geometry prediction using a transformer network, followed by energy‐minimized folding to generate the final 3D structure [90].

Results

Protein Retrieval and Preliminary Characterization

The sequences of 5 Mpox virus proteins previously reported to be implicated in Mpox diagnosis, B21R [91], N3R [92], A30L [93], E8L [94], and B18R [94] (having accession numbers Q8V4Q7, Q8V4Q2, Q8V4U9, Q8V4Y0, and Q8V4R0, respectively) were downloaded from the UniProtKB database for preliminary analyses and epitope prediction. Signal peptide analyses predicted the presence of signal peptide sequences only in the B21R and N3R antigens. The signal peptide prediction results from the SignalP 6.0 server correlated with those from the TOPCONS server (Table 1). For TM domain prediction analyses, the TOPCONS server predicted the presence of TM regions in 3 of the 5 antigens, whereas 2 antigens were predicted to contain TM regions on the DeepTMHMM 1.0 server. For the combined prediction, the number of TM domains ranged from 1 − 3. Both servers predicted the presence of TM domains in Q8V4Q7 and Q8V4Y0, whereas Q8V4U9 was predicted to contain TM domains only by the TOPCONS server. However, the servers showed disparities in the number of TM domains for Q8V4Q7, with the DeepTMHMM 1.0 server predicting 3 TM domains in contrast to the 1 TM domain predicted by the TOPCONS server (Table 1).

Design of the Chimeric Multi‐Epitope Diagnostic Antigens

The predicted epitopes with high antigenicity scores (above the default threshold of 0.4) were combined via GSGSG flexible linkers to design two multiepitope chimeric antigens (MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2), with MP‐MEDA‐1 comprising 12 linear B‐cell epitopes and MP‐MEDA‐2 comprising 11 linear B‐cell epitopes. In addition, 10×His tags were added at the N‐terminal regions of both chimeric antigens (to facilitate purification and identification) and separated from the epitopes using the HRV 3C protease sequence (LEVLFQ/GP). In summary, MP‐MEDA‐1 comprises 308 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of 32.6 kDa, whereas MP‐MEDA‐2 comprises 294 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of 30.9 kDa (Figure 1).

Physicochemical Properties and Solubility Analysis

The MP‐MEDA‐1 chimeric antigen, composed of 308 amino acids, was predicted to have a molecular weight (MW) of 32.6 kDa and a theoretical pI of 7.25, indicating that the protein is slightly basic. On the other hand, the MP‐MEDA‐2 antigen, comprising 294 amino acids, was predicted to have a molecular weight of 30.9 kDa and a theoretical pI of 6.08, indicating that the protein is moderately acidic. Both proteins displayed the same predicted half‐life of 30 h in vitro in mammalian reticulocytes, > 20 h in yeast, and > 10 h in vivo in E. coli. The predicted instability index (II) was 30.40 for MP‐MEDA‐1 and 42.32 for MP‐MEDA‐2. These scores classify MP‐MEDA‐1 as stable and MP‐MEDA‐2 as slightly unstable (the threshold score is 40.00). The predicted grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) was −0.585 for MP‐MEDA‐1 and −0.525 for MP‐MEDA‐2, indicating that both proteins are hydrophilic and can interact with water molecules. The predicted aliphatic indices were 59.81 for MP‐MEDA‐1 and 60.99 for MP‐MEDA‐2, suggesting that both proteins have the potential to exhibit thermostability. The suggested thermal stability results are in line with the predicted melting temperature (T m) values of 46.6°C for MP‐MEDA‐1°C and 42.9°C for MP‐MEDA‐2 on the DeepSTABp server. The results from the TemStaPro tool revealed a reduction in thermal stability for both proteins, with the average temperature increasing from 40°C to 65°C, with MP‐MEDA‐2 displaying a better stability profile across the different temperatures (Figure S1, Table S1). The predicted solubility scores were 0.6556 for MP‐MEDA‐2 and 0.5481 for MP‐MEDA‐2 on the NetSolP 1.0 server. In addition, the predicted solubility scores on the DeepSoluE server were 0.7254 for MP‐MEDA‐2 and 0.7953 for MP‐MEDA‐2. The solubility scores predicted by the two servers suggest that both designed chimeric antigens are soluble upon expression in bacteria (Table 3).

Antigenicity and Thermal Stability Prediction

The VaxiJen v2.0 and ANTIGENpro servers were used to predict antigenicity scores for both multiepitope diagnostic antigens. The predicted antigenicity scores were 0.6126 for MP‐MEDA‐1 and 0.7491 for MP‐MEDA‐2 on the VaxiJen v2.0 server. In addition, the predicted antigenicity scores were 0.945750 for MP‐MEDA‐1 and 0.953502 for MP‐MEDA‐2 on the ANTIGENpro server. The predicted scores by both servers suggest that the designed chimeric antigens are antigenic, with MP‐MEDA‐2 having a slightly higher antigenic score than MP‐MEDA‐1 (Table 3).

Secondary Structure, Solvent Accessibility, and Intrinsic Disorder Prediction

The secondary structures of the multi‐epitope antigens were predicted using the PSIPRED and NetSurfP‐3.0. MP‐MEDA‐1 was predicted to consist of 10.0% alpha helices, 19.2% beta strands, and 70.8% coils. Moereover, MP‐MEDA‐2 was predicted to consist of 11.2% alpha helices, 22.5% beta strands, and 66.3% coils (Figure 2). Considering solvent accessibility, MP‐MEDA‐1 was predicted to contain 303 (98.38%) amino acid residues exposed (RSA > = 20%), whereas 5 (1.62%) residues were predicted to be buried (RSA < 20%). In addition, 249 (80.84%) were predicted to be found at interaction sites. For MP‐MEDA‐2, 284 (96.6%) amino acid residues were predicted to be exposed (RSA > = 20%), while 10 (3.4%) residues were predicted to be buried (RSA < 20%). Compared with MP‐MEDA‐1, which has 249 residues (80.84%), MP‐MEDA‐2 was predicted to have fewer residues at interaction sites, with 144 (48.98%) residues predicted to be found at interaction sites (Figure S2A, B, C, and D). Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Following protein–protein docking, molecular dynamics simulation was performed by the iMODs server to investigate the stability of the complexes formed by the interactions between the designed diagnostic antigens and the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM. A significant degree of distortion was observed in all the complexes, with each residue in the complex playing a role in their stability (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12A). The B‐factor plots (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12B) indicate the correlation between the mobility of the docked complex NMA and the PDB score (proportional to the mean RSMD). The predicted eigenvalues for the interactions between MP‐MEDA‐1 and the IgA, IgG, and IgM Fab regions were 5.33247 × 10–7, 8.210414 × 10–6, and 5.940199 × 10–6, respectively (Figures 7, 8, and 9C). For MP‐MEDA‐2, the predicted eigenvalues were 1.028857 × 10–5, 8.0122801 × 10–6, and 7.373463 × 10–6 for the interactions with the IgA, IgG, and IgM Fab regions, respectively (Figures 10, 11, and 12C). These values indicate the energy required to deform the structure. A lower value means easier deformation. Each normal mode of the formed complexes is linked to variance plots that represent both the individual variances (in purple) and the cumulative variances (in green) (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12D). The elastic network maps predicted the intricate connections between pairs of atoms in all the different complexes. The gray dots indicate stiffer regions, whereas the lighter dots indicate more flexible regions (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12E). In addition, the covariance matrix for each of the complexes revealed the patterns between pairs of residues in the dynamic regions of the complexes (related, unrelated, and anti‐related motions in complex structures are represented in blue, red, and white, respectively) (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12F). These results suggest that the designed multiepitope diagnostic antigens can interact with the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM, forming complexes with favourable molecular stability. The results from the AGGRESCAN3D v.2.0 server predicted that all complexes formed between the designed antigens and the selected Ig Fab regions contained significant proportions of soluble and aggregation‐prone residues (Figure S3).

Gene Sequence Codon Optimization and In Silico Restriction Cloning

The Java Codon Adaptation Tool (JCat) server was used to perform codon optimization on the gene sequences encoding for the designed multiepitope diagnostic antigens for expression in the E. coli K12 strain. The codon‐optimized sequences contained 924 nucleotides for MP‐MEDA‐1 and 882 nucleotides for MP‐MEDA‐2. A predicted codon adaptation index (CAI) of 1.0 was predicted for both sequences, whereas GC contents of 49.2% and 51.7% were predicted for MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2, respectively. The GC contents of the codon‐optimized sequences were close to the 50.7% GC content of the E. coli K12 strain, suggesting that both proteins can be suitably expressed in bacteria. Generally, the GC content of bacterial genomes ranges from 25% to 75% [98], and a GC content between 30% and 70% is recommended for optimal protein expression [99]. In addition, the predicted CAI for each codon‐optimized sequence suggests the possibility of high‐level expression in bacteria. Lastly, in‐silico cloning was performed to insert the codon‐optimized sequences for both antigens into the bacterial vector pET30a (+) via SnapGene software v8.0.2 (Figure 13).

Discussion

Given the public health and socioeconomic impact of the last Mpox outbreaks, there has been a consensus that continuous surveillance will be imperative in strengthening the response and mitigating the impact of the outbreak, especially in sub‐Saharan Africa, where the highest numbers of cases have been reported [100]. Although genomic surveillance can generate very valuable data, many African countries lack the capacity to execute large‐scale genomic surveillance studies, and it is likely that the virus is circulating but remains undetected owing to limited genomic surveillance [101, 102]. Serosurveillance studies can provide valuable data for understanding population immunity, tracking viral transmission, and guiding public health decision‐making [103]. A serological assay to assess immunity to Mpox is urgently needed to enhance the current understanding of humoral immune responses to both Mpox infection and vaccination [104]. Multiepitope antigens based on B‐cell epitopes have been proposed as an approach to serodiagnostic test development with increased sensitivity and enhanced specificity [105, 106] and have been tested for several diseases. This project focused on the use of computational strategies to design and characterize two chimeric multiepitope antigens for Mpox serosurveillance (Figure S6).

To design multiepitope diagnostic antigens that can be used for Mpox serosurveillance, five viral proteins previously implicated in the development of diagnostic tools were used to predict linear B‐cell epitopes, and antigenic epitopes were selected to design two antigens (MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2). The designed chimeric antigens were predicted to be antigenic, with MP‐MEDA‐2 predicted to be slightly more antigenic than MP‐MEDA‐1, based on results from the VaxiJen v2.0 and ANTIGENpro servers (Table 2). The antigenicity of both diagnostic antigens supports their potential deployment as tools for Mpox serosurveillance since antigenicity refers to an interaction between antigenic determinants (epitopes) and antibodies or specific T‐cell receptors for an antigen [107]. The antigenicity of the designed chimeric antigens was also supported by the presence of large proportions of exposed residues (and interacting residues), which could interact with antibodies in the development of antibody‐capture tests for serosurveillance [108].

The designed diagnostic antigens were predicted to possess suitable physicochemical properties. The predicted instability index revealed that although MP‐MEDA‐1 was more stable, MP‐MEDA‐2 was slightly unstable since the II (42.32) was still close to the threshold of 40. On the other hand, the GRAVY values and aliphatic index values predicted for both antigens indicated that both diagnostic antigens were hydrophilic and exhibited thermal stability. The hydrophilic nature of both proteins is supported by the large proportions of hydrophilic amino acids present in the sequences (Table 3). In addition, the presence of these hydrophilic amino acids could enhance the stability of antigen–antibody complexes through charge–charge interactions [109]. The physicochemical properties of both designed antigens support their role as tools for Mpox serosurveillance.

The 3D structures of both antigens were predicted, refined, and validated via the ProsA‐web and ERRAT servers. The Z‐scores of the validated 3D structures fell within the range for proteins of comparable sizes predicted through X‐ray crystallography. In addition, the Ramachandran and ERRAT plots suggested a high confidence in the predicted 3D structures. For Ramachandran plots, a high percentage of residues in the most favored regions (> 90%) is generally regarded as a good indicator of high‐quality protein structure [110]. The predicted ERRAT scores also suggested the high quality of the 3D structures of both proteins. ERRAT scores of 80 and above generally suggest accurate predictions [111]. Following structural validation, disulfide engineering was performed to further enhance the structural stability of both antigens. Both MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 were each engineered to contained two intra‐chain disulfide bridges. Since antigen‐specific IgA, IgG, and IgM antibodies have been detected in Mpox‐infected patients [20, 21], the Fab regions of selected immunoglobulins were used to investigate the interactions with the designed antigens. The Fab regions were selected for convenience since they are responsible for antigen recognition through their variable sites [112]. Binding energy, binding affinity, and interaction analyses suggested that MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 formed stable complexes with the Fab region of all the selected immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM), with several residues at the antigen–antibody complex interfaces. From the PRODIGY server, for MP‐MEDA‐1, the lowest ΔG was predicted for the interaction with the Fab region of IgM. In contrast, the lowest Kd (dissociation constant) was predicted for the interaction with the Fab region of IgA. However, the ΔGs for the interactions between IgA and IgM were very similar (Table 4). For MP‐MEDA‐2, the lowest ΔG and Kd values were predicted for the interaction with the Fab region of IgA. Nevertheless, all the complexes formed between the designed antigens and Fab regions of the selected immunoglobulins presented low Kd values, indicating high‐affinity interactions. In ELISA, the affinity of the antigen‐antibody interaction has been reported to be crucial for assay sensitivity and accuracy [113]. Therefore, the high‐affinity interactions predicted between the designed antigen and the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM could translate into enhanced diagnostic parameters for both antigens. NMA analyses were performed to analyze the molecular mobility and comparative deformability of the complexes formed between the designed antigens and the Ig Fab regions. The data obtained (including low eigenvalue values) indicated favorable flexibility of all the docked complexes.

The E. coli system was selected to express the multiepitope subunit diagnostic antigens since it is the most efficient host system for recombinant protein expression [114]. Codon optimization, as well as the predicted MFE for the mRNA secondary structures, supports the possibility of high levels of expression for both proteins. However, mRNA structure prediction yielded structures with low confidence. This implies that the mRNA structures may not be very reliable.

A key limitation of this study is that, thus far, all the data have been generated using computational tools. It would be important for MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 to be expressed and assessed in serological assays to establish them as tools for Mpox serosurveillance. Nevertheless, given the success of this approach in the computational design of diagnostic antigens for other pathogens, the data obtained provide valuable insights into the potential role of the designed diagnostic candidates as serological tools for Mpox control.

Comparison With Existing Mpox Diagnostic Approaches

Current research on Mpox serological assay development has focused primarily on single‐protein antigens. For instance, Yates et al. (2023) developed a high‐throughput multiplex microsphere immunoassay (MIA) using a combination of mpox‐specific peptides and cross‐reactive OPV proteins, resulting in specific MPXV infection detection with 93% sensitivity and 98% specificity [91]. Nevertheless, the sophistication of the assay method limits its applicability in LMIC settings. In addition, Taha et al. (2023) optimized an N3R‐based peptide ELISA test with one peptide combination, achieving up to 86% sensitivity and 90% specificity [92]. However, peptides as diagnostic reagents for ELISA often suffer from poor immobilization and reduced antibody diversity, which can impact assay performance [56]. Our multiepitope approach integrates epitopes from five proteins (B21R, N3R, A30L, E8L, B18R) previously validated for Mpox serodiagnosis, potentially offering broader epitope coverage and enhanced sensitivity compared to single‐protein or peptide formats.

Recent computational studies have focused on the design of multiepitope vaccine candidates incorporating B‐ and T‐cell epitopes with adjuvants [28, 115]. While these vaccine studies focused on the induction of protective immune responses, this study aims to develop diagnostic antigens specifically optimized for robust antibody capture in serological assays. These distinct applications require different design considerations. Our exclusive focus on B‐cell epitopes without immunostimulatory components ensures suitability for diagnostic rather than the immunogenic applications required for vaccine development.

Translational Roadmap

Since all the results reported here are obtained from computational studies, the successful translation of MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 into functional serodiagnostic tools will require a structured, multi‐stage validation pathway. The immediate next efforts will be focused on the production and biochemical characterization of both antigens as recombinant proteins. This will involve expression in a suitable system such as E. coli, followed by purification using Nickel‐immobilized metal affinity chromatography (since each antigen has an N‐terminal 10×His tag) and size‐exclusion chromatography [116]. This will be followed by biochemical characterization through SDS‐PAGE and Western blotting to confirm the purity, size, and identity of both antigens [117]. Subsequently, the antigens must be rigorously evaluated for their serological performance using well‐characterized serum samples. This will entail developing an immunoassay platform, likely an ELISA at this early stage (due to its simplicity and throughput) [118], and testing it against comprehensive serum panels (including samples from confirmed Mpox cases across different phases of infection, if possible, vaccinated individuals, and negative controls) to determine the diagnostic parameters. In addition, it would be important to assess any potential cross‐reactivity of the antigens with samples from persons infected with other orthopoxviruses [104]. Following this preliminary validation, a thorough evaluation of diagnostic performance is essential. This phase will require testing in larger, well‐defined cohorts to definitively determine clinical sensitivity and specificity, with direct comparison to the PCR‐based gold standard. Finally, to ensure real‐world utility, particularly in resource‐limited settings where Mpox is often endemic, the assay format must undergo field testing and optimization. This final stage can assess, among other things, assay stability under field conditions, acceptability, and performance in the intended operational environments. This will ensure that the developed diagnostic tests are not only accurate but also practical and robust for field application.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study, while providing a structured computational approach for the design of diagnostic antigens, has key limitations that must be considered. Firstly, the findings presented here are entirely derived from in silico predictions and, therefore, require rigorous experimental validation. This is because although the bioinformatics tools employed are well‐established and widely used, they cannot guarantee the actual performance of the antigens in wet‐lab testing. Secondly, the analysis of protein dynamics of the antigen‐Fab region interactions, while informative, relied on normal mode analysis rather than more exhaustive atomistic molecular dynamics simulations, which would provide a more detailed picture of conformational flexibility [119]. This is due to the high computational requirements for such experiments, given that both antigens were docked against the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM. Thirdly, a critical gap for diagnostic development is the lack of computational assessment of potential cross‐reactivity with antibodies against other orthopoxviruses, such as vaccinia or cowpox [120], which is essential for ensuring assay specificity. Furthermore, the bacterial expression system proposed in the project for the expression of MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 may not replicate native viral post‐translational modifications, such as glycosylation, which could alter epitope presentation and necessitate alternative eukaryotic expression platforms [121]. Finally, both designed antigens are based on epitopes predicted only from Mpox clade I proteins, and their conservation and diagnostic relevance for the currently circulating clades require verification through analysis of sequences from emergent strains. Future work must therefore focus on experimental validation of these candidates, comprehensive cross‐reactivity testing, and evaluation of their performance in diverse populations and assay formats.

Conclusions

In the last few years, two Mpox outbreaks have occurred, with the virus spreading across more than 100 countries, leading to multiple deaths and causing severe social and economic disruptions on a global scale. In the present study, two multiepitope chimeric antigens (containing linear B‐cell epitopes) were designed using computational techniques. The designed multiepitope diagnostic antigens demonstrated favorable features (including antigenicity, thermal stability, and 3D structural features). In addition, the designed antigens demonstrated stable interactions with the Fab regions of IgA, IgG, and IgM (which have been reported to be involved in Mpox progression and have been assessed in serodiagnosis. From the results obtained, it can be concluded that MP‐MEDA‐1 and MP‐MEDA‐2 demonstrate features that can be further harnessed in the development of diagnostic tools for Mpox serosurveillance. Nevertheless, it is important to validate the computational results obtained from this study in vitro and in vivo experiments.
