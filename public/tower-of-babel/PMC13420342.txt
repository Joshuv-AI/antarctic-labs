Ultrasound‐Assisted Arthroscopic Remplissage
Authors: Kazuma Miyatake, Yohei Kusaba, Hyonmin Choe, Reina Nakamura, Hirokazu Arakawa, Shinya Tsujiku, Yutaka Inaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent anterior shoulder dislocation, especially in cases with an engaging Hill–Sachs lesion, can lead to high recurrence rates following Bankart repair alone. Remplissage, an adjunct technique involving the infraspinatus and teres minor, enhances stability by converting the intra‐articular Hill–Sachs lesion into an extra‐articular defect. However, it is technically demanding and requires precise anatomical identification. We describe an ultrasound‐assisted arthroscopic remplissage technique that enhances procedural accuracy and safety. In this method, ultrasound is used intraoperatively to identify the infraspinatus, teres minor, and the Hill–Sachs lesion. Percutaneous needle placement and anchor insertion are guided under real‐time ultrasound visualization. This approach facilitates accurate fixation using the double‐pulley technique, ensuring optimal tissue compression and stability. Ultrasound complements conventional arthroscopy by enabling soft tissue visualization, guiding safe portal placement, and improving anchor orientation, especially for less‐experienced surgeons. This technique offers a safer and more effective alternative in treating recurrent shoulder instability with engaging Hill–Sachs lesions.

Recurrent anterior shoulder dislocation often occurs owing to trauma or sports activities, leading to functional impairment and decreased quality of life if not treated appropriately. Arthroscopic Bankart repair is widely performed as the first‐choice surgical procedure; however, cases with extensive bone loss reportedly have a high recurrence rate. In cases of Hill–Sachs lesions, Bankart repair alone may not sufficiently reduce the redislocation risk caused by bone defects. 1

The remplissage procedure has gained attention as it can control anterior instability owing to suturing and securing the infraspinatus, teres minor, and joint capsule to the Hill–Sachs lesion on the humeral head. 2 Remplissage is performed together with Bankart repair, providing extra‐articular reinforcement to enhance stability and reduce the recurrence rate. 3

Although remplissage is performed arthroscopically, it is often perceived as technically demanding, and the decision to perform it as an additional procedure depends on the surgeon's experience. Remplissage requires time to learn and is considered challenging, particularly for less‐experienced surgeons. Ensuring an adequate surgical field and improving suturing techniques are essential; thus, new assistive technologies are expected to be beneficial.

Recently, the use of ultrasound to assist arthroscopic procedures has advanced, significantly improving the visualization of soft tissues. 4 , 5 Ultrasound‐assisted technology is useful for real‐time confirmation of soft tissue positioning and reducing the risk of intraoperative damage to critical structures. In remplissage procedures, accurately identifying the infraspinatus and teres minor during surgery enhances the precision of suturing and fixation.

Ultrasound‐assisted arthroscopy complements conventional arthroscopic surgery, enabling safer and more precise procedures. This technique describes a method of ultrasound‐assisted arthroscopic remplissage.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Ultrasonographic Findings

Prior to initiating the surgery, ultrasound scanning is performed. Beneath the deltoid, the rotator cuff comprises the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis, and teres minor muscles, which are attached to the humerus. The infraspinatus inserts primarily into the middle facet of the greater tuberosity, whereas the teres minor inserts mainly into the inferior facet (Figure 1a). Palpating the scapular spine and the posterior angle of the acromion and placing the probe parallel to the scapular spine and slightly inferior to the posterior angle of the acromion allows visualization of a short‐axis view of the glenohumeral joint.

Operative Technique

Initially, the anterior labrum‐inferior glenohumeral ligament complex is released from the glenoid cavity until the 06:00 position. An anchor is inserted into the glenoid cavity, and the suture is placed through the complex without tying the knot. Next, Hill–Sachs remplissage is performed, followed by Bankart repair.

After debriding the subdeltoid bursa, the superficial layers of the infraspinatus and teres minor are exposed under arthroscopy. The Hill–Sachs lesion is confirmed arthroscopically, and the transducer is placed posteriorly to identify the Hill–Sachs lesion on ultrasonography. Ultrasound‐assisted arthroscopic surgery is performed up to the step of confirming the Hill–Sachs lesion and creating the portal, with viewing consistently from the posterior portal. The lesion is visualized in long‐axis mode, ensuring the tip of the scope is in view (Figure 2). While visualizing the infraspinatus and teres minor, a needle is inserted percutaneously into the Hill–Sachs lesion under ultrasound guidance using the crosswise technique (Figure 3). One needle is inserted through the previously created posterolateral portal, whereas the other is introduced slightly superiorly to the posterolateral portal, creating an additional portal.

Ultrasound‐guided needle insertion into the Hill–Sachs lesion in the left shoulder with the patient in the beach‐chair position. Arthroscopic visualization was performed through the posterior viewing portal. While visualizing the infraspinatus and teres minor, a needle is inserted percutaneously into the Hill–Sachs lesion using the crosswise technique under ultrasound guidance. (*, bony depression.)

With both needles in place, a scalpel is inserted along the fiber orientation of the rotator cuff, and a slotted cannula is guided to the Hill–Sachs lesion (Figure 4). Two anchors are inserted beneath the infraspinatus and teres minor through a slotted cannula. Anchor positions are confirmed in a previously debrided bursal area. Each suture is retrieved through separate portals, and the double‐pulley technique is used to secure the joint capsule, infraspinatus, and teres minor to the Hill–Sachs lesion (Figure 5). As the sutures are finally tied, each suture is slid to allow for compression from above while securing the joint capsule and rotator cuff and filling the Hill–Sachs lesion.

Anchor insertion into the Hill–Sachs lesion in the left shoulder with the patient in the beach‐chair position. Arthroscopic visualization was performed through the posterior viewing portal. After inserting 2 needles, an incision is made along the needles. Then, a slotted cannula is inserted into the incision path, and the anchor is placed into the Hill–Sachs lesion.

DISCUSSION

Bankart repair is the standard treatment for recurrent anterior shoulder dislocations. In cases of significant bone loss or for collision athletes, the Latarjet procedure has a lower redislocation rate. 6 Consequently, arthroscopic remplissage is primarily performed by experienced shoulder surgeons. Surgeons less accustomed to arthroscopic remplissage often find it challenging to accurately and efficiently insert the needle into the Hill–Sachs lesion.

Ultrasound‐assisted surgery has recently gained popularity. 4 , 5 , 7 Unlike fluoroscopy, ultrasonography allows soft tissue visualization. For example, ultrasound‐guided portal creation enables the safe identification of nerves. Additionally, ultrasound provides a clear image of bone surfaces, allowing simultaneous visualization of the Hill–Sachs lesion and the infraspinatus and teres minor, facilitating accurate needle insertion. Ultrasound guidance also simplifies the determination of the appropriate anchor placement direction. Performing intra‐articular procedures arthroscopically while monitoring extra‐articular structures in real‐time using ultrasound is crucial to ensure safe and accurate surgery.
