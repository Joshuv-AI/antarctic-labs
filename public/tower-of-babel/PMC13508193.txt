Patient capacity and constraints in managing multimorbidity: a qualitative study in primary care settings in Kerala, India
Authors: Selvarajan Valsa Shiby Kripa, Sandhya Kizhakke Neelamana, Athira Krishnan, Priya Babu, Balasankar Jayapal Mini, Thoniparambil Ravindranathanpillai Lekha, Linju Joseph, Panniyammakal Jeemon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity, the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions, poses significant challenges to patients, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like India. Limited research has been done to understand the patient treatment burden and capacity from India, which is essential to design and implement effective interventions for managing multimorbidity. We explored the barriers and facilitators to patient capacity for managing multimorbidity in primary care settings in Kerala, India.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with patients diagnosed with two or more chronic conditions (n = 42) seeking care in primary care settings in Kerala. Data were analysed thematically using two conceptual frameworks of treatment burden and patient capacity: Burden of Treatment Theory (BoTT) and Cumulative Complexity Model (CuCoM) as theoretical lenses.FindingsOur findings show that the complexity of conditions, financial constraints, and cultural practices were barriers to patient capacity. Facilitators that contributed to patient capacity were social support, accessible healthcare, and a positive attitude towards health. Primary care services provide selective care for patients with multimorbidity, leading to treatment burdens outweighing patient capacity. Financial constraints further impacted participants’ ability to access out-of-pocket medications.ConclusionThis study highlights how the complexity of multimorbidity and financial constraints in primary care settings in Kerala influences patient capacity. Understanding how treatment burden and patient capacity influence patients in India and similar LMIC settings will enable the design of interventions that enhance patient capacity and reduce treatment burden within the cultural and health system context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03402-6.Keywords: Multimorbidity, Treatment burden, Patient capacity, Primary care, Qualitative research

Introduction

Multimorbidity, the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions within an individual, poses significant challenges to healthcare systems and individual well-being worldwide [1, 2]. Globally, the prevalence of multimorbidity is rising, and in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as India, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are significant contributors to mortality and morbidity. The multimorbidity rate varies in India, with the national prevalence reported at 7.2% [3]. However, in state-level studies, the prevalence rate ranges from 28% in Odisha [4] to 45.4% [5] in different districts of Kerala. The growing prevalence impacts society and health systems by affecting the working population and increasing health expenditures [6].

A key concept in understanding the patient experience of multimorbidity is the treatment burden, which is the workload of healthcare for patients and the effects of this on well-being [7, 8]. Based on previous research from high-income countries, people living with multimorbidity often struggle with attending frequent medical appointments, undergoing various tests, and adhering to complex medication regimens, indicating a high treatment burden [2, 9–12] However, there is limited literature from LMICs on treatment burden associated with multimorbidity. A few studies from LMICs highlights important contextual influences such as financial insecurities, restricted access, and lack of opportunities and availability of treatment, which exacerbate the treatment burden [13–16].A recent study from South Africa highlights that many patients face challenges in self-management due to limited health literacy, insufficient support systems, and emotional distress [13].

Kerala, the focus of this study in India, responded to the growing prevalence of NCDs with a series of health reforms in the state [17–20]. Several initiatives were brought about to improve healthcare utilisation in the public health systems in Kerala, such as screening for diabetes and hypertension, provision of free medicines for NCDs, and availability of electronic health records. Additionally, national initiatives such as Ayushman Bharat [21] entailed improvements in comprehensive primary care by setting up health and wellness centres that focus on prevention of NCDs. As part of a large multi-methods project [22–24], we explored patients’ experiences with multimorbidity in managing care within the context of health reforms in Kerala. We found that patients experienced condition-related, health system-related, and self-care-related workload [23].

Appreciation of these patient experiences are important to patients and their carers, as well as for the development of patient-centred interventions for managing MLTCs. To further understand the impact of multimorbidity on patients’ lives, we aimed to explore the patient capacity against the treatment burden. Two conceptual models, the Burden of Treatment Theory (BoTT) and the Cumulative Complexity Model (CuCoM), provide valuable frameworks for understanding treatment burden and the role of patient’s capacity in managing care [12, 24, 25].The BoTT theory posits that improving patients’ ability to manage their care involves strengthening collective capacity, social networks, and social capital. Patient capacity is the available abilities and resources a patient can mobilize to address the demands of healthcare, and their lives placed on them [12, 25]. The CuCoM helps to explain why patients with multimorbidity often struggle with managing their health and advocates for a more holistic, patient-centred approach to care [25]. Previous studies on patient capacity and multimorbidity in Kerala are scarce. Hence, this study addresses this gap by exploring how patients with multimorbidity mobilise their capacities to manage their care.

This study aimed to explore the experiences of patients with multimorbidity regarding management of multimorbidity and related treatment burden. The specific objective was to understand the factors that influence the capacity of patients with multimorbidity in Kerala, India, to navigate and cope with treatment burden.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews among individuals with multimorbidity who visited primary care settings in Kerala, India [11]. A qualitative exploratory descriptive design was deemed appropriate due to the limited existing research on patient capacity among individuals with multimorbidity in India. This design approach allowed for a rich, accessible summary of participants’ accounts in everyday language. The design’s flexibility also supported an iterative process, where emerging findings informed subsequent data collection and analysis.

Settings

The study was carried out in family health centres (FHCs) located in the Northern, Central and Southern districts of Kerala from June 2022 to January 2024.

In 2017, the Government of Kerala initiated the “Aardram Mission” under the broader “Nava Kerala Mission” to enhance the state’s primary healthcare system. As part of this initiative, existing Primary Health Centres (PHCs) were upgraded to FHCs. These revamped facilities offer expanded services, with better staffing, extended outpatient hours, improved laboratory support, and comprehensive care for NCDs such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), mental health conditions, and cancer [26, 27].

We purposively selected upgraded Family Health Centres (FHCs) from northern, central and southern Kerala in collaboration with district programme managers, considering the level of FHC upgradation, NCD burden, urban rural representation, and willingness to participate. From an initial short list of five FHCs per region, the final selection (3 northern, 3 central,4 southern) was guided by geographical representation, logistical feasibility, and the expectation of reaching data saturation.

Study participants, sampling, and recruitment

Our target population for this study was patients with multi-morbidity seeking care in the primary care settings in Kerala. We contacted FHCs and explained the study purpose and enquired about their interest in the study. Interested FHCs were subsequently visited by the researchers to conduct the study. We used purposive sampling to recruit participants who met the eligibility criteria (Table 1). Eligible participants who had visited FHCs for their appointments were given information regarding the study by the nurses at the FHC. Those who expressed interest were recruited either in person or via telephone using contact details provided by staff or patients. Recruitment continued until meaning saturation was achieved. Guided by Hennink et al.‘s [28] distinction between code and meaning saturation, the study followed an iterative process where core themes around challenges in living with and managing multimorbidity were considered saturated after 39 interviews. Three additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation [29, 30], but no new codes emerged, supporting the adequacy of the sample size.

Participant eligibility

Participants were eligible for the study if they:

• Were adults (aged 18 years and above) diagnosed with two or more chronic conditions (multimorbidity).

• Had been seeking care and was on treatment from the primary care settings in Kerala.

• Were able to provide informed consent and participate in an interview either face to face or telephonically.

Participants were excluded if they:

• Had severe cognitive impairments or mental health conditions (such as dementia or severe depression declared by the medical officer in the FHC) that hindered their ability to engage in an in-depth interview.

• Were unwilling or unable to participate in an interview due to time constraints, discomfort, or communication barriers.

Data collection

Data collection was done in two phases (June 2022 to December 2022; November 2023 to January 2024) to allow for iterative data collection and analysis to respond to participants’ insights and explore theoretical areas based on the initial interviews. Our semi-structured topic guide was modified iteratively as data collection progressed [31]. The topic guide for the initial data collection phase broadly explored patients’ experiences of their illness, their efforts for treatment and self-management, and care-seeking at primary care and other health facilities. The topic guide was revised after the initial data collection and preliminary analysis to understand the patient’s capacity to manage their multiple conditions specifically logistical support with appointments and visits, support for navigation of health system and financial constraints and emotional support from family and friends. The guide (see online supplement Box S1) was made by reviewing the relevant literature and discussions with all team members.

Before the interviews, all interested participants received verbal explanation and written information sheets. Participants could choose between face-to-face or telephone interviews and select their preferred language; all opted for Malayalam which is the local language in Kerala. Informed consent for participation and audio recording was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For in-person interviews, written consent was secured; for telephonic interviews, verbal consent was obtained after reading aloud the participant information sheet and was audio-recorded at the start of each call to ensure compliance with ethical standards for remote data collection.Telephone interviews were scheduled at participants’ convenience with confirmation calls, while face-to-face interviews took place privately in FHCs, ensuring confidentiality and anonymity throughout. Participants received a token of appreciation (items worth 500 Rs) for their time. The interviews were conducted by eight researchers (LJ, LTR, AK, SKN, KSVS, PB, BJM), all trained in qualitative methodology with either a public health or sociology background. To ensure consistency among interviewers, they were trained to use topic guides and write field notes. Additionally, debriefing sessions ensured all interviewers were fully informed of the topic guide. All the interviews were audio-taped and lasted for approximately 30–60 min.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed into English by the research team. To ensure accuracy and consistency, one team member (LTR) cross-checked each transcript against the corresponding audio recording to identify and correct any discrepancies. We used a hybrid (abductive) approach to thematic analysis, following the codebook method as described by Braun and Clarke [32].This approach allowed for both deductive coding based on theoretical constructs and inductive coding to capture novel insights emerging from the data. Initially, two researchers (LJ and LTR) developed a coding framework informed by key domains from the Burden of Treatment Theory (BoTT) and the Cumulative Complexity Model (CuCoM), after familiarisation with a subset of transcripts (n = 5). A brief overview of the BoTT and CuCoM domains, highlighting their relevance and role in informing the coding framework, has been included as Box 2 in the supplementary section. At the same time, we remained open to identifying new codes not encompassed by these theoretical frameworks. New codes were defined after discussions among team members.

All anonymised interview transcripts were independently coded in pairs (AK, SKN/KSVS, PB/BJM) using the open-source software Taguette [33]. To ensure reliability the coding teams conducted discussions to ensure consistent understanding and application of the codebook. Additionally, LTR reviewed the coded transcripts enabling analyst triangulation [34], and any discrepancies were resolved through team discussions involving LJ and PJ, resulting in consensus on final coding.

Themes and sub-themes were generated through iterative team discussions, during which related codes were clustered and mapped to domains in the framework. These discussions helped ensure that themes were internally coherent and analytically distinct. Themes were refined to reflect the barriers and facilitators of patient capacity for managing treatment burden and self-management and were supported with illustrative quotes.

We used team-based reflexive practices [35] throughout the analysis to account for researcher positionality and potential biases. The multidisciplinary team comprising public health, clinical medicine, social science, and health systems research backgrounds engaged in regular discussions to challenge assumptions and ensure diverse perspectives shaped the coding and theme development. Analytic memos were used to document evolving interpretations, coding decisions, and methodological reflections [36].

Ethics

The Institutional Ethical Committee approval for the study was obtained from the Sree Chitra Tirunal Institute for Medical Science and Technology, Trivandrum (IEC/1543). All the participants were provided with verbal and documented study information, following which written informed consent was obtained. All data were anonymised before data analysis.

Findings

We interviewed 42 participants (aged 41–82 years) with two or more long-term conditions (Table 2). Of the 42 participants, the mean age of the participants is 61.1 years with a standard deviation of 9.4 years and 24 were females. All participants reported having at least two chronic conditions. Additionally, 33 participants reported three or more chronic conditions. Notably, two participants had six chronic conditions. The most reported long-term conditions were diabetes (37 participants) and hypertension (36 participants). Socio-demographic information

We identified several factors influencing patient capacity to manage their multiple conditions. We organised our findings into two themes, corresponding sub-themes (Fig. 1), and supporting quotes in the manuscript. Additional quotes can be found in the online supplement Table S3.

Barriers and facilitators influencing patient capacity; the balancing act

Barriers

Most participants in this study described experiencing treatment burden and explained factors that hindered their capacity to engage in self-management activities. Barriers were classified as complexities of multiple conditions, cultural practices, work-life dynamics, and financial capacity.

Complexities of multiple conditions

Participants described their illness journeys in detail, and there appeared to be differences in capacities for participants with more than two conditions specifically due to the illness severity, uncertainties, and complexities. For example, participants highlighted how issues relating to knee pain or back pain persisted even after treatment from primary care and how they had to seek care from other forms of treatment, such as Ayurveda or other local practitioners.

“Due to diabetes, I face several physical constraints. Once, I had an episode of hypoglycemia while riding a bike, that made me fall and resulted in an accident. Previously I used to work in a quarry and regularly went to the gym, but nowadays, I feel very unwell and mostly sit while working. For my diabetes, I am consulting a doctor at the FHC, and for my varicose veins, I am seeing a specialist. The varicose veins give me a lot of pain and often lead to wounds that are difficult to heal because of my diabetes.”(P17, A participant in their 50s with two chronic conditions).

Several participants reflected that they found it difficult to manage their multiple conditions compared to the initial phases when dealing with a single long-term condition. They attributed this growing challenge to increased physical limitations and the emotional stress from declining physical abilities. Along with this, they described how having several providers and limited time with each HCP often limited their capacity to understand their medical conditions and treatment plans in their entirety. For example, one participant explained how they had missed a recommended test by the specialist.

“Initially, the doctor told me to check the thyroid, but I didn’t notice that on the OP ticket. Later, I checked in another hospital, and they told me the previous doctor had already asked me to check my thyroid. So, after that, I am consulting for thyroid problems here (FHC) only."(P41, A participant in their 60s with six chronic conditions).

Most participants with two conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension, get their monthly medicines from primary care. However, not all prescribed medications were consistently available for patients with more than two conditions. FHCs occasionally experienced stock shortages, requiring patients to purchase medications from private pharmacies or return to tertiary care centres. This increased travel, costs, and burdens related to remembering, sourcing, and procuring multiple medications from various locations. Arranging the purchase of specific medications from external sources to avoid missed doses adds further burden on patients.

“Also, when medicines were not available in this facility, I used to go to XYZ CHC. For that, I must take a bus from here.” (P36, An older adult (60–65 years old) with two chronic conditions).

Many participants described their challenges with managing their multiple medication regimens and reported being unclear about prescribed drugs, their purpose, and dosage. Memory-related challenges due to polypharmacy and advancing age were also highlighted as reasons for missing medications.

" I take my medicines myself, but I have difficulties in remembering things. So, most of the time, I may take the medicines after the specified time. I have three sons, so there are three daughters-in-law at home. They will remind me to take medicines. But sometimes, even after that, I forget.” (P7, an older adult (60–70 years old) with three chronic conditions).

Participants reported long waiting queues at primary care, resulting in delays in obtaining tokens for consultation. Further, they shared that they had to wait in queues at labs again to get their reports. Some participants, particularly those employed, mentioned that they have had to miss work during hospital visits. This added to the treatment burden, which acts as a barrier to patient capacity.

Cultural practices, and work-life imbalances

Many participants expressed difficulties in changing their dietary patterns, particularly regarding reducing rice consumption and fried snacks. Traditionally, rice and rice-based preparations are a staple diet in Kerala and are generally the most dominant portion in any meal. Additionally, several participants felt that self-management instructions like diet control and physical activity were given regardless of considering the patient’s ability to follow them regularly, necessitating tailored self-management information from the HCPs.

While they adhere to most dietary recommendations, they admit occasional indulgence in sugar products and struggle to control their consumption.

“And the most difficult part is controlling my diet. For lunch I want rice. My doctor advised me to reduce the amount of rice I consume and have more vegetables." (P28, a middle-aged female (40–45 years old) with four chronic conditions)

Participants reported that family members often influenced their decision-making regarding lifestyle changes recommended by healthcare providers. Some participants shared that family preferences limited their ability to follow prescribed dietary modifications. Additionally, many reported challenges in balancing household chores, family responsibilities, and health needs, leaving limited time and energy for their own care.

“My husband, daughter, and I are all on medications. He has been bedridden for a long time but is doing better now, though he has issues with uncontrolled urination. The doctor suggested it might be due to high blood pressure. I make sure everything is prepared for him… It makes me sad, but I must care for them.” (P36, an older adult (60–70 years old) with two chronic conditions).

Similarly, participants who had previously engaged in physical activity shared that job demands frequently interfered with their exercise routines. They explained that busy work schedules disrupted their daily walking routines, leaving little time for physical activity.

“There’s no time for that (physical activity). 9-9.30 I must start working. It’s a small shop that I run. So, if I open at 9-9.30, I close at night at 8-8.30, so I reach home at 9-9.30"(P38, an older adult (60–65 years old) with four chronic conditions).

Financial capacity

Financial constraints were one of the key factors influencing patient’s capacity. Participants described how financial difficulties impacted their decision-making on healthcare access, dictating their treatment choices, and how having multiple conditions increased their financial constraints. Most participants reported receiving treatment at both private hospitals and FHCs. They expressed concerns about the expenses for diagnostic tests and travel to multiple providers.

“We have to look after the expenses for these blood tests and other advised tests. Sugar is tested before and after food, so we must pay for both tests. That doctor will ask to do all the required check-ups. So, all these are expenses."(P35, a participant in their 50 s with four chronic conditions).

Many participants highlighted that the monthly follow-ups at FHCs were difficult as they had to regularly miss work, resulting in loss of pay, which added to the financial burden. They also felt the monthly visits were unnecessary as they hardly noticed any changes in their medicines or doses.

“I have to come here once a month. They will give medication for one month only. On that day, we cannot go for the job, and I lose my daily wage."(P36, an older adult (60–65 years old) with two chronic conditions).

Several participants reported a decline in physical functioning due to multiple health conditions, which impacted their employment. The onset and progression of these ailments drastically reduced their work capacities. Some participants reported an inability to continue working, others shifted to less physically demanding jobs, and some had to quit altogether, further straining their financial stability. This financial insecurity led to significant emotional distress. Additional stress was imparted due to financial dependence on other family members for their livelihood and care.

“It is regarding financial issues. We don’t have much income but a lot of expenses. It is difficult to manage. When I keep thinking about that, I have tension about what we will do and how we will manage.” (P42, a participant in their 50s with two chronic conditions).

Many participants explained that they needed to balance their household expenditures and medical expenses, often leading to treatment discontinuation. Some participants also mentioned that financial constraints impacted self-monitoring at home; for instance, one participant reported difficulty monitoring blood sugar due to a lack of testing strips. The high cost of medications at private pharmacies compounded these financial difficulties, often forcing participants to skip doses. Others sought subsidised options, such as Jan Aushadi, despite their own concerns about medication quality, to maintain consistent intake. Additionally, some participants reported turning to alternative treatments for chronic conditions like persistent pain that failed to resolve through medicines. However, these options also introduced further costs, adding to the overall burden of managing their health.

Facilitators

Interview participants reported several factors that act as facilitators for managing their long-term conditions. These were positive outlooks towards health, social support systems, and accessibility of health facilities.

Positive outlooks toward health

Many participants described how they relied on their faith and prayer to relieve stress and pain. Some others have reported developing a belief or sense of trust in their body’s resilience.

“Then we’ve got a lot of prayers, and we’re controlling it that way. When we engage in prayer, we will not know anything.” (P42, a middle-aged participant(50–55 years) with two chronic conditions).

Many participants reported that over time, they had come to terms with the chronic nature of their conditions, which has led participants to accept the necessity of ongoing medication. Some participants shared experiences of fatal disease outcomes within the family due to chronic conditions and explained how these incidents influenced them in changing their attitude towards their multiple conditions management. Many of our participants found ways that enabled them to make active decisions to live with their conditions and engage in entertainment activities.

“I watch serials(television) to get away from my tension. That doesn’t mean that I am addicted to serials. I don’t spend a lot of time alone, so if there is a situation where I need to stay at home alone, I will go to the house next door.” (P26, a participant in their 50s with three chronic conditions).

Many participants reported adjusting to their multiple chronic conditions took time. However, they tried to know their condition, symptoms, and blood value changes they needed to look out for. These activities made them feel better about their health. Many participants have made significant changes, such as quitting smoking, reducing alcohol intake, adhering to feasible dietary restrictions, and increasing physical activity.

“If someone offers me a cigarette, I’ll say, ‘No, I don’t want one; I’ve quit.’ That’s a decision I have made for myself. I also avoid adding sugar to my tea. If I want tea at a hotel, I’ll ask for tea without sugar. These are all choices we make for ourselves.” (P2, a 71-year-old participant with four chronic conditions).

Several participants made several adjustments over the years to fit their medication routine. Participants reported a range of methods they have adopted for adhering to medications, such as placing medicines in labelled covers, keeping them readily available and visible, separating packages for medicines during travel, etc.

“I have kept my thyroid medicines in the kitchen itself. So early in the morning, I wake up and enter the kitchen, I can see it, and I don’t miss my medicine. I will take one tablet then and there."(P35, a participant in their 50s with four chronic conditions).

Social support systems

Most participants described how their social networks enabled them to manage their chronic conditions. Spouses and other family members were frequently reported as the primary sources of support. Participants explained a range of care areas where this support was evident, such as accompanying them to visit HCPs, sharing the load of communicating with HCPs, and supporting them in self-management activities at home. Several participants shared instances of family members staying with them during hospital admissions, ensuring they were not alone during their care journey. Additionally, other family members often contributed by bringing food or providing other forms of practical support.

“When I fall ill, my wife takes me to hospital. I got admitted to Medical College, N (Government Hospital) three times, and my wife came and stayed as a bystander. My children usually bring some food for us to have at the hospital.” (P2, an older adult (70–75 years old) with four chronic conditions).

Many participants explained that they found people with similar conditions in their religious institutions, among their relatives, and their colleagues. Participants described using these social connections to discuss their conditions and management. Many participants reflected that these conversations with their peers gave them health-related information, dietary tips, and information on current health schemes and benefits such as health insurance cards. Peer influence within social circles (friends and colleagues) and adopting healthy behaviours like morning walks with neighbours and colleagues facilitated patient capacity. Few participants reported that the “Kudumbashree” meetings, a poverty eradication and women empowerment programme implemented by the State Poverty Eradication Mission (SPEM) of the Government of Kerala, also facilitated health-related discussions and information on current health schemes.

“In the church, a lot of people are there to interact. Most of them have these conditions. So, we used to talk about our conditions when we met. Some dietary tips like avoiding rice at night, eating vegetables, etc., are also being discussed."(P39, an older adult (70–75 years old) with four chronic conditions).

Many participants also reported how they became acquainted with healthcare providers at FHCs during the monthly visits. Several participants felt that the knowledge of their medical history and family situations fostered a personal connection with healthcare providers, prompting them to manage their care diligently. Communicating with nurses and pharmacists at FHCs regarding their self-management issues was easier due to the familiarity. Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) are community health workers working within the health system in Kerala, and several participants reported maintaining regular contact with them, thus facilitating health-related information exchange and medication management.

“I had medicines only until yesterday. First, I thought I would ask her (ASHA) to bring. But I couldn’t go and meet her. If I had told her, she would have brought me the medicine."(P27, a participant in their 60s with two chronic conditions).

Accessibility of health facilities

Most participants reported convenience and accessibility as the key reasons for seeking primary care healthcare services. Many cited the geographical proximity of the FHC for choosing to seek care from the FHC. They highlighted advantages such as reduced inconveniences of traveling long distances to seek care and the financial burden of traveling. Participants also described how the accessibility of primary care made it easier for them to adhere to their monthly treatment plans, such as regular follow-up, lab tests for monitoring, and medicines available at FHC.

“We live very near to this centre (FHC). We will come here in the first instance anyway. If we show the doctors here, that will be enough. Only if there are no doctors or they are on leave, we need to go somewhere else. We can walk if needed as this FHC is very near.” (P7, a participant in their 60s with three chronic conditions).

Discussion

Our findings illustrate the barriers and facilitators influencing patients’ capacity to manage multimorbidity in primary care settings in Kerala. However, our analysis indicates that factors diminishing patient capacity—such as the complexities of managing multiple conditions, cultural practices, work-life dynamics, and financial constraints—often outweigh the factors that facilitate patient capacity. This highlights the need for developing interventions that reduce the impact of treatment burden and factors that enhance patient capacity.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies from other LMICs illustrating how the treatment burden was increased due to a lack of free and accessible health services that catered to the needs of patients with multimorbidity, thus increasing the financial constraints [13, 14, 37].Our study further shows that a lack of tailored health information and care for their symptoms led patients with multimorbidity to seek care from alternate healthcare providers, increasing out-of-pocket payments.

Financial constraints emerged as a key factor shaping patients’ healthcare decisions; it compounded the challenges of managing multiple chronic conditions. Diagnostic tests, travel to multiple providers, and frequent follow-ups were cited as significant expenses, with daily wage earners highlighting the loss of income as a major burden, consistent with literature from other LMICs [13, 14, 38, 39].The impact of financial constraints extended to self-monitoring, leading to gaps in care. Declining physical functioning due to multimorbidity further reduces work capacities, forcing some to quit jobs, shift to less demanding roles, or rely entirely on family members for financial support. Emotional distress was another burden, as participants expressed feelings of helplessness and anxiety about their financial stability. In previous literature, multimorbidity is prevalent among individuals from lower socio-economic conditions who often face difficulties in managing their healthcare [39–41]. Existing limited finances and multimorbidity together created a cycle of financial insecurity, reduced access to care, and emotional distress, highlighting the critical need for systemic interventions that address these intersecting challenges in the socio-economic and healthcare realms. Broader solutions that target interventions to improve social determinants of health are crucial to enhancing individuals’ capacity [18]. The Ayushman Bharat initiative, aimed at achieving universal health coverage, has the potential to alleviate the financial burden of individuals with multimorbidity by ensuring access to essential health services without catastrophic expenditures [21].

Seeking alternative treatments for unresolved chronic pain is common. However, they incurred further costs, exacerbating their economic struggles. Patients with long-term conditions in India often seek care from several providers for trade-offs between costs and symptom relief [42–44]. Strengthening primary health care services to make them more functional and accessible has been identified as a key factor in improving health care utilisation and continuity of care particularly for individuals managing multimorbidity [45]. Whilst there have been several changes to the public health system, our findings highlight patients with multimorbidity require tailored patient-centred care in primary care.

Our study reiterates the difficulties patients with chronic conditions face in managing their diet, particularly the consumption of rice. Dietary practices are deeply rooted in cultural contexts, underscoring the need for self-management support and information tailored to these cultural aspects [46]. Further, our study emphasizes how social support offered by family members and peers facilitates the management of multimorbidity. Community action programmes have played a crucial role in empowering patients and caregivers in managing chronic illnesses through education and engagement [45]. Interventions designed to enhance social support [20] by building the capacities of family members and caregivers, coupled with health information and self-management support through peer groups [47–49] may further strengthen patients’ capacity for managing multiple long-term conditions. Our study demonstrated that BoTT and CuCoM are useful frameworks for understanding treatment burden and patient capacity. However, along with the existing domains, it is necessary to incorporate contextual factors such as the availability of health services and the role of culture to fully explain factors affecting patient capacity from Kerala and other similar settings. This study has some limitations. While our study provided a descriptive overview of the patient’s experiences with managing multimorbidity among those seeking care at primary care settings, they may be different from experiences at a community level, particularly in a pluralistic health system such as India where patients often navigate multiple systems of care. Therefore, future studies should consider looking at the patient experiences of treatment burden at the community level specifically. Face-to-face interviews conducted during working hours may have reduced participation from the working population. However, we attempted to mitigate this by offering telephonic interviews and making multiple visits to health centres to enhance inclusivity. As a qualitative study, our focus is on transferability, and the findings highlight challenges that can arise even in a state with primary care system while offering insights for other contexts when considering a multimorbidity perspective in chronic disease management. Additionally, we suggest that future quantitative studies, such as cross-sectional surveys, could help to measure treatment burden and patient capacity at scale and identify patterns across different socio-demographic groups. Such efforts would be instrumental in informing patient-centred interventions and health system responses to multimorbidity in India.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted the multifaceted challenges and facilitators influencing patient capacity for patients with multimorbidity seeking care in the primary settings in Kerala. Factors such as complexities of multiple conditions, including treatment burden, cultural practices, work-life imbalances, and financial constraints, present significant barriers; social support from family, peers, and access to primary care emerge as key facilitators. Our findings suggest that tailored, patient-centred care, incorporating culturally relevant self-management support and health information, is essential for improving outcomes in multimorbidity management. Furthermore, enhancing the capacity of family members and caregivers, alongside peer group support, may strengthen patients’ ability to manage multiple long-term conditions.
