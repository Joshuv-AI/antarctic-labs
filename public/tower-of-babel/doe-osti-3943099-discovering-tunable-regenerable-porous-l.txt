ABSTRACT
Chemical separations consume 10–15% of U.S. energy, with biogas purification requiring material
that provide efficient separation of CH4 from CO2 and removal of dilute contaminants. Current
separation methods suffer from limited selectivity and high regeneration costs. This project
addresses the need for energy-efficient, selective separation technologies by exploring tunable,
regenerable porous liquids (PLs) for biogas upgrading. The project develops a machine learning
framework to predict gas selectivity in PLs and investigates low-energy regeneration pathways via
isostatic compression using a custom high-pressure cell. Experimental challenges in measuring fluid
compressibility led to recommendations for alternative testing methods. Key accomplishments
include development of deep learning models for metal-organic framework (MOF) adsorption
prediction and fine-tuning large language models to design novel PL compositions. The results
establish foundational principles and predictive tools for designing PLs with industrial relevance,
enabling advances in energy-efficient chemical separations and scalable biogas purification
technologies.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Chemical separations represent a significant portion of the United States’ energy consumption, with
biogas purification posing particular challenges due to the presence of dilute, corrosive contaminants
such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). Traditional separation technologies often
suffer from limited selectivity, high regeneration energy demands, and undesirable byproducts,
limiting their effectiveness for biogas upgrading.
This project explores porous liquids (PLs), an emerging class of materials combining permanent
porosity with liquid fluidity, offering tunable and regenerable gas separation capabilities. The
research focuses on developing a data-driven machine learning framework to predict gas selectivity
in PLs, specifically targeting methane (CH₄) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) separation relevant to biogas
purification. Concurrently, the project investigates low-energy regeneration mechanisms through
isostatic compression using a custom-designed high-pressure cell.
Key accomplishments include the synthesis of methane-selective metal-organic frameworks (MOFs),
development of deep learning surrogate models for MOF adsorption prediction, and fine-tuning of
large language models (LLMs) to design novel PL compositions. Experimental efforts to measure
fluid compressibility revealed challenges with the initial pressure vessel design, leading to
recommendations for alternative testing approaches to improve measurement accuracy.
The outcomes provide fundamental chemical insights and predictive tools for rational design of
tunable, regenerable porous liquids, advancing energy-efficient chemical separations with potential
industrial impact in biogas upgrading and beyond.

1. INTRODUCTION
Chemical separations are a critical component of the United States’ energy infrastructure, accounting
for approximately 10–15% of the nation’s total energy consumption.1 Among these, the purification
and upgrading of biogas streams present unique challenges due to the presence of dilute, caustic
contaminants such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), carbon and sulfur oxides
(SOₓ) at parts-per-million (ppm) levels and the requirement to shift the methane (CH4) and carbon
dioxide (CO2) ratio. Effective removal of these contaminants is essential to enable safe grid
injection, prevent corrosion, and produce high-quality biofuels. Conventional separation
technologies, including aqueous amine scrubbing and solid sorbents like activated carbon, often
suffer from limited selectivity, high regeneration energy demands, and the generation of undesirable
byproducts such as oxygen, which restrict their applicability in biogas upgrading.
Porous liquids (PLs) represent an emerging class of materials that combine the permanent porosity
characteristic of porous solids with the fluidity of liquids.2 These materials are typically composed of
discrete porous hosts, such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), suspended in solvents whose
molecular size sterically excludes them from entering the host’s internal pore space, thereby
preserving permanent porosity in the liquid phase.3 PLs offer the potential for highly selective gas
separations with tunable chemical and structural properties,4 alongside advantages in processability
and regeneration not accessible to traditional solid adsorbents.5
Despite the promise of PLs, the vast combinatorial space of possible solvent-host mixtures renders
empirical screening approaches infeasible. Furthermore, the fundamental mechanisms governing gas
selectivity and regeneration in PLs remain insufficiently understood, particularly for challenging
separations such as H₂S removal from biogas. Addressing these gaps requires an integrated
approach combining computational modeling, machine learning, and experimental validation.
This project undertakes a one-year pilot study to develop a data-driven machine learning framework
to predict gas selectivity in PLs, focusing on CH₄/CO2 separation as a representative biogas
purification challenge. Concurrently, the project explores low-energy regeneration pathways via
isostatic compression, employing a custom-designed high-pressure cell to investigate pore collapse-
induced gas desorption mechanisms.
The outcomes of this study aim to establish fundamental chemical principles and predictive
capabilities for the rational design of tunable, regenerable PLs with industrial relevance for energy-
efficient chemical separations.

4.1. Predicting Adsorptivity of Metal-Organic Frameworks using Deep Learning
Full Reference: Montes de Oca Zapiain, D., Robinson Brown, D.C., Bobbitt, N.S., Kramer, S.,
Rimsza, J.M., and Nenoff, T.M., “Predicting Adsorptivity of Metal-Organic Frames using Deep
Learning” Separation and Purification Technology (2026) – in prep
Full Abstract: Herein, we utilize a combination of supervised and unsupervised learning to develop
an accurate and computationally efficient surrogate model to predict adsorption of N2 across MOF
structures. First, we leverage unsupervised learning techniques to distill a new, low-dimensional set
of features that capture the structural complexity of MOFs address to enable the design and
predictive adsorption of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). MOFs have complex hierarchical
structures that form materials with high porosity and internal surface area, resulting in widespread
applications in gas separation, sensing, and selectivity. Developing a fundamental understanding of
the relationships that exist between the MOF structure and composition and its corresponding
performance is of paramount importance to accelerate MOF design and tailoring for emerging
separation needs. Despite recent successes in leveraging machine learning to extract usable
knowledge from existing results or pre-compiled MOF databases, development of a robust and low-
dimensional MOF representation that integrates the atomistic, structural, chemical, and other
descriptors needed to characterize the MOF structure is still needed. This gap is addressed by
utilizing unsupervised learning techniques to distill a new, low-dimensional representation that
captures the hierarchically complex structure of MOFs. The validity and usefulness of the proposed
low-dimensional representation is demonstrated by using these features as inputs for an accurate and
computationally efficient deep learning base model (i.e., supervised learning technique) to predict
adsorption of N2 across MOF structures.

4.2. Fine-Tuning LLMs to Design Novel Porous Liquid Compositions for Enhanced Biogas
 Separations
Full Reference: Robinson Brown, D.C., Montes de Oca Zapiain, D., Root, H.D., Nenoff, T.M.,
and Rimsza, J.M. "Fine-Tuning LLMs to Design Novel Porous Liquid Compositions for Enhanced
Biogas Separations" Separations and Purification Technology (2026) – in prep
Full Abstract: Porous liquids (PLs) are a class of liquids and colloids with permanent internal
porosity for selective gas sorption. Often, PLs are composed of porous nanoparticle sorbents like
metal organic frameworks (MOFs) suspended in a sterically or chemically excluded solvent. Until
recently, materials design for PLs has relied on broad heuristics and a trial-and-error approach to
discover novel compositions with high selectivity. In the present work, large language models
(LLMs) and cheminformatic methods were leveraged to streamline the discovery of novel PL
compositions with emergent CO2-selectivity for biogas separations applications. The lightweight
version of Meta’s Llama 3.2 with one billion parameters was trained on the hypothetical MOF
(hMOF) database using the low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning approach to predict the utility
of a given MOF for biogas separations. Specifically, MOFs with predicted CO2/CH4 selectivity
greater than 5 were deemed industrially viable to scrub CO2 from biogas. Then, a set of 86,000
solvent candidate molecules were scraped from the PubChem chemical information database. A
Python-based cheminformatics library (RDKit) was used to rapidly estimate the radius of gyration
distribution, P(Rg), for the proposed solvents. By searching for solvents with minimal overlap
between P(Rg) and MOF pore window size (the pore limiting diameter), the possible solvents were

reduced from tens of thousands to tens of candidate solvents. Further solvent down-select used
solvent properties (e.g., boiling and melting point temperatures), solvent-sorbent compatibility (e.g.,
solubility and reactivity), and solvent cost to generate an experimentally testable set of tens of
solvent-sorbent pairs. This high throughput, in silico materials discovery workflow will accelerate
materials discovery for PLs with emergent gas separations potential and commercial viability.

5. PRESSURE TESTING

5.1. Introduction
The Structural Mechanics Laboratory was first tasked with providing a measurement of the
compressibility of a fluid. Progress was made with the original assembly but ultimately was decided
to abandon this approach in favor of a different method. These alternative options were not
investigated due to lack of funding. This memo serves as an account of the work performed
including findings and complications, as well as a review of recommended alternatives.

5.2. Test Setup and Methodology
Our goal was to measure the compressibility of porous liquids to find the pressure level where a
MOF captures a target molecule or compound, and if the compound can be released upon the
removal of pressure. A first approach in the Structural Mechanics Laboratory experimental work
package number SML4606 created a custom pressure vessel assembly. Figure 5-1 shows this design
and cutaway of the chamber. The load required to press in the top plunger would be recorded by a
load frame while the displacement required to do so would be measured using linear variable
differential transducers (LVDTs) around the exterior of the vessel. The simplified volume would
provide an easy way to convert the load recorded into pressure and the displacement into a change
in volume.

Therefore, the assembly was modified to ensure a proper seal. The specific seal (American High

Without the simple cylinder of fluid but rather the fluid in the cylindrical section and some
nontrivial amount of fluid around the seal, the original derivation relating the bulk modulus to
just force, area, measured plunger displacement, and original height of fluid is no longer valid.
Returning to the original equation for bulk modulus shows that the change in volume could still
come from the change of the plunger motion, but the original volume must be known. Again,
this initial volume measurement was complicated by the revisions to the design of the pressure
vessel. The only method to find the quantity of fluid in the vessel was determined to be through
an accurate weight measurement and using density to find the original volume of working fluid.

5.3. Test Results and Conclusions
The first task was to evaluate the new seal design for leaks. Leak tests were conducted with a twin
Teledyne Isco 100D syringe pump in building 860, room 126. The pump applied 10 ksi to check for
leaks by monitoring flow rate and pressure rise; high flow with minimal pressure increase would

 17

indicate leaks. Although no data was recorded, initial checks confirmed the new design was leak-free
at 10 ksi.
Next steps involved measuring the quantity of fluid added to the assembly. Numerous setbacks were
experienced at this point, with the assembly having inconsistent fills. Multiple attempts shown in
Figure 5-3 were used to hold the plunger at a fixed height and therefore set an initial volume, but the
quantity of water added did not correspond to these fixed volumes. The considerable overall weight
of the assembly complicated using small variations in weight to find the amount of water added.
Typical scales of that range did not provide the precision necessary to measure the difference in
microliters in fill amounts for such a small fluid volume relative to the large weight of the assembly.

 Figure 5-3: Fill method with syringe pump

Issues with the original assembly led to difficulties with consistent filling, apart from the ability
to measure the fluid in the assembly, which was designed with one single fill port on top that was
the only inlet to both fill with fluid and bleed air. While a syringe pump could be used to fill and
bleed using a large volume of water and repetitive air bleeding cycles to check for leaks, this could
not be used for the porous liquids of interest because the synthesis of fluid could only be done
in small volumes. However, a successful test of bulk modulus of water seen in Figure 5-4 was
performed using this large-volume fill process.

Data from load frame experiments is included in Figure 5-5 from when the assembly was filled and
placed in the load frame to examine the compressibility of water. These tests were performed after
the filling process to compare to expected literature values and prove the assembly would perform.
Unfortunately, the initial volume of fluid could not be accurately measured, and thus the data of

the assembly was removed after the experiment. The presence of air shows in the long lag of
compressive load before the rising linear portion of the curve. Ultimately, presence of air in the
assembly prevented consistent filling and measurement of bulk modulus or compressibility. This led
to the recommendation to abandon this approach to testing the compressibility of liquids.

5.4. Recommendations and Future Work
 The successful measurement of water bulk modulus using a large-volume syringe pump as
the pressure application method and with a fill method that ensured no entrained air in a chamber to
account known volume changes, inspired two alternative methods that could be used to perform the
compressibility measurement. Both alternative methods use a small-volume, high-pressure syringe, but
one approach uses a syringe pump, and the other approach would use a load frame to apply
force/pressure. While the filling and bleed process needs development, the simpler volume of the
syringe should allow for easier removal of air from the working volume. Some syringe pumps are
unable to reach 10 ksi pressures (which depends on the area of the syringe cylinder) so a load frame
could be a viable alternative. However, it would also require additional fixturing to place the syringe
in the load frame and align it to the actuator axis. A calibrated pressure transducer could also be
used to directly measure the pressure rise. Both alternative methods address the filling and bleeding
of air, simpler measurement of initial fluid volumes with a smaller mass syringe, simple measurement
of volume change, and reliable application of force that does not require large volumes of liquid.
