Finite-State Transducers in the Wheeler Setting
Giovanna D’Agostino, Andrea Paradiso

Abstract




1
Introduction

In this work we take a preliminary step toward understanding the connections between Wheeler automata and finite-state transducers.

Wheeler graphs form a class of edge-labeled directed graphs that admit a sorting of their nodes that is consistent with the co-lexicographic order of the labels of paths ending at them. A finite-state automaton whose underlying graph is a Wheeler graph is called a Wheeler automaton, and the languages recognized by such automata are called Wheeler languages
[
1
]
. Wheeler languages form a proper subclass of the regular languages; in fact, they are a subclass of the star-free languages. They have attracted considerable interest thanks to structural properties enabling highly efficient indexing, pattern matching and compression
[
4
,
2
,
7
]
. They provide a formal automata-theoretic foundation for data structures inspired by the Burrows–Wheeler transform, bridging language theory and practical text indexing.
For this reason, Wheeler languages are important both as a mathematically natural subclass of regular languages and as a model for highly efficient algorithms on strings, graphs, and automata.

A natural way to deepen our understanding of a language class is to study the transformations that preserve it, either under direct image or under inverse image. This perspective has proved fruitful in the regular setting, where rational functions definable by finite-state sequential transducers (FSTs,
[
6
]
) are known to preserve regularity under both images and inverse images, and where algebraic tools, such as syntactic invariants associated with transductions, provide structural information about the transformations involved. Sequential transducers are a fundamental model for deterministic input-to-output transformations, capturing computations in which the output is produced online while the input is read from left to right.
They are important both theoretically, as a well-behaved subclass of finite-state transductions, and practically, as efficient models for tasks such as text processing, coding, normalization, and stream transformation (see
[
8
]
for a recent survey).

Identifying classes of transducers that are compatible with the Wheeler structure may therefore help clarify the boundaries of Wheeler languages and provide a more dynamic, transformation-oriented view of this class. In this work we define a subclass of sequential transducers and argue that
it is
the natural class of transducers to consider in the setting of Wheeler languages.

Preliminaries

We first fix some conventions before introducing Wheeler transducers in detail. Throughout the paper, whenever an alphabet
\Sigma
is given, it is assumed to be equipped with an order denoted by
\prec
. Moreover, given two strings
\alpha=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n}
and
\beta=b_{1}b_{2}\dots b_{m}
in
\Sigma^{*}
, their co-lexicographic order is defined by overloading the symbol
\prec
:

\displaystyle\alpha\prec\beta\iff

\displaystyle\bigl(n<m\land(\forall j<n)(a_{n-j}=b_{m-j})\bigr)\lor

\displaystyle(\exists i)(a_{n-i}\prec b_{m-i}\land(\forall j<i)(a_{n-j}=b_{m-j}))

We assume that the notions of deterministic (DFA)/nondeterministic (NFA) automata are known to the reader. Given a deterministic finite-state automaton
A=(Q,s,\Sigma,\delta,F)
over an ordered alphabet
(\Sigma,\prec)
, a natural partial order on its states can be defined in terms of the strings reaching them. For each state
q\in Q
, let
I_{q}=\{\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\delta(s,\alpha)=q\}
denote the set of strings reaching
q
. The co-lexicographic partial order
<_{A}
on the set of states of
A
is defined as follows: for states
p,q\in Q
, we set

p<_{A}q\iff\forall\alpha\in I_{p}\forall\beta\in I_{q}(\alpha\prec\beta)

A
Wheeler automaton
is then defined as a deterministic finite-state automaton in which the order
<_{A}
is a total order

We next consider the transducer notion. A one-way
subsequential transducer
(which we simply call a transducer in this paper) computes a partial function from
\Sigma^{*}
to
\Gamma^{*}
, where
\Sigma

\Gamma

It is defined as a tuple

1.

is the underlying DFA,

2.

\omega:Q\times\Sigma\to\Gamma^{*}
,

3.

is the terminal function, which associates with each final state a string to be concatenated to the output produced while reading an accepted input string.

We call
\delta
and
\omega
the

and

transition functions, respectively, and we extend them as usual to
Q\times\Sigma^{*}

Given a transducer

the partial function

is accepted by the underlying DFA

(see

3
Wheeler Transducers

We introduce the notion of deterministic finite-state Wheeler transducers and study their fundamental properties. We first show that the class is closed under composition, and we also prove that Wheeler languages are closed under inverse images of Wheeler transductions. We then turn to minimization: building on Choffrut’s theory of minimal sequential transducers
[
3
]
, we introduce a refinement of the syntactic Choffrut equivalence, denoted
\sim_{f}^{c}
, and show that it characterizes exactly the functions realizable by a Wheeler transducer. Finally, we provide a machine-independent characterization of functions computed by Wheeler transducers in terms of monotonicity.
All proofs are given in full detail in the appendix.

We first present our proposal for the class of Wheeler transducers.

Definition 1

A transducer
T=(Q,s,F,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,\omega,t)
is said to be
a Wheeler transducer if, denoting by
<_{\mathrm{in}}
the input order

<_{A_{T}}
on
Q
induced by the underlying DFA

A_{T}=(Q,s,F,\Sigma,\delta)
, the following conditions hold:

1.

the order
<_{\mathrm{in}}
is total on
Q
;

2.

for every state
q\in Q
, the function
\omega(\alpha)=\omega(s,\alpha)

is monotone, either increasing or decreasing, on
I_{q}
.

A state
q
in a Wheeler transducer is called
increasing
or
decreasing
according to the second condition above.

An example of a Wheeler transducer is shown in Fig.
1
, where the
co-lex order on states is
q_{0}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{1}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{2}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{5}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{3}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{6}<_{\mathrm{in}}q_{4}
(a total order) and one can verify that condition 2 of Definition 1 is satisfied because, for each state
q
, the restriction of
\omega(\alpha)
to
I_{q}
is monotone
(in some cases both increasing and decreasing, since
I_{q}
is a singleton).
For instance,
\omega(\alpha)
is decreasing on
I_{q_{5}}
.

Remark 1

Given a DFA
D=(Q,s,F,\Sigma,\delta)
we define the
identity transducer
Id_{D}

based on
D
as follows:

Id_{D}=(Q,s,F,\Sigma,\Sigma,\delta,Id,t),

where
t(q)=\epsilon
, for all
q\in F
.
Then one can easily check that
D
is a Wheeler DFA iff
Id_{D}
is a Wheeler transducer.

First, we prove that the class of Wheeler transducers is closed under composition:

Lemma 1

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two Wheeler transducers. Then the composition
\|T_{2}\|\circ\|T_{1}\|
is realized by a Wheeler transducer.

Proof

The sequential transducer
T_{2}\circ T_{1}
realizing the composizion
\|T_{2}\|\circ\|T_{1}\|
is defined, as in the case of non Wheeler transducer, as follow:

•

the set of states is
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
;

•

the initial state is
(s_{1},s_{2})
;

•

the set of final states is
F_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}=\{(q,r):q\in F_{1}\wedge\delta_{2}(r,t_{1}(q))\in F_{2}\}
;

•

the input transition function is
\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((q,r),a)=(\delta_{1}(q,a),\delta_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,a)))
;

•

the output transition function is
\omega_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((q,r),a)=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,a))
;

•

the terminal function is
t_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}(q,r)=\omega_{2}(r,t_{1}(q))t_{2}(\delta_{2}(r,t_{1}(q)))
.

Then, by induction on the length
|\alpha|
of
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
, it is possible to check that the following holds:

\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((q,r),\alpha)=(\delta_{1}(q,\alpha),\delta_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha))).

(1)

As in the non Wheeler case we can also check that

||T_{2}\circ T_{1}||=||T_{2}||\circ||T_{1}||
, as proved below.
On the one hand, by putting
q_{\alpha}=\delta_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)
and
\gamma_{\alpha}=\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)
, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha\in\operatorname{Dom}(\|T_{2}\|\circ\|T_{1}\|)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\alpha\in\operatorname{Dom}(\|T_{1}\|)\ \wedge\ \|T_{1}\|(\alpha)\in\operatorname{Dom}(\|T_{2}\|)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q_{\alpha}\in F_{1}\ \wedge\ \delta_{2}\bigl(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}t_{1}(q_{\alpha})\bigr)\in F_{2}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\in\operatorname{Dom}(\|T_{2}\circ T_{1}\|)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha)\in F_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\bigl(q_{\alpha},\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha})\bigr)\in F_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q_{\alpha}\in F_{1}\ \wedge\ \delta_{2}\bigl(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha})\bigr)\in F_{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q_{\alpha}\in F_{1}\ \wedge\ \delta_{2}\bigl(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}t_{1}(q_{\alpha})\bigr)\in F_{2}.

Hence, the domains of the functions
||T_{1}||\circ||T_{2}||
and
||T_{1}\circ T_{2}||
are equal.

We are left to prove that, if
\alpha\in Dom(||T_{1}\circ T_{2}||)
, then

||T_{1}\circ T_{2}||(\alpha)=||T_{1}||\circ||T_{2}||(\alpha).

First notice that, by definition, we have

\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha)=(q_{\alpha},\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}))

hence, by definition of
t_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}
, it holds

t_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}(\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha))=\omega_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))t_{2}(\delta_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))).

It follows

||T_{2}\circ T_{1}||(\alpha)=\omega_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha)t_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}(\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha))=

\omega_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha})\omega_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))t_{2}(\delta_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha})))

Noticing that

\omega_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha})\omega_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))=\omega_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))=\omega_{2}(s_{2},||T_{1}||(\alpha))

and

\delta_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}),t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))=\delta_{2}(s_{2},\gamma_{\alpha}t_{1}(q_{\alpha}))=\delta_{2}(s_{2},||T_{1}||(\alpha))

we obtain

\displaystyle||T_{2}\circ T_{1}||(\alpha)=\omega_{2}(s_{2},||T_{1}||(\alpha))t_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},||T_{1}||(\alpha)))

\displaystyle=||T_{2}||(||T_{1}||(\alpha))=

\displaystyle=||T_{2}||\circ||T_{1}||(\alpha).

Finally, we have to check that the transducer
T_{2}\circ T_{1}
is Wheeler. We start proving that the partial order
<
defined on
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
by

(q,r)<(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}

is total. In fact, we prove that this order coincides with the order
<_{mon}
on
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
defined by

\displaystyle(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(q<_{in}q^{\prime})~~\lor

\displaystyle[q=q^{\prime}\land(q\in Q^{+}\implies r<_{in}r^{\prime})\land(q\in Q^{-}\implies r>_{in}r^{\prime})]

(where we use the same symbol
<_{in}
both for the Wheeler order on
Q_{1}
and the Wheeler order on
Q_{2}
, and
Q^{+},Q^{-}
denote the set of states where
\omega(\alpha)
is monotone increasing and decreasing over
I_{q}
, respectively ).

Let us first prove that the relation
<_{mon}
is a total order: anti-symmetric, transitive and total.

We begin by proving that the relation
<_{mon}
is anti-symmetric. If
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, we prove that

(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r)
does not hold. This is clear if
q<_{in}q^{\prime}
, while if
q=q^{\prime}
and
q\in Q^{+}
then, by definition of
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, we have
r<_{in}r^{\prime}
which leads to
\lnot((q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r))
. Symmetrically, if
q\in Q^{-}
, then we have that
r>_{in}r^{\prime}
which implies that
\lnot((q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r))
.

For transitivity, if
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
, we want to prove that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
. If
q<_{in}q^{\prime}\leq_{in}q^{\prime\prime}
or
q\leq_{in}q^{\prime}<_{in}q^{\prime\prime}
then
q<_{in}q^{\prime\prime}
and
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
follows. If
q=q^{\prime}=q^{\prime\prime}
and
q\in Q^{+}
, then
r<_{in}r^{\prime}<_{in}r^{\prime\prime}
which leads to
r<_{in}r^{\prime\prime}
and
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
; if
q\in Q^{-}
, then
r^{\prime\prime}<_{in}r^{\prime}<_{in}r
which leads to
r^{\prime\prime}<_{in}r
and
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
.

The remaining property to prove is that the order is total. Given
(q,r)
and
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
such that
(q,r)\not=(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
we want to prove that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime}r^{\prime})
or
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r)
hold. Due to the fact that
T_{1}
is a wheeler transducer,
(Q_{1},<_{in})
is a total order, hence we have
q<_{in}q^{\prime}
,
q^{\prime}<_{in}q
, or
q=q^{\prime}
. The first two cases leads to
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime}r^{\prime})
or
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r)
. If
q=q^{\prime}
, then suppose
q\in Q^{+}
(the case for
q\in Q^{-}
is symmetric). Since
(q,r)\not=(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
we have
r\not=r^{\prime}
and, since, due to Wheelerness of
T_{2}
, the order
(Q_{2},<_{in})
is total, then either
r<_{in}r^{\prime}
or
r^{\prime}<_{in}r
hold, leading to
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
or
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})<_{mon}(q,r)
.

Now we can prove that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\implies I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}
.
First of all, observe that by
1
we have

\alpha\in I_{(q,r)}\Leftrightarrow\alpha\in I_{q}\land\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\in I_{r}.

(2)

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\in I_{(q,r)}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\delta_{T_{2}\circ T_{1}}((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha)=(q,r)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\delta_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)=q\ \wedge\ \delta_{2}\bigl(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\bigr)=r

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\alpha\in I_{q}\ \wedge\ \omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\in I_{r}.

Consider
(q,r),(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
such that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
. Let’s first suppose that
q\neq q^{\prime}
. Then, we have
q<_{in}q^{\prime}
, that is
I_{q}\prec I_{q^{\prime}}
and

\alpha\in I_{(q,r)}\land\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}\Rightarrow\alpha\in I_{q}\land\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{q^{\prime}}\Rightarrow\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}.

It follows that
q\neq q^{\prime}
implies
I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}
.

Next, suppose
q=q^{\prime}
and
q\in Q^{+}
; then we have
r\neq r^{\prime}
, and, by definition of
<_{mon}
, this implies
r<_{in}r^{\prime}
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\alpha\in I_{(q,r)}\ \wedge\ \alpha^{\prime}\in I_{(q,r^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\alpha\in I_{q}\ \wedge\ \alpha^{\prime}\in I_{q}\ \wedge\ \omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\in I_{r}\ \wedge\ \omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha^{\prime})\in I_{r^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\prec\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}.

where the second to last implication holds because
T_{2}
is a Wheeler transducer, hence,
r<_{in}r^{\prime}
implies
I_{r}\prec I_{r^{\prime}}
, while the last implication follows since
T_{1}
is a Wheeler transducer and
q\in Q^{+}
.
In conclusion, if
q\in Q^{+}
we have that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q,r^{\prime})
implies
I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q,r^{\prime})}
.

Suppose now that
q\in Q^{-}
. By definition of
<_{mon}
we have that
r>r^{\prime}
allowing us to write:

\displaystyle\alpha\in I_{(q,r)}\ \wedge\ \alpha^{\prime}\in I_{(q,r^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\alpha\in I_{q}\ \wedge\ \alpha^{\prime}\in I_{q^{\prime}}\ \wedge\ \omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\in I_{r}\ \wedge\ \omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha^{\prime})\in I_{r^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha)\succ\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}.

where the second to last implication holds because
T_{2}
is a Wheeler transducer, hence
r>r^{\prime}
implies
I_{r}\succ I_{r^{\prime}}
, while the last implication follows since
T_{1}
is a Wheeler transducer and
q\in Q^{-}
. Hence, if
q\in Q^{-}
we have that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q,r^{\prime})
implies that
I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q,r^{\prime})}
.

This proves that
(q,r)<_{mon}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\implies I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}
. It follows that the order

<
, defined on
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
by

(q,r)<(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow I_{(q,r)}\prec I_{(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})},

includes the total order
<_{mon}
and so
<
is total. Hence, the first condition of a Wheeler transducer is satisfied.

We now consider the second Wheeler condition. Notice that
\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\alpha)=\omega_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\alpha))
can be proven, by induction, as follows. If
\alpha=\varepsilon
, then, by definition,
\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
and
\omega_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\varepsilon)))=\omega_{2}(s_{2},\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
. If
\alpha=\beta a
then
\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta a)=\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta)\cdot\omega(\delta((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta),a)
. By definition of
\delta
we have that
\delta((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta)=(\delta_{1}(s_{1},\beta),\delta_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta)))
and by calling
\delta_{1}(s_{1},\beta)=q
and
\delta_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta))=r
we obtain
\omega(\delta((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta),a)=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,a))
. By inductive hypothesis we have that

\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta a)=\omega_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta))\cdot\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,a))
. Since
r=\delta_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta))
we can rewrite
\omega((s_{1},s_{2}),\beta a)
as

\omega_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta))\cdot\omega_{2}(\delta_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta)),\omega_{1}(q,a))=\omega_{2}(s_{2},\omega_{1}(s_{1},\beta a)).

If
(q,r)\in Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
we prove that:

•

if
q
and
r
are concordant, that is, they are either both increasing or both decreasing, then
(q,r)
is increasinng;

•

if
q
and
r
are discordant, that is, they are respectively increasing and decreasing or decreasing and increasing, then
(q,r)
is decreasing.

Suppose
q
and
r
are both increasing and
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{(q,r)}
with
\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}
. By definition,
\omega((q,r),\alpha)=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha))
and
\omega((q,r),\alpha^{\prime})=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha^{\prime}))
. Since
q
is increasing and
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{q}
then we have that
\omega_{1}(q,\alpha)\preceq\omega_{1}(q,\alpha^{\prime})
and since
r
is also increasing, by calling
\beta=\omega_{1}(q,\alpha)
and
\beta^{\prime}=\omega(q,\alpha^{\prime})
, from
\beta\prec\beta^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in I_{r}
we have

\omega((q,r),\alpha)=\omega_{2}(r,\beta)\preceq\omega_{2}(r,\beta^{\prime})=\omega((q,r),\alpha^{\prime}),

proving that the state
(q,r)
is increasing.
Similarly this is done if
q
and
r
are both decreasing.

Suppose now that
q
and
r
are discordant, e.g.
q
is increasing and
r
is decreasing, and
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{(q,r)}
with
\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}
. By definition we have
\omega((q,r),\alpha)=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha))
and
\omega((q,r),\alpha^{\prime})=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha^{\prime}))
. Since
q
is increasing it is the case that
\omega_{1}(q,\alpha)\preceq\omega(q,\alpha^{\prime})
. Since
r
is decreasing, by calling
\beta=\omega_{1}(q,\alpha)
and
\beta^{\prime}=\omega(q,\alpha^{\prime})
, from
\beta\preceq\beta^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in I_{r}
we have that
\omega_{2}(r,\beta)\succeq\omega_{2}(r,\beta^{\prime})
, that is,

\omega((q,r),\alpha)=\omega_{2}(r,\omega_{1}(q,\alpha))\succeq\omega_{2}(r,\omega(q,\alpha^{\prime}))=\omega((q,r),\alpha^{\prime}),

proving that the state
(q,r)
is decreasing.
The remaining case (
q
decreasinng and
r
increasing) is analogously proved.

To support the claim that Wheeler transducers are well suited to representing the dynamics of Wheeler languages, we prove that
Wheeler languages are closed under inverse images of Wheeler transducers.

Lemma 2

Suppose
T
is a Wheeler transducer and let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
be a Wheeler language. Then the inverse image
||T||^{-1}(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is a Wheeler language.

Proof

Let
W=(Q_{W},s_{W},F_{W},\Gamma,\delta_{W})
be a Wheeler DFA recogizing
\mathcal{L}
and
consider the transducer
Id_{W}
defined as in Remark
1
. By the previous lemma we know that

Id_{W}\circ T

We next prove that the domain coincides with

In the general case, given a partial function

Then

Definition 2

Let

be a partial function. Then the equivalence relation

\displaystyle\alpha\sim^{c}_{f}\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]^{\pm}\subseteq[\alpha]_{f}\;\wedge\;\hat{f}\;\text{is monotone (increasing or decreasing) over }[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]^{\pm}

Lemma 3

Given a partial function
f:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Gamma^{*}
the relation

Proof

If
\alpha\sim_{f}^{c}\alpha^{\prime}

\alpha a\sim_{f}^{c}\alpha^{\prime}a

For simplicity we only consider the case in which

Next we prove that, under the hypothesis that

\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[\alpha a,\alpha^{\prime}a]

\beta\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))

\beta^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))

\alpha^{\prime}a\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))

Hence we just have to consider the case where

be a Wheeler transducer with
||T||=f
. We define the equivalence
\sim_{T}
on
\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))

\alpha\sim_{T}\alpha^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow\delta(s,\alpha)=\delta(s,\alpha^{\prime})

We will prove that
\sim_{T}
is a refinement of
\sim^{c}_{f}

\sim_{T}
has finite index, this will imply that
\sim^{c}_{f}
has finite index as well.

Suppose
\alpha\sim_{T}\alpha^{\prime}
and
\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}
. We have to prove that
[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]\subseteq[\alpha]_{f}

\hat{f}
is monotone over
[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]

(Q,s,F,\Sigma,\delta)
is a Wheeler DFA the set

is convex, that is,
\forall\beta\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))(\alpha\preceq\beta\preceq\alpha^{\prime}\Rightarrow\beta\in I_{\delta(s,\alpha)})

[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]\subseteq[\alpha]_{f}
.

If
q=\delta(s,\alpha)
, let
y=\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}}\omega(q,\gamma)t(\delta(q,\gamma)).
Then

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\alpha)=\bigwedge_{\alpha\gamma\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))}f(\alpha\gamma)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{\alpha\gamma\in\operatorname{Pref}(dom(f))}\omega(s,\alpha\gamma)t(\delta(s,\alpha\gamma))=

\displaystyle=\omega(s,\alpha)\bigwedge_{\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}}\omega(q,\gamma)t(\delta(q,\gamma))=\omega(s,\alpha)y.

Similarly, for all
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
with
\alpha\preceq\beta\preceq\beta^{\prime}\preceq\alpha^{\prime}

we have
\hat{f}(\beta)=\omega(s,\beta)y,\hat{f}(\beta^{\prime})=\omega(s,\beta^{\prime})y
. Then, if
q
is an increasing state in

\hat{f}(\beta)=\omega(s,\beta)y\preceq\omega(s,\beta^{\prime})y=\hat{f}(\beta^{\prime}),

while, if

\hat{f}(\beta)=\omega(s,\beta)\succeq\omega(s,\beta^{\prime})y=\hat{f}(\beta^{\prime})

In either cases, the function
\hat{f}

Following the approach of

]
for Wheeler languages, we can also give a transducer-independent characterization of functions defined by a Wheeler transducer. We first introduce the notion of eventual monotonicity.

Definition 3

A sequence
(\gamma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be eventually monotone if there exists
n_{0}
such that either
\gamma_{n}\preceq\gamma_{m}
for all
m>n>n_{0}
(increasing) or
\gamma_{n}\succeq\gamma_{m}
for all
m>n>n_{0}
(decreasing).

if the sequence
(f(\alpha_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually monotone whenever
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an eventually monotone sequence in
dom(f)
.

Remark 2

In the previous definition, the two sequences
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(f(\alpha_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
need not be consistent with each other with respect to the order, e.g. it is possible that the sequence
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually increasing while
(f(\alpha_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually decreasing.

\gamma_{n_{1},1},\gamma_{n_{1},2},\gamma_{n_{1},3},\gamma_{n_{1},4},\gamma_{n_{1}+1,1},\gamma_{n_{1}+1,2},\gamma_{n_{1}+1,3},\gamma_{n_{1}+1,4},\dots

is increasing but its
\hat{f}
image, that is, the sequence

\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1},1}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1},2}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1},3}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1},4}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1}+1,1}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1}+1,2}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1}+1,3}),\hat{f}(\gamma_{n_{1}+1,4}),\dots

is not eventually monotone.
Since all
\gamma_{i,j}
belongs to a unique
\sim_{f}^{c}
class,
we can reason as before and construct a monotone sequence in
dom(f)
with a non eventually monotone
f
-image, contradicting the hypothesis.

Hence, we proved that the congruence
\sim_{f}^{c}
must have finite index, implying that the function
f
is Wheeler.

4
Conclusions and Future Work

The study initiated here paves the way for a broader research program
on the structural theory of Wheeler transducers.

Several fundamental problems concerning decidability and complexity remain unresolved. The most immediate is the decidability of membership of a transducer in the class of Wheeler transducers and the complexity of such a decision procedure. A related problem is that of Wheeler realizability: given an arbitrary transducer, can one decide whether an equivalent Wheeler transducer exists? The complexity of the minimization procedure induced by
\sim_{f}^{c}
likewise remains open.

A further direction for future work is to generalize the notion of Wheeler transducer beyond the present setting. In particular, it would be interesting to develop a hierarchy of transducers mirroring the hierarchy of regular languages induced by the deterministic width of their co-lexicographic order (
[
5
]
). Such a hierarchy could provide a finer classification of transducers, relating their structural complexity to the degree to which their underlying automata depart from the Wheeler case. This may also lead to new algorithmic techniques for indexing and processing transductions with bounded co-lexicographic width.

Beyond the deterministic case, a natural extension is the study of Wheeler relations, analogously to rational relations in the regular setting, obtained by dropping the determinism requirement. In order to proceed in this direction, it is necessary to develop a theory of nondeterministic Wheeler transducers and to understand which properties of rational relations survive in the Wheeler setting.
Finally, a logical characterization of Wheeler functions, analogous to the
connection between regular functions and monadic second-order logic, is another
direction for future work.
