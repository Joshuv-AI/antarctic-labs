Introduction

The superior vena cava (SVC) is a well-recognized non-pulmonary vein (PV) focus in atrial fibrillation (AF) and has been implicated in AF initiation and recurrence after pulmonary vein (PV) isolation.1 However, the SVC is an uncommon site of origin for re-entrant atrial tachycardia (AT).2 Although recent advances in high-density mapping technologies have enabled more detailed assessment of complex atrial arrhythmias, the electrophysiological characteristics and optimal ablation strategy for intra-SVC re-entrant AT remain unclear. We describe a rare case of intra-SVC re-entrant AT precisely characterized by high-resolution mapping and entrainment pacing and successfully treated using a circular multi-electrode pulsed-field ablation (PFA) catheter.

Case presentation

An 83-year-old woman with a history of PV isolation using radiofrequency ablation 1 year earlier was referred for a second ablation due to recurrent AT. Her heart rate was 79 bpm during AT, and blood pressure was 99/66 mmHg. Renal function was normal, and thyroid function was within normal limits. She had no history of additional cardiac interventions or surgery. A high right atrial–coronary sinus (HRA–CS) catheter was inserted via the right internal jugular vein. After transseptal puncture, activation mapping of the AT with an OCTARAY™ Mapping Catheter (CARTO 3; Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) demonstrated a re-entrant circuit involving left atrial (LA) posterior wall (Figure 1A). Entrainment pacing was conducted at the LA posterior, and the post-pacing interval (208 ms) was nearly identical to the tachycardia cycle length (200 ms) (Figure 1B). Because bilateral PVs were reconnected, PFA, including PV re-isolation and LA posterior wall isolation, was performed using the PulseSelect PFA system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). During application to the LA roof, the tachycardia did not terminate, but the activation sequence changed, and the tachycardia cycle length was prolonged from 200 to 220 ms. These findings suggested that ablation modified the substrate of the initial AT, resulting in transition to a different AT (AT2).

Because activation mapping of AT2 in the LA demonstrated passive activation from the RA, subsequent RA mapping was performed. When the closely spaced OCTARAY catheter was positioned within the SVC, the recorded electrograms covered almost the entire tachycardia cycle length (Figure 2A). High-resolution mapping of the RA revealed a confined re-entrant circuit within the SVC sleeves, characterized by transverse re-entrant propagation (Figure 3AB). Continuous fragmented potentials were recorded by the closely spaced multi-electrode catheter inside the SVC, indicating slow conduction participating in the tachycardia circuit (Figure 2B). Entrainment pacing inside the SVC, with a post-pacing interval (202 ms) nearly identical to the tachycardia cycle length (216 ms), confirmed intra-SVC re-entry (Figure 2C). A post-pacing interval at the site below the SVC-RA junction did not match the tachycardia cycle length. Entrainment pacing at a different site unexpectedly terminated the AT.

During sinus rhythm, the earliest activation site was confirmed by 3D electroanatomic mapping (Figure 3C). The local activation time map, displayed with the early-meets-late function of the CARTO system (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ), identified an intra-SVC line of conduction block corresponding to the functional barrier forming the SVC re-entrant circuit. After confirming that the circuit was sufficiently distant from the sinus node, the area 2 cm above the sinus node was tagged on the map, and this level was targeted as the application site for PFA for safety. The PulseSelect catheter was placed in the SVC with the fifth electrode downward toward the isolation line (Figure 4).3 The HRA-CS catheter was withdrawn before PFA delivery. Circumferential seven PFA applications were performed at a level of 2 cm above the sinus node. Post-procedural mapping confirmed the intended acute procedural effect and complete SVC isolation without sinus node dysfunction or phrenic nerve palsy (Figure 3D). Neither AT nor AF was inducible during atrial tachypacing with isoproterenol infusion. During a follow-up period of 14 months, the patient remained free from arrhythmia recurrence.

Discussion

Intra-SVC re-entrant AT is an exceptionally rare arrhythmia, and its precise mechanism has not been fully elucidated. In the present case, high-resolution mapping combined with entrainment pacing definitively demonstrated a re-entrant circuit confined within the SVC myocardial sleeves, characterized by transverse re-entrant propagation. Although the SVC post-pacing interval was 14 ms shorter than the tachycardia cycle length, this small discrepancy may have reflected capture of a relatively broad region of the SVC sleeves or adjacent tissue, or pacing-related latency, both of which can influence post-pacing interval assessment during entrainment pacing.4 The observed findings were not consistent with focal AT, upper-loop re-entry, or SVC fibrillation with exit block, given the activation pattern, stable cycle length, and findings from entrainment pacing. Moreover, passive LA activation from the right atrium argued against a macro-re-entrant mechanism involving interatrial epicardial connections. Triggered activity and abnormal automaticity are considered the main mechanisms underlying these focal discharges, and multiple reports of AT originating from the SVC implicate the same mechanisms.5,6 The heterogeneous and discontinuous myocardial sleeve conduction may contribute to the arrhythmogenicity of the SVC, and a line of conduction block within the SVC may also play an important role in the maintenance of this AT.3,7 In this case, high-resolution mapping identified a line of conduction block within the SVC that served as a functional barrier, allowing formation of a stable transverse re-entrant pathway. ‘Coherent mapping’ could be performed during sinus rhythm, allowing identification of the functional barrier forming even during sinus rhythm.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of successful treatment of intra-SVC re-entrant AT using a circular multi-electrode PFA catheter. PFA has an established role in AF ablation owing to its favourable safety profile, and its use for SVC isolation may minimize the risk of phrenic nerve injury.3,8 Targeted ablation of the critical isthmus may terminate AT, whereas circumferential SVC isolation is preferred to prevent AF recurrence.9 With radiofrequency ablation, a minimal ablation strategy is often required to avoid phrenic nerve injury; however, PFA allows SVC isolation with less concern for phrenic nerve palsy.

Sinus node dysfunction remains the most important concern when performing SVC isolation using PFA. Therefore, accurate identification of the sinus node is essential when treating with PFA. In this case, the tachycardia was terminated by entrainment pacing, and mapping of the earliest activation site during sinus rhythm was feasible. Instead of an automated multi-pulse sequence, PFA was delivered during sinus rhythm using manually triggered single-shot applications, permitting immediate cessation of energy delivery if any unexpected conduction disturbances were observed. We previously reported a case series showing that circumferential PFA applications targeting at a site 2 cm above the sinus node were feasible, achieving sufficient lesion formation while preserving sinus node function.3 Because a circular multi-electrode array PFA catheter generates an electric field centred on the fifth electrode, aligning the catheter with the fifth electrode downward towards the target site enables predictable and intended lesion formation. In this case, the ablation target was set 2 cm above the sinus node, and this level sufficiently covered the AT circuit, allowing effective lesion formation.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates effective management of intra-SVC re-entrant AT using a circular multi-electrode PFA catheter. High-resolution mapping identified a rare re-entrant circuit confined to the SVC. Owing to its tissue selectivity, PFA enables safe and effective SVC isolation while minimizing the risk of phrenic nerve injury.
