Porous bioactive glass-based scaffolds containing CRT glass and hydroxyapatite: morphological and structural analysis (Part I)
Authors: Olga Kędzia, Małgorzata Lubas, Agata Dudek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porous scaffolds based on bioglass 45S5 were produced with the addition of a constant amount of cathode ray tube (CRT) glass cullet and a variable content of hydroxyapatite (HAp − 1.5, 5 and 10 wt%). Porous materials were fabricated using a foaming method with water glass as a pore-forming agent, and the influence of thermal treatment parameters was analysed for four sintering variants, including a one-step sintering process (900 and 950 °C) and a two-step sintering process. The study focuses on analysis of the morphology and structure of the obtained materials, in particular pore architecture, surface porosity, phase composition, and the nature of chemical bonding. Quantitative image analysis showed median pore diameters in the range of approximately 317–640 μm, and the highest surface porosity, reaching ~ 59%, was obtained for samples sintered at 950 °C with the lowest HAp content. Increasing the HAp fraction led to a reduction in pore size and porosity, particularly at higher sintering temperatures. SEM observations confirmed a developed, partially interconnected pore network, while FTIR and XRD analyses revealed the presence of characteristic functional groups of bioglass and an amorphous–crystalline structure involving calcium silicates, sodium–calcium phosphates, and strontium-containing phases originating from CRT cullet. The presented results constitute Part I of the study and establish the process–structure relationships in porous bioactive glass scaffolds modified with CRT cullet and HAp, providing a basis for subsequent analyses of mechanical properties and biological response, which will be presented in a separate publication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57808-4.

Introduction

The increase in demand for implant materials in the field of regenerative medicine is a result of the growing number of surgical procedures such as joint arthroplasty or reconstruction of bone defects after injuries, tumours, or infections. The progress that has taken place in the field of biomaterials allows the design of advanced structures with high biocompatibility, bioactivity, and properties similar to natural bone tissue. Implant materials increasingly support regeneration of bone tissue and are a response to patient expectations concerning improvement of quality of life and maintenance of physical fitness.

bioglass 45S5, the composition of which was developed more than half a century ago, is today known as a biomaterial capable of forming a durable and safe bond with bone tissue. The limited silica content and the constant calcium to phosphorus ratio (Ca/P approximately equal to 5) in the composition of bioglass 45S5 cause active release of ions from its surface in a biological environment. Release of ions as a result of biodegradation of bioglass initiates formation of a silica gel layer and next an amorphous CaO–P₂O₅ phase. This process leads to nucleation of crystals of hydroxycarbonate apatite, which is responsible for adhesion and proliferation of osteoblasts1. A porous scaffold based on bioglass, in contrast to a dense form, imitates the structure of bone and has a developed internal surface favourable for ion exchange, thanks to which the process of replacement of biomaterial by regenerated tissue proceeds more effectively2. It should be noted that introduction into the composition of bioglass of biogenic elements such as magnesium, strontium, barium, zinc, copper, and silver may stimulate regeneration of bone tissue, influence angiogenesis, or inhibit inflammatory states at every stage of biodegradation of the biomaterial and regeneration of bone tissue3.

Porous structures can be obtained in many ways. Among the most known methods there are the polymer foam replication method, sol–gel method, leaching, electrospinning, phase separation, powder sintering, as well as modern additive techniques4. Independently of the applied fabrication method, an effective scaffold for regeneration of bone tissue should fulfil a number of critical structural, mechanical, and biological requirements that enable adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of cells. Structural features are responsible for gas exchange, transport of nutrients, and removal of metabolites. The key parameters include parameters there are porosity, size and distribution of pores, their interconnection and shape, as well as thickness of walls. Mechanical strength of the structure in the best case should correspond to the properties of the replaced tissue5. The rate of biodegradation of the scaffold should be adequate to the rate with which new tissue regenerates. The biodegradation rate should be assessed with consideration of bioactive ions, which promote biomaterial devitrification and thereby directly influence the overall degradation efficiency4.

In processes of sintering of glass powder, it is possible to obtain foam by application of a suitable pore forming agent which undergoes decomposition at high temperature, generating a gas phase and forming pores in a viscous glass mass. Formation of stable bubbles in the glass mass is possible only when grains of glass powder join together into a mass of appropriate viscosity. Low surface tension favours cell formation and typically decreases with increasing temperature, whereas high viscosity suppresses bubble coalescence and ensures mechanical stability of the structure. It is also crucial that dissociation of the pore forming agent takes place in a wide temperature range and that the partial pressure of gas increases gradually, enabling controlled formation of pores. In order for the foaming process to proceed effectively, the pore forming agent should not dissociate before reaching the softening temperature of glass. A properly selected foaming agent, its particle size, and the temperature–time conditions of the process have significant importance for pore size, distribution of porosity, and chemical and mechanical stability of the obtained material (Blaeß & Müller, 2022;6,7. Not fulfilling these conditions may result in uncontrolled cracking of pore walls, insufficient foaming, or destabilization of the cellular structure. One of the known pore forming agents is water glass, used both as a component supporting the process of foam formation and as the main foaming agent. Addition of water glass to the glass mixture lowers the foaming temperature and supplies water necessary for activation of the foaming process8. During sintering, physically bound water evaporates first and leaves the material as water vapor, whereas chemically bound water actively participates in the activation of the foaming process9.

In the study an addition of glass cullet from cathode ray tube (CRT) was used, which contains strontium and barium ions10. According to literature data, the incorporation of strontium oxide and barium oxide into the porous bioactive glass may enhance its bioactivity and contribute to therapeutic functionality. Strontium has been reported to exhibit osteoanabolic effects, including the stimulation of osteoblast activity and inhibition of osteoclast activity, as well as limitation of inflammatory responses11. Barium has also been reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects and to stimulate muscle work and mineralization of bone, which makes it a valuable element in implantology3. Hydroxyapatite was also used in this study, which is a mineral component of natural bone tissue and exhibits biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, and has the ability to form a strong bond with living bone. Hydroxyapatite is often added to composites based on bioglass in order to increase their ability to start the mechanism of bone tissue regeneration12. According to Ielo (2022) introduction of hydroxyapatite into composites with bioglass improves mechanical properties of biomaterials and also enables better control of solubility and rate of biodegradation. Hydroxyapatite favours nucleation of hydroxycarbonate apatite in a physiological environment, accelerating integration with bone tissue. In porous structures the presence of hydroxyapatite may also influence a reduction of pores with large diameters together with formation of pores with smaller diameters which take part in cell migration and vascularization of tissue13. Thus, the combination of Bioglass 45S5, CRT cullet, and HAp was selected to integrate a bioactive silicate glass matrix with Sr- and Ba-containing modifying oxides and a calcium-phosphate component related to the mineral phase of bone.

The sintering process enables nucleation of crystalline phases and as it was shown by Fernandes (2018) glass crystalline materials possess higher mechanical strength resulting from dense microstructure and fine uniformly distributed crystallites. Presence of crystalline phases very often limits bioactivity of biomaterials in comparison with amorphous structure of bioglass, however the newest studies show that high porosity may compensate for this effect improving bioactive properties of the material. Therefore, crystallization in Bioglass 45S5-based porous materials should be considered as a factor with a dual effect. On the one hand, the formation of crystalline phases may decrease the reactivity and dissolution rate of the amorphous glassy network, which can reduce the initial bioactive response2. On the other hand, fine and uniformly distributed crystalline phases may reinforce the glass-ceramic structure and improve mechanical stability, which is important for porous scaffolds exposed to handling and implantation-related stresses14. Glass crystalline structures may undergo heterogeneous degradation, during which first the amorphous structure undergoes degradation and later crystalline phases, which may support long term integration with surrounding tissue provided that the material does not lose prematurely mechanical properties15. In scientific works it was also observed that hydroxycarbonate apatite deposits to a greater degree in the presence of inclusions of crystalline structures than on a smooth surface of amorphous bioglass16.

Although CRT cullet and HAp have been separately investigated as modifiers of bioactive glass-based materials, their simultaneous incorporation into porous Bioglass 45S5 scaffolds obtained by sintering with foaming has not been sufficiently described. In particular, there is limited information on the behaviour of a three-component system comprising bioglass 45S5, CRT cullet, and HAp, in which a constant CRT-derived Sr- and Ba-containing glass component interacts with variable HAp content under different one-step and two-step sintering conditions. This creates a need to clarify how the simultaneous presence of CRT cullet and HAp in bioglass 45S5 matrix affects pore architecture, porosity, crystallization behaviour, and the amorphous–crystalline structure of bioglass 45S5-based foams.

In the present study, such glass-ceramic foams were produced by sintering with foaming using water glass as pore forming agent, while particular experimental series were subjected to different temperature–time sintering conditions. The obtained foams were subjected to microstructural and structural analysis. The results enabled the evaluation of the effects of sintering parameters and HAp content on pore size, porosity, and the internal structure of the obtained biomaterials. The novelty of the work lies in the comprehensive morphological and structural analysis of a three-component porous scaffold system, including the effects of HAp content and one-step or two-step sintering conditions on pore architecture, porosity, crystallization, and phase composition.

The present work is limited to the morphological and structural analysis of porous bioactive glass scaffolds modified with CRT cullet and HAp. Investigations of mechanical properties and biological response, requiring separate experimental procedures, will be presented in a separate publication.

Synthesis of bioglass 45S5 and preparation of specimens for sintering

To melt bioglass 45S5, the following raw materials were used: SiO₂ (TKSM “Biała Góra”, 99.3%), Na₂CO₃ (> 99.9%), CaCO₃ (99.07%), and H₃PO₄ (P.P.H. “Stanlab”, 85%). The glass batch was calculated according to the elemental composition of 45S5 bioactive glass (45 wt% SiO₂, 24.5 wt% Na₂O, 24.5 wt% CaO, and 6 wt% P₂O₅). Due to its volatility, orthophosphoric acid was added to the dry raw materials immediately prior to placing the homogenised set into the furnace. The bioactive glass was melted and subsequently ground according to the procedure described in previous work17. To obtain porous glassy materials, powder with a particle size below 50 μm was used. To prepare specimens based on bioglass 45S5, CRT panel glass cullet and HAp were used as additives. CRT cullet was initially mechanically crushed and subsequently milled in a planetary ball mill (Fritsch Pulverisette 6) for 5 min at a rotational speed of 300 rpm. The obtained powder was sieved through a 50 μm mesh and used as a modifying raw material in an amount of 25 wt%. The proportion of CRT cullet was selected so that the molar content of strontium oxide and barium oxide in the final batches did not exceed 2 mol%, primarily due to the potential cytotoxic effects associated with higher BaO concentrations3,18. Therefore, 25 wt% CRT cullet was selected as a compromise between introducing Sr- and Ba-containing components into the glass matrix and maintaining their content within a range considered safe for further biomaterial development. The second component used for specimen preparation was commercial HAp (Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂), which was sieved through a 50 μm mesh and incorporated at 1.5 wt%, 5 wt%, and 10 wt%. Water glass (Bosman; molar ratio MR: 1.6–2.6; SiO₂: 61–72%; dry matter content: 35–40%, according to the manufacturer’s datasheet) was used as a binder in an amount of 0.8 ml per sample and simultaneously served as the foaming agent. Four sample series (A–D) were prepared, each comprising three compositions. In total, 12 batches were fabricated and subjected to sintering with simultaneous foaming in order to obtain porous glassy materials. Each sample contained 25 wt% CRT cullet, while the remaining fraction consisted of varying amounts of HAp (1.5 wt%, 5 wt%, or 10 wt%) and bioglass 45S5. The HAp content was selected to evaluate how its increasing amount affects pore development, porosity, and crystallization behaviour, while Bioglass 45S5 remained the dominant matrix component. Each series was sintered under different time–temperature conditions. Therefore, Bioglass 45S5 constituted the main bioactive glass matrix, CRT cullet was maintained at a constant level as a glass modifier supplying Sr- and Ba-containing components, whereas HAp was the variable calcium-phosphate additive used to assess its effect on the structure of the foamed materials. The specimen preparation procedure involved weighing the individual raw materials to obtain sets with a total mass of 2 g each. The dry components were homogenized in a mortar, followed by the addition of 0,8 ml of water glass. The samples were formed into pellet-shaped specimens using a copper ring mould with a diameter of 2 cm. All specimens were sintered in a muffle furnace (SNOL 8.2/1100). Series A samples were introduced into a furnace preheated to 900 °C and sintered for 20 min, whereas Series B samples were sintered at 950 °C for 20 min. After the dwell time, the samples were removed from the furnace and cooled in air (Fig. 1b – A, B). Series C and D samples were subjected to two-step sintering. This procedure was introduced to compare direct sintering in a preheated furnace with sintering carried out during gradual heating from room temperature. The temperature of 750 °C was selected as an intermediate stage of the sintering process preceding the final foaming step at 900–950 °C. Gradual heating was applied to allow preliminary consolidation of the compact and more progressive removal of water originating from the water glass before the main foaming stage. The introduction of the intermediate temperature was intended to limit rapid gas release and uncontrolled pore growth during further heating, thereby supporting more stable development of the porous structure. The specimens were placed in a cold furnace. During the first 30 min, the furnace was heated from room temperature to 750 °C. Over the subsequent 30 min, the temperature was increased to 900 °C (Series C) or 950 °C (Series D), followed by a 5 min dwell time at the target temperature and subsequent air cooling (Fig. 1b – C, D).

Research methodology

A preliminary evaluation of the twelve porous samples (Table 3 - A1–D3) was performed using a laboratory stereoscopic microscope (OPTA-TECH, 20× magnification). Fracture surface observations were conducted in bright-field mode using a digital microscope (Keyence VKX-7000). To obtain flat fracture surfaces, fine white abrasive paper was used. After polishing, the samples were cleaned with compressed air to remove glass dust accumulated in the pores. The analysis focused on imaging pores present on the fracture surfaces. For each sample, one micrograph was recorded at each of the following magnifications of 20×, 40×, and 50×. Surface morphology analysis was carried out based on micrographs acquired at 40× magnification. For each sample, 15 pores were identified in the images and measured in two mutually perpendicular directions. The obtained data were subjected to statistical analysis, and the median of the mean pore diameter values was determined as a representative measure of pore size, minimizing the influence of extreme values. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were performed using a JEOL JSM-6610 LV microscope at magnifications of 250× and 1500× on samples sputter-coated with gold. The chemical composition of selected microregions was determined using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (SEM-EDS JEOL JSM-5400). SEM observations were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a working distance of 10 mm and a beam current of 50–55 µA, in high-vacuum mode. Structural characterization was carried out using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Shimadzu IRAffinity-1 S) with the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) technique. Spectra were recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹, with 60 scans per measurement and a spectral resolution of 2 cm⁻¹. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Seifert XRD 3003 T-T) was performed to determine the phase composition of the obtained materials. A cobalt anode X-ray source was used, with a wavelength of λCo = 0.17902 nm. Measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 10° to 100°. Diffraction patterns were analysed using the basic version of Match! software (Crystal Impact, version 3.15).

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess differences between the sample groups (A–D). The analysis included both physicochemical parameters determined by the hydrostatic method and parameters derived from computer-assisted image analysis. Statistical significance was assumed at p < 0.05.

Depending on the level of contamination, intensity of light reflections, presence of surface protrusions, inclusions, and crystalline phases, multiple image-processing operations available in ImageJ were required. Brightness and contrast adjustment, followed by contrast enhancement with 2%-pixel saturation (without histogram equalization), enabled improved detection of pore edges. Noise reduction using the Despeckle function and removal of bright and dark outliers—defined as pixels differing by at least 25 intensity units from the median of neighbouring pixels within a 12 px radius—allowed elimination of black inclusions and light reflections visible in the raw images. Smoothing improved overall image quality by removing artifacts remaining after outlier removal. Colour threshold segmentation enabled detection of pores that were not filled with residual polishing powder, which remained in some pores after fracture surface preparation. The image obtained after colour segmentation was converted into an 8-bit grayscale image. Classical thresholding of the 8-bit image automatically defined lower and upper grayscale threshold values, separating the background from features of interest (pores). This step allowed the detection of small pores filled with white polishing residue that would otherwise have remained undetected. Both thresholding operations were performed using the white colour selection and Otsu’s algorithm. The resulting binary image was subjected to morphological operations, including median filtering, hole filling, erosion, and dilation. These transformations smoothed pore boundaries, removed isolated pixels, filled indentations, and eliminated internal cavities within pores17.

Based on the processed particle contour images, quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. During image analysis, single-pixel objects were excluded from the quantitative evaluation, as they were considered to represent noise or segmentation artefacts rather than real particles. Macro- and microstructural characterization

As shown in the lower row of Fig. 1a, the green bodies prepared for sintering had the form of pellets with a diameter of 1.9 cm and a height not exceeding 0.5 cm. Upon sintering with foaming, the corresponding specimens shown in the upper row of Fig. 1a exhibited a clear increase in volume. After forming, the samples containing 10% HAp exhibited smooth surfaces. In contrast, samples with lower HAp content were more difficult to shape, as the set rapidly absorbed water glass. As a result, the mass became dry and the formed specimens were prone to crumbling, with visible surface irregularities. After sintering (Fig. 1a), all samples increased in volume and exhibited an average mass loss of approximately 0.7 g (mass measurement was considered approximate). The mass loss resulted from evaporation of physically bound water and dehydration of chemically bound water, affecting all set components, but predominantly water glass. Observation of the external surfaces revealed that Series A samples exhibited limited open porosity, which increased with increasing HAp content. The remaining samples (Series B, C, and D) did not display a significant amount of open pores. Only isolated open pores and subsurface pores separated from the environment by thin walls were observed. The surfaces of all samples were vitrified and free of visible cracks. Fracture observations enabled macroscopic evaluation of mechanical resistance as well as pore size and distribution. Series B samples required less force to break than Series A samples. Series B specimens fragmented into large pieces with relatively few small fragments, whereas Series A samples required greater force and disintegrated into numerous small fragments. All Series C samples exhibited very high mechanical resistance and were difficult to fracture. In Series D, samples adhered strongly to the working surface but fractured under relatively low applied force. In all series, increasing HAp content resulted in improved mechanical resistance. Materials containing 10% HAp required higher force to fracture, whereas samples containing 1.5% HAp fractured under minimal impact. These observations should be treated as preliminary and will be verified by quantitative mechanical testing in the second part of the study. All samples shown in Fig. 2 were white, vitrified, and porous. Preliminary macroscopic observations of fracture surfaces indicated that Series A and C exhibited finer pores, while in Series B and D pores underwent greater expansion. Additionally, numerous fine pores were observed beneath the vitrified outer layer of Series C and D samples. In contrast, Series A and B samples exhibited significantly larger pores directly beneath the outer surface.

Preliminary surface assessment indicated that increasing the sintering temperature was associated with an increase in pore size. In micrographs of specimens belonging to Series A and C (Fig. 3: A1–A3; C1–C3), it can be observed that increasing HAp content leads to refinement of the cellular structure, with a greater number of fine pores appearing within the walls between larger pores. This tendency was not observed in Series B specimens (Fig. 3: B1–B3), whereas in Series D fractures (Fig. 3: D1–D3), pores within the walls occurred in limited quantities. It can therefore be concluded that increasing HAp content promotes refinement of the cellular structure within pore walls without excluding the presence of larger pores. As stated in the Introduction, sintering time and temperature are key factors influencing cellular structure refinement. A finer structure is favoured by two-step sintering or sintering in a preheated furnace at lower temperatures. Fine gas bubbles trapped within the glass-crystalline matrix between pores indicate abrupt termination of the foaming process, which might have continued if the specimens had remained in the furnace for a longer time. In fracture micrographs of all the specimens containing 10% HAp, interconnected pores can be observed (example: Fig. 3 – B3). A higher HAp content simultaneously reduces the relative amount of Bioglass 45S5, while the CRT cullet content remains constant at 25. Therefore, the more frequent occurrence of interconnected pores in these specimens should be attributed mainly to the combined effect of increased HAp content, reduced bioglass 45S5 fraction, and the applied sintering conditions, rather than to a change in CRT cullet content. In Series D, elongated pores are present, resulting from gas bubble coalescence. In Series B and D specimens, a reduced wall fraction was observed because expanding pores reached large dimensions and mutually compressed each other. It should be noted that in Series B, there is a pronounced size difference between small and the largest pores, particularly in samples containing 1.5% or 5% HAp. In contrast, Series D exhibits greater uniformity in pore size and distribution.

In Series A and C, the cellular structure did not develop as extensively as at higher temperatures due to excessive melt viscosity. At elevated temperatures, pores grow, and sufficiently low viscosity facilitates wall rupture, leading to pore coalescence into irregular chambers20. Micrographs of these series show that small pores enclosed within walls retain a spherical shape and are not deformed. Gas bubble coalescence is most often caused by prolonged sintering time, a rapid temperature increase, or conditions in which gas cannot be uniformly distributed within the glass mass21. This may occur when the batch is not homogenized and the glass viscosity varies locally, or when impurities and growing crystalline phases are present, facilitating wall rupture and initiating the growth of large cells22. In the investigated specimens, crystalline phases were present and may contribute to localized pore growth. After reaching the sintering temperature and being held for the specified dwell time, the samples were air-cooled. Abrupt termination of the foaming process preserves the cellular structure. Slower cooling (cooling together with the furnace) could promote the formation of more spherical pores but would not favour the preservation of interconnected porosity21. Characteristic struts (bridges) can be observed in fracture microstructures of Series A (example: Fig. 3 – A3) and Series C (example: Fig. 3 – C2). Such bridges typically form during sintering, increasing mechanical strength while simultaneously reducing porosity23.

Figure 4 and 5 presents SEM microstructures of the investigated specimens at magnifications of 250× and 1500×. In SEM images of Series A specimens, a reduction in the size of pores located within the walls between individual structural cells is observed with increasing HAp content. Wall surfaces and internal pore surfaces in this series are smooth. In the specimen containing 10% HAp (Fig. 4 – A3–1500×), inclusions measuring 5.5–7 μm are present. The SEM microstructure of Series B specimen containing 1.5% HAp (Fig. 4 – B1–250×) indicates the presence of flattened pores within the walls. This deformation may result from pressure exerted by expanding large pores21. For specimens containing 5% and 10% HAp (Fig. 4 – B2, B3–250×), pores are fine and predominantly spherical. At 1500× magnification, rough internal pore surfaces become evident. In Series C microstructures, very thin walls are visible, with a thickness of only 5 μm at their thinnest point (Fig. 5 – C3–1500×). At characteristic junction nodes where three or more pores meet, newly forming fine pores can be observed. The size of these pores varies with HAp concentration and is smallest in specimens with the highest HAp content. Micrographs of specimens containing 5% and 10% HAp (Fig. 5 – C2, C3–250×) show elongated and deformed pores. Fine inclusions originating from crystalline phases are present in each specimen of this series. In the sample containing 5% HAp (Fig. 5 – C2–1500×), pores filled with clearly visible fine-grained precipitates are observed. Similarly filled pores were found in the specimen containing 5% HAp in Series D. Selected image regions containing pores filled with fine-grained pore-filling precipitates were subjected to EDS analysis, which revealed reversed phosphorus-to-silicon ratios and elevated strontium and barium contents compared to wall analyses in specimens lacking such characteristic pores (Table 5). The determined chemical composition does not indicate that the material filling the pores is a residue of the initial batch components. Rather, the EDS results suggest the formation of chemically distinct fine-grained precipitates during sintering. These precipitates may be related to crystalline phase formation inside the pores, as described by Spiridonov and Orlova (2003); however, their crystalline nature was not directly confirmed in the present study. In Series D, local pore-merging features were observed: in the specimen containing 10% HAp (Fig. 5 – D3–250×), two pores can be seen merging, while in the specimen containing 1.5% HAp (Fig. 5– D1–250×), a small gas bubble is seen merging with a larger cellular structure. As previously noted, at the high temperature applied (series 950 °C), the internal pore surfaces exhibit a rough texture.

Figure 7 presents the porous surface parameters obtained using the Analyze Particles tool in ImageJ, including pore number, surface porosity, and shape factor. The lowest pore number was observed in Series B specimens, with comparable values for Series D. In both series, the number of pores ranged from 100 to 150. In Series B, the highest pore number was observed in specimens containing 1.5% HAp and the lowest in that containing 10% HAp, whereas Series A and C specimens exhibited higher pore numbers, ranging from 100 to 200. In Series C, the trend was reversed, with the 10% HAp sample showing the highest number of pores and the 1.5% HAp sample the lowest. Pore count measurements are subject to significant error because the algorithm performs best on smooth surfaces where small pores are clearly visible and filled with powder residuals from sample preparation (Fig. 3 – C3). However, the analysis was less reliable for surfaces with a high number density of closely spaced fine pores.

Surface porosity values in Series A were similar across samples, averaging 27.66% ± 0.93%, with the highest value observed in the 5% HAp sample (28.41% ± 4.45%). In Series B, surface porosity decreased with increasing HAp content: 1.5% HAp – 59.46% ± 2.44%; 5% HAp – 48.84% ± 4.30%; 10% HAp – 34.32% ± 5%. In Series C and D, surface porosity also decreased with HAp content. The highest porosity in Series D was 59.38% ± 0.72% for the 1.5% HAp sample, while the corresponding Series C sample showed 31.25% ± 1.31%. Both series exhibited the lowest porosity in 10% HAp specimens (C3–22.54% ± 2.58%; D3–30.27% ± 4.64%). For 5% HAp specimens, Series C showed 28.38% ± 1.43%, and Series D 40.47% ± 1.75%. The lowest surface porosity was observed in Series C and A specimens, while the highest porosity was found in 1.5% HAp samples of Series B and D. The data indicate a tendency toward higher surface porosity at higher sintering temperatures and toward lower surface porosity with increasing HAp content. Image analysis using the proposed algorithm provided quantitative information on surface porosity and pore-related parameters, complementing the results obtained by hydrostatic weighing. These data are important because the expected mechanical response of porous glass-crystalline materials depends not only on total porosity, but also on pore size, pore morphology, pore coalescence, and pore interconnectivity. Larger or coalesced pores may act as stress concentrators, whereas pore refinement and a more homogeneous pore-size distribution may contribute to improved apparent fracture resistance23.

Pore shape factors ranged from 0.7 to 0.8 with an error margin of ± 0.2. A slight decrease in shape factor was observed in Series A, B, and D with increasing HAp content, whereas Series C exhibited the opposite trend. Many fine, round pores with residual polishing powder on the surface of sample C3 were easily detected during 8-bit segmentation, resulting in a higher shape factor and increased pore count despite low surface porosity. The most challenging specimens for image analysis were those with uneven surfaces or with very large pores whose boundaries could not be clearly resolved, even after applying the full set of image processing operations.

Surface porosity did not differ significantly between the groups, although a tendency toward differentiation between the groups was observed (p = 0.076). No statistically significant differences were found for pore number or shape factor (p > 0.05). Thus, although differences in surface porosity and pore-related parameters were observed descriptively between the sample series, these variations should be interpreted as tendencies rather than as statistically confirmed differences within the investigated experimental design.

Density, water absorption and porosity analysis

Physicochemical studies, including the determination of apparent density (1), total porosity (2), and water absorption (3) of specimens containing bioglass 45S5, CRT cullet, and HAp, were performed using the hydrostatic weighing method in distilled water. For each specimen, at least two measurements were carried out, except for the Series B specimen containing 10% HAp (B3), for which only a single measurement was possible. After determining the dry mass, specimens were placed in hot water and boiled for 2 h. After boiling, the specimens were transferred to a vessel with distilled water. Cooled specimens were weighed in a hydrostatic setup for both submerged and water-saturated states. Measurement results are presented in Fig. 8.

Fig. 8Parameters determined: apparent density (1), total porosity (2), water absorption (3); * - indicates statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05).

Parameters determined: apparent density (1), total porosity (2), water absorption (3); * - indicates statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05).

The apparent density (1) of Series A specimens was similar, ranging from 0.78 g/cm³ (A1, A2) to 0.81 g/cm³ (A3). For Series B, the highest apparent density was found in the 10% HAp specimen (B3, 0.72 g/cm³), the lowest in the 5% HAp specimen (B2, 0.58 g/cm³), and an intermediate value (0.63 g/cm³) for the 1.5% HAp specimen (B1). In Series C, apparent density increased gradually with HAp content from 0.81 g/cm³ to 1.02 g/cm³. In Series D, density changes resembled those in Series B: the highest apparent density (0.82 g/cm³) was observed for the 10% HAp specimen (D3), the lowest (0.67 g/cm³) for the 5% HAp specimen (D2), and the intermediate value (0.69 g/cm³) for the 1.5% HAp specimen. The 1.5% HAp specimens contained the largest number of closed pores, which was also confirmed in microscopic analysis. Furthermore, microscopy revealed the presence of open and interconnected pores in specimens containing 10 wt% HAp, although these specimens generally exhibited lower total porosity. Series C, sintered in a two-step process up to 900 °C, exhibited the highest density, whereas the lowest density specimens were in Series B, sintered at 950 °C for 20 min. Due to the higher density of HAp relative to bioglass 45S5, the apparent density of specimens containing 10% HAp is higher than that of other sets. At higher sintering temperatures, apparent density decreases due to increased mass loss during sintering. Specimens with 1.5% HAp in Series B and D exhibited higher apparent density while also showing very high porosity and water absorption, likely due to the presence of open pores within a dense framework. Densification of the glass skeleton, and hence increased mass, may be caused by crystalline phases. It is also important to note that fragments of the analysed specimens were not homogeneous in terms of pore shape and distribution, which may have affected the apparent density and other physicochemical parameters.

Total porosity (2) in Series A, similar to apparent density, was comparable across specimens, averaging 27.43% ± 0.58%. The highest total porosity in this series was found in the 5% HAp specimen (28.20% ± 0.38 – A2). In Series B, total porosity decreased with increasing HAp content: 1.5% HAp (B1) – 59.29% ± 0.87%, 5% HAp (B2) – 51.67% ± 1.40%, 10% HAp (B3) – 31.45%. In Series C and D, total porosity similarly decreased with HAp content. The highest total porosity was observed in the 1.5% HAp specimen from Series D (63.80% ± 0.48 – D1), whereas the corresponding 1.5% HAp specimen from Series C was 32.43% ± 5.71% (C1). The lowest total porosity in both series was observed in 10% HAp specimens, which were very similar (C3–22.94% ± 2.29%; D3–22.50% ± 0.14%). For 5% HAp specimens, Series C exhibited 23.86% ± 2.67% (C2), while Series D showed 42.50% ± 1.38% (D2). The lowest total porosity was found in Series C and A specimens, whereas the highest porosity occurred in 1.5% HAp specimens from Series B and D. The lowest total porosity corresponded to specimens with the highest HAp content. In general, porosity decreased with increasing HAp content, except for the 1.5% HAp specimens from Series B and D. Higher porosity in Series B and D suggests that process temperature promotes pore formation and growth, whereas the results for specimens containing 10 wt% HAp suggest a tendency toward limited pore expansion and reduced porosity, indicating structural stabilization at higher HAp content25,26. The highest porosity in 1.5% HAp specimens may suggest that small amounts of HAp facilitate foaming. However, previous studies17 demonstrated that bioglass 45S5 + CRT cullet specimens (without HAp) sintered under Series A conditions achieved 38% porosity, indicating that even small HAp additions slightly inhibit pore formation, and process temperature plays a dominant role. Total porosity for all specimens correlates with surface porosity determined by computer image analysis.

Water absorption (3) results followed the same trends as porosity. In Series B and D (1.5% and 5% HAp), water absorption was significantly higher than total porosity, with a similar discrepancy observed in the 5% HAp specimen from Series A. The highest water absorption was observed in 1.5% HAp specimens from Series B (93.89% ± 5.36% – B1) and Series D (90.71% ± 1.73% – D1). The 5% HAp specimens showed 88.70% ± 2.76% (B2) and 63.85% ± 6.88% (D2). Water absorption in 10% HAp specimens in both series was closer to porosity values: Series B – 43.88% (B3), Series D – 27.53% ± 0.42% (D3). In Series A, the highest absorption was found in the 5% HAp specimen (56.28% ± 0.67 – A2), while in Series C the 1.5% HAp specimen showed the highest absorption (39.83% ± 7.64 – C1). In Series A, 1.5% and 10% HAp specimens had similar absorption values (A1–54.21% ± 3.27%; A3–33.73% ± 0.54%). The 5% HAp specimen in Series C showed 28.52% ± 3.39% (C2), and the lowest absorption was in the 10% HAp specimen (22.43% ± 2.81 – C3). Specimens with water absorption significantly exceeding total porosity contain open pores. Fragments used in testing had external surfaces largely composed of high-volume open pores. Additionally, boiling prior to weighing may have created micropores, allowing water to penetrate internal pores and explaining high absorption values. Surfaces were lightly dried before weighing, but due to the porous nature of the material, uniform drying was not possible. The results also indicate that lower sintering temperature, as well as higher HAp content, limits the formation of open pores. Consequently, a low number of open pores reduces the material’s water absorption capacity. Similar trends in water absorption were observed for specimens sintered at the same final temperature using different heating regimes, except for the 5 wt% HAp specimens from Series A and C. Therefore, it is difficult to clearly assess the effect of the sintering method on water uptake.

One-way ANOVA confirmed that the differences in apparent density between the analysed groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05), which is consistent with the observed variations related to HAp content and sintering conditions; however, total porosity and water absorption did not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). This indicates that, within the investigated experimental range, apparent density was the parameter most clearly affected by the applied processing and compositional conditions. In contrast, the observed changes in total porosity and water absorption should be interpreted as descriptive trends, which may be related to the heterogeneous pore architecture of the investigated scaffolds.

Although compressive strength was not measured in the present study, the obtained density and porosity results allow a preliminary discussion in relation to literature data for porous bioactive glass scaffolds. According to Baino et al.5, the mechanical performance of bioactive glass-based scaffolds is strongly dependent on porosity, pore architecture, interconnectivity, and processing conditions. In general, higher porosity and larger interconnected pores may reduce compressive strength, whereas densification of the glass-ceramic skeleton and the presence of crystalline phases may contribute to mechanical stabilization. Therefore, in the present materials, the relatively high porosity observed especially in Series B and D may negatively affect compressive strength, while the higher apparent density and increased HAp content in selected samples may support structural stability. Direct compressive strength measurements are planned as the next stage of this research and will enable quantitative correlation with the structural parameters determined in the present work.

Bands at 420 cm⁻¹ and 440 cm⁻¹ correspond to symmetric bending vibrations of O-Si-O bonds27. In the bioglass 45S5 spectrum, the 440 cm⁻¹ band is broad, whereas the 420 cm⁻¹ band is sharp and intense. Similar bands are present in the specimen spectra, although the 420 cm⁻¹ band is not visible in all samples. In D1, C3, B1, and A2 specimens, the nearby intense and broad 440 cm⁻¹ band may partially obscure the 420 cm⁻¹ band. The 490 cm⁻¹ band corresponds to O-Si-O bending vibrations29. It may also overlap with P-O bending vibrations (~ 520 cm⁻¹)28. The 560 cm⁻¹ band in bioglass 45S5 corresponds to O-P-O bending vibrations in PO₄³⁻ groups; similarly, the 550 cm⁻¹ band in specimens is attributed to the same vibrations28,30. The 590–600 cm⁻¹ band is associated with M-O bonds in metal oxides, specifically Sr-O vibrations31, originating from strontium oxide in CRT-containing specimens. This band is absent in bioglass 45S5. Bands at 660 cm⁻¹ (all spectra) and 740 cm⁻¹ (shifted to 720 cm⁻¹ in bioglass 45S5) correspond to symmetric Si-O-Si stretching vibrations in Na₂Ca₂Si₃O₄28. Bands at 860 cm⁻¹ and 890 cm⁻¹ in bioglass 45S5 are assigned to Si-O-2NBO bonds, indicating the presence of non-bridging oxygens, a feature relevant to its dissolution and ion release in biological environments32. The 930 cm⁻¹ band (all spectra) corresponds to Si-O stretching with non-bridging oxygens, potentially forming Si-O-Ca bonds in contact with body fluids or SBF; it may overlap with asymmetric PO₄ stretching (~ 940 cm⁻¹)28. In the 1000–1100 cm⁻¹ range, a strong broad band of Si-O-Si asymmetric stretching is observed27. A medium-intensity P-O symmetric stretching in HAp (~ 1035 cm⁻¹) may be obscured beneath the Si-O-Si band30. In bioglass 45S5, the broad asymmetric Si-O-Si peak is shifted to 980 cm⁻¹. The intense 1450 cm⁻¹ peak in bioglass 45S5 is attributed to carbonate (CO₃²⁻) stretching vibrations30. The presence of carbonates may result from reactions between the bioactive glass surface and atmospheric CO₂ after synthesis. The finer particle size increases surface area, facilitating this reaction. Weak bands at 1450 cm⁻¹ in the specimen spectra indicate reduced surface reactivity. A weak 1650 cm⁻¹ band in all specimens corresponds to OH groups from water hydration29.

Structural studies by XRD

The crystalline structure of the investigated samples was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) — the obtained diffractograms are shown in Fig. 10. Analysis of the XRD diffractograms enabled identification of crystalline phases in the tested samples. In the first stage of these studies, the bioglass 45S5 sample was analysed in order to confirm the amorphous structure of the obtained base glass. The diffractogram of the bioglass 45S5 sample (Fig. 10 – BG45S5) confirms the amorphous structure through a characteristic broad amorphous halo in the angular range 2θ from 20 to 40°. No peaks originating from crystalline phases are present in the diffractogram. The diffractograms of the investigated samples (Fig. 10 – series: A, B, C, D) exhibit a raised background characteristic of an amorphous structure. The amorphous halo occurs in the angular range from 20 to 40° 2θ, corresponding to the range of bioglass 45S5. The diffractograms of the tested samples also contain peaks characteristic of crystalline phases formed as a result of crystallization of bioglass 45S5 and of additives introduced into it in the form of CRT cullet and HAp. A crystalline phase characteristic for all samples is wollastonite. Wollastonite may occur already at 700 °C for α-wollastonite to 1250 °C. It occurs mainly on the surface of bioglass and facilitates formation of the HAp layer in the presence of physiological fluids or artificial blood plasma (“Simulated Body Fluid (SBF)”)33. The diffractograms also show crystalline phases of calcium phosphate (CaP₂O₆) and β-rhenanite, which form as a result of depolymerization of the low-temperature crystalline phase of sodium phosphate (NaPO₃) and its reaction with calcium. Calcium phosphate occurs in small amounts in samples sintered at 900 °C and disappears at higher temperatures. At 900 °C, sodium calcium phosphate Ca₁₀Na(PO₄)₇ appears. The β-rhenanite phase (β-NaCaPO₄), which is biocompatible and bioresorbable, is present in all samples. Its presence is possible in the temperature range 700 to 1000 °C. A similar situation occurs for the CaNaPO₄ phase and the buchwaldite phase. Peaks of these phases overlap with peaks originating from β-rhenanite and may be present in all samples34. This overlap results from the similar chemical composition and crystal structure of calcium–phosphate–silicate-based phases, which exhibit comparable interplanar spacings and therefore produce coincident or closely spaced diffraction peaks. The combeite phase occurs in the structure of bioglass 45S5 at 900 °C35. In the analysed samples, the presence of two crystalline phases containing strontium originating from CRT cullet was demonstrated: layered sodium–strontium disilicate (Na₂SrSi₂O₆) and calcium–strontium silicate (Ca₃SiSrO₈)36. The presence of these phases is beneficial because, according to the literature37, they may support regeneration of bone lost during osteoporosis. In all diffractograms, peaks characteristic of hydroxyapatite can also be recognized, overlapping with peaks of other crystalline phases. Peaks originating from SiO₂ can also be identified in the diffractograms. Silica may be present in all samples because water glass, an aqueous silicate solution, was used for sample forming. Therefore, the identified crystalline phases can be linked to the individual components of the batch: wollastonite, combeite, and sodium–calcium phosphate phases are mainly associated with crystallization of the bioglass 45S5-based matrix; Sr-containing silicate phases are related to the CRT cullet contribution; and HAp-related reflections together with calcium-phosphate phases indicate the presence of HAp and its reactions during sintering. Analysis of peak intensities showed that Series A and C were characterized by lower intensity of crystalline peaks than Series B and D. These results indicate that at 900 °C crystallization proceeded to a lesser extent, whereas weak intensity of the amorphous halo in the case of the sample sintered in two stages at this temperature may indicate that crystalline phases are strongly dispersed in the amorphous glass structure. The analysis showed that samples containing more HAp and sintered in two stages exhibited higher reflection intensities than samples sintered in a preheated furnace and containing less HAp.

In the diffractograms, a slight peak shift (up to 1.5°) toward higher values is observed compared with reference patterns. This shift is most likely caused by large dopant ions introduced together with CRT cullet, which modify unit cell dimensions and introduce tensile stresses into the crystal lattice. Despite the introduction of CRT cullet into the batch, which lowers melt viscosity and contains elements that reduce the tendency of the glass mass to crystallize, the tested samples showed the presence of crystalline phases. This can be explained by the high crystallization tendency of bioglass 45S5, which constituted the largest fraction of each batch. The crystallization tendency of bioglass 45S5 results from the presence of a small amount of silica and a large amount of network modifiers in bioglass 45S538. In addition, gas pressure generated during foaming and the applied sintering conditions may significantly influence the resulting porous structure (Blaeß & Müller, 2022). In the case of two-stage sintering (Series C, D), samples were held at temperatures corresponding to crystallization for sufficiently long times to enable formation of crystalline phases, which may take the form of dispersed grains in the glass matrix or precipitate locally in pores39. Taken together, the results indicate that the final structure of the foams was governed primarily by the sintering regime and HAp content, while CRT cullet acted as a constant modifier of the glass composition, contributing Sr- and Ba-containing components and influencing the crystallization behaviour of the Bioglass 45S5-based matrix. From the application perspective, this crystallization may be beneficial for mechanical stabilization of the porous glass-ceramic skeleton, but it may also reduce the initial bioactivity compared with a fully amorphous bioglass 45S5 matrix; therefore, the high porosity and presence of calcium-phosphate phases are important factors that may compensate for this effect40–42.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates the feasibility of producing porous glass-ceramic materials based on bioglass 45S5 modified with CRT cullet and HAp using the sintering-with-foaming method. The simultaneous incorporation of CRT cullet and HAp into three-component porous scaffolds based on bioglass 45S5 represents a novel approach not previously reported in the literature.

The obtained materials exhibited a well-developed, interconnected pore structure, with porosity reaching up to 59%, while increasing HAp content led to reduced pore size and enhanced densification.
