Feasibility of implementing a community-based participatory group programme to improve access to healthcare for people with disabilities in Luuka district, Uganda: a mixed-methods pilot study
Authors: Tracey Smythe, Andrew Sentoogo Ssemata, Abdmagidu Menya, Joanna Drazdzewska, Annemijn Sondaal, Sarah Marks, Moses Iyereget, Margaret Mugisa, Peter Waiswa, Femke Bannink Mbazzi, Hannah Kuper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with disabilities experience substantial barriers to healthcare, yet few community-based interventions have been evaluated to address these inequities in low-resource settings. We assessed the feasibility of Participatory Learning and Action for Disability (PLA-D) groups to improve healthcare access for people with disabilities in Luuka district, Uganda.MethodsPilot-testing of the PLA-D approach was conducted from May 2023 to March 2024, with the establishment of five PLA-D groups in Luuka district, Uganda. The groups were led by trained facilitators and each included approximately 35–48 members (total 204) who met biweekly for a cycle of 11 sessions. Groups identified healthcare access challenges and implemented local solutions. Guided by Bowen’s feasibility framework, we assessed acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality and limited-efficacy outcomes through (1) 28 in-depth interviews and five focus group discussions with participants, non-participants and facilitators, (2) monitoring group activities, (3) group observation and (4) 128 baseline and 153 endline quantitative questionnaires of participants with disabilities and caregivers.ResultsPLA-D was acceptable and perceived as relevant by participants, with strong demand for continued group activities. Sessions were delivered largely as intended although facilitator mentoring and logistical support were important for maintaining implementation quality. Convenient meeting times and locations, local organisation, peer support and collective problem-solving supported participation. Participants reported increased knowledge, confidence and collective capacity to seek healthcare and advocate for their needs. Quantitative findings suggested possible improvements in healthcare access among adults with disabilities; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the pilot design and limited statistical power.ConclusionsPLA-D was feasible, acceptable and valued by participants in this rural Ugandan setting. The intervention shows promise as a community-based approach to addressing healthcare access barriers for people with disabilities, but future implementation should strengthen facilitation support, health system linkages and scalability planning before larger-scale evaluation.Keywords: Health Services Accessibility, Community-Based Participatory Research, Community Participation, Program Evaluation

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Participatory learning and action (PLA) approaches are effective at reducing maternal and neonatal mortality. Consequently, PLA is recommended by the WHO as a strategy for improving maternal and newborn health and reducing mortality and has been adapted to address other groups and health conditions. However, participatory approaches to promote disability inclusive health are lacking and PLA has not been used explicitly for people with disabilities.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

A pilot study of five groups showed that PLA for Disability (PLA-D) is feasible to deliver in rural settings in Uganda. Emerging evidence showed that PLA-D was acceptable and in demand among the target population and predominantly delivered with fidelity (it was delivered as originally designed and planned). There was evidence that the intervention was associated with improved access to healthcare and decreased healthcare needs reported by participants.

Introduction

Globally, there are over 1.3 billion people with disabilities, more than 80% of whom live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 2 People with disabilities include those with long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments, which interact with various barriers and restrict their full participation in society.3 They often experience exclusion and negative attitudes and are more likely to be poor with limited access to education and employment.1 2 A key challenge in the lives of many people with disabilities is exclusion from healthcare, which leads to difficulties in achieving good health4,8 and is the focus of this study.

A growing literature shows that people with disabilities have worse health on average than others in the population.2 4 There are several reasons for this gap. Underlying impairments (eg, physical impairment from stroke) can be linked to consequent health risks (eg, pressure sores). People with disabilities are also on average older, poorer and more marginalised and often experience higher risk factors to poor health such as violence, physical inactivity and hypertension,2 6 which contribute to poorer health.4 In addition, they often experience compounded barriers to accessing healthcare, including inaccessible transport and facilities, untrained providers and high costs2 7 9 and so are more likely to have unmet healthcare needs than their peers.1 People with disabilities also often encounter worse quality and affordability of healthcare services.1 These factors contribute to worse health outcomes and higher mortality rates for people with disabilities, including in Africa.10 These patterns can also be observed in Uganda, the context for this study, where approximately 12.5% of the population have disabilities.11 Improving healthcare access for people with disabilities is therefore crucial for achieving health targets and development goals, including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 3 on ‘Good health for all’ and Universal Health Coverage.12 Moreover, exclusion from healthcare violates the rights of people with disabilities as outlined in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and national laws.12 Good healthcare is also important to people with disabilities and their families as it affects their ability to survive and enjoy good health. Finally, improving healthcare access for people with disabilities can be cost-saving2 by preventing unnecessary healthcare costs and enhancing services for all through universal design.

There is strong rationale for the development of participatory approaches to improve access to healthcare for people with disabilities. Two systematic reviews13 14 and an Evidence and Gap Map on disability-inclusive development15 failed to identify good examples of disability-inclusive health interventions. The WHO recommends several ‘top-down’ interventions,1 2 such as removing physical barriers to health facilities, making healthcare affordable, training healthcare workers on disability issues and investing in specific services like rehabilitation. However, these may not be sufficient as barriers will vary locally.7 Therefore, the participation of people with disabilities in developing locally appropriate solutions is crucial, aligning with the ‘Nothing about us, without us’ principle of the disability movement, and ensuring that interventions are relevant and effective. One key example of participatory approaches is participatory learning and action (PLA) which uses a ‘bottom up’ approach to empower communities to identify and address local issues collaboratively, creating low-cost, scalable solutions. PLA has successfully reduced maternal and neonatal mortality by convening women’s groups to identify healthcare barriers, develop solutions and evaluate outcomes.16 PLA is sustainable,17 scalable18 and equitable,19 and it is recommended by the WHO to improve maternal and child outcomes.20 PLA has been implemented in various countries, including in Uganda.21 PLA has not yet been specifically used for people with disabilities, but evidence from Nepal showed no difference in community group attendance between women with and without disabilities.22 There is also evidence from the Philippines on the potential for participatory approaches for people with disabilities; the W-DARE project (Women with Disability taking Action on Reproductive and sexual health) improved access to healthcare for women with disabilities.23

We hypothesise that PLA for people with disabilities (PLA-D) will empower people with disabilities to identify and tackle locally relevant barriers, such as lack of health education and decision-making powers and poverty. The PLA-D approach is complemented by advocacy activities to engage community members and encourage action (eg, supporting transport to healthcare facilities). Additionally, health system strengthening interventions, including training of health workers on disability and addressing facility accessibility, are implemented alongside PLA-D. Overall, these actions are expected to improve healthcare access, health, well-being and ultimately reduce mortality for people with disabilities.

The PLA-D approach was co-designed in 2022, based on in-depth interviews (IDIs) with people with disabilities and a range of other key stakeholders, systematic reviews, and a 4-day design workshop.24 25 The PLA content, including the facilitator manual, materials and session plans, was adapted to be appropriate to the needs of people with disabilities in Uganda. This paper reports on our pilot-study of the PLA-D groups, using the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework guiding both intervention development and evaluation.26 The purpose of the pilot was to evaluate the feasibility of implementing the groups and to inform the design of a future cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT).

Methods

Overview

We undertook the feasibility study in Luuka District, Uganda, between May 2023 and March 2024. The district was chosen because it is a key site for ongoing research by the School of Public Health, Makerere University, one of the study collaborators, which has established networks in the district that aided project implementation. In the 2014 census, Luuka district had a population of 238,000, of which over 95% are rural and engaged in agriculture, and a 12% prevalence of disability (ages 5+).11 The mixed-methods study was conducted between 2023 and 2024 and research was undertaken as a partnership between researchers from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM), MRC/Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI) & LSHTM Uganda Research Unit, Makerere University and the Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs) Women and Children First and Amref Health Africa in Uganda. The study protocol was registered and published.27 We used the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting checklist (online supplemental file 1) in preparation and reporting of this paper.

Pilot-testing the PLA-D groups

We established five pilot PLA-D groups in Bukanga Sub-county, Luuka district. In collaboration with the District Health Office, we purposively selected communities in villages representing various contexts including rural and semi-urban settings, access to services and presence of active Village Health Teams. While statistical representativeness was not the aim, these criteria were used to capture diversity in implementation contexts. We worked with community members (eg, community health workers, local leaders and representatives of persons with disabilities) using community registers and local knowledge to identify households with people with disabilities. These individuals were then informed about the purpose and structure of the PLA-D groups and invited to participate.

Membership was also open to family members/caregivers of people with disabilities and other key community members (eg, village leaders), with a focus on the participation of people with disabilities. Inclusion criteria included residence within the selected communities and willingness to participate in PLA-D activities. Efforts were made to include participants across different age groups, genders, types of disability to reflect a range of experiences and inclusion of community members without disabilities to foster inclusive dialogue, shared understanding and collective problem-solving across community groups. This design follows the PLA model used in maternal and neonatal health and intends to engage the broader community in supporting people with disabilities and their caregivers while exploring their role, fostering dialogue and identifying challenges in mixed groups. Each group aimed to include approximately 35 people, with at least 20 participants being people with disabilities.

The groups chose their meeting locations to ensure accessibility and agreed the meeting schedule. Meetings were led by a locally trained community facilitator chosen by group members. The facilitator was required to be either a person with a disability or a caregiver of a person with a disability, with priority given to people with disabilities. The facilitators and groups operated under the supervision of subcounty supervisors, including a parish chief and a health assistant, who were coordinated by the subcounty group manager who is a community development officer. The team received training in the PLA-D approach from Amref Health Africa in Uganda and the MRC/UVRI & LSHTM Uganda Research Unit. These institutions also provided oversight for the formation and implementation of the PLA-D groups. Groups met biweekly, following the PLA 11 session cycle with a trained facilitator (figure 1). This included sessions on exploring health and access to services, identifying and prioritising healthcare access barriers and solutions, and developing and implementing the prioritised solutions (eg, savings schemes, growing nutritious food), and evaluating the impact of the implemented solutions. Further detail of the intervention is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Feasibility assessment

We used the Bowen framework to evaluate the feasibility of the PLA-D groups.28 Our main focus was the first four components of the framework: acceptability, demand, implementation and practicality. We did not consider the components of adaptation, integration and expansion for this pilot study as they relate to issues of scaling existing programmes. Limited-efficacy testing was undertaken to develop and refine evaluation tools of the groups to inform a future cluster RCT.

Programme monitoring

Attendance of sessions and frequency of sessions were monitored through a register that was collected by the facilitators and reported to Amref Health Africa in Uganda after each session.

Pre–post quantitative survey

A survey was administered to PLA-D group participants at baseline (meeting two—to allow the groups to be fully functional post establishment) and endline (meeting 11—last session) of the programme. The questionnaire was developed in English and translated into Lusoga. It was interviewer-administered by researchers in the language of choice of the respondent. Questionnaires are based on existing tools (WHO Quality of Life (WHOQOL) BREF,29 well-being of Older People cohort in Uganda,30 WHO Model Disability Survey31 and SINTEF survey,32 which have all been widely and previously used in Uganda; therefore, no formal psychometric re-validation, translation/back-translation or extensive tool adaptation was undertaken as part of this study. As this was a feasibility study, the tools were not newly translated, culturally adapted or psychometrically validated for this study. However, the study team reviewed the instruments for contextual appropriateness and acceptability among participants with disabilities. A small number of items considered potentially sensitive or less appropriate for the study context were removed, including the WHOQOL-BREF item on satisfaction with sex life and selected SINTEF items on violence.

Questionnaires are anticipated to take up to 30 min to complete and administered by trained research assistants to accommodate varying literacy levels and functional abilities, with additional explanation provided where necessary to support participant understanding while maintaining the intended meaning of the questions.

Caregivers completed the questionnaire for children with disabilities under 18 years of age and for people with disabilities who were unable to do so themselves. 128 questionnaires were completed at baseline (100 adults with disabilities, 28 children with disabilities) and 153 questionnaires were completed at endline (129 adults with disabilities, 24 children with disabilities).

Semistructured interviews and focus group discussions

A total of 49 of the scheduled 55 semistructured key-informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with the group facilitators via phone after each meeting session. These interviews aimed to review the progress of the sessions, identify what worked well, challenges that arose and areas for improvement. This information was used to make real-time adjustments to future sessions, including further enhancing practices of inclusion. Six interviews were missed due to the facilitators being either unavailable or unreachable within the scheduled interview time frame.

Following the final session, we conducted 17 semistructured IDIs with participants who remained engaged throughout the PLA-D cycle. The IDIs were conducted with 3–4 participants selected from each group, ensuring a diverse representation of disability types, severity, gender and socioeconomic background. Interviewees included people with disabilities, their caregivers, and PLA-D members without disabilities. Six IDIs were conducted with individuals eligible for PLA-D who attended six or fewer sessions to explore their reasons for dropping out of the programme. Five focus group discussions (FGDs) were also held with group members. These were conducted in Lusoga or Luganda based on the respondent’s preference and focused on several key areas: participant satisfaction with the groups, perceived challenges and facilitators in group implementation, areas for improvement and the overall positive and negative impacts of the programme. The 5 FGDs involved 6–8 participants from each of the five PLA-D groups, excluding those who took part in the IDIs. Finally, KIIs were conducted with all five group facilitators. These interviews provided comprehensive data to triangulate findings from the FGDs and explore issues identified during the discussions.

The IDIs lasted approximately 45–60 min, while FGDs took 60–90 min. To support the inclusion of people with disabilities, we provided sign language interpretation, accessible interview locations, transport and used researchers skilled in communicating with those with cognitive impairments. However, occasionally caregiver interviews were conducted for people with severe communication difficulties, such as those with hearing loss or severe intellectual/cognitive impairments. Caregiver interviews were also used for children with disabilities under 18 years of age.

Direct observation of groups

Direct observation of 6 of 11 sessions for each group was undertaken by the researchers using a checklist guide. The checklist contained information about the participatory approaches used by facilitators, the level of perceived interest and engagement of participants and notes of any aspects of the group meeting which went well or did not go well.

Data management

The interviews and FGDs were audio recorded with informed consent. These recordings were then transcribed and translated into English if initially conducted in Luganda or Lusoga. The transcripts were anonymised using pseudonyms to protect participant identity during analysis. Quantitative survey data were anonymised with participant codes and entered into a REDCap database for analysis. All data handling adhered to General Data Protection Regulation requirements to ensure confidentiality and security.

Data were analysed using a thematic approach grounded in the Bowen framework to assess feasibility. This involved deductive coding qualitative data according to the framework’s components of acceptability, demand, implementation and practicality. The coding process was conducted with NVivo V.14 software to systematically organise and categorise data into relevant themes and subthemes. Two trained researchers (ASS and AM) coded the interview responses. Preliminary analysis was discussed with the research team (ASS, AM, TS and HK) to ensure that interpretations were credible and valid. Frequent discussions with the research team took place throughout the data analysis phase. We undertook a narrative synthesis of the findings. Member checking was not conducted to validate findings.

This thematic analysis focused on understanding how well the PLA-D intervention could be implemented in practice, considering factors such as participant acceptability, the demand for the programme, the practical aspects of implementation, and any challenges encountered. The satisfaction of content, organisation and facilitator quality were compared and analysed descriptively.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarise the quantitative data using STATA V.17. All baseline questionnaires were used for analysis of participant demographics. Disability status was determined through self-report of functioning status. Category of functional difficulty was coded as ‘yes’ for people with disabilities or those children for whom caregivers reported ‘cannot do at all’ or have ‘a lot of difficulty’ (or caregiver responses of ‘a lot more’ with regard to the variable ‘controlling behaviour’, ‘anxious’ or ‘sad’ or ‘daily’ for adult responses on anxiety or depression). Only individuals that had both baseline and endline questionnaires completed were included for the analysis of limited efficacy (n=88 for people with disabilities, n=24 for children with disabilities). We used pairwise t-tests for limited efficacy testing to assess binary changes (yes/no) from before to after participation in the group programme. These tests compared specific outcomes: the need for healthcare in the last 3 months, the experience of receiving healthcare when needed and the need for overnight stays in healthcare facilities in the past 6 months. We assessed whether there were differences in overall ratings of health and quality of life before and after participating in PLA-D with χ2 tests.

During data synthesis, the research team sought to use quantitative data to explain and illustrate qualitative findings, and look for congruence and incongruence between qualitative and quantitative findings.

Difference from the published protocol27: We expected the group size to be approximately 35 people; however, due to demand this number increased in three groups to a maximum of 48 participants.

Informed consent

Consent was sought from each participant after a thorough explanation, provision of an information leaflet and sufficient time for consideration. Signed consent was obtained, including thumbprints if necessary. Participants were informed of their right to refuse participation without providing reasons and could withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their treatment or participation in PLA-D groups. A simplified consent form was used for those with communication or intellectual impairments, and consent was also obtained from their caregivers. Caregiver interviews were conducted for children with disabilities under 18 years of age. We minimised the risk of distress during interviews or discussions on sensitive topics by reminding participants of their right to refuse to answer questions or withdraw at any time. Interviewers were trained to be non-judgemental, provide breaks, monitor signs of distress or fatigue, and offer appropriate support or referrals.

Patient and public involvement

The development and pilot testing of PLA-D was in partnership with people with disabilities. During the development phase we: shared plans with people with disabilities and their organisations; interviewed people with disabilities on their perceived healthcare needs and suggestions for group activities; included people with disabilities in the advisory group and in the co-design workshop. People with disabilities also had strong involvement in the implementation and pilot testing of PLA-D, as: group members; group facilitators (wherever possible); research study participants; researchers/data collectors (wherever possible) and advisory group members.

Author reflexivity statement

Our interdisciplinary team comprises researchers from the UK and Uganda. In acknowledgement of the influence of our positionality on this study, we have prioritised participatory methods, engaged local stakeholders and amplified the voices of people with disabilities to ensure equitable research. We have fostered collaboration in our team to balance methodological expertise with contextual knowledge.

Results

The groups included people with disabilities, caregivers and those interested in the same problem of health inequity. The number of participants enrolled in the group ranged from 35 to 48 members. A total of 128 persons with disabilities completed the PLA-D baseline survey questionnaire and characteristics of enrolled participants are shown in table 2. Overall, 55% (n=70) of PLA-D group participants were female and 45% (n=58) were male, with 22% (n=28) of participants under the age of 18 and 20% (n=25) over the age of 60. The groups included people with a range of functional difficulties. Generally, people with disabilities in the group were poor and had low levels of education.

Sociodemographic and health data of participants with disabilities at baseline

Participants in the PLA-D group reported diverse, but complementary, goals in the baseline survey. Adults with disabilities were focused on accessing financial support, such as access to loans and savings opportunities, and valued social networking to build friendships and a sense of belonging. They also emphasised the need for health-related knowledge, access to assistive devices, and personal empowerment through skill development and confidence-building activities. Similarly, caregivers of children with disabilities primarily sought financial support, social networking, and learning opportunities. They reported that they hoped to start businesses, save money, and improve their caregiving skills, while also valuing the emotional support provided through shared experiences to alleviate stress and stigma. Both groups shared a strong desire for mutual support and improved well-being.

Acceptability

The endline survey results indicate high satisfaction with the PLA-D programme among adults with disabilities, with 85% (n=74/87) reporting satisfied or very satisfied with the content, 79% (n=69) with the organisation, and 91% (n=79) with the facilitators. For caregivers of children with disabilities, 63% (n=15/24) were very satisfied or satisfied with the programme overall, 63% (n=15) with the organisation, and 79% (n=19/24) with the facilitators. These findings are supported by the qualitative data, described below.

PLA-D was considered acceptable by participants as it provided opportunities to engage with others facing similar challenges. Before this pilot intervention, there were no groups that focused on people with disabilities in the study villages. Participants felt that creating such a group highlighted the importance of people with disabilities. The groups addressed various issues and many recognised poor health as a major concern. No previous groups had focused on the health of people with disabilities and these efforts were valued and seen as attractive. The groups demonstrated that their health concerns mattered. Participants narrated:

What motivated me to join the PLA-D group was health care services. We were behind and lacked support, so I had to join this health initiative because, given my condition, I couldn’t move from my place. This group opened my eyes concerning access to health and healthcare services. (Female participant)

Before the PLA-D groups started, as persons with disabilities, we didn’t know our rights. We also feared visiting health facilities. But when the project began, we sat together and encouraged each other. This has motivated us to participate in most community activities and it has improved on our health. (Male, Participant)

Participants developed meaningful solutions with the support of facilitators who guided them. Together they deepened their understanding of their health needs and fostered a shared sense of purpose. Participants also reported a strong social identity and appreciated the opportunity to share experiences and problem-solve collectively, as demonstrated by the following excerpt:

I am grateful for this initiative for persons with disabilities and also for recognising our presence. We sensitise persons with disabilities that a person with a disability can do something individually and this is moving on very well (Male participant).

All groups have continued their activities for 12 months after the official programme ended. Each group has continued to meet bi-weekly to support their income-generating activities, such as poultry farming and crop cultivation, and for their income-generating and saving scheme meetings, from which they borrowed money for their health needs.

The facilitators found the programme acceptable as it addressed key barriers to healthcare for people with disabilities, such as discrimination, poor infrastructure and lack of representation. They also noted the positive impact of the programme on participants’ confidence and understanding, with the group now showing active engagement as demonstrated in the following excerpt:

Initially, people were hesitant to join the group. They had low self-esteem, so most of them couldn’t stand up and speak in public. The group is now moving smoothly because they have gone through the facilitation and gained a good understanding. (Facilitator, Male).

Demand

The survey showed high levels of healthcare needs among adults with disabilities 77% reported needing healthcare in the past 3 months, but only 53% received the care they required (table 2). Additionally, more than half (53%) expressed dissatisfaction or strong dissatisfaction with the healthcare services they received. Similarly, for children with disabilities (n=28), 79% (n=22) needed healthcare in the past year, and 61% (n=17) required healthcare in the last 3 months. However, 32% (n=9) of caregivers reported not receiving the needed care, and 60% were either dissatisfied (n=9) or very dissatisfied (n=8) with the healthcare services. Nearly half of children (46%, n=13) had experienced hunger due to food insecurity, which exacerbated their health challenges. PLA-D was recognised as addressing the unmet needs of participants through discussions and learning with others.

Demand for the PLA-D group was further demonstrated by the high attendance; the majority of participants (n=110/128, 85%) attended most sessions. A total of 38 (30%) people attended all sessions, while 34 (27%) missed only one session. Demand was also expressed by the participants in interviews. Participants mentioned having few other opportunities to problem-solve before this programme. Participants were eager to meet regularly because they felt it was a unique opportunity to learn and discuss important issues, and missing a meeting could make it harder to catch up, especially as many have been isolated and were forming new connections. One participant noted,

Every time we have a meeting there is something I gain. For example, we may learn about how they will help us at the health facilities and the next time we may look at how to overcome long distances (Participant, Female).

The initial group size limit was set at 35 members, but some groups exceeded this due to increased community demand by meeting 6. By the end of the pilot, group sizes were as follows: group 1–35 participants, group 2–40 participants, group 3–46 participants, group 4–35 participants and group 5–48 participants. The limit was capped in groups 2 and 4, and uncapped in others to examine how larger group sizes may work.

There were some concerns around demand, such as negative community perceptions, financial difficulties, poor past experiences and a lack of perceived benefits, which left some potential members feeling disconnected from group objectives and hesitant to attend. Participants mentioned challenges such as leadership issues, meeting times and locations, and distrust due to perceived mismanagement as factors that may limit the use of PLA-D. Additional barriers included gender roles and the inclusion of people without disabilities as demonstrated by the excerpt:

My husband said “now even people without disabilities are allowed to join the group and yet they had said that the group is for people with disabilities. Is that really a group?”… I left the group because my husband stopped me. (Participant, female)

Time demands from work or farming were also reported to potentially limit the uptake of PLA-D in the future. Mistrust, such as fears of losing savings, and health problems posed barriers to attendance. Facilitators noted that communication issues, members’ financial struggles and fatigue may lead to disengagement in future groups. Despite these obstacles, participants stayed committed to their shared goals and PLA-D was generally viewed as suitable, satisfying and attractive to programme recipients.

Implementation

Five PLA-D groups were facilitated by five facilitators and five co-facilitators, who were people with disabilities and caregivers of people with disabilities. Co-facilitators were included in the programme to ensure continuity, as our initial plan only included five facilitators. In addition to selecting their facilitators, each group appointed a four-member committee comprising a chairperson, secretary, treasurer and welfare officer to help manage group activities, develop ownership and strengthen local leadership. This structure empowered both the groups and facilitators by fostering self-governance, shared responsibility and equitable decision-making. These positions were generally filled by people with disabilities.

Trainers from Amref Health Africa in Uganda and the MRC/UVRI & LSHTM Uganda Research Unit conducted two 4-day training sessions (the first at the programme’s initiation and the second at its midpoint) for group facilitators, supervisors, and managers at the subcounty headquarters. These sessions focused on enhancing disability knowledge and equipping participants with PLA approaches to support group and community engagement.

Throughout the programme, monthly group monitoring visits and peer learning sessions were conducted to reinforce facilitators’ and supervisors’ skills. These sessions aimed to strengthen their capacity, provide ongoing support, and ensure consistency and adherence to the PLA approach in group meetings. The groups ran between May 2023 and March 2024, with 3 months of implementation of solutions. The solutions implemented included establishing income-generating activities (ie, brick making, poultry farming, crop cultivation), raising funds to transport healthcare workers to the village to conduct a medical camp, and lobbying the subcounty leadership to grade the road to the health facility for improved accessibility.

The PLA-D programme showed potential for successful delivery. The programme was largely delivered with fidelity, as demonstrated by the direct observation, and implementers made progress towards fostering unity and commitment within the group. Participants also expressed the desire for broader implementation to ensure all people with disabilities could benefit. All groups joined a savings plan group and all groups participated in income-generating activities, such as brick making and poultry farming. There were also interactions with the health service, where some healthcare workers attended the groups to share information.

There were, however, some challenges in execution. Facilitators worked hard to accommodate the varying pace at which participants understood the material and offered additional support and reminders to ensure all members stayed engaged. The programme experienced issues with late attendance and hesitancy to participate. These barriers were overcome with strong support from both facilitators and group committees. However, logistical issues such as frequent changes in meeting venues impacted morale and attendance, in particular for participants with limited access to transportation. Consistent venue availability and better engagement strategies could further enhance the programme’s delivery.

Participants also suggested further improvements. Adults with disabilities called for increased financial support to expand savings and income-generating activities, more resources such as assistive devices, and enhanced continuous education and advocacy in health services and disability awareness. Similarly, caregivers requested greater financial assistance, improved livelihood opportunities, and better access to health and education services for children with disabilities. Participants proposed incorporating health initiatives, such as health camps or outreach programmes, inviting healthcare workers to meetings, and establishing links to services to improve overall health outcomes and strengthen community support systems:

Some of our peers with disabilities cannot reach the health facilities because they are far away…[healthcare workers] should offer us treatment from the community because some of our colleagues find it hard to go to Bukanga health facility. If healthcare workers come to our group, we will receive treatment, and that will make us happy and healthy. (Participant, Female FGD)

Some participants also recommended restricting group membership to a single village and separating the PLA-D from the savings scheme. They advised that more stable and accessible venues were needed as occasionally the meeting place changed. Group members reported several challenges affecting their participation. These included financial difficulties, mobility problems, and interpersonal conflicts. Some participants suggested a less inclusive composition:

I suggest that persons without disabilities should be excluded from the group so that it remains with persons with disability only. In my view, the people fighting our facilitator are people who recently joined. (Participants family FGD)

We did not initially plan for Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLA) to be essential for all groups. However, on request, we supported every group to establish one. This initiative was linked to the income-generating activities such as poultry farming, crop cultivation, brick making proposed by the groups. The VSLA consisted of group members, who contributed a weekly savings of as low as Uganda shillings 2000 (approximately US$0.50) to a shared fund. This adjustment to the implementation plan ensured all participants had access to financial support opportunities. This shared fund approach promoted financial independence and created a safety net for group members particularly in the case of medical emergencies from which they could borrow, interest-free, to cover healthcare expenses such as transportation and medication.

Practicality

The practicality of PLA-D was facilitated by holding the group sessions at suitable locations in the community, including places of worship such as churches, schools, health facilities and members’ homes. Costs that were covered by the programme included transport and airtime for facilitators. All groups ran the allocated sessions. Costs for refreshments, although not planned, were also provided. As part of the project, Amref Health Africa in Uganda provided each group Uganda shillings 400 000 (US$106) as seed money towards their initiative to address the identified barriers. This seed financing to the savings plan was instrumental in addressing the real-time feedback given by the groups and facilitators:

With close monitoring and support, we could get feedback and make changes. For example, we realised early on that refreshments were key—people kept asking, 'What will we eat?' Also, some meetings took a second day because there was a lot of discussion to cover, so we had to step in and support coordination. Amref co-ordinator

Some participants felt that the sessions were too long, and the time of day had an impact on attendance and engagement. Time management of the facilitators was generally good and the content was generally finished within the allocated time. However, some sessions ran longer than expected due to the amount of content covered during those meetings. There was a preference to hold the meetings in the afternoon after household and farming responsibilities.

Balanced participation within groups was encouraged through a pair of facilitators, who brought different perspectives to guiding discussions and decision-making. Both facilitators were mentored and this support helped maintain the programme quality and improve facilitator skills. Sessions were organised in a semi-circular seating arrangement to ensure all members were included and to enable the facilitator to actively engage all members. Groups also elected a four-member committee (chairperson, secretary, treasurer, welfare officer), with these positions most often filled by people with disabilities, to encourage local leadership and shared responsibility. Facilitators actively encouraged contributions from quieter members while managing dominant voices.

The predominant barriers identified to accessing healthcare services were poverty related and therefore income generating activities were often adopted, such as brick-making and chicken rearing. However, groups required ongoing guidance and support to identify steps in order to implement their solutions. A cost analysis for each group was not conducted.

Limited efficacy

In the endline survey, participants reported improvement in health outcomes after participating in the PLA-D programme, although this sample was not powered to detect significant differences. Among adults, the percentage needing healthcare in the last 3 months decreased from 78% pre-PLA-D to 58% post-PLA-D, and those receiving care when needed increased from 55% to 75%. The proportion of adults staying overnight in health centres or hospitals did not show a change. For children, the percentage needing healthcare in the last 3 months decreased slightly from 63% to 54%, and those receiving care when needed improved from 71% to 92%, though overnight stays did not show notable change and these changes were not statistically significant (table 3).

Discussion

This pilot study shows that PLA-D is feasible in several key areas. PLA-D was acceptable and well received, as participants valued a programme that directly addressed their needs, especially since there were no prior support groups for people with disabilities. Demand for the programme was high, with strong attendance at meetings. The groups were largely implemented as planned and facilitation of the programme material was consistent. In terms of practicality, having two facilitators that were mentored through delivery of the programme helped to maintain the programme quality and improve facilitator skills. While this study focused on feasibility rather than efficacy, early outcomes from this small sample suggest that over the course of PLA-D, perceived need for healthcare among participants decreased and access to healthcare improved.

As this was a feasibility study, it was not designed or powered to detect statistically significant changes in health outcomes or healthcare access, particularly among children with disabilities, where the sample size was small. The quantitative findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of effectiveness. Although some outcomes showed favourable patterns, including reported access to healthcare, future adequately powered studies are needed to assess whether PLA-D leads to sustained improvements in healthcare access and well-being among adults and children with disabilities.

While the endline survey data showed high levels of satisfaction with PLA-D, qualitative findings revealed occasional challenges such as mistrust, logistical barriers and tensions over group composition. This apparent discrepancy may reflect participants’ valuing of the programme’s overall benefits despite experiencing specific difficulties. The satisfaction may have been driven by the perceived value of the intervention in a context where opportunities for participation, support and inclusion are limited. The implementation of PLA-D therefore shows promising feasibility, supporting its potential as an effective model to improve the health of people with disabilities. These findings align with the broader literature on inclusive health interventions for people with disabilities, in particular the ‘nothing about us without us’ ethos of the disability movement and the need for participatory interventions. Future iterations of the PLA-D intervention should therefore retain elements that foster engagement and perceived benefit, while more directly addressing the structural and contextual barriers that limit consistent participation. Additionally, while the inclusion of community members without disabilities aligns with the participatory ethos of the PLA approach, our findings suggest that mixed-group composition introduced important tensions around voice, representation and power within group discussions. This highlights a key feasibility consideration that achieving inclusivity does not automatically translate into equitable participation and requires careful facilitation of inclusive community dialogue where persons with disabilities can discuss sensitive issues safely and without feeling overshadowed during the implementation of PLA-D. Such an approach could preserve the benefits of community-wide engagement while strengthening the intervention’s inclusivity and acceptability.

The findings from this mixed-methods study support the assumption that more appropriate programmes are developed when informed by research that identifies and addresses the unique challenges faced by the target group. Co-designed interventions that reflect the needs and preferences of those affected are more likely to be appropriate and impactful.33 There are a few limited examples of how these approaches have been used to improve the health of people with disabilities in LMICs. For example, the W-DARE project used participatory action research to improve sexual and reproductive health access to healthcare for women with disabilities.23 This involved creating five impairment-specific groups for women with disabilities and one group for parents of children with disabilities, which met biweekly over ten sessions to identify and address key healthcare access issues. This demonstrated the value of involving people with disabilities in identifying barriers and shaping locally relevant responses. The project’s qualitative evaluation reported positive outcomes, including improved knowledge and confidence among women to seek services.34 In Nepal, a co-designed intervention to improve menstrual hygiene management for women with intellectual disabilities was found to be feasible and acceptable.35 36 In Brazil and Colombia, a co-designed parent support group was created using a participatory community-based approach and it was shown to empower families of children with congenital Zika syndrome.37 38

However, PLA-D differs in important ways. First, it has a broader disability focus, engaging persons with diverse impairments rather than focusing on a specific subgroup or health condition. Second, it focuses on general healthcare access, an area where persons with disabilities face multiple and intersecting barriers, including inaccessible facilities, poor provider awareness, transport constraints, stigma and weak referral linkages. Third, PLA-D applies the PLA model within rural Ugandan communities, creating a structured process through which persons with disabilities, caregivers, community members and local stakeholders can jointly identify problems and co-develop practical solutions.27 In this sense, PLA-D contributes to the literature by extending participatory disability-inclusive approaches beyond condition-specific or population-specific interventions towards a broader community-based model for addressing disability-related healthcare barriers. This positions PLA-D as a potentially scalable community-based approach for addressing disability-specific barriers to healthcare in low-resource settings, while also highlighting the need for careful facilitation to ensure that broad community participation does not dilute the voices and priorities of persons with disabilities.

More broadly, our results align with studies on similar participatory approaches to improve health. The PLA approach has been shown to be effective and scalable. PLA was associated with a 22% reduction in maternal mortality and a 20% reduction in neonatal mortality,16 by convening women’s groups to identify healthcare barriers, develop solutions and evaluate outcomes.16 Similarly to our pilot results, many of the solutions implemented in PLA address financial barriers (eg, creating emergency funds for medical care) as well as improving access through other means (eg, arranging bicycle ambulances and lobbying local health authorities for more staff).

PLA has also been successful in addressing diabetes care in Bangladesh.39 Groups encourage collaborative problem-solving and shared decision-making, and these processes align with the importance of co-creating health solutions. Previous examples of PLA show that facilitated group activities helped participants work together and identify shared goals, which created positive support and improved confidence and social support, and these also seemed to be key mechanisms in our pilot study.

The strengths of this feasibility study include its reflective practice to guide study processes, along with a strong methodology that incorporated real-time feedback, such as incorporating two facilitators and ensuring all groups accessed a loan saving scheme. This feedback helped develop a programme that was both culturally and linguistically appropriate. We used a mixed-methods approach (eg, questionnaires, interviews, focus groups) to assess feasibility, allowing us to cross-check data from different sources. For example, participant acceptability was evaluated through satisfaction scores, interviews and focus groups. However, the study also has limitations to consider when interpreting the results. The small sample size prevents us from drawing definitive conclusions about the programme’s efficacy, despite promising results related to healthcare needs for adults with disabilities. The qualitative interviews were conducted primarily with participants who were engaged in the intervention, which may have led to an overrepresentation of more positive experiences and perspectives. We also recognise that individuals who discontinued participation or were less engaged may have had different experiences that are not fully captured in this study. The findings are not intended to be statistically generalisable but rather to provide contextually grounded insights that may be transferable to similar settings.

Additionally, we did not fully explore why participants dropped out of the group, limiting our understanding of the programme’s acceptability, demand and practicality. Finally, since the study focused on those already in the programme, we could not explore why those who did not enrol chose not to participate in PLA-D. These factors will both be addressed in a future RCT.

Establishing feasibility according to the complete Bowen framework, will require future studies to test PLA-D in new environments (adaption), consider how it can be included into an existing infrastructure (integration), examine the extent to which it can be expanded to other populations or settings (expansion) and test the efficacy of the intervention in a future larger-scale trial. Our study highlights the feasibility for participatory groups with a health focus among people with disabilities, but also identifies potential improvements to the PLA-D design. For example, we did not initially anticipate that VSLAs were needed. The need for seed funding during the implementation phase emerged as an important implementation consideration. Although seed funding is not commonly included in standard PLA groups, the provision of UGX 400,000 per group appeared to support early group engagement and collective action. This may be especially important in PLA-D, given the high levels of poverty and economic exclusion experienced by many people with disabilities.40 In the future, for PLA-D to be scalable, sustained buy-in from government, NGOs and community structures will be essential. While a formal economic evaluation was beyond the scope of this feasibility study, these findings provide important preliminary insights into the resources, adaptations and partnerships that would be required for future scale-up.

Conclusions

PLA-D shows promising potential in five key areas of feasibility. The groups have potential to be an important tool for community health and social support services for people with disabilities in Uganda.
