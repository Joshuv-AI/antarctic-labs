Adaptive Memory Crystallization for Autonomous AI Agent Learning in Dynamic Environments
Rajat Khanda, Mohammad Baqar, Sambuddha Chakrabarti, Satyasaran Changdar

Abstract
Autonomous AI agents operating in dynamic environments face a persistent challenge: acquiring new capabilities without erasing prior knowledge. We present Adaptive Memory Crystallization (AMC) , a memory architecture for progressive experience consolidation in continual reinforcement learning. AMC is conceptually inspired by the qualitative structure of synaptic tagging and capture (STC) theory, the idea that memories transition through discrete stability phases, but makes no claim to model the underlying molecular or synaptic mechanisms. AMC models memory as a continuous crystallization process in which experiences migrate from plastic to stable states according to a multi-objective utility signal. The framework introduces a three-phase memory hierarchy ( Liquid–Glass–Crystal ) governed by an Itô stochastic differential equation (SDE) whose population-level behavior is captured by an explicit Fokker–Planck equation admitting a closed-form Beta stationary distribution. We provide proofs of: (i) well-posedness and global convergence of the crystallization SDE to a unique Beta stationary distribution; (ii) exponential convergence of individual crystallization states to their fixed points, with explicit rates and variance bounds; and (iii) end-to-end Q-learning error bounds and matching memory-capacity lower bounds that link SDE parameters directly to agent performance. Empirical evaluation on Meta-World MT50, Atari 20-game sequential learning, and MuJoCo continual locomotion consistently shows improvements in forward transfer ( + 34 +34 – 43 % 43\% over the strongest baseline), reductions in catastrophic forgetting ( 67 67 – 80 % 80\% ), and a 62 % 62\% decrease in memory footprint.



I
Introduction

Autonomous AI agents deployed in open-ended settings—robotics,
autonomous driving, adaptive software—must continuously acquire new skills
while preserving prior competencies. This
stability–plasticity

dilemma
[
16
]
is widely regarded as the central
unsolved problem in lifelong machine learning.

Standard deep reinforcement learning (RL) agents rely on a fixed-size
experience-replay buffer
[
25
]
combined with stochastic
gradient descent. When the task distribution shifts, gradient updates on
new data overwrite weights encoding old behaviors—a phenomenon known as

catastrophic forgetting

[
29
]
.
Existing mitigations fall into three families:

regularization
methods
[
21
,
47
]
, which add parameter-protection penalties;

dynamic architecture
methods
[
36
,
27
]
, which grow or partition network capacity; and

memory-replay
methods
[
37
,
3
,
34
]
, which curate which
experiences to replay. Each family has known failure modes for
long-lived agents (Section
II
).

Neuroscience offers a complementary design principle:

synaptic consolidation

[
9
]
. Under the
Synaptic Tagging and Capture (STC) hypothesis
[
13
]
,
early-phase LTP provides a short-lived synaptic tag; only experiences
that trigger plasticity-related protein (PRP) synthesis elevate those
tags into durable late-phase LTP. The resulting multi-timescale memory
hierarchy
[
28
,
23
]
confers both
rapid acquisition and long-term stability.

Our contribution.
We introduce
Adaptive Memory
Crystallization (AMC)
, which operationalizes STC dynamics for deep RL
agents. Each buffered experience
e_{i}
carries a scalar

crystallization state

c_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
that evolves according to a
utility-driven SDE. The crystallization state gates both the per-sample
learning rate and the buffer eviction policy, producing a
Liquid–Glass–Crystal memory hierarchy that enforces the
stability–plasticity trade-off without growing the model.

Key contributions:

1.

Rigorous SDE formulation
(Section
III
):
complete well-posedness proof for the crystallization SDE, closed-form
stationary distribution via Fokker–Planck analysis, and exponential
convergence bounds with explicit rates.

2.

Three-phase architecture
(Section
IV
):
Liquid, Glass, and Crystal buffers with capacity fractions and
phase-modulated learning rates justified by the SDE dynamics; the
crystal-buffer fraction exceeds the error-bound minimum given by
Corollary
V.11
while satisfying the coverage
constraint of Theorem
V.9
.

3.

Agent-level convergence and capacity
(Section
V
):
end-to-end bound linking crystallization parameters to Q-learning error;
matching lower bound on memory capacity.

4.

Empirical validation
(Section
VI
):
systematic evaluation on Meta-World MT50
[
46
]
, Atari

[
5
]
, and MuJoCo
[
43
]
with
50-seed statistics and Holm–Bonferroni-corrected significance tests.

5.

Interpretability analysis
(Section
VII
):
visualization of crystallization dynamics and comparison with biological
consolidation timescales
[
13
]
.

II
Related Work

Regularization.

EWC
[
21
]
penalizes changes to Fisher-important
weights; SI
[
47
]
estimates importance via path
integrals; MAS
[
1
]
uses activation gradients. All
share a fundamental limitation: the constraint matrix grows with task
count, which can severely limit plasticity at scale and, in practice,
leads to negligible forward transfer on long task sequences

[
8
]
.

Dynamic architectures.

Progressive Neural Networks
[
36
]
allocate a new
column per task; PackNet
[
27
]
iteratively prunes and
freezes subnetworks. Memory cost grows
O(T)
in the number of tasks

T
, which becomes impractical beyond moderate task counts for
resource-constrained agents.

Replay methods.

Uniform replay (ER)
[
25
]
breaks temporal correlation but
gives no consolidation signal. PER
[
37
]
weights
by TD-error but ignores long-term relevance. HER
[
3
]

relabels goals but is restricted to goal-conditioned tasks.
NEC
[
34
]
stores full trajectories non-parametrically
but scales poorly. While several methods use prioritized sampling
(PER) or explicit episodic memory (NEC), none introduces a
continuous, utility-driven
stabilization
mechanism that
progressively locks in experiences according to their long-term value.

Biologically-inspired memory.

Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) theory
[
28
,
23
]
distinguishes fast hippocampal and slow cortical
learning but does not specify consolidation dynamics. Prior work on
STDP
[
6
]
operates at the synaptic level; our
contribution is a
buffer-level
consolidation mechanism for deep RL.

AMC vs. prior work.

Unlike regularization, AMC consolidates
data
rather than

parameters
, so the constraint set does not grow. Unlike
architectural methods, AMC uses fixed-size buffers. Unlike existing
replay methods, AMC provides a principled, utility-driven stability signal
grounded in STC theory and analyzed with formal convergence guarantees.

III-B

Problem Setting

Let
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)
be an MDP

[
40
]
. The agent seeks a policy

\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
maximising

J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r_{t}]
.
In the
continual learning
setting the agent faces a sequence of
MDPs
\{\mathcal{M}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
arriving one after another; we drop
explicit task indices where clear.

At each step the agent collects
e_{t}=(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})
and stores
it in a finite buffer. The buffer has total capacity
N
and must serve
learning across all tasks seen so far.

III-C

Crystallization State and Well-Posedness

Definition III.1

(Crystallization State)
.

Each buffered experience
e_{i}
is associated with a
crystallization
state

c_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
.
c_{i}=0
represents a fully plastic (liquid)
memory;
c_{i}=1
a fully stable (crystal) memory.

Definition III.2

(Multi-Objective Utility)
.

For experience
e_{i}=(s,a,r,s^{\prime})
the utility at time
t
is

U_{i}(t)=w_{1}\,\delta_{i}(t)+w_{2}\,N_{i}(t)+w_{3}\,V_{i}(t),\;w_{j}\geq 0,\;\textstyle\sum_{j}w_{j}=1,

(1)

where the three components are:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\bigl|r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{\theta}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{\theta}(s,a)\bigr|,

(2)

\displaystyle N_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\!\bigl(-n(s,a)/Z\bigr),

(3)

\displaystyle V_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{e_{j}\in\mathrm{kNN}(e_{i})}[\delta_{j}(t)].

(4)

Here
n(s,a)
is the visitation count of
(s,a)
,
Z>0
a normalisation
constant, and
\mathrm{kNN}(e_{i})
the
k
nearest successor experiences
in state space.

Definition III.3

(Crystallization SDE)
.

The crystallization state of experience
e_{i}
evolves according to the
Itô SDE on
[0,1]
:

dc_{i}=\underbrace{\bigl[\alpha\,U_{i}(t)(1-c_{i})-\beta\,c_{i}\,I_{i}(t)\bigr]}_{\text{drift }f(c_{i},t)}\,dt+\underbrace{\sigma\sqrt{c_{i}(1-c_{i})}}_{\text{diffusion }g(c_{i})}\,dW_{t},

(5)

where
\alpha,\beta>0
are consolidation and decrystallization rates,

\sigma\geq 0
is the noise coefficient,
W_{t}
is a standard Wiener
process, and
I_{i}(t)\in\{0,1\}
is an interference indicator

I_{i}(t)=\mathbf{1}\bigl[\exists\,e_{j}:\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|+\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|<\varepsilon,\;|r_{i}-r_{j}|>\delta_{r}\bigr].

(6)

Theorem III.4

(Well-Posedness and Non-Explosion)
.

Let
U_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
be measurable and uniformly bounded, and let

I_{i}(t)\in\{0,1\}
. Then:

(i)

(Existence and uniqueness.)
The SDE (
5
)
has a unique strong solution
c_{i}(t)
for every initial condition

c_{i}(0)\in[0,1]
.

(ii)

(Invariance.)
The interval
[0,1]
is positively
invariant:
c_{i}(0)\in[0,1]\Rightarrow c_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
a.s. for all

t\geq 0
.

Proof.

The SDE (
5
) belongs to the class of
Jacobi
(or
Wright–Fisher) diffusions on
[0,1]

[
19
]
.
We invoke well-posedness results for this class and verify the required
conditions.

(i) Existence and uniqueness.

Write
dc_{i}=f(c_{i},t)\,dt+g(c_{i})\,dW_{t}
.

Interior local Lipschitz condition.

On any compact
[a,b]\subset(0,1)
with
0<a<b<1
, the drift satisfies

|f(c,t)-f(c^{\prime},t)|\leq(\alpha+\beta)|c-c^{\prime}|
(global Lipschitz in
c
, since

U,I\in[0,1]
). The diffusion
g(c)=\sigma\sqrt{c(1-c)}
satisfies

|g(c)-g(c^{\prime})|\leq(\sigma/2)|c-c^{\prime}|/\sqrt{a(1-b)}
, which is Lipschitz on

[a,b]
. Hence
f
and
g
are locally Lipschitz on the
interior

(0,1)
.

Boundary behaviour.

At
c=0
:
g(0)=0
and
f(0,t)=\alpha U_{i}(t)\geq 0
.
At
c=1
:
g(1)=0
and
f(1,t)=-\beta I_{i}(t)\leq 0
.
Both boundaries are
entrance boundaries
in the Feller sense
(the process reaches them with zero probability under our parameters
and is pushed inward by the drift), so the standard
Existence-Uniqueness Theorem extends to the closed interval
[0,1]
;
see
[
19
]
, Chapter VI.

Linear growth on
\mathbb{R}
.

Extending
f
and
g
to
\mathbb{R}
by setting
f\equiv 0
and

g\equiv 0
outside
[0,1]
:
|f(c,t)|\leq(\alpha+\beta)(1+|c|)
and

|g(c)|\leq(\sigma/2)(1+|c|)
, so linear growth holds globally.
By
[
48
]
Theorem 5.2.1, a unique strong solution
exists with
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
a.s.

(ii) Invariance.

Apply the comparison theorem for SDEs
[
19
]
: since

g(0)=g(1)=0
and the drift points strictly inward at both boundaries,
any solution starting in
[0,1]
remains in
[0,1]
a.s. Formally, let

\underline{c}\equiv 0
(resp.
\bar{c}\equiv 1
) be the constant lower
(resp. upper) barrier; the drift conditions
f(0,t)\geq 0
and

f(1,t)\leq 0
ensure the comparison holds pathwise.
∎

III-D

Fokker–Planck Analysis

Assumption III.5

(Timescale Separation)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be the ratio of the consolidation timescale to the
utility update timescale. Formally, rescale time so that

c_{i}(\cdot)
evolves on
O(1)
time units while
U_{i}(\cdot)
and

I_{i}(\cdot)
evolve on
O(\varepsilon)
time units (
\varepsilon\ll 1
).
Assume
U_{i}(\cdot)
and
I_{i}(\cdot)
are ergodic Markov processes with
unique invariant measures of averages
\bar{U}
and
\bar{I}
, and that
all moments are bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
.

Proposition III.6

(Averaging Principle)
.

Under Assumption
III.5
, the slow component
c_{i}(t)

converges weakly as
\varepsilon\to 0
to the solution of the
averaged SDE

dc_{i}=\bigl[\alpha\bar{U}(1-c_{i})-\beta\bar{I}\,c_{i}\bigr]\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{c_{i}(1-c_{i})}\,dW_{t},

(7)

with error of order
O(\varepsilon)
in the weak topology over
any finite time interval
[0,T]
.

Proof.

This is an instance of the stochastic averaging principle for
fast–slow systems
[
33
]
. The fast process

(U_{i}(t/\varepsilon),I_{i}(t/\varepsilon))
satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem 11.1 in
[
33
]
(ergodicity, bounded
moments, and uniformly Lipschitz coupling); the conclusion is exactly
weak convergence to the averaged SDE (
7
) at rate

O(\varepsilon)
.

In our setting,
\varepsilon\approx 1/5000
(one consolidation per

\sim
5000 training steps), giving an averaging error of order

2\times 10^{-4}
per unit of slow time. This is negligible for the
stationary distribution analysis of Theorem
III.9
.
∎

Remark III.7
.

The averaging principle above converts the non-stationary SDE
(
5
) into the time-homogeneous averaged SDE
(
7
). All subsequent analysis (Fokker–Planck equation,
stationary distribution, convergence bounds) applies to
(
7
), with the understanding that results hold for the
original system up to the
O(\varepsilon)
averaging error.

Let
p(c,t)
denote the probability density of
c_{i}(t)
.

Theorem III.8

(Fokker–Planck Equation)
.

Under the dynamics (
5
) and Assumption
III.5
,
the density
p(c,t)
evolves on the slow crystallization timescale as

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial c}\!\bigl[\mu(c)\,p\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial c^{2}}\!\bigl[D(c)\,p\bigr],

(8)

with
averaged
drift
\mu(c)=\alpha\bar{U}(1-c)-\beta c\bar{I}

and diffusion
D(c)=\sigma^{2}c(1-c)
,
where
\bar{U}
and
\bar{I}
are the ergodic averages of
Assumption
III.5
.

Proof.

By Proposition
III.6
,
c_{i}(t)
is described (up to

O(\varepsilon)
averaging error) by the time-homogeneous averaged SDE
(
7
) with constant coefficients
\bar{U}
and
\bar{I}
.
Standard Itô–Fokker–Planck theory
[
35
]
then
applies directly to (
7
).

Applying Itô’s formula to
\phi(c_{i}(t))
for any test function

\phi\in C_{c}^{2}(0,1)
:

\displaystyle d\phi(c_{i})

\displaystyle=\bigl[\phi^{\prime}(c_{i})\mu(c_{i})+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})\sigma^{2}c_{i}(1-c_{i})\bigr]dt

\displaystyle\quad+\phi^{\prime}(c_{i})\sigma\sqrt{c_{i}(1-c_{i})}\,dW_{t},

where
\mu(c)=\alpha\bar{U}(1-c)-\beta\bar{I}\,c
.
Taking expectations and integrating by parts twice yields (
8
)
for the averaged dynamics, exact up to
O(\varepsilon)
.
∎

Theorem III.9

(Unique Stationary Distribution)
.

Suppose
\sigma>0
. Define

\rho=(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})/\sigma^{2}
.
The Fokker–Planck equation (
8
) admits a unique stationary
density
p_{\infty}
on
(0,1)
given by

p_{\infty}(c)=Z^{-1}\,c^{A-1}(1-c)^{B-1},\qquad A=\frac{2\alpha\bar{U}}{\sigma^{2}},\quad B=\frac{2\beta\bar{I}}{\sigma^{2}},

(9)

i.e., a Beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}(A,B)
, where

Z=B(A,B)
is the Beta function. Consequently

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\infty}}[c]=A/(A+B)=\alpha\bar{U}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})
.

Proof.

Existence and reversibility.

The averaged SDE (
7
) is a Jacobi (Wright–Fisher)
diffusion on
(0,1)
with
D(0)=D(1)=0
. Both endpoints are either
entrance or exit boundaries in the Feller classification

[
19
]
; specifically, with
\mu(0)=\alpha\bar{U}>0

and
\mu(1)=-\beta\bar{I}<0
, both endpoints are
entrance boundaries
:
the process is pushed away from each endpoint and never reaches them
in finite time. For such diffusions, the Fokker–Planck operator is
self-adjoint with reflecting boundary conditions, implying
detailed
balance
(
J\equiv 0
)
[
35
]
. Setting
J=0
:

\frac{d}{dc}\ln p_{\infty}(c)=\frac{2\mu(c)}{D(c)}-\frac{D^{\prime}(c)}{D(c)}.

(10)

Substituting
\mu(c)=\alpha\bar{U}(1-c)-\beta c\bar{I}
and

D(c)=\sigma^{2}c(1-c)
(so
D^{\prime}(c)=\sigma^{2}(1-2c)
):

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu(c)}{D(c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2[\alpha\bar{U}(1-c)-\beta\bar{I}\,c]}{\sigma^{2}c(1-c)}=\frac{2\alpha\bar{U}}{\sigma^{2}c}-\frac{2\beta\bar{I}}{\sigma^{2}(1-c)},

\displaystyle\frac{D^{\prime}(c)}{D(c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-2c)}{\sigma^{2}c(1-c)}=\frac{1}{c}-\frac{1}{1-c}.

Substituting into (
10
) and collecting
terms over
c
and
(1-c)
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dc}\ln p_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{2\alpha\bar{U}}{\sigma^{2}}-1\Bigr)\frac{1}{c}-\Bigl(\frac{2\beta\bar{I}}{\sigma^{2}}-1\Bigr)\frac{1}{1-c}

\displaystyle=\frac{A-1}{c}-\frac{B-1}{1-c},

where the last line uses
A=2\alpha\bar{U}/\sigma^{2}
and

B=2\beta\bar{I}/\sigma^{2}
by definition (
9
).
Integrating:
\ln p_{\infty}(c)=(A-1)\ln c+(B-1)\ln(1-c)+\mathrm{const}
,
giving
p_{\infty}(c)\propto c^{A-1}(1-c)^{B-1}
.
This is integrable on
(0,1)
if and only if
A,B>0
, i.e.
\alpha\bar{U}>0
and
\beta\bar{I}>0
, which holds for any positive
parameters
\alpha,\beta,\bar{U},\bar{I}>0
regardless of
\sigma
.

Uniqueness.
The diffusion
D(c)>0
for
c\in(0,1)
, so the
operator is uniformly elliptic on every compact sub-interval. By the
Perron–Frobenius theorem for Fokker–Planck operators

[
35
]
, the null space of the adjoint generator is
one-dimensional, giving uniqueness.

Convergence.
By Bakry–Émery theory
[
4
]
,
the spectral gap of the generator is positive (the drift is
\log
-concave
near the stationary mean), giving exponential convergence

\|p(\cdot,t)-p_{\infty}\|_{L^{1}}\leq Ce^{-\lambda t}
for some
\lambda>0
.
∎

Corollary III.10

(Phase Occupancy at Stationarity)
.

Under
p_{\infty}=\mathrm{Beta}(A,B)
with
A,B>0
, the stationary
fraction of time a
single
experience
e_{i}
spends in each phase is

\displaystyle\pi_{L}

\displaystyle=I_{{\tau_{L}}}(A,B),

(11)

\displaystyle\pi_{G}

\displaystyle=I_{{\tau_{C}}}(A,B)-I_{{\tau_{L}}}(A,B),

(12)

\displaystyle\pi_{C}

\displaystyle=1-I_{{\tau_{C}}}(A,B),

(13)

where
I_{x}(A,B)
is the regularised incomplete Beta function.

Remark III.11
.

At the default operating parameters (
\alpha=0.05
,
\beta=0.005
,

\sigma=0.005
,
\bar{U}=0.5
,
\bar{I}=0.1
), one obtains
A=2000
,

B=40
, and
c^{*}=A/(A+B)\approx 0.98
. Since
c^{*}\gg\tau_{C}
, a fully
consolidated individual experience spends nearly all time in the crystal
phase (

). The buffer capacity fractions

N_{L}\!:\!N_{G}\!:\!N_{C}\!=\!10\!:\!5\!:\!1
are therefore an

engineering design choice
that reserves capacity for incoming
liquid and transitioning glass experiences, not a direct consequence of
these
\pi
values. At parameter regimes where
c^{*}
straddles
\tau_{C}

(e.g. reduced
\alpha
or increased
\beta
), the fractions
\pi_{L}
,

\pi_{G}
,
\pi_{C}
provide principled guidance for capacity allocation.

III-E

Individual Crystallization Dynamics

Theorem III.12

(Exponential Convergence of Mean)
.

Fix experience
e_{i}
with time-constant utility
U_{i}
and interference

I_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Then:

(i)

\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(t)]\to c_{i}^{*}\triangleq\dfrac{\alpha U_{i}}{\alpha U_{i}+\beta I_{i}}

as
t\to\infty
.

(ii)

|\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(t)]-c_{i}^{*}|\leq|c_{i}(0)-c_{i}^{*}|\,e^{-\lambda_{i}t}
,
where
\lambda_{i}=\alpha U_{i}+\beta I_{i}>0
.

(iii)

\operatorname{Var}[c_{i}(t)]\leq\dfrac{\sigma^{2}c_{i}^{*}(1-c_{i}^{*})}{2\lambda_{i}}\cdot(1-e^{-2\lambda_{i}t})\leq\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{8\lambda_{i}}
.

Proof.

(i) and (ii) Mean dynamics.

Let
m(t)=\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(t)]
. Taking expectations of (
5
)
(the diffusion term has zero expectation):

\dot{m}(t)=\alpha U_{i}(1-m)-\beta I_{i}m=-\lambda_{i}\bigl(m-c_{i}^{*}\bigr).

(14)

This is a linear ODE with decay rate
\lambda_{i}
; solving gives

m(t)=c_{i}^{*}+(m(0)-c_{i}^{*})e^{-\lambda_{i}t}
, which establishes both (i)
and (ii).

Note: (
14
) holds exactly because the SDE’s drift is
linear in
c_{i}
, so the expectation decouples from higher moments.

(iii) Variance dynamics.

Let
v(t)=\operatorname{Var}[c_{i}(t)]
. Applying Itô’s lemma to

c_{i}^{2}
gives
d(c_{i}^{2})=2c_{i}\,dc_{i}+(dc_{i})^{2}
, so

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\!\bigl[\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]-m^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[c_{i}f(c_{i},t)]+\mathbb{E}[g(c_{i})^{2}]-2m\dot{m}.

Since
f(c,t)=-\lambda_{i}(c-c_{i}^{*})
and using

\mathbb{E}[c_{i}f(c_{i})]=\mathbb{E}[c_{i}\cdot(-\lambda_{i}(c_{i}-c_{i}^{*}))]=-\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]-c_{i}^{*}m)
:

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda_{i}(\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]-c_{i}^{*}m)+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(1-c_{i})]-2m\dot{m}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda_{i}v+\sigma^{2}(m-m^{2}-v).

Thus
\dot{v}\leq-2\lambda_{i}v+\sigma^{2}m(1-m)\leq-2\lambda_{i}v+\sigma^{2}/4
.
By Grönwall’s inequality,

v(t)\leq e^{-2\lambda_{i}t}v(0)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{8\lambda_{i}}(1-e^{-2\lambda_{i}t})
.
Since
v(0)\leq 1/4
(variance of
[0,1]
-valued r.v.), the bound

v(t)\leq\sigma^{2}/(8\lambda_{i})
holds for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

Theorem III.13

(Catastrophic Forgetting Resistance)
.

Suppose
U_{i}
is constant and
I_{i}=1
(worst-case: sustained interference
throughout
[0,T]
). Let
c_{i}(0)=c_{0}\in(\tau_{C},1)
(already crystallized)
and
\lambda_{i}=\alpha U_{i}+\beta>0
.

(Primary bound.)
Using the variance bound of
Theorem
III.12
(iii) and Markov’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl[c_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}\bigr]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}/(8\lambda_{i})}{(c_{i}^{*}-\tau_{L})^{2}},

(15)

where
c_{i}^{*}=\alpha U_{i}/\lambda_{i}
. This bound is
uniform in
T
,
requires no diffusion-domination argument, and is tightest when

c_{i}^{*}\gg\tau_{L}
(i.e., when the drift strongly resists forgetting).

(Supplementary
T
-explicit bound.)
Via stochastic domination
of
\sigma\sqrt{c_{i}(1-c_{i})}
by
\sigma/2
and a Gaussian tail estimate
on the dominated Brownian motion:

\mathbb{P}\!\bigl[c_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}\bigr]\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\lambda_{i}(c_{0}-\tau_{L})^{2}}{\sigma^{2}T}\right)\cdot e^{-\lambda_{i}T/2}.

(16)

This bound is tightest in the regime
\lambda_{i}T=O(1)
and
shows forgetting probability decays with
T
(via the

e^{-\lambda_{i}T/2}
factor). Both bounds confirm that forgetting
is suppressed by drift strength
\lambda_{i}
and the margin

c_{i}^{*}-\tau_{L}
.

Proof.

Primary bound (
15
).

By Chebyshev’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}[c_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}]=\mathbb{P}[c_{i}^{*}-c_{i}(T)>c_{i}^{*}-\tau_{L}]\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}[c_{i}(T)]}{(c_{i}^{*}-\tau_{L})^{2}}.

By Theorem
III.12
(iii),
\operatorname{Var}[c_{i}(T)]\leq\sigma^{2}/(8\lambda_{i})

for all
T\geq 0
, yielding (
15
).

Supplementary bound (
16
).

Let
\Delta=c_{0}-\tau_{L}>0
and
m(t)=\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(t)]
.
By Theorem
III.12
(ii),
m(T)\geq c^{*}+\Delta e^{-\lambda_{i}T}
.

Step 1 (centring).

Define
\tilde{c}_{i}(t)=c_{i}(t)-m(t)
. The centred process satisfies

d\tilde{c}_{i}=\sigma\sqrt{c_{i}(1-c_{i})}\,dW_{t}
(zero drift, same diffusion).

Step 2 (domination).

Since
c_{i}(1-c_{i})\leq 1/4
, the process
\tilde{c}_{i}
is stochastically
dominated pathwise by
B_{t}=(\sigma/2)W_{t}

[
48
]
.

Step 3 (tail bound).

The event
\{c_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}\}
requires

\tilde{c}_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}-m(T)\leq-\Delta(1-e^{-\lambda_{i}T})
.
By the Gaussian reflection principle applied to
B_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}T/4)
:

\mathbb{P}[c_{i}(T)<\tau_{L}]\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\Delta^{2}(1-e^{-\lambda_{i}T})^{2}}{\sigma^{2}T}\right).

Step 4 (simplification).

Using
(1-e^{-x})^{2}\geq xe^{-x}
for
x=\lambda_{i}T>0
:

\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\lambda_{i}\Delta^{2}e^{-\lambda_{i}T}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\lambda_{i}\Delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot Te^{-\lambda_{i}T}\right).

Writing
Te^{-\lambda_{i}T}=T\cdot e^{-\lambda_{i}T/2}\cdot e^{-\lambda_{i}T/2}\geq(T/e)\cdot e^{-\lambda_{i}T/2}
and using
T\geq 1/\lambda_{i}
gives
the product form (
16
).
∎

III-F

Connection to Statistical Physics

The crystallization dynamics admit a natural interpretation in statistical
physics that provides additional theoretical grounding and design intuition.

Freidlin–Wentzell quasi-potential.

For the Jacobi (Wright–Fisher) SDE (
5
), the appropriate
potential is the quasi-potential of Freidlin–Wentzell large-deviation
theory
[
48
]
. Define

\mathcal{F}(\{c_{i}\})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\Bigl[A_{i}\ln c_{i}+B_{i}\ln(1-c_{i})\Bigr],

(17)

where
A_{i}={2\alpha U_{i}}/{\sigma^{2}}
and
B_{i}={2\beta I_{i}}/{\sigma^{2}}
.
The first term penalises departures of
c_{i}
from 1 for high-utility
experiences; the second penalises departures from 0 under sustained
interference. Together they define a potential landscape whose minima
correspond to the two absorbing-boundary regimes
c_{i}\to 0
and
c_{i}\to 1
.

Proposition III.14

(Multiplicative-Noise Langevin Representation)
.

Define the Langevin diffusivity
D_{L}(c)=\sigma^{2}c(1-c)/2
, related to
the Fokker–Planck diffusion of Theorem
III.8
by

D_{\mathrm{FP}}(c)=2D_{L}(c)=\sigma^{2}c(1-c)
.
Under Assumption
III.5
(frozen parameters
U_{i}\equiv\bar{U}
,

I_{i}\equiv\bar{I}
), the SDE (
5
) is equivalent to the
multiplicative-noise Langevin equation

dc_{i}=-D_{L}(c_{i})\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial c_{i}}\,dt+\sqrt{2D_{L}(c_{i})}\,dW_{t},

(18)

with the time-independent potential
\mathcal{F}
of (
17
)
(using
A_{i}=2\alpha\bar{U}/\sigma^{2}
,
B_{i}=2\beta\bar{I}/\sigma^{2}
).
Its unique stationary distribution is the Gibbs measure

p_{\infty}(c)\propto e^{-\mathcal{F}(c)}=c^{A_{i}-1}(1-c)^{B_{i}-1}
,
recovering
\mathrm{Beta}(A_{i},B_{i})
of Theorem
III.9
.

Remark III.15
.

When
U_{i}(t)
and
I_{i}(t)
are time-varying,
\mathcal{F}(c,t)
becomes
time-dependent and the Gibbs-measure interpretation holds only
instantaneously. Under Assumption
III.5
, the potential
self-averages to the time-independent
\mathcal{F}
on the slow
crystallization timescale. This is analogous to Born-Oppenheimer
separation in quantum chemistry: the fast-varying utility “field”
enters the slow
c_{i}
dynamics only through its time average.

The factor-of-two difference between
D_{L}
and
D_{\mathrm{FP}}
is the
standard Itô–Fokker–Planck convention: the SDE noise coefficient

\sigma\sqrt{c(1-c)}
satisfies both

[\sigma\sqrt{c(1-c)}]^{2}=D_{\mathrm{FP}}
and

\sqrt{2D_{L}}=\sigma\sqrt{c(1-c)}
.

Proof.

Compute
-D_{L}(c)\,\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial c
:

\displaystyle-D_{L}(c)\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma^{2}c(1-c)}{2}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{A_{i}}{c}+\frac{B_{i}}{1-c}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bigl[A_{i}(1-c)-B_{i}c\bigr].

Substituting
A_{i}=2\alpha U_{i}/\sigma^{2}
and
B_{i}=2\beta I_{i}/\sigma^{2}
:

=\alpha U_{i}(1-c_{i})-\beta I_{i}c_{i},

which is exactly the drift in (
5
). ✓

The noise coefficient
\sqrt{2D_{L}(c)}=\sigma\sqrt{c(1-c)}
matches
(
5
). ✓

Stationarity: the Fokker–Planck equation for (
18
) has
diffusion
D_{\mathrm{FP}}=2D_{L}
and drift
-D_{L}\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial c=\mu(c)
,
so the stationary ODE is exactly (
10
), already
solved to give
p_{\infty}=\mathrm{Beta}(A_{i},B_{i})
in Theorem
III.9
.
∎

Remark III.16
.

This representation differs from the standard additive-noise Langevin
equation because
D(c)
is state-dependent. In particular, the drift
of (
5
) is
not

-\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial c
but

-D(c)\,\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial c
, a distinction that matters
when interpreting the free-energy landscape.

The shape of
\mathcal{F}
has a useful phase interpretation. The Beta
density
c^{A_{i}-1}(1-c)^{B_{i}-1}
is unimodal when
A_{i},B_{i}>1
, and
U-shaped (bimodal) when
A_{i}<1
or
B_{i}<1
. The crystal-dominant
regime requires
A_{i}>1
, i.e.
\sigma^{2}<2\alpha U_{i}
; the liquid-dominant
regime requires
B_{i}>1
, i.e.
\sigma^{2}<2\beta I_{i}
. Operating with

\sigma
small relative to
\min(\sqrt{2\alpha\bar{U}},\sqrt{2\beta\bar{I}})

ensures the Beta distribution is unimodal and concentrated near

c^{*}=\alpha U_{i}/(\alpha U_{i}+\beta I_{i})
.

III-G

Euler–Maruyama Discretization Error

Proposition III.17

(Discretization Accuracy)
.

The Euler–Maruyama update satisfies strong order
1/2
and weak
order
1
convergence to the true SDE solution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|c_{i}^{\Delta t}(t)-c_{i}(t)|^{2}\bigr]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{str}}\,\Delta t^{1/2},

(19)

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}[\phi(c_{i}^{\Delta t}(T))]-\mathbb{E}[\phi(c_{i}(T))]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{wk}}\,\Delta t,

(20)

for any smooth
\phi
, where
C_{\mathrm{str}},C_{\mathrm{wk}}
depend
on
\alpha,\beta,\sigma,T
but not on
\Delta t
.

Proof.

Standard Euler–Maruyama theory
[
22
]
requires
locally Lipschitz drift and diffusion with linear growth, verified in
Theorem
III.4
. The boundary projection

c\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}(c,0,1)
introduces
O((\Delta t)^{3/2})

error per step (dominated by Euler–Maruyama), preserving the
stated convergence rates.
∎

For
\alpha=0.05
,
\sigma=0.005
, the mean relaxation time is

\lambda_{i}^{-1}\approx 20
steps;
\Delta t=1
(one step per episode)
achieves
<1\%
weak-order error, which is our default setting.

V
Theoretical Analysis

V-A

Assumptions

Assumption V.1

(Lipschitz Value Function)
.

The optimal Q-function
Q^{*}
is
L
-Lipschitz:

|Q^{*}(s,a)-Q^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|\leq L(\|s-s^{\prime}\|+\|a-a^{\prime}\|)
.

Assumption V.2

(Bounded Rewards)
.

|r|\leq R_{\max}
for all
(s,a)
.

Assumption V.3

(Sufficient Exploration)
.

The agent’s policy visits all state-action pairs infinitely often a.s.

Assumption V.4

(Decaying Step Sizes)
.

The per-experience step size decomposes as

\eta_{t}\;=\;\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}\cdot(1-c_{i}(t))^{2},

(22)

where
\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}\downarrow 0
is a deterministic sequence
satisfying the Robbins–Monro conditions

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}=\infty
and

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}^{2}<\infty

(e.g.
\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}=\eta_{0}/(1+\kappa t)
with
\kappa>0
).

Remark V.5
.

For
plastic
experiences (
c_{i}^{*}<\tau_{C}
): by
Theorem
III.12
(i),
c_{i}(t)\to c_{i}^{*}
a.s., so

\eta_{t}=\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}(1-c_{i}^{*})^{2}+o(\eta_{\mathrm{base},t})
.
Since
0<(1-c_{i}^{*})^{2}\leq(1-\tau_{L})^{2}\leq 1
, this sequence inherits the
Robbins–Monro properties of
\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}
:

\sum_{t}\eta_{t}\geq(1-\tau_{C})^{2}\sum_{t}\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}=\infty
and

\sum_{t}\eta_{t}^{2}\leq\sum_{t}\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}^{2}<\infty
.

For
crystal
experiences (
c_{i}^{*}\geq\tau_{C}
):

\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}(1-\tau_{C})^{2}\to 0
. These experiences
do
not
need to satisfy Robbins–Monro; by design they hold stable
estimates and contribute only a bounded bias (Theorem
V.7
).

In implementation,
\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}
is scheduled as a standard
decaying learning rate (e.g. cosine or step decay); the

(1-c_{i})^{2}
factor then modulates it
per experience
according to
the crystallization state at replay time.

Assumption V.6

(Uniform Crystal Coverage)
.

The crystal experiences
\{(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{C}}
are
distributed such that the covering radius

r_{\mathrm{cov}}(N_{C})=\sup_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\min_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\|(s,a)-(s_{i},a_{i})\|
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathrm{cov}}(N_{C})]=O(N_{C}^{-1/2})
.
This holds when crystal experiences are spread over the visited
state-action space with density proportional to visitation frequency.

V-B

Agent Convergence

Theorem V.7

(Q-Learning Convergence Under AMC)
.

Under Assumptions
V.1
–
V.6
, with

\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}\downarrow 0
satisfying Robbins–Monro
(Assumption
V.4
) and crystal-buffer fraction
f_{C}=N_{C}/N
,
the AMC Q-learning agent satisfies

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\bigr]\leq\frac{2\gamma R_{\max}L}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{C}}{\sqrt{N_{C}}},

(23)

where
f_{C}=N_{C}/N
is the crystal-buffer fraction. Under the stationary
distribution of Theorem
III.9
,

f_{C}
is bounded above by

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\infty}}[c_{i}^{*}]=\alpha\bar{U}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})
.

Proof.

Partition the buffer at any time
t
into:

•

Plastic experiences

\mathcal{P}
: those with
c_{i}(t)<\tau_{C}
,
learning rate
\eta_{t}(c_{i})\geq\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}(1-\tau_{C})^{2}>0
.

•

Crystal experiences

\mathcal{C}
: those with
c_{i}(t)\geq\tau_{C}
,
learning rate
\eta_{t}(c_{i})\leq\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}(1-\tau_{C})^{2}\to 0
.

Step 1: Plastic experiences converge.

(Uses Assumptions
V.1
,
V.3
,
V.4
.)
Let
\epsilon_{t}^{\mathcal{P}}=\sup_{e_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}\|Q_{t}(s_{i},\cdot)-Q^{*}(s_{i},\cdot)\|_{\infty}
.
Plastic experiences have step sizes
\eta_{t}\geq\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}(1-\tau_{C})^{2}>0

satisfying Robbins–Monro (Remark
V.5
).

IS correction and convergence.

The stratified replay weights
P(e_{i})\propto|\delta_{i}|^{\nu}
(with IS
correction exponent
\nu=0.4
in Algorithm
2
) introduce
a biased sampling distribution. The IS weight
w_{i}^{\mathrm{IS}}=(N\cdot P(e_{i}))^{-\nu}
, normalised by
\max_{j}w_{j}^{\mathrm{IS}}
,
corrects for this bias. Under Assumption
V.3
(all pairs
visited infinitely often) and the IS correction, the effective
per-pair update frequency is asymptotically uniform:

\sum_{t}\eta_{t}\mathbf{1}[e_{i}\text{ sampled at }t]\to\infty
a.s.
This is the key requirement for Q-learning convergence

[
10
]
; the IS weights do not affect the asymptotic
limit, only the convergence rate
[
37
]
.
Hence
\epsilon_{t}^{\mathcal{P}}\to 0
a.s. as
\eta_{\mathrm{base},t}\to 0
.

Step 2: Crystal bias is bounded.

(Uses Assumption
V.1
.)
Each crystal experience
e_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
was last updated at some time

t_{i}\leq t
when it was still plastic; its Q-estimate satisfies

\|Q_{t_{i}}(s_{i},\cdot)-Q^{*}(s_{i},\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
Under Assumptions
V.1
and
V.6
, the worst-case
bias at a query point
(s,a)
from the nearest crystal point is

L\cdot r_{\mathrm{cov}}(N_{C})
. By Assumption
V.6
,

\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathrm{cov}}]=O(N_{C}^{-1/2})
,
hence expected interpolation bias
O(L/\sqrt{N_{C}})
.
Weighting by the crystal sample fraction
f_{C}=N_{C}/N
:

\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\sup_{(s,a)}|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}Q_{t}(s,a)-\mathcal{T}Q^{*}(s,a)|_{\mathcal{C}}\bigr]\leq f_{C}\cdot\frac{L}{\sqrt{N_{C}}}.

(24)

Step 3: Propagate through Bellman operator.

(Uses Assumption
V.2
.)
By Bellman error propagation
[
10
,
32
]
:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\epsilon_{t}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\cdot\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\sup_{(s,a)}|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}Q_{t}-\mathcal{T}Q^{*}|\bigr].

By Step 1, the plastic sampling error vanishes; the remaining term is the
crystal bias (
24
) times the Bellman contraction factor.
Using
|Q|\leq R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
(Assumption
V.2
) to bound
the single Bellman step:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\epsilon_{t}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{f_{C}\,L}{\sqrt{N_{C}}}=\frac{2\gamma R_{\max}L}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{C}}{\sqrt{N_{C}}}.

∎

Remark V.8
.

The bound (
23
) is minimised by taking
f_{C}
small (few
crystal experiences) and
N_{C}
large (dense crystal coverage). These
objectives conflict:
N_{C}=f_{C}N
, so
f_{C}/\sqrt{N_{C}}=\sqrt{f_{C}/N}
.
The optimal
f_{C}^{*}=\beta\bar{I}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})
(Corollary
V.11
)
achieves the smallest ratio consistent with the per-experience dynamics.
Under the stationary distribution (Theorem
III.9
), the
long-run mean crystallization level is
c^{*}=\alpha\bar{U}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})
,
which equals
1-f_{C}^{*}
. Increasing
\beta
(faster decrystallization)
simultaneously lowers
c^{*}
, reduces
f_{C}^{*}
, and tightens the bound.

V-C

Memory Capacity Bound

Theorem V.9

(Memory Capacity: Upper and Lower Bounds)
.

(Sufficient condition.)

To achieve
\limsup_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\epsilon
with
probability at least
1-\delta
, it is
sufficient
to have total
buffer capacity

N\geq\frac{4\gamma^{2}L^{2}R_{\max}^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{4}\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\beta\bar{I}}{\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I}}\cdot\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{\delta}.

(25)

(Necessary condition.)

Any Q-learning algorithm using a finite buffer of size
N
in an MDP with

|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|
reachable pairs requires

N=\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}\log(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|/\delta))

to achieve
\epsilon
-optimality with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Remark V.10
.

From
\epsilon\leq K\sqrt{f_{C}/N}
(obtained by substituting
N_{C}=f_{C}N

into Theorem
V.7
), one directly obtains

N\geq K^{2}f_{C}/\epsilon^{2}
(linear in
f_{C}
).
Evaluating at the optimal
f_{C}^{*}=\beta\bar{I}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})

gives the factor
\beta\bar{I}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})=f_{C}^{*}
in
(
25
). For general
f_{C}
the bound reads

N\geq(K^{2}/\epsilon^{2})\cdot f_{C}\cdot\log(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|/\delta)
.
The necessary condition is a classical information-theoretic bound

[
42
]
that applies to any algorithm.

Proof.

Sufficient condition.

Let
K=2\gamma R_{\max}L/(1-\gamma)^{2}
.
From Theorem
V.7
with
\limsup\epsilon_{t}\leq\epsilon
:

K\frac{f_{C}}{\sqrt{N_{C}}}\leq\epsilon\;\Longrightarrow\;N_{C}\geq\frac{K^{2}f_{C}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

Since
N_{C}=f_{C}N
, dividing both sides by
f_{C}
gives

N\geq K^{2}f_{C}/\epsilon^{2}
.
Substituting
f_{C}=f_{C}^{*}=\beta\bar{I}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})
:

N\geq\frac{K^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\beta\bar{I}}{\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I}}=\frac{4\gamma^{2}L^{2}R_{\max}^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{4}\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\beta\bar{I}}{\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I}}.

The factor
\beta\bar{I}/(\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I})=f_{C}^{*}

matches (
25
) directly.
Applying Hoeffding’s inequality over the
N_{C}
crystal estimates
with a union bound over
|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|
pairs
adds the
\log(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|/\delta)
factor,
yielding (
25
).

Necessary condition.

By a standard information-theoretic argument
[
42
]
,
distinguishing between MDPs that differ by
\epsilon
in Q-value at each
of
|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|
pairs requires at least

\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}\log(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|/\delta))
samples
in total, which directly lower-bounds the required buffer size.
∎

Corollary V.11

(Optimal Phase Allocation)
.

For fixed total capacity
N
, consider the two-component error objective

J(f_{C})=w_{C}\,\frac{f_{C}}{\sqrt{N_{C}}}+w_{P}\,\frac{1-f_{C}}{\sqrt{N_{P}}},

(26)

where
N_{C}=f_{C}N
,
N_{P}=(1-f_{C})N
, and the SDE-motivated weights are

w_{C}=\sqrt{\beta\bar{I}}
(crystal bias magnitude, proportional to the
decrystallization rate) and
w_{P}=\sqrt{\alpha\bar{U}}
(plasticity
coverage, proportional to the consolidation rate).
Substituting
N_{C}
and
N_{P}
:

J(f_{C})=N^{-1/2}\bigl[w_{C}\sqrt{f_{C}}+w_{P}/\sqrt{1-f_{C}}\bigr]
.
Setting
\partial J/\partial f_{C}=0
and solving:

f_{C}^{*}=\frac{w_{C}^{2}}{w_{C}^{2}+w_{P}^{2}}=\frac{\beta\bar{I}}{\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I}}.

(27)

For default parameters (
\alpha=0.05
,
\beta=0.005
,
\bar{U}=0.5
,

\bar{I}=0.1
),
f_{C}^{*}\approx 0.020
(2.0%). The empirical operating
point
N_{C}/N\approx 6.25\%
exceeds this minimum to additionally satisfy
the coverage constraint of Theorem
V.9
; the two-term
objective
J(0.0625)
remains within 8% of its minimum value
J(f_{C}^{*})

at the Meta-World scale.

long-term retention. New crystal experiences comprise primarily
generalizable motor primitives, while task-specific transitions remain
in the liquid or glass buffers.

VIII
Discussion

VIII-A

Why Crystallization Rather Than Parameter Regularization?

EWC and related regularization methods protect
parameters
:
they add penalty terms to the loss that resist changes to weights
important for prior tasks. AMC instead protects
data
: it
determines which buffered experiences are sufficiently stable to
constrain future learning. This distinction has several practical
consequences.

First, regularization penalties accumulate over tasks. After
K
tasks,
EWC must maintain
K
Fisher matrices (or their diagonal approximation),
each of size
|\theta|
, where
|\theta|
is the number of network
parameters. In contrast, AMC’s overhead is
O(N)
per consolidation
step regardless of
K
, making it suitable for unbounded task sequences.

Second, parameter-based methods protect the
entire
function encoded
by protected weights. If a weight is important for Task 1 but harmful for
Task 3, EWC cannot selectively relax it. AMC instead handles this through

interference detection
(
6
): when new data contradict
a crystallized experience, the decrystallization term
\beta c_{i}I_{i}

dissolves the experience back into the glass phase, where it can be revised.
This selective melting has no analogue in regularization approaches.

Third, the convergence guarantee of Theorem
V.7
provides an
explicit formula linking
(\alpha,\beta,N_{C})
to the residual Q-learning
error, enabling practitioners to
design for
a target accuracy level
by adjusting buffer sizes. EWC offers no such quantitative guidance.

VIII-B

Relationship to Complementary Learning Systems Theory

The CLS theory
[
28
,
23
]
distinguishes
a fast-learning hippocampal system and a slow-consolidating neocortical
system. AMC’s Liquid–Glass–Crystal hierarchy is a computational
realisation of CLS within the experience-replay buffer:

•

The
Liquid buffer
corresponds to the hippocampal system:
high capacity, rapid encoding, FIFO eviction, full learning-rate plasticity.

•

The
Crystal store
corresponds to the neocortex:
stable long-term representations, low learning rate, indefinite retention.

•

The
Glass buffer
is an intermediate consolidation zone with
no direct biological analogue, but can be interpreted as representing
the process of repeated hippocampal replay during slow-wave sleep

[
45
]
, during which memories are either
strengthened (promoted to Crystal) or allowed to fade (demoted to Liquid).

One key distinction from standard CLS implementations is that AMC uses
a
single
neural network whose parameter updates are gated by
crystallization level, rather than separate network modules. This avoids
interference between the hippocampal and cortical modules and allows the
agent’s value function to smoothly interpolate between stability
and plasticity across the buffer.

VIII-C

Connections to Curriculum Learning and Prioritized Replay

The utility function (
1
) shares structure with curriculum
learning and prioritized replay, but differs in a crucial respect.
PER
[
37
]
selects experiences by current TD-error

\delta_{i}(t)
—a measure of
current surprise
. This tends to
over-sample experiences that are currently hard, regardless of whether
they will remain informative in the future. As the agent improves,
previously high-priority experiences become uninformative, but they are
still replayed because their TD-error remains high in the buffer.

AMC’s utility (
1
) avoids this by incorporating

downstream value

V_{i}(t)
(the expected TD-error of successor
states), which penalises experiences that are transiently surprising
but lead to well-understood futures. Empirically, this reduces
oversampling of rare, narrow corridors in the state space that do not
generalize, in favour of high-frequency transitions near decision points.

Furthermore, AMC’s crystallization mechanism introduces temporal
smoothing: a single high-TD-error experience does not immediately
crystallize; it must maintain high utility over multiple consolidation
steps. This smoothing guards against noise spikes in the reward signal
and prevents over-consolidation of outlier transitions.

VIII-D

Empirical vs. Theoretical Forgetting Bounds

Theorem
III.13
provides an exponential bound on the
probability of catastrophic forgetting as a function of crystallization
level, training duration
T_{k}
, and noise
\sigma
. The bound predicts
that at the default settings (
\tau_{C}=0.7
,
\sigma=0.005
), the
per-experience forgetting probability is less than
10^{-4}
for any
task lasting fewer than
T_{k}=50{,}000
steps.

The supplementary bound (
16
) uses stochastic
domination and is pessimistic because it ignores the positive drift.
The primary bound (
15
) is tighter for large

T
but independent of
T
. Empirically, over 50 seeds on Meta-World,
Task 1 performance degrades by at most
1.2\%
after 49 subsequent
tasks (vs.
31.4\%
for VR). The gap between theoretical and empirical
forgetting is consistent with the bounds being loose: they use
worst-case diffusion domination (
\sigma/2
) and ignore the stabilizing
positive drift. Despite their looseness, both bounds provide
actionable guarantees: setting
\tau_{C}\geq 0.7
and
\sigma\leq 0.01

ensures
\mathbb{P}[\text{forgetting}]<10^{-4}
for any individual
crystal experience.

(3) modifying the training step to use phase-stratified sampling and

The base RL algorithm (SAC, DQN, TD3, etc.) is otherwise unchanged.

IX
Limitations and Future Work

IX-A

Current Limitations

Computational overhead.

Consolidation (Algorithm

) adds
\approx 15\%

wall-clock time due to Q-network forward passes over all
N
buffered

O(N/B_{\mathrm{forward}})
, where

B_{\mathrm{forward}}
is the GPU batch size for the forward pass.
Asynchronous consolidation on a separate CPU thread (with a
copy of the Q-network) can reduce this to
<5\%
in GPU-bound settings;
we leave this optimization to a follow-up engineering contribution.

V_{i}

Assumption of off-policy replay.

AMC is designed for off-policy agents that maintain and sample from an
experience buffer (DQN-family, SAC, TD3). On-policy methods (PPO

[
38
]
, A3C
[
31
]
) discard
experiences after a single gradient update and do not maintain a buffer.
The crystallization SDE and Fokker–Planck analysis are mathematically
applicable, but the architecture would need modification: instead of a
separate buffer, crystallization states would annotate a ring buffer of
recent rollouts, with crystal experiences replayed in a hybrid
on/off-policy training regime.

Fixed phase thresholds.

The thresholds
\tau_{L}
and
\tau_{C}
are global constants. In
environments with highly heterogeneous task difficulties, different
experience subsets may benefit from different thresholds. Task-adaptive
thresholds learned from validation performance are a straightforward
extension but require task-boundary detection.

Single-agent scope.

AMC does not address multi-agent settings. In cooperative multi-agent
RL
[
12
]
, agents could benefit from sharing
crystallized experiences, but this raises privacy and communication
cost questions beyond the scope of this work.

IX-B

Future Research Directions

Meta-learned consolidation rates.

Learning
(\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
via a meta-objective

[
11
]
over a distribution of task sequences would
eliminate the remaining hyperparameter sensitivity. The gradient of
the meta-loss with respect to consolidation parameters can be computed
through the Fokker–Planck stationary distribution
(Theorem
III.9
), which is differentiable in
\alpha,\beta,\sigma
.

Hierarchical crystallization.

In hierarchical RL
[
41
]
, high-level subgoal
transitions and low-level motor primitives operate on different
timescales. A two-level AMC hierarchy—with faster consolidation rates
for low-level experiences and slower rates for high-level options—would
align crystallization dynamics with the temporal abstraction inherent in
hierarchical control.

Offline RL and imitation learning.

In offline RL
[
24
]
, the agent learns from a fixed
dataset without environment interaction. Crystallization could
identify high-quality demonstrations for constrained policy optimisation,
providing a principled alternative to heuristic data weighting.
In imitation learning, expert demonstrations could be initialised at

c_{i}=\tau_{C}
(glass phase) and allowed to crystallize or dissolve
based on their alignment with the agent’s learned value function.

Safe reinforcement learning.

AMC’s crystal store provides a natural mechanism for safety-critical
applications
[
15
]
: experiences involving
constraint violations (crashes, boundary exceedances) could be assigned
high negative utility and forced to crystallize, permanently biasing
the agent against unsafe state-action pairs. Releasing the agent for
deployment only after safety-relevant experiences reach
c>0.95
would
provide a concrete, theoretically grounded safety certificate.

Multi-modal agents.

Modern agents for embodied AI
[
39
]
process vision,
language, and proprioception simultaneously. Different modalities may
benefit from different consolidation rates: language instructions change
slowly (high
\alpha
), visual scenes change rapidly (lower
\alpha
),
and proprioceptive signals are highly task-specific (low
\alpha
,
high
\beta
). Modal-specific utility functions and consolidation rates
are a natural extension of the AMC framework.

X
Conclusion

We presented
Adaptive Memory Crystallization (AMC)
, a
biologically grounded, mathematically rigorous framework for progressive
experience consolidation in continual deep RL. The core contribution is
a utility-driven stochastic crystallization mechanism whose
population-level dynamics are governed by an explicitly derived
Fokker–Planck equation admitting a closed-form Beta stationary
distribution. Rigorous proofs establish well-posedness of the SDE,
exponential convergence of individual crystallization states, and an
end-to-end Q-learning error bound that links crystallization parameters
to task performance. A matching lower bound on memory capacity confirms
that AMC’s 6.25% crystal-buffer allocation exceeds the error-bound
minimum
f_{C}^{*}\approx 2\%
while satisfying the coverage constraint
at the evaluated problem scales.

Empirically, AMC achieves 34–43% improvements in forward transfer
over the strongest per-benchmark baseline, 67–80% reductions in
catastrophic forgetting, and a 62% decrease in memory footprint across
Meta-World MT50, Atari-20, and MuJoCo continual locomotion. Ablation
confirms that each component—crystallization dynamics, phase-modulated
learning rates, and interference detection—contributes independently.

Appendix B
Hyperparameter Design Rationale

Buffer capacity split
N_{L}:N_{G}:N_{C}=10:5:1

The recommended split allocates 62.5% of capacity to the liquid buffer,
31.25% to glass, and 6.25% to the crystal store. This is an

engineering design choice
, not a mathematical consequence of
Corollary
III.10
; we explain the reasoning below.

Why 6.25% crystal.

Corollary
V.11
gives the error-bound minimum at

f_{C}^{*}=\frac{\beta\bar{I}}{\alpha\bar{U}+\beta\bar{I}}=\frac{0.0005}{0.0255}\approx 0.020.

The chosen
f_{C}=0.0625>f_{C}^{*}
satisfies the error minimum with margin
and additionally meets the coverage constraint of
Theorem
V.9
: at the Meta-World MT50 scale
(
|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\approx 10^{5}
) with
\epsilon=0.05
and

\delta=0.05
, (
25
) requires
N_{C}\gtrsim 4{,}800
, which is
achieved by
f_{C}=0.0625
with the total buffer
N=10^{5}
.

Per-experience stationarity.

For completeness, the stationary distribution (Theorem
III.9
)
at defaults gives
A=2000
,
B=40
,
c^{*}=0.98
. Since
c^{*}\gg\tau_{C}=0.7
,
a consolidated individual experience spends nearly all time above
\tau_{C}
.
This confirms that crystallized experiences are robustly retained; it is
a property of individual dynamics and does not directly determine
buffer capacity fractions.

Phase threshold
\tau_{C}=0.7

The threshold
\tau_{C}=0.7
balances forgetting resistance against
plasticity. From Theorem
III.13
, a crystal experience
with
c_{0}=\tau_{C}+\varepsilon
and
T_{k}=50{,}000
steps satisfies

\mathbb{P}[c_{i}(T_{k})<\tau_{L}]\leq\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{2\lambda_{i}\varepsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}T_{k}}\Bigr)\cdot e^{-\lambda_{i}T_{k}/2}.

At defaults (
\lambda_{i}\approx 0.0505
,
\sigma=0.005
,
\varepsilon=0.4
),
both factors are negligible, confirming that
\tau_{C}=0.7
provides
strong forgetting resistance across all benchmarks in this evaluation.
Lower
\tau_{C}
accelerates crystallization but reduces the margin

\varepsilon
; higher
\tau_{C}
strengthens guarantees but slows the
glass-to-crystal transition. The sensitivity analysis in
Table
VII
confirms robustness to
\pm 0.1
perturbations
around
\tau_{C}=0.7
.
