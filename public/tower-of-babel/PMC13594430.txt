Leadless pacemaker implantation during pregnancy: Quantitative assessment of fetal radiation exposure
Authors: Ayan Suleimenovich Abdrakhmanov, Simona Avramova-Cholakova, Gaziza Marat, Zhanasyl Ayanovich Suleymen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leadless pacemakers offer an alternative to conventional pacing by eliminating lead- and pocket-related complications. However, their use during pregnancy remains very limited because of concerns related to radiation exposure. We describe the clinical experience of a 17-week pregnant patient who presented with episodes of dizziness, profound weakness, and syncope. Further evaluation revealed second-degree atrioventricular block, Mobitz type II, and transient third-degree atrioventricular block documented by Holter monitoring, indicating the need for permanent pacing. The patient underwent implantation of a Micra AV (Medtronic, USA) leadless pacemaker under fluoroscopic guidance using optimized radiation safety protocols.The estimated fetal absorbed dose was 1.4 mGy, well below reported thresholds for deterministic effects and associated with a very low additional radiation risk compared with background risks of congenital malformations and childhood cancer. The maternal effective dose was 15 mSv. Post-procedure follow-up demonstrated complete symptom resolution, an uncomplicated continuation of pregnancy, and delivery of a healthy infant.This case illustrates the practical feasibility of leadless pacemaker implantation during pregnancy when careful radiation minimization strategies are applied and adds real-world experience to the limited data available for critical pacing indications in pregnant patients.

Key Findings ▪Leadless pacemaker implantation during pregnancy may represent a feasible option in selected patients when radiation exposure is carefully minimized and managed by a multidisciplinary team.▪In this case, quantitative fetal dose assessment suggested low radiation exposure with fluoroscopic guidance performed outside the primary fetal field.▪Modular leadless pacing may provide a potential approach to restoring atrioventricular synchrony without transvenous leads in patients with evolving conduction disease.▪This report adds incremental real-world evidence to the limited literature on leadless pacing strategies in complex clinical scenarios.

Introduction

Disorders of cardiac electrical conduction include conditions such as progressive heart block and symptomatic bradycardia. The generally accepted treatment is pacemaker implantation. Although pacemaker implantation under fluoroscopic guidance has been performed during pregnancy, ionizing radiation may pose potential risks to embryonic and fetal development, and radiation exposure therefore remains a primary concern in clinical decision-making.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

To date, experience with leadless pacemaker implantation during pregnancy remains extremely limited. Only isolated cases have been reported, including a case of leadless Micra pacemaker implantation at 31 weeks of gestation in a patient with transient complete heart block and recurrent syncope.7

Here, we describe our clinical experience with leadless pacemaker implantation in a patient at 17 weeks’ gestation who presented with second-degree atrioventricular block (Mobitz type II) and episodes of transient complete atrioventricular block.

Case report

A 40-year-old woman, currently in her fourth pregnancy with 3 previous deliveries, presented with palpitations, dizziness, recurrent syncope, and generalized weakness and was referred to a cardiology center for further evaluation. Her medical history included second-degree atrioventricular block (Mobitz type II), previously documented on Holter electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular systolic function with no structural or valvular abnormalities. Repeat Holter ECG monitoring revealed progression to third-degree atrioventricular block with a maximum pause of 3.5 seconds (Figure 1).Figure 1Pre-procedure Holter electrocardiographic monitoring demonstrating atrioventricular conduction abnormalities.

Pre-procedure Holter electrocardiographic monitoring demonstrating atrioventricular conduction abnormalities.

Given the clinical importance of distinguishing functional from intrinsic atrioventricular conduction abnormalities—particularly during pregnancy, when permanent pacing has implications for both maternal and fetal outcomes—a diagnostic workup was performed. Intravenous atropine was administered in incremental doses up to a total of 2 mg at 3-minute intervals to assess chronotropic response. The absence of ventricular rate acceleration supported the diagnosis of intrinsic conduction system disease rather than a functional, vagally-mediated block. Following atropine administration, the maternal heart rate stabilized at 69 beats per minute.

Although atropine is classified as a pregnancy category C medication by the US Food and Drug Administration, single-dose intravenous administration in selected clinical settings, including anesthesia and cardiovascular emergencies, has been reported. In this case, atropine was used as part of a controlled diagnostic strategy to facilitate timely and informed clinical decision-making.

Considering the patient’s gestational age (17 weeks), severity of symptoms, absence of reversible causes, potential hemodynamic consequences of recurrent bradycardia for both mother and fetus, and the low complication rate and patient-centered advantages associated with leadless pacemaker implantation,8,9 the multidisciplinary team—comprising cardiology, electrophysiology, and obstetrics specialists—decided to proceed with permanent pacing. The patient was actively involved in the decision-making process and expressed a clear preference for definitive treatment. Written informed consent was obtained.

The procedure was performed via right femoral venous access using the Micra AV delivery system. A leadless Micra AV pacemaker was implanted in the right ventricle and positioned in the lower-third of the interventricular septum (Figure 2). A lead apron was placed over the maternal abdominal and pelvic regions to provide additional fetal shielding. Electrical testing confirmed appropriate device function. The conceptus absorbed dose was estimated using the CODE software product. The pregnancy trimester, beam quality (including x-ray tube voltage and total beam filtration), dose–area product (DAP), and fluoroscopic projections were entered as required by the software. Pacemaker implantation was available as a predefined procedure within the software and was selected for dose estimation. Maternal effective dose was also estimated using the National Cancer Institute dosimetry system for Radiography and Fluoroscopy (NCIRF) software product, version 3.0, based on the same input parameters.10

The total procedural DAP was 55.4 Gy·cm2. The estimated conceptus absorbed dose was 1.4 mGy, and the maternal effective dose was 15 mSv. The fetal exposure is reported in absorbed dose (mGy) rather than effective dose (mSv), because the latter relies on tissue weighting factors averaged by population age and sex and is not relevant for fetal risk assessments.

After the procedure, the patient was monitored jointly by a cardiologist and an obstetrician–gynecologist for 5 days. The post-operative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged home. During the remainder of the pregnancy, she was followed by a cardiologist and a high-risk pregnancy specialist at 4–6-week intervals. Delivery occurred at term (39 weeks) via spontaneous vaginal birth without anesthesia. The newborn weighed 3050 g, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. No complications were observed in either the mother or the infant. No further episodes of syncope or atrioventricular block were documented during the remainder of pregnancy or the postpartum period.

Scheduled follow-up visits were performed at 3, 6, and 12 months after leadless pacemaker implantation. Device function and cardiovascular status were assessed at each visit. The patient reported no symptoms during follow-up. At 12 months, Holter ECG monitoring demonstrated no evidence of atrioventricular block or other rhythm disturbances (Figure 3). Discussion

In the present case, a fluoroscopically guided interventional procedure was performed in a patient at 17 weeks’ gestation, when fetal radiation exposure is a significant clinical concern. Radiation risks are highest in early gestation, particularly during organogenesis and the early fetal period (weeks 3–15 post-conception), remain lower but still relevant during weeks 16–25, and decrease during later stages of pregnancy.

Potential radiation effects after the third week post-conception are commonly classified into deterministic and stochastic effects. Deterministic effects (eg, fetal death, growth retardation, malformations, and neurodevelopmental impairment) occur only above certain absorbed dose thresholds. These effects are unlikely below 100–200 mGy, although higher sensitivity has been described during the pre-implantation period. Below these thresholds, the primary concern is stochastic effects, especially childhood cancer, with a risk of about 0.6% per 100 mGy.

Applying a risk coefficient of 1.2 × 10-2 per Gy, as recommended by International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) Publication 90 and implemented in CODE software, the 1.4 mGy conceptus dose in our case adds an estimated 0.02% risk. This value is low compared to baseline risks for congenital malformations (1%–6%) and childhood cancer (approximately 0.2%). These findings align with ICRP Publication 84, which indicates that fluoroscopically guided procedures outside the conceptus region (e.g. thorax) are permissible during pregnancy if radiation protection is optimized.

Recent UK, Europe, and US guidelines do not routinely recommend patient contact shielding for fluoroscopic procedures outside the diaphragm-to-knee area if beam collimation is adequate.11,12 This recommendation is based on evidence that internal scatter mainly contributes to fetal dose, and misplaced shielding can increase radiation exposure by interfering with automatic brightness control. Studies with anthropomorphic pregnant phantoms suggest that abdominal and pelvic lead shielding reduces fetal dose by less than 3% during cardiac procedures.13 In the present case, shielding was applied at the patient’s request, as its use remains permitted and provided additional maternal reassurance. Dose-reduction strategies were carefully implemented, including optimized collimation, low-dose fluoroscopy settings, avoidance of magnification, minimization of fluoroscopy time and cine acquisition, and appropriate patient positioning.

The total DAP recorded during the procedure (55.4 Gy·cm2), corresponding to a maternal effective dose of 15 mSv, lies within the upper range of reported values for pacemaker implantation. Published diagnostic reference levels for single- and dual-chamber pacemaker implantation range from approximately 0.86 to 36 Gy·cm2,14 while reported effective doses vary between 1.4 and 17 mSv.15 The procedure framerate was 15 pps. Potential for pacemaker protocol optimization using lower framerates can be further sought. Although the maternal effective dose in this case was relatively high for this type of procedure, the absorbed dose to the conceptus remained very low, resulting in minimal additional radiation risk. However, the long-term consequences of the exposure remain unknown.

One limitation of this case is that intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) was not used. Although ICE may offer additional anatomical visualization and further improve procedural precision, fluoroscopic guidance was selected in accordance with institutional workflow and available resources.

Conclusion

This clinical case illustrates that leadless pacemaker implantation may represent a feasible and minimally invasive option for the management of conduction disorders requiring permanent pacing during pregnancy when careful radiation minimization strategies are applied. As the conceptus lies outside the primary radiation beam during pacemaker implantation, the additional radiation exposure to the unborn child is expected to be low. Quantitative radiation dose assessment in this case confirmed low fetal exposure and underscores the importance of multidisciplinary planning and radiation dose optimization to support favorable maternal and fetal outcomes.
