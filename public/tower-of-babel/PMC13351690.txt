Heterogeneous expression patterns of the T2D-associated kinesin-4 KIF21A in pancreatic islet endocrine cells
Authors: Syed N Barmaver, Nala Hamilton, Guoqiang Gu, Irina Kaverina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe β cells in the pancreatic endocrine islets preferentially secrete insulin in specific subdomains of the plasma membrane adjacent to the vasculature (i.e., hot spots). Impaired insulin secretion and β -cell dysfunction are central features of Type-2 Diabetes, yet the cytoskeletal machinery that supports directional secretion and secretory hot spots remains incompletely defined. KIF21A is a plus-end-directed kinesin-4 motor protein that anchors microtubule plus ends to the cell cortex. However, the role of KIF21A in pancreatic islet endocrine cells and potential link to type 2 diabetes (T2D) remain unexplored.MethodsKIF21A mRNA and protein levels were analyzed using bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq data using proteomics databases. Kif21a protein distribution was assessed by immunofluorescence in isolated mouse islets using super-resolution microscopy. Likewise, immunostaining of insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, laminin, and detyrosinated tubulin is also performed.ResultsWe show that KIF21A is downregulated in T2D human islets at both the mRNA (RNA-seq) and protein (quantitative proteomics) levels. We also demonstrate cell-type-specific enrichment of Kif21a protein (δ > α > β) in intact islets, confirming the hierarchy suggested by single-cell transcriptomics. We also show that within each endocrine lineage, Kif21a protein shows pronounced cell-to-cell heterogeneity, consistent with endocrine sub-states and functional specialization. And most importantly, we show that implicating Kif21a is spatially enriched at the rosettes and laminin-rich interfaces at vasculature-oriented secretion sites (hot spots), where microtubule anchoring is expected to shape targeted granule delivery.ConclusionsKIF21A is a T2D-associated gene with cell-type-specific and heterogeneous expression in islet endocrine cells. KIF21A may have a cortical microtubule-anchoring function and may contribute to the directed granule delivery to the vasculature for regulated hormone secretion.Keywords: Molecular motor, Microtubule (MT), Insulin, Glucagon, Somatostatin, Type-2 diabetes (T2D), Pancreas, α-cells, β-cells, δ-cells, Secretion

Introduction

Endocrine cells within pancreatic islets are critical regulators of metabolism. This function is achieved via the tightly regulated secretion of several essential hormones. Among these, β cells, which comprise the majority (∼70%) of islet cells, produce and secrete insulin to regulate glucose homeostasis via glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, or GSIS. The next in abundance are α cells, which produce glucagon and GLP-1 to potentiate insulin secretion via paracrine stimulation. Glucagon can directly stimulate insulin secretion via binding to glucagon receptors (GCGR) on β cells. Glucagon signaling in β cells also elevates cAMP and promotes CREB phosphorylation, with improved β -cell function and survival [[1], [2], [3]]. In contrast, the hormone secreted by the third most abundant endocrine cell type (5% of islet cell population), δ cells, somatostatin, is thought to provide negative regulation to other islet cells, thereby ceasing the secretion response [1,[4], [5], [6]]. Such complex regulation is required to prevent insufficient GSIS, which results in diabetes, and over-secretion, which leads to hypoglycemia [7,8].

Studies over the last decades, including ours, have highlighted the roles of the microtubule (MT) cytoskeleton in regulated endocrine secretion, with the emphasis on β cells and insulin [9,10]. It has become clear that MTs serve as tracks for MT-dependent molecular motors transporting insulin secretory granules (ISGs), a canonical role as in other secretory cells. Yet MTs in β cells are also responsible for the withdrawal of excessive ISGs to prevent over-secretion and hypoglycemia [[11], [12], [13]]. Such a dual function is supported by a complex architecture of the MT network: the current view is that MT tracks with their plus ends extending toward the cell periphery (end-on MTs) promote IG delivery, while submembrane MT bundles withdraw ISGs from the secretion sites [14]. How this β-cell MT organization is formed and regulated is not fully understood. At the same time, very little knowledge exists about MT organization and regulation in other islet cells. One burning question is the role of unconventional kinesin motors in the organization of the MT network as tracks for regulated IG transport. In recent years, kinesin-4 KIF21A came into focus in the molecular motor field as an important factor in MT network organization. In this study, we aim to evaluate the potential contribution of this molecule in islet endocrine cells.

KIF21A is a plus-end–directed motor protein, whose main function is thought to be the regulation of the MT network. KIF21A can dampen MT dynamics in vitro and stabilize peripheral MT plus ends by anchoring them to the cell cortex in HeLa cells [15]. KIF21A's direct interactions with focal adhesion proteins talin, KANK1, and KANK2 result in sub-cellular MT anchoring in the vicinity of integrin-based adhesion to the extracellular matrix (ECM) [[15], [16], [17]] [18]. This MT-anchoring capacity of KIF21A makes it a plausible candidate for stabilizing the delivery tracks for ISGs in β-cells, which secrete directionally toward the site of contact with the vascular ECM [[19], [20], [21]]. Another function has been proposed for KIF21A due to its interaction with the brefeldin A-inhibited guanine nucleotide-exchange protein BIG1, which regulates membrane trafficking at the Golgi. Because of this interaction, it is possible that KIF21A is involved in ER-Golgi or post-Golgi vesicular transport or secretory cargo processing through the Golgi [22].

The functions of KIF21A are regulated via a conserved autoinhibition through interactions between its motor domain and tail domain [[23], [24], [25]]. When KIF21A autoinhibition is released, the motor domain is available to bind MTs, and the tail domain becomes available to associate with either cortical proteins, leading to MT anchoring [18,23], or with BIG1, allowing for the proposed KIF21A function in Golgi trafficking [22].

The capacity of KIF21A to be rapidly and tightly regulated makes this motor a plausible candidate player in rapid metabolic responses, such as endocrine secretion. Indeed, recent computational analyses have identified KIF21A as a potential shared biomarker between type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and other metabolic conditions [26] [27]. In addition, among kinesins, KIF21A is highly expressed in pancreatic β cells, and β-cell subtypes with high secretory output have elevated KIF21A expression, making it a cell-relevant regulator of insulin granule positioning and exocytosis [14] [28].

In this study, we demonstrate that KIF21A is differentially expressed in three major endocrine cell types (α, δ, and β -cells) and preferentially localized to the interface of β cells with the vasculature (Figure 1A). Our data put KIF21A in focus as a potential contributor into defining endocrine cell types, heterogeneity, and directional secretion.

Results

KIF21A is downregulated in T2D in human and mouse pancreatic islet cells

Previously, our labs performed a comprehensive screening of kinesins and MT-associated proteins (MAPs) in pancreatic β cells, aiming to identify molecular motors and regulators that may control insulin secretion. These analyses revealed that among the kinesin superfamily, KIF21A is one of the highly expressed in mature β cells, making it a plausible, cell-relevant regulator of insulin granule positioning and exocytosis [14]. Also, our single-cell RNA-seq indicates that β-cell subtypes with high secretory output have elevated KIF21A expression, linking KIF21A to the β-cell states that underpin robust insulin secretion [28]. Here, we utilized the Alberta Diabetes Institute IsletCore multi-omics resource to evaluate KIF21A abundance in human islets from donors with type 2 diabetes (T2D) versus nondiabetic controls. Bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed that normalized KIF21A transcript levels were significantly reduced in T2D islets compared with controls (Figure 1B). Concordantly, quantitative proteomics demonstrated a significant decrease in KIF21A protein abundance in T2D islets relative to controls (Figure 1C).

To understand whether this T2D-associated KIF21A reduction affected distinct endocrine cell types, we utilized existing single-cell data from CELLxGENE and IsletCore repositories.

Interestingly, KIF2A single-cell mRNA content was reduced in β (Figure 1D), α (Figure 1E) and δ-cells (Figure 1F) in human T2D donor samples as compared to healthy counterparts.

Similarly, single-cell mRNA content analysis in normal versus T2D mouse models β and δ cells showed the same trend (Supplemental Fig. 1 A, C). The data from wild-type versus T2D mouse α-cells indicated a significant difference, but the trend was opposite to the human data (Supplemental Fig. 1B), indicating that certain regulations of α cells in standard T2D mouse models do not mimic human disease. Interestingly, specific anti-diabetic treatment protocols differentially affected KIF21A expression in endocrine cells (Supplemental Fig. 1D–I). In human islets, single-cell mRNA levels were compared across treatment groups including oral medications and Metformin at various durations (2, 3, 5, and 6 years), sourced from the CELLxGENE database. In mouse models, single-cell mRNA levels were compared across T2D models (db/db, multiple low-dose STZ) and interventions including vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG), pair-feeding, GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, and insulin therapy, as described in Hrovatin et al. [29]. (Supplemental Fig. 1G–I). The data suggest that certain therapeutic interventions modulate pathways involving KIF21A in pancreatic endocrine cells. Altogether, KIF21A expression in endocrine cells correlates with the disease state and may be involved in diabetes-associated dysfunction or compensation in major mammalian pancreatic endocrine cell types.

KIF21A mRNA content is heterogeneous across and within major islet endocrine cell types

Detailed comparison of single-cell RNA seq data for the three endocrine cell types reveals the highest KIF21A expression in δ-cells, followed by α-cells, with the lowest expression in β-cells (Figure 1H, I). This suggests differential roles of this motor in distinct islet cell types. Moreover, within each lineage, KIF21A single-cell RNA counts exhibited broad dispersion with some individual cells deviating substantially from the average, indicating that, beyond the overall δ > α > β hierarchy, KIF21A expression and, potentially, its functional impact is heterogeneous at the single-cell level within each cell type (Figure 1H, I). These data suggest similarities in KIF21A function between human and mouse endocrine cells. Notably, single-cell RNA-seq data from both human (Figure 1G, H) and mouse (Figure 1I) islets consistently show the same δ > α > β KIF21A expression hierarchy, demonstrating conservation of this expression pattern across species.

KIF21A protein content within mouse islets is cell-type specific

To corroborate the multi-omics observations, we directly compared the protein content of Kif21a in single mouse pancreatic islet cells by immunostaining. Our analyses revealed lower Kif21a intensity in β-cells (identified by insulin immunostaining) as compared to non-β-cells in the same islet (Figure 2A–C). Kif21a intensity in glucagon-positive α-cells (Figure 2D–F) was higher than in the majority of surrounding islet cells. Even more striking enrichment of Kif21a was found in somatostatin-positive δ-cells (Figure 2G–I) and compared to other cells in the same islet. These data indicate that the Kif21a expression between islet cell types (δ > α > β) detected at the RNA level is maintained on the protein level.

Single-cell Kif21a protein content is heterogeneous within each major islet cell type

Assessing intra-cell-type variability of Kif21a content in immuno-stained islets, we found pronounced heterogeneity in Kif21a signal intensities within each endocrine cell population. In insulin-positive β-cells, Kif21a staining displayed a variable pattern of accumulation ranging from high to low intensity (Figure 3A, B, compare red versus white cell outlines). Expression levels within the β-cell population were normally distributed, based on Shapiro–Wilk test (Figure 3C).

Enrichment of Kif21a in β cells concentrated at rosettes and laminin-rich interfaces

Studies in cancer cells and neurons [15,30] indicate that KIF21A assists vesicle delivery by anchoring MT plus ends to distinct sites at the cell cortex, and that peripheral localization of this protein is tightly associated with its function. Thus, we next addressed the subcellular distribution of Kif21a within β-cells in mouse islets.

The majority of pancreatic endocrine secretion is directed into the islet vasculature, which can be identified in optical islet sections by the rosette tissue architecture, where four or more endocrine cells converge. Tubulin staining revealed that enhanced MT content at the rosettes, suggesting enrichment of anchored stable MT ends in these regions (Figure 4A).

Interestingly, while mean Kif21a intensity per β cell was moderate compared to neighboring α- and δ-cells (Figure 2), high- Kif21a -content was found in the rosette regions (Figure 4B, C). Moreover, Kif21a -rich areas were adjacent to vascular ECM accumulations detected by laminin immunostaining (Figure 4D–F). To directly confirm the presence of stable MTs at rosettes, we performed immunostaining for detyrosinated (Glu-) tubulin, a well-established marker of long-lived, stable MT polymers [31]. Glu-tubulin was indeed enriched at rosette regions (Figure 4G, H), demonstrating that stable MT plus ends are preferentially anchored at these vascular-facing secretion sites, consistent with KIF21A's known role in cortical MT stabilization.

These data suggest that the Kif21a function in cortical anchoring of MT plus ends is conserved in β-cells, and that this anchoring is restricted to the preferred sites of secretion, likely promoting targeted delivery of insulin granules for direct secretion into the bloodstream. Importantly, we found that in human islets, KIF21A protein is also accumulated at rosette structures (Supplemental Fig. 2). This demonstrates the conservation of KIF21A spatial pattern in human β cells and suggests a potential translational significance of this protein for human health.

Discussion

In this report, we highlight KIF21A, a kinesin-4 motor protein involved in organizing MT networks for directional intracellular transport, as a potentially significant factor in the identity and function of pancreatic endocrine cells. We dissect the evidence that KIF21A is downregulated in T2D pancreatic islet cells, adding to the catalog of genes with altered expression in diabetic islets. Significant alterations in gene expression and functional protein profiles in islet cells in T2D are known to reflect high metabolic demand, chronic stress, and compensatory pressures [32,33]. KIF21A downregulation in diabetes may contribute to pathophysiology because it could impair insulin secretion through defects in MT anchoring at the cell cortex [15] and/or biogenesis and transport of ISGs from the Golgi [22] [9]. Alternatively, given that ATP levels are reduced in T2D islets due to impaired mitochondrial function [34], downregulation of energy-consuming motor proteins may represent an adaptive response to preserve cellular energy under metabolic stress.

Importantly, we found that KIF21A protein is differentially expressed in specific endocrine cell types. These findings provide a novel distinctive feature of endocrine cell types and suggest that KIF21A contributes to unique trafficking or secretory mechanisms in each cell type. The high KIF21A content in δ-cells is particularly interesting. δ-cells secrete somatostatin, a powerful paracrine inhibitor that reduces glucagon secretion by ∼95% [35] and also robust inhibitor of insulin [6,36], which is particularly important for the feedback control of insulin secretion dosage and for the suppression of insulin secretion during stress responses [37]. This potent inhibitory function relies on rapid and efficient somatostatin delivery to all adjacent cells. Despite the lack of detailed studies of MT organization in δ cells, we speculate that extensive MT anchoring across the plasma membrane by KIF21A would be beneficial for this response. Indeed, in δ-cells, we detect Kif21a distribution throughout the δ-cell cytoplasm, rather than the site-specific Kif21a accumulation observed in β-cells. In addition, extensive cytoplasmic KIF21A location might indicate an alternative function in the secretory cargo biogenesis and transport [22] [9]. All the same speculations apply to potential roles of Kif21a in α cells, whose Kif21a levels are also relatively high.

In contrast, β-cells exhibit a lower Kif21a expression, yet it is specifically concentrated at the cellular interface with the vasculature. Such distribution argues for a specific role of Kif21a in site-specific MT anchoring at the vascular interface. This potential function is consistent with findings that locate other components of the MT-anchoring complex at the vascular interface) [[15], [16], [17]]. This location is thought to be initiated by the vascular ECM protein interaction with β-cell integrins [38], which triggers the assembly of focal adhesions and adhesion-dependent MT-anchoring protein complexes (including LL5β, liprin-α1, liprin-β, and ELKS) [[15], [16], [17]]. Mechanistically, in other cell types, KIF21A is recruited to the cell cortex by a focal adhesion protein KANK1/KANK2, which binds integrins via the mechanosensitive protein talin [[16], [17], [18]]. We anticipate that in β cells a similar protein chain establishes a molecular link between ECM at the vascular interface and KIF21A-mediated MT anchoring. Furthermore, the studies in the field and our recent work directly demonstrate that insulin exocytic events occurs at the periphery of ELKS/LL5β patches in β-cells [19,[39], [40], [41]], placing KIF21A within a well-defined cortical MT stabilization complex at these functional domains. This is an important step toward our understanding of MT network structure in β cells, where the majority of the MT network is non-directional or parallel to the cell membrane [9,10,14] and the vast majority of ISGs are not transported directionally [[10], [11], [12],42]. Rare directional ISG movement events [12,43] and the directional insulin secretion at specific hot spots [19,21,42,44] cannot be explained by such structure and have triggered prior hypotheses of the existence of a specialized sub-population of MT tracks directly leading to the secretory sites. Future studies are needed to test whether KIF21A plays a critical role in specialized MT formation.

Another important message from our data is the Kif21a′s contribution to heterogeneity within each endocrine cell type. The growing body of evidence demonstrates cellular heterogeneity within the endocrine pancreas, where the secretory capacity of individual cells significantly varies within each cell type [[45], [46], [47]]. Recent single-cell analysis has confirmed that this heterogeneity relies on variable epigenetic modification and expression of hormones and islet lineage markers, directly linking transcriptomic variation to physiological properties [48,49]. Our prior data indicate that MTs contribute to the well-established functional heterogeneity in β-cells [9,13,37,45,50], but it remains unclear whether specific MT motors are involved in this phenomenon. Here, we show that Kif21a within α, β, and δ-cells display a heterogeneous pattern of accumulation. In β-cells, the heterogeneity encompassed a subset with elevated Kif21a expression consistent with previously described high-secretory β-cell states [28]. Elevated Kif21a in highly secreting β-cell subtypes suggests that it mediates high secretory capacity. If KIF21A maintains the submembrane MT architecture that supports fast granule recruitment and priming, its insufficiency or mis-regulation could impair the phase I insulin secretion. Accordingly, loss or reduction in KIF21A levels could shift β-cells toward low-output states seen in early stages of type 2 diabetes [28]. Conversely, excessive KIF21A activity might overly stabilize peripheral MTs, potentially trapping granules subcortically and altering secretion dynamics, highlighting the need for optimal, not merely maximal, KIF21A function. In addition, because kinesin-4 motors regulate MT growth and pausing at the cortex, KIF21A may integrate extracellular cues (e.g., glucose-induced signaling) with cytoskeletal remodeling, a process known to be disrupted in diabetic β-cells [11,[51], [52], [53]].

While heterogeneity of MT networks in two other endocrine cell types remains to be studied, it likely affects paracrine regulation within islets, as β-cells achieve precise insulin secretion through extensive crosstalk with glucagon-producing α cells and somatostatin-producing δ-cells [6,54].

Materials and methods

Mice

CD-1 (ICR) mice used in this study was obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA). All procedures involving animals were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol M18000195; investigators GG and IK) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation.

Isolation of islets from mice

Pancreatic islets were isolated from 8- to 16-week-old mice by collagenase inflation via the common bile duct. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. A 4 mL syringe fitted with a 26–30 G needle bent to ∼45° was loaded with collagenase solution (∼2 mL of 0.8 mg/mL collagenase P, MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MI, USA in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS), Corning, Corning, NY, USA). After laparotomy, the duodenum was clamped at the bile duct entry to prevent reflux, and the pancreas was inflated by intraductal injection. The pancreas was excised, collected into residual collagenase solution, and digested at 37 °C for 15 min with intermittent mixing until opaque. The digest was diluted with HBSS (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) to 15 mL, centrifuged at 700 × g for 2 min at 4 °C, and washed 3–4 times with islet medium until the supernatant was clear. Islets were hand-picked under a dissection microscope into culture dishes (≤100 islets per dish). Islets were cultured in RPMI 1640 containing l-glutamine (Gibco, Dublin, Ireland) 11 mM glucose, 10% heat-inactivated (HI) fetal bovine serum (Atlanta Biologicals, Flowery Branch, GA, USA), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 [1,55,56].

Immunofluorescence staining of isolated islets

Isolated islets were handled under a dissection microscope in uncoated 96-well U-bottom plates. Islets were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS containing 0.1% saponin, 150 μl/well, for 1 h at room temperature (RT); for insulin staining, fixation was extended overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed 3 × for 10 min in PBS +0.1% saponin, then blocked for 1 h at RT in 5% donkey serum/1% BSA +0.1% saponin. Primary antibodies prepared in fresh blocking buffer (Guinea pig anti-insulin 1:100 [Dako (Santa Clara, CA) Cat# A0564; RRID: AB_10013624], mouse monoclonal anti-glucagon 1:10,000 [clone K79bB10, MilliporeSigma Cat# G2654; RRID: AB_259852], Mouse monoclonal anti-α-Tubulin 1:100 [clone DM1A, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA Cat# T9026; RRID: AB_477593], Rabbit anti-KIF21A (1:50) [Sigma–Aldrich Lot # A118339], Rat Anti-Somatostatin (1:100), [Sigma–Aldrich Lot # MAB354], Rat anti-Laminin β-1 monoclonal (1:100) [Clone LT3, Invitrogen Cat# MA5-14657], Rabbit anti-Detyrosinated α-tubulin (RM444) (1:125)[ Q71U36, RevMab Biosciences], were clarified by centrifugation (4 °C, maximum speed, 30 min); 30 μl supernatant/well was added and islets were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h. After three washes as above, secondary antibodies (1:200 each: Alexa Fluor 568 anti-mouse, DyLight 650 Goat anti-guinea pig [Invitrogen Cat# SA5-10097], Goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 [Invitrogen Cat# A-11008], Alexa Fluor goat anti-rat 568 [Invitrogen Cat# A-11077]) in blocking buffer were clarified similarly, applied at 30 μl/well, and incubated at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by three washes. For mounting, islets were transferred to cleaned slides, residual buffer removed, and immediately covered with vectashield to prevent drying. Coverslips were gently applied to avoid bubbles, excess medium removed, and edges sealed with nail polish. Slides were protected from light and stored at 4 °C or −20 °C and imaged as soon as possible.

Imaging

Fluorescence imaging was performed on a Nikon AX NSPARC super-resolution microscope with a Plan APO 100 × /NA1.45 oil-immersion objective. Pancreatic islets were transferred to pre-cleaned (using 70% ethanol) glass slides, mounted in VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium (Vector Labs Cat# H-1000 Burlingame, CA), and sealed under coverslips prior to imaging at room temperature. Super-resolution acquisition was carried out in NSPARC mode with sequential channel acquisition using fluorophore-appropriate excitation/emission settings, with exposure minimized to limit photobleaching while maintaining signal-to-noise. Image geometry and calibration were set as follows: field of view 1024 × 1024 pixels; lateral calibration 0.072 μm/pixel, with z-stacks acquired at 0.15 μm steps. Post-acquisition processing included uniform background subtraction and Richardson-Lucy deconvolution (5 iterations) applied to each channel following acquisition.

Image quantification

Analyses were performed in FIJI (ImageJ). For all measurements, raw fluorescence images were used.

Kif21a intensity per cell

Regions of interest (ROIs) for specific islet cell types were delineated on the corresponding marker channel. Nuclear regions were identified on the DAPI channel and excluded by subtracting the nuclear mask from the cell-type ROIs. Mean fluorescence intensity of KIF21A was measured within each nucleus-excluded cell-type ROI after background subtraction. Background intensity was measured in adjacent cell-free regions and subtracted from all KIF21A measurements prior to normalization. To normalize, each ROI's background-subtracted KIF21A intensity was divided by the total islet KIF21A mean fluorescence intensity, yielding normalized mean fluorescence intensity: normalized Mean fluorescence intensity = (ROI Mean fluorescence intensity − background)/(total islet KIF21A intensity). To compare cell type X (e.g. α-cells) from cell type Y (e.g. non-α cells), we selected cell type X and adjacent cell type Y to quantify mean fluorescence intensity of KIF21A.

Kif21a intensity in rosettes

Rosette structures were delineated on the rosette-defining channel using the Magic Wand tool in ImageJ with consistent tolerance settings across images. The selected rosette ROI was transferred to the KIF21A channel, and the background-subtracted KIF21A MFI was measured within the rosette ROI. Where indicated, values were normalized to the total islet KIF21A mean fluorescence intensity.

Kif21a intensity in vicinity of vascular ECM

Laminin-positive and laminin-negative ROIs were defined on the laminin channel. KIF21A MFI was measured in each ROI after background subtraction and normalized by the total islet KIF21A fluorescence intensity, as described for cell-type ROIs.

KIF21A protein and transcript analysis of database data

To analyze KIF21A abundance in disease and cell type, we utilized two wildly used and public databases: HumanIslets database (an Alberta Diabetes Institute IsletCore multi-omics resource) and CELLxGENE. To gather data from HumanIslets we searched ‘KIF21A’ in the FeatureView search tool and downloaded bulk protein and bulk gene expression (RNA-seq) with the media data description: “Corrected diabetes status, Type2-None” (Figure 1B-C). Using the same FeatureView tool we downloaded cell type expression data by clicking the SC link for the Corrected diabetes status, Type2-None Bulk Gene Expression (RNA-seq) dataset and downloading a CSV file. The cell types of interest (⍺, β, δ) were then selected and plotted [29]. All details on the whole tissue and islet isolation can be accessed in the following link (https://xia-lab.github.io/HumanIsletsDoc/).

The CELLxGENE database was used to gather single cell data from non-diabetic and type-2 diabetic donors. The following collection's data was then downloaded and analyzed: “A pancreatic islet scRNA-seq atlas from 48 non-diabetic, prediabetic and type 2 diabetic individuals of matched demographic and ethnic backgrounds.” [57] The directions on how to download and analyze this data is provided in the following GitHub page: https://github.com/NalaHam/KIF21A-manuscript.
