Nanoparticle Biofunctionalization of Bioprosthetic Heart Valves to Modulate Mechanisms of Structural Valve Deterioration
Authors: Angus J. Grant, Alex H.P. Chan, Xueying S. Xu, Kieran Lau, Timothy C. Mitchell, Juichien Hung, Praveesuda L. Michael, Miguel Santos, Aeryne Lee, Jacopo Giaretta, Tiffany Goh, Chi-Hui Tsao, Anna Waterhouse, Sina Naficy, Jelena Rnjak-Kovacina, Martin K.C. Ng, Steven G. Wise, Richard P. Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Bioprosthetic tissue is increasingly recognized as the preferred biomaterial solution for surgical and transcatheter heart valve replacements.1 Although mechanical heart valves are made from highly durable materials including stainless steel, titanium, or pyrolytic carbon, patients require lifelong anticoagulation to prevent valve thrombosis, placing them at ongoing risk of clinically significant bleeding.2 Introduced in the 1960s, bioprosthetic valves made from glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine or porcine pericardial tissue aimed to improve blood compatibility and reduce the need for lifelong anticoagulation.3 In the early 2000s, bioprosthetic tissue also proved compatible with transcatheter delivery systems, enabling valve deployment using minimally invasive endovascular procedures. These combined advantages have led to the widespread adoption of bioprosthetic valves over mechanical valves, despite limitations centered around durability.

Bioprosthetic valves remain susceptible to host–material interactions that ultimately compromise structural integrity and lead to valve degradation. These issues are especially pronounced in younger patients, where the rate of pericardium degeneration is accelerated, resulting in valves with a significantly reduced functional lifespan.4 Over their lifetime, the leaflets of bioprosthetic valves are subject to a series of interconnected biological processes that impact overall valve function and longevity, collectively comprising a condition known as structural valve deterioration (SVD).5 In the early stages, platelet aggregation on the pericardium surface leads to microthrombi formation, characterized as subclinical leaflet thrombosis (SLT).6,7 SLT can lead to hypoattenuated leaflet thickening, restricting leaflet motion and compromising valve function.8 Studies have also demonstrated a strong association between inflammation and leaflet fibrosis, where underlying proinflammatory markers such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and transforming growth factor-β can promote fibrotic pannus formation around the valve, progressively contributing to leaflet thickening and dysfunction.5,6 In the long term, calcium deposits accumulate, particularly in regions of high mechanical stress at the leaflet base, leading to valve stenosis. Calcification also shares several common inflammatory mediators and pathologic features with fibrosis, creating a cycle that accelerates both processes and exacerbates valve deterioration.5,9 Efforts to mitigate SVD have largely focused on improving the surface chemistry of pericardium tissue.

Since the introduction of animal-derived pericardium over 50 years ago, the field has focused on chemical pretreatment as the dominant approach to mitigate the biological factors driving SVD. Although major valve companies have developed their own proprietary methods, most approaches aim to cap or eliminate residual free aldehyde groups from the pericardium surface, which are believed to be drivers of inflammation and calcification.5 Recent examples, such as RESILIA (Edwards Lifesciences) and AOA (Medtronic) tissue technologies, show promising midterm results in terms of high rates of freedom from SVD in surgical valves, although these data are largely derived from low- to intermediate-risk surgical cohorts, with limited representation of younger or higher-risk patients.10,11 Ongoing studies are evaluating the long-term performance of these technologies and investigating whether these benefits translate effectively to transcatheter valves.12,13 However, current tissue treatment technologies remain inherently passive. Capping aldehyde groups so pericardium tissue is less of a catalyst for SVD is a “passive” approach designed to endure and/or delay biological stress rather than disrupt the underlying cascade of SVD, suggesting that its benefits will be inherently limited as long-term biological processes continue to act on the tissue. An alternative approach that actively modulates the biological mechanisms driving SVD represents a new, “active” strategy with the potential to dramatically improve long-term bioprosthetic valve durability.

Minimally invasive interventions for atherosclerosis have leveraged active strategies through the integration of localized drug elution. Drug-eluting stents and balloons locally release therapeutic agents that modulate multiple aspects of their surrounding vascular biology, improving performance and extending durability.14,15 However, this technology has not yet been applied to bioprosthetic valves because of technical challenges encountered at the leaflet surface, including the need for complex drug-crosslinking strategies compatible with glutaraldehyde-fixed tissue16 and the difficulty of incorporating the relatively thick polymer intermediates required for drug elution onto the leaflet surface without compromising leaflet motion.17 Effective functionalization of bioprosthetic valves with therapeutic drugs, in a manner that preserves leaflet mechanics, would represent a major shift from conventional approaches, enabling the next generation of bioprosthetic valves to be capable of directly modulating the biology at the leaflet interface and potentially enhancing resilience to SVD. Here, we present a novel approach for drug biofunctionalization of leaflets using plasma polymerized nanoparticles (PPNs), enabling local modulation of thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification while preserving leaflet mechanics and valve performance.

Methods

PPN synthesis and collection

PPNs were produced in a capacitively coupled radiofrequency plasma reactor under sterile conditions in a formulation shown to be nontoxic and biocompatible as previously described.18 Briefly, a radiofrequency-driven (13.52 MHz) plasma discharge was generated at a pressure of 200 mTorr by ionization of a gaseous mixture comprising argon, nitrogen, and acetylene. The total gas flow rate was set constant at 19 standard cm3/min and monitored by mass flow controllers (Alicat Scientific) throughout the process. The radiofrequency power was delivered by a radiofrequency power supply (Seren) and coupled to the plasma using an automatic matching box (Seren) to maximize power delivery. The plasma radiative emission fingerprint was monitored in situ via optical emission spectroscopy using an Ocean Optics HR4000. The optical emission spectroscopy process was used for quality control purposes by tracking the emission dynamics of selected molecular species, including molecular nitrogen excited states and ions and CN molecular radicals. The nanoparticles produced within the plasma bulk were collected in 24-well polystyrene plates (Corning) directly from the active plasma volume as previously described.18 After synthesis, the plates were brought to atmospheric pressure by venting the chamber with nitrogen and removed from the vacuum chamber for storage at room temperature and in ambient air for at least 48 hours before resuspension in aqueous solution. Because PPNs are produced in a dry plasma state, they can be stored in their native dry state and only need to be resuspended in solution as needed before functionalization with molecular cargo. Here, PPNs were dispersed in ultrapure nuclease-free water directly from the well plates into 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes. The concentration of PPNs in solution was determined by UV/VIS spectroscopy as previously described,18 by measuring the optical density of PPNs in solution at 500 nm.

PPN immobilization onto pericardium

PPNs were collected in 24-well polystyrene plates (Corning) and dispersed in ultrapure nuclease-free water directly from the well plates into 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes.19 Samples of glutaraldehyde (0.625%) fixed pericardium (Maverick Biosciences) were washed twice in saline (2 × 5 minutes) and treated with 4% L-cysteine (pH 1.8) for 6 hours on a plate shaker at room temperature. PPNs resuspended in nuclease-free water were then added to the pericardium at concentrations ranging from 5 × 109 to 5 × 1011 PPNs/mL overnight on a shaker at 4 °C. PPN concentration used in experiments was 5 × 1010 PPNs/mL unless otherwise specified. Samples were then washed once in nuclease-free water to remove unbound PPNs, followed by treatment with apixaban (20 μM), the small molecule NLRP3-mediated inflammation inhibitor MCC950 (20 μM), or naturally occurring antioxidant phytic acid (20 μM) for 30 minutes, again on a shaker at 4 °C. Samples were washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 2 × 5 minutes) to remove unbound cargo. All treatments were done in 96-well plates for 6-mm biopsy punches or 24-well plates for larger pericardium samples.

Scanning and transmission electron microscopy

Pericardium samples designated for scanning electron microcopy (SEM) analysis were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 1 hour followed by 2% osmium tetraoxide (ProSciTech) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 1 hour. After thorough washing in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, samples were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol and hexamethyldisilane (Sigma Aldrich) and dried in a desiccator overnight. Samples were mounted on SEM stubs (ProSciTech) and coated with a 10-nm layer of sputtered gold (Emitech K550X). Samples were imaged on the Zeiss Sigma VP HD scanning electron microscope. Quantification of PPN coverage was done through manual false coloring of PPNs on pericardium surface using Adobe Photoshop and analyzing area of color coverage using ImageJ. SEM-energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy was done using the Thermo Scientific Phenom XL Desktop scanning electron microscope.

For SEM imaging of PPN-apixaban (gold tagged) functionalized pericardium, apixaban (20 μM) was first conjugated to 10-nm gold nanoparticles (Abcam, ab269933) as per manufacturer instructions. Apixaban-gold conjugates were then conjugated to PPN-coated pericardium and prepared for SEM as above. Imaging was performed using backscattered electrons on the Zeiss Sigma VP HD scanning electron microscope to visualize the gold nanoparticles.

For transmission electron microscopy imaging, PPNs were collected on a 300-mesh grid with an ultrathin carbon film supported by a lacey carbon film (ProSciTech). Imaging was performed with an FEI Tecnai T12 transmission electron microscope at an acceleration tension of 120 kV.

Retention of PPNs under peristaltic flow

Pericardium patches (1.2 cm × 0.5 cm) were treated with 4% L-cysteine followed by PPNs. Samples were prepared for SEM imaging without being subject to peristaltic flow, serving as the t = 0 baseline (control group). Separate samples were sutured into a one-quarter–inch inner diameter Tygon tubing (ND-100-65; Saint-Gobain) and connected to a peristaltic pump loop system and washed with PBS for 28 days at 110 rpm. PBS was removed twice a week and replaced with fresh, sterile PBS. After 28 days, these washed samples were then prepared for SEM imaging. PPN coverage was then quantified using the false coloring technique described above. For retention under flow analysis, 1 image for each replicate was quantified.

Free aldehyde visualization

Pericardium rectangles (1 cm × 1.2 cm) were prepared according to their designated treatment group and were incubated in 1 mL Schiff's reagent stain (Sigma Aldrich, 3,952,016) for 20 minutes. Schiff’s reagent reacts with aldehyde groups to form a purple color. Samples were therefore imaged, and the amount of purple on each sample was quantified using ImageJ.

Cell culture

Human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs, passage 3-6; Cell Applications), THP-1 human monocytic leukemia cells (passage 3-6; Sigma Aldrich), and human dermal fibroblasts (ThermoFisher Scientific) were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified incubator. HCAECs were cultured in Mesoendo medium (Merck), THP-1s in RPMI-1640 medium (Merck), and human dermal fibroblasts in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum (10% v/v) and 1% antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). Cell culture media was replaced with fresh media every 2 to 3 days. Cells were subcultured after reaching ≥85% confluency by aspirating the old medium, washing with PBS, and adding fresh medium. HCAECs and human dermal fibroblasts were trypsinized, whereas THP-1s were aspirated from the flask. Resulting cell suspensions were transferred to a falcon tube for centrifugation (1,200 rpm, 5 minutes). The supernatant was aspirated, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium. Cell concentration was determined by staining with Trypan blue (1:1) and manually counting cells using a hemocytometer. The cell suspension was triturated before seeding the required volume onto a new T175 flask and adding fresh medium.

Endothelial cell viability and attachment

HCAECs were seeded onto 6-mm pericardium samples prepared according to their respective treatment group. For cell viability assay, HCAECs were seeded (8,000 cells per pericardium) onto samples. After 24 hours, cell-seeded samples were incubated with Alamar Blue reagent (1:10 with cell culture media) for 3 hours, and viability was quantified using a microplate reader (Infinite M1000 PRO) for fluorescence analysis. Pericardium without cells was also incubated with Alamar Blue for background subtraction. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 560 and 590 nm, respectively. For cell attachment, HCAECs were seeded (25,000 cells per pericardium) onto pericardium samples and left for 1 hour. Samples were then gently rinsed 3 times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, and washed with PBS (3 × 5 minutes) followed by fluorescent staining of F-actin with actinRed 555 ReadyProbes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells on the pericardium were visualized and imaged using an Axiovert (Zeiss) fluorescent microscope.

Biaxial testing

The mechanical properties of the samples were tested using an ElectroForce 200N 2 Motor Plan (1 motor per axis). Briefly, pericardium samples (1.6 cm × 1.6 cm) were hooked and connected to the 4 arms of the biaxial tester. Four hooks for each side of the sample were used to guarantee an equal distribution of the load. Before testing, the samples were preloaded to achieve a 1.0-N load. The samples were then stretched up to 3.5 mm in both directions (axial and transversal) with a strain rate of 0.1 mm/s. The maximum load (achieved at 3.5-mm displacement) and the stiffness (calculated as the ratio between the load and the displacement) of each sample was recorded. Cyclic tests were also carried out, with the samples subjected to 10 cycles of a 3.5-mm displacement at 0.01 mm/s.

Water contact angle

Pericardium tissue samples were cut into 1 cm × 1-cm squares and treated according to their respective treatment groups. Material surface wettability was measured via contact angle goniometry (Theta Flex, Attension; Biolin Scientific). Samples were initially dabbed dry with paper towels, and a 5 μL droplet of Milli-Q water was placed on the pericardium surface. The water contact angle between the water droplet and the pericardium surface was immediately measured using the system's inbuilt analysis software, OneAttension.

Whole blood clotting assay

Wells in a 24-well plates were coated with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Plates were then washed 3 times with PBS and left to dry overnight. Pericardium samples were cut into 0.8 × 1.2-cm patches, prepared according to their treatment group, preweighed, and placed into individual wells. Fresh human blood was obtained from donors (male or female, 18-60 years old) with informed consent, in accordance with guidelines of The University of Sydney Office for Research Integrity, Human Ethics Committee (Project 2014/244). Blood was drawn using standard venipuncture into polypropylene syringes (Terumo) with heparin (0.3 U/mL; Pfizer) and 10% w/v saline. Blood (750 μL/well) was added onto samples and left for 1.5 hours in an orbital shaking incubator (IntelliStack; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 70 rpm and 37 °C. Blood was removed, and samples were washed 3 times with PBS before being weighed. Clot weight was calculated by subtracting the weight of the samples after blood incubation by initial weight. Photographic images were taken for representative purposes. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit for platelet factor 4 (PF4; Abcam, ab100628) was used to quantify PF4 secretion after whole blood clotting according to manufacturer's instructions. Platelet-poor plasma was obtained by first centrifuging blood solution at 200g for 14 minutes and then by centrifuging the resulting plasma at 2,000g for 10 minutes at 22 °C.

Platelet-rich plasma assay

Whole blood was obtained as above and centrifuged for 15 minutes at 1,000 rpm in swinging rotors. The supernatant, platelet-rich plasma was removed and incubated with each sample for 60 minutes while rocking. Samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer and imaged by SEM as previously described.

In vitro inflammation assay

Pericardium samples (6-mm diameter circular discs) were prepared according to their respective treatment groups. THP-1s (70,000 cells) were seeded onto individual pericardium samples and immediately treated with phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (100 ng/mL) for 24 hours to induce adhesion onto the pericardium. Cells were then stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (1 μg/mL) for 3 hours at 37 °C, followed by adenosine triphosphate (1.25 mM), and left at 37 °C, inside the humidified incubator (5% CO2) overnight. ELISA kits for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; Abcam, ab181421) and IL-1β (Abcam, ab214025) were used to quantify cytokine levels in supernatants according to manufacturer's instructions.

In vitro calcification assay on tissue culture plastic

Human dermal fibroblasts were seeded onto a 12-well plate (30,000 cells per well) and left to grow until 90% confluency. Cells were then starved using starving media (Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium with 0.5% fetal bovine serum and 1% 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin) overnight and replaced with standard complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle media along with treatment groups including 50 μM phytic acid. The next day, media were replaced with calcification media (3 mM CaCl2 in complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium) and fresh 50 μM phytic acid. Calcification media and treatment were changed every other day. After 14 days, cells were washed in PBS (2 × 5 minutes) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. Cells were washed again in PBS (2 × 5 minutes) and stained with Alizarin red for 20 minutes with gentle rocking on a plate shaker. Staining solution was then removed, and cells were washed with Milli-Q water (5 × 1 minute) before being left to dry overnight at room temperature. Ten percent acetic acid (v/v) was added to wells for 30 minutes to solubilize the staining, and the contents were transferred to individual 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes. Eppendorf tubes were vortexed for 30 seconds and placed under heat at 85 °C for 10 minutes. After heating, samples were placed on ice for 5 minutes before 500 μL of contents were transferred to new Eppendorf tubes. Then, 500 μL of 10% ammonium hydroxide (v/v) was added to the tubes and mixed, 200 μL of contents was added to 96-well plates, and absorbance at 405 nm was measured using a Tecan M-1000 plate reader.

Calcification solution

Pericardium tissue samples (6-mm diameter circular discs) were prepared according to their respective treatment groups. Samples were placed in Eppendorf tubes containing 1 mL of calcium phosphate solution composed of 3.87 mM calcium chloride and 2.32 mM dipotassium phosphate in Tris buffer (pH 7.4). Samples were incubated on a plate shaker set to 120 rpm at 37 °C for 1 month. Calcium phosphate solution within each test tube was replaced every other day. Samples were then prepared and imaged under SEM as previously described.20,21

In vivo rat subcutaneous model

The study was approved by the University of Sydney Animal Ethics Committee (protocol no. 2024/AE002532). Experiments were conducted in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purpose. Ten-millimeter diameter pericardium tissue samples were prepared according to their respective drug treatment groups. Pericardium samples were washed in saline before subcutaneous implantation. Four 1- to 1.5-cm dorsal incisions were made to create subcutaneous pockets separated by ∼2 cm. Pericardial tissue samples (untreated, PPN-coated, PPN-drug functionalized, or drug alone treated) were inserted into each pocket, and incisions were closed with 4-0 PDS interrupted sutures. Animals were monitored daily for wound healing, behavior, and general health throughout the 28-day implantation period before they were killed and tissue collected for histological analysis. After 28 days, scaffolds were explanted and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at room temperature. Scaffolds were then dehydrated through an ascending ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm (HM340 semi-automated microtome; Thermo Fisher). Histopathology staining included Mason's trichrome for collagen to visualize fibrous capsule formation and Alizarin red S to visualize calcification.

Pulse duplicator valve testing

An expired Evolut R 26-mm diameter transcatheter aortic valve implantation valve (Medtronic) was tested, both before and after PPN coating, in a pulse duplicator (HDTi-6000; BDC Laboratories) for hydrodynamic performance under standard aortic conditions (1 valve, 10 pulses): 70 beats/min, 5 L min-1 cardiac output, 35% systolic duration, 120 mm Hg systolic pressure, 80 mm Hg diastolic pressure, isotonic saline solution of 0.9% sodium chloride as the test fluid, and 37 °C fluid temperature. Output metrics included the effective orifice area (a measure of how well the valve opens), regurgitant fraction (a measure of how well the valve coapts during closing), and mean gradient (a measure of the transvalvular pressure gradient across the valve). These parameters were compared between the uncoated and PPN-coated valve and against the ISO 5840-2 standards to demonstrate proper and clinically relevant functionality. Valve opening and closing were also qualitatively analyzed through the resulting images processed by the Statys software (BDC Laboratories). For the coating, the valve was pretreated with 4% L-cysteine and incubated in a 30-mL falcon tube containing 5 × 1,010 PPNs/mL suspended in nuclease-free water and left on a shaker overnight at 4 °C.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. All datasets were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). Equality of variance was assessed using an F-test (for comparisons between 2 groups) or the Brown-Forsythe test (for datasets with ≥3 groups). Groups were compared using the Student t test and either 1-way or 2-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post-hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons with a control. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9 (Graphpad Software), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Immobilization of PPN on pericardium

Glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine pericardium sheets (Supplemental Figure 1) were used as a model substrate to develop and optimize PPN coatings before application to bioprosthetic valves. Circular samples of 6-mm diameter were biopsy punched from sheets, providing size-controlled samples for high-throughput evaluation of functionalization efficiency and bioactivity (Figure 1A). Glutaraldehyde fixation generates surface aldehyde groups that we found to markedly inhibit PPN binding. To mitigate this effect, samples were pretreated with L-cysteine, a well-established aldehyde-quenching agent that caps reactive aldehyde groups. Samples pretreated with L-cysteine demonstrated enhanced PPN coverage (Figure 1B). This pretreatment resulted in more homogenous PPN attachment, with increasing PPN coverage across the pericardium surface as concentration increased (Figures 1B and 1C). Photomicrographs of PPN-treated pericardium appeared dark brown, serving as a qualitative visual indicator of PPN coverage and further demonstrated the benefit of L-cysteine pretreatment to enable efficient PPN binding. Given the constant motion and dynamic forces that bioprosthetic leaflets are subject to in vivo, we performed initial retention studies to model the durability of PPN coatings under simulated physiological conditions. A custom-built closed-loop system driven by a peristaltic pump was used to replicate pulsatile flow using PBS (Figure 1D). Pericardium samples were subjected to flow for 28 days at an accelerated RPM equating to approximately 12 weeks of cardiac cycles at average heart rate of 70 beats/min. SEM images taken before (prewash) and after (postwash) the 28-day flow period showed qualitatively that PPN coverage was uniformly reduced across the pericardium surface, without evidence of uneven or localized reductions (Figure 1E). Quantification of PPN coverage after flow showed retention of 74% ± 3% of prewash PPN coverage (P = 0.001) (Figure 1F). This indicated a level of stability that may be sufficient to support early to intermediate postimplantation therapeutic activity for valves in vivo.

Reduction of free aldehyde groups

Glutaraldehyde fixation creates residual free aldehyde groups that have been linked to cytotoxicity and leaflet calcification.5,22 L-cysteine treatment can facilitate a nucleophilic addition reaction between its thiol (-SH) or amine (-NH2) groups and the free aldehyde (-CHO) functional groups, resulting in a stable thiazolidine derivative that effectively neutralizes the free aldehyde group (Figure 2A). This reaction was validated in L-cysteine pretreatment of pericardium tissue using a Schiff-base reagent stain. Control untreated pericardium samples showed strong purple staining indicating high residual levels of free aldehyde groups. In contrast, no positive staining was observed on pericardium samples pretreated with L-cysteine only or L-cysteine + PPN (Figure 2B). Endothelial cell attachment and viability was assessed to determine cell compatibility of PPN coatings (Figure 2C). HCAECs attached to L-cysteine only and L-cysteine + PPN treated samples appeared larger and were more abundant compared with untreated controls (Figure 2D). Quantification of HCAEC number and spreading after acute attachment showed a 5.8-fold increase in cell number and 7.9-fold increase in cell spreading on L-cysteine + PPN treated samples compared with untreated controls, respectively (P = 0.020 and P = 0.036, respectively) (Figures 2E and 2F). This was consistent with Alamar Blue measurements, which showed a 13.1-fold increase in metabolic activity (P = 0.001) (Figure 2G). Notably, cell compatibility observed in L-cysteine + PPN groups were not significantly different from L-cysteine treatment only, indicating the observed improvements were largely a result of L-cysteine treatment rather than unmodified PPNs.

Mechanical and surface properties of PPN-coated pericardium

Distinct mechanical properties are required for pericardium tissue to function effectively as a valve leaflet.23 Biaxial mechanical testing was performed to assess whether L-cysteine or L-cysteine + PPN treatment altered these properties (Figure 3A). Control pericardium tissue exhibited anisotropic properties where in the axial direction, samples exhibited higher stiffness and a larger maximum load at maximum deformation compared with the transversal direction (Figures 3B and 3C). Importantly, L-cysteine only and L-cysteine + PPN treatment showed no significant differences compared with control in either stiffness or maximum load (Figures 3B and 3C) in either direction. A cyclic deformation test was also performed to evaluate changes in mechanical performance under repeated loading, specifically assessing any reduction in maximum loading capacity. L-cysteine + PPN treatment showed no significant difference in fatigue compared with control, indicating that PPN coating does not compromise mechanical durability over repeated deformation cycles (Figure 3D). In contrast, L-cysteine–only treated pericardium exhibited a significant relative decline in maximum load, reaching 76% of its initial value after 10 cycles. However, this higher rate of degradation is largely attributable to the elevated initial maximum load observed in L-cysteine–only treated tissue (Supplemental Figure 2). To further characterize the surface level effects of PPN treatment, water contact angle measurements were performed to assess changes in tissue wettability (Figure 3E). L-cysteine treatment resulted in a 1.6-fold increase in water contact angle compared with untreated pericardium (11° ± 2° vs 29˚ ± 4°, P = 0.007) (Figure 3F). L-cysteine + PPN treatment partially reversed this effect, reducing the angle to 22° ± 0.6°, although it remained significantly higher than that of untreated pericardium (P = 0.046). L-cysteine + PPN treated tissue is hereafter referred to as “PPN.”

Leaflet mechanics of PPN-coated bioprosthetic valve leaflets

A major challenge of translating drug functionalization strategies to bioprosthetic valves is to ensure the coatings do not interfere with the complex mechanics of leaflet motion. Modifications to the leaflet surface must preserve key hydrodynamic metrics commonly used to evaluate valve performance, including regurgitant fraction, effective orifice area, and positive pressure differential. PPN coatings were applied to a self-expanding transcatheter porcine bioprosthetic aortic valve (Evolut R 26-mm valve; Medtronic) using a scaled-up dip coating procedure to accommodate the large valve size and complex leaflet geometry. Visual inspections of dark brown–colored pericardium leaflets combined with SEM imaging verified successful PPN coating of the transcatheter valve (Figure 7A). Consistent with observations on the leaflets, PPN coating was also evident on the pericardial skirt, stent frame, and suture materials (Supplemental Figure 5). Valves were then deployed in an industry-standard BDC pulse duplicator system to subject valves to physiologically relevant flow and pressure (Figure 7B). Aortic views of the deployed valve within the pulse duplicator showed uninterrupted motion of the valve leaflets when coated with PPN in either an open or closed state (Figure 7C). This was verified through readouts from the pulse duplicator showing no significant differences in regurgitant fraction, effective orifice area, and mean gradient when comparing the PPN-coated valve with an untreated valve (Figures 7D-7F). Discussion

The durability of current bioprosthetic heart valves is limited by SVD, a pathologic combination of interconnected biological processes including thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification.5,6 With the rapid adoption of transcatheter valves in younger patient populations, durability is expected to become an increasingly important consideration.26 Current strategies to reduce SVD are primarily limited to chemically treating the glutaraldehyde pericardial tissue to mask calcium binding sites. However, the long-term effectiveness of these technologies is yet to be confirmed, and their application to transcatheter heart valves has only recently been introduced. Furthermore, these treatments offer only a passive approach to reducing SVD and its underlying biological processes. An alternative approach that actively modulates the biology at the leaflet interface could potentially enhance bioprosthetic valve resilience to SVD. Here, we demonstrated the potential of a novel nanoparticle platform PPN to facilitate active functionalization of exemplars from distinct drug families on bioprosthetic tissue. By showcasing the platform across 3 distinct drugs, we exemplify how it can separately target each of 3 underlying biological modes of SVD including thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification.

A strategy for efficient PPN coating on glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine pericardium was established. Pretreating the pericardium samples with 4% L-cysteine for 6 hours before PPN application enhanced coating coverage, resulting in a simple 2-step dip-coating protocol totaling 18 hours. Coating uniformity and density were also macroscopically confirmed by qualitatively measuring pericardium color change. Previously reported coating strategies for bioprosthetic tissue, such as layer-by-layer assembly27,28 and chemical covalent grafting,29 rely on multistep chemical protocols that hinder scalability and routine validation. Layer-by-layer techniques involve the sequential deposition of oppositely charged, drug-laden layers and require multiple immersion and rinsing steps, making the process time consuming and poorly suited for large-scale application. Similarly, covalent grafting with wet chemical crosslinkers typically requires surface prefunctionalization via 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide/N-Hydroxysuccinimide (EDC/NHS) chemistry and additional coupling reactions.30 Both approaches further depend on complex analytical techniques, including x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, elemental mapping, attenuated total reflection fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and SEM, to verify coating integrity and uniformity, adding labor and instrumentation burdens that limit large-scale use. Moreover, chemical crosslinkers are not always fully biocompatible and can negatively impact cell viability, further limiting their translational potential.31 In contrast, PPNs are applied by simple dip-coating with immediate visual confirmation, simplifying optimization and screening. Importantly, PPN coatings demonstrated good short- to medium-term retention under flow in vitro, remaining on the pericardium surface after 12 weeks of simulated pulsatile stress. Given the distinct temporal profiles of SVD mechanisms, with thrombosis and inflammation typically manifesting acutely and calcification progressing over time,5 our findings demonstrate retention sufficient for acute windows appropriate for reducing early blood clots and inflammatory processes. However, we acknowledge that further long-term stability testing is required to confirm relevance to chronic stages of deterioration. Although calcification is generally considered to be a chronic failure mode, early events including clot formation disrupting leaflet motion are contributors,6 leading us to consider that early changes to biological responses could ultimately have lasting effects beyond the period that PPNs are present.

Residual aldehyde groups on glutaraldehyde-fixed pericardium are increasingly recognized as contributors to early SVD, prompting substantial clinical and commercial efforts to develop effective aldehyde-neutralizing strategies.32 Accordingly, we evaluated the ability of L-cysteine to reduce residual aldehyde groups using a Schiff-base reagent stain. Pretreating glutaraldehyde-fixed pericardium with L-cysteine successfully reduced free aldehyde groups, and this reaction was unaffected by subsequent PPN coating. This stability reflects thiazolidine ring formation before PPN addition. The amine and carboxyl groups present on the PPN surface are not strong enough nucleophiles to outcompete the thiol group of the cysteine, making reversal of this reaction highly unlikely.18 Despite prior mention of L-cysteine as a potential aldehyde-reducing agent for pericardium,33 our results demonstrate for the first time the ability of L-cysteine to effectively reduce residual aldehydes on pericardium with a single 6-hour incubation step. This is comparable with or even superior to current gold standard aldehyde-capping technologies including RESILIA, which takes between 4 and 6 hours,34 and AOA treatment, which takes 48 to 120 hours for optimal performance.35 Because many commercial bioprosthetic valves already incorporate aldehyde capping, PPN coatings could be applied directly to these valve leaflets without the need for L-cysteine pretreatment. This positions PPN as a compatible add-on that does not require alteration of existing leaflet chemistry or design.

Improving the cell compatibility of fixed pericardium is essential for positive cellular remodeling and long-term valve durability.5 Re-establishing an intact leaflet endothelium maintains a nonthrombogenic surface, regulates inflammation, and supports valve resilience.36 The effect of the PPN coating on endothelial cell attachment and viability was therefore investigated using HCAECs, a standard and widely used model for assessing endothelial responses. Although these cells are not valvular endothelial cells and do not fully recapitulate valvular-specific biology, they provide a practical and relevant system for evaluating endothelial interactions with PPN coatings. HCAEC attachment and viability improved on L-cysteine and L-cysteine + PPN treated pericardium to a similar degree, suggesting that L-cysteine treatment largely drove these effects. This aligns with prior studies where L-glutamic acid pretreatment improved endothelialization and reduced thrombosis in pericardial grafts in vivo.37 Importantly, PPN attachment after L-cysteine pretreatment did not compromise endothelial compatibility, confirming PPN's low cytotoxicity and highlighting the coatings biocompatibility.38

Distinct mechanical properties are essential for pericardium to function effectively as a valve leaflet.23 Biaxial mechanical testing assessed whether L-cysteine or PPN treatment altered these properties. Pericardium exhibited anisotropic mechanical properties, consistent with its aligned orientation of collagen fibers. Neither L-cysteine nor PPN negatively impacted pericardium stiffness or maximum load in axial or transversal directions. L-cysteine has previously been shown to preserve tensile properties by maintaining collagen architecture.33 PPN coatings also showed no change in mechanical performance after repeat loading, confirming surface confined modification without affecting bulk tissue properties. This preservation of mechanical integrity reinforces the potential for integrating PPNs into existing bioprosthetic valve designs without compromising leaflet function.

Water contact angle is a common measure of wettability used to assess how coatings alter surfaces of materials. Treated pericardium presented as more hydrophobic compared with untreated pericardium. Reduced wettability likely resulted from L-cysteine reacting with pericardium resident aldehyde groups to form hydrophobic thioether bonds, increasing the water contact angle.39 PPNs have been shown to increase hydrophilicity,40 which was also observed compared with L-cysteine–only samples. However, the final contact angle remained markedly higher than that of untreated tissue. Moderate hydrophobicity may benefit valve performance by limiting water uptake and swelling, preserving mechanical integrity.41 It may also reduce protein adsorption and associated thrombogenic responses.42 These findings indicate that PPN treatment enables surface functionalization while reducing wettability to potentially support long-term leaflet durability.

After PPN characterization, the coating was functionalized with therapeutic cargo. PPNs have previously demonstrated versatility as nanocarriers, enabling direct drug conjugation without the need for additional linker chemistry.18 The direct factor Xa inhibitor, apixaban, was selected as a therapeutic agent to be functionalized on PPN-coated pericardium because of its anticoagulation properties and use clinically as an oral anticoagulant after valve placement.43,44 However, systemic administration of oral anticoagulants is often associated with an increased risk of bleeding, necessitating localized presentation to minimize systemic exposure.45,46 Apixaban functionalized PPN-coated pericardium reduced thrombus weight and platelet activation compared with control, highlighting that apixaban remained functional postfunctionalization. Although previous studies showed that PPNs can bind bioactive cargo on synthetic polymers,40 this work extends those findings to a biologically relevant tissue surface, indicating that PPN coatings are adaptable to more complex, nonsynthetic substrates. PPN coatings can effectively present bioactive apixaban on glutaraldehyde-fixed bioprosthetic tissue to reduce thrombosis burden. Comparable antithrombotic effects have been achieved using anticoagulant-laden nanogel coatings.47 However, these approaches required multistep chemical treatments, adding complexity and limiting scalability. In contrast, PPNs enable simple dip-coating and linker-free drug attachment within 30 minutes, offering clinical feasibility without impacting physical properties. From a clinical perspective, this strategy also addresses a critical limitation in the use of systemic anticoagulation after valve replacement procedures. Oral drugs like apixaban have been shown to reduce the incidence of SLT; however, their systemic use has been associated with increased bleeding and thromboembolic events.45 Localized drug presentation at the leaflet surface where SLT originates may offer a safer alternative with reduced off-target effects. These results supported further exploration of the PPN platform for drugs targeting other modes of bioprosthetic valve failure.

MCC950 is a selective NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor that blocks IL-1β secretion without affecting broader immune signaling pathways such as TNF-α secretion,48 distinguishing it from broad-spectrum vascular anti-inflammatory agents including colchicine and canakinumab, which have been associated with increased infection risk.49,50 Despite strong preclinical efficacy, clinical development of MCC950 was terminated because of hepatotoxicity observed during daily, high-dose systemic administration in phase II trials.51 However, localized, surface-restricted delivery at substantially lower doses may enable preservation of its anti-inflammatory benefits specific to NLRP3-mediated inflammation while minimizing off-target effects, reframing MCC950 as a potentially delivery-dependent therapeutic rather than intrinsically unsafe. Preserving the selectivity of MCC950 to IL-1β secretion provided a stringent test of PPN-mediated drug functionalization and its ability to maintain bioactivity at the tissue surface. Selectivity was successfully retained after MCC950 functionalization, with reduced IL-1β but unchanged TNF-α levels after macrophage stimulation. MCC950 has previously been shown to reduce fibrosis and inflammation around implanted polymers,52 demonstrating that localized presentation can achieve similar NLRP3 inflammasome–mediated anti-inflammatory effects without suppressing broader host immune function. The observed in vivo reduction of fibrotic capsule thickness of PPN-MCC950–coated pericardium further validated this concept and proved that drug functionality was conserved. No effect occurred with free, passively adsorbed MCC950, underscoring the importance of PPNs in enabling therapeutically meaningful drug presentation in vivo. This marks the first demonstration of PPNs functioning as an effective in vivo tissue coating capable of sustaining localized drug activity. Although our studies were conducted in a subcutaneous fibrosis model, largely due to greater accessibility than cardiac valve models, the results provide proof-of-concept that PPN-MCC950 coatings can modulate local NLRP3-mediated inflammation and fibrotic responses. This has key implications for bioprosthetic valves, which trigger foreign body responses causing leaflet fibrosis and extracellular matrix deposition. Notably, leaflet fibrosis has been observed as early as 60 days postimplantation,6 restricting mobility and impairing valve function, ultimately leading to SVD. Fibrosis is increasingly recognized as an intermediate stage of SVD, linking early thrombosis with later calcification.53,54 Developing leaflets that can locally inhibit inflammation and promote better integration with the vasculature is crucial for long-term valve performance and would represent a significant advance in valve durability. Although MCC950 was used here as an exemplar, PPN coatings presenting other well-characterized cardiovascular anti-inflammatory agents, such as colchicine or sulindac, could also be used in this setting, leveraging the versatility of the platform to accommodate diverse classes of drugs.

Improving resistance to calcification remains a major industry focus for enhancing valve durability and a key target for drug functionalization. Bioprosthetic valve calcification involves calcium phosphate deposition on collagen and elastin within the pericardial extracellular matrix, forming hydroxyapatite crystals.5 Phytic acid, a calcium-chelating drug, has been shown to inhibit calcification by blocking calcium crystal nucleation and growth in vascular applications such as decellularized grafts and arterial tissues.55 These findings support its potential utility in bioprosthetic valve leaflets, where similar extracellular matrix–driven calcification mechanisms occur. In our in vitro accelerated calcification model, PPN-coated groups reduced hydroxyapatite formation, consistent with anticalcification effects of aldehyde-capping pretreatments like L-cysteine.47,56, 57, 58 This supports current strategies that target residual aldehydes to limit calcification. However, the added value of PPN-phytic acid functionalization became evident in vivo. Although L-cysteine pretreatment alone contributed to a trend toward reduced calcification, significant reductions were only achieved in PPN-phytic acid functionalized groups. This demonstrates that combining passive tissue treatments with active drug functionalization enhances anticalcification efficacy. Although these results highlight the potential of PPN-phytic acid coatings, it is important to note that other calcium-targeting drugs have the potential to achieve similar effects.

Leaflet mechanics can be significantly altered by changing leaflet thickness even on a micron scale. One study evaluated valves with varying leaflet thickness using a pulse duplicator system and concluded that increasing leaflet thickness from 0.24 mm to 0.46 mm resulted in a reduction in effective orifice area (reduced from 2.5 cm2 to 1.1 cm2) and an increase in mean pressure gradient from 8 mm Hg to 25 mm Hg.59 It also demonstrated that even changing leaflet thickness by 25 μm can markedly affect hydrodynamic performance. PPNs are approximately 100 to 200 nm in size18 and therefore translated to no observable changes in leaflet mechanics when coated onto bioprosthetic valve leaflets. However, polymer coatings currently range between 5 and 15 μm and therefore are believed to directly impact leaflet mechanics, making them unsuitable for valve applications.60 This demonstrates that the simple, 2-step dip-coating process used for PPNs preserves critical mechanical endpoints while providing a translatable coating strategy for commercial transcatheter valves.

Study limitations

The in vivo findings in this study were derived from a 28-day rat subcutaneous implantation model, which, although established for assessing novel bioprosthetic tissues, does not replicate the hemodynamic forces, blood contact, or long-term biological remodeling that occur in implanted heart valves. Consequently, the results should be regarded as preliminary and proof-of-concept. Calcification and SVD develop over years in patients, and no currently available small or large animal model accurately reproduces these chronic processes. In addition, the accelerated in vitro models used to assess thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification cannot capture complex in vivo interactions such as immune modulation and leaflet motion dynamics. Finally, the present work focused on short-term performance and single-drug functionalization for each biological pathway. Further studies are required to examine multidrug loading, coating stability, and long-term pharmacologic activity under physiological flow. Despite these limitations, the findings provide strong foundational evidence supporting the translational potential of PPN-based biofunctional coatings for bioprosthetic heart valves.

Conclusions

In this study, we present a novel approach for the biofunctionalization of bioprosthetic valves using PPNs. PPN coatings can potentially enhance the long-term durability of bioprosthetic valves by enabling a diverse range of targeting strategies to actively address key biological mechanisms driving SVD. PPN-apixaban functionalized pericardium effectively provided enhanced resistance to clotting and platelet activation. Similarly, PPN-MCC950 functionalized pericardium showed a reduction in NLRP3-mediated inflammation in both in vitro and in vivo inflammatory models. Finally, PPN-phytic acid coatings inhibited hydroxyapatite crystal formation in vitro and reduced accelerated calcification in vivo. These findings establish PPNs as powerful platforms to immobilize different types of therapeutics directly at the leaflet surface without altering leaflet mechanics. This approach is scalable and functionally compatible with transcatheter self-expanding bioprosthetic valves. COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: SVD remains a major limitation of bioprosthetic heart valves, driven by thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification. Current strategies rely on passive tissue treatments that fail to actively engage with these biological processes, resulting in long-term valve failure. This study introduces a PPN-based coating capable of functionalizing glutaraldehyde-fixed valve leaflets with targeted bioactive drugs, enabling local modulation of thrombosis, inflammation, and calcification without altering mechanical performance. For clinicians, these findings introduce a potential leaflet-modification strategy that may improve valve durability and reduce the need for repeat surgical interventions.
