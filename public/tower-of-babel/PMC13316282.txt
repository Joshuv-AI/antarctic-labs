Reprogramming macrophage mechanosensation via TRPV4 modulating mechano-immunotherapy controls fibrotic encapsulation of biomaterial implants
Authors: Xueying Xu, Alex HP Chan, Yunfei Hu, Kieran Lau, Billie Pardavi, Ian L Chin, Matt Crago, Sina Naficy, Angus J Grant, Khoon S Lim, Steven G Wise, Lining Arnold Ju, Richard P Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModulating how macrophages sense mechanical cues offers a novel strategy to control fibrosis around implanted biomaterials. We term this approach ‘mechano-immunotherapy’, which involves the desensitization of immune mechanosensory pathways to control the host response. Here, we use RN-1734 (RN), a model small molecule to demonstrate the proof-of-concept that pharmacologically disrupting macrophage mechanosensation can mitigate fibrosis. In vitro, RN reduced calcium influx and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in J774. a2 macrophages. These effects were strictly context-dependent with efficacy observed only in macrophages on high-stiffness (10% w/v) GelMA hydrogels, with no significant impact on those in softer (5% w/v) hydrogels. In vivo, RN selectively attenuated fibrotic capsule formation around implanted electrospun scaffolds but not smooth hydrogels. Notably, despite hydrogels releasing ∼6-fold more drug than scaffolds, fibrosis was reduced only in the scaffold group, suggesting that therapeutic efficacy is driven by the inhibition of high mechanosensory input rather than the loaded drug concentration alone. Spatial transcriptomics revealed that macrophages acted as the primary mechanosensors at the tissue-implant interface. Unsupervised global principal component analysis revealed that RN acted predominantly on early day 3 macrophages. The strongest effect was observed in surface-adhered mechanosensitive macrophages, where RN treatment enhanced their M2-like phenotype and promoted their dispersal from clustered aggregates into broader distribution within the scaffold. This redistribution was accompanied by a marked reduction in the recruitment of interstitial macrophages from the surrounding tissue, which were enriched for matrix-forming gene signatures. Together, these findings suggest that pharmacological desensitization of immune mechanosensors may represent a promising, context-specific approach to improve biomaterial integration.Keywords: TRPV4, Mechanobiology, Mechanosensation, Foreign body response, Biomaterials

Introduction

Implanted medical devices and biomaterials are essential tools in modern medicine, but their long-term performance is often compromised by the body's immune response. An exaggerated, pathological foreign body response upon material implantation can lead to chronic inflammation and thick, dense fibrotic encapsulation, ultimately impairing implant function and longevity. For decades, strategies to reduce foreign body responses and implant fibrosis have focused on material modification strategies aimed at dampening or preventing inflammation. These include the integration of bespoke surface chemistries that serve as immunomodulatory cues [1], the incorporation of bioactive coatings to deliver anti-inflammatory drugs [2], and/or the design of biomimetic architectures that replicate native extracellular matrix features [3,4] to influence immune cell behaviour. While these strategies act primarily by modifying the material itself to indirectly shape the host response, they do not address the underlying biology of how the body senses and responds to the implant. Understanding how immune cells recognise and engage implanted biomaterials may reveal additional biological drivers of foreign body inflammation.

Tissues within the body exhibit a remarkable degree of mechanical and architectural heterogeneity. Soft tissues, blood vessels, nerves, fat, cartilage, and bone each possess distinct mechanical properties. For instance, fat and nerve tissues typically exhibit Young's moduli in the range of ∼0.1-1 kPa, blood vessels around 0.1-10 kPa, cartilage from 0.5 to 1 MPa, and cortical bone up to 20 GPa [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In contrast, common implantable biomaterials such as polymers, metals, and ceramics, tend to have uniform and significantly higher stiffness profiles often several orders of magnitude greater than the surrounding tissue into which they are implanted. Studies have demonstrated that this mechanical mismatch disrupts local tissue homeostasis, contributing to chronic inflammation and fibrotic encapsulation [9,10]. While many have proposed that making biomaterials softer to match native tissue stiffness could improve biocompatibility, replicating the intricate mechanical architecture of tissues, including their layered structure and cellular heterogeneity, remains a significant challenge, particularly when using synthetic materials that are inherently stiff, despite advances in biomaterial manufacturing [11]. Additionally, in some applications with load-bearing or structural implants, such as stents, mechanical stiffness is critical to implant performance [[12], [13], [14]], making the softening of their materials an impractical or undesirable solution. These challenges highlight the need for alternative strategies that go beyond simply adjusting material architecture and instead directly target the biological mechanisms through which the immune system recognizes and responds to mechanical mismatches at the implant–tissue interface.

Cells continuously monitor and respond to the mechanical properties of their environment through a process known as mechanosensation. Mechanosensation in immune cells is mediated in part by mechanosensitive ion channels that detect mechanical stimuli such as membrane stretch, shear stress, or resistance encountered during cell adhesion and spreading on substrates of varying stiffness [15,16]. These mechanical cues are transduced into intracellular biochemical signals, facilitated by intracellular calcium influx, which then activates downstream pathways involved in cytoskeletal remodelling, gene expression, and inflammatory responses [15,16]. Importantly, mechanosensory signalling can influence macrophage activation and polarization, guiding transitions between pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing (M2) states that shape the overall inflammatory and fibrotic response to biomaterials [17,18]. Among the diverse families of mechanosensitive ion channels, Piezo1 and transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) have emerged as key mediators of immune cell responses to mechanical cues from biomaterials [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. While separate genetic deletion studies have implicated both Piezo1 and TRPV4 as mechanotransduction regulators of macrophage-driven inflammation and fibrosis in response to subcutaneously implanted polyacrylamide gels [20,22], it remains unclear whether this biology can be harnessed therapeutically. While selective inhibitors of mechanosensitive ion channels have been shown to modulate inflammation in other biological settings, their ability to directly suppress the foreign body response and reduce fibrosis around implanted biomaterials in vivo has not been demonstrated, representing a promising and underexplored strategy in translational biomaterials research.

In this study, we investigated whether targeted inhibition of TRPV4 could serve as a new viable therapeutic strategy to suppress inflammation and fibrosis during foreign body remodelling of implanted biomaterials, representing a single early example within a broader concept of ‘mechano-immunotherapy’. RN-1734 (RN) is a selective and well-characterized small-molecule antagonist of TRPV4, with established efficacy in blocking TRPV4-mediated calcium signalling [23,24]. We focused on TRPV4 as a representative mechanosensitive channel because of its prominent role in macrophage mechanotransduction and well-characterized pharmacology, acknowledging that numerous other mechanosensitive channels exist with complementary inhibitors, and that systematically testing all channel-inhibitor pairs is beyond the scope of this study. Our use of RN served as a proof-of-concept for the broader strategy of targeting mechanosensory pathways to modulate the foreign body response. We showed that TRPV4 inhibition dampened macrophage activation in a stiffness-dependent manner, with its effects becoming more apparent under conditions where cells experience stronger mechanical cues both in vitro. These patterns were further characterized using in vivo spatial transcriptomic profiling to map localized macrophages responses around implanted biomaterials. These findings highlighted selective mechanosensory inhibition as an effective strategy to potentially improve the integration and long-term performance of implanted biomaterials.

Methods

Cell culture

Murine macrophages (J774. a2, passage 3–8, Sigma Aldrich) were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Macrophages were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Life Tech, 11995065) supplemented with Fetal Bovine Serum (10% w/v) and 1% Penstrep (Sigma, P0781-100 ML). The cell culture media was replaced with fresh media every 2-3 days. Cells were subcultured after reaching ≥85% confluency by aspirating the old medium, washing with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS; Sigma, P4417), and adding fresh medium (10 mL). A cell scraper was used to mechanically detach macrophages from the flask. Resulting cell suspension was transferred to a falcon tube for centrifugation (1200 rpm, 5 min). The supernatant was aspirated, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium (2–5 mL, depending on the culture flask). The cell suspension was triturated before seeding the required volume onto a new T175 flask and adding fresh medium (20 mL).

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, after settled overnight, cells were treated with drug groups (100 μL medium in total with drug) at 80 μM, 40 μM and 20 μM RN (Merk/Sigma, 616520). Cultures were left to incubate for 3 days. Cell viability was quantified using an Alamar Blue (Life Technologies, DAL1100) assay at a ratio of 1:10 with fresh supplemented DMEM media. Plates were incubated for 3 h and quantified using a microplate reader (Infinite M1000 PRO) for fluorescence analysis. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 560 and 590 nm, respectively. Complementary attachment assay and live/dead assay of RN-treated cultures was conducted parallel to viability assay. For attachment assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well followed by immediate treatment specified earlier, after 1 h of settling, cells were washed with PBS, replaced medium, then sent for imaging using IncuCyte SX5. A live/dead cell assay was performed on J774. a2 cells. A total of 2 × 104 cells in 100 μL of supplemented DMEM media were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. After allowing the cells to adhere for 3 h, they were placed with 80 μM, 40 μM and 20 μM RN primed media. The cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The next day, a live/dead staining solution containing 0.01% Calcein Green AM (ThermoFisher, C3099) and 1% Propidium Iodide (PI; ThermoFisher, P1304MP) in PBS was added to the wells without removing media. After incubating at room temperature for 10 min, cell images were captured using an IncuCyte SX5 imaging system. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software to quantify live and dead cells, % of live cells was calculated as (1- (PI stained/Total cells)) %.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, after settling overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator, 1 μg/mL Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma, L2630-10 MG) were added to the cells, co-treated with or without 80 μM RN, supernatant was collected 18 h post drug incubation. To study the effects of TRPV4 agonism on inflammatory cytokine release, 100 ng/ml LPS, 50 μM GSK1016790A (Sigma, GSK1016790A) and 10 μM 4α-Phorbol 12,13-didecanoate (4αPDD; Sigma/Merk, P8014-1 MG) were added to the cell culture respectively. 4 h post drug addition, supernatant was collected for ELISA assay. ELISAs for TNFα (Abcam, ab208348), IL-6 (Abcam, ab222503) and MCP-1 (Abcam, ab208979) were used to quantify cytokine levels to examine RN's effect according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Fura-2 calcium imaging

Changes in intracellular calcium were measured using a ratiometric calcium indicator dye Fura-2 AM (ThermoFisher. F1221) and a Nikon Ti-E microscope equipped with a CrestOptics X-light V3 spinning disk system. Cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per dish onto the glass surface of a glass-bottom imaging dish and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The following day, cells were incubated in drug-primed media for 4 h (for pre-treatment groups) or in media only. Subsequently, cells were washed in HBSS twice to get rid of media that may interfere with Fura-2 loading and loaded with 2 μM Fura-2 AM in the presence of 0.01% Pluronic F-127 (ThermoFisher, P3000MP) in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; ThermoFisher, 14065056) for 30 min in the dark. After dye loading and washing, cells were imaged in a live-cell chamber maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were excited at 340 nm and 380 nm with coolLED, with emission collected at 510 nm. Baseline fluorescence was recorded for 2 min, one frame every 500 ms. Cells were then stimulated with 100 μg/mL LPS, and imaging continued for an additional 20 min. For the Ca2+-free HBSS (ThermoFisher,14185052) control, all procedures were identical, except that during imaging, cells were maintained in Ca2+-free HBSS to assess Ca2+ influx under extracellular calcium depleted conditions. 340/380 fluorescence ratios over time were recorded for each cells using NIS-Elements software.

Western blot

J774. a2 macrophages were seeded at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates containing 2 ml of supplemented DMEM. After overnight incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), the medium was replaced with drug-primed media, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. Cells were then lysed using Mammalian Cell lysis kit (Sigma, MCL1-1 KT). Total protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay (ThermoFisher, 23225), and 5 μg protein were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min in LDS loading buffer (ThermoFisher, NP0008). Samples were resolved MES SDS Running Buffer (ThermoFisher, B0002) and transferred to PVDF (ThermoFisher, IB24001) membranes using semi-dry transfer machine (ThermoFisher, iBLOT2). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS (Abcam, ab3523), Arg-1 (Abcam, ab91279), NF-κB p65 (ThermoFisher, MA5-15160), and α-tubulin (Abcam, ab7291) (all 1:2000, diluted in 5% BSA in TBS-T; ThermoFisher, J60448. AP). After three washes in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:1000 in 5% skim milk; Sigma, P8375-1KU; ThermoFisher, 31430) for 2 h at room temperature. Signal was developed using ECL substrate (Millipore, WBLUR0500) and detected using a chemiluminescent imaging system. Image analysis was performed using Image Lab (Bio-Rad), and quantified band intensities which are normalised to α-tubulin. Membranes were stripped using a mild stripping buffer, washed twice in TBST, re-blocked, and re-probed with primary antibodies as described above. Complete removal of prior antibodies and preservation of antigenicity were verified by ECL imaging before incubation with subsequent primary antibodies.

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) fabrication

A 15% (w/v) GelMA (Sigma, 900629-5G) stock solution was prepared in PBS and warmed to 50 °C alongside PBS and 3% (w/v) Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP; Sigma, 900889-5G). Working solutions of 10% and 5% GelMA were prepared by diluting the stock in warm PBS containing 0.1% LAP. For hydrogel casting, 200 μL of GelMA solution was added to each well of a 24-well plate, 36 μL to each well of a 96-well plate, and 150 μL to glass-bottom dishes. Samples were photo crosslinked for 3 min using a 405 nm LED Crosslinker (SKU0004) from Gelomics.

Compression test

Compression testing was performed on 5 and 10% GelMA hydrogels (6 mm diameter x 1 mm height) following photo-crosslinking as described above using MCR 702e MultiDrive (Anton-Paar) equipped with an upper parallel plate of 8 mm diameter. Load-extension curves were obtained by compressing the hydrogels at a travel rate of 0.4 mm/min. Curves were then converted to stress-strain curves with the compressive modulus calculated from the linear region of the curves taken from 0.1 to 17.1%strain.

Murine macrophages on GelMA assessments

Cell viability was assessed 24 h post-seeding as described previous in section X. Brightfield images were captured using the IncuCyte SX5 system to evaluate cell morphology (N = 3). From each well, 100 μL of cell culture supernatant was collected for and cytokine analysis by ELISA, then replaced with AlamarBlue solution for viability assay as mentioned above. The medium collected was subsequently used for ELISA, following the manufacturer's protocol.

Fura-2 Ca flux assay

J774. a2 macrophages were detached from culture flasks, centrifuged, and resuspended in phenol red-free DMEM. Suspended cells were treated with either 160 μM RN or non-treated for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. Following treatment, cells were loaded with 2 μM Fura-2 AM in the presence of 0.01% Pluronic F-127 (in phenol red-free DMEM) for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C. After loading, cells were seeded into black 96-well plates pre-coated with 10% or 5% GelMA hydrogels. Fluorescence measurements were performed using a CLARIOstar Plate Reader at 37 °C. Excitation wavelengths of 340 nm and 380 nm were used, with emission collected at 510 nm from the top of the plate. Readings were taken every minute for 60 min. TCP/10% and 5% GelMA background fluorescence (wells without cells) was subtracted from both 340 nm and 380 nm signals prior to ratio calculation.

Fabrication of polycaprolactone (PCL, scaffold) and silk hydrogels (hydrogel)

Polycaprolactone (PCL) (scaffold) were produced by electrospinning using a custom system (IME Medical Electrospinning, Waalre, Netherlands). A 10% w/v PCL solution was prepared in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol (HFP) and mixed overnight at room temperature until fully dissolved. The solution was then transferred to a syringe (Terumo Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and delivered through a 20-gauge needle at a flow rate of 4 mL/h. Electrospinning was carried out under an applied voltage of 20 kV with a 16 cm working distance to a grounded stainless steel drum collector (10 cm diameter) rotating at 500 rpm. The resulting fibrous mats were carefully removed, washed, air-dried to eliminate residual solvent, and punched into 6 mm circular discs using a biopsy punch. To fabricate 5% silk hydrogels, an 8% (w/v) silk fibroin solution was diluted and mixed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP; ThermoFisher, 31490) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Sigma,8222871000) at a concentration of 1%, with MilliQ water. After thoroughly mixing, cast into silicone 6 mm diameter circular molds with a height of 1 mm. The mixture was then gelated at room temperature for approximately 40 min.

Characterisation of the materials (SEM/AFM)

To characterise the scaffold and silk hydrogel samples, we employed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for surface imaging and atomic force microscopy (AFM) for 3D surface morphology, surface mechanical properties (Young's modulus), and roughness measurements. Samples were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) for 1 h, followed by post-fixation in 2% osmium tetroxide (ProSciTech) in 0.1 M PB for another hour. After thorough washing in 0.1 M PB, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, treated with hexamethyldisilazane (Sigma Aldrich, MO, USA), and dried overnight in a desiccator. For SEM imaging, samples were mounted on SEM stubs (ProSciTech), sputter-coated with a 10 nm layer of gold (Emitech K550X) and imaged using a Zeiss Sigma VP HD Scanning Electron Microscope. AFM measurements were performed using a Nanowizard V Bioscience system (JPK Instruments/Bruker Berlin) with a commercially available cantilever (SAA-SPH-1UM, Bruker Nano Surfaces) featuring a cylindrical tip (1 μm radius), a pre-calibrated spring constant of 0.174 N/m, and a resonant frequency of 9.113 kHz in liquid. Surface roughness and Young's modulus were assessed in contact mode using Force Spectroscopy – Smart Mapping. Multiple 50 × 50 μm force volume maps with 1 μm pixel resolution were acquired per sample condition, with a maximum loading force of 2 nN to ensure appropriate indentation depths. Force curves were recorded and processed using JPK Instruments software, applying a Hertzian contact model for analysis.

Mouse subcutaneous implant model

Study approval was obtained from the University of Sydney Animal Welfare Committee (protocol number: 2023/2283). Experiments were conducted in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purpose. C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Animal Resources Centre, Australia. The animals were housed in a self-ventilated OptiMice system in groups of five mice per standard size cage. The environment is conditioned to have fixed daily 12-h light/dark cycles and temperature maintained at 21 °C. Mice had libitum access to food and water at all times. Prior to implantation, both scaffolds and hydrogels were incubated in an 80 μM solution of RN in PBS for 24 h 15 male C57BL/6 mice were randomly allocated into groups of 3 time points (3, 7 and 14 days), with n = 5 in each group. Isoflurane anaesthesia was induced 4% and maintained 2%, mice had dorsal surfaces shaved, sterilized with 80% ethanol. Four 1.5 cm incisions were then cut through the skin to create four subcutaneous pockets. A single scaffold, scaffold + RN, hydrogel or hydrogel + RN was inserted into each incision. The wounds were closed with 6.0 Nylon sutures. At day 3, 7, 14, mice (n = 5) were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and skin biopsies (1.5 × 1.5 cm2) together with scaffold implants were collected for histological analysis.

Histological analysis

Masson's trichrome staining was performed following Dowland's protocol [25]. Samples were fixed in 10% formalin overnight and then transferred to 70% ethanol. After dehydration in a tissue processor overnight, samples were embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned transversely at a thickness of 5 μm, deparaffinized, and stained. Stained slides were imaged using a Zeiss Axio scanning microscope. Capsule thickness was quantitatively assessed using ImageJ software. For each sample, ten measurements were taken from the skin (epidermal) side of the scaffold and averaged to obtain a single value per animal. Data represent the mean of five scaffolds per group per time point (N = 5).

Spatial transcriptomics

To investigate the transcripts, change in the implant tissue at single cell resolution, spatial transcriptomics technology was performed using the Xenium analyzer platform (10x Genomics). Paraffin embedded mouse back tissue (n = 3-4 per condition) was cut and prepared on the manufacturer slides, nucleus free water was used for collecting the slides and followed by 60 min of 45-degree oven air dry. Slides were further treated according to manufacturer's protocol. Reads generated from Xenium Analyzer were compiled and processed using Seurat (v5.2) installed on RStudio (v4.4.3). Other packages used include tidyverse, patchwork, dplyr, tibble, Matrix, readxl, ggplot2, sctranform, and EnhancedVolcano. Gene expression data were analyzed and the number of principal components selected for downstream analysis was informed by ElbowPlot function. The following dimensional reduction and clustering were completed using the standard Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis workflow on Seurat. Gene markers for each cluster were determined by fold change and percentage of cells expressing that gene using the FindAllMarkers function. Cell clusters were annotated manually, and images were generated with Xenium Explorer v4. Gene ontology analysis was conducted with enrichR (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Cell distance from scaffold was measured using R script with packages sp and sf. Outline of scaffold was informed by Masson's trichrome staining. Each cell distance from the edge of the scaffold was saved as a metadata. Code availability on https://github.com/ahpchan/RN-1734.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM and indicated in figures as ∗ p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 version 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Comparisons between groups were performed using appropriate statistical tests, including unpaired two-tailed t-tests for two-group comparisons and one- or two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons for experiments involving more than two groups or multiple time points.

Results & discussion

Baseline characterisation of RN-1734 effects on macrophages in vitro

RN-1734 (RN) is a widely established selective inhibitor of the TRPV4 mechanosensitive ion channel that blocks the influx of extracellular calcium [23,24,26] (Fig. 1A). To determine an appropriate working concentration of RN-1734, murine J774. a2 macrophages viability was assessed across a dose range of 20–160 μM. While cells remained viable between 20 and 80 μM, a significant reduction in viability was observed at 160 μM (SuppFig. S1). To avoid confounding effects associated with cytotoxicity, subsequent experiments were performed using RN-1734 within the 20–80 μM range, and basic cell functions were evaluated. While the addition of RN showed no observable effects on macrophage morphology, a dose-dependent decrease in cell attachment was found with a significant 69% reduction (268 ± 19 vs 84 ± 8 cells/mm2, p < 0.001) occurring only at the 80 μM dose compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1B and C). However, this decrease in attachment was not associated with any negative impacts on cell viability (adhered and non-adherent cells) nor proliferation (adhered cells) (Fig. 1D and E). These findings suggested that TRPV4 inhibition could influence macrophage adhesion as a key early event in foreign body responses, without compromising macrophage viability. This justified further testing under inflammatory activation, where calcium-dependent signalling plays a prominent role.

RN-1734 inhibits macrophages extracellular calcium influx following LPS stimulation

Following seeding, macrophages were stained with Fura-2 prior to stimulation with LPS to visualise and quantify the effect of adding RN on the degree of calcium influx (Fig. 1F). Immediately following stimulation, macrophages showed a rapid calcium influx lasting ∼90s before returning to baseline, indicated by a ∼40% increase in peak F340/F380 ratios compared to pre-stimulation levels (Fig. 1G and H). Pre-treatment with RN substantially reduced this influx as shown by flattened F340/F380 traces and a significant 76% reduction in peak influx compared to LPS alone (0.37 ± 0.02 vs 0.09 ± 0.01 ΔF340/F380, p < 0.0001). Importantly, this signal originated from extracellular rather than intracellular calcium stores, as stimulation in calcium-free HBSS produced no influx and significantly lower levels compared to LPS alone. Representative images show that calcium influx occurs primarily in the cytoplasm, with minimal signal detected in the nucleus nor observable changes to cell morphology (Fig. 1I; real-time videos SuppFig. S2).

TRPV4 modulates macrophage activation and polarization

Having established that RN blocks TRPV4-mediated calcium influx, we next examined whether this modulation of calcium signalling translated into changes in downstream inflammatory and polarization responses. LPS-stimulated macrophages treated with RN exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in classical pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (Fig. 2A–C), with significant decreases of 73% and 77% in TNF-α and IL-6, respectively, observed at the 80 μM dose compared to control (1252 ± 121 vs 340 ± 48 and 4618 ± 503 vs 1069 ± 219 pg/mL, p < 0.01 and p < 0.0001). Moreover, RN had no effect on MCP-1 secretion.

Western blot analysis (full-length blots provided in SuppFig. S3) of polarization markers at 80 μM showed that RN significantly reduced LPS-induced iNOS expression by 100% while preventing a decrease in Arg1, yielding a significant 24% increase in expression when compared to LPS control (2.8 ± 0.43 vs 0 ± 0 and 3.8 ± 0.02 vs 4.7 ± 0.07 × 106, respectively, p < 0.0001 and p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D–F). This effect was observed to be at least partially mediated through NF-κB signaling, as RN treatment reduced phosphorylation of the p65 subunit by 41% (2.2 ± 0.08 vs 1.3 ± 0.18, p < 0.01) relative to LPS control (Fig. 2G).

Having established that TRPV4 antagonism reduces LPS-induced calcium influx and cytokine secretion, we established whether direct activation of TRPV4 was sufficient to drive macrophage activation. Since LPS signals primarily through TLR4 [27], its effects may only be indirectly dependent on TRPV4. To determine whether TRPV4 itself can induce inflammatory responses, we treated macrophages with separate TRPV4 agonists to assess calcium influx and cytokine secretion (Fig. 2H). Macrophages were treated with the established TRPV4 agonists 4α-Phorbol 12,13-didecanoate (4α-PDD) or GSK1016790A (GSK) in the absence of LPS. As expected, both agonists induced calcium influx (Fig. 2I; only GSK shown; real-time videos SuppFig. S4). This calcium influx was accompanied by increased TNF-α secretion in both agonists (Fig. 2J and K), which was not significantly different to LPS stimulation alone, indicating that direct TRPV4 activation is sufficient to induce a pro-inflammatory response. Pre-treatment with RN-1734 significantly reduced the calcium influx induced by both 4α-PDD and GSK, indicating its direct inhibition of TRPV4 activity (SuppFig. S5). Additionally, activation with both TRPV4 agonists in calcium-free containing media did not induce TNF-α secretion, demonstrating that extracellular calcium influx is required for TRPV4-mediated macrophage activation (SuppFig. S6).

RN modulates macrophage activation in a stiffness-dependent manner

The previous experiments demonstrated that TRPV4 inhibition suppresses macrophage activation in response to strong inflammatory stimuli such as LPS. We next asked whether TRPV4 also mediates responses to more subtle, persistent mechanical cues likely to also exist at the implant–tissue interface, independent of pathogen-derived signals. To examine whether TRPV4 mediates macrophage responses to substrate stiffness, we used gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels of 5% (soft) and 10% (stiff) to provide a controlled model of low versus high mechanical input. The 5% and 10% (w/v) GelMA concentrations were selected based on established characterizations demonstrating their distinct stiffnesses, which elicit differential macrophage responses such as M1 polarization and cytokine secretion on stiffer matrices [28,29]. These values mimic physiological soft (e.g., native ECM) and stiff (e.g., fibrotic) environments relevant to biomaterial integration. Compression testing confirmed that 10% GelMA exhibited a 954% higher compressive modulus compared to 5% GelMA (23.85 ± 0.17 vs 2.5 ± 0.17 kPa, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), consistent with its designation as a stiffer substrate (Fig. 3A and B), indicating that higher GelMA content increased stiffness under compressive loading.

Macrophages exhibited moderately greater viability when plated on soft gels compared to stiff gels, however RN treatment had no effect on viability in either condition (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, brightfield imaging revealed distinct differences in cell organization where macrophages cultured on stiff gels formed densely packed clusters, whereas those on soft gels grew as more evenly dispersed single cells after 24-h culture (Fig. 3D). Strikingly, RN treatment disrupted these clusters on stiff gels, restoring a single-cell morphology resembling that observed on soft gels.

Fura-2 calcium flux assay over the first 1-h window immediately after seeding revealed gradual increases in intracellular calcium in macrophages on both stiff and soft gels (Fig. 3E), in contrast to the transient bursts observed with LPS stimulation previously. F340/F380 ratios in macrophages on both stiff and soft gels peaked at ∼40 min. RN treatment attenuated these levels of calcium influx on both substrates, but a significant 71% reduction was observed only on stiff gels compared to untreated controls (0.18 ± 0.03 vs 0.05 ± 0.01, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3F). Representative images confirmed cytoplasmic localization of calcium influx, with no alterations in overall cell morphology at 1 h (Fig. 3G).

Surprisingly, cytokine analysis showed a distinct profile compared to the LPS assays. RN treatment did not affect TNF-α secretion under either stiffness condition (Fig. 3H). Instead, both IL-6 and MCP-1 secretion were sensitive to substrate stiffness. Macrophages on stiff gels exhibited a significant 78% and 42% reduction in baseline IL-6 and MCP-1 secretion, respectively, compared to macrophages on soft gels (3936 ± 281.1 vs 852.6 ± 135.3 and 174.4 ± 10.73 vs 101.4 ± 3.91 pg/mL, p < 0.0001 and p < 0.001) (Fig. 3I and J); on TCP, the extreme rigidity far exceeds the physiological stiffness range and, together with non-physiological surface chemistry, likely saturates macrophage mechanosensing and limits cytokine responsiveness [30,31]. Further treatment with RN reduced IL-6 secretion only under stiff conditions by 45% (3936 ± 281.1 vs 2155 ± 206.2 pg/mL p < 0.001), whereas MCP-1 secretion was reduced by RN across both stiff and soft gels by 20% and 26% respectively (174.4 ± 10.73 vs 139.2 ± 9.51 and 101.4 ± 3.91vs 75.02 ± 2.32 pg/mL, p < 0.05 and p < 0.05).

RN reduces fibrotic encapsulation in implants with greater mechanical cues and architectural complexity

Extending these in vitro findings, we next sought to determine whether these selective mechanosensory effects translated in vivo. Subcutaneous implantation of 5% (soft) and 10% (stiff) GelMA hydrogels for up to 14 days revealed fibrotic capsule formation around both substrates, however RN treatment did not significantly alter capsule thickness in either group (SuppFig. S7). These results suggest that, while the hydrogels exhibited measurable stiffness differences in vitro, the TRPV4-dependent mechanosensory pathways that drove differential responses on stiffer substrates were potentially not sufficiently engaged on GelMA in vivo.

To examine the effects of stronger mechanosensory input on macrophage-drive fibrosis, we selected complementary biomaterial implants representing opposite ends of the mechanical and architectural spectrum in an electrospun PCL scaffold and a silk hydrogel (Fig. 4A). SEM imaging showed that the electrospun PCL scaffold provided a fibrous, porous surface with pronounced topographical variation, whereas by comparison the hydrogel was smooth, non-porous, and architecturally uniform (Fig. 4B). Using AFM nanoindentation, to assess surface mechanics, further verified that the PCL scaffold exhibited higher stiffness, with peak values approximately 311% greater than the hydrated silk hydrogel (149.0 ± 16.88 vs 4.79 ± 1.41 kPa, p < 0.01), and a heterogeneous stiffness landscape across the surface (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the hydrogel demonstrated relatively uniform stiffness. Roughness mapping further highlighted the disparity in topography with the scaffold displaying pronounced peaks and valleys with average roughness approximately 1749% higher than the hydrogel (656.8 ± 122.4 vs 37.56 ± 11.61 μm, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4D). Due to the combination of higher surface stiffness and greater topological complexity, it was reasoned that the PCL scaffold likely provided a higher and more heterogeneous mechanosensory input compared to the hydrogel. This distinction was further underscored by both implants exhibiting different loading and release profiles of incubated small-molecule drugs (SuppFig. S8). Hydrogels released roughly 6-fold more drug than scaffolds (1411 ± 31.01 vs 210.3 ± 6.24 ng, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4E), reinforcing that these materials represent fundamentally different mechanobiological and biochemical contexts rather than direct comparators.

Following 14 days of subcutaneous implantation, fibrotic capsules developed around both biomaterials. Notably, RN treatment resulted in a 56% reduction in capsule thickness around the PCL scaffold implants only (141.10 ± 17.64 vs 62.67 ± 4.94 μm, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4F), consistent with a likelihood of increased engagement of TRPV4-dependent mechanosensory pathways. In contrast, despite having greater theoretical drug loading, no significant effect of RN was observed in silk hydrogel implants, potentially indicating that conditions surrounding its implantation was insufficient to elicit a measurable response. Representative images at day 14 show fibrotic capsules with similar collagen content as visualized by trichrome staining (Fig. 4G).

Spatial transcriptomic profiling of cellular composition at the tissue-implant interface

To explore this concept further and unravel the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these differential fibrotic responses, we performed longitudinal spatial transcriptomic profiling specifically of the implant–tissue interface region at days 3 and 14 across both biomaterial types and treatment groups. This approach allowed us to assess how RN impacted macrophage transcriptional states and spatial organization over time (Fig. 5A), providing insight into whether the observed reductions in capsule formation correlate with shifts in mechanosensory-driven immune activity. We first interrogated the cellular composition at the implant–tissue interface by aggregating single-cell transcriptomic data across treatments for each material and visualized them using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), splitting both scaffold and hydrogel plots by timepoints day 3 and day 14 (Fig. 5B and C). For both scaffold and hydrogel implants, two major clusters consistent with macrophage and fibroblast markers appeared at the tissue-implant interface. A smaller cluster of cells expressing neuronal markers was also detected, likely corresponding to peripheral nerve-associated cells within the skin. This suggested that, at the developing fibrotic capsule, macrophages and fibroblasts were the predominant cell types. Comparing across day 3 and day 14 within both scaffolds and hydrogels, showed that macrophages and fibroblasts displayed distinct transcriptional phenotypes, reflected in the markedly different UMAP shapes between the two timepoints for both materials. Splitting each timepoint by treatment (control vs RN, SuppFig. S9) revealed no substantial differences in cluster structure, suggesting that RN treatment did not introduce major proportion shifts of phenotypes in either macrophages or fibroblasts.

Quantitative cluster frequency analysis indirectly confirmed these observations. The main significant difference observed was a higher abundance of macrophages at day 3 in scaffold implants compared to hydrogels (1958 ± 289 vs 881 ± 259 cells/mm2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). This suggested that the early macrophage response was greater at the surface of scaffolds than at hydrogels. Within scaffolds, macrophages were significantly more abundant than fibroblasts at day 3 (1958 ± 289 vs 433 ± 175 cells/mm2, p < 0.001). A similar but non-significant trend was observed in hydrogels, suggesting that macrophages act as the predominant early responders in both implant types. By day 14, however, this balance shifted. Fibroblast abundance increased markedly in both materials, with the strongest expansion occurring in hydrogels (147 ± 25 vs 1902 ± 244 cells/mm2, p < 0.0001). In contrast, macrophage numbers declined in scaffolds but remained steady in hydrogels, persisting at levels comparable to day 3. Notably, RN treatment did not significantly affect macrophage or fibroblast abundance at any timepoint or in either material, indicating that its effects were unlikely mediated through changes in cell recruitment/composition. Collectively, these findings showed overall, that macrophages were the predominant early responders, particularly enriched in scaffolds, whereas fibroblasts expanded later and more prominently in hydrogels.

Macrophages dominate early mechanosensory gene expression at implant surfaces

To investigate which cell types drove the link between mechanosensation and downstream fibrotic responses, we defined two supervised gene panels: a mechanosensory panel including Piezo1, TRPV4, Itga/b, and related genes, and a pro-fibrotic panel encompassing classical immune and fibrotic markers such as Col1a1, NF-κB, and IL-6 (Fig. 5E). Using these panels, we generated feature plots across the aggregated UMAPs for both materials and timepoints (Fig. 5F). Visual inspection of the feature plots indicated that mechanosensory panel genes were almost exclusively enriched in macrophages at day 3, whereas pro-fibrotic panel genes were predominantly localized to fibroblasts at day 14 across both scaffold and hydrogel interfaces. These results suggested that macrophages and fibroblasts were the principal cell types mediating the mechanosensation-to-fibrosis axis.

To better resolve the magnitude to which each cell type contributed to mechanosensory versus pro-fibrotic programs and how their roles evolve over time, we quantified transcript density for each panel across macrophages and fibroblasts (Fig. 5F and G). Macrophages in scaffolds expressed significantly higher levels of mechanosensory transcripts than hydrogel macrophages at day 3 (29064 ± 4026 vs 15899 ± 6157 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.05). Within scaffolds, macrophages also exhibited higher mechanosensory transcript density than fibroblasts at the same timepoint (29064 ± 4026 vs 9512 ± 3240 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.05). These findings suggested that the elevated mechanosensory gene expression in macrophages at scaffold interfaces may reflect the higher mechanical cues provided by scaffolds, and that macrophages, rather than fibroblasts, serve as the primary mediators of mechanosensory sensing in this environment. Tracking mechanosensory gene expression over time revealed distinct temporal dynamics between cell types. From day 3 to day 14, macrophages overall showed a general decline in mechanosensory gene expression, which was significant in scaffold-associated macrophages (29064 ± 4026 vs 10976 ± 1041 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.0001), but not in those at hydrogel surfaces. In contrast, fibroblasts overall displayed an increase in mechanosensory gene expression from day 3 to day 14 in both materials, reaching significance only in hydrogels (1794 ± 380 vs 15971 ± 1659 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.01). These patterns suggested a potential shift in mechanosensory function over time, where macrophages may act as the dominant early sensors, while fibroblasts increasingly assume this role during later stages of capsule development. However, it is important to note that this interpretation was limited to transcriptional signatures and did not directly measure functional mechanosensory activity. Importantly, despite the temporal shifts in mechanosensory gene expression, RN treatment was still not found to have a significant effect on these two supervised transcriptional measures at this timepoint in either cell type or material.

Fibroblasts drive late-stage pro-fibrotic gene expression at implant interfaces

Similar quantification of pro-fibrotic gene expression also revealed distinct temporal dynamics between scaffolds and hydrogels. At day 3, both macrophages and fibroblasts associated with scaffolds exhibited significantly higher pro-fibrotic gene expression compared to their counterparts in hydrogels (58166 ± 8437 vs 28579 ± 10367 and 56336 ± 19124 vs 14485 ± 3188, respectively, transcripts/mm2, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01), suggesting that both cell types mounted a stronger fibrotic response to scaffolds at early timepoints. By day 14, the most striking change in pro-fibrotic gene expression occurred in fibroblasts associated with hydrogels increasing significantly from day 3 (14485 ± 3188 vs 133568 ± 6665 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.0001), ultimately surpassing that of hydrogel-associated macrophages (133568 ± 6665 vs 27306 ± 2804 transcripts/mm2, p < 0.001). These dynamics indicated that while scaffolds elicited a stronger early pro-fibrotic response, over time hydrogel-associated fibroblasts progressively upregulated fibrotic signalling, highlighting a potentially delayed but substantial contribution to fibrosis. Notably again, RN treatment did not significantly alter pro-fibrotic gene expression in any cell type or material at either timepoint. This may reflect that the effects of RN on transcriptional programs occur at earlier timepoints than captured here, or that supervised analysis using pre-defined gene panels overlooked unanticipated transcriptional changes that are relevant to these timepoints. These possibilities prompted us to take an unbiased, unsupervised approach to identify the major transcriptional programs most strongly impacted by RN treatment at days 3 and 14.

Unsupervised transcriptomic profiling of RN effects in vivo

To capture unbiased transcriptional differences associated with RN treatment, we performed a separate principal components analysis (PCA) on scaffold- and hydrogel-associated cells across both timepoints and treatments. In both materials, samples separated distinctly along the PC1 axis according to timepoint, with day 3 samples positioned on the left and day 14 samples on the right, reflecting temporal progression and transcriptionally distinct remodelling. No comparable separation was observed in hydrogel samples along either principal component, indicating that RN treatment did not induce detectable transcriptional changes in this material, and therefore we focused subsequent analyses exclusively scaffolds.

However, only in scaffolds, we observed an additional separation between control and RN-treated samples along the PC2 axis. Control samples appeared shifted toward higher (positive) PC2 values and RN-treated samples toward lower (negative) PC2 values, suggesting treatment-specific transcriptional programs. This separation along PC2 accounted for 10.74% of variance and was most pronounced at day 3 (Fig. 6A and B). We next examined the top PC2 gene loadings driving this separation (SuppFig. S10). Genes driving the negative PC2 direction (RN-treated scaffolds) were enriched for M2-like macrophage biological processes, with GO terms including Positive Regulation of Apoptotic Cell Clearance (GO:2000427) [32,33], Positive Regulation of Macrophage Differentiation (GO:0045651) [34], and Positive Regulation of Type 2 Immune response (GO:0002830) [34] (Fig. 6C)). In contrast, Genes enriched in the positive PC2 direction (control scaffolds) were associated with fibrosis-related pathways, including Extracellular Matrix Organisation (GO:0030198) [35,36], Collagen Fibril Organisation (GO:0030199) [35,36], External Encapsulating Structure Organisation (GO:00415229) [35,36] (SuppFig. S11). Together, these findings highlighted that the dominant transcriptional effects of RN treatment occurred in macrophages, shifting them away from pro-fibrotic states and toward processes aligned with an M2-like, anti-inflammatory and reparative phenotype. In contrast, control scaffolds were transcriptionally characterized by fibroblast-driven fibrotic programs, consistent with fibroblasts serving as the main effectors of capsule formation. These results suggest that RN primarily acted on macrophages, and by altering their activation state, indirectly influenced fibroblast fibrotic programs to reduce capsule thickness.

Differential expression analysis of PC2 loading genes at days 3 and 14 revealed no significant upregulation in RN-treated groups, which was unexpected given the separation observed in PCA (SuppFig. S12). To better resolve these patterns, we instead generated heatmaps of the top PC2 genes. At day 3, control scaffolds exhibited a pronounced downregulation of these genes, whereas RN-treated scaffolds maintained comparatively higher expression (Fig. 6D), suggesting that RN mitigated the early transcriptional suppression associated with pro-fibrotic programming. By day 14, however, this distinction was less apparent, with both control and RN-treated scaffolds displaying widespread downregulation (Fig. 6E), indicating that RN's effects on macrophage-associated transcriptional states may be most prominent during the acute response and wane over time.

To determine where these transcriptional changes were occurring, we spatially mapped the PC2 loading genes back onto the scaffold sections. RN treatment resulted in a clear reduction of PC2-positive loadings and a corresponding increase in PC2-negative loadings, consistent with the PCA results (Fig. 6F). These shifts were spatially localized to the scaffold–tissue interface. Interestingly, RN treatment increased the density of PC2-negative loadings within the scaffold interior, in contrast to controls where these signals were concentrated almost exclusively at the outer surface. Because PC2-negative loadings corresponded to M2-like, reparative programs, this redistribution suggested that RN was not only potentially altering macrophage transcriptional states but could have also been enabling these cells to penetrate deeper into the scaffold. To more precisely define this dynamic transcriptional behaviour at the cellular level, we next performed subclustering analyses of the distinct macrophage populations.

Identification of macrophage states most responsive to RN at the scaffold surface

Having established that RN-driven transcriptional changes are localized to the scaffold–tissue interface, we next sought to determine which macrophage subsets were most affected. To do this, we aggregated all macrophages from the original UMAPs across both materials and timepoints, and performed subclustering into finer subpopulations (Fig. 7A). This analysis yielded eight distinct macrophage subclusters. Their distribution was strongly time-dependent with subclusters 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 7 largely restricted to day 3 (Fig. 7B), whereas subclusters 1 and 3 appeared predominantly at day 14 (Fig. 7C). This temporal partitioning aligned with our PCA results, where day 3 and day 14 samples segregated along PC1, confirming that distinct macrophage phenotypes emerge at different stages of the response and were the primary driver of transcriptional differences observed in the PCA. However, the greater number of subclusters present at day 3 suggested higher phenotypic diversity and plasticity during the acute phase of scaffold remodelling.

Subcluster frequency analysis revealed that each timepoint was dominated by specific macrophage states (Fig. 7D). At day 3, subclusters 0 and 2 accounted for the majority of macrophages (47% and 25%, respectively), while at day 14 this shifted to subclusters 1 and 3 (60% and 20%). All remaining macrophage subclusters were present at low frequencies, contributing minimally to the overall population (SuppFig. S13). RN treatment appeared to differentially regulate only the dominant subclusters at day 3 (Fig. 7E). Subcluster 0, which was highly abundant in controls, trended lower with RN, whereas subcluster 2, which was relatively less represented, was significantly increased by 83% as a result of RN (487 ± 116 vs 892 ± 263 cells/mm2, p < 0.05). At day 14, however, subcluster frequencies were unaffected by treatment, potentially aligning with the idea that RN exerted its effects at earlier stages when macrophages were more plastic. To determine how RN affected macrophage subclusters, we mapped unsupervised PC2 loadings back onto each subcluster to identify which were transcriptionally responsive. PC2 negative loadings, representing an M2-like profile, were most prominent in day 3 subclusters compared to day 14 (Fig. 7F). At day 3, subcluster 0 exhibited the highest baseline M2-like signal but was unaltered by RN treatment. In contrast, subcluster 2 showed a significant increase in M2-like programming under RN, indicated by a 215% increase in PC2 negative loading transcript counts (1628 ± 85 vs 5141 ± 1983 counts, p < 0.05). For PC2 positive loadings, indicative of pro-fibrotic profiles, the greatest expression levels were found in subcluster 0 at day 3 and subcluster 1 at day 14 (Fig. 7G). However, RN treatment did not induce any transcriptional changes in these populations. Together, these findings suggested that subcluster 2 was the primary macrophage population transcriptionally responsive to RN, exhibiting both increased abundance and enhanced M2-like programming.

To determine whether RN affected macrophages beyond transcriptional changes, we analyzed their spatial distribution. This revealed significant differences across all macrophage subclusters. A consistent trend was that RN treatment promoted closer association of macrophages with the scaffold surface and/or increased their infiltration into the scaffold body (Fig. 7H). Focusing on day 3, when RN effects were most pronounced, subcluster 0 cells, which are normally found in the tissue surrounding the scaffold (interstitial), became more closely associated with the scaffold surface after treatment. More strikingly, subcluster 2, identified as the most responsive to RN, was normally surface-restricted but showed deeper scaffold infiltration following RN treatment (Fig. 7I, arrows). This potentially suggested that transcriptional changes by RN on subcluster 2 drove the most pronounced effects on the scaffold environment, indirectly influencing the spatial distribution and behaviour of the other macrophage subclusters.

Mechanosensitive reprogramming of M2 macrophages by RN at the tissue-implant interface

To further understand this RN-induced spatial behaviour at the tissue–implant interface, we mapped early macrophage subclusters back onto scaffolds. This confirmed our previous quantifications, with surface-associated subcluster 2 macrophages appearing clustered along the scaffold surface in controls, which then became more abundant and dispersed after RN treatment, promoting greater scaffold infiltration. This was highly consistent with our earlier spatial mapping of PC2-negative genes localised to inside the scaffold body. Meanwhile, the interstitial subcluster 0 population decreased in number and shifted closer to the implant surface, bringing it into closer proximity with scaffold-associated subcluster 2 macrophages.

To understand the potential functional implications of these spatial patterns, we examined the defining gene sets for each subcluster (SuppFig. S14). Interstitial macrophages (subcluster 0) expressed genes associated with extracellular matrix production and fibrosis, including Col1a1, Col1a2, Bgn, Nfkb1/2, and Rsad2. More interestingly, surface-adhered macrophages (subcluster 2) expressed more mechanosensory genes like Piezo1, Itgav and Itga5, and immunomodulatory genes such as Cd36, Tgfb1, and Ccl2, indicative of a mechanosensitive M2-like phenotype. These cells were largely limited to the surface of control scaffolds, often forming dense clusters and RN treatment was found to markedly disrupt this clustering and facilitate their dispersion and deeper infiltration into the scaffold. Collectively, these results revealed the dynamic remodelling of macrophage populations at the scaffold by RN treatment (Fig. 7J).

Discussion

Implanted biomaterials create a mechanical mismatch with surrounding tissue, generating cues that drive immune activation and fibrosis. These mechanosensitive responses highlight the potential to therapeutically target mechanosensation to modulate the foreign body response and limit fibrotic outcomes. This study was designed as a proof-of-concept to determine whether pharmacological modulation of mechanosensitive ion channels could achieve similar outcomes and complement these genetic studies to lay the groundwork for translationally feasible therapeutic strategies. Prior work with TRPV4 knockout mouse models have established TRPV4 as a critical mediator of mechanosensation in biomaterial implant fibrosis. RN-1734 (RN) was selected as a well-characterized TRPV4 inhibitor with validated mechanisms for disrupting calcium-dependent mechanosensation, enabling the testing of whether suppression of mechanosensory signaling can reduce immune-driven fibrosis.

Across a range of doses up to 80 μM, RN was non-cytotoxic and primarily reduced macrophage attachment. While these RN concentrations are higher than typically reported, the aim was not to achieve exclusive channel inhibition, but rather to assess whether pharmacological desensitization of mechanosensitive ion channels could therapeutically modulate FBR-associated immune responses. The use of high doses reflects an intentional strategy to robustly suppress mechanosensory signaling rather than dissect channel-level specificity. However, while reduced selectivity to TRPV4 at higher concentrations cannot be fully excluded, prior work by Richter et al. demonstrated that at even higher concentrations (500 μM), RN-1734 produces functional effects consistent with TRPV4-mediated mechanosensation without evidence of broad ion channel suppression [37]. Initial selectivity of RN-1734 for TRPV4 was established through comparative activity profiling across TRP channels in overexpression systems [38]. Future studies applying similar comparative profiling at higher doses, alongside genetic or orthogonal inhibition strategies, will be important to more precisely define channel-specific contributions under the conditions used in this study. Here, RN was demonstrated to prevent rapid, extracellular calcium influx in J774a.2 macrophages consistent with previous studies using other TRPV4 inhibitors, such as HC-067047, which have shown similar calcium-blocking effects [39,40]. While RN specifically has been studied in microglia with comparable results [23], this is the first demonstration that RN effectively inhibits TRPV4-mediated calcium influx in J774a.2 macrophages under inflammatory stimulation. Additionally, it was observed that RN could serve as a direct regulator of macrophage activation. RN not only limited pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion but also promoted M2-like polarization, shifting macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory state. This demonstrated dual functionality of dampening LPS-induced activation while simultaneously modulating polarization phenotype. RN treatment appeared to nearly abolish iNOS expression, demonstrating that mechanosensitive pathways are critical and non-redundant to classical macrophage activation. Further experiments using TRPV4 agonists showed calcium influx comparable to LPS stimulation, supporting the central role of TRPV4-mediated mechanosignalling in these responses and reinforcing the functional relevance of the observed RN effects. These findings have important implications for RN in targeting foreign body responses in vivo by potentially suppressing implant-induced immune activation, reducing the magnitude of inflammation triggered when immune cells encounter an implant, and limiting fibrotic encapsulation by promoting a macrophage phenotype conducive to tissue integration.

In the absence of pathogen-derived activation, RN was observed to exert its effects in a manner dependent on substrate stiffness. Macrophages seeded on stiff hydrogels showed decreased viability, consistent with studies reporting that excessive mechanical stress transmitted through TRPV mechanosensors on rigid matrices triggers sustained calcium influx, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to caspase-dependent apoptosis or necroptosis [41,42]. This stiffness-induced cell death can be further amplified in pro-inflammatory M1 environments, whereas softer matrices promote survival and an anti-inflammatory phenotype. While RN did not affect baseline viability, it selectively attenuated calcium influx only on stiff hydrogels but not on soft hydrogels. This stiffness-dependent selectivity reflects the well-established role of mechanosensitive ion channels in mediating immune signaling primarily under elevated mechanical stress. On soft substrates, basal membrane tension and cytoskeletal forces are lower, and TRPV4 activation by substrate stiffness is minimal, explaining the lack of measurable effect. Previous studies have implicated TRPV4 in mechanotransduction across immune and stromal cells [20,23,24,39], but our results extend this work by showing that its contribution becomes most pronounced under conditions of elevated substrate stiffness. Mechanosensitive ion channels are capable of directly sensing mechanical cues of substrate stiffness through force-induced conformational changes or membrane tension. Direct mechanosensitivity of TRPV4 to matrix stiffness is well-documented in multiple cell types, including macrophages and fibroblasts, with stiffness-dependent Ca2+ influx abolished by TRPV4 inhibition or knockout on rigid substrates [43,44].

Furthermore, the observed cytokine profiles highlighted partially overlapping but distinct inflammatory programs in macrophages. LPS stimulation elicited a broad classical activation signature (TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1), with RN reducing TNF-α and IL-6 while sparing MCP-1. In contrast, substrate stiffness alone induced a more restricted response dominated by IL-6 and MCP-1 without significant TNF-α secretion, both of which were markedly suppressed by RN. This divergence indicated that mechanosensation engages partially distinct inflammatory pathways compared with canonical LPS/TLR4 signaling, potentially giving rise to a “mechano-induced” inflammatory module that operates alongside, rather than fully redundant with, traditional “pathogen-induced” activation. These findings suggest the existence of stiffness-selective signaling branches that could potentially be therapeutically targeted in sterile, biomaterial-driven fibrosis. The clustering of macrophages on stiff gels, and its disruption by RN, further highlighted the role of mechanosensitive ion channels in shaping cellular organization. Given that macrophage clustering has been linked to enhanced paracrine signalling and the formation of multinucleated giant cells during foreign body responses [20,45], this effect may represent an early anti-fibrotic mechanism. More broadly, these results highlighted that RN reduced stiffness-dependent calcium influx as a key regulator of macrophage responses to substrate stiffness, selectively dampening mechanosensory signals induced by higher mechanical input.

These stiffness-dependent effects were also observed in vivo. Due to the lack of robust fibrotic responses around GelMa hydrogels, deemed to be too soft in an in vivo context, electrospun scaffolds and bulk hydrogels were employed for comparative testing. RN reduced fibrotic capsule formation around scaffolds by not hydrogel implants. The scaffolds, with higher surface stiffness and greater topological complexity, likely provided stronger and more heterogeneous mechanosensory cues than the smoother, softer hydrogels. However, we acknowledge that these implant types differ not only in stiffness but also in material composition and architecture, and therefore cannot be considered purely stiffness-matched controls. However, this diversity is representative of clinically relevant biomaterial classes and importantly, our aim here was not to directly compare silk versus PCL but instead use these contrasting materials to provide a framework allowing us to probe how macrophages respond to low versus high mechanosensory input in vivo and subsequently test the efficacy of RN across the furthest ends of the broad spectrum of mechanical stimuli. This investigative approach also did introduce additional experimental complexity. Scaffolds were porous while the hydrogels were not, which would in theory increase capsule formation in hydrogels, though capsules were found to be similar between the two implants. Electrospun scaffolds were also noticeably thinner than hydrogels, so all analyses focused on the tissue–material interface, where immune and stromal cells directly sense and first respond to mechanical cues [46,47], minimizing the impact of overall implant thickness on observed responses. Furthermore, drug release modelling indicated that hydrogels could load and release more RN than scaffolds, which might have been expected to enhance drug effects in hydrogels. However, scaffolds still showed greater reduction in capsule formation.

Spatial transcriptomics was used to study these effects and identified macrophages and fibroblasts as the dominant cell types at the tissue–implant interface. Analysis of global UMAP cluster structure revealed no major treatment-dependent shifts in macrophage or fibroblast subpopulations, suggesting that RN did not induce distinct changes in population of either cell type. However, our temporal analysis of mechanosensory and pro-fibrotic gene expression highlighted a potential division of labour between macrophages and fibroblasts during the foreign body response. Macrophages appeared to sense and amplify early mechanical input, while fibroblasts progressively assumed responsibility for sustaining fibrosis as the capsules matured. This pattern aligns with prior studies showing that macrophages act as early mechanosensors and cytokine producers that activate fibroblasts [48,49], whereas fibroblasts later upregulate mechanotransduction pathways which facilitate their differentiation into contractile myofibroblasts [39,40]. Our findings extend these concepts by showing at the transcriptional level that stronger mechanical input is associated with higher levels of mechanosensory gene activity, whereas weaker input corresponds to reduced engagement of these pathways. These collective findings also support the notion that macrophages are the earliest mechanosensitive cells to encounter both mechanical cues and the locally released drug at the implant surface. Between the two implants, mechanosensory gene expression in macrophages was also more pronounced in scaffolds than in hydrogels, supporting the conclusion that scaffolds generated higher mechanosensory input. This aligns the observed effects of RN decreasing capsule thickness in vivo with the stiffness-specific anti-inflammatory effects of RN observed in vitro, reinforcing the interpretation that RN efficacy is dependent on mechanical context. Although RN did not substantially alter the curated mechanosensory or fibrotic gene signatures, this analysis was limited to predefined gene panels. This motivated subsequent unsupervised analyses to identify broader transcriptional programs influenced by RN.

Unbiased transcriptomic analyses using PCA strengthened the central premise that RN not only exerted its in vivo effects in a mechanically dependent but also a macrophage-centered manner. The absence of detectable RN-driven transcriptional separation PCA analysis of hydrogel samples reinforced the idea that RN activity required a sufficiently strong mechanosensory context. In contrast, the emergence of a distinct RN-dependent transcriptional axis exclusively in scaffolds, represented by clear separation in PCA analysis of scaffold samples, highlighted that RN efficacy was contingent on material-driven mechanical cues rather than generalized pharmacological activity. Importantly, the transcriptional programs underlying the separation were dominated by macrophage-associated, M2-like and reparative pathways in RN treated scaffolds, whereas untreated scaffolds were characterized by fibroblast-driven extracellular matrix and fibrotic signatures. This supported a model in which RN did not directly suppress fibrosis at the level of fibroblasts but instead reprogrammed early macrophage activation states away from pro-fibrotic signaling. In this framework, fibroblast-mediated capsule formation was potentially secondarily attenuated through altered immune–stromal crosstalk, positioning macrophages as the primary cellular targets through which RN modulated the foreign body response. Collectively, these findings support that while RN may interact with other mechanosensitive channels, its effects are largely macrophage-specific without inducing detectable broad off-target transcriptional changes in other cell types.

The temporal specificity of these effects further supports this interpretation. RN-associated transcriptional differences were most pronounced during the acute phase (day 3), suggesting that RN acted early to reshape macrophage responses at the tissue–implant interface, rather than inducing sustained or irreversible changes in fibrotic programs. The convergence of transcriptional states at later timepoints is consistent with transient RN dampening of early pro-fibrotic trajectories in macrophages rather than abolishing downstream remodelling processes entirely in fibroblasts. Furthermore, the spatial redistribution of reparative macrophage-associated gene signatures into the scaffold interior provided a potential mechanistic link between macrophage state and tissue integration. By promoting reparative phenotypes and potentially enabling deeper cellular infiltration, RN may have shifted the balance from surface-confined fibrotic encapsulation toward more permissive and favourable scaffold–tissue interactions. Collectively, these findings reinforce the hypothesis that RN functions as a context-dependent immunomodulator, acting primarily on macrophages in mechanically active environments to indirectly limit fibrotic encapsulation.

Further macrophage subclustering analyses refined the understanding of RN modulation by revealing that its primary effects were exerted not through changes in total macrophage abundance, but through selective reprogramming and redistribution of early macrophage subpopulations. The restriction of RN-responsive effects to day 3 macrophage subsets supported the notion that RN acted during a window of heightened macrophage plasticity, consistent with our earlier transcriptomic and PCA-based findings. RN showed reduced trends of interstitial macrophages (subcluster 0) transcriptionally identified by genes associated with extracellular matrix production and fibrosis, including Col1a1, Col1a2, Bgn, Nfkb1/2, and Rsad2, suggesting a matrix-producing phenotype [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. Their interstitial positioning also aligned with prior studies showing that macrophages in these regions coordinate fibrotic capsule formation [54,55]. The reduction of this population provided a clear mechanistic link to the observed decrease in fibrotic encapsulation.

More interestingly, RN did not simply enhance M2-like transcriptional programs within existing macrophage populations but also altered their spatial organization. Surface-adhered macrophages (subcluster 2) were transcriptionally identified as expressing mechanosensory genes like Piezo1, Itgav and Itga5, and immunomodulatory genes such as Cd36, Tgfb1, and Ccl2, indicative of a mechanosensitive M2-like phenotype involved in regulating the immune microenvironment rather than directly depositing matrix [15,[56], [57], [58], [59]]. These cells were largely limited to the surface of control scaffolds, often forming dense clusters, potentially consistent with foreign body giant cell formation (FBGC) [60,61] and the positive expression of mechanosensory components of surface-adhered macrophages [56,62,63]. Once adhered, these cells primarily coordinate recruitment and signalling rather than matrix deposition [60,61]. RN treatment disrupted this clustering, analogous to in vitro observations with macrophages on stiff 10% GelMA hydrogels, which likely facilitated their dispersion and deeper infiltration into the scaffold. The concurrent increase in M2-like transcriptional programs in this subcluster 2 further suggested a shift from a pre-FBGC phenotype toward a more immunomodulatory state, with its increased proliferation also supporting a more anti-inflammatory microenvironment at the scaffold. This shift may have thus indirectly reduced the interstitial, matrix-forming subcluster 0 population, together contributing to the overall reduction in capsule formation.

It should be noted however that the observed spatial redistribution of macrophages cannot be fully decoupled from the physical architecture of the implanted materials. The porous structure and interconnected spaces of the scaffolds likely facilitated macrophage infiltration and movement, potentially amplifying the apparent effects of RN treatment. In contrast, the non-porous hydrogels may have physically constrained macrophage motility, limiting both cellular redistribution and downstream transcriptional responses. This material-dependent physical context may therefore have influenced the observed outcomes in a manner that complicates direct comparisons between scaffolds and hydrogels. Future studies examining materials that decouple stiffness from architectural features would help to more clearly resolve the relative contributions of mechanics versus structure. Nevertheless, the scaffold's porosity was available in both control and treated groups, yet macrophages remained clustered at the surface in controls. RN treatment did not create porosity but rather ‘unlocked’ the macrophages from their mechanosensory arrest, facilitating deep infiltration of Subcluster 2 into the existing scaffold architecture. These findings reinforce that RN-driven modulation of macrophage mechanosensation, particularly under higher mechanosensory input from either surface stiffness, roughness, or architecture, remains a dominant factor shaping macrophage behaviour and ultimately reducing fibrotic encapsulation. However, while spatial transcriptomics enabled identification of RN-associated macrophage subclusters defined by coordinated multigene programs, conventional single-marker immunohistochemistry may not be sufficient to fully resolve and validate these transcriptionally defined states. In this study, macrophage and fibroblast phenotypes were defined by unsupervised multi-gene signatures, including the RN-responsive macrophage subcluster 2 characterized by the PC2 negative loadings comprising a 30-gene program. Although immunohistochemistry can localize selected proteins, it cannot capture the breadth of macrophage heterogeneity or overlapping activation states revealed by these transcriptional programs. This highlights the need for higher-dimensional validation approaches such as multiplex imaging or spatial proteomics to better map RN-induced changes in mechanosensation-linked cell states within the foreign body response.

Traditionally in the bioactive materials field, where efforts have largely focused on modifying the material itself to enhance bioactivity, our study demonstrates a complementary strategy that instead enhances material bioactivity via therapeutic modulation of the surrounding cell/tissue mechanosensation. This approach tunes the immune response to mechanical mismatch and reduces fibrosis, and is relevant to implants where significant biomechanical mismatches occur and drive foreign body responses. Emerging bioinspired biomaterials increasingly leverage immune–material crosstalk, using mechanical, biochemical, and structural cues to actively guide host responses rather than passively mimicking tissue [64]. In this context, complementary approaches that directly modulate immune mechanosensation could form part of a broader mechano-immunotherapy strategy that works alongside material design to improve integration and long-term function.

Conclusion

Using RN-1734 as a model drug, we provide proof-of-concept that modulation of macrophage mechanosensation can fundamentally rewire the early immune response to implanted biomaterials. RN promoted the dispersion of surface-adhered, mechanosensitive macrophages and facilitated their infiltration into porous scaffolds, thereby limiting the recruitment of interstitial, matrix-producing macrophages and reducing fibrotic capsule formation. This uncovers a previously unappreciated mechanism in which the spatial organisation and transcriptional state of macrophages are coupled, and manipulation of one population indirectly reshapes the behaviour of others. Importantly, the magnitude of these effects appears to be associated with the degree of mechanosensory input detected by early-stage macrophages. By linking stiffness-dependent macrophage signalling to tissue-level remodelling, we hope to establish a foundation for future, more mature ‘mechano-immunotherapy’ approaches that directly tune immune mechanosensation to guide anti-fibrotic outcomes. These findings suggest that targeting mechanosensitive pathways in immune cells may represent a potentially effective strategy to control fibrosis and enhance long-term integration of implantable biomaterials. Additionally, while our analyses identified macrophages as the dominant mechanosensitive population at the implant interface, TRPV4 is also known to regulate endothelial function and vascular remodelling. Future studies examining how pharmacological modulation of mechanosensitive pathways influences vascularization and long-term tissue integration across different biomaterial contexts will be important for translational development. Broader validation across diverse material classes and clinically relevant implantation contexts (e.g., orthopaedic or cardiovascular environments) will also be necessary to determine whether this represents a generally applicable approach.
