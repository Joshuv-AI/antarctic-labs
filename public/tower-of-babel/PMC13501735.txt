ABSTRACT

Background

Basic Life Support (BLS) is a critical, life-saving intervention that plays a key role in improving survival rates following cardiac arrest. Early recognition, prompt initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and effective chest compressions are essential components of the chain of survival and have been shown to significantly increase survival outcomes worldwide [1–5]. Nurses are among the healthcare professionals most likely to encounter patients requiring immediate resuscitative care; therefore, acquiring and maintaining BLS knowledge and psychomotor skills during undergraduate education is of paramount importance [6, 7].

Simulation-based education has become one of the most widely used approaches for teaching CPR and BLS skills in nursing programs. Training with manikins allows students to practice technical skills in a safe and controlled environment, improve self-confidence, and enhance skill retention [8, 9]. Simulation modalities vary in their ability to replicate real clinical conditions. High-fidelity manikins offer realistic anatomical features, physiological responses, and objective performance feedback that facilitate psychomotor skill acquisition and clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, their high cost, maintenance requirements, and limited availability may reduce accessibility, particularly in institutions with constrained educational resources, highlighting the need for low-cost and accessible alternatives that support repeated skills practice [10, 11].

Extraordinary circumstances such as pandemics, natural disasters, or other events that disrupt face-to-face education may substantially limit students’ access to institutional skills laboratories and clinical training environments, creating significant challenges for the delivery of psychomotor skills education. The COVID-19 pandemic represented a prominent global example of such disruption, forcing nursing schools to rapidly transition to distance learning and substantially reducing opportunities for laboratory-based practice. Although e-learning platforms proved effective for delivering theoretical content, maintaining psychomotor skills training remained a major educational challenge [11]. These circumstances highlighted the need for flexible, accessible, and sustainable educational strategies capable of supporting skill development outside conventional laboratory settings. Consequently, interest in low-cost home-based educational approaches increased as a means of ensuring continuity of nursing skills education and providing opportunities for repeated independent practice under challenging conditions.

One such approach is the use of homemade manikins constructed from easily available and recyclable materials. These low-cost models provide students with opportunities to practice BLS skills independently and repeatedly at home, supporting experiential learning and continuity of education. Previous studies have reported that homemade CPR training tools can improve knowledge, skill performance, and learner engagement while reducing dependence on expensive simulation equipment [12–15]. These approaches may help minimize inequalities in access to simulation-based training and support large-scale skill acquisition.

Although previous studies have demonstrated that low-cost or homemade manikins can effectively improve cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training outcomes, the existing evidence has several important methodological limitations. Most studies have evaluated homemade manikins as stand-alone educational tools or within face-to-face training environments, with limited investigation of their integration into structured e-learning or blended learning models [13, 14, 16–18]. Furthermore, the majority have focused primarily on immediate post-training performance, providing limited evidence regarding the retention of knowledge and psychomotor skills over time [15, 18]. Although laboratory-based education using commercial manikins remains the predominant approach in undergraduate nursing education, considerable heterogeneity exists regarding educational design, learning environments, and simulation modalities, limiting the generalizability of current findings [19]. Likewise, systematic reviews have shown that blended learning can effectively enhance clinical skill acquisition; however, evidence supporting repeated home-based practice using low-cost homemade manikins remains scarce [20]. Recent reviews have further highlighted the need for more rigorous studies evaluating standardized low-cost educational interventions and their effects on long-term learning outcomes in nursing education [16, 21]. Therefore, the effectiveness of a structured educational approach that combines e-learning with repeated home-based practice using a low-cost homemade manikin has not yet been adequately established, particularly with respect to knowledge acquisition, observable procedural performance, and short-term retention. Evaluating such accessible and cost-effective educational strategies is essential for promoting sustainable and equitable psychomotor skills education, especially in settings with limited access to laboratory resources or large student cohorts.

Theoretical framework

This study can be conceptually grounded in Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, which emphasizes learning as a cyclical process involving concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation [22]. Within this framework, the use of a homemade manikin provided students with a concrete, hands-on experience, allowing them to actively engage in the psychomotor components of Basic Life Support (BLS). The integration of e-learning facilitated abstract conceptualization by delivering theoretical knowledge and structured guidance. Through repeated practice and self-recorded performance, students engaged in reflective observation, enabling them to evaluate and refine their skills. Finally, the opportunity for continuous practice at home supported active experimentation, reinforcing learning and promoting skill retention. This blended approach aligns with experiential learning principles and may explain the observed improvements in both knowledge and practical performance. Furthermore, the accessibility of low-cost simulation tools enhances opportunities for repeated experiential cycles, which are essential for mastery of complex clinical skills [22]. Accordingly, the present intervention was intentionally designed to align with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle by integrating theoretical instruction, repeated home-based procedural practice, self-observation through performance recording, and reflective learning, thereby providing a conceptual framework for understanding the educational outcomes of the study.

From a global health perspective, this approach can contribute to reducing inequalities in access to simulation-based education. While high-fidelity simulators are effective, they are often expensive and not readily available. In this context, low-cost training models offer a practical and scalable alternative to support equitable skills development, especially in resource-constrained settings. Such approaches can be particularly valuable in low- and middle-income countries and during periods like the COVID-19 pandemic when access to traditional simulation laboratories is limited [23–27].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin, supported by structured e-learning, among undergraduate nursing students. Specifically, the study examined changes in theoretical knowledge and observable procedural performance immediately after the intervention and evaluated the retention of these learning outcomes one month later. In addition, students’ self-reported willingness to perform CPR, confidence in their skills, and perceptions of the educational intervention were explored as secondary educational outcomes.

Beyond evaluating educational effectiveness, this study is expected to contribute to the evidence base regarding sustainable, accessible, and resource-sensitive approaches to psychomotor skills education by examining the combined use of structured e-learning and repeated home-based practice using a homemade manikin. The findings may inform curriculum development and support more equitable access to Basic Life Support training in undergraduate nursing education.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a quasi-experimental single-group pretest–posttest design with a one-month follow-up to evaluate the effectiveness of low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin among undergraduate nursing students. The study was conducted between October and December 2021 at the Faculty of Health Sciences during the COVID-19 pandemic.

A single-group pretest–posttest design was selected for practical, ethical, and educational reasons. First, Basic Life Support training constituted a mandatory component of the undergraduate nursing curriculum; therefore, withholding training from a control group was considered ethically inappropriate. Second, the primary aim of the study was to evaluate the feasibility and educational effectiveness of a low-cost home-based BLS training approach using a homemade manikin rather than to compare it directly with conventional laboratory-based or commercial simulation methods. Third, pandemic-related restrictions and logistical constraints limited the feasibility of implementing a randomized controlled design.

Although the faculty possessed a clinical skills laboratory equipped with commercial CPR manikins, students’ access to these facilities was substantially restricted because of infection control measures implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic and the large number of enrolled students. Consequently, opportunities for repeated laboratory-based psychomotor practice were limited. To ensure continuity of psychomotor skills education and provide students with equitable opportunities for repetitive practice outside the university setting, a low-cost home-based BLS training approach using a homemade manikin was implemented.

This educational strategy was specifically designed to facilitate independent practice in a home environment while minimizing dependence on institutional laboratory resources. Accordingly, the intervention emphasized repeated procedural skill acquisition through structured online instruction and self-directed home practice rather than a comprehensive simulation experience incorporating standardized clinical scenarios, assigned learner roles, or high-fidelity simulation technology.

The study population consisted of undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the Basic Life Support course during the 2021–2022 academic year. A total of 148 students who voluntarily agreed to participate and completed all stages of the educational intervention and outcome assessments were included in the study.

Educational intervention: Low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin

The educational intervention consisted of low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin, supported by synchronous online instruction and standardized educational materials. The theoretical component comprised a 40-minute live online session delivered via Zoom, during which participants received instruction based on the current American Heart Association Basic Life Support recommendations [3]. The session included an overview of the BLS algorithm, demonstration of the procedural steps, and guidance on the preparation and appropriate use of the homemade manikin.

To reinforce learning, participants were provided with two standardized instructional videos of approximately four minutes each, demonstrating the complete BLS sequence, correct chest compression technique, airway positioning, ventilation procedure, and the construction of the homemade manikin. During the online session, students also received visual demonstrations and written instructions regarding manikin preparation, and the videos remained accessible throughout the intervention period to facilitate repeated review and self-directed learning.

To support psychomotor skill practice outside the laboratory environment, students were instructed to construct a homemade manikin using simple and readily available household materials. The manikin consisted of a pillow simulating the thoracic structure and a 1.5-L polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottle placed internally to provide resistance during chest compression practice. The construction process and the final appearance of the homemade manikin are presented in Fig. 1. This low-cost model enabled students to engage in repeated home-based practice despite limited access to institutional skills laboratories during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Preparation of homemade manikin

Students were encouraged to practice the complete BLS procedure repeatedly at home until they felt confident in performing the entire algorithm before submitting their final performance recording. No minimum number of practice repetitions was mandated, thereby allowing self-paced learning while promoting repeated psychomotor rehearsal according to individual educational needs.

Data collection tools and procedure

Two researcher-developed instruments were used to evaluate the educational outcomes of the intervention: the Basic Life Support (BLS) Knowledge Assessment Form and the Basic Life Support Skills Assessment Form. Both instruments were developed in accordance with the current American Heart Association (AHA) Basic Life Support guidelines and are provided as Supplementary File 1 [28].

The Basic Life Support Knowledge Assessment Form consisted of ten multiple-choice questions assessing theoretical knowledge related to adult BLS. Each correct response was scored as 1 point, whereas incorrect responses received 0 points, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 10. To establish content validity, the instrument was reviewed by an expert panel of ten nursing specialists, resulting in a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 1.00. Agreement among experts was confirmed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W = 0.500, df = 9, p < 0.001). Prior to the main study, the instrument was pilot-tested with 25 undergraduate nursing students from the same academic year who were not included in the final study sample, and internal consistency was evaluated using the Kuder–Richardson 20 (KR-20) coefficient, yielding a reliability coefficient of 0.82.

The Basic Life Support Skills Assessment Form was developed using the same AHA recommendations and consisted of 20 observable procedural items representing the essential steps of the BLS algorithm. Each correctly performed step was scored as 1 point, whereas omitted or incorrectly performed steps received 0 points, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating better observable procedural performance. The checklist was evaluated by the same panel of ten nursing experts, demonstrating excellent content validity (CVI = 1.00). Reliability testing was performed in the same independent pilot group of 25 students not included in the main study, resulting in a KR-20 coefficient of 0.91, indicating high internal consistency.

Data were collected between October and December 2021. Baseline theoretical knowledge was assessed immediately before the educational intervention using the Basic Life Support Knowledge Assessment Form (pre-test). Following completion of the 40-minute synchronous online educational session and access to the standardized instructional videos, participants independently practiced the BLS procedure at home using the homemade manikin. Students were encouraged to review the educational materials and engage in repeated self-directed practice until they felt confident performing the complete BLS algorithm. The instructional videos and written guidance remained accessible throughout the intervention period to facilitate repeated psychomotor rehearsal.

Immediately after completion of the educational intervention, participants completed the post-test knowledge assessment and submitted a continuous, unedited video demonstrating the complete BLS procedure using the homemade manikin. Practical performance was evaluated using the Basic Life Support Skills Assessment Form based on these video recordings. To assess short-term retention, both the knowledge assessment and practical performance evaluation were repeated one month after the intervention. The one-month follow-up interval was selected because previous studies have demonstrated that BLS knowledge and psychomotor skills begin to decline within the first weeks after training if not reinforced, making this period appropriate for evaluating early retention of learning outcomes [18].

Observable procedural performance was evaluated based on video recordings submitted by the students using standardized assessment criteria. Students submitted one continuous, unedited video recording demonstrating the complete Basic Life Support algorithm using the homemade manikin in their home environment. Recordings were required to provide a stable full lateral view, allowing assessment of body position, hand placement, compression rhythm, airway positioning, and adherence to the procedural sequence.

Two experienced nursing academics who were blinded to participant identity and assessment time independently evaluated all recordings using the standardized skills checklist. Prior to assessment, both evaluators reviewed the scoring criteria to ensure consistency in interpretation. Inter-rater agreement was excellent (κ = 1.00, p < 0.001), and because of the perfect agreement, the final analyses were based on the first evaluator’s scores.

Given the characteristics of the homemade manikin and the absence of sensor-based feedback technology, parameters such as exact chest compression depth (5–6 cm), complete chest recoil, compression force, and anatomical resistance could not be objectively quantified. Instead, these items were evaluated visually according to observable adherence to the standardized checklist and correct procedural technique. Therefore, the practical assessment should be interpreted as reflecting observable BLS procedural performance rather than comprehensive CPR quality.

Secondary educational outcomes

In addition to knowledge acquisition and observable procedural performance, students’ self-reported responses to the educational intervention were evaluated as secondary educational outcomes. Immediately before and after completion of the educational intervention, participants were asked two researcher-developed single-item questions assessing their willingness to intervene in the event of a cardiac arrest (“Would you intervene in the event of a cardiac arrest?“) and their confidence in performing cardiopulmonary resuscitation correctly (“Do you feel confident performing CPR correctly?“). Response options for both questions were “Yes,” “No,” and “Undecided.”

Immediately after completing the home-based BLS training and performance assessment, participants were also asked to indicate the emotions they experienced during the educational process. They were allowed to select one or more predefined emotional responses, including excitement, fear, comfort, confusion, and amusement. These variables were collected descriptively to provide supplementary information regarding students’ perceptions of the educational intervention and were not intended to represent validated psychometric outcomes. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for all self-reported responses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with skewness and kurtosis values. Because the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric statistical methods were used for subsequent analyses. Changes in knowledge scores across the three time points (before training, immediately after training, and one month after training) were analyzed using the Friedman test. When the Friedman test indicated a statistically significant difference, pairwise comparisons between time points were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with an appropriate adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni correction). Effect sizes were calculated to assess the magnitude of differences. For Friedman test results, Kendall’s W was used as an effect size measure, and for Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, effect size (r) was calculated using the formula r = Z / √N. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.1), medium (0.3), and large (0.5) according to Cohen [29].

Observable procedural performance scores, which were measured at two time points (immediately after training and one month after training), were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 148 undergraduate nursing students participated in the study. The mean age of the participants in the study was 20.45 ± 1.29 years, and 75.7% were female. While 86.5% of the participants were general high school graduates, 31.1% had received first aid training before. While 45.3% of the participants witnessed the basic life support application before, 45.2% saw the application through television. Only 8% of the participants had previously participated in the practice, and 4% were involved in a hospital setting.

Knowledge scores differed significantly across the three measurement points (before training, immediately after training, and one month after training) (Friedman χ² = 128.194, p < 0.001, Kendall’s W = 0.43), indicating a moderate-to-large effect size. The mean knowledge score increased from 5.08 ± 1.96 before training to 7.99 ± 1.56 immediately after training. At one-month follow-up, the mean score slightly decreased to 7.40 ± 1.63 but remained significantly higher than the baseline level.

Pairwise comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed significant improvements between pre-training and immediately after training (Z = − 9.548, p < 0.001), and between pre-training and one month after training (Z = − 3.382, p = 0.001). For pairwise comparisons, Effect size calculations indicated large effects for the comparisons between pre-training and post-training (r = 0.78), and pre-training and one-month follow-up (r = 0.67), while a small-to-moderate effect was observed between post-training and follow-up (r = 0.28). Discussion

The present study demonstrated that low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin, supported by synchronous e-learning, significantly improved undergraduate nursing students’ theoretical knowledge and observable procedural performance, with these improvements largely maintained at one-month follow-up. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence indicating that accessible and cost-effective educational strategies can effectively supplement conventional laboratory-based BLS education and support psychomotor skill acquisition, particularly in settings with limited educational resources [16, 21].

The significant increase in knowledge scores immediately after the intervention and the maintenance of these gains at follow-up suggest that combining structured online instruction with repeated home-based practice promotes meaningful cognitive learning and short-term knowledge retention. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that blended learning approaches improve theoretical understanding and facilitate retention of clinical knowledge among undergraduate nursing students [11, 30, 31]. The continued availability of instructional materials throughout the intervention period may have further supported self-directed learning and repeated review, thereby contributing to sustained knowledge acquisition.

Although a statistically significant decline in observable procedural performance was identified one month after the intervention, overall performance remained satisfactory. This finding is consistent with previous evidence indicating that psychomotor skills begin to deteriorate shortly after training when opportunities for reinforcement are limited [18]. Consequently, providing learners with accessible opportunities for repeated independent practice appears essential for maintaining competence in life-saving procedures such as BLS.

Beyond the overall improvement in procedural performance, item-level analysis provided important insight into the educational value of the homemade manikin. Skills primarily related to chest compression mechanics, including appropriate hand placement, identification of the compression site, timely initiation of compressions, and maintenance of compression rhythm, demonstrated consistently high performance throughout the study period. These findings suggest that the homemade manikin provided sufficient structural support to facilitate repetitive practice of fundamental compression techniques, consistent with previous reports evaluating low-cost CPR training models [13, 14]. In contrast, lower performance was observed for procedural components requiring greater psychomotor coordination or clinical judgment, including airway management, ventilation technique, AED application, and uninterrupted continuation of CPR. Similar observations have been reported in recent reviews suggesting that although low-cost educational models effectively support acquisition of fundamental psychomotor skills, they cannot fully reproduce the anatomical realism and performance feedback provided by commercial or high-fidelity simulators [16]. Therefore, the educational strengths of homemade manikins appear to be concentrated in repetitive compression-based motor skills, whereas competencies requiring realistic anatomical landmarks, integrated airway management, and complex clinical decision-making may still benefit from laboratory-based training using commercial manikins.

In addition to improvements in knowledge and observable procedural performance, students reported greater willingness to intervene in a cardiac arrest situation and increased confidence in performing CPR following the educational intervention. Furthermore, positive emotional responses, particularly excitement and amusement, were commonly reported, suggesting that the intervention was well accepted and may have enhanced learner engagement. Together, these findings indicate that repeated home-based practice combined with structured online instruction may positively influence learners’ perceived readiness to respond to emergency situations. Nevertheless, because willingness, confidence, and emotional responses were assessed using researcher-developed descriptive questions rather than validated psychometric instruments, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory educational outcomes that complement the primary results rather than standardized measures of self-efficacy or emotional status.

From an educational perspective, the present intervention provided a practical solution for maintaining psychomotor skills education during the COVID-19 pandemic, when access to institutional skills laboratories was substantially restricted. The combination of standardized online instruction and repeated home-based practice enabled students to continue developing procedural competence despite limited opportunities for laboratory-based learning. Beyond the pandemic context, this educational model may also be valuable in nursing schools with limited infrastructure or large student cohorts where repeated access to commercial simulation resources is constrained.

Overall, the findings support the integration of low-cost home-based BLS skills training using a homemade manikin as a supplementary educational strategy within undergraduate nursing curricula. Rather than replacing conventional laboratory-based simulation, this approach may expand opportunities for repetitive independent practice, improve equitable access to psychomotor skills education, and reinforce procedural competence through self-directed learning.

However, there are several limitations to consider when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, the study employed a quasi-experimental single-group pretest–posttest design without a control or comparison group. Consequently, improvements in knowledge and observable procedural performance can not be attributed exclusively to the educational intervention. Repeated exposure to the assessment instruments, independent study using external educational resources, additional self-directed practice during preparation for the performance recordings, and the natural progression of learning over time may also have contributed to the observed outcomes. Therefore, causal interpretations regarding the effectiveness of the intervention should be made with caution.

Second, although the homemade manikin provided an accessible and practical platform for repeated home-based practice, it did not incorporate sensor-based feedback technology capable of objectively measuring critical CPR quality indicators such as chest compression depth, complete chest recoil, compression force, or ventilation quality. Accordingly, practical performance was evaluated using a standardized observational checklist based on video recordings and should be interpreted as observable BLS procedural performance rather than comprehensive CPR quality or overall clinical competence. Future studies incorporating objective feedback devices or validated simulation technologies would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of psychomotor performance.

Third, the intervention was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, when access to institutional skills laboratories was substantially restricted. Although this educational context justified the implementation of a low-cost home-based training model and reflects a realistic educational need during the pandemic, the findings may not be fully generalizable to institutions with unrestricted access to simulation laboratories or commercial CPR manikins.

Fourth, students’ willingness to intervene in a cardiac arrest situation, confidence in performing CPR, and emotional responses to the educational intervention were assessed using researcher-developed descriptive questions rather than validated psychometric instruments. Therefore, these variables should be regarded as exploratory secondary educational outcomes that provide supplementary information regarding learners’ perceptions of the intervention and should not be interpreted as standardized measures of self-efficacy, confidence, or emotional status.

Finally, this study was conducted at a single institution with undergraduate nursing students and included a follow-up period of only one month. Although the findings support the educational value of low-cost home-based BLS skills training using a homemade manikin, further multicenter randomized controlled studies with longer follow-up periods and objective performance measurement systems are needed to evaluate long-term knowledge retention, psychomotor skill maintenance, and transfer of learning to real clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that low-cost home-based Basic Life Support (BLS) skills training using a homemade manikin, supported by synchronous e-learning, was associated with significant improvements in undergraduate nursing students’ theoretical knowledge and observable procedural performance. The improvements observed immediately after the intervention and their maintenance at one-month follow-up suggest that combining structured online instruction with repeated home-based practice may represent an effective supplementary educational strategy for supporting BLS learning.

Although a modest decline in procedural performance was observed over time, overall performance levels remained satisfactory, emphasizing the importance of repeated practice for maintaining psychomotor skills. The findings further suggest that low-cost homemade manikins can provide learners with accessible opportunities for independent practice outside institutional skills laboratories, thereby reinforcing procedural learning and supporting self-directed education.

However, because the homemade manikin did not provide objective feedback regarding CPR quality indicators and the study employed a single-group design without a control group, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Rather than replacing laboratory-based education or commercial simulation technologies, low-cost home-based BLS skills training using a homemade manikin should be considered a complementary educational approach that expands opportunities for repetitive practice and promotes more equitable access to psychomotor skills training, particularly in resource-constrained educational settings.

Relevance for clinical practice

Basic Life Support (BLS) is a fundamental life-saving intervention, and the ability of future nurses to perform timely and effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is essential for improving patient outcomes. The findings of this study suggest that low-cost home-based BLS skills training using a homemade manikin may enhance nursing students’ opportunities for repeated independent practice and contribute to the development and maintenance of observable procedural competence.

Although this educational approach cannot replace laboratory-based training supported by commercial manikins or objective feedback technologies, it may serve as a practical supplementary strategy for reinforcing psychomotor learning, particularly when access to institutional training facilities is limited. Such an approach may be especially valuable in nursing schools with large student cohorts, limited educational infrastructure, or emergency situations such as pandemics that restrict face-to-face education.

By increasing opportunities for repetitive practice and supporting self-directed learning, low-cost home-based BLS training may contribute to greater learner preparedness for clinical practice while promoting more equitable access to skills education across diverse educational settings. Future studies incorporating objective performance measures and randomized controlled designs are warranted to further establish the role of this educational model in nursing education.
