ScoringBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Tabular Foundation Models with Proper Scoring Rules
Jonas Landsgesell, Pascal Knoll

Abstract
Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN ( 11 ) and TabICL ( 21 ) already produce full predictive distributions, yet prevailing regression benchmarks evaluate them almost exclusively via point-estimate metrics (RMSE, R 2 R^{2} ). These aggregate measures often obscure model performance in the tails of the distribution—a critical deficit for high-stakes decision-making in domains like finance and clinical research, where asymmetric risk profiles are the norm. We introduce ScoringBench , an open benchmark that computes a comprehensive suite of proper scoring rules—CRPS, CRLS, Interval Score, Energy Score, weighted CRPS, and Brier Score—alongside standard point metrics, providing a richer picture of probabilistic forecast quality. We evaluate realTabPFNv2.5 fine-tuned with different scoring-rule objectives and TabICL relative to untuned realTabPFNv2.5 across a suite of regression benchmarks. Our results confirm that model rankings depend on the chosen scoring rule and that no single pretraining objective is universally optimal ( 19 ; 23 ) . This demonstrates that for applications sensitive to extreme events, the choice of evaluation metric is as much a domain-specific requirement as the data itself. ScoringBench is available at https://github.com/jonaslandsgesell/ScoringBench . A live preview of the current leaderboard is available at https://scoringbench.bolt.host/ . The leaderboard is maintained via git pull requests to ensure transparency, traceability, agility and reproducibility.

Related Work

Tabular Deep Learning.

Prior-Data Fitted Networks (TabPFN)
(
10
;
11
)
have shifted tabular learning from the dominant paradigm of gradient-boosted decision trees toward transformer-based in-context learners.
Rather than updating model weights at training time, TabPFN meta-learns a prior over datasets and performs inference in a single forward pass, naturally producing a full predictive distribution over an adaptive output grid.
TabICLv2
(
21
)
extends this idea by estimating 999 quantile levels, yielding rich distributional outputs without explicit weight updates at test time.
Both architectures are therefore inherently
probabilistic
: they predict probability mass functions (or dense quantile functions) rather than single point estimates.
Despite this capability, prevailing evaluations of these models still rely almost exclusively on point-estimate metrics such as RMSE and
R^{2}
, effectively discarding the distributional information these architectures are designed to provide. ScoringBench is designed to close this evaluation gap.

Probabilistic Forecasting.

The theoretical foundations for evaluating probabilistic predictions were laid by
7
, who characterized the space of strictly proper scoring rules—scoring rules
S(F,y)
for which the expected score is uniquely minimized when the forecast
F
equals the true data-generating distribution.

6
further formalized the goal of probabilistic forecasting as maximizing
sharpness
subject to
calibration
, providing the conceptual basis for the diagnostics reported in ScoringBench.
Importantly, while all strictly proper scoring rules share the same population-level minimizer, they induce different inductive biases during finite-sample training
(
23
)
.

19
demonstrated empirically that model rankings change substantially depending on which proper scoring rule is used (the Spearman correlation between Brier-score and log-score rankings in their study is only
0.15
), and
1
showed that a broad class of proper scoring rules—including the logarithmic, CRPS, quadratic, and energy scores—penalize under- and overestimation asymmetrically.
The scoring rules computed in ScoringBench span a wide range of sensitivities: the CRPS
(
7
)
weights all quantile levels equally; the energy score
(
7
)
generalizes CRPS via the parameter
\beta\in(0,2)
, interpolating between median- and mean-optimal behavior
(
15
)
; the interval score
(
7
)
targets specific central prediction intervals; and the weighted CRPS
(
8
)
allows practitioners to emphasize particular regions of the predictive distribution.
Together, these rules enable a multifaceted assessment of forecast quality that no single metric can provide.

Two prominent tabular benchmarks, TabArena

and TALENT
(

R^{2}

These metrics reward accurate conditional-mean predictions but are blind to the quality of the full predictive distribution—its calibration, sharpness, and tail behavior.

Benchmarking with Proper Scoring Rules

We refer the reader to
7
;
8
for a comprehensive introduction to proper scoring rules and
15
for a discussion in the tabular distributional regression setting. We summarize the definitions of the scoring rules implemented in ScoringBench.
ScoringBench computes the following proper scoring rules alongside standard point metrics.
All definitions follow
7
;
8
unless otherwise noted.

CRPS.

The Continuous Ranked Probability Score weights all quantile levels equally:

\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\left(F(x)-\mathbf{1}_{x\geq y}\right)^{2}dx.

For a discretized output, CRPS is equivalent to the
\beta{=}1
energy score.

CRLS.

The Continuous Ranked Logarithmic Score (Exceedance Probability Score)
(
14
)
replaces the squared difference with a logarithm:

\mathrm{CRLS}(F,y)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log\left|F(x)+\mathbf{1}_{y\leq x}-1\right|dx.

CRLS gives more weight than CRPS to events predicted to be very unlikely and penalizes crude errors in the predicted mean more severely than either CRPS or the log score
(
14
)
.

Interval Score.

Targeting the
(1-\alpha)
central prediction interval with bounds
[l,u]

(
7
)
:

\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha}(F,y)=(u-l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(l-y)\,\mathbf{1}_{y<l}+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y-u)\,\mathbf{1}_{y>u}.

\beta
-Energy Score.

For
\beta\in(0,2)
,
\mathbf{S}_{\beta}(F,y)=\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-y\|^{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{\beta}
.
For point-mass forecasts, the optimal estimate minimizing expected
\beta
-energy score interpolates between the median (
\beta{=}1
, MAE) and the mean (
\beta{=}2
, MSE), leading to different inductive biases for different
\beta

(
15
)
.

Weighted CRPS (wCRPS).

L_{\mathrm{wCRPS}}=\int w(x)\,(F(x)-\mathbf{1}_{x\geq y})^{2}\,dx
with weight function
w
emphasizing left tail, right tail, or center
(
8
)
.

Log Score.

The (negative) log-score is the mean negative log-density of the predictive distribution evaluated at the observed target
(
good1952rational
)
:

\mathrm{LogS}(F,y)=-\log f(y),

where
f=dF/dx
is the predictive density. For a histogram forecast with bin probability
p_{k}
and bin width
w_{k}
, the density at
y
is
p_{k^{*}}/w_{k^{*}}
with
k^{*}
the bin containing
y
.

Brier Score.

Applied bin-by-bin, the Brier score measures mean squared error between the predicted probability mass function and the one-hot target bin indicator:

\mathrm{Brier}(F,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(p_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{k=k^{*}}\right)^{2},

where
k^{*}
is the bin containing
y
. Lower is better.

Sharpness.

Sharpness measures the concentration of the predictive distribution.

22
define it as the average predicted standard deviation:

\mathrm{Sharp}(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(F_{n})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}[X^{2}]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}[X]\bigr)^{2}}.

Sharpness alone is not a proper scoring rule; it should be considered jointly with calibration. A well-calibrated model with low sharpness (small predicted standard deviations) provides more actionable uncertainty estimates than one that is calibrated but diffuse.

Dispersion.

Dispersion measures heterogeneity in the model’s predicted uncertainties across the test set.

22
define it as the standard deviation of the per-sample predicted standard deviations:

\mathrm{Disp}(F)=\sqrt{\mathrm{var}_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(F_{n})}\right)}.

Dispersion is not a proper scoring rule; it answers the question “how much does the model’s confidence vary across samples?” A low-dispersion model assigns similar uncertainty estimates to all instances, while a high-dispersion model distinguishes some instances as more predictable than others. Dispersion complements sharpness: two models with the same average sharpness may differ substantially in dispersion.

Higher dispersion is favorable only when true underlying errors are heteroscedastic (i.e., when prediction difficulty genuinely varies across samples). If errors are homoscedastic, a model with high dispersion wastes capacity on spurious confidence variation.
We report dispersion alongside sharpness and other metrics for completeness, but treat it as a secondary diagnostic tool (not a primary optimization target) that is only meaningful for datasets with heteroscedastic errors.

Coverage.

For a nominal
(1-\alpha)
prediction interval
[l,u]
extracted from the predictive CDF, empirical coverage is the fraction of test points falling inside the interval:

\mathrm{Cov}_{1-\alpha}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{l_{i}\leq y_{i}\leq u_{i}}.

We report coverage at the 90% level (
\mathrm{Cov}_{90}
, target 0.90) and the 95% level (
\mathrm{Cov}_{95}
, target 0.95). A well-calibrated model achieves coverage close to the nominal level; the interval score (IS) jointly penalizes over-wide intervals and coverage shortfalls.

How to rank Multiple Models on Multiple Datasets

When evaluating the performance of multiple distributional regression models—such as TabPFN, its fine-tuned variants and TabICL—across a diverse benchmark of 66 datasets, relying on simple aggregated averages may be insufficient and prone to drawing spurious conclusions (for example due to outliers in performance on some datasets).
Rigorous statistical tests are necessary to ensure that the observed rankings represent true performance differences rather than random noise
2
.
However, the methodology used to conduct these tests must carefully manage the hierarchical nature of cross-validated performance data.

One statistical pitfall in such evaluations is pseudoreplication, which occurs when non-independent observations are inappropriately treated as independent. Consider a standard biological analogy
17
: if a researcher tests a treatment on a specific mouse and takes five consecutive measurements of that same mouse, analyzing those five measurements as if they came from five different subjects artificially inflates the degrees of freedom. This fundamentally violates the assumption of independence, leading to highly overconfident p-values and a heightened risk of Type I errors. In our experimental setup, a distinct dataset acts as the ”mouse,” and the 5-fold cross-validation (CV) runs represent the repeated measurements. To ensure true statistical independence and avoid pseudoreplication, our evaluation framework explicitly averages the 5 CV folds first, yielding a single independent performance observation per dataset (blocking performance metrics per dataset
17
).

To draw reliable conclusions, this variance must be accounted for without leaning on unverified statistical assumptions.
Traditional parametric tests, such as the paired t-test or ANOVA, assume that the performance differences are normally distributed and strictly commensurate across datasets—assumptions that are routinely violated in multi-dataset machine learning evaluations
2
.
Consequently, non-parametric sampling-based methods, specifically permutation tests, are strongly preferred; they provide high statistical power without making unjustified assumptions about the underlying distribution of the test statistics
4
.
To operationalize this, we apply an empirical permutation test to rank the models.
Since different datasets have different difficulty due to different signal-to-noise ratios, dealing with unnormalized performance scores across datasets can be misleading.
One way to normalize is to look at ranking of model performances within each dataset, which is a common approach in the machine learning community
2
.
The procedure constructs a null distribution and calculates significance through the following steps:

1.

Data Aggregation: The per-fold metrics are averaged to yield a single performance score per model, per dataset, explicitly preventing pseudoreplication.

2.

Transformation and Normalization: The raw performance scores are transformed into ranks to resolve the incommensurability of values across disparate datasets
2
.
Datasets where all models achieve identical scores (zero variance) are dropped.

3.

Observed Test Statistic: The observed average rank is calculated for each model across all datasets.

4.

Null Distribution Construction: A null distribution is constructed by running 20,000 simulations. In each simulation, the normalized scores are randomly shuffled within each dataset row independently. The mean of these permuted scores is recorded for each model, building a comprehensive distribution of outcomes under the null hypothesis (which assumes no systematic difference between the models).

5.

Empirical P-Value Calculation: For each model, the empirical p-value is estimated by counting the number of simulated means that are equal to or more extreme than the actually observed mean. A pseudo-count is added to avoid zero p-values, resulting in the formula
p=(counts+1)/(nsim+1)
.

6.

Final Ranking: The models are ranked primarily by their empirical p-value in ascending order. Any ties in significance are broken using the original observed raw performance means.

We acknowledge that using ranks as a basis for the above permutation test has its own limitations, for example it discards information about the magnitude of performance differences and can be sensitive to ties. We plan to explore alternative normalization approaches in future work (e.g. min-max scaling of performances on datasets or z-scoring of performances on datasets).

While the importance of aligning evaluation metrics with application-specific utility is a well-established principle among leading statisticians—most notably popularized by
7
;
8
;
9
and colleagues — its practical implementation remains frequently overlooked in modern data science workflows. In general, we observe—as expected from
15
—that the ranking of models depends on the specific method used to penalize errors (i.e., the chosen proper scoring rule for probabilistic forecasts or the scoring function for point estimates).
The implication for a high risk use-case is that certain models may penalize the right errors while others are not. As Gneiting pointed out, the choice of an evaluation score depends on the type of error which is acceptable for an application.

In weather forecasting, for instance, events more extreme than usual may imply higher damage; consequently, a weighted CRPS is recommended
9
. Similar logic applies to financial risk management, where an underestimation of ”Value at Risk” (VaR) or ”Expected Shortfall” during market crashes is far more detrimental than a slight overestimation of volatility during stable periods. In structural health monitoring, an error that underestimates the stress on a bridge is catastrophic, whereas an overestimation merely leads to conservative maintenance schedules.

To this end, we conclude that typical datasets should be enhanced with the desired evaluation metric so that training can focus on the relevant aspects of modeling. Furthermore, we note that most publicly available datasets lack a description of a ”desired error structure.” This argument is especially obvious when considering Bregman divergences for estimating a conditional mean
15
: while all Bregman divergences allow to estimate a mean, they result in different residual structures. A typical business case—such as liquidity forecasting or energy grid balancing—will allow some errors while not allowing others. This logic applies not only to scoring functions but also to scoring rules and probabilistic forecasts
15
.

Conclusion

ScoringBench provides an open, extensible benchmark for evaluating tabular regression models under a rich suite of proper scoring rules. By moving beyond traditional point-estimate metrics, we provide a framework for aligning model behavior with real-world utility and risk tolerances. Evaluating realTabPFNv2.5 variants fine-tuned on different scoring objectives demonstrates that the choice of training objective shapes model behavior beyond what population-level propriety guarantees.

This is especially relevant for high-risk applications—such as liquidity forecasting in finance or dose-response modeling in pharma—where the penalty for underestimating a target may differ vastly from overestimating it. Relying solely on RMSE risks obscuring these meaningful differences in probabilistic forecast quality and may lead to the deployment of models that are poorly calibrated for usual or extreme scenarios alike. We encourage the tabular machine learning community to adopt distributional evaluation metrics that reflect the decision-theoretical foundations of their specific applications. ScoringBench is available at
https://github.com/jonaslandsgesell/ScoringBench
