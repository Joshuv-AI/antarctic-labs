Coherent-state ansatz for the Holstein polaron in one and two dimensions
Connor M. Walsh, Igor Boettcher, Frank Marsiglio

Abstract
The Holstein model often serves as an archetype for electron-phonon interactions and polaron formation in solids. However, precise descriptions of the Holstein polaron are difficult when the phonon frequency is small and the electron-phonon coupling is strong, due to the presence of many phonons in the ground state. We present a semi-analytical approximation that consists of a variational ansatz with clouds of phonons surrounding the electron in the form of coherent states. This becomes particularly simple and exact in the Lang–Firsov limit. We determine the domain of validity away from this limit, and further explore the improvement achieved with a removal of the requirement that the phonon clouds form coherent states. Both approximations work extremely well at strong coupling, and both work surprisingly well also at weak coupling. The coherent-state ansatz provides a simple and intuitive picture of the polaron ground-state wavefunction, and in addition predicts accurate values for the ground-state energy and effective mass.

In spite of the tremendous advances by Bonča et al.

In this work, we treat the Holstein polaron by using an approximation scheme that evolves from the Lang–Firsov limit
54
. The Lang–Firsov limit (a name that we use interchangeably with the strong-coupling limit) is a ground-state wavefunction that is a Bloch superposition of localized coherent states, which we describe in detail later. Applying the Hamiltonian to the Lang–Firsov limit generates coherent states of phonons that neighbour the electron, and so we are motivated to explore an approximation consisting of families of these coherent states. In fact, if one simply examines the exact ground-state wavefunction, it immediately becomes evident that the components with the largest amplitudes occur in coherent-state-like families.

In the following, we will explore two options, one in which actual coherent states are used (we call this the coherent-state ansatz (CSA)), and one in which the same Fock states comprising the coherent states are used, but with arbitrary coefficients to allow deviations from coherent states (we call this the restricted Hilbert space (RHS) approximation). These two variations, while both numerical, are extremely computationally efficient and as a consequence can be extended to higher-dimensional lattices at minimal computational cost. In both cases, we compare the principal features of the many-body wavefunctions and compute observables such as the ground-state energy or the polaron effective mass. This quantifies the validity of the various approximations as a function of coupling strength. For brevity, we constrain our discussion to one- and two-dimensional (2D) cubic lattices, aiming to highlight key differences between the two; it has been shown that the Holstein polaron in three dimensions exhibits qualitatively similar behaviour to its 2D counterpart

18

II
Model

The Holstein model describes electrons on a lattice in the tight-binding formulation, with lattice ions modelled as independent Einstein oscillators of mass
M
and frequency
\omega_{\rm E}
, and a linear coupling between electrons and lattice vibrations. The Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigl(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i}\Bigr)\\
+\sum_{j}\biggl[\frac{\hat{p}_{j}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{1}{2}M\omega_{\rm E}^{2}\hat{x}_{j}^{2}\biggr]-\alpha\sum_{j}\hat{n}_{j}\hat{x}_{j},

(1)

where
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}
(
\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}
) creates (annihilates) an electron at lattice site
j
,
\hat{n}_{j}=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}
counts the number electrons at site
j
, and
\hat{x}_{j}
and
\hat{p}_{j}
represent the position and momentum of the ion at site
j
. Here,
t
is the hopping integral, setting the energy scale for the electron motion, and
\alpha
denotes the strength of the electron-phonon coupling. In the first term, the sum is taken over all pairs of nearest-neighbouring sites, while the other sums are taken over all sites on the lattice.

In what follows, we set Planck’s constant
\hbar=1
. Using the standard relations

\hat{x}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2M\omega_{\rm E}}}\bigl(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}+\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\bigr),\quad\hat{p}_{j}=i\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{\rm E}}{2}}\bigl(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}-\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\bigr),

the Hamiltonian (
1
) can be re-written as

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigl(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i}\Bigr)+\omega_{\rm E}\sum_{j}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}-g\omega_{\rm E}\sum_{j}\hat{n}_{j}\bigl(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}+\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\bigr),

(2)

where
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}
(
\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}
) creates (annihilates) a phonon excitation at site
j
and we have introduced the dimensionless coupling constant
g={\alpha}/({\omega_{\rm E}}{\sqrt{2M\omega_{\rm E}}})
.

While the parameter
g
is useful in some settings, especially in strong-coupling perturbation theory, we mainly make use of the alternate dimensionless coupling constant
\lambda~=~{g^{2}\omega_{\rm E}}/({2dt})
, where
d
is the dimension of the lattice (in this work,
d=1,2
). The parameter
\lambda
is used frequently in the literature, as it better characterizes the strong- and weak-coupling regimes (very roughly,
\lambda<1
corresponds to weak coupling,
\lambda>1
to strong coupling, and
\lambda\approx 1
to the crossover region). Throughout the paper, we measure energies in units of
t
, such that the system is parametrized by the two dimensionless quantities
\omega_{\rm E}/t
and
\lambda
.

For a lattice with
N
sites, we specify the phonon states by an
N
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{n}
of occupation numbers. Explicitly,

\ket{\mathbf{n}}=\ket{n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{N}}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j})^{n_{j}}}{\sqrt{n_{j}!}}\ket{0}

(3)

represents a configuration in which
n_{1}
phonons occupy the first lattice site,
n_{2}
phonons occupy the second site, and so on (the labelling of the
N
sites is schematic, and thus can be applied to lattices in any dimension). The vacuum state without phonons is denoted by
\ket{0}
. The states
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\ket{\mathbf{n}}
form a complete orthonormal basis for the single-electron Hilbert space, with
i=1,2,\dots,N
. Even on a finite lattice, the complete Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional owing to the fact that the phonon numbers
n_{j}
are unbounded. The number of phonons required to describe the ground state grows quickly with the coupling strength
\lambda
, especially for small frequencies
\omega_{\rm E}
. For example, the Lang–Firsov transformation
54
predicts that the average number of phonons is approximately
g^{2}=2d\lambda/(\omega_{\rm E}/t)
.

The Hamiltonian (
2
) is translationally invariant and therefore can be block diagonalized in sectors with total momentum
\mathbf{k}
. Therefore, a convenient basis for the problem consists of the Bloch states

\ket{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{i}}}\hat{T}_{\mathbf{R}_{i}}\Bigl(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\ket{\mathbf{n}}\Bigr),

(4)

where
\hat{T}_{\mathbf{R}_{i}}
is the translation operator by the lattice vector
\mathbf{R}_{i}
. Throughout, we take site 1 to be the origin, such that
\mathbf{R}_{1}=0
. The states
\ket{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}}
are constructed with periodic boundary conditions before taking the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
, whereupon the momentum
\mathbf{k}
becomes a continuous variable taking on arbitrary values in the Brillouin zone.

III
Methods

The basis states in
Eq.

4
represent linear superpositions, where the state
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\ket{\mathbf{n}}
is copied onto all other lattice sites
i\neq 1
through translations. Here,
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\ket{\mathbf{n}}
is an object consisting of an electron at the origin and some surrounding phonons whose position relative to the electron is given by
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
. We emphasize that this choice of basis is distinct from the momentum-space representation obtained via Fourier transform; it is explicitly a real-space basis where phonons occupy definite positions with respect to the electron. To describe a given basis state, it is sufficient to specify the phonon configuration
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
, with the electron assumed to be at the origin. For a given value of
\mathbf{k}
, the Bloch state (
4
) can be uniquely re-constructed from knowledge of the vector
\mathbf{n}
.

For the methods employed in this work, this allows us to treat the infinite lattice directly, eliminating finite-size boundary effects. To do so, the infinite Hilbert space is truncated to exclude phonons on sites far from the electron, i.e. far from the origin. Furthermore, we impose an upper bound
N_{\rm max}
on the number of phonons, discarding states with more than
N_{\rm max}
phonons on any one site. This results in a finite-dimensional subset of the complete Hilbert space. The exact truncation procedure is different for each of the three methods, which we will describe shortly.

Our primary focus is on the ground state, which corresponds to
\mathbf{k}=0
. Examination of exact numerical results in the strong-coupling regime suggests that despite the presence of many phonons in the ground state, phonons tend to appear in highly specific configurations. As a result, only a relatively small subset of the Hilbert space is required to accurately describe the ground-state wavefunction,
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
. Specifically, the only basis states that are required consist of a large cloud of phonons occupying a single lattice site in the electron’s vicinity, with exceptionally few phonons anywhere else in the system. Such states can be categorized into families, each parametrized by the number of phonons
n
in the phonon cloud. The five families of states which contribute most prominently are depicted in
Fig.

1
, along with a plot of their corresponding amplitudes
c_{\nu}=\braket{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}|\Psi_{0}}
for a particular choice of model parameters. Here the label
\nu=(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})
is a generic index. Note that the results shown in
Fig.

1
are from the numerically exact result obtained with the modified form of the Bonča–Trugman (BT) method. They show very systematic behaviour both in the set of families that contribute and in the smoothness of the
n
-dependence within a given family, but the individual coefficients in fact are allowed to vary freely to minimize the energy. Indeed, it is this observed smoothness that motivates the CSA described in the next section.

Figure 1:
Plot of the numerically exact ground-state wavefunction for a 1D chain with
\omega_{\rm E}=0.2t
and
\lambda=1.5
, showing the amplitudes of states belonging to the five families depicted on the right. Dashed lines connect points within a given family and serve as a guide to the eye. States belonging to these families contribute the majority of the total probability density, with
\sum_{\nu}|c_{\nu}|^{2}=0.997
for the five families depicted here. On the right, arrows represent the location of the electron, while the number of phonons on a given lattice site is indicated in the attached box, with
n=0,1,\dots
labelling individual states within each family. Sites not explicitly labelled contain no phonons. As explained in the main text, the actual states used are Bloch superpositions, over all sites on the lattice, of the states depicted here. Except for those in the blue family (top), each state has an equivalent counterpart, obtained by reflecting across the electron site, which is also included in the appropriate family.

Throughout the paper, we will refer to these families by the colour of their surrounding boxes in
Fig.

1
. The hierarchy of families, from most to least important, can be pictured as follows (from top to bottom in the figure):

•

Blue:
The electron and the phonon cloud occupy the same site, with no other phonons in the system.

•

Orange:
The electron and the phonon cloud occupy neighbouring sites, with no other phonons in the system.

•

Green:
The electron and the phonon cloud occupy the same site, with one additional phonon on a neighbouring site.

•

Red:
The electron and the phonon cloud occupy neighbouring sites, with one additional phonon on the same site as the electron.

•

Brown:
The electron and the phonon cloud occupy second-neighbouring sites, with no other phonons in the system.

The blue family of states is completely local, with all particles on the same site. States belonging to the other families, however, appear as inherently asymmetrical. For the sake of definiteness, the pictorial legend in
Fig.

1
depicts particles on sites to the right of the electron, but the equivalent states with particles on the left are also included in their respective families. Similarly, the 2D square lattice has four equivalent sets of states, obtained by
90^{\circ}
rotations. Additionally, the brown family in 2D also includes states where the phonon cloud occupies one of the four states diagonally opposite the electron. Altogether, for a given value of
n
, the brown family then has eight members, one for each site that can be reached from the electron using two hops. We refer collectively to such sites as the electron’s second neighbours.

III.0.1
Coherent-state ansatz (CSA)

In strong-coupling perturbation theory, the polaron ground state for
\lambda\to\infty
is a coherent state given by the Lang–Firsov formula
54

\ket{\Psi_{0}}_{\rm LF}=e^{-g^{2}/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{g\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\ket{0}\!,

(5)

which represents a linear combination of basis states belonging to the blue family in
Fig.

1
. A close examination of numerically exact ground-state wavefunctions in the strong-coupling regime, such as the one shown in
Fig.

1
, suggests that even for finite
\lambda
, the contributions from these blue states approximately resemble a coherent state, albeit with a modified coherence factor
\alpha<g
. Somewhat surprisingly, the other families also appear to take the form of coherent states, a result not predicted by perturbation theory.

Motivated by this observation, we propose the variational ansatz

\ket{\Psi(\mathbf{k})}=w_{\rm b}\ket{\Psi_{\rm b}}+w_{\rm o}\ket{\Psi_{\rm o}}+w_{\rm g}\ket{\Psi_{\rm g}}+w_{\rm r}\ket{\Psi_{\rm r}}+w_{\rm br}\ket{\Psi_{\rm br}}\!,

(6)

where
w_{\mu}
are arbitrary coefficients and
\ket{\Psi_{\mu}}
are coherent states corresponding to the families depicted in
Fig.

1
. The subscripts
\mu\in\{\rm b,\ o,\ g,\ r,\ br\}
stand for the colours of the five families. In 1D, these coherent states for
\mathbf{k}=0
are given by the expressions

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}e^{\alpha_{\rm b}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}}\ket{0}\!,

(7)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\Bigl(e^{\alpha_{\rm o}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i+1}}+e^{\alpha_{\rm o}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i-1}}\Bigr)\ket{0}\!,

(8)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\Bigl(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i+1}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i-1}\Bigr)e^{\alpha_{\rm g}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}}\ket{0}\!,

(9)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\Bigl(e^{\alpha_{\rm r}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i+1}}+e^{\alpha_{\rm r}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i-1}}\Bigr)\ket{0}\!,

(10)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm br}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\Bigl(e^{\alpha_{\rm br}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i+2}}+e^{\alpha_{\rm br}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i-2}}\Bigr)\ket{0}\!,

(11)

where
\alpha_{\mu}
are variational parameters and where normalization factors have been omitted for clarity. In the above expressions, the two mirror-symmetric versions of each family are explicitly accounted for. To construct the CSA in 2D, the sums in
Eqs.

8
,
9
,
10
and
11
are modified to include the four nearest neighbours and eight second neighbours as needed. We have written the CSA states here for
\mathbf{k}=0
, as appropriate for computing ground-state properties. When considering
\mathbf{k}\neq 0
, the variational parameters
\alpha_{\mu}(\mathbf{k})
and
w_{\mu}(\mathbf{k})
explicitly depend on
\mathbf{k}
, and additional Bloch phases appear in Eq. (
7
)-(
11
). More details are provided in
Appendix

A
.

To determine the ground-state energy, we vary the parameters
\alpha_{\mu}
and the coefficients
w_{\mu}
in Eq. (
6
) to minimize the energy expectation
\braket{\hat{H}}=\bra{\Psi}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi}
. We emphasize the simple form of the CSA wavefunction, which contains only nine free parameters (five variational parameters
\alpha_{\mu}
and five weights
w_{\mu}
with a normalization constraint) regardless of coupling strength, frequency, or dimension. In contrast, the RHS and BT methods described below require a number of states on the order of thousands and millions, respectively. As we shall see in
Section

IV
, the CSA yields ground-state energies which are remarkably similar to those from the RHS approximation, of which it is a special case. In addition to the fact that the computational complexity is independent of dimension or the choice of parameters, the CSA admits an intuitive picture of the polaron ground state: a superposition of coherent states whose positions relative to the electron are given by the legend in
Fig.

1
.

III.0.2
Restricted Hilbert space (RHS) approximation

The CSA described in the foregoing section imposes two main constraints, namely restricting the Hilbert space to just five families and constraining the individual families to take the form of coherent states. Maintaining the restriction to the same five families of states, we relax the second constraint, allowing the weight of individual states within a family to vary freely. We then compute the ground-state energy via exact diagonalization (ED): we refer to this as the RHS approximation.

As an example, for the blue family for
\mathbf{k}=0
in the RHS approximation we write

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\rm b}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}^{(\rm b)}\frac{(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0}\!,

(12)

with arbitrary weights
d_{n}^{(\rm b)}
. The associated coherent state in the CSA corresponds to the Poissonian distribution
d_{n}^{(\rm b)}\propto(\alpha_{\rm b})^{n}/\sqrt{n!}
, with a single variational parameter
\alpha_{\rm b}
, which produces the term
e^{\alpha_{\rm b}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}}|0\rangle
in
Eq.

7
after summing over the phonon numbers
n
. Explicit expressions for all five families and arbitrary
\mathbf{k}
are given in
Appendix

B
.

Restricting the Hilbert space to include only states from the five families results in a small Hamiltonian matrix
H(\mathbf{k})
, which we diagonalize to find the lowest-energy eigenstate. The necessary matrix elements
H_{\mathbf{m}\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{k})=\bra{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{m}}\hat{H}\ket{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}}
are given by

H_{\mathbf{m}\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{k})=-t\sum_{j}A_{1j}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{j}}}\bra{\mathbf{m}}\hat{T}_{\mathbf{R}_{j}}\ket{\mathbf{n}}\\
+\delta_{\mathbf{m}\mathbf{n}}\omega_{\rm E}\sum_{j}n_{j}-g\omega_{\rm E}\bra{\mathbf{m}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}+\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{1})\ket{\mathbf{n}}\!,

(13)

with
A
the adjacency matrix of the lattice defined in
Eq.

27
and the sums taken over all lattice sites. The matrix dimension is made finite by imposing a phonon cutoff
N_{\rm max}
and considering only states with phonon clouds of size
n\leq N_{\rm max}
. Due to the small size of the RHS, we can take
N_{\rm max}
to be very large, ensuring properly converged results.

The expression in
Eq.

13
has a simple interpretation. Due to the matrix element
A_{1j}
, the first term sums over sites neighbouring the origin. The hopping term thus contributes a factor of
-te^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}_{j}}}
if the phonon numbers
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{m}
differ only by shifting all phonons by one site. The phonon term is diagonal and equal to
\omega_{\rm E}N_{\rm ph}
, where
N_{\rm ph}
represents the total number of phonons in the system. The coupling term contributes a factor of
-g\omega_{\rm E}\sqrt{n_{1}+1}
or
-g\omega_{\rm E}\sqrt{n_{1}}
if the states
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
and
\ket{\mathbf{m}}
differ by a single phonon at the origin but are otherwise identical. This last fact is due to the relations

\hat{a}^{\dagger}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}\!,\quad\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}\!.

(14)

To make the hopping term in
Eq.

13
more concrete: the phase factor is
e^{ik_{x}}
if the two states
\ket{\mathbf{m}}
and
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
match after shifting the phonons in
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
one site to the right;
e^{-ik_{x}}
if the states match after shifting one site to the left;
e^{ik_{y}}
for a shift of one site upward; and
e^{-ik_{y}}
for a shift of one site downward.

Of course, the number of families included in the RHS and the CSA is arbitrary. Greater accuracy can, in principle, be achieved by considering more than five families. However, as suggested by the plot in
Fig.

1
, the red and brown families already contribute relatively little. Indeed, the curves which correspond to the excluded families all lie well below the brown curve on the plot, and thus the error induced by their omission is small at strong coupling.

III.0.3
Modified Bonča–Trugman (BT) algorithm

For numerically exact results against which to benchmark our approximation methods, we use the Bonča–Trugman (BT) algorithm
18
as modified in
55
. Here, a subset of the Hilbert space is generated by repeated application of the Hamiltonian to a given collection of states, known as the seed states. This then becomes the basis for a Hamiltonian matrix
H(\mathbf{k})
, which is used to determine the ground-state energy via ED.

The process of generating basis states is as follows. In the basis of Bloch states, the Hamiltonian has a diagonal piece, namely the phonon term
\omega_{\rm E}\sum_{j}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}
, and an off-diagonal piece

\hat{H}_{\rm off}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigl(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{i}\Bigr)-g\omega_{\rm E}\sum_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\bigl(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}+\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\bigr).

(15)

We generate new states for our truncated basis by acting with
\hat{H}_{\rm off}
on the existing states. Applying
\hat{H}_{\rm off}
to a particular state yields:

•

a state with one additional phonon at the origin,

•

a state with one fewer phonon at the origin, and

•

q
states, where
q
is the coordination number of the lattice, in which the electron has hopped from the origin to one of the neighbouring sites.

Recalling that the basis states
\ket{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}}
are superpositions of identical states
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\ket{\mathbf{n}}
copied onto all sites of the lattice, we choose a convention wherein we represent each basis state by
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\ket{\mathbf{n}}
, its component with the electron at the origin. In order to maintain the appropriate projection, states resulting from a hop must be translated such that the electron is returned to the origin. In this way, the hopping portion of the Hamiltonian is effectively applied by shifting all phonons in the system by one lattice site.

This is perhaps most clearly seen through an example. Consider a state in 1D with 4 phonons on the site with the electron and 2 phonons on the site to its left, represented schematically by

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ \hat{4}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(16)

where the hat denotes the location of the electron. Note that
\hat{H}_{\rm off}
consists of two pieces. The coupling piece has terms proportional to
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{a}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{j}
, which generate the states

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ \hat{5}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\quad\text{and}\quad\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ \hat{3}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(17)

respectively. The hopping piece yields the two states

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ \hat{2}\ 4\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\longrightarrow\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 0\ \hat{2}\ 4\ 0\ \cdots}

(18)

and

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ 4\ \hat{0}\ 0\ \cdots}\longrightarrow\ket{\cdots\ 2\ 4\ \hat{0}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(19)

where we replaced the resultant states in
Eqs.

18
and
19
by their equivalent counterparts with the electron at the origin. In this manner, the state in
Eq.

16
generates the four states

\displaystyle\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ \hat{5}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(20)

\displaystyle\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 2\ \hat{3}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

\displaystyle\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 0\ \hat{2}\ 4\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

\displaystyle\ket{\cdots\ 2\ 4\ \hat{0}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

where hops are represented heuristically by a shift of all phonons in the system. This procedure is easily generalized to a 2D lattice.

The process of building the truncated basis is iterative. We denote by
n_{H}
the number of times we have applied the Hamiltonian, such that the seed states constitute the
n_{H}=0
layer. Applying
\hat{H}_{\rm off}
to each of the seed states generates a collection of candidate states for the next layer. From the candidates we remove any states which were already included in the seed, and the remaining states constitute the
n_{H}=1
layer. If a state ever appears multiple times in the same layer, we keep only one copy.

The procedure is then carried out again, applying
\hat{H}_{\rm off}
to all states in the
n_{H}=1
layer and discarding any of the resulting states which belong to one of the previous layers. This yields the
n_{H}=2
layer. The process is repeated until the desired number of layers
N_{H}
is reached. The set of all states included in the layers
n_{H}=0,1,\dots,N_{H}
becomes the basis for the Hamiltonian matrix
H(\mathbf{k})
, whose elements are computed according to
Eq.

13
.

The original work by Bonča et al. used an electron on a bare lattice as the seed, represented schematically by

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 0\ \hat{0}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(21)

which yields extremely accurate results for small to moderate values of the coupling strength
18
;
52
. However, this procedure breaks down at strong coupling, where the number of phonons needed to describe the ground state becomes very large. Motivated by the Lang–Firsov limit, we treat the strong-coupling regime by using the Lang–Firsov seed states

\ket{\cdots\ 0\ 0\ \hat{n}\ 0\ 0\ \cdots}\!,

(22)

with
n=0,1,\dots,N_{\rm max}
for a large but finite cutoff
N_{\rm max}
. These states are precisely the basis states needed to construct
Eq.

5
. Though the notation is suggestive of a 1D lattice, these seeds work equally well in 2D and in higher dimensions. We note that the bare-lattice (or weak-coupling) seed is, in fact, contained in the Lang–Firsov (or strong-coupling) seed.

The size of the truncated basis grows linearly with the phonon truncation value
N_{\rm max}
but exponentially with
N_{H}
, with the number of basis states given approximately by
(N_{\rm max}+1)\times(d+1)^{N_{H}}
. This demonstrates the importance of the strong-coupling seed. In 2D, for example, with
\omega_{\rm E}=0.1t
and
\lambda=1
, the number of phonons needed for converged results is approximately
90
. This limit is achieved efficiently through the strong-coupling seed, but to generate this many phonons from the weak-coupling seed would require
N_{H}\geq 90
, resulting in a matrix dimension on the order of
3^{90}\approx 10^{43}
. Not only is this computationally infeasible, but it is also extremely wasteful: as we show in this work, very few of these states are actually required to achieve highly accurate strong-coupling results.

The BT algorithm proves very efficient at generating appropriate basis states for describing the polaron ground state, by providing a natural tradeoff between the total number of phonons and their distance from the electron. Because the application of the Hamiltonian to a given layer can
either
create more phonons
or
move them away from the electron, states with the most phonons have these phonons on sites in the immediate vicinity of the electron, while states with phonons furthest from the electron contain a relatively small total number of phonons.

Our most accurate results are obtained by using the bare-lattice seed for the weak-coupling regime and the Lang–Firsov seed for the strong-coupling regime. For intermediate coupling strengths, we employ both seeds and choose the one which yields the lower ground-state energy. Although the bare-lattice seed is contained in the Lang–Firsov seeds, the former allows us to include a larger
N_{H}
in practice, and thus may give superior variational solutions. We ensure that results are converged in both the phonon truncation
N_{\rm max}
and the number of layers
N_{H}
.

V
Conclusion and Outlook

At strong coupling, properties of the Holstein polaron are difficult to compute owing to the large number of phonons present, even in the ground state. We have presented two approximative methods for calculating ground-state polaron properties. These methods are designed specifically to target the strong-coupling regime, and are extremely computationally efficient, making it feasible to apply them to higher-dimensional lattices. Despite their simplicity, they give results which agree reasonably well with more computationally taxing exact methods.

The first method (CSA) is a variational ansatz, consisting of coherent states of phonons in the vicinity of the electron on the lattice. At strong coupling, it gives very accurate ground-state energies and reasonable approximations to the polaron mass. Ground-state wavefunctions computed using the CSA are similar to the exact results and allow for an intuitive picture of the many-body ground state in terms of families of coherent phonon states surrounding the electron in real space. The second method (RHS) uses exact diagonalization on a restricted subset of the Hilbert space consisting of the individual Fock states contained within the CSA. For strong coupling, the accuracy gained from relaxing the coherent-state constraint is small. For weak and moderate coupling strengths, however, the RHS improves upon the CSA significantly, especially in 2D. The RHS also correctly predicts a smooth crossover in 1D, and a very rapid crossover in 2D, between a weak- and a strong-coupling polaron.

The methods outlined in this paper are simple to use and require very little computational resources. As such, they are promising for obtaining approximate results to more difficult problems. A natural extension would be to study a two-electron system with an electron-electron repulsion. In this so-called bipolaron problem, already studied in 1D in the regime of intermediate and large
\omega_{E}/t

17
, the interaction between polarons is studied in order to determine under which circumstances the electron-phonon interaction leads to the formation of bound pairs. For smaller values of
\omega_{E}/t
, it is useful to have access to simple methods which give good approximate descriptions of a polaron with fewer basis states.

Another promising avenue of exploration would be to treat the Holstein polaron on more complex geometries such as the honeycomb, kagome, or pyrochlore lattices, to name a few that have recently been of interest in condensed matter theory. Obtaining exact solutions to these and other related problems would require significant effort. The CSA, however, can be applied to arbitrary lattices with only minimal tweaking of the procedure described in this work. The RHS, which is extremely computationally efficient compared with other ED methods, could similarly be applied to such problems with modest effort.
