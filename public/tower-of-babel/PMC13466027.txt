Semantic memory navigation in mild cognitive impairment: Automated markers with neural and biofluid correlates

Ferrante, Joaquín Valdés, Joaquín Migeot, Alejandro Sosa Welford, Loreto Olavarría, Patricia Lillo, Daniela Thumala-Dockendorff, Cecilia Okuma, Mauricio Cerda, Fernando Henríquez, Patricia Pelle, Claudia Duran-Aniotz, Agustín Ibáñez, Luciana Ferrer, Andrea Slachevsky, Adolfo M. García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Verbal fluency tasks are ubiquitous in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) screenings. Yet, their assessment is traditionally limited to valid response counts. This subjective approach constrains analysis to univariate methods and overlooks which semantic memory dimensions are affected, introducing human bias while limiting informativeness. We tackled these gaps with a novel automated framework. Ninety-six participants (53 with MCI, 43 cognitively unimpaired individuals) performed phonemic and semantic fluency tasks alongside standard cognitive tests. Word properties (e.g., frequency, granularity, length) and timing features (e.g., number of pauses) were (i) automatically extracted to discriminate between groups via machine learning classification, (ii) benchmarked against standard cognitive measures (Trail Making Test-A, Trail Making Test-B, episodic memory subscore from the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination, digit span, and Mini-mental State Examination), and (iii) used to predict brain patterns and plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (pTau217) concentration. Our approach yielded robust classification performance when using word properties and speech timing features combined (Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] = 0.81, 95% confidence interval [CI] = [0.71, 0.89]), outperforming cognitive measures (AUC = 0.77, CI = [.68, 0.85]). Frequency, granularity, and semantic distance correlated with the gray matter volume of semantic-related regions commonly atrophied in MCI. No fluency feature was associated with functional connectivity patterns. Granularity was moderately associated with pTau217 levels. In sum, automated fluency analyses facilitate MCI detection, capturing fine-grained neurocognitive and biomarker patterns in the condition.

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (McDonnell et al., 2020). The condition involves cognitive difficulties that surpass age- and education-related expectations without hindering daily activities (Gauthier et al., 2006). Its prevalence can reach 20% in adults above age 60, and its presence entails an 8-to-15% risk of dementia conversion (Gauthier et al., 2006). Clinical diagnosis requires cognitive assessments, including measures of memory and attention, typically accompanied by verbal fluency tasks (producing as many words as possible in one minute).

The latter are widely used because of their brevity (Ferrante et al., 2023), sensitivity across MCI phenotypes (Weakley et al., 2013), and utility to predict neuropsychological outcomes (Weakley et al., 2013; Nutter-Upham et al., 2008), brain anomalies (Clark et al., 2014), and incident dementia (Sutin et al., 2019). However, most studies target valid word counts, hindering insights on which aspects of semantic memory are most affected (Ferrante et al., 2023). Also, this approach rests on subjective application of widely varying criteria, which compromises the comparability and generalizability of results (Olabarrieta-Landa et al., 2017). Moreover, it reduces each word to a single value, constraining analyses to univariate methods (Ferrante et al., 2023).

Such caveats can be overcome by automating the capture and analysis of multivariate response properties (Ferrante et al., 2023). Each word can be decomposed into variables vulnerable to cognitive decline, including frequency (occurrences in daily language use), granularity (conceptual precision), phonemic and syllabic length (number of phonemes and syllables, respectively), familiarity (recognizability of the referent), concreteness, and phonological neighbors (number of words with similar sublexical structure). Moreover, task recordings can be analyzed for indices of word retrieval effort, such as the number and duration of pauses and phonated segments, total speech time, and other timing metrics (García et al., 2022; Gosztolya et al., 2019;Beltrami et al., 2018). Such properties can be used to discriminate patients from cognitively unimpaired individuals (CUIs) and to capture cognitive and neural alterations (Ferrante et al., 2023; García et al., 2022).

Rarely attempted in MCI, this approach has proven useful in AD research. Across semantic and phonemic fluency tasks, AD patients produce words with high frequency, low granularity, and many phonological neighbors (Ferrante et al., 2023), thus favoring highly accessible spaces within semantic memory. While some of these variables (e.g., granularity) remain unexplored in MCI, the preference for high-frequency words has been replicated in this condition (Fraser et al., 2018) –which further involves speech timing alterations during fluency and other verbal tasks, indicating more effortful word retrieval (Gosztolya et al., 2019; Beltrami et al., 2018). Suggestively, joint analysis of linguistic and acoustic features can enhance patient discrimination (Gosztolya et al., 2019), even outperforming classical cognitive measures (Fraser et al., 2019).

Some such features may predict neurobiological anomalies. MCI typically involves atrophy (Köhler et al., 1998) and hypoconnectivity (Liu et al., 2016) of (para)hippocampal/temporal and fronto-limbic regions, whose integrity can be predicted by specific word properties (e.g., frequency, granularity, semantic distance) (Toro-Hernández et al., 2024; Ferrante et al., 2023), and speech timing (e.g., pauses) (Kircher et al., 2004; Pistono et al., 2016). Moreover, in other tasks, specific word properties (e.g., frequency) and timing (pause-related) features have been linked to pTau concentrations –a core plasma-derived marker of MCI and AD (Cho et al., 2022; Young et al., 2024). Response property analysis, then, could capture neural and biofluid signatures of the condition.

Here, we examined whether automated analysis of fluency response properties can reveal MCI markers. Word property and timing features were obtained from patients and CUIs, used for machine learning classification, benchmarked against standard cognitive tests, and correlated with neuroimaging (volume, fMRI connectivity) and biomarker (pTau217) measures. First, we predicted that word properties and timing features (i) would yield maximal classification of MCI patients and CUIs when analyzed jointly rather than separately, (ii) matching classical cognitive measures. We further hypothesized that classification (iii) would be mainly driven by specific properties (e.g., frequency, granularity), which, in turn, (iv) would correlate with temporohippocampal/fronto-limbic brain patterns and pTau217 concentrations. We also explored how our response property approach compares against text and audio embeddings. Briefly, this study aims to test new, streamlined fluency metrics for MCI.

Methods

The study’s methods are summarized in Fig. 1.

Participants

Our study comprised 96 native Spanish-speaking older adults, all aged 70 years or older (53 with MCI, 43 CUIs) from the GERO cohort (Fig. 1A1) (Young et al., 2024). Recruitment was based on a door-to-door approach in the general Chilean population. Individuals were excluded if they presented (i) a clinical diagnosis of dementia and/or neurological disease; (ii) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores <21 and/or Pfeffer questionnaire scores >2; (iii) a history of institutionalization; (iv) illiteracy; (v) visual and auditory deficit impeding cognitive evaluation and/or the capacity to maintain an independent living; (vi) severe mobility impairment; (vii) severe psychiatric/neurological disorders, brain tumor, subdural hematoma and/or progressive supranuclear palsy, history of head trauma, recent stroke; or (viii) a fatal disease with less than a year of survival (Slachevsky et al., 2020). Following reported protocols (Slachevsky et al., 2020), all of them completed neurological, neuropsychological, and neuropsychiatric evaluations, including semi-structured interviews and standardized cognitive tests. A trained neurologist evaluated all participants to verify that they met the inclusion criteria.

All participants exhibited Clinical Dementia Rating scores of 0.5 or lower and were functionally independent. No participant exhibited signs of dementia, operationally defined as the presence of both cognitive impairment, indicated by a MMSE score below 21, and functional dependence, defined by a Pfeffer Functional Activities Questionnaire (PFAQ) score greater than 2. We classified participants as having MCI according to the NIA-AA research framework: cognitively impaired but functionally independent (Jr and R, 2018). In line with current clinical criteria (Jr and R, 2018), individuals were assigned to the MCI group based on neuropsychological performance if (i) their scores on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Delgado et al., 2019) fell below 21, and/or (ii) their Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT) (Grober et al., 2010). Total Immediate Free Recall score fell below 27, and/or (iii) their FCSRT Total Free Recall score fell below 47, all based on country-specific norms (Delgado et al., 2019; Ramos-Henderson et al., 2025). This group presented deficits in overall cognitive status, measured through MMSE; episodic memory, assessed through the FCSRT and the episodic memory subscore from the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-III (ACE-III) (Matias-Guiu et al., 2017); attention, tapped with the Trail-Making Test A (TMT-A) (Arango-Lasprilla et al., 2015); and working memory, as measured through Trail-Making Test B (TMT-B) and digits span backwards (Wechsler and Edition, 1997). Also, they exhibited predominant left temporal pole atrophy and hippocampal- and parahippocampal-cortical hypoconnectivity (Supplementary Material 1). The MCI group further exhibited significantly higher concentrations of pTau217 (Table 1). CUIs had intact cognitive abilities (with MoCA scores > 20) and presented no history of neurological or psychiatric issues, drug abuse or alcoholism. Both groups were matched for sex, handedness, age, and years of education (Table 1). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional ethics’ committee CEC SSM Oriente under code 29092020.

Fluency tasks

Participants completed phonemic and semantic fluency tasks, requiring them to utter as many words as they could say starting with the phonemes /f/, /a/, and /s/, or belonging to the category ‘animals’, respectively (Fig. 1A2). Following standard procedures, they were given one minute per task and instructed not to produce proper names, numbers, repetitions, words from the same family, or morphological variations of the same word. The tasks were counterbalanced across participants. Responses were audio-recorded and transcribed automatically using Google’s Text-to-Speech API. Transcriptions were then checked and edited by at least two examiners, following a standardized protocol (Ferrante et al., 2023). Unintelligible words, metalinguistic comments, and interventions from the evaluators were discarded. As in recent work (Ferrante et al., 2023), features were extracted from the totality of all remaining words, irrespective of whether they were valid or invalid in terms of standard scoring criteria. All participants produced at least five words per task.

Feature extraction

Word properties

As in previous research (Ferrante et al., 2023), each word’s granularity was calculated using the NLTK library in Python to access Word-Net, a hierarchical graph of nodes representing increasingly specific concepts leading from the highest hypernym (‘entity’) to more specialized terms (e.g., ‘animal’, ‘dog’, ‘bulldog’). Granularity refers to the number of nodes between a word and the top-node ‘entity’. For instance, bin-3 words are closer to ‘entity’ than bin-10 words, with the former indicating more general concepts.

In addition, we used the EsPal database (Duchon et al., 2013) to decompose each response into six lexico-semantic features: word frequency (logarithmic frequency per million), phonological neighborhood (number of words generated by substituting, adding, or omitting a phoneme), phonemic length (number of phonemes), syllabic length (number of syllables), familiarity (rated on a scale from 1: not familiar to 7: highly familiar), and concreteness (rated on a scale from 1: highly abstract to 7: highly concrete). Missing values for the last two features were imputed with those corresponding to the first synonym of the given word, automatically obtained from the WordReference webpage (English to French, 2026) and accessed through a Python script.

Also, following reported methods (Toro-Herńandez et al., 2024; Sanz et al., 2022; Ferrante et al., 2025), we calculated semantic distance, capturing conceptual proximity between successive words. To this end, we converted each word into a vector with a Spanish FastText model and then computed the cosine distance between adjacent vectors. High semantic distance values reflect jumps between semantically distant concepts, reflecting sparser vocabulary navigation strategies. Finally, we computed phonological distance between adjacent words via the Damerau–Levenshtein metric, which computes the minimum number of single-character edits (insertions, deletions, substitutions or adjacent transpositions) required to change one string into another (Nikolaev et al., 2020). For example, sol to sal requires one substitution (distance = 1), whereas sol to soledad requires four insertions (distance = 4). We computed this distance between each pair of successive words in a transcript and normalized each value by the maximum possible distance for that pair (i.e., the length of the longer word) so that all distances ranged from 0 (identical) to 1 (completely different). Consecutive repeated words were skipped.

We calculated the mean, minimum, and maximum of each variable per subject and averaged them across tasks (Fig. 1B).

Audio- and text-based embeddings

We also gauged our approach against text- and audio-based embeddings. Text embeddings were derived through XLM-RoBERTa’s base model (Conneau et al., 2020), which yields 768-dimensional contextual embeddings for each token. Audio embeddings were obtained upon resampling recordings to 16 kHz with Microsoft’s self-supervised WavLM (Chen et al., 2022) base-plus model (built on the HuBERT framework) and averaging the resulting frame-level representations. Dimensionality of both embedding types was reduced via principal component analysis, retaining the n components that explained at least 90% variance.

Speech timing features

Audio recordings were converted to .wav format, downmixed to mono (single channel), encoded in GSM Full Rate, resampled to 16 kHz (Haulcy and Glass, 2021), compressed with a factor of 8, and bandpass-filtered from 200 Hz to 3.4 kHz. Audios were loudness-normalized (Haulcy and Glass, 2021) using a normalization filter of the FFmpeg multimedia framework (Tomar, 2006). Also, a background noise reduction (denoising) step was applied using the FullSubNet model (Hao et al., 2021). Next, we used the Silero voice activity detection model (Team, 2021) to segment audio signals into speech and non-speech regions (Haulcy and Glass, 2021). Finally, through a PRAAT script (De Jong and Wempe, 2009), we automatically extracted the following speech timing features from each recording: number of phonated segments (peaks in intensity preceded and followed by dips in intensity), average phonated segment duration (speaking time over number of phonated segments), number of pauses (silent intervals), pause duration (average duration across all pauses), phonation time (total speech time, after pause removal), speech rate (number of syllables over total duration) and articulation rate (number of syllables over phonation time). We calculated the mean of each variable per subject and averaged them across tasks (Fig. 1B).

Behavioral data analysis

Machine learning analysis

We conducted machine learning analyses to test the robustness of this approach for automated detection of MCI at the subject level (Fig. 1C). The data was divided using a nested, stratified cross-validation framework: in each of ten outer folds (repeated with ten distinct random seeds to capture split variability), we standardized all features to z-scores within the training set before applying the same scaling to the test set. We then conducted a ten-fold inner cross-validation on that training data to optimize hyperparameters via Bayesian optimization, targeting area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values. At each outer iteration, the optimal model was evaluated on its unseen fold, and the resulting predictions (labels and probabilities) were concatenated across folds to yield a complete out-of-sample prediction vector. From this pooled output, we also computed accuracy, F1-score, recall, precision, and normalized expected cost, and derived bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI) (1000 resamples) for each metric. Classification analyses were performed using (i) word properties only, (ii) speech timing features only, and (iii) their combination via early fusion –i.e., a concatenation of individual modality features (Baltrušaitis et al., 2018). As a sensitivity analysis, we also repeated all machine-learning analyses without imputing missing values for variables lacking them.

In all cases, we used XGBoost classifiers, a highly scalable, robust, and fast algorithm that has been successfully used in previous work for automated AD detection (Ferrante et al., 2025). To test for robustness across different machine learning models, we replicated the analyses with logistic regression, linear discriminant analysis, and quadratic discriminant analysis. To quantify feature importance, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game-theoretic attribution method that decomposes each individual prediction into additive feature contributions (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). SHAP values were computed on held-out data within each cross-validation fold to avoid information leakage. For tree-based models, we used TreeSHAP (exact/efficient Shapley computation for ensembles); for non-tree models, KernelSHAP can be used analogously. Global feature importance was defined as the mean absolute Shapley value across subjects, and features were ranked accordingly. We also inspected signed SHAP distributions to characterize directionality of effects at the group level. To assess the discriminative power of our models, we retrained them after randomly shuffling group labels across participants (null models). Machine learning analyses were performed on Python 3.11 and the Scikit-learn package (v. 1.2.1) (Pedregosa et al., 2011).

Also, to gauge the clinical value of our automated approach vis-à-vis standard cognitive measures, we used the same classification pipeline detailed above using the MMSE total score; episodic memory subscore from the ACE-III; TMT-A (time and errors); TMT-B (time and errors); and digit span tasks (forward and backward). Altogether, then, this cognitive classifier was fed with 8 features. We reiterated this analysis with the same sets of models used with our fluency data.

Neuroimaging methods

Data acquisition, preprocessing, and processing

MRI and fMRI scans (Fig. 1A3) were available for 30 MCI patients and 25 CUIs. Data was acquired on a Siemens Skyra 3T scanner using a 20-channel head coil. Following reported protocols (Ferrante et al., 2023), structural T1 images were obtained with the following parameters: matrix = 224 × 224 × 208; 208 slices; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; flip angle = 8°; TR = 1700 ms; TE = 2.25 ms. Resting-state fMRI data were acquired with EP2D-BOLD pulse sequences, sequentially interleaved (pair-ascending first), with these parameters: matrix = 76 × 76 × 46; 46 slices; slice thickness = 3 mm; voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm3; flip angle = 90°; TR = 2660 ms; TE = 30 ms; number of volumes = 300; total duration = 13.3 min. During the fMRI session, participants were instructed not to think of anything in particular and to remain still, awake, and with eyes closed.

All scans were manually set to the anterior commissure and reoriented. Participants’ gray matter (GM) volume was estimated via voxel-based morphometry, following reported protocols (Panman et al., 2019). Preprocessing was performed with SPM12 (Ashburner et al., 2014) (Fig. 1D1) in MATLAB R2021a. Images were segmented between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white matter (WM), and GM, normalized to MNI space using DARTEL, and smoothed. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was estimated from native-space tissue maps obtained through VBM8 segmentation (https://neuro-jena.github.io/software.html#vbm). After removal of the first five volumes (Ferrante et al., 2023), functional and anatomical data were preprocessed using a standard preprocessing pipeline in the CONN v22.a toolbox (Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012). This included: (1) realignment with correction for susceptibility distortion interactions; (2) slice-timing correction; (3) outlier detection using ART (global signal z-threshold = 5, motion threshold = 0.9 mm); (4) direct segmentation, MNI-space normalization, and resampling to 3 mm isotropic voxels; (5) smoothing with a 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel; (6) denoising by regressing out potential confounds (WM, CSF, realignment and scrubbing); and (7) band-pass filtering between 0.008 and 0.09 Hz. Quality control measures did not significantly differ between groups (Supplementary material 2).

Brain-behavior correlations

Multiple regressions (Fig. 1D3) were run in SPM12 to examine if the top features correlated with patients’ GM volume, controlling for TIV, age, sex, years of education, and handedness, and corrected using TFCE (5000 permutations) (Ferrante et al., 2023). These analyses were run on the 30 MCI patients for whom neuroimaging data was available. The alpha level was set at p < .05, FWE-corrected (Smith and Nichols, 2009). Given that some of our target features were shown to capture brain anomalies only for the semantic fluency condition (Ferrante et al., 2023), we reiterated the analyses for the semantic and fluency conditions separately.

We also performed multiple regression analyses in the CONN v22.a toolbox (Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012) (Fig. 1D2) to examine if the top features correlated with seed-to-voxel functional connectivity, controlling for global correlation, age, sex, years of education, and handedness. All analyses were corrected using TFCE (5000 permutations) (Ferrante et al., 2023), with the alpha level was set at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected (Smith and Nichols, 2009).

Biomarker methods

Data acquisition, preprocessing, and processing

Biomarker data was available for 36 participants (Fig. 1A4). Blood samples were collected in EDTA plasma tubes and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 min at room temperature. Following centrifugation, plasma was aliquoted into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Concentrations of plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, NfL, and GFAP were quantified using the Neurology 4-Plex E kit on the Single Molecule Array (Simoa) HD-X analyzer, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Quanterix, Billerica, MA). Plasma p-tau217 levels were measured with a commercially available Simoa assay (ALZpath, Carlsbad, CA) on the same HD-X platform. All assays were performed by technicians blinded to clinical information. Biomarker quantification was conducted using immunoassays that included quality control (QC) samples at both low and high concentration ranges. Intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) ranged from 5.1% to 8.3%, and inter-assay CVs ranged from 7.4% to 10.1%. No QC data were excluded from the analysis.

Biomarker-behavior correlations

Given the selective between-group differences in plasma pTau217, we examined whether this biomarker correlated with the top-5 features of our best-performing fluency classifier (Fig. 1D4). Given the non-normality of the data, we used Spearman’s correlations –separately considering semantic and phonological tasks.

Results

Machine learning results

Results from XGBoost analyses and alternative classifiers showed that classification between MCI patients and CUIs was consistently better when based on word properties (AUC = 0.78, CI = [0.68, 0.87]; accuracy = 0.71, CI = [0.61, 0.79]) than on speech timing features (AUC = 0.61, CI = [0.49, 0.72]; accuracy = 0.60, CI = [0.51, 0.69]), and maximal when combining both dimensions (AUC = 0.81, CI = [0.71, 0.89]; accuracy = 0.78, CI = [0.70, 0.85]). The latter result outperformed that obtained when considering standard cognitive tests (AUC = 0.77, CI = [0.68, 0.85]; accuracy = 0.75, CI = [0.67, 0.82]) When ratings for the missing feature were dropped (Supplementary material 4), classification metrics changed minimally (AUC = 0.78 [0.69, 0.87]; accuracy = 0.67 [0.57, 0.76]). The best-performing embedding-based models yielded chance-level results (audio embeddings: AUC = 0.47 [0.35, 0.59]; text embeddings: AUC = 0.45 [0.34, 0.57]) (Supplementary material 4).

Feature importance analysis revealed that the top-5 features were frequency, semantic distance, granularity, syllabic length, and average syllable duration. Descriptive statistics of the mean values of each fluency feature showed differences in the expected direction for MCI patients relative to CUIs, with univariate tests revealing significant differences for frequency, granularity, phonation time, number of phonated segments, number of pauses, and pause length. All feature sets yielded chance-level results upon randomly shuffling participants’ labels. For details, see Fig. 2, Table 2, and Supplementary materials 3 and 5.

Brain-behavior correlations

Across persons with MCI, in the semantic fluency task, word frequency showed a negative correlation with the GM volume of the left insula, cingulate, and middle occipital cortices; the right middle and inferior frontal gyri; and the bilateral superior frontal gyrus (Fig. 3, left). Positive correlations with GM volume were also observed for granularity in the left superior frontal gyrus (Fig. 3, middle) and for semantic distance in the left inferior temporal and fusiform gyri (Fig. 3, right). No significant correlation was observed between functional connectivity patterns and any of the top features (all p-values > 0.095). For details, see Supplementary material 6.

Biomarkers-behavior correlations

Mean granularity was negatively correlated with pTau217 concentration in the semantic fluency task (Fig. 4) (r = −0.21, p = 0.049). No other correlation between any of the top-five features and pTau217 levels reached statistical significance in either fluency task (all p-values > 0.067). For details, see Table 3.

Discussion

We used automated verbal fluency metrics to identify and characterize MCI. Word properties and speech timing features outperformed standard cognitive measures in discriminating MCI cases, while specific lexical-semantic features were associated with gray matter volume in regions supporting memory and word retrieval. No features correlated with functional connectivity, and granularity showed a negative association with pTau217. Below we discuss these findings highlight scalable speech-based markers.

Word properties outperformed timing metrics for single-subject MCI identification, mirroring studies showing stronger AD/MCI discrimination from linguistic (e.g., word embeddings, rates of specific phrase types) than audio features (e.g., speech embeddings, prosody features) (Gosztolya et al., 2019; Haulcy and Glass, 2021). Still, timing features added value, as combining them with word properties yielded the best MCI–CUI classification, with an AUC of 0.81. This performance matches or exceeds that of other digital speech studies using longer, less typical tasks (Karande and Kulkarni, 2020; Fraser and Komeili, 2021;Nagumo et al., 2020), supporting previous evidence (Gosztolya et al., 2019; Fraser et al., 2019; Martínez-Nicolás et al., 2021) that subject-level MCI detection is optimized by integrating linguistic and timing features.

As in previous research (Fraser et al., 2019; García-Gutíerrez et al., 2023), such machine learning outcomes were similar to those obtained through standard cognitive measures. Classification of MCI patients and CUIs based on scores from validated screening, memory, and executive measures reached AUCs of 0.77. This is a non-trivial clinical point, as fluency tasks take only one minute per version, whereas successive administration of the MMSE, TMT-A, TMT-B, ACE-III, and digit span tasks can take roughly 45 min. Since primary care consultations typically last roughly 19 min (Gottschalk and Flocke, 2005), such promising time-to-yield ratio invites further research to test the generalizability of our approach across clinical contexts.

In contrast, embedding-based models yielded chance results. Previous dementia studies showed integrated audio and text embeddings delivered only modest classification performance (Devahi et al., 2025), falling substantially below models that employ interpretable phonetic and phonological features (Kurdi, 2024). In line with this pattern, our response property approach seems to surpass embedding-based analyses in both interpretability and discriminability.

Our results also identify specific alterations in semantic memory navigation in MCI. Compared with CUIs, MCI patients showed timing changes—fewer and longer pauses, reduced phonation time—and produced more frequent, less granular words, all p-values < 0.045 (Supplementary material 5). Similar timing profiles have been reported in MCI and AD using other paradigms (Nasreen et al., 2021; Balogh et al., 2023;Kleiman and Galvin, 2024). In time-constrained fluency tasks, fewer pauses may reflect longer pauses and lower lexical output, which reduce between-word silence periods. Word-property findings also align with prior evidence that frequency discriminates MCI (Linz et al., 2019), while frequency and granularity discriminate AD (Ferrante et al., 2023). Accordingly, MCI semantic search may favor easily retrievable words, marked by strong use-based consolidation and conceptual vagueness. The robustness of frequency and granularity in both MCI and recent AD fluency work (Ferrante et al., 2023) suggests a shared semantic-memory phenotype, warranting comparative research (Balogh et al., 2023).

Additional insights were obtained through brain-behavior correlations, particularly involving the semantic task. First, word frequency negatively correlated with the gray matter volume of frontal, insular, and limbic regions, mirroring findings from AD3 (Caro et al., 2026), and suggesting that the lower the integrity of these regions, the greater the tendency to favor more entrenched (less demanding) items during semantic memory search. Second, granularity was positively associated to volume of the left superior frontal gyrus, likely reflecting this region’s sensitivity to the level of abstraction of words (Harpaintner et al., 2020). Third, semantic distance positively correlated with the volume of left ventral temporal regions, replicating association between this variable and the left posterior inferior temporal volume in CUIs (Liu et al., 2021) as well as neurofunctional signatures of semantic memory in other clinical populations (Toro-Herńandez et al., 2024). Given that no correlations emerged between top features and functional connectivity, these word properties seem specifically relevant as neuroanatomical predictors in MCI.

Finally, granularity in the semantic task was negatively associated with plasma pTau217 levels. Previous MCI research has linked this biomarker to linguistic outcomes such as semantic intrusion errors during word-list learning and animal fluency, suggesting that AD pathology may affect access to fine-grained lexico-semantic information (Lohi et al., 2025; Curiel et al., 2025). However, this result should be considered exploratory, as the pTau217 association modest, borderline significant, based on a small subsample. Replication in larger, biomarker-characterized cohorts is therefore needed (Palmqvist et al., 2024; Palmqvist et al., 2025; Barthélemy et al., 2024).

Our findings have clinical implications. Traditional verbal fluency measures rely on valid response counts, which miss specific vocabulary search patterns in MCI. By identifying the word types favored by patients, our approach helps characterize semantic memory profiles and refine syndrome phenotyping. Its fully automated pipeline also reduces limitations of subjective scoring (Gauthier et al., 2006) and supports integration into clinician-friendly apps (Garcia et al., 2024; García et al., 2025), key steps for clinical adoption. These metrics appear to match longer examiner-based tests in MCI detection (Caro et al., 2026), highlighting their time efficiency. Their performance in participants aged ≈77 years, with relatively low education, is also relevant given the difficulty of distinguishing MCI from non-pathological aging-related decline after age 75 (García, 2026). Overall, fine-grained digital fluency metrics may help address the need for scalable MCI markers (Whelan et al., 2022).

Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. First, although our sample size surpasses those from similar studies (Clark et al., 2014), replications should be conducted with more participants. Second, due to the demographics of the GERO cohort (Slachevsky et al., 2020), our sample was mainly female. It would be useful to test whether our findings hold in sex-balanced samples. Third, we lacked longitudinal data to examine whether the present metrics can anticipate conversion to dementia –a vital question to be addressed in future works. Fourth, since biomarker cut-offs are absent for the Chilean population, and those from other regions are inadequate, relevant comparisons were made at the group level, as no validated population-specific thresholds exist for plasma AT (N) biomarkers in Latin America and inter-assay variability limits cross-population generalization (García et al., 2026, García et al., 2025). Once local biomarker norms are available, future works could explore more precise associations with fluency features. Finally, our approach should be tested across typologically different languages to meet the recent call for cross-linguistic equity in the field (García et al., 2023; García et al., 2026; García et al., 2025).

Conclusion

Overall, current findings suggest that response property analysis can expand the utility of fluency tasks for MCI assessments. Specific features can discriminate persons with MCI from CUIs, matching standard measures, mapping onto relevant neuroanatomical patterns, and exhibiting potential to capture biomarker concentrations. Further work along these lines could enhance our understanding of semantic memory dysfunctions in the elderly, while revealing novel, scalable markers of MCI.
