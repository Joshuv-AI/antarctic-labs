Perioral myoclonia with absences in children: a case report and literature review
Authors: Mei Jin, Fan Yang, Yi Qu, Yaning Sun, Jia Gao, Xiaojie Yang, Suzhen Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A retrospective analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical characteristics, diagnostic challenges, and management strategies in a child with perioral myoclonia with absences (PMA). The case involved a 10-year-old boy with a six-month history of intermittent seizure episodes, which had worsened over the previous month. Clinical manifestations included transient blank stares, cessation of movement, unresponsiveness to verbal cues, and rhythmic twitching at the corners of the mouth, without limb rigidity or urinary/fecal incontinence. The duration of the seizures ranged from 10 to 15 s. Video electroencephalography (VEEG) showed bilateral synchronous and symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges persisting for 10–15 s, with rhythmic twitching captured on perioral EMG. The patient became seizure-free for 8 months only with 1000 mg daily of valproate after 2 weeks treatment. PMA is an idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome characterized by typical absence seizures with perioral myoclonia. VEEG monitoring is crucial for definitive diagnosis. For children with typical absence seizures accompanied by perioral twitching, perioral EMG should be performed alongside routine deltoid EMG to clarify seizure type. PMA might be classified either as a special subtype of typical absence or as an independent seizure type. Paying close attention to the special phenomenon will help to clarify the clinical spectrum of this epileptic disorder.

Introduction

Perioral myoclonia with absences (PMA) was first described as an epileptic syndrome by Panayiotopoulos et al. in 1994 [1]. This condition is characterized by rhythmic contractions of the perioral muscles, accompanied by frequent absence seizures. Notably, PMA is yet to be confirmed in the most recent 2022 classification of epileptic syndromes established by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) [2]. The incidence of PMA is low, and there have been limited reports globally. In this study, we collected clinical data from a child diagnosed with epilepsy who exhibited perioral rhythmic contractions alongside absence seizures. By analyzing the features of this condition and reviewing the relevant literatures, we aimed to summarize the latest research in PMA to enhance recognition and deepen the understanding of this condition.

Case report

A 10-year-old boy presented with a six-month history of intermittent seizure episodes, which had worsened over the previous month. Six months earlier, he had experienced his first episode without an identifiable cause, the event was characterized by transient blank stares, cessation of movement, unresponsiveness to verbal cues, and rhythmic twitching at the corners of the mouth, without limb rigidity or urinary/fecal incontinence. The duration of the seizures ranged from 10 to 15 s. Initially, seizures occurred once weekly. However, over the past month, their frequency increased to 3–4 times daily. All episodes transpired while the patient remained conscious and exhibited normal psychomotor function during interictal periods. The patient denied any psychiatric symptoms or cognitive impairment between episodes. His past medical and family histories were unremarkable.

On admission, the physical examination results were unexceptional. Laboratory evaluations, including peripheral blood cells and thyroid function test results, were normal. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed lymphocytic pleocytosis (5 × 10^6/L) with normal protein concentration. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, including T2/FLAIR sequences, revealed no abnormalities.

Video-electroencephalogram (VEEG) monitoring captured numerous clinical episodes consistent with his presentation. During these events, the patient exhibited transient blank stares, cessation of movement, unresponsiveness to verbal cues, and rhythmic twitching at the corner of the mouth. The duration of each episode was approximately 15s before resolving spontaneously. The background VEEG demonstrated normal activity recorded with eyes closed, with a 9–10 Hz alpha rhythm observed in the bioccipital regions (Fig. 1). Interictal VEEG revealed a generalized 2.5- to 3-Hz spike-wave discharges (Fig. 2). Ictal VEEG showed bilateral synchronous and symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges persisting for 10–15 s, with rhythmic twitching captured on perioral EMG (Fig. 3). Hyperventilation and intermittent photic stimulation under 20- to 30-Hz photic frequency could induce discharges with clinical manifestations.

Treatment and follow-up: The patient was diagnosed with PMA. Oral administration of sodium valproate commenced as treatment intervention. There was no epileptic discharge in the VEEG following 3 months of treatment, and the patient became seizure-free for 8 months only with 1000 mg daily of valproate.

Literature review

A systematic search of PubMed (including MEDLINE) was conducted for human studies published in English up to August, 2025, using the keywords “[Perioral myoclonia] AND [Absence seizures]”. Relevant reference lists were also reviewed for additional articles. Summarize the demographic data, family history, age at seizure onset, classification of seizure, antiseizure medicines, auxiliary examinations (including VEEG and CT/MRI scans), as well as the outcomes reported in the literature.

Our literature review identified 30 cases of PMA [3–17]. Analysis of these cases, including the present one, showed that 19 patients (61.3%) were males; the onset predominantly occurred during childhood, at a median age of 10 (range: 7–15) years. Multiple seizure types were observed: generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS), typical absences, focal motor seizures, and myoclonic seizures. Of these, GTCS was identified as the most common type, two patients (6.5%) experienced absence status epilepticus. Ten patients (32.3%) had a history of febrile seizures, as well as nine patients (29%) reported having a family history of epilepsy. Cranial imaging findings were normal in all instances examined. VEEG recordings were conducted for all 31 patients (100%); background activity was predominantly normal, whereas interictal VEEG revealed bursts of bifrontal dominant or generalized spike-wave complexes. Ictal EEG demonstrated bilateral synchronous symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges lasting for several seconds, often provoked by hyperventilation and intermittent photic stimulation under 20- to 30-Hz photic frequency, and synchronized EMG indicated a one-to-one correspondence between the spike components and rhythmic perioral myoclonic jerks. Photosensitivity was noted in four patients (12.9%), whereas seven patients (22.6%) were initially misdiagnosed as having focal motor epilepsy, which was exacerbated after treatment with oxcarbazepine (OXC) or carbamazepine (CBZ). Among the cases studied, 13 patients (41.9%) received monotherapy with anti-seizure medications (ASMs), whereas 18 (58.1%) were treated with a combination of two to three ASMs. Notably, 25 patients (80.6%) achieved seizure-free, whereas six patients (19.4%) experienced inadequate control and continued to suffer from persistent seizures of varying severity.The clinical features of these patients are summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

PMA is a rare idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome characterized by specific features, such as an age of onset ranging from 2 to 13 years, with an average of approximately 10 years, and no significant gender bias in incidence rates. Patients frequently exhibit typical absence seizures, accompanied by rhythmic clonic movements involving the perioral muscles. The rhythmic contractions of the orbicularis oris and depressor anguli oris muscles result in indrawing of the corners of the mouth, and in severe instances, the involvement of the masticatory muscles may lead to jaw jerking. Patients with PMA account for less than 1% of individuals diagnosed with typical absence epilepsy, and approximately two-thirds have a family history of epilepsy. Most patients demonstrate normal intelligence levels, and neurological examinations and neuroimaging findings typically reveal no specific abnormalities [1].

Clinically, approximately one-fifth of PMA cases are prone to misdiagnosis as oral automatisms or focal motor epilepsy [18]. Clinical presentations and/or EEG findings indicating asymmetric clonic seizures and/or unilateral EEG discharges are the primary contributors to this misdiagnosis. Studies investigating the factors leading to misdiagnosis within idiopathic generalized epilepsies have identified that rhythmic myoclonic jerks are often erroneously interpreted as partial motor seizures; this includes instances related to PMA itself. The likelihood of misdiagnosis increases when motor symptoms, particularly localized movements such as those involving perioral muscle activity, are prominent during absence seizures; this risk is further heightened if the interictal EEG reveals focal abnormalities [19]. Additionally, Panayiotopoulos et al. noted that impaired consciousness at the onset of PMA can be challenging to recognize, which further complicates accurate diagnosis and contributes significantly to its misclassification as partial epilepsy [1]. The differential diagnosis of PMA versus focal motor epilepsy are as follows: First, PMA typically presents with absence seizures accompanied by symmetric or asymmetric movements of the orbicularis oris muscle. Ictal EEG findings reveal generalized, synchronous 3-Hz spike-wave complexes, occasionally exhibiting higher amplitudes over anterior (frontopolar/frontal) regions; these are not discharges of partial origin. Notably, absence status epilepticus does not occur in patients with focal motor epilepsy. In contrast, absence status epilepticus occurs in approximately 6.5% of patients with PMA. VEEG monitoring combined with simultaneous perioral EMG recording is essential for the accurate diagnosis of PMA. Precise recording and analysis of interictal and ictal EEG discharge frequency and location, along with synchronized EMG parameters, significantly reduces the rate of misdiagnosis. If PMA is incorrectly diagnosed as focal epilepsy, for instance, due to asymmetric perioral jerking or misinterpretation of status as epilepsia partialis continua, and is subsequently treated with sodium channel-blocking ASMs, such as CBZ or OXC, clinical seizures may be exacerbated. Therefore, accurate diagnosis is crucial for implementing appropriate therapeutic interventions.

Several studies have shown discrepancies in the association between PMA and photosensitivity. In the present study, the statistical analysis indicated that 12.9% of patients with PMA exhibited photosensitivity. The underlying mechanism can be analyzed as follows: the baseline excitability of the cerebral cortex, particularly within the visual cortex, is abnormally elevated; specific photic stimuli (especially at certain frequencies, such as 15–20 Hz) can drive neurons in the visual cortex to generate abnormally intense and highly synchronized electrical activity. These abnormal discharges rapidly propagate through rich intrinsic neural connections within the cerebral cortex via corticocortical fibers, which activate specific regions of the motor cortex responsible for controlling particular muscle groups, triggering sudden paroxysmal firing of neurons in these regions. Thus, clinical seizures are induced.

Monotherapy with sodium valproate (VPA) can be used to treat PMA. Alternatively, VPA can be combined with ethosuximide, low-dose lamotrigine, or clonazepam. Administration of midazolam or diazepam can terminate the episode of status epilepticus [7]. Some studies have indicated that levetiracetam may eliminate GTCS but does not suppress perioral myoclonic jerks [8]. The present study described a child with perioral rhythmic twitching accompanied by typical absence seizures. Interictal EEG showed 2.5-to 3-Hz bifrontal dominant or generalized spike-wave complexes. Ictal EEG revealed bilateral synchronous symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges persisting for 10–15 s. A one-to-one correspondence existed between the EEG discharges and perioral rhythmic twitching. Seizures were induced by hyperventilation and photic stimulation. GTCS and absence status epilepticus were absent. There was a good response to monotherapy with VPA. Future developments and long-term prognosis require further follow-up.

It is also difficult to differentiate PMA from other epileptic syndromes that feature typical absences seizures, including eyelid myoclonia with absences (EMA), myoclonic absence epilepsy (MAE), and neck myoclonia with absences (NMA). Typical absence often include clonic components, most frequently rhythmic eyelid blinking. When a child presents with episodic staring, unresponsiveness, and concurrent eyelid blinking, typical absence seizures with eyelid myoclonia should be considered and differentiated from EMA. Simultaneous EMG and EEG recordings of the eyelids are crucial. Typical absence with eyelid clonia is bilateral, synchronous, symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges on ictal EEG. Generalized 4-to 5-Hz spike-wave bursts on ictal EEG with a one-to-one correspondence between the spike component and eyelid myoclonic jerks on EMG suggest EMA. Marked photosensitivity and eye closure sensitivity are characteristic. Polytherapy with ASMs is often required and has a poor prognosis [20]. MAE is accompanied by rhythmic jerking of the upper and/or lower limbs. Ictal EEG shows bilateral synchronous symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges. Deltoid EMG reveals rhythmic muscle contractions corresponding to the jerks. It is often drug-resistant and associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Photosensitivity may be present in some patients [21, 22]. NMA is accompanied by rhythmic head nodding and, sometimes, head deviation. Ictal EEG shows bilateral synchronous symmetrical 3-Hz spike-wave discharges. Sternocleidomastoid EMG shows rhythmic muscle contractions, whereas the deltoid shows no significant EMG activity and is not yet classified as a distinct epileptic syndrome by the ILAE. NMA is often effectively controlled with VPA monotherapy or VPA combined with topiramate. This study has certain limitations. The findings may be challenging to generalize to other patient populations, particularly those with differing pathological or physiological conditions, thereby constraining the applicability of its clinical implications. To address the limitations inherent in a single case study, we propose the following approaches: first, by meticulously detailing each step and methodology employed in this research, enabling other researchers to replicate the study; concurrently, we will disclose all data and analytical processes to enhance the transparency of our results. Second, we recommend conducting a systematic review and comparison of similar cases.

In conclusion, early accurate diagnosis of PMA is essential, as it enables effective guidance of targeted therapeutic interventions and facilitates reliable prognostic assessment. First, PMA must be carefully differentiated from oral automatisms to prevent misdiagnosis as focal motor epilepsy - a distinction critical to avoiding inappropriate treatment with sodium channel-blocking ASMs, which may paradoxically exacerbate clinical seizures. Second, prognosis varies significantly across different epilepsy syndromes; therefore, simultaneous video-EEG monitoring is indispensable for accurate syndrome classification. In children presenting with typical absence seizures accompanied by perioral twitching, routine deltoid EMG should be complemented by perioral EMG to definitively characterize the ictal semiology. In the future, with more related studies, PMA may be classified either as a special subtype of typical absence or as an independent seizure type. Regardless of the classification, paying close attention to this special phenomenon will help clarify the clinical spectrum in patients with this attack.
