Efficient Adsorptive Removal of Methyl Violet Dye from Wastewater Using Functionalized Groundnut Shell Biochar: Application, Mechanism, Modeling, and Optimization Study

Md Asik Uzzaman Asa, Md Mahmudur Rahman, Jubayer Ahmed, Bijoy Chandra Ghos, Salah Knani, Mustafizur Rahman Naim, Reem Alreshidi, Md Ismail Hossain

Abstract

This investigation focuses on the abatement of methyl violet (MV) dye from industrial effluents using the deployment of functionalized biochar prepared from methanol‐extracted groundnut shell residue. The biochar was functionalized by 30% tartaric acid(C4H4O6) to boost the surface functionality and adsorption performance. It was determined by conducting SEM, FTIR, XRD, and ZP analysis that a porous structure was developed with high concentrations of oxygen‐carrying functional groups and favorable surface charge qualities. Batch adsorption studies were designed to evaluate the effect of influential parameters on the removal performance. The equilibrium behavior was successfully interpreted using several mathematical models. The maximum adsorption capacity was found ∼88.57 mg/g. The kinetic and thermodynamic evaluation revealed that both pseudo‐first‐order and pseudo‐second‐order models closely represent the experimental data, suggesting that the adsorption behavior arises from multiple interacting synergistic pathways rather than a single dominant mechanism, and the adsorption proceeds spontaneously, followed by an endothermic process. Additionally, the Box‐Behnken and experimental designs were used to show that the system is highly sensitive to the relationship between operational parameters. The results, when combined, reveal that the FGNB biochar derived from secondary waste biomass provides a possible solution to dye‐contaminated wastewater treatment that is economically feasible and eco‐friendly.
This study focused on the production and application of secondary waste, namely ground nutshell residual mass (after MeOH extraction)‐derived FGNB‐based multifunctional nanoadsorbents in industrial wastewater treatment technology. The FGNB nanosorbent would be a sustainable alternative to fossil‐based, hazardous ones for environmental protection and would facilitate a zero‐waste strategy. The synergistic mechanism and modeling could be beneficial for improved prediction and data interpretation.

Introduction

Rapid industrialization and urbanization have significantly increased the generation of industrial wastewater, posing serious challenges to global water sustainability. Industries such as textile, leather, paint, printing, cosmetics, and metal processing discharge large volumes of dye‐containing effluents, many of which are released into natural water bodies without adequate treatment, particularly in developing countries [] A major environmental concern arises from the widespread practice in many developing countries, including Bangladesh, where industrial effluents are often discharged directly into natural water bodies without adequate treatment. These discharges contaminate both groundwater and surface water systems, such as rivers, canals, lakes, ponds, and coastal regions. Reports from the Bangladesh Department of Environment indicate that tannery industries alone release approximately 21,600 m3 (5.7 million gallons) of hazardous waste into rivers daily. In addition, the Dhaka Water Supply and Sewerage Authority (WASA) has reported that nearly 12,000 m3 (420,000 cubic feet) of untreated wastewater is continuously discharged into water bodies surrounding industrial zones such as Tejgaon, Badda, and Mohakhali, with textile washing and dyeing operations identified as the primary contributors []. It was estimated that one textile industry alone generated a waste and sludge of up to 56 million tons annually and 0.5 million tons yearly []. Synthetic dyes and toxic pollutants that are contained in these wastewaters cause serious threats to human health and aquatic life. Over 10,000 unique dye compounds have been produced in the world, with an aggregate production of over 700 thousand tons per annum. These dyes find a lot of application in those industries, mainly in adding color to a variety of products [, ]. MV is a commonly used dye in these industrial sectors, which has poor fixation and hydrolyzes, leading to the release of high proportions into water bodies. Its abundance reduces penetration of light, obscures photosynthesis, interferes with metabolism in the water, leading to ecotoxicity [, ]. Moreover, synthetic dyes like MV are one of the most toxic xenobiotics [, ]; these compounds, once introduced into the human body, are likely to remain within the vital organs, and they never get removed [, ]. This carcinogenic dye is associated with severe chronic disorders, including neurotoxicity, cardiovascular complications, cytotoxicity, carcinogenicity, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, endocrine disruption, inhibition of hemoglobin synthesis, paralysis, and, in extreme cases, mortality []. Moreover, such environmental contamination directly conflicts with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG 6) [], which emphasizes the provision of clean water and adequate sanitation. The continued discharge of untreated pollutants contributes to the deterioration of safe drinking water sources and exacerbates the lack of proper sanitation facilities for the general population [, ]. So, it is significant that these dyes be eliminated in effluents before the effluents are released into water bodies as a measure of protection to the health of people and the ecosystems as well []. However, several wastewater treatment methods do exist like Physical treatment methods; including sedimentation and filtration, membrane filtration [, , ], coagulation–flocculation [] electrocoagulation/electro flotation [, ], Chemical treatment methods [], Biological treatment methods, Photocatalysis, Adsorption (e.g., activated carbon, biochar, nanomaterials) etc. which are used to purify real‐time effluence like MV dye; disintegrated by those industries []. Among the available wastewater treatment technologies, adsorption has emerged as a promising approach because of its cost‐effectiveness, simple operation, low energy consumption, and high pollutant removal efficiency []. In contrast, many conventional treatment methods suffer from inherent drawbacks [, ], including elevated operational and disposal costs, potential formation of toxic byproducts [], complex process optimization, high labor and energy demands, technological limitations, and the possibility of generating secondary pollutants [, , ]. This study has, therefore, followed the batch adsorption methodology to treat the huge industrial effluent for its ingenuity and low expenditure, fast processing and high removal efficiency, flexibility in operations, accurate kinetics, and isotherm studies [, ]. Although activated carbon commercially obtained is widely used in the batch process as an adsorbent, it is quite expensive; this limits its use on a large scale []. Consequently, more interest has been focused on the design of alternative adsorbents that are cheap, sustainable, and made of waste products. The main goal of such processes is to attain effective dye elimination in wastewater and minimize total treatment costs. Agro‐industrial residues represent an abundant and underutilized biomass resource that can be converted into efficient adsorbents for wastewater treatment. These resources may be transformed into useful bio‐adsorbents having significant adsorption capacity. These materials have also been successfully used as dye removers in several studies with rice husk‐derived charcoal [], mahogany bark charcoal [], and jute stick‐based charcoal [] among others. As an abundant agro‐industrial byproduct, groundnut shells are easily accessible during peanut processing and are generally a noxious byproduct or waste that is usually dumped or burned, causing environmental disposal problems []. This ground nutshell can be separated into two parts after extraction: one is the extracted part, and the other is the residual part. After extraction of the extracted part, which is generally an active material, it is widely accepted for pharmaceutical or biomedical uses. However, approximate 1% of the ground nutshell's total mass is really extracted; the rest (more than 99%) is regarded as waste, which is enormous on an industrial scale and has no discernible purpose. This discarded fraction, referred to as residual mass, primarily constitutes solid waste and poses significant health risks. Improper disposal of these residues may contribute to air pollution and has been associated with respiratory illnesses, including asthma and (COPD) pulmonary infections, and, in severe cases, lung cancer []. In addition to being detrimental to the economy, this huge number of residual parts disrupts biodiversity and, most significantly, produces secondary pollutants. In this work, using this waste in line, it is possible to create something valuable from this waste, which is consistent with the circular economy []. Hence, the proposed approach demonstrates the potential of converting methanol‐extracted groundnut shell residue into a value‐added adsorbent, thereby contributing to biomass waste valorization and sustainable wastewater treatment. Moreover, the material has a high content of lignocellulose, which adds to the occurrence of several surface functional groups, engaging hydroxyl (─OH), carbonyl (─C═O), and carboxyl (─COOH), which are important as active binding sites in the course of the biosorption process []. Additionally, it serves as a sustainable bio‐adsorbent that demonstrated high dye removal efficiency in the present study [, ]. Conversely, the methanol‐extracted residual groundnut shell, which retains abundant cellulose and other functional biopolymers, can be valorized as a promising precursor for the production of high‐performance adsorbents. This potential arises from its favorable physicochemical characteristics, robust thermomechanical stability, well‐defined morphology, and environmentally sustainable nature []. The valorization of agro‐industrial residues as adsorbents provides a practical and sustainable alternative to commercial adsorbents, reducing material costs while supporting wastewater treatment in resource‐limited regions []. Hence, the residual mass of groundnut shells can be an outstanding adsorbent that should be utilized in developing countries such as Bangladesh as a cheaper substitute. The adsorption systems are affected by many parameters during operation, which include the pH of the solution, the quantity of pollutant present in it, the dosage of adsorbent used, the contact time period, and the temperature []. The assessment of the comparative impact of these parameters and the determination of the most favorable conditions is vitally important in achieving the highest adsorption. This can be achieved through various methods. The traditional approach involves batch experiments, where each parameter is tested individually while others are held constant. Alternatively, adsorption performance can be assessed at selected parameter levels, with results analyzed statistically from a limited set of experiments []. The response surface methodology is an effective tool for evaluating the effects of multiple factors and optimizing their levels. It accounts for interactions between factors, making it a reliable statistical approach for analyzing wastewater treatment systems. Among the available optimization strategies, the Box–Behnken design represents an efficient statistical approach derived from a three‐level fractional factorial framework, widely used for modeling and process optimization []. This research involved the utilization of biochar produced using the remaining mass of ground nutshell, which was functionalized with 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) to increase its capacity to adsorb the organic dye mixture of MV. In this study, methanol‐extracted groundnut shell residue was converted into 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) acid‐functionalized biochar (FGNB) and evaluated as an adsorbent for methyl violet (MV) removal. Batch adsorption experiments and Box–Behnken response surface methodology were employed to investigate adsorption behavior and optimize the operating conditions. Although biochar and activated carbon have been widely investigated for dye adsorption, the application of methanol‐extracted groundnut shell residue, an abundant secondary agro‐industrial waste, remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to convert this residual biomass into 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) acid‐functionalized biochar for methyl violet (MV) adsorption while integrating adsorption experiments with Box–Behnken Design (BBD) for process optimization. Various objectives were established in this investigation. To start with, biochar was synthesized with a representative agro‐waste precursor, which is groundnut (Arachis hypogaea) shell. Second, the biochar acquired was chemically treated at 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) to alter the physicochemical, thermomechanical, and morphological characteristics. Third, the efficiency of adsorption of the modified material was determined, and an optimization of the adsorption process was made for the removal of MV in aqueous media (synthetic type). Also, the adsorption behavior was analyzed in the framework of different kinetic and isotherm models, such as the Langmuir, the Freundlich, the Temkin, intra‐particle diffusion, and the Elovich models, to have a deeper understanding of the governing processes. Lastly, the Box–Behnken experimental design was used to optimize the process conditions and evaluate the effect of interactions among the operational variables.

Experimental

Materials

Groundnut samples employed in this work were sourced from a local market in Rajshahi, Bangladesh (24°22′26′′N, 88°36′04′′E). The chemicals were used in their acquired state and were analytical grade or better. All aqueous solutions were made with ultrapure Milli‐Q water. High‐purity methanol (CH3OH (99.5%) was available from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany, and all experiments were conducted using materials of consistent quality. The MV dye (solid powder) has been obtained in Germany under E Merck Ltd. The other chemicals bought in this experiment were Sodium Hydroxide (∼99%) and Hydrochloric acid (37%), which are of high purity and sourced from BDH in England. Tartaric acid (C4H4O6) solid crystalline powder (molecular weight around 150.09 g/mol) with a purity of 99.5% was purchased from BDH in England. Whereas the Deionized (DI) water of high purity, along with distilled water, was obtained from the Biopolymers and Sustainable Environmental Research Laboratory, Bangladesh Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (BCSIR), Rajshahi.

Methods

Collection, Preparation, and MeOH Extraction of Groundnut Shell

Initially, the shells of the groundnuts harvested were selected by hand. The shells were then finely cut, cleaned well to get rid of the impurities, and they were dried under the sun (a matter of 2 h). The dry product was then milled using a mechanical mill to acquire homogeneous particles. Then the resulting coarse powder was placed in the chamber of the solvent extraction apparatus, namely, the Soxhlet instrument, for methanol extraction by following the proper protocol reported by and left to stand for around 14 h at around 60°C, whereas the solvent was methanol as well as the ratio of solid nutshell to MeOH solution was kept fixed around 1:5. Upon extraction, the extract was separated by conducting rotary evaporation meanwhile the solvents were also recovered as well as the residue was collected and was filtered through a Whatman filter paper (150 mm diameter). The filtrate was stored for later analysis, and the rest of the solid residue was also used as a precursor to the production of FGNB biochar. A schematic illustration of groundnut shell waste processing into functionalized biochar is demonstrated in Figure .

Synthesis of Raw Biochar From the Residual Mass of Ground Nutshell

The residual mass obtained from ground nutshell powder (after phytochemicals isolation via MeOH extraction) was first subjected to drying to eliminate any remaining moisture. The material was placed in a vacuum oven (ED‐115, Binder, Germany) and maintained at 105°C ± 1°C for approximately 2 h to ensure the final removal of moisture and other residual chemicals/fractions. After drying, the material was further processed using a mechanical grinder (mixture grinder, India) to obtain a fine and homogeneous powder. Subsequently, the finely ground powder was transferred into a crucible, covered with a lid to limit oxygen availability, and subjected to thermal treatment in a muffle furnace (L‐31C6, Germany). Thermal conversion of the biomass into biochar was performed under oxygen‐limited conditions to promote controlled carbonization. The solvent was evaporated under ambient laboratory conditions. This was maintained at approximately 600°C ± 2∘C until the weight of the residue material in the crucible did not increase any further, a process that in turn was timed at 6 h, which was recorded using a thermosensitive pyrometer. The combustion process was very strictly controlled and controlled in an outdoor system by not allowing a direct combustion process, and an open‐air heating process guaranteed a stable carbonization process and optimized the production of biochar. Next, the resulting biochar was collected from the muffle furnace and was transformed into a fine powder through hammer milling. A schematic representation of the preparation route for FGNB is provided in Figure .

Activation of Raw Biochar by Tartaric Acid

The obtained biochar was subsequently functionalized using a 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) solution at a mass‐to‐volume ratio of 1:20 by using a magnetic stirrer at 120°C with continuous stirring at 1500 rpm for 4 h (shown in Figure ).

Biochar prepared from ground nutshell is subjected to 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) functionalization, as it is worth noting that the incorporated tartrate (C4H4O−) groups, abundant with lone pair electrons, play a key role in boosting adsorption performance by forming coordination bonds between the adsorbate and adsorbents through the chemisorption procedure. After completion of the reaction, the obtained product was allowed to cool to room temperature under dark conditions. After cooling to ambient temperature, the material was separated by filtration using Whatman filter paper and rinsed thoroughly with a freshly prepared 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) solution to eliminate residual impurities. Then dried in an electric oven and preserved in a moisture‐proof screw‐cap Duran bottle for further characterization and application in crude industrial wastewater treatment for the selective separation of the targeted MV dye molecules via adsorption technology.

Box–Behnken Design

Box–Behnken Design (BBD) is a statistical experimental design (a response surface methodology, RSM) that employs optimization of process variables and interaction effects among the responses of process variables. It is especially effective in the estimation of second‐order (quadratic) models without the need to have a complete factorial experimental design. BBD uses a mixed level of variables with middle and extreme levels, not involving experiments at extreme corners of the experiment; it is safer and more feasible in chemical and environmental processes []. The Box–Behnken design (BBD) is commonly utilized as an efficient statistical tool for optimizing important adsorption variables such as contact time, adsorbent dosage, solution pH, and initial dye concentration, while reducing the number of required experimental trials. This statistical approach facilitates the development of a second‐order (quadratic) predictive model that describes the relationship between independent variables and the response, such as adsorption capacity or removal efficiency. In addition, BBD enables the identification of significant factors and their interactions through analysis of variance (ANOVA), thereby providing a reliable framework for process optimization and a deeper understanding of system behavior []. Box–Behnken Design was used in the present work to statistically investigate and enhance the adsorptive removal of MV dye in aqueous medium using FGNB. The optimization model incorporated three main independent variables, namely, adsorbent dosage (B: 0.1–0.3 g/100 mL), initial dye concentration (C: 25–40 mg/L), fixed solution volume (100 mL), contact time (A: 30–150 min), and pH (5.0). Design‐Expert Software (Version 11.0, Stat‐Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA) was used to analyze the data and test the importance of the linear, interaction, and quadratic effects of the variables (−1 (low), 0 (center), +1 (high)) to the adsorption process. A 100 mL of MV solution of desired concentration (25 mg/L to 40 mg/L pH 5) was transferred to a 250 mL polyethylene bottle, and 0.1 g to 0.3 g of FGNB was added to the solution, as per each run. The mixtures were then spun by an orbital shaker (250 rpm) at the desired contact time (30 min to 150 min) at 45 to adsorb the dye and afterwards, to establish the efficiency of dye removal (mg/g), the suspension was filtered, and measures of the filtrates were taken in a UV–vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 520 nm. The experimental data were also analyzed with the help of some nonlinear regression method whereby a second‐order polynomial model Equation was adjusted on the responses (observed) to determine the influential model terms and provide the estimate of the most desirable conditions. Characterization Methods

FTIR‐ATR Analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared‐Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR‐FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the surface functional groups present in the FGNB prepared from methanol (CH3OH)‐extracted groundnut shell residue. FTIR‐ATR is a strong surface‐sensitive method, which gives qualitative data on the chemical composition and functional moieties involved in the adsorption processes []. FTIR is applied to provide a definitive molecular fingerprint of surface properties of a chemical bond, such as hydroxyl (─OH), carbonyl (C═O), aromatic (C═C) features, by monitoring the infrared radiation absorption of the chemical bonds of the surface at characteristic wavenumbers. These functional groups may also provide functional sites of hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, pi‐pi interactions, and complexation with dye molecules, including the MV molecule. The variation of peak positions or intensities observed before and after adsorption can provide information about the reactions of the biochar surface with the adsorbate. The positions of functional groups in the adsorption process can be clarified by these changes [, ]. Comparative analysis before and after dye adsorption typically shows noticeable shifts or changes in peak intensity, particularly in the ─OH, C═O, and aromatic regions, confirming the involvement of these functional groups in adsorption. These observations indicate that several interaction pathways, such as electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and π‐π stacking interactions between the adsorbent surface and dye molecules, contribute to the adsorption of MV onto the biochar surface. Overall, FTIR‐ATR analysis confirms that the biochar possesses a rich array of oxygenated and aromatic functional groups, which significantly enhance its adsorption capability by facilitating strong interactions with dye molecules. Measurements of the sample at every point of the project were made using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR‐ATR). It was a PerkinElmer L1600300 Spectrum (Serial numbers 115, 061) of Llantrisant, UK. The samples were directly placed onto the diamond ATR crystal without the use of potassium bromide (KBr) pellet preparation. Isopropyl alcohol was used to wash the prism entirely before any measures were taken. One scan was done on each specimen at least three times; spectra were recorded at a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 to a resolution of approximately 5 cm−1as measured by the Deuterated Triglycine Sulfate (DTGS) detector []. For a more detailed evaluation of adsorption characteristics and structural features, the obtained spectra were processed and interpreted using PerkinElmer Spectrum IR software (version 10.6.2).

XRD Analysis

The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted to determine the crystalline structure and composition of the prepared FGNB in terms of phase. During a normal XRD process, the sample of the FGNB is finely powdered so that it is even and is then subjected to monochromatic X‐rays. The diffracted X‐rays are measured as a dependence on the diffraction angle (2θ) and form a diffraction pattern that reflects the structure of the atoms in the material being studied. The received peaks are observed based on their positions, intensities, and widths to determine the crystalline phases and the level of structural order. The XRD pattern of FGNB usually displays a broader diffraction peak at 2θ = 20 − 30°, which is typical of amorphous carbon structures that develop in the process of disordered placement of aromatic layers in the pyrolysis process. This amorphous form is favorable to adsorption practices in that it is linked to increased surface heterogeneity and more active sites. Besides the broad hump, sharp peaks can be observed because of the existence of crystalline mineral phases, silica or calcite that were products of the inorganic constituents of the biomass. The intensity of these peaks is usually lowered by means of chemical modification or acid treatment, which is evidence that mineral impurities have been removed and that the carbonaceous structure is improved. Zeta Potential Analysis

Zeta potential analysis was conducted to evaluate the surface charge characteristics and colloidal stability of the FGNB derived from methanol (CH3OH)‐extracted groundnut shell residue. Zeta potential represents the electrical potential at the slipping plane of particles dispersed in a liquid medium and is a critical parameter for understanding electrostatic interactions between adsorbent surfaces and charged pollutants [, ]. Zeta potential, which is the electrokinetic potential located at the slipping plane of a colloidal particle, is a good indicator of the net electrical charge carried by the particle surface. Its determination is carried out routinely by electrophoretic light scattering, and the corresponding values are expressed in millivolts (mV). As a result, zeta potential serves as a key indicator for evaluating the stability of colloidal systems as well as the surface charge characteristics of adsorbent materials in aqueous media. A high absolute zeta potential value, regardless of whether it is positive or negative, generates strong electrostatic repulsive forces between particles, thereby promoting dispersion stability; conversely, when the values of zeta potential are close to zero, the system will be prone to aggregation. In adsorption studies, the zeta potential is used to define the change of surface charge with pH and functionalization, which has a direct effect on the interaction between adsorbent and adsorbate species. In adsorption studies, zeta potential is an important tool to understand the surface charge of the adsorbent. By graphically representing the net surface charge over a continuum of pH values, researchers can gain a strong predictive framework for the electrostatic companionship between the adsorbent and charged pollutants, such as the MV cation, over a range of solution conditions. The pH value at which the zeta potential disappears is called the isoelectric point, which is the exact condition in which the surface has no net charge. Consequently, there are marked transitions in adsorption efficiency close to this critical pH corresponding to a change from attractive to repulsive interfacial forces. Thus, the zeta potential data provide a consistent explanation for the observed pH‐dependent variations in the adsorption capacity and directly couple surface charge polarity with speciation and ionic state of the dye.

Optical Microscopic Analysis

The surface morphology and structure of the FGNB made using methanol (CH3OH) extracted residues of the groundnut shell were examined with the help of optical microscopy. Even though optical microscopy offers a comparatively lower resolution than the electron microscopy method, it can be used as a convenient method to undertake a first‐line examination of the particle distribution, surface heterogeneity, and macro‐ and meso‐scale pore characteristics []. The optical micrographs show that the biochar has a highly heterogeneous and irregular morphology, which is filled with fragmented structures, uneven particle dispersibility, and discrete pore‐like vacuums. The black spots on the micrograph refer to compact carbonaceous structures, and the light spots refer to porous structures or less compact structures. Such characteristics imply that the extraction and functionalization processes of methanol drastically altered the initial lignocellulosic framework of the groundnut shell, resulting in the appearance of a rough and porous surface. Such morphological modifications are crucial for adsorption applications, as they enhance the accessibility of active sites and promote effective diffusion of adsorbate molecules into the internal pore network. The observed irregular and interconnected pore structures indicate the development of a hierarchical architecture, which facilitates both surface adsorption and intra‐particle diffusion mechanisms [, ]. Additionally, the removal of extractives during CH3OH treatment likely increased surface exposure and improved the availability of functional groups responsible for adsorption. In addition, optical microscopy is non‐destructive, quick, and easy, thus making it appropriate for the preliminary morphological examination of biomass‐derived biochar adsorbents. It gives an initial description of the shape, size distribution, roughness on the surfaces, and heterogeneity of the adsorbent particle before and after adsorption. These characteristics impinge on the accessibility of active sites and the dynamics of adsorption. The optical images can be used to show macroscopic variation in color, surface coverage, and agglomeration when adsorbates like dyes bind to adsorbent surfaces and thus assist in other quantitative analyses. Optical microscopy is used to supplement other techniques like FESEM and TEM by providing a wider view and allowing rapid screening of samples before proceeding to more detailed analysis. FESEM Analysis

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) is an advanced surface‐imaging technique that employs a finely focused, high‐energy electron beam to generate high‐resolution micrographs of material morphology and microstructural features. With the use of FESEM, the surface texture, particle morphology, pore distribution, and surface heterogeneity can be observed at much higher magnifications than traditional optical microscopy, and, therefore, this technique is essential to characterize adsorbent materials at both the micro‐ and nanoscale. FESEM analysis is a common procedure that is undertaken in the field of adsorption research to evaluate the morphological properties that dictate the performance of an adsorbent. The roughness of the surface, the network characteristics of the pores, and the presence of active sites, which are revealed by the SEM images, have a significant impact on the dynamics of interactions between the veneer of an adsorbent and the adsorbate molecules that could be dyes or heavy metal ions. Compared to FESEM micrographs of the surface before and after the adsorption technique, scientists can identify alterations in surface texture that can be attributed to the binding of pollutants, identify the effectiveness of functionalization or activation steps taken on the adsorbent, and find relationships between the observed surface features and the efficiency of the adsorption process, as well as to the mechanistic processes. FESEM, therefore, offers important information on the physical properties of adsorbent materials that form the basis of the adsorption behavior of adsorbent materials in wastewater treatment processes []. This method facilitated extensive visualization of the roughening of the surface, diameter of pores and voids, and decorated particles, agglomeration behavior, coarse wrinkles, cracks, and morphological homogeneity []. Furthermore, quantitative assessments of particles or pore size and their distribution can be determined using advanced image‐processing software such as ImageJ and Microsoft Excel.

UV–Vis‐NIR Spectroscopic Analysis

The optical characteristics and electronic transitions of the obtained FGNB were analyzed with UV‐NIR spectroscopy, since this method offers useful information about the structure and surface of carbon‐based material. The UV–vis‐NIR spectrum usually has absorption characteristics at the ultraviolet, visible, and near‐infrared wavelengths, which have been correlated to the various electronic transitions in the material. The absorption bands that are observed in the UV wavelength (200–400 nm) are usually interrelated with π‐π* transformations of aromatic C═C bonds found within the graphitic domains of the biochar structure. These movements are evidence of the existence of conjugated systems that are formed because of the carbonization process. Also, weaker absorption characteristics at this region can be attributed to π‐π* transitions that are caused by the lone pair electrons of oxygen‐encompassing functional groups like carbonyl (C═O) and hydroxyl (─OH) groups []. The absorption in the visible region (400–700 nm) is generally linked to the existence of the surface functional groups, structural defects, and chromophore moieties. The functional groups introduced during chemical treatment, for example, 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) modification, can contribute to the light absorption in this range because of the higher surface heterogeneity and the appearance of other electronic states. Investigation of the Equilibrium Mechanism of Adsorption

Kinetic analysis of adsorption and adsorption isotherm behavior are fundamental in determining the performance of biochar‐based adsorption systems because the two parameters can help in elucidating the mechanisms underlying adsorption as well as the processes that could modify the rate []. Biosorption kinetics is mainly regulated by mass transfer processes that take place at the solid‐liquid interface, where solute transport resistance can play a large role in defining the overall rate of adsorption. In general, there are several steps in the adsorption of dissolved contaminants on solid adsorbents []. Initially, the pollutant species migrate from the bulk solution toward the external boundary layer surrounding the adsorbent particles, a step commonly referred to as film diffusion. Later, the adsorbate molecules move to the external surface of the adsorbent. At the instant step, the intra‐particle diffusion occurs, in which the pollutants enter the internal pore structure of the adsorbent. In the final stage, adsorption occurs at the internal active sites of the adsorbent, where the interaction may proceed via weak physical forces or through the formation of chemical bonds, depending on the characteristics of both the adsorbent and the adsorbate. Adsorption Thermodynamics

Thermodynamic analysis is important in determining whether the adsorption technique is feasible and how the approach takes place. The key thermodynamic parameters, namely Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°), enthalpy change (ΔH°), and entropy change (ΔS°), were evaluated to assess the spontaneity and energetic characteristics of the adsorption system. Negative values of ΔG° signify that the adsorption occurs spontaneously, whereas positive values suggest that the process is thermodynamically unfavorable under the given conditions. The enthalpy change (ΔH°) provides insight into the nature of the adsorption process. A positive ΔH° value signifies an endothermic process, where adsorption is favored at higher temperatures, whereas a negative ΔH° suggests an exothermic process. The magnitude of ΔH° provides insight into the nature of the adsorption process; values below approximately 40 kJ/mol are generally indicative of physisorption, whereas higher values suggest the involvement of chemisorption mechanisms []. The entropy change (ΔS°) indicates the extent of randomness at the solid‐solution interface during adsorption. A positive ΔS° value signifies the growth of randomness and disorder, usually because of the movement of water molecules and the augmented mobility of adsorbate species at the interface. Conversely, a negative ΔS° suggests a decrease in randomness, indicating a more ordered system after adsorption. All in all, the thermodynamic parameters give detailed information on the adsorption process, such as its spontaneity, heat exchange, and structural modification at the interface. Such parameters are necessary in the determination of the feasibility and mechanism of adsorption, as well as optimization of operational conditions in practice.

Results and discussions

Effect of Influential Parameters

Effect of Temperature on MV Dye Adsorption

Figure illustrates the influence of temperature on the adsorption capacity of MV dye onto the prepared FGNB. The results indicate a consistent increase in adsorption capacity as the temperature rises from 25°C to 65°C, indicating that elevated temperatures favor the adsorption technique. The given enhancement could be attributed to the fact that the higher the temperature of dye molecules, the higher the level of kinetic energy, the faster their movement, and the higher the mass transfer between the bulk phase and those adsorption sites that are available to them. Additionally, higher temperatures may contribute to the activation of previously inaccessible adsorption sites and the expansion of pore structures, thereby improving the overall adsorption performance []. Its optimum adsorption efficiency is found at about 55°C, indicating that the adsorbent surface and dye molecules interact best at this temperature. This has most often been linked to an endothermic adsorption reaction, in which heating enhances the interactions between the adsorbates and the adsorbent by increasing the strength of the adsorbate‐adsorbent interactions, as well as by enhancing the affinity of the FGNB surface toward the dye molecules. Nevertheless, there is a minor loss in adsorption capacity at high temperatures (e.g., 65°C, and this could be attributed to the erosion of adsorption forces or the potential erosion of surfaces at high temperature. Thermal agitation may cause the adsorbent surface to interact with the dye molecules unintentionally, causing a decrease in adsorption efficiency at very high temperatures. The identical pattern is observed in past researchers, where the adsorption capacity reaches a maximum temperature after which it decreases because of the instability of the adsorbed species [, ]. The overall findings demonstrate that temperature exerts a pronounced influence on the adsorption process. Any subsequent reduction or plateau observed at elevated temperatures suggests the presence of limiting factors such as weakened adsorbate–adsorbent interactions or partial desorption. This behavior underscores the necessity of identifying an optimal temperature range to ensure maximum adsorption efficiency and process stability.

Effect of Contact Time on MV Dye Adsorption

Figure depicts how contact time influences the adsorption capacity of the MV to the FGNB, evidently indicating a highly time‐sensitive adsorption process. The capacity to adsorb is found to increase gradually with a rise in contact time, which implies that the effectiveness of uptake improves with time between the adsorbate and adsorbent. During the initial stage, the rapid uptake of dye can be attributed to the strong concentration gradient between the bulk solution and the adsorbent surface, along with the abundant availability of vacant active sites. It is the first stage, which is characterized by an increased rate of mass movement and rapid movement of dye molecules toward the exterior surface of the biochar. The richness of the accessible binding sites increases adsorption immediately; thus, adsorption capacity increases sharply at the earliest stages of the process. The adsorption rate is slowing down with the increasing contact time (60–150 min), suggesting that adsorption shifts toward surface adsorption to intra‐particle diffusion. At this podium, the dye molecules will enter further into the pores of the biochar, and the availability of active sites will start to reduce because of successive occupancy. The maximum adsorption capacity occurs at approximately 150 min, indicating that at this point, equilibrium is overshadowed. On top of this optimal contact time (e.g., 180 min), there is a slight reduction or level off in adsorption capacity. Such an action can be explained by the saturation of active locations and the formation of a dynamic balance between adsorption and desorption processes. Moreover, there is a possibility of small but significant desorption or redistribution of dye molecules in the adsorbent surface because of a longer contact period. In general, the detected trend denotes a classic adsorption process, which involves an initial fast stage and a gradual advance to the equilibrium. The findings reveal that contact time is a very important parameter that can affect adsorption efficiency and that there must be an optimum time that can result in the maximum dye removal. This is comparable to the earlier research on the dye adsorption concerning biochar and other carbon adsorbents [, ]. At our work, the maximum efficiency of MV by FGNB was 52.18 mg/g.

Effect of Initial Dye Concentration

The earliest dye concentration is a critical parameter influencing adsorption performance []. Figure reveals that the initial concentration of MV has a strong influence on the adsorption capacity of the dye. One can see a growing tendency as the concentration of the dye is increased, and when the concentration is approximately 75 mg/L, the adsorption is the maximum one can arrive at. This behavior can be explained by the enhancement of the mass transfer driving force at higher concentrations. The greater the difference in concentration between the solution phase and the adsorbent surface, the better the mass transfer, and thus, dye molecules find their way to the available active sites and efficiently occupy them. Consequently, a greater number of adsorption sites become occupied, leading to improved adsorption capacity within this concentration range []. At a low starting concentration, the amount of dye molecules in solution is small in comparison with the number of obtainable adsorption sites and thus optimum coverage is not achieved on the adsorbent surface. The higher the concentration, the higher the number of dye molecules that will be available to react with the active sites and thus occupy the sites, leading to increased adsorption efficiency. However, beyond the optimum concentration (around 75 mg/L), a decline in adsorption capacity is observed at intermediate levels (e.g., 100 mg/L). The reduction in adsorption performance can be explained by the progressive occupation of available binding sites, along with increased competition among dye molecules for the finite number of active sites on the biochar surface. At higher concentrations (e.g., 125 mg/L), a softened increase in adsorption capacity is again perceived, which may be due to multilayer adsorption or enhanced interactions between dye molecules and the porous structure of the biochar. This behavior suggests that while surface saturation limits adsorption at intermediate concentrations, additional mechanisms such as pore filling or aggregation of dye molecules may contribute to adsorption at higher concentrations. Overall, the observed trend highlights the significant role of initial dye concentration in governing adsorption performance. The increase in adsorption capacity at lower to moderate concentrations indicates efficient utilization of active sites, whereas the variations at higher concentrations reflect the balance between site availability and mass transfer limitations. The obtained results are in agreement with earlier reports on dye removal employing carbon‐derived adsorbent materials [, ].

Effect of Adsorbent Dosage

One of the operational parameters is adsorbent dosage, which plays a significant part in ascertaining the structural properties of modified biochar and its pollutant disposal capacity. As the dosage of the adsorbent is increased, the removal is expected to improve because of increased surface area and active binding sites. Saturation will, however, not have a proportional increase in the removal efficiency when the adsorbent is added beyond an optimal dosage. Such plateau behavior is explained by the surplus of adsorption sites compared to the quantity of the pollution, combined with the rise in surface area, resulting in underutilization of active centers []. The reason why Ho et al. were able to arrive at the above conclusion is that they thought the greater the amount of biochar that was used, the greater the attainability of the active sites []. As the adsorbent dosage increases, the adsorption capacity initially shows a corresponding enhancement; however, this trend eventually approaches a plateau as the available active sites become saturated. Beyond this point, additional adsorbent does not significantly contribute to further adsorption, indicating that the system has reached its maximum adsorption capacity. However, on the other the rate of removal goes down. A suitable amount of adsorbent dose must be chosen to economize on costs, particularly in large‐scale applications []. The consequence of the adsorbent dosage on the adsorption performance of MV dye is depicted in Figure . Per the results, there is an evident increase in adsorption capacity within the adsorbent dosage range, 30 to 120 mg. Observing the results, a clear increment in adsorption capacity can be traced; however, there is a tendency toward stabilization at high. This rise in the efficiency of the adsorption process at the beginning may be validated by the increased number of obtainable surface‐acting sites and the rise in the total surface area that is available to bind the dye. An increase in adsorbent dosage provides a greater number of available binding sites, thereby enhancing the interaction between dye molecules and the adsorbent surface, and thus, the better the required interaction occurs. At the mid‐level doses, the adsorption capacity attains close optimum values, which indicates that binding sites have been utilized. Nevertheless, above a certain dose level (over 120 mg), the rate of change decreases, which implies approaching the state of equilibrium at a slow pace. This process can relate to overlapping or aggregation of the adsorbent particles with increased concentrations that could decrease the effective surface area and interrupt access to some active sites. Moreover, constant cation of the dye in solution causes a decreased ratio of adsorbate to adsorbent at larger dosages, which causes overuse of the adsorption sites. Diffusion implications and alterations in the adsorption equilibrium dynamics are also likely to influence the minimal fluctuations that are noticed at the high doses. With the system being in the saturation stage, there is no proportional increase in the adsorption capacity according to adsorbent mass, and this underscores the need to optimize the dosage to operate the system effectively. The same trends were observed in past reports when adsorption efficiency showed an increment with adsorbent dosage to an optimum level, beyond which no further substantial increment was noticed [, ]. In general, the findings prove that adsorbent dosage is a significant parameter with consideration of the disposal of MV dye, and an optimum dosage is needed to achieve maximum adsorption performance with minimum use of adsorbent material.

Effect of Solution pH

The graph exposing the effect of solution pH on adsorption is demonstrated in Figure . An important parameter that has an impact on adsorption is the pH level, as it influences the electric character of adsorbents and the target ions []. In practical wastewater systems, the presence of various coexisting ions can significantly influence the adsorption process. These background ions often compete with target pollutants for the attainable active sites on the adsorbent surface, thereby diminishing the overall adsorption efficiency of biochar‐based composites [, ]. Such competitive interactions can alter adsorption behavior, particularly under conditions of elevated ionic strength. For instance, Li et al. observed that the removal efficiency of fluoroquinolones (FQs) using magnetic manganese dioxide‐modified biochar declined as the ionic strength of the solution increased. This behavior was explained by the influence of chloride ions associated with FQs, along with the presence of competing cations in the solution, which interfered with the interaction between the adsorbate molecules and the adsorbent surface []. The dominance of solution pH on the adsorption behavior of MV dye is illustrated in Figure . The results clearly demonstrate that pH exerts a substantial influence on the adsorption techniques, with noticeable variations in adsorption capacity observed across the investigated pH range. It has a higher adsorption efficiency at a relatively elevated pH (around 3), probably because in acidic conditions, the electrostatic interactions that occur between the positively charged dye molecules and the negatively charged functional groups on the biochar surface become favorable. When the pH level is lower, the surface of the adsorbent might be protonated, but certain interactions like π‐π stacking and van der Waals force can also make successful adsorption. With a rise in pH closer to neutral (pH 6), the adsorption capacity is moderate. This behavior can be clarified by the reduced intermolecular attraction among dye molecules, along with intensified competitive interactions at the adsorbent surface, particularly in the presence of hydroxyl ions in the solution. The surface charge of the biochar starts to change in this pH range, which affects the availability and activity of adsorption sites. Beyond higher pH values (pH 7–8), the adsorption efficiency is observed to decrease significantly. The reason behind this reduction is that the concentration of OH ‐ions in solution when adsorbing the dye increases; these ions may occupy the adsorption sites of the dye and cause electrostatic repulsion in the case of negative surface charges. Also, the alterations in the dye speciation and the weakening of the adsorbing forces between the adsorbate and adsorbent may further restrict adsorption at an alkaline condition. This general pattern shows that an acidic environment prefers the adsorption of MV dye, and therefore, pH must be optimized to get the best removal efficiency. This trend has been in line with already existing literature on cationic dye adsorption involving carbon‐based adsorbents, in which electrostatic forces and surface functional groups have predominantly contributed to the adsorption behavior [, ].

Zeta Potential Analysis

The zeta potential of the prepared biochar was also measured, and the results are illustrated in Figure . The zeta potential values were found to have a range of about −75 to −5 mV with a sharp peak at −40 mV, which shows that the surface charge of the biochar is predominantly negative. The magnitude of the negative surface charge plays a crucial role in determining adsorption performance, particularly for cationic contaminants such as MV. The wide distribution to the negative side (up to −70 mV) also suggests the existence of the active sites with high binding ability, which could lead to a better adsorption activity. Also, the similarity of zeta potential distribution indicates that the modification process has produced homogenous functional group incorporation on the entire surface and reduced the heterogeneity of adsorption sites. Overall, the analysis of zeta potential indicates that the biochar has good surface charges, which play a critical role in its efficacy as a cationic contaminant adsorbent.

Mechanism Model

Surface diffusion or intra‐particle diffusion usually controls the rate‐limiting mechanism in an adsorption process []. The experimental data were analyzed using the Weber–Morris intra‐particle diffusion model to determine the controlling step. The resultant plots missed the origin, showing that diffusion intra‐particle is not the only mechanism that controls the rate. This implies that there are other mechanisms, like diffusion in the boundary layer, which also play a role in the total adsorption kinetics. Other kinetic processes, such as boundary layer diffusion and other simultaneous rate‐limiting mechanisms, could also control the adsorption process [, ]. Figure describes the intra‐particle diffusion and Elovich models applied to the adsorption kinetics of MV on synthesized biochar. The adsorption data show a sharply rising curve that becomes gradually level, showing the process to be sequential through many phases taking place until equilibrium. It is a multi‐step feature of an adsorption which is diffusive and by surface. The Elovich model best fits the data throughout the entire time domain, which is consistent with an energetically heterogeneous surface and indicates a significant contribution from chemisorption processes. This model also suggests that the adsorption rate decreases exponentially with surface coverage, which matches experimental observations of a rapid uptake in the initial state followed by a progressive slowdown as equilibrium is reached. Conversely, the intra‐particle diffusion model has a less accurate fit at smaller contact times, which shows that the diffusion inside the pores is not the only controlling rate process. Such a non‐linearity indicates that the adsorption process is non‐linear, meaning that there are several stages that the adsorption follows, such a process as diffusion through the boundary layer and interaction with the surface, before intra‐particle diffusion becomes influential. Thus, surface reactions and diffusion interactions determine the general adsorption process, instead of a limiting step. These results coincide with already published research on the dye adsorption of the carbon‐type adsorbents [, , ]. As indicated by the positive value of intercept (3.32 ± 0.920) for K
int obtained in this case on MV adsorption, external mass transfer and intra‐particle diffusion are part of the overall rate‐controlling mechanism []. The adsorption process was rapid at the initial moments (α = 7.83 ± 0.00036), then decreased gradually with the period. Proposed adsorption mechanism

Figure illustrates a visual presentation of our suggested synergistic approach of sorption addressing the selective separation of the MV dye onto FGNB sorbents from bulky crude industrial wastewater. Activation with 30% tartaric acid (C4H4O6) will produce many active binding sites at the surface of the functionalized biochar. The experimental findings obtained from the FTIR, zeta potential, surface morphology, adsorption kinetics, and isotherm analyses collectively indicate that the high removal efficiency of MV by FGNB can be attributed to the combined effect of its structural and chemical characteristics. The modification process leads to the formation of a well‐developed mesoporous framework characterized by interconnected cavities and accessible pore channels, which enhance surface exposure. In addition, the treatment process enriches the surface with oxygen‐containing functional groups such as ─COOH and ─OH, thereby increasing the number of available active sites for dye binding and improving adsorption performance []. In addition, the aromatic carbon framework of the biochar promotes π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of methyl violet and the graphitic domains of FGNB, thereby enhancing adsorption affinity. This enhancement is primarily attributed to chemisorption, an irreversible chemical bond‐forming process (between the binding sites of sorbents and the active edge of pollutant molecules) in which the mesoporous structure of the nanosorbent facilitates intra‐particle diffusion. Electrostatic interactions also play an important role in the adsorption process []. Under the optimized pH conditions, the negatively charged functional groups present on the FGNB surface favor the adsorption of the cationic methyl violet molecules through electrostatic attraction. Furthermore, the porous structure of the functionalized biochar provides abundant accessible adsorption sites, enabling pore diffusion and pore‐filling mechanisms that contribute to the overall adsorption capacity. During this process, pollutant molecules migrate from regions of higher concentration in the bulk solution toward lower concentration sites within the adsorbent matrix, leading to effective adsorption. Therefore, the excellent adsorption performance of FGNB, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 88.57 mg/g, is attributed to the synergistic contribution of these complementary adsorption mechanisms rather than to any single interaction. This multi‐mechanistic adsorption behavior explains the high affinity of the prepared adsorbent toward methyl violet and highlights the effectiveness of converting methanol‐extracted groundnut shell residue into a value‐added adsorbent for sustainable wastewater treatment.

Comparative study with others

Although the impressive adsorption performance of many advanced nanomaterials and engineered composites has been reported, these materials are often expensive to produce, involve compacted preparation processes, and may raise environmental issues []. On the other hand, biomass‐based adsorbents like biochar have attracted much attention because of their low cost, renewability, environmentally benign nature, and surface modifiability [, ]. These can be derived from crop residues and further treated physically or chemically to enhance their adsorption capacity. To this end, comparisons between different adsorbents are required to assess their effectiveness, feasibility, and potential for large‐scale use. These evaluations provide important insights into the influence of characteristics such as surface area, pore structure, and surface chemistry on the adsorption performance []. And they help to identify sustainable solutions that offer both effective treatment and economic and environmental benefits. Accordingly, this study is accompanied by a detailed comparison with other reported adsorbents to evaluate the performance of the prepared groundnut shell biochar. This allows us to compare its adsorption performance with other materials, and it underscores its potential as an economical, environmentally sustainable, and scalable approach to wastewater treatment. Table provides comparative data on the efficiency of different adsorbents applied for the disposal of toxic contaminants, like metals and dyes, under different operating conditions. It illustrates the diversity in terms of adsorption capacity, depending on the adsorbent, the pollutant, and the adsorption system being used. Various high‐tech adsorbents, including TiO2‐based metal composites, hybrids of graphene oxide, nanocellulose modified coal, and activated composites of chitosan, show significant removal efficiencies in the case of numerous heavy metals. For example, TiO2‐metal composites exhibit significant removal efficiencies for Cu (II), Ag(I), Ni (II), Cd (II), Cr (VI) and Mn (II) from photo‐deposition, suggesting their versatility. Likewise, graphene oxide/titanate composite materials display good capacities greater than 530 mg/g for Pb (II) and Cu (II), indicating the potential of carbon‐nanostructure‐based materials for heavy metal disposal. In the reported systems, some adsorbents exhibit very high adsorption capacities. Cellulose xanthate obtained from sugarcane bagasse has capacities of 712.89 mg/g for Pb (II) and 181.18 mg/g for Cu (II), while activated chitosan‐modified coal also exhibits good performance with capacities greater than 230 mg/g. Such high capacities suggest the high affinity of modified biopolymers and modified materials for metal ions, attributed to the large number of functional groups [, ]. By contrast, some natural and low‐cost adsorbents, like the cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) from banana rachis and nanostructures derived from cashew nutshell, show relatively low adsorption capacities but are intriguing options due to their low cost and sustainability. The type of adsorption processes adopted in the studies range from batch adsorption to fixed‐bed column and continuous systems. Although batch systems are widely employed for laboratory‐scale assessment, column‐based and continuous systems are more suitable for industrial deployment because of their practicality and scalability. Crucially, this study, which uses biochar derived from groundnut shell, shows a reasonably good adsorption capacity of 64.82 mg/g for the removal of methyl dye in batch systems. While high‐tech nanomaterials perform better in terms of adsorption capacity, sustainable bio‐based adsorbents present an attractive combination of performance, cost, and environmental compatibility, making them viable alternatives for large‐scale water treatment.

Conclusion

The findings of this study show that biochar formed using groundnut shell waste material has a high prospect of being used as an efficient adsorbent in the elimination of MV in aqueous systems those that are frequently released untreated into natural water bodies. The adsorption performance was systematically assessed utilizing batch experiments, while the optimization of key process parameters was achieved through the application of Box–Behnken design within the framework of Response Surface Methodology (RSM). Also, the adsorption process was thoroughly assessed by equilibrium, kinetic, and thermodynamic parameters to have a more profound understanding of the adsorption mechanisms behind the reaction. The analysis of the obtained characterization gave the main physicochemical properties of the obtained adsorbent, and the comparative analysis of FTIR before and after dye uptake showed that there was adsorption mainly at the surface of the adsorbent. FTIR help to clarify the roles of functional groups in the adsorption process. The effect of the distinctive operational parameters was also studied methodically by carrying out batch experiments, and optimization was then done to identify optimal conditions of the technique. For an inaugural dye concentration of 40 mg/L, the optimal operating conditions were identified as natural pH, an adsorbent dosage of 60 mg, a contact time of 60 min, and a temperature of 45°C. Under these conditions, efficient adsorption performance was achieved. However, a significantly enhanced removal performance was achieved under acidic conditions, especially near pH 3, suggesting that electrostatic forces are the primary contributors to the adsorption process. Under acidic conditions, stronger electrostatic attraction between positively charged dye species and negatively charged surface functionalities enhances the adsorption efficiency. To examine equilibrium behavior, the experimental results were analyzed using Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models, all of which showed high correlation coefficients. The strong agreement of both the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models with the experimental data suggests that the adsorption process involves a combination of mechanisms. This indicates the simultaneous contribution of physisorption and chemisorption during the uptake of the dye onto the adsorbent veneer. The Temkin model yielded a heat of adsorption value of 100.91 J/mol, suggesting the predominance of chemisorption mechanisms. According to the Langmuir isotherm, the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity of the biochar for MV was determined to be 88.57 mg/g. Kinetic analysis further indicated that both pseudo‐first‐order and pseudo‐second‐order models adequately describe the adsorption behavior, implying that physical and chemical interactions occur simultaneously during the adsorption process. In addition, the intra‐particle diffusion and Elovich mechanism models provided further insight into the adsorption rate and underlying kinetic behavior, allowing the conclusion that both diffusion‐controlled and surface‐related adsorption take place to promote the overall kinetics. Thermodynamic evaluation indicated that the adsorption proceeds spontaneously and is thermodynamically favorable within the investigated temperature range, and the character of the heat exchange is similar, the calculated thermodynamic indicators are respected. The Box–Behnken response surface analysis furnished good quantitative data in adsorption. Accordingly, the initial dye concentration turned out to be the most important factor that affects adsorption. The 3D response surface plots revealed the effect all parameters have on the result. Moreover, polynomial equations were developed to describe the relationship between adsorption capacity and the experimental variables and can predict the outcome when the parameters are varied within the ranges that are examined, and optimization of the other adsorption conditions. These equations optimize the process conditions of the minimization of the contact time, minimization of the adsorbent dosage and concentration of dye, and the equilibrium adsorption capacity was determined under fixed operating conditions, including a contact time of 90 min, an adsorbent dosage of 0.1 g, and an initial MV concentration of 40 mg/L.
