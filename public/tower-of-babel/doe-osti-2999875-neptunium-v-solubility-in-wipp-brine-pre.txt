 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The oxidation state and solubility of neptunium (Np) in 5 M NaCl and high ionic strength synthetic
WIPP (Waste Isolation Pilot Plant) brines were systematically investigated as a function of pCH+
(7-11) in the presence and absence of WIPP-relevant organic ligands, including EDTA
(Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid), oxalate, citrate, and acetate, as well as inorganic ligands such
as borate and carbonate at a temperature of 23 ± 2°C. This study was conducted through long-term
batch solubility experiments spanning approximately 950 days, using an undersaturation approach.
A comprehensive set of experimental and spectroscopic techniques, including UV-VIS-NIR
spectroscopy and Np LIII-edge X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS), was employed to identify
the solubility-controlling Np solid phases and the predominant aqueous Np species present in the
samples.
The results confirmed a significant impact of borate on the aqueous speciation and solubility of
Np(V) in 5 M NaCl at pCH+ 9. The interactions of organic ligands with Np(V) were observed to
differ between 5 M NaCl and synthetic WIPP brines. In the WIPP brine, the kinetics of the
interactions varied, with these effects diminishing over time. In contrast, the interactions of organic
ligands in 5 M NaCl were more stable. Additionally, the effect of carbonate on Np(V) solubility
was most pronounced at pCH+ values of 9 and 11. Model calculations indicated that the total soluble
Np species at pCH+ 9.3 should be 4.02 x 10-7 M under repository-relevant conditions, which is
consistent with the concentration determined in these experiments: 3.84 × 10-7 ± 1.35 × 10-8 M
[Domski and Sisk-Scott 2019].
Another key finding of this study was that both the liquid phase, analyzed by UV-Vis, and the solid
phase, measured by X-Ray Absorption Near Edge Spectroscopy (XANES), confirmed that Np
maintained its dominant +V oxidation state across the phases under investigation. Solid-phase
characterization through Extended X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (EXAFS) analysis revealed
that the redox-controlling solid phase consisted of KNpO2CO3(s) or K3NpO2(CO3)2, which also
aligns with the predictions of the WIPP model [Domski and Sisk-Scott 2019].
The data generated from this study quantify the effects of organic compounds, borate, and
carbonate on the solubility of Np(V), thereby providing empirical validation for the WIPP actinide
model. The results will play an important role in informing decisions and recommendations made
in the upcoming recertification of the WIPP (CRA-2026). The experiments were conducted by the
Los Alamos National Laboratory - Carlsbad Operations (LANL-CO) Actinide Chemistry and
Repository Science Program (ACRSP) as part of a broader initiative to establish realistic
parameters related to actinide chemistry within the current WIPP Performance Assessment (PA)
model and to enhance the conceptual model for WIPP chemistry in support of ongoing
recertification efforts. These experiments were performed in compliance with the U.S. Department
of Energy-Carlsbad Field Office (US DOE-CBFO) approved test plan, “Experimental Strategy to
Challenge Actinide Solubility Predictions” (LCO-ACP-26). All data reported were generated
under the LANL-CO WIPP Quality Assurance (QA) Program, adhering to the DOE-CBFO Quality
Assurance Program Document (QAPD). In addition to this interim report, the 950-day solubility
experiments will be continued, further characterization studies will be conducted, and more
comprehensive results will be obtained to prepare the final report.

1.0 INTRODUCTION
The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) is the United States' sole deep geologic repository for high-
level radioactive waste and the first such facility globally designed to dispose of defense-related
transuranic (TRU) radioactive waste in a salt-based geologic medium. Located 2,150 feet
underground in an ancient salt formation, WIPP permanently isolates defense-generated TRU
waste [Kirkes and Patterson 2014, CRA 2014, Schramke et al. 2020]. Ensuring the safe disposal
of this radioactive waste is imperative to prevent human and environmental exposure.
The potential for containment breaches, leading to the release of radionuclides and their
subsequent migration through the surrounding geological matrix, is a critical consideration in
safety assessments for waste repositories. The migration of actinides is governed by various
geochemical processes, including redox transformations, colloid formation, sorption and
precipitation, desorption and dissolution, solubility, and complexation with ligands. These
processes are heavily influenced by the composition of intruding brine. A comprehensive
understanding of the geochemistry of the repository waste forms is essential for accurately
assessing the long-term performance of a radioactive waste disposal facility [Silva and Nitche
1995, Runde 2002, Runde 2000, Choppin 2007].
Within the actinide series, the pentavalent neptunium ion [Np(V)] is the most stable oxidation state
in aqueous solutions. This stability makes Np(V) a key representative for studying the aqueous
chemistry of all actinides in the (+V) oxidation state. In the context of the WIPP scenario, Np is
particularly noteworthy, as it is the primary daughter product of americium-241 (241Am) decay (via
alpha decay and neutron capture), leading to an increase in its inventory over time. As such, Np
becomes progressively more significant during the 10,000-year performance period of the
repository.

Neptunium-237 (237Np), with a half-life of 2.14 x 106 years, is one of the principal contributors to
the radiotoxicity of spent nuclear fuel in a deep geologic repository after several centuries of
storage. Neptunium is a redox-sensitive element, exhibiting complex chemical behavior and
solubility characteristics. It can exist in five oxidation states, ranging from III to VII, in aqueous
environments [Lemire 2001]. The electronic states and chemical speciation of Np are highly
dependent on local geochemical conditions, including pH, redox potential (Eh), and ionic strength.
Under reducing conditions typical of natural groundwater, Np(IV) predominates, whereas in more
oxidizing waters, Np(V) is the most prevalent species. Np(IV) is significantly less soluble, forming
hydroxo complexes that are approximately five orders of magnitude less soluble than the
corresponding Np(V) complexes [Neck and Kim 2001, Silva and Nitche 1995, Kaszuba and Runde
1999].

Neptunium solubility in brines:
Solubility experiments are highly relevant to the PA of the near field of a nuclear waste repository
in the event of a brine intrusion. In saline solutions, the aqueous Np(V) species are in equilibrium
with redox-controlling solid phases, which are influenced by factors such as salt concentration,
pH, the presence or absence of ligands, CO2 partial pressure, and other parameters. The solubility
and interaction behavior of the NpO2+ ion in single-component systems, such as NaClO4 and NaCl
solutions, in the presence and absence of carbonate has been described successfully by considering
the interaction between NpO+ − 2−
 2 , OH , Cl , ClO4 and CO3 [Neck et al. 1994, Fanghänel et al. 1995,
 − −

Neck et al. 1995].
Novak et al. (1996) investigated the solubility behavior of Np(V) in three synthetic Na-K-Mg-Cl
brines simulating WIPP conditions, with ionic strengths [Itot] of 0.84, 2.97, and 7.78, respectively,
in the presence of CO2(g) using an oversaturation experimental approach. The pH value ranges
from 7.1 to 7.5. Dissolved Np(V) concentrations decreased by several orders of magnitude within
the first 100 days of the experiment. The solid phases formed in all experiments were identified as
KNpO2CO3•xH2O(S) by X-ray powder diffraction analysis [Novak et al. 1996a].

Organic effects:
Inorganic and organic ligands, through interactions with metals in aqueous solutions, can alter the
oxidation state on one hand, and on the other hand, they can either increase or decrease the
solubility of metals by forming complexes. They can also stabilize the new oxidation state which
may precipitate to form a secondary solid phase [Runde 2000]. There are various organic
components of nuclear wastes which are disposed in the WIPP repository. The complexation of
NpO2+ with acetate, citrate, EDTA and oxalate have been studied in saline solutions [Novak et al.
1996b, Pokrovsky et al. 1998, Moore et al. 1999].
Reed et al. (1998) investigated the effects of oxalate, citrate, and EDTA on the oxidation state of
Np(VI) in ERDA-6 at pCH+ 8 and10 in G-Seep (Salado) brine at pCH+ 5 and 7. The presence of
organic complexants facilitated the rapid reduction of Np(VI) in G-Seep brine at pH values of 5
and 7. In contrast, at pH values of 8 and 10 in ERDA-6 brine, carbonate and hydrolytic complexes
predominated, thereby inhibiting or slowing the reduction of Np(VI) by the organic ligands
present. The author also discussed the competition between Np-organic and Np-carbonate
complexation in the presence of carbonates in the system. Although Np(VI)-carbonate complexes
were stable in the absence of organic ligands, the author reported that in the presence of both
carbonates and organic ligands, Np(VI) was reduced to Np(V) and Np(IV). The reduction of
Np(VI) was attributed to the presence of organic complexants in solution; in the absence of
carbonates, the reduction to Np(V) and Np(IV), along with precipitation, occurred more rapidly
[Reed et al. 1998].

Borate effects:
 − −
Brush (1989) reported borate present mainly as H3BO3 and HB4 O7 or H3BO3 and B(OH)4 a
naturally occurring compound in the Salado Formation brine. The concentration of borate in the
intergranular brines at WIPP is approximately 160 mM, calculated as mono-boric acid, ranking
just behind chloride and sulfate [Snider 2003, Brush 1989].
The speciation of borate in aqueous systems is highly complex, potentially influenced by factors
such as pH, ionic strength, and total boron concentration. To date, only a limited number of
experimental studies have been conducted on borate complexation with select lanthanides and
actinides. Most of these studies focus on Nd(III), Eu(III), and Cm(III) as analogs for the trivalent
actinides Am(III) and Pu(III). However, a systematic study of actinide-borate complexation
remains to be performed [Schott et al. 2014, 2015; Hinz et al. 2015].
Hinz et al. (2020) investigated the interaction of borate ([B]ₜₒₜ = 0.004 M to 0.16 M) through a
series of comprehensive solubility experiments conducted in 0.1–5.0 M NaCl and 0.25–4.5 M
MgCl₂ solutions, with pH values ranging from 7.2 to 10.0 (pHₘ = –log[H⁺]). The study employed
various analytical techniques, including X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), X-ray
Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy
(EDS), Ultraviolet-Visible-Near Infrared (UV-Vis-NIR), and synchrotron-based spectroscopic
techniques such as XAS. UV-Vis-NIR analysis clearly indicated the formation of weak Np(V)–
borate complexes under mildly alkaline conditions, with polyborate species likely contributing to
the complex formation. The presence of Np(V)–borate complexes was found to have a minimal
effect on the solubility of Np(V) in near-neutral to weakly alkaline pH conditions. However, a
significant reduction in Np(V) solubility (3 to 4 orders of magnitude) was observed in NaCl and
dilute MgCl₂ solutions with [B]ₜₒₜ = 0.16 M and pHₘ ≤ 9. XRD, XPS, SEM–EDS, and XAS
analyses confirmed the formation of previously unrecognized Na–Np(V)–borate(cr) and Mg–
Np(V)–borate(cr) solid phases in NaCl and dilute MgCl₂ solutions, respectively. While
undersaturation solubility experiments with the Mg–Np(V)–borate(cr) phase demonstrated very
low solubility in 4.5 M MgCl₂ solutions, the transformation of NpO₂OH(am) was kinetically
hindered and was not observed within the timeframe of this study in such concentrated brines
[Hinz et al. 2020].
Carbonate effects:
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) will be produced at the WIPP by the assumed microbial degradation of
coexisting cellulosic, plastic, and rubber (CPR) materials. The generated CO₂ has the potential to
dissolve in brine and subsequently react with the engineered barrier material, magnesium oxide
(MgO), leading to the formation of an insoluble magnesium carbonate (MgCO₃) compound. In
addition, calcium and iron (II) are present within the WIPP and are capable of forming insoluble
carbonates [Borkowski et al. 2009]. Previous studies have demonstrated that in carbonate-free
solutions, solid phases such as NpO₂OH(s) and Np₂O₅(s) are thermodynamically stable [Lierse et
al. 1985, Kaszuba and Runde 1999, Kim 2006). In contrast, in equilibrium conditions where

of WIPP-Relevant Organic and Inorganic Ligands Page 13 of 41

carbonate is present, the solubility-limiting solid phases are represented by M3NpO₂CO₃·xH₂O and
M₃NpO₂(CO₃)₂ (M = Na⁺, K⁺), as evidenced by XRD analysis. Therefore, a critical characteristic
of the solubility-limiting solid phases of Np(V) is their pronounced dependence on the
concentration of alkali carbonates [Neck et al. 1994, Novak et al. 1997, Al Mahamid et al. 1998].
Experimental objectives and scope:
WIPP PA calculations require more detailed data on the solubilities of Np(V) in WIPP brines. A
review of the literature reveals that a series of studies on the solubility and speciation of Np have
generally been conducted in simple background electrolyte media such as NaClO₄, NaCl, MgCl₂,
or CaCl₂, with the results primarily derived from these solutions [Shilov et al. 2012, Gaona et al.
2013, Hinz et al. 2015, Fellhauer et al. 2016, Petrov et al. 2017].
A well-established and systematic examination of these complicated environmental systems in the
WIPP brines is crucial to describe the chemical behavior of the actinides sufficiently. Before the
ternary system (actinide--brine component—ligand complexation) interaction can be described,
the different binary systems, including actinide/brine, actinide/organic ligand and actinide/borate
must be investigated.
The aqueous chemistry of Np(V) in 5 M NaCl and synthetic WIPP brines as a function of pCH+ in
presence and absence of borate, WIPP relevant ligands (EDTA, oxalate, citrate, acetate) and
carbonate at 𝑇𝑇 = 23 ± 2 ∘C was thoroughly studied by long-term batch solubility experiments
(approximately 950 days) from an undersaturation approach. The data obtained from this work
quantify the effects of WIPP-relevant concentrations of organics, borate, and carbonate on the
solubility of Np(V), which can then inform the WIPP actinide model and provide
recommendations for future WIPP recertifications.

2.0 EXPERIMENTAL
2.1 Chemicals

NaCl, HNO3, NaOH, HCl, NaHCO3, MgCl2·6H2O CaCl2·2H2O Na2B4O7·10H2O, LiCl, KCl,
NaBr, Na2SO4 (Fisher Scientific), HBr, oxalate [disodium oxalate], EDTA [disodium
EDTA·2H2O] (Sigma-Aldrich), citrate [citric acid anhydrous] (Fluka), acetate [acetic acid], (Acros
Organics) were used in this study. All solutions were prepared with high purity water (HPW, 18.2
MΩ-cm). All solutions and samples were prepared by first degassing HPW with nitrogen for 5-6
hours and then bringing it inside an inert gas (nitrogen) glovebox at 23 ± 2 °C. All brine
preparations, final Np stock preparations, experimental setup, and sampling were conducted in a
nitrogen atmosphere glovebox. Samples remained in the glovebox for the duration of the
experiments. Oxygen partial pressure was kept below 10 ppm throughout the experiments.
Samples were doubly contained in sealed vessels to prevent any exposure to oxygen and prevent

2.5 pH and Eh measurement

Measurements of pH were made using a combination pH electrode (Orion ROSS). The hydrogen
ion concentration (pCH+= –log [H+], in molal units) was determined according to pCH+ = pHexp +
ΔpH, as described previously in the literature [Altmaier et al. 2003, Borkowski et al. 2009], where
pHexp is the measured pH value and ΔpH is the empirical correction factor accounting for the liquid
junction potential of the electrode and the activity coefficient of H+. Values for ΔpH used in the
present study were 0.86, 0.87, 1.12, and 1.11 for 5.0 M NaCl, pCH+ 11, pCH+ 9, and GWB (Generic
Weep Brine), respectively.
Oxidation-reduction potential measurements were obtained using an Orion A221 meter equipped
with a Mettler Toledo InLab glass redox microelectrode with a platinum ring. Corrections from

of WIPP-Relevant Organic and Inorganic Ligands Page 18 of 41

redox potential to Eh were made by adding the Ag/AgCl redox potential from the meter reading
Zobell solution (LabChem). In Eh measurements, especially in solutions with high ionic strength,
the uncertainty can be up to 200 mV. In order to perform more precise measurements and reduce
the error, after each Eh measurement the electrode was washed with 1 M HCl, then water, then 1
M NaOH, and the final wash with water (total cleaning process time is 1-2 hours), and after every
two or three measurements the electrode filling solution, 3 M KCl, was replaced with fresh solution
[Altmaier et al. 2010].

3.0 RESULTS & DISCUSSION

3.1 Liquid phase speciation by UV-Vis-NIR

Aqueous Np species and oxidation states were investigated using UV-Vis-NIR after 60 days for
samples in equilibrium with Np(V)O2OH(am) in 5 M NaCl and synthetic WIPP brine with and
without borate (pCH+ 7 and 9) . The NIR spectra of Np in the supernatant between 940 and 1040
nm are displayed in Figure 1. The absorption intensity of Np(V) at λ = 981–988 nm systematically
decreases with increasing pCH+, due to lower Np solubility under more alkaline conditions. We
observed a peak shift of the Np(V) aquo ion in 5 M NaCl at pCH+ 7 and 9, indicative of
contributions from Np(V) chloro-complexes, NpO2(OH2)5–nCln1-n, and Np(V) hydrolysis species,
and NpO2(OH2)5-m(OH)1-m, respectively. Similar results for Np(V) in NaCl and CaCl2 solutions
were reported in Fanghänel et al. 1995, Fellhauer et al. 2016, and Petrov et al. 2017.

One inadequately understood component of WIPP brine is borate, even though it comprises a
significant portion of the brine, with a concentration near160 mM. To investigate the borate effect
on Np speciation in WIPP brine, two additional pCH+ 7 and pCH+ 9 samples in synthetic WIPP
brine without borate were prepared and analyzed by UV-Vis-NIR (Figure 1a and 1b). The Np(V)
absorption spectra of synthetic WIPP brine in the absence of borate show the characteristic Np(V)
absorption band with a peak maximum of λmax ~982 nm (and a small shoulder at ~990 nm) at pCH+
7 and λmax ~ 983 nm at pCH+ 9. This minor peak shift to a higher wavelength could be caused by
the presence of weak complexants, including chloride, sulfate, hydroxide, or other brine
components, and results in a slightly greater shift than observed for solutions in 5 M NaCl alone.

Maiwald et al. (2020) studied Np(V)-sulfate interactions in aqueous solutions as a function of total
ligand concentration (Na2SO4), ionic strength (NaClO4), and temperature (T = 20–85 oC) at pH 4.3
by using UV-Vis-NIR. They reported a single species of NpO2(SO4)- at 983.1 nm through peak
deconvolution of the absorption spectra [Maiwald 2020]. The NIR spectra of Np in synthetic WIPP

into a solid phase.
The Np(V)-borate interaction was probed further using samples containing 10 mM ≤ [B]tot ≤ 100
mM in 5 M NaCl at pCH+ 7 and 9 (Figure 2a and 2b). At [B]tot = 10 mM in the 5 M NaCl at pCH+

observed at pCH+ 9 is more pronounced and suggests the possible formation of more than one
Np(V) borate species at higher pCH+.

3.2 Solubility results

3.2.1 Organic effect on solubility of Np(V) in 5 M NaCl and in WIPP brine
Figure 3 shows the solubility of Np in the presence and absence of citrate, acetate, oxalate, and
EDTA in undersaturation experiments in synthetic WIPP brines and in 5 M NaCl (a) as a function
of time, up to 950 days and (b) as a function of pCH+ (7, 9, 11). Strikingly, the solubility of Np is
~2 orders of magnitude higher in 5 M NaCl compared to synthetic WIPP brine. Some variation is
seen between samples containing different organics, illustrating the effect of organic complexants
on Np solubility, which is more pronounced for the 5 M NaCl system at pCH+ 9 after 950 days than
in WIPP brine. In particular, the Np-oxalate system demonstrated greater solubility than other
organics in 5 M NaCl. When interpreting these results in conjunction with the Eh values, it is
observed that in samples containing citrate, the Eh values are significantly lower compared to other
organics, indicating more reduced conditions. This suggests that within the sample, Np(V) may be
reduced to Np(IV), and this can be explained by the lower solubility of Np(IV) compared to Np(V).
Furthermore, the literature contains detailed studies demonstrating that in the presence of citrate,
Np(V) is reduced to Np(IV) and U(VI) is reduced to U(IV) [Bonin et al. 2007, Francis and Dodge
2008].
The solubility of Np(V) in WIPP brine shows variability over time. Initially, this difference is
observed to be significant, but as time progresses, the effect of organics on Np solubility gradually
decreases. Particularly after about one year, the solubility of Np was observed to be in the same
order of magnitude. By the end of 950 days, all samples, except for acetate, converge at nearly the
same solubility value. Additionally, it was observed that in the initial sampling (two-week
sampling), the solubility of Np was lowest in the presence of oxalate, but over time, it followed a
different trend compared to other organics. The reason for this is that the amount of oxalate initially
used was above the solubility value in WIPP brine at pH 9. In other words, oxalate that precipitated
initially in WIPP brine gradually dissolved, suggesting the formation of news complexes with Np.
It was also observed that the solubility of Np increases by up to two orders of magnitude in NaCl
systems in the presence of organics, whereas a similar effect is not observed in WIPP brine
containing divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ (see Table 1). The most likely explanation for
this is that divalent cations in the brine, especially Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, tend to form highly stable
complexes with these organic complexing agents, thereby competing with actinides for complex
formation and effectively reducing the impact of organic complexation on actinide solubility

Np(V) Solubility in WIPP Brine: Presence and Absence LCO-ACP-42, Rev. 0
of WIPP-Relevant Organic and Inorganic Ligands Page 22 of 41

[Serne et al. 2002, Hummel et al. 2005, CRA 2019]. Alternatively, cations such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺
can participate in the formation of ternary complexes involving both Np and the organics, and can
function as stabilizing counter-ions for negatively charged complexes, thereby promoting the
formation of stable ternary or quaternary complexes such as Ca/Mg-RN-L or Ca/Mg-RN-OH-L
(RN: radionuclide, L: Ligand) [Felipe-Sotelo et al. 2015, DiBlasi et al. 2021].

The effect of [B]tot (0 ≤ [B]tot ≤ 160 mM) on Np solubility in 5 M NaCl and synthetic WIPP brines

presence of borate in synthetic WIPP brine at pCH+ 9 had a less pronounced effect on Np solubility
compared to the NaCl system, with a decrease of less than 0.5 log units observed.

3.2.3 Carbonate effect on Np(V) solubility in 5 M NaCl
The solubility of NpO2(OH)(am) was investigated as a function of pCH+ and carbonate
concentrations of 1 mM ≤ [CO2− 2−
 3 ] ≤ 100 mM in 5 M NaCl and [CO3 ] = 16 mM in synthetic WIPP
brine at pCH+ 7, 9, and 11 using an undersaturation experimental approach (Figure 6). Because
NaCl solutions do not have a significant pCH+ buffering capacity, the addition of sodium carbonate
solutions to a 5 M NaCl solution caused a slight changed in the pCH+ after initial adjustment. The
samples were equilibrated over 950 days.

The solubility of NpO2(OH)(am) as a function of carbonate concentration (1 mM ≤ [CO₃²⁻] ≤ 100
mM) in 5 M NaCl and at pCH+ 7, 9, and 11 was evaluated using an undersaturation experimental
approach, as depicted in Figure 6. In the experiments, no significant variations in NpO2(OH)(am)
solubility were observed at pCH+ 7 in 5 M NaCl, whereas a slight decrease in Np concentration
was recorded at pCH+ 9 with increasing carbonate concentrations. In a comparable investigation
by Nitsche et al. (1992), a slight reduction in Np solubility was noted in Yucca Mountain
groundwater, where [CO₃²⁻] = 11.44 mM at pCH+ 6.7, in contrast to [CO₃²⁻] = 2.81 mM at pCH+
7.0. A marked increase in solubility was observed in the sample containing 100 mM carbonate at
pCH+ 11, attributable to the predominance of carbonate complexation over the hydrolysis of Np
under alkaline conditions [Nitsche et al. 1992]. In their study, Runde (2000) explored the solid-
liquid equilibria of Np(V) in 5 M NaCl with varying carbonate concentrations and demonstrated
that, at low carbonate concentrations, the solubility-controlling solid phase for both curves was the
hydrated ternary Np(V) carbonate, NaNpO2CO3•3.5H2O(s). This solid phase initially forms at
higher carbonate concentrations and is kinetically favored to precipitate, yet it is
thermodynamically unstable. Over time, it undergoes a transformation to the thermodynamically
stable equilibrium phase, Na3NpO2(CO3)2•nH2O(s). Thus, the observed decrease in Np solubility
in the Np-carbonate system implies the conversion to the more stable Na3NpO2(CO3)2•nH2O(s)
solid phase [Neck et al. 1995, Runde 2000].

approximately 6 months. Figure 7 presents the solid-state reference spectra of Np(IV)O₂(s) and

measured samples consist of 95–99% Np(V), with an associated uncertainty of ±8%. The

In order to investigate the local atomic environment of Np in more detail, Np LIII-edge EXAFS
spectra for the solid phase equilibrated at pCH⁺ ≈ 9 with 16 mM carbonate in synthetic WIPP brine
were analyzed. To better distinguish the Np=Oax and Np-Oeq interactions that separate Np(V) from
Np(IV), the Np(IV)O₂(cr) sample was also analyzed and compared with the results. The relevant
EXAFS structural parameters are summarized in Table 8. As shown in Figure 9, each sample
exhibits different EXAFS patterns. These differences can be attributed to the types of ligands
present in the samples and changes in the coordination environments. Curve fitting analysis was
challenging due to noise from low Np concentrations and the proximity of equatorial and distant
O and C shells to the O=Np=O multiple scattering shell.
For Np(IV)O₂(cr), the FT shows a single prominent peak consistent with a single coordination
shell consisting of approximately 8 O atoms at a distance of 2.33 Å. Although the hydration
number typically assigned to tetravalent actinides is around 8, there have been relatively few direct
studies, and the results cover a range of values [Conradson 1998, Choppin 1983]. In addition to
this speciation, a Np-Np scattering contribution at longer distances of 3.7 angstroms was observed
in EXAFS data, which is attributed to An(IV)O₂ precipitation, mostly coordinated by a single shell
of 8 O atoms at ∼2.36 Å and 12 An(IV) at 3.82 Å [Balboni et al. 2020, Conradson et al. 1998,
Allen et al. 1997].
The first shell Np(V) in synthetic WIPP brine at pCH+ 9 with 16 mM carbonate in the 1.86–1.89 Å
range corresponds to Np=Oax atoms, and these values are consistent with the reported 1.82–1.89
Å for the solubility of Np(V)O₂(OH)(am) in 5 M NaCl [Petrov et al. 2017] and 1.82–1.86 Å in 4.5
M CaCl₂ [Fellhauer et al. 2016].
The second shell in the 2.49–2.59 Å range is likely due to equatorial O atoms, which could be
attributed to water molecules or carbonate groups. The narrow range of Np=O bond lengths
indicates that the neptunyl units in the samples are well-preserved, and no significant structural

Np(V) Solubility in WIPP Brine: Presence and Absence LCO-ACP-42, Rev. 0

degradation has occurred. The LCFA analysis of XANES spectra and the EXAFS fit for Np-Oeq
at 2.54 Å confirm the presence of well-ordered neptunyl(V) species.
Additionally, a Np-C interaction was observed at 3.03 Å. To confirm this more clearly, a second
modeling was performed without considering the Np-C interaction. Figure 9 shows two different
modeling results: one where the Np-C interaction contributes to the model, and one where it does
not. As clearly shown in this figure, EXAFS modeling shows that the three-shell model (Np=Oax
+ Np–Oeq + Np–O–C) provides a better fit than the two-shell model (Np=Oax + Np–Oeq). This
result supports the idea that carbonate groups directly form complexes with Np(V) and play an
important role in the coordination environment. In addition, previous solubility studies reported
Np–Oeq distances of 2.34–2.43 Å [Petrov et al. 2017] and 2.29–2.44 Å [Fellhauer et al. 2016],
which are slightly shorter than the observed 2.59 Å values in the presence of carbonate in our
investigated sample [Clark et al. 1996]. This suggests that carbonate ions may affect the stability
of Np(V) species when they are in the equatorial coordination sphere.
In the presence of carbonate, a contribution from potassium (K) atoms was detected at
approximately 4.1 Å in the EXAFS spectrum. Furthermore, in both samples, no formation of
polynuclear Np species or Np precipitations, which would lead to a prominent Np–Np peak around
3.7 Å in the FT, was observed.
Previous studies have shown that K-containing Np(V) solid phases may precipitate in alkaline
environments. This can occur even though the Na⁺ concentration is much higher than the K⁺
concentration. The solubility constants determined by Fanghänel et al. (1995) and Novak et al.
(1997) show that K-containing Np(V) phases have lower solubility than their Na+ homologs
[Fanghänel et al. 1995, Novak et al. 1997]. In this context, it is believed that a K⁺-containing phase
has formed in the present study. The presence of K could affect the solubility and environmental
mobility of Np(V); therefore, it should be considered an important factor in waste management
and long-term radioactive material migration models.
In conclusion, the obtained EXAFS and XANES data highlight the effects of carbonate and K⁺
presence on Np(V) structures and provide important information on the long-term geochemical
behavior of Np. Future studies should aim to investigate the stability of Np(V) complexes under
different ionic strength and pH conditions to better understand their effects on environmental
transport pathways.

4.0 CONLUSION
This study provides an in-depth, long-term investigation into the solubility behavior and oxidation
state of Np in high ionic strength environments relevant to the WIPP. Through a comprehensive
series of batch solubility experiments conducted over approximately 950 days under
undersaturation conditions, the redox behavior and aqueous speciation of Np in 5 M NaCl and
synthetic WIPP brines were systematically characterized as a function of pCH+ (7–11) and in the
presence of both inorganic (carbonate, borate) and organic ligands (EDTA, oxalate, citrate, and
acetate).
The results consistently demonstrated that Np persists in the +V oxidation state under all tested
conditions, as confirmed by both UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy in solution and XANES analysis of
the solid phase. EXAFS spectroscopy further identified the redox-controlling solid phases as
KNpO₂CO₃(s) or K₃NpO₂(CO₃)₂, which are consistent with solid phases predicted by the WIPP
geochemical model. These findings indicate that Np(V) remains the dominant oxidation state in
both solution and solid phases under repository-relevant conditions and support the robustness of
the current WIPP PA.
The influence of organic ligands varied depending on the chemical environment: in 5 M NaCl,
ligand–Np(V) interactions were observed to become more stable over time, whereas in synthetic
WIPP brines, kinetic effects initially led to elevated solubility levels, which gradually decreased,
indicating the establishment of a dynamic equilibrium. These differences underscore the
importance of matrix-specific behavior in actinide–organic interactions, particularly under high
ionic strength conditions.
Calculated Np(V) concentrations using the WIPP thermodynamic model (based on the
geochemical equilibrium modeling code EQ3/6) were in good agreement with experimental
results, indicating that the system approached thermodynamic equilibrium under the experimental
conditions.
Overall, this dataset contributes essential empirical validation to the WIPP actinide chemistry
model and provides critical insight into the chemical behavior of Np(V) under repository-relevant
conditions. These results will inform parameter updates for the CRA-2026 recertification and
contribute to refining the conceptual model used in WIPP PA calculations.
