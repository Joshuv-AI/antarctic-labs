A Complexity Measure for Active Learning in Multi-group Mean Estimation
Abdellah Aznag Rachel Cummings Adam N. Elmachtoub

Abstract
We study a max-risk objective for active learning in a multi-group mean estimation d d -armed bandits: a learner adaptively allocates a budget of T T samples across d d groups to minimize the worst-case uncertainty index max k ∈ [ d ] ⁡ σ k 2 / n k \max_{k\in[d]}\sigma_{k}^{2}/n_{k} , where σ k \sigma_{k} is the standard deviation of the distribution of arm d d , and n k n_{k} is the number of times arm d d is sampled. We develop a local minimax framework and prove the first general lower bound for this objective, valid for any finite-variance hypothesis class. The bound separates difficulty into three orthogonal factors: a budget term, a heteroscedasticity index measuring how unevenly the uncertainty is spread across arms, and a model-dependent complexity measure, the Variance Local Curvature ( VLC \mathrm{VLC} ), which captures how much information a local change of variance creates inside the hypothesis class. For smooth classes, the VLC \mathrm{VLC} is a reparametrization of a variance–Fisher information, with closed-form values for common families. Benchmarking against the strongest available upper bound ( 5 ) shows near-optimality up to logarithmic factors in broad regimes, and pinpoints a systematic gap in highly heterogeneous instances. Our proof introduces two key ingredients: a loss-induced ℓ 1 \ell_{1} geometry on the decision space, and a representation-based instance generator that reduces hard-instance construction to an explicit random matrix calculation.

Introduction

Active learning studies sequential sampling rules that choose where to collect the next observation under budget constraints. This differs from classical estimation, where data are often treated as given. In this paper, we are concerned with settings where the key decision is where to sample from next. This setting appears in online experimentation, clinical trials, and any application where we estimate group-level quantities under a shared budget and want a controlled, balanced estimation noise. While a substantial literature designs high-performing adaptive policies for these problems, far less is known about the fundamental performance limits they face.

Model.

We consider a multi-armed bandit setting where the goal is to actively learn the means of the arms’ distributions, and where accuracy is measured by the uncertainty index per-arm
\sigma^{2}/n
. Fix a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
of distributions on
\mathbb{R}
with finite variance.
An
instance
is a
d
-tuple of distributions
\bm{D}=(D_{1},\dots,D_{d})\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
, where each
D_{k}

has mean
\mu_{k}
and standard deviation
\sigma(D_{k})>0
. We write

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}):=(\sigma_{1}(\bm{D}),\dots,\sigma_{d}(\bm{D}))\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, where
\sigma_{k}(\bm{D}):=\sigma(D_{k})
.

The class
\mathcal{H}
is known, but the instance
\bm{D}
(and hence
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D})
) is unknown. For a fixed, known horizon
T
, a (possibly adaptive)
policy

\pi
sequentially chooses arms
A_{1},\dots,A_{T}\in[d]
and observes the
corresponding samples. Let

n_{k}\;:=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{A_{t}=k\}\qquad(k\in[d])

be the terminal number of pulls of arm
k
, and write
\bm{n}:=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
.
The terminal risk is

R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\max_{k\in[d]}\ \frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{n_{k}},

and the corresponding optimal benchmark risk is

R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\\
\sum_{k}n_{k}=T\end{subarray}}R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma})\;=\;\frac{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}{T}.

(1.1)

Remark 1.1

(Confidence-interval representation)

Each per-arm index
\sigma_{k}^{2}/n_{k}
is (up to a constant) the squared width of a confidence interval for the mean

\mu_{k}
built from the
n_{k}
samples of arm
k
. The risk
R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma})=\max_{k\in[d]}\sigma_{k}^{2}/n_{k}
is
therefore the
widest
such interval across arms, and minimizing it amounts to controlling the worst-estimated
mean rather than the best one.

We measure performance by
normalized regret
, defined for a policy
\pi
and instance
\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
as

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\bm{n}\sim(\pi,\bm{D})}\!\left[\frac{R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}))}{R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}))}-1\right],

(1.2)

where the randomness is induced by the policy
\pi
and the samples drawn from
\bm{D}
through the random counts
\bm{n}
. We study information-theoretic lower bounds on the normalized regret defined in (
1.2
).

Remark 1.2

(Effect of normalizing)

We use normalized rather than standard regret because it is scale-free and captures the right asymptotics: any
policy pulling every arm
\sim T
times already drives the
un
normalized excess risk to zero, so standard
regret cannot isolate the difficulty of learning the variances. Dividing by
R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma})
makes the
quantity vanish only as the optimal allocation is approached. One recovers a bound on standard regret simply by
multiplying the bound of Theorem
2.3
through by
R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma})
.

Local neighborhood.

Fix a base standard-deviation vector
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and define its normalized variance profile

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\frac{\bm{\sigma}^{\odot 2}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}\in\Delta_{d}.

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})
is scale-free and encodes the optimal normalized allocation that yields
R^{*}(\bm{\sigma})
. For
\rho\in(0,1)
, define the local instance class

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\Big\{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}:\ \big\|\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}))/\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})-\bm{1}\big\|_{2}\leq\rho\Big\}.

Local minimax lower bound.

Since the arms are unlabeled, we restrict attention to permutation-invariant policies (i.e., policies invariant to permutations of arm labels). This is possible because any policy can be symmetrized by a random permutation of labels at time
0
without worsening worst-case regret over a permutation-closed instance class; let
\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}
denote this class. Fix a radius
\rho\in(0,1)
and a tolerance
\tau>0
. Define the
\tau
-tolerant symmetric policies by

\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\Big\{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}:\ \sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D})\leq\tau\Big\}.

The tolerance
\tau
restricts attention to policies whose worst-case normalized regret over the local class is
controlled, and is primarily a
proof-technical
device: our lower-bound argument requires prior
control on how a policy allocates its budget on
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})
, and without such control the
information-theoretic reduction does not yield a non-vacuous bound. We make the precise role of
\tau
explicit in
Remark
1.3
below. In the learnable asymptotic setting, one is naturally interested in regimes where

\tau\downarrow 0
. The corresponding local minimax value is defined by

\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})}\ \sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}),

with the convention
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})=+\infty
if
\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}=\emptyset
.

Remark 1.3

(On the role of the tolerance
\tau
)

The tolerance does not change the local minimax value in the learnable regime. Writing

\tau^{\star}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}}\sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D})
for the
unconstrained value, one indeed has
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})=\tau^{\star}
for every
\tau\geq\tau^{\star}

and
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})=+\infty
for
\tau<\tau^{\star}
. The parameter
\tau
instead enters through

analysis
: the lower bound we prove (Theorem
2.3
) holds for
\tau
-tolerant policies and carries a
multiplicative penalty
1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau)
. Restricting to small
\tau
(the learnable regime) sends

\epsilon(\rho,\tau)\to 0
and makes the bound sharp, whereas imposing no restriction at all (
\tau=+\infty
) only
yields the weaker penalty
\epsilon(\rho,+\infty)
, which can be vacuous. Since
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}=\tau^{\star}

throughout the learnable regime, the resulting bound also lower bounds the unconstrained local minimax value.

Benchmark and notion of sharpness.

Our goal is to lower bound
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
. To interpret such a lower bound, suppose that (by separate algorithmic work) one constructs a permutation-invariant policy
\pi^{\star}
with a
uniform local worst-case guarantee

\sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi^{\star},\bm{D})\leq\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma}),

where
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})
is an explicit function of
(T,d,\bm{\sigma},\rho)
. Then, we have by construction,

\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})\;\geq\;\sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi^{\star},\bm{D})\;\geq\;\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}(\bm{\sigma}).

A meaningful lower bound therefore does two things. First, it must apply at a tolerance level
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})
for which an
admissible policy exists, i.e.,
\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}(\bm{\sigma})\neq\emptyset
. Second, it should satisfy a small gap between the achievable guarantee and
the minimax value
\frac{\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}{\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}(\bm{\sigma})}
. In particular, a
\tilde{O}(1)
guarantee on this ratio implies local minimax near-optimality.

1.1
Our Contributions

Conceptually, our central novelty is an
information-theoretic
approach of the non-additive, max-type
objective defined in (
1.2
). Specifically, we show that its local minimax difficulty is governed by a single curvature
functional, the Variance Local Curvature (VLC), rather than by tail conditions on
\mathcal{H}
. Our main contributions are threefold:

1.

A general local minimax lower bound framework.

We introduce a local minimax formulation parameterized by a locality radius
\rho
and a tolerance
\tau
,
and prove a lower bound on
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
that cleanly separates budget, heteroscedasticity,
and model curvature. The bound is expressed through a one-arm curvature functional
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
,
and applies to general hypothesis classes
\mathcal{H}
with finite variance (see Section
2
).

2.

Sharp characterization for smooth classes and a principled benchmark.

For smooth classes, we relate
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
to a variance–Fisher information quantity via a quadratic KL expansion,
yielding explicit expressions for many standard families. We then benchmark our lower bound against the strongest available
general upper bound, showing that it is close in broad regimes (up to logarithmic factors) and
pinpointing a systematic gap in highly heterogeneous instances. This comparison provides both a refined understanding of when
current algorithms are provably near-optimal and concrete guidance on how future upper-bound analyses might be sharpened
(see Section
3
).

3.

A principled method for generating hard instances.

We develop an
instance generation
viewpoint for lower bounds: rather than guessing an adversarial family
directly in
\mathcal{H}^{d}
, we parameterize local hard families through a representation map. Concretely, we encode
nearby decisions by a hypercube code and a linear map
A
, and view the adversary as choosing the full representation

x\mapsto Ax
. The construction
reduces the core lower bound problem to an explicit matrix optimization problem, which can be handled with sharp random matrix theory machinery. To the best of our knowledge, this “instance generator” viewpoint is new in this setting and may be
useful on its own for other non-additive objectives (see Section
4
).

1.2
Related Work

Our setting is closely related to stochastic multi-armed bandits (MAB)
(
14
)
in that data are acquired by pulling arms.
The lower-bound literature for classical MAB is extensive
(
15
,
12
,
9
,
7
)
,
and many proofs ultimately reduce to controlling the probability of confusing nearby instances via information-theoretic inequalities
(e.g., Pinsker-type arguments
(
18
)
). A key difference is that these techniques are typically tailored to bandit objectives whose
regret is additive over time (cumulative regret) or has a canonical discrete structure (best-arm identification). Our objective is neither:
the normalized regret in (
1.2
) is a non-additive, max-type functional of the terminal allocation. As a result, standard MAB
lower-bound templates do not apply directly and must be adapted to the loss-induced geometry of our decision problem.

Early work introduced this problem for bounded feedback models, motivated in part by fairness constraints

(
17
,
1
,
10
)
.
2
studied the case where
\mathcal{H}

consists of bounded distributions with known support and provided an algorithm with regret

\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{d^{3}\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}\sqrt{T}}\right)
, leaving open the corresponding
lower bounds.
8
proposed a different algorithm and analyzed additional settings for

\mathcal{H}
, including sub-Gaussian distributions with a known sub-Gaussian parameter bound and Gaussian
distributions; the lower-bound question remained unresolved.

More recently,
4
obtained the near-optimal worst-case upper bound

\tilde{O}\!\left(\sqrt{d/T}\right)
for this objective and provided the first lower-bound results in
the Gaussian case. In particular, they showed that a suitably normalized isotropic Gaussian instance attains
regret on the order of
\tilde{\Omega}\!\left(\sqrt{d/T}\right)
, matching the worst-case upper bound up to
logarithmic factors. To the best of our knowledge, these are
the only existing lower bounds for the problem we are studying
. Our main theorem provides a local minimax lower bound for any hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
with finite variance,
expressed in terms of a one-arm information curvature
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
and the variance’s heteroscedasticity. This yields a unified lower-bound framework for this problem.

From a technical point of view, our work can be embedded within the information geometry literature
(
16
)
. Recently, information geometry
(
3
,
6
,
13
,
11
)
has provided promising tools for tackling non-linearity aspects in learning problems. Our core idea,
instance generation
, essentially assumes that
\mathcal{H}
can be embedded in a statistical manifold. This establishes a natural correspondence between the set of instances
\mathcal{H}
, and a (locally) Euclidean space where calculations are more interpretable. In turn, this reduces the problem of finding a lower bound to optimizing over a (locally) Euclidean space.

Variance Local Curvature and Main Theorem

Definition 2.1

(Variance Local Curvature)

Fix
(\sigma,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times(0,1)
and define

B_{\rho}(\sigma)\;:=\;\Bigl\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:\ \Bigl|\frac{v^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr|\leq\rho\Bigr\}.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)
denote the set of measurable selectors

\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)\;:=\;\Bigl\{D:B_{\rho}(\sigma)\to\mathcal{H}\ \text{measurable}:\ \mathrm{Var}(D(v))=v^{2}\ \ \forall v\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)\Bigr\}.

Equivalently,
B_{\rho}(\sigma)=\bigl[\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho},\,\sigma\sqrt{1+\rho}\,\bigr]\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an interval of
admissible standard deviations around
\sigma
.

The
\rho
-Variance Local Curvature is

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\;:=\;\inf_{D\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)}\;\sup_{v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)}\frac{\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\,\|\,D(v_{b}))}{\Bigl(\frac{v_{a}^{2}-v_{b}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}}\;\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\},

(2.1)

with the convention
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})=+\infty
if
\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)=\emptyset
. Moreover, the Variance Local Curvature is defined as

\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H}):=\mathrm{VLC}_{0^{+}}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})=\limsup_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H}).

Although Definition
2.1
optimizes over all measurable selectors, the resulting curvature is usually
easy to evaluate, as it admits simple closed forms in many standard settings (see Table
1
).

Remark 2.2

(Examples)

To build intuition for
\mathrm{VLC}
, we look at the following two extremes.

(i) A rich class with vanishing curvature.
Let

\mathcal{H}=\{(1-\varepsilon)\delta_{0}+\varepsilon\delta_{C}:\varepsilon\in(0,1)\}
with
C\gg 0
. A distribution here
has variance
\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)C^{2}
, so the variance can be moved by an arbitrarily small change in

\varepsilon
once
C
is large, while
\mathrm{KL}
between two such distributions depends only on
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})

and stays bounded. Variance is thus “cheap” to change in
\mathrm{KL}
, and
\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\to 0
as

C\to\infty
.

(ii) A rigid class with curvature bounded below.
Let
\mathcal{H}=\{\mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2}):\sigma>0\}
be
the centered Gaussian family. By Lemma
3.2
(see the Normal row of Table
1
),

\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})=\tfrac{1}{4}
for every
\sigma>0
.

2.1
Main Result

Our main theorem lower bounds the local minimax value
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
in terms of the
one-arm complexities
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})
and the heteroscedasticity of the standard deviations
\bm{\sigma}
. We introduce the heteroscedasticity index, which describes how uneven the randomness is between arms.

\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma}):=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\|\bm{\sigma}^{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\right).

We are now ready to state our main theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(Local minimax lower bound)

There exist universal constants
C>0
such that for any
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, there exists a minimal resolution
\rho^{*}\propto\sqrt{\frac{d}{T}}
such that for any
\tau>0
, and any
\rho\;\geq\;\rho^{\star}
, the local minimax value satisfies

\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})\;\geq\;C\Bigl(1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau)\Bigr)\sqrt{\frac{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{0}}{T}}\,\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma})}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}},

where
\epsilon(\rho,\tau)
vanishes when
(\rho,\tau)\to(0,0)
.

Our lower bound decomposes the difficulty of mean estimation into three orthogonal ingredients: a
budget

term, a
heteroscedasticity
term, and a
structural
term.

\underbrace{\bigl(1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau)\bigr)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Error term}\end{subarray}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{0}}{T}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Budget}\end{subarray}}\;\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\underbrace{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Heteroscedasticity}\end{subarray}}\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Model curvature}\end{subarray}}}.

The budget contribution is standard and almost ubiquitously present in learning theory. The term
\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{0}
represents the
effective dimentionality
of the problem. Moreover, the multiplicative correction
1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau)
captures two sources of approximation:
\rho
controls
the locality scale (finer perturbations as
\rho\downarrow 0
), and
\tau
restricts attention to relevant
policies. In the vanishing regime
\rho\downarrow 0
and
\tau\downarrow 0
, this correction tends to
1
.
We discuss these regime conditions and their interpretation in Section
2.2
.

The heteroscedasticity weights
\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma})
describe how unevenly the randomness is spread across arms.
They are scale invariant: multiplying
\bm{\sigma}
by a constant does not change these weights,
but redistributing mass across coordinates does. When the variances are equal (more homogeneous
uncertainty), the learner must hedge broadly, since many arms can potentially dominate the max-risk
objective. This is analogous to worst-case rates in learning where difficulty scales with the number of
coordinates that can be simultaneously active: a diffuse signal forces exploration across many directions.
By contrast, when uncertainty is concentrated on a few arms, the problem becomes effectively lower-dimensional:
only a small subset can plausibly determine the maximum, and a policy can focus its budget there. In this sense, concentrated uncertainty is easier because it reduces the number of arms that must be balanced at comparable precision to control the max-risk objective; equivalently,
\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{0}\cdot\sum_{k}\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma})
acts as an effective number of relevant arms.

The structural term
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})
measures how much
information
is created by
a local change in variance within the model class. It is a curvature: larger
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
means that
nearby variance levels are strongly separated in KL, so variance is easier to learn and allocation mistakes
become less persistent; smaller
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
means the class is richer in ways that allow variance to
change while producing little KL signal, making it intrinsically harder to discriminate nearby variance
profiles. This mirrors familiar phenomena in statistical learning and nonparametrics: in rigid, well-specified
models, local parameters are easily identifiable, while in richer classes the
same functional can have weaker effective curvature and require substantially more data. Here this manifests
directly in the factor
1/\sqrt{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}}
: weaker curvature enlarges the “local indistinguishability”
region, forcing larger regret even when the budget and heteroscedasticity are held fixed. A more detailed discussion on the
\mathrm{VLC}
for smooth classes
\mathcal{H}
is deferred to Section
3
.

The bound in Theorem
2.3
separates the effect of the class
\mathcal{H}
from the effect of the instance
\bm{D}
.
The class
\mathcal{H}
enters through the operator
\mathrm{VLC}(\,\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
, which assigns to each
variance level
\sigma
a local information curvature. The instance
\bm{D}
influences the bound in two local
ways: first through the
heteroscedasticity profile

\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}))
, which quantifies how much
arm
k
contributes to the
directional
uncertainty relevant for the allocation decision, and second through
the evaluation of the curvature operator at the realized variance levels,
\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma_{k}(\bm{D})\mid\mathcal{H})
.
Concretely, the contribution of arm
k
takes the form

\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{D}))}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma_{k}(\bm{D})\mid\mathcal{H})}},

so
\mathcal{H}
determines the curvature profile
\sigma\mapsto\mathrm{VLC}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})
, while
\bm{D}
determines
where this profile is evaluated and how the heteroscedasticity (and thus the effective difficulty) is distributed across arms.

2.2
Discussions

Worst-case bounds and interpolation over subclasses.

For
\rho\in(0,1)
, call a distribution
D\in\mathcal{H}

\rho
-interior
if its
standard deviation
\sigma(D)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(D)}
admits a nonempty local selector set, i.e.,

D\in\mathcal{H}^{\circ}_{\rho}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}\big(\sigma(D)\big)\neq\emptyset.

Given any subclass
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\circ}_{\rho}
, define its worst-case
\rho-
VLC by

\mathrm{VLC}^{\star}_{\rho}(\mathcal{G}\mid\mathcal{H})\;:=\;\inf_{D\in\mathcal{G}}\ \mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}\big(\sigma(D)\mid\mathcal{H}\big)\ \in\ \mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}.

Then, for each
(D,\ldots,D)\in\mathcal{G}^{d}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\sigma\cdot\bm{1})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma(D)\mid\mathcal{H})}}=\frac{\|\mathrm{Het}(\bm{1})\|_{1}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma(D)\mid\mathcal{H})}}=\frac{1-\frac{1}{d}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma(D)\mid\mathcal{H})}}.

Applying Theorem
2.3

and

\mathrm{Het}
captures that
\mathrm{Het}^{+}
misses: the
directional

This behavior is also algorithmically natural: if all but one arm are (nearly) deterministic, then after a handful of samples one can certify their variance is (close to) zero and essentially stop allocating budget to them. The remaining uncertainty is concentrated on the single noisy arm, so an optimal procedure should quickly focus its pulls there, yielding vanishing “allocation identification” regret in that boundary regime.

Guidelines for sharpening future upper bounds.

This comparison suggests concrete guidelines for sharpening future upper-bound analyses. First, rather than controlling each
\sigma_{k}
independently and then union-bounding over
k

normalized
variance profile (equivalently, the Neyman weight vector) and its fluctuations in directions tangent to the simplex. Moreover, one should allow confidence budgets and error metrics that are
instance-weighted
(e.g., weighted by
\lambda^{\star}
) rather than uniform across arms, so that arms with vanishing variance can be eliminated quickly. Currently, such refinements are not captured by the current upper bound strategies.

4
Proof Outline

Overview.

Lower bounds are obtained by randomizing over instances that are close enough to generate nearly indistinguishable data, yet far enough that their optimal allocations differ substantially, forcing any policy to make costly mistakes. While we follow this template here as well, we introduce two key ingredients that will be crucial in overcoming two separate obstacles.

(i) Identifying the geometry:

the first obstacle is to identify the right geometry on the set of decisions: we need a notion of distance that is compatible with the regret. In the classic multi-armed bandit
setting with additive regret, one typically has

\mathrm{Regret}_{T}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}]\cdot\Delta_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{n},\bm{\Delta}\rangle\right],

where
\bm{\Delta}
is the suboptimality gap vector. In particular, regret is linear in the sampling counts

\bm{n}
, which makes separation essentially linear. In other words, the right geometry that measures between two different decisions is the euclidean geometry. In our setting, the normalized excess risk takes the max form

\frac{R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma})}{R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma})}-1\;=\;\frac{\max_{k\in[d]}\ \sigma_{k}^{2}/n_{k}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}/T}-1,

which is non-additive and non-linear in
\bm{n}
, and therefore the right geometry is not euclidean. We show that the right geometry for our problem is the
\ell_{1}
geometry: separation between decisions is measured (up to a universal constant) by
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.

(ii) Identifying the adversarial instance:
the second obstacle is to construct the adversarial family in a way that is both local and analyzable. In an additive objective one can often propose a simple perturbation family and check that it is hard. Here this is much less clear: the hardness depends on anisotropy coming from the instance and from the class
\mathcal{H}
(through
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
), so there is no single “obvious” hard direction. Our approach is therefore not to guess a hard family, but to parameterize
all
local hard families we care about through a representation map. Concretely, we encode nearby decisions by a hypercube code
x\in\mathcal{X}
and a linear map
A
, and view the adversary as choosing the representation
x\mapsto Ax
. This renders the lower bound calculable: the same representation controls both distinguishability (via average KL) and decision separation (via the
\ell_{1}
geometry), and the problem reduces to a tractable matrix optimization. This viewpoint is what makes the construction interpretable and what allows us to use sharp first-order calculations to handle anisotropy in closed form. In particular, it is this viewpoint that will allow us to capture the refined heteroscedasticity that we believe is relevant to the problem.

From the two ideas to the bound.
Combining these ingredients gives the following pipeline, carried out
in full in Appendix
A
. (1) We fix the decision-relevant level set (the scale-free variance
profile). (2) Over this slice we select
maximally indistinguishable distributions, quantified by
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
. (3) We re-index
the resulting hard instances by nearby
decisions
, turning the problem into a testing problem on the decision
set. (4) We embed a hypercube code
\mathcal{X}
through a linear map
A
and lower bound the regret by a
decision-mismatch (classification) cost measured in the
\ell_{1}
geometry.

5
Conclusions

Our results characterize the local minimax difficulty of adaptive allocation in the non-degenerate regime where

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\in(0,\infty)
at the relevant locality scales. Two natural extensions go
beyond this setting.

First, the curvature
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
is defined through a quadratic normalization in the variance coordinate. For extremely rich classes, KL separation under local variance perturbations can be smaller than quadratic, in
which case the quadratic curvature collapses and the canonical
\sqrt{T}
scaling may no longer be the right resolution. Conversely, for classes that are too constrained, local variance neighborhoods may be infeasible or induce singular KL behavior. A refined local complexity notion that captures the correct power-law (or more general) modulus of KL with respect to variance perturbations would provide a principled way to treat both degeneracies within a single framework.

Second, the proof strategy we introduce (namely, an adapted geometry for decision separation, and a representation-based instance generation for constructing hard families) should extend to other non-additive objectives on allocations, where standard bandit lower-bound templates do not apply directly. Understanding the corresponding geometries and deriving sharp performance limits in these settings is, in its own right, an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Proof Overview of Theorem
2.3

In this section, we provide the road map to derive Theorem
2.3
, and defer the technical proofs for the intermediary lemmas to Appendix
B
.

The proof builds a lower bound by designing a collection of nearby instances that strategically confuse any learning policy. The idea is to make these instances close enough to be statistically indistinguishable but not so close that misidentifying them yields negligible regret. Achieving this balance requires controlling both their scale (how far they are) and their orientation (how they are arranged). We face mainly two obstacles:

Obstacle (i): geometry.
The first challenge, that of scale, is addressed by introducing a problem-specific notion of distance that measures how costly it is to allocate resources incorrectly.

Obstacle (ii): representation.
The second challenge, that of orientation, recognizes that even when instances are at the right distance, their relative positioning still matters: rotating or reshaping them can make the problem significantly harder.

To systematically optimize these two aspects, we lift the search for hard instances into a structured space of configurations and reduce the lower bound construction to an explicit optimization tradeoff over this space. This yields a principled framework that identifies the most confusing yet consequential perturbations of the original problem, establishing the desired lower bound.

A.1
Constructing the Lower Bound Framework

To establish a lower bound on the local minimax value
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
, our strategy is to
restrict the full, complex local class
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})
to a smaller, more tractable, and maximally
challenging subset, that will be bijective to a ball inside the
d-
simplex
\Delta_{d}
. This construction proceeds in three main steps. First, we restrict attention to a
decision-relevant
level set
to isolate the regret due to learning (rather than changes in the benchmark
risk) This is
Step 1
. Second, over this restricted set, we select distributions that are as statistically indistinguishable as
possible, a notion quantified by
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
. This is
Step 2
. Finally, we show that the resulting class
of hard instances can be re-parameterized by a simple geometric set of nearby decisions, which forms the basis of
the subsequent reduction to testing. This is
Step 3
.

Step 1: fixing the decision-relevant level set.

The benchmark risk

\bm{\sigma}

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})\;:=\;\frac{\bm{\sigma}^{\odot 2}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}\in\Delta_{d}.

Fixing
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})

\rho\in(0,1)

\rho

(A.1)

Given

Rather than working directly with variance vectors, we parameterize local alternatives by decisions in the simplex.

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\lambda})\;:=\;\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}\,\sqrt{\bm{\lambda}},

so that
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\lambda}))=\bm{\lambda}
. For each
i\geq 1
, we then define the corresponding
hard instance

D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\;:=\;D^{(i)}\!\bigl(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr)\in\mathcal{H}^{d}.

The next lemma formalizes the resulting correspondence: decisions in
\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
index a local
family of hard instances, and restricting to this family can only strengthen a lower bound.

Lemma A.1

(Decision-indexed hard instances)

For each
i\geq 1
and each
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
,

\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{\sigma}(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda}))\bigr)=\bm{\lambda}.

Moreover, for any policy
\pi
,

\sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D})\;\geq\;\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \sup_{\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}\mathrm{Regret}\!\bigl(\pi,D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr),

and the same inequality holds after taking
\inf_{\pi}
. In particular,

\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})\geq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})}\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \sup_{\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}\mathrm{Regret}\!\bigl(\pi,D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr).

Lemma
A.1
shows that the search for a worst-case instance reduces to a search over a local
set of decisions
\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, with statistical indistinguishability controlled armwise by

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H})
through the selectors (
A.1
).

A.2
Decision Mismatch

Next, we rewrite the normalized regret into a form that highlights the discrepancy between a policy’s terminal
decision and the target decision induced by the instance. Fix
i\geq 1
and a decision

\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
. By construction, the decision-indexed instance

D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})
has decision-relevant profile
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})))=\bm{\lambda}
.
When a policy
\pi
is run on this instance, it produces terminal counts
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
and hence a
random terminal decision

\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\;:=\;\frac{\bm{n}}{T}\in\Delta_{d}.

The regret can therefore be viewed as the expected cost of implementing
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
when the target
decision is
\bm{\lambda}
.

We formalize this by introducing the
decision mismatch
function
r(\cdot\|\cdot)
, which quantifies the
penalty for acting according to one decision when another is the target. In our setting,

r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda})\;:=\;\left\|\frac{\bm{\lambda}}{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}}\right\|_{\infty}-1\;=\;\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\bar{\lambda}_{k}}-1,

(A.2)

with the convention
r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda})=+\infty
if
\bar{\lambda}_{k}=0
for some
k
.
A direct calculation from the definition of normalized regret shows that, for each
i
and
\bm{\lambda}
,

\mathrm{Regret}\!\bigl(\pi,D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda})\right],

(A.3)

where the expectation is over the randomness of the policy and the samples through the random terminal decision

\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
.

The advantage of (
A.3
) is that it decouples analytic and statistical components. The
non-additive structure of the objective is entirely encapsulated by the deterministic function
r(\cdot\|\cdot)
,
while the behavior of any policy
\pi
is reduced to the distributional properties of a single random vector, the
terminal decision
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
. This highlights a fundamental departure from classic bandit frameworks:
in those settings, regret is typically a cumulative sum of separable per-round losses, whereas here the objective
is evaluated holistically on the terminal allocation. Consequently, the relevant geometry is induced by
r
on the
decision set, rather than by additive per-arm contributions.

Finally, while the notation
r(\cdot\|\cdot)
is suggestive of a divergence, it is simply a problem-specific cost
function derived from the regret definition. It satisfies the basic separation property

r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda})=0\implies\bm{\lambda}=\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
and
r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda})>0
for
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\neq\bm{\lambda}
,
and increases as
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
deviates from the target
\bm{\lambda}
.

A.3
Structured Adversarial Construction

The analysis now requires simplifying the supremum over the local decision set
\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})

to make the lower bound tractable. The core strategy is to replace this continuous set with a finite, structured
collection of hardest-to-distinguish alternatives. This reduction is principled: shrinking the set of possible
instances into a finite set makes the problem easier for the policy (in fact, it makes the problem as easy as classification on a specific loss function), hence any lower bound derived on the restricted family remains
valid for the original problem. The resulting task is a discrete multi-class testing problem.

A finite “sign-cube” of directions.

The central step is to replace the continuum of deviation directions by a finite set that preserves the essential
combinatorial difficulty. We restrict to a finite design

\mathcal{X}\subset\{-1,+1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp},

so that instead of estimating a continuous decision, the policy must identify which label
x\in\mathcal{X}
was
chosen by the adversary.

Embedding the code into the local decision set.

With the combinatorial structure fixed by
\mathcal{X}
, we embed these discrete directions into the local geometry
around the base decision
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\in\Delta_{d}
. A raw direction
x\in\mathcal{X}
must be mapped to a
valid nearby decision. We do so by choosing a linear map
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and defining

\bm{\lambda}_{x}\;:=\;\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}x\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

(A.4)

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. This parameterization cleanly separates (i) the discrete directions

x\in\mathcal{X}
from (ii) the orientation/anisotropy encoded by
\bm{A}
.

For (
A.4
) to be valid, we must ensure

\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
. This is equivalent to

\bm{A}\mathcal{X}\perp(\bm{\lambda}^{\star}),\text{ }\text{and}\quad\Bigl\|\bm{A}\mathcal{X}\Bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d}.

(A.5)

Lemma A.2

(Exact feasibility conditions for the local simplex)

Fix
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\in\Delta_{d}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\bm{\lambda}_{x}\;:=\;\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}x\Bigr).

Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, i.e.

\bigl\|\bm{\lambda}_{x}/\bm{\lambda}^{\star}-\bm{1}\bigr\|_{2}\leq\rho
and
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{d}
.

(ii)

The vector
\bm{u}_{x}:=\bm{A}x
satisfies the two constraints

\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}.

In particular, if
\bm{A}\mathcal{X}\subset(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})^{\perp}
and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\bm{A}x\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
,
then
\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
.

From decisions to hard instances.

Using the decision-indexed generator from Section
A.1
, each candidate decision
\bm{\lambda}_{x}

induces a hard instance

\bm{D}_{i}(x)\;:=\;D_{i}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})\in\mathcal{H}^{d},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X}.

Thus, the adversary randomizes over the finite family
\{\bm{D}_{i}(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
.

Separation: regret reduces to decision mismatch on the constructed family.

The key point is that, on this family, normalized regret is exactly the expected mismatch between the random
terminal decision
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{n}/T
and the target decision
\bm{\lambda}_{x}
.

Lemma A.3

(Separation on the constructed family)

For every policy
\pi
, every
i\geq 1
, and every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\sim(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))}\!\left[r\!\left(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\ \Big\|\ \bm{\lambda}_{x}\right)\right],

where
r(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the decision mismatch defined in (
A.2
).

Together, Lemmas
A.10
–
A.3
reduce the lower bound problem to analyzing a
finite multi-class testing problem over
x\in\mathcal{X}
, with statistical indistinguishability controlled by

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
through the instance generator
\bm{A}
and decision separation controlled by the loss-induced geometry
r
.

A.4
Reduction to a classification problem

A direct lower-bound analysis of the expected mismatch cost

\mathbb{E}\big[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda})\big]
is difficult, since it requires characterizing the distribution
of the policy’s random terminal decision
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{n}/T
for an arbitrary adaptive policy. To bridge
this continuous–discrete gap, we follow a standard information-theoretic template: we discretize the local decision
set by a finite packing and reduce the problem to a multi-class testing task. The novelty in our setting is that,
because the loss is non-additive and non-Euclidean, we must first identify a loss-compatible geometry that turns
misclassification into a quantitative regret penalty.

The minimum-mismatch decoder

Fix a finite set of candidate decisions
\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
(as constructed in
Section
A.3
). Given an output
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, we decode a label by nearest-neighbor
in the mismatch loss
r
:

Definition A.4

(Minimum-mismatch decoder)

Given
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, define

\bar{x}\ \in\ \operatorname*{argmin}_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\ r\!\left(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\big\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}}\right).

(A.6)

This decoder is canonical for our objective: it projects the continuous terminal decision onto the discrete packing
using exactly the loss that defines regret.

A loss-induced geometry on decisions

To lower bound regret by a term that depends only on the discrete event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
, we need a function that
quantifies the intrinsic cost of confusing two labels. We define this cost through a minimax ambiguity principle.

Definition A.5

(Symmetric ambiguity cost)

For
\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}\in\Delta_{d}
, define

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})\;:=\;\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}}\ \max\!\left\{r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}),\ r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{b})\right\}.

By construction,
\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})
is the smallest worst-case mismatch incurred by a single
decision when the target is either
\bm{\lambda}_{a}
or
\bm{\lambda}_{b}
. This is the loss-induced notion of
separation we use throughout the lower bound.

Lemma A.6

(Projection reduces regret)

Fix
\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
and let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be the true label. For any policy
and the decoder
\bar{x}
from Definition
A.4
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[r\!\left(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\big\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x}\right)\right]\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}\!\left(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}\right)\right]\;\geq\;\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)\cdot\min_{b\neq x}\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{b}).

The first inequality converts regret into an ambiguity penalty, and the second isolates the misclassification
probability. Thus, the lower bound reduces to controlling two factors: a statistical classification error and a
geometric separation term.

A tractable
\ell_{1}
separation

A key simplification in our setting is that the geometry induced by
\tilde{r}
is equivalent to the

\ell_{1}
geometry on
\Delta_{d}
.

Lemma A.7

(
\ell_{1}
lower bound for
\tilde{r}
)

For all
\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}\in\Delta_{d}
,

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})\ =\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}-\bm{\lambda}_{b}\|_{1}.

In particular, if the finite family
\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}
is well separated in
\ell_{1}
, then any misclassification
event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
forces a nontrivial regret penalty. In the next subsections, we bound the classification
error via Fano (using structured KL control from
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
) and bound the decision separation via the
explicit parameterization of
\bm{\lambda}_{x}
.

A.5
Bounding the classification error

The reduction in Lemma
A.6
shows that the regret lower bound factors through the
misclassification probability
\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)
. This is a multi-class testing problem: the adversary draws
a label
x\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
, the policy observes the bandit transcript
\mathcal{F}_{T}
, and outputs a
decoded label
\bar{x}=\bar{x}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
. The ability of any policy to succeed is limited by the amount of
information the transcript carries about
x
. A standard information-theoretic tool is Fano’s inequality, which
lower bounds
\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)
when the mutual information
I(x;\mathcal{F}_{T})
is small.

For bandit models,
I(x;\mathcal{F}_{T})
can be upper bounded by average pairwise transcript KL, and transcript KL
decomposes into a sum over arms of expected pulls times arm-level KL. Our local construction provides control of
both terms. First, by construction of the VLC selectors (Step 2 in Section
A.1
), the arm-level KL
between two labels is proportional to the squared decision perturbation, with proportionality constant

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})
(up to a vanishing
o(1)
term along
i\to\infty
). Second, for
policies in the local minimax class, the tolerance condition forces sampling proportions to remain in the local
regime, yielding the pull-count control
\mathbb{E}[n_{k}]\approx T\,\lambda_{k}^{\star}
(up to a vanishing error as

(\rho,\tau)\to(0,0)
). Combining these two controls yields a geometric upper bound on mutual information in terms
of a Frobenius norm, stated next.

Lemma A.8

(Fano bound with structured KL)

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\{\pm 1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
be nonempty and let
x\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
.
Let
\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
be defined by (
A.4
) for some
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
and set

\bm{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{Cov}\big(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})\big),\qquad\bm{\Gamma}:=\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\Big)_{k=1}^{d},

where
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})
is the base decision. There exists a function

\varepsilon(\rho,\tau)
with
\varepsilon(\rho,\tau)\to 0
as
(\rho,\tau)\to(0,0)
such that for any policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
,

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)\ \geq\ 1-\frac{\frac{2T\rho^{2}}{d}\,\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\,\bigl(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau)\bigr)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{X}|}.

In particular, the error term does not depend on the choice of the policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
.

Lemma
A.8
isolates the statistical difficulty in a single geometric quantity:

\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{F}^{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The diagonal matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
encodes armwise information
curvatures through
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})
, while
\bm{\Sigma}
encodes the geometry of the
design
\mathcal{X}
. The embedding map
\bm{A}
transports the design into the decision neighborhood, and the
Frobenius term captures their interaction at the level of mutual information.

A.6
Bounding the decision cost

Having bounded the classification error, we now lower bound the second component in the regret reduction: the
conditional decision cost
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\mid\bar{x}\neq x]
. The key point
is that our hard family is indexed by hypercube labels
x\in\mathcal{X}\subset\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, so the disagreement
structure of
x-\bar{x}
has a simple combinatorial form. Define the disagreement projector

\bm{P}_{\neq}\ :=\ \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq\bar{x}_{k}\}\big),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad x-\bar{x}\;=\;2\,\bm{P}_{\neq}x.

This representation allows us to connect decision separation directly to the
\ell_{1}
geometry of the loss.

Lemma A.9

(Lower bound on conditional decision cost)

Assume the candidates are parameterized as

\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}x\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X}\subset\{\pm 1\}^{d},

and define
\bm{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
. Then on the event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
,

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1},

and hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\mid\bar{x}\neq x\right]\ =\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \Big|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right].

The equality in Lemma
A.9
consists of a conventional scale factor
\rho/\sqrt{d}
and a
core structural term
\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,\bm{P}_{\neq}x\|_{1}
. This term quantifies the magnitude of the
decision penalty by composing the policy-dependent disagreement vector
\bm{P}_{\neq}x
with the embedding

\bm{A}
and the baseline scaling
\bm{\Lambda}
. It is precisely this interaction that creates the adversary’s
tradeoff: the same embedding
\bm{A}
that controls statistical indistinguishability through the Frobenius term
in Lemma
A.8
also controls the magnitude of the penalty incurred when the policy makes an
error.

A.7
Tuning the design

The structured construction reduces the lower bound to a discrete testing problem indexed by
(\mathcal{X},\bm{A})
.
We now explain how these objects are chosen so as to (i) make the classification problem hard and (ii) ensure that
any error is costly, while maintaining feasibility of the local parameterization.

Design of
\mathcal{X}
.

The set
\mathcal{X}
plays a purely combinatorial role: it defines the discrete set of candidate directions and
hence the number of labels the policy must distinguish. To make the testing problem intrinsically hard, we want

|\mathcal{X}|
to be exponentially large in
d
. At the same time, to amplify the cost of any error, we want

\mathcal{X}
to be well-separated in Hamming distance so that any misclassification produces a large disagreement
pattern (and hence a large projector
\bm{P}_{\neq}
). These two requirements are met by a standard
Gilbert–Varshamov construction. Such a design only exists in even dimensions
d\in 2\cdot\mathbb{N}
. For simplicity, we assume that
d
is even. Proving the lower bound on odd dimensions can be easily extended by dropping one arm or padding with a dummy coordinate.

Lemma A.10

(Hypercube design)

There exists a universal constant
\kappa>0
and a set
\mathcal{X}\subset\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
such that

\min_{x\neq x^{\prime}}d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\geq d/4
and
\log|\mathcal{X}|\geq\kappa d
.

For the remainder of the analysis we fix such a design
\mathcal{X}
.

Design of the representation map
\bm{A}
.

Once
\mathcal{X}
is fixed, the remaining degree of freedom is the embedding
\bm{A}
, which controls the

orientation
of the packing inside the local decision neighborhood through

\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{A}x)
. The key point is that the same

\bm{A}
governs both sides of the lower bound:

•

Distinguishability (classification).
The mutual information bound from Fano yields a term of the form

\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{F}^{2}
, where
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X}))
and

\bm{\Gamma}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H}))
.

•

Decision cost.
On the event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
, the
\ell_{1}
geometry yields a term of the form

\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\|_{1}
, where
\bm{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
.

Thus, choosing
\bm{A}
amounts to choosing an embedding that is simultaneously hard to distinguish and costly to
confuse. We select
\bm{A}
by a deterministic derandomization argument; let us explain the motivation.

At this point the lower bound depends on
\bm{A}
through two competing geometric quantities (one governing
distinguishability, one governing the cost of confusion), and optimizing this tradeoff directly over all matrices
is analytically hard. Random matrix theory provides a mature calculus for computing expectations of spectral and
norm functionals under rotations, so averaging over a symmetric matrix ensemble often yields clean first-order
expressions for the quantities we need.

We separate anisotropy from orientation by writing a right polar-type decomposition

\bm{A}\;=\;\bm{S}\bm{O},

where
\bm{S}
is a deterministic positive semi-definite matrix that encodes the anisotropy we wish to impose, and
\bm{O}
is an
orthogonal matrix that randomizes orientation.

The anisotropy matrix
\bm{S}
fixes the local shape of the
construction and prepares the problem for a uniform orthogonal randomization: since
\bm{S}
is supposed to deal with any anisotropies,
averaging uniformly over
\bm{O}
is the natural way to remove orientation effects. Concretely, we do not average over the whole orthogonal group: to maintain feasibility we restrict the orientation
to orthogonal matrices that send the (normalized) base direction
\bm{\lambda}
to the all-ones direction
\bm{1}
.
Since orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean norms, we impose this constraint at the level of unit vectors and sample

\bm{O}
from the compact set

\mathsf{O}(\bm{\lambda}\!\to\!\bm{1})\;:=\;\Bigl\{\bm{O}\in\mathsf{O}(d):\ \bm{O}\,\frac{\bm{\lambda}}{\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{2}}=\frac{\bm{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigr\}.

We take
\bm{O}
to be
uniform
over
\mathsf{O}(\bm{\lambda}\!\to\!\bm{1})
in the canonical way induced by Haar
measure: fix any deterministic
\bm{Q}\in\mathsf{O}(d)
such that

\bm{Q}\,\frac{\bm{\lambda}}{\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{2}}=\frac{\bm{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
, draw
\bm{U}
Haar-uniform from the stabilizer
subgroup
\mathrm{Stab}(\bm{1}):=\{\bm{U}\in\mathsf{O}(d):\bm{U}\bm{1}=\bm{1}\}
, and set
\bm{O}=\bm{U}\bm{Q}
.
This law is supported on
\mathsf{O}(\bm{\lambda}\!\to\!\bm{1})
, is invariant under the natural symmetries of the
constraint, and does not depend on the particular choice of
\bm{Q}
. Since
\mathsf{O}(\bm{\lambda}\!\to\!\bm{1})
is
compact, the resulting distribution is a bona fide probability measure and the expectations we use are
well-defined; we therefore omit the standard measure-theoretic formalities.

After this symmetrization step, the
analysis depends on
\bm{S}
only through rotation-invariant quantities. We then derandomize: once we compute the
average tradeoff over
\bm{O}
, there must exist a fixed orthogonal
\bm{O}
(and hence a fixed
\bm{A}=\bm{S}\bm{O}
)
that achieves at least the same tradeoff. This reduces the hard instance design to choosing the deterministic
anisotropy
\bm{S}
(optimized later), while the orthogonal factor
\bm{O}
serves as the analytic device that
makes the calculation tractable.

Lemma A.11

(Representation Design)

Let
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X}))
,
\bm{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, and

\bm{\Gamma}:=\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\Big)_{k=1}^{d}.

There exist
\bm{A}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X}),\bar{x}}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\bm{A}_{\star}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \middle|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{A}_{\star}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}}\ \gtrsim\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}},

where
\gtrsim
hides universal constants and a possible error term that is only a function of
d
. Moreover, the inequality above is scale invariant for
\bm{A}_{\star}
, i.e., it remains valid for any
t\cdot\bm{A}_{\star}
for
t>0
.

Feasibility and resolution.

Finally, the packing must remain inside the local neighborhood. Feasibility requires that the multiplicative
perturbations in
\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{A}x)
preserve positivity and
normalization. This imposes a resolution constraint relating
\rho
and
(d,T)
.

Lemma A.12

(Feasibility and resolution)

Setting
\rho^{*}=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{T}{}}\right)
implies that for all
\rho\gtrsim\rho^{*}

\bm{A}_{\star}\mathcal{X}\perp\bm{\lambda}^{\star}
and
\|\bm{A}_{\star}\mathcal{X}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
.

Together, Lemmas
A.10
–
A.12
complete the tuning of the adversarial
construction:
\mathcal{X}
provides a large, well-separated label set;
\bm{A}
provides a favorable embedding
capturing both orientation and anisotropy; and
\rho
must exceed the budget-imposed resolution
\rho^{\star}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d/T}
.

A.8
Putting everything together: Proof of Theorem
2.3

We now synthesize the preceding components into a proof of Theorem
2.3
. Throughout, fix a base profile

\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, a radius
\rho\in(0,1)
, and a tolerance
\tau>0
such that the local minimax class

\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
is nonempty. Let
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}:=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})
and

\bm{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
.

Fix
\rho\geq\rho^{\star}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{d/T}
. Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
be a design with

\log|\mathcal{X}|\geq\kappa d
and
\min_{x\neq x^{\prime}}d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\geq d/4
(which existence is guaranteed via Lemma
A.10
). For a
linear map
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
(chosen below), define candidate decisions

\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}x\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

and let
\bm{D}_{i}(x):=D_{i}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})
be the corresponding decision-indexed instances constructed from the

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
selectors. For any policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
, let

\bar{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{n}/T
be its terminal decision and let
\bar{x}
be the minimum-mismatch decoder
(see Definition
A.4
).

Step 1: reduce regret to classification error and decision cost.

By Lemma
A.3
, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right].

Applying Lemma
A.6
yields

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right]\ \geq\ \Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\mid\bar{x}\neq x\right].

(A.7)

Step 2: bound the classification error by Fano.

Let
x_{\star}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
be uniform on
\mathcal{X}
. Lemma
A.8
gives

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \Pr(\bar{x}\neq x_{\star})\ \geq\ 1-\frac{\frac{2T\rho^{2}}{d}\,\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\,(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau))+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{X}|},

(A.8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X}))
and
\bm{\Gamma}:=\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\Big)_{k=1}^{d}.

Since
\log|\mathcal{X}|\geq\kappa d
, it suffices to choose the resolution
\rho>0
so that the fraction in
(
A.8
) is bounded away from
1
. This is possible if we choose

\frac{2T\rho^{2}}{d\log|\mathcal{X}|}\,\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\ \geq\frac{1}{2}\iff\rho\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{d\log|\mathcal{X}|}{T\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}^{2}}}.

(A.9)

Then, we have

\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x_{\star})\ \geq\ 1-\frac{1+\epsilon(\rho,\tau)}{2}-\frac{\log 2/\kappa}{d}=\Omega(1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau)).

Step 3: bound the conditional decision cost.

On
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
, Lemma
A.9
yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\mid\bar{x}\neq x\right]\ =\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \Big|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{T}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \Big|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}},

(A.10)

where
\bm{P}_{\neq}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq\bar{x}_{k}\})
.

Step 4: choose a good orientation and conclude.

The remaining task is to choose an embedding
\bm{A}
that makes the ratio between the decision-cost term in
(
A.10
) and the distinguishability term in (
A.9
) large. This is achieved by the
representation lemma (Lemma
A.11
), which provides a choice of
\bm{A}
such that, up to universal constants and vanishing terms,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\bm{A}_{\star}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \middle|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{A}_{\star}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}}\ \gtrsim\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\star}\left(1-\left(\lambda_{k}^{\star}/\|\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\right)}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}},

(A.11)

Combining
(
A.7
), (
A.10
), (
A.11
), the constant lower bound on

\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)
, as well as
\log|\mathcal{X}|\geq\kappa d
yields

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\ \gtrsim\ (1-\epsilon(\rho,\tau))\sqrt{\frac{d}{T}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}},

where we implicitly used that the constructed instance is feasible by Lemma
A.2
. By recalling that

\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\propto\bm{\sigma}
and that
\mathrm{Het}
is scale-invariant, and taking
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})}
(which is possible since the error term
\epsilon
does not depend on the choice of
\pi
, by Lemma
A.8
) and using Lemma
A.1
, we complete the proof for Theorem
2.3
.

Appendix B
Proof of Technical Lemmas

Proof B.1

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.
Fix
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and write
\theta:=\sigma^{2}
. Recall

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})=\inf_{D\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)}\ \sup_{v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)}\frac{\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\,\|\,D(v_{b}))}{\Bigl(\frac{v_{a}^{2}-v_{b}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}},\qquad B_{\rho}(\sigma)=\Bigl\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:\Bigl|\frac{v^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr|\leq\rho\Bigr\}.

Let
\theta_{a}:=v_{a}^{2}
and
\theta_{b}:=v_{b}^{2}
. Then
v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
implies

\theta_{a},\theta_{b}\in[\,\theta(1-\rho),\ \theta(1+\rho)\,].

Moreover, the denominator becomes

\Bigl(\frac{v_{a}^{2}-v_{b}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}=\Bigl(\frac{\theta_{a}-\theta_{b}}{\theta}\Bigr)^{2}.

Step 1: lower bound.

Fix any selector
D\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)
. For any
v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
,

D(v_{a})\in\mathcal{H}
has variance
\theta_{a}
and
D(v_{b})\in\mathcal{H}
has variance
\theta_{b}
. Therefore

\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\,\|\,D(v_{b}))\ \geq\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{H}:\\
\mathrm{Var}(Q)=\theta_{a}\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{H}:\\
\mathrm{Var}(P)=\theta_{b}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,P).

Take
\Delta:=\theta_{a}-\theta_{b}
. By Assumption
3.1
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists

\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that if
|\Delta|\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}
then

\inf_{\mathrm{Var}(P)=\theta_{b}}\inf_{\mathrm{Var}(Q)=\theta_{a}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\ \geq\ \Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta_{b})}{2}-\varepsilon\Bigr)\Delta^{2}.

Since
\theta_{b}\in[\theta(1-\rho),\theta(1+\rho)]
and
I_{\text{Var}}(\cdot)
is finite at
\theta
under the assumption,
we may choose
\rho
small enough so that
I_{\text{Var}}(\theta_{b})=I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)\,(1+o_{\rho}(1))
uniformly over

\theta_{b}
in this interval. Hence, uniformly for all

v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
with
|\theta_{a}-\theta_{b}|\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}
,

\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\,\|\,D(v_{b}))\ \geq\ \Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}-\varepsilon\Bigr)(\theta_{a}-\theta_{b})^{2}\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)).

Dividing by
\bigl((\theta_{a}-\theta_{b})/\theta\bigr)^{2}
gives

\frac{\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\,\|\,D(v_{b}))}{\bigl((\theta_{a}-\theta_{b})/\theta\bigr)^{2}}\ \geq\ \theta^{2}\Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}-\varepsilon\Bigr)\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)).

Taking the supremum over
v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
(restricting to pairs with sufficiently small
|\theta_{a}-\theta_{b}|
is
allowed since the supremum is over a larger set), then taking the infimum over selectors
D
, yields

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\ \geq\ \theta^{2}\Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}-\varepsilon\Bigr)\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)).

Finally, send first
\rho\downarrow 0
(so
o_{\rho}(1)\to 0
) and then
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
to obtain

\liminf_{\rho\downarrow 0}\ \mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\ \geq\ \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\,I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)\ =\ \frac{\sigma^{4}}{2}\,I_{\text{Var}}(\sigma^{2}).

(B.1)

Step 2: upper bound.

We show the matching upper bound by constructing a selector whose KL matches the quadratic upper expansion.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Assumption
3.1
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all

|\Delta|\leq\delta_{\varepsilon}
,

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{H}:\\
\mathrm{Var}(P)=\theta\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{H}:\\
\mathrm{Var}(Q)=\theta+\Delta\end{subarray}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\ \leq\ \Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}+\varepsilon\Bigr)\Delta^{2}.

Moreover, the assumption provides a
C^{2}
curve
\{P_{\vartheta}:\vartheta\in(\theta-\eta,\theta+\eta)\}\subset\mathcal{H}

with
\mathrm{Var}(P_{\vartheta})=\vartheta
that attains this upper bound up to
o(\Delta^{2})
. Choose
\rho_{0}>0
such that

\theta(1+\rho_{0})<\theta+\eta
and
\theta(1-\rho_{0})>\theta-\eta
. For any
\rho\in(0,\rho_{0})
, define a selector

D\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rho}(\sigma)
by

D(v)\;:=\;P_{v^{2}},\qquad v\in B_{\rho}(\sigma).

Then for any
v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
, writing
\Delta=\theta_{a}-\theta_{b}
, the
C^{2}
curve property yields

\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\|D(v_{b}))=\mathrm{KL}(P_{\theta_{a}}\|P_{\theta_{b}})\ \leq\ \Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}+\varepsilon\Bigr)\Delta^{2}\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)),

uniformly over
v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)
as
\rho\downarrow 0
. Dividing by
\bigl(\Delta/\theta\bigr)^{2}
gives

\sup_{v_{a},v_{b}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma)}\frac{\mathrm{KL}(D(v_{a})\|D(v_{b}))}{\bigl((v_{a}^{2}-v_{b}^{2})/\sigma^{2}\bigr)^{2}}\ \leq\ \theta^{2}\Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}+\varepsilon\Bigr)\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)).

Taking the infimum over selectors yields

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\ \leq\ \theta^{2}\Bigl(\frac{I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)}{2}+\varepsilon\Bigr)\cdot(1+o_{\rho}(1)).

Sending
\rho\downarrow 0
and then
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
gives

\limsup_{\rho\downarrow 0}\ \mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\ \leq\ \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\,I_{\text{Var}}(\theta)\ =\ \frac{\sigma^{4}}{2}\,I_{\text{Var}}(\sigma^{2}).

(B.2)

Step 3: conclude.

Combining (
B.1
) and (
B.2
) yields

\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\mathcal{H})\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{4}}{2}\,I_{\text{Var}}(\sigma^{2})\,\bigl(1+o_{\rho}(1)\bigr),

for
\rho
small enough, where
o_{\rho}(1)\to 0
as
\rho\downarrow 0
. This proves the lemma.

Proof B.2

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.
Fix
i\geq 1
and
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, and set

\bm{v}\;:=\;\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\lambda})\;=\;\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}\,\sqrt{\bm{\lambda}}.

Since
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})
, we have
\sigma_{k}^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\,\lambda_{k}^{\star}
for each

k
, hence

\Bigl|\frac{v_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\Bigr|\;=\;\Bigl|\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}-1\Bigr|\;\leq\;\rho,\qquad k\in[d],

so
v_{k}\in B_{\rho}(\sigma_{k})
and the selector
D_{k}^{(i)}(v_{k})
is well-defined. By construction,

D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\;=\;D^{(i)}(\bm{v})\;=\;\bigl(D_{1}^{(i)}(v_{1}),\dots,D_{d}^{(i)}(v_{d})\bigr),

and each selector satisfies
\mathrm{Var}(D_{k}^{(i)}(v_{k}))=v_{k}^{2}
. Therefore

\bm{\sigma}\!\bigl(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr)=\bm{v}
, and hence

\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{\sigma}(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda}))\bigr)\;=\;\mathcal{N}(\bm{v})\;=\;\frac{\bm{v}^{\odot 2}}{\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}}\;=\;\frac{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\,\bm{\lambda}}{\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}\;=\;\bm{\lambda},

where we used
\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Next, since
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
and

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})))=\bm{\lambda}
, we have

D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})
(and in fact also

R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma}(D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})))=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}/T=R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma})
by the identity above). Hence,
for any policy
\pi
and any
i\geq 1
,

\sup_{\bm{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(\bm{\sigma})}\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D})\;\geq\;\sup_{\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}\mathrm{Regret}\!\bigl(\pi,D_{i}(\bm{\lambda})\bigr).

Taking
\limsup_{i\to\infty}
yields the second display in the lemma. Applying
\inf_{\pi}
to both sides preserves
the inequality, and restricting the infimum to
\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
gives the final
displayed bound for
\mathcal{V}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
.

Proof B.3

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.
Fix
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\in\Delta_{d}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
, and let
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Write

\bm{u}_{x}:=\bm{A}x,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{u}_{x}\Bigr).

Since
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\in\Delta_{d}
has strictly positive coordinates (here
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma})
with

\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
), the componentwise ratio is well-defined and satisfies

\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{x}}{\bm{\lambda}^{\star}}=\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{u}_{x}.

(B.3)

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

Assume
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
. By definition,

\Bigl\|\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{x}}{\bm{\lambda}^{\star}}-\bm{1}\Bigr\|_{2}\leq\rho.

Using (
B.3
), this is equivalent to

\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\leq\rho,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}.

Moreover,
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{d}
implies
\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}_{x}\rangle=1
. Expanding the sum constraint,

\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}_{x}\rangle=\Big\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{u}_{x}\Bigr)\Big\rangle=\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle=1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle,

so
\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}_{x}\rangle=1
is equivalent to
\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle=0
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

Assume
\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle=0
and
\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
.
Then (
B.3
) gives

\Bigl\|\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{x}}{\bm{\lambda}^{\star}}-\bm{1}\Bigr\|_{2}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\leq\rho,

so the
\ell_{\infty}
neighborhood constraint holds. Next, the same expansion as above yields

\langle\bm{1},\bm{\lambda}_{x}\rangle=1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{u}_{x}\rangle=1,

so
\bm{\lambda}_{x}
has unit sum. Finally, for each coordinate
k
,

1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}u_{x,k}\ \geq\ 1-\rho\ \geq\ 0\qquad(\text{since }\rho\in(0,1)\text{ and }u_{x,k}\geq-\|\bm{u}_{x}\|_{2}\geq-\sqrt{d}),

and since
\lambda_{k}^{\star}\geq 0
, this implies
(\lambda_{x})_{k}\geq 0
for all
k
. Therefore
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{d}
.
Combining
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{d}
with the neighborhood bound proves
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
.

The final statement follows immediately: if
\bm{A}\mathcal{X}\subset(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})^{\perp}
, i.e.

\langle\bm{\lambda}^{\star},\bm{A}x\rangle=0

, and

, then condition (ii) holds for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, hence

\{\bm{\lambda}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
.

Proof B.4

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.
Fix a policy
\pi
, an index
i\geq 1
, and a label
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Let
\bm{\sigma}_{x}:=\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})

denote the standard-deviation vector associated with
\bm{\lambda}_{x}
, so that by construction of the instance
generator,

\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{D}_{i}(x)\bigr)=\bm{\sigma}_{x},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}_{x})=\bm{\lambda}_{x}.

Run
\pi
on
\bm{D}_{i}(x)
and let
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
be the terminal pull counts, with terminal decision

\bar{\bm{\lambda}}:=\bm{n}/T
. For this realized
\bm{n}
, the risk is

R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}_{x})=\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\sigma_{x,k}^{2}}{n_{k}},

with the convention
R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}_{x})=+\infty
if
n_{k}=0
for some
k
with
\sigma_{x,k}>0
. Since

\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\sigma}_{x})
, we can write
\sigma_{x,k}^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}_{x}\|_{2}^{2}\,\lambda_{x,k}
for
each
k
, hence

R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}_{x})=\|\bm{\sigma}_{x}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\lambda_{x,k}}{n_{k}}.

Moreover,

R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma}_{x})=\frac{\|\bm{\sigma}_{x}\|_{2}^{2}}{T},

so for every realized
\bm{n}
,

\frac{R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}_{x})}{R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma}_{x})}-1=\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{T\,\lambda_{x,k}}{n_{k}}-1=\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\lambda_{x,k}}{\bar{\lambda}_{k}}-1=r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x}),

where in the last step we used the definition of
r
in (
A.2
) (with the convention

r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x})=+\infty
if
\bar{\lambda}_{k}=0
for some
k
).

Taking expectation over the randomness of the policy and the samples (equivalently, over the induced random
terminal decision
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
) yields

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{R(\bm{n};\bm{\sigma}_{x})}{R^{\star}(\bm{\sigma}_{x})}-1\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right],

which is the claimed separation identity.

Proof B.5

Proof of Lemma
A.6
.
Fix the true label
x\in\mathcal{X}
and a realization of the terminal decision
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\in\Delta_{d}
.
Let
\bar{x}\in\text{argmin}_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}})
be the minimum-mismatch decoder
(Definition
A.4
). By definition of
\tilde{r}
(Definition
A.5
),
for any
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}
,

\max\!\Big\{r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x}),\ r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\Big\}\ \geq\ \tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}).

Applying this inequality with
\bm{\lambda}=\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
yields

\max\!\Big\{r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x}),\ r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\Big\}\ \geq\ \tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}).

Moreover, since
\bar{x}
minimizes
r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}})
over
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\leq r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})
, hence the maximum on the
left-hand side equals
r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})
. Therefore,

r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})\ \geq\ \tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}).

Taking expectation over the randomness of
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
(induced by the policy and samples) gives the first
inequality in the lemma:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right]\ \geq\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\right].

For the second inequality, decompose according to the event
\{\bar{x}=x\}
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\,\mathbf{1}\{\bar{x}=x\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\,\mathbf{1}\{\bar{x}\neq x\}\right].

Since
\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{x})=0
(take
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\lambda}_{x}
in the defining infimum), the
first term is
0
. On the event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
we have

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\geq\min_{b\neq x}\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{b})
, hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})\right]\ \geq\ \Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)\cdot\min_{b\neq x}\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{b}),

which is the desired bound.

Proof B.6

Proof of Lemma
A.7
.
Fix
\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b}\in\Delta_{d}
. Recall that

r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a})=\left\|\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{a}}{\bm{\lambda}}\right\|_{\infty}-1=\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{\lambda_{k,a}}{\lambda_{k}}-1,

with the convention
r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a})=+\infty
if
\lambda_{k}=0
for some
k
with
\lambda_{k,a}>0
.
We start with the following elementary fact:

\forall\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0},\qquad\Delta_{d}\cap(\bm{u},+\infty)\neq\emptyset\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{k=1}^{d}u_{k}<1,

(B.4)

where
(\bm{u},+\infty):=\{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \lambda_{k}>u_{k}\ \forall k\}
.

Let
t\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})<t

\displaystyle\iff\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}}\max\!\bigl(r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}),\,r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{b})\bigr)<t

\displaystyle\iff\exists\,\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}:\ \max\!\bigl(r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}),\,r(\bm{\lambda}\|\bm{\lambda}_{b})\bigr)<t

\displaystyle\iff\exists\,\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}:\ \max_{k\in[d],\,u\in\{a,b\}}\frac{\lambda_{k,u}}{\lambda_{k}}<1+t

\displaystyle\iff\exists\,\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{d}:\ \forall k\in[d],\ \frac{1}{1+t}\max(\lambda_{k,a},\lambda_{k,b})<\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle\iff\frac{1}{1+t}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\max(\lambda_{k,a},\lambda_{k,b})<1\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:fact-simplex-lowerbounds})}

\displaystyle\iff\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\max(\lambda_{k,a},\lambda_{k,b})\Big)-1<t.

Since this equivalence holds for all
t\geq 0
, it follows that

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\max(\lambda_{k,a},\lambda_{k,b})\Big)-1.

Finally, using
\max(x,y)=\frac{x+y+|x-y|}{2}
and
\sum_{k}\lambda_{k,a}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k,b}=1
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{d}\max(\lambda_{k,a},\lambda_{k,b})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{k,a}+\lambda_{k,b}+|\lambda_{k,a}-\lambda_{k,b}|\bigr)=1+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}-\bm{\lambda}_{b}\|_{1}.

Therefore,

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{a},\bm{\lambda}_{b})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\lambda}_{a}-\bm{\lambda}_{b}\|_{1},

as claimed.

Proof B.7

Proof of Lemma
A.8
.
Fix a nonempty
\mathcal{X}\subset\{\pm 1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
, a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and a policy

\pi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\rho,\tau}(\bm{\sigma})
. Let
x\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
and let
\mathcal{F}_{T}
denote
the bandit transcript generated by running
\pi
on the instance
\bm{D}_{i}(x)
. For any decoder
\bar{x}=\bar{x}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
,
Fano’s inequality gives

\Pr(\bar{x}\neq x)\ \geq\ 1-\frac{\log 2+I(x;\mathcal{F}_{T})}{\log|\mathcal{X}|}.

(B.5)

Moreover, since
x
is uniform, the mutual information admits the standard upper bound

I(x;\mathcal{F}_{T})\ \leq\ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{KL}\!\Big((\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\,\Big\|\,(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(y))\Big),

(B.6)

where
(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))
denotes the law of the transcript under policy
\pi
and instance
\bm{D}_{i}(x)
.

Step 1: bandit KL decomposition.

For fixed
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, the canonical KL decomposition for bandit transcripts yields

\mathrm{KL}\!\Big((\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\,\Big\|\,(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(y))\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{n}\sim(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))}[n_{k}]\;\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})\,\Big\|\,D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{y})\Big),

(B.7)

where
D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})
denotes the
k
-th arm distribution in
\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})
.

Step 2: controlling pull counts via tolerance.

Let
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}:=\bm{n}/T
be the terminal decision under
(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))
. By Lemma
A.3
,

\mathrm{Regret}(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right]\leq\tau.

Since
r(\cdot\|\bm{\lambda}_{x})
is convex in its first argument, Jensen’s inequality implies

r\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bm{\lambda}}]\,\big\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x}\big)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[r(\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\,\|\,\bm{\lambda}_{x})\right]\ \leq\ \tau.

Unpacking the definition
r(\bm{u}\|\bm{v})=\|\bm{v}/\bm{u}\|_{\infty}-1
, this gives, for every
k\in[d]
,

\frac{(\lambda_{x})_{k}}{\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]}-1\ \leq\ \tau\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]\ \geq\ \frac{(\lambda_{x})_{k}}{1+\tau}.

Using
\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]=1
, we obtain the complementary upper bound

\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]=1-\sum_{j\neq k}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{j}]\ \leq\ 1-\frac{1}{1+\tau}\sum_{j\neq k}(\lambda_{x})_{j}=\frac{(\lambda_{x})_{k}+\tau}{1+\tau}\ \leq\ (\lambda_{x})_{k}+\tau.

Since
\bm{\lambda}_{x}\in\Delta_{\rho}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
, we have
(\lambda_{x})_{k}\leq(1+\rho)\lambda_{k}^{\star}
, hence

\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]\ \leq\ (1+\rho)\lambda_{k}^{\star}+\tau\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau))\,\lambda_{k}^{\star},

(B.8)

where one may take, e.g.,
\varepsilon(\rho,\tau):=\rho+\tau/\min_{j\in[d]}\lambda_{j}^{\star}
, which satisfies

\varepsilon(\rho,\tau)\to 0
as
(\rho,\tau)\to(0,0)
for fixed
\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\in\Delta_{d}
.

Combining (
B.8
) with
\mathbb{E}[n_{k}]=T\,\mathbb{E}[\bar{\lambda}_{k}]
yields

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{n}\sim(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))}[n_{k}]\ \leq\ T\,\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau)).

(B.9)

Step 3: controlling arm-level KL via
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}
.

By construction of the instance generator, the variance of arm
k
under
\bm{D}_{i}(\bm{\lambda})
is

\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda})^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\,\lambda_{k}
. Using the selectors in (
A.1
)
and the definition of
\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})
, we have

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})\,\Big\|\,D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{y})\Big)\ \leq\ \mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\,\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})^{2}-\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{y})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}.

Since
\sigma_{k}^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\,\lambda_{k}^{\star}
and
\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda})^{2}=\|\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\,\lambda_{k}
, we get

\frac{\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})^{2}-\sigma_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{y})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}=\frac{(\lambda_{x})_{k}-(\lambda_{y})_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}.

Under the parametrization
\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{A}x)
,

(\lambda_{x})_{k}-(\lambda_{y})_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{\star}\cdot\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,[\bm{A}(x-y)]_{k},

so the ratio equals
\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}[\bm{A}(x-y)]_{k}
, and therefore

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{x})\,\Big\|\,D^{(i)}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}_{y})\Big)\ \leq\ \frac{\rho^{2}}{d}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\,[\bm{A}(x-y)]_{k}^{2}.

(B.10)

Step 4: averaging and Frobenius form.

Plugging (
B.9
) and (
B.10
) into (
B.7
) gives

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{KL}\!\Big((\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\,\Big\|\,(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(y))\Big)\ \leq\ \frac{T\rho^{2}}{d}\,(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau))\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\,[\bm{A}(x-y)]_{k}^{2}.

With
\bm{\Gamma}:=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H}))_{k=1}^{d}
, the sum equals

(x-y)^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{A}(x-y)
. Averaging over
x,y\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
i.i.d., and using

\mathbb{E}[(x-y)(x-y)^{\top}]=2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X}))=2\bm{\Sigma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\to\infty}\ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{KL}\!\Big((\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(x))\,\Big\|\,(\pi,\bm{D}_{i}(y))\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T\rho^{2}}{d}\,(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau))\cdot 2\,\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Gamma}\,\bm{A}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{A}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\frac{2T\rho^{2}}{d}\,(1+\varepsilon(\rho,\tau))\,\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\,\bm{A}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}^{2}.

Combining this bound with (
B.5
)–(
B.6
) yields the stated inequality.

Proof B.8

Proof of Lemma

.
Fix
i\geq 1
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
. On the constructed family we have

\bm{\lambda}_{x}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}x\Bigr),\qquad\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\Bigl(\bm{1}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{A}\bar{x}\Bigr).

Therefore

\bm{\lambda}_{x}-\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\odot\bigl(\bm{A}(x-\bar{x})\bigr)=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(x-\bar{x}),

where
\bm{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})
. By Lemma
A.7
,

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\lambda}_{x}-\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}}\|_{1}=\frac{\rho}{2\sqrt{d}}\,\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(x-\bar{x})\big\|_{1}.

Next, recall
\bm{P}_{\neq}:=\text{diag}(\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq\bar{x}_{k}\})
. For each coordinate
k
,
if
x_{k}=\bar{x}_{k}
then
(x-\bar{x})_{k}=0
, and if
x_{k}\neq\bar{x}_{k}
then necessarily
\bar{x}_{k}=-x_{k}
and

(x-\bar{x})_{k}=2x_{k}
. Hence

x-\bar{x}\;=\;2\,\bm{P}_{\neq}x,

and plugging this into the previous display yields, on the event
\{\bar{x}\neq x\}
(and in fact always),

\tilde{r}(\bm{\lambda}_{x},\bm{\lambda}_{\bar{x}})=\frac{\rho}{2\sqrt{d}}\,\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(2\,\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d}}\,\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\,\bm{A}\,(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}.

Proof B.9

Proof of Lemma
A.10
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of subsets of
\{-1,1\}^{d}
satisfying both orthogonality and separation, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}:=\Bigl\{\mathcal{X}\subset\{-1,1\}^{d}:\ \mathcal{X}\subset\bm{1}^{\perp},\ \min_{x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\geq\frac{d}{4}\Bigr\}.

Let
m
be the largest size in
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.,

m:=\max_{\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{X}|.

Proving the lemma boils down to proving
\log m=\Omega(d)
. First, notice that
\mathcal{E}
is non-empty, since the singleton

\Bigl\{(1,\ldots,1,-1,\ldots,-1)\Bigr\}

(consisting of
d/2
ones and
d/2
minus ones) lies in
\mathcal{E}
. Let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{E}
be a set of
maximal size. We introduce the balls associated with the
0
-norm
\|x\|_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq 0\}
:
for
x\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
and
r>0
,

B_{0}(x,r):=\Bigl\{y\in\{-1,1\}^{d}:\ \|x-y\|_{0}<r\Bigr\}.

We claim that

\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}=\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\Bigl(B_{0}(y,d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}\Bigr).

(B.11)

The inclusion
\subseteq
is immediate. Assume for the sake of contradiction that it is strict, i.e., there exists

x\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
with
x\perp\bm{1}
such that

\forall y\in\mathcal{S},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq y_{k}\}\geq\frac{d}{4},

or equivalently
\min_{y\in\mathcal{S}}d_{H}(x,y)\geq d/4
. In particular
x\notin\mathcal{S}
, and the new set

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{S}\cup\{x\}
still satisfies orthogonality and separation, hence
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
,
contradicting the maximality of
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore (
B.11
) holds.

Next, using (
B.11
) and a union bound,

|\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|=\Bigl|\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(B_{0}(y,d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}\bigr)\Bigr|\leq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl|B_{0}(y,d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}\bigr|.

By symmetry of
\{-1,1\}^{d}
, the sets
B_{0}(y,d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
have constant size over

\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
. In particular, they have the same size as

B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
, where
y_{0}=(1,\ldots,1,-1,\ldots,-1)
consists of
d/2
ones and
d/2
minus ones.
Therefore,

|\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|\leq|\mathcal{S}|\cdot|B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|=m\cdot|B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|,

or equivalently

m\ \geq\ \frac{|\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|}{|B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|}.

It remains to prove that this log-ratio grows linearly in
d
. The numerator can be calculated easily: choosing an
element from
\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
is equivalent to choosing
d/2
placements for
+1
and setting the
remaining
d/2
placements to
-1
, hence

|\{-1,1\}^{d}\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|=\binom{d}{d/2}.

We now upper bound the denominator. Let
x\in B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}
and let

u:=\|x-y_{0}\|_{0}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\lfloor d/4\rfloor\}
. We claim that
u
must be even. Indeed, for each
k\in[d]
,

\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq y_{0,k}\}=\frac{1}{2}|x_{k}-y_{0,k}|\equiv\frac{x_{k}-y_{0,k}}{2}\pmod{2},

so

u=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}\{x_{k}\neq y_{0,k}\}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{k}-y_{0,k})=\frac{1}{2}(\langle\bm{1},x\rangle-\langle\bm{1},y_{0}\rangle)\equiv 0\pmod{2},

where we used
x\perp\bm{1}
and
y_{0}\perp\bm{1}
. Hence
2\mid u
, and we write
u=2u^{\prime}
.

For
u=2u^{\prime}
, choosing
x
such that
\|x-y_{0}\|_{0}=2u^{\prime}
amounts to choosing exactly
u^{\prime}
coordinates among the

+1
entries of
y_{0}
and
u^{\prime}
coordinates among the
-1
entries of
y_{0}
to flip, hence there are

\binom{d/2}{u^{\prime}}^{2}
such vectors. Therefore,

|B_{0}(y_{0},d/4)\cap\bm{1}^{\perp}|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u<d/4\\
2\mid u\end{subarray}}\binom{d/4}{u/2}^{2}=\sum_{u^{\prime}<d/8}\binom{d/4}{u^{\prime}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{\lfloor d/4\rfloor}\binom{d/4}{u}^{2}\leq\binom{d/4}{d/8},

where the last inequality follows from Vandermonde’s inequality. Consequently,

m\ \geq\ 2\cdot\frac{\binom{d}{d/2}}{\binom{d/4}{d/8}}=\frac{2\,d!\,[(d/8)!]^{2}}{[(d/2)!]^{2}\,(d/4)!}.

We show that the lower bound above grows exponentially in
d
. By Stirling’s formula,

n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}\Bigl(\frac{n}{e}\Bigr)^{n}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\Bigr),

so a direct substitution yields

\frac{2\,d!\,[(d/8)!]^{2}}{[(d/2)!]^{2}\,(d/4)!}=16\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{d}\Bigr)\Bigr)\cdot 2^{3d/4}.

Therefore, there exists
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{E}
such that
\log|\mathcal{X}|=\Omega(d)
, completing the proof.

Proof B.10

Proof of Lemma
A.11
.
Fix a policy (and hence a decoder
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
) and write

\bm{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})),\qquad\bm{\Lambda}:=\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star}),\qquad\bm{\Gamma}:=\text{diag}\!\Big(\lambda_{k}^{\star}\,\text{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})\Big)_{k=1}^{d}.

Let
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}:=\bm{\lambda}^{\star}/\|\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\|_{2}
and define the constrained orthogonal class

\mathsf{O}(\bm{1}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\lambda}})\;:=\;\Bigl\{\bm{O}\in\mathsf{O}(d):\ \bm{O}\,\frac{\bm{1}}{\sqrt{d}}=\hat{\bm{\lambda}}\Bigr\}.

Let
x\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{X})
and
\bm{O}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{O}(\bm{1}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\lambda}}))
be independent,
and set
\bm{A}:=\bm{S}\bm{O}
, where
\bm{S}=\text{diag}(\bm{s})
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries. For a realization
(\bm{O},x)
define

Since
\mathcal{X}\subset\bm{1}^{\perp}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[x]\in\bm{1}^{\perp}
and
\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{1}=\bm{0}
, hence

\mathrm{range}(\bm{\Sigma})\subset\bm{1}^{\perp}

\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{O}^{\top})
and invariance of the uniform law on

\mathsf{O}(\bm{1}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\lambda}})
on the orthogonal complement

, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{O},x}\!\left[V(\bm{O})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{O},x}\!\left[\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{O}^{\top})\right]=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{S})\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma})}{d-1}.

On the event

, since
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\min_{x\neq x^{\prime}}d_{H}(x,x^{\prime})\geq d/4

x-\bar{x}=2\bm{P}_{\neq}x
and
x-\bar{x}\in\bm{1}^{\perp}

\bm{P}_{\neq}x\in\bm{1}^{\perp}

\|\bm{P}_{\neq}x\|_{2}=\sqrt{d_{H}(x,\bar{x})}\ \geq\ \sqrt{d/4}.

\bm{O}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{O}(\bm{1}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\lambda}}))
,
the random vector
\bm{O}v

in
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{\perp}
.
Let

and set

U(\bm{O}_{\star},x)\geq c\,V(\bm{O}_{\star})
, i.e.

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}_{\star}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \middle|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}_{\star}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}}\ \geq\ c\geq\frac{\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\star}s_{k}\sqrt{1-\left(\lambda_{k}^{\star}/\|\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\|_{2}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{S})\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma})}{d-1}}}.

Using
\text{Tr}(\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\star}\text{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})s_{k}^{2}
and
\text{Tr}(\Sigma)\leq d
, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}_{\star}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \middle|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{S}\,\bm{O}_{\star}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}}\ \geq\frac{\frac{d-1}{d}\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\star}s_{k}\sqrt{1-\left(\lambda_{k}^{\star}/\|\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\|_{2}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\star}\text{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})s_{k}^{2}}}.

Taking the argmax of the RHS above over
\bm{s}>\bm{0}_{d}
with
\|\bm{s}\|_{2}=1
and setting
\bm{S}_{\star}=\text{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})
, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|\bm{\Lambda}\bm{S}_{\star}\,\bm{O}_{\star}(\bm{P}_{\neq}x)\big\|_{1}\ \middle|\ \bar{x}\neq x\right]}{\big\|\sqrt{\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{S}_{\star}\,\bm{O}_{\star}\,\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\big\|_{F}}\ \gtrsim\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\star}\left(1-\left(\lambda_{k}^{\star}/\|\bm{\lambda}^{\star}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\right)}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Het}_{k}(\bm{\lambda}^{\star})}{\mathrm{VLC}_{\rho}(\sigma_{k}\mid\mathcal{H})}},

where
\gtrsim
ignores numerical constants and error terms. This yields the desired inequality. Thus the lemma is proved for
\bm{A_{\star}}=\bm{S}_{\star}\bm{O}_{\star}
. Finally, the ratio in the LHS is scale-invariant in
\bm{A}
: replacing
\bm{A}
by
t\bm{A}
multiplies both numerator and
denominator by
t
.
