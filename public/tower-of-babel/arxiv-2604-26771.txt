Abstract
We present the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf spectral Atlas (SUDA), a homogeneous sample of 1675 ultracool dwarfs with continuous 0.75–5 μ \mu m spectroscopy from SPHEREx QR2. Using the SAND and ATMO2020++ atmospheric model grids, we derive atmospheric parameters and calculate bolometric luminosities ( L bol L_{\rm bol} ). We combine the inferred T eff T_{\mathrm{eff}} and radii with evolutionary tracks to estimate masses and ages for the full sample. Evolutionary surface gravities ( log ⁡ g \log g ) are also reported for sources with parallaxes. In the 1700–2500 K range, the atmospheric log ⁡ g \log g from spectral fitting are systematically lower than the evolutionary log ⁡ g \log g , with a median offset of about 1.1 dex, likely reflecting residual degeneracy between log ⁡ g \log g and metallicity in low resolution SPHEREx spectra. We also construct an empirical spectral atlas by grouping the spectra in the adopted parameter space, using the T eff T_{\mathrm{eff}} and the evolutionary log ⁡ g \log g . The resulting atlas contains 52 parameter bins and spans T eff ≃ 700 T_{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq 700 – 3000 3000 K. Molecular indices reveal a coherent atmospheric sequence across the sample. H 2 O and CH 4 strengthen toward lower T eff T_{\mathrm{eff}} , whereas CO and CO 2 rise below ∼ \sim 1500 K and turn over near ∼ \sim 1000 K. Comparison with model sequences shows that the CO 2 index is a useful empirical metallicity tracer at T eff ∼ 800 T_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 800 –1300 K. Together, these results establish SUDA as a reference sample for linking observed 0.75–5 μ \mu m spectral morphology to atmospheric and evolutionary trends in ultracool dwarfs.



I
Introduction

Ultracool dwarfs, spanning the latest M dwarfs through the L, T, and Y spectral classes, occupy the coolest and lowest mass end of stellar and substellar populations
(
J. D. Kirkpatrick et al. 1999
;
A. J. Burgasser et al. 2002
;
M. C. Cushing et al. 2011
)
. Their atmospheric parameters, including effective temperature (
T_{\rm eff}
), surface gravity (
\log g
), and metallicity ([M/H]), together with derived physical properties such as bolometric luminosity (
L_{\rm bol}
), mass, radius, and age, are central to understanding star formation at the lowest masses, the boundary between stars and brown dwarfs, and the connection between stars, brown dwarfs, and giant exoplanets. These quantities are also relevant to the radiation environments and potential habitability of planets around low mass stars, since stellar activity and ultraviolet emission in M dwarfs evolve with spectral type and age and strongly affect circumstellar habitable zone conditions
(
B. Stelzer et al. 2013
;
E. L. Shkolnik & T. S. Barman 2014
;
X. Li et al. 2024
;
X. Li et al. 2025
)
. Because ultracool dwarfs emit most of their energy in the near and mid infrared, infrared spectroscopy is essential for reliable physical characterization
(
S. K. Leggett & P. Tremblin 2025
;
W. W. Meynardie et al. 2025
;
M. Voyer et al. 2025
;
A. Lueber et al. 2026
)
.

Among these quantities, the bolometric luminosity plays a central and unifying role. Unlike atmospheric parameters inferred from spectral fitting,
L_{\rm bol}
is comparatively independent of atmospheric models and links spectroscopy to derived physical properties through evolutionary models
(
I. Baraffe et al. 2015
;
M. W. Phillips et al. 2020
;
G. Chabrier et al. 2023
)
. Accurate determinations of
L_{\rm bol}
are critical for constraining masses, radii, and ages, and for breaking degeneracies among
T_{\rm eff}
,
\log g
, and metallicity that commonly arise in atmospheric fitting
(
A. Sanghi et al. 2023
;
S. A. Beiler et al. 2024
)
. Improving the accuracy and homogeneity of bolometric luminosity measurements is therefore essential for a more physically consistent view of the ultracool dwarf population.

One persistent limitation has been wavelength completeness. As ultracool atmospheres cool, an increasing fraction of the emergent flux shifts beyond
\sim
2.5
\mu
m, making the 3–5
\mu
m region critical for both luminosity estimates and atmospheric interpretation
(
D. Saumon & M. S. Marley 2008
;
M. S. Marley et al. 2021
)
. This wavelength range also contains key molecular diagnostics, especially CO and CO
2
, that are sensitive to chemistry, composition, and thermal structure in the coldest brown dwarfs
(
B. W. P. Lew et al. 2024
;
E. Manjavacas et al. 2024
;
J. K. Faherty et al. 2025
)
.

Previous large ultracool dwarf spectroscopic studies focused on optical and near infrared wavelengths shortward of
\sim
2.5
\mu
m, with widely used libraries built primarily from facilities such as IRTF/SpeX and Magellan/FIRE
(
Z. Zhang et al. 2021
;
A. J. Burgasser et al. 2025
;
E. Mader et al. 2026
)
. As a result, many luminosity estimates still rely on heterogeneous SED assembly, combining partial spectra with sparse mid infrared constraints and model interpolation or extrapolation across unobserved regions
(
S. B. Dieterich et al. 2014
;
J. C. Filippazzo et al. 2015
;
A. Sanghi et al. 2023
)
. For example,
A. Sanghi et al. 2023
derived atmospheric parameters and physical properties for more than a thousand ultracool dwarfs from optical to mid infrared SED compilations and atmosphere model comparisons. That work established an important benchmark, but the spectroscopic coverage was limited to 1–2.5
\mu
m, with the remainder of the SED constrained only by photometry.

The SPHEREx (Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer) mission provides a direct way to overcome this limitation through all sky spectra from 0.75–5
\mu
m with continuous wavelength coverage
(
P. M. Korngut et al. 2018
;
B. P. Crill et al. 2020
;
J. J. Bock et al. 2025
;
R. Akeson et al. 2025
;
SPHEREx Team 2025
)
. Its combination of full sky coverage and continuous infrared spectroscopy enables homogeneous characterization of a vast ultracool dwarf population. In particular, SPHEREx provides uniform access to the 3–5
\mu
m regime, where molecular opacity windows and thermal emission contribute substantially to both bolometric flux and atmospheric diagnostics. A recent SPHEREx based study has constructed the largest spectral sample of ultracool dwarfs to date, demonstrating the value of these data for population level studies of ultracool dwarf atmospheres and spectral diversity
(
J. Gagné et al. 2026
)
.

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has demonstrated the diagnostic power of 0.75–5
\mu
m spectroscopy for cold brown dwarfs
(
K. L. Luhman et al. 2024
;
B. W. P. Lew et al. 2024
;
Z. Tu et al. 2025a
;
Z. Tu et al. 2025b
)
, but necessarily in relatively small targeted samples. SPHEREx complements JWST by providing spectroscopy at lower resolution but for orders of magnitude more objects in a homogeneous survey, enabling population scale studies that were previously impractical.

In this work, we use SPHEREx quick release spectra to construct the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf spectral Atlas (SUDA) and derive a uniform set of atmospheric parameters together with derived physical properties for a large sample selected from UltracoolSheet
(
W. M. J. Best et al. 2024a
)
. The key advance is the combination of sample size and continuous 0.75–5
\mu
m coverage, which allows us to examine atmospheric parameters, bolometric luminosities, an empirical spectral atlas, and carbon bearing molecular behavior within one homogeneous sample. The following sections describe the data, sample construction, and fitting methodology, and then present the resulting bolometric luminosities and evolutionary constraints, the empirical spectral atlas, and molecular index trends. Details of sample quality control, flux calibration, and supporting analyses are provided in the appendices.

II
Data and Sample

This section describes the SPHEREx spectral data, the sample construction, and the screening steps that define the final SUDA sample.

II.1
SPHEREx Spectral Data

SPHEREx provides the continuous 0.75–5.0
\mu
m spectroscopy needed to study ultracool dwarfs within one homogeneous survey. The spectra used in this work were retrieved from the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA), and this wavelength coverage includes the near and mid infrared regimes where ultracool stars and brown dwarfs emit most of their bolometric flux, especially the critical
3
–
5~\mu
m region. Observations are obtained with the Wide Field Linear Variable Filter (LVF) Imaging Spectrograph.

SPHEREx provides spectra with an angular resolution of
6.15^{\prime\prime}
, corresponding to its photometric spatial resolution. This enables systematic studies of isolated ultracool stars and brown dwarfs across the sky, while motivating careful treatment of unresolved multiples and crowded regions (Appendix
A
).

IRSA began weekly releases of SPHEREx spectral image data in July 2025 as Quick Release 1 (QR1). In October 2025, IRSA initiated Quick Release 2 (QR2), which reprocessed the full archive with improved calibration and data quality. All spectra used in this work are drawn from QR2.

SPHEREx spectra are delivered in six bands with continuous coverage from
0.75
to
5.0~\mu
m. The four bands at
0.75
–
3.82~\mu
m provide low resolution spectra with typical resolving power
R\sim 40
, while the two bands at
3.82
–
5.0~\mu
m provide higher spectral resolution at
R\sim 110
–130. Preflight simulations predict
5\sigma
point source sensitivities of
m_{\rm AB}\sim 18.5
–19 at
0.75
–
3.8~\mu
m and
m_{\rm AB}\sim 16.6
–18 at
3.8
–
5.0~\mu
m
(
B. P. Crill et al. 2025
)
.

II.2
Sample Selection from the UltracoolSheet

We selected the SUDA targets from UltracoolSheet, propagated them to the SPHEREx epoch, and matched them to SPHEREx QR2 spectra to build the final analysis sample. UltracoolSheet

We adopt the J2000 coordinates (RA, Dec) from UltracoolSheet and propagate the source positions to the SPHEREx observing epoch using reported proper motions. III
Atmospheric Model Fitting

We use a uniform two stage fitting pipeline to derive atmospheric parameters for the full SPHEREx sample. We first perform minimum
\chi^{2}
fitting on model grids to identify an initial solution for each source, then refine that solution with nested sampling Bayesian inference.

III.1
Initial Grid Based
\chi^{2}
Fitting

We first use grid based
\chi^{2}
fitting to obtain an initial atmospheric solution for each spectrum. Our primary model grid is the Spectral ANalog of Dwarfs (SAND;
E. Alvarado et al. 2024
), which spans 700–4000 K and covers the broad surface gravity and metallicity range required by this sample. Its demonstrated performance across L/T transition spectra
(
Z. Tu et al. 2025b
)
also makes it well suited to our heterogeneous data set.

We evaluated
\chi^{2}
for all recommended
2
2

2

https://zenodo.org/records/11582126
.
SAND spectra after convolving models to the SPHEREx resolution (
R=40
over
0.7
–
3.8~\mu
m and
R=110
over
3.8
–
5.0~\mu
m). Following Equations (6) and (7) in
S. A. Beiler et al. 2023
, we solve for a wavelength independent flux scaling and adopt the minimum
\chi^{2}
parameter set as the preliminary solution for each object.

For a subset of very cool sources, we also fit with the ATMO2020++ model grid (
A. M. Meisner et al. 2023
), which is optimized for late T/Y regimes and now spans
[\mathrm{M/H}]=-1.0
to
+0.3
. For objects with preliminary SAND fits of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq 1300
K, we repeat the same minimum
\chi^{2}
procedure with ATMO2020++ and adopt the model family with the smaller reduced
\chi^{2}
.

III.2
Bayesian Parameter Inference with Nested Sampling

We then refine those initial solutions with Bayesian inference to obtain the final parameter estimates and uncertainties. After the initial
\chi^{2}
step, we use the
UltraNest
nested sampling framework
(
J. Buchner 2016
;
J. Buchner 2019
;
J. Buchner 2021
)
for each source. For each object, we use the atmospheric model family (SAND or ATMO2020++) preferred by the initial optimization.

For a given model grid, we linearly interpolate in all available parameter dimensions in logarithmic flux space. For SAND, the native grid at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}<2500
K covers
\log g=4.0
–6.0. For sources with initial solutions at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}<2500
K and
\log g=4.0
, we extend the grid to
\log g=3.5
by linear extrapolation in logarithmic flux space using the two lowest
\log g
points at fixed
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, [M/H], and [
\alpha
/Fe]. Extrapolations below
\log g=3.5
are not allowed.

Posterior sampling is performed under a Gaussian likelihood. We define an effective variance at each wavelength point
i
as

\sigma_{\mathrm{eff},i}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}+\left(f\,F_{\mathrm{obs},i}\right)^{2},

(1)

where
\sigma_{i}
is the reported observational uncertainty and
f
is a fractional error inflation parameter. The log likelihood is then given by

\ln\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\left(F_{\mathrm{obs},i}-F_{\mathrm{mod},i}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{eff},i}^{2}}+\ln\left(2\pi\sigma_{\mathrm{eff},i}^{2}\right)\right].

(2)

Here
F_{\mathrm{obs},i}
is the observed flux at wavelength point
i
, and
F_{\mathrm{mod},i}
is the corresponding model flux after convolution to instrumental resolution and flux scaling.

Prior ranges are informed by the initial
\chi^{2}
solution:
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\pm 200
K,
\log g\pm 1
dex, and uniform priors over the full model grid ranges for remaining parameters (including [M/H] and, when available, [
\alpha
/Fe]). For the flux scaling parameter
\log(R^{2}/D^{2})
, we use a uniform prior centered on the initial
\chi^{2}
value with half width 0.1 dex. The fractional error inflation parameter is assigned a uniform prior of
0\leq f\leq 0.2
.

From the nested sampling posteriors, we adopt medians as parameter estimates and the 16th/84th percentiles as uncertainties.

IV.1
Bolometric Fluxes and Bolometric Luminosities

IV.1.1
Bolometric Flux Estimation

We derive bolometric fluxes by integrating composite spectra that combine the observed data with the best fitting models. The observed spectrum is used where data are available, and the model is used outside the observed wavelength range.

For sources for which SAND provides the best fit, the observed spectrum replaces the model over the observed range in the composite SED. Because the SAND grid spans
\sim 0.1
–
999\,\mu
m, no extra end extrapolation is required.

For sources for which ATMO2020++ provides the best fit, we use the same integration scheme but account for the more limited model coverage (
\sim 0.2
–
30\,\mu
m): a linear extrapolation is applied at
\lambda<0.2\,\mu
m and a Rayleigh Jeans tail at
\lambda>30\,\mu
m.

Uncertainties in
F_{\mathrm{bol}}
are propagated with Monte Carlo sampling. In each realization, atmospheric parameters and flux scaling are drawn from their posteriors, observed fluxes are perturbed within reported errors, and an additional 5% absolute calibration term is applied (Appendix
A.2
). We then recompute
F_{\mathrm{bol}}
with the same composite integration procedure.

IV.1.2
Bolometric Luminosities from Parallax

For sources with parallaxes, we convert the measured bolometric fluxes into direct estimates of
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
. Bolometric luminosities are computed from the bolometric flux as

L_{\mathrm{bol}}=4\pi d^{2}F_{\mathrm{bol}},

(3)

where
d
is the distance to the source. Distance uncertainties are propagated by sampling
d
for each Monte Carlo realization in the conversion from
F_{\mathrm{bol}}
to
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
. Bolometric luminosities are reported in units of
\log(L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\odot})
, adopting a solar bolometric luminosity of
L_{\odot}=3.828\times 10^{33}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}

(
E. E. Mamajek et al. 2015
)
.

IV.1.3
Machine Learning Luminosity Estimates

For sources without parallaxes, we estimate
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
from atmospheric parameters with XGBoost. Because
F_{\mathrm{bol}}
cannot be converted to
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
without a distance estimate, we adopt a supervised machine learning estimator that predicts
\log(L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\odot})
from atmospheric parameters instead of relying on empirical relations between spectral type and absolute magnitude
(
T. J. Dupuy & M. C. Liu 2012
;
A. Sanghi et al. 2023
)
.

We use an XGBoost regressor to map
(T_{\mathrm{eff}},\,\log g,\,[\mathrm{M/H}])
to
\log(L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\odot})
. Because these inputs are available for the full sample, the estimator can be applied uniformly. This ML estimate is conceptually distinct from the luminosity calculation based on parallax. XGBoost is a gradient boosted decision tree algorithm that has been widely adopted in astronomy for regression and classification problems owing to its flexibility, robustness to nonlinear parameter dependencies, and strong performance on heterogeneous data sets
(
R. Andrae et al. 2023
;
H. Zhao et al. 2024
;
J.-H. Wang et al. 2025
;
Z. Zhang & Y. Li 2025
)
.

For each source without parallax, we report the luminosity predicted by the ML model,
\log(L_{\mathrm{bol}}/L_{\odot})
, and its uncertainty from Monte Carlo propagation of atmospheric parameter uncertainties through the trained model. These ML luminosities are used only for sources without parallax. Details of model training and validation are given in Appendix
D
.

(T_{\mathrm{eff}},R)

For sources with parallaxes, the radius used for this interpolation is obtained from the fitted spectral scaling,

The most prominent difference occurs at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq 1700

\log g_{\rm evol}-\log g_{\rm atm}\simeq 1.1

A likely explanation is a residual degeneracy between
\log g

\log g
from 4 to 6 dex over this temperature range produces only subtle spectral changes, and these changes are concentrated mainly in the
K

IV.3
The Spectral Atlas

We present an empirical SPHEREx atlas built directly from the observed spectra of sources with parallaxes. Sources are grouped using the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
fitted with atmospheric models and the
\log g
inferred from evolutionary models, with bin widths of
\Delta T_{\mathrm{eff}}=100
K and
\Delta\log g=0.5
centered on the atlas grid points. Within each bin, we retain screened spectra, interpolate them onto a common wavelength grid matched to the native SPHEREx resolving power, normalize them in the 1.27–1.33
\mu
m region, and define the atlas spectrum as the wavelength by wavelength median of the retained members. Bins with fewer than five acceptable members are excluded, leaving 52 atlas bins constructed from 968 spectra of sources with parallaxes.

This construction is intended to preserve the characteristic morphology of the observed spectra associated with different regions of the adopted
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\log g
parameter space, while suppressing random noise, residual artifacts, and source specific peculiarities. Because the atlas is assembled from normalized observations by taking the median in each bin, it should be interpreted primarily as an empirical reference sequence in the observed 0.75–5
\mu
m domain, rather than as an absolute flux or purely model driven template set.

Figure
5
presents the resulting atlas as two vertically offset spectral sequences, ordered primarily by decreasing
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
. With this ordering, the dominant temperature driven evolution of the SPHEREx spectral morphology is seen directly: the broad molecular bands strengthen and change shape systematically from the late M regime through the T/Y regime. The labels retain the adopted
\log g
value for each bin, but the gravity dependence is weaker than the temperature dependence in much of the atlas. In particular, at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\gtrsim 1800
K, spectra with similar temperatures but different
\log g
values show only weak differences at the SPHEREx spectral resolution, indicating limited gravity sensitivity for the warmer sources.

. For each model family, three metallicity tracks are shown: blue for
[\mathrm{M/H}]=+0.3
, green for
[\mathrm{M/H}]=0.0
, and red for
[\mathrm{M/H}]=-0.5
.

We use molecular indices to connect the observed SPHEREx spectra to temperature, metallicity, and atmospheric physics. H
2
O and CH
4
indices mainly trace temperature dependent C/O chemistry
(
T. R. Geballe et al. 2002
)
, whereas CO and CO
2
indices are also sensitive to mixing, metallicity, and non equilibrium effects in the 3–5
\mu
m window
(
S. K. Leggett et al. 2007
;
T. R. Geballe et al. 2009
)
.

We compute all four indices for our SUDA sample using the continuum to feature ratio

I_{\rm mol}=\langle F_{\lambda,\,\rm cont}\rangle/\langle F_{\lambda,\,\rm feat}\rangle
, following Section 5.3 of
Z. Tu et al. 2024
. To ensure robust trends, we retain only measurements with uncertainty
\sigma(I_{\rm mol})\leq 0.75
. The lowest temperature regime is extended with T/Y dwarfs of JWST NIRSpec spectra from
Z. Tu et al. 2025b
(orange points in Figure
6
). For comparison, Figure
6
also shows model index curves at
\log g=5.0
for three metallicities in each model family:
[\mathrm{M/H}]=+0.3
(blue),
0.0
(green), and
-0.5
(red). The SAND sequences are shown as dashed curves and the ATMO2020++ sequences as solid curves.

For H
2
O and CH
4
, we recover the strengthening toward lower
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
seen in near infrared classification work
(
I. S. McLean et al. 2003
;
T. R. Geballe et al. 2002
;
A. J. Burgasser et al. 2006
)
, now mapped directly along the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
sequence for a homogeneous sample. Both indices show a clear slope break, with a steeper increase between about 1300 and 1100 K. This coordinated behavior is consistent with rapid chemical evolution across the L/T regime, where CH
4
becomes favored and water band contrast increases as atmospheres cool
(
D. Saumon & M. S. Marley 2008
;
M. S. Marley et al. 2021
)
. Residual scatter likely reflects secondary physical dependencies, such as gravity and metallicity
(
K. N. Allers & M. C. Liu 2013
)
, and a small number of outliers caused by large fluctuations in some SPHEREx spectra within the molecular index bandpasses.

Empirically, the CO and CO
2
indices show a coherent but nonmonotonic temperature dependence. At
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\gtrsim 1500
K, both indices show little overall temperature dependence. Below
\sim 1500
K, both begin to rise, reach maxima around
\sim 1000
K, and then decline again toward lower temperatures, with CO
2
exhibiting the larger overall amplitude. At a given
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, both indices also show substantial dispersion, especially in the CO
2
panel of Figure
6
.

A plausible physical interpretation of this shared behavior is that the two indices respond to related low temperature atmospheric physics rather than to independent trends. The rise below
\sim 1500
K likely reflects changing thermal chemical structure across the L/T regime, which strengthens the CO and CO
2
bands relative to the local continuum
(
S. K. Leggett et al. 2007
;
C. V. Morley et al. 2012
;
T. Tsuji et al. 2011
;
S. Sorahana & I. Yamamura 2012
)
. Below
\sim 1000
K, the decline of both indices is most plausibly linked to carbon chemistry, as carbon is increasingly partitioned into CH
4
in the late T regime, reducing the conditions that favor strong CO and CO
2
absorption in the observable photosphere
(
K. J. Zahnle & M. S. Marley 2014
)
. Some individual objects can nevertheless retain relatively strong CO absorption below
\sim
1000 K, most plausibly because vertical mixing quenches the CO abundance by dredging up CO rich gas from deeper, hotter layers
(
C. Visscher & J. I. Moses 2011
)
.

Comparison with the model sequences clarifies both the relative performance of the two model families and the diagnostic value of these indices. For H
2
O and CH
4
, the SAND and ATMO2020++ sequences are broadly consistent with each other and both reproduce the main observed temperature trend reasonably well. For CO and CO
2
, however, the two model families diverge much more strongly, and ATMO2020++ provides the closer overall match to the observed relation. In these two carbon bearing indices, the separation between different metallicity model tracks explains most of the observed dispersion, indicating that metallicity is the dominant driver of the CO and CO
2
index scatter. The CO
2
index is especially informative in this respect: in the approximate range
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 800
–1300 K, it shows the strongest metallicity sensitivity among the four indices while retaining a relatively coherent temperature dependence. This makes the CO
2
index a useful empirical metallicity tracer for brown dwarfs in this temperature interval.

Overall, the molecular indices trace a clear atmospheric sequence across the ultracool dwarf population. H
2
O and CH
4
mainly follow the temperature sequence, while CO and CO
2
are additionally sensitive to metallicity and to atmospheric physics that depends on the adopted model in the 3–5
\mu
m regime. Together, these indices provide an empirical probe of both the dominant temperature progression and the secondary atmospheric parameters operating in ultracool dwarfs.

VI
Conclusion

Our main results show how a homogeneous SPHEREx sample links observed ultracool dwarf spectra to atmospheric parameters, bolometric luminosities, evolutionary constraints, and molecular trends. The main conclusions are as follows.

1.

We constructed the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf spectral Atlas (SUDA) from SPHEREx QR2 data and assembled a final sample of 1675 ultracool objects with reliable 0.75–5
\mu
m spectroscopy. This sample provides a homogeneous set of atmospheric parameters, including
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\log g
, and [M/H], derived within a uniform framework using the SAND and ATMO2020++ model grids.

2.

By combining the best fitting models with the observed spectra, we directly calculated
L_{\rm bol}
for sources with parallaxes. For sources without parallax information, we used XGBoost to estimate
L_{\rm bol}
from
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\log g
, and [M/H]. We also used radii together with
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and C23 evolutionary models to derive masses, ages, and evolutionary surface gravities. The evolutionary
\log g
differ systematically from the atmospheric
\log g
inferred from spectral fitting in the 1700–2500 K range, where the fitted atmospheric
\log g
values are typically lower by a median offset of about 1.1 dex. This discrepancy likely reflects the limited ability of low resolution SPHEREx spectra to separate
\log g
from [M/H] in L dwarf atmospheres, because gravity dependent spectral changes are weak and can be partly absorbed by metallicity variations across the broad SAND metallicity grid.

3.

We constructed an empirical spectral atlas in the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\log g
diagram directly from the observed SPHEREx spectra of sources with parallaxes. The spectra were binned using the
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
fitted with atmospheric models and the
\log g
inferred from evolutionary models, providing an observational reference sequence for ultracool dwarfs over 0.75–5
\mu
m that links spectral morphology to the adopted physical parameter scale.

4.

The molecular index analysis reveals a coherent atmospheric sequence across the sample. H
2
O and CH
4
strengthen toward lower
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, with a steeper rise between
\sim
1300 and 1100 K, and the SAND and ATMO2020++ model sequences reproduce these trends in broadly consistent ways. CO and CO
2
show a shared nonmonotonic evolution: both rise below
\sim
1500 K, reach a turnover near
\sim
1000 K, and decline at lower temperatures. For these carbon bearing indices, the two model families differ much more strongly, with ATMO2020++ providing the closer overall match to the observed relation. Their dispersion is explained primarily by metallicity, and the CO
2
index is especially sensitive to metallicity in the range
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim 800
–1300 K, making it a useful empirical tracer of metallicity for brown dwarfs in this temperature interval.

Taken together, this work provides a homogeneous empirical basis for studying ultracool dwarfs across the 0.75–5
\mu
m range. By combining a large spectral atlas, uniformly derived atmospheric parameters, bolometric luminosities, constraints from evolutionary models, and molecular index diagnostics within a single sample, SUDA links observed spectral morphology to atmospheric chemistry, substellar cooling, and the boundary between stars and brown dwarfs. It therefore provides an observational basis for future population studies, model calibration, and further investigations of the coolest and lowest mass sources with future infrared space telescopes.

Appendix A
Sample Quality Control

This appendix summarizes the screening and calibration steps that define the final SUDA analysis sample.

A.1
High S/N Source Screening

We applied quality and astrophysical cuts to isolate a reliable set of SPHEREx spectra for the main analysis. First, we filtered individual spectral points using SPHEREx data quality flags. Following the SPHEREx Explanatory Supplement
3
3

3

The documentation in
https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/data/SPHEREx/docs/overview_qr.html
(version 1.3, Table 10), we retained only flux measurements with flag value
2^{21}
.

For each spectrum, we then computed the median S/N over retained wavelength points and excluded spectra with median S/N
<2
. We also applied astrophysical cuts using UltracoolSheet metadata to reduce systematics from unresolved systems and complex environments. Specifically, we removed unresolved binaries/multiples (
multiple_unresolved_in_this_table
), sources flagged as problematic (
flag
), and objects associated with active star forming regions (e.g., Taurus, Chamaeleon, Lupus, and IC 348) identified through
age_category
.

After these cuts, we performed visual inspection and photometric consistency checks to mitigate contamination from blended sources within the SPHEREx beam. Extracted spectra were compared against available 2MASS
J
,
H
,
K_{s}
(or MKO
J
,
H
,
K
when 2MASS was unavailable) and WISE
W1
photometry. Synthetic broadband fluxes were computed from the spectra using filter profiles from the SVO Filter Profile Service
4
4

4

http://svo2.cab.inta-csic.es/theory/fps3/
and compared with catalog photometry.

When SPHEREx fluxes were significantly higher than catalog photometry, we inspected WISE and ZTF images to assess contamination by nearby sources within
6
–
10^{\prime\prime}
of the target; clearly contaminated objects were removed. When SPHEREx fluxes were significantly lower, we checked SPHEREx images and source alignment to identify possible positional offsets or centroid mismatches.

After all quality and selection cuts, the final sample contains 1675 ultracool stars and brown dwarfs with reliable SPHEREx spectra spanning
0.75
–
5~\mu
m.

A.2
Flux Calibration

We use external photometry to evaluate the absolute flux consistency of the SPHEREx spectra. We compare the spectra with broadband photometry using 2MASS
J
,
H
, and
K_{s}
when available, MKO
J
,
H
, and
K
otherwise, and WISE
W1
to anchor the longer wavelength scale. For each source, synthetic photometry was computed in these bands and used to derive a linear scaling factor
R
by minimizing
\sum_{i}\left(F_{\mathrm{phot},i}-R\,F_{\mathrm{syn},i}\right)^{2},

where
F_{\mathrm{syn},i}
denotes the synthetic flux computed from the SPHEREx spectrum through the
i
-th photometric bandpass, and
F_{\mathrm{phot},i}
is the corresponding photometry value.

Figure A1. Distribution of the linear flux scaling factor
R
. The histogram is normalized to unit area. The solid curve shows the best fitting Gaussian profile with mean
\mu=1.00
and standard deviation
\sigma=0.05
, while the dashed vertical lines indicate the
\pm 1\sigma
interval.

Figure A2. Comparison between the SPHEREx spectrum of HIP 73786B (blue) and the best fitting SAND atmospheric model (orange). The dashed vertical line marks the atmospheric He emission line at 1.083
\mu
m.

The resulting distribution of
R
values (Figure
A.2
) is sharply peaked around unity, with a Gaussian fit of
\mu\simeq 1.00
and
\sigma\simeq 0.05
. This indicates good agreement between the SPHEREx flux scale and external photometry for most sources and implies an overall calibration uncertainty of about
5\%
, consistent with the 3–5% level reported in the SPHEREx Explanatory Supplement.

A small fraction of sources show significant deviations from unity. These outliers are mainly associated with incomplete QR2 spectral coverage in one of the near infrared bands (
J
,
H
, or
K
), or with very few spectral points within a band, which can bias synthetic photometry. In most cases, only one or two bands are affected.

The distribution also shows an excess of sources with
0.8\lesssim R\lesssim 0.9
, relative to the approximately symmetric interval at
1.1\lesssim R\lesssim 1.2
. We attribute this low
R
excess to terrestrial atmospheric emission line contamination (Section
A.3
), which can enhance Band 1 and Band 2 flux and bias
R
low, particularly through the synthetic
J
band flux.
Given the overall agreement between SPHEREx spectra and external photometric constraints, we did not apply additional flux rescaling to most sources. Extra rescaling with the fitted factor
R
was applied only when SPHEREx spectra showed clear offsets that were consistent across bands relative to broadband photometry.

A.3
Atmospheric Emission Line Screening

We identified a subset of spectra affected by short wavelength atmospheric emission line contamination. These objects show anomalously elevated short wavelength flux, most prominently in Band 1 (0.744–1.116
\mu
m), relative to the best fitting atmospheric models.

Inspection of the corresponding SPHEREx images indicates that this excess is caused by terrestrial atmospheric emission line contamination, dominated by the He line at 1.083
\mu
m with additional near infrared auroral contributions. A detailed description is provided in Section 2.3 of the SPHEREx Explanatory Supplement.

Figure
A.2
illustrates this effect. For affected sources, we performed checks source by source and excluded contaminated blue end regions by truncating spectra shortward of 1.2
\mu
m; only the 1.2–5
\mu
m range was retained for subsequent analysis.
