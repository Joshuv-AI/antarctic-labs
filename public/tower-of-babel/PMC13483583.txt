Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales infection at a South African paediatric hospital
Authors: Yemah Bockarie, James Nuttall, Hafsah Deepa Tootla, Ebenezer Aniakwaa-Bonsu, Brian Eley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) cause significant morbidity and mortality. The global dissemination of CRE is a public health concern, and its impact on children is increasingly being recognised in low-and middle-income countries (LMIC). Studies describing paediatric CRE infections in LMIC are limited. Therefore, this study describes the incidence risk, clinical and microbiological characteristics, treatment, and outcome of children with CRE infections at Red Cross War Memorial Children’s Hospital (RCWMCH) in Cape Town, South Africa.MethodsA retrospective description was completed on CRE infections diagnosed between January 2016 and December 2021. Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from hospital records and the National Health Laboratory Service database.ResultsThe overall incidence risk was 8.4/10,000 admissions. Of 85 infections with sufficient clinical and/or antibiotic information, the median age at CRE diagnosis was 3.9 months (interquartile range 0.8–44.4) and (68/85, 85%) were healthcare-associated. Sites of CRE infection included bloodstream (33/85, 39%), urinary tract (19/85, 22%) and respiratory tract (18/85, 21%). Klebsiella species (58/85, 68%) of which Klebsiella pneumoniae (57/58) predominated, and Serratia marcescens (19/85, 22%) were most isolated. Carbapenemase genes were detected in 63 of 72 isolates tested and included blaOXA-48 (46/63, 73%) and blaNDM (17/63, 27%). The isolates were most susceptible to amikacin (67/85, 79%) and tigecycline (66/85, 78%). Mortality within 30 days of diagnosis occurred in (28/85, 33%). On multivariable analysis, any organ dysfunction (risk ratio (RR) 3.2, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.1–9.3), cardiovascular dysfunction or shock (RR 1.8, 95% CI 1.1–3.0), and ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin-based therapy (RR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1–3.2) were significantly associated with mortality within 30 days of CRE infection diagnosis.ConclusionThis study adds to the limited number of paediatric CRE studies from Africa. The predominant pathogen causing CRE infection was blaOXA-48-producing K. pneumoniae. In our setting where ceftazidime-avibactam and colistin are used as last-resort agents, the finding of receipt of these agents and organ dysfunction as independent risk factors for mortality suggest that severity of disease and delayed initiation of definitive antibiotic therapy may be impacting mortality. These observations warrant further investigation in larger prospective hospital-based paediatric studies.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13764-y.

Background

Enterobacterales is an order of Gram-negative bacteria responsible for a wide spectrum of human infections in healthcare and community settings, and include clinically important genera such as Klebsiella, Escherichia, Enterobacter, and Serratia [1]. With the increasing use of carbapenems worldwide, the proliferation of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) has become a major global public health threat [1, 2]. These organisms cause significant morbidity and mortality, many of which also carry additional genes that render them resistant to multiple other classes of antibiotics [1]. Enterobacterales acquire carbapenem resistance predominantly via the acquisition of plasmid-mediated carbapenemase genes. Carbapenemases are classified by molecular structure into either Ambler class A or class D β-lactamases which have a serine residue in their active site, or class B metallo-β-lactamases (MBL) which possess zinc in their active site [1, 3]. Clinically relevant carbapenemase genes include blaKPC and blaGES in Ambler class A, blaNDM, blaVIM, blaIMP in Ambler class B, and blaOXA in Ambler class D [1, 3]. Uncommonly, carbapenem resistance may occur with mutations causing outer-membrane porin lesions or loss in extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) or AmpC hyperproducing Enterobacterales species [2].

In the United States (US), an overall CRE incidence rate of 0.70 cases per 100,000 paediatric population was reported from multi-site laboratory and population-based surveillance of hospitalised and non-hospitalised children during the period 2016–2020, while in the CRACKLE-2 study involving adults and children, the mean CRE admission rate was 57 per 100,000 admissions across 49 hospitals in 2016–2017 [4, 5]. In Italy, 3.5% of all invasive Enterobacterales infections in children hospitalised in a tertiary centre from 2013 to 2017 were caused by carbapenemase-producing CRE (CP-CRE) [6]. A European point-prevalence survey of healthcare-associated infections (HAI) in children including neonates across 1149 hospitals identified Enterobacterales as the most frequent organism cultured (15%), with 9% being carbapenem-resistant [7]. In China, surveillance showed a paediatric CRE prevalence of 6.4% among Enterobacterales isolates of hospitalised children from 2005 to 2017 [8]. Paediatric CRE prevalence data from sub-Saharan Africa is however scanty. In South Africa, a CRE prevalence of 5 per 1000 admissions among hospitalised neonates was reported from 2015 to 2016 in one tertiary centre [9], while South African laboratory-based surveillance identified 5,258 CRE bloodstream infections among adult and paediatric patients across 14 hospitals from 2015 to 2021 [10].

As low-and-middle-income countries (LMIC) increasingly recognise the impact of CRE infection and escalation in children, a growing number of reports are describing paediatric CRE epidemiology including infection and carriage, microbe characteristics and outcome [9, 11–25]. These LMIC studies show that infections are predominantly healthcare-associated and mostly occurring in children with comorbidities. Affected children included all age groups, and bloodstream, respiratory tract, intra-abdominal, urinary tract, and skin and soft tissue were common sites of infection [9, 11–25]. In South Africa, presenting clinical features during a CRE outbreak among hospitalised neonates included apnoea, temperature and glucose instability, abdominal signs and shock [25]. In three case-control studies, previous antibiotic treatment, liver transplantation, haematologic malignancies, urinary catheterisation, thrombocyte count and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube feeding emerged as independent risk factors for CRE infection [20, 22, 24].

Klebsiella pneumoniae was most isolated in these studies, however Escherichia coli was predominant in a study among Egyptian children with cancer and bloodstream infections [17], while a study in Mexico found both Klebsiella species and E. coli equally caused invasive infection among children [21]. Metallo-β-lactamases with blaNDM genes were most frequently detected in five studies [11, 12, 14, 18, 21] from China, India, South Africa, and Mexico (102/137 isolates), whereas in four studies [13, 15, 19, 20] from China, Columbia and Argentina, blaKPC genes were predominant (103/130 isolates). Crude mortality from these highlighted LMIC studies ranged from 0% to 60%, with targeted treatment involving use of monotherapy or combination therapy [9, 11–13, 16–25]. Antibiotic treatment included polymyxins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones, tigecycline and rarely newer β-lactam-β-lactamase-inhibitor (BLBLI) combinations. Risk factors for paediatric CRE mortality in one setting included mechanical ventilation, septic shock and isolation of carbapenem-resistant (CR) K. pneumoniae [24].

As CRE infection continues to escalate in healthcare settings, it remains an important public health concern for LMIC because of the limited capacity for molecular typing, research and surveillance [3]. Data gaps in low-resource settings impede accurate mapping of the evolving CRE enzymology which further compromise public health preparedness and resource allocation [26]. Limited diagnostics make CRE outbreaks difficult to detect, track, and control [3, 26]. These constraints coupled with the limited number and access to effective therapeutic agents particularly for paediatric patients pose significant challenges for clinicians in LMIC. Periodic evaluation of CRE infection epidemiology is therefore warranted to understand its clinical impact. Overall, information relating to paediatric CRE infection in sub-Saharan Africa remains limited. To our knowledge, only a few studies focusing exclusively on CRE infection in children managed at South African healthcare facilities have been published [3, 11, 12, 25, 27, 28]. Thus, the objectives of this observational study were to describe the incidence risk, clinical and microbiological characteristics, antibiotic treatment, and outcome among children hospitalised with CRE infection at a tertiary-level children’s hospital in South Africa.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective descriptive study was conducted at Red Cross War Memorial Children’s Hospital (RCWMCH), a 292-bedded paediatric referral hospital comprising a paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), and comprehensive medical and surgical services (excluding a newborn nursery) in Cape Town, South Africa. Provision of tertiary care is to the Western Cape province and surrounding provinces. Hospitalised children aged 0 to 16 years with CRE infection diagnosed between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2021 were included in this analysis. A descriptive retrospective study had previously examined CRE infection at RCWMCH from 2010 to 2015, so the time period of 2016 to 2021 was selected to provide continuity [11].

Study definitions

CRE was defined as Enterobacterales demonstrating non-susceptibility to at least one of the carbapenem antibiotics or detection of carbapenemase production [29]. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints of ≥2 μg/mL for meropenem and imipenem, or ≥1 μg/mL for ertapenem were used to define non-susceptibility as per the annually published Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines at the time of the study period and included CLSI 2016, CLSI 2017, CLSI 2018, CLSI 2019, CLSI 2020 and CLSI 2021 (CLSI 2016–2021).

CRE infection was considered when signs and symptoms of disease were associated with CRE isolated from a normally sterile site (blood, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, pericardial fluid, peritoneal fluid, joint/synovial fluid, bone, internal body sites or muscle) or urine [30]. CRE infection in this study was also considered when CRE was isolated from non-sterile sites such as soft tissue and respiratory samples including tracheal aspirate samples, where there were signs and symptoms attributed to CRE, and where the clinicians, infectious disease and clinical microbiology teams supported the clinical correlation with CRE infection.

Recurrent CRE infection was diagnosed when signs and symptoms of disease were present with CRE that was isolated more than 21 days after the first confirmed laboratory diagnosis, where effective antibiotic therapy and appropriate source control had been instituted [10].

CRE infection was considered as present on admission (POA) when CRE was isolated on the day of hospital admission, two days before admission, or the calendar day after admission and healthcare-associated (HAI) when CRE was isolated on or after the third calendar day of admission [31].

Chronic disease was considered as any medical condition (congenital or acquired) expected to last for at least 3 months that limits functionality, causing a medical need higher than expected for a child of that age, and severe enough to require dependency on specialised services for ongoing treatments, interventions, or other forms of support [32].

More study definitions are listed in Additional file 1 (table S1) in the supplementary information.

Data collection

Children with CRE isolates from clinical samples during the study period were identified from the National Health Laboratory Service (NHLS) electronic database. Surveillance samples such as rectal swabs and stool were not included. Infections caused by Proteus, Providentia or Morganella species were excluded because these microbes show inherently elevated MICs to imipenem [29]. Clinical and routine laboratory information was extracted from hospital records. This information included age, sex, HIV test results, selective antibiotic exposure during the 12-month period prior to CRE infection, clinical findings at the time of obtaining specimens for CRE culture, antibiotic treatment, outcome of the CRE infection, and full blood count and C-reactive protein (CRP) results. Microbiological results were obtained from the NHLS database and included the CRE species, the antibiogram of each isolate, and results of genotypic or phenotypic carbapenemase testing where available. Where microbiology data identified children with multiple CRE cultures, a careful examination of the clinical data was done, and recurrence was only considered when the signs and symptoms of disease were present after effective antibiotic therapy and appropriate source control of the first episode was established with a minimum of 21 days after the first laboratory diagnosis. Each episode was entered as an independent observation. All data were entered into standardised datasheets.

Microbiological procedures

Samples for culture were processed at the NHLS microbiology laboratory at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town, South Africa. Blood was inoculated in BacT/ALERT PF Plus or FA Plus bottles and incubated in the BacT/ALERT automated blood culture system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Positive blood cultures (with Gram-negative bacilli on microscopy) and other clinical samples were inoculated onto blood, boiled blood and MacConkey agar, and incubated under CO2, anaerobic and aerobic conditions at 35–37 °C. Bacterial isolates were identified, and susceptibility testing performed using the VITEK 2® automated system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Susceptibility test results were interpreted (excluding tigecycline) according to annually published CLSI breakpoints (CLSI 2016–2021). In 2019, for Enterobacterales (excluding Salmonella species), breakpoints for susceptibility to ciprofloxacin were reduced from 1ug/ml to 0.25ug/ml. Susceptibility test results for tigecycline were interpreted using FDA interpretive criteria [33]. Identification of CRE was done if carbapenem MICs were classified as non-susceptible (CLSI 2016–2021). Carbapenem non-susceptibility was confirmed using ertapenem, imipenem and meropenem Epsilometer tests (ETESTs) (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) when available. Colistin susceptibility testing was performed when feasible, using broth microdilution at Charlotte Maxeke Infection Control Laboratory, Johannesburg, South Africa or Pathcare Laboratory, South Africa, on isolates that were pan-resistant to non-β-lactam antibiotics (defined as CRE isolates showing in vitro non-susceptibility to all tested non-β-lactam antibiotics as per CLSI breakpoints, CLSI 2016–2021), or when treatment with colistin was initiated or being considered. Carbapenemase characterisation from isolates prior to 2019 was performed at the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD), Johannesburg, South Africa using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)(LightCycler 480 Probes Master kit and Lightmix Carbapenemase Modular kits (Roche Diagnostics, IN, USA) on the LightCycler 480 II instrument) to detect blaNDM, blaVIM, blaIMP, blaKPC, blaGES, and blaOXA-48-like genes. After 2019, carbapenemase characterisation was performed using the RESIST-4 O.K.N.V® (Coris BioConcept, Gembloux, Belgium) lateral flow assay that detects NDM, VIM, KPC and OXA-48-like enzyme production. Negative carbapenemase results with the RESIST-4 O.K.N.V lateral flow assay prompted testing with the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM), when the mCIM test became available in the laboratory to exclude other carbapenemase production. Isolates with no carbapenemase detected and with ETEST MICs that were susceptible to ertapenem, imipenem and meropenem were revised to non-CRE.

Data analysis

All CRE infections were used in the calculation of incidence risk which was estimated per 10,000 hospital admissions. The total number of admissions over the study period was obtained from the Information Management unit, RCWMCH. Positive culture results without evidence of infection in medical records were regarded incidents of colonisation and excluded from the analysis. Only the first culture result of each CRE infection was included in the analysis. Additional positive cultures from the same infection episode were excluded from the analysis. The remaining analyses were completed on CRE infections with sufficient clinical and antibiotic information. Where recurrence of CRE was established as per the study definition, each event was considered as an independent event and entered independently for both proportion and outcome analyses.

Data was captured into a Microsoft Excel 2010 spreadsheet (Microsoft, USA) and analysed using STATA 15.0 (College Station, Texas, USA). Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) because they were not normally distributed. Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison of continuous variables, while Pearson’s chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables. To explore factors associated with 30-day mortality, log-binomial regression was used to estimate risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals. Univariable analysis was first performed for each exposure variable. Subsequently, all exposure variables were included together in a single multivariable model so that the effect of each variable was estimated while accounting for the others. Risk ratio analysis was used because the prevalence of death (outcome) was high (38%), and use of odds ratios would have overestimated the true mortality risks. A 2-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and incidence risk

During the 6-year study period, there were 113,071 hospital admissions to RCWMCH and 95 CRE infections. These infections were used to estimate the incidence risk. Ten infections had insufficient clinical and/or antibiotic data and were excluded from all subsequent analyses. One study participant experienced two CRE infections; both infections were included and treated as independent infections. Thus, information on 85 CRE infections from 84 study participants were used in the remaining analyses (Fig. 1). The median age at CRE culture was 3.9 months (IQR 0.8–44.4 months) and (48/85, 57%) were males. There was a significant association between age and type of infection (HAI versus POA) (p = 0.03). The median length of stay for HAI before CRE culture was 15 days (IQR 7.0–28.5 days). Sixty percent (51/85) had one or more chronic diseases (Additional file 3, Table S3). Of 75 participants (75/85, 88%) with known HIV status, one (1/75, 1%) was found to be living with HIV. Eighty-two percent (70/85) had a PICU stay during hospitalisation which was significantly longer in children with HAI (19 vs 9 days, p = 0.02). Prior exposure to intravenous antibiotics in the 12 months preceding admission occurred in (38/85, 45%), mostly to carbapenems (24/38, 63%) (Table 1).

Clinical and laboratory features at time of CRE isolation

Forty-nine percent (42/85) of CRE infections had temperatures ≥ 38 °C at the time of CRE diagnosis. Abdominal distension (38/85, 45%) and respiratory distress (37/85, 44%) were common presenting features. Sites of CRE infection included the bloodstream (33/85, 39%), urinary tract (19/85, 22%), and the respiratory tract (18/85, 21%) (Table 2, Additional file 4, Figure S1). Antibiotic treatment

Empiric therapy was administered in (92%, 78/85) of the CRE infections, of which (35/78, 45%) was appropriate according to the study definition used (Table 2) (Additional File 1, Table S1). Similarly, (78/85, 92%) received definitive therapy, of which (70/78, 90%) was appropriate. For those who did not receive definitive therapy (7/85), 6 died and 1 survived. Of the 6 who died, the median time to death from culture diagnosis was 3 days (IQR 1.0–4.0). Care was redirected towards palliation for 4 participants because of poor prognosis, while the remaining 2 participants died before the antibiogram was known. The patient who survived was commenced on empiric ertapenem and down-referred to another hospital in a stable clinical condition for ongoing care, but no further documentation was recorded on whether treatment was optimised at the down-referred facility based on the antibiogram. Overall, the median time from culture to appropriate antibiotics (empiric or definitive) was 2 days (IQR 0.0–3.0) (Table 3). Colistin use in 11 infections was always given as combination therapy, and most frequently with ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem or amikacin. Colistin susceptibility results were available for 3 infections, with all reported as MIC < 2 µg/mL. Discussion

Our study has documented an escalating trend of paediatric CRE infections at RCWMCH from 5.6/10,000 admissions in 2016 to 18.2/10,000 admissions in 2021, peaking at 20.5/10,000 admissions in 2020. This trend agrees with surveillance reports which record a doubling in CRE bacteraemia rates in our province from 7% in 2015–2018 to 13.9% in 2019–2020 [34, 35]. Our prevalence is lower than the CRE infection prevalence of 5/1000 reported from hospitalised neonates elsewhere in South Africa [9]. A previous study in our institution recorded only 10 invasive paediatric CRE infections between 2010 and 2015 [11]. Reports on paediatric CRE incidence risk in sub-Saharan Africa remains scarce. The main reasons for the increase in CRE cases at RCWMCH likely include the generalisation of a CRE outbreak that started in the neonatal unit of a neighbouring tertiary hospital with widespread community colonisation [25], increased use of empiric meropenem in paediatric units in Cape Town during and after the neonatal CRE outbreak with the resultant selection of CRE, transfer of colonised patients from other hospitals in Cape Town to RCWMCH and suboptimal infection control practices. Changes in hospital admission patterns could have also played a role in the peak observed in 2020, as that year recorded the lowest total admissions. Reductions in hospital admissions in 2020 were mainly due to significant decreases in paediatric admissions for lower respiratory tract infections and diarrhoeal diseases coincidental with the height of the COVID-19 pandemic restrictions [36, 37]. The high CRE incidence in our facility may also reflect its tertiary status, as CRE infection and carriage episodes in our province show 70% aggregation in tertiary facilities [38].

Most of the infections were healthcare-associated (80%), consistent with other LMIC paediatric studies [11, 18, 19, 21, 24]. High institutional propagation in LMIC may reflect resource-constrained barriers yet underscore the need to redirect resources towards optimising practices such as regular and thorough environmental cleaning and equipment reprocessing, auditing compliance with hand hygiene protocols, maintaining adequate staffing numbers and sustaining antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) activities [39].

Many of the children in our study had predisposing factors for CRE infection including PICU stay, central venous access devices, mechanical ventilation, surgical procedures or interventions, urinary bladder catheterisation, previous exposure to antibiotics and chronic diseases other than HIV, consistent with previous studies from LMIC [3, 11, 12, 20, 22, 24]. Sixty percent of study participants had chronic underlying diseases, contrasting with another study in the USA, where more than half of hospitalised children with CRE infection had no documented comorbidities [40]. The presence of comorbidities may prolong hospital stay, increase economic burden and decrease quality of life. Cardiac, gastrointestinal and haematological-oncological diseases were common underlying conditions in our study. The immunocompromised state of the latter category poses a risk for invasive CRE infection. A risk of invasive CRE 2.1 times higher with Klebsiella infection and approximately 6 times higher with prior carbapenem usage in children has been reported in children with cancer or post-haematopoietic stem cell transplantation [19]. There was only one participant living with HIV in our study population, reflecting the success of the vertical transmission prevention programme in our province. This contrasts with another South African CRE study where HIV infection was present in 31% of participating adults and children [41]. Infection with HIV has not yet been reported as an independent risk factor for CRE infection or mortality among children in high prevalence areas [9]. The most common site of CRE isolation was the bloodstream (39%). The retrospective study design and the presence of multiple comorbidities and overlapping systemic processes made it difficult to reliably and consistently differentiate primary from secondary bloodstream infections for every patient. However, many patients (82%) had central vascular devices during hospitalisation, raising the possibility that some bloodstream infections may have been catheter-associated. Additionally, a substantial proportion of patients required mechanical ventilation during hospitalisation (77%), and respiratory tract CRE isolates were common (21%). However, ventilation-associated pneumonia could not be reliably defined in this population due to the lack of inclusion of predefined ventilation-associated pneumonia diagnostic criteria during data capture. Further studies should incorporate standardised surveillance definitions to better characterise the epidemiology of ventilation-associated pneumonia among mechanically ventilated children with CRE infections. Meanwhile, these observations underscore the importance of strengthening infection prevention practices including appropriate implementation of evidence-based central catheter and ventilation-associated pneumonia care bundle pathways to optimise patient safety and reduce healthcare-associated infections in critical care settings.

Klebsiella species, particularly K. pneumoniae being the leading bacterial isolate in our study is consistent with other South African studies [10, 12, 28, 38]. This is reflected elsewhere in Africa as documented in a systematic review reporting Klebsiella in 72.2% of CRE among 169 studies in adults and children [3]. In contrast, Enterobacter species (41%) was predominant in a USA paediatric cohort [40]. A progressive rise in CRE infections caused by S. marcescens was observed from 2019 to 2021. Serratia marcescens intermittently causes invasive infection at RCWMCH and is responsible for 2–3% of healthcare-associated bloodstream infections per annum [42]. The increasing number of CR S. marcescens has not been recognised as an outbreak. However, several factors may be driving the observed increased numbers, notably, infection prevention and control (IPC) lapses and the antibiotic selection pressure associated with increased carbapenem prescribing in critically ill patients. The propagation of CR S. marcescens in clinical settings remains concerning, as it is intrinsically resistant to colistin, which is a last-resort antibiotic in many LMIC settings. Coupled with the lack of access to newer BLBLIs, this severely narrows therapeutic options, and may potentiate the risk of treatment failure and death.

Prior to 2016, blaNDM-expressing isolates frequently caused CRE infection at our facility [11]. From 2019 onwards, blaOXA-48 became the predominant carbapenemase identified from CP-CRE infections in our setting and this trend is also reflected in South African surveillance data [10]. The combination of horizontal spread through promiscuous plasmids and vertical spread of high-risk multidrug-resistant clones has contributed to the global ascendancy of blaOXA-48 like-carbapenemases, assisting its survival in different environments [43]. Expansion of the multidrug-resistant oxacillinase-expressing K. pneumoniae sequence type (ST)307 clone has been well documented in South Africa and its spread may have been facilitated by intra-hospital, inter-hospital, inter-city, and inter-province movements of patients [44]. Similar to our study, the two most reported carbapenemase genes from African studies were blaNDM (43.1%) and blaOXA-48-like (42.9%) in 8478 isolates, however more than half of the carbapenemases characterised in this review were from South Africa (5091/8478, 60.0%) [3]. This skewed representation reflects the diagnostic barriers faced by many sub-Saharan African countries including lack of access to molecular characterisation, further impeding consistent CP-CRE surveillance across the continent, and aggravating paucity of the continental carbapenemase landscape [3, 26]. Additionally, resource-constrained laboratories who do not have access to genotypic testing may find the phenotypic diagnosis of blaOXA-like variants challenging as they weakly hydrolyse carbapenems and broad-spectrum cephalosporins, which may contribute to its under-recognition, under-reporting and silent spread in low-resource hospital and community settings [35, 43].

In our study, 87.5% of isolates tested were carbapenemase producers, comparable to the 93% prevalence reported in South African surveillance studies [10] but markedly higher than the 29% of carbapenemase producers documented among CRE isolates in a paediatric USA cohort [40]. The high rate of institutional CP-CRE in our resource-constrained setting warrants the continued need for robust IPC measures and sustained AMS activities to slow the progression of antimicrobial resistance and reduce HAIs. Furthermore, knowledge of the local CP-CRE epidemiology is important to guide empiric treatment and appropriate definitive therapy with newer BLBLIs when available [3, 45]. In our study, pan-resistant isolates were all NDM-S. marcescens, with high mortality observed. Publications from LMIC are highlighting the emerging challenges of difficult-to-treat CRE isolates [12, 16, 27].

The crude mortality documented in our study is comparable to that reported in a previous study conducted in our institution [11], and other South African studies [4, 24, 25]. It also falls within the wide range of mortality rates (0 to 60%) reported from paediatric CRE studies in LMIC settings outside of sub-Saharan Africa [13, 16–20, 22–24]. In comparison with a high-income setting, our 30-day mortality of 33% was higher than that reported in a USA cohort, which recorded a 30-day mortality of 13.9% [40]. Mortality rates vary considerably based on the study setting, patient characteristics, presence of comorbidities, microbe characteristics and access to newer effective antimicrobial agents. The high proportion of children with chronic underlying disease in our study may have contributed to the observed mortality higher mortality.

Experience with BLBLIs vary among LMIC. One hundred percent therapeutic success was achieved in a study of six children in China with CRE receiving ceftazidime-avibactam as salvage therapy post-liver transplantation after treatment failure with tigecycline-containing regimens [13]. In contrast, our study demonstrated a high 30-day mortality of 75% in patients who received ceftazidime-avibactam. Similarly, another study in our institution demonstrated high all-cause mortality of 53% among 17 patients receiving ceftazidime-avibactam and requiring PICU intervention for CRE infection [28]. In our setting as in many resource-limited settings, cost limits access to ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin necessitating their usage as last-resort therapies, often for the sickest patients. Hence negative treatment outcomes with these antimicrobial agents may reflect disease severity and delayed initiation of effective therapy. Therefore, the association of mortality and receipt of ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin in our study may be secondary to confounding. Furthermore, delays in accessing these agents limit the effect of exposure in those who die early in the course of illness. Moreover, the accurate determination of the onset of infection in patients presenting with infection on admission remains challenging in retrospective studies. These observations warrant further exploration, and prospective studies are needed to better understand the impact of delays in appropriate therapy on mortality and other clinical outcomes.

Most antibiotics demonstrated low susceptibility to CRE isolates, however amikacin showed relatively high susceptibility at 79%, suggesting that it should be adopted as a suitable empiric agent for multidrug-resistant nosocomial infections in a setting like ours where CRE infections are escalating, and therapeutic options remain limited.

Studies delineating paediatric CRE mortality risk in LMIC are few [16–18, 24]. In our study, any organ dysfunction, cardiovascular dysfunction or shock, and receipt of ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin-based therapy were significantly associated with 30-day mortality on multivariable analysis. Cardiovascular dysfunction or shock which emerged as a mortality risk in our study, compares to shock and requirement for inotropic support which has been cited in other studies, however, mechanical ventilation, CR K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection and respiratory source from these studies were not mortality risk factors in our study [18, 24]. The small study size likely accounts for the lack of significance of other factors. Despite the higher 30-day mortality seen in patients receiving ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin compared to those who did not, no difference was noted in the time to receiving appropriate antibiotics.

Our study had several limitations. The retrospective study design made bias unavoidable because of limitations in the completeness of clinical and laboratory data. It is possible that some previous exposures to healthcare facilities and antibiotics were not captured electronically, including healthcare-seeking in private hospitals or beyond the provincial catchment area. Additionally, the sample size was small and underpowered to fully explore risk factors for 30-day mortality. Furthermore, we lacked some microbiological insight as CRE diagnostic capacity during this time was being expanded locally and ETEST MIC, carbapenemase characterisation, and colistin susceptibility testing were not performed in all isolates. Moreover, where isolates had carbapenemase testing performed, the utilisation of different platforms and different laboratories across the study period coupled with differences in diagnostic sensitivity and specificity between these methods may have introduced some bias. Despite these limitations, our findings have provided important insights into the epidemiology of CRE in our institution.

Conclusion

This study estimated the incidence risk of CRE at RCWMCH over a 6-year period, provided insight into the clinical and microbiological characteristics, and explored factors contributing to 30-day mortality. Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae was the most predominant CRE, and majority of the carbapenemase genes were blaOXA-48 like. A high proportion of CRE isolates were susceptible to amikacin, suggesting its suitability as empiric therapy for high-risk patients in our setting. The observed mortality risk of ceftazidime-avibactam and/or colistin-based therapy is likely due to confounding. The escalation of CRE in paediatric populations should encourage robust IPC and AMS interventions, while larger, prospective, hospital-based studies are needed to fully delineate CRE epidemiology including mortality risk factors and underlying mediators in resource-limited settings.
