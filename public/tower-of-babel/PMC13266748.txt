Patient Insights to Improve Naloxone Access and Decision Support
Authors: Rebecca Boraz, Scott P Stumbo, Sarah J Leitz, Bobbi Jo H Yarborough
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIncreased access to naloxone has contributed to reductions in opioid-related overdose. However, many individuals prescribed naloxone do not fill their prescription. Better understanding modifiable patient-level barriers to naloxone receipt may inform health system–level implementation strategies that improve naloxone availability.MethodsA quality improvement survey was emailed to adult members of Kaiser Permanente Northwest with a valid email address who had an order for naloxone between September 15, 2023 and September 15, 2024 that appeared unfilled in pharmacy records. Using χ2 tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables, the authors compared individuals who filled vs did not fill a naloxone prescription.ResultsA total of 481 responses were received (19% response rate). More than half (57.5%) of respondents reported not obtaining naloxone at a pharmacy or other community resource. Primary reasons included: perceived lack of need (55.3%), unawareness of practitioner recommendation (14.9%), uncertainty about its purpose (13.1%), cost concerns (7.3%), and unfamiliarity with naloxone (4.4%). Fewer than 10 respondents cited logistical barriers.DiscussionResults from this quality improvement survey are consistent with previous studies of barriers to naloxone use. Among patients who did not fill a prescription for naloxone, many did not assess their personal risk of overdose to be high, and some resented a perceived implication that they might be abusing opioids. Others were unaware that a naloxone prescription was written until notified by a pharmacist. Cost and logistical barriers were less common concerns, each for less than 10% of those not filling a prescription.ConclusionClear, nonjudgmental communication about the risk for opioid-related overdose, even among people using their opioids appropriately, and education about the purpose of naloxone may improve access.Keywords: opioid, naloxone, prevention, access, overdose, clinical decision support

Introduction

Although opioid overdoses in the United States declined in 2024, the epidemic remains a persistent public health crisis. 1 Increased access to naloxone, which is a medication used to rapidly reverse opioid-related overdose, has contributed to this reduction. 2 In 2021, Kaiser Permanente Northwest convened leaders and stakeholders to develop a Zero Overdose quality improvement initiative, with the primary goals of increasing naloxone awareness and access for patients at risk of overdose. Efforts, as part of this initiative, included mailers to patients, patient-facing educational posters about naloxone at pharmacies (with a QR code for more detailed instructions), updated emergency response protocols for overdose events within medical facilities, building a robust infrastructure to monitor patients at risk of overdose, and correlating prescriptions of naloxone for these patients.

Data obtained from Kaiser Permanente’s Enterprise Pharmacy Information Management System revealed that when prescribed, approximately 70% of patients determined to be at high risk for an opioid-related overdose obtain naloxone through Kaiser Permanente pharmacies. 3 Patients are identified as high risk based on one or more of the following criteria: receiving an opioid prescription > 50 morphine milligram equivalents, a new diagnosis of opioid use disorder (OUD), an existing diagnosis of OUD with a concurrent opioid prescription, history of an overdose, and/or an overdose history combined with a prescription for a benzodiazepine or Z-drug. 4,5

A practitioner’s prescription carries weight and initiates several downstream actions. Within Kaiser Permanente Northwest, it prompts medication reconciliation during medical visits by the medical assistant and alerts pharmacists to inquire about the order, even when the patient is filling an unrelated prescription. In addition, once a prescription is placed, a Kaiser Permanente Northwest member becomes eligible to use their pharmacy benefits to help offset the cost. Without a practioner’s order or proactive monitoring, a patient’s need for naloxone may go unrecognized within the medical system, leaving a critical gap in overdose prevention efforts.

A survey was conducted as part of a Kaiser Permanente Northwest quality improvement initiative aimed at understanding the barriers faced by patients who received a naloxone prescription but did not fill it, and identifying meaningful, patient-informed, scalable, and sustainable actions the medical system can take to improve access.

Methods

Using electronic health record data, the authors identified 7302 adult (aged > 18) Kaiser Permanente Northwest Health Plan members with a naloxone order between September 15, 2023, and September 15, 2024. Of these, 4713 (64.5%) had filled their naloxone prescription within Kaiser Permanente Northwest and therefore were not eligible to receive a survey. At the time of the survey, 2531 had an active order but no evidence of a pharmacy dispense in the electronic health record and an active email address. These individuals were invited via email to participate in a web-based survey with an opportunity to enter a lottery for a gift card. Up to 2 reminders were sent to nonrespondents over the 10 days following the initial email invitation. In a selection bias analysis, the authors compared responders to nonresponders. The authors then compared individuals who were learned to have filled their prescription at an external pharmacy or through community resources and those who did not fill a naloxone prescription using χ2 tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables. The authors could have excluded the former group (as they excluded those known to fill their prescription within Kaiser Permanente Northwest). However, they maintained them in the sample to examine whether there may have been demographic or modifiable clinical characteristics that differed between the 2 subgroups that might have suggested different implementation strategies or intervention opportunities, as well as to discover facilitators of naloxone receipt. Open-ended responses were read and coded by one author (Scott P Stumbo) using a modified thematic analysis 6 and a template approach 7 suitable to very short survey responses. The Kaiser Permanente Interregional Institutional Review Board determined that this was a quality improvement project and did not require consent.

Results

A total of 478 responses were received, yielding a 18.9% response rate (478, of 2531). The average respondent age was 62, 64% of respondents were female, > 90% were White, 57% had Medicare insurance, 8% had Medicaid, and 35% were commercially insured. Furthermore, 72% of individuals had a prescription for an opioid medication, 19% had an OUD, and 9% did not have evidence of either. A selection bias analysis revealed that response rates differed by sex (female 21.2% vs male 15.2%, P < .001), race (White 20.2% vs Black 6.1%, Native American 7.7%, P < .001), age (62.5 years for respondents vs 56.2 for nonrespondents, P < .001), and insurance type (Medicare insurance 24.8% vs commercial 15.7% or Medicaid 9.4%, P < .001). Response rates were higher among individuals who were written a naloxone prescription because they had an active opioid prescription than among those who were written a naloxone prescription because they had an OUD (22% vs 11.5%, P < .001).

Among survey respondents, 42.5% (203 of 481) reported that they had obtained naloxone from a non–Kaiser Permanente pharmacy or community resource, and a small portion may have filled their prescription between the data pull and when the survey was sent. The majority of respondents (57.5%) reported not obtaining naloxone. Some primary reasons included: perceived lack of need (n = 152, 55.3%), unawareness of practitioner recommendation (n = 41, 14.9%), uncertainty about its purpose (n = 36, 13.1%), cost concerns (n = 20, 7.3%), and unfamiliarity with naloxone (n = 12, 4.4%). Fewer than 10 respondents cited logistical barriers such as pharmacy issues, forgetting to pick up the medication, having too many prescriptions, or planning to obtain it soon. In open-ended response options, 18 responded in ways that suggested they thought a naloxone prescription was inappropriate for them because they did not abuse drugs or associate with people who did. For example, one respondent indicated, “I do not hang out with junkies, so I have no reason to need Narcan in the first place.” Approximately 10 respondents reported feeling judged or offended by the offer of naloxone. One commented, “I felt extremely judged by this. I am not an addict nor someone to misuse my medication. It felt quite icky.” Another said, “I'm not a heroin user! I use Suboxone because I was addicted to kratom. I'm offended by the pharmacists constantly offering me Narcan like I'm a junkie.”

When asked what Kaiser Permanente could have done better to promote naloxone receipt, several themes emerged. Several respondents (n = 40) felt it would have been helpful if the practitioner had explained that they were ordering the prescription rather than just placing the order. One respondent commented, “The doctor should've brought it up in the conversation. He never even talked about it. It just showed up in my medication list to fill.” Even more (n = 47) reported they would have appreciated the opportunity to have a conversation about why the practitioner felt the prescription was necessary and its benefits. One noted, “Doctors could perhaps explain that it is preventative in nature and not indicative of concern on the part of the doctor.” Another stated, “That it is better to have on hand just in case. You may never use it, but it is better to be safe than sorry. Accidents happen, and having this available can save your life or the life of someone else.” Personalizing the message to the risks of the individual and emphasizing possession of naloxone as a preventative measure seemed important in these comments. Twelve survey respondents recommended treating people with respect to increase prescription fills. One respondent’s comment summarized many of these themes by saying, “We do NOT need it for ourselves and again feels like a big insult after years of [being] clean. . . . This prescription feels offensive but I will fill this in case there is an opportunity to save someone else’s life. It’s embarrassing to even pick it up. Put the focus on saving others and it will be filled. Stop telling us you expect us to mess up.” Twenty-three survey respondents suggested making naloxone free might enhance naloxone receipt.

Discussion

Survey responses combined with the pharmacy dispensing data suggest that when a practitioner places a naloxone order, patients do obtain the medication approximately 80% of the time. This uptake, combined with patient-reported motivations (including understanding the importance of naloxone and their desire for altruism in helping fight the opioid epidemic), underscores the importance of clear communication and naloxone education by the practitioner. Notably, 42.5% of respondents indicated they had obtained naloxone even in cases where the prescription was not filled at the pharmacy, and 44.8% of those respondents still cited their clinician’s advice as the reason for obtaining naloxone.

Research regarding barriers to outpatient use of naloxone in primary care has been ongoing since the first wave of the opioid epidemic of prescription opioids. 8 In an early study, Binswanger and colleagues found that primary care physicians demonstrated knowledge gaps about outpatient naloxone use, including uncertainty about whom to prescribe to and fears about offending patients. 8 A more recent study indicated this fear persists among pharmacists. 9 Studies of naloxone barriers among family members, friends, 10 and patients, 11 including the present survey, have consistently found that a subset of patients are offended as stigma remains a substantial barrier. Other previously described patient barriers corroborated in the present study, which may contribute to perceived stigma. include insufficient patient education, 11,12 lack of awareness that naloxone was ordered, 13 lack of perceived need, 10 and fear that need for naloxone represents overdose risk associated with opioid misuse. 12 These reiterated findings reinforce the importance of communication about the naloxone order using personalized, nonjudgmental messaging, and the fact that the presence of a prescription alone does not ensure follow-through. Notably, from the survey findings, of the patients who did not obtain naloxone, over half (55.3%) intentionally did not fill the prescription due to perception of low urgency or limited awareness of personal overdose risk. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines for prescribing opioids for chronic pain recommend that patients and clinicians engage in shared decision-making, a collaborative process for determining treatment decisions where both patient and prescriber are able to weigh the advantages and disadvantages of treatment options, in light of known benefits, risks, and personal preferences. The results suggest that patient education and shared decision-making around treatment decisions should extend to naloxone use for individuals receiving opioid medications or with OUD, as such a participatory process, done respectfully, could empower patients, improve patient education, reduce lack of awareness of naloxone orders, and allow for clarification of potential need, even in the absence of opioid misuse.

Optimizing clinical decision support tools can address questions of who to prescribe naloxone to 8 by using electronic health record data to identify high-risk patients. One cost-effective step further is to optimize a Best Practice Alert with patient messaging points to prompt the prescription and reinforce stigma-free education. Operational strategies such as targeted patient outreach (eg, pharmacy technician outreach calls to patients prescribed > 50 morphine milligram equivalents) 14 have demonstrated meaningful improvements in safety, particularly among high-risk populations. However, these interventions rely on staffing capacity. By contrast, technology-based prompts can promote more consistent practices across practitioners by standardizing workflows aligned with typical medication ordering. This, in turn, naturally enables pharmacist-led conversations by prompting them to check in about naloxone orders during routine interactions.

As outlined by respondents, addressing cost barriers is also important for some; when a practitioner orders naloxone, pharmacists can leverage the patient’s insurance benefits or help connect them with available medical financial assistance programs to support those facing affordability challenges. These insights extend beyond the clinical encounter and can be reinforced by pharmacists who play a key role in patient education and in addressing financial barriers.

In response to these findings, Kaiser Permanente Northwest redesigned Best Practice Alerts to trigger when an action during an appointment indicates elevated risk of overdose (eg, adding a new diagnosis of OUD). For these high-risk patients, the alerts prompt naloxone ordering and encourage practitioner–patient conversations that reflect the feedback the authors received. The alerts will trigger for all high-risk patients and at appointments but will suppress for 2 years (the shelf life of naloxone) if there’s an active order in the chart. See the Figure. Additionally, the authors created targeted training for both pharmacists and practitioners with video modeling of supportive conversations with patients. Similar approaches could be adopted by other health systems with the shared goal of improving how practitioners and patients engage in meaningful conversations about overdose risk and the critical role of naloxone. Kaiser Permanente Northwest will measure the effect of the quality improvement strategies going forward.

Limitations of the present study that should be considered include the potential for selection bias. The survey results may be more likely to represent older, White, and female Medicare recipients receiving prescription opioids than other clinical and demographic groups. However, the consistency of results with several previously published studies, including studies sampling not only patients but family members, friends, practitioners, and pharmacists, indicates that in spite of this, the findings may generalize across individuals eligible for naloxone prescriptions. The sample size may be considered small. However, the response rate was within the range of health surveys administered online. 15 The authors did not measure adverse events including overdose in this study.

Conclusion

This patient survey underscored the importance of a strong practitioner–patient partnership when addressing overdose risk across all channels through which patients may obtain naloxone, including state-run distribution programs when patients receive naloxone outside the health system. From the current and previous findings, the implications are clear. In order to improve naloxone access and reduce opioid-related overdose risk, practitioners must first identify individuals at elevated risk of overdose and then adopt a nuanced, unbiased approach to discussing that risk, tailoring the conversation to the patient’s specific circumstances, while placing an order for naloxone.
