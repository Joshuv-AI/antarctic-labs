Medical Use and Misuse of Addictive Prescription Drugs and Mental Health Among Adolescents in Estonia
Authors: Liina Veskimäe, Sigrid Vorobjov, Inge Ringmets, Kersti Pärna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to describe the prevalence and characteristics (sources, motivations, perceived health risks, accessibility) of medical use and misuse of addictive prescription drugs (APDs), and the prevalence of poor mental wellbeing and self-harm among adolescents in Estonia. In addition, this study examined associations between APD use status (non-use, medical use, misuse) and mental health indicators (mental wellbeing, self-harm).MethodsData came from the nationally representative 2024 European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) survey of 15–16-year-olds in Estonia (n = 2,019). Respondents were classified as non-users, medical users, or misusers based on lifetime APD use. Motivations, sources, perceived health risks, accessibility, mental wellbeing, and self-harm were assessed. Multivariable logistic regression examined associations between APD use status and mental health indicators.ResultsOverall, 27.8% of adolescents reported APD medical use and 18.3% misuse, with higher prevalence among girls. Sedatives and sleeping pills were the most commonly medically used and misused APDs. Most misused APDs were obtained from home, and self-treatment motives predominated. Poor mental wellbeing (44.4%) and self-harm (17.6%) were most prevalent among misusers. APD misuse (. non-use) was associated with poor mental wellbeing, self-harm and female gender; misuse (vs. medical use) with self-harm; and APD medical use (vs. non-use) with self-harm and female gender. The strongest association was found between APD misuse (vs. non-use) and self-harm (adjusted odds ratio = 2.78).ConclusionsAPD medical use and misuse are common among Estonian adolescents. APD misuse co-occurred with poor mental wellbeing and self-harm, highlighting the importance of integrating APD misuse prevention into broader adolescent mental health and substance use strategies.

Introduction

Psychotropic prescription drugs constitute a broad class of medications used to treat mental and behavioral disorders and pain, including in children and adolescents. These medications can be categorized by their primary therapeutic effects and the conditions they target. Some psychotropic prescription drugs, such as opioids, anxiolytics, hypnotics and sedatives, stimulants, and certain muscle relaxants, have pharmacological properties associated with misuse, dependence, and addiction (Taylor et al., 2019). Importantly, these medications do not inevitably lead to addiction in all users. Addiction typically develops only when specific biological, psychological, and social risk factors are present (Carmona et al., 2020; Cragg et al., 2019). Thus, although their use does not necessarily lead to addiction, they have recognized addiction potential. In this study, for conceptual clarity and consistency, these medications are referred to as addictive prescription drugs (APDs).

Although APDs are essential for treating various psychiatric and somatic conditions, their misuse poses significant health risks. Misuse includes using medication without a prescription, in larger amounts, more frequently, longer than prescribed, or taking medicine for non-medical purposes (e.g., for intentional intoxication) (SAMHSA, 2016). Misuse can lead to tolerance, dependence, cognitive impairment, overdose, and worsening mental health symptoms (Edlund et al., 2015), sometimes progressing even to suicidal ideation (Ford & Perna, 2015).

APD misuse among adolescents has emerged as an increasing public health concern worldwide. Recent studies report that 9.8% of Spanish adolescents aged 14–18 years misused at least one category of APDs in 2018–2019 (Weidberg et al., 2022), and 7.1% of US 12th graders reported non-medical use of prescription drugs in 2024 (Miech et al., 2025). According to the 2024 European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) survey, 14% of European adolescents aged 15–16 years reported lifetime non-medical use of pharmaceuticals, with misuse of tranquilizers or sedatives (8.5%) and painkillers (6.9%) being the most prevalent (ESPAD Group, 2025b). Studies have identified notable gender differences: girls tend to exhibit higher prevalence of non-medical use of psychoactive prescription medications in adolescent samples (Carrasco-Garrido et al., 2019; Scalese et al., 2025) and are more likely to report self-medication motives in response to psychological distress, whereas boys more frequently misuse these drugs for experimental or recreational reasons (Mitchell et al., 2024). Beyond prevalence, previous studies have also described patterns and contexts of adolescent APD misuse. Research suggests that access often occurs through family members, peers, or leftover prescriptions (Faraone et al., 2020; Forrest et al., 2025), reflecting both household availability and prescribing practices. Motivations for misuse vary, including self-medication for psychological or physical symptoms, academic performance enhancement, experimentation, and recreational use (Abraham et al., 2021; Faraone et al., 2020). Adolescents may perceive prescription drugs as safer than illicit substances, which may contribute to misuse (Abraham et al., 2021).

Research also indicates that adolescents who misuse prescription drugs often engage in other substance use, such as alcohol, tobacco, and illicit drug use, increasing the risk of polysubstance-related harms (Baroni et al., 2023; Faraone et al., 2020; McCabe et al., 2006; Milner et al., 2014). These behaviors are important to consider because they may reflect shared underlying risk factors with APD misuse and contribute to broader substance use exposure and normalization.

At the same time, mental health problems among adolescents are on the rise globally (Bie et al., 2024; Brody & Hughes, 2025), representing one of the leading causes of disease burden and disability in this age group (WHO, 2025). Around half of all mental disorders emerge during adolescence, underscoring the importance of prevention efforts within the first two decades (Solmi et al., 2021).

In this study, mental health is used as an umbrella concept referring to a continuum that includes both positive aspects of psychological functioning (such as mental wellbeing) and negative manifestations of psychological distress (such as self-harm behaviors), rather than only clinically diagnosed mental disorders. Poor mental health, such as symptoms of anxiety, depression, or elevated stress, have increased over the past decade, particularly among girls (Brody & Hughes, 2025). Poor mental health has been linked to a higher likelihood of self-medication and misuse of prescription drugs as adolescents attempt to cope with psychological distress, loneliness, or academic pressure (Espitia-Cepeda et al., 2025; Weyandt et al., 2022). Conversely, the APDs misuse may exacerbate psychological distress, highlighting a bidirectional relationship between mental health and substance misuse. Adolescence represents a critical developmental period marked by rapid psychological, emotional, and social changes, during which both mental health vulnerabilities and exposure to APDs may emerge. Understanding this intersection is essential for identifying at-risk groups and for designing preventive and therapeutic interventions that address both substance misuse and underlying psychological distress. However, the findings on the relationship between medical use, misuse, and mental health outcomes remain inconsistent. These findings highlight the need for integrated assessments of APD use status, access pathways, motivations, perceptions, and mental health outcomes within the same population.

In Estonia, limited empirical research has focused on adolescent APD use and misuse despite indications of increasing mental health problems (Oja et al., 2023) and rising misuse of prescription drugs among young people. A comprehensive understanding is needed of the extent of APD use and misuse among adolescents, their mental health status, and the interconnections between these domains.

The objectives of this study were (1) to describe the prevalence and characteristics (sources, motivations, perceived health risks, accessibility) of medical use and misuse of addictive prescription drugs (APDs), and the prevalence of mental health indicators (mental wellbeing and self-harm), and (2) to analyze the associations between APD use status (non-use, medical use, misuse) and mental health indicators among a nationally representative cohort of 15–16-year-old adolescents in Estonia in 2024.

Methods

Study Population and Design

This secondary analysis used individual-level data of Estonian schoolchildren aged 15–16 years from the 2024 ESPAD study. ESPAD is a cross-sectional survey conducted every 4 years since 1995 in multiple European countries, including Estonia. The ESPAD study employed proportionate stratified sampling based on geographical regions (NUTS3 level for Estonia), with one 8th and one 9th grade class selected from each school. The final sampling unit was the school class. Parents were informed about their child's inclusion in the study with passive informed consent. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. In 2024, the school participation rate in Estonia was 58%, whereas the class participation rate was 60%. The student participation rate, calculated as the proportion of students in participating classes who completed the questionnaire, was 81%. Overall, coverage was very high, with the survey capturing 97% of the target population in Estonia (ESPAD Group, 2025a).

Self-administered online questionnaires in Estonian and Russian languages were used to collect data. The data collection was carried out by pretrained research assistants and completed in the classroom. The completed questionnaires were centrally checked and cleaned by the National Research Council of Italy, which is responsible for data management and secure storage within the ESPAD framework (ESPAD Group, 2025a).

Sample

The 2024 sample comprised 2,029 respondents in the 8th and 9th grades who were born in 2008. Approximately half were girls (49.4%). To construct the three mutually exclusive groups, we included only respondents who provided answers to the prescription medication items, including those reporting never-use. Respondents who did not answer any of these items were excluded (n = 10), resulting in a final analytical sample of 2,019 adolescents.

For adjusted regression models, complete case analysis was applied, including only respondents with available data on all variables included in each model. For analyses of independent variables (e.g., motivations for use, accessibility, etc), respondents were excluded only if they had missing data for the specific variable being analyzed, whereas their available data were retained for other analyses. Consequently, the analytical sample size varied slightly across analyses.

Measures

APD misuse was measured by asking: “On how many occasions in your lifetime (if any) have you used any of the following drugs (e.g., sedatives, painkillers to get high, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) medications and muscle relaxants) without a doctor's authorization?” with response options “0,” “1–2,” and “3 or more.” Responses were grouped into: (1) any lifetime misuse of at least one APD (yes) and (2) no misuse of any APD (no). It is important to note that medicine misuse encompasses a broader spectrum (Barrett et al., 2008; McHugh et al., 2023). In the present study, APD misuse specifically refers to the use of prescription medication without a prescription or doctor's authorization. The misuse item referred broadly to painkillers used “to get high” and did not specifically distinguish opioids from other prescription painkillers.

Medical use of APDs (e.g., sedatives, prescription painkillers (opioids), ADHD medications and muscle relaxants) was measured by asking: “Have you ever taken one or more of the following drugs because a doctor told you to take them?” with response options “No, never,” “Yes, but for less than 3 weeks,” and “Yes, for 3 weeks or more.” Responses were grouped into: (1) adolescents who had never used any of these APDs (no) and (2) those who had used at least one (yes).

Based on responses to the APD medical use and misuse items, adolescents were classified into three mutually exclusive APDs use status groups: (1) non-users, who reported no lifetime medical use or misuse of any APDs; (2) medical users, who reported lifetime use of APDs only as prescribed by a doctor; and (3) misusers, who reported any lifetime use of APDs without a doctor's authorization, regardless of medical use history. Accordingly, adolescents classified as misusers could also report prior medical use of APDs. Therefore, estimates of overall medical use include both exclusive medical users and adolescents who also reported misuse.

All prevalence estimates presented in this study are based on any self-reported lifetime use (i.e., at least one occasion), and therefore include adolescents who may have experimented with APDs only once or several years prior to the survey.

No images of the APDs were included in the questionnaire, but the following text preceded the APD use questions: “Sedatives/sleeping pills (Xanax, Rivotril, Imovane, Somnols, Seduxene, Diazepam, Radedorm, etc.), ADHD medications (Concerta, Affenid, Medikinet, etc.), and muscle relaxants (Lyrica, Pregabalin, Pregamid, Siranalen, Neurontin, etc.) are prescribed by a doctor for therapeutic purposes. Pharmacies are not permitted to dispense them without a doctor's prescription. No specific examples of prescription painkillers were provided in the questionnaire.

Perceived health risk was measured by asking: “How much do you think people risk harming themselves (physically or in other ways), if they … try different prescription drugs once or twice or regularly?” with response options “No risk,” “Slight risk,” “Moderate risk,” “Great risk,” and “Don’t know.” Three groups were formed: (1) low or no risk, (2) medium or high risk, and (3) do not know.

Perceived access to APDs was measured by asking “How difficult do you think it would be for you to get following prescription drugs without a doctor's authorization if you wanted?” Response options were impossible, very difficult, fairly difficult, fairly easy, very easy, and don’t know. For analysis, responses were grouped into four categories: (1) impossible; (2) difficult (very or fairly difficult); (3) easy (fairly or very easy); and (4) don’t know.

Sources of non-prescribed APD use were assessed using the question: “If you have used prescription drugs without a doctor's authorization, where did you obtain them? Please mark all that apply.” Response options included: I have never used them; offered by a family member, friend, or acquaintance; from a street dealer; from the internet; from a pharmacy without a prescription; from a pharmacy using someone else's prescription; taken from home; and from another source (please specify).

Motivations for the misuse of APDs were assessed by asking: “For what reasons have you used prescription drugs without a doctor's authorization? Mark all that apply.” Response options included: I have not used them; to enjoy parties; to help when feeling depressed or nervous; to improve mood when feeling low; because the sensation is pleasurable; to get high; to make social gatherings more enjoyable; to fit in with a group; to enhance parties and celebrations; to forget about problems; because it is fun; to be liked; to avoid feeling left out; to help with falling asleep; and for another reason (please specify). Open-ended responses were reviewed and, where applicable, reclassified into existing categories (e.g., sleep- or anxiety-related reasons) or grouped as pain-related. Responses that did not fit these categories were retained as “other reasons.” Seven groups were formed: (1) to help with falling asleep or staying asleep; (2) to self-manage symptoms of anxiety, depression, or stress; (3) to feel good or get high; (4) to make social gatherings or parties more enjoyable; (5) to fit in socially or avoid exclusion; (6) to relieve pain; and (7) for other reasons.

Mental well-being was measured using the World Health Organization-Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5), a validated five-item scale rated from 0 (“at no time”) to 5 (“all of the time”), reflecting how frequently respondents experienced positive feelings during the previous 2 weeks. Scores range from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating better well-being. Following standard guidelines, scores below 13 indicated poor mental well-being and the need for further assessment, whereas scores of 13 or higher indicated good mental well-being (Topp et al., 2015; World Health Organization, 2024).

Self-harm was measured by asking: “During the last 12 months have you deliberately hurt yourself?” with response options “Never,” “Yes, while using alcohol or drugs,” and “Yes, but NOT while using alcohol/drugs.” Two groups were formed: (1) never (no) and (2) deliberate self-harm (yes).

Risk behavior was measured with three past-month items on tobacco use (cigarettes, e-cigarettes, snus), alcohol use and cannabis use, each dichotomized as yes/no. “Have you ever smoked cigarettes (excluding digital and alternative smoking products)/used e-cigarettes/used moist snuff (snus)/had any alcoholic beverage to drink/used cannabis during the last 30 days?”

Statistical analysis

The analyses were structured in two parts. First, descriptive analyses were used to describe the prevalence and characteristics of APD medical use and misuse, including sources and motivations for misuse and perceived health risks and accessibility, as well as the prevalence of poor mental wellbeing and self-harm across APD use status groups. Second, multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine associations between APD use status (non-use, medical use, and misuse) and mental health indicators (mental wellbeing and self-harm).

Lifetime prevalence (%) of APD medical use and misuse was estimated overall and by gender. Among adolescents reporting APD misuse, sources of access and motivations were described as proportions, allowing multiple responses. Perceived health risks, perceived accessibility, mental wellbeing, and self-harm were examined across APD use status groups, with additional gender-stratified descriptive analyses. Differences between APD use status groups and between genders were assessed using chi-squared tests, and post-hoc pairwise two-sample tests of proportions were conducted when overall group differences were statistically significant. For pooled analyses, responses on perceived health risks and accessibility were combined across APD categories.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine associations between APD use status and mental health indicators by modelling three pairwise comparisons: misuse vs. non-use, misuse vs. medical use, and medical use vs. non-use. Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All models were adjusted for gender, mental wellbeing, self-harm, perceived health risk, accessibility to APDs, and risk behaviors (past-month tobacco, alcohol, and cannabis use), entered simultaneously.

Given Estonia's small size, relatively homogeneous population and high participation rates, no clustering, stratification, or weighting was applied. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. All analyses were conducted in Stata MP, version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Of the total sample, 66.1% were non-users, 15.6% were exclusive medical users, and 18.3% were APD misusers. Girls were more represented among exclusive medical users (57.1%) and misusers (58.8%), whereas boys constituted the majority of non-users (55.0%). There was a statistically significant association between gender and APD use status (p < .001).

APDs Medical Use and Misuse

Overall, 27.8% of adolescents reported any lifetime medical use of at least one APD, including adolescents who also reported misuse (24.0% of boys and 31.7% of girls; p < .001). Of these, 34.2% had used more than one APD (35.5% of boys vs. 33.2% of girls; p = .569). As shown in Figure 1(a), medical use of sedatives or sleeping pills was reported by 12.2% of adolescents, more often by girls than boys (14.6% vs. 9.9%; p < .001). ADHD medications were used medically by approximately 10% of both genders, and prescription painkillers (opioids) were the most commonly used APD class for medical purposes (14.9%). Muscle relaxants were least common (5.2%) (Figure 1(a)).

ADHD: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.

In comparison, 18.3% of adolescents reported misusing at least one APD (21.6% of girls and 14.8% of boys; p < .001). Among misusers, 33.9% had misused more than one APD (38.8% of boys vs. 30.4% of girls; p = .093). Sedatives/sleeping pills were the most frequently misused APDs (13.3%), with higher prevalence among girls (16.8% vs. 10.0%; p < .001). Misuse of painkillers to get high was reported by 6.5% of adolescents, whereas fewer than 5% reported misusing ADHD medications or muscle relaxants (Figure 1(b)).

Overlap was observed between medical use and misuse: 67.0% of misusers (72.8% of boys and 63.0% of girls; p = .047) also reported medical use, whereas 43.9% of medical users (44.9% of boys and 43.0% of girls; p = .660) also reported misuse.

Sources and Motivations for Misuse

APDs used for misuse were most often obtained from home (41.6%) or from a family member, friend, or acquaintance (36.1%). These were also the most common sources in both boys and girls. Less common sources included pharmacies without a prescription (20.1%), street dealers (14.1%), the internet (10.0%), and using someone else's prescription (10.7%). Boys were significantly more likely than girls to obtain APDs from street dealers, from the internet, and from pharmacies using someone else's prescription (Table 1).

Perceived Health Risks and Accessibility

Figure 2(a) presents the distribution of adolescents according to their APD use status, gender, and perceived health risk of occasionally misusing APDs. The association between APD use status and perceived health risk was statistically significant (p < .001). In both genders, misusers more often perceived occasional APD misuse as posing low or no health risk compared with medical users and non-users, although pairwise comparisons between boys and girls for low/no vs. medium/high perceived risk were not statistically significant.

Similarly, misusers more often perceived APDs as easy to obtain compared with medical users and non-users in both genders (Figure 2(b)). Pairwise analyses showed that misusers were significantly more likely to report easy accessibility and less likely to report impossible accessibility compared with both medical users and non-users (all p < .001). Statistically significant gender differences in perceived accessibility were observed within misuse group.

Mental Wellbeing and Self-Harm

Overall, 44.4% of adolescents reported poor mental wellbeing, with higher prevalence among girls than boys (54.6% vs. 34.6%, p < .001). Poor mental wellbeing was more common among APD misusers (58.3%) and medical users (46.9%) compared with non-users (39.9%) (p < .05). This pattern was evident in both genders, with misusers reporting the highest prevalence of poor mental wellbeing (Figure 3(a)).

Overall, 17.6% of adolescents reported self-harm in the past 12 months, including 22.3% of girls and 13.1% of boys (p < .001). Self-harm was most prevalent among APD misusers (40.4%), particularly among those who had misused more than one APD (60.8%), followed by medical users (18.7%) and non-users (11.1%) (p < .001). Similar patterns were observed in both genders (Figure 3(b)).

Associations Between APD Use Status and Mental Health Indicators

Adolescents with poor mental wellbeing reported higher rates of medical use (31.4% vs. 24.7%) and misuse (23.6% vs. 14.1%) than those with good wellbeing. Self-harm showed even larger differences in APD use status: 46.8% of self-harming adolescents reported medical use and 41.9% reported misuse, compared with 23.8% and 13.2%. respectively, among those without self-harm.

Logistic regression analyses (Table 3) showed that poor mental wellbeing significantly increased the odds of APD misuse compared with non-use (aOR = 1.49) but did not distinguish medical users from misusers or non-users. Self-harm was the strongest predictor: adolescents reporting self-harm had almost threefold higher odds of misuse vs. non-use (aOR = 2.78), as well as increased odds of misuse vs. medical use (aOR = 1.66) and medical use vs. non-use (aOR = 1.57). Gender differences remained significant in the adjusted models: girls had higher odds of both misuse (aOR = 1.46) and medical use (aOR = 1.52) compared with boys, although misuse did not differ from medical use by gender.

Discussion

Prevalence of APDs Medical Use and Misuse

This study provides an overview of the medical use and misuse of APDs among adolescents in Estonia. The findings reveal that both medical use and misuse are relatively common, particularly among girls.

This study found that more than one in four (27.8%) Estonian adolescents had used at least one APD for medical purposes during their lifetime, whereas almost one in five (18.3%) reported lifetime APD misuse. According to the latest ESPAD report, the European average for pharmaceutical misuse was 14.0%. Overall, the prevalence of APD misuse among Estonian adolescents was higher than the European average and most neighbouring countries (ESPAD Group, 2025a). In comparison, prevalence was lower in Finland (6.3%), Sweden (12.0%), and Latvia (8.9%), but higher in Lithuania (29.0%).

Differences between countries may reflect variation in prescribing patterns, national medicine regulation systems, and differences in adolescent healthcare pathways. Sedatives/sleeping pills were the most commonly medically used and misused medications. For medical use, prescription painkillers (opioids) were also commonly reported, whereas, for misuse, the questionnaire referred more broadly to painkillers used to get high. Muscle relaxants were least frequently reported in both contexts. Among medical users, one-third reported using more than one APD, and a similar proportion of misusers reported polysubstance misuse. These findings indicate that APD exposure, both medical and non-medical, is quite widespread among Estonian adolescents, with evidence of multi-drug involvement.

Girls were more likely than boys to both medically use and misuse APDs, consistent with previous studies and recent ESPAD findings (Carmona et al., 2020; Carrasco-Garrido et al., 2019; ESPAD Group, 2020, 2025a). These differences may reflect gendered patterns in symptom reporting, with girls reporting higher levels of psychological distress, greater healthcare utilization, and more internalizing coping strategies (Golinelli et al., 2025; Ricci et al., 2026; Salk et al., 2017). This pattern contrasts with many other substance use behaviors, such as illicit drug use, which are typically more prevalent among boys, highlighting the distinct gender profile of prescription drug misuse among adolescents. However, previous research and recent ESPAD findings show narrowing or partially reversing gender differences in alcohol use, with declines often greater among boys and, in some countries, girls reporting similar or higher levels of alcohol use and heavy episodic drinking, reflecting broader shifts in social norms related to adolescent substance use (ESPAD Group, 2025a; Kudre et al., 2021).

Although some adolescents reported both medical use and misuse of APDs, these behaviors should not be viewed as part of the same continuum. Medical use reflects treatment and contacts with healthcare services, whereas misuse represents non-medical consumption associated with different behavioral and psychosocial factors and potential health risks. Two-thirds of adolescents who had misused APDs also reported medical use, with overlap more pronounced among boys. One possible explanation is greater familiarity with APDs or increased access to medications stored at home among adolescents reporting both medical use and misuse. However, the cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions regarding temporal pathways between medical use and misuse.

Sources, Motives and Perceptions of APD Misuse

The finding that most misused APDs were obtained from home or from relatives and friends rather than from street dealers or from internet is consistent with previous literature (Novak et al., 2016; Schepis et al., 2019; Veliz et al., 2023). This suggests that access to APDs through peers or family medicine cabinets may contribute to misuse. One in five respondents reported obtaining an APD from a pharmacy without a prescription; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as discussed in the section on Strengths and Limitations. Overall, the results align with broader adolescent substance use research showing that availability through peers, social networks, and household environments is consistently associated with substance use (Nowak et al., 2018).

The motivations reported, primarily to improve sleep, manage psychological distress, or to get high, illustrate both the self-treatment and recreational motives for misuse and are consistent with previous literature (Nkouonlack et al., 2023; Schepis et al., 2021). Open-ended responses not captured by predefined options further highlighted heterogeneous and context-specific reasons, including curiosity or experimentation rather than a single dominant motive. Self-treatment motivations support the association between poor mental health and substance use as a coping strategy, and may indicate adolescents’ limited access to mental health services, reluctance to seek professional help (Landry et al., 2023), or lack of awareness of available support options. In Estonia, previous reports have highlighted challenges in timely access to child and adolescent mental health services, including shortages of specialists and long waiting times, although the extent of unmet treatment need among adolescents remains difficult to quantify (Ministry of Social Affairs, 2022; Sisask, 2023). Boys more often reported obtaining APDs from street dealers or the internet, suggesting gender differences in access pathways. In addition, boys were more likely to report misusing APDs to make parties more enjoyable, indicating some differences in motivations for misuse.

Perceptions of prescription medications as medically legitimate and less harmful may further contribute to misuse (Arria et al., 2008; Fleary et al., 2013). In contrast, research on adolescent substance use consistently shows higher perceived risk for illicit drugs, whereas risk perceptions for alcohol and tobacco are more strongly influenced by social norms, availability, and cultural context (ESPAD Group, 2025a; Kudre et al., 2021; Tamson et al., 2021). This perception may be reinforced by the declining use of traditional substances among young people in Estonia (Oja et al., 2023), potentially contributing to a shift towards alternative risk behaviors such as APD misuse, although this remains hypothetical and requires further research.

Consistent with these patterns, APD misusers perceived both lower health risks and greater availability compared with medical users and non-users. Almost half of misusers perceived occasional APD misuse as posing low or no health risk, compared with almost one in three of non-users. Misusers were also more likely to perceive APDs as easy to obtain compared with medical users and non-users, suggesting that lower risk perception and easier access may contribute to misuse. These findings align with earlier research (Forrest et al., 2025) and with broader substance use literature, including alcohol and cannabis use, showing that lower perceived risk and higher perceived availability are associated with increased substance use among adolescents (Kudre et al., 2021; Tamson et al., 2021). Together, these results suggest that APD misuse may partly reflect broader substance use vulnerability patterns rather than representing a distinct behavioral phenomenon.

From a prevention perspective, these findings suggest that reducing access, through improved prescribing practices, secure storage, and family-focused education, alongside providing realistic and nuanced information about the potential risks of APD misuse, rather than solely emphasizing harms, may therefore be an effective strategy to curb misuse.

Mental Wellbeing and Self-Harm

Almost half of adolescents reported poor mental wellbeing and around one in five reported self-harm, both of which were more common among girls and strongly associated with APD misuse, whereas self-harm was also associated with medical use. Poor mental wellbeing in this study reflects lower levels of positive mental functioning and subjective wellbeing rather than clinically diagnosed mental disorders. These high prevalence rates and marked gender differences align with previous research showing that adolescent girls are more likely to experience psychological distress and engage in self-harm (McManus et al., 2019; Patalay & Fitzsimons, 2021). Although our data did not allow to determine whether adolescents reporting poor mental wellbeing and/or self-harming behavior had sought professional help, the high prevalence of both nonetheless underscores the need for widespread screening and targeted interventions to identify mental health problems and ensure appropriate support.

Poor mental wellbeing and self-harm were most prevalent among APD misusers, and adolescents reporting these problems were more likely to report both medical use and misuse of APDs. Multivariable analyses confirmed that poor mental wellbeing was associated with 1.5 times higher odds of misuse, but not with medical use compared with non-use. One possible explanation is that adolescents using APDs medically may have been receiving treatment for underlying problems, although this could not be assessed in the current study. These findings indicate an association between psychological distress and APD use, particularly misuse. However, poor mental wellbeing as measured in this study reflects lower subjective wellbeing rather than clinically diagnosed mental disorders or treatment need. In addition, adolescents receiving APDs for medical reasons may have experienced poor mental wellbeing prior to treatment initiation, which cannot be disentangled in this cross-sectional design. The questionnaire also did not capture other forms of treatment, such as psychological therapy, or other psychiatric medications.

Substance misuse and mental health problems are closely interrelated, and adolescents using APDs likely represent a heterogeneous group with varying levels of psychological distress and substance use behaviors (Nkouonlack et al., 2023; Schepis et al., 2021). Therefore, the observed association between poor mental wellbeing, self-harm, and APD misuse should not be interpreted solely as self-medication for mental health problems, but rather as part of broader psychological vulnerability related to substance use. This interpretation aligns with previous research (Guo et al., 2016; Sampasa-Kanyinga et al., 2021) showing that poor mental wellbeing and self-harming behavior often co-occur with substance misuse among adolescents, particularly among those facing greater psychosocial or socioeconomic vulnerability (Patrick et al., 2012).

Overall, girls consistently reported higher prevalence of both APD medical use and misuse, poorer mental wellbeing, and more self-harm compared with boys. Girls also had higher adjusted odds of both medical use and misuse, although they were not more likely than boys to misuse relative to medical use APDs. This aligns with known gender differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, mood and anxiety symptoms (Moin et al., 2023), and higher levels of psychological distress among girls (Richert et al., 2020).

The associations between poor mental wellbeing, self-harm, and APD misuse suggest that prevention efforts should extend beyond regulating access and prescription controls to include mental health support and early intervention programmes targeting at-risk adolescents. Evidence from several high-income countries suggests increasing levels of psychological distress and self-harm among adolescents over the past decade, particularly among girls (Patalay & Fitzsimons, 2021). At the same time, reducing diversion and accessibility, alongside addressing misconceptions about the safety of prescription medications, should remain a public health priority.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It is based on a large, nationally representative sample and provides a comprehensive assessment of APDś medical use and misuse.

However, some limitations should be noted. The self-reported data may be affected by recall or social desirability bias, potentially leading to under- or overestimation of APD use or self-reported mental wellbeing and self-harming behavior. In addition, some respondents may have misunderstood questions related to prescription medications and reported over-the-counter medications instead. This limitation is particularly relevant for the misuse item on painkillers used to get high, which did not distinguish opioids from other prescription or non-prescription painkillers. Given that prescription-only medications are tightly regulated in Estonia, this may have led to some misclassification in self-reported sources of medication and may also have affected estimates of the prevalence of prescribed use and misuse of prescription medications. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between mental wellbeing and APD use. Although the questionnaire included categorical information on lifetime frequency of APD misuse (0, 1–2, 3+ occasions), this study did not examine frequency or dosage patterns in the analyses. Consequently, the findings are limited to the presence or absence of medical use and misuse rather than intensity or clinical severity. In addition, the measures used in this study do not allow distinction between reduced mental wellbeing and clinically diagnosed mental health disorders. The study focused on Estonian adolescents aged 15–16 years who were present in class on the survey day, which may restrict generalizability to other age groups or to those absent during data collection. Finally, as the ESPAD questionnaire captures only lifetime use, the findings reflect lifetime rather than current or recent APD use and may include adolescents who experimented with APDs only once or several years ago.

Conclusions

This study shows that both medical use and misuse of APDs are common among Estonian adolescents, with higher rates among girls. A considerable proportion of adolescents reported both medical use and misuse of APDs. Almost half of adolescents reported poor mental wellbeing and one in five reported self-harm, both more prevalent among APD misusers, who often obtained medications from home, reported motives related to sleep problems, psychological distress, or recreational purposes, and perceived them as low-risk and easily accessible. The observed associations between APD misuse, poor mental wellbeing, and self-harm highlight that APD misuse frequently co-occurs with lower levels of mental wellbeing and psychological distress. Preventing APD misuse therefore requires more than restricting access or correcting risk perceptions; it necessitates an integrated public health approach that strengthens early identification and monitoring of adolescent substance use, mental wellbeing, and psychological distress, ensures access to appropriate care when indicated, and reduces diversion within families.
