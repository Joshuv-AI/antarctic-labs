A remark on the comparison of the sum and the maximum of positive random variables
Kazuki Okamura

Abstract
. We disprove a conjecture stated in a recent paper by Arnold and Villasenor concerning the sum and the maximum of independent and identically distributed half-normal random variables. Our method is applicable to generalized gamma distributions.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
60E05; 62E10

1.
Introduction

Recently, Arnold and Villasenor
[
2
]
provided two proofs that the distributions of the sum and the maximum of two independent half-normal random variables are of the same type.
More specifically,
it holds that

X_{1}+X_{2}=\sqrt{2}\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\quad\textup{ in distribution}

(1.1)

if
\displaystyle X_{1}
and
\displaystyle X_{2}
are independent and have a common half-normal distribution.
They also showed that under some regularity conditions, (
1.1
) implies that
\displaystyle X_{1}
has a half-normal distribution.
In
[
2
, Section 4]
, they conjectured that for
\displaystyle n\geq 3
,

X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}=(n!)^{1/n}\max\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}\quad\textup{ in distribution}

(1.2)

if
\displaystyle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent and have a common half-normal distribution.

The purpose of this note is to disprove the conjecture.
Our arguments are applicable to a class of distributions containing all generalized gamma distributions, which are originally introduced by
[
4
]
.

Assume that
\displaystyle X_{i},i\geq 1
, are independent and identically distributed random variables supported on
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
.
Let
\displaystyle S_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\displaystyle M_{n}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}
for
\displaystyle n\geq 1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Gamma
be the gamma function and define

\displaystyle h(x)\coloneqq\frac{\log\Gamma(x+1)}{x},x>0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle X_{1}
has a strictly positive density
\displaystyle f
supported on

\displaystyle(0,\infty)
and that there exist four positive constants
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2},\alpha
, and
\displaystyle\beta
such that

\lim_{x\to+0}\frac{f(x)}{x^{\alpha-1}}=c_{1}.

(1.3)

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-\log f(x)}{x^{\beta}}=c_{2}.

(1.4)

Assume that
\displaystyle n\geq 2
and

\beta<\frac{\log n}{\log n+h(\alpha)-h(n\alpha)}.

(1.5)

Then there is no constant
\displaystyle C
such that
\displaystyle S_{n}=CM_{n}
in distribution.

If
\displaystyle X_{1}
has the standard half-normal distribution,
then (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) hold with
\displaystyle c_{1}=\sqrt{2/\pi},c_{2}=1/2,\alpha=1
, and
\displaystyle\beta=2
.
For
\displaystyle\alpha=1
and
\displaystyle\beta=2
,
condition (
1.5
) is equivalent to
\displaystyle n\geq 3
;
this can be verified by induction on
\displaystyle n
.
For every
\displaystyle\beta\in(0,1]
, (
1.5
) holds for every
\displaystyle n\geq 2
.
We remark that
\displaystyle\log n+h(\alpha)-h(n\alpha)>0
for every
\displaystyle n\geq 2
by Proposition
2.3
(i) below.

We show Theorem
1.1
by contradiction.
The main idea of the proof is to first compare the asymptotic behavior of
\displaystyle P(S_{n}\leq x)
and
\displaystyle P(M_{n}\leq x)
as
\displaystyle x\to+0
and then compare the asymptotic behavior of
\displaystyle P(S_{n}>x)
and
\displaystyle P(M_{n}>x)
as
\displaystyle x\to+\infty
.

2.
Proof

First, we compare the asymptotic behavior of
\displaystyle P(S_{n}\leq x)
and
\displaystyle P(M_{n}\leq x)
as
\displaystyle x\to+0
.
Let
\displaystyle C_{\alpha,n}\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(n\alpha+1)^{1/(n\alpha)}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)^{1/\alpha}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If there exists a constant
\displaystyle C
such that
\displaystyle S_{n}=CM_{n}
in distribution,
then
\displaystyle C=C_{\alpha,n}
.

Proof.

We see that for every
\displaystyle x>0
,

P(S_{n}\leq x)=\int_{D_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n},

where
\displaystyle D_{1}\coloneqq\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(0,\infty)^{n}\colon x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}<x\}
.
By the change of variables
\displaystyle x_{i}=xu_{i}
,
we obtain that for every
\displaystyle x>0
,

P(S_{n}\leq x)=x^{\alpha n}\int_{D_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f(xu_{i})}{(xu_{i})^{\alpha-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{\alpha-1}du_{1}\cdots du_{n},

where
\displaystyle D_{2}\coloneqq\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in(0,\infty)^{n}\colon u_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}<1\}
.
By (
1.3
),
we can apply the dominated convergence theorem and obtain that

\lim_{x\to+0}\int_{D_{2}}\left|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f(xu_{i})}{(xu_{i})^{\alpha-1}}-c_{1}^{n}\right|\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{\alpha-1}du_{1}\cdots du_{n}=0.

Using this together with the identity
\displaystyle\int_{D_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{\alpha-1}du_{1}\cdots du_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)^{n}}{\Gamma(n\alpha+1)}
,
we obtain that

\lim_{x\to+0}\frac{P(S_{n}\leq x)}{x^{\alpha n}}=c_{1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)^{n}}{\Gamma(n\alpha+1)}.

(2.1)

We see that for every
\displaystyle x>0
,

P(M_{n}\leq x)=P(X_{1}\leq x)^{n}=x^{\alpha n}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(xu)}{(xu)^{\alpha-1}}u^{\alpha-1}du\right)^{n}.

By (
1.3
),
we can apply the dominated convergence theorem and obtain that

\lim_{x\to+0}\frac{P(M_{n}\leq x)}{x^{\alpha n}}=\frac{c_{1}^{n}}{\alpha^{n}}.

(2.2)

By (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we see that
\displaystyle C=C_{\alpha,n}
.
∎

Second, we compare the asymptotic behavior of
\displaystyle P(S_{n}>x)
and
\displaystyle P(M_{n}>x)
as
\displaystyle x\to+\infty
.
By Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle S_{n}=C_{\alpha,n}M_{n}
in distribution.
Without loss of generality, we can let
\displaystyle c_{2}=1
.

By the assumption (
1.4
),
for every
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, it holds that for large
\displaystyle x
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(-(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta}\right)\leq f(x)\leq\exp\left(-(1-\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta}\right)
.

By integration by parts,
it holds that for every
\displaystyle\beta>0
and
\displaystyle z>0
,

\int_{z}^{\infty}\exp(-t^{\beta})dt=\frac{\exp(-z^{\beta})}{\beta}z^{1-\beta}-\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}\int_{z}^{\infty}t^{-\beta}\exp(-t^{\beta})dt.

Hence we see that for large
\displaystyle x
,

P(X_{1}>x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\exp(-(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta})}{\beta(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta-1}}

(2.3)

and

P(X_{1}>x)\leq 2\frac{\exp(-(1-\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta})}{\beta(1-\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta-1}}.

(2.4)

We give a lower bound for
\displaystyle P(M_{n}>x)
.
Since
\displaystyle f
is strictly positive on
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle P(X_{1}>x)>0
for every
\displaystyle x>0
.
We see that

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(M_{n}>x)}{P(X_{1}>x)}=n.

(2.5)

By (
2.3
) and (
2.5
),
we see that for large
\displaystyle x
,

P(M_{n}>x)\geq\frac{n\exp(-(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta})}{4\beta(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}x^{\beta-1}}.

(2.6)

We give an upper bound for
\displaystyle P(S_{n}>x)
.

We first consider the case where
\displaystyle\beta\geq 1
.
Since
\displaystyle\beta\geq 1
, by convexity,
we see that
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\beta}\geq n^{1-\beta}S_{n}^{\beta}
.
By this and the exponential Chebyshev inequality,
we see that for every
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle x>0
and
\displaystyle n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(S_{n}>x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\beta}\geq n^{1-\beta}x^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1-\epsilon)^{\beta}n^{1-\beta}x^{\beta}\right)E\left[\exp\left((1-\epsilon)^{\beta}X_{1}^{\beta}\right)\right]^{n}.

(2.7)

By (
1.4
),

\displaystyle E\left[\exp\left((1-\epsilon)^{\beta}X_{1}^{\beta}\right)\right]<\infty
.
By (
1.5
), it holds that
\displaystyle C_{\alpha,n}^{-\beta}<n^{1-\beta}
.
Now we take a sufficiently small
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
such that
\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{\beta}C_{\alpha,n}^{-\beta}<(1-\epsilon)^{\beta}n^{1-\beta}
.
By this, (
2.6
) and (
2
),
we obtain that

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{P\left(M_{n}>x/C_{\alpha,n}\right)}{P(S_{n}>x)}=\infty.

This contradicts
\displaystyle S_{n}=C_{\alpha,n}M_{n}
in distribution.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case where
\displaystyle\beta\geq 1
is completed.

We next consider the case where
\displaystyle 0<\beta<1
.
By (
1.4
),
\displaystyle E[\exp(tX_{1})]=\infty
for every
\displaystyle t>0
.
By
[
3
, Theorem 2]
,

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(S_{n}>x)}{P(M_{n}>x)}<\infty.

(2.8)

By Lemma
2.1
, if there exists a constant
\displaystyle C
such that
\displaystyle S_{n}=CM_{n}
in distribution,
then
\displaystyle C=C_{\alpha,n}
.
By (
2.8
) and (
2.5
),
we obtain that

\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(X_{1}>x)}{P(X_{1}>x/C_{\alpha,n})}=\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(M_{n}>x)}{P(M_{n}>x/C_{\alpha,n})}>0.

(2.9)

By Lemma
2.2
(i) below,
\displaystyle h(x)
is strictly increasing.
Hence
\displaystyle C_{\alpha,n}>1
for
\displaystyle n\geq 2
.
By (
2.3
) and (
2.4
),

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(X_{1}>x)}{P(X_{1}>x/C_{\alpha,n})}=0.

This contradicts (
2.9
).
The proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case where
\displaystyle 0<\beta<1
is completed.

The following assertions provide properties of
\displaystyle h
.

Lemma 2.2
.

(i)
\displaystyle h
is strictly increasing on
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
.

(ii)
\displaystyle g(x)\coloneqq h(\exp(x))
is strictly convex on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

(i) Let
\displaystyle\psi
be the digamma function.
Then
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\log\Gamma(x+1)=\psi(x+1)
.
Since the digamma function is strictly increasing,
\displaystyle\log\Gamma(x+1)
is strictly convex.
Hence, the secant slope
\displaystyle\frac{\log\Gamma(x+1)-\log\Gamma(1)}{x}=h(x)
is strictly increasing on
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
.

(ii)
Since
\displaystyle h(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s+1)ds
,
we see that

th^{\prime}(t)=\psi(t+1)-\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s+1)ds=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}s\psi^{\prime}(s+1)ds.

Since
\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)=\exp(x)h^{\prime}(\exp(x))
,
it suffices to show that
\displaystyle q(s)\coloneqq s\psi^{\prime}(s+1)
is strictly increasing.
By an integral expression of the trigamma function (
[
1
, Formula 6.4.1, p.260]
),

\psi^{\prime}(s+1)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r\exp(-sr)}{\exp(r)-1}dr.

Hence

q(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-r)\rho\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)dr,

(2.10)

where
\displaystyle\rho(t)\coloneqq\frac{t}{\exp(t)-1}
.
Since
\displaystyle\rho
is strictly decreasing,
\displaystyle q
is strictly increasing.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Set
\displaystyle a_{n}\coloneqq\frac{h(n\alpha)-h(\alpha)}{\log n}
for
\displaystyle n\geq 2
.
Then

(i)
\displaystyle a_{n}<1
for every
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=1
.

(ii)
\displaystyle(a_{n})_{n}
is strictly increasing.

(iii) There exists
\displaystyle N(\alpha,\beta)\geq 2
such that (
1.5
) holds if and only if
\displaystyle n\geq N(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

(i) By (
2.10
),
\displaystyle q(s)<1
for every
\displaystyle s>0
.
Hence

g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\exp(x)}\int_{0}^{\exp(x)}q(s)ds<1.

Since
\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{g(\log(n\alpha))-g(\log\alpha)}{\log(n\alpha)-\log\alpha}
,
\displaystyle a_{n}<1
.
By Stirling’s formula,

h(x)=\frac{\log\Gamma(x+1)}{x}=\log x-1+O\left(\frac{\log x}{x}\right),\quad x\to\infty.

Hence
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=1
.

(ii) Let
\displaystyle b_{n}\coloneqq h((n+1)\alpha)-h(n\alpha)
and
\displaystyle c_{n}\coloneqq\log(n+1)-\log n
for
\displaystyle n\geq 1
.
Since
\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i}}
, it suffices to show that
\displaystyle\left(\frac{b_{n}}{c_{n}}\right)_{n}
is strictly increasing.
We see that

\frac{b_{n}}{c_{n}}=\frac{g(\log((n+1)\alpha))-g(\log(n\alpha))}{\log((n+1)\alpha)-\log(n\alpha)}.

By the mean value theorem and Lemma
2.2
(ii),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{b_{n}}{c_{n}}\right)_{n}
is strictly increasing.

(iii) This follows from (i) and (ii).
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle n=3
.
For the half-normal distribution, we have an alternative proof.
It is easy to see that

E\left[S_{3}^{2}\right]=E[(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})^{2}]=3E[X_{1}^{2}]+6E[X_{1}]^{2}=3+\frac{12}{\pi}.

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

E\left[M_{3}^{2}\right]=1+\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle F
be the density and distribution functions of
\displaystyle X_{1}
respectively.
Then the density function of
\displaystyle M_{3}
is given by
\displaystyle g(x)=3f(x)F(x)^{2}
.
Since
\displaystyle xf(x)=-f^{\prime}(x)
, it holds that

E\left[M_{3}^{2}\right]=3\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)F(x)^{2}dx=-3\int_{0}^{\infty}xF(x)^{2}f^{\prime}(x)dx.

By integration by parts,

\int_{0}^{\infty}xF(x)^{2}f^{\prime}(x)dx=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(F(x)^{2}+2xf(x)F(x))f(x)dx.

Since
\displaystyle 2xf(x)^{2}=-\frac{d}{dx}f(x)^{2}
, we obtain that

\int_{0}^{\infty}(F(x)^{2}+2xf(x)F(x))f(x)dx=\frac{1}{3}-\int_{0}^{\infty}F(x)(f(x)^{2})^{\prime}dx=\frac{1}{3}+\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)^{3}dx.

Hence,

E\left[M_{3}^{2}\right]=1+3\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)^{3}dx=1+\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}.

∎

By Lemma
2.1
,
if there exists a constant
\displaystyle C
such that
\displaystyle S_{3}=CM_{3}
in distribution,
then
\displaystyle C
would be equal to
\displaystyle 6^{1/3}
and hence
\displaystyle 3+\frac{12}{\pi}=6^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(1+\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\right)
.
Then
\displaystyle\pi\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3},6^{1/3})
, which contradicts the fact that
\displaystyle\pi
is a transcendental number.
We see that
\displaystyle\frac{3+\frac{12}{\pi}}{1+\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi}}
is approximately
\displaystyle 3.24
and
\displaystyle 6^{\frac{2}{3}}
is approximately
\displaystyle 3.30
; they are numerically close to each other.
