Spatial analysis of malignant-immune cell interactions in the tumor microenvironment using topological data analysis
Authors: Seol Ah Park, Davide Gurnari, Paweł Dłotko, Yongsoo Kim, Jooyoung Hahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spatial interactions between malignant and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment are important for tumor immunobiology and patient outcomes. However, analytical tools that can extract rigorous yet interpretable spatial features and link them to patient outcomes remain limited. We propose a framework integrating TDA with statistical approaches to extract interpretable spatial features characterizing malignant-immune interactions. We introduce Topological Malignant Region (TopMR), which uses topological persistence to automatically define regions of malignant cells, providing an objective reference for spatial analysis even when tumor boundaries are ambiguous. Global-scale infiltration is quantified using signed distances from immune cells to the TopMR boundary and local-scale interactions are captured via malignant cell density around individual immune cells. These global and local features are integrated into a unified signed distance-density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD) space, enabling comprehensive characterization of spatial patterns. We apply this framework to high-resolution multiplex immunofluorescence images of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, analyzing both malignant-enriched and tumor border regions. Two-stage hierarchical clustering stratifies patients based on spatial interaction patterns, revealing associations with survival outcomes. This framework provides an end-to-end pipeline from spatial feature extraction to clinical interpretation, suggesting how region-aware spatial analysis can capture biologically meaningful patterns linked to patient survival.

Introduction

Elucidating the spatial interactions between malignant and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is critical for understanding tumor progression and predicting patient outcomes1,2. The spatial distribution of these cells reflects complex biological processes such as immune infiltration and evasion, and spatial analysis can help disentangle these processes and link them to clinical prognosis3,4. From an analytical perspective, spatial analysis within the TME typically involves quantifying spatial relationships among different cell populations (e.g., tumor and immune cells) and interpreting how these quantified patterns reflect their interactions. Therefore, developing metrics that provide both rigorous quantification and biological interpretability is essential.

Numerous studies have developed methods to extract spatial features from cellular imaging data to correlate these features with patient outcomes. Point-based analyses have quantified tumor-immune organization using cell densities and nearest-neighbor statistics5, whereas graph-based models have represented cellular neighborhoods to capture structural patterns and enable patient clustering6. Another integrative framework has outlined major categories of spatial analysis, such as localization-, distance-, and graph-based metrics, highlighting their potential clinical relevance7. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, combining spatial descriptors (e.g., Ripley’s L-function) with conventional cell counts and densities has yielded complementary prognostic insights8. These approaches predominantly rely on cell-to-cell proximity, capturing local spatial relationships within limited neighborhoods.

Beyond local interactions, some studies have shown that region- and boundary-scale organization, such as tumor-stroma rims or hypoxic cores, can strongly influence immune exclusion, infiltration, and clinical outcomes, sometimes outperforming nearest-neighbor metrics in a context-dependent manner3,9–11. Indeed, direct cell-to-cell distances are often biased by local sampling density and structural voids: dense malignant regions yield smaller distances, whereas stromal gaps inflate them. Without accounting for spatial inhomogeneity, such proximity measures may capture sampling artifacts rather than true biological structure12. These issues motivate moving beyond local metrics toward mesoscopic, region-aware representations that are both robust and biologically interpretable.

Building on this motivation, recent studies have begun to consider regions of cells to identify spatial interactions between tumor and immune cells. A workflow13 characterized spatial distributions of multiple immune markers within pathologist-annotated tissue regions using point pattern statistics and cluster analysis. A statistical framework14 extended Ripley’s K-function to quantify region-to-cell rather than cell-to-cell interactions by computing distances from tumor-defined regions and applying K-function analyses across levelsets. The method effectively showed spatial patterns in histopathological images across patients. Although the tumor region was manually selected by experts, this work showed the potential of region-to-cell approaches for investigating spatial patterns within the TME. Additionally, Topological Data Analysis (TDA)15–17 has emerged as a distinct mathematical framework that characterizes spatial organization through topological features rather than conventional statistics. One approach, based on chromatic alpha complexes, quantifies spatial relationships among differently labeled cells by tracking the scale at which shared empty regions appear as virtual cell boundaries expand18. Another framework employs Dowker and Witness persistent homology to capture proximity and clustering patterns among synthetic cell populations19. Dowker persistence encodes pairwise interactions, whereas Witness persistence treats one population as landmarks to assess the spatial distribution of others. Although not yet applied to biological data, these topology-based approaches may possibly offer a rigorous mathematical framework for quantifying intermixing between cell types.

In this study, we propose a TDA-based integrative framework that combines topological and statistical approaches to extract interpretable spatial interaction features from the TME and relate them to clinical outcomes. Our framework comprises four components, each designed to address a key limitation of traditional spatial analyses: (1) we define a Topological Malignant Region (TopMR) to reconstruct the global organization of malignant cells, providing a topologically defined tumor regions even when explicit margins are absent or irregular; (2) we extract global and local interaction features—global features quantify immune infiltration through signed distances from the TopMR and local features capture malignant cell density around individual immune cells, reflecting micro-scale heterogeneity; (3) we employ Ball Mapper20 to summarize the resulting high-dimensional interaction space, enabling visualization and micro-clusters of spatial patterns across all datasets; and (4) we link these summarized interaction patterns to patient survival using a two-stage hierarchical clustering approach that accounts for varying numbers of data samples per patient and automates parameter selection. Together, these components combine topological abstraction, quantitative analysis, and biological interpretability to offer a coherent approach for characterizing tumor-immune interaction with potential clinical relevance. Finally, we apply our framework to point cloud data derived from high-resolution multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) images of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), annotated as either malignant cell-enriched or tumor border regions exhibiting distinct spatial organizations. TopMR robustly captures diverse global morphologies of malignant cell organization across both image types. By integrating global and local interaction features, our framework captures distinct spatial interaction patterns that are indicative of differences in patient survival outcomes.

Malignant and immune cells in tissue are spatially non-random due to underlying biological architecture. Structural components such as necrotic zones, stromal regions, vascular lumens, and non-malignant compartments often create spatial voids or clustered distributions (Figs. 3 and 5). We therefore propose a method to capture underlying spatial organization from the given point cloud, providing robustness against heterogeneous or irregular cell distributions. It enables the analysis to focus on biologically meaningful malignant-immune interactions rather than structural biases.

To extract the spatial organization, we employ tools from TDA. In a Vietoris-Rips filtration, a distance threshold is gradually increased, and all pairs of points closer than this threshold are connected by an edge. Whenever every pair within a group of points lies within the threshold, the group forms a simplex: an edge for two points, a triangle for three, a tetrahedron for four, and so on. The collection of all such simplices is a simplicial complex, which generalizes a network by recording not only pairwise connections (edges) but also higher-order relationships among points that are all pairwise within the threshold. Because the construction depends only on pairwise distances, it promotes connectivity and provides robust global shape extraction. As the threshold grows, topological structures such as connected components (H0) and loops (H1) emerge and subsequently disappear (e.g., components merge and loops are filled in by higher-dimensional simplices). This entire process is summarized in a persistence diagram (PD), which records the birth and death of each such structure as a function of the distance threshold15,16.

Small perturbations in point positions produce only small changes in pairwise distances. Therefore, birth and death values shift only slightly, making PDs stable under positional noise such as cell-center detection, segmentation errors, and a small fraction of phenotype misclassifications. Structures with long lifetimes (large gap between birth and death) can represent robust spatial patterns of the point cloud, whereas short-lived structures near the diagonal of the PD typically reflect noise or local irregularities. Ideally, the PD should cover all scales, but this becomes computationally challenging for images with a large number of points21. This motivates a region-to-cell (malignant region) perspective, rather than a purely cell-to-cell (e.g., nearest-neighbor) view, for capturing these relationships more robustly. Accordingly, we quantify malignant-immune spatial interactions using signed distances from the TopMR boundary to each immune cell (TopMR-to-cell), since TopMR captures regions of malignant cells.

Ripley’s L-function

In Case 1, Ripley’s L-function (first panel in the fourth row) exceeds the theoretical complete spatial randomness (CSR) line at larger radii (approximately r > 60), suggesting global clustering or attraction, consistent with previous findings8. In Case 2, the L-function remains below the CSR line, indicating spatial separation between the two populations. In Case 3, the L-function lies far below the CSR line, likely due to local spatial isolation of the two cell types. Since this method relies on global spatial statistics and assumes homogeneity, it provides limited information for spatially heterogeneous or unevenly distributed patterns.

Generalized Ripley’s K-function

The generalized Ripley’s K-function14 was originally defined using only positive distances from a region. Since our framework requires both positive and negative distances from TopMR, we use our signed distance metric in this computation. The distance range for the generalized K-function is set using the global minimum and maximum distances observed across all three cases, so that a common range is used to account for differences in image size. In Case 1 (first panel in the fifth row), only green bars are observed across all distance zones, indicating an excess of CD163+ cells relative to a uniform reference (cell accumulation). In Case 2 (second panel in the fifth row), gray bars (statistically unreliable estimates) dominate at short distances because most CD163+ cells lie far from malignant cells, leaving too few neighboring points to compute stable statistics near the malignant region. Green bars appear only at large distances, indicating accumulation far from malignant cells; however, such long-distance accumulation is difficult to interpret biologically in terms of local malignant-immune interaction. In Case 3 (third panel in the fifth row), red bars (depletion) appear at short distances and green bars (accumulation) emerge at larger distances. This transition from depletion near the malignant region to accumulation farther away reflects the partially mixed pattern and provides more nuanced information than Ripley’s L-function in this example. Despite these advantages, the generalized Ripley’s K-function poses practical challenges for large-scale analysis. When a single global distance range is applied across images of varying size and malignant-immune separation, many images may produce numerous gray bars (unreliable estimates), which become zeros in the resulting feature matrix. This leads to highly sparse feature vectors, complicating clustering or classification. Conversely, if the distance range is adapted separately for each image, the physical meaning of the distance axis is no longer consistent across images: a given column in the feature matrix no longer corresponds to a fixed distance scale. In that case, feature values remain dense but lose direct interpretability in terms of absolute distances from the malignant region.

The quantitative measures mh, mv, and e1 in the proposed feature matrix enable effective characterization of both the extent and directionality of immune infiltration across all three cases. By explicitly encoding signed distances from the TopMR boundary and evaluating tumor-cell density at the locations of immune cells along this distance axis, and by using TopMR as a global representation of malignant-cell regions, our method enables a quantitative categorization of the three cases that is biologically interpretable as high, low, or heterogeneous infiltration.

Ball Mapper reveals continuous trajectories of malignant-immune interaction

Having quantified the per-image malignant-immune interaction as 18-dimensional vectors, we summarize their organization across all images using Ball Mapper, a topological method that represents a high-dimensional dataset as a graph of overlapping micro-clusters; the graph then provides the basis for our interpretation on the DLBCL feature matrix.

The input is the feature matrix (Eq. (13)) whose rows capture immune cell infiltration and the local concentration of malignant cells across three aggregated phenotypes. Using the cosine dissimilarity and the parameter ϵ of the spatial granularity, Ball Mapper represents this dataset as a simplified graph with nodes and edges (e.g. Fig. 6). A node consists of row vectors (images) with similar characteristics; thus, each node in the graph can be regarded as a micro-cluster in the feature space. An edge between nodes indicates that there is at least one common feature shared by nodes. Adjusting ϵ allows control over the resolution of the graph, with smaller values producing a more detailed graph and larger values generating broader connections among features. A sensitivity analysis of the Ball Mapper graph over a ±50% range of ϵ0 = 4.5 ⋅ 10−2 shows that (i) ϵ0 is the value at which the dominant connected component forms, and (ii) the pairwise co-membership of samples, which samples share at least one node, is locally stable in a neighborhood of this value. Because Ball Mapper produces an overlapping cover rather than a partition, stability is assessed at the level of sample pairs rather than node identities. In this section, we assess whether the spatial interaction patterns identified above associate with patient survival outcomes. While Ball Mapper effectively captures similarity among 18D feature vectors from images, it operates at the image-level rather than the patient-level. Given the variable number of images per patient (ranging from 3 to 48), directly associating Ball Mapper nodes with survival outcomes (e.g., by averaging survival times across images in each node) would be biased. To address this, we adopt a two-stage hierarchical clustering (HC) strategy, using Euclidean distance with Ward linkage. Stage 1: we cluster images by their spatial interaction patterns in nodes of the Ball Mapper graph. Stage 2: For each patient, we compute the normalized distribution of their images across the clusters obtained from Stage 1. We then group patients by how their images distribute across clusters, i.e., patients with similar image distributions are grouped together. The two-stage HC yields patient-level clusters that can be used for Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis.

We therefore require an algorithm for choosing cluster numbers at both stages and for providing statistical stability. To more fully explore the feature geometry in the 18D space and assess stability, we rerun Ball Mapper multiple times after randomly permuting the rows of the feature matrix (Eq. (13)). These permutations leave the data unchanged but produce complementary coverings, allowing us to observe the same structure in high-dimensional space from multiple views and assess which patterns are stable. With the multiple runs with permuted feature matrices, we determine the optimal number of clusters for both stages based on p-values from survival analysis rather than selecting them manually. Using this approach, we can both strengthen the statistical reliability of Ball Mapper and inform the optimal number of clusters for HC, thus combining the strengths of topological and statistical methods29.

Discussion

This study presents a computational framework based on TDA that enables end-to-end analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5 in Supplementary Section S6 of Supplementary Information) of malignant-immune spatial interactions, from interpretable feature extraction (considering both global and local aspects) to patient survival interpretation. We first computationally define regions of malignant cells, called TopMR, based on the topological persistence of the distribution. At the global scale, immune cell infiltration is quantified by signed distance functions from TopMR boundaries, which are robust against noise (e.g., cell-center extraction errors). Locally, malignant cell densities around individual immune cells are assessed using density functions. PCA summarizes each image’s point cloud data in the signed distance-density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD) space to characterize malignant-immune interactions. Key features (averaged signed distance, averaged density, and the first two principal components) are visualized using Ball Mapper graphs. These graphs reveal continuous transitions of spatial interaction patterns linked to patient survival outcomes. To ensure robustness, we repeatedly apply Ball Mapper to shuffled feature matrices, creating multiple perspectives on the data structure. Each Ball Mapper graph is further summarized into clusters via a two-stage HC. To interpret the relationship between spatial interactions and survival, we associate final survival probabilities (KM analysis) with the averaged spatial features (Ball Mapper nodes) across immune phenotypes. From these associations, we construct a representative mean\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space by dividing cases into two survival groups. We use this averaged space, excluding outliers, to highlight general trends in malignant-immune interactions while mitigating artifacts from small sample sizes or phenotype mislabeling.

This framework provides two key advantages for spatial TME analysis. First, the automatic extraction of regions of malignant cells (TopMR) based on topological persistence provides an objective reference for quantifying immune-malignant spatial relationships. The spatial positioning of immune cells (whether infiltrating, at boundaries, or excluded) can be associated with immunotherapy response and patient prognosis, however manual tumor boundary annotation can vary between pathologists, particularly when boundaries are ambiguous. Second, the integration of TDA and statistical methods enables comprehensive end-to-end analysis linking spatial patterns to clinical outcomes. By capturing malignant-immune interactions at both global and local scales and connecting these features to survival through interpretable visualization and clustering, our approach provides a complete pipeline from computational spatial analysis to patients’ survival interpretation.

We apply the framework to DLBCL data, where our approach robustly handles complex and heterogeneous spatial cell distributions from both TR and BR images. The separate annotation of TR and BR images given by pathologists is expected to be important because the malignant-enriched regions or border regions of the tumors often represent distinct biological contexts with different immune infiltration patterns. In the patient cohort used in this study (see also Supplementary Table S2 in Supplementary Section S7 of Supplementary Information), our analysis revealed distinct spatial patterns in the mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space associated with event-free survival probabilities between TR and BR regions. Higher proportions of BR images correlate with improved survival, suggesting that well-defined tumor borders are beneficial clinically. This may relate to a previous report30 where a diffusely infiltrative PCNSL subtype (lymphomatosis cerebri) was difficult to diagnose and associated with poor prognosis. This suggests that ill-defined, widespread tumor infiltration may correlate with worse outcomes. However, further biological and molecular studies are needed to clarify any causal relationship.

In TR images, a notable survival-related difference is the positioning of CD8+ T cell subsets (CD3+CD8+PD-1− and CD3+CD8+PD-1+) in the mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space. In patients with good survival, these T cells are positioned closer to the zone of strong immune infiltration than other phenotypes, a feature absent in the poor survival group. These T cells are located near the TopMR periphery with reduced malignant cell density in their vicinity, possibly reflecting active tumor clearance by CD8+ T cells in the good survival group. Beyond CD8+ T cell positioning, the good survival group shows closer spatial proximity among all immune phenotypes in the mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space compared to the poor survival group. This closer positioning may indicate more coordinated immune activity or enhanced immune surveillance, though the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. In addition, CD3+CD8+PD-1− cells show a larger mean e1 magnitude, indicating widespread distribution in the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space. This dispersed pattern is associated with survival advantages, likely enhancing immune efficacy.

Analyzing BR images independently, we find that patients whose tumor border regions exhibit spatial TME patterns resembling those of malignant-enriched regions (characterized by high malignant cell density and infiltration) have lower survival probabilities. This pattern suggests that infiltrative tumor borders may represent an adverse prognostic indicator in DLBCL, similar to findings in colorectal cancer31 and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors32,33.

Despite the strengths of the proposed framework, several aspects warrant further development. The PCA-based pattern characterization in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{\mathcal{DD}}$$\end{document}sDD space offers parameter-free, compact features that respond continuously to variations within the normal range, but it can be sensitive to large variations. We apply the framework to a DLBCL cohort of 78 patients. The reported survival associations are statistically significant within this cohort and may have prognostic relevance. However, these associations are not adjusted for established clinical or molecular prognostic factors and remain to be validated in an independent cohort. Larger cohorts that account for these factors are needed to establish their generalizability and prognostic value. Broader clinical translation also faces a practical barrier, as the mIF imaging used in this study is more resource-intensive than routinely available whole-slide imaging34, motivating future adaptation of the framework to more accessible imaging modalities. Although the spatial patterns of PD-1+ and PD-L1+ cells can be examined by tracing back to individual patients within clusters, the aggregated nature of the clustering approach limits detailed interpretation specific to these activation markers. Potential future directions include enhancing robustness against such variability, expanding the application to multiple cohorts and cancer types, and developing a classifier that fully utilizes spatial interaction features for clinical outcome prediction. Additionally, while this study focuses on malignant-immune interactions, exploring spatial interactions among different immune cell types represents a valuable direction for extending this framework. Since our framework operates on pre-processed point data instead of raw images, it is not tied to a specific imaging platform or preprocessing pipeline. Applying it to other spatial data types that provide cell-level coordinates and phenotype annotations, such as single-cell spatial transcriptomics platforms (e.g., Xenium and CosMx), represents another promising direction.

Persistent homology and simplicial complex construction

The persistent homology computation described in the Results section is performed using the GUDHI library21. Pairwise Euclidean distances among malignant cell positions are precomputed, and a Vietoris–Rips complex is constructed with the simplex tree built up to dimension two. The filled riangles of the extracted simplicial complex are rasterized into a binary mask, which serves as the input for the subsequent signed distance computation.

Ball Mapper graph construction

The Ball Mapper graphs presented in the Results section are constructed using the pyballmapper Python library20. Given a point cloud X equipped with a dissimilarity measure and a radius parameter ϵ, Ball Mapper constructs an open cover of X by balls of radius ϵ. The algorithm iterates through the points and, for each point not yet covered, creates a ball of radius ϵ centered at that point; all points within the ball are assigned to the corresponding node. Because a single point can lie within multiple balls, the resulting cover is overlapping. A graph is then formed by placing an edge between any two nodes that share at least one common point. In this study, the cosine dissimilarity is used as the distance measure, and the radius ϵ0 = 4.5 ⋅ 10−2 is selected as described in the Results section.

Hierarchical clustering and survival analysis

The two-stage hierarchical clustering (HC) and survival analysis described in the Results section are implemented in R. At both stages, HC is performed using the Ward.D2 linkage criterion with Euclidean distance, via the pheatmap R package; cluster assignments are obtained with the cutree function. In Stage 1, Ball Mapper nodes are clustered by their averaged 18-dimensional features to form Node Clusters. In Stage 2, patients are clustered by the normalized distribution of their images across these Node Clusters to form Patient Clusters. Kaplan–Meier survival curves are estimated using the survfit function from the survival R package, and p-values are obtained from the log-rank test as implemented in the survminer R package.
