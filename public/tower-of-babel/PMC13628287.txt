The Role of Glymphatic Clearance in Affective Decision‐Making in Adolescents with Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury

Hongping Wang, Jiahua Zhang, Ruizi Pan, Xiaoqian Ye, Rong Wang, Ke Lv, Yue Yu, Qianqian Li, Ting Zhang, Dongpeng Wu, Dai Zhang, Yanghua Tian

Abstract

ABSTRACTBackgroundAdolescents with non‐suicidal self‐injury (NSSI) frequently exhibit severe sleep disturbances and heterogeneous cognitive impairments, yet the underlying neurophysiological basis remains unclear. The glymphatic system, a sleep‐dependent macroscopic clearance network, is critical for metabolic waste removal and neurocognitive homeostasis. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the physiological role of glymphatic function in linking sleep burden to distinct cognitive performances in adolescents with NSSI.MethodsWe assessed 44 NSSI adolescents and 33 healthy controls (HCs). Using resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI), glymphatic function was quantified by calculating the coupling strength between global blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent (gBOLD) signals and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow. To capture the heterogeneity in their cognitive profiles, we evaluated “hot” affective decision‐making via the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) and “cold” executive function via the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST). Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).ResultsIn the NSSI group, poorer sleep quality was positively correlated with stronger gBOLD‐CSF coupling (r = 0.317, p = 0.036), and stronger gBOLD‐CSF coupling predicted better IGT performance, especially during the high‐load strategy phase (Block 3) (r = 0.447, p = 0.002). Furthermore, glymphatic function fully mediated the relationship between sleep quality and affective decision‐making performance (indirect effect = 0.682, 95% CI [0.062, 1.515]). Interaction analyses confirmed this physiological‐behavioral coupling was disease‐specific (present only in NSSI; interaction p = 0.011) and domain‐specific (supporting “hot” affective cognition rather than “cold” executive function).ConclusionsIn NSSI adolescents, sleep‐associated glymphatic upregulation reflects a stress‐induced compensatory response. This mechanism domain‐specifically preserves high‐load affective decision‐making against severe psychopathological stress. These findings highlight brain metabolic clearance as a novel physiological target for bolstering cognitive resilience in high‐risk youth.
In adolescents with non‐suicidal self‐injury (NSSI), characterized by sleep disturbances and metabolic burden, a disease‐specific, compensatory up‐regulation of glymphatic function (stronger gBOLD‐CSF coupling) selectively supports “hot” (affective) cognition while leaving “cold” (executive) cognition unlinked, functioning as a pathological buffer.[CNS-32-e71159-g001.webp]

Introduction

Non‐suicidal self‐injury (NSSI) refers to the intentional and recurrent infliction of damage to one's own body in the absence of suicidal intent []. Global meta‐analyses reveal the alarming scale of this issue, estimating that over 22% of the general youth population engages in such behaviors []. Consequently, NSSI has emerged as one of the most formidable clinical challenges in contemporary adolescent psychiatry [, ]. Beyond immediate somatic injury, NSSI is closely linked to a spectrum of adverse clinical outcomes, notably cognitive impairments, sleep dysregulation, and heightened impulsivity [, , ]. However, current behavioral studies regarding cognitive and decision‐making functions in NSSI yield markedly inconsistent results. While some studies report widespread cognitive deficits in executive control and affective decision‐making [, ], others find that many NSSI adolescents perform normally on specific cognitive tasks [, ]. This high degree of behavioral heterogeneity suggests that relying solely on overarching cognitive phenotyping is inherently limited, highlighting the need to investigate the underlying neurophysiological metrics.

To decode this heterogeneity, it is crucial to recognize that cognitive processing is not monolithic; it can be broadly dissociated into “hot” affective decision‐making and “cold” executive function [, ]. “Hot” cognition involves emotion‐ and reward‐laden processing governed heavily by the limbic‐striatal circuitry, whereas “cold” cognition relies on the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for emotionally neutral, logic‐based problem solving []. In the NSSI population, the limbic‐striatal networks governing emotion regulation and “hot” cognition are specifically targeted and chronically hyperactive [, ]. Sustaining such highly activated cognitive networks requires extremely efficient cerebral metabolic homeostasis []. This chronic hyperactivation generates a substantial metabolic burden, thereby rendering these specific “hot” circuits highly dependent on efficient metabolic clearance. The glymphatic system, which serves as the brain's primary paravascular metabolic clearance pathway, has been established as a critical physiological mechanism for maintaining this neurophysiological and cognitive health [, ]. Crucially, glymphatic function is intimately tied to sleep quality, as sleep acts as the primary driver of cerebral metabolic clearance []. Given that severe sleep disturbances are a quintessential clinical feature of the NSSI cohort [, ], it remains an unanswered question how such extreme sleep deprivation reshapes glymphatic clearance dynamics, and how these physiological alterations subsequently map onto domain‐specific cognitive performance.

Measuring glymphatic activity in living humans has traditionally been difficult. However, recent neuroimaging advances offer a non‐invasive solution by tracking the coupling between the global blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent (gBOLD) signal and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow []. The physiological mechanism behind this metric is straightforward. During sleep or resting states, slow neural waves cause natural drops in cerebral blood volume []. This reduction acts like a physiological piston, drawing CSF into the brain tissue to wash away metabolic waste [, ]. By capturing this dynamic process, gBOLD‐CSF coupling serves as an effective, observable marker of the brain's clearance capacity. It has become a valuable tool for linking sleep quality with cognitive performance across various psychiatric conditions [, , ].

To decipher the physiological mechanisms underlying cognitive heterogeneity in self‐injuring youth, we quantified glymphatic function in NSSI adolescents using gBOLD‐CSF coupling. We specifically aimed to clarify how glymphatic integrity differentially impacts “hot” affective decision‐making versus “cold” executive function. Given the intrinsic link between sleep and metabolic clearance, we further hypothesized that glymphatic function mediates the relationship between severe sleep disturbances and specific cognitive outcomes. Ultimately, this study aims to reveal how sleep‐driven physiological alterations map onto the complex behavioral profiles of this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Adolescents with NSSI were consecutively recruited from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Inclusion criteria were: (1) primary diagnosis of NSSI according to DSM‐5; (2) sufficient cognitive capacity to complete assessments; and (3) provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: acute psychosis or suicidality, pervasive developmental disorders, organic brain diseases, unstable medical conditions, or MRI contraindications (e.g., claustrophobia, metallic implants). Concurrently, healthy controls (HCs) with no lifetime history of psychiatric diagnoses or NSSI behaviors were recruited via community advertisements.

Neuropsychological Assessment

All participants underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological battery administered by trained psychiatrists. The characteristics of self‐injurious behaviors were evaluated using the Adolescent Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury Assessment Questionnaire (ANSAQ) and the Clinician‐Rated Severity of Non‐Suicidal Self‐Injury (CRSNSSI) [, ]. These instruments were used to comprehensively measure the frequency and overall severity of NSSI behaviors over the past year. To quantify the severity of emotional dysregulation, the 17‐item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD‐17) was employed to assess depressive symptoms []. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index was administered to evaluate subjective sleep quality over the preceding month []. Barratt Impulsiveness Scale 11 Chinese Revised Version (BIS‐11) is composed of three subscales: cognitive impulsiveness, motor impulsiveness, and non‐planning impulsiveness []. Each subscale has scores ranging from 10 to 50, where higher scores denote increased impulsivity. Specifically, the total score and subscales were analyzed to distinguish stable personality traits from state‐dependent cognitive decision‐making deficits. These clinician‐rated scales served to establish the baseline of clinical burden in the NSSI group.

To comprehensively assess cognitive profiles across both affective and executive domains, two distinct paradigms were utilized. This approach allowed for a comparative analysis of how brain clearance relates to emotion‐based versus logic‐based processing. Iowa Gambling Task (IGT): Used to assess “hot” affective decision‐making under ambiguity. The net score (calculated as the number of advantageous choices minus disadvantageous choices) and the block‐by‐block learning curve were used as primary indices of reward‐based decision‐making strategies [, ]. Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST): Used to assess “cold” executive function, specifically cognitive flexibility and set‐shifting capabilities []. Total errors and perseverative errors were recorded to index the integrity of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex‐mediated executive control [, ], serving as a contrast to the affective processing measured by the IGT.

Image Acquisition

MRI data were acquired using a 3.0‐T Discovery GE750 MR scanner (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK) equipped with an 8‐channel head coil. Foam padding and earplugs were employed to restrict head motion and attenuate noise. We acquired high‐resolution T1‐weighted anatomical images (TR/TE = 8.16/3.18 ms, TI = 450 ms, flip angle = 12°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256) consisting of 188 sagittal slices with an isotropic voxel size of 1 mm3. Data quality was verified by two experimenters to confirm full brain coverage. For functional MRI, a total of 217 echo‐planar imaging (EPI) volumes were collected. Acquisition parameters were: TR/TE = 2400/30 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 192 × 192 mm2, and matrix = 64 × 64. Each volume contained 46 slices (thickness = 3 mm), resulting in a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3. Continuous MR‐compatible video monitoring of open‐eye fixation ensured participant wakefulness, with the operator intervening to pause the session, re‐brief the participant, and repeat the scan should prolonged eye closure or drowsiness occur.

Data Preprocessing and gBOLD‐CSF Coupling Analysis

Preprocessing

Preprocessing was performed using DPABI v6.0 (https://rfmri.org/dpabi) and SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) implemented in MATLAB 2023b. We discarded the initial 10 volumes to ensure magnetization equilibrium. Subsequent steps included slice timing correction, motion realignment, linear trend removal, spatial smoothing (4‐mm FWHM kernel), and band‐pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Crucially, to preserve hemodynamic information essential for this specific analysis, we explicitly omitted the standard nuisance regression of global, CSF, and motion signals [].

Regions of Interest (ROI) Definition

Cortical gray matter ROIs were derived from the Harvard‐Oxford atlas. To prevent signal loss from registration, these ROIs were inverse‐transformed from MNI‐152 space to each subject's native space. CSF inflow ROIs were manually delineated on the basal fMRI slices near the cerebellum, where inflow effects are maximal []. ROI anatomical accuracy was verified via co‐registered T1 images (Figure ).

gBOLD‐CSF Coupling Calculation

Signal extraction followed established protocols [, ]: CSF signals were taken from unsmoothed images, and gBOLD signals from unnormalized images. Time series were Z‐normalized and averaged within ROIs. We computed cross‐correlations in MATLAB using gBOLD as the reference across lags of ±12 s. Since BOLD oscillations precede CSF flow, manifesting as a strong anticorrelation at positive lags [], we quantified coupling strength using the peak anticorrelation. To link cerebral blood volume oscillations to CSF inflow, we also correlated the negative gBOLD derivative with CSF signals. For interpretation, the coupling index was inverted so that higher positive values reflect a stronger association between global brain activity and CSF flow.

Validation

We validated the coupling specificity using a permutation test; gBOLD and CSF time series were randomly shuffled across subjects 10,000 times to generate a null distribution. Statistical significance (p‐values) was determined by comparing the observed coupling index against this null distribution.

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS v26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Python 3.8 scripts (Scipy/Statsmodels). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent samples t‐tests were utilized for normally distributed data, whereas non‐parametric tests (e.g., Kruskal–Wallis H tests) were applied for variables violating normality.

Demographic and Clinical Comparisons

Continuous variables (e.g., age, clinical scores, gBOLD‐CSF coupling) were compared using two‐sample t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests based on data distribution, while categorical variables (e.g., gender) were assessed using Chi‐square tests.

Brain‐Behavior Associations

To investigate the clinical and cognitive significance of glymphatic function, partial correlation analyses were conducted within the NSSI group, controlling for age, gender, and mean framewise displacement (FD) to mitigate the potential confounding effects of demographic and head motion factors. Specifically, bivariate unadjusted correlations and subsequent partial Pearson correlations were used to examine the associations between the strength of gBOLD‐CSF coupling and continuous variables, including clinical burden and cognitive performance metrics. For the ordinal clinical variable (i.e., CRSNSSI grade), partial Spearman's rank correlation was applied. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini‐Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure was applied separately to the primary cognitive outcome metrics and the supplementary clinical variables. Furthermore, to determine whether the relationship between glymphatic clearance and cognitive preservation was disease‐specific, a general linear model (GLM) was employed to test the Group × Coupling interaction effect on cognitive performance, adjusting for the same covariates.

Subgroup and Exploratory Mediation Analyses

Given the high prevalence of sleep disturbances in our cohort, an extreme‐group approach was employed to isolate distinct clinical phenotypes and maximize physiological contrast []. Specifically, the NSSI cohort was stratified by PSQI tertiles—a mathematically established threshold for optimizing group differentiation []: the top (PSQI > 12) and bottom (PSQI ≤ 10) tertiles defined the NSSI‐High and NSSI‐Low subgroups, respectively. To rigorously rule out the confounding effect of micro‐head movements and pharmacological interventions on fluid dynamics, a Quade's non‐parametric analysis of covariance (rank‐based ANCOVA) was subsequently employed, adjusting strictly for mean FD and medication status.

We conducted a simple mediation analysis to explore the mechanistic links among sleep quality, glymphatic function, and decision‐making in the NSSI group. In this model, subjective sleep quality (PSQI score) was set as the independent variable. Affective decision‐making (IGT net score) served as the dependent variable. Glymphatic strength, defined as the inverted gBOLD‐CSF coupling magnitude, was used as the mediator. All analyses were performed using the Python Statsmodels module based on ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. We estimated three main pathways: the effect of the independent variable on the mediator (path a), the effect of the mediator on the dependent variable (path b), and the direct/total effects on the dependent variable (paths c′ and c). To test the significance of the indirect effect, we utilized a non‐parametric bootstrapping procedure with 5000 resamples. The mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include zero. Statistical significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Validation of the gBOLD‐CSF Coupling

The group‐averaged cross‐correlation between gBOLD and CSF signals exhibited a biphasic pattern across both the entire cohort (N = 77) and the HC group (N = 33; Figure , Left). Specifically, a significant positive peak emerged at a lag of −2.4 s (r = 0.15, p < 0.001), followed by a significant negative peak at a lag of +4.8 s (r = −0.15, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the cross‐correlation between the negative first derivative of the gBOLD signal and the CSF signal yielded a significant positive peak at zero lag (r = 0.14, p < 0.001; Figure , Right) in both groups. Representative time series illustrated that CSF inflow peaks consistently coincided with the descending phase of the gBOLD signal (Figure ). Consequently, the coupling strength at the +4.8 s lag was utilized as the primary metric for all subsequent group comparisons.

Mediating Role of Glymphatic Function in Sleep‐Cognition Pathways

Path analysis within the NSSI cohort revealed that glymphatic strength fully mediated the relationship between subjective sleep quality (PSQI scores) and affective decision‐making (IGT Net Scores; Figure ). Indirect effect of sleep quality on IGT performance via glymphatic coupling was statistically significant (ab = 0.682), with the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval [0.062, 1.515] strictly excluding zero. Furthermore, the direct effect of PSQI scores on IGT Net Scores was not significant when accounting for this mediator. Crucially, this mediating pathway remained robust and statistically significant even after incorporating baseline depressive severity as an additional covariate in the model (indirect effect = 0.686, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.039, 1.613]; Figure ). This confirms that the observed sleep‐glymphatic‐cognition mechanism operates independently of the generalized depressive burden.

Discussion

This study investigated the macroscopic fluid dynamic mechanisms underlying specific cognitive profiles in adolescents with NSSI. By quantifying resting‐state gBOLD‐CSF coupling, we observed a distinct “clinical‐physiological dissociation.” Our core findings are threefold: (1) In patients with severe subjective sleep disturbances, glymphatic metrics exhibited atypical hyperactivity, which mediated the association between sleep pressure and the preservation of “hot” affective decision‐making; (2) This physiological‐behavioral link was specific to both the affective domain and task phases with high emotion‐cognitive regulation load (IGT Block 3), remaining independent of baseline impulsivity and “cold” executive functions; (3) The predictive value of glymphatic function on strategy execution was unique to the NSSI cohort. Together, these results provide a novel physiological perspective on how the adolescent brain maintains specific network operations under severe psychopathological burden.

A pivotal finding of this study is the mediating role of the glymphatic system between subjective sleep disturbances and the preservation of “hot” affective cognition. While classical models conventionally posit that disrupted sleep architecture impairs glymphatic clearance [], our data from the NSSI cohort revealed a divergent pattern. Rather than attenuating the coupling signal, exacerbated sleep complaints positively predicted stronger gBOLD‐CSF coupling, which in turn supported sustained IGT performance. This unexpected relationship points toward a stress‐induced compensatory response under pathological conditions.

Evidence from converging lines of research supports this interpretation. Recent neuroimaging work indicates that acute sleep deprivation can trigger a “biphasic adaptation” in fluid dynamics, eliciting a transient compensatory surge in gBOLD‐CSF coupling once a specific sleep pressure threshold is breached []. The cumulative sleep burden endured by NSSI patients likely acts as a similar, albeit prolonged, physiological stressor. From a network perspective, this phenomenon aligns with the cortical compensation hypothesis. During sleep deprivation, the brain frequently relies on the hyperactivation of prefrontal or limbic regions to maintain cognitive performance []. The atypical enhancement in glymphatic coupling observed here may reflect the clearance response required to offset the heightened metabolic demands of these overworked neural circuits. Furthermore, at the cellular level, microenvironmental shifts driven by chronic stress and waste accumulation can provoke glial responses, potentially modulating AQP4 channels to upregulate fluid clearance [, ].

Crucially, the viability of such a robust physiological compensation is likely tethered to the patients' neurodevelopmental stage. In stark contrast to the structural decline of the glymphatic system seen in older adults with neurodegenerative diseases [, ], adolescence is characterized by peak neural plasticity and high metabolic capacity []. This substantial physiological reserve provides the biological capital necessary to systematically mobilize clearance mechanisms under extreme duress. Consequently, the elevated coupling signature appears to reflect an adaptive response of the young nervous system, striving to manage cumulative metabolic loads and scaffold essential affective decision‐making networks amidst severe clinical deterioration.

The observed behavioral patterns—both within the IGT and between the distinct cognitive paradigms—further suggest that cognitive function in this cohort is not inherently intact, but rather exhibits a “conditional maintenance” contingent upon physiological compensation. During the initial, ambiguous phase of the IGT (Block 1) [], the NSSI group significantly underperformed compared to HCs. This early behavioral deficit broadly reflects the baseline emotional dysregulation and inhibitory challenges characteristic of the disorder []. As the task advanced to the explicit risk and strategy‐formation phase (Block 3), this behavioral gap notably narrowed—a recovery directly correlated with macroscopic clearance metrics []. Such a dynamic trajectory implies that the sharply escalating metabolic demands of sustained emotion regulation necessitate enhanced fluid clearance, allowing patients with more robust compensatory coupling to demonstrate greater resilience in suppressing impulsive choices and shifting toward advantageous strategies.

Extending this rationale helps clarify the differential associations observed between “hot” and “cold” cognitive domains. “Hot” affective decision‐making relies heavily on the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and limbic‐striatal networks [, ]—regions frequently characterized by pathological hyperactivity in self‐injuring adolescents []. The ensuing local metabolic pressure likely renders the sustained operation of these affective circuits exceptionally dependent on efficient waste clearance [, ]. The “cold” executive network, subserved primarily by the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) [, ], operates under different constraints. Experiencing less direct emotional perturbation during neutral cognitive processing, the baseline metabolic demands within the dlPFC might not reach the critical threshold required to trigger brain‐wide compensatory clearance. This neurobiological disparity elegantly explains why the statistical coupling between macroscopic clearance efficiency and cognitive performance emerges selectively under the high metabolic load of “hot” affective processing.

Interaction analyses confirmed the disease‐specificity of this physiological‐behavioral link: glymphatic function predicted strategy execution exclusively in the NSSI group. In healthy brains, baseline metabolic homeostasis easily accommodates routine cognitive challenges []. For NSSI adolescents enduring chronic psychopathological attrition, however, the relative efficiency of the glymphatic system becomes a critical factor constraining their vulnerable affective networks. This observation offers a novel perspective for clinical intervention. The macroscopic metabolic clearance mechanism serves not just as a physiological metric, but as a vital buffer sustaining partial cognitive resilience in this vulnerable population. Future clinical strategies might benefit from moving beyond standard symptom management. Exploring interventions that gently optimize sleep architecture (e.g., targeted slow‐wave sleep enhancement) or lifestyle modifications to bolster brain metabolic clearance could prove translationally valuable [, , ]. Prioritizing the enhancement of underlying physiological compensatory capacities may provide foundational support to protect affective decision‐making networks.

Several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design of this study inherently limits our findings to correlational associations, precluding definitive causal inferences. Future longitudinal investigations are essential to ascertain whether the observed compensatory upregulation reflects a transient acute stress response or a sustained pathological adaptation. Second, while gBOLD‐CSF coupling provides a robust macroscopic index of global brain clearance [], it lacks the spatial resolution necessary to directly quantify localized metabolite clearance rates within specific neural micro‐environments. Third, stratifying the cohort into extreme sleep subgroups resulted in relatively small sample sizes. Although the moderate‐to‐large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.684) supports the robustness of this physiological divergence, these subgroup findings should be considered preliminary and warrant replication in larger independent cohorts. Fourth, the significant female skew (86.4%) in our NSSI sample accurately reflects the epidemiological profile of the disorder [, ], but limits the generalizability of our neurobiological findings to male patients. Subsequent research involving larger, medication‐naive cohorts is warranted to effectively disentangle intrinsic neurovascular dynamics from pharmacological effects. Finally, the potential for undetected transient sleep remains a limitation, even with continuous video tracking of participant wakefulness. Resolving this requires future studies to utilize fast‐fMRI coupled with simultaneous pupillometry or EEG/EOG. By capturing objective arousal states, these multimodal approaches could help untangle the underlying neural and physiological drivers governing the observed macroscopic fluid coupling alterations [, , ].

In summary, this study highlights the glymphatic system as a crucial compensatory buffer that scaffolds specific cognitive functions in adolescents with comorbid MDD and NSSI. Confronted with the dual burdens of cumulative sleep disturbances and severe psychopathological stress, the developing nervous system appears to systematically mobilize its physiological reserves to meet the heightened metabolic demands of affective decision‐making networks. Beyond advancing our pathophysiological understanding, these findings provide compelling neuroimaging evidence for the brain's dynamic capacity to orchestrate physiological compensation under extreme psychiatric duress, ultimately pointing toward novel neurometabolic targets for clinical intervention.
