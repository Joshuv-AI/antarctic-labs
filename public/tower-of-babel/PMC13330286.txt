Introduction

The production of mammalian red blood cells (RBCs) occurs on the erythroblastic island (EBI) [1, 2]. The EBI is composed of a central nursing macrophage with erythroblasts at distinct developmental stages and was first identified by Marcel Bessis in 1958 [3]. Since the discovery of the EBI, there has been a growing body of evidence that indicates that EBI macrophages are crucial in facilitating erythropoiesis [4–8]. This process requires EBI macrophages to highly express adhesion genes (CD163, CD169, Vcam1), growth factors (Igf1, Vegf), phagocytosis-related genes (Mertk, Axl, Timd4, CD163), iron metabolism genes (Hmox1,Trf), and nucleus-digesting genes (Dnase2α) [4].

Anemia affects one-third of the world’s population and contributes to increased morbidity and mortality, decreased work productivity, and impaired neurological development [9–11]. The use of recombinant erythropoietin (EPO) is often successful in the treatment of anemia associated with chronic kidney disease. Nonetheless, numerous erythroid blood disorders, including hemolysis, sepsis, Diamond-Blackfan anemia (DBA), and congenital hypoplastic anemia, often show resistance to EPO [12, 13]. For patients who are resistant to EPO, glucocorticoids (GCs) are the main agents that directly increase RBC production [14]. Numerous studies have shown that GCs enhance erythropoiesis in humans and mice by boosting burst forming unit-erythroid (BFU-E) self-renewal and increasing colony forming unit-erythroid (CFU-E) in vitro [12, 15]. Researchers have also demonstrated that GCs prompt monocytes to differentiate into macrophages, thus aiding EBI formation and subsequently increasing erythrocytes in vitro [16, 17]. However, despite these findings, it remains unclear whether EBI macrophages are required for GC-induced erythropoiesis in vivo, and the potential molecular mechanisms by which GC regulates macrophage function within the EBI niche are poorly defined. In particular, how macrophage-mediated iron metabolism is coordinated with erythropoietic demand under GC stimulation remains largely unknown.

In this study, we sought to determine whether and how EBI macrophages mediate GC-induced erythropoiesis. Specifically, we aimed to (1) define the requirement of EBI macrophages in GC-driven erythropoiesis in vivo, (2) characterize functionally distinct macrophage subsets involved in erythropoiesis, and (3) elucidate the molecular mechanisms linking GC signaling to macrophage-dependent iron metabolism and erythropoietic support. To address these questions, we combined in vivo macrophage depletion and genetic models with multi-omics analyses and functional assays. We identify CD163+ BM macrophages as a key subset that coordinates EBI formation and iron metabolism in response to GC stimulation. Furthermore, we demonstrate that GC regulates CD163 expression and macrophage function through a GR-GRIP1-dependent epigenetic mechanism, thereby enhancing erythropoiesis. These findings provide novel insights into the cellular and molecular basis of GC responsiveness and highlight macrophage-mediated iron handling as a potential therapeutic target in EPO-resistant anemia.

Materials and methods

Human samples. Bone marrow samples were collected from healthy controls (HCs) and patients diagnosed with autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) after ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (2021-KY-0575-002).

Mice and rat. Wild-type (WT) mice and CD163-/- mice (was used as a general knockout model, with its origin C57BL/6J), Gypa-eGFP-cre mice, Epor-tdtomato-cre mice, sprague-dawley (SD) rat, and Epor-eGFP-cre rat were housed at the Experimental Animal Center of Zhengzhou University Animal Facility. Adult mice and rat aged 2–3 months were used for the studies. All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhengzhou University Laboratory Animal Center (ZZU-LAC20210416[04]).

Antibodies and reagents. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1, the chemicals and reagents in Supplementary Table 2, and the primers in Supplementary Table 3.

Drug treatment. For wild-type and CD163-/- mice, dexamethasone (DEX) was administered to mice at a dose of 5 mg/kg for either 4 or 7 consecutive days. For Gypa-eGFP-cre transgenic mice, a single DEX intervention at the same dose was performed, and animals were harvested 24 hours later. For rat experiments, rats were subjected to daily intraperitoneal injection of DEX at a dose of 5 mg/kg for a continuous 10-day treatment period.

For macrophage depletion experiments, the clodronate-loaded liposome (40337ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology, which include 40337ES: clodronate liposomes, and 40338ES: control liposomes (PBS)) injection experiment was performed as previously described [18]. Briefly, mice were administered clodronate liposomes or control liposomes on three consecutive days prior to the initiation of the DEX treatment protocol, specifically on days -3, -2, and -1 relative to the first DEX dose on day 0.

Preparation of single-cell suspension from human, mouse and rat BM. Mouse and rat BM single cell suspension were prepared as previously described [19]. Human single cell suspension was prepared as described previously [20]. The details are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Flow cytometry assay and fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Mouse erythroblast staining was assessed as we previously described [21, 22]. Rat erythroblast staining was performed as previously described [23]. The details for mouse, rat and human erythroblast and macrophage staining, analyses, cell sorting are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

In vitrohuman EBI formation and EBI enrichment and cytospins, Giemsa and Prussian blue staining, quantification. Erythroblasts were derived from cord blood CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), as previously described [24, 25]. Macrophages were also derived from cord blood CD34+cells, as outlined in our earlier research [4]. EBIs in mouse BM were enriched and quantified, as our previous study [4, 22]. The details are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunofluorescence staining of paraffin embedded bone marrow cells. The details are described in previously published paper [26]. The details are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Quantification of serum iron, unsaturated iron-binding capacity (UIBC), total iron binding capacity (TIBC) and BM tissues iron were measured as described [27]. The details are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Data analysis. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of HCs were downloaded from the GEO database GSE120221. scRNA-seq data (mouse, rat, AIHA patients) have been deposited in the NODE database (https://www.biosino.org/node/index). Library preparation, sequencing and analysis of scRNA-seq, RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, CUT&Tag and ChIP-seq are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Blood parameters analysis and quantitative RT-PCR are described in our previously published papers [4, 22].

Statistical analysis. GraphPad Prism 10.0 software was used for statistical analysis. The data were analyzed using t-test. Significance was indicated as at least p <0.05 for all statistical analyses.

Results

BM EBI macrophages are required for the effects of GC on erythrocyte production in vivo

Previous studies have shown that GCs act directly on erythroid progenitor cells to increase production of mature RBCs in vitro [12, 15]. EBI macrophages also play significant roles in supporting erythropoiesis [1, 4, 28]. In line with these findings, we decided to determine whether the erythropoiesis-promoting effect of GC is associated with EBI macrophages in vivo. We administered clodronate-liposome to mice once daily for three consecutive days, as previously described, which led to an almost complete eradication of macrophages in the BM [18, 29]. We first confirmed the deletion efficiency of clodronate liposome, which indeed led to a more than 90% deletion of macrophages in the BM (Supplementary Figures 1A and B). Thereafter, we injected DEX into clodronate liposome pretreated mice or control liposome pretreated mice for 4 days or 7 days, respectively, to observe the effects on erythropoiesis (Fig. 1A). Treatment of mice with DEX for 7 days significantly increased peripheral blood RBCs, hemoglobin (HGB), and hematocrit (HCT), while decreasing the proportion of reticulocytes (Retic) compared to control liposome control group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, these DEX-induced effects were completely abolished in mice pretreated with clodronate liposomes (Fig. 1B), indicating that EBI macrophages are required for the effects of DEX on erythrocyte production. The spleen responds to stress erythropoiesis [30], however, DEX administration does not induce spleen erythroid cell production, suggesting the main erythrocytes production site is BM following DEX administration (Supplementary Figure 1C and D). To define the cellular mechanisms for the observed effect, we first quantified total BM cell numbers and found that sustained DEX administration led to significant increases in the total BM cell numbers in control mice but not in the clodronate liposome-pretreated mice (Fig. 1C). We then assessed the percentage and absolute cell numbers of erythroid progenitors and erythroid cells at all maturational stages using the previously established methods [19, 31]. The gating strategies for erythroid progenitors and erythroblasts are shown in Supplementary Figures 1E-G, respectively. Quantitative analyses showed that DEX administration increased the proportion of BM RBCs, accompanied by a relative reduction in intermediate erythroid populations, in control mice but not in clodronate liposome–pretreated mice (Fig. 1D and F). Importantly, absolute cell quantification revealed a robust increase in BM reticulocytes and RBCs following DEX treatment in control mice, whereas this effect was completely abolished upon macrophage depletion (Fig. 1E and G). These data indicate that GC enhances erythroid cell production in an EBI macrophage-dependent manner in vivo.

Glucocorticoid enhanced the EBI formation and iron metabolism of human CD34+HSCs-derived macrophages in vitro

We further examined the effects of GC on human erythropoiesis using the in vitro co-culture system we developed previously [4, 22]. We cocultured DEX-pretreated or control of human CD34+ HSCs-derived macrophages with day-7 erythroblasts. DEX-pretreated macrophages markedly promoted erythroid output after 24h of coculture (Fig. 2A) and significantly enhanced EBI formation (Fig. 2B-D). Moreover, DEX pretreatment increased macrophage phagocytosis of extruded nuclei (Fig. 2E and F), indicating enhanced efferocytic capacity. To investigate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced erythropoiesis-supporting functions, we performed bulk RNA-seq on human CD34+ HSC-derived macrophages with or without DEX treatment. Principal component and correlation analyses revealed distinct clustering between control and DEX-treated macrophages (Supplementary Figures 2A and B). A total of 2,700 genes were differentially expressed (log₂FC > 1, FPKM > 10), including 1,589 upregulated and 1,928 downregulated genes (Supplementary Figures 2C and D; Supplementary Table 4). KEGG pathway analysis showed that DEX treatment upregulated pathways related to Phosphatidyqinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)- Protein Kinase B (PI3K-Akt) signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, cholesterol metabolism, and efferocytosis, while chemokine signaling, TNF signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation were downregulated (Fig. 2G and H). GSEA further indicated enrichment of heme metabolism-related genes in DEX-treated macrophages (Fig. 2I). Notably, DEX treatment increased expression of genes associated with erythropoiesis-supporting EBI macrophages, including those involved in adhesion (CD163, SIGLEC1, ITGAV), phagocytosis (CD163, MERTK, AXL, C1QA, C1QB, C1QC), heme catabolism (HMOX1, LRP1, BLVRB), iron metabolism (SLC40A1, TF, SLC48A1, FTL), and erythroid growth factors (IGF1, VEGFB, GAS6), as well as transcription factors (NCOA4, MAFB, MAF) (Fig. 2J). qRT-PCR and flow cytometry confirmed that DEX treatment upregulated the expression of CD163, CD169, MERTK, HMOX1, and other key genes (Fig. 2K-P). Taken together, these data suggest that GC promotes the acquisition of an EBI-like macrophage program with enhanced iron-handling and erythropoiesis-supporting features in human CD34+ HSC-derived macrophages in vitro, which likely contributes to improved erythropoiesis.

Glucocorticoid promoted erythropoiesis via regulating EBI formation and iron metabolism in vivo

We next examined whether GC promotes erythropoiesis by enhancing the erythropoiesis-supporting functions of mice EBI macrophages in vivo. Since EBI macrophages provide iron for erythroid maturation, we first evaluated systemic iron alterations following DEX treated mice. No significant changes were observed in serum TIBC, UIBC, or iron levels (Fig. 3A). While, our results indicated that DEX treatment increases serum ferritin, transferrin, and EPO, and decreases hepcidin (Fig. 3B-E), suggesting enhanced iron mobilization and redistribution together with increased erythropoietic activity under DEX treatment. These systemic changes prompted us to examine iron handling within the bone marrow EBI niche. We then assessed whether these systemic alterations were reflected at the level of the BM microenvironment. DEX treatment markedly increased iron content in BM tissue (Fig. 3F and G) and led to greater iron accumulation at the membrane of EBI macrophages (Supplementary Figures 3A and B), indicating augmented iron export activity toward adjacent erythroblasts. In parallel, DEX administration significantly enhanced EBI formation (Fig. 3H and I) and upregulated CD71 expression on late-stage erythroblasts [32] (Fig. 3J and K), reflecting an adaptive response to increased iron demand.

To further dissect the underlying mechanism, we performed bulk RNA-seq on control and GC-treated Bone Marrow-Derived Macrophages (BMDM). DEX treatment induced distinct transcriptional profiles (Supplementary Figure 2E-H) enriched for pathways related to cell adhesion, phagocytosis, and iron homeostasis, with upregulation of erythropoiesis-supporting genes such as Cd163, Siglec1, Mertk, Hmox1, Slc40a1, and Trf (Fig. 3L, Supplementary Figure 2I). GSEA confirmed enrichment of heme metabolism pathways (Fig. 3M). qRT-PCR analysis revealed increased mRNA expression of Cd163, Siglec1, Mertk, and Hmox1 in vitro. Leveraging our Epor-tdTomato-Cre mouse model [21], we further validated these findings at the protein level by flow cytometry, demonstrating that DEX administration induced robust upregulation of CD163, Hmox1, Siglec1, and Mertk in BM Epor+ EBI macrophages. (Fig. 3N and O; Supplementary Figures 2J, 3C, 3D). Moreover, DEX administration also increases the number of BM EBI macrophages (Supplementary Figure 3E and F). Taken together, these data indicate that GC promotes a transcriptional and functional reprogramming of bone marrow EBI macrophages toward an erythropoiesis-supporting and iron-handling phenotype in vivo, which is associated with enhanced local erythroid production.

Validation of GC-induced erythropoiesis and iron remodeling in a rat model with closer physiological relevance to humans

To assess whether a similar response could be observed in rats, which closely mimic those in humans due to the expandable BM spaces and a less important role in extramedullary erythropoiesis in both species [23], we then examined the effects of DEX administration in our newly generated Epor-eGFP-cre rat or SD rat in vivo (Supplementary Figure 4A). As expected, DEX treatment significantly increased RBC, HGB, HCT levels, and total BM cell numbers (Supplementary Figure 4B and C), which is similar to mice results treated with DEX. Next, we investigated whether erythropoiesis was enhanced in the BM following DEX administration by flowcytometry, as previously described [23]. The enhanced RBC, HGB, and HCT levels were further confirmed by increased BM mature erythroid cells (Supplementary Figure 4D and E, Supplementary Figures 5A), which is consistent with those in mice. We also found no significant alterations in TIBC, UIBC, and serum iron levels in rat following DEX administration (Supplementary Figure 4F). While, we found that DEX treatment increases serum ferritin, TF, EPO, but decreases hepcidin (Supplementary Figure 4G-J). Importantly, we also observed an enhancement in local iron recycling in BM EBI macrophages, which was accompanied by increased EBI formation (Supplementary Figure 4K and N). Furthermore, increased CD71 levels were observed in late-stage rat BM erythroid cells following DEX administration (Supplementary Figures 4O and P). Notably, DEX treatment also significantly increased the expression of CD163 in rat BM Epor+ EBI macrophages, whereas it slightly increased the expressions of CD169 and Vcam1 (Supplementary Figures 4Q, R and S, Supplementary Figure 5B). Together, these results demonstrate that DEX administration in rats recapitulates the erythropoietic and macrophage-associated changes observed in mice.

BM CD163+ macrophages exhibit enhanced phagocytic, iron metabolic, and EBI-associated features across species

Since GC enhances erythropoiesis by promoting EBI formation, iron metabolism, and CD163 expression, we first characterized the identity of BM Ly6G− F4/80hiCD163+ macrophages by flow cytometry. These macrophages exhibited high expressions of Vcam1, Siglec1, Mertk, Axl, Timd4, and Hmox1 compared with BM Ly6G− F4/80hiCD163- macrophages (Fig. 4A, B). Using Gypa-eGFP-cre mice [33], we observed that, under homeostatic conditions, CD163+ macrophages displayed a markedly enhanced capacity to engulf senescent RBCs compared with CD163- macrophages. (Fig. 4C, D). Consistently, Prussian blue staining revealed increased iron accumulation in CD163+ macrophages compared with CD163- macrophages (Fig. 4E), supporting their enhanced phagocytic and iron-handling capacity. To further define their molecular features, we performed scRNA-seq of mouse BM cells. After quality control, 24,033 cells were analyzed and grouped into six major cell types, including macrophages identified by expression of Adgre1, CD68, Fcgr1, and C1qa (Supplementary Figures 6A-D, Supplementary Table 6). Reclustering of macrophages revealed three transcriptionally distinct subsets: CD163+ macrophages (Cd163, Hmox1, Mertk, Vcam1, Slc40a1), Negr1+ macrophages (Fcgr4, Negr1), and H2+ macrophages (H2-Aa, H2-Ab1, Cd74, Tnf) (Supplementary Figures 6E, F). Differential expression and GO enrichment analyses showed that CD163+ macrophages were enriched for phagocytosis, iron ion homeostasis, and iron transport pathways compared with CD163− subsets (Fig. 4F-H). In line with these findings, CD163+ macrophages expressed higher levels of adhesion genes (Siglec1, Vcam1, Itgav), phagocytosis-related genes (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Mertk, Axl, Timd4), and iron metabolism genes (Slc40a1, Hmox1, Trf, Tfrc) (Fig. 4I). By integrating the expression levels of EBI-associated functional markers, an EBI functional score demonstrated that CD163+ macrophages possess significantly enhanced EBI activity (Fig. 4J). Correlation analysis further demonstrated that Cd163 expression is strongly associated with phagocytosis, heme metabolism, and iron transport, particularly with Hmox1 (Fig. 4K and L). Importantly, rat and human BM scRNA-seq data revealed that CD163+ macrophages share similar EBI-related and iron metabolic gene signatures (Supplementary Figures 7-8, Supplementary Tables 7-8). Collectively, these findings suggest that CD163+ macrophages represent a conserved macrophage subset with enriched signatures of phagocytosis, EBI-related functions, and iron metabolism across species.

A GC-GRIP1-CD163 transcriptional program is associated with enhanced EBI features

Given the established role of CD163 in systemic and tissue iron homeostasis—through scavenging senescent erythrocytes and serving as an adhesion receptor essential for EBI assembly [7, 34–36]—we postulated that GC promotes erythropoiesis by reinforcing EBI integrity, erythrophagocytosis, and iron recycling via induction of CD163 in BM macrophages. To address this hypothesis, we first assessed the phagocytic capacity of BM EBI macrophages in vivo following DEX administration. DEX treatment significantly enhanced the uptake of senescent RBCs by BM macrophages. While both CD163+ and CD163− populations exhibited increased phagocytic activity, CD163+ EBI macrophages consistently displayed a markedly superior erythrophagocytic capacity (Fig. 5A and B), in agreement with predictions derived from single-cell transcriptomic analyses. To elucidate the transcriptional mechanisms underlying GC-induced CD163 expression, we performed an integrated multi-omics analysis combining RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, GR-ChIP-seq, GRIP1-CUT&Tag, and H3K27ac-ChIP-seq datasets from control and DEX-treated macrophages [37]. DEX exposure globally increased chromatin accessibility within ±3 kb of ATAC-seq peaks (Fig. 5C), with newly accessible regions predominantly localized to distal intergenic (34.5%) and intronic (25.7%) elements (Fig. 5D). Differential accessibility analysis identified 1,470 regions with increased and 1,040 regions with decreased accessibility upon DEX treatment (Fig. 5E). Genes associated with DEX-induced open chromatin regions were significantly enriched in pathways governing inflammation resolution, cell adhesion, and phagocytosis (Fig. 5F). Motif enrichment analysis revealed glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) as the dominant regulatory motifs within DEX-induced accessible regions (Fig. 5G), implicating direct involvement of glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling. Integrative analysis of RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets identified a discrete transcriptional module of ten DEX-upregulated genes—Siglec1, Cd163, Mertk, Hmox1, Klf2, Tmem37, Maf, Slc40a1, Trf, and Mafb—that collectively encode core components of erythrophagocytosis, iron handling, and macrophage identity (Fig. 5H). Given that GR transcriptional activity critically depends on co-regulators, including GR-interacting protein 1 (GRIP1) [38–40], we next examined GR and GRIP1 genomic occupancy. GR-ChIP-seq and GRIP1-CUT&Tag analyses revealed robust DEX-induced recruitment of both GR and GRIP1, with preferential enrichment at distal regulatory elements and promoter regions (Supplementary Figures 9A, B, D, and E). Concordantly, H3K27ac-ChIP-seq demonstrated increased deposition of active histone marks at promoters of DEX-responsive genes [41], indicative of enhancer–promoter activation (Supplementary Figures 9C and F). Notably, integrated multi-omics prioritization identified Cd163 as the most prominently upregulated transcriptional target downstream of GC–GR signaling (Fig. 5I, left). Strikingly, while GR-ChIP-seq showed limited enrichment at the Cd163 locus, GRIP1-CUT&Tag revealed pronounced GC-dependent occupancy, suggesting a noncanonical GR regulatory mode in which GRIP1 plays a dominant role in Cd163 activation (Fig. 5I, right). Consistent with this model, luciferase reporter assays and co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed functional and physical interactions among CD163, GR, and GRIP1 (Fig. 5J and K). Collectively, these data support a GC–GRIP1–CD163 transcriptional axis in macrophages, whereby GC reprogram chromatin accessibility and coactivator recruitment to enhance CD163 expression, which is associated with enhanced EBI features.

CD163 deficiency abolishes glucocorticoid-induced erythropoiesis by disrupting EBI formation and iron metabolism

On the basis of these findings, we postulated that GC enhance erythropoiesis via BM CD163+ macrophages and that CD163 is essential for this effect. Accordingly, we established CD163−/− mice lacking CD163 expression in mature BM macrophages (Supplementary Figures 10A-D) to interrogate the requirement of CD163 in GC-driven erythropoiesis. Both WT and CD163− /− mice were treated with DEX to assess their erythropoietic responses (Fig. 6A). Under steady-state conditions, there were no significant differences in RBC count, HGB, HCT, Retic or BM cell counts between CD163− /− and WT mice (Fig. 6B and C). By contrast, following DEX administration, WT mice showed greater increases in circulating RBCs, HGB, HCT, Retic and BM cell counts than CD163− /− mice (Fig. 6B and C). Consistently, DEX treatment led to a higher proportion of mature RBCs and enhanced EBI formation in WT mice compared with CD163− /− mice (Fig. 6D-F, Supplementary Figures 11A-C). Serum iron indices (TIBC, UIBC, and serum iron) were unchanged in both groups (Supplementary Figure 11D). However, DEX injection increased serum ferritin levels in WT mice but decreased them in CD163− /− mice (Fig. 6G). Similarly, while serum Trf levels rose in both groups, WT mice exhibited a significantly greater fold increase, approximately 1.7-fold. (Fig. 6H). Additionally, a significant increase of serum EPO levels and decrease of serum hepcidin levels was noted in WT mice following DEX injection, however, the opposite result was observed in CD163-/- mice following DEX injection (Fig. 6I and J). Correspondingly, BM iron content was higher in WT mice than in CD163− /− mice after DEX treatment (Fig. 6K, Supplementary Figure 11E). Iron staining of BM EBIs revealed fewer macrophages with membrane-associated or intracellular iron in CD163− /− mice compared with WT mice (Supplementary Figures 11F and G). Furthermore, DEX treatment markedly upregulated CD71 expression in late-stage erythroid cells from WT mice, whereas this response was absent in CD163− /− mice (Fig. 6L).

To investigate the molecular characteristics of BM macrophages under different conditions, we performed scRNA-seq on BM macrophages isolated from WT, CD163− /−, DEX-treated WT, and DEX-treated CD163− /− mice. After clustering macrophages based on lineage-specific markers (Supplementary Figure 12A-E, Supplementary Table 9), macrophage subsets were analyzed further. To first determine how GC affects the CD163+ macrophage compartment in vivo, we analyzed BM CD163+ macrophages before and after DEX administration in WT mice. Notably, DEX treatment led to a significant increase of CD163+ macrophages as well as the expressions of Siglec1 [4], C1qa, C1qb, Vcam1 [4], Timd4 [4], Nr1h3, Axl [4], Blvrb, Tango2, Hmox1 [4], Slc48a1, Tmem37, Slc40a1, and Abcg2 (Supplementary Figure 12F-I). To further characterize the functional state of BM macrophages under different conditions, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEX treatment markedly upregulated pathways related to cell adhesion, phagocytosis, efferocytosis, heme metabolism, and iron homeostasis from WT but not CD163−/− mice (Fig. 6M). Consistently, genes associated with these pathways were significantly higher in DEX-treated WT macrophages than in DEX-treated CD163− /−counterparts. These genes included Siglec1, C1qa, C1qb, Vcam1, Timd4, Nr1h3, Axl, Blvrb, Tango2, Hmox1, Hmox2, Slc48a1, Tmem37, Slc40a1, and Abcg2 (Fig. 6N). Collectively, our results indicate that CD163 deficiency reduced the effects of GC in promoting the maturation of BM erythroid cells due to impaired EBI formation and iron metabolism.

Glucocorticoid promoted erythropoiesis by correcting the impaired EBI formation and iron metabolism in patients with AIHA

Macrophages in the spleen and liver act as scavengers that engulf and degrade antibody-coated RBCs, leading to iron overload-a key mechanism underlying anemia in AIHA [42, 43]. As expected, GC treatment increased RBC count, HGB, and HCT while reducing serum iron levels, indicating alleviated iron overload and improved erythropoiesis (Fig. 7A and B). To explore whether GC promotes erythropoiesis by modulating EBI formation and iron metabolism in CD163+ macrophages, we performed scRNA-seq on BM CD45+ cells from healthy controls (HCs, n=2), treatment-naïve AIHA patients (n=2), and GC-treated AIHA patients (n=4). After quality control, 61,434 cells were clustered into 14 groups and classified into nine major cell types, including T cells, B cells, plasma cells, neutrophils, erythroid cells, and monocytes/macrophages (Supplementary Figures 13A-H, Supplementary Table 10). Sub-clustering of monocytes/macrophages revealed five subsets, including CD163+ and CD163− macrophages (Supplementary Figures 13H and I, Supplementary Table 10). In treatment-naïve AIHA patients, CD163+ macrophages exhibited significant enrichment of iron metabolism-related pathways, such as phagocytosis regulation, heme metabolism, iron ion transport, ferroptosis, and efferocytosis (Fig. 7C), consistent with iron overload. Inflammatory pathways-including Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α)signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), and Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling-were also upregulated, indicating inflammation-associated suppression of erythropoiesis [44]. GC treatment markedly reduced both iron metabolism and inflammatory signatures (Fig. 7C), suggesting restoration of macrophage homeostasis. Moreover, the EBI score was decreased in treatment-naïve AIHA but significantly increased following GC therapy (Fig. 7D). Expression of erythropoiesis-related genes-including CD163, FTL, FTH1, NR1H3, SLC48A1, and BLVRB was decreased in treatment-naïve AIHA but was upregulated after GC treatment (Fig. 7E). Similarly, PPARG and GDF15 expression increased only after GC administration (Fig. 7E). In contrast, proinflammatory genes CD80 and TLR2 were elevated in treatment-naïve AIHA but suppressed after GC treatment, whereas the anti-inflammatory gene MRC1 showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 7E). Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining assay confirmed increased CD163 expression on BM macrophages in GC-treated patients, consistent with the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 7F and G). Collectively, these findings indicate that GCs promote erythropoiesis in AIHA patients, at least in part, by restoring EBI formation and correcting iron metabolism and inflammatory dysregulation in BM CD163+ macrophages.

Discussion

In addition to their well-established anti-inflammatory properties and anti-apoptotic [8, 45–49], GCs have long been recognized to promote erythropoiesis through effects on hematopoietic and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and are clinically used to treat refractory anemias such as Diamond-Blackfan anemia, hemolysis, and sepsis-associated anemia [12, 50–52]. Our findings extend this progenitor-centric view by highlighting EBI macrophages as an important cellular component of GC action in vivo. Through cross-species single-cell transcriptomic analyses, we show that CD163 expression marks a macrophage program enriched for phagocytosis, iron metabolism, and cell-cell adhesion, providing a molecular basis for their enhanced EBI-associated features. Notably, GC upregulates CD163 expression through a GRIP1-GR-associated pathway, which is linked to enhanced EBI integrity, iron utilization, and erythrophagocytosis. In addition to this functional reprogramming, we observed that GC administration increases the number of Epor+ EBI macrophages. This expansion of EBI macrophages may further contribute to the pro-erythropoietic effects of GC by providing a larger niche to support erythroblast maturation and by increasing the overall capacity for iron recycling within the BM. Therefore, the GC-driven enhancement of erythropoiesis likely involves both qualitative and quantitative changes in EBI macrophages: an increase in macrophage number, as well as CD163-dependent functional specialization that promotes efficient erythrophagocytosis and iron handling. These findings suggest that GC coordinates niche expansion with macrophage reprogramming to optimize erythropoietic output. Consistent with this model, CD163 deficiency markedly attenuates GC-induced erythropoietic responses, accompanied by impaired EBI formation and altered iron homeostasis, suggesting that macrophage-dependent niche remodeling contributes to GC responsiveness beyond progenitor-intrinsic effects. Collectively, these findings support a model in which CD163+ macrophages function as key regulators integrating iron metabolism and erythropoiesis, with potential implications for GC-responsive anemias, including AIHA and other stress erythropoietic conditions.

Glucocorticoids directly stimulate the self-renewal of HSPCs, thereby promoting RBC production in vitro [12, 15]. Beyond this direct effect, EBI macrophages critically regulate erythroblast maturation through physical interactions and iron recycling [7, 45, 53–57]. Our and other studies have revealed that BM EBI macrophages are highly heterogeneous, with CD163 showing marked expression variability [4, 17]. CD163 functions not only as a scavenger receptor for hemoglobin and Hb-based substrates following haptoglobin depletion [58, 59], but also as an erythroblast adhesion receptor essential for EBI formation [7]. In this study, we demonstrate that GC enhances erythropoiesis by transcriptionally upregulating CD163 via the GR-GRIP complex. Previous study indicated that GC reprograms macrophage metabolism through cytoplasmic GR to activate PDH and the TCA cycle, leading to the accumulation of anti-inflammatory metabolites such as itaconic acid [45]. Moreover, GC maintains metabolic homeostasis and insulin sensitivity in obesity through GR-STAT6-dependent activation of anti-inflammatory genes, including CD163 [60] Collectively, our findings support a model in which GC promotes erythropoiesis, at least in part, through GRIP1-associated transcriptional activation of CD163, highlighting a potential mechanism underlying GC-mediated regulation of erythropoiesis.

In steady state, splenic red pulp macrophages primarily clear senescent RBCs from circulation [30, 61, 62]. BM macrophages share the machinery for RBC scavenging and iron recycling, locally supplying iron through ferritin secretion or ferroportin-mediated export [4, 63, 64]. Our study further shows that GC treatment is associated with increased CD163 expression alongside enhanced EBI formation and iron metabolic features in BM macrophages across mice, rats, and humans, both in vitro and in vivo. Correspondingly, CD163−/− mice exhibited impaired BM EBI formation and altered local iron metabolism but no overt anemia under steady-state conditions. In line with this, GC enhanced BM EBI formation and iron-related parameters in WT but not CD163−/− mice, accompanied by increased erythropoietic output. Together, these findings suggest that CD163 contributes to the erythropoiesis-promoting effects of GC, at least in part through modulation of CD163+ BM EBI macrophages.

AIHA is an autoimmune disorder characterized by autoantibody-mediated destruction of RBCs, for which GCs remain the first-line therapy [65–67]. Beyond their well-established effects on suppressing autoantibody production and macrophage-mediated erythrophagocytosis, emerging evidence indicates that immune dysregulation within the BM niche contributes substantially to disease progression. Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses of BM immune cells from AIHA patients across disease stages have revealed pronounced T-cell infiltration, clonal expansion, and cytokine dysregulation, with inflammatory signaling pathways markedly upregulated in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells as well as monocyte populations during disease relapse [68]. Consistent with these observations, we found that CD163+ EBI macrophages from treatment-naïve AIHA patients exhibited a robust inflammatory transcriptional program. Although EBI macrophages have been classically characterized as M2-like cells that support erythroid maturation [4, 17, 28], this designation has largely been derived from homeostatic settings. Our findings indicate that, under autoimmune inflammatory pressure, EBI macrophages can deviate from this canonical erythropoiesis-supportive state and acquire features associated with inflammatory activation. Because inflammatory macrophage programs have been previously linked to suppression of BM erythropoiesis [69], this shift may compromise the erythroid-supportive function of the BM niche.

It is important to distinguish this proposed BM niche–mediated mechanism from the established anti-hemolytic effects of GC. The present data do not allow us to determine the relative contribution of reduced RBC destruction versus improved BM erythropoiesis to the clinical hematologic response. Rather, our findings suggest that, beyond limiting hemolysis, GC treatment is associated with partial normalization of the BM macrophage niche. Specifically, GC-treated patients showed attenuation of inflammatory signaling, normalization of iron- and heme-handling pathways, reduction of ferroptosis-related programs, and restoration of EBI formation in CD163+ macrophages. Consistent with the systemic and BM iron overload observed in AIHA patients [42, 43], CD163+ macrophages also display enhanced phagocytic activity alongside upregulation of iron and heme transport pathways, as well as ferroptosis-related programs. Importantly, GC treatment attenuates inflammatory signaling, normalizes iron metabolic pathways, and restores EBI formation in CD163+ macrophages. Thus, we propose that GC therapy may exert dual effects in AIHA: a well-established anti-hemolytic effect that reduces immune-mediated RBC destruction, and a potential BM niche–modulating effect that restores CD163+ EBI macrophages toward a more erythropoiesis-permissive state. Nevertheless, whether this niche-level remodeling contributes independently to hematologic recovery remains to be determined.

Conclusions

In summary, our study supports a model in which GC-induced upregulation of CD163 in EBI macrophages is associated with enhanced iron metabolic activity within the BM EBI niche and increased erythropoietic output (Fig. 8). These findings highlight a potential mechanism by which GC modulates the erythroid microenvironment. From a translational perspective, targeting macrophage-mediated iron handling and EBI function may represent a potential strategy to improve erythropoietic responses while mitigating the adverse effects associated with GC therapy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we focused on membrane-bound CD163 and demonstrated its upregulation following GC treatment, we did not evaluate the soluble form of CD163 [70], which can be generated through ectodomain shedding and may exert distinct biological functions. Therefore, the relative contribution of membrane-bound versus soluble CD163 in DEX-induced erythropoiesis remains to be determined. Second, our analysis was performed at a shorter time point, which may not fully capture the dynamic changes in erythropoiesis and macrophage function. Longer-term studies will be required to assess the temporal effects of DEX and the sustainability of macrophage-mediated erythropoietic support. Third, while our data suggest that CD163+ macrophages are associated with enhanced iron metabolism, we did not directly track iron flux. Future studies using labeled iron tracing approaches will be necessary to more precisely define iron transfer dynamics within EBIs. Fourth, although our findings support a functional role for CD163+ macrophages, we did not perform tissue macrophage-specific CD163 genetic manipulation, which would be required to establish a definitive causal role of CD163 in regulating erythropoiesis. Five, the GR–GRIP1 complex interaction and its functional activity are well established in this study, however, the role and functional relevance of CD163 in tethering this complex to target DNA for transcriptional regulation still require further clarification. Finally, the number of clinical samples from patients with AIHA included in this study was relatively limited. Larger cohorts will be needed to validate the clinical relevance and generalizability of our findings.
