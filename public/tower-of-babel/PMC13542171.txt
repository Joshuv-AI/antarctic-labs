Visible-light-driven CO2 photoreduction to solar fuels over a Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 0D/3D/2D QDs Schottky/Z-scheme ternary heterojunction photocatalyst
Authors: Hossein Kadkhodayan, Taher Alizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, the increasing concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) pollutants in the Earth’s atmosphere due to the expansion of factories and vehicles has caused destructive effects, including an increase in the greenhouse effect, long-term droughts, insufficient rainfall, melting polar ice, and climate change. The various methods are available for removing carbon. Photocatalytic processes have emerged as promising and sustainable approaches for CO2 conversion because they don’t make harmful byproducts, affect most contaminants, do not have any detrimental effects on the environment, don’t need expensive, complicated equipment, are readily recoverable, recover, and use again. Perovskite is a novel kind of photocatalyst that exhibits excellent photocatalytic performance. Photocatalysts with more than one structure are better at covering a bigger area. Perovskite photocatalysts can reduce pollutants more quickly and better than other mono-photocatalysts. When exposed to visible light, this investigation intends to make a modern perovskite photocatalyst that exhibits enhanced performance and can convert carbon dioxide into solar fuels. To achieve this, g-C3N4 (gCN), a non-metallic co-photocatalyst; Bi2ZnTiO6 (BZTO), a double perovskite; and zero valent nickel/copper (Ni/Cu) nanoparticles, a metallic co-photocatalyst, were synthesized. XRD, SEM, EDX/mapping, UV–vis, DRS, and PL experiments were employed to look at the phase, crystallographic, structural, and photocatalytic properties. The performance of the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 perovskite/g-C3N4 0D/3D/2D QDs Schottky/Z-scheme ternary heterojunction nanocomposite was optimized and improved using Taguchi’s experimental design. The temperature of the reaction, how rapidly it was agitated, how long it took, how much CO2 and photocatalyst were present, visible irradiation intensity, interval between photoreduction site and light source, and the pH of the reaction are all critical parameters. To achieve the best conditions according to the Taguchi method, the solution should have a reaction medium pH of 5.0, a temperature of 25 °C, a mixing rate of 200 rpm, a photoreduction time of 300 min, a photocatalyst dosage of 1.0 g/l, a CO2 concentration of 400 mg L−1, a light intensity of 70 W, distance between light source and aqueous solution. The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 Schottky/Z-scheme ternary heterojunction nanocomposite transformed the CO2 with a proficiency rate of 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (total CO2 conversion efficiency 95%) into the main solar fuels of methane (CH4) gas with a productivity ratio of 863.5 μmol g−1 h−1 (selectivity efficiency 50%) and methanol (CH3OH) with a productivity rate of 690.8 μmol g−1 h−1 (selectivity efficiency 40%). Introduction

In recent years, due to the increase in emissions and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration in the Earth’s atmosphere due to human activities, it has left undesirable effects such as an increase in the greenhouse effect, long-term droughts, lack of precipitation, melting of polar ice caps, global warming, and climate change. There are several ways to reduce and manage the amount of carbon dioxide that is released into the air1–4. Among various CO2 mitigation strategies, photocatalytic conversion has emerged as a promising approach due to they don’t make toxic byproducts, they don’t need complicated and expensive equipment, and they are readily recoverable, recover, and reuse. Perovskite photocatalysts are a new kind of photocatalyst that performs high photocatalytic performance5–8. There are many different types of semiconductors in their structure, each with its own strong and distinct photocatalytic capabilities9–12. Photocatalysts with mono-structure can only accomplish a limited photocatalytic activity. Perovskite photocatalysts perform better than single-structure photocatalysts because they can oxidize-reduce pollutants with high efficiency and reaction rate13–15.

CO2 is a thermodynamically stable molecule with a high C=O bond energy, which makes its activation kinetically challenging. This is why most efforts to lower or eliminate CO2 emissions don’t work well or at all. Using robust photocatalysts that can be used again is a cheap, simple, rapid, and efficient way to mitigate CO2 emissions. The photoreduction processes were employed to convert carbon dioxide into valuable chemicals like methane and methanol. These might be utilized to create clean energy and fuels that can be transported. Also, this helps solve the problem of carbon dioxide emissions accumulation in the Earth’s atmosphere4,16,17. There are several factors that might affect how well the photocatalytic photoreduction of carbon dioxide to hydrocarbon fuels works. It is essential to consider the kind of photocatalyst, the size and shape of the photocatalyst particles, the type and amount of doping elements added to the photocatalyst, the amount of photocatalyst used, the concentration of the CO2, and other parameters. The reaction temperature, environmental pressure, length of the irradiation time, phase of the reaction medium, concentration of dissolved oxygen in the water, pH of the solution, presence of inorganic ions, type, intensity, and location of the light source, and other factors might all change the process18–22.

Perovskites have attracted significant attention from researchers and scientists. Perovskites are crystalline materials with a general formula ABX₃, characterized by a distinctive three-dimensional framework that have high reaction rates, a wider operating range, and the ability to react with several different types of pollutants simultaneously7,13. As a result, due to the additional and closely spaced electron levels created in the structure of perovskite photocatalysts compared to single-structure photocatalysts, the efficiency of effective separation of electron–hole pairs (e−/h+) is increased (the probability of electron–hole pair recombination is reduced). The operation of redox reactions is facilitated23. The general structure of perovskite photocatalysts can be presented as follows:

Ax+1B|2x−1|CyZx+2y+3 (Z = O, S and halogens; 0 ≤ x, y ≤ 1); ABZ3, A2BZ4, A2BCZ6, A3BCDZ9.

Generally, perovskites are categorized into five significant classes:(i) simple perovskite; ABZ3 (Z=O, S, X=Cl−, Br−, I−) including SrTiO3, BaTiO3, ZnTiO3, and … (ii) single perovskite; A2BZ4 for example ZnIn2S4, Cu2WS4, La2NiO4, K2NiF4, Zn2SnO4, Cu2AgInS4, Ag2WO4, and BiVO4 (iii) Binary perovskite; A2BB’Z6 such as La2ZnTiO6, Cs2AgBiCl6, Bi2MoO6, Rb2LiTlF6, Ba2SrWO6, Cs2AgInCl6, GdBaCo2O6, Na2ZrTeO6, Cs2TiBr6, and Bi2WO6 (iv) Ternary perovskite; A3BB’Z9 such as La2SrFe2TiO9, SrCaFe2WO9, Ba3NiTaSbO9, Sr3CoFeMoO9, Bi2WZnTiO9, BaSr2MgTa2O9, and Ba2K2Te2O9 (v) Quaternary perovskite; AB3B4’Z9 for example CaCu3Ti4O12, CaCu3NiReO12, CeMn3V4O12, AgZn3Ti4O12, and SrZn3Ti4O1229–33.

The bismuth zinc titanate perovskite is a novel material that is formed by combining and mixing multiple semiconductors, such as zinc oxide, bismuth oxide, and titanium dioxide, into one structure. Each of these semiconductors has its own strong and distinct photocatalytic characteristics. Therefore, perovskite photocatalysts exhibit enhanced activity than photocatalysts with just one structure. In this research, bismuth zinc titanate (Bi2ZnTiO6) double perovskite will be synthesized as a photocatalytic double perovskite with a suitable energy bandgap in the range of CO2 pollutant reduction and active exposed to visible light radiation. The study will also look at the graphite nitride carbon, which is made of non-metallic co-photocatalyst, and the nickel/copper nanoparticles, which are made of metallic co-photocatalyst. To achieve the optimum conditions, the Bi2ZnTiO6 3D double perovskite with both metallic and non-metallic co-photocatalysts is required. The zero-dimensional (0D) nickel/copper quantum dot metallic co-photocatalysts may be added to the Bi2ZnTiO6 perovskite photocatalyst in this work. The incorporation of metallic Ni/Cu nanoparticles can facilitate charge separation through Schottky junction formation, thereby suppressing electron–hole recombination. When a conductive metal and a semiconductor are placed next to each other, a Schottky junction is formed, which makes it easier for electricity to pass across their interface. Quantum dots are particles that are both nanoscale and zero-dimensional at the same time. Also, to achieve the highest photoreduction efficiency of CO2, a two-dimensional (2D) non-metallic co-photocatalyst made of graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) should be added to the structure of Bi2ZnTiO6 perovskite. Graphite carbon nitride is a kind of two-dimensional (2D) non-metallic co-photocatalyst. This will give carbon dioxide more places to adhere, which will make them more stable and speed up the photocatalytic activity. The g-C3N4 has a highly reactive surface that, on the one hand, can absorb more radiant energy and, on the other hand, can adsorb and store carbon dioxide molecules. The testing and analysis done in this work demonstrate that the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary nanocomposite could be a novel, highly effective photocatalyst to convert carbon dioxide reactant into valuable fuels. It is also relatively stable in terms of structure, has significant photocatalytic properties, and may be utilized more than once. The synthesized nanocomposite exhibits structural stability, reusability, and promising photocatalytic performance under visible-light irradiation.

Experimental part

Materials and methods

The Cu (NO3)2·6H2O, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, TiO2-P25, BiCl3, melamine, citric acid, and K2CO3 were provided from Merck. Using an X-ray diffraction (XRD) model (Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer set to 40 kV and 30 mA of Cu Kα), the structural characteristics of the produced particles were assessed. To further analyze the morphology and structure of the Bi2ZnTiO6, Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6, and Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 specimens, a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (model: MIRA3 TESCAN SEM) was used. Diffuse Reflection Spectroscopy (DRS, Avaspec-2048-TEC) was used to determine the energy band gap (Eg) of the Bi2ZnTiO6, g-C3N4, Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6, Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 and Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 specimens. Using the Ultraviolet–Visible spectrometer (UV–Vis) (model: Lambda850 Model, Perkin Elmer), the absorbance spectra of the carbon dioxide concentrations were measured. The elemental composition was identified using Energy Dispersive X-ray spectra (EDX) (model: F20 STWIN electron microscope (Tecnai G2, FEI Co); 200 kV). Additionally, the elemental mapping analysis (model: 7000 Shimadzu, Japan) was used to examine the atom distribution in the composition of the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 nanocomposite. The visible light irradiation source was a 70-W Xe arc lamp (CEL-HXF70, Beijing Aulight Co. Ltd.) equipped with an ultraviolet-cutoff filter (λ > 420 nm). Using a gas chromatography (GC) (Agilent model) was used to identify the gaseous products produced by the photoreduction of carbon dioxide.

Preparation of Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskite by sol–gel approach

Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskite was synthesized via a sol–gel method. Citric acid (2.0 g) was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of stoichiometric amounts of BiCl3 and Zn(NO3)2·6H2O as precursors under continuous stirring. TiO2-P25 was dissolved in an acidic HNO3/HF solution under controlled conditions to facilitate titanium incorporation into the sol. All HF-related procedures were performed in a fume hood with appropriate safety precautions. The mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 120 min to obtain a homogeneous gel. It could keep the time at 120 min, the speed at 200 rpm, and the temperature at 70 °C. After the impurities were removed, the gel was heated to 350 °C for 1 h to remove residual organic species. The powdered Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskites were heated to 700 °C for two hours and then utilized for photocatalytic testing34,45,46.

Synthesis of graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4 (gCN) by heat programming procedure

Using a particular heat program, 50 mL of deionized water and 5 g of melamine were mixed to generate graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4(gCN)). The mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 120 min at 200 rpm. The solution was dried to 80 °C and was placed in a continuous process for 120 min at 520 °C (5 °C per minute) to get the water out34,47.

Manufacturing the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 by reduction co-precipitation

To prepare the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 specimen, 1.0 g of synthesized Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskites was incorporated in 25 mL of deionized water, and henceforth, 0.1 g of metallic salts of nickel (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) and copper (Cu(NO3)2.6H2O) was added to the provided solution. Then 5 mL of Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) solution was gradually introduced as reducing agent to convert Ni2+ and Cu2+ ions to their metallic states (Ni° and Cu°).

The suspension was stirred at 70 °C to ensure uniform deposition of metal nanoparticles onto the Bi2ZnTiO6 surface. The resulting solid was filtered, washed, dried, and calcined at 350 °C for 1 h48.

Construction of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 by hydrothermal approach

The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary nanocomposite was manufactured using a hydrothermal method. 50 mL of distilled water was used to spread out 1.1 g of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 sample at first. After being subjected to ultrasonic dispersion for an hour, 0.1 g of g-C3N4 non-metallic co-photocatalyst was poured into a solution containing Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 sample. The Bi2ZnTiO6 particles were combined with zero-valent Ni/Cu nanoparticles and g-C₃N₄ under hydrothermal conditions to achieve uniform dispersion. The mixture was then stirred under optimal circumstances, which were a pH of 7, a reaction temperature of 70 °C, a rotation velocity of 200 rpm, and a retention time of 120 min. This was done using a magnetic stirrer that could be set to a specific temperature. The solution was then placed in an autoclave at 180 °C for 2 h. It was cleansed numerous times with deionized water once it was found. After that, Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 powder was desiccated at 80 °C for 12 h34.

Photoreduction process of CO2

A 70-W Xe arc lamp was used with an ultraviolet-cutoff filter to make the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite work well to photoreducte CO2. The first step was to add 1 L of deionized water and 0.4 g of pure sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) to the photoreactor. Alternatively, a more accurate alternative method can be used to bubble high-purity CO2 gas into an aqueous suspension containing 1.0 g/L of photocatalyst until CO2 reaches saturation. The end product was a solution that had 400 mg L−1 of carbon dioxide in it. This was done to show how carbon dioxide gas and water combine. Next, 1.0 g Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 nanocomposite was poured into a solution containing 400 mg L−1 of dissolved carbon dioxide (carbonate (CO32−) inside the photoreactor. The solution was shaken in the dark for 20 min to evaluate the influences of surface adsorption. No detectable CH4 or CH3OH was observed in the absence of light. Then, a visible light irradiation source with a power of 70-W was used to beam visible light on the water solution containing the CO2 for five hours at 25 °C. It was investigated how the concentration and absorbance of CO32− ions in the solution changed after the procedure was finished using an Ultraviolet–Visible spectrometer. The carbonate ion absorbs ultraviolet light at a wavelength of 220 nm, and it gives a sharp and strong peak in this area. After visible-light irradiation for 5 h at 25 °C, gaseous products were analyzed using gas chromatography. Product identification and quantification were performed using calibration curves of standard gases. The photoreduction efficiency was calculated based on the amount of carbon-containing products generated per gram of photocatalyst per hour (μmol g−1 h−1).

Results and discussion

Analysis of prepared photocatalysts

As shown in Fig. 2, XRD analysis was performed to determine the crystallinity and phase type of the Bi2ZnTiO6, Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6, and Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalysts. Every pattern in the provided XRD spectra of the Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskite had diffraction peaks that, instead of superfluous peaks, are consistent with the standard JCPDS reference pattern, confirming phase purity and crystallinity. According to the JCPDS index: 37–0243 (Fig. 2a), the different planes of (112), (110), (222), (400), (200), (440), (211), and (622) support the organization of the Bi2ZnTiO6 structure with 2θ values of 22°, 25°, 3°3, 3°6, 4°5, °65, °72, and °75, respectively. The JCPDS file 87-0721’s peaks match those in the Ni/Cu nanoparticles XRD pattern. A characteristic diffraction peak at 2θ = 22°, the g-C3N4 nanosheets peak was also seen at (002). Additionally, the organization of the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6 structure (JCPDS: 70-1795) is supported by the different planes of (111), ()220), (311), (222), (400), and (422) with 2θ values of °22, °25, °33, 4°2, °45, and °51, respectively (Fig. 2b). The peak of the g-C3N4 XRD pattern is associated with JCPDS file number 871526. The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst’s XRD analysis showed a peak at (002) in Fig. 2c with a 2θ angle of 22°, in accordance with the typical XRD pattern with JCPDS: 87-1526.

The band alignment analysis reveals that the CB of g-C3N4 is the most negative, making it the primary site for electron accumulation and reduction reactions, while the VB of Bi2ZnTiO6 is the most positive, favoring oxidation reactions. This configuration supports a direct Z-scheme charge transfer mechanism, in which photogenerated electrons in the CB of Bi2ZnTiO6 recombine with holes in the VB of g-C3N4, effectively preserving strong redox potentials in the remaining electrons and holes. Overall, the Mott–Schottky analysis corroborates the enhanced photocatalytic performance of the composites, particularly the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 system, by providing quantitative insight into carrier density, band positions, and charge transfer dynamics. These results further confirm the proposed Z-scheme charge transfer mechanism.

Impact of the main variables on the photoreduction of CO2 to solar fuels

Effect of solution pH

The pH of the reaction medium plays a critical role in photocatalytic CO2 reduction, as it influences surface charge properties, adsorption behavior, and the availability of reactive species. As shown in Fig. 11a, the highest CO2 photoreduction efficiency was achieved at pH 5. This enhancement can be attributed to improved CO2 adsorption and the increased availability of protons, which facilitate multi-electron reduction pathways toward CH4 and CH3OH. Under slightly acidic conditions, charge transfer processes are kinetically favored without inducing structural instability in the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 heterojunction.

Impact of reaction temperature

At this stage, it was investigated how CO2 was photoreduced at temperatures between 25 and 80 °C. Figure 11b demonstrates that how changes in different temperatures affect the photoreduction efficiency of the CO2. As shown in Fig. 11b, when the temperature goes up from 25 to 65 °C, the efficacy of carbon dioxide photoreduction goes down from 1727 (CO2 conversion efficiency 95%) to 1295.25 μmol g−1 h−1 (75% efficiency). When the temperature rose from 65 to 80 °C, the photocatalytic reduction efficiency of CO2 pollutants went up from 1295.25 (75% efficiency) to 1467.95 μmol g−1 h−1 (85% efficiency). One of the temperatures that was looked at was 25 °C, which was the optimal operating temperature to mitigate CO2 emissions.

Influence of reaction time

When exposed to visible light, the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite exhibits time-dependent CO2 photoreduction activity, as shown in Fig. 11c. It can be observed from Fig. 11c that how successfully CO2 were photoreduced after being in contact for varied amounts of time. The photoreduction yield of the CO2 pollutant was increased up to a reaction time of 300 min. After this point, the conversion efficiency didn’t vary substantially; thus, 300 min was determined as the optimal reaction time to reach steady-state conditions. The photoreduction efficiency for dissolved CO2 gas to usable products was around 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (total CO2 conversion efficiency 95%) after 300 min of contact time when the light was visible. The main products were methane gas and methanol. Methanol was made at a rate of 690.8 μmol g−1 h−1 (40% efficiency), whereas methane gas was produced at a rate of 863.5 μmol g−1 h−1 (50% efficiency).

Impact of photocatalyst dosage

It was investigated how varying amounts of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite changed the capacity of CO2 to photoreduce in the range of 0.5 to 2.0 g/l (Fig. 11d). The CO2 pollutant’s photoreduction efficiency increases from 949.85 (55% efficiency) to 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (total CO2 conversion efficiency 95%) when the quantity of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 goes up from 0.5 to 1.0 g/l (Fig. 11d). A plausible explanation is that the more Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 nanocomposite there is, the more active sites. When quantities of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst were incremented to more than 1.0 g/l, the photoreduction yield of CO2 was reduced to 1450.68 μmol g−1 h−1 (84% efficiency). The drop is because the excessive photocatalyst loading increase light scattering and reduce photon penetration, which makes it less effective.

Effect of CO2 concentration

Figure 11e shows that it looked at how well the reduction works with varied levels of CO2, from 100 to 600 mg L−1. The initial CO2 concentration has an effect on how successful CO2 control is. A photocatalytic activity increases when there is a pollutant with sufficient concentration. Photoreduction proficiency of the CO2 at 400 mg L−1 had the highest impact. Additionally, photoreduction didn’t work as well at levels above 400 mg L−1.

Impact of stirring speed

The agitation rate in a photocatalytic reaction may significant how effectively the photocatalyst functions and how it responds. In reality, photocatalytic activity is higher when the rotation rate is faster. This is because it makes it easier for mass to move, which speeds things up. The reaction mixture containing Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst was stirred at speeds of 100, 200, 300, and 400 rpm to observe how the speed of stirring influences the photoreduction and conversion of dissolved CO2 into usable products. As shown in Fig. 11f, the 200 rpm was the optimal mixing speed since it got rid of more than 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (95% efficiency) of the CO2 pollutants.

Effect of the interval between the reaction medium and the visible irradiation source

Figure 11g shows that the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 heterojunction photocatalyst’s ability to convert CO2 into solar fuels depends on how far away the light source is from the solution surface. The distances were considered between 5 and 20 cm. According to Fig. 11g, the best distance between the reaction liquid and the visible light was 5 cm. At this distance, CO2 photoreduced the most, with an effectiveness of over 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (total CO2 conversion efficiency 95%). The more distant the light source is from the solution’s surface, the less light intensity decreases with distance it may seem. This makes it more challenging to utilize photocatalysis to convert carbon dioxide into valuable chemicals.

Influence of irradiation light intensity

The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 heterojunction photocatalyst can turn CO2 into usable fuels when it is subjected to varied levels of visible light, from 10 to 90 W. The data in Fig. 11h reveal that the 70 W light exhibited the highest activity on the CO2 pollutant. Photoreduction didn’t function any better on CO2 when there was more than 70 W of light. It might also harm the photocatalyst’s structure, which would make it less effective. Table 4 shows the best results obtained at the end.

Evaluating the reusability and selectivity

A photocatalyst has to be able to be used more than once and survive a long time in order to perform successfully. Five recovery cycles were made in a row to see the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary heterojunction photocatalyst. Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite was put in a centrifuge, washed it with water and ethanol a few times, and thereupon dried it at 70 °C for 24 h to get it back. It can be observed from Fig. 12 that the photocatalytic performance went down by a very small amount after the fifth recovery period. The data demonstrate that the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst may be easily recovered and utilized again.

Conclusion

The study was able to clearly show that Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 0D/3D/2D QDs Schottky/Z-scheme ternary nanocomposite is a high-performance heterojunction photocatalyst. According to this research, loading zero valent Ni/Cu metallic co-photocatalyst and g-C3N4 non-metallic co-photocatalyst into the Bi2ZnTiO6 structure makes its efficiency better at CO2 photoreduction into usable fuels. The results revealed that adding the zero valent Ni/Cu nanoparticles to the Bi2ZnTiO6 structure made the Bi2ZnTiO6 binary perovskite’s energy band gap lower. Also, adding a non-metallic co-photocatalyst g-C3N4 to the double perovskite Bi2ZnTiO6 structure can increase the specific surface area for reaction and adsorption of carbon dioxide pollutant on the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst. When visible light radiation hits the Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst structure, electrons in the valent band of graphitic carbon nitride are first excited and transferred to its conduction band due to its low energy band gap. This produces pairs of holes and electrons (e−/h+) in the g-C3N4 structure. By transferring and consuming electrons from the conduction band of graphite carbon nitride to reduce the carbon dioxide substrate, an electron is quickly transferred from the conduction band of Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskite to the valent band of g-C3N4. As the conduction band of Bi2ZnTiO6 double perovskite empties, an electron from its valent band replaces the hole accumulation in the conduction band, and the valent band acquires a positive charge. Metallic Ni/Cu nanoparticles act as electron sinks due to Schottky barrier formation, effectively trapping photogenerated electrons and suppressing electron–hole recombination. Nickel/copper nanoparticles have a hole accumulation (positive charge or hole), and this photogenerated holes exhibit strong oxidative potential and may take electrons from solvent molecules such as water. When the correct conditions were reached, the Ni-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst could achieved total CO2 conversion efficiency 95% (1727 μmol g−1 h−1 production rate). The Structural stability and selectivity of Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst were very high and can be utilized over and over again, even after five cycles of recycling and continued use. The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst outperformed previously reported photocatalysts that had been created in the last several years. The Ni/Cu-loaded Bi2ZnTiO6/g-C3N4 photocatalyst is a highly efficient nanocomposite since it can convert CO2 with the photoconversion rate of 1727 μmol g−1 h−1 (total CO2 conversion efficiency 95%) into valuable solar fuels.
