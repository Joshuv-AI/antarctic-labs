Contemporary practices in scoping review protocol registration: a meta-research analysis of high-impact health journals (2024–2025)
Authors: Fernando César Paraizo Borges, Raissa Tabosa Ferreira, Kevin Henrique Azevedo Duarte, Clarice Wanderley de Sousa, Ana Beatriz Maia Fernandes, Manuella Rocha Boaventura Pinheiro, Thereza Eduarda Medeiros Pimentel, Gustavo Andrade de Oliveira, Manuela Queiroz Reis, Maria Heloísa Pignataro Lange, Maycon Willian Fontes da Costa, Giovanna Kettlen Mendes Silva, Bruno Fontenele Carvalho, Fabio Gamboa Ritto, João Vitor dos Santos Canellas

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScoping reviews are widely used across the health sciences to map evidence, clarify concepts, and identify knowledge gaps. Although protocol development and registration are increasingly encouraged, empirical evidence describing contemporary registration practices remains limited.MethodsThis study aimed to evaluate current practices in scoping review protocol registration and reporting in high-impact health sciences journals published between 2024 and 2025. We conducted an empirical meta-research study of scoping reviews published in first-quartile journals according to the Journal Citation Reports and indexed in PubMed. Data were extracted independently using a standardized form and included protocol registration status, registry platform, public accessibility, peer-reviewed protocol publication, reported protocol components, and methodological guidance cited. Descriptive analyses were used to assess registration patterns and methodological consistency. Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to explore factors associated with protocol registration reporting and the presence of a peer-reviewed protocol.ResultsAmong 2,546 screened records, 1,267 scoping reviews underwent full data extraction. Most protocols were registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (n = 478). Protocol content frequently included core methodological elements but also reported additional items beyond those addressed in the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis. Background

Scoping reviews have become an established approach for mapping the available literature, clarifying concepts and definitions, and identifying knowledge gaps within a given area. Their use has expanded substantially across the health sciences, reflecting the growing demand for evidence synthesis methods capable of addressing broad and heterogeneous questions [1].

In parallel with this growth, methodological practices originally developed for systematic reviews, particularly protocol development and registration, have increasingly been adopted in scoping reviews to enhance rigor, transparency, and reproducibility [2]. However, scoping review protocols remain highly variable in structure, terminology, and the level of methodological detail provided, which may limit their ability to support prespecification and methodological traceability.

Although several frameworks and reporting guidance documents inform the conduct and reporting of scoping reviews [2–7], comparatively little empirical evidence describes how scoping review protocols are currently registered and what content they include in real-world practice, particularly in high-impact journals. As a result, protocols may range from well-structured methodological documents to brief descriptions that omit key elements, reducing reproducibility and hampering readers’ ability to interpret deviations from planned methods.

Protocol registration is intended to support transparency and may help identify selective reporting. Yet, when protocol content is inconsistently structured or includes components that are not explicitly aligned with scoping review methodology, these objectives may be undermined [8]. In addition, uncertainty regarding which methodological guidance should inform scoping review protocols, and the limited availability of scoping review–specific protocol guidance, may further contribute to heterogeneity in protocol content. This situation may be exacerbated by the absence of structured methodological verification or validation mechanisms within registration platforms, potentially allowing key elements to be omitted or inconsistently reported.

Meta-research provides an opportunity to systematically examine contemporary methodological practices and derive insights grounded in observed research behavior. Accordingly, this study evaluated how scoping review protocols published between 2024 and 2025 in high-impact health sciences journals were developed, registered, and reported, with the objectives of characterizing current registration practices, identifying patterns of content variability, and informing future efforts to strengthen transparency and reproducibility in scoping review protocols.

Methods

Study design

The study protocol was registered in INPLASY [9], a publicly accessible registry for evidence synthesis protocols, under the registration code INPLASY202580057. The detailed search strategy used to identify eligible scoping reviews is available at https://doi.org/10.37766/inplasy2025.8.0057.

Journal selection

We searched for scoping reviews published in top-ranked journals identified using the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) database produced by Clarivate Analytics. Journals were eligible if they were classified in the first quartile (Q1; top 25%) within their respective JCR subject category and indexed in PubMed.

Given the large number of JCR subject categories, the analysis was restricted to 23 pre-specified health sciences categories. All eligible categories were numerically coded, and the final set was selected using a random number table: Public, Environmental & Occupational Health; Pharmacology & Pharmacy; Medicine, General & Internal; Oncology; Neurosciences; Surgery; Psychiatry; Clinical Neurology; Nursing; Endocrinology & Metabolism; Pediatrics; Dentistry, Oral Surgery & Medicine; Sport Sciences; Nutrition & Dietetics; Ophthalmology; Dermatology; Rheumatology; Pathology; Rehabilitation; Health Care Sciences & Services; Urology & Nephrology; and Psychology, Clinical.

Eligibility criteria

This meta-research included scoping reviews that met the following criteria: publication between June 22, 2024, and June 22, 2025; indexing in PubMed; and explicit identification as a “scoping review” in the title or abstract. Editorials, commentaries, and peer-reviewed protocols without an associated completed scoping review were excluded. Due to the large number of eligible articles and operational constraints, a post hoc reduction of the analytical sample was performed, limiting data extraction to approximately 1,200 randomly selected scoping reviews.

Studies for which the full text was not accessible through institutional subscriptions and scoping review protocols that were not publicly available were also excluded. All eligible articles were exported to EndNote 2025, and shared libraries were created to facilitate collaboration among reviewers during screening and data extraction.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by trained reviewers using a standardized form and subsequently reviewed by an expert author. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussion, and all extracted variables are presented in S1 Table.

For each included scoping review, the following information was collected: article title; journal name; publication date; country of the corresponding author; registry platform; registration ID; and whether a peer-reviewed version of the protocol had been published (yes/no), including the journal of publication when applicable. Reported peer-reviewed protocols were independently verified through assessment of the referenced publication and associated registration information whenever available.

For each referenced protocol, additional data were extracted, including protocol status (updated vs. not updated), registration date (used to calculate the registration-to-publication interval), protocol sections reported (e.g., rationale, objectives, eligibility criteria, search strategy, data charting, dissemination plan), and the methodological frameworks or reporting guidelines cited.

All extracted data were cross-checked by a second expert reviewer to ensure accuracy and consistency prior to synthesis.

Data analysis and synthesis

Findings were summarized using descriptive statistics and presented in tables and figures to illustrate trends in protocol registration, reporting practices, and methodological content. A narrative synthesis was conducted to describe key characteristics and patterns across protocols.

Categorical variables representing reported protocol items and their classification according to the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Joanna Briggs Institute) were used to construct a treemap visualization, in which the area of each element reflected its frequency across included protocols.

All analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (2025-06-13) for macOS.

Two binary outcomes were defined at the review level: (A) reporting of protocol registration in the published scoping review article; and (B) presence of a registered protocol that had undergone external peer review prior to publication.

For both outcomes, publisher, journal, JCR category, and corresponding author country were evaluated as categorical explanatory variables.

Post hoc regression analyses

Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to assess factors associated with each outcome. Given the presence of sparse data, rare categories, and quasi-complete separation, Firth penalized maximum likelihood logistic regression was employed to reduce small-sample bias and ensure stable model estimation.

Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05. All models simultaneously included publisher, journal, and corresponding author country as covariates, with estimates derived using profile penalized likelihood methods. These post hoc analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Results

The PubMed search identified 2,546 articles published between June 22, 2024, and June 22, 2025, of which 1,267 scoping reviews met the inclusion criteria and underwent full data extraction. Among these, 549 reviews reported a registered protocol. Of those, 483 protocols were included in the final analysis, as the remaining records were not publicly accessible or did not provide open access to the full protocol content. During the study period, the included scoping reviews were authored by corresponding authors from a wide range of countries, reflecting the global uptake of scoping reviews within the health sciences. The largest proportions of publications originated from the United States of America (14.76%) and Australia (13.97%), followed by the United Kingdom (10.1%) and Canada (8.92%). Additional substantial contributions were observed from China (6.39%), Italy (4.5%), and Brazil (4.26%). A second tier of contributing countries included several European nations, such as Germany (2.84%), the Netherlands (2.37%), Spain and Switzerland (2.05% each), Ireland (1.97%), Sweden (1.58%), and France and Portugal (1.5% each), as well as Denmark (1.42%) and Japan (1.34%). A detailed summary of the geographic distribution of corresponding authors is presented in Fig. 1.

In terms of protocol registration, fewer than half of the included scoping reviews reported having a registered protocol. Overall, 43.3% of the reviews indicated that a protocol had been registered on a publicly accessible platform, whereas the remaining 56.7% did not report any form of protocol registration. A small proportion of the included scoping reviews reported having a peer-reviewed protocol published in a scientific journal. Overall, just 5.1% of the scoping reviews in the sample had an associated protocol that underwent formal peer review and was published as a standalone article.

Among the 549 scoping reviews that reported a registered protocol, a limited number of registration platforms accounted for the vast majority of citations. The Open Science Framework (OSF) was by far the most frequently reported platform, accounting for 478 registrations, representing the majority of scoping review protocols identified during the study period. PROSPERO was the second most commonly cited registry, with 46 protocols, followed by INPLASY, which accounted for 14 registrations. Other platforms were used only sporadically, including Figshare, UMIN-CTR, ClinicalTrials.gov, and institutional repositories.

The included scoping reviews were concentrated within a limited number of major international publishers. BMJ Group, Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, MDPI, and JMIR Publications accounted for the majority of publications, with BMJ Open being the single most frequent journal. High publication volumes were also observed in journals such as Journal of Medical Internet Research, Journal of Clinical Medicine, BMC Public Health, Journal of Advanced Nursing, and Journal of Clinical Nursing. Springer Nature and Elsevier demonstrated particularly broad journal coverage, with scoping reviews distributed across multiple clinical and public health specialties. In contrast, other publishers and independent journals contributed a relatively small proportion of studies (Fig. 2).

Analysis of the reported protocol items demonstrated a clear predominance of core methodological components recommended by the JBI Manual, particularly those related to study identification and evidence mapping. The most frequently reported items included type of review, eligibility criteria, data charting/data extraction procedures, search strategy, and information sources, followed by other mandatory elements such as review objectives, population or participants, research questions, data synthesis approaches, funding, and context.

Conversely, a substantial number of protocols reported items not explicitly addressed by JBI guidance. These included elements commonly observed in systematic review protocols, such as sampling plans, hypotheses, statistical models, risk-of-bias assessment and quality appraisal, as well as detailed procedural components related to screening and data extraction processes. As illustrated in the treemap visualization (Fig. 3), mandatory items accounted for the largest proportion of reported content, while a wide range of additional items was also frequently reported across protocols (S2 Table).

Items are categorized as JBI mandatory (green), JBI conditional (orange), or not addressed by JBI guidance (blue)

Across the analyzed protocols, authors cited a wide range of methodological and reporting documents, including Arksey and O’Malley [10], Levac et al. [3], PRISMA-ScR [5], PRISMA-P [11], SPIRIT [12], MECIR [13], PRESS [14], ROSES [15], the JBI Manual [6], and the framework described by Peters et al. [2]. Although a wide range of methodological documents is frequently cited, only the last two listed sources provide explicit and contemporary guidance for the development of scoping review protocols.

Post hoc regression analyses

As a post hoc analysis, multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression models were fitted to explore factors associated with protocol registration reporting (Outcome A) and the presence of a peer-reviewed registered protocol (Outcome B).

Outcome A: reporting of protocol registration

In the adjusted model, statistically significant associations were observed across journals, publishers, and corresponding author country (S3 Table).

At the journal level, several journals showed higher odds of reporting protocol registration when compared with the reference group, including the Journal of Medical Internet Research (OR 5.28, 95% CI 1.36–23.22), the International Journal of Medical Informatics (OR 6.32, 95% CI 1.26–38.72), and BMJ Open (OR 4.32, 95% CI 1.22–16.83). Other journals demonstrated borderline associations, including the aggregated group of other journals (OR 2.73, 95% CI 0.99–8.67).

With respect to publishers, increased odds of reporting protocol registration were observed for journals published by the BMJ Group (OR 12.98, 95% CI 1.29–1759.08) and JMIR Publications (OR 46.72, 95% CI 1.33–17,087.20), although confidence intervals were wide, reflecting substantial heterogeneity and limited sample sizes across categories.

At the country level, reviews with corresponding authors based in Brazil (OR 4.00, 95% CI 1.95–8.88) and Portugal (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.07–9.12) showed significantly higher odds of reporting protocol registration. In contrast, lower odds were observed for corresponding authors from Ghana (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.02–0.90), Iran (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.02–0.74), Switzerland (OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.73), and the United States of America (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.37–0.89).

For Outcome B, fewer statistically significant associations were identified (S3 Table).

At the publisher level, reviews published in journals from the American Medical Association (OR 12.83, 95% CI 1.05–103.84), Independent University Press (OR 34.23, 95% CI 1.59–5305.02), and Wolters Kluwer (OR 4.85, 95% CI 1.05–18.99) demonstrated higher odds of having a peer-reviewed registered protocol.

At the country level, corresponding authors from Canada (OR 3.37, 95% CI 1.00–11.85), Germany (OR 11.25, 95% CI 3.34–42.62), Ghana (OR 23.28, 95% CI 3.38–190.65), Norway (OR 8.72, 95% CI 1.32–47.32), and South Africa (OR 8.78, 95% CI 2.18–37.05) were more likely to have a peer-reviewed registered protocol.

No consistent or statistically significant associations were observed across most journal-level categories for Outcome B.

Overall, the post hoc analyses demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in protocol registration practices across publishers, journals, and geographic regions. Reporting of protocol registration (Outcome A) showed multiple statistically significant associations, whereas the presence of a peer-reviewed registered protocol (Outcome B) was less frequently observed and appeared to be concentrated within a limited subset of publishers and countries. Wide confidence intervals were observed across several categories, reflecting sparse data within some strata. Collectively, these findings suggest that protocol registration practices may be shaped more strongly by publisher-level policies and geographic research cultures than by journal-specific editorial requirements.

Discussion

This large-scale meta-research provides a comprehensive evaluation of contemporary scoping review protocol registration practices in high-impact health sciences journals and extends the existing literature beyond protocol reporting alone. By examining protocol accessibility, protocol content, publication of peer-reviewed protocols, methodological guidance cited by authors, and factors associated with registration practices, the study offers a broader understanding of how scoping review protocols are currently developed and registered in practice.

A principal finding of the present study was the substantial variability observed in protocol registration practices. Fewer than half of published scoping reviews reported having a registered protocol, even among journals occupying the highest quartile of impact within their respective disciplines. Although this proportion represents a substantial improvement compared with findings reported in 2016, when only 13% of scoping reviews documented the existence of a protocol [8], protocol registration has not yet been consolidated as a normative methodological standard for scoping reviews. In contrast to systematic reviews, where prospective protocol registration is widely regarded as mandatory, scoping reviews continue to occupy an intermediate methodological space in which transparency practices remain variably applied.

Among reviews that reported protocol registration, activity was highly concentrated within a small number of platforms, with the Open Science Framework (OSF) accounting for the majority of registrations. Its prominence may be partly explained by accessibility, flexibility, and the absence of eligibility restrictions. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Ferreira et al., who similarly identified OSF as the predominant platform for scoping review protocol registration [16]. Because registration platforms do not routinely impose standardized methodological requirements or content screening, protocols may range from comprehensive methodological documents to brief narrative descriptions, limiting reproducibility and interpretability. In addition, OSF permits non-public registrations, and approximately 12% of protocols in our sample remained inaccessible after article publication, thereby constraining transparency and limiting assessment of prespecification and protocol adherence.

A further concern is the continued registration of scoping review protocols in registries not designed to accommodate this study design. PROSPERO, despite not formally accepting scoping reviews, was the second most frequently cited registry, and a small number of protocols were registered in clinical trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov and UMIN-CTR. These practices suggest persistent uncertainty regarding appropriate registration venues and may also reflect editorial or institutional pressures to provide a registration identifier regardless of methodological alignment with scoping review principles.

Beyond registration practices, substantial conceptual inconsistency was observed in protocol content. While essential elements such as eligibility criteria, search strategies, and data charting procedures were commonly reported, many protocols incorporated components traditionally associated with systematic reviews or primary research designs, including hypotheses, predefined outcomes, statistical models, risk-of-bias assessments, and quality appraisal frameworks. This pattern reflects ongoing uncertainty regarding the fundamental purpose of scoping reviews, which are intended to map and characterize existing evidence rather than to estimate effects or generate pooled quantitative outcomes. However, the role of critical appraisal in scoping reviews remains debated, and some methodological perspectives consider the absence of formal risk-of-bias assessment to be an important distinguishing feature between scoping reviews and systematic reviews. Similar concerns were identified more than a decade ago by Colquhoun et al. [17], and our findings indicate that these conceptual ambiguities continue to influence contemporary practice. Recent methodological literature has likewise highlighted persistent uncertainties regarding the conduct, reporting, and interpretation of scoping reviews [18].

The observed variability in protocol content appears partly attributable to the fragmented landscape of methodological guidance available to authors. Although numerous documents are frequently cited, including Arksey and O’Malley [10], Levac et al., [3] PRISMA-ScR [5], PRISMA-P [11], SPIRIT [12], and other reporting or search-related guidelines, these sources differ substantially in scope and intended application. Among the currently available guidance documents, the JBI Manual [6] and the framework proposed by Peters et al. [2] provide some of the most explicit and contemporary guidance for scoping review protocol development. The concurrent use of guidance documents designed for reporting, conduct, or other review types may therefore inadvertently reinforce inconsistency in protocol structure and methodological emphasis. Recent efforts to update the PRISMA-ScR reporting guideline have similarly identified numerous emerging methodological considerations and additional reporting items, further suggesting that methodological standardization in scoping reviews remains an evolving area of research [19].

Taken together, our findings reinforce the importance of protocols as tools for documenting methodological intent and supporting transparency in scoping reviews. Protocols facilitate assessment of how review methods are planned and reported. When protocols are absent, inconsistently structured, or based on substantially different methodological assumptions, interpretation and assessment of methodological transparency across scoping reviews may become more challenging.

In this context, registration platforms could play a more active role by incorporating structured methodological alignment checks, completeness verification, and scoping review–specific guidance to support more transparent and interpretable registrations. Although publication of peer-reviewed protocols may provide an additional opportunity for external methodological scrutiny and transparency, the extent to which this practice meaningfully improves methodological rigor or reduces bias in scoping reviews remains uncertain. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that publication of peer-reviewed protocols remains uncommon, occurring in approximately 5% of scoping reviews.

Post hoc regression analyses demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in protocol registration practices across publishers, journals, and geographic regions. Reporting of protocol registration was associated with journal, publisher, and corresponding author country, suggesting that registration practices continue to be shaped by editorial policies, local research cultures, and differing methodological perspectives across the scoping review literature.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was restricted to scoping reviews published in Q1 journals indexed in PubMed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to lower-ranked journals or disciplines outside the health sciences. Second, the one-year study period may not fully capture longer-term temporal trends in registration practices. In addition, variability in terminology and reporting across scoping reviews may have resulted in residual misclassification despite extensive harmonization procedures during data extraction and synthesis.

Furthermore, non-public registrations could not be evaluated, potentially leading to conservative estimates of content heterogeneity and limiting the assessment of transparency and accessibility. Although the JBI Manual and the framework proposed by Peters et al. were used as the primary references for content classification because they provide explicit and contemporary guidance for scoping review protocol development, multiple methodological approaches to scoping reviews exist, and alternative frameworks may also be appropriate depending on the objectives and context of the review.

Finally, the present study did not assess agreement between registered protocols and the final published scoping reviews. Consequently, no conclusions can be drawn regarding protocol adherence, selective methodological modifications, or discrepancies between planned and reported review methods.

Knowledge gaps

Several important knowledge gaps remain. First, little empirical evidence is available regarding the extent to which protocol registration influences the methodological quality, transparency, reproducibility, or credibility of scoping reviews. Second, the impact of protocol accessibility and the consequences of non-public registrations remain poorly understood. Finally, limited evidence exists regarding agreement between registered protocols and final published scoping reviews, making it difficult to determine how frequently methodological modifications occur during the review process and how such changes are reported.

Future directions

Future research should evaluate the relationship between protocol registration and methodological quality in scoping reviews, including the extent to which registration contributes to transparency and reproducibility. Studies assessing agreement between registered protocols and completed reviews would provide important insights into protocol adherence and methodological modifications over time. In addition, developing and evaluating scoping review–specific protocol standards, reporting guidance, and registration platform requirements may improve consistency while preserving the methodological flexibility that characterizes scoping review methodology.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-research provides empirical insight into how scoping review protocols are currently developed and registered within high-impact health sciences journals. By systematically examining registration practices, protocol content, and the methodological guidance cited by authors, the study highlights substantial variability between contemporary research practices and currently available methodological recommendations. The findings demonstrate considerable heterogeneity in protocol content, registration approaches, and use of methodological guidance across published scoping reviews. At the same time, they reinforce that important methodological discussions remain ongoing regarding the role, interpretation, and practical implications of protocol registration and methodological standardization in scoping reviews.
