Safety and efficacy of distal versus conventional radial artery cannulation for invasive blood pressure monitoring: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tian Hao, Yuhan Sha, Wenxiu Niu, Haohao Xie, Jie Cheng, Lin Zhang, Ruirui Qi, Xiaomeng Han, Fei Zheng, Tingwei Wang, Ruofan Zhang, Jie Li, Kejing Mu, Zhan Hao, Zhigang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To compare the safety and effectiveness of distal radial artery (DRA) versus conventional radial artery (CRA) catheterization for invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring. This meta-analysis followed PRISMA guidelines. Randomized controlled trials published up to December 30, 2025 were systematically searched in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias. Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 18.0, and evidence quality was evaluated with the GRADE system. 12 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 1,790 participants were included. For the primary outcomes, compared with CRA, DRA was associated with lower incidences of haematoma (RR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.24–0.73), catheter blockage (RR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.81), and bleeding (RR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.11–0.59), but a longer catheter insertion time (MD = 26.89, 95% CI: 5.28–48.50). For the secondary outcomes, DRA was associated with a shorter haemostasis time and reduced waveform instability, with no significant differences in first-attempt success rate or pain scores. Subgroup analysis showed a higher first-attempt success rate in patients aged ≤ 65 years (RR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.02–1.28). DRA catheterization for invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring appears to be associated with fewer complications and more stable arterial waveform acquisition than CRA catheterization, despite a longer insertion time. DRA may represent a feasible alternative in surgical and intensive care settings; however, further high-quality studies are needed to confirm its long-term safety and generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10877-026-01451-x.

Introduction

Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring is a cornerstone of critical care medicine, anesthesia, and cardiovascular interventional practice, enabling accurate assessment of circulatory status, guiding fluid resuscitation, and optimizing pharmacological management [1]. Due to its superficial location, well-defined anatomy, and appropriate vessel diameter, the radial artery remains the most commonly used access site in clinical practice. Conventional radial artery (CRA) catheterization is typically performed proximal to the wrist crease [2]. However, numerous studies have reported procedure-related complications, including haematoma, oozing, catheter occlusion, and distal ischemia [3]. These risks are particularly pronounced in patients requiring repeated punctures or prolonged monitoring, in whom vasospasm and endothelial injury may lead to unstable hemodynamic waveforms and, in severe cases, impaired hand perfusion [4, 5]. Moreover, in patients with a history of transradial interventional procedures or pre-existing forearm vascular injury, the availability of the CRA access site may be limited, thereby increasing technical difficulty and the risk of complications [6].

The distal radial artery (DRA) puncture technique has emerged as an alternative approach to arterial catheterization and has attracted growing clinical interest [7]. The DRA is located within the anatomical snuffbox, distal to the radial styloid process and proximal to the formation of the palmar arterial arches [8]. Anatomically, this region is characterized by a superficial arterial course, palpable pulsation, minimal surrounding soft tissue, and extensive anastomotic connections with the superficial palmar arch, deep palmar arch, and dorsal carpal branches [9]. These features provide robust collateral circulation, which may substantially reduce the risk of distal ischemia and impaired cutaneous perfusion. Additionally, the DRA puncture site lies on the dorsoradial aspect of the hand, spatially distinct from the conventional CRA access site, facilitating hemostasis and early functional recovery [10]. Importantly, this approach preserves proximal forearm vessels, thereby maintaining options for future vascular access. Despite increasing reports describing the use of DRA for cardiovascular interventions and invasive arterial pressure monitoring, its comparative safety and efficacy relative to CRA remain incompletely defined, and high-quality systematic evidence is lacking [11, 12].

Accordingly, this study aimed to compare DRA and CRA catheterization in terms of procedural safety and efficacy using a meta-analysis. The findings may inform clinical decision-making, guide the selection of optimal arterial access sites, and ultimately improve patient safety and comfort.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. The protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The registration number is CRD420251275432. Further revisions and additions will be tracked in PROSPERO.

Literature search

We conducted a systematic literature search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, the VIP Database, and SinoMed, covering the period from database inception to 30 December 2025. The search strategy incorporated keywords and subject terms including “distal radial artery”, “conventional radial artery”, “catheter insertion”, “invasive blood pressure monitoring”, “blood pressure”, and “haemodynamics.” A detailed description of the search strategy is presented in Table S1. All retrieved studies were rigorously evaluated for eligibility. Titles and abstracts were screened initially, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles. In addition, reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually examined to identify additional eligible publications.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) Study population: adult patients (≥ 18 years); (2) Intervention: invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring via DRA catheterization; (3) Control: catheterization of the CRA; (4) Outcomes: reporting at least one safety outcome (e.g., procedure-related complications) or efficacy outcome (e.g., catheterization success rate or haemodynamic monitoring performance); (5) Population characteristics: no restrictions on sex, country, or ethnicity; and (6) Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Studies were excluded during the eligibility assessment if they met any of the following criteria: (1) failure to directly compare DRA catheterization with CRA catheterization; or (2) publication types including reviews, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, duplicate publications, or studies with inaccessible full texts or insufficient data.

Outcomes

In this study, efficacy was defined in terms of procedural performance during arterial catheterization, including catheter insertion time, first-attempt success rate, catheter blockage and arterial waveform quality (e.g., waveform instability). Safety was defined as catheterization-related adverse events, including haematoma, bleeding, as well as procedure-related outcomes (haemostasis time and pain).

A summary of reported complications is provided in Table S2. Haematoma was reported in nine studies, while bleeding and catheter blockage were each reported in four studies. Other complications were reported in only one or two studies with very low event rates and were therefore not suitable for quantitative synthesis. Accordingly, the most frequently reported events-haematoma, bleeding, and catheter blockage-were selected for quantitative analysis.

The primary outcomes were haematoma, bleeding, catheter insertion time, and catheter blockage. Outcome definitions were based on those used in the original studies. In general, haematoma was defined as localized swelling at the puncture site after catheterization, sometimes accompanied by skin discoloration or elevation, bleeding as persistent oozing after catheterization. Catheter insertion time was defined as the interval from skin disinfection to confirmation of a valid arterial waveform. Catheter blockage was defined as the inability to obtain or maintain a usable arterial waveform (e.g., dampened or absent waveform).

Secondary outcomes included haemostasis time, waveform instability, first-attempt success rate, and pain. Haemostasis time was defined as the duration required to achieve haemostasis after catheter removal, while waveform instability referred to impaired waveform quality, such as damping or distortion.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (H.T. and S.Y.) independently extracted data, with any disagreements resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (W.X.). In accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, all records identified at each stage of the review process were systematically assessed before data extraction. Initially, titles and abstracts were screened to evaluate relevance, and clearly irrelevant studies were excluded. Full texts of the remaining articles were then retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were included for data extraction. Extracted information included author names, article titles, year of publication, country or region, participant characteristics (sample size, mean age, and sex), intervention details, catheterization method, and reported outcome measures.

Quality assessment

Two authors (H.T. and S.Y.) independently assessed the risk of bias according to the recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration. The assessment covered five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was classified into three categories: low risk, some concerns, and high risk. We used the RoB 2 tool to evaluate the quality of the included studies.

In addition to the assessment of individual studies, the overall certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, which classifies evidence quality into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low. This approach considers multiple domains, including study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias, magnitude of effect, dose-response relationship, and potential residual confounding. Although evidence derived from randomized controlled trials is initially rated as high quality, it may be downgraded to moderate, low, or very low certainty based on five factors: limitations in study design or execution, indirectness of evidence, unexplained heterogeneity or inconsistency, imprecision of effect estimates, and a high likelihood of publication bias [13].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 and Stata version 18.0. RevMan 5.4 was used for data synthesis and meta-analysis, with pooled results presented as forest plots. For continuous outcomes, effect estimates were expressed as mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD), while relative risk (RR) was calculated for dichotomous outcomes, each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ² test and quantified with the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low (I² < 50% and P > 0.10), and a random-effects model was used for substantial heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50% and P ≤ 0.10), after excluding clinically significant sources of heterogeneity. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness of pooled estimates by sequentially excluding each study and recalculating the overall effect. Publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots in RevMan 5.4 and statistically with Egger’s linear regression test in Stata 18.0, with P < 0.05 indicating significant asymmetry. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to clinically relevant variables, including age and the use of ultrasound guidance during the initial puncture attempt.

Results

Study selection

A total of 2,813 records were identified through database searches, of which 2,441 remained after duplicate removal. During the title and abstract screening stage, 2,416 records were excluded based on the eligibility criteria, including irrelevant topics, review articles, conference proceedings, studies involving pediatric populations, or those failing to report primary outcome measures. Of the remaining records, 10 articles were unavailable in full text, and three studies were non-randomized controlled trials. Ultimately, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the selected studies

A total of 12 studies were included, comprising 10 conducted in China (83.3%), one in Japan [14] (8.3%), and one in Italy [15] (8.3%), with a total sample size of 1,790 participants. The study populations consisted of intensive care unit (ICU) patients (n = 7, 58.3%) [14–20] and surgical anesthesia patients (n = 5, 41.7%) [21–25]. The mean age of participants ranged from approximately 38 to 74 years. Male participants accounted for 986 cases (55.1%), indicating a relatively balanced sex distribution.

Regarding interventions, all experimental groups underwent DRA puncture, most commonly at the anatomical snuffbox, whereas control groups received CRA catheterization. Two studies [23, 25] used ultrasound guidance for the initial puncture (17%), three [15, 19, 20] applied ultrasound only after a failed initial attempt (25%), and seven relied solely on anatomical landmarks (58%). Risk of bias assessment

A systematic risk-of-bias assessment was conducted for the 12 included studies using the RoB 2 tool (Fig. 2). Regarding random sequence generation, one study [22] did not explicitly report the method and was therefore classified as having some concerns about bias in this domain. Three studies [19, 20, 25] employed computer-generated random numbers, three studies [17, 21, 24] used random number tables, and two studies [15, 18] applied simple randomization. In contrast, three studies [14, 16, 23] used quasi-random allocation methods (e.g., admission order or odd–even numbering) and were classified as having a high risk of selection bias. For deviations from intended interventions, four studies [15, 19, 20, 25] explicitly used sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes, with one study [20] further employing independent dual management of envelopes to minimize investigator bias. However, several studies raised concerns due to the lack of blinding and potential operator-dependent performance bias, particularly in smaller studies. Overall, most studies were classified as having a low risk or some concerns, but three studies [14, 16, 23] were classified as having a high risk of bias.

Safety of DRA

Meta-analysis of nine studies showed a reduced incidence of puncture-site haematoma in the DRA group (RR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.24–0.73) (Fig. S1). Likewise, four studies reported puncture-site bleeding, with a lower incidence observed in the DRA group (RR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.11–0.59) (Fig. S2). Haemostasis time was reported in six studies and was shorter in the DRA group (MD = -2.52, 95% CI: -3.78 to -1.26) (Fig. S3). Pain scores were evaluated in three studies and showed no difference between the DRA and CRA groups (SMD = -0.85, 95% CI: -2.73-1.03) (Fig. S4). All pooled safety outcomes are summarized in Table 2.

Efficacy of DRA

Five studies evaluated catheter insertion time between DRA and CRA cannulation. Pooled analysis showed that catheter insertion time was significantly longer in the DRA group compared with the CRA group (MD = 26.89, 95% CI: 5.28–48.50) (Fig. S5). Nine studies assessed the first-attempt success rate and showed no difference between the DRA and CRA groups (RR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.94–1.15, P = 0.40) (Fig. S6). Four studies assessed catheter blockage, which occurred less frequently in the DRA group than in the CRA group (RR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.81) (Fig. S7). Three studies reported waveform instability during hemodynamic monitoring, and meta-analysis demonstrated a lower incidence in the DRA group compared with the CRA group (RR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.20–0.63) (Fig. S8). All pooled efficacy-related outcomes are summarized in Table 2.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis stratified by age indicated that DRA was associated with a lower first-attempt success rate in patients aged > 65 years (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.74–0.94), but a higher success rate in those aged ≤ 65 years (RR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.02–1.28). A statistically significant subgroup effect was observed (P = 0.0002) (Fig. S9).

Subgroup analysis stratified by ultrasound use at the initial attempt showed that the pooled effect was not statistically significant in the ultrasound-guided subgroup (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.87–1.47, P = 0.37; I² = 73%). Likewise, no statistically significant effect was observed in the subgroup without ultrasound guidance (RR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.91–1.16, P = 0.67; I² = 78%). No significant subgroup effect was detected (P = 0.53) (Fig. S10).

Publication bias

Publication bias for haematoma was assessed. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. S11) suggested slight asymmetry. Egger’s regression test showed no statistically significant small-study effects (P = 0.409), indicating no clear evidence of publication bias. However, given the limited number of included studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Detailed results of the Stata analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S12).

Quality of evidence

According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was assessed for eight outcomes. One outcome was rated as high certainty, five as moderate certainty, and two as low certainty.

High-certainty evidence was observed for haematoma, supported by consistent results, low risk of bias, adequate sample size, and narrow confidence intervals. Catheter insertion time, haemostasis time, waveform instability, catheter blockage, and bleeding were rated as moderate certainty, primarily due to heterogeneity related to age differences across studies or imprecision arising from limited sample sizes and wide confidence intervals. The certainty of evidence for first-attempt success rate and pain at the puncture site was low, mainly due to substantial heterogeneity. Detailed GRADE assessments are presented in Table 3.

Discussion

Compared with CRA puncture, DRA catheterization may better preserve peripheral circulation by maintaining the proximal radial artery trunk and collateral flow via the superficial and deep palmar arches and the ulnar artery, thereby providing a stable anatomical basis for fingertip perfusion [26, 27]. Even in the presence of vasospasm or transient flow restriction, the impact on hand perfusion remains minimal, helping to preserve tissue oxygenation [28]. In contrast, CRA-related complications such as haematoma, prolonged compression, and vasospasm may impair distal perfusion and microcirculatory flow. The DRA puncture site, with thinner surrounding soft tissue and shorter compression duration, reduces sustained vascular compression and secondary microcirculatory compromise [29]. This may partly explain the lower incidence of haematoma, oozing, and catheter blockage observed in this study. However, most current evidence relies on clinical complications or vascular patency as safety endpoints, with limited direct assessment of peripheral and microcirculatory perfusion. Notably, the Allen test mainly reflects macrocirculatory compensation and lacks sensitivity to subtle microcirculatory changes in the fingertip [30]. Future studies incorporating techniques such as laser Doppler flowmetry, near-infrared spectroscopy, skin temperature gradients, and capillary refill time may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of microcirculatory protection. In terms of effectiveness, catheter insertion time was longer in the DRA group than in the CRA group, likely due to the smaller vessel diameter and the higher technical demands of puncturing the distal radial artery. Puncture at the anatomical snuffbox also requires more precise positioning and needle control, increasing procedural complexity, particularly for less experienced operators. Despite this, waveform stability was superior in the DRA group, possibly due to reduced wrist movement interference, more stable catheter fixation in superficial tissues, and proximity to the superficial palmar arch, which ensures more consistent local blood flow and clearer pressure waveforms for monitoring [31, 32].

A study by Achim A. suggests that ultrasound-guided puncture may be superior to the traditional palpation technique, as it enables accurate identification of the artery and real-time assessment of vessel diameter and depth, which may facilitate vascular access [33]. However, in contrast to these expectations, our meta-analysis did not demonstrate a significant effect of ultrasound guidance on the first-attempt success rate. This finding may be explained by two main factors. First, first-attempt success may be influenced more by vascular characteristics and operator technique than by visualization alone [34]. Although ultrasound provides real-time imaging of the artery, it does not alter intrinsic factors such as vessel diameter, depth, or arterial condition, which are critical determinants of successful cannulation [35]. This interpretation is supported by the age-stratified subgroup analysis, which showed that DRA was associated with a lower first-attempt success rate in patients aged > 65 years but a higher rate in those aged ≤ 65 years, with a statistically significant subgroup effect. These findings suggest that patient-related vascular characteristics may play a more important role in determining first-attempt success than procedural adjuncts such as ultrasound guidance. Second, methodological factors may also have contributed to the lack of a detectable effect. The limited number of studies using primary ultrasound guidance, together with substantial heterogeneity among studies, may have reduced the statistical power to detect a significant difference, potentially obscuring any true benefit of ultrasound guidance [36]. Future studies should use standardized ultrasound protocols, with clearer reporting of operator training and experience, and focus on patient groups with more challenging vascular conditions to better define the role of ultrasound guidance in first-attempt success.

Variations in procedural techniques among the included studies may represent an important source of heterogeneity for certain outcomes. Reported puncture angles ranged from approximately 15° to 45°, which may influence needle entry stability and, consequently, first-attempt success rates and pain scores. Additionally, only a small proportion of studies routinely employed ultrasound guidance for DRA puncture, which may partly account for inconsistencies between catheter insertion time and first-attempt success [37]. Differences were also observed in catheterization techniques (modified Seldinger technique versus direct puncture) and catheter sizes (20G versus 22G). These technical factors are particularly relevant in elderly patients with compromised vascular conditions and may attenuate the procedural efficiency advantages of DRA [38]. Furthermore, incomplete methodological reporting in some studies-particularly regarding operator experience-may have introduced bias, as variations in operator experience could have influenced procedural outcomes and contributed to the observed heterogeneity.

Although this study demonstrates the short-term safety and monitoring stability of DRA catheterization, current evidence largely focuses on peri-procedural outcomes, and its long-term vascular effects remain inadequately characterized [39]. The long-term patency of the artery following DRA catheterization is uncertain, particularly given its relatively small lumen, and there is limited follow-up regarding the risk of late stenosis or occlusion associated with repeated puncture or prolonged catheterization [40, 41]. Additionally, the impact of DRA on future vascular access-including repeat invasive arterial monitoring, transradial interventions, dialysis access creation, and arterial sampling-remains unclear, and its long-term sustainability has yet to be established [37]. Preservation of the proximal radial artery may be especially important in patients who are likely to require repeated transradial procedures, coronary artery bypass grafting, or future arteriovenous fistula creation [39]. In this context, distal radial access may be advantageous, as it preserves proximal arterial integrity and maintains options for subsequent vascular access. However, proximal radial access remains more appropriate in certain situations, particularly when a larger vessel is required, when the distal radial pulse is weak or impalpable, or when procedural complexity necessitates a more stable and conventional route [42]. Accordingly, the choice of access should be individualized based on patient characteristics and procedural considerations.

This study has several limitations. First, although 12 randomized controlled trials (n = 1,790) were included, most were single-center studies conducted in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed for some outcomes, and the overall certainty of evidence was low. Additionally, although hemodynamic monitoring accuracy was prespecified in the original PROSPERO protocol, it could not be directly evaluated because of limited and non-standardized reporting across the included studies. As a result, waveform instability was used as a surrogate indicator of monitoring performance. However, this outcome reflects signal quality rather than actual measurement accuracy and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, most included studies focused on short-term complications, with limited data on long-term vascular patency and delayed complications, highlighting the need for further research.

Conclusion

DRA catheterization for invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring appears safer than conventional radial artery catheterization, with fewer complications and more stable arterial waveform acquisition, despite a longer insertion time. DRA appears feasible in surgical and intensive care settings; however, further high-quality studies are needed to confirm its long-term safety and generalizability. Overall, DRA catheterization has the potential to become a standardized approach for invasive arterial monitoring.
