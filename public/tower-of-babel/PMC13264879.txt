Whole truth: resuscitating trauma patients with whole blood
Authors: Jennifer M Gurney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHemorrhage remains the leading cause of death from potentially survivable injuries in both military and civilian trauma. Whole blood (WB) has repeatedly proven to be the most physiologically coherent resuscitation strategy for life-threatening hemorrhage, yet modern transfusion systems have historically prioritized inventory efficiency and component fractionation over biologic fidelity. This syllabus synthesizes contemporary military and civilian evidence for WB resuscitation, with emphasis on fresh WB (FWB) obtained through walking blood banks (WBBs) and low-titer group O WB (LTOWB), the predominant WB product currently used in civilian trauma. The historical ‘back to the future’ arc of transfusion practice, pathophysiology of trauma-induced blood failure, and the most current transfusion outcome data are summarized.The future of both military and civilian trauma resuscitation should adopt a layered approach to transfusion—this supports the best outcomes for trauma patients and also supports the trauma system. Precision resuscitation frameworks that assess clinical outcomes for type and timing of blood transfusion have the potential to support advanced resuscitation practices. Although the academic literature lacks publications on comparisons of LTOWB and FWB, the military is actively performing analysis to determine if there are outcome benefits for FWB and revisiting the concept for fresher (or younger) whole blood to the sickest bleeding patients—acknowledging that such an approach challenges traditional blood-banking priorities and imposes logistical constraints on blood banking and trauma systems. The balance between what is best for the patient and what is feasible in the trauma system must be taken into consideration. WB (and especially younger LTOWB and FWB) has the potential to reduce total product exposure, simplify logistics, and save lives. Future directions should include prehospital WB expansion, civilian WBB surge capability, quality metrics for blood, and pragmatic trials.Keywords: Blood, blood transfusion

Introduction

Severe hemorrhage kills quickly. For trauma surgeons, the core problem is not abstract: minutes of uncontrolled bleeding translate into oxygen debt, worsening coagulopathy, and rapid progression to irreversible shock. In both military and civilian trauma, two time-sensitive interventions are paramount: hemorrhage control and appropriate resuscitation. Military experience and civilian trials have shaped today’s damage control resuscitation (DCR) paradigm, emphasizing early blood products, avoidance of crystalloid, prehospital hemorrhage control when appropriate, rapid control of bleeding, and early hemostatic therapy.1,4

Yet, even as modern protocols have moved toward balanced component therapy (eg, 1:1:1 plasma:platelets:RBC), the physiologic target has always been to recapitulate whole blood (WB). WB is not simply a nostalgic return to prior practice; it is a coherent biologic therapy for trauma-induced blood failure. WB delivers oxygen-carrying capacity, coagulation factors, platelets, buffers, and oncotic support in native proportions with a single line item and a single set of compatibility decisions.

The re-emergence of WB in the past decade has been driven by two converging forces1: military experience demonstrating feasibility and survival benefit with warm fresh WB (FWB) from5 walking blood banks (WBB) in combat casualties and2 civilian observational evidence and guidelines supporting low-titer group O WB (LTOWB) as a safe and effective emergency release blood product. Specifically, LTOWB is group O WB that has been screened for low levels of anti-A and anti-B antibodies. This minimizes the risk of transfusion reactions, allowing it to be used as a universal blood product for patients of any blood type in a critical emergency.6 But adoption remains uneven, and debates persist—often centered on inventory concerns rather than clinical physiology, especially in the prehospital environments and in ‘blood deserts’.

This is written for general surgeons and trauma surgeons who need a pragmatic, evidence-based synthesis and a forward-looking proposal that moves beyond ‘should we use WB?’ to ‘how do we use the right WB for the right patient at the right time?’

It is not debated: hemorrhage is the leading cause of death from survivable injuries in both military and civilian trauma settings. Although the principles of DCR, including early blood product administration, have advanced survival, WB transfusion has emerged as an optimal resuscitation strategy for hemorrhagic shock. Historically, WB was the cornerstone of transfusion medicine; however, the shift to component therapy during the latter half of the 20th century created logistical and clinical inefficiencies (online supplemental material 1).

Definitions and operational concepts

Whole blood

WB is blood that has not been fractionated into components. It contains RBCs, plasma with clotting factors, and platelets in native proportions. WB may be warm and fresh (from WBB) or cold-stored (and Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved and titer-tested (LTOWB)); it may be type-specific or group O; it may be leukoreduced or non-leukoreduced depending on system and indication.

Fresh whole blood

FWB refers to WB collected and transfused shortly (usually within minutes to hours) after donation obtained through a WBB. When transfused warm shortly after collection, it is often described as warm FWB (WFWB).7 8 Although most of the military’s experience in Iraq and Afghanistan, 2002–2020, has been with type-specific, crossmatched WFWB, the use of warm fresh LTOWB is also an important capability. This requires the forward identification of prescreened, low-titer donors who can be called on when needed. FWB retains high platelet function and strong coagulation factor activity compared with stored products. In military doctrine, FWB is used as an emergency capability when stored products are not ideal for the clinical scenario (clinically insufficient), unavailable, or logistically constrained.

Walking blood bank

A WBB is a planned, trained, rehearsed process to obtain FWB from screened donors in an emergency situation.7 9 Donors are ideally prescreened; in-theater or on-scene screening typically includes donor history questionnaire and rapid testing prior to donation. WBB-derived FWB is not FDA-approved for civilian product in the USA. WBBs are commonly implemented in deployed military environments and are central to surge capability planning. FWB from a WBB can either be type specific or low-titer group O.

Low-titer group O blood

LTOWB is WB collected from group O donors with ‘low’ anti-A and anti-B antibody titers. It is the main WB product currently used in civilian trauma resuscitation because it can be used as an emergency release, near-universal product in urgent hemorrhage. However, ‘low titer’ is not standardized, and thresholds vary by institution and testing method (often ranging from ~1:50 to ~1:256). Variability in titer thresholds remains a barrier to consistent practice and comparative outcomes studies.10 11

Cold-stored LTOWB

Cold-stored LTOWB refers to LTOWB stored at 2–6°C (typically for up to 21–35 days depending on preservative solutions and local policies). This is the product used in civilian settings in the USA (whereas the military uses LTOWB from WBBs as well). Cold storage improves bacterial safety and logistics but decreases platelet function over time; nonetheless, WB still provides simultaneous oxygen carrying capacity and plasma factors with simplified delivery.

Why the military uses FWB, but not civilians

FWB is not approved by the FDA or accredited by the Association for the Advancement of Blood & Biotherapies for routine civilian use primarily due to the increased risks associated with its collection and transfusion outside of rigorously controlled environments. The central issue is the inability to perform the same level of comprehensive infectious disease screening on FWB that is mandatory for the standard blood supply.

Standard banked blood components undergo extensive testing for a panel of transfusion-transmissible diseases like HIV and hepatitis before they are released. FWB, collected in emergencies from a WBB, is transfused too quickly for these tests to be completed, heightening the risk of disease transmission. Although military use is justified in deployed settings, secondary to the challenges with blood supply and because the FDA does not have purview of deployed medical care, the wide availability of safer, fully tested component therapies in civilian hospitals currently makes the risks of FWB unacceptable to the FDA for routine practice.

Blood and war: lest we forget

Transfusion practices from the battlefield—for over a century—have helped shape current civilian practices. The current best practice management can be summarized by the following statement by Dr LB Robertson 1916 in the British Medical Journal: ‘The indications for blood transfusion are based on the fact that transfused blood is the best substitute for blood lost in acute hemorrhage’. An understanding of transfusion practices in the enduring military conflicts demonstrates not only how transfusion management has come full circle but elucidates how war is often the source of advancement and innovation in medicine. This progress is fueled by the intense and concentrated patient experience combined with the resultant national impetus to improve battlefield outcomes; it yields great medical and surgical achievements.1,3

The ‘truth’ of history: a century-long cycle of adoption, abandonment, and rediscovery

War and major military conflicts have repeatedly driven advances in medical care, largely due to the concentrated experience with severely injured patients and a national imperative to reduce battlefield mortality. Across every major US conflict since the Civil War, one lesson has emerged consistently: hemorrhage kills early, and blood transfusion saves lives. Yet, despite being learned and relearned over more than a century, this lesson has been repeatedly forgotten in peacetime.

By the end of World War I, blood transfusion was widely practiced at American Expeditionary Force hospitals with academic surgical leadership, with reports of up to 50 units transfused daily. It is estimated that more than 10 000 WB transfusions were performed in 1918 alone, though formal outcome data were limited. The British military and Royal Army Medical Corps embraced transfusion more aggressively than their US counterparts, entering World War II with an established transfusion capability, whereas the USA did not (figure 1).1 3

Early walking blood bank—the Spanish Civil War. Dr. Norman Bethune’s mobile transfusion service demonstrated the feasibility and lifesaving impact of rapid, fresh whole blood transfusion near the point of injury. (Image: Library and Archives Canada - no restrictions on use)

Between World War I and World War II, the value of blood far forward became increasingly apparent. During the Spanish Civil War, Canadian surgeon Norman Bethune developed a centralized blood transfusion service with mobile blood banks capable of delivering WB directly to the front lines. His system enabled hundreds of liters of blood to be transfused monthly, with up to 100 transfusions per day during periods of intense fighting. Bethune demonstrated several enduring principles that remain relevant today: transfusion must occur early; blood must move toward the wounded patient and organized systems can reliably deliver lifesaving transfusion in austere environments.12 In effect, prehospital blood transfusion was born decades before modern trauma systems existed, and the concept of the ‘golden hour of opportunity’ was recognized well before World War II.13

When the USA entered World War II, blood banking was again not incorporated into initial medical planning, despite lessons from World War I. Early in the war, most transfusions consisted of FWB collected at field blood banks by ‘bleeding teams’ (figure 2), frequently using group O blood for all recipients. By 1944, the scale of combat overwhelmed local collection capacity, prompting systematic logistical support for blood programs. In March 1945 alone, up to 62 000 units of WB were shipped from the USA to the European and Pacific theaters. By the end of World War II, the military, working closely with the American Red Cross, had built an unprecedented national blood program, collecting more than 13 million pints of WB. As after World War I, however, this integrated military-civilian blood capability was not sustained in peacetime.

The Korean War (1950–1953) again highlighted the consequences of failing to preserve transfusion lessons. As a consequence, thousands of US servicemembers bled to death on the battlefield—the risk of not preserving wartime lessons learned is paid for in the blood of those who exsanguinate on the battlefield before battlefield transfusion capabilities are established. The USA entered the conflict without an operational military blood program; the first blood shipments did not arrive until more than 2 months into combat operations. Long supply lines and the short shelf life of WB led to significant wastage, driving innovation in plastic blood bags and extended red blood cell storage. These advances improved logistics but subtly shifted focus away from the clinical superiority of WB toward storage efficiency and transportability.13 14

During the Vietnam War, WB and WBBs remained integral to combat casualty care. Surgical hospitals routinely activated WBBs when large-volume transfusion was anticipated, and between 1965 and 1971 approximately 1.3 million units of WB were shipped to Vietnam, with roughly 600 000 units transfused to more than 330 000 US and allied casualties. Despite this extensive experience, the late Vietnam era marked a turning point away from WB—which was not driven by clinical evidence, but by economic, logistical, and perceived safety advantages of blood fractionation. Component therapy allowed longer storage, broader distribution, and treatment of multiple patients from a single donation, but no prospective studies compared component therapy with WB at the time of adoption. Additionally, trauma resuscitation increasingly emphasized crystalloid resuscitation during the Vietnam War. This practice was adopted in civilian care, and hemorrhage was initially treated with crystalloid resuscitation. The teaching was that crystalloid should be administered at a volume equal to three times the estimated blood loss, and this was subsequently incorporated into Advanced Trauma Life Support and became standard practice for three decades. This approach, heavily influenced by physiology studies in animal models (rather than clinical outcomes), became widely accepted and institutionalized in civilian trauma education and practice. Over time, it became clear that this strategy was associated with predictable complications—including dilutional coagulopathy, tissue edema, pulmonary failure, abdominal compartment syndrome, and multiple organ dysfunction. It would take decades, and another battlefield, to reassess these assumptions.

The conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan forced a re-examination of hemorrhage resuscitation under conditions of extreme injury severity, limited resources, and long evacuation times. Data from these conflicts demonstrated that early hemostatic resuscitation, particularly with WB and WB-equivalent strategies, was associated with improved survival (figure 3). The military re-adopted warm FWB through WBBs in 2002 and, in 2017, reintroduced LTOWB to simplify resuscitation and extend WB capability far forward into the prehospital space, where type-specific transfusion is too risky. These experiences directly influenced modern DCR and catalyzed renewed civilian interest in WB.

Across more than a century of conflict, the battlefield has repeatedly taught the same lesson: patients bleed WB, and survival improves when WB is returned early. Each departure from this principle has been followed by rediscovery—often at great human cost. The contemporary resurgence of WB in both military and civilian trauma is not a novel innovation but rather a return to a durable, evidence-supported truth reinforced by history.

The whole truth: physiology of WB compared with component therapy

Blood failure: physiology dictates therapy

Blood failure describes the physiologic consequences of uncontrolled hemorrhage. Severe bleeding results in a quantitative loss of oxygen-carrying capacity and a qualitative failure of hemostasis, leading to rapid accumulation of oxygen debt, cellular hypoxia, metabolic acidosis, and progressive coagulopathy. Within minutes of hemorrhage-induced hypoperfusion, trauma-induced endotheliopathy develops, characterized by glycocalyx shedding, activation of fibrinolysis, dysregulated coagulation, and immune dysfunction. Clinically, we see this as ‘the lethal triad’—and although it is much more physiologically complicated than that, blood failure will result in death if it is not treated effectively and rapidly. Blood failure is not the failure of a single blood component; it is the failure of the blood organ system. As such, treatment must replace what is lost: WB—at the same time, rapidly achieving hemorrhage control and restoring physiology (online supplemental material 2).

Physiologic advantages of WB

WB most closely replicates the composition of blood lost during hemorrhage. It delivers red blood cells, plasma, platelets, and coagulation factors in native proportions, providing integrated oxygen delivery, hemostasis, and volume expansion in a single product.

Key physiologic advantages include:

Optimized composition: WB has a hematocrit of ~35–38% and retains approximately 80–85% of predonation coagulation factor activity, compared with ~60% in reconstituted component therapy.

WB versus component therapy: clinical context

Component therapy remains the most commonly transfused strategy in both civilian and military practice because it optimizes storage conditions, inventory flexibility, and targeted replacement (eg, RBCs for anemia, plasma for factor deficiency, platelets for thrombocytopenia). These advantages are logistical—not physiologic. In non-trauma settings, this makes sense. However, in the bleeding trauma patient, or any patient who is rapidly bleeding, the best therapy is to replace what they are losing: WB.15 16

In contrast, WB delivers all necessary elements of resuscitation in a smaller volume, with fewer additives, and with superior early coagulation profiles. Compared with balanced component resuscitation, WB provides higher hematocrit, higher platelet counts, more intact coagulation factors, and less citrate exposure—all these parameters are particularly relevant in the actively bleeding patient.

Simplicity and logistics: why LTOWB matters

LTOWB can be safely transfused to patients of any ABO type, providing a universal, immediately available resuscitation product. This simplicity is a critical advantage in the prehospital setting, during early emergency department resuscitation, and in mass casualty or resource-constrained environments—in these scenarios, balanced component therapy is less streamlined and logistically feasible, as is doing a type-specific walking blood (in military settings).10 11

Blood typing should still be initiated early after hospital arrival. Once hemorrhage control is achieved, the patient is stabilized, or LTOWB supply is limited, transfusion may transition to type-specific, cross-matched component therapy. WB is therefore best viewed not as a replacement for component therapy but as the optimal initial therapy for life-threatening hemorrhage.

Five lessons learned: 25 years of recent US military battlefield transfusion

Enduring lessons from military blood transfusion

Lesson 1: timing is decisive

Timely transfusion, ideally within 30–40 min, is the single most important determinant of survival for casualties with exsanguinating hemorrhage. A sentinel article in the Journal of American Medical Association in 2017 demonstrated that casualties who received transfusion within 36 min of injury had significantly higher survival rates compared with those who received delayed transfusion. Every minute of delay increased the risk of death from hemorrhage, with the ‘golden half-hour’ proving decisive. The study confirmed what forward surgeons had long observed anecdotally: blood must be delivered at or near the point of injury, not hours later at a rearward surgical facility where hemorrhage control is attained. In Large Scale Combat Operations (LSCO), where evacuation may be delayed for hours due to distance, air denial, or enemy action, survival hinges on the ability to transfuse far forward. When it comes to timely transfusion, minutes matter.17

Lesson 2: what is transfused matters

Multiple studies showed that FWB, obtained through WBB, provides superior outcomes to CT alone. FWB from outperforms CT by restoring oxygen delivery and clotting function in a single product. Component blood products are valuable, especially when able to be delivered in a balanced ratio to support ‘hemostatic resuscitation’, but component products are difficult to deliver in the ratio of WB. WB is ‘naturally premixed’ component therapy… and, it is what the casualty is bleeding. Of note, in the studies cited, FWB is added to a CT resuscitation.9

Blood supplied by the US Department of Defense’s Armed Services Blood Program (ASBP) supplied blood products are the ones that are immediately available for transfusion. And, since minutes matter, getting any blood product on board will enable the survival of bleeding casualties. When FWB augments the resuscitation, survival is improved. LTOWB is ASBP-supplied and is logistically much easier than a balanced CT resuscitation at the provider level given that it is one unit of blood to transfuse compared with three units of component products. The logistical ease and feasibility is from the medical provider perspective. However, when considering the ‘trauma system’, LTOWB has constraints in that it has a shelf life of either 21 days or 35 days (depending on preservative), whereas component products have a much longer shelf life (RBC 42 days and frozen plasma can be kept frozen for over a year). This underscores the need for a resilient approach to blood transfusion on the battlefield; there is not one solution, but multiple, and they need to overlap. Additionally, organizing a WBB may not be tactically feasible in certain high-threat environments. So, although FWB from WBB unquestionably improves survival when compared with CT, there must be a balanced and layered approach for hemorrhaging casualties.

The first study demonstrating that ‘what is transfused matters’ and that FWB was superior to component therapy was in 2009.9 Over 350 combat casualties analyzed, those who received FWB had a higher survival. In 2022 a study with over 1100 combat casualties demonstrated a fivefold increase in survival in combat casualties who received FWB.7 Unquestionably, FWB added to the resuscitation strategy decreases mortality. This could be because it has a better complement of clotting factors and a better capability to deliver oxygen as well as decreased preservatives compared with component therapy (figure 4). The physiological superiority of WB is intuitive: it replaces what is lost—WB—not blood fractionated into components. Component therapy is far superior to clear fluids, is still used widely around the world, and will likely be a substantial part of blood support for LSCO; if used in balanced ratios, it can have similar outcomes to LTOWB. However, component therapy requires multiple products with different storage requirements, limited shelf lives, and more preservatives.

Lesson 3: LTOWB as the universal product

The third lesson of Global War on Terror (GWOT) is the rediscovery and validation of LTOWB as a universal battlefield transfusion product, meaning it could be given to a casualty with any blood type. LTOWB is defined as group O WB with anti-A and anti-B titers <1:256, making it safe for transfusion to all recipients, regardless of blood type. This is why prescreening with ‘titer testing’ is essential prior to deployment. It functions as a ‘universal donor’ product, reducing the logistical and clinical complexity of blood supply in austere environments.

Although LTOWB had been recognized since World War I as a lifesaving option, ASBP (and civilian donor centers) stopped producing the product after the Vietnam War, and it was not reintroduced into the US military system until 2016. Bureaucratic inertia, safety concerns, and reliance on civilian transfusion practices (which were able to capitalize on selling blood component therapy at a great profit compared with one unit of WB) all contributed to LTOWB disappearing from the blood armamentarium until it was rapidly reintroduced by the ASBP in 2016. This suspension of the use of LTOWB on the battlefield underscores a recurring theme: the US military repeatedly relearns lessons at the cost of Service Members’ lives. Since its reintroduction, LTOWB rapidly became the preferred transfusion product in military settings and is now increasingly adopted in civilian trauma centers. Its use simplifies logistics, maximizes survivability, and aligns with DCR principles. For future LSCO, LTOWB must be readily available at all roles of battlefield of care.

Following the military’s lead, civilian trauma centers started using LTOWB to resuscitate hemorrhaging patients. It should be noted that at the provider level, and in both military and civilian trauma scenarios, LTOWB decreases the logistical burdens of transfusion. Additionally, in busy civilian trauma systems, if the LTOWB is not used on trauma patients it gets broken down into blood components, which have longer shelf lives, and recirculated back to busy civilian hospitals, which decreases wastage. This parallel does not hold true in the military system where blood will get shipped to the AOR, and if it is not used, it is wasted. Given the shorter shelf life of LTOWB compared with CT, there are serious ‘system logistics’ that must be considered. So, although LTOWB is much more logistically feasible at the clinical (tactical) level, operationally it is challenging to maintain stocks in dispersed operational environments.

The clinical evidence supporting LTOWB continues to evolve, creating a complex picture for decision-makers. The largest study to date, a multicenter analysis by Hazelton and colleagues sponsored by the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, demonstrated a significant survival benefit for trauma patients who received prehospital LTOWB compared with those who only received component therapy. This18 landmark finding provides strong support for the use of LTOWB in early hemorrhage control. However, it is important to note that other, smaller single-center analyses have not consistently found a survival benefit, leading to ongoing debate within the trauma community. This discrepancy highlights the need for continued research to refine which patient populations benefit most and under what logistical circumstances.

Lesson 4: futility is a myth – there is no transfusion cut point

Even massively transfused patients survived when blood was available. Combat casualties who have received >100 units of blood have a >70% chance of survival. Arbitrary rationing of blood transfusion and creating thresholds risks preventable deaths.

The 2023 Journal of Trauma article—Finding the bleeding edge: 24-hour mortality by unit of blood product transfused in combat casualties from 2002 to 2020—demonstrated that there was no clear point at which transfusion became futile. Survivability19 persisted even in extreme resuscitations, provided blood was available. This finding has operational consequences: commanders and clinicians must resist rationing based on arbitrary cutoffs. In LSCO, where mass casualties will strain resources, the temptation to impose ‘futility thresholds’ will be strong. Evidence from GWOT warns against this. The better strategy is layered redundancy of resuscitation capabilities and prepositioned capacity to absorb surges and improve battlefield survivability. Although LSCO will impose other challenges that might require rationing all resources, the fact that there is no futility cutpoint for amount of transfusion should be a planning factor for all roles of care.

Lesson 5: WBBs, as part of a layered approach, get blood where needed

WBBs are a critical capability that allows FWB to be provided at the right place and time for injured servicemembers. Initially a last-resort measure, this unique capability must be retained, expanded, and normalized so that it becomes a standing capability, not a last resort given the survival benefit or warm, FWB.FWB from WBBs can be blood-type-specific or can be LTOWB. FWB from WBB can be introduced into a resuscitation when the team is running short on ASBP supplied products or if the casualty’s clinical situation warrants the use of FWB. This requires having standardized WBB training across the entire military and WBB programs with robust supplies such as single blood bags and easy to use point of care testing capabilities.

Introducing FWB into the resuscitation should not be reserved only for when there is a shortage of ASBP-supplied blood, but it should be part of a layered approach for saving lives of casualties at risk of death from hemorrhage. It is a clinical game-changer in that it can rapidly restore physiology and provide hemostasis. Additionally, in LSCO, where evacuation timelines are extended and logistics contested, reliance on shipments of ASBP manufactured LTOWB and component therapy is a recipe for preventable mortality. A layered approach, anchored by LTOWB and supported by CT, WBBs, drone delivery, Theater Gateways, and theater WB collection must be the path forward.

WB for non-trauma hemorrhage

Although trauma remains the primary focus, non-trauma populations benefit from LTOWB resuscitation:

Obstetric hemorrhage: postpartum hemorrhage accounts for significant maternal mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings. Studies, such as those by Nowadly et al, demonstrate that WB effectively reduces transfusion requirements and improves hemostatic parameters in obstetric emergencies, particularly in cases of uterine atony and invasive placentation. The safety profile of LTOWB in obstetrics is supported by Smith et al, who found no major adverse outcomes in non-trauma WB transfusions, including obstetric cases.

The impetus for civilian walking blood banks

In addition to what is being transfused, the timing of transfusion is critical. Many advances in trauma and critical care emphasize that the more expeditious the intervention, the more efficacious the therapy. Well-understood, early interventions that result in improved treatment effects when administered in a timely fashion are antibiotics in sepsis, time to neurosurgical intervention for extra-axial traumatic hemorrhage, time to tourniquet placement for extremity hemorrhage, time to intervention for stroke therapy, time to revascularization in myocardial infarctions, and time to hemostatic transfusion in hemorrhage. Time is critical, and although it seems obvious that hemorrhaging patients die quickly, urgency for transfusion is not always appreciated; minutes matter when it comes to hemorrhagic shock.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, blood shortages became a reality in urban centers. However, blood shortages, and especially the lack of platelets, are common, especially in rural areas. In the case of a prolonged pandemic, there could be an even greater situation of degraded stocks and disrupted supply chains, and blood banks could be depleted without ability for resupply. The blood supply is susceptible to not meeting needs either because of decreased supply or increased demand given that blood banks are optimized for maximum efficiency. efficiency is desirable during routine practice, it is not optimized for a prolonged surge, and there are multiple points of potential failures. The possibility for cascading effects of supply chain failures during a crisis could be catastrophic and result in large numbers of death for otherwise survivable injuries or medical diseases.

WBBs are viable options to build redundancy and resilience in the blood supply. Utilization of the WBB and leveraging the extensive military experience with WBBs would bridge the period until the interruption of the supply chain is resolved and FDA approved products could be available. It is critical for national preparedness that national and regional WBB plans get developed. San Antonio has been able to develop a WBB with the LTOWB donors used for the well-known ‘Brothers in Arms’ Program. The creation of a civilian WBB could be lifesaving for patients if there was a MASCAL event. Currently, US hospitals and blood banks institute campaigns to collect blood on demand to meet the need for blood products during shortages by often appealing to repeat donors. This current system could be formalized into the development of WBBs available to respond during times of increased need for blood.

Summary

Early in the recent wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, WB was used in US Military operations primarily by forward-deployed teams that were equipped with a limited supply of packed red blood cells; WB transfusion during this period was driven by necessity rather than clinical indication. Over time, through both focused empiricism and investigations of comparative efficacy that demonstrated improved survival with WB, battlefield hospitals began using WB not just when components were unavailable, but because of the clinical superiority of WB. Both LTOWB and FWB from WBBs save lives and are superior to component therapy. National preparedness requires a resilient blood supply and the ability to have WBBs incorporated into contingencies. The WBBs must become a viable option when FDA approved blood products required to save lives are not immediately available. WBBs can be trained, but they need to be rehearsed as well. Having WB widely available will decrease death from potentially survivable injuries (online supplemental material 3).
