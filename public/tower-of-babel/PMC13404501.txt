Effect of music therapy dosage on social communication and emotion regulation in children with autism spectrum disorder: study protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yueying Chen, Yingxu Pan, Dushimimana Adalbert, Hai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough the positive effects of music therapy on improving social and emotional issues in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are well-established, the relationship between specific intervention doses and treatment outcomes remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol will quantitatively evaluate the impact of music therapy dosage on social communication skills and emotional regulation functions in children with ASD.Methods and analysisThis protocol is registered with PROSPERO and follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols. We will search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Medline and China National Knowledge Infrastructure for randomised controlled trials on music therapy dosage in children with ASD from database inception to November 2025. Two independent reviewers will conduct study selection, data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment. A random-effects model will be used for meta-analysis (Stata V.16.0, RevMan V.5.4) with Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation for evidence quality. Subgroup analyses and publication bias tests (funnel plots, Egger’s test) are planned.Ethics and disseminationThis research protocol is based on data from published literature and does not involve the collection of new raw data or individual patient data; therefore, no ethical approval is required. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are intended for presentation at relevant scientific conferences and will be published in a peer-reviewed academic journal.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251063906.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent qualitative deficits in social interaction and communication, often co-occurring with difficulties in emotion regulation, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities.1 The global prevalence of ASD has been increasing annually. Data indicate that the worldwide incidence of ASD has reached approximately 1%, with the incidence among children in high-income countries rising to 2.76% (equivalent to 1 in 36 children).2 3 Children with ASD often exhibit symptoms of emotional dysregulation and social interaction impairments, such as emotional outbursts, difficulties in emotion recognition and social avoidance, which significantly impact their daily functioning and quality of life.4 Music therapy, as a multisensory intervention modality, employs elements such as melody, rhythm and improvisation to promote neural plasticity. Music therapy uses melody, rhythm and improvisation to promote neural plasticity, improving social engagement, emotional recognition, self-expression and reducing anxiety-related behaviours. It is particularly beneficial for the emotional, social and cognitive development of children with autism.5 6 Substantial evidence indicates music therapy enhances autistic children’s social skills, emotional regulation and behavioural adaptation.7 The beneficial effects of music therapy on children with ASD have been widely acknowledged. It contributes to the enhancement of social skills and emotional regulation in children with ASD across varying degrees of severity, subtypes and age groups. This includes children with deficits in verbal expression, those exhibiting impairments in social interaction and individuals diagnosed with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders, such as both ASD and intellectual disability.8 Notably, the differential application of music therapy intensity exerts varying impacts on intervention outcomes.9

Music therapy has progressed from a complementary method to a standardised, science-based neuromodulation intervention for children with ASD, primarily targeting social communication and emotion regulation. However, heterogeneity in intervention parameters—such as duration, frequency and cycles—along with inconsistent outcome measures, complicates the establishment of optimal clinical protocols. Existing studies indicate that low-dose music therapy (one 30 min session per week over a 5-month period, totaling up to 20 sessions) may yield short-term effects on emotional stability in children with ASD, while high-dose intervention (three 30 min sessions per week over the same period, totaling up to 60 sessions) may more significantly improve social initiation behaviours.10 Furthermore, intervention formats of music therapy (individual or group sessions) and therapeutic settings (clinical institutions or home environments) may modulate the efficacy of treatment dosage, warranting further investigation in subsequent analyses.11

Emerging research highlights the potential of music therapy dosage in promoting core social dimensions in children with autism, such as joint attention, social initiation, communicative interaction and emotional resonance—all fundamental to building interpersonal relationships and social integration.12 13 Specifically, music therapy at tailored intensity levels has been shown to reduce lethargy and stereotyped behaviours while enhancing social engagement, emotional synchronicity and attempts at verbal communication.14 Additionally, music therapy dosage provides space to explore and redefine social intervention models and efficacy assessment standards for children with autism.15

Emotion regulation of children with autism has been explored in diverse dynamic contexts, including daily family interactions and community activities. Research indicates that emotion regulation plays an integral role in the development of social skills and the maintenance of psychological well-being.16 Music therapy helps children with autism establish connections between sensory experiences and emotional states. Through rhythmic synchronisation and melodic expression, it facilitates clear emotional articulation, challenges stereotyped repetitive behaviours and social barriers, thereby enhancing emotional stability, social engagement willingness, interpersonal skills and self-awareness—a process that ultimately contributes to the development of self-efficacy and social identity.17 18

Dosage refers to the amount of therapeutic medication required to achieve a specific physiological response.19 In the context of non-pharmacological interventions, dosage denotes the ‘total quantity’ provided and/or received during actual implementation of the intervention, quantified by structural parameters such as frequency, duration and cycle length, as well as process parameters including delivery dose and receiving dose. However, there is no unified standard for defining dosage across different studies.20 In this systematic review and meta-analysis, ‘music therapy dosage’ is operationalised as a combination of the following quantifiable intervention parameters: single-session duration (min), treatment frequency (times/week), total number of sessions (total number of sessions) and total intervention duration (number of weeks). Additionally, intensity is classified based on the frequency of interventions per unit time (eg, low intensity: ≤1 session/week; moderate intensity: 2–3 sessions/week; high intensity: ≥4 sessions/week). We will extract these dosage parameters from each included randomised controlled trial (RCT) and systematically evaluate the independent and interactive effects of each dosage component on social communication and emotional regulation outcomes in children with ASD.

The dosage of music therapy can improve the social skills and emotional regulation in children with autism, manifested as enhanced social competence and improved emotional control.21 Precise adjustment of the intervention dosage in music therapy provides a unique perspective for investigating how different music intensities affect emotional regulation in children with autism, thereby facilitating further exploration of the relationship between emotional regulation and social functioning—particularly in individuals with high-functioning autism.22 While preliminary evidence has been established regarding the impact of music therapy dosage on social competence and emotional regulation in children with ASD, there remains a lack of systematic reviews and meta-analyses to consolidate existing research. This review aims to address this gap, taking into account individual differences, music therapy modalities, treatment duration and frequency of sessions, while also considering how factors such as the child’s age, environmental context and severity of symptoms may influence therapeutic outcomes. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are expected to provide an evidence-based foundation for optimising key parameters of music therapy intensity—such as session length, frequency and treatment duration—thereby advancing the dual objectives of enhancing social competence and improving emotional regulation in children with ASD. This work seeks to offer scientific guidance for developing individualised intervention protocols in clinical practice.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was not included in this study protocol as it is a systematic review and meta-analysis of published literature, with no direct involvement of patients or the public in the design, conduct or dissemination of the research

Protocol and registration

This systematic review has been prospectively registered in the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) database. In order to uphold methodological rigour and ensure full transparency throughout the review process, we have strictly followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols statement.23 As the study involves the synthesis and analysis of previously published data without the collection of new primary data, ethical approval was not required. This methodological stance reflects our dedication to conducting a systematic review that aligns with the highest standards of methodological integrity and ethical research practice.

Data sources and search strategies

To systematically evaluate the effect of music therapy dosage on social communication and emotion regulation in children with ASD, a comprehensive literature search will be conducted across five electronic databases, namely Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, Medline and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure. The search will cover all retrievable records in each database from their respective inception dates up to November 2025, with the objective of encompassing a broad and up-to-date body of evidence. The search strategies were meticulously developed by research team members YC and DA, incorporating all relevant terms and their variants in English: “children with ASD”,“autistic children”, “social communication ability”, “social interaction”, “social skills”,“pragmatic communication”, “emotion regulation”, “emotion modulation”, “affect regulation”, “emotion dysregulation”, “emotional control”, “affective control”, “arousal regulation”, “music therapy” and “music therapy dosage”. These keywords have been adapted for corresponding searches in Chinese-language databases to ensure comprehensive global literature coverage. Please refer to table 1 for details. All retrieved records will be exported to EndNote V.X9 for centralised management. Duplicate entries will be identified and removed via a two-step procedure: automated deduplication using the software’s built-in algorithm, followed by independent manual verification by two reviewers (YC and DA) against author names, titles and publication dates. This sequential process ensures the integrity of the final literature set for subsequent review.

Eligibility criteria

Type of study

This systematic review and meta-analysis will exclusively focus on RCTs, thereby providing a robust methodological framework to evaluate the effects of music therapy dosage on social competence and emotion regulation in children with autism.

Types of participants

The study employs all-encompassing inclusion criteria, admitting children with ASD under 18 years old regardless of demographic or clinical characteristics, thereby facilitating a broad and representative population analysis.

Interventions and comparators

The comparative analysis will distinguish between control groups—participants receiving no music therapy—and experimental groups receiving music therapy with predefined dosage parameters, for example, session frequency, duration total treatment hours.

Types of outcome measures

The primary outcome measure of interest is social competence, while the secondary outcome measure centres on the improvement or adjustment of emotion regulation capabilities. For assessing social communication skills, we employ all validated standardised assessment tools, including but not limited to the Social Communication Questionnaire, Social Response Scale-2, Verlan Adaptation Behavior Scale and Social Skills Improvement System. For evaluating emotional regulation abilities, we use all validated standardised tools such as the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, Emotional Regulation Checklist and Difficulties in Emotional Regulation Scale. Any measurement tool published in peer-reviewed journals with comprehensive validity and reliability reports is eligible for inclusion in this review.

Language restriction

Following the inclusive principle, no restrictions on language or publication date will be applied, ensuring the assembly of a diverse and comprehensive body of research findings to facilitate subsequent analysis.

Study selection and data extraction

The study selection and data extraction processes will be rigorously conducted by two independent reviewers, YC and DA, to ensure the integrity and accuracy of the systematic review. Initially, both reviewers will perform a preliminary assessment of all retrieved articles by meticulously examining their titles, abstracts and full texts. This assessment will be guided by pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine the eligibility of each study for inclusion in the review (figure 1). In instances where discrepancies or disagreements arise between the two reviewers, resolution will be sought through discussion. If a consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer, YP, will be consulted to make a final determination.

Following the confirmation of study eligibility, a detailed data extraction phase will commence. This stage involves the systematic collection of key study characteristics and outcomes. Specifically, the following information will be extracted from each included study: author names, publication year, source of publication, country of origin, total sample size, age and gender distribution of participants, dropout rates, type of music therapy intervention, single-session duration (min), treatment frequency (times/week), total number of sessions (total number of sessions) and total intervention duration (number of weeks) and intensity. This comprehensive data extraction is essential to ensure that the review can thoroughly elucidate the impact of music therapy dosage on social communication and emotion regulation in children with ASD, thereby facilitating a nuanced analysis and interpretation of the findings.

Methodological quality assessment

To ensure the integrity and validity of our systematic review, the methodological quality of each included study will undergo rigorous assessment. This evaluation will be conducted independently by two researchers, YC and DA, using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool V.2.24 This tool comprises five domains: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain will be judged as ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’, leading to an overall risk-of-bias judgement for each study result.

The specific criteria to be assessed include: random sequence generation to evaluate selection bias, allocation concealment to further assess selection bias, blinding of participants and personnel to address performance bias, blinding of outcome assessment to mitigate detection bias, incomplete outcome data to determine attrition bias, selective outcome reporting to identify reporting bias and any other potential sources of bias. These assessments will form the basis for a comprehensive and detailed documentation and analysis of the risk of bias profile for each individual study.

The evaluation process will be performed using Review Manager V.5.4 software, which allows for both graphical representation and detailed tabulation of the risk of bias assessments. This approach not only ensures a transparent and systematic examination of the methodological quality of each study but also aids in identifying those with the highest levels of rigour and reliability. In the event of disagreements between the two primary assessors, resolutions will be sought through discussion. If a consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer, YP, will be consulted to make a final determination. This multi-tiered review process underscores our commitment to upholding the highest standards of methodological scrutiny and ensures the robustness of the conclusions drawn in the systematic review.

Grading of evidence

According to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach,25 the quality of evidence for each specific outcome will be categorised. This methodology classifies evidence quality into four grades: high, moderate, low and very low, providing a transparent and structured process for systematically assessing the certainty of the evidence.

Statistical analysis

Addressing missing data

For studies with incomplete or missing data (eg, failure to report mean, SD, sample size or effect size), we first contact the corresponding author by email to request the original data. If no response is received within 2 weeks or the data cannot be obtained, we estimate missing values using the methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook: (1) for reports of median, IQR or range, we employ the formula proposed by Wan et al26 to calculate mean and SD; (2) for reports of t-values, F-values, p values and corresponding sample sizes, we use the method recommended by Altman and Bland27 to back calculate the effect size and its SE and (3) if none of the above information is available, the study is included only in a systematic review for qualitative description and not in quantitative meta-analysis synthesis.

Analysing treatment effects

Meta-analysis will be performed using RevMan V.5.4 software, developed by the Cochrane Collaboration. For continuous outcome variables, if the included studies employed the same measurement tools, the weighted mean difference will be used for pooling; if different measurement tools were used, the standardised mean difference will be applied. Results will be presented with 95% CIs in forest plots. Statistical significance is pre-defined as a p value <0.05. Heterogeneity among studies will be quantified using the I² statistic and the Cochrane Q test (χ² test). The I² value will be used to assess the magnitude of heterogeneity. Significant heterogeneity is considered present if the I² value exceeds 50% and the accompanying p value is <0.10. Depending on the I² value, the analysis will be conducted using either a fixed-effects model or a random-effects model. If substantial heterogeneity is observed (I² >50%), subgroup analyses will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis will be conducted to test the robustness of the meta-analysis results. Each individual study will be sequentially excluded, and the meta-analysis will be re-performed to observe whether the overall effect size changes significantly.

Subgroup analysis

Variables such as age, intervention dosage, type of music therapy and intervention format have been empirically demonstrated or theoretically proposed to modulate behavioural outcomes and neurodevelopmental interventions in children with ASD.8 10 11 However, few studies have systematically examined the specific moderating roles of these variables within the dose-response relationship of music therapy. In light of the aforementioned definition of dosage, we will conduct subgroup analyses based on these variables to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. The planned subgroup analyses include: participant characteristics (age range and gender distribution); intervention dosage parameters: single-session duration (min), treatment frequency (times/week), total number of sessions (total number of sessions) and total intervention duration (number of weeks) and intervention intensity.

Evaluating publication bias

When the number of included studies for a given outcome is at least 10, publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and quantified with Egger’s linear regression test. If the test suggests the potential presence of publication bias (Egger’s test p value <0.10), this will be explicitly acknowledged and discussed in the manuscript.

If quantitative synthesis is deemed inappropriate, the results of each individual study will be summarised and discussed, taking into account potential biases and the significance of the findings. Following the integration of results, interventions demonstrating efficacy will be highlighted, providing valuable insights for future research endeavours and informed decision-making.

Discussion

Music therapy has positive effects on improving social communication and emotion regulation in children with ASD, and these effects vary with different intervention dosage levels. Miranda’s research indicates that music interventions at different frequencies can enhance emotion regulation and the quality of social interaction, but high-frequency, long-term music therapy may lead to more sustained and stable improvements in the long run.28 Although prior research supports these benefits, few studies have focused on analysing differences in treatment dosage. There is a lack of a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis in the academic field specifically integrating evidence on the impact of music therapy dosage on the social abilities and emotion regulation of children with autism.

Our proposed systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address this gap in the literature by providing a rigorous examination of the effects of music therapy dosage on social competence and emotion regulation in children with ASD, thereby enabling a more in-depth analysis of the specific outcomes associated with different dosage levels. We acknowledge that the impact of music therapy may vary depending on differing frequencies and intervention model. Our analysis will account for factors such as the type of music therapy intervention, specific dosage parameters and methodological approaches. This methodology ensures our capacity to uncover findings that can offer valuable insights for enhancing social communication and emotion regulation in children with ASD.

By systematically synthesising and analysing existing research, this study is expected to be a pioneering work in this field, establishing a foundation for future academic inquiry and clinical practice. Our findings are anticipated not only to confirm the therapeutic value of music therapy but also to underscore its potential as a precise and effective intervention for enhancing social competence and emotion regulation in children with autism. Through this comprehensive review and evaluation, we aim to deliver an up-to-date synthesis of evidence that could significantly advance the understanding of music therapy dosage as a therapeutic modality. This can enhance the precision of its clinical application and the standardisation of intervention protocols for children with ASD, thereby optimising the stability of therapeutic outcomes and the degree of individualised adaptation in promoting social communication and emotion regulation.

This study has several limitations. Currently, the operational definition of music therapy dosage varies considerably across primary studies, which may limit the precision of our dose-response analysis. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on published studies, which consequently excludes grey literature and unpublished works that could potentially offer valuable insights into the effects of music therapy on social competence and emotion regulation in children with ASD. While this approach offers practical benefits in terms of the comprehensiveness of data retrieval and the efficiency of the screening process, it may introduce publication bias, as studies with positive results are more likely to be published than those with negative or inconclusive findings.

Furthermore, at the current stage, research on the mechanisms through which music therapy dosage affects the social and emotional functioning of children with autism remains in its preliminary exploratory phase. The relevant empirical literature is not only scarce in quantity but also requires significant improvement in the scientific rigour and methodological robustness of its study designs. Although some existing studies have yielded exploratory findings of referential value, the persuasive power and consistency of the current evidence are still insufficient to clearly define the relationship patterns between therapeutic dosage and intervention outcomes. This research landscape clearly indicates an urgent need within the field for RCTs with more standardised and rigorous methodology, capable of precisely distinguishing the effects of different dosage levels. Consequently, future related research requires systematic planning and practical advancement to address the core gaps in the current evidence base, thereby further deepening the academic understanding of implementing precision interventions through music therapy for this population.
