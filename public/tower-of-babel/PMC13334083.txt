Commercial prices relative to Medicare were lower under an employer-based reference pricing program
Authors: Roslyn C Murray, Christopher M Whaley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReference-based pricing has shown promise in reducing hospital prices and spending for state employee health plans. However, its impact among other commercially insured populations is not well understood.MethodsWe examined price and spending differences under an alternative health plan's reference pricing program for self-funded employers relative to prices paid by employers and commercial payers from Round 5.1 of the Employer Hospital Price Transparency Study. The program we studied paid hospitals at approximately 140% of Medicare payments.ResultsWe found that the prices paid to hospitals were significantly lower than prices paid by other commercial payers. As a percentage of Medicare, the reference pricing program paid, on average, 118% points less for inpatient services and 162% points less for outpatient services. Relative to the prices paid by other commercial payers, we estimated that employers saved $417 million under the reference-based pricing program.ConclusionThese savings represent 56.5% of what they would have spent on hospital care had they paid similar rates as other commercial payers.

Introduction

The United States leads the world in per-capita health spending, largely due to high and variable provider prices that are not consistently linked to quality.1,2 Relative to both peer nations and public insurers, prices paid by the approximately 160 million Americans with employer-sponsored insurance are particularly high.3,4 Collectively, employer-sponsored insurance accounts for $1.5 trillion in health care spending annually.5 Health insurance premiums and deductibles have grown far faster than both inflation and worker wages. The annual family premium has reached $26 000 and the average deductible for individuals purchasing insurance through the exchanges is nearly $3000.6 As an increasing share of worker compensation is funneled toward health care, workers have less in take-home pay.

One of the major drivers of rising health care spending for employer-sponsored insurance is hospital prices, which have increasingly diverged from the prices paid by public payers. Research has found that these prices are not associated with higher quality care or costs, but hospital market power.7-10 At the same time, concentrated insurance markets often fail to counterbalance provider bargaining leverage. Dominant insurers have limited incentives to negotiate low prices—-only the most favorable prices relative to other insurers. High hospital prices can work in their favor, creating barriers for new insurers who struggle to create competitive, affordable provider networks.11,12

Self-funded plans, which represent about 60% of those with employer-sponsored insurance, tend to pay higher prices than fully insured plans due to the diminished incentive from third-party administrators in negotiating lower payments.13,14 Therefore, new alternative health plans have emerged that base prices on a percentage of Medicare payments (ie, “reference-based pricing”) rather than on negotiated discounts off hospital charges, as traditional insurers do. While reference-based pricing has shown promise in reducing hospital prices and spending for state employee health plans, its impact among other commercially insured populations is not well understood.15 In particular, it is unclear if smaller employers and purchasers that lack the size and geographic concentration of state employee plans have the same ability to implement reference-based pricing. To inform these purchasing decisions, this study compares prices under a reference-based pricing program offered through an alternative health plan to self-funded employers to prices paid by other employers and commercial payers.

Methods

Reference-based pricing intervention

We evaluated a reference-based pricing plan developed and deployed by Imagine360, a service-provider for group health plans that offers alternative pricing models. The plan covers approximately 300 000 lives (average member age 40; 43% female) across all 50 states and Washington, D.C. Employer clients average 650 members and span multiple sectors, including senior living, auto and equipment dealerships, trucking, and hospitality.

Under the model, there are no negotiated contracts. Instead, when an enrolled patient receives care at a hospital, the hospital is paid through the greater of either the cost of care plus 12% or the Medicare payment plus 20%. This structure results in hospital payments that are, on average, approximately 140% of Medicare. Most providers accept these payment terms. If the patient receives a balance bill, Imagine360 manages the balance billing process and resolves the dispute. Patients typically do not pay more than the amount listed on their explanation of benefits. About 2% of patients received a balance bill in 2025, down from 15% in 2014.

Study data and outcomes

We obtained 225 618 reference-based pricing de-identified claims from 194 392 patients in 2024. These data were provided at the hospital-level and included inpatient and outpatient facility allowed amounts (ie, the sum of negotiated payments across all claims within each hospital), along with the equivalent Medicare allowed amounts and service volumes. Medicare allowed amounts were also reported in aggregate at the hospital level as the total amount Medicare would have paid for the same mix of services. Average prices per admission or procedure were then calculated using these hospital-level totals and service counts. “Relative prices” were calculated by dividing commercial payments per service by Medicare payments. We also used data from Round 5.1 of the Employer Hospital Price Transparency Study, which compiles claims data from self-funded employers, health plans, and state all-payer claims databases across the United States and covers about 85% of short-term acute care and critical access hospitals.

Our study's main variables included average inpatient and outpatient facility prices, Medicare payments, and prices paid as a percentage of Medicare payments (ie, “relative prices”). Because our data are from one point in time, the results are descriptive. Data were at the hospital level and initially included 4013 short-term acute care and critical access hospitals. Maryland hospitals were excluded due to its unique all-payer hospital payment system. We excluded hospitals that did not have data available in Round 5.1 of the Employer Hospital Price Transparency study. Our final sample included 188 657 claims at 2684 hospitals.

Analysis

For our analysis, we first compared relative prices between the reference-based pricing initiative and prices paid by other commercial payers from Round 5.1 of the Employer Hospital Price Transparency study (ie, “counterfactual prices”) at the state level. To calculate state-level relative prices under reference pricing and the counterfactual, we calculated a weighted average using inpatient and outpatient service volumes.

Next, we estimated how much employers saved from reference-based pricing by comparing actual spending with estimates of what spending would have been if the employers had paid hospitals at counterfactual prices. At the hospital level, we calculated the average price paid per inpatient admission and outpatient procedure by dividing the allowed amount by the number of admissions and procedures performed. We calculated the average Medicare payment paid per admission and procedure using the same approach. To estimate the average counterfactual price, we applied the relative price ratios from the Employer Price Transparency Study to Medicare payments per admission or procedure. We then calculated the difference between the counterfactual and reference-based pricing payments, and multiplied that difference by the number of admissions or procedures to determine the total spending differences.

Limitations

Our analysis has several limitations. First, our analysis is descriptive and does not draw any causal conclusions about the impact of the reference-based pricing model. Second, our study is cross-sectional, examining price differences in one year, and does not capture trends or changes in prices and spending over time. Third, our analysis does not account for the costs of administering the program. As a result, savings may be over- or underestimated depending on the administrative costs associated with self-funded employer plans. Finally, because data are from different years, we assumed that commercial insurance prices relative to Medicare payments in 2022 were similar in 2024. Given that hospital prices paid by employer-sponsored insurance have increasingly diverged from Medicare payments over time, our assumption likely yields a conservative estimate.16

Discussion

We found that prices under reference-based pricing for self-funded purchasers are 57% lower than prices paid by other commercial payers. Importantly, these price differences are observed among the same sets of hospitals, rather than being driven by variations in patient hospital choice or network breadth. Given the scale of inpatient and outpatient hospital services, overall savings translate to approximately 24% of total medical spending.5 Of note, reducing broader US health spending by 24% would be approximately equivalent to moving US per-capita health care spending to the second-highest country, Switzerland.17

To the best of our knowledge, our study provides the first analysis of reference-based pricing for self-funded employers that are not state purchasers. Montana and Oregon state employee health plans implemented Medicare-based reference pricing in 2016 and 2019, respectively. Audits of Montana's program found that it saved the state $15.9 million annually.18 An evaluation of Oregon's program—-which limited hospital prices to 200% of Medicare—-found that it reduced spending by approximately $50 million annually, equivalent to 4% of total plan spending.15 Our estimates of potential savings from the employer-based reference pricing initiative are much larger in magnitude because the model covers significantly more lives and pays lower prices, on average.

Although most of these reference-based pricing initiatives have used Traditional Medicare payments as the benchmark, we are agnostic on whether the Medicare program has established the “right” price for each service and market. Medicare payments are explicitly based on hospitals’ own reported costs and are designed based on hospital-provided chargemaster and cost-to-charge ratios. Further, they are designed to, in aggregate, provide for a break-even plus 1% operating margin for efficient hospitals.19 While other reference prices may be available, such as commercial or Medicaid payments, they often lack the transparency and availability of Medicare payments. Further, existing reference-based pricing programs do not pay for member services using Medicare reimbursement rates. Rather, these initiatives use the Medicare payment framework as a chassis on which to base payments or payment ceilings. Such use of “yardstick” payment models are common when the underlying costs of providing a good or service are either unobservable to regulators and purchasers or highly manipulatable.20 Reference-based pricing can simplify price negotiations, allowing purchasers to bargain over a scale of Medicare payments (eg, approximately 140% in the program evaluated in this study, approximately 235% in Montana, and no more than 200% in Oregon), rather than the price for each service individually.

As noted previously, most hospitals accept the reference-based price as payment in full and very few cases result in a balance bill that is resolved by the alternative health plan. Therefore, these hospitals—-which also receive inpatient and outpatient prices that are 118% and 162% points higher from other payers—-have demonstrated a willingness to accept prices at 140% of Medicare. However, the feasibility of wider adoption is unclear. While the model does not require contract negotiation, it is unclear if hospitals would be willing to accept rates closer to 140% of Medicare for their broader commercial patients. Medicare prices are designed for efficient hospitals to generate a 1% margin on patient care and research suggests that larger hospitals can break even with commercial prices set at 135% of Medicare payments, allowing them to cover their costs at 140% of Medicare and generate a small margin on patient care.21 But whether rates paid at approximately 140% of Medicare is an appropriate benchmark across all markets remains uncertain, particularly in light of substantial variation in commercial payment levels across hospitals, markets, and states.22

While employers and other purchasers may be concerned about “cost-shifting” (ie, hospitals compensating for lower prices under reference-based pricing through raising prices for their broader commercially insured population), there is little evidence that cost-shifting occurs on a large or widespread scale.23,24 Further, in Oregon, there was no evidence that prices increased for the non-state employee population at the targeted hospitals in the first few years of reference-based pricing.15 Research in Oregon similarly found limited evidence of adverse effects on access to care or patient experience.25,26 However, the present analysis does not examine potential impacts on access to care or clinical outcomes.

Although employers have attempted numerous other policies and programs—-including patient-focused price transparency tools, “consumerism” in the model of high-deductible plans, and narrow and tiered networks—-to reduce prices, none of these models have had similar success.27-32 Some programs, such as rewards programs that pay patients to price shop, likely cost more to administer and run than they generate in savings.33 Also of limited success are anti-trust-focused policies that aim to inject competition into consolidated health care markets that drive higher prices.34 Consolidation continues apace through hospital mergers, hospital-physician vertical integration, and newer forms of consolidation driven by private equity and insurer-led “payvider” organizations.35

The results of this study raise critical questions about the role of employers and other health care purchasers, as health expenses are borne not only by employers but also come directly from worker wages and other benefits.36 The Department of Labor's guidelines state that purchasers and employers that operate self-funded plans have a fiduciary responsibility to ensure that plan dollars are most efficiently used in the best interest of plan participants.37 Thus, if some employers are able to obtain discounts of 57%, do other employers and purchasers now have a fiduciary responsibility to attempt to obtain similar discounts? Further, these models often exist on top of an existing insurance model (eg, a third-party administrator [TPA] model). Self-funded employers rely on brokers, consultants, and/or TPAs to design and construct networks. However, unsatisfied with the prices within these networks, some employers and purchasers have elected to work with other organizations to obtain better prices, such as through reference-based pricing. Thus, another question raised is concerning the value of insurers and networks when their negotiated prices are supplanted by much lower reference-based rates.

Conclusion

Employers play a critical role in financing the US health care system and providing insurance to over 160 million Americans. Yet, this market faces financial strain due to rising prices, particularly among hospitals. Existing efforts to reduce spending have largely been unsuccessful, as have broader policy solutions. The wide degree of variation of US hospital prices that are driven by consolidation activities and not linked to quality or outcomes has led many policy experts to propose reference-based pricing. We find that these efforts may generate substantial savings.
