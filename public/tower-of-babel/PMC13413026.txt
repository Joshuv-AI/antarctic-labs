The effect of pH stability and aqueous solubility on the oral bioavailability of a novel antileishmanial agent
Authors: Maria Rincon Nigro, Tolulope Adebusuyi, Manvir Kaur, Huan Xie, Jing Ma, Esther Olaleye, Dong Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOJT007 is a novel drug class with potent antiproliferative effects against Leishmania Major. The drug is amphoteric with a key basic amine pKa of 5.8 and acid liable. Pre-systemic metabolism in gastric acidic compartment is suspected as a major factor for its low oral bioavailability. To validate this biopharmaceutical assumption, we evaluated in vitro stability and solubility profiles of OJT007 in various buffers (pH 1.2–7). We further conducted a pharmacokinetic drug-drug interaction study where OJT007 was orally administered alone or following pre-treatment of rabeprazole, a prescription proton pump inhibitor that reduces stomach acid using rat as an animal model. The in vitro degradation of OJT007 was rapid at pH 1.2–3 with less than 50% drug remaining within 30 min. Solubility of OJT007 was also pH-dependent with a solubility of 31.3 μg/mL at pH 1.2 and practically insoluble at pH above 6. Interestingly in the in vivo studies, there were approximately 40% reduction in the mean maximum plasma drug concentration (Cmax) and the mean area under the plasma concentration versus time curve (AUC0−∞) in the rat group pre-treated with rabeprazole as compared to OJT007 alone. As opposed to our initially hypothesized, OJT007 degradation under gastric acidic pH was not a major factor controlling oral bioavailability of the drug. Instead, low solubility at a higher pH might have contributed to the observed low bioavailability. The findings illustrate that optimizing oral bioavailability for OJT007 requires formulation strategies that simultaneously address solubility at intestinal pH and stability in the stomach.Keywords: pH-dependent solubility, Oral bioavailability, Pharmacokinetics, Antileishmanial, LC-MS/MS, Methionine aminopeptidase inhibitor

Introduction

Two major factors that dominate pre-systemic drug absorption and metabolism are solubility and stability of a drug in the GI tract. The gastrointestinal pH is a crucial factor that affects drugs’ stability and solubility (DePestel et al., 2004). For a drug to be absorbed, it must first dissolve in GI fluids, survive the chemical environment of the GI lumen, and traverse the intestinal epithelium (Food, gastrointestinal pH, and models of oral drug absorption; Oral Drug Absorption: Prediction and Assessment). Oral bioavailability (F) is thus a product of three sequential fractions: F = Fa × Fg × Fh, representing the fraction absorbed across the intestinal mucosa, the fraction escaping gut-wall metabolism, and the fraction escaping hepatic first-pass metabolism, respectively (Savjani et al., 2012).

During pre-clinical drug development, solubility and stability of a drug may not always go hand-in-hand, because both factors are pH-dependent for many drugs. The gastrointestinal tract presents a pH gradient spanning nearly seven log units from the fasting stomach pH 1–3 to the drug absorption site of small intestine pH 5–7.5. For a weakly acidic drug with pKa near the intestinal pH of 6, the drug is predominantly unionized in the stomach but partially ionized in small intestine. (Zhou et al., 2005, Psachoulias et al., 2011). On the other hand, drugs containing hydrolysis-susceptible functional groups such as esters, lactams, or Schiff base imines may degrade at low gastric pH before they can be absorbed, reducing the effective dose that reaches the intestine (Pilbrant and Cederberg, 1985, McConnell et al., 2010). The clinical management of this problem is commonly dependent on formulation optimization. One example is an anti-HIV drug didanosine (Videx), whose acid lability necessitated buffered or enteric-coated formulations; critically, didanosine retains adequate solubility at higher pH, making pH-elevation strategies viable (DePestel et al., 2004, Velasque et al., 2007).

OJT007 is a methionine aminopeptidase inhibitor with potent activity against L. major (Rodriguez et al., 2020). It is an amphoteric Schiff base compound bearing a basic amine with a pKa of 5.8, a weakly acidic phenol (pKa 8.7), and a second basic amine with an extremely low conjugate acid pKa (approximately −2.2). Previous studies from our group have found that OJT007 was acid liable and rapidly glucuronidated following oral administration (Rincon Nigro et al., 2022). However, it was unclear whether and to what extent OJT007 degradation under the gastric acid pH would contribute to its low oral bioavailability. Thus, in this study we assessed in vitro pH-dependent solubility and stability profiles of OJT007 over pH range of 1.2–10. We further evaluated the oral bioavailability of OJT007 under an increased gastric pH using a rat model whose stomach acid secretion was inhibited by co-administering a protein pump inhibitor, rabeprazole.

Materials and methods

Materials

The OJT007 (purity > 90%) was purchased from MolPort (Riga, Latvia). Voriconazole, formic acid, LC-MS/UPLC grade methanol, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). LC-MS grade water and acetonitrile were purchased from J.T. Baker Chemical Co. (Phillipsburg, NJ, USA). The blank rat plasma was purchased from Innovative Research (Novi, MI, USA). Adult, male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Envigo RMS (Alice, TX.). All other chemicals and reagents were used as received.

LC-MS/MS method

A previously reported LC-MS/MS method was used for the quantification of OJT007 in plasma and urine in the in vivo pharmacokinetic studies (Rincon Nigro et al., 2021). Briefly, the analysis was accomplished using a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UHPLC system (Columbia, MD, USA). Voriconazole was used as an internal standard (IS). The chromatographic separation was achieved using an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) with a gradient mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and a sample injection volume of 2 μL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% v/v formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% v/v formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Gradient elution was employed with 5% to 98% B from time 0 to 1.8 min and was kept constant at 98% B for 1.7 min, then 98% B was changed to 5% B from 3.5 to 3.7 min, and kept constant at 5% from 3.7 min to 5 min. The MS/MS analysis was performed on a 4000 QTRAP® triple quadrupole mass spectrometer system equipped with a Turbo Ion Spray ion source (SCIEX, Redwood City, CA, USA). The quantification was performed using multiple reaction monitoring with the transition of m/z 325→205 for OJT007 and 350→101 for IS. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion electrospray (ESI) mode (Rincon Nigro et al., 2021). Under these conditions, OJT007 and voriconazole have retention times of 2.16 and 1.72 minutes, respectively. The assay was linear for OJT007 concentration from 5 to 1000 ng/mL and from 10 to 1000 ng/mL in plasma and urine, respectively.

Plasma sample preparation

Standard and test plasma samples were prepared by protein precipitation. Briefly, OJT007 working solutions were prepared by diluting the stock solution in 50% acetonitrile in water at final concentrations of 10, 5, 2.5, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.10, and 0.05 μg/mL. Standard plasma samples were prepared by mixing 90 μL of blank plasma with 10 μL of OJT007 working standard solution to obtain the following concentrations: 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 ng/mL. vortexed for 30 seconds. The precipitant (500 μL of acetonitrile containing the IS) was added, vortexed, and centrifuged for 15 minutes at 14,000 rpm. The supernatant was then injected for LC-MS/MS analysis. Test samples were prepared similarly with 100 μL of plasma.

UPLC quantification method development

A UPLC analytical method was developed to quantitate OJT007 in the in vitro stability and solubility studies. The UPLC method was based on previously reported LC conditions (Rincon Nigro et al., 2022). The UPLC system consisted of Waters Acquity UPLC with photodiode array detector and Empower software; column, BEH C18, (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7μm). The mobile phase consisted of 100% acetonitrile (B) and 0.1% formic acid in water (A). Gradient elution was employed with 5% to 98% B from time 0 to 2.8 min and was kept constant at 98% B for 1.2 min, then 98% B was changed to 5% B from 4 to 5.2 min, and kept constant at 5% from 5.2 min to 6 min. The drug was monitored at UV wavelength of 343 nm and injection volume was 10 μL. Standard samples of OJT007 were prepared in the mobile phase with concentrations ranging from 0.62–100 μM. The assay was reproducible with precision and accuracy within 15%.

pH solubility profiles

The solubility of OJT007 was determined under pH conditions of 1.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. Standard USP buffers were prepared and used for this experiment: 0.2 M hydrochloric acid for pH 1.2, 0.2 M acid phthalate buffer for pH 3–4, neutralized phthalate buffer for pH 5, and 0.2 M phosphate buffer for pH 6–7. Excess OJT007 was added to respective buffered systems. The mixtures were placed in a water bath at room temperature for 24 hours, shaking at 50 rpm to reach solubilization equilibration. The samples were then centrifuged and filtered using sterile 0.22 μm syringe filters. The filtrates were injected onto the UPLC system to quantify OJT007 concentrations and subsequently calculating solubility of OJT007 in various pH conditions. Each buffered cosolvent was tested in triplicate.

Solution state pH stability

The effect of varying pH conditions on the stability of the drug was evaluated by incubating OJT007 in buffers of various pH. Standard USP buffers (USP-NF) were prepared and used for this experiment: 0.2 M hydrochloric acid for pH 1.2 and 2, 0.2 M acid phthalate buffer for pH 3–4, neutralized phthalate buffer for pH 5, 0.2 M phosphate buffer for pH 6–8 and 0.2 M borate buffer for pH 9–10. Buffers spiked with OJT007 (100 μg/mL) were incubated at 37°C water bath. Aliquots of the buffer solution were collected at 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 24 hrs. The collected samples were diluted with 50% acetonitrile in water to obtain appropriate concentrations and analyzed by the UPLC.

Animal model and handling

An animal protocol used in this work was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Texas Southern University. Additionally, all animal studies were conducted in compliance with the National Institute of Health “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 8th Edition”. Experiments were carried out using adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (mean body weight 350 g). Upon arrival, the animals were housed under normal conditions, including free access to food and water and controlled room temperature environment with 12-hour light-dark cycles from 6 am to 6 pm daily. These conditions were maintained for at least one week before starting animal experiments to allow for adequate acclimatization of the animal.

OJT007 pharmacokinetic studies

We first evaluated pharmacokinetics of OJT007 following intravenous bolus administration in rats. Five rats weighing 300–350 g were cannulated through the jugular vein under anesthesia one day before the study. On the day of experiment, each rat received a 5 mg/kg intravenous dose of OJT007 cosolvent formulation (2.5 mg/mL). Serial blood samples (200 μL each) were collected from each rat before dose and at 2, 5, 15, 30, 60, 90 minutes, and 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 24 hours post-dose. After blood centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 3 minutes, the plasma supernatants were collected and immediately stored at −80°C and analyzed within 14 days using a validated LC-MS/MS method (Rowland, 1972). We also collected urine samples up to 24 hours post-dose and immediately stored at −80°C and analyzed them within 14 days.

We then evaluated the effect of gastric acid inhibition on oral bioavailability of OJT007 using a crossover study design in another group of rats. Briefly, adult male SD rats (n=4) weighing 296–338 g were cannulated through the jugular vein under anesthesia one day before the study. The rats were fasted overnight prior to and for four hours after drug administration. Water was always available to the rats. In the morning of dosing, rats were placed in metabolism cages and given a 25 mg/kg oral dose of OJT007 cosolvent formulation (2.5 mg/mL) via oral gavage. Serial blood samples (200 μL each) were collected before dose and at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 24 hours post-dose. Plasma samples were separated and stored at −80°C pending LC-MS/MS analysis of OJT007 concentrations. Urine samples were also collected up to 24 hours post-dose pending LC-MS/MS analysis of OJT007. Following a week’s washout period, the rats were fasted overnight and placed in metabolism cages in the morning of dosing. Each rat was pretreated with an intravenous bolus administration of 10 mg/kg rabeprazole (10 mg/mL dissolved in normal saline) 30 min before the oral administration of 25 mg/kg OJT007 cosolvent formulation (2.5 mg/mL). Serial blood and urine samples were collected similarly as described earlier and stored immediately under −80°C pending quantification of OJT007 using LC-MS/MS.

Finally, a third group of fasting rats were dosed with an intravenous bolus administration of 10 mg/kg rabeprazole. Under anesthesia, the abdominal cavity was opened, and the stomach was located. The antrum cardiacum and pylorus of the stomach were ligated. An incision to the stomach wall was made, and the gastric juice pH was measured with a pH meter at 0.5, 1, and 2 hours after rabeprazole administration.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Plasma OJT007 concentration versus time data was analyzed for each rat using a non-compartmental and compartmental model (Win-Nonlin v8.3, Pharsight Corp, Mountain View, CA). The area under the time curve (AUC) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. The slope of terminal phase (obtained by linear log estimation using at least three data points in the terminal elimination phase) was used to derive elimination rate constant (kel). The terminal elimination half-life (T1/2) was calculated as 0.693/kel. The systemic plasma clearance (CL) was calculated as Dose/AUC from the intravenous OJT007 dosing group.

The effect of gastric pH on oral bioavailability of OJT007 was evaluated based on the difference between the AUC of animals treated with and without rabeprazole.

Statistical analysis

Experimental values are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SigmaPlot v11.0 (La Joya, CA). Paired t-test at a 0.05 level of significance was used to determine statistical significance across treatments on exposure to pharmacokinetic parameters.

Results

pH solubility profile of OJT007

OJT007 was found to be soluble at a concentration of up to 31.1 μg/mL at a pH of 1.2. However, the drug was essentially insoluble when the pH was at or above 3 (Table 1). Under the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS), a drug is considered highly soluble if the highest clinical dose is soluble in 250 mL of aqueous media across pH 1–6.8 (Benet and Zia-Amirhosseini, 1995). Given that OJT007 solubility falls below 0.1 μg/mL at all pH values above 1.2, and assuming a dose of 25 mg/kg (~8.75 mg for a 350 g rat, extrapolated proportionally), OJT007 is classified as a BCS Class II compound: high permeability (predicted from its lipophilicity and large Vss) and low solubility at intestinal pH. We have developed a co-solvent formulation consisted of PEG 400 : Tween 80 : DMSO (57:29:14, w/w). This formulation is to be diluted with normal saline or water to a final concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, suitable for animal pharmacokinetic studies.

Pharmacokinetics of OJT007 in rats

Fig. 3 showed plasma concentration versus time profile for OJT007 following an IV administration. OJT007 displayed a bi-exponential disposition with a rapid distribution to the tissue compartment within the first 2 hours, followed by a slow elimination. Effect of co-administered rabeprazole on oral bioavailability of OJT007

We conducted in vitro studies that indicated drug instability under acidic conditions. This raised the question of whether gastric degradation could contribute to drug loss prior to absorption in intestinal mucosa. Thus, to determine the effect of gastric fluids under fasting conditions on the absorption of OJT007, the AUC of rats treated with and without rabeprazole was assessed.

Determination of gastric pH

Treatment with rabeprazole (10 mg/kg) increased the pH of the stomach fluid to 6.5–7 at 30 minutes following rabeprazole administration. The high pH of the stomach was maintained at pH 7–7.5 for at least 1 hour period. Two hours after rabeprazole administration, the stomach pH decreased to 4.5–5. The results demonstrated that treatment with rabeprazole effectively increased the gastric pH in our study rats and was consistent with previously reported data.

Pharmacokinetics of OJT007 with and without co-administered rabeprazole

Fig. 4 showed the mean+/−SD OJT007 plasma concentration versus time profile with and without rabeprazole pre-treatment. To our surprise, the area under the plasma concentration versus time curve for OJT007 was significantly larger in rats without rabeprazole pre-treatment than that of rats with rabeprazole pre-treatment. The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and the time to Cmax were also higher and longer, respectively, when OJT007 was administered alone. Table 3 summarizes the pharmacokinetic parameters obtained with and without rabeprazole pre-treatment. Following pretreatment with rabeprazole, statistically significant reductions in Cmax (P = 0.022) and AUC0−∞ (P = 0.011) of approximately 50% were observed compared to that of administering OJT007 alone. Interestingly, significant reductions in tmax (p<0.05) were also observed. Mean relative oral bioavailability of OJT007 with co-administered rabeprazole as compared with OJT007 alone was 41.5%. With rabeprazole pretreatment, absolute F% falls to approximately 9.1%. The Tmax was also significantly shorter with rabeprazole (3.50 ± 1.91 vs. 2.13 ± 0.63 hr; p < 0.05).

Discussion

The result rejected our original hypothesis that given the instability at the stomach acidic pH, OJT007 oral bioavailability would increase when stomach pH was raised by co-administering rabeprazole, a strong protein pump inhibitor (PPI). Thus, the data suggested that the mechanisms driving the observed poor oral bioavailability for OJT007 was not primarily due to the stomach acidic degradation of OJT007. With respect to the mechanism of why co-administered rabeprazole caused decrease in oral bioavailability of OJT007, we ruled out possibilities of GI motility or hepatic metabolism changes by rabeprazole. Studies have determined that rabeprazole use does not affect gastric emptying of drugs nor the metabolism mediated by CYP (Kosugi et al., 2015, Miura et al., 2006). Thus, the counterintuitive finding can best be explained through the lens of a pKa/lipophilicity framework, when gastric pH is elevated from ~2 to 6.5–7.0 by rabeprazole, the intragastric environment crosses the pKa of the basic amine (5.8). Under these conditions, a substantially greater fraction of OJT007 exists in its unionized, lipophilic form in the stomach and crucially, the solubility of the unionized form in high pH according to our in vitro studies is <0.1 μg/mL. The drug therefore precipitates in the gastric lumen before it can transit to the small intestine, drastically reducing the dissolved drug pool available for subsequent intestinal absorption (Rautio et al., 2018). High gastric pH has been shown to significantly decrease the absorption of basic drugs which have low solubility at high pH (Mitra and Kesisoglou, 2013; Faber et al., 2017). In contrast, under normal gastric conditions (pH 1–3), even though a fraction of OJT007 degrades, the remainder stays dissolved in the ionized form and is carried into the duodenum, where some fraction may dissolve transiently and be absorbed before full precipitation. This represents the central tradeoff of OJT007’s physiochemistry: gastric acid simultaneously destroys a portion of the drug and keeps the surviving fraction soluble. Eliminating the acid removes the degradation but also removes the solubility window, resulting in a net decrease in absorbed drug. Neither condition is optimal, which explains why absolute oral bioavailability remains low (~21.8% in the current study) regardless of gastric pH, further studies are required to confirm the mechanism driving the decreased exposure. Gastric pH was confirmed to have risen to 6.5–7.0 within 30 minutes of rabeprazole administration and was maintained at pH 7.0–7.5 for at least 1 hour (Section 3.4.1), ruling out insufficient acid suppression as a confounding explanation for the observed decrease in absorption. The rabeprazole data thus demonstrates that, despite significant acid degradation in the stomach, acid instability is the lesser barrier compared to pH-dependent precipitation at higher gastric pH. A high coefficient of variation was observed for Vz/F in the rabeprazole group (CV ~61%) indicates substantial inter-individual variability and statistically significant differences in Cmax and AUC0−∞ were obtained with a n = 4.

OJT007 showed bi-exponential disposition following an intravenous dose administration. Its apparent volume of distribution was greater than the total body water in rats, possibly due to drug partition into tissues (Davies and Morris, 1993). The mean systemic clearance (CL) was 2.3 L/h/kg, which accounts for about 68% hepatic blood flow (Davies and Morris, 1993). Since the systemic clearance of a drug after intravenous administration reflects hepatic elimination, we can calculate the hepatic extraction ratio by taking the ratio of systemic clearance to liver blood flow (Benet, 2010; Rowland, 1972). The extraction ratio (ER) for OJT007 is 0.69, which would make OJT007 a moderate to high extraction ratio drug (Benet and Zia-Amirhosseini, 1995). A good correlation was observed between the predicted hepatic clearance obtained from the in vitro studies (2.61 L/hr/Kg) and the observed in vivo clearance in rats. The mean percentage of the dose excreted unchanged in urine after 24 hours was 0.39%, suggesting extensive metabolism, which was consistent with our in vitro studies. (Rincon Nigro et al., 2022)

Our findings underscore a critical challenge in drug development; the complex interplay between pH-dependent solubility and stability in determining oral bioavailability (F%), which is governed by F = Fa × Fg × Fh, where Fa represents the fraction absorbed (Savjani et al., 2012; Food, gastrointestinal pH, and models of oral drug absorption). OJT007 exhibits a “pH paradox” where optimal solubility occurs under acidic conditions (31.3 μg/mL at pH 1.2) when the drug is most unstable (t1/2 < 0.5 hours), while chemical stability is achieved at neutral pH where solubility falls below detection limits (<0.1 μg/mL). This combined with the pKa of 5.8 and the drugs lipophilicity, creates a scenario where no pH condition in the GI tract simultaneously provides adequate solubility and adequate stability, contributing to low oral bioavailability (Rincon Nigro et al., 2022). This is a fundamental constraint imposed by the molecule’s physical chemistry, not by its formulation. Even if the solubility of OJT007 at intestinal pH is improved through formulation, hepatic first-pass metabolism remains a significant barrier. The IV pharmacokinetic data show a hepatic extraction ratio of 0.69 (CL = 2.28 L/h/kg against a rat hepatic blood flow of ~3.3 L/h/kg), classifying OJT007 as a moderate-to-high extraction drug. For such compounds, the hepatic availability term Fh = 1- ER = 0.31, meaning that even if 100% of the dose were absorbed across the intestinal mucosa (Fa × Fg = 1.0), systemic bioavailability would be capped at approximately 31% by hepatic extraction alone. The low oral bioavailability thus reflects the combined impact of poor intestinal absorption due to low solubility (low Fa) and extensive hepatic glucuronidation (low Fh). The near-complete absence of unchanged drug in urine (Au/Dose = 0.39%) further corroborates that systemic clearance is overwhelmingly hepatic. Any formulation strategy aimed at improving oral bioavailability must therefore address both Fa and Fh simultaneously.

The primary site of absorption for OJT007 is most likely the upper small intestine (duodenum and proximal jejunum), where the intestinal mucosa is highly permeable and absorptive surface area is greatest. As drug transits from the stomach into the duodenum, the rising pH (5–6.5) drives a rapid shift from ionized to unionized species, transiently generating permeant, lipophilic molecules at the intestinal surface. However, because this transition simultaneously reduces solubility below 0.1 μg/mL, only the fraction that remains dissolved during the brief transit through the pH gradient is available for transcellular absorption. The sigmoid concentration–time profile and Tmax of 3.5 hours observed after oral dosing are consistent with slow, dissolution-rate-limited absorption in the upper GI tract. Colonic absorption is unlikely to contribute meaningfully given the low solubility at colonic pH (6–8) and reduced absorptive area. Whether P-glycoprotein-mediated efflux at the enterocyte apical membrane further limits absorption of the unionized, lipophilic fraction remains to be determined; this is addressed as a limitation in Section 4.1 Similar challenges have been documented with marketed drugs such as ketoconazole (47–95% AUC reduction with acid suppression) (Mitra and Kesisoglou, 2013; Chin et al., 1995) and atazanavir (76% AUC decrease with omeprazole) (Le Tiec et al., 2005), while didanosine successfully overcame acid lability through buffered formulations because it maintained adequate solubility at elevated pH (DePestel et al., 2004; Velasque et al., 2007). For OJT007, traditional strategies like enteric coating would protect against degradation but exacerbate precipitation at intestinal pH, necessitating alternative approaches such as prodrug development or nanosuspension formulations that address both stability and solubility across the GI pH range (Cole et al., 2008; Serajuddin, 2007; Vo et al., 2013; Kazi et al., 2019; Rautio et al., 2018). These results emphasize that optimizing oral bioavailability requires balancing multiple competing physicochemical properties, and that understanding the solubility-stability relationship early in development is essential for rational formulation design and determining whether a promising compound can be successfully developed as an oral therapeutic.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgement. First, the small sample size in the oral crossover study (n = 4) limits statistical power. A minimum of n = 6 animals per group is generally recommended for pharmacokinetic crossover studies, and a formal power analysis was not performed a priori. This limitation is acknowledged in accordance with animal welfare considerations and resource constraints. Second, this study was conducted exclusively in male Sprague-Dawley rats under fasting conditions. Gastric pH, transit time, bile secretion, and metabolic enzyme activity may differ in fed animals, female animals, or in humans, limiting direct clinical extrapolation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that OJT007 faces a critical “pH solubility-stability paradox” that affect its oral bioavailability. This inverse relationship between stability and solubility creates competing biopharmaceutical constraints that contributes to the observed oral bioavailability. Co-administration of rabeprazole decreased OJT007 bioavailability by approximately 40–50%, confirming that reduced solubility at higher pH represents a more profound barrier to absorption than acid instability of the drug. This work emphasizes that optimizing oral bioavailability requires a holistic understanding of the interplay between pH-dependent solubility and stability across the entire gastrointestinal tract. These findings underscore a fundamental principle in drug development: successful translation of a potent drug candidate into an effective oral medicine requires early identification and resolution of competing physicochemical properties that collectively determine bioavailability.
