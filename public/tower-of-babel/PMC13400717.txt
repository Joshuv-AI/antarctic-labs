Ontogeny of Rat Recognition Memory, Exploratory Activity and Anxiety Measured by Novel Object Recognition Test and Open Field Test
Authors: Leah Leslie Dias, Sourav Sajan Panikkassery, Jefina Olive Ben Samuel, Uvashree Shrinivas, Sareesh Narayanan Naduvil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOntogeny refers to the gradual changes in behaviour and physiological processes that occur across the lifespan of an organism, which are associated with significant changes in sensory, emotional and social systems.PurposeIn this study, the changes that occur in object recognition memory, exploratory activity and anxiety-like behaviour were studied in 6-, 12- and 24-month-old rats as a result of their normal developmental and/or ageing process, with the help of two behavioural testing paradigms, such as the novel object recognition test (NORT) and open field test (OFT).MethodsAnimals were randomly assigned to three experimental groups: Group 1: 6-month-old—representing early adulthood, a stage characterised by peak physiological and cognitive function. Group 2: 12-month-old—representing middle-aged, a transitional phase with potential early signs of age-related changes. Group 3: 24-month-old—representing late adulthood and early ageing, a period associated with progressive neurobiological and behavioural alterations. The NORT and OFT were carried out using the Panlab-SMART automated behavioural video tracking system.ResultsResults demonstrate that recognition memory was only mildly decreased in middle-aged animals, and remained mostly intact throughout adulthood and in aged animals. Anxiety-like behaviour did not show significant differences across age groups. In contrast, rearing and exploratory activity declined with age, suggesting the behavioural changes are more reflective of exploratory and locomotor behaviours. Stretching and other risk-assessment behaviours did not change as the age progressed.ConclusionWhen combined, these results imply that exploratory and locomotor behaviours are more susceptible to ageing, compared to recognition memory and anxiety-like behaviours. The results also highlight the importance of using multiple behavioural assays to holistically unravel emotionality changes across various age groups in rats. Further research is needed to understand better the molecular, cellular and neurobiological mechanisms driving these behavioural trajectories in rats.

Introduction

Ontogeny refers to the gradual changes in behaviour and physiological processes that occur across the lifespan of an organism. During this time, sensory, emotional and social systems undergo significant changes. Behavioural responses such as recognition memory, exploratory activity and anxiety-like behaviour may vary across different stages of life due to physiological and neurobiological changes. Recognition memory, the ability to distinguish between familiar and novel stimuli, is a fundamental cognitive function essential for survival, learning and decision-making. 1 Novel object recognition (NOR) involves both familiarity detection and recollection. 2 Research reveals that the medial temporal lobe regulates this behaviour. 3 These structures undergo substantial postnatal development and reorganisation in rats, monkeys and humans, and there is ongoing controversy over the role of the hippocampal complex versus the perirhinal cortex in object recognition abilities.4, 5 In rodents, recognition memory is frequently assessed using the NOR task, which relies on their natural tendency to explore novel objects more than familiar ones. 6 This paradigm provides a non-invasive method for evaluating cognitive function and memory retention across developmental stages in rodents. 7 It is argued that the ontogeny of recognition memory follows a distinct trajectory, with immature memory capabilities in young rodents that progressively improve as they reach adulthood. 6 These behavioural changes are thought to be closely associated with the maturation of key neural structures, particu larly the hippocampus, perirhinal cortex and prefrontal cortex, which contribute to different aspects of recognition memory formation and retention. 6 Although object recognition memory has been well characterised in adult rats, it is not well characterised in middle-aged and aged (late adulthood) rats, demanding research in the field.

Beyond memory development, emotional states, particularly anxiety, play a crucial role in shaping cognitive performance. Anxiety can either impair or enhance recognition memory, depending on factors such as stress exposure, age and neurobiological changes. 8 For a large number of individuals, adolescence is a period of heightened susceptibility to anxiety-like disorders.9, 10 This is possibly due to developmental changes in gonadal and adrenal hormone production, which are associated with this developmental period of life.11, 12 However, the phenotype of this behaviour is not very clear in stages such as early adulthood, middle age and late adulthood in rats. The open field test (OFT) is commonly used to measure anxiety-like behaviours in rodents by assessing exploratory tendencies in a novel environment.13, 14 Interestingly, exploratory behaviour itself undergoes developmental shifts, with adolescent and adult rodents exhibiting different responses to novel environments due to changes in anxiety regulation and risk assessment.15, 16 These behavioural changes are likely driven by the ongoing maturation of neural circuits involved in emotion processing, including the amygdala, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. 8 Understanding the ontogeny of recognition memory, exploratory activity and anxiety-like behaviours is crucial, as both cognitive and emotional functions undergo significant developmental and ageing-related transformations influenced by neurobiological and environmental factors. 15 Research in this field has broader implications for understanding neurodevelopmental disorders and age-related cognitive changes in both animals and humans. 17 Moreover, knowledge of the ontogeny of recognition memory, exploratory behaviour and anxiety-like behaviour in laboratory animals has implications for developing anxiolytic and/or memory-improving drugs suitable for different age groups. Although this is evident to the scientific community, relatively little is known about the ontogenic effects of object recognition memory, exploratory activity and anxiety-like behaviour in rats. Therefore, the current study was designed to investigate the changes that occur in object recognition memory, exploratory activity and anxiety-like behaviour in 6-, 12- and 24-month-old rats as a result of their normal developmental and/or ageing process with the help of two behavioural testing paradigms, such as the novel object recognition test and open field test.

Methods

Animals

Male albino Wistar rats, aged 6-, 12- and 24-month, were used for the study. The Wistar strain was selected due to its well-documented use in behavioural and cognitive neuroscience research, particularly in studies assessing memory formation, anxiety and sensorimotor gating. All animals were housed in the Central Animal House Facility of Ras Al Khaimah Medical and Health Sciences University (RAKMHSU), UAE. Rats were housed in polypropylene cages (41 × 28 × 14 cm) in groups of three/cage to facilitate social interaction while preventing overcrowding. The rats were maintained in a temperature-controlled (set at 22°C ± 2°C), well-ventilated animal facility, with a relative humidity of 50%–60%. The light/dark cycle was maintained at 12:12 h (light:dark), with lights on at 7:00 am and off at 7:00 pm. Light intensity within the housing area was kept at an average light intensity of 150 lux, ensuring standard circadian conditions. Food and water were provided (ad libitum), with a standard rodent chow diet meeting all nutritional requirements. Water was supplied in polycarbonate bottles fitted with stainless steel sipper tubes, and bottles were replaced and cleaned every 2 days to maintain hygiene. The cages contained autoclaved woodchip bedding, changed twice weekly. These housing conditions aligned with those recommended in previous developmental behavioural studies.6, 18 During the housing period, all animals were observed daily for signs of distress, illness or abnormal behaviour. Only healthy rats displaying normal activity and grooming behaviours were included in different experimental groups. All investigators have handled animals every alternate day after they have been assigned to a specific group, and this continued until the behavioural experiments. The experiment was conducted by an experimenter who was blinded to the age group of the animals to prevent observer bias. The study procedures were approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (RAKMHSU-REC-034-2021/22-UG-M).

Experimental Groups and Randomisation

To investigate cognitive and behavioural differences across various age groups, animals were assigned to three experimental groups: Group 1: 6-month-old (n = 8)—representing early adulthood, a stage characterised by peak physiological and cognitive function. Group 2: 12-month-old (n = 8)—representing middle-aged, a transitional phase with potential early signs of age-related changes. Group 3: 24-month-old (n = 4)—representing late adulthood and early ageing, a period associated with progressive neurobiological and behavioural alterations. In order to ensure random allocation of animals to each group, from a pool of similar-aged animals, each animal was allotted to a group by the lottery method. Although six rats were assigned to the third group, in the course of the study, two animals died. Rats were marked using non-toxic fur dye to allow for individual identification throughout the study. All efforts were made to minimise discomfort and distress in the animals. Daily checks were conducted to monitor weight, fur condition, hydration status and overall activity levels. Any rat displaying signs of distress or illness was removed from the study and monitored separately. Timely checks were also carried out to determine sensory or motor impairments, and none were observed during the course of the study in any of the animals in the different groups. Standardised handling procedures were implemented to minimise stress, ensuring habituation to human interaction.

Behavioural Testing

The behavioural testing was conducted in the order of OFT, followed by NORT. To ensure consistency, all behavioural assessments were conducted in a dedicated testing room separate from the housing area. The testing room was temperature- and humidity-controlled to match housing conditions, minimising external variables. Before each session, rats were gently handled for 3 min to acclimate them to the experimenter. This approach has been shown to reduce handling-induced stress and improve behavioural consistency​. Each experimental session was conducted at the same time of day (between 10:00 am and 5:00 pm) to control for circadian influences on activity and memory performance. Only one rat was tested at a time in any given experiment to prevent social influence on behaviour. Following each behavioural test, rats were returned to their home cages, and the apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol to remove residual odours that could influence subsequent trials. Ambient noise levels were controlled to minimise external stressors that could influence behavioural responses. The SMART automated behavioural video tracking system (Super Pack, version 3.0, Barcelona, Spain) was utilised for all behavioural recording and analysis. The primary endpoint of the study is the recognition memory performance determined by the NORT, and exploratory and/or anxiety-like behaviours determined by the OFT. All other behavioural measures derived from the NORT and OFT were considered as secondary endpoints.

Novel Object Recognition Test

The NORT was conducted in a square arena (LE800SC, Panlab, Barcelona, Spain) with dimensions of 90 (W) × 90 (D) × 40 (H). During the test, two objects were placed at opposite corners of the arena. Objects were selected based on their age-appropriate dimensions, ensuring they were no taller than twice the size of the test rat to facilitate interaction​. Objects were composed of non-porous materials, such as plastic, rubber or ceramic, to allow for thorough cleaning and prevent scent marking. The novel object was similar in size and material to the familiar object. The novel object was consistently placed on the right side of the arena throughout the experiment. The SMART tracking system was employed to detect and quantify object exploration, with interaction zones defined in centimetres using SMART software from the object’s centre to ensure precise measurements​. Before actual testing, spatial exploration in the open field confirmed no significant location or side preference in any animal. The NORT was conducted in three phases: Habituation phase: During this phase, each rat was allowed to explore the empty testing arena for 10 min per day over three consecutive days to acclimate to the environment. This phase aimed to reduce novelty-induced anxiety, thereby ensuring a reliable baseline for subsequent behavioural assessments​. Familiarisation phase: During this phase, two identical objects were placed in the opposite corners of the test arena. Each rat was introduced into the arena, facing away from the objects, but facing the opposite side wall, and permitted to explore for 3 min. Test phase: This phase was conducted 24 h after the familiarisation phase. During this phase, one of the two identical objects—the one from the top right corner of the apparatus was replaced with a novel object of similar size and texture but a different shape and colour. Each rat was reintroduced into the arena and allowed to explore for 3 min. The time spent interacting with the novel and familiar objects was recorded. Recognition index, which is the time spent exploring the novel object/time spent exploring the novel object + time spent exploring the familiar object (total object exploration time), was calculated and reported. Additionally, other relevant exploratory parameters were also recorded and reported. Preference for the novel object was considered to be indicative of intact recognition memory.6, 19, 20

Open Field Test

This test was conducted in a 90 (W) × 90 (D) × 40 (H) square arena constructed from non-reflective black Plexiglas (Square arena, LE800SC, Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). The height of the walls was sufficient to prevent escape while minimising external visual stimuli. The arena was illuminated with a low-intensity white light (approximately 50 lux) to ensure consistent lighting conditions. Ambient noise levels were controlled to minimise external stressors that could influence behavioural responses​. Before the behavioural testing, the test arena was divided into three zones (periphery, intermediate and centre) in the software. The software allowed precise tracking of the animals’ movement patterns, latency to move, distance travelled and time spent in specific regions of interest within the arena​. During the test, each rat was placed in the centre of the open-field arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min, and both exploratory and anxiety parameters were recorded and analysed. Following each trial, the arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate residual olfactory cues before testing the next rat. 21

Statistical Analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the data followed a Gaussian distribution, they were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences among age groups (6-, 12-, and 24-month-old rats). Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate. The data that did not follow a Gaussian distribution were analysed by a non-parametric test; the Kruskal–Wallis test and post hoc Dunn’s test were carried out for comparing all pairs of columns. A p value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0, CA, USA).

Results

Ontogeny of Recognition Memory

The percentage of time spent by rats in the periphery was not significantly different in various age groups studied (Figure 1A). The values were 76.46% ± 4.29% in the 6-month group, 86.98% ± 6.03% in the 12-month group, and 76.05% ± 8.51% in the 24-month group, but they were not found to be statistically significant. A similar result was also evident in the percentage of time spent on the familiar object (Figure 1B). The 6-month-old rats spent 14.69% ± 4.38% of their time examining the familiar object, but the 12-month-old rats spent only 10.15% ± 5.51%, and the 24-month-old group found to be spending 15.71% ± 7.38%, with the familiar objects. However, these were not significantly different in any of the groups studied. In contrast, the percentage of time spent on the novel object was significantly decreased in the 12-month group (2.87% ± 1.09%) compared with both the 6-month (8.85% ± 1.43%) and 24-month (8.23% ± 1.58%) groups. One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test, revealed a significant difference in the means of the various groups studied (Figure 1C, p = .008).

The total distance travelled by rats during the test was also significantly different in the 6-month (2,527 ± 268.3 cm), 12-month (1,302 ± 176.7 cm) and 24-month (2,230 ± 183.2 cm) groups. One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test, revealed a significant difference in the means of the various groups studied (Figure 1D, p = .002). Distance travelled in the periphery was also significantly different in the 6-month (2014 ± 251.7 cm), versus 12-month group (1107 ± 149.4 cm) but not with 24-month (1742 ± 201.3 cm) group when analysed by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test (Figure 1E, p = .01). Additionally, global activity was also significantly decreased (Figure 1F, p = .01) in the 12-month group (22,419 ± 4,718) compared to the 6-month group (50,981 ± 8,306) but not with the 24-month group (38,362 ± 2,064). Total rearing duration was also significantly decreased in both the 12-month (48.90 ± 23.05 s) (Figure 1G, p = .001) and 24-month groups (27.36 ± 14.21 s) (Figure 1H, p = .002) compared to the 6-month group (135.9 ± 12.04 s). On the other hand, immobility duration was significantly increased in the 12-month group (105.0 ± 10.70 s) (Figure 1H, p = .002; Figure 2B) compared to the 6-month group (52.04 ± 8.40 s) (Figures 1H and 2A), but not compared to 24-month-old rats (77.77 ± 1.89 s) (Figures 1H and 2C) when analysed by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test.

The recognition index was comparatively less in 12-month-old rats (0.2018 ± 0.07 or ~20% of the total object exploration time) when compared to 6-month-old rats (0.4235 ± 0.06 or ~42% of the total object exploration time) and 24-month-old rats (0.3999 ± 0.07 or ~40% of the total object exploration time) (Figure 1I), but this difference was not found to be statistically significant. The reduction in novel object exploration by 12-month-old rats was evident in the representative video tracking of the paths taken by animals during the NORT, as presented in Figure 2, which demonstrates decreased general exploratory behaviour and comparatively reduced interaction with the novel object in the 12-month-old group (Figure 2B), compared to 6-month-old (Figure 2A) and 24-month-old (Figure 2C) groups.

Discussion

The cognitive and emotional parameter changes in young, middle-aged and aged rats were assessed by performing two classic behavioural paradigms, such as NORT and OFT. It was evident from the results that recognition memory was only slightly reduced in 12-month-old rats compared to the other groups, but general locomotor and exploratory activity during NORT was found to be reduced in 12- and 24-month-old rats compared to 6-month-old rats, suggesting a possible ontogenic effect on these parameters. Although the anxiety-like behavioural parameters were not significantly different across different age groups, increased centre exploration observed in older rats may reflect a possible ontogenic effect on these behaviours.

The results on recognition memory demonstrate that the current study did not detect a robust recognition memory deficit in any age group studied. Although with the current data, we cannot categorically state that recognition memory is preserved across adulthood and ageing, it indicates that cognitive function associated with object recognition is robust against age-related decline within the examined age spectrum. This contrasts with other domains of memory, such as episodic or spatial memory, which are more vulnerable to deterioration due to ageing. The NORT data of 12-month-old rats are intriguing because it may be a time window where a transient decline in recognition memory may take place, and further research is needed to prove this argument. These findings suggest that recognition memory is largely stable across adulthood and ageing, with a temporary dip at middle age rather than a progressive decline, which is consistent with previous reports indicating that object recognition memory relies on neural substrates such as the perirhinal cortex and hippocampus, which reach early functional maturity and remain relatively preserved across the lifespan.3, 6, 22 The possibility of interference from decreased exploratory behaviour and increased anxiety-like behaviour in middle-aged animals (12-month-old) with cognition could be another plausible reason for the comparatively poor performance of 12-month-old rats in the NORT. Reports demonstrate that increased anxiety-like behaviour and the resultant decreased exploratory drive could interfere with several cognitive tasks, including novel object recognition memory. 23 The OFT data demonstrate that the most notable effect was on locomotor activity at 12-month-old rats, as reflected by decreased total distance travelled, reduced mean speed and increased immobility. However, the time spent in the centre, intermediate and peripheral zones did not differ significantly across groups, suggesting that anxiety-like behaviour itself did not markedly change as age progressed. It could be argued that the apparent reduction in centre exploration in 12-month-old rats is likely attributable to reduced exploratory activity. Interestingly, the relatively reduced performance observed in the 12-month-old group compared to the 24-month group may suggest that middle age represents a transitional phase in these behaviours rather than a linear age-related decline. However, a definitive pattern cannot be concluded due to the limited sample size in the 24-month group and variability across age groups. Further studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these findings.

The parallelism index data further confirmed this exploratory pattern in 12-month group because this value was found to be decreased in 12-month group in the intermediate and peripheral zones when they moved through these areas compared to the 24-month-old rats, suggesting that the intermediate and peripheral areas are more familiar and safe compared to the centre zone. This highlights the importance of documenting both anxiety related and exploratory parameters in the OFT, as reduced exploratory activity and anxiety-like responses can be interlinked. Furthermore, the reduced rearing duration in both 12- and 24-month-old rats compared to the 6-month group indicates a decrease in exploratory behaviour with age. This is consistent with prior work connecting rearing behaviour to hippocampal activity and environmental uncertainty.24, 25 In addition, age-related reductions in locomotor and exploratory activity have been consistently reported.26, 27 This may confound the interpretation of anxiety-like behaviour in paradigms such as the OFT.13, 28 The fact that stretching behaviours did not differ significantly across ages suggests that risk-assessment responses are less sensitive to age-related changes.

There could be multiple reasons for all the above subtle behavioural changes that we have observed in various age groups. This may include, but is not limited to, increased emotionality, 27 (although this was not very evident in 12-month-old rats in this study compared to 6-month-old rats). Age-related changes take place in the hippocampus and perirhinal cortex, and potentially decreases the ability to assess novel cues. 29 Moreover, studies suggest the involvement of somatostatin in regulating cognitive and exploratory behaviours, as it was reported that reduced levels of somatostatin may interfere with cognition and explorative behaviours.30, 31 Further studies are required to unravel the molecular mechanisms that underlie these subtle behavioural changes in rats.

Current results indicate that recognition memory is generally stable across adulthood, with only a temporary reduction in middle age, while exploratory activity and rearing behaviour decline as the age progresses. Although OFT measures a certain aspect of anxiety-like behaviour in rodents, to have a holistic analysis of anxiety-like behaviours, additional assays can be included in the overall behavioural assessment battery. Therefore, the lack of testing animals on other anxiety assays, such as the elevated plus maze, could be considered a limitation of the current study. Anxiety-like behaviour, as measured by zone preference in the OFT, remained largely unchanged (under the current study conditions and specificity of the assay method), which underscores the need for multiple complementary behavioural paradigms to dissect emotional reactivity across the lifespan when studying these behaviours in rodents.

Another limitation of the present study was the low sample size in the 24-month group, following animal deaths, which is a common challenge in ageing studies and should be considered while interpreting the above findings. Since the work was only done in male animals, sex-dependent differences were not explored. This could have been noteworthy information, as there is evidence of gender influence on stress response and memory performance. In addition, the integration of other behavioural paradigms, such as contextual fear conditioning tasks, would have provided more evidence and understanding of the behaviour of various age groups studied. In this report, we also could not correlate the behavioural assay results with histological/hormonal/neurotransmitter data, which could have been valuable information to categorically prove the molecular or neurological basis for the changes that we have observed in animals of different ages.

Conclusion

Recognition memory appears unaffected throughout adulthood and ageing. Anxiety-like behaviour did not show significant differences across age groups. In contrast, exploratory activity and rearings declined with age, suggesting that the ontogenic changes are more reflective of locomotor and exploratory behaviours. Stretching and other risk-assessment behaviours, however, did not change as the age progressed. When combined, these results imply that exploratory and locomotor behaviours are more susceptible to ageing compared to recognition memory. The results also highlight the importance of using multiple behavioural assays to holistically unravel emotionality changes across various age groups of rats. Further research is needed to understand better the molecular, cellular and neurobiological mechanisms driving these behavioural trajectories in rats.
