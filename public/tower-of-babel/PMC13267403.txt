4-hydroxy-5-methyl-3(2H)-furanone mediates widespread interkingdom communications between fungi and prokaryotes
Authors: Xinyu Qian, Yan-Bo Zhao, Heng Zhang, Wenguang Yang, Kai Sun, Songying Ouyang, Xihui Shen, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFungal–prokaryotic interactions are widespread in natural and host-associated habitats. These interactions are believed to play crucial roles in ecosystem functioning and health. However, the cell–cell interaction mechanisms between these two types of microorganisms remain largely unknown.ResultsWe demonstrate that fungi Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Cryptococcus neoformans secrete 4-hydroxy-5-methyl-3(2H)-furanone (MHF), which chemoattracts Pseudomonas aeruginosa via the dCache_1-type ligand-binding domains (LBDs) of the chemoreceptors PctA and TlpQ. We also solved high-resolution structures of PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD in complex with MHF. Bioinformatic analyses suggest that the MHF synthase Cff1p is widespread among fungi, and functionally distinct dCache_1-containing transmembrane receptors capable of sensing MHF are prevalent across diverse bacteria and archaea. Functional assays confirm MHF binding enhances enzymatic activities of representative dCache_1-type LBD-containing receptors. Finally, communication network analysis suggests that MHF-mediated fungal–prokaryotic interactions are widespread between members of the fungal phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota and the bacterial phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota.ConclusionsThese findings identify MHF as a broadly utilised fungal signal mediating extensive interkingdom communications between fungi and prokaryotes, highlighting its potential role in shaping microbial community dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04138-3.Keywords: Fungal-prokaryotic interactions, Fungal signal molecule, Chemotaxis, Crystal structures, Communication network

Background

As the unseen majority on Earth, microbes comprise enormous genetic diversity and usually form complex communities that collectively function as a microbiome in various habitats around the globe [1]. Within microbiomes, members of the microbiota, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, algae, protists and viruses, are connected by diverse types of intrakingdom and interkingdom interactions [2]. Prokaryotes and fungi are often the two main groups of a microbiome and frequently coinhabit a multitude of environments, from abiotic surroundings, such as soils and food products, to host habitats, including animal gut, plant rhizosphere and polymicrobial infection sites [3]. Prokaryotes and fungi are both essential for the functioning of most ecosystems and also crucial for health and diseases in various organisms including humans, animals, plants and insects [1]. While intrakingdom interactions within prokaryotic and fungal communities have been widely studied, there is an increasing need for deciphering interactions between prokaryotes and fungi and functions of their interkingdom interactions that drive microbial community dynamics.

Interactions and communications between prokaryotic and fungal cells can be achieved via cellular contacts, physical associations as well as chemical signalling [4]. Prokaryotic-fungal interactions can result in different outcomes for each partner and are critical in shaping the structure and function of microbial communities [5]. Mechanisms of prokaryotic-fungal interactions include antibiosis, nutritional interactions, signalling-based interactions, protein secretion as well as genetic exchange, leading to a variety of biological effects that vary from antagonism to mutualism [6]. While antibiosis and nutritional interactions between prokaryotes and fungi have been widely recognised and studied, there are still few studies that reveal signalling-based interactions between these two microbial groups [4]. Chemotaxis of bacteria towards fungi and fungally derived molecules has been reported to play an important role in the establishment of many prokaryotic-fungal interactions [4]. For instance, fusaric acid secreted by the phytopathogenic fungus Fusarium oxysporum chemoattracted a biocontrol Pseudomonas fluorescens strain that colonises the fungal hyphae [7, 8], while trehalose that accumulates to high levels in the hyphae of the ectomycorrhizal fungus Laccaria bicolor chemoattracted and promoted the growth of its helper P. fluorescens strain [9, 10]. Quorum-sensing (QS) molecules and secondary metabolites produced by bacteria and fungi have also been shown to act as signalling molecules that mediate interkingdom interactions [11–14]. The QS molecule farnesol secreted by the fungus Candida albicans was shown to inhibit production of the Pseudomonas quinolone signal and pyocyanin in Pseudomonas aeruginosa [12], while the QS signal 3-oxo-C12 homoserine lactone produced by P. aeruginosa inhibits C. albicans filamentation [13]. Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been reported to produce a volatile compound 4-hydroxy-5-methyl-3(2H)-furanone (MHF) that is a mimic of the bacterial QS signal autoinducer-2 (AI-2) and can be detected by Vibrio harveyi through the AI-2 receptor LuxP to drive bioluminescence [15]. In addition to S. cerevisiae, a number of fungal species including Cryptococcus neoformans were also shown to encode the MHF synthase Cff1p and have the potential to produce MHF [15]. However, MHF-mediated interactions between fungi and bacteria other than Vibrio species remain to be explored.

Prokaryotes have evolved a wide range of signal transduction systems that enable them to sense and respond to changes in population density and community composition, local environmental conditions as well as chemical cues derived from their co-habitating fungi, plants or animals [16]. Major families of signal transduction proteins in prokaryotes include small-molecule-binding transcriptional regulators, two-component histidine kinases (HKs), methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins (MCPs), serine/threonine kinases (STKs), serine phosphatases (SPs), diguanylate cyclases (DGCs) and/or c-di-GMP-specific phosphodiesterases (PDEs), and adenylate- or guanylate cyclases (ACs/GCs) [17]. While intracellular signal transducers such as transcriptional regulators recognise internal signals or extracellular signals that diffuse through the cell membrane, the canonical forms of transmembrane receptors, including HKs, MCPs, STKs, SPs, DGCs/PDEs and ACs/GCs, contain an extracytosolic ligand-binding domain (LBD) flanked by two transmembrane regions that sense signals in the extracytosolic space [16–19]. A large number of different extracytosolic LBD types have been identified in prokaryotes, among which the dCache_1-type LBD is the predominant extracellular sensory domain and is found in all major families of transmembrane receptors [17, 20–24]. LBDs categorised into dCache_1 have been shown to recognise structurally diverse ligands, such as amino acids, sugars, organic acids, polyamines, quaternary amines, purine derivatives and the QS signal AI-2 [25–31]. Moreover, some dCache_1 domains have been shown to recognise host-derived cues such as neurotransmitters or plant root exudates, thus mediating interactions between prokaryotes and their hosts [26, 32, 33]. However, there are no instances where prokaryotes sense fungal signals or metabolites via the dCache_1 domain of transmembrane receptors.

Here, we provide a report of P. aeruginosa chemotaxis towards two fungi, S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans. We further found that dCache_1 domain-containing MCPs PctA and TlpQ mediate chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa towards the fungus-derived chemotactic signal MHF. The high-resolution structures of PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD in complex with MHF have also been elucidated using X-ray crystallography. More importantly, our bioinformatics analysis and experimental verification suggest that hundreds of fungal species possess the MHF synthase Cff1p, while signal transduction proteins harbouring a dCache_1 domain with the ability to sense MHF are widely distributed in prokaryotes. Therefore, our results reveal that MHF is a universal fungal signal that mediates widespread interkingdom communications between fungi and bacteria.

Results

P. aeruginosa exhibits chemotaxis towards S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans

While P. aeruginosa has been shown to exhibit chemotaxis towards a variety of signalling molecules and metabolites secreted by bacteria, plants and animals [31, 33], whether fungal metabolites chemoattract this bacterium remains unknown. By a soft agar plate chemotaxis assay, we found that P. aeruginosa showed positive chemotactic responses (RI values > 0.52) to culture agar blocks of actively growing S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans that were placed at the centre of the soft agar plates (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the culture supernatants of the two fungi grown in MSB medium to late-exponential phase were found to attract P. aeruginosa in soft agar (Fig. 1B). In contrast, P. aeruginosa showed no chemotaxis towards fungi-free MSB agar blocks and fresh MSB medium (Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, the culture supernatants produced by the concentrated cells of the two fungi, but not the fresh MSB medium, were found to induce chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa in the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assay (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that P. aeruginosa exhibits chemotactic response towards metabolites secreted by S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans.

PctA and TlpQ mediate chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa towards S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans

To identify chemoreceptors involved in chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa to fungal metabolites, the single deletion mutants lacking each of the 26 predicted chemoreceptor genes encoded by P. aeruginosa [25] were examined for their chemotactic response to the culture supernatants produced by the concentrated cells of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans. Whereas most of these mutants showed no obvious difference in the chemotactic response, the mutants ΔpctA and ΔtlpQ displayed significantly reduced chemotaxis compared to the wild type (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, expression of pctA and tlpQ in the corresponding mutants fully restored their chemotaxis towards the supernatants of the concentrated cultures of the two fungi, and the double deletion mutant ΔpctAΔtlpQ completely lost the ability to respond to the fungal supernatants (Fig. 2C, D). In addition, the double mutant has lost the capacity to migrate towards the culture supernatants of the two fungi in the soft agar plate chemotaxis assay (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that the chemoreceptors PctA and TlpQ are essential for the chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa towards metabolites secreted by S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans.

MHF is a fungal chemotactic signal that attracts P. aeruginosa via PctA and TlpQ

Both PctA and TlpQ harbour a periplasmic LBD categorised into the dCache_1 domain model (PF02743) [34, 35]. Whereas PctA-LBD has been shown to bind 17 proteinogenic amino acids and the nonproteinogenic amino acid L-ornithine, TlpQ-LBD recognises various biogenic amines and neurotransmitters such as polyamines, tyramine, 2-phenylethylamine, histamine, serotonin, dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine [33–38]. Nevertheless, both of the LBDs have the capacity to bind the bacterial QS signal AI-2 (Fig. 3A), and thus PctA and TlpQ co-mediate chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa towards AI-2 [15, 25]. Moreover, S. cerevisiae has been reported to produce an AI-2 mimic MHF (Fig. 3A), and C. neoformans was also shown to encode the enzyme Cff1p required for MHF production [15]. We thus speculate that PctA and TlpQ may sense MHF secreted by S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans. Indeed, S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans were found to secrete MHF, which reaches maximal levels of 4.6–5.7 μM in the late-exponential phase (Fig. 3B, C). In contrast, deletion of the CFF1 gene encoding Cff1p in both S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans completely abolished their capacity to produce MHF (Fig. 3B, C). Furthermore, the ΔCFF1 mutants of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans lost their ability to attract P. aeruginosa in the soft agar plate chemotaxis assay, while complementation of the ΔCFF1 mutant of S. cerevisiae with a plasmid harbouring the CFF1 gene from S. cerevisiae or C. neoformans restored the wild-type phenotypes (Fig. 3D). In the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assay, the culture supernatants produced by the concentrated cells of the ΔCFF1 mutants of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans attracted P. aeruginosa cells at levels significantly lower than those by wild-type S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans, and complementation significantly restored the ability of the ΔCFF1 mutant of S. cerevisiae to attract P. aeruginosa (Fig. 3E). Of note, the culture supernatants of the ΔCFF1 mutants of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans still had the ability to induce chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa in the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assay (Fig. 3E), suggesting that S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans certainly secrete other compounds to attract P. aeruginosa. Given the broad chemosensory capacity of P. aeruginosa and the fact that PctA and TlpQ of P. aeruginosa can also sense many other ligands such as amino acids and polyamines [33–37], we then investigated the chemotactic responses of P. aeruginosa to MHF, L-proline, L-valine, L-tryptophan, and spermidine by quantitative capillary assays at a range of ligand concentrations. P. aeruginosa was found to exhibit notable chemotactic responses to all these tested ligands at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 100, and 1,000 μM (Fig. 3F and Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Taken together, the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assays showed that MHF produced by S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans at maximal levels up to ~ 5 μM partially contributes to the chemotactic response of P. aeruginosa, but MHF is not the sole chemoattractant secreted by S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans to attract P. aeruginosa. Nevertheless, P. aeruginosa exhibited chemotaxis to MHF in a PctA- and TlpQ-dependent manner in the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assay (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis showed that MHF binds with high affinity to PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD (Kd = 0.29–0.36 μM) (Fig. 3H, I), indicating that MHF is a high-affinity ligand for both PctA and TlpQ. Collectively, these results indicate that MHF is a physiologically relevant ligand for PctA and TlpQ, and S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans can secrete MHF to attract P. aeruginosa via the chemoreceptors PctA and TlpQ.

To understand the molecular determinants for MHF recognition by PctA and TlpQ, we solved the high-resolution structures of PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD in complex with MHF, and the overall structures are illustrated in Fig. 4 and Additional file 1: Table S1.

A previous study has determined the crystal structures of PctA-LBD in complex with several amino acids [35]. Consistent with this study, PctA-LBD, adopting a structure typically observed for the dCache_1 domain model, is composed of a long N-terminal helix and two α/β structural subdomains that are termed membrane-distal and membrane-proximal modules (Fig. 4A). The asymmetric unit contains two copies, and well-defined electron density for MHF was observed in two monomers, enabling the ligand placement to be determined (Fig. 4A-C). The loop connecting β4 and β5 encircles the MHF molecule within the membrane-distal module. The MHF molecule is coordinated through hydrogen-bond interactions involving residues Y121, R126, W128 and A147, while additional stabilisation is provided by surrounding hydrophobic and aromatic residues including Y101, F109, Y144, and I153 (Fig. 4D, E). The acidic residues D146 and D173 further contribute to shaping the ligand-binding pocket (Fig. 4D, E). Consistent with this binding mode, alanine substitutions of key residues involved in ligand coordination, including R126, W128, Y144, D146 and D173, markedly reduced the binding affinity of MHF for PctA-LBD (Fig. 4F and Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Notably, R126, W128, Y144, D146 and D173 of PctA-LBD have also been previously identified as key residues for its binding to AI-2 and amino acids such as L-methionine, L-tryptophan, and L-isoleucine [25, 35], suggesting that MHF, AI-2 and amino acids will compete with each other for the conserved ligand-binding pocket within the membrane-distal module of PctA-LBD. PctB and PctC are two paralogs of PctA in P. aeruginosa and can also bind and mediate chemotaxis to some amino acids [35, 36]. In contrast to PctA-LBD, however, PctB-LBD and PctC-LBD were found to bind MHF with low affinities (Kd = 89–91 µM) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A, B). Both PctB and PctC contain four of the five conserved residues in their dCache_1 domains, with PctB possessing E146 at the position corresponding to D146 of PctA and PctC possessing F147 at the position corresponding to Y144 of PctA (Additional file 1: Fig. S4), leading us to speculate that the variation in one of the five conserved residues may cause PctB-LBD and PctC-LBD to have lower affinities for MHF. Indeed, substitution mutations of the non-conserved residue to the conserved residue within PctB-LBD (E146D) and PctC-LBD (F147Y) increased their MHF binding affinities to levels (Kd = 0.36–0.38 µM) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C, D) comparable to that of PctA-LBD (Fig. 3H). These results, together with the chemotaxis assays (Figs. 2 and 3G), suggest that MHF is a physiologically relevant ligand for PctA, but not for PctB or PctC, and further support the observation that the five conserved residues of PctA are key residues for MHF binding.

As described in the initial TlpQ-LBD structure in complex with histamine [33], the MHF-bound TlpQ-LBD adopts an overall fold similar to that of PctA-LBD but contains an extended insertion between β-strands β1 and β2 (Fig. 4G). As observed for PctA-LBD, two TlpQ-LBD molecules are present in the asymmetric unit, and MHF binds within the binding pocket of the membrane-distal module (Fig. 4G-I). In contrast to PctA, ligand recognition in TlpQ is dominated by hydrophobic and aromatic interactions. The MHF molecule is enclosed within a well-defined aromatic and hydrophobic pocket formed by residues Y126, Y158, M172, W192, and Y208 (Fig. 4J, K). Notably, the furan ring of MHF is positioned parallel to the aromatic side chain of Y158, supporting a stabilising π-π stacking interaction. A single hydrogen bond is observed between the carbonyl oxygen of MHF and Y208, while surrounding residues including E170, E176, D210 and D239 contribute to shaping the binding cavity (Fig. 4J, K). Furthermore, alanine substitutions of Y208, D210, or D239, which have been previously identified as key residues for the binding of TlpQ-LBD to AI-2 and histamine [25, 33], significantly weakened MHF binding to TlpQ-LBD (Fig. 4L and Additional file 1: Fig. S5). These results suggest that when MHF, AI-2 and histamine are present simultaneously, they will compete with each other for binding to the same ligand-binding pocket within the membrane-distal module of TlpQ-LBD.

Widespread Occurrence of MHF synthases Cff1p in fungi

A previous study has suggested that the MHF synthase Cff1p is widespread in a variety of fungal species [15]. Cff1p from S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans are both cupin superfamily proteins containing a Cupin_5 domain (PF06172) and share 34.5% amino acid sequence identity with each other. Searches for homologs of S. cerevisiae Cff1p using the BLASTP program against the NCBI non-redundant protein database with a cutoff E-value of 1E-3 and a coverage threshold of 60%, followed by domain predictions against the Pfam database with an E-value cutoff of 1E-5 showed that proteins that possess a putative Cupin_5 domain are widely distributed in fungal species, including members of the phyla Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota and Mucoromycota (Additional file 2: Table S1). These Cff1p homologs share > 26.5% amino acid sequence identity with Cff1p of S. cerevisiae. Interestingly, dozens of Cff1p homologs were predicted to possess two putative Cupin_5 domains (Additional file 2: Table S1).

Amino acid sequence alignment of Cff1p homologs showed that a key catalytic residue corresponding to E44 of S. cerevisiae Cff1p that is required for MHF production [15] is conserved in 96.6% of these Cff1p homologs (Fig. 5A and Additional file 2: Table S2). Moreover, another residue that matches with the putative catalytic residue H96 of S. cerevisiae Cff1p [39] is conserved in 97.7% of the Cff1p homologs (Fig. 5A and Additional file 2: Table S2). In agreement, whereas the supernatant of the ΔCFF1 mutant of S. cerevisiae lost the ability to induce bioluminescence, the supernatants of its derivatives expressing wild-type Cff1p from S. cerevisiae or C. neoformans produced activity comparable to that of wild-type S. cerevisiae in the V. harveyi MM32 reporter assay (Fig. 5B). However, the supernatants of the ΔCFF1 mutant derivatives expressing the two Cff1p proteins with an alanine substitution in individual conserved residues induced bioluminescence at levels far below those of the ΔCFF1 mutant derivatives expressing wild-type Cff1p (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the ΔCFF1 mutant derivatives expressing point mutants of the two Cff1p proteins lost the ability to attract P. aeruginosa in the soft agar plate chemotaxis assay (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assays showed that the culture supernatants produced by the concentrated cells of the ΔCFF1 mutant derivatives expressing point mutants of Cff1p attracted P. aeruginosa cells at levels significantly lower than those by the ΔCFF1 mutant derivatives expressing wild-type Cff1p (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that the two highly conserved positions of the Cff1p proteins corresponding to E44 and H96 of S. cerevisiae Cff1p are key for the presumptive enzymatic activity for MHF production. Cff1p proteins with the two conserved residues are distributed in 373 fungal genera within the phyla Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota and Mucoromycota, with more than 98% found in members of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Fig. 6 and Additional file 2: Table S2).

Proteins with the dCache_1 domain capable of sensing MHF are widely distributed in bacteria and archaea

Our previous study has shown that all major types of signal transduction proteins in bacteria that contain dCache_1 domain with five conserved amino acid residues corresponding to R126, W128, Y144, D146, and D173 in the active pocket of PctA-LBD have the ability to sense AI-2 [25, 40]. These five conserved residues of PctA-LBD are also involved in MHF binding (Fig. 4D-F), leading us to speculate that this type of dCache_1 domains may also have the propensity to recognise MHF. Among 61727 dCache_1 domains belonging to 61694 prokaryotic signal transduction proteins in the Pfam database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/pfam/PF02743/) (Additional file 2: Table S3), 7029 dCache_1 domains from 7023 proteins were found to contain all the five conserved residues (Fig. 7A and Additional file 2: Table S4). Among these, 6651 dCache_1 domains are from 6645 predicted signal transduction proteins in prokaryotes, including MCPs, HKs, STKs, SPs, DGCs/PDEs, ACs/GCs and several hybrid receptors categorised as HKs/ACs/GCs and SPs/HKs, while 378 dCache_1 domains are from 378 uncharacterised proteins with domains of unknown function or without any predicted functional domains or motifs (Fig. 7B and Additional file 2: Table S4). While these transmembrane receptors containing dCache_1 domains with five conserved sites are distributed in 23 bacterial phyla, 5 archaeal phyla and some unclassified bacteria, the vast majority of them are found in members of the bacterial phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota, with MCPs being the most dominant type of signal transduction proteins (Fig. 7B and Additional file 2: Table S4).

To examine the ability of this type of dCache_1 domains to bind MHF, we randomly selected 16 from 12 bacterial phyla and 2 from 2 archaeal phyla predicted to function as sensors of MCPs, HKs, STKs, SPs, DGCs/PDEs or ACs/GCs, and prepared recombinant His6-dCache_1 proteins from the ΔluxS mutant of E. coli BL21 (DE3). The binding analysis by ITC showed that all of them bind MHF with high affinity (Kd = 0.27–0.81 μM) (Fig. 7C and Additional file 1: Figs. S6-S8). Given that dCache_1 domains with the five conserved residues corresponding to R126, W128, Y144, D146 and D173 of PctA have been previously reported to bind amino acid ligands [34], six of the MHF-binding dCache_1 domains were further subjected to amino acid binding analysis by ITC. As expected, the six MHF-binding dCache_1 domains interacted with L-proline, L-valine and L-tryptophan with affinities varying from 0.38 μM to 72 μM (Additional file 1: Figs. S9-S11). Therefore, whereas dCache_1 domains with the five conserved residues certainly possess the ability to bind amino acid ligands, their binding affinities for some amino acids may be relatively low. To verify whether MHF can exert regulatory effects on these dCache_1-type receptors, two full-length proteins predicted to function as HK and DGC were expressed and purified (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). MHF was found to induce the autokinase activity of the purified full-length HK A0A1T5BG79 from Malaciobacter marinus (Fig. 7D) and the c-di-GMP synthase activity of the purified full-length DGC A0A0C2BP83 from Noviherbaspirillum autotrophicum (Fig. 7E and Additional file 1: Fig. S13), further supporting that MHF is a ligand for the transmembrane receptors containing dCache_1 domains with the five conserved residues. Of note, although TlpQ has been predicted to be evolutionarily distinct from PctA [35] and does not possess all the five conserved residues in the ligand-binding pocket [25], it was also found to bind MHF (Figs. 3I and 4G-L), suggesting the existence of yet unrecognised MHF-binding motifs. Taken together, our results suggest that signal transduction proteins harbouring an extracytoplasmic dCache_1 domain with the capacity to sense MHF are widespread in prokaryotes.

MHF-mediated communication network between fungi and prokaryotes

To better understand the MHF-mediated communications between fungi and prokaryotes, we constructed the interaction network between potential MHF-producing fungi species and MHF-sensing prokaryotic species based on the predicted MHF synthases and receptors. While the fungal phylum Ascomycota contains the largest number of species possessing the MHF synthase Cff1p, they are mainly distributed in the classes Dothideomycetes, Sordariomycetes, Leotiomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Saccharomycetes and Lecanoromycetes (Fig. 8 and Additional file 2: Table S5). Basidiomycota is the second largest fungal phylum containing potential MHF-producing species, and these species are mainly distributed in the class Agaricomycetes (Fig. 8). MCPs and DGCs/PDEs, as the first and second largest types of MHF receptor proteins in bacteria, respectively, mainly function in Pseudomonadota and Bacillota (Figs. 7B and 8). HKs are the third largest type of MHF receptors in bacteria and mainly function in Bacillota and Cyanobacteriota, while MHF receptors predicted to function as ACs/GCs are mainly found in Cyanobacteriota (Figs. 7B and 8). SPs and MCPs are the first and second largest types of MHF receptors in archaea and mainly found in the phylum Euryarchaeota, but MHF receptors predicted to function as DGCs/PDEs and ACs/GCs are not found in archaea (Figs. 7B and 8).

MHF-mediated communication network between fungi and prokaryotes. The left nodes represent fungal genera (372) with the potential to produce MHF, while the right nodes represent prokaryotic genera (593) that harbour potential dCache_1-containing MHF receptors (CamRs). The fungal genera were grouped according to their phylum, and the colours of the nodes represent fungal classes to which they belong (Of the 373 fungal genera, the genus Thelocarpon was not included in the communication network analysis as this genus could not be classified at the class level). Bacteria and archaea were grouped according to the functional types of the potential MHF receptors, and the colours of the nodes represent bacterial and archaeal phyla to which they belong

Obviously, MHF-mediated interkingdom communications widely occur between members of the fungal classes Dothideomycetes, Sordariomycetes within Ascomycota and Agaricomycetes within Basidiomycota and members of the bacterial phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota (Fig. 8). While MHF produced by diverse fungi genera seems to be able to regulate bacterial functions and behaviours via all major types of transmembrane signal transduction proteins, chemotaxis is suggested to be the most common response of bacteria to this fungal signal. Moreover, MHF is suggested to target DGCs/PDEs to regulate intracellular levels of c-di-GMP, the important bacterial second messenger that controls a wide range of biological processes, in bacterial genera mainly belonging to Pseudomonadota and Bacillota (Fig. 8). MHF can also modulate the autokinase activity of HKs that are mainly distributed in Bacillota and Cyanobacteriota (Fig. 8), thus altering phosphorylation levels and activity of the cognate response regulators. In addition, a number of bacterial genera are found to contain more than two types of MHF receptor proteins (Fig. 8), suggesting that some bacteria may simultaneously employ several signal transduction systems to sense MHF secreted by fungi. In comparison, chemotaxis and modulation of the activity of SPs are suggested to be the main responses of archaea to MHF. Together, the communication network analysis suggests that MHF mediates widespread and diversified interkingdom communications between fungi and prokaryotes.

Discussion

Prokaryotes and fungi are dominant members of most microbiomes, and their interactions can shape microbial community structure and affect the growth, fitness, host colonisation as well as pathogenicity of the interacting partners [41]. While mechanisms of fungal-prokaryotic interactions are diverse, signalling-based interactions with diffusible molecules are undoubtedly crucial for interkingdom communication and interference [2, 41]. However, small molecules involved in fungal-prokaryotic signalling and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely undefined [5]. Here, we report that two fungi S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans secreted MHF to chemoattract P. aeruginosa via targeting the dCache_1 domain-containing MCPs PctA and TlpQ. We also identify the MHF-binding sites of PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD by solving the structures of the PctA-LBD-MHF and TlpQ-LBD-MHF complexes. Furthermore, our results suggest that hundreds of fungal species possess the MHF synthase Cff1p and thus have the potential to produce MHF, while more than 6000 dCache_1-containing proteins predicted to function as MCPs, HKs, DGCs/PDEs, SPs, STKs or ACs/GCs from members of 23 bacterial phyla and 5 archaeal phyla are potential receptors to sense MHF. Our study reveals a previously unrecognised MHF-mediated mechanism underpinning the interactions between prokaryotes and fungi, and highlights the extensiveness and complexity of interkingdom communication driven by MHF.

A fascinating issue raised by our study is the ligand promiscuity of the chemoreceptors PctA and TlpQ. Rather than functioning solely as AI-2 or MHF receptors, PctA was initially identified as an amino acid receptor and later shown to bind several other ligands [25, 34–37, 42]. Similarly, TlpQ was identified as a receptor for ethylene, different polyamines, histamine, tyramine and a number of different neurotransmitters [32, 33, 37, 38]. Since all data currently available indicate that each ligand binds to the conserved ligand-binding pocket within the membrane distal module in both PctA and TlpQ [25, 32, 35, 36], ligands will compete with each other for binding to the same receptor. When P. aeruginosa comes into contact with metabolites secreted by fungi, the output of PctA and TlpQ is thus not simply a response to MHF, but an output defined by the abundance of many different compounds such as amino acids and polyamines. Indeed, the culture supernatants from the ΔCFF1 mutants of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans that have lost their capacity to produce MHF still attracted P. aeruginosa in the quantitative capillary chemotaxis assay (Fig. 3E), indicative of the presence of other chemoattractants secreted by the two fungi. Therefore, the signal promiscuity in ligand recognition and the abundance of different ligands in complex natural environments need to be taken into account when estimating the physiological relevance of both PctA and TlpQ.

Another important remaining issue is the physiological benefit of MHF-mediated movements of P. aeruginosa via chemotaxis to fungi. The biocontrol bacterium P. fluorescens WCS365 was shown to sense the fungal secondary metabolite fusaric acid and move via chemotaxis to colonise the hyphae of the phytopathogenic fungus F. oxysporum, which is crucial for the ability of the biocontrol strain to compete with the fungus for sites on the root, and also for root exudate nutrients [7, 43]. Chemotaxis of the biocontrol strain towards the fungal hyphae and subsequent colonisation were also suggested to bring the bacterium closer to nutrients such as amino acids, organic acids, and sugars secreted by the fungus [7]. When P. aeruginosa migrates and comes into closer contact with fungi via chemotaxis, the bacterium will compete with fungi for limited nutrients and space resources. Moreover, since P. aeruginosa is known to produce some antifungal virulence factors and thus shows antifungal activity against a number of fungal species [44–49], chemotaxis of P. aeruginosa towards fungi may also result in more effective killing of fungi. The potential biological relevance of MHF sensing by P. aeruginosa via chemotaxis remains to be further studied.

While many fungal species were previously suggested to harbour the potential to synthesise MHF [15], LuxP is present only in Vibrio spp. [50, 51], leading to the fact that the MHF-LuxP interkingdom signalling process can only occur between fungi and Vibrio species. Our current study, however, suggests that MHF-mediated interkingdom interactions widely occur between members of the fungal phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota and the bacterial phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota. Members of these fungal and bacterial phyla were often reported to inhabit the same niches and interact with each other [52–54]. For instance, in the murine gut, the enteropathogen Yersinia pseudotuberculosis was shown to sense the fungal QS signal tyrosol to enhance its antifungal T6SS activity against C. albicans and S. cerevisiae [54]. The soil bacterium Acinetobacter baumannii was reported to stimulate virulence factor production by C. neoformans found in soil associated with pigeon excreta, whereas another soil bacterium Bacillus safensis inhibited virulence factor production and biofilm formation by this fungus [55, 56]. Furthermore, our study also shows that prokaryotic dCache_1-containing receptors that sense fungal MHF are diverse in the mode of action, which will lead to different biological responses in prokaryotes and thus affect the relationship between prokaryotes and fungi. Chemotaxis of bacteria towards fungi via sensing of MHF by MCPs may result in physical contact and facilitate the establishment of other fungal-prokaryotic interactions such as antagonism, competition, and mutualism [4–6]. Sensing of MHF by DGCs/PDEs or ACs/GCs is able to modulate intracellular levels of the bacterial second messengers c-di-GMP, cAMP or cGMP, thus making it possible to regulate important biological processes such as motility, biofilm formation, virulence, cellular differentiation, and stress adaptation [25, 57, 58]. Sensing of MHF by HKs, SPs or STKs may change the phosphorylation state of target proteins with important functions, thus regulating diverse aspects of bacterial growth and behaviour such as oxidative stress resistance, capsular polysaccharide synthesis and spore development [59–61]. These potential biological effects underpinned by fungal-prokaryotic interaction mechanisms via Cff1p-MHF-CamRs warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, our work identifies MHF secreted by many fungi species as a physiologically relevant ligand for the dCache_1 domain-containing MCPs PctA and TlpQ of P. aeruginosa, and further provides evidence for dCache_1-containing functionally distinct receptors with the potential to sense MHF, suggesting that MHF-mediated interkingdom interactions occur widely between prokaryotes and fungi. These findings highlight the complexity of fungal-prokaryotic interactions through the Cff1p-MHF-CamRs signalling pathway. Co-occurrences of organisms from different kingdoms with the potential to produce MHF will further expand the MHF-mediated communication network between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Further studies are needed to understand the pleiotropic roles and ecological significance of MHF in such presumptive intra- and interkingdom interactions.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plasmid constructions, and culture conditions

All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2. All primers used in this study were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 and the sequences of the primers are listed in Additional file 1: Table S3. Unless otherwise indicated, P. aeruginosa strain PAO1, its mutants and complemented strains with the deleted genes expressed on the plasmid pME6032 [62] were grown in Luria Bertani (LB) medium containing the appropriate antibiotics at 37°C. The V. harveyi strain MM32 [63] was grown in AB medium [64] at 30°C. S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-5D (MATa; ura3-52; MAL2-8c; SUC2) and C. neoformans var. grubii H99 were grown in YPD medium [65] at 30°C. S. cerevisiae strains carrying pYES2 or its derivatives were grown in SC-Ura medium [66] at 30°C. The DNA fragments encoding the dCache_1 domains of 18 signal transduction proteins, and two full-length genes encoding dCache_1-containing signal transduction proteins were synthesised by Genewiz (Suzhou, China). To express and purify the soluble GST- and His6-tagged recombinant proteins, the gene fragments were cloned into pGEX-6P-1 and pET-28a, respectively, and then transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) lacking luxS. When necessary, antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: kanamycin, 50 μg ml−1; ampicillin, 100 μg ml−1; tetracycline, 20 μg ml−1 for E. coli, and 150 and 200 μg ml−1 for P. aeruginosa in liquid cultures and on plates, respectively; nourseothricin, 100 μg ml−1.

Deletion of the CFF1 gene (YML079W) in S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-5D was performed using the CRISPR-Cas9 system [67]. The guide RNA (sgRNA) sequence targeting the CFF1 gene was designed and inserted into the SwaI and BclI sites of pML104. The 5' and 3' flanking regions of the CFF1 gene were amplified separately and ligated together by overlap extension PCR to produce the recombination template. The pML104 derivative and the recombination template were transformed together into the S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-5D cells [68]. Cells were recovered at 30 °C for 1 h and spread onto SC-Ura agar, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 2–3 days. The mutant with deletion of the CFF1 gene was identified by PCR and DNA sequencing. The pML104 derivative in the ΔCFF1 mutant was eliminated by selection on YPD medium containing 1 mg ml−1 of 5-fluoroorotic acid.

Deletion of the CFF1 gene (CNAG_06071) of C. neoformans H99 was generated by replacing the coding sequence with the nourseothricin resistance gene [69]. Overlapping PCR products were generated using a nourseothricin resistance cassette and the 5' and 3' flanking sequences of the CFF1 coding sequence. The PCR products were introduced directly into strain H99 by electroporation, and the resulting mutants were confirmed by PCR and sequencing. For complementation of the CFF1 gene from S. cerevisiae or C. neoformans in S. cerevisiae, the pYES2 derivatives were transformed into the ΔCFF1 mutant of S. cerevisiae, with yeast transformants selected on SC-Ura plates. When needed, 2% galactose was added to induce expression of plasmid-borne CFF1.

Chemotaxis assays

For soft agar plate gradient assays. P. aeruginosa strains were grown overnight in M9 minimal medium [70] with 0.1% (w/v) glucose, washed twice and diluted to an OD600 of 0.3 in fresh M9 medium. Relevant strains of C. neoformans were cultured in YPD medium with shaking for 24 h, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended at an OD600 of 0.1 in mineral salts basal (MSB) medium that was modified from a previously described medium formula [71] (5 g L−1 glucose was added as a carbon source and pH was adjusted to 6.5), followed by incubation at 30 °C with shaking to late-exponential phase. The fungal cultures were centrifuged to harvest the supernatants. On the other hand, the fungal cultures were inoculated (10% inoculum, v/v) into MSB medium with 1.2% (w/v) Bacto agar before the agar solidified (40–45°C), followed by incubation at 30 °C for 24 h. Similarly, S. cerevisiae strains carrying no plasmids were grown in MSB medium with 0.01% (w/v) uracil to obtain the culture supernatants and agar blocks, while S. cerevisiae strains carrying pYES2 or its derivatives were grown in MSB medium with 2% galactose but without glucose or uracil to obtain the culture agar blocks. Then, agar blocks (3 mm diameter) containing actively growing S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans were cut from the MSB plates. 10 μl aliquots of the fungal supernatants or the fungal agar blocks were placed onto MSB soft agar plates containing 0.25% (w/v) Bacto agar. After overnight incubation for gradient formation, 2 μl aliquots of bacterial suspensions in M9 medium were placed horizontally to each of the fungal spots or blocks. All plates were incubated at 30 °C for 18 h and then examined for chemotaxis. The distance from the site of bacterial inoculation to the colony edges closest to (D1) and furthest from (D2) the fungal source was measured and the response index (RI) values were calculated as follows: RI = D1/(D1 + D2) [72]. Bacterial colonies with RI values larger than 0.52 were considered to show an attractant response, colonies with RI values between 0.48 and 0.52 were considered to indicate no chemotaxis, and colonies with RI values less than 0.48 were considered to show a repellent response.

For quantitative capillary chemotaxis assays, overnight cultures of relevant strains of P. aeruginosa in LB medium were diluted to an OD600 of 0.05 in M9 medium supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) glucose, and then grown at 37 °C with shaking until the OD600 reached 0.4. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed twice with MSB medium without glucose and resuspended in the same medium to an OD600 of 0.1. S. cerevisiae CEN.PK113-5D, C. neoformans H99 and their ΔCFF1 mutants that do not carry pYES2 were cultured with shaking in YPD medium for 24 h, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended in fresh MSB medium with or without 0.01% uracil, respectively, to an OD600 of 10.0, followed by overnight incubation at 30 °C with shaking. S. cerevisiae strains carrying pYES2 or its derivatives were grown in SC-Ura medium for 24 h to obtain the cell pellets, followed by resuspension in fresh MSB medium with 2% galactose but without glucose or uracil at an OD600 of 10.0 and subsequent overnight incubation. Culture supernatants of the concentrated cells were then collected to be used as chemoattractants in the capillaries as described previously [25]. Capillaries filled with fresh MSB derivative media were used as negative controls in the assays.

Determination of growth curves of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans and MHF concentrations in their culture supernatants

Relevant strains of S. cerevisiae and C. neoformans were cultured in YPD medium at 30 °C with shaking for 24 h, washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended in MSB medium with or without 0.01% uracil, respectively, at an OD600 of 0.1, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 24 h. At each time point with an interval of 2 h, cell growth was monitored by measuring the OD600. The culture supernatant at each time point was collected and lyophilised, followed by resuspension in distilled H2O to obtain a 10-fold concentrated supernatant. The concentrated supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane, and the MHF content was determined using an LC-30A UPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan). Components were eluted isocratically with 10% acetonitrile in 0.5% aqueous formic acid at a flow rate of 0.2 ml min−1. The detection wavelength was 280 nm. The concentration of MHF was determined from a standard curve obtained with known concentrations of MHF.

Induction of bioluminescence of V. harveyi by fungal culture supernatants

S. cerevisiae strains carrying pYES2 or its derivatives were grown in MSB medium with 2% galactose but without glucose or uracil to the late-exponential phase, and then the culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane to remove residual cells. Bioluminescence in V. harveyi MM32 was induced by the addition of the prepared fungal culture supernatants according to the method described previously [70]. Fresh MSB medium with 2% galactose but without glucose or uracil was used as a control and results are shown as fold induction relative to the light production induced by the medium control alone.

ITC analysis

ITC experiments were performed using a Nano ITC Standard Volume isothermal calorimeter (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE), with a titration temperature of 25 °C, a stirring rate of 250 rpm and a total of 25 successive injections for each titration experiment. The protein purity was examined by SDS-PAGE (Additional file 1: Figs. S14 and 15), and then the proteins were dialysed against a Tris buffer (25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) and diluted in the same buffer to final concentrations of 10 μM. MHF was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 0.5 M stock solution, and further diluted in the same Tris buffer to final concentration of 300 μM. Amino acids were dissolved in distilled water to prepare a 25 mM (for L-Tryptophan) or 100 mM (for L-proline and L-valine) stock solution, and further diluted in the same Tris buffer to final concentration of 200 μM. All solutions were degassed before loading into the calorimeter. In control experiments, the MHF and amino acid solutions were titrated into the buffer in the sample cell to obtain the heats of dilution. The heats of dilution were subtracted from the raw titration data before the binding curves were fitted to a one-site binding model.

Crystallisation and data collection

PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD were mixed with 1 mM MHF at a concentration of 23 mg mL−1 and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The resulting proteins were used for initial crystallisation screening and subsequent crystal optimisation by the sitting-drop and hanging-drop vapour-diffusion methods at 16°C. The complex crystals of PctA-LBD and MHF were grown in 2.0 M ammonium sulfate, and TlpQ-LBD-MHF crystals appeared in the condition containing 0.1 M CHES (pH 9.5) and 20% (w/v) PEG8000. Harvested crystals were cryoprotected in 20% (v/v) ethylene glycol and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Structure determination and analysis

X-ray diffraction data were collected at the beamline BL10U2 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) using a CCD detector. The raw data were processed and scaled using the HKL3000 program [73]. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) entries 5T65 and 6FU4 were used as the templates for PctA-LBD and TlpQ-LBD model building, respectively, in PHENIX [74]. All structural figures were generated in Pymol (http://pymol.org) and protein–ligand interactions were analysed using LigPlot + [75]. Data collection and refinement statistics are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Bioinformatic search for Cff1p homologs in fungi

Searches for homologs of S. cerevisiae Cff1p were performed using the BLASTP program against the NCBI non-redundant protein database with a cutoff E-value of 1E-3 and a coverage threshold of 60%. Protein sequences were further analysed using HMMER (version 3.4) against the Pfam-A database (database version: Pfam35.0) with an E-value cutoff of 1E-5, and only the sequences that possess a Cupin_5 domain were retained for further analysis. These Cupin_5-containing Cff1p homologs were subjected to taxonomic classification by TaxonKit (version 0.20.0) using the NCBI Taxonomy database. The resulting Cff1p homologs are listed in Additional file 2: Table S1.

Identification of dCache_1-containing proteins

61694 dCache_1-containing proteins were downloaded from the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) based on Uniprot release 2024_04. Protein functional domain prediction was performed using HMMER (version 3.4) against the Pfam-A database (database version: Pfam35.0) with an E-value cutoff of 1E-5. The dCache_1-containing proteins were classified based on their predicted output domains. The Uniprot numbers, Pfam Domain architecture, output domains, and their taxonomy are shown in Additional file 2: Table S3.

Illustration of conserved residues using WebLogo 3

1447 Cff1p proteins and 61727 dCache_1 domains from 61694 dCache_1-containing proteins were separately aligned using Muscle v5.1 [76]. Aligned columns not represented in Cff1p of S. cerevisiae (YML079W) and in the dCache_1 domain of PctA were removed and conserved residues were illustrated using WebLogo 3. The amino acid numbering was determined based on the sequences of S. cerevisiae Cff1p and PctA for the sequence logos of Cff1p proteins and dCache_1 domains, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis of fungal Cff1p proteins with two conserved residues

Fungi that possess Cff1p proteins with two conserved residues corresponding to E44 and H96 of S. cerevisiae Cff1p were classified according to the taxonomy. One representative sequence for each genus was selected and the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE (version 2.3.6) based on the Poisson distribution model with 1000 bootstrap replications [77]. The phylogenetic tree was visualised and embellished using the online iTOL (v5) tool (https://itol.embl.de/) [78].

Analysis of the distribution of dCache_1-containing proteins with five conserved residues in prokaryotes

The 7023 dCache_1-containing proteins containing five conserved residues corresponding to R126, W128, Y144, D146, and D173 of PctA were categorised as MCPs, HKs, STKs, SPs, DGCs/PDEs, ACs/GCs, HKs/ACs/GCs, SPs/HKs, and uncharacterised proteins based on their output domains, and the prokaryotes that possess these proteins were grouped according to the taxonomy. The package from ape R was used to generate a sunburst diagram to illustrate the phylogenetic distribution of the prokaryotes containing the functional groups of dCache_1-containing proteins with five conserved residues. The iTOL (v5) tool [78] was used for visualisation of the sunburst diagram.

Purification of membrane proteins

The gene encoding full-length transmembrane DGC A0A0C2BP83 was cloned into pGEX-6P-1 with a GST tag, and the gene encoding full-length transmembrane HK A0A1T5BG79 was cloned into pET-28a with a His6 tag. The constructed recombinant plasmids were transformed into the ΔluxS mutant of E. coli BL21 (DE3). After IPTG induction, cells were harvested and lysed, followed by supernatant collection via centrifugation. Membrane fractions containing A0A0C2BP83 and A0A1T5BG79 were collected by ultracentrifugation at 200,000 × g and 4 °C for 1 h, and then resuspended using a high-salt buffer [59]. Then, they were ultracentrifuged at 350,000 × g for 1 h, and this process was repeated twice. The obtained membrane fractions were further purified with GST-binding resin (Novagen, Madison, WI) or Ni2+-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni2+-NTA) His-binding resin (Novagen, Madison, WI). The protein purity was evaluated by SDS-PAGE. The total protein concentration was measured using the Bradford method. Uncropped images of SDS-PAGE gels are provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S16.

In vitro kinase assay

In vitro kinase assay was carried out as described previously [59, 79]. 5 μM A0A1T5BG79 and 250 μM ATP-γ-S (ab138911, Abcam) were incubated in the presence of 0, 5, 25, or 50 μM MHF in a 20-μl reaction system containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM NaCl, 25 mM KCl, and 1 mM DTT. After reaction at 30 °C for 30 min, 2.5 mM p-nitrobenzyl mesylate (ab 138910, Abcam) was added to alkylate the thiophospholyation site for 60 min at 30 °C, and the reaction was terminated by the addition of 2 × SDS loading buffer. The thiophosphate ester-modified proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with rabbit anti-thiophosphate ester antibody (ab92570, Abcam) for 8–10 h at 4°C. After washing 5 times with TBST at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with a goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (SA00001-2, Proteintech) for 4 h at 4°C. For control, the total protein amount was also determined by incubation first with mouse anti-His antibody (AB0002, Abways), followed by incubation with goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (DY60203, DIYIBIO, China). Signals were detected using the ECL system. Uncropped images of western blots are provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S16.

In vitro DGC activity assay

The in vitro DGC activity of A0A0C2BP83 was determined using an HPLC-based method as described previously [79]. 70 μg of A0A0C2BP83 and 100 μM GTP were incubated in the presence of 0, 100 or 200 μM MHF in a 200-μl reaction system containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) and 5 mM MgCl2. After reaction for 0, 30, and 60 min at 30 °C, 50 μl of the reaction solution was withdrawn at each of the corresponding time points, heated at 100 °C for 5 min, and centrifuged to remove denatured proteins, followed by filtering through a 0.22-μm membrane. The samples were injected into an HPLC system equipped with a C18 reversed-phase column and a UV detector. The mobile phase consisted of 98% A (150 mM Na2HPO4, pH 5.2) and 2% B (acetonitrile) and the flow rate was 1 ml min−1. The detection wavelength was 254 nm. The content of c-di-GMP synthesised in the reaction was calculated from the standard curve established using known concentrations of c-di-GMP (SML1228, Sigma).

Construction of MHF-mediated communication network between fungi and prokaryotes

The potential MHF-producing fungi species possessing predicted MHF synthase and the potential MHF-sensing prokaryotic species possessing predicted dCache_1-type MHF receptors were grouped according to the taxonomy at the genus level. Bacteria and archaea possessing predicted MHF receptors were further grouped according to the functional types of the receptors. Then, we used Cytoscape [80] to construct MHF-mediated potential communication network between 372 fungal genera and 593 prokaryotic genera.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of data was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software or Microsoft Excel 2021. Two groups were compared by the two-tailed, unpaired Student's t-test. Data were presented as mean ± s.d. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
