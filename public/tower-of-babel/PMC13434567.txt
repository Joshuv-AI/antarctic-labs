Fully-connected microwave photonic multi-beamformer with fast beam-steering for broadband wireless communication
Authors: Ziheng Ni, Liangjun Lu, Kai Fu, Haowei Zhang, Tianxiang Wang, Chao Wang, Huashan Yang, Wen Tong, Jianping Chen, Linjie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeamforming technology featuring broad bandwidth, fast beam switching speed and multi-user access capability plays a pivotal role in the next-generation wireless communication systems. Here, we report a fully-connected broadband microwave photonic multi-beamforming architecture based on silicon optical true-time delay lines (OTTDLs). Unlike conventional partially-connected schemes, the fully-connected network allows each radio frequency beam to be synthesized by all antenna elements, enhancing beamforming gain and multi-beam scalability. Experimental measurements demonstrate squint-free beam steering across 13 directions with deflection angles exceeding ±50.0° and a fast beam reconfiguration speed of 60 ns. Compared with partially-connected schemes, our architecture achieves stronger received signals, delivering a 2.9 dB gain at 13.1 GHz for an identical 8-element antenna array. A 64-QAM downlink signal with 400 MHz bandwidth achieves an error vector magnitude (EVM) of 7.6% over 20-m outdoor wireless transmission. These results provide a scalable solution for broadband microwave photonic multi-beamforming and support future 5 G and 6 G wireless networks.Subject terms: Integrated optics, Microwave photonics

Introduction

In the realm of 5 G wireless communication, beamforming technology has played a crucial role in enhancing system performance, ensuring efficient spectrum utilization, and providing high-quality communication services1–3. The necessity for beamforming stems from its ability to direct RF signals with high precision, reducing interference and increasing signal strength4,5. In the context of the next generation wireless communication, beamforming technology must satisfy multifaceted requirements. Firstly, it must support multiple frequency bands to accommodate the diverse spectral allocations across heterogeneous communication scenarios6. Secondly, high-speed beam switching is a critical requirement driven by the stringent timing constraints of communication protocols, which necessitate rapid reconfiguration for ensuring seamless connectivity7–9. For instance, in 5 G NR with 240 kHz subcarrier spacing, the cyclic prefix (CP) duration is only about 290 ns7, implying that practical beam switching should ideally be completed within this interval to avoid symbol distortion. This places stringent demands on the hardware reconfiguration time. Additionally, beamforming systems must facilitate large bandwidths, particularly addressing the challenge of avoiding beam squinting effects10. Finally, the ability to perform multi-beam transmissions is vital to improve throughput and connectivity, especially in dense urban areas where simultaneous multi-user access is required11. These advancements in beamforming are pivotal to unlocking the full potential of 5 G and paving the way for the upcoming 6 G wireless systems.

Beamforming in modern wireless communication systems can be implemented using various architectures, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These include both electronic (Fig. 1a, b) and photonic (Fig. 1c, d) approaches, each offering distinct implementation frameworks and performance characteristics. From a structural perspective, electronic beamforming can be realized via partially-connected12–15 or fully-connected architectures12,14–19. In the partially-connected scheme shown in Fig. 1a, each antenna element is connected to only one RF chain and thus contributes to a single beam. Conversely, the fully-connected architecture illustrated in Fig. 1b establishes a complete interconnection between each antenna element and all RF chains, enabling simultaneous participation in multi-beam formation. This scheme significantly enhances the utilization efficiency of antenna elements. As a result, for a given antenna array size, the fully-connected architecture yields higher beamforming gain, making it particularly advantageous for high-efficiency multi-beam transmission. In fact, as derived in Supplementary Section S1, the per-beam gain of a fully-connected array can be up to N (number of beams) times that of a partially-connected one, provided hardware efficiencies are comparable. In practice, efficiency losses reduce the gain advantage, yet fully-connected architectures still exhibit superior per-beam SNR performance compared with partially-connected architectures. However, electronic fully-connected architectures encounter critical limitations when scaled to support high-capacity, multi-band wireless systems. Firstly, signal crosstalk between closely integrated RF channels becomes increasingly severe due to electromagnetic interference (EMI) and limited isolation in compact circuit layouts, degrading the overall signal fidelity20,21. Secondly, electronic implementations often lack the flexibility to support simultaneous beamforming across widely separated frequency bands, as their analog front-ends and RF circuits are typically narrowband or require complex, power-hungry tunable components22,23.

Microwave photonic beamforming architectures, shown in Fig. 1c, d, are gaining significant attention owing to their inherent immunity to electromagnetic interference and their capability to support multi-band signal processing—features that offer clear advantages over their electronic counterparts. Figure 1c depicts a phase-shifter-based microwave photonic beamforming system24–26, where a laser source is split into multiple channels and modulated via multi-channel optical phase shifters. A reference laser source provides a coherent optical carrier. The phase-modulated signals are optically combined with the reference and converted to microwave signals via photodetection before radiation through the antenna array. Figure 1d presents a distinct microwave photonic beamforming approach based on tunable optical delay lines27–34. Unlike phase shifters, delay lines provide true-time-delay control that inherently eliminates frequency-dependent beam squinting issues35,36—a critical performance metric for ultra-wideband wireless communication systems. Moreover, a unique advantage of microwave photonic beamforming systems is their natural compatibility with optical fiber fronthaul links in centralized radio access networks (C-RANs)37,38. Since both the signal transport and beamforming are performed in the optical domain, microwave photonic beamforming networks can be seamlessly integrated with existing fiber infrastructures, enabling centralized processing, wideband transmission, and support for multiple frequency bands. This integration simplifies the overall system architecture, reduces RF hardware overhead at radio remote units (RRUs), and offers a unified photonic platform for both data transport and spatial signal processing.

Although several optical solutions for multi-beamforming have been proposed39–42, most current implementations still suffer from critical trade-offs between bandwidth, switching speed, and beamforming connectivity. Specifically, existing microwave photonic beamforming systems often lack a fully-connected architecture, limiting element utilization efficiency, or rely on phase-shifting mechanisms that introduce beam squint under wideband operation. Furthermore, few demonstrations have successfully achieved wideband multi-beamforming with fast dynamic switching41–45—capabilities that are essential for meeting the demanding requirements of next-generation mobile standards such as 3GPP 5 G NR and future 6 G networks. The CP-level timing constraint further underscores the importance of achieving sub-300 ns reconfiguration, making fast beam switching not only desirable but mandatory for protocol compatibility.

In this work, we introduce a novel microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming architecture based on optical true time delay lines (OTTDLs) designed to meet the stringent requirements of the next generation wireless communication systems. By employing wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) and multi-channel OTTDLs, our system enables simultaneous wideband multi-beamforming with full utilization of all antenna elements, thereby significantly improving beamforming gain and beam directivity. Under the identical antenna array conditions (8-element array), the fully-connected configuration achieves a signal magnitude gain of 2.9 dB at 13.1 GHz relative to the partially-connected case. Unlike conventional phase-shifting architectures, our design offers frequency-independent delay control, eliminating beam squint and supporting ultra-wide instantaneous bandwidths. Crucially, the proposed architecture achieves nanosecond-scale beam reconfiguration with a measured switching time of just 60 ns—satisfying the stringent beam reconfiguration speed requirements of 3GPP 5 G NR protocols7–9. Experimental validation in a microwave anechoic chamber demonstrates the effectiveness of our design, with precise beam steering across 13 distinct directions over a broad frequency range of 12.4–13.6 GHz. Real-time wireless communication tests using a 400-MHz-bandwidth 64 quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) signal further confirm the high transmission quality enabled by our architecture. These advancements position our architecture as a scalable, high-speed solution for broadband, squint-free beamforming in the next-generation wireless communication networks, effectively addressing the challenges of multi-beamforming and fast switching time.

Results

Microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming network

Figure 2a illustrates the proposed microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming network for transmitting N beams with a shared phased array antenna. In general, a fully-connected beamforming network is defined as an architecture in which every input RF chain is connected to all antenna elements through a reconfigurable network of phase shifters, combiners, or delay elements, thereby allowing each antenna element to simultaneously contribute to the formation of multiple independent beams. This general definition highlights the essential feature of full connectivity, while the proposed scheme further exploits microwave photonic techniques to realize such a network with improved bandwidth, multi-band capability, and reduced crosstalk.

In the central unit (CU), N-channel baseband signals were first converted into analog signals by high-speed digital-to-analog converters (DACs) and subsequently upconverted to intermediate RF frequencies to generate N-channel RF chains. The generated RF signals are then modulated into the optical domain by N directly modulated lasers (DMLs), with each operating at a distinct wavelength (λ₁ to λN, corresponding to beams 1 to N, respectively). After modulation, the N analog optical signals are multiplexed using an N × 1 WDM into a single optical channel, and then transmitted via an optical fiber to an RRU located at the transmission tower. Upon arrival at the RRU, the multiplexed optical signal is demultiplexed by a 1 × N WDM, separating the different wavelength channels (where λ₁ to λN remain uniquely mapped to beams 1 to N), with each corresponding to a unique transmit beam. Each demultiplexed optical signal is subsequently passed through a 1 × M optical power splitter and divided into M branches, corresponding to the M antenna elements of the phased array. These split optical signals are fed into microwave photonic beamforming chips, where each chip contains M OTTDLs for adjusting the delays of all the optical channels. By controlling the time delay differences between the channels, the direction of the resultant microwave beams can be dynamically adjusted. The chips operate as distinct functional modules, and the optical outputs associated with the same antenna element are interconnected externally beyond the chip boundary, which ensures modularity, scalability, and compatibility with different phased array sizes. An increase in the number of delay lines allows for the expansion of the antenna array and the number of beams that can be formed. The M × N split optical signals are then combined through M N × 1 WDMs. The combined signals are fed into M photodetectors (PDs) to convert to microwave signals. These converted microwave signals are then radiated through the shared phased array antenna. The direction of each beam can be independently controlled by adjusting the corresponding OTTDLs, ensuring that the beams do not interfere with each other. In contrast to traditional electronic methods discussed earlier, the proposed microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming network offers the advantages of eliminating inter-channel crosstalk and supporting multi-band microwave frequencies. Furthermore, the use of OTTDLs for phase shifting the microwave signals avoids the beam squinting issue, a common issue in conventional electronic systems. A detailed scalability analysis of the proposed fully-connected beamforming network is provided in Supplementary S2.

The silicon microwave photonic beamformer chip used in the system consists of eight identical OTTDLs based on the 6-bit switchable delay line structure, as shown in the inset of Fig. 1a. Each OTTDL can realize delay tuning from 0 to 310.59 ps with a delay step Δt of 4.93 ps. Each OTTDL includes 7 MZI-based electro-optic (EO) switches to digitally select the delay paths with different lengths to realize discrete delay tuning. As shown in the inset in Fig. 2a, the EO switch in the OTTDL has the same structure as ref. 46 and comprises two 2 × 2 multimode interferometers (MMIs) interconnected by two waveguide arms. A TiN microheater is integrated on one arm for initial phase biasing, while two PIN-diode phase shifters are implemented on both arms for push–pull EO modulation46. The high-speed EO unit leveraging the free-carrier dispersion (FCD) effect of the PIN-diodes ensures rapid beam switching of <100 ns, addressing the critical ultra-low latency demands for real-time beam management in the next-generation wireless systems. However, free-carrier absorption (FCA) in the EO unit inherently accompanies the refractive index change, leading to an additional power imbalance between the two MZI arms that exacerbates crosstalk of the EO unit. To mitigate this, as designed in ref. 47, we exploit the TO microheater to bias at the quadrature point (phase difference is π/2), and thus only a π/2-phase shift is required for either of the EO phase shifters to realize fast switching between the cross and bar states. As shown in the inset of Fig. 2a, the P++ (N++) region of the upper phase shifter is connected with the N++ (P++) region of the bottom phase shifter. By applying a negative or a positive voltage to port 5 and port 6, only one of the PIN-diode-based phase shifters is turned on, enabling push-pull EO switching47. The delay waveguides are designed with 2-µm-wide ridge waveguides to reduce the transmission loss and delay errors induced by fabrication deviation. We also insert two variable optical attenuators (VOAs), controlled by PIN diodes, before each pair of delay waveguides to enable delay-state calibration and suppress leaked noise. The detailed design of the chip can be seen in Supporting Information S3. Figure 2b shows the microscope image of the fabricated chip with a footprint of 10.90 mm × 3.76 mm. Figure 2c shows the chip after packaging.

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed 8-channel OTTDL chip, we conducted an in-depth characterization of key metrics, including insertion loss, switching response time, group delay accuracy, and power consumption. Before the measurement, we implemented a calibration procedure to ensure the proper functioning of all the delay lines (for details, see Supplementary Information S4). Figure 3a presents the fiber-to-fiber insertion loss, including coupling loss, measured at a wavelength of 1555 nm across all 64 delay states for each of the 8 channels on the chip. Leveraging the low-loss TO biasing and the push-pull switching scheme, the chip exhibits a low insertion loss of <10.0 dB at the 1555 nm wavelength. The loss variation between different channels is 1.3 dB, mainly attributable to coupling discrepancies during the chip packaging. To further understand the origin of the insertion loss, we performed a detailed breakdown analysis based on device-level and wafer-level measurements (Supplementary Information S5). Each electro-optic switch exhibits an insertion loss of approximately 0.90 dB (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Since each delay line channel contains seven such switches, this contributes a total switch loss of ~6.30 dB. The average propagation loss of the 2-μm-wide delay waveguides is 0.15 dB/cm (Supplementary Fig. 7a), and the longest delay path spans approximately 2.79 cm, resulting in a waveguide loss of ~0.42 dB. The fiber-to-chip coupling loss after packaging is 1.70 dB/facet, leading to a total coupling loss of 3.40 dB. Summing these contributions, the total insertion loss per channel is estimated as 6.30 dB (EO switches) + 0.42 dB (straight waveguide) + 0.17 dB(Euler bend waveguide) + 3.40 dB (coupling) = 10.29 dB, which aligns well with the measured values shown in Fig. 3a. The complete optical transmission spectra for all 64 delay states across the 8 channels are provided in Supplementary Information S7.

Figure 3b presents the temporal response of delay state reconfiguration for OTTDLs and the control module. The blue curve represents the applied electrical control pulse, while the red curve indicates the corresponding optical delay response. The measured switching time of approximately 60 ns includes both the intrinsic optical response time of the MZIs and the latency introduced by the electrical driver circuitry. Although individual electro-optic MZI elements can achieve sub-10-nanosecond switching speeds (Supplementary Fig. 8b), the overall delay line response is slower due to signal propagation delays through the control network, sequential triggering of multiple MZI stages, and the rise/fall characteristics of the electrical control signals. Nonetheless, the sub-100-nanosecond reconfiguration speed satisfies the requirements for real-time beam steering in next-generation wireless communication systems, where rapid adaptive control is critical.

Figure 3c shows the measured group delay responses of all 64 delay states of the first channel OTTDL when the VOAs are on. Due to the suppressed in-channel noises, the delay spectra have a flat response over the measured frequency range. The complete S21 responses and group delay spectra for all 64 delay states across the 8 channels are provided in Supplementary Figs. 11–13, respectively. Figure 3d shows the delay error statistics of all 64 delay states of the 8-channel OTTDLs at the 43.5 GHz frequency. The delay error, defined as the deviation from target delay values, is less than 0.6 ps for all the states. The low delay error is primarily attributed to the use of wide-ridge waveguide structures, which exhibit reduced sensitivity to fabrication-induced width variations. As a result, the group delay remains highly stable across fabricated OTTDLs, enabling accurate and repeatable delay control, critical for broadband beamforming applications.

The power consumption of the 8-channel 6-bit OTTDLs mainly comes from the TiN microheaters, the PIN phase shifters in the optical switches, and the VOAs. The power consumption for thermal phase adjustment and EO switching of each switch is ~10 mW and ~2.85 mW, respectively. The power consumption of each VOA is ~80 mW. Under typical operation conditions with 56 active switches and 48 active VOAs, the total power consumption of the chip is ∼4.56 W. In the prototype, VOAs are employed mainly for calibration and power management within the optical beamforming network. They are used to fine-tune and verify the delay states of the optical switching delay line and to suppress residual optical leakage caused by the limited extinction ratio of high-speed electro-optic switches. To reduce system-level power consumption, alternative nonintrusive delay-state characterization and control techniques that eliminate the need for VOAs have been reported48,49. In parallel, improving the extinction ratio of electro-optic switches offers a complementary route to suppress crosstalk intrinsically, thereby enabling the complete removal of VOAs and a substantial reduction in system-level power consumption. Recent studies have explored improved electro-optic switch designs to mitigate crosstalk without relying on external attenuation components50. These experimental results collectively demonstrate the low-loss, fast-switching, highly accurate, and power-efficient characteristics of the OTTDLs, highlighting their potential for scalable, high-performance microwave photonic beamforming in broadband wireless communication systems.

Microwave photonic beam-steering and multi-beam wireless communication

To experimentally validate the beam-steering performance of the proposed chip, we constructed a beam pattern measurement system in a microwave anechoic chamber, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. Figure 4b shows the photograph of the complete experimental setup within the microwave chamber. Figure 4c presents the simulated radiation patterns using the measured delay characteristics of the OTTDLs, with antenna spacing set to half-wavelength at the highest frequency of 40 GHz to optimize for high-frequency operation. Under a fixed inter-channel delay difference of 4.93 ps, the main lobes across 12-40 GHz maintain consistent direction at 23.2°, confirming squint-free performance. Additional simulated beam patterns at steering angles of 52.1° are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. Importantly, Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 13 report the measured delay spectra of the OTTDLs, indicating an operational bandwidth of 3–43.5 GHz. Therefore, the delay line exhibits multi-frequency capability. Wireless communication systems typically demand support for multiple frequency bands. Our delay lines, capable of covering an extensive frequency range, can be seamlessly adapted to different bands with just a simple antenna replacement. Figure 4d, e present the experimental results of the beam directionality test for various frequency signals, with a testing frequency range spanning from 12.4 GHz to 13.6 GHz, spaced by 0.1 GHz intervals. Within this measured frequency band, the main-beam direction remains frequency-independent, consistent with the theory. The relatively narrow 1.2-GHz measurement span is primarily constrained by the antenna bandwidth—chosen for high-gain operation around 13 GHz—rather than by the optical delay line itself. Notably, measurement data verifies that our delay lines support a broad frequency range of 3–43.5 GHz (Supplementary Fig. 13). The results highlight the beam steering performance for different inter-channel delay differences, ranging from −6 × 4.93 ps to 6 × 4.93 ps. Specifically, Fig. 4d shows the beam pattern when the inter-channel delay difference is 0 ps. Figure 4e shows the dynamic beam steering under the delay differences of ±4.93 ps, ±3 × 4.93 ps, and ±6 × 4.93 ps, respectively, consistent with theoretical predictions for the 8-element phased array. Other measured beam radiation patterns under other delay differences are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 15. The experimental results demonstrate the system’s capability for precise beam steering and confirm the effectiveness of the proposed architecture in practical microwave photonic beamforming applications. Although the proposed 6-bit delay-line architecture theoretically supports up to 19 discrete steering states, the number of experimentally reported angles is constrained by both the quantitative accuracy of the planar near-field to far-field transformation (PNFFT) method at highly oblique angles and the physical visibility bound imposed by the chosen center frequency and antenna spacing. In particular, at the experimental center frequency of 13 GHz (d = λ/2 at 13 GHz), only delay indices k = −7… + 7 correspond to physically realizable main beams, while larger indices would imply |sin θ| > 1 and thus cannot form a visible beam. These considerations are discussed in detail in Supplementary S8.

To assess the practical viability of our microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming network, we conducted outdoor wireless communication tests, with the experimental system architecture depicted in Fig. 5a. Figure 5b compares the multi-beam transmission performance of the fully-connected architecture used in this experiment with a conventional partially-connected architecture. The fully-connected scheme not only supports simultaneously, independently controlled multi-beam transmission, but also achieves higher beamforming gain and narrower beamwidth due to more efficient utilization of antenna elements. Figure 5c compares the measured magnitude of the downlink RF signal received by user end2 at a beam angle of 30.8° under two configurations: a partially-connected architecture in which each beam is formed by only 4 antenna elements, and a fully-connected architecture in which all 8 antenna elements participate in the beamforming of every beam. Under the identical antenna array conditions (8-element array), the fully-connected configuration achieves a signal magnitude gain of 2.9 dB at 13.1 GHz relative to the partially-connected case. This gain reflects the improved beamforming performance enabled by the coherent signal combination from all array elements. Additionally, the two beams generated in the experiment exhibit a full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of approximately 12.7° at broadside (0°) and 14.7° at 30.8° under the fully connected configuration, respectively, demonstrating strong angular resolution across directions. In contrast, under a partially-connected architecture, the corresponding FWHMs increase to 24.3° and 25.2° at the same respective angles, revealing a significant degradation in beam sharpness. This widening of the beamwidth can be attributed to the reduced aperture size in the partially-connected architecture. Figure 5d presents the corresponding 64-QAM constellation diagram for the downlink signal (400 MHz bandwidth), achieving an EVM of 7.6% under the fully-connected scheme. The high constellation fidelity confirms that the architecture can support high-order modulation with minimal signal degradation. For more detailed communication test results, including comprehensive single-beam and multi-beam performance evaluations conducted in a controlled anechoic environment, refer to Supplementary S9. These results validate the superior signal quality, beam directivity, and spectral efficiency enabled by the fully connected approach, making it well-suited for scalable, high-capacity 5 G and future wireless systems.

Discussion

In this work, we present a practical implementation of a microwave photonic fully-connected beamforming network, with its performance quantitatively benchmarked against state-of-the-art approaches in Table 1. The system has been successfully demonstrated in real-world wireless communication tests, achieving a transmission distance of ~20 m with 64QAM modulation. Compared to traditional beamforming methods, our approach not only supports a wide operational frequency range (3–43.5 GHz) but also exhibits superior electromagnetic interference immunity, effectively addressing the crosstalk limitations of electronic systems. As detailed in Table 1, our architecture demonstrates fully-connected capability with independent multi-beam control—a feature not achieved in most existing OTTDL implementations. The system experimentally achieves nanosecond-scale switching (60 ns) and squint-free beam steering across ±50.3° with 13 resolvable angles, significantly exceeding the performance of recently reported alternatives. Notably, our work establishes the first fully-connected microwave photonic multi-beamforming system that can support independent beam control while maintaining a wide instantaneous bandwidth and sub-100 ns switching speed. Consequently, this microwave photonic beamformer provides a comprehensive solution for future high-capacity wireless communication systems.

Methods

Fabrication and packaging of the eight-channel OTTDL chip

The chip was fabricated on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) platform with a 220-nm-thick top silicon layer and a 3-μm buried oxide (BOX) layer using the standard silicon photonic processes. The fabricated chip was then packaged by the SJTU-Pinghu Institute of Intelligent Optoelectronics. The metal pads on the chip were first connected to the PCB using gold wire-bonding for external electrical control. A pair of optical fiber arrays was then coupled with the chip and fixed with ultraviolet (UV) curable adhesive. The coupling loss after the package is 1.70 dB/facet. The chip and the PCB were mounted on a metal baseplate that incorporates a thermoelectric cooler (TEC) and a closed-loop temperature controller to maintain stable operation. The TEC provides up to 25 W of cooling capacity at an ambient temperature of 25 °C, which sufficiently compensates the chip’s typical power dissipation of approximately 4.56 W. These measures ensure stable PIN biasing and EO switching performance.

Optical transmission measurement

We employed a tunable laser source capable of sweeping across the 1520–1580 nm wavelength range to characterize the insertion loss and transmission spectra of the chip. A polarization controller (PC) was used to adjust the polarization state of the input light to the transverse electric (TE) polarization before coupling it into the input port of the chip. The output light from each channel was directed to an optical power meter to record the transmitted optical power. We sequentially tested all 8 output channels of the chip. For each channel, we measured the optical transmission spectra under 64 distinct delay states. The complete experimental setup is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9a.

OTTDLs’ switching time measurement

A single-wavelength continuous-wave (CW) laser operating at 1550 nm served as the optical source. The optical signal was processed by the OTTDLs and then amplified by an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) to compensate for the on-chip and coupling losses. The amplified optical signal was directed to a high-speed PD for optical-to-electrical conversion. The electrical output from the PD was connected to one channel of the oscilloscope. Meanwhile, a second oscilloscope channel received a control pulse signal from the chip’s control module, which served as a timing reference for delay state switching. This control pulse corresponded to the electrical voltage changes applied to the optical switch elements and VOAs in the OTTDL and triggered the transition between delay states. Following the control pulse, transient intensity fluctuations in the PD’s electrical signal were observed, indicating the dynamic reconfiguration of the delay state. Once the signal intensity stabilized, the delay state was considered successfully switched. The time interval from the control pulse to signal stabilization was defined as the delay line switching time. The complete experimental setup is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 9b.

Electrical S21 measurement

For the S21 measurements, a single-wavelength CW laser source (λ = 1550 nm) was employed as the optical carrier. The output of the laser was passed through a PC to align the polarization state, and was then fed into a commercial Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM), where it was intensity-modulated by the RF signal generated from port 1 of a vector network analyzer (VNA). The modulated optical signal was subsequently injected into the input port of the photonic beamforming chip under test. The optical signal was processed by the OTTDLs and then amplified using an EDFA to compensate for on-chip and coupling losses. After amplification, the signal was detected by a high-speed PD and sent to port 2 of the VNA, completing the S21 measurement loop. The VNA measured both the amplitude and phase responses of the entire electrical-optical-electrical link, including the response of the OTTDL chip. To fully assess the chip’s beamforming performance, we sequentially measured all 8 channels across 64 delay states per channel. The complete experimental setup is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9c.

Experimental setup for microwave photonic beam-steering

A narrow-linewidth CW laser functioned as the optical carrier, modulated by the MZM driven by an RF signal from the VNA. The modulated optical signal was then split into eight channels via a 1 × 8 optical power splitter. Each channel was routed into our chip, which introduced adjustable differential delays in the optical domain. These delayed optical signals were subsequently amplified by a multi-channel EDFA and converted back to RF signals through PDs. The resulting eight RF signals were fed into an 8-element phased array antenna with an element pitch of 11.54 mm (half-wavelength at the center frequency of 13 GHz) for beam steering. All measurements were conducted in a microwave anechoic chamber to eliminate interference from external RF signals and ensure a controlled testing environment. On the receiving end, a horn antenna was employed as a probe to capture the radiated signal from the phased array. The received signal was routed back to the VNA, which measured the S21 scattering parameter to extract both amplitude and phase, thereby enabling accurate reconstruction of the radiation patterns.

Experimental setup for microwave photonic multi-beam wireless communication

Two independent RF data streams were generated at the CU through digital baseband processing, where user-specific data was first encoded and digitally modulated (e.g., using QAM). The modulated digital signals were then converted to analog form via DACs, upconverted to an intermediate frequency of 13 GHz, and subsequently used to directly modulate two DMLs operating at ITU-T channels C21 (1560.61 nm) and C25 (1557.36 nm), respectively. The modulated WDM optical signals were combined and transmitted over approximately 1 km of single-mode optical fiber to a remote RRU located at a transmission tower. At the RRU, the multiplexed optical signals were demultiplexed into two separate wavelength channels, each corresponding to a distinct beam. Each channel was further split by a 1 × 8 optical power splitter into eight identical power paths and then routed into an 8-channel OTTDL chip. These delay lines provided precise, programmable time delays between channels in the optical domain, enabling beamforming and steering. After a delay, the 16 optical signals (8 per beam) were amplified using a multi-channel EDFA and recombined using 8 WDMs into eight optical streams. These streams were converted into RF signals via high-speed PDs and radiated through the 8-element phased array antenna. On the receiver side, a 4 × 4 antenna array with a center frequency of 13 GHz and an approximate antenna gain of 16 dB was deployed at a distance of approximately 20 m from the transmit antenna array. In the experiment, two beams were simultaneously transmitted at the steering angles of 0° and 30.8°, respectively. The 4 × 4 receiving antenna array was placed at a fixed position 20 m away from the transmitter. For each beam, both the transmitting and receiving antenna arrays were mechanically adjusted in azimuth to ensure optimal alignment—i.e., to maximize the received signal power. Once the alignment was optimized for each beam, the received signals were recorded. The received signals were further amplified by low-noise amplifiers (LNAs) and processed by an electrical spectrum analyzer (ESA), enabling assessment of signal quality under realistic transmission conditions. The complete system of the receiver is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9d.
