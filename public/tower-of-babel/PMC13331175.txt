Prevalence of chameleons in patients with ischemic stroke: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Nury Tatiana Rincón Cuenca, Andrés Felipe Herrera Ortiz, Miguel Angel Ruiz, Laura María Camargo, Cesar Alejandro Luzardo Neira, Luisa Fernanda Velasquez Bejarano, Angie Melissa Montoya Caro, Santiago Andrés Monroy Santos, Jaime Eduardo Rodriguez, Carlos Fernando Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Stroke is a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, with timely identification being crucial for effective treatment. However, some patients present as “stroke chameleons” (SC) displaying atypical stroke symptoms that mimic other conditions, often leading to delayed diagnosis and poorer outcomes. This study aims to assess the prevalence and clinical characteristics of SC in a comprehensive stroke center in Colombia.Methods: This cross-sectional study involved ischemic stroke patients admitted to a high-level medical center in Bogotá, Colombia, from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023. We identified SC, defined as patients with a confirmed acute stroke in whom a stroke was not initially suspected, as indicated by a negative Cincinnati prehospital stroke scale and no activation of the stroke protocol. We evaluated the proportion of SC, their clinical and imaging characteristics, and compared them with non-SC patients.Results: Among 635 ischemic stroke patients, 78 (12.2%) were identified as SC, while 557 (87.8%) were identified as non-SC. SC exhibited a lower National Institutes of Health stroke scale score and were more likely to present with altered mental status (38% vs. 24%, p = 0.008), dizziness (30% vs. 16%, p = 0.004), and behavioral changes (8% vs. 2%, p = 0.005). SC displayed less middle cerebral artery involvement (24% vs. 56%, p = 0.047) and more multi-territory involvement (34% vs. 17%, p <0.001). In addition, SC had a higher rate of normal computed tomography scans.Conclusion: SC are a significant subset of ischemic strokes, presenting with atypical symptoms that complicate the diagnosis. Recognizing these non-traditional presentations is essential for timely diagnosis, treatment, and potentially improved outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Stroke ranks as the third leading cause of death globally, resulting in approximately 7.3 million fatalities each year. In addition, it is recognized as the fourth leading cause of death and disability combined, with around 11.9 million individuals suffering a stroke annually. Despite ongoing efforts to reduce the risk of stroke through lifestyle changes and adequate blood pressure management, it remains a significant contributor to global mortality, disability, and healthcare costs.[9]

The prompt and accurate identification of a stroke is essential for initiating time-sensitive reperfusion therapies, such as thrombolysis and thrombectomy, and for implementing early secondary prevention measures.[19] These interventions have been shown to reduce the risk of recurrence and improve clinical outcomes. Errors in stroke diagnosis include overestimation or false positives due to stroke-like symptoms (called stroke mimics) and underestimation or false negatives from atypical presentations (referred to as stroke chameleons [SC]), with the latter causing significant delays in diagnosis and treatment.[1]

SC may initially present with ambiguous or subtle symptoms, making diagnosis challenging. These symptoms can include psychosis, weakness, encephalopathy, and altered mental status.[4] Their frequency has not been well studied[1]; some studies have reported a prevalence of around 13%,[8,10,12] while others have observed wide variability, with rates ranging from 2% to 26%.[4,10,16] In addition, because SC are less likely to be thoroughly investigated and treated, they can experience worse outcomes, including higher rates of disability and mortality at 12 months.[10] Here, we present a single-center cross-sectional study reporting the prevalence of SC in a group of patients admitted to a comprehensive stroke center in Bogotá, Colombia, from January 2021, to December 31, 2023. In addition, we reported their clinical features and associated factors compared to non-SC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023, using convenience sampling to collect data from the Stroke Center database of a fourth-level medical center in Bogotá, Colombia. The study aimed to identify all patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke and determine the prevalence of SC in this population. It followed the strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology guidelines. The local ethics committee approved this research, ensuring compliance with national legislation (Article 5, Law 8430 of 1993) and adherence to the principles outlined in the Helsinki declaration. Since data collection occurred after patient discharge and all sensitive information, as well as any information that could identify the patient, was removed, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Participants

We included all patients aged 18 and older who were confirmed to have an ischemic stroke. These patients were required to undergo neuroimaging, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scans, which confirmed the presence of acute or early subacute ischemic lesions. We excluded patients who were concurrently diagnosed with hemorrhagic stroke, subdural or epidural hematoma, pregnant individuals, and those in the immediate, recent, or late postpartum periods. Furthermore, patients who were under sedation and receiving mechanical ventilation were also excluded from the study.

We included only patients with a negative CPSS at triage (explicitly documented or implied), as a positive CPSS should prompt stroke suspicion. Patients with a positive CPSS who were not suspected of having a stroke were considered cases of misrecognition of a typical presentation rather than SC and were classified accordingly in this study.

Previous studies have indicated a prevalence of SC at approximately 13%.[8,10,12] Based on this information, we calculated the sample size using a precision level and an alpha value of 5%. We applied the formula: Alpha value constant × prevalence (1-prevalence)/precision.[14] Recognizing that the primary outcome is expressed as a proportion, we determined the final sample size to be 174 patients. We decided to use the whole database of patients, containing 681 patients, exceeding this minimal value.

Data extraction, variables, and missing data

Clinical and demographic data were gathered from the EHR. Past medical history, including hypertension, dementia, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, and alcohol consumption also retrieved. Signs and symptoms were categorized in: Dizziness, syncope, seizures, paresthesia/ dysesthesia, dysarthria, dysphagia, headache, altered mental status (agitation, somnolence, and stupor), confusion (disorientation, cognitive changes, incoherent speech, delirium, or muddled thoughts), behavioral changes (labile mood, depression, easy crying, hetero-aggressiveness, abulia, or inhibition), visual disturbances blurred vision, scotomas, amaurosis, diplopia, and nystagmus); and movement disorders (hemiballismus, chorea, dyskinesias, limb-shaking palsy, and myoclonus). Each patient could be included in more than one category if presented with multiple signs or symptoms. Symptoms from the CPSS were not included as we considered its presence an exclusion from the SC group. CT and/or MRI were reviewed to identify the ischemic territory. When missing data constituted <20%, hot deck or mean imputation was applied according to the nature of the variable.[11]

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to determine the proportion of SC at a Latin American reference institution. The secondary objective was to identify the symptoms most commonly associated with this condition. The third objective was to analyze the differences between patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke on admission and those classified as SC.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using the software STATA 17 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX) and IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics for Macintosh, version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative variables were assessed for normality using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. They were then described with measures of central tendency (mean or median) and dispersion (standard deviation or interquartile range). The qualitative variables of interest were presented in terms of absolute and relative frequency distributions. The Chi-square test was used to compare the proportion of SC in this study with that reported in the literature. The association analysis included calculating unadjusted odds ratios (OR) and the Chi-square test, and applying a multiple logistic regression model. Statistical significance was defined at a p < 0.05.

Primary objective

The database will be checked to identify those corresponding to SC and those correctly diagnosed with acute or subacute ischemic stroke. This will enable the calculation of the proportion of SC in patients with acute or subacute ischemic stroke.

Secondary objective

Among patients classified as SC, the most frequent symptoms will be recorded, categorized, and expressed as absolute and relative frequencies.

Tertiary objective

The differences in characteristics between patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke upon admission and those classified as SC will be assessed. Logistic regression will be performed for categorical variables, adjusting for potential confounders. Linear regression will be used for continuous variables, adjusting for confounding factors. Results will be reported as P-values and OR. Statistical significance will be considered when the p < 0.05 and the OR confidence interval excludes 1,0.

DISCUSSION

Our study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and clinical characteristics of SC patients. Among the 635 ischemic stroke patients included in our sample, 12.2% were identified as SC, which aligns with the literature reporting a prevalence of approximately 13%.[8,10,12] However, this figure may still be underestimated, as many patients might remain undiagnosed.

A notable difference between the SC and non-SC groups is their clinical presentation. The SC group exhibited a lower NIHSS score, which is to be expected as the NIHSS is skewed to traditional MCA stroke symptoms. As previously reported, there was a significantly higher likelihood of presenting with non-traditional stroke symptoms or non-focal neurological symptoms[16] such as altered mental status, headache, behavioral changes, and movement disorders. Previous studies report altered alertness as the most common symptom, with a prevalence ranging from 20% to 35.3%.[2,4,8]

There was a high rate of dizziness among SC (29.5% vs. 16%, p = 0.004). This is not surprising as the differentiation between peripheral and central vertigo is often challenging to do, even for trained physicians using proven clinical techniques such as the HINTS examination.[15] Behavioral changes (7.7% vs. 1.8%, p = 0.004), and movement disorders (12.8% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.011) are potentially focal but uncommon symptoms,[5] and were somewhat frequent, similar to previous reports suggesting that these symptoms occur in about 10% of SC cases,[2] These atypical symptoms, as highlighted in the literature, can obscure the underlying diagnosis of ischemic stroke and potentially delay crucial interventions.

In contrast, the non-SC group presented with higher NIHSS scores and more classic stroke symptoms, such as dysarthria (56.4% vs. 15.4%, p = 0.001) and dysphagia (16.2% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.004), a potentially focal symptom,[3,18] which are often more readily recognized as indicators of stroke but may also be present in several differential diagnoses.

Our study identified significant differences in the affected ischemic territories. The SC group demonstrated a lower incidence of MCA involvement (24.4% vs. 56.4%, p ≤ 0.001) and a higher rate of ischemia across multiple territories (33.3% vs. 17.1%, p ≤ 0.001). In addition, SC showed increased involvement of the ACA and the PICA. This finding aligns with existing literature, which reports that 37% of posterior circulation strokes are initially missed, compared to only 16% for anterior circulation strokes.[1,2,8]

The low diagnostic yield of the CT scan for both groups underscores the importance of the clinical suspicion of stroke in the context of acute or subacute neurological syndromes. It is well recognized that early ischemic changes may be difficult to detect on CT, especially if performed very early or in cases of low-volume infarcts.[6,7,13,17] In this context, maintaining clinical suspicion, considering repeat imaging, and performing MRI when available are key elements for appropriate diagnosis, particularly in patients with atypical stroke presentations. However, in many middle- and low-income settings, access to MRI is limited, and CT remains the primary imaging modality guiding initial decision-making. Therefore, strengthening clinical recognition of atypical presentations becomes essential.

Strokes affecting less common arterial territories, such as the posterior circulation, often present with greater clinical variability and less typical lateralizing focal signs, which contributes to diagnostic difficulty. In contrast, MCA strokes typically present with more recognizable symptoms, such as motor weakness and speech disturbances. The more diffuse and non-clearly focal nature of symptoms in these cases adds complexity to the diagnostic process and contributes to the chameleon phenomenon. In this context, targeted training and continuous education in emergency settings, including triage personnel, nursing staff, and physicians, may play a critical role in improving early recognition of atypical stroke presentations and reducing the incidence of SC.

This study faces potential biases that could impact its findings. First, limiting the study to patients from a single institution may reduce the external validity of the results. Using a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method is another concern, as it inherently introduces selection biases. Furthermore, the variability in clinical experience among the clinicians evaluating patients upon admission could lead to diagnostic errors, affecting the proportion of cases identified as SC. This concern was mitigated by considering a negative CPSS as a requirement for classifying a patient as an SC, since a positive CPSS should raise concern for a stroke in most of the patients. Moreover, the analysis accounted for confounding factors affecting both the diagnosed ischemic stroke and the SC groups, particularly for the tertiary objective, thereby reducing potential confounding bias. By implementing these measures, the study aims to strengthen its validity and overall reliability.

A strength of this study is that our center is a private high-level reference institution with wide access to both CT scans and MRI, and most of the patients with a potentially neurologic pathology will have one or both images on time, facilitating the detection of strokes even when the diagnosis is initially overlooked. In addition, the ED team undergoes regular training in stroke recognition, reducing the likelihood of misdiagnosing typical stroke syndromes.

CONCLUSION

This study shows that SC are frequent in clinical practice, with a prevalence of 12.2%. Male sex, non-focal neurological signs, uncommon stroke symptoms, multi-territorial, ACA or PICA circulation involvement, and low NIHSS scores were more frequently associated with SC. CT scan was negative in a significant proportion of patients (both SC and non-SC). Non-MCA and multiple-territory strokes were also more common in SC. This information is valuable to improve awareness and reduce misdiagnosis in non-classic ischemic stroke patients.
