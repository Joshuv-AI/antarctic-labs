Irreducibility of Semigroup Morphisms
Paul. C. Bell, Eva Foster, Daniel Reidenbach

Abstract
We study the notion of irreducibility of semigroup morphisms. Given an alphabet Σ \Sigma , a morphism φ : Σ + → Σ + \varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+} is irreducible if any factorisation φ = ψ 2 ∘ ψ 1 \varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1} can only be satisfied if ψ 1 \psi_{1} or ψ 2 \psi_{2} is a trivial morphism. Otherwise, φ \varphi is reducible. We introduce the notion of irreducibility, characterise this property and study a number of fundamental questions on the concepts under consideration.



1
Introduction

A morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, defined for an alphabet
\Sigma
is
irreducible
if the equality
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, implies that
\psi_{1}
or
\psi_{2}
is an automorphism, e.g., a renaming. Irreducibility provides a natural notion of primitivity in the endomorphism monoid of the free semigroup, similar to irreducibility in other algebraic structures.

Composition and decomposition of morphisms has been studied in various forms. Ehrenfeucht and Rozenberg
[
6
]
explore the notion of
simplifiable
and
elementary
homomorphisms, where a non-injective morphism
\varphi:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{3}^{+}
is simplifiable if it can be expressed as
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
, where
\psi_{1}:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}^{+}
,
\psi_{2}:\Sigma_{2}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{3}^{+}
and
|\Sigma_{2}|<\min\{|\Sigma_{1}|,|\Sigma_{3}|\}
. A morphism is elementary if it is not simplifiable. We highlight the restriction that the intermediate alphabet (
\Sigma_{2}
) of the composition is strictly smaller than the domain and range alphabets of
\varphi
. Their work proves that it is decidable to determine if a given morphism is elementary, but does not investigate decompositions without an alphabet reduction, nor do they investigate the non-uniqueness of factorisations. Similarly, Berstel, Perrin and Reutenauer
[
12
]
investigate the notion of
indecomposable codes
. In contrast to Ehrenfeucht and Rozenberg, they admit an equivalent to prime factorisation under composition, though their framework again does not specify that all alphabets considered must coincide.

In the present paper, we also study the decomposition of morphisms, but we mostly restrict ourselves to Parikh-positive morphisms – that is, the images of
\varphi
contain every letter of the alphabet it is defined for at least once. This restriction means that the domain, range and all intermediate alphabets coincide and allows us to consider more naturally chains of compositions. We provide, among other things, a characterisation of irreducible morphisms under this constraint, characterise when a morphism is a factor of another morphism and explore when complete factorisations of morphisms into irreducible components are unique.

2
Preliminaries

An

(over

. The size of a set

and the length of a word

by
|w|
. We denote the

, or the unique word of length 0, by

. The notation
\Sigma^{+}
refers to the set of all non-empty words over
\Sigma
, or more precisely, the
free semigroup
generated by
\Sigma
, and
\Sigma^{*}=\Sigma^{+}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
, or the
free monoid
generated by
\Sigma
. For the
concatenation
of two words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{+}
, we write
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}
or simply
w_{1}w_{2}
. The result of an
n
-fold concatenation of a word

is denoted by

, and we call

periodic
, with a period
w
. We call a word

a
factor
of a word

if, for some
u_{1},u_{2},\in\Sigma^{*}
,

. For any words

,

stands for the number of (possibly overlapping) occurrences of

in

. The notation

represents the set of symbols in the word

. For a set

,

. The
Parikh vector
of

, denoted by

is the vector
(|w|_{a_{1}},|w|_{a_{2}},\dots,|w|_{a_{n}})
, where
n=|\Sigma|
. A word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is
Parikh-positive
if, for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma
,
|w|_{a_{i}}\geq 1
.

A
(semigroup) (homo-)morphism
is a mapping compatible with concatenation, i. e., for alphabets
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
,
\varphi:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}^{+}
is a morphism if it satisfies
\varphi(u\cdot v)=\varphi(u)\cdot\varphi(v)
for all words
u,v\in\Sigma_{1}^{+}
– hence a morphism is fully defined as soon as it is declared for all symbols in
\Sigma_{1}
.
The
identity morphism

\text{id}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfies
\text{id}(a_{i})=a_{i}
for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma
. A morphism
\varphi:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}^{+}
is
injective
if, for all
u,v\in\Sigma_{1}^{+}
,
\varphi(u)=\varphi(v)
implies
u=v
. A morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
is an
automorphism
if
\varphi
is injective, and, for every
a_{i}\in\Sigma_{1}
,
|\varphi(a_{i})|=1
(i. e., it is a renaming of letters). A morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
is
Parikh-positive
if the word
w=\varphi(a_{1})\varphi(a_{2})\cdots\varphi(a_{n})
is Parikh-positive. For the
composition
of two morphisms
\varphi,\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, we write
\varphi\circ\psi
, i. e., for every
w\in\Sigma^{+}
,
\varphi\circ\psi(w)=\varphi(\psi(w))
. If, for morphisms
\varphi,\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
,

, we call
\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
a
factorisation
or

. In Proposition

, we show that all morphisms where the range alphabet is smaller than the domain alphabet are reducible. Therefore, and to avoid undesirable technical phenomena, we primarily study the irreducibility of Parikh-positive morphisms in our paper. For Parikh-positive morphisms
\mu,\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, we say that
\mu
is a
factor
of
\varphi
if there exist Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
. We say that
\mu
is a
left
or
right factor
of

if there exist morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\varphi=\mu\circ\psi_{1}
or
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu

3
Observations and Fundamental Characterisations

In this section, we aim to define formally and characterise the concept of reducibility for a Parikh-positive semigroup homomorphism. Unlike the factorisation of integers into prime factors, the decomposition of a morphism into irreducible factors is not unique. We establish this and other fundamental properties and provide illustrative examples.

Definition 1

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a morphism for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
. Then
\varphi
is
reducible
if there exist morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
such that
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
, and neither
\psi_{1}
nor
\psi_{2}
is an automorphism. A morphism
\varphi
is
irreducible
if it is not reducible.

Example 1

Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be morphisms for
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
, where
\varphi_{1}(a)=a,\varphi_{1}(b)=aabb
and
\varphi_{2}(a)=a,\varphi_{2}(b)=babb
. Then, for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
where
\psi_{1}(a)=a,\psi_{1}(b)=ab,\psi_{2}(a)=a,
and
\psi_{2}(b)=abb
, it follows that
\varphi_{1}=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
. It is clear to see that
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are not automorphisms, and so
\varphi_{1}
is a reducible morphism. Using an exhaustive search seen in Appendix
0.A
, we can establish that
\varphi_{2}
is an irreducible morphism.
\Diamond

Clearly, verifying irreducibility by exhaustive search is inefficient. We therefore seek a more direct characterisation. Our approach is motivated by the observation that the structure of a morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
is reflected in
\varphi(w)
, for any word
w\in\Sigma^{+}
. As a morphism is defined by a set of words, each corresponding to the image of an
a_{i}\in\Sigma
, it is apparent that, for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, satisfying
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
, each of the images
\varphi(a_{i})
must reflect the structure of both
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
.

We begin by considering sufficient conditions for irreducibility. The first of these provides additional justification for the restriction to Parikh-positive morphisms, and the second is closely linked to the Defect Theorem
[
7
]
.

Proposition 1

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a morphism for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
define
W=\{\varphi(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
. If
|\symb(W)|<n
, then
\varphi
is reducible.

Proof

Let
w\in\Sigma^{+}
be an arbitrary word with
|w|\geq 2
. Let
\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a morphism that satisfies
\psi(a_{j})=a_{j}
for
a_{j}\in\symb(W)
and
\psi(a_{k})=w
for
a_{k}\notin\symb(W)
. It follows that
\varphi=\varphi\circ\psi
. Note that neither
\varphi
nor
\psi
is an automorphism, thus
\varphi
is reducible.
∎

Proposition 2

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and define
W=\{\varphi(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
. If
|W|<n
, then
\varphi
is reducible.

Proof

Note that
|W|<n
implies the existence of
a_{i},a_{j}\in\Sigma
,
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
satisfying
\varphi(a_{i})=\varphi(a_{j})
. We then define a morphism
\psi_{1}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
by
\psi_{1}(a_{k})=a_{k}
for all
k
,
1\leq k\leq n
with
k\neq i
and
\psi_{1}(a_{i})=a_{j}
; noting thus that
\psi_{1}
is not an automorphism. Let a second morphism
\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be given by
\psi_{2}(a_{k})=\varphi(a_{k})
for all
k
,
1\leq k\leq n
, with
k\neq i
, and
\psi_{2}(a_{i})=\beta
for
\beta\in\Sigma^{+}
where
|\beta|\geq 2
is arbitrary. Note that
\psi_{2}
is also not an automorphism, and
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
as required.
∎

Before characterising reducibility of a morphism, we introduce the notion of a factor basis – a vital tool for our investigation of irreducible morphisms.

Definition 2

Let
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
be an alphabet, and
W\subset\Sigma^{+}
. We denote any set
V\subset\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
|V|\leq|W|
and
W\subseteq V^{+}
as a
factor basis (of
W
)
. Furthermore, given a morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, with
W=\{\varphi(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
we call any factor basis
V
of
W
a
factor basis of
\varphi
. We call a factor basis
V
of
W

trivial
if
V=W
or
V=\Sigma
. Otherwise, a factor basis is
non-trivial
.

We now state the first main result of this section, which characterises the reducibility of morphisms.

Theorem 3.1

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, where
n\geq 2
, and define
W=\{\varphi(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
. Then
\varphi
is reducible if and only if there exists a non-trivial factor basis
V
of
W
.

Proof

We prove the “if” direction first. Assume that such a factor basis
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m}\}
exists, with
m\leq n
. Thus, for each
a_{i}\in\Sigma
, we have that
\varphi(a_{i})=v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{j}}
for all
i_{\ell},\ 1\leq i_{\ell}\leq n
and
j\geq 1
. Note that this factorisation may not necessarily be unique, so choose any. Then, define
\psi_{1}(a_{i})=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{\ell}}
and
\psi_{2}(a_{i})=v_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Thus,
\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}(a_{i})=\psi_{2}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{\ell}})=v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{\ell}}=\varphi(a_{i})
as required, and
\varphi
is reducible since neither
\psi_{1}
nor
\psi_{2}
is an automorphism. To see this, note that if
\psi_{1}
were an automorphism, then
\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}(a_{i})\in W
for all
i,\ 1\leq i\leq n
, and thus
V=W
. If
\psi_{2}
is an automorphism, then
V=W
.

We now prove the “only if” part of the proof. Assume that
\varphi
is reducible and let
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
with neither
\psi_{1}
nor
\psi_{2}
an automorphism. Define
v_{i}=\psi_{1}(a_{i})
, with
V=\{v_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}
and
u_{i}=\psi_{2}(a_{i})
, with
U=\{u_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
. Then
w_{i}=\varphi(a_{i})=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}(a_{i})=\psi_{2}(u_{i})\in V^{+}
.

We prove that if there exists some
1\leq k\leq n
such that
|u_{k}|=\ell\geq 2
, then we may assume that
V\neq W
. Assume first that
V=W
. This implies that
w_{k}=v_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}\cdots v_{k_{\ell}}\in V^{+}
, where
v_{k_{i}}=\psi_{2}(u_{k_{i}})
and
u_{k}=u_{k_{1}}\cdots u_{k_{\ell}}
. Since
V=W
,
\{v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}},\ldots,v_{k_{\ell}}\}\subseteq W
implying that
w_{k}\in(W\setminus\{w_{k}\})^{+}
and thus there exists
V^{\prime}\subset\Sigma^{*}
such that
V^{\prime}\subset W\subseteq V^{\prime+}
. Let
\psi_{1}^{\prime},\psi_{2}^{\prime}:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
be defined as
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a_{i})=a_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
where
i\neq k,\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a_{k})=k_{1}k_{2}\cdots k_{\ell},\ \psi_{2}^{\prime}(a_{i})=\varphi(a_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
where
i\neq k
and
\psi_{2}^{\prime}(a_{k})=\alpha
,
where
\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}
is some arbitrary word different from
w_{k}
of length
|\alpha|\geq 2
. Now,
\varphi(w)=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}(w)
for all
w\in\Sigma^{+}
. Note that
\psi_{1}
is not an automorphism since
\ell\geq 2
and
\psi_{2}
is not an automorphism since
|\alpha|\geq 2
. We have now found another factorisation
\varphi=\psi_{2}^{\prime}\circ\psi_{1}^{\prime}
such that
V^{\prime}=\{\psi_{2}^{\prime}(a_{i})\}\subset W
and
V^{\prime}
satisfies the conditions of the theorem.

The final consideration is if
|u_{j}|=1
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. Since
\psi_{1}
is not an automorphism, this implies that there exists
1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n
such that
\psi_{1}(a_{j_{1}})=\psi_{2}(a_{j_{2}})
. However this means that
w_{1}=\varphi(a_{j_{1}})=\psi_{2}(\psi_{1}(a_{j_{1}}))=\psi_{2}(\psi_{1}(a_{j_{2}}))=\varphi(a_{j_{2}})=w_{2}
and thus
|W|<n
, giving a contradiction.
∎

Next, we give the ‘pre-image’ of the composition of a given morphism. As morphisms may not be injective, it follows that these pre-images are not necessarily unique and we thus define a set of pre-images. This is a useful tool throughout the paper.

Definition 3

Let the alphabet
\Sigma
satisfy
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}
, and let
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset\Sigma^{+}
with
m\leq n
. For any
w\in V^{+}
, we define
\tau_{V}(w)
as the set of all words
a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}
that satisfy
w=v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{k}}
.

The following corollary to Theorem
3.1
shows that a factor basis corresponds to a left-factor in the factorisation of a morphism.

Corollary 1

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, and let
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset\Sigma^{+}
with
m\leq n
.
V
is a factor basis of
\varphi
if and only if there exist Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, for a factorisation
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
such that
V=\{\psi_{2}(a_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.

Proof

We begin by proving the “only if” direction. Assume that
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\}
is a factor basis for
\varphi
. Then, by definition,
\varphi(a_{i})\in V^{+}
for all
i,\ 1\leq i\leq n
. We can define
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
as
\psi_{2}(a_{i})=v_{i}
and
\psi_{1}(a_{i})=\tau_{V}(\varphi(a_{i}))
for
i,1\leq i\leq n
. Thus, for each
a_{i}\in\Sigma
,
\varphi(a_{i})=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}(a_{i})
.

We now prove the “if” part of the proof. Assume that
\varphi
is reducible – that is, there exist Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
that are not automorphisms, where
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
. For all
a_{i}\in\Sigma,
we can define
\psi_{1}(a_{i})=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{m}{{}_{j}}}
for
m_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, as
\varphi(a_{i})=(\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1})(a_{i})
, we note that
\varphi(a_{i})=\psi_{2}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{m_{j}}})=\psi_{2}(a_{i_{1}})\psi_{2}(a_{i_{2}})\cdots\psi_{2}(a_{i_{m_{j}}})
. Therefore, by Theorem
3.1
it follows that
\varphi
admits the factor basis
V=\{\psi_{2}(a_{1}),\psi_{2}(a_{2}),\dots,\psi_{2}(a_{n})\}
.
∎

We now illustrate an application of Theorem 1 and non-uniqueness of decomposition of morphisms.

Example 2

Let
\varphi:\{a,b\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a,b\}^{+}
be the morphism satisfying
\varphi(a)=aba^{3}ba,\varphi(b)=a^{3}(ba)^{2}
. There exists non-trivial factor bases
V_{1}=\{a,ab\}
and
V_{2}=\{a,ba\}
that satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, and so
\varphi
is reducible. In fact, for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3},\psi_{4}:\{a,b\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a,b\}^{+}
satisfying
\psi_{1}(a)=ba^{2}ba,\psi_{1}(b)=a^{2}b^{2}a,\psi_{2}(a)=a,\psi_{2}(b)=ba,\psi_{3}(a)=aba^{2}b,\psi_{3}(b)=a^{3}b^{2},\psi_{4}(a)=a
and
\psi_{4}(b)=ba
, we can observe that
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}=\psi_{4}\circ\psi_{3}
.
\Diamond

The following example shows how Theorem
3.1
can be used to prove that a morphism is irreducible.

Example 3

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be the morphism that, for
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
, satisfies
\varphi(a)=aaabcbc,\varphi(b)=acbcc,\varphi(c)=bbabb
. Then we can show that
\varphi
is irreducible by illustrating that there does not exist a non-trivial factor basis
V
of
\varphi
. Noting that
|\symb(\varphi(c))|=\min\{|\symb(\varphi(a))|,|\symb(\varphi(b))|,|\symb(\varphi(c))|\}
, we choose to begin our considerations with
\varphi(c)
. Informally, this means we consider the image with the ‘least structure’. We now see that an element
v_{1}\in V
must be a prefix of
\varphi(c)
, so we consider all non-empty prefixes of
\varphi(c)
. If
v_{1}\in\{bb,bba,bbab,bbabb\}
, then
v_{1}
is not a factor of
\varphi(a)
or
\varphi(b)
, and so it must follow that
\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\in\{v_{2},v_{3}\}^{+}
. We then consider
v_{2}\in V
as a non-empty prefix of
\varphi(b)
. If
v_{2}\in\{ac,acb,acbc,acbcc\}
, then
v_{2}
is not a factor of
\varphi(a)
or any suffix of
\varphi(c)
. It then must follow that
\varphi
admits the trivial factor basis
V_{1}=\{aaabcbc,acbcc,bbabb\}
. We now consider when
v_{1}=b
and
v_{2}=a
, which means that
v_{3}=c
. Therefore,
\varphi
also admits the trivial factor basis
V_{2}=\{a,b,c\}
. We have shown exhaustively that
\varphi
only admits trivial factor bases, and by Theorem
3.1
, is irreducible.
\Diamond

Since factorisations of a morphism are not necessarily unique, a single morphism may admit multiple factor bases. To decompose a morphism into irreducible components, we must determine which factor bases give the most “refined” factorisation. This phenomenon is captured by the following concept.

Definition 4

Given factor bases
V_{1}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
and
V_{2}=\{v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},\dots,v^{\prime}_{m}\}
, where
V_{1}\neq V_{2}
,
V_{1}
is
more derived
than
V_{2}
if
V_{2}\subseteq V_{1}^{+}
.

We can represent the hierarchy of derivation of factor bases as a directed graph, where each vertex represents a factor basis, and the directed edges represent the derivation relationship between the factor bases. The graph branches when there exists more than one derivation of a factor basis, or equivalently when the morphism represented by the factor basis does not have a unique factorisation.

Example 4

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be the morphism for
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
, where
\varphi(a)=abababab,\varphi(b)=ababcabbba,\varphi(c)=cabbbaabab
. Note that
\varphi
admits non-trivial factor bases:
V_{1}=\{abab,c,abbba\},V_{2}=\{ab,c,abbba\},V_{3}=\{abab,ca,bbba\},V_{4}=\{ab,ca,bbba\},V_{5}=\{abab,cab,bba\},V_{6}=\{ab,cab,bba\},V_{7}=\{abab,cabb,ba\},V_{8}=\{ab,cabb,ba\},V_{9}=\{abab,cabbb,a\},V_{10}=\{ab,cabbb,a\},V_{11}=\{ab,c,bba\}
and
V_{12}=\{a,b,ca\}
. We show the derivation graph in Appendix
0.B
.

\Diamond

Proposition 3

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and let
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset\Sigma^{+}
with
m\leq n
be a factor basis of
\varphi
. The following statements are equivalent:

•

The morphism
\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\psi(a_{i})=v_{i}
for
i,\ 1\leq i\leq m

is reducible.

•

There exists a factor basis
V^{\prime}\subset\Sigma^{+}
that is more derived than
V
.

Proof

By Corollary
1
, a factor basis corresponds to a leftmost factor of
\varphi
. If we assume that
\psi
is irreducible and there exists a factor basis
V^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},\dots,v^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\}\subset\Sigma
of
\psi
that is more derived than
V
, then by definition, each
v_{i}\in V^{\prime+}
. We then consider
\tau_{V^{\prime}}(v_{i})
. As
V\neq V^{\prime}
, there exists
j,\ 1\leq j\leq m
, such that
|\tau_{V^{\prime}}(v_{j})|\geq 2
. Therefore, the morphism
\rho:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\rho(a_{i})=\tau_{V^{\prime}}(v_{i})
is not an automorphism. We can then see that
\psi=\psi^{\prime}\circ\rho
, where
\psi^{\prime}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfies
\psi^{\prime}(a_{i})=v^{\prime}_{i}
for
i,\ 1\leq i\leq n
. Therefore
\psi
is reducible and we have obtained a contradiction.

We now assume that
\psi
is reducible, and aim to show that there exists a factor basis
V^{\prime}
that is more derived than
V
. Since
\psi
is reducible, we know that there exists non-trivial morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
such that

\psi
admits a non-trivial factor basis

and
\psi_{2}(a_{i})=v^{\prime}_{i}

is more derived than

.
∎

We now note that irreducibility is preserved under equivalence of morphisms, which formalises the idea that two morphisms are the same up to composition with an automorphism.

Definition 5

Let
\varphi,\widetilde{\varphi}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive morphisms. Then
\varphi
is
equivalent
to
\widetilde{\varphi}
, denoted
\varphi\cong\widetilde{\varphi}
, if there exist automorphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
that satisfy
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi_{1}
.

Proposition 4

Equivalence of morphisms forms an equivalence relation.

Proof

We must show that equivalence is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. To prove reflexivity, we note that if the automorphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
are equal to the identity morphism, which is an automorphism, then
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\varphi\circ\psi_{1}
, and
\varphi
is equivalent to itself. To prove symmetry, we note that for automorphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, there exists
\psi_{1}^{-1},\psi_{2}^{-1}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
where
\psi_{1}^{-1}\circ\psi_{1}=\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}=\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi_{2}=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}=\text{id}
. Then, if
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi_{1}
, it follows that
\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}=\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi_{2}\circ\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}
, which gives us
\widetilde{\varphi}=\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}
, and therefore
\widetilde{\varphi}\cong\varphi
. To prove transitivity, we have
\varphi_{1}\cong\varphi_{2}
and
\varphi_{2}\cong\varphi_{3}
. This gives us
\varphi_{1}=\psi_{2}\circ\varphi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}=\psi_{2}^{\prime}\circ\varphi_{3}\circ\psi_{1}^{\prime}
. We can then see that
\varphi_{1}=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{2}^{\prime}\circ\varphi_{3}\circ\psi_{1}^{\prime}\circ\psi_{1}
. We note that automorphisms are closed under composition, thus
\varphi_{1}\cong\varphi_{3}
.
∎

Proposition 5

Let
\varphi,\widetilde{\varphi}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be morphisms for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, where
\varphi\cong\widetilde{\varphi}
. Then
\varphi
is reducible if and only if
\widetilde{\varphi}
is reducible.

Proof

Let
\varphi
be an reducible morphism, and therefore
\varphi
admits a non-trivial factor basis
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset\Sigma^{+}
where
m\leq n
. If
\widetilde{\varphi}=\psi\circ\varphi
for some automorphism
\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, then we can construct a factor basis
V^{\prime}=\{\psi(v_{i})\mid v_{i}\in V\}
. As the factor basis
V
is non-trivial, the factor basis
V^{\prime}
will also be non-trivial, and so
\widetilde{\varphi}
is reducible. As the multisets
\{\varphi(a_{i})\mid a_{i}\in\Sigma\}
and
\{\widetilde{\varphi}(a_{i})\mid a_{i}\in\Sigma\}
are equal then the factor basis
V
also applies to
\widetilde{\varphi}
, and so
\widetilde{\varphi}
is also reducible.
∎

We now turn to another natural question for the decomposition of a morphism into irreducible components: given Parikh-positive morphisms
\varphi,\mu:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
, is
\mu
a factor of
\varphi
? The second main result of this section characterises when a morphism is a factor of another morphism.

Theorem 3.2

Let
\mu,\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive morphisms for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}
. Then
\mu
is a factor of
\varphi
if and only if there exists a set
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m}\}\subset\Sigma^{+}
, with
m\leq n
, such that, for every
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
,

i)

\varphi(a_{i})\in V^{+}
and

ii)

there exists a word
s_{i}\in\tau_{V}(\varphi(a_{i}))
satisfying
s_{i}\in\{\mu(a_{1}),\mu(a_{2}),\ldots,\mu(a_{n})\}^{+}
.

Proof

We first prove the “only if” direction, i. e., we assume that there exist Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
, and we show that the factor basis
V=\{\psi_{2}(a_{1}),\psi_{2}(a_{2}),\ldots,\psi_{2}(a_{n})\}
has the desired properties. Firstly, we note that the equality
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
, and particularly the fact that
\psi_{2}
is the leftmost morphism in the factorisation of
\varphi
, directly implies that, for every
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
\varphi(a_{i})
must be an element of
V^{+}
. Otherwise, there would be an
a_{k}
satisfying
\varphi(a_{k})\neq\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}(a_{k})
, contradicting our assumption. Secondly, for every
i
, Definition
3
implies that
\tau_{V}(\varphi(a_{i}))
needs to contain a word
s_{i}
that satisfies
\psi_{2}(s_{i})=\varphi(a_{i})
. Due to the equality
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
, this means that
s_{i}
must be in
\{\mu(a_{1}),\mu(a_{2}),\ldots,\mu(a_{n})\}^{+}
. Hence, the second condition of the theorem also holds true.

We now consider the “if” direction of the theorem, i. e., we assume that there exists a factor basis
V
with the two stated properties, and we show that this implies the existence of Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
satisfying
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
. We define these morphisms as follows:

•

In order to determine
\psi_{1}:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
, we first consider the factor basis
Z=\{\mu(a_{1}),\mu(a_{2}),\ldots,\mu(a_{n})\}
. Since the second property of the factor basis
V
postulates that
s_{i}\in Z^{+}
, we may conclude that there exists a word
s^{\prime}_{i}\in\tau_{Z}(s_{i})
satisfying
\mu(s^{\prime}_{i})=s_{i}
. We then define
\psi_{1}(a_{i})=s^{\prime}_{i}
.

•

Let
\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
be the morphism that, for every
i
, satisfies
\psi_{2}(a_{i})=v_{i}
.

Due to the first property of the factor basis
V
, we know that
\varphi(a_{i})=v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{k}}
for some
k\geq 1
, and
v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}},\ldots,v_{i_{k}}\in V
. Furthermore, the second property of
V
implies, that for a fixed indexing of
V
and for all
i
, the morphism
\mu
maps
s_{i}^{\prime}
to a word
s_{i}=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}
that is in
\tau_{V}(\varphi(a_{i}))
.

We can therefore observe the following: For every
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
,

•

\psi_{1}(a_{i})=s^{\prime}_{i}
,

•

\mu(s^{\prime}_{i})=s_{i}=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}
,

•

\psi_{2}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}})=v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{k}}
.

In summary, we have established that
\varphi(a_{i})=\psi_{2}(\mu(\psi_{1}(a_{i})))
for every
i
and, hence, that the existence of the factor basis
V
implies the existence of morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
satisfying
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\psi_{1}
.
∎

We now discuss some examples to illustrate Theorem

and its proof.

Example 5

Let
\mu,\varphi:\{a,b\}^{+}\to\{a,b\}^{+}
be the morphisms satisfying
\mu(a)=bb
,
\mu(b)=a
,
\varphi(a)=aaaab
, and
\varphi(b)=baaaa

to establish if

, we can consider the following potential factor bases

of

that satisfy the first condition of the theorem:
\{a,b\}
,
\{aa,b\}
,
\{aaaa,b\}
and
\{aaaab,baaaa\}
. We can also observe that

and indexing

is a factor of

, and the proof of Theorem

illustrates that
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\mu\circ\ \psi_{1}
, where

,

, and

\Diamond

Example 6

Let
\mu,\varphi:\{a,b\}^{+}\to\{a,b\}^{+}
be the morphisms satisfying
\mu(a)=aab
,
\mu(b)=aba
,
\varphi(a)=ababbababbabb

If we choose

While Theorem
3.2
is complex, the above examples demonstrate that it yields a decision procedure with a search space that is potentially far smaller than an exhaustive search over all possible morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
.
It is therefore interesting to consider the complexity of determining if
\mu
is a factor of
\varphi
in Theorem
3.2
, although we do not derive a hardness result for this problem. The problem appears likely to be NP-complete; sharing as it does many similarities with the notion of
simplifiability
and
elementariness
shown NP-complete in
[
11
]
. Given a set of words
W\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
for an alphabet
\Sigma
, then
W
is simplifiable if and only if there exists a set of words
V\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
such that
W\subseteq V^{*}
and
|V|\leq|W|-1
; this problem is NP-complete
[
11
]
. Setting
W=\{\varphi(w)|w\in\Sigma\}
then the existence of such a set of words
V
with cardinality
|V|\leq|W|-1
is equivalent to asking if there exists a factor basis of
W
of cardinality no more than
|W|-1
, leading to the following corollary of Theorem 4.2
[
11
]
.

Corollary 2

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism over an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
. Determining if there exists a factor basis of
\varphi
of cardinality no more than
|\Sigma|-1
is NP-complete.

A related problem to that considered in Theorem
3.2
is to determine if
\mu
is a left-factor of
\varphi
, i. e., to determine if there exists
\alpha:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
such that
\varphi=\mu\circ\alpha
. This problem can be shown to be efficiently decided as we now do in Proposition
6
. We may note that from Neraud’s result on the NP-completeness of simplifiability of a set of words
[
11
]
, it implies that given a morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
over alphabet
\Sigma
, determining if
\varphi
has a left-factor whose image contains no more than
|\Sigma|-1
words is similarly NP-complete.

Proposition 6

Let
\mu,\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be morphisms for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu\neq\varphi
and
\mu
not an automorphism. Determining if
\mu
is a left-factor of
\varphi
can be determined in linear time.

Proof

We now show that determining if
\mu
is a left-factor of
\varphi
can be determined in linear time. By Corollary
1
, this is equivalent to determining if
V
is a factor basis for
\varphi
. We may assume that each element of
V
is biprefix, i. e., that
V\cap V\Sigma^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}\cap V=\emptyset
. If this is not the case, then clearly there exists some factor basis
V^{\prime}
of
V
such that
V\subseteq(V^{\prime})^{+}
where
V^{\prime}
is biprefix, and then
\mu=\mu_{1}\circ\mu_{2}
, where
V^{\prime}
is the factor basis of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is not an automorphism.
Since
V
is biprefix, then each
w_{j}\in W
is uniquely decipherable over words in
V
(i. e.,
V
is a
code
for
W
), and thus
\mu
is a left-factor of
\varphi
if and only if each element of
W
can be written as the concatenation of elements of
V
, which can be checked in linear time by the unique decipherability property. ∎

4
Uniqueness of Factorisations

In the present section, we further explore the non-uniqueness of the factorisation of a morphism into its irreducible components. We identify two forms of non-uniqueness: non-uniqueness of the irreducible factors, and uniqueness of the irreducible factors but non-uniqueness of factorisations. For a morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
to have non-unique factors,
\varphi
must admit distinct maximally derived factor bases, as illustrated in Proposition
3
. This occurs when for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma
,
\varphi(a_{i})
must lie in the intersection of two distinct semigroups. For a morphism to have unique factors but a non-unique factorisation, we investigate when morphisms commute under composition.

A similar question is investigated by Karhumäki,
[
10
]
, who considers intersections of the form
\{x,y\}^{*}\cap\{u,v\}^{*}
for
x,y,u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, notably characterising the words that lie in the intersection of monoids of rank 2. We study a related but distinct question: given a morphism
\varphi
inducing the set of words
W=\{\varphi(a_{1}),\varphi(a_{2}),\dots,\varphi(a_{n})\}
, when do there exist distinct sets
V_{1},V_{2}
of size
n
such that for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma
,
\varphi(a_{i})\in V_{1}^{+}\cap V_{2}^{+}
? We provide some conditions for this phenomenon.

Proposition 7

Every Parikh-positive morphism over a unary alphabet has a unique factorisation into irreducible elements up to reordering.

Proof

Let
M=\{\varphi:\{a\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a\}^{+}\}
be the set of Parikh-positive morphisms over a unary alphabet.
Every
\varphi\in M
satisfies
\varphi(a)=a^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The composition of Parikh-positive morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in M
that satisfy
\psi_{1}(a)=a^{n_{1}}
and
\psi_{2}=a^{n_{2}}
gives

\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}=a^{n_{1}n_{2}}
for
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that the lengths of irreducible factors of any
\varphi
correspond to the prime factors of
n
. We can also see that
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
, and as multiplication of integers is commutative, the order of the morphisms does not impact the composition. Thus, we can conclude that every morphism
\varphi\in M
has a unique factorisation into irreducible elements up to reordering.
∎

From this, we can see that in the unary alphabet, the irreducible morphisms correspond to those of length
p\in\mathbb{N}
, where
p
is prime.

Corollary 3

There exist infinitely many irreducible morphisms.

Next, we consider non-uniqueness of the irreducible factors of a binary morphism. We begin by formalising the phenomenon seen in Example
2
.

Proposition 8

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism and let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\dots,\psi_{n}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive irreducible morphisms for
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
that satisfy
\varphi=\psi_{n}\circ\psi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\psi_{1}
. The factorisation of
\varphi
is not unique if there exists a
j\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\psi_{j}\circ\psi_{j-1}\circ\cdots\circ\psi_{1}(a)=w_{1}
and
\psi_{j}\circ\psi_{j-1}\circ\cdots\circ\psi_{1}(b)=w_{2}
such that, without loss of generality,
w_{1}=a^{+}(ba^{+})^{+}
and
w_{2}=a^{+}(ba^{+})^{*}
.

Proof

If there exists a Parikh-positive morphism
\mu:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
that satisfies
\mu(a)=w_{1},\mu(b)=w_{2}
and is a factor of
\varphi
, then there exists a choice between
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
as two irreducible factors of
\mu
, where
\rho_{1}(a)=a,\rho_{1}(b)=ab,\rho_{2}(a)=a
and
\rho_{2}(b)=ba
.
Then, for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
,
\mu
can be expressed as
\mu=\psi_{1}\circ\rho_{1}=\psi_{2}\circ\rho_{2}
. Thus, the factorisation of
\mu
, and therefore the factorisation of
\varphi
is not unique, as
\mu
is a factor of
\varphi
.
∎

Next, we provide a sufficient condition for binary morphisms with an image of length 1 to not have a unique factorisation.

Proposition 9

Let
\varphi,\mu:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive morphisms for
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
such that
\mu
is a factor of
\varphi
. If
|\mu(b)|=1
and there exist distinct
x,y\in\Sigma^{+}
with
|x|,|y|\geq 2
each satisfying at least one of:

i)

\mu(a)\in\{x,\mu(b)\}^{+}
, or

ii)

\mu(a)\in y(y)^{+}
,

and there does not exist a morphism
\rho:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
such that
\rho(x)=y
or
\rho(y)=x
, then
\varphi
does not admit a unique factorisation into irreducible morphisms.

Proof

Let
\mu:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism satisfying
|\mu(b)|=1
. Then the factor bases
\mu
admits are of the form
V=\{v,\mu(b)\}
, for some
v\in\Sigma^{+}
. By definition, if
\mu(a)\in\{x,\mu(b)\}^{+}
for some
x\in\Sigma^{+}
, then
\mu
admits the factor basis
V_{1}=\{x,\mu(b)\}
. As
|x|\geq 2
, we note that
V_{1}
is a non-trivial factor basis. If
\mu(a)\in y(y)^{+}
, then
\mu
admits the factor basis
V_{2}=\{y,\mu(b)\}
. As
|y|\geq 2
and
\mu(a)\neq y
, we note that
V_{2}
is a non-trivial factor basis. If there exists a morphism
\rho:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\rho(x)=y
or
\rho(y)=x
, then by Proposition
3
, the factor bases containing
x
and
y
represent the same factorisation, and the factorisation of
\mu
is not unique. Therefore, if
\varphi
does not have a unique factorisation into irreducible morphisms, it must have a factor
\mu
with more than one distinct factorisation. ∎

In Proposition
7
, we show that there are infinitely many irreducible morphisms over a unary alphabet. We now show that we can make similar observations for larger alphabets, by defining a natural embedding of morphisms.

Proposition 10

Let
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
be alphabets satisfying
\Sigma_{1}\subset\Sigma_{2}
. If
\varphi_{1}:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{1}^{+}
is a reducible, Parikh-positive morphism, then there exists a Parikh-positive morphism
\varphi_{2}:\Sigma_{2}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}^{+}
such that
\varphi_{2}(a_{i})=\varphi_{1}(a_{i})
for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma_{1}
.

Proof

We begin by defining the embedding a morphism over
\Sigma_{1}
to a morphism over
\Sigma_{2}
.
Let
\varphi_{1}:\Sigma_{1}^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}_{1}
be a morphism satisfying
\varphi_{1}(a_{i})=w_{i}
for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma_{1}
. We extend
\varphi_{1}
to a larger alphabet by defining a morphism
\varphi_{2}(a_{i})=w_{i}
for
a_{i}\in\Sigma_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}(a_{j})=a_{j}
for
a_{j}\in\Sigma_{2}\setminus\Sigma_{1}
. If
\varphi_{1}
is reducible, then
\varphi_{1}
admits a non-trivial factor basis
V_{1}\subset\Sigma_{1}^{+}
. It follows that
\varphi_{2}
admits the non-trivial factor basis
V_{2}=V_{1}\cup\{\Sigma_{2}\setminus\Sigma_{1}\}
, and therefore is reducible.
∎

Corollary 4

For any alphabet
\Sigma
, the set of Parikh-positive irreducible morphisms over
\Sigma
is infinite.

After we have given sufficient conditions for reducible morphisms with non-unique factorisations, we now establish a characteristic condition for a large class of binary morphisms, motivated by the following examples.

Example 7

Consider the morphism

So far, we have been investigating non-unique factorisations when the factors of the morphism are not unique – that is, when a morphism admits distinct factor bases. To conclude this section, we now briefly consider a distinct source of non-uniqueness: when the irreducible factors of a morphism are unique, but their order is not. Specifically, we investigate when two morphisms commute under composition.

Commutativity of morphisms is not a new question – for example, Honkala
[
9
]
characterises the commutativity of morphisms which have upper triangular incidence matrices, a concept investigated further in Section
5
. The following proposition gives a simple condition for the commutativity of morphisms.

Proposition 12

Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive morphisms for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
. Then the composition of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
is commutative if there exists a non-empty set
S\subset\Sigma
such that:

i)

for all elements
s\in S,\varphi_{1}(s)\in S^{+}
, and

ii)

for all elements
r\notin S,\varphi_{1}(r)=r
and
\varphi_{2}(r)\in(\Sigma\setminus S)^{+}
.

Proof

We define morphisms
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
where
\varphi_{1}(a_{i})=w_{i}
for
a_{i}\in S
and
\varphi_{1}(a_{j})=a_{j}
if
a_{j}\notin S
for arbitrary words
w_{i}\in S^{+}
and
\varphi_{2}(a_{i})=w_{i}
for
a_{i}\notin S
and
\varphi_{2}(a_{j})=a_{j}
for
a_{j}\in S
. As the sets
S
and
\Sigma\setminus S
and their images are, by definition, disjoint, the images of the set
S
are not impacted by the images of
\Sigma\setminus S
, and thus
\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}=\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}
.
∎

5
Incidence Matrices

We now use incidence matrices to investigate the reducibility of morphisms. Incidence matrices are widely applied to the study of morphisms, as seen in
[
5
]
, where they are utilised to solve the decreasing length conjecture. We show that morphism composition corresponds to matrix multiplication, thus it is natural to investigate if the factorisation of matrices with non-negative integer entries gives insights into the (ir-)reducibility of morphisms. This allows us to state results on the set of morphisms represented by a single incidence matrix.

Definition 6

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a Parikh-positive morphism for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
. We define
P(\varphi)=(m_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
where
m_{i,j}=|\varphi(a_{j})|_{a_{i}}
and we call
P(\varphi)
the
incidence matrix
of
\varphi
. Furthermore, if the morphism is not specified, we call any square matrix
P
with non-negative integer values an incidence matrix.

To illustrate this definition, we give the following example:

Example 9

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be the morphism for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
that satisfies
\varphi(a)=abcaab,\varphi(b)=babbbba,\varphi(c)=caabccca
. Then
\varphi
admits the incidence matrix
P(\varphi)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&2&1\\
2&5&0\\
3&1&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

\Diamond

As previously seen in
[
5
]
, incidence matrices can be used to calculate the Parikh vector of the image of a word under a given morphism. As a corollary of Proposition 4.2
[
5
]
, we can see that multiplication of incidence matrices corresponds to composition of morphisms.

Proposition 13
(Proposition 4.2
[
5
]
)

Let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be a morphism for an alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
w\in\Sigma^{+}
be a word, then it follows that
(|\varphi(w)|_{a_{1}},|\varphi(w)|_{a_{2}},\dots,|\varphi(w)|_{a_{n}})=P(\varphi)(|w|_{a_{1}},|w|_{a_{2}},\dots,|w|_{a_{n}})
.

Corollary 5

Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be Parikh-positive morphisms for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and define
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
. Then
P(\varphi)=P(\psi_{2})P(\psi_{1})
.

Proof

We can see that
|\varphi(a_{i})|_{a_{j}}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}|\psi_{2}(a_{k})|_{a_{j}}|\psi_{1}(a_{i})|_{a_{k}}=P(\varphi)_{i,j}
. ∎

This proposition shows that if a morphism
\varphi_{1}
is reducible, then
P(\varphi_{1})
can be expressed as a product of incidence matrices. The following proposition and example shows that the converse is not true – given a morphism
\varphi_{2}
and its incidence matrix
P(\varphi_{2})
, being able to factorise
P(\varphi_{2})
does not necessarily mean that
\varphi_{2}
is reducible, merely that there exists a reducible morphism
\varphi_{3}
such that
P(\varphi_{2})=P(\varphi_{3})
. This allows us to consider sets of morphisms represented by a single incidence matrix. We say an incidence matrix
P

exclusively represents (ir-)reducible morphisms
if, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every morphism
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{n}
satisfying
P(\varphi_{i})=P
is (ir-)reducible.

Proposition 14

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
be an incidence matrix, and, for a
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P^{\prime}=kP
. Then the incidence matrix
P^{\prime}
does not exclusively represent irreducible morphisms.

Proof

Multiplying a matrix
M
by a constant
k
is equivalent to pre or post multiplying
M
by the matrix
D\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
, where
D=kI^{n\times n}
. The incidence matrix
D
represents only one morphism – the morphism
\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
such that
\psi(a_{i})=a_{i}^{k}
for all
a_{i}\in\Sigma
. It follows that there exists a morphism
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
with incidence matrix
P
that can be transformed into one represented by
P^{\prime}
by some composition with the morphism
\psi
. Thus, the resultant morphism is always reducible. However, this would require the morphism to satisfy
\varphi(a_{j})\in\{a_{1}^{k},a_{2}^{k},\dots,a_{n}^{k}\}^{+}
for all
a_{j}\in\Sigma,
which does not describe all morphisms with incidence matrix
P^{\prime}
– these matrices are not necessarily reducible or irreducible.
∎

Example 10

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}^{2\times 2}
be the incidence matrix that satisfies
M=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}8&4\\
10&6\end{smallmatrix}\big)
. Note that
M=2\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\
5&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
be the morphisms satisfying
\varphi_{1}(a)=a^{2}b^{2}a^{4}b^{2}a^{2},\varphi_{1}(b)=a^{6}b^{2}a^{2}b^{4}a^{2},\varphi_{2}(a)=aba^{3}b^{2}aba^{2}
and
\varphi_{2}(b)=a^{5}b^{3}aba^{3}b^{2}
. Note that
P(\varphi_{1})=P(\varphi_{2})=M
. It follows that
\varphi_{1}
admits the factor basis
V=\{bb,aa\}
, and
\varphi_{2}
does not admit a non-trivial factor basis. Thus,
\varphi_{1}
is reducible and
\varphi_{2}
is irreducible.
\Diamond

Therefore, it is natural to consider when incidence matrices exclusively represent (ir-)reducible morphisms. We next show how the property of exclusively representing (ir-)reducible morphisms can be preserved under elementary transformations of matrices.

Proposition 15

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
be an incidence matrix that exclusively represents (ir-)reducible morphisms. Then the following operations are robust with respect to preserving (ir-)reducibility:

i)

swapping the
i^{\text{th}}
and
j^{\text{th}}
rows of the matrix and

ii)

swapping the
i^{\text{th}}
and
j^{\text{th}}
columns of the matrix.

Proof

These operations correspond to pre (or respectively post) multiplying by a permutation matrix
M
. To find
M
, for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we swap the
i^{\text{th}}
and
j^{\text{th}}
columns of the identity matrix
I^{n\times n}
. In terms of morphisms, let
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
for
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
be a Parikh-positive irreducible morphism. This corresponds to composing
\varphi
with an automorphism
:\psi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
satisfying
\psi(a_{i})=a_{j},\psi(a_{j})=a_{i}
and
\psi(a_{k})=a_{k}
for
i,j,k,\ 1\leq i,j,k\leq n
which satisfy
i\neq j\neq k
.
By Proposition
5
, these morphisms are equivalent as composition with an automorphism does not impact the property of reducibility, and so if
P(\varphi)
exclusively represents (ir-)reducible morphisms,
MP(\varphi)
and
P(\varphi)M
will also hold this property.
∎

We next investigate incidence matrices that represent only one morphism. The following proposition links monomial matrices, that is, square matrices such that every row and column has exactly one non-zero entry, to the reducibility of morphisms.

Proposition 16

Let the monomial matrix
P\in\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
be an incidence matrix. If two of its elements are greater than 1, then
P
exclusively represents reducible morphisms.

Proof

A monomial matrix represents exactly one Parikh-positive morphism,
\varphi:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}
for an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, where for
a_{i},a_{j}\in\Sigma
,
\varphi(a_{i})=a_{j}^{m_{k}}
with
1\leq k\leq n
. Then, we can see that
\varphi
admits the factor basis
V=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j}^{m_{k}},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{n}\}
for some
a_{j}\in\Sigma
. We can see that this factor basis is non-trivial, and by Theorem
3.1
,
\varphi
is reducible.
∎

The following proposition establishes matrices that exclusively represent a set of reducible morphisms.

Proposition 17

Let the incidence matrix
P\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2\times 2}
satisfy:

i)

P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}2&2\\
2&2\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,

ii)

P\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2\times 2}
satisfy
P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\

for

,

satisfy

, where

are not coprime,

satisfy

for

.

Then

Proof

Beginning with case i), we note that a word

with

satisfies

. Thus, any morphism
\varphi
with incidence matrix

satisfies one of three conditions:

A)

|\varphi(a)|_{aa}=|\varphi(b)|_{aa}=1
or
|\varphi(a)|_{bb}=|\varphi(b)|_{bb}=1
,

B)

\varphi(a)
or
\varphi(b)
is periodic, or

C)

\varphi(a)=abba
and
\varphi(b)=baab
, or equivalent morphisms.

If
\varphi
satisfies condition A), it follows that

admits the factor bases

or

respectively, thus by Theorem

If
\varphi
satisfies condition B), then
\varphi(a)=u_{1}^{2}
or
\varphi(b)=u_{2}^{2}
for

. Then
\varphi
admits the non-trivial factor bases
V_{3}=\{u_{1},\varphi(b)\}
or
V_{4}=\{\varphi(a),u_{2}\}
respectively, and by Theorem
3.1
is reducible.

If
\varphi
satisfies condition C), then
\varphi
admits the factor basis
V_{5}=\{ab,ba\}
, and by Theorem
3.1
is reducible. Therefore, every morphism satisfied by
P
in case i) is reducible.

For case ii), we observe that the matrix

In contrast to the above proposition, we now explore incidence matrices that exclusively represent irreducible morphisms.

Proposition 18

Let the incidence matrix
P\in\mathbb{N}^{2\times 2}
satisfy
P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&y\\
z&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)
for
y,z\in\mathbb{N}
where
y=1
and
z=0
(or vice versa) or
y>1
and
z>1
. Then
P
exclusively represents irreducible morphisms.

Proof

The morphisms
\varphi:\{a,b\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a,b\}^{+}
represented by the incidence matrix
P
must be of the form
\varphi(a)=b^{k_{1}}ab^{k_{2}},\varphi(b)=a^{m_{1}}ba^{m_{2}}

for
k_{1},k_{2},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
k_{1}+k_{2}=y
and
m_{1}+m_{2}=z
. We now consider cases for the elements of the factor basis
V=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
of
\varphi
, showing that
V=\{a,b\}
or
V=\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
, and thus it must hold true that
V
is a trivial factor basis.

We first consider the case where
v_{1}=a^{n_{1}}
, for
n_{1}>1
. It follows that
v_{1}
is not a factor of
\varphi(a)
as
|v_{1}|_{a}>|\varphi(a)|_{a}
. For the factor basis to be non-trivial, it must follow that
v_{2}=\varphi(a)
, but
\varphi(b)\notin\{v_{1},v_{2}\}^{+}
as
|v_{2}|_{b}>|\varphi(b)|_{b}
, and
|v_{1}^{k}|_{b}=0
, we obtain a contradiction for a non-trivial factor basis where
v_{1}=a^{n_{1}}
for
n_{1}>1
, or by the same argument on
\varphi(b)
, where
v_{2}=b^{n_{2}}
for and
n_{2}>1
.

Next, we consider the cases where
v_{1}
satisfies
|v_{1}|_{a}\geq 1
and
|v_{1}|_{b}\geq 1
. If both
|v_{1}|_{a}>1
and
|v_{1}|_{b}>1
, then
v_{1}
will not be a factor of either
\varphi(a)
or
\varphi(b)
and as neither word is periodic, the factor basis cannot be generated by
v_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
|v_{1}|_{a}=1
. If
|v_{1}|_{b}=1
, then
v_{1}
is a factor of both
\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
. However, it would not be possible that there exists a
v_{2}
to create a factor basis of
\varphi
– the only symbols in
\varphi(a)
that are not in the factor

are

\varphi(b)
that are not in the factor

are

If
|v_{1}|_{a}=1
and
|v_{2}|_{b}>1
, then
v_{1}
cannot be a factor of
\varphi(b)
. Therefore, to obtain a factor basis of
\varphi
,
v_{2}=\varphi(b)
and thus
v_{1}=\varphi(a)
. The equivalent arguments can be made for
v_{2}
if

. As there do not exist any non-trivial factor bases, it follows that
\varphi
must be an irreducible morphism.
∎

We have seen that factorisation of matrices is necessary for reducibility of the associated morphisms. Thus, we investigate when a matrix with non-negative integer entries can be expressed as the product of two matrices of the same form. This problem has been investigated in its own right, without the connection to word homomorphisms in a series of papers by Baeth
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
and Heilbrunn
[
8
]
. In these papers, the authors provide necessary conditions for non-negative integer matrices to be reducible. We convert one of these results into a proposition for morphisms.

A row (resp. column),
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
of a matrix
P

dominates
another row (resp. column)
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})
of P if
a_{i}\geq b_{i}
for
i,1\leq i\leq n
.

Proposition 19

Let the incidence matrix
P\in\mathbb{N}^{2\times 2}
satisfy
P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,
such that

i)

there exists a pair
(i,j)\in\{(\alpha,\beta),(\alpha,\gamma),(\delta,\beta),(\delta,\gamma)\}
where
\gcd(i,j)\neq 1
, and

ii)

a row (resp. column) of
P
dominates the other row (resp. column) of
P
.

Then
P
does not exclusively represent irreducible morphisms.

Proof

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=k
, where
k\neq 1
– symmetric arguments can be made for other cases. We can then note that

P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}k\alpha^{\prime}&k\beta^{\prime}\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\big)=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}k&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\prime}&\beta^{\prime}\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\big).

Therefore, there exists a non-trivial decomposition of
P
and there exists a morphism represented by
P
that is reducible.

If
P=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\end{pmatrix}
for
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
v_{1}
dominates
v_{2}
, then we can rewrite
P
as

P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\big)=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha-\beta&\beta\\
\gamma-\delta&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\big)\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)
.
Therefore, there exists a non-trivial decomposition of
P
and there exists a morphism represented by
P
that is reducible.
∎

Example 11

Let
P=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\
4&7\end{smallmatrix}\big)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\
2&6\end{smallmatrix}\right)
be an incidence matrix. Note that
P
satisfies the second condition of Proposition
19
. Hence, there exists a reducible morphism
\varphi:\{a,b\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a,b\}^{+}
such that
P(\varphi)=P
. Indeed, for
\varphi(a)=a^{2}b,\varphi(b)=a^{2}ba^{2}b^{6}
and, for morphisms
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\{a,b\}^{+}\rightarrow\{a,b\}^{+}
defined as
\psi_{1}(a)=a,\psi_{1}(b)=ab,\psi_{2}(a)=a^{2}b,\psi_{2}(b)=aab^{6}
, we can see that
\varphi=\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}
.

Hence, Proposition
19
implies that matrices need to be “non-coprime” or “non-dominated” if they exclusively represent irreducible morphisms.
