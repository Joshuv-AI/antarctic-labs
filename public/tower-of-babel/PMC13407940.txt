Comparison of Laser and Conventional Root Canal Disinfection Protocols in an dual-biofilm in-vitro model
Authors: Jingyi Wang, Wenxu Li, Wanxin Lan, Ling Ye, Bo Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo compare the effect of laser and other four traditional root canal disinfection methods on the removal of microorganisms in the root canal of refractory periapical periodontitis. 155 single-canal premolars were made into 13 mm long standard roots, infected with E. faecalis and C. albicans, randomly divided into five groups, and treated with A: lateral syringe irrigation group; B: ultrasonic irrigation group; C: sonic irrigation group; D: Er∶ YAG laser in SWEEPS mode group; E: photodynamic therapy group for root canal irrigation. Root canal samples were collected with 25# K files before and after irrigation. The irrigation effectiveness was quantitatively analyzed by flat colony counting method and observed by scanning electron microscope and confocal laser scanning electron microscope. The Er∶ YAG laser group had the highest clearance efficiency for E. faecalis (P < 0.05), and the ultrasonic irrigation effect was lower than that of Er∶ YAG laser (P < 0.05), while there was no difference between that of sonic irrigation group, photodynamic therapy group and lateral syringe irrigation group (P > 0.05). These five methods were all able to clear C. albicans well without significant difference (P > 0.05). SEM and confocal laser SEM showed that Er∶ YAG laser groups were more effective at removing smear layers and total bacteria. The performance of Er∶ YAG laser eliminating E. faecalis and C. albicans and the smear layer was found superior in the root canal of refractory apical periodontitis. It is recommended to employ Er∶ YAG laser for subsequent root canal decontamination following root canal preparation. Clinical trial number: Not applicable.Keywords: Refractory apical periodontitis, Enterococcus faecalis, Candida albicans, Er∶ YAG laser, Ultrasonic activated irrigation, EDDY irrigation

Introduction

Refractory apical periodontitis (RAP) is a chronic inflammatory disease resulting from failure of root canal retreatment for pulpitis and apical periodontitis [1]. RAP arises from a complex interplay between microbial factors and host defense mechanisms against the invasion of periradicular tissues. Bacterial virulence factors, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and lipoteichoic acid (LTA), contributed to persistent inflammation and periapical bone resorption [2, 3]. Therefore, eliminating pathogens and controlling infection is a critical objective of root canal treatment.

Mechanical preparation is essential for establishing access to the apex, facilitating irrigant to remove bacteria [4]. However, due to variations in root canal morphology, conventional irrigation methods often fail to remove the microbial biofilms from isthmuses, accessory canals and apical ramifications. Additionally, both root canals and dentinal tubules are closed-ended, increasing the challenge of effective irrigation. An ideal irrigation method should deliver the irrigant to as much root canal systems as possible, provide continuous irrigation, effectively remove biofilms, and prevent irrigants extrusion through the apical foramen that might damage periapical tissues [4].

Syringe irrigation remains a classic irrigation technique in endodontics, but its efficacy depends on needle tip proximity to the apex, available space in the apical third, and irrigant flow rate [5–7]. Ultrasonic activation generates acoustic streaming and transient acoustic cavitation, improving irrigant delivery and wall shear stress, potentially facilitating chemical reactions [8, 9]. In contrast, sonic agitation operates at a frequency too low to induce acoustic streaming or transient acoustic cavitation [10, 11]. And tip-to-wall contact is often inevitable, leading to dentin cutting and smear layer formation [12]. Laser‑activated irrigation (LAI) using Er: YAG or Cr: YSGG lasers relies on rapid heating of the irrigant to produce optic cavitation, and has been shown to be more effective than ultrasonic activation in eliminating biofilms and hard‑tissue debris when the laser tip is placed at the working length [13–15]. Modifications to LAI, including Photon-Initiated Photoacoustic Streaming (PIPS) [16] and Shock-Wave Enhanced Emission Photoacoustic Streaming (SWEEPS), allow tip placement in the pulp chamber, which is advantageous for narrow root canals. However, the efficacy of PIPS may be compromised under some conditions, and research on SWEEPS remains limited [17]. Photodynamic therapy (PDT), which uses a photosensitizer activated by visible light (660–880 nm) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), has also shown cytotoxic effects against oral pathogens [18].

Most in vitro models use mono‑species E. faecalis biofilms grown for 3–21 days, which do not reflect the polymicrobial nature of RAP or the established, treatment‑resistant state of chronic infection. Furthermore, C. albicans co‑exist with E. faecalis and upregulate virulence genes, yet few studies have examined dual‑species biofilms. Additionally, the SWEEPS mode of Er: YAG laser has not been systematically compared with other techniques in a mature dual‑species biofilm model. Moreover, no previous study has concurrently assessed bacterial reduction using both CFU and CLSM. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the disinfection efficacy of five irrigation methods – conventional syringe irrigation, ultrasonic activation, sonic activation, Er: YAG laser in SWEEPS mode, and PDT. Bacterial reduction was evaluated by CFU counting and CLSM viability staining.

Materials and methods

Standard root models preparation

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-CT-2023-411). The study included a total of 178 intact single-root canal premolars extracted from healthy adults aged 18 to 55 years, who required orthodontic treatment, at West China Hospital of Stomatology. The crowns were sectioned below the cement-enamel junction, using a water-cooled diamond drill, to achieve a standardized 13 mm root length. A #10 K-file (MANI, Japan) was inserted into each canal until it was just visible at the apical foramen, and the working length was determined by withdrawing the instrument 1 mm. Root canal preparation was performed using ProTaper rotary instruments (Dentsply, Switzerland) up to size F3. The apical foramen of each specimen was sealed using composite resin material (BEAUTIFIL II LS, SHOFU, Japan). All prepared specimens were then placed into Eppendorf tubes (2 mL; Eppendorf, Hamburg Germany). A schematic diagram illustrating the process of model construction is presented in Fig. 1. Disinfection of the specimens was carried out in an autoclave at conditions of 103.5 Kpa and 121 ℃ for 20 min. Three teeth were randomly selected and incubated in 5 mL of sterile BHI liquid medium for 48 h. Subsequently, samples were collected using sterile paper points and cultured under anaerobic conditions (10% CO2, 10% H2, and 80% N2) at 37 °C for an additional 48 h. The absence of colony growth on the medium confirmed successful disinfection.

The establishment of standard root models. (A) Crown truncation to obtain a standardized root length. (B) Root canal preparation using K‑files and ProTaper rotary instruments. (C) Apical sealing to close the apex. (D) Fixation of the root to the centrifuge tube

Biofilm formation

The standard root canals were inoculated with a dual-species biofilm suspension of E. faecalis and C. albicans, adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland units. Each canal were filled with 1 mL of the suspension and incubated for 28 days. Fresh suspensions were replenished every two days throughout the incubation period. After incubation, sterile paper points were used to collect samples from each root canal for plating on BHI agar plates, allowing for quantification of colony-forming units (CFUs) of E. faecalis and C. albicans before irrigation and disinfection protocols. To validate the establishment of the RAP model, five randomly selected teeth were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), while another five were analyzed using confocal microscopy.

Irrigation experiments

Out of the remaining 165 models prepared, a random subset of 10 models was selected as blank controls. The remaining 155 models were evenly divided into five groups, each containing 31 models (Fig. 2). Among these groups, 26 models were utilized for colony-forming units (CFUs) analysis, whereas the remaining five models underwent SEM and confocal microscopy observation.

Experimental flowchart. From 178 single‑rooted premolars, 3 was used for sterilization and sterility checks. After biofilm inoculation, 5 specimens were examined by SEM and 5 by CLSM to confirm model establishment. The remaining 165 specimens were randomly allocated to five groups (n = 31 per group, 26 for CFU and 5 for CLSM): lateral syringe irrigation (A), ultrasonic (B), sonic (C), Er: YAG laser (D), and photodynamic therapy (E)

In the syringe irrigation group, initial irrigation was implemented by delivering 2 mL of 1% NaOCl at a flow rate of 1 mL/10 s (total 20 s per irrigation) with a 27 gauge needle. The needle tip was positioned 1 mm short of the working length. The procedure was repeated three times, with a 10 s pause between cycles. Total active irrigation time: 60 s. Final rinse: 4 mL normal saline (1 mL/10 s, 27 gauge needle). A comparison of the five irrigation processes is presented in Table 1.

Comparison of irrigation protocols

After initial irrigation with 2 mL of 1% NaOCl (as above), ultrasonic activation was performed using a K2 file (Acteon Satelec, France) attached to an ultrasonic device (P5 Newtron XS, Acteon Satelec) 20 s. The file was positioned 1 mm short of the working length. Following activation, a second irrigation with 2 mL NaOCl was performed. The irrigation - activation sequence was repeated for a total of 3 cycles. Total active activation time: 60 s. Final rinse of 4 mL saline was the same as other groups.

Sonic activation was performed using EDDY sonic‑powered irrigation tips (VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany) at an air pressure of 2.5 bar. The polyamide tip was positioned 1 mm short of the working length. According to the manufacturer, the tip oscillates at an optimum frequency of 5,000–6,000 Hz. After initial irrigation with 2 mL of 1% NaOCl, the sonic tip was activated continuously for 60 s (single cycle). Final saline rinse was as usual.

In the Er: YAG laser group, after initial irrigation the NaOCl solution was activated using an Er: YAG laser (LIGHTWALKERM021-5AF/1, Fotona, Slovenia) at 2940 nm, SWEEPS mode with the following parameters: average power 0.3 W, frequency 15 Hz, pulse energy 20 mJ, pulse duration 50 µs, fiber diameter 300 μm. The fiber tip was positioned 1 mm stably below the root canal orifice. Each cycle consisted of 20 s irradiation followed by a 10 s pause. A total of 3 cycles were applied, giving a total irradiation time of 60 s and total delivered energy of 18 J per sample. Finally, 4 mL of normal saline was delivered. Additionally, no signs of thermal damage, such as deformation of sample containers or discoloration of the culture medium, were observed in the present experiment.

PDT was performed using a PerioWave HHL-1000 laser device (Zhengzhou Jiatai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China). A 0.01 mg/mL solution of methylene blue (photosensitizer) in sterile phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) was freshly prepared. A volume of 1 mL of the photosensitizer was injected into the root canal and allowed to incubate for 5 min in the dark. A diode laser operating at a wavelength of 660 nm with an output power of 200 mW was used. The laser fiber (diameter 2 mm) was positioned 3 mm short of the working length. Irradiation was applied continuously for 60 s. After irradiation, the canal was rinsed with 4 mL of normal saline.

Sampling of bacteria and colony forming units

Two opposite sites within the same root canal were marked before sampling to ensure consistent and representative collection from each tooth. The sampling of bacteria was conducted at two distinct time points (T0, which means before irrigation and T1, which means after irrigation) and marked sites within the standard teeth. After drying the root canal with sterile paper points, a 25# H-file was used to vigorously lift and insert the file three times along the root canal wall from the apex to the crown. The working end of the H-file was fully submerged in a centrifuge tube containing 50 uL of BHI medium. Following ultrasonic cleaning for 30 min, the H-file was carefully removed from the tube. The bacterial samples were serially diluted and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h on BHI and Komaga media to determine colony-forming units (CFUs).

Scanning electron microscope

Five random selected teeth from each group were rinsed twice with PBS to remove unbound bacteria, and then fixed in glutaraldehyde for 24 h. Subsequently, a gradient dehydration process was conducted using alcohol concentrations of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% for 15 min each. After the dehydration process, the specimens were dried and coated with gold using a desktop sputtering system (Technics Hummer, USA). Finally, an examiner blinded to the group allocation inspected the specimens using SEM.

Confocal microscope scanning microscope

Five randomly selected teeth were retrieved from centrifuge tube, sectioned with a hamber and stained with Syto-9/Propidium iodide (PI) fluorochromes (LIVE/DEAD BacLight™, Invitrogen, USA). The samples were gently washed with 1 mL of PBS buffer to eliminate any unbound fluorescent dye and subsequently examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope. The biofilm within the RAP root canal model was visualized using ZEN software (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Statistical analysis

In this experiment, the data were subjected to logarithmic transformation for CFU counting analysis. Subsequent analysis was performed using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-parametric comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to compare multiple groups of samples. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 and SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, USA).

Results

Evaluation of the efficacy of different root canal irrigation techniques in eliminating bacteria

Before irrigation (T0), baseline colony counts of E. faecalis and C. albicans showed no significant differences among the five experimental groups (E. faecalis: p = 0.45; C. albicans: p = 0.61), confirming consistent initial biofilm loading across groups (Table 2). After irrigation (T1), no significant differences in C. albicans counts were observed among all five groups (Kruskal‑Wallis, H = 3.15, p = 0.53) (Table 2). By contrast, the log reduction factor (LRF = log₁₀ T0 – log₁₀ T1) of E. faecalis counts differed significantly across groups (one‑way ANOVA, F(4, 125) = 16.84, p < 0.001, η² = 0.35). The LRF(T0-T1) CFUs of E. faecalis in Er∶ YAG laser irrigation group were significantly higher than all five groups all p < 0.001, Cohen’s d > 1.6). The ultrasonic group showed a significantly higher LRF than the syringe, sonic, and PDT groups, ranking second (p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.8–1.2). No significant differences were observed among the lateral syringe irrigation, sonic activation irrigation and photodynamic groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2). These results indicate that Er: YAG laser in SWEEPS mode, followed by ultrasonic activation, produced the most effective reduction of culturable E. faecalis, whereas the remaining three methods performed equivalently and poorly against this species.

Observations from the scanning electron microscope

SEM examination confirmed that untreated biofilms (blank control) formed a thick, confluent layer on the root canal wall, with E. faecalis invading into dentinal tubules (Fig. 3.A). This mature, structurally complex biofilm represents a clinically challenging scenario, as intratubular bacteria are known to resist conventional irrigation. In the syringe irrigation group, some dentin tubules were exposed but residual E. faecalis remained inside, while the smear layer was largely intact (Fig. 3.B), which suggests that passive irrigation alone fails to remove surface debris or adequately penetrate tubules. In contrast, ultrasonic irrigation achieved complete smear layer removal and full tubule exposure, with only sparse residual E. faecalis (Fig. 3.C). In the sonic irrigation group, however, parts of the smear layer and a minor quantity of E. faecalis still resided (Fig. 3.D), reflecting its weaker cleaning efficacy. Remarkably, Er: YAG laser produced complete tubule exposure with no E. faecalis and only occasional C. albicans (Fig. 3.E), demonstrating the most thorough biofilm elimination among all tested methods. PDT, however, left the smear layer intact and showed abundant colonization of both species on the canal wall (Fig. 3.F), failing to achieve meaningful surface disinfection.

Observation from the CLSM

In the non-irrigated group, 85.5% of the covered bacteria on root canal walls are viable, confirming the presence of the biofilm (Fig. 4). Notably, viable bacteria penetrated deeper than 200 μm into dentinal tubules, with no significant difference in live/dead counts across tubule depths (P > 0.05), highlighting the limitation of irrigation agents in reaching intratubular niches.

The statistical results (Table 3) revealed that, all five treatment groups showed a significant reduction in total fluorescence compared to the control (one‑way ANOVA, F(5, 24) = 18.73, p < 0.001, η² = 0.80), and the proportion of dead bacteria increased significantly in every group (all p < 0.001, Dunnett’s test). However, contrary to the CFU results, no significant differences in bactericidal rate (live/dead ratio) were observed among the five irrigation methods (Kruskal‑Wallis, H = 3.82, p = 0.43, ε² = 0.02). This contrasts with the CFU findings and likely reflects membrane‑intact but non‑culturable state induced by Er: YAG laser, as well as the low statistical power of the CLSM analysis (n = 5, power ≈ 0.46). These points are elaborated in the Discussion.

Discussion

Hand files and rotary Ni-Ti files in combination with NaOCl irrigation and calcium hydroxide constitute the traditional protocol employed for RCT. However, persistent infection between microorganisms and host immunity leads to RAP. The treatment option for RAP is endodontic microsurgery; however, the financial costs and procedural complexity associated with this surgical intervention pose a significant burden on patients. Therefore, various adjunct therapies have been proposed to enhance the efficacy of pathogen removal from root canals, thereby improving the success rate of root canal retreatment. Currently, there is mixed evidence on the effectiveness of these new technologies in eliminating bacteria from root canals. Some studies report no significant advantage over conventional syringe irrigation [15, 19, 20], while others demonstrate superior performance for certain activation methods [21–24]. To provide clinicians with an experimental basis for decision‑making, this study conducted a comprehensive comparison of lateral syringe irrigation, ultrasonic irrigation, sonic irrigation, Er: YAG laser (SWEEPS mode), and PDT.

Our main finding is that Er: YAG laser in SWEEPS mode, combined with 1% NaOCl, achieved the greatest reduction of both E. faecalis and C. albicans, followed by ultrasonic activation. No statistically significant differences were observed among syringe irrigation, sonic irrigation, and PDT. These results align with previous reports that laser‑activated irrigation (LAI) can be more effective than ultrasonic agitation in eliminating biofilms [13–15], while the limited efficacy of sonic irrigation and PDT under the present conditions is consistent with literature questioning their performance [28, 29].

Several methodological choices may explain why our study detected clearer advantages for SWEEPS‑modulated Er: YAG laser compared to some earlier studies. First, we used a dual‑species biofilm. Recent researches demonstrate that a mixed microbiota contributed to periapical lesions in both root filled and retreated teeth. However, existing models chose to incubate mono-species biofilm of E. faecalis in consideration of simplicity, standardization and reproducibility [1, 13, 24]. E. faecalis is a Gram-positive facultative anaerobe, one of the top three most prevalent species in root filled canals [2]. More and more studies suggests that when C. albicans, also associated with RAP [15, 25–27], co-exist with E. faecalis, genes involving tissue adhesion, hyphal formation, fungal invasion, and biofilm formation are significantly upregulated [28]. Second, the 28‑day incubation period was chosen to simulate an established, treatment‑resistant biofilm resembling the chronic infection of RAP rather than typical 3‑ to 21‑day models [29]. Third, a relatively low NaOCl concentration (1%) was used to avoid ceiling effect and allow detection of incremental benefits. Clinical irrigation typically uses 2.5–5.25% NaOCl [30]; therefore, while our results demonstrate the potential of SWEEPS as an adjunct under mild chemical conditions, they cannot be directly extrapolated to high‑concentration protocols without further validation. Fourth, to address the limitations of CFU quantification, we integrated SEM and CLSM to enhance the accuracy and visualization of our experimental results. While SEM provided valuable insights into the morphological characteristics of colonies on the root canal surface, it lacked the capability for precise quantification [31]. CLSM technique emerged as an optimal method for visualizing biofilms, offering detailed structural information and enabling real-time detection while maintaining cellular and tissue integrity. The number of live and dead bacteria can be determined based on the fluorescence signal value, which can reflect the effect of disinfection and irrigation.

However, An important internal inconsistency in our data deserves critical discussion. CFU counts showed a significant advantage of Er: YAG laser over other groups, whereas CLSM viability staining revealed no significant differences in the proportion of live/dead bacteria. In terms of the experimental procedure, extra washing steps during dying decrease the accuracy and efficiency of viability measurements [32]. Moreover, the CLSM viability staining (SYTO9/PI) staining method is highly susceptible to photobleaching, leading to a gradual decline in fluorescence signal intensity during imaging [33]. And, this discrepancy likely arises from the fundamental difference between the two assays as well. CFU quantifies the ability of bacteria to reproduce on solid media, which can be lost under sublethal injury even if membrane integrity is maintained. In contrast, SYTO9/PI primarily reports membrane integrity. Er: YAG laser is known to induce photothermal and mechanical stress that may inactivate bacterial metabolic activity or cause sublethal membrane damage without immediate permeabilization, leading to a “viable but non-culturable” (VBNC) state or proliferation-incompetent state, which needs more investigations to validate. Consequently, these cells would be counted as “live” by CLSM but would not form colonies on plates. Regardless, our CFU results reflect the clinically relevant endpoint of loss of regrowth ability, while CLSM data alone should not be overinterpreted as a measure of complete lethality. Additional factors include the small sample size for CLSM (n = 5 per group), which post‑hoc power analysis showed to be underpowered (≈ 0.46) for detecting moderate differences. Therefore, we consider CFU results as the more clinically relevant endpoint (loss of regrowth potential), while CLSM data alone should not be over‑interpreted as a measure of complete lethality.

We acknowledge several limitations beyond those already discussed. (1) EDTA was not used in the present irrigation protocol. Therefore, this study does not address smear layer removal. The presence of smear layer in clinical settings might reduce the accessibility of both NaOCl and laser to underlying biofilms. Thus, caution is needed when extrapolating our findings to conditions with intact smear layer. (2) Lack of maturity quantification: Although 28‑day incubation is accepted for mature biofilms, we did not perform pre‑treatment quantitative imaging (e.g., thickness, biomass) to confirm batch‑to‑batch standardization. (3) Sample size for imaging: The CLSM analysis was exploratory and underpowered. (5) Factors such as host immune response, complecated root canal system and additional microbial species present in vivo are not considered. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in more clinically relevant models. External validity: Our single‑rooted, straight canal model does not replicate complex anatomies (isthmuses, apical ramifications) where irrigation differences may be magnified. And the dual-species biofilm model represents an improvement over mono-species approaches, it does not fully replicate the polymicrobial nature and structural complexity of RAP. Future studies should employ animal models or more anatomically challenging in vivo systems, include higher NaOCl concentrations, standardize exposure times across groups, and use larger cohorts for imaging endpoints. In conclusion, within the constraints of this in vitro dual‑species biofilm model, Er: YAG laser in SWEEPS mode with 1% NaOCl demonstrated the greatest reduction in culturable bacteria. These findings suggest that SWEEPS‑modulated laser activation may serve as a highly effective adjunctive disinfection strategy. Nevertheless, given the substantial differences between the experimental model and clinical reality – including root canal anatomy, fluid dynamics, and the periapical environment – direct clinical translation is not warranted based on this study alone. Future research should include animal models, ex vivo anatomically complex specimens, and well‑controlled clinical trials before any recommendation for routine clinical use can be made.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro dual‑species biofilm model, Er: YAG laser in SWEEPS mode combined with 1% NaOCl achieved the greatest reduction of culturable E. faecalis and completely removed the smear layer, followed by ultrasonic activation. Sonic irrigation and syringe irrigation showed limited cleaning efficacy, and PDT failed to substantially reduce bacterial counts or remove the smear layer under the tested conditions. These findings suggest that Er: YAG laser may be a promising adjunctive disinfection strategy for root canal treatment. Further validation using polymicrobial models, animal studies with induced periapical lesions, and well‑designed clinical trials is required before any recommendation for routine clinical use can be made.
