Sustainable utilization of fly ash in AA8011 metal matrix composites for enhanced wear performance: implications for energy efficiency and resource optimization
Authors: Abhijit Bhowmik, Raman Kumar, Ramachandran T, Shahbaz Juneja, Hrushikesh Sarangi, Ashwin Jacob, Pardeep Singh Bains, Priyaranjan Samal, N Ashok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper examines sustainable development and tribological performance of fly ash reinforced AA8011 aluminium matrix composites by stir casting. Composites with 0, 4, 8 and 12 wt.% fly ash content were made and tested under dry sliding conditions using pin on disc apparatus. The Taguchi L16 orthogonal array with the aid of the multi-response optimization through the use of the Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was used to analyze wear rate, frictional force, and coefficient of friction (COF). The experimental outcomes indicate that the wear rate was 0.00447–0.00813 mm3/m, 4.84–9.89 N frictional force, 0.228–0.711 Coefficient of Friction (COF). The best parameters were found to be 8 wt.% fly ash, 30 N load, 3 m/s sliding velocity and 3200 m sliding distance which gave a maximum Grey Relational Grade (GRG) of 0.831. The results of ANOVA showed that applied load had the greatest contribution (48.95%), and then followed by the sliding distance (23.57%), but fly ash content had a smaller but significant contribution (3.12%). The confirmatory test showed that there was a small error of 2.6% in the value of predicted and experimental GRG. SEM analysis revealed the shift towards the severe abrasive wear in the base alloy to the mild oxidative wear with the stable mechanically mixed layer in higher reinforcement level. The research confirms that the wear resistance with the use of fly ash is enhanced, and sustainable development of materials through the effective use of industrial waste is improved.Keywords: AA8011, Fly Ash, Metal matrix composite, Energy efficiency, Sustainable materials, Waste valorization, Wear behaviorSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The shift to sustainable energy systems in the world has heightened the pressure on the use of materials with low consumption of resources and green manufacturing methods. The choice to make the energy generation and storage technologies is not solely about achieving energy efficiency but also includes the creation of novel engineering substances that have the potential to save energy, make the equipment more durable, and less harmful to the environment throughout the industrial sector1–4. In that regard, the aluminium matrix composites (AMCs) have become promising materials to be used in transportation, aerospace, and structural systems as they are stronger weight to weight ratio, less corrosive, and better tribological5–8. This is the contribution of such lightweight and high-performance materials to energy savings, which includes lowered fuel consumption and operational loads and helps in the grander decarbonization objectives9–13. This contribution of lightweight and high-performance materials to energy savings includes reduced frictional losses, lower fuel consumption, minimized operational loads, and improved durability during the service life of engineering components, thereby supporting broader decarbonization objectives. The relationship between tribological performance and energy efficiency can be quantitatively explained through frictional power loss. For a sliding system, the frictional force is given by Ff = μN, where μ is the coefficient of friction and N is the applied normal load. The corresponding frictional power loss can be expressed as Pf = μNv, where v is the sliding velocity. Consequently, a reduction in the coefficient of friction directly lowers frictional energy dissipation during operation. In automotive systems, such reductions can contribute to lower fuel consumption by decreasing mechanical losses in moving components, while in industrial machinery they can reduce power demand and operating costs. Therefore, improvements in wear resistance and friction behavior achieved through fly ash reinforced AA8011 composites have direct implications for energy-efficient operation and sustainable engineering design.

One of the most important things in sustainable material development is the suitable use of industrial waste and by-products14–17. Fly ash is a by-product of thermal power plants that are based on coal and is produced in bulk across the world and is a major problem in terms of its environmental and disposal issues18–21. The use of fly ash as a reinforcement compound in the aluminium alloys does not only solve the problem of offering a cost-effective alternative to standard ceramic reinforcements, but also complies with the principles of the circular economy, as the waste is transformed into a value-added product. This practice will facilitate saving of resources, decrease landfills, and minimize the environmental impact of the entire composite manufacturing process22–25. Additionally, the AMCs that have fly ash support improved mechanical and wear properties and can be effectively used in the sphere where durability and reliability are of great importance to provide the energy-efficient operation. Stir casting is one of the most commonly known techniques of fabricating metal matrix composites, which is a cost effective and scalable method used to produce this type of composite25–27. It is especially suitable for sustainable manufacturing systems because of its simplicity, relatively low energy consumption during fabrication, and suitability for large-scale production. Nonetheless, particle agglomeration and uneven distribution are the issues that should be resolved in order to reach the best results with materials. Correct management of processing factors, stirring rate, temperature and reinforcement pre-treatment is critical in homogeneous dispersion and enhanced interfacial bonding of matrix and reinforcement28–31. Wear and friction are the important aspects which contribute to the efficiency and life of a mechanical system. Overwear causes waste of materials, more maintenance and wastage of energy particularly in transportation and industrial equipment. Consequently, tribological behavior of materials should be enhanced to increase the efficiency of the system and minimize energy requirements of operations. Reinforced with particulars, aluminium alloys show high wear resistance and load-bearing capacity improvements32–34. It is also because the presence of fly ash particles is part of the formation of a protective tribolayer, which will ensure that a contact between two metals is minimized, thus wear and friction are minimized. Statistical and optimization techniques are vital in order to evaluate and optimize the performance of such composites in a systematic manner35–37. Grey relational analysis, together with the Taguchi method offers a viable method of multi-objective optimization since the performance attributes in complex systems are transformed into one evaluative measure. It makes it possible to find the best parameters of the process with less experimental cost and better reliability. Also, analysis of variance (ANOVA) assists in measuring the contribution of each factor, which also provides more information about the controlling processes of wear behaviour38.

Sevlam et al.39 prepared AA6061/fly ash composite with 0%, 4, 8, and 12 percent reinforcements through the compocasting technique. Their results indicated that the composite with 12 wt.% fly ash experienced the greatest improvement with the hardness increasing by 132.21% and tensile strength by 56.95% than the alloy without reinforcement. Kulkarni et al.40 synthesized A356 based composites using single and hybrid additions of Al2O3 and fly ash through stir casting, where fly ash reduced the density of the composite relative to Al2O3 but did not reduce the mechanical performance. Al/fly ash composites were prepared by Kumar et al.41 with 9, 14, and 19 percent of reinforcement and microhardness and wear resistance were found to increase with the increased fly ash content; microstructural analysis showed uniform dispersal of particles in the matrix. In another study, Kumar et al.42 researched on Al6082/B4C /fly ash composites prepared using the stir casting method and concluded, according to Taguchi analysis, that the particular wear rate was inversely proportional to the load, but directly proportional to the sliding velocity. Prasad and Ramachandra43 studied LM6/fly ash composite in the 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 reinforcement levels and discovered that hardness improved and wear rate decreased with the rise in the fly ash content. Sharma et al.44 tested Al/fly ash composites of the 2, 4, and 6 reinforcement and noted that the wear decreased by 13.6 percent with increased reinforcement and the coefficient of friction increased. Moreover, Sharma et al.45 examined AA6082/(SiC + Gr) hybrid composites produced in the process of stir casting and indicated the increase in microhardness (52 -84 VHN) and ultimate tensile strength (163–189 MPa) as the reinforcements were added. In their study, Poria et al.46 investigated the behavior of LM4-TiB 2 composites at different loads and sliding velocities and found that TiB 2 was a highly effective hardening agent and wear resistor. A comparison between aluminium matrix composites reinforced by SiC and TiB 2 was conducted by Johny James et al.47, which showed that TiB2 reinforced composites were stronger and had higher wear resistance as compared to those reinforced with SiC.

Although several studies have investigated fly ash reinforced aluminium matrix composites based on alloys such as AA6061, A356, LM6, and Al6082, limited attention has been given to the interfacial characteristics of AA8011/fly ash composites and their direct influence on tribological performance. In the present work, particular emphasis has been placed on matrix–reinforcement bonding, homogeneous particle dispersion, and the role of interfacial stability in mechanically mixed layer (MML) formation during dry sliding wear. Furthermore, the study integrates Taguchi–Grey Relational Analysis with sustainability-driven material design to establish the combined effect of tribological optimization and industrial waste valorization.

AA8011 differs metallurgically from the more extensively investigated AA6061 and A356 alloys because it contains Fe- and Si-bearing intermetallic constituents embedded within a predominantly aluminium matrix. These intermetallic phases influence melt flow characteristics, particle wettability, and interfacial reactions during composite fabrication. When ceramic-rich fly ash particles are introduced, achieving uniform dispersion and strong matrix–particle bonding becomes particularly challenging because of differences in surface energy, density, and thermal expansion behaviour between the reinforcement and matrix. In addition, the relatively lower precipitation-strengthening capability of AA8011 places greater importance on efficient load transfer across the matrix–reinforcement interface for improving wear resistance. Consequently, understanding the interfacial characteristics of AA8011/fly ash composites is essential for controlling particle retention, minimizing particle pull-out, stabilizing mechanically mixed layer formation, and enhancing tribological performance under dry sliding conditions.

Various reinforcements such as silicon carbide (SiC), aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), graphite (Gr), boron carbide (B₄C), and titanium carbide (TiC) are widely used in aluminium matrix composites to improve mechanical and tribological performance. SiC is extensively utilized because of its high hardness, excellent wear resistance, and thermal stability; however, it may reduce ductility and machinability. Al₂O₃ provides high strength, corrosion resistance, and thermal insulation properties, though its brittleness can limit toughness. Graphite reinforcement is beneficial for self-lubricating behavior and reduced friction coefficient, but excessive addition may decrease hardness and load-bearing capacity. B₄C possesses extremely high hardness and low density, making it suitable for lightweight high-strength applications, although its higher cost and processing complexity are limitations. TiC reinforcement offers superior hardness, high-temperature stability, and strong interfacial bonding with aluminium matrices, but non-uniform particle distribution may affect composite consistency.

In general, aluminium matrix composites exhibit several advantages such as high specific strength, lightweight characteristics, improved wear resistance, better thermal properties, and enhanced energy efficiency in engineering applications. Nevertheless, AMCs also possess certain limitations including reduced ductility, higher fabrication cost, difficulties in machining, particle agglomeration during processing, and challenges associated with recycling and interfacial bonding control.

During this research, AA8011/fly ash aluminium matrix composites were prepared through the stir casting method and their dry sliding wear characteristics explored considered at different operating conditions. This is not merely the focus on the improvement of tribological performance but also on the more general aspect of sustainable manufacturing and energy efficiency than is referred to in the work. This work will be relevant to the design of improved materials that will enable the power-saving systems, specifically in transport and industrial use, by incorporating waste use, economical processing, and energy efficiency, and align with the worldwide sustainability and climate-resilience goals.

Experimentation

Material selection

The matrix that has been chosen is AA8011 aluminium because of its wide usage in the industrial sector predominantly used in the extrusion process of aluminium. The alloy chosen is advisable in components that demand a high strength over weight ratio and also enables it to manufacture the complicated geometries that give smooth and polished surfaces which are easily anodized to give anodized components. Table 1 presents the chemical composition of the AA8011 matrix alloy in weight percentage (wt.%). Different content levels of fly ash particles (0, 4, 8 and 12 wt.%) having size 30 μm combined with parent material. The fly ash reinforcement possessed an average particle size of approximately 30 μm and was sieved prior to fabrication to obtain a uniform particle size range, thereby ensuring homogeneous particle distribution and minimizing agglomeration during stir casting. The fly ash powder was sieved using ASTM standard sieves to obtain a particle size range of approximately 25–35 μm, corresponding to an average particle size of 30 μm. This particle size interval was selected to achieve a balance between uniform dispersion and particle stability during stir casting. Particles within this range exhibit lower susceptibility to agglomeration compared with finer particles, while also minimizing sedimentation effects associated with coarser particles. Furthermore, the selected particle size provides sufficient mechanical integrity to resist fracture under the hydrodynamic shear forces generated during vortex stirring at 300 rpm. As a result, severe clustering was minimized and homogeneous reinforcement distribution was achieved throughout the AA8011 matrix. Fly ash is an industrial byproduct abundantly available from coal-fired thermal power stations, making it an economical alternative compared to conventional reinforcement materials. The fly ash used in the present study was classified as Class F fly ash, primarily containing silica (SiO₂), alumina (Al₂O₃), and iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) phases. The high siliceous nature of Class F fly ash improves hardness, thermal stability, and interfacial characteristics with the AA8011 aluminium matrix, thereby contributing to enhanced wear resistance.

AA8011 was selected as the matrix alloy because of its widespread industrial availability, low processing cost, excellent recyclability, and favorable castability characteristics. Although AA8011 is conventionally employed in packaging and foil applications, its Fe and Si contents promote the formation of finely dispersed Al–Fe-Si intermetallic phases that contribute to improved hardness and wear resistance relative to commercially pure aluminium. In contrast to high-strength 6xxx and 7xxx series alloys, which are primarily developed for structural load-bearing applications through precipitation hardening, the present work emphasizes the development of a sustainable and economically viable tribological material. The combination of AA8011 with fly ash reinforcement offers a lightweight, low-cost, and environmentally responsible alternative capable of achieving enhanced wear performance through improved load transfer, increased surface hardness, and stable mechanically mixed layer formation during sliding. Therefore, AA8011 provides an appropriate platform for evaluating the tribological benefits of industrial waste valorization while maintaining manufacturing scalability and resource efficiency.

Fabrication process

Blocks made of aluminium alloys AA8011 were melted using induction furnace in a graphite crucible. The flyash reinforcement powder was heated up in a muffle furnace at about 450 °C in order to drive off moisture and surface contaminants. A heating to approximately 400 °C was done to reduce thermal gradients in order to produce permanent mould and ensure homogeneous solidification during casting. Molten matrix Magnesium (2 wt.%) was included to enhance the wettability to allow the interfacial bondage between the aluminium matrix and flyash particles to be stronger. The improved wettability facilitated stronger AA8011/fly ash interfacial bonding, which contributed to uniform reinforcement distribution and enhanced load transfer during tribological loading. Mechanical stirring was carried out at a speed of 300 rpm for 10 min after the melt temperature reached 750 °C. The controlled stirring speed and duration were selected to ensure uniform dispersion of fly ash particles within the molten AA8011 matrix and to minimize particle agglomeration and centrifugal segregation during processing. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the process of casting and preparation of the samples.

Schematic representation of stir casting fabrication route.

Although uniform particle distribution was achieved under the present laboratory-scale stir casting conditions, industrial-scale casting may introduce challenges such as particle sedimentation and sludge formation due to prolonged melt holding and density differences. In large-scale production, additional measures including electromagnetic stirring, optimized melt holding time, bottom pouring techniques, and controlled slurry viscosity may be required to maintain homogeneous reinforcement distribution throughout the ingot.

Wear test

The wear tests were conducted on dry sliding and pin-on-disc using DUCOM tribometer (Model TR-20LE-M5) as shown in Fig. 2. As-cast samples were accurately machined into cylindrical pins with a diameter of 6 mm and length of 40 mm so that there were equal contact conditions during the experimentation. The counter-face disc was EN31 hardened steel (62 HRC, ~ 720 HV), selected due to its high wear resistance and standard use in tribological testing. Each wear test was repeated three times for every material condition and test parameter combination to ensure reproducibility. The reported results are presented as the average of three readings. To achieve the repeatability and accuracy of results, all pin specimens were washed with acetone to clean the surface of the specimens before every experiment to eliminate any form of surface contamination, which could have included oil, dust, or oxide. The interfacial temperature at the contact surface was not directly monitored during the experiments; however, all tests were conducted under controlled room temperature conditions, and the effect of frictional heating was inferred from wear surface evolution and tribolayer formation. The pin specimen was maintained at rest with respect to the rotating disc during the tests with a definite normal load. The movement speed was sliding was realized by rotating the disc at a controlled speed, and the track radius was kept constant as it was required by the experimental conditions. A sensor system built in to monitor the level of frictional force at the point of contact was continuously monitored in real time, therefore, allowing calculation of the coefficient of friction during the course of the test. This enabled the evaluation of the tribological behavior in different conditions in detail.

Pin on Disc wear resting setup.

This method combined both minimizing experimental effort and effective analysis of the interaction between parameters and optimization of wear performance.

The sliding distance range of 800–3200 m was selected to capture both the running-in and steady-state wear regimes of the AA8011/fly ash composites. Shorter distances (~ 800 m) primarily represent the initial surface adaptation stage characterized by asperity interaction and wear debris generation, whereas longer distances (1600–3200 m) facilitate the development and stabilization of tribolayers and mechanically mixed layers that govern steady-state wear behavior. These distances are representative of cumulative sliding exposure encountered in practical tribological components such as sliding bearings, piston-cylinder assemblies, and guide mechanisms, where long-duration sliding performance and wear stability are critical design considerations.

The selected tribological parameters were chosen to represent practical dry sliding conditions encountered in automotive and industrial tribomechanical systems such as brake components, sliding bearings, and piston–cylinder interfaces. The applied load range (15–60 N) simulated varying contact stresses, while the sliding velocity range (1–4 m/s) represented moderate operating speeds where frictional heating and tribolayer formation become significant. The sliding distance (800–3200 m) was selected to evaluate both running-in and steady-state wear behavior. These parameter ranges were also consistent with previously reported studies on aluminium matrix composites, enabling reliable comparison of tribological performance.

Results and discussion

Energy dispersion X-ray spectroscopy

The EDX analysis of the AA8011/Fly ash composite and the corresponding SEM micrograph of the selected area is provided in Fig. 3. To further verify the spatial distribution of the reinforcement phase, EDX elemental mapping was conducted over a wider cross-sectional area of the AA8011/fly ash composite. The elemental maps corresponding to Al, O, Si, and Fe revealed a relatively uniform distribution of fly ash-associated elements throughout the analysed region without noticeable agglomeration or segregation. The homogeneous dispersion observed in the elemental maps corroborates the effectiveness of the stir-casting parameters and supports the microstructural uniformity inferred from the SEM observations. The mapping results provide additional evidence that the fly ash particles were successfully incorporated and distributed within the AA8011 matrix. The elemental spectrum (left side) shows the components of the composite with their one-dimensional views, in which the highest peak is found with the elemental composition of aluminium (Al), which indicates that the primary elemental composition of the composite is AA8011. Possessing smaller peaks along with the oxygen (O), silicon (Si), and iron (Fe) is also visible along with aluminium. The existence of oxygen and silicon implies the inclusion of fly ash particles, which are mainly high in silica and alumina phases. Small amounts of iron could either be part of the underlying alloy or could be found in impurities of the fly ash. The abrupt and high sharpness of the aluminium peak indicates that the matrix is distributed and continuous. In the meantime, the less significant strength maxima values of the reinforcing components prove that fly ash is dispersed as a secondary phase instead of creating clusters and agglomerates. The selected analysis region is represented in the SEM image (right side) and the microstructure is relatively homogeneous and has a smooth surface morphology. The observed uniformity in the SEM micrographs can be attributed to the controlled particle size distribution and pre-sieving of the fly ash reinforcement before composite fabrication. There are no significant hollows or cracks that could mean that the aluminium matrix, and fly ash particles are bonded well. This observation confirms the formation of a stable AA8011/fly ash interface with minimal particle agglomeration, which is essential for improved tribological stability and wear resistance. No distinct evidence of Mg₂Si phase formation was observed in the EDX spectra. This suggests that any potential reaction between Mg and the silica constituents of fly ash, if present, is limited and localized at the interface. The enhanced tribological performance is therefore mainly associated with improved wettability, homogeneous particle distribution, and stronger matrix-reinforcement bonding facilitated by Mg addition. The emphasized area is the precise point at which the EDX data were obtained, which means that the compositional analysis reflects the microstructure that was actually observed.

The developed regression model exhibited a high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.94, Adj. R2 = 0.91), indicating that the model adequately explains the variability in Grey Relational Grade within the experimental domain.

The ANOVA was evaluated at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). The analysis indicates that applied load is the most influential factor on grey relational grade, whereas fly ash content (p = 0.931) and sliding velocity (p = 0.965), despite being included in the model, exhibit statistically insignificant contributions within the studied range, suggesting a comparatively minor direct effect on wear performance under the present experimental conditions.

Worn surface analysis

To assess the possible influence of frictional heating during dry sliding, a theoretical flash temperature estimation was performed for the most severe wear condition (60 N load and 4 m/s sliding velocity). Using the maximum measured friction force (~ 9.89 N), the frictional power generated at the contact interface was estimated as approximately 39.6 W. According to classical flash temperature theory for metallic sliding contacts, the localized asperity temperature may rise significantly above the bulk temperature and is expected to reach approximately 120–180 °C. Such localized heating accelerates surface oxidation and promotes the formation of oxide-rich wear debris. The compacted oxides, together with fragmented reinforcement particles, contribute to the formation of a stable mechanically mixed layer (MML), which acts as a protective tribolayer. Consequently, frictional heating plays an important role in the observed transition from severe abrasive/adhesive wear in the unreinforced AA8011 alloy to mild oxidative wear in the fly ash reinforced composites.

SEM images of the worn surfaces can give an understanding of wear mechanisms that take place under various levels of reinforcement and sliding conditions on AA8011/fly ash composites (Fig. 10). Figure 10a, which is a graph of the unreinforced alloy (0 wt.% fly ash) under the loads of 15 N, sliding velocity of 1 m/s, and a sliding distance of 800 m, the worn surface surface is marked mainly by severe ploughing marks and prominent burning spots. The heavy and sustained grooves show severe plastic deformation as a result of metal-to-metal contact. Lack of reinforcement results in ineffective load-bearing capacity which permits asperities of the counterface to easily penetrate the surface. The fact that there are spots of localized burning indicates that there is some frictional heating and softening of materials and this only enhances the wear of adhesives. All in all, wear in this condition is characterized by abrasive and adhesive wear that is very material removing. Figure 10b shows that the worn surface in the case of the composite with 4 wt.% fly ash, load of 15 N, 2 m/s sliding speed and sliding distance of 2400 m has a relatively less ploughing and more smooth texture. Even though certain grooves can be observed, they are less deep and intensive in comparison with the unreinforced one. The presence of fly ash particles is beneficial in increasing the hardness and inhibits plastic deformation. Nevertheless, burning spots can still be observed, which means that the process of frictional heat generation is still important at high sliding velocity and distance. The wear process in this case changes to mild abrasive wear where the reinforcement partially protects against the wear. In Fig. 10c that shows the composite with 8 wt.% fly ash, under the weight of 15 N load, sliding velocity of 3 m/s, and sliding distance of 3200 m, a clear mechanically mixed layer (MML) can be seen on the worn surface. This layer develops as a result of wear debris, oxides, and fragmented particles of reinforcement which are compacted and smeared. The MML herself is an effective tribolayer which implies that there is less direct contact observed between the surfaces in a slide, and thus wear is minimalized. The stability of this mechanically mixed layer is strongly associated with improved interfacial bonding between AA8011 and fly ash particles, which enhances load-bearing capability and suppresses severe abrasive wear. Its surface is smoother with a lesser number of deep grooves, which means that it experiences less plastic deformation. The existence of such a steady layer implies less wear resistance and a transition to oxidative wear processes. These reinforcement particles are important in stabilizing this layer and ensuring that serious loss of materials is avoided. Figure 10d illustrates the worn morphology of the composite containing 12 wt.% fly ash under operating conditions of 15 N load, 4 m/s sliding speed, and a sliding distance of 1600 m. The surface exhibits minimal ploughing marks along with the formation of a stable mechanically mixed layer, indicating the presence of slight plastic deformation during the wear process. High content of reinforcement makes the composite much harder and load bearing. The MML is less discontinuous and less stable and serves as a solid lubricant coating and leads to less friction and wear. Despite the fact that some localized plastic deformation is still obvious, it is much less intensive than with the unreinforced alloy. The wear predominant in this scenario is mild oxidative wear and strong tribolayer protection. Compared with conventional SiC-reinforced aluminium matrix composites reported in the literature, the developed AA8011/10 wt.% fly ash composite exhibited slightly higher specific wear rates under comparable dry sliding conditions due to the relatively lower hardness of fly ash particles. Nevertheless, the wear resistance remained within a competitive range because the fly ash reinforcement promoted stable mechanically mixed layer formation and improved load-bearing capability. In addition, unlike SiC reinforcements, fly ash offers significant advantages in terms of lower processing cost, lower composite density, and sustainable waste valorization, making it an attractive alternative reinforcement for tribological applications requiring both performance and environmental benefits.

SEM images of Worn surface analysis.

The dominant wear mechanism in the unreinforced AA8011 alloy was severe abrasive and adhesive wear, evidenced by deep ploughing grooves, plastic deformation, and localized burning spots. With increasing fly ash reinforcement, the wear mechanism shifted toward mild oxidative wear due to the enhanced hardness and load-bearing capability of the composite. SEM observations further confirmed the formation of a mechanically mixed layer (MML), particularly in the 8 wt.% and 12 wt.% fly ash composites. The MML was formed through the compaction of oxide debris, fragmented reinforcement particles, and wear debris during sliding. This stable tribolayer minimized direct metal-to-metal contact and improved wear resistance. Localized delamination was also observed due to subsurface crack propagation under repeated sliding stresses; however, the extent of delamination was significantly lower in reinforced composites because of the stabilizing effect of the MML. These observations are consistent with earlier tribological studies on aluminium matrix composites reported in the literature.

Within the investigated operating range (15–60 N load and 1–4 m/s sliding velocity), the mechanically mixed layer remained sufficiently stable in the reinforced composites, and no catastrophic tribolayer failure was observed. Nevertheless, wear performance progressively deteriorated at higher loads, particularly above 45 N, indicating increased susceptibility to oxide-layer disruption. It is anticipated that at loading and sliding conditions beyond the present experimental range (> 60 N and/or > 4 m/s), excessive frictional heating, subsurface crack growth, and repeated shear stresses may destabilize the oxide-rich mechanically mixed layer, leading to its breakdown and a transition toward severe delamination wear. Determination of the exact critical transition boundary requires dedicated high-load and high-speed tribological investigations and is proposed as future work.

Sustainability implications and resource efficiency

The current research has a high sustainability potential due to the incorporation of industrial waste (fly ash) in the aluminium matrix composites. It will be an appropriate method to use in the concept of the circular economy because a high quantity by-product of thermal power plants is converted into a valuable engineering material. Rather than using traditional methods like landfilling, which leads to the pollution of soil and air, fly ash is used again, which leads to the decrease in the environmental pressure and waste reduction. Sustainable Utilization of Fly Ash in AA8011 Composites to produce energy efficient and environmentally friendly manufacturing was presented in Fig. 11. Resource efficiency wise, the partial substitution of traditional ceramic reinforcements by fly ash reduces material costs as well as energy consumption due to extracting and processing of raw materials to a large extent. One more technique used in this work, stir casting, also contributes to sustainability as it does not need much energy, requires simple maintenance, and can be used on mass production. This not only renders the generated composites environmentally friendly but also cost effective to large scale industrial application. The improved wear resistance and reduced coefficient of friction observed in the developed AA8011/fly ash composites contribute directly to reduced frictional energy losses during component service life. Additionally, the stir casting route employed in this work requires comparatively lower processing energy than several advanced composite fabrication techniques, further supporting sustainable manufacturing. Components that have improved tribological performance undergo less degradation of their materials, they require less maintenance and they are highly serviced. This would translate to a decrease in fuel use in transportation systems, a decrease in downtime in machinery systems and a decrease in lifecycle emissions. In addition, the Taguchi techniques of optimization of the process parameters through the use of the grey technique reduces experimental trials hence saves material and energy. The determination of the best conditions makes the implementation of the best condition to achieve the highest performance at least compromise wastes thus the sustainable nature of manufacturing. All in all, this study helps in achieving sustainable development objectives by merging waste valorization, efficient processing and performance improvement into one framework. The results indicate the promise of such composites in ensuring a lower environmental impact and high engineering performance especially in the automotive and industrial industries.

Sustainable Utilization of Fly Ash in AA8011 Composites for Energy-Efficient and Eco-Friendly Manufacturing.

From an economic perspective, the utilization of fly ash as a reinforcement material significantly reduces the overall composite processing cost compared to conventional ceramic reinforcements due to its low market value and abundant availability as an industrial waste by-product. In addition, the stir casting route offers lower fabrication and energy costs, making the developed AA8011/fly ash composites more cost-effective than conventional AA8011 alloy systems for large-scale industrial applications.

Conclusions

The current research effectively shows the construction and performance analysis of AA8011/fly ash metal matrix composites by having a clear focus on tribological improvement and sustainability. The process of using fly ash as a means of reinforcement in addition to enhancing the wear resistance, it is also resource efficient by applying industrial waste. The study provides strong relationship between the process parameters and the wear behaviour by conducting systematic experimentation and optimization via Taguchi and grey relational analysis. The findings indicate that material composition and operational conditions are essential in shaping up the performance and as such, the composites developed are adequate in energy-saving and durable engineering applications.
