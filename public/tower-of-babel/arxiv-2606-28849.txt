1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and an integer
h\geq 1
, define the ordered
h
-fold representation function by

R_{A,h}(n)=\#\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{h})\in A^{h}:\ a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}=n\}.

Equivalently, if

G_{A}(x)=\sum_{a\in A}x^{a},

then

\sum_{n\geq 0}R_{A,h}(n)x^{n}=G_{A}(x)^{h}.

We also write

A(n)=\#\{a\in A:\ a\leq n\}

for the counting function of
A
. The problem considered here is when
R_{A,h}(n)
can be eventually increasing, or strictly increasing, when the complement
\mathbb{N}\setminus A
is infinite.

The following theorem, due to Erdős, Sárközy and Sós (see
[
4
,
5
]
), shows that for the ordered representation function no genuinely co-infinite example is possible.

Theorem A

(Erdős–Sárközy–Sós)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The function
R_{A,2}(n)
is eventually increasing if and only if
A
is cofinite; that is, there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that

\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\geq n_{0}\}\subseteq A.

Related versions for unordered and restricted unordered two-fold representation functions were also studied by Erdős, Sárközy and Sós.

For
h\geq 3
, based on the Rudin–Shapiro sequence, Dombi
[
3
]
gave a set of density
1/2
whose higher representation functions are eventually increasing.

Theorem B

(Dombi)
.

There exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of asymptotic density
1/2
such that, for every fixed integer
h>4
, the function
R_{A,h}(n)
is eventually increasing.

Dombi
[
3
]
also proposed the following conjectural obstruction to strict monotonicity in the co-infinite case.

Dombi’s Conjecture
.

If
\mathbb{N}\setminus A
is infinite, then
R_{A,h}(n)
cannot be eventually strictly increasing.

This conjecture is now known to be false. Shallit
[
8
]
constructed co-infinite sets with strictly increasing three-fold representation functions by methods from automata theory and logic.

Theorem C

(Shallit)
.

There exists a co-infinite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
R_{A,3}(n)
is eventually strictly increasing.

Let

\mathcal{B}=\{0\}\cup\{m\geq 1:\text{ the base-4 expansion of }m\text{ begins with }1\text{ or }2\}.

For Shallit’s result one may take
A=\mathcal{B}
; in fact
R_{\mathcal{B},3}(n)
is strictly increasing.
Shallit also remarked that
R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n)
is eventually strictly increasing for every fixed integer
h\geq 3
.
In this paper, we improve this result by giving a polynomial order of

R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n)
for every integer
h\geq 3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every fixed integer
h\geq 3
, there exist constants
c_{h},C_{h}>0
, depending only on
h
, such that for every integer
n\geq 1
,

c_{h}n^{h-2}\leq R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n)\leq C_{h}n^{h-2}.

Moreover,

R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n)>0

for every
n\geq 0
.

Remark
.

The exponent
h-2
is the natural scale. For the full set
\mathbb{N}
,

R_{\mathbb{N},h}(n)=\binom{n+h-1}{h-1},

and hence

R_{\mathbb{N},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathbb{N},h}(n)=\binom{n+h-1}{h-2}\asymp_{h}n^{h-2}.

Thus Theorem
1.1
says that the base-
4
set
\mathcal{B}
has the same first-difference order as
\mathbb{N}
, despite having an infinite complement with long gaps.

In Dombi’s paper, the following problem is also posed.

Dombi’s Problem
.

Let
h\geq 3
. Is there any set
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
R_{A,h}(n)
eventually increasing and satisfying

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(n)}{n}=\alpha\quad\text{ with }\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2}?

For the set
\mathcal{B}
one has

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{B}(n)}{n}=\frac{2}{3},\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{B}(n)}{n}=\frac{8}{9}.

Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{B}(n)/n
does not exist.

In the following theorem, we give a new set
\mathcal{C}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}(n)/n
exists and is not equal to
1/2
, and such that
R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n)
is strictly increasing for every fixed integer
h\geq 3
. This answers Dombi’s problem in the form stated above. The more delicate case of prescribed density
d\in[0,1)
remains open and is formulated in Problem
1.4
below.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a co-infinite set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}(n)}{n}=1

and, for every integer
h\geq 3
,

R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n+1)>R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n)\qquad(n\geq 0).

Remark 1.3
.

In the above theorem, we construct
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{2^{j}:\ j\geq 10\}
. It would be interesting to determine whether the set
\mathbb{N}\setminus\{4^{n+2}:\ n\geq 0\}
from Bell and Shallit
[
2
]
also has this property.

By numerical computations, we can see that the set
A
in Theorem B by Dombi does not satisfy

R_{A,3}(n)
strictly increasing, since
R_{A,3}(22)=78
and
R_{A,3}(23)=75
. Motivated by this,
we pose the following problems for further research.

Problem 1.4
.

For which
d\in[0,1)
does there exist a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{A(n)}{n}=d

such that
R_{A,h}(n)
is strictly increasing for every integer
h\geq 3
?

Problem 1.5
.

Given two real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
with
0\leq\alpha<\beta\leq 1
, does there exist
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{A(n)}{n}=\alpha
and

\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{A(n)}{n}=\beta
such that
R_{A,h}(n)
is strictly increasing for every
h\geq 3
?

In the other direction, some results further characterize the obstruction caused by sparsity: if a set
A
is sparse, then the corresponding unordered, restricted unordered two-fold representation functions and
R_{A,h}(n)
cannot be monotonically increasing. For these results, one may refer to
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
]
.

2.
Notation and recurrences

Throughout the rest of the paper we write

R_{h}(n)=R_{\mathcal{B},h}(n),\qquad\Delta_{h}(n)=R_{h}(n+1)-R_{h}(n)\qquad(n\geq 0).

We put
R_{h}(n)=0
for
n<0
. Let

c_{n}=R_{1}(n),\qquad b_{n}=R_{2}(n),\qquad a_{n}=R_{3}(n),

with
a_{n}=b_{n}=c_{n}=0
for
n<0
, and define

\beta_{j}=b_{j+1}-b_{j},\qquad\alpha_{j}=a_{j+1}-a_{j}\qquad(j\in\mathbb{Z}).

Thus

\alpha_{-2}=\beta_{-2}=0,\qquad\alpha_{-1}=\beta_{-1}=1.

The self-similar description of
\mathcal{B}
gives

\mathcal{B}=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{j\geq 0}\{4^{j},4^{j}+1,\ldots,3\cdot 4^{j}-1\}.

Equivalently, its indicator sequence satisfies, for
n\geq 0
,

(1)

c_{4n}=c_{4n+1}=c_{4n+2}=c_{n},\qquad c_{4n+3}=c_{n}-\delta_{n,0},

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta. Let

C(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}x^{n},\qquad P(x)=1+x+x^{2}+x^{3}.

Then (
1
) is equivalent to the functional equation

(2)

C(x)=P(x)C(x^{4})-x^{3}.

Since the representations are ordered, the generating function of
R_{h}
is
C(x)^{h}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 0
, one has

\beta_{4n}=\beta_{n-1},\quad\beta_{4n+1}=\beta_{n-1}+\delta_{n,1},\quad\beta_{4n+2}=\beta_{n-1}-2c_{n}+2c_{n-1}-\delta_{n,1},\quad\beta_{4n+3}=\beta_{n}.

Proof.

Squaring (
2
) gives

(3)

C(x)^{2}=P(x)^{2}C(x^{4})^{2}-2x^{3}P(x)C(x^{4})+x^{6}.

Since

P(x)^{2}=1+2x+3x^{2}+4x^{3}+3x^{4}+2x^{5}+x^{6},

coefficient extraction from (
3
) gives, for every
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle b_{4n}

\displaystyle=b_{n}+3b_{n-1}-2c_{n-1},\qquad b_{4n+1}=2b_{n}+2b_{n-1}-2c_{n-1},

\displaystyle b_{4n+2}

\displaystyle=3b_{n}+b_{n-1}-2c_{n-1}+\delta_{n,1},\qquad b_{4n+3}=4b_{n}-2c_{n}.

For example, the coefficient of
x^{4n+2}
in
P(x)^{2}C(x^{4})^{2}
is
3b_{n}+b_{n-1}
. The coefficients in the remaining two terms are
-2c_{n-1}
and
\delta_{n,1}
, respectively. Subtracting consecutive formulas in the above four equations gives

\beta_{4n}=\beta_{n-1},\quad\beta_{4n+1}=\beta_{n-1}+\delta_{n,1},\quad\beta_{4n+2}=\beta_{n-1}-2c_{n}+2c_{n-1}-\delta_{n,1},\quad\beta_{4n+3}=\beta_{n}.

The conventions
a_{n}=b_{n}=c_{n}=0
for
n<0
account for the small exceptional terms appearing at
n=1
and
n=2
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
n\geq 0
, one has

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{n-2}+2\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-2}-\delta_{n,2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+1}

\displaystyle=3\alpha_{n-1}+\alpha_{n-2}-3\beta_{n-2}+3(c_{n-1}-c_{n-2})+\delta_{n,2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+2}

\displaystyle=4\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+3}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}+3\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-1}.

Proof.

Cubing (
2
) gives

C(x)^{3}=P(x)^{3}C(x^{4})^{3}-3x^{3}P(x)^{2}C(x^{4})^{2}+3x^{6}P(x)C(x^{4})-x^{9}.

Extracting coefficients of
x^{4n+s}
,
s=0,1,2,3
, we have

\displaystyle a_{4n}

\displaystyle=a_{n}+12a_{n-1}+3a_{n-2}-6b_{n-1}-6b_{n-2}+3c_{n-2},

\displaystyle a_{4n+1}

\displaystyle=3a_{n}+12a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}-9b_{n-1}-3b_{n-2}+3c_{n-2}-\delta_{n,2},

\displaystyle a_{4n+2}

\displaystyle=6a_{n}+10a_{n-1}-12b_{n-1}+3c_{n-1},

\displaystyle a_{4n+3}

\displaystyle=10a_{n}+6a_{n-1}-3b_{n}-9b_{n-1}+3c_{n-1}.

To illustrate the extraction, the coefficient of
x^{4n}
in the term
P(x)^{3}C(x^{4})^{3}
is
a_{n}+12a_{n-1}+3a_{n-2}
, while the remaining terms contribute
-6b_{n-1}-6b_{n-2}+3c_{n-2}
.
Subtracting consecutive formulas in the above four equations and rewriting differences as
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{n-2}+2\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-2}-\delta_{n,2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+1}

\displaystyle=3\alpha_{n-1}+\alpha_{n-2}-3\beta_{n-2}+3(c_{n-1}-c_{n-2})+\delta_{n,2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+2}

\displaystyle=4\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{4n+3}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}+3\alpha_{n-1}-3\beta_{n-1}.

∎

3.
Bounds for the two-fold and three-fold differences

We first prove the two-sided bound for
\beta_{n}
. The upper bound will be used for the lower estimate on
\alpha_{n}
; the lower bound is the key input for the upper estimate on
\alpha_{n}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
n\geq 0
,

-1\leq\beta_{n}\leq 2.

Proof.

The initial values are

\beta_{0}=1,\qquad\beta_{1}=1,\qquad\beta_{2}=-1,\qquad\beta_{3}=1.

Since
\beta_{4m+3}=\beta_{m}
by Lemma
2.1
, by induction, it follows that

(4)

\beta_{3\cdot 4^{t}-1}=\beta_{2}=-1\quad(t\geq 0),\qquad\beta_{4^{t}-1}=\beta_{3}=1\quad(t\geq 1).

We prove
\beta_{m}\leq 2
by induction on
m
. Write
m=4N+s
, where
s\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. The cases
s=0,1,3
follow immediately from Lemma
2.1
and the induction hypothesis. If
s=2
, then

\beta_{4N+2}=\beta_{N-1}-2c_{N}+2c_{N-1}-\delta_{N,1}.

If
c_{N-1}\leq c_{N}
, the extra term is nonpositive and the induction hypothesis gives the result. If
c_{N-1}=1
and
c_{N}=0
, then by the structure of
\mathcal{B}
we must have
N=3\cdot 4^{t}
for some
t\geq 0
. Hence (
4
) gives

\beta_{4N+2}=\beta_{3\cdot 4^{t}-1}+2=1.

Thus
\beta_{m}\leq 2
in all cases.

It remains to prove
\beta_{m}\geq-1
. Again write
m=4N+s
. The cases
s=0,1,3
follow immediately from Lemma
2.1
and the induction hypothesis. For
s=2
, use

\beta_{4N+2}=\beta_{N-1}-2c_{N}+2c_{N-1}-\delta_{N,1}.

If
(c_{N-1},c_{N})\neq(0,1)
, then
-2c_{N}+2c_{N-1}\geq 0
except for the harmless subtraction of
\delta_{N,1}
; when
N=1
the right-hand side is
\beta_{0}-1=0
. Hence the induction hypothesis gives
\beta_{4N+2}\geq-1
. If
(c_{N-1},c_{N})=(0,1)
, then
N=4^{t}
for some
t\geq 1
. Therefore (
4
) gives

\beta_{4N+2}=\beta_{4^{t}-1}-2=-1.

This proves
\beta_{m}\geq-1
for all
m\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
n\geq 78
,

\alpha_{n}=R_{3}(n+1)-R_{3}(n)\geq\frac{n}{5}+4.

Moreover,
\alpha_{n}>0
for every
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Put
L(x)=x/5+4
. We claim the following propagation statement: if
N_{0}\geq 6
and

\alpha_{n}\geq L(n)\qquad(N_{0}-2\leq n<4N_{0}),

then

\alpha_{n}\geq L(n)\qquad(n\geq N_{0}-2).

Indeed, assume inductively that the estimate is known for
N_{0}-2\leq n<4N
, where
N\geq N_{0}
. Using Lemmas
2.2
and
3.1
, specifically
\beta_{j}\leq 2
, and using
c_{N-1}-c_{N-2}\geq-1
, we get

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N}

\displaystyle\geq 2L(N-2)+2L(N-1)-6\geq L(4N),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+1}

\displaystyle\geq 3L(N-1)+L(N-2)-9\geq L(4N+1),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+2}

\displaystyle\geq 4L(N-1)-6\geq L(4N+2),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+3}

\displaystyle\geq L(N)+3L(N-1)-6\geq L(4N+3).

This proves the induction step and hence the propagation statement.

It remains only to verify the initial interval. A direct exact finite verification from the definition of
\mathcal{B}
gives

\min_{78\leq n\leq 319}(5\alpha_{n}-n-20)=3.

Taking
N_{0}=80
in the propagation statement yields
\alpha_{n}\geq n/5+4
for all
n\geq 78
. The same finite computation gives

\min_{0\leq n<78}\alpha_{n}=1,

so
\alpha_{n}>0
for
0\leq n<78
, while the displayed lower bound gives positivity for
n\geq 78
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
n\geq 0
,

\alpha_{n}=R_{3}(n+1)-R_{3}(n)\leq 3n+2.

Proof.

Let
U(x)=3x+2
. First, direct computation from the definition of
\mathcal{B}
gives

\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{7}=2,3,1,2,3,7,5,6,

so
\alpha_{n}\leq U(n)
for
0\leq n\leq 7
.

We now use strong induction. Let
m\geq 8
, and write
m=4N+s
with
s\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. Then
N\geq 2
, so the indices
N-2,N-1,N
are nonnegative and smaller than
m
. Assume
\alpha_{j}\leq U(j)
for all
j<m
. By Lemma
2.2
, the lower bound
\beta_{j}\geq-1
from Lemma
3.1
, and the trivial inequalities
c_{N-1}-c_{N-2}\leq 1
and
\delta_{N,2}\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N}

\displaystyle\leq 2U(N-2)+2U(N-1)+3=12N-7\leq 12N+2=U(4N),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+1}

\displaystyle\leq 3U(N-1)+U(N-2)+7=12N\leq 12N+5=U(4N+1),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+2}

\displaystyle\leq 4U(N-1)+3=12N-1\leq 12N+8=U(4N+2),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4N+3}

\displaystyle\leq U(N)+3U(N-1)+3=12N+2\leq 12N+11=U(4N+3).

Thus
\alpha_{m}\leq U(m)
. The induction is complete.
∎

4.
The convolution lift and the two-sided bound

We need a simple lower-density estimate for
\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For a positive integer
M
, let

\mathcal{B}(M)=\#\{b\in\mathcal{B}:0\leq b\leq M\}.

Then

\mathcal{B}(M)\geq\frac{1}{6}M.

Proof.

Choose
t\geq 0
such that
4^{t}\leq M<4^{t+1}
. If
t=0
, the result is immediate. Assume
t\geq 1
. Since
\mathcal{B}
contains the intervals
[4^{j},3\cdot 4^{j}-1]
for
0\leq j<t
, we have

\mathcal{B}(M)\geq 1+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}2\cdot 4^{j}=1+\frac{2(4^{t}-1)}{3}>\frac{2}{3}\cdot 4^{t}=\frac{1}{6}\cdot 4^{t+1}>\frac{1}{6}M.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For positive integers
p,q
and
n\geq 0
,

R_{p+q}(n)=\sum_{u=0}^{n}R_{p}(n-u)R_{q}(u).

Consequently,

(5)

\Delta_{p+q}(n)=\sum_{u=0}^{n}\Delta_{p}(n-u)R_{q}(u)+R_{p}(0)R_{q}(n+1).

Proof.

For the first identity, group an ordered
(p+q)
-tuple according to the sum
u
of its last
q
entries. The first
p
entries then have sum
n-u
, giving
R_{p}(n-u)R_{q}(u)
choices. Summing over
u
gives the convolution formula. Applying this formula at
n+1
and at
n
, and using the convention
R_{p}(-1)=0
, gives (
5
).
∎

By Lemma
4.2
, if
\Delta_{p}(n)>0
for every
n\geq 0
for some positive integer
p
, then for every
p^{\prime}>p
we have
\Delta_{p^{\prime}}(n)>0
for every
n\geq 0
. We also use the following elementary upper estimate.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every integer
h\geq 1
and
N\geq 0
,

\sum_{u=0}^{N}R_{h}(u)\leq\binom{N+h}{h}

and

R_{h}(N+1)\leq\binom{N+h}{h-1}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, the number
\sum_{u=0}^{N}R_{h}(u)
is at most the number of ordered
h
-tuples of nonnegative integers with sum at most
N
, which is
\binom{N+h}{h}
. Similarly,
R_{h}(N+1)
is at most the number of ordered
h
-tuples of nonnegative integers with sum
N+1
, which is
\binom{N+h}{h-1}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If
h=3
, then
R_{\mathcal{B},3}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{B},3}(n)=\alpha_{n}
. Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
give the upper bound and the lower bound after adjusting the constants on the finite interval
1\leq n<78
. For instance, one may take

c_{3}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{5},\min_{1\leq n<78}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{n}\right\}>0,\qquad C_{3}=5.

The strict positivity for
h=3
is exactly the second assertion of Lemma
3.2
.

Now fix
h\geq 4
and put
k=h-3
.
First we prove the lower bound and positivity. By Lemma
4.2
, applied with
p=3
and
q=k
, we have

(6)

\Delta_{h}(n)\geq\sum_{u=0}^{n}\alpha_{n-u}R_{k}(u).

Since
R_{k}(0)=1
and
\alpha_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 0
, this implies

(7)

\Delta_{h}(n)>0\qquad(n\geq 0).

Now assume

n\geq N_{h}:=\max\{156,4k\}.

If
0\leq u\leq n/2
, then
n-u\geq n/2\geq 78
. Hence Lemma
3.2
gives

\alpha_{n-u}\geq\frac{n-u}{5}+4\geq\frac{n}{10}.

Therefore, by (
6
),

(8)

\Delta_{h}(n)\geq\frac{n}{10}\sum_{0\leq u\leq n/2}R_{k}(u).

Set

M=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2k}\right\rfloor.

Since
n\geq 4k
, we have
M\geq n/(4k)
. Every ordered
k
-tuple in
(\mathcal{B}\cap[0,M])^{k}
has sum at most
kM\leq n/2
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.1
,

\sum_{0\leq u\leq n/2}R_{k}(u)\geq\mathcal{B}(M)^{k}\geq\left(\frac{M}{6}\right)^{k}\geq\left(\frac{n}{24k}\right)^{k}.

Combining this with (
8
), we obtain, for all
n\geq N_{h}
,

(9)

\Delta_{h}(n)\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(\frac{1}{24k}\right)^{k}n^{k+1}=\frac{1}{10}\left(\frac{1}{24(h-3)}\right)^{h-3}n^{h-2}.

To cover the finite interval
1\leq n<N_{h}
, define

c_{h}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{10}\left(\frac{1}{24(h-3)}\right)^{h-3},\ \min_{1\leq n<N_{h}}\frac{\Delta_{h}(n)}{n^{h-2}}\right\}.

By (
7
), the second minimum is taken over finitely many positive numbers, hence
c_{h}>0
. The lower bound follows from the definition of
c_{h}
and (
9
).

It remains to prove the upper bound. Again use Lemma
4.2
with
p=3
and
q=k
. Since
R_{3}(0)=1
, Lemma
3.3
gives, for every
n\geq 0
,

\Delta_{h}(n)=\sum_{u=0}^{n}\alpha_{n-u}R_{k}(u)+R_{k}(n+1)\leq(3n+2)\sum_{u=0}^{n}R_{k}(u)+R_{k}(n+1).

By Lemma
4.3
,

(10)

\Delta_{h}(n)\leq(3n+2)\binom{n+k}{k}+\binom{n+k}{k-1}.

In particular, if
n\geq 1
, then
n+k\leq(k+1)n
and
3n+2\leq 5n
, so

\Delta_{h}(n)\leq\left(5\frac{(k+1)^{k}}{k!}+\frac{(k+1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\right)n^{k+1}.

Thus the desired upper bound holds with

C_{h}=5\frac{(h-2)^{h-3}}{(h-3)!}+\frac{(h-2)^{h-4}}{(h-4)!}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let

E=\{2^{j}:\ j\geq 10\},\qquad\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{N}\setminus E.

Then
\mathcal{C}
is co-infinite. Moreover, if

E(N)=\#\{a\in E:\ a\leq N\},

then
E(N)=O(\log N)
, and hence

\mathcal{C}(N)=N+1-E(N).

It follows that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}(N)}{N}=1.

We next prove strict monotonicity. Let

D(x)=\sum_{a\in E}x^{a}.

The generating function of
\mathcal{C}
is

G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{C}}x^{b}=\frac{1}{1-x}-D(x).

We first prove the result for
h=3
. Put

S_{3}(n)=R_{\mathcal{C},3}(n).

Since

\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{3}(n)x^{n}=G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)^{3},

we have

(11)

S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)=[x^{n+1}](1-x)G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)^{3},

where
[x^{n+1}](1-x)G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)^{3}
denotes the coefficient of
x^{n+1}
in
(1-x)G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)^{3}
.

Let
N=n+1
. Using
G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=1/(1-x)-D(x)
, we get

(1-x)G_{\mathcal{C}}(x)^{3}=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2}}-\frac{3D(x)}{1-x}+3D(x)^{2}-(1-x)D(x)^{3}.

It follows from (
11
) that

S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)=N+1-3E(N)+3r_{2}(N)-r_{3}(N)+r_{3}(N-1),

where
r_{i}(N)
denotes the ordered number of representations of
N
as a sum of
i
elements of
E
.
Since
r_{2}(N)\geq 0
and
r_{3}(N-1)\geq 0
, we obtain

S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)\geq N+1-3E(N)-r_{3}(N).

Every representation

N=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3},\qquad a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in E,

is determined by the ordered pair
(a_{1},a_{2})
, since then
a_{3}=N-a_{1}-a_{2}
. Thus

r_{3}(N)\leq E(N)^{2}.

Consequently,

S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)\geq N+1-3E(N)-E(N)^{2}.

If
N<2^{10}
, then
E(N)=0
, and so
S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)\geq N+1>0
.
Now assume
N\geq 2^{10}
and put
m=E(N)
. Then
m\geq 1
, and from the definition of
E
we have
N\geq 2^{m+9}
. Hence

N+1-3E(N)-E(N)^{2}=N+1-3m-m^{2}\geq 2^{m+9}+1-3m-m^{2}.

For every
m\geq 1
, one has
m^{2}+3m<2^{m+9}
. Therefore

S_{3}(n+1)-S_{3}(n)>0

for every
n\geq 0
.

Finally let
h\geq 4
. By the convolution identity,

R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n)=\sum_{u=0}^{n}R_{\mathcal{C},3}(n-u)R_{\mathcal{C},h-3}(u).

Thus

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=0}^{n}\bigl(R_{\mathcal{C},3}(n+1-u)-R_{\mathcal{C},3}(n-u)\bigr)R_{\mathcal{C},h-3}(u)

\displaystyle+R_{\mathcal{C},3}(0)R_{\mathcal{C},h-3}(n+1).

Since
0\in\mathcal{C}
, the term corresponding to
u=0
in the above sum is strictly positive, and all other terms are nonnegative. Hence

R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n+1)-R_{\mathcal{C},h}(n)>0.

This proves the theorem for every
h\geq 3
.
∎
