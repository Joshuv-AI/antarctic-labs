 ABSTRACT
Current light-water nuclear reactors rely on a variety of different sensors and sensor applications to meet
their structural health monitoring needs throughout the entirety of the reactor primary, secondary, and con-
tainment systems. Optical fiber–based sensor technologies could provide solutions to reduce the sensor
system footprint while enhancing the measurement fidelity and spatial resolution by leveraging distributed
monitoring techniques. Moreover, advanced reactors may require optical fiber–based sensors for structural
health monitoring because their operating temperatures will exceed the limits of conventional transducers
used to acquire dynamic strain or acoustic data in nuclear power plants. Therefore, this report describes
experiments targeting the development of high-temperature bonding techniques that would allow for po-
tentially long lengths of fibers to be bonded to metallic reactor components in advanced reactor systems.
The high temperatures experienced within target application, next-generation nuclear reactors, necessi-
tate a high-temperature resistant bond to limit the amount of tension on the fiber at the target application
temperature. The primary bonding method investigated in this work is brazing; hot-rolling has also been
investigated to a lesser extent. Both techniques are well-suited to bonding optical fibers to large reactor
components such as primary coolant piping, pressure vessels, or heat exchangers. Optical frequency do-
main reflectometry was used to monitor the strain in metal-coated optical fibers before, during, and after
the high-temperature bonding process. On select optical fibers that were successfully bonded, additional
thermal cycling was performed to assess the extent to which the fiber remained bonded based on the ex-
pected thermal expansion of the test specimen material. The results of the various experiments yielded
the following general conclusions: (1) brazing is a viable technique for bonding and allows significant
compressive strain to be applied to the fiber at room temperature; (2) hot-rolling is a viable technique as
well, which has been more optimized than the brazing technique for bonding, but less residual compressive
strain has been observed with this technique; and (3) both techniques will need further development and
optimization to demonstrate bonding of a long length of fiber that can provably operate at relevant temper-
atures for an advanced nuclear reactor application.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Structural health monitoring (SHM) is crucial for understanding and predicting the degradation of compo-
nents or systems in any environment involving stress, high temperatures, corrosion, radiation exposure, or
other phenomena. Commercial nuclear power plants (NPPs) are no exception. The current fleet of NPPs is
aging, and many of these NPPs are seeking extensions to their operational licenses. Data collected through
structural health monitoring techniques can support decisions regarding extensions by informing predic-
tions of future degradation of system components and structures. In addition, on-line SHM data can help to
reduce shutdown time by enabling predictive maintenance and reducing the need for manual inspections,
which ultimately improves NPPs cost-competitiveness. Finally, the safe, daily operations of NPPs can be
ensured by being fully informed on the potential faults or failures in any of the regions of the NPP system,
including the core, reactor vessel, heat exchanger, steam generator, coolant piping network, and contain-
ment structure.
One of the approaches for SHM of existing NPPs relies on dynamic and static strain measurements. Vi-
brational modes and frequencies, as well as static strains, can provide indications of degradation and the
potential need for preventative maintenance. Piezoelectric transducers and strain gauges are two common
sensors that have been used to obtain such data (Hutton, Friesel, and Dawson 1993; Nakamura and Terada
1985; Smith, Rao, and Gopal 1979; Cheng 2007; Koo and Kim 2000; Huang, Qu, and Xiao 2024; Ko and
Kim 2018; Damiano and Kryter 1990). These sensors can be used to measure acoustic emissions, and such
techniques are considered highly mature (Meyer et al. 2011). Acoustic emission measurements can allow

for the detection of cracks, which is highly relevant to SHM of the reactor pressure vessel (Hutton, Friesel,
and Dawson 1993; Bamford and Rishel 2002). Acoustic emission measurements can also enable leak de-
tection in the coolant piping network (Nakamura and Terada 1985; Cheng 2007). Aside from acoustic
emission measurements, the resonant frequencies of components with moving parts, such as pumps and
motors, can be monitored to ensure that these moving components are operating properly (Koo and Kim
2000); this can be achieved with the same types of transducers. In general, these strain-based measure-
ments are applied to all regions of the NPP system except the core internals. The core internal structural
health is typically deduced indirectly through techniques such as neutron detector noise analysis or by pe-
riodic use of ultrasonic examination (Meyer et al. 2011). There are instances of direct vibration monitoring
using strain gauges within the core itself (Pettigrew 2013), but long-term operation implications are un-
clear, and general adoption by industry seems doubtful.
Regarding next-generation reactors (i.e., Gen IV reactors), the general trend is that they are designed to
operate at temperatures considerably higher than those of Gen II and Gen III reactors. Whereas Gen II–III
reactors tend to be limited to temperatures under 400◦ C, Gen IV reactors are designed to have operating
temperatures between 400–800◦ C (Dandan Chen et al. 2021; Čížek et al. 2021). In addition, these reactors
will operate with higher displacement doses within and near the reactor core (Dandan Chen et al. 2021).
The challenge with this jump in temperature, and general environment harshness, is that conventional,
commercially available sensors to acquire the necessary strain data to support SHM in Gen IV reactors
become significantly more limited. On one hand, piezoelectric sensors could be developed and brought
to an appropriate technology readiness level to acquire these measurements, building upon advances in
high-temperature piezoelectric materials (Jiang et al. 2013). On the other hand, optical fiber–based sensors
could be used to acquire strain data; in fact, there are several instances of optical fibers being used to mea-
sure vibrations at very high temperatures (Dong Chen et al. 2020; Mahissi et al. 2021; Qian et al. 2022;
André et al. 2014; Birri et al. 2025).
Optical fiber–based sensors have many benefits with regard to utilization in NPPs; these benefits apply to
Gen II and III reactors, but their strengths especially apply to the challenges inherent to the operation of
Gen IV reactors. Besides the fibers themselves having high temperature stability, optical fibers are gener-
ally less invasive than other transducers because of their extremely small size, and they are also immune
to electromagnetic interference. In addition, there are a variety of sensing options that enable distributed
or quasi-distributed sensing, such as the implementation of cascaded fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs), optical
frequency domain reflectometry (OFDR), or distributed acoustic sensing (DAS). These distributed sens-
ing technologies have been demonstrated in numerous high-temperature and high-radiation environments
(Sweeney, Schrell, and Petrie 2020; Ruiz-Lombera et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2021; Hyer,
Giuliano, and Petrie 2023; Wu et al. 2021; Raab et al. 2019). Optical fibers can also be coated with metals
such as Cu, Ni, and Au to protect the fiber and support their use in various component embedment strate-
gies.
The primary challenge to be overcome with respect to the implementation of optical fiber–based sensors
for SHM in NPPs is the long-term bonding of the optical fibers to the components or structures that they
are intended to monitor. The coefficients of thermal expansion of most metal alloys are generally more
than one order of magnitude greater than that of fused silica glass. This results in the fiber seeing signifi-
cant tensile stresses during operation at nuclear reactor–relevant temperatures when it is bonded to a metal
at relatively low temperatures. It is possible to develop bonding strategies that introduce compressive strain
in the fiber at low (room) temperature such that the fiber does not experience significant tension at reactor
operational temperatures. The use of room temperature bonding techniques such as electroplating limits
optical fiber–based strain monitoring to modest temperatures that are generally not relevant for NPPs. Al-
ternatively, some bonding techniques involve applying local heating and/or pressure to the fiber, which

 2

places the optical fibers under some level of pre-compression; these techniques include advanced manu-
facturing techniques such as ultrasonic additive manufacturing (Petrie et al. 2019), laser powder bed fu-
sion (Hyer and Petrie 2024; Havermann et al. 2015), and directed energy deposition (Snider et al. 2023;
Zou et al. 2018; Zou et al. 2020). These techniques have enabled strain measurements up to Gen II and
III–relevant temperatures. For Gen IV reactors, perhaps the most promising techniques are those in which
the bond is formed while the bulk component is at very high temperatures; these techniques include braz-
ing (Sandlin et al. 2007), spark plasma sintering (Zhang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025), and possibly hot
rolling.
Brazing specifically is a high-temperature bonding technique that has numerous positive attributes. Firstly,
it has been shown to be successful at high temperatures, based on the work of Sandlin, in which an FBG
was brazed to an Inconel 600 transducer and successfully thermally cycled up to 600◦ C a total of three
times, the longest hold time being 68 hours (Sandlin and Hokkanen 2003; Sandlin et al. 2007). Secondly, it
is a relatively simple technique, such that off-the-shelf components and non-specialized equipment can be
used to perform the brazing of the optical fibers. Finally, brazing is one of the few high-temperature bond-
ing techniques capable of bonding long lengths of fiber and thus enables distributed or quasi-distributed
static or dynamic strain sensing.
For these reasons, a number of brazing experiments were conducted in this work, building upon experi-
ments which have been conducted in a previous report (Birri et al. 2024), to assess and progress the feasi-
bility of brazing as a bonding technique for optical fibers to be used for NPP SHM. These brazing exper-
iments were conducted in several arrangements to identify the optimal way to ensure consistent bonding
along an extended length of fiber, minimize the chance for fiber breakage, and ensure that the fiber is sub-
jected to appropriate levels of pre-compression. The authors of this report have also collaborated with re-
searchers from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), who prepared hot-rolled fiber specimens
that were analyzed as part of this work and are reported on herein. This report summarizes all of these
tests and provides recommendations on bonding strategies for long lengths of optical fibers using a braz-
ing technique.

 2. MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT USED
2.1 METAL COATED FIBERS
The experimental studies conducted in this work considered both Au- and Cu–coated silica optical fibers
for the brazing analyses. Both coating materials are commercially available and have different advantages
and disadvantages; these tradeoffs make it useful to conduct experiments on both coating types. Copper
has the benefit of being a stronger material (and thus more protective of the fiber), and it enables limited
optical losses for typical optical interrogation wavelengths up to Gen IV nuclear reactor–relevant temper-
atures in an inert environment (Popov et al. 2012). However, Cu does have the disadvantage of significant
oxidation when in oxygen-rich environments, with both cuprous and cupric oxide forming even at Gen II
and III reactor–relevant temperatures (Choudhary et al. 2018). Gold, on the other hand, is considered inert,
and so it is inherently resistant to oxidation. However, it has been observed that these coatings tend to be
more fragile (Laarossi, Quintela-Incera, and López-Higuera 2019), and gold-coated fibers can have high
levels of optical attenuation at low temperatures (< 200◦ C) (Reinsch and Henninges 2010).
The Cu-coated fibers used herein were Cu1300 (IVG Fiber; Toronto, Ontario, Canada). These fibers have a
Ge-doped core (9 µm diameter), a pure silica cladding (125 µm diameter), and a Cu coating with an addi-
tional inner carbon layer (total coating diameter of 165 µm). The Au-coated fibers used in this work were
ASI9.0/125/155G (Fiberguide Industries; Arlington, VA, USA). These fibers have a Ge-doped core (9 µm
diameter), a pure silica cladding (125 µm diameter), and an Au coating (155 µm diameter).

In all experiments, the same general approach was used to fabricate fiber sensors, which could then be

2.2 OPTICAL BACKSCATTER REFLECTOMETER
An optical backscatter reflectometer (OBR) was used to interrogate the optical fibers, which were bonded
to specimens by either brazing or hot-rolling. The fibers were interrogated at select time intervals be-
fore, (in some cases) during, and after the high-temperature bonding processes. Specifically, Luna Innova-
tions’ (Roanoke, VA, USA) OBR-4600 system was used with a Luna Innovations FOS-008 optical switch.
The OBR operates on the principle of OFDR, which is described in detail in a previous paper (Sweeney,
Schrell, and Petrie 2020) but is described briefly herein. OFDR involves the use of a tunable laser source
(TLS) to launch light into a Mach–Zehnder interferometer coupled to a fiber under test (FUT), which in
this case is depicted in Figure 1. Random density fluctuations in the FUT cause Rayleigh-backscattered
light to interfere with the light launched by the TLS, which is ultimately collected by photodetectors. The
intensity of the light, as a function of optical frequency (or wavelength), can be converted to the time-of-
flight domain with a Fourier transform. Individual segments along the length of fiber (associated with dis-
cretized times of flight) can then be isolated, and the inverse Fourier transform can be performed to de-
termine the spectral (frequency-dependent) intensities resulting from backscattered light originating from
individual fiber segments. Then, cross-correlations can be performed between a target scan (i.e., when the
FUT is heated and/or strained) and a reference scan (i.e., when the FUT is at a known temperature and
strain state) to determine a spectral shift in each fiber segment, which can be correlated to a local change
in temperature or strain in the fiber. This process ultimately enables a distributed measurement of either
parameter, assuming that one can decouple the two parameters.
The Luna Innovations OBR was used in this work to enable monitoring of the distributed spectral shift of
the fiber before, during, and after each of the aforementioned bonding processes. The manner in which
spectral shift progresses throughout a bonding process provides information on how the fiber is simulta-
neously responding to the elevated temperatures and the resulting differential thermal expansion. After
cooldown, spectral shift before and after bonding was compared to enable the calculation of residual strain
in the fiber, which provides insight into the temperature at which the fiber was ultimately bonded (and pre-
sumably at a neutral strain state) and the expected operational temperatures the sensor could survive for
SHM purposes. Finally, because OFDR also provides amplitude data as a function of fiber length, any po-
tential breaks in the fiber or other losses in reflectivity can be understood as a function of time and corre-
lated to aspects of the thermal cycle associated with the bonding process.
For all OFDR performed in this work, the TLS was centered at 1550 nm with a scanning range of 21.14 nm.
The gain on the OBR was set to 24 dB. In those experiments that involved bonding of the fiber using the
hot-rolling technique, the cross-correlation to determine spectral shift was performed by comparing the
scans of the fiber taken before and after hot-rolling, at room temperature. However, for the experiments re-
ported herein that involved furnace brazing of the fiber, an adaptive reference technique (Sweeney, Schrell,
and Petrie 2020) was used to monitor the fiber throughout the bonding process at time intervals of 2.5 min.
Specifically, the optical spectra from each segment of the FUT were tracked in time and then cross-correlated
with spectra from the same segment at other times, which can be structurally represented as a graph object
(Sweeney, Sweeney, and Petrie 2021). Dijkstra’s method was used to identify optimal routes to traverse
the graph for each segment to minimize an uncertainty metric and thus generate a unique, optimal path
between the initial reference scan and all subsequent scans (Sweeney and Petrie 2024). Each segment is
treated separately, which allows them to be processed in parallel while also expanding the range of spectral
shifts resolvable with OFDR through the use of adaptive reference techniques (Sweeney and Petrie 2022).
Scans were automatically obtained during the brazing experiments using a custom Python script that was

 5

used in a previous study (Sweeney and Petrie 2024). The script interfaces with a TCP/IP port in the OBR-
4600 software and allows for the control of the TLS scanning range, the sweep rate, time intervals between
scans, and other parameters. This same analysis approach, including the use of Dijkstra’s method, was ap-
plied during analysis of data obtained during heated testing of select brazed or hot-rolled fiber sensors.

2.3 HIGH-TEMPERATURE TESTING EQUIPMENT
High-temperature furnaces were used to melt braze wires and ultimately bond the metal-coated fibers to
the surrounding metals as the furnace slowly cooled and the braze solidified. The melting and solidifica-
tion of the braze material occurred on the timescale of tens of minutes. Contrarily, an oxy-acetylene torch
was used to melt braze material around the perimeter of a metal-coated fiber during a “rapid” braze exper-
iment. In this case, the melting and solidification of the braze material occurred on the timescale of sec-
onds, and there was no intention of bonding the fiber to surrounding metal components. The idea was that
the solidification and contraction of the braze material during cooling would introduce compressive strain
in the fiber. The fiber with its outer braze material could then be secured to metal components through lo-
calized tack welding or other processes. More details regarding the rapid brazing experiments are provided
in Section 3.3.
The high-temperature furnaces used for fiber brazing are shown in Figure 2(a) and 2(b). The high-temperature
furnace in Figure 2(a) is a Thermo Scientific Lindberg Blue M Model and was used for horizontal braz-
ing experiments, in which the fiber was laid within a brazing apparatus along with braze material (in the
form of wires) that melts around the fiber. The Lindberg Blue M furnace has a heated length of 61 cm
(24 in.) and a total furnace length of approximately 85 cm. The high-temperature furnace in Figure 2(b)
is an Evenheat HF TAP 1210B and was used for vertical brazing experiments, in which the fiber is packed
tightly within a brazing apparatus along with similar braze material. The Evenheat furnace has a heated
height of 33 cm and a total furnace height of 48 cm. This Evenheat furnace was not used for any of the
brazing experiments in this work, but it is an important piece of equipment for vertical brazing configura-
tions that have been attempted in the past (Birri et al. 2024). Temperatures in all furnace experiments were
monitored with a type-K thermocouple, which was interrogated at 1 Hz with a Squirrel data logger (2020
Series, Grant Instruments).

2.4 X-RAY RADIOGRAPHY
For the experiment described in Section 3.1, the uniformity of the braze within each capillary tube was
determined using x-ray radiography. Tubes were loaded into a sample holder apparatus designed to hold
the relatively weak capillary tubes vertically with hose clamps placed at regular intervals along the vertical
stem to assist in forming mosaic images. The capillary tubes and the vertical stem with reference clamps
as fiducial markers were imaged using a Zeiss VoluMax 9 Titan with a pixel size of 70 µm and an x-ray
source accelerating voltage of 200 kV operating at 750 W. Copper plates were used to filter out low-energy
x-rays that would not substantially contribute to the sample image. An overlapping series of such images
were captured along the length of the capillary tubes and were manually stitched together based on the
positions of the hose clamps on the vertical stem in each image. The resulting image contrast within each
capillary tube is dependent on local x-ray attenuation, resulting in darker regions where braze is present.

 3. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
3.1 HORIZONTAL CAPILLARY TUBE BRAZE TEST
In the Lindberg Blue M furnace, an experiment was conducted that involved the brazing of both a Cu-
coated and an Au-coated fiber within two capillary tubes. An image of the braze apparatus is shown in
Figure 3(a), and an image of the apparatus inserted into the furnace during optical interrogation is shown
in Figure 3(b). The optical fibers protrude from the apparatus through vacuum epoxy (Loctite Hysol 1C).
Two valves on the apparatus allow for the evacuation of the internals of the apparatus, followed by back-
filling with ultra-high-purity Ar. The evacuation and backfill process was performed 5 times before ex-
perimental testing to ensure an inert environment so that any potential oxidation of the braze could be
mitigated. The capillary tubes themselves had an inner diameter of 1.32 mm and an outer diameter of
1.57 mm. A total of seven braze wires of alloy BAg-5 (45% Ag, 30% Cu, and 25% Zn) with a diameter
of 0.397 mm were packed alongside the fibers—but only in the 61 cm (24 in.) region, which would be con-
sidered the heated region of the furnace.
The apparatus was kept at a positive pressure of 3–5 psi during the test to prevent the possibility of air in-
gression. The type-K thermocouple used to monitor temperature was placed at the axial center of the fur-
nace (i.e., in the axial center of the heated region). The temperature was ramped at a rate of 5 ◦ C/min to
650 ◦ C, held at 650 ◦ C for 20 min, and cooled at a rate of 5 ◦ C/min back to room temperature.
This horizontal configuration was chosen due to the issues that were faced with a vertical capillary tube
brazing configuration, which was implemented and documented in a previous report (Birri et al. 2024).
It was found that the molten braze would run down the capillary due to gravity-driven flow, which inad-
vertently broke both a Cu- and Au-coated fiber upon solidification. It was hypothesized that a horizontal
configuration would enable a pool to form, with a small void space along the length of the capillary above
the pool, and that the fiber would be within the pool by carefully packing the fibers and braze wires such
that the fiber is below all of the braze wires.

 in capillary tubes and (b) the apparatus inserted in the furnace during optical interrogation.

3.2 HORIZONTAL U-CHANNEL BRAZE TEST
In the Lindberg Blue M furnace, an experiment was conducted that involved the brazing of a Cu-coated
fiber within a U-shaped channel, as shown in Figure 4(a). The channel was approximately 61 cm (24 in.)

 8

long. The Cu-coated fiber was loosely pinned to the bottom of the channel using two tack-welded, bent
steel strips. Seven braze rods of alloy BAg-7 (56% Ag, 22% Cu, 17% Zn, and 5% Sn) were loaded on top
of the fiber, with a diameter of 1.6 mm and a length of 45.7 cm. The U-shaped channel was then contained
in a larger, 2.54 cm (1 in.) outer diameter tube, which acted as the primary vessel of the inerted apparatus
in which the brazing was performed, as shown in Figure 4(b). The U-shaped channel was kept level by its
placement on top of a half-round rod with a slightly smaller diameter than the inner diameter of the tube.
The fiber protruded from the apparatus through vacuum epoxy (Loctite Hysol 1C). Like with the horizon-
tal capillary braze test, the backfill process was performed 5 times before experimental testing to ensure an
inert environment so that any potential oxidation of the braze could be mitigated.
The apparatus was inserted into the furnace as shown in Figure 4(c). Like with the horizontal capillary
braze test, the apparatus was kept at a positive pressure of 3–5 psi during the test to prevent the possibil-
ity of air ingression, and the type-K thermocouple was placed at the axial center of the furnace. The tem-
perature was ramped at a rate of 5 ◦ C/min to 700 ◦ C, held at 700 ◦ C for 20 min, and cooled at a rate of 5
◦ C/min back to room temperature.

This particular braze configuration was chosen so that visual inspection of the braze material itself would
be possible after testing. While the U-shaped channel is not necessarily an ideal implementation for sens-
ing applications, this test provides a crucial understanding of how the braze material behaves after being
heated in the manner described (i.e., a slow furnace braze). Such insights can be used to better understand
the results of the capillary tube braze test described in the previous section. It is also a relatively simple
apparatus to set up, as it does not involve careful packing of braze wires and fibers into small tubes.

3.3 HORIZONTAL RAPID BRAZE TEST
Open brazing was performed using an oxyacetylene welding torch applied as the heat source to melt the
braze. As shown in Figure 5, the setup included using a 9.52 mm diameter SS304 rod with a machined
1.19 mm diameter channel along the surface of the rod in which the optical fiber was placed. A Cu-coated
fiber was prepared and laid in the channel on the rod and secured with tape at the ends. BAg-1 braze (nom-
inal composition of 45% Ag, 15% Cu, 16% Zn, and 24% Cd) wire (0.8 mm diameter) was cut to length

and placed in the channel on top of the fiber. The flame was applied to the bottom of the rod to raster back
and forth underneath the channel. The flame was originally applied directly over the top of the braze wires,
but the intensity of the flame caused immediate fiber failure. Heating was performed until the braze melted
and bonded the fiber to the rod.

3.4 ASSESSMENT OF HOT-ROLLED FIBERS
In addition to the brazing experiments described in the previous sections, tests were conducted to assess
the integrity of the bonding of Cu-coated fibers, which were embedded via a hot-rolling technique at PNNL.
The details of the hot-rolling embedding process can be found in a separate report (Kalsar et al. 2025).
Three different hot-rolled Al-6061 specimens were fabricated at PNNL. Prior to hot-rolling, Cu-coated
fiber assemblies were prepared and scanned with the OBR-4600 at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).
Two of these specimens were rolled at a 40% reduction, while the third was rolled at a 20% reduction; only
the specimen rolled at a 20% reduction resulted in a fully intact fiber, and thus only this specimen was fur-
ther analyzed at ORNL.
Upon receipt of the specimen with retained integrity, the fiber was scanned once again with the OBR-4600
to determine the residual compressive strain on the fiber due to the hot-rolling process. The fiber was then
tested in a simple thermal cycling test up to 80◦ C to assess whether or not the thermal strain on the fiber
would follow the expected strain based on the Al-6061’s coefficient of thermal expansion. An image of the
hot-rolled specimen on a hot plate used for this testing is shown in Figure 6. As with previously described
brazing experiments, a type-K thermocouple was placed adjacent to the specimen. Not shown in the pic-
ture is additional insulation that was placed on top of the specimen and thermocouple to prevent significant
conductive and convective heating losses at the top surface of the specimen.

 4. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
4.1 HORIZONTAL CAPILLARY TUBE BRAZE TEST RESULTS
Figure 7 shows the results from the OFDR scans that were taken throughout the horizontal furnace braze
of the Cu-coated fiber and during the full heating profile, which is shown in Figure 7(c). Figure 7(a) shows
the amplitude as a function of length along the fiber and time. The figure shows clearly that during the
cooldown portion of the heating profile, two breaks occurred within the estimated length of the fiber over
which the braze alloy was bonding. These breaks are evidenced by the large increases in backscattered
light intensity that occurred at approximately times 0.17 d and 0.20 d; therefore, only a short length of the
fiber, closer to the insertion location of the fiber into the capillary tube, survived the full furnace heating
profile.
Within the region of the fiber that did survive the entire test, a relatively large negative spectral shift was
observed as the fiber eventually cooled to room temperature. This means that the fiber was put into an
axially compressed state after cooling, implying that the fiber was bonded at a high temperature with the
braze alloy. For a select location of the fiber within this region of the fiber that survived, the strain (after
converting from spectral shift) in this location is plotted as a function of time and plotted alongside the fur-
nace temperature as measured by the type-K thermocouple in Figure 7(b). After cooling, the Cu-coated
fiber reached a compressed state of approximately −8400 µm/m. For context, assuming sound bonding
with the braze alloy, this would correspond with reaching a zero-strain state at ≈ 400◦ C if the capillary
tube were strain-coupled to a stainless steel 316 component.
Figure 8 shows the results from the OFDR scans that were taken throughout the furnace braze of the Au-
coated fiber and during the full heating profile, which is shown in Figure 8(c). Figure 8(a) shows that a
break occurred within the estimated braze region at approximately 0.18 d. Unlike with the Cu-coated fiber
shown in Figure 7(a), this break occurred further downstream within the fiber, meaning a larger length of
the fiber survived in the estimated braze region. However, a comparison of Figure 7(b) and Figure 8(b)
shows that the spectral shift results are less promising for the Au-coated fiber than for the Cu-coated fiber.
The negative spectral shift along the length of the fiber in the survived braze region after cooldown was
significantly less pronounced for the Au-coated fiber than that of the Cu-coated fiber, except for a very
short region located at the fiber insertion point into the braze region. This signifies that the Cu-coated fiber
may have bonded to a greater extent to the braze alloy used in this experiment, and at a higher temperature,
than the Au-coated fiber. The resultant strain in the Au-coated fiber after furnace cooldown is shown in
Figure 8(c) for three select locations along the region of the fiber that survived the braze. Depending on the
location, the compressive strain ranges from being relatively close to an unstrained-state (−300 µm/m) to a
moderately high compressive strain (−5400 µm/m).
The x-ray radiographs were analyzed to identify the potential reasons for the non-uniform spectral shift
(proportional to strain) profiles in Figures 7 and 8. The stitched radiographs are shown in Figure 9. Note
that the image in Figure 9 shows the fiber such that the insertion point of the fiber is at the top of the im-
age, which is the inverse of the plots in Figures 7 and 8.
In both the Cu-coated fiber and the Au-coated fiber, gaps in braze material are present along the length
of the fiber. Ideally, the braze would have solidified in a uniform manner; the non-uniform solidification
indicates that surface tension effects in the liquid state of the braze likely prevented the braze from flowing
and uniformly filling the capillary. It should be noted that a void space was expected because the volume
of the braze wires was lower than the internal volume of the capillary tube. The intent of the horizontal
braze configuration was to allow the braze to create a pool along the length of the capillary, encapsulating
the fiber, with the void space above the pool surface distributed evenly along the capillary.

Inspection of the fiber after brazing was possible due to the openness of the U-shaped channel. Figure 11

without causing breaks along its length.

 5. CONCLUSION
In this work, four experiments were conducted to further evaluate techniques for high-temperature bonding
of optical fibers intended for SHM applications in current and next-generation nuclear reactor systems.
The first experiment involved the horizontal furnace brazing of Cu-coated and Au-coated fibers in stain-
less steel tubes. This experiment revealed that for Cu-coated fiber specifically, it is possible to achieve
significant levels of residual compressive strain on the fiber after brazing, which would enable Gen IV
reactor–relevant temperature applications. However, horizontal brazing in capillary tubes presents issues
with respect to applying a consistent braze along the length of the fiber, resulting in a high chance of fiber
breakage.
The second experiment involved horizontal furnace brazing of a Cu-coated fiber within a U-shaped stain-
less steel channel. This experiment revealed that it is possible to braze a long length of fiber without fiber
breakage in this configuration, but the braze material did not consistently bond. We hypothesize that the
poor bonding was caused by volatilization issues with Zn in the brazing alloy; further testing in this config-
uration with a more optimal braze material is recommended.
The third experiment involved rapid brazing of a Cu-coated fiber with an oxyacetylene torch to a stain-
less steel rod with a milled channel. This experiment revealed that the rapid brazing approach resulted in
the successful bonding of a short length of fiber. Post-braze thermal testing up to 120◦ C resulted in mea-
sured fiber strains that were consistent with the expected thermal strain levels in the embedded region of
the fiber.
The fourth experiment involved post-embedding thermal testing of a hot-rolled Cu-coated fiber in a short
length of Al-6061, which was prepared by our collaborators at PNNL. This experiment revealed that hot-
rolling is a viable technique for achieving residual compressive strain after hot-rolling, and thermal testing
up to 80◦ C yielded expected thermal strain levels in the embedded region of the fiber.
The general conclusion to be drawn from these experiments is that both brazing and hot-rolling are promis-
ing techniques for the high-temperature bonding of optical fibers and may enable static and dynamic strain
monitoring in both Gen III and Gen IV nuclear reactor systems. Additional experiments will be needed to
develop strategies for bonding longer lengths of fiber and to demonstrate their coupling to relevant struc-
tural materials at operating temperatures that are relevant for Gen III and Gen IV reactors.
