A feasibility and preliminary effectiveness study of a tiered gynecological laparoscopic training curriculum in southwestern China
Authors: Xue Dong, Yihong Chen, Xin Tan, Xi Zeng, Tao Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEssential yet deficient gynecological laparoscopy training in China demands urgent structured solutions. This study aimed to develop and preliminarily evaluate a specialized laparoscopic surgical training curriculum for enhancing surgical skills among gynecologists.MethodsOur laparoscopic training program ran from September 2022 to December 2025 and comprised three phases. Phase I developed a laparoscopic skills curriculum through needs assessment, hierarchical task analysis, and expert panel consensus. Phase II surveyed 112 participants (junior, intermediate, and senior groups) using Likert-scale and ranking questions to evaluate satisfaction and perceived effectiveness. Phase III conducted pre-/post-training assessments of pattern cutting and suturing tasks in 37 participants, analyzing relationships to external variables. We employed the Shapiro–Wilk test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and paired samples t-test for statistical analyses (significance: P < 0.05).ResultsA total of 112 trainees were enrolled and divided into junior (n = 34), intermediate (n = 47), and senior (n = 31) groups. Overall training satisfaction was high and comparable among groups across most dimensions, except that prior laparoscopic training experience was associated with greater satisfaction regarding training productivity. Trainees showed significant post-training improvements in self-assessed laparoscopic skills, including hand–eye coordination, bimanual dexterity, energy device use, and suturing (all P < 0.001). Perfused organ modules were the most preferred training component overall, with notable level-stratified differences: seniors favored perfused and ex vivo organs, intermediates preferred perfused organs and box-trainers, whereas juniors prioritized box-trainers and theoretical instruction. Post-training objective assessments (n = 37) revealed significantly reduced operation time in pattern cutting and suturing tasks, as well as improved suturing quality (all P < 0.05). Multivariate regression showed that improvements in suturing skills were not significantly associated with age, seniority, or previous training history.ConclusionsTraining preferences differed by proficiency level: juniors preferred theory and simulators, whereas intermediate and senior trainees favored perfused and ex vivo practice. This stratified model is feasible and effective for standardized laparoscopic training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09652-3.Keywords: Gynecology, Laparoscopic surgery, Simulation training, Surgical education

Introduction

With the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgery in gynecology, laparoscopic techniques have become an essential core competency for modern gynecological surgeons. The laparoscopic apparatus alters surgeons' visual and tactile perceptions during procedures, characterized by the absence of depth perception and spatial orientation cues. Surgeons must rely on visual references to reconstruct three-dimensional anatomical relationships from two-dimensional imaging systems, resulting in a steep learning curve for novices [1].

Traditional skill acquisition in laparoscopy predominantly follows an apprenticeship-based teaching model. However, this experiential training paradigm faces three critical limitations: unequal distribution of clinical educational resources leading to inconsistent training access, fragmented skill acquisition processes that hinder procedural mastery, and unavoidable patient safety risks during intraoperative teaching [2, 3]. These constraints render the traditional approach inadequate to meet the growing demand for standardized, scalable, and homogeneous surgical skill development in contemporary medical education.

Over the past decade, the American College of Surgeons has strongly advocated for the integration of simulation-based training into surgical curricula [4]. Such programs have been widely incorporated into surgical residency programs across North America and Europe [5, 6]. Emerging simulation platforms enable trainees to systematically acquire laparoscopic proficiency through controlled non-clinical environments, effectively mitigating patient safety risks while accelerating skill acquisition [7]. Nevertheless, a nationwide survey of 1,324 Chinese gynecologists revealed significant skill gaps, with < 20% (4% residents, 14% attending physicians) capable of independently performing complex procedures, while 76.6% remained limited to surgical assistance roles [8]. This underscores the critical need for structured training programs, as evidenced by established correlations between simulation-based training and improved patient safety [9, 10].

This study aimed to develop and preliminarily evaluate a competency-driven training curriculum specifically designed to enhance laparoscopic surgical proficiency among gynecologists, while generating empirical evidence to inform evidence-based optimization of surgical training paradigms.

Methods

This laparoscopy training program, integrated into China's surgical residency curriculum, was a continuing education program in Sichuan Province. The study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital (No. 2025507). Our research was divided into three phases. Phase I involved the design of the training system. In Phase II, gynecologists were recruited to complete a demographic questionnaire and pre-/post-training subjective surveys. Phase III focused on pre- and post-training objective evaluations.

Phase I: laparoscopic training system course design

Laparoscopic training system development and implementation framework

The laparoscopic surgical training program was developed through a collaborative initiative between multidisciplinary experts from West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University, integrating clinical expertise in advanced gynecological laparoscopy with pedagogical methodologies in surgical education. The curriculum design followed a systematic five-phase development cycle: frontline needs assessment, competency-based framework construction, iterative hands-on training module refinement, high-fidelity simulation integration, and continuous performance evaluation with feedback-driven optimization. This iterative process ensured alignment between training objectives and clinical practice requirements.

Curricular architecture and pedagogical progression

The program adopted a three-tiered instructional approach to facilitate knowledge acquisition and skill transfer. Initially, trainees engaged with pre-course online theoretical instruction, which comprised three-dimensional anatomical visualizations, physiological impact analyses of pneumoperitoneum, standardized laparoscopic instrumentation protocols, and procedure-specific technique skills demonstrations (including salpingectomy, oophorocystectomy, myomectomy, hysterectomy, and oncologic interventions). These asynchronous materials were supplemented with preparatory assignments to establish foundational competency prior to hands-on training.

The core practical component featured a two-tiered structure integrating online learning with hands-on simulation training. Online learning involved live surgical broadcasts with real-time expert commentary, enabling trainees to observe intraoperative decision-making and technical execution across six clinical cases. Simulation training implemented hierarchical skill progression through virtual reality and simulator drills—progressing from foundational techniques (transferring, thread passing/navigation, pattern cutting, and suturing and knot-tying, Fig. 1) through intermediate procedures (salpingectomy, ovarian cystectomy) to advanced operations (complex adhesiolysis, deep myomectomy, total hysterectomy)—alongside biological tissue modules utilizing ex vivo porcine organs for salpingectomy, ovarian cystectomy, and myomectomy, and perfused organ systems (co-developed with BoYiTimes Medical Technology, Beijing) for hysterectomy, salpingectomy, ovarian cystectomy, and lymphadenectomy-specific vascular skeletonization (Fig. 2). All ex vivo biological tissues were commercially sourced food-grade materials purchased from a local market (including porcine organs, chicken thighs, beef, and porcine intestines). No live animals were used in any component of this training program. All participants completed ≥ 2 procedural rehearsals per complexity tier until meeting predefined proficiency standards, with this integrated approach bridging the simulation-clinical gap through competency-based skill acquisition.

Expert mentorship and training procedure

Participants underwent proficiency-based stratification into senior, intermediate, and junior cohorts with mandatory online didactic completion, followed by operative demonstrations and cohort-specific training wherein junior trainees executed ≥ 3 simulator sessions plus competency-aligned VR drills, intermediates performed ≥ 2 sessions, and seniors optionally conducted single-session refinement; subsequent ex vivo and live-animal modules implemented cross-tier dyads (senior-intermediate/senior-junior/intermediate-junior) with procedure-tiered assignments—juniors in salpingectomy/cystectomy with suturing, intermediates in myomectomy suturing, vaginal cuff closure, and hysterectomy, and seniors in precision vascular dissection/repair—all facilitated through a central mentorship model featuring board-certified laparoscopic surgeons (≥ 10 years' experience) providing real-time console guidance, intraoperative decision-making analysis, complication management demonstrations, and personalized feedback throughout the training continuum.

Phase II: evaluation of expert-reviewed satisfaction and self-assessment questionnaires

From December 2022 to December 2025, we recruited volunteer gynecologists from Southwest China to participate in the training program, including residents, attending physicians, and senior consultants. The trainees were categorized into junior, intermediate, and senior groups according to their proficiency in laparoscopic surgery.

Junior group: Regardless of years of working experience, all trainees had been exposed to gynecologic laparoscopy but were unable to perform any gynecologic laparoscopic procedures independently, with no prior participation in simulation-based laparoscopic training. Intermediate group: Regardless of years of working experience, trainees were able to perform some laparoscopic procedures independently, including salpingectomy, adnexectomy, and cystectomy. Senior group: Attending physicians or those with higher professional titles who had performed more than 100 supervised laparoscopic procedures and could independently perform simple total hysterectomy.

Expert-reviewed surveys were administered pre- and post-training (Supplementary file). A panel of three board-certified gynecological laparoscopic surgeons (each with > 10 years of experience) assessed content relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness through iterative review. Pilot testing on five gynecologists not included in the final sample led to minor wording revisions. No formal internal consistency assessment (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was performed, as the questionnaires were designed for program evaluation rather than as validated psychometric instruments. The surveys, built and managed via WJX online tool (Changsha Ranxing IT Ltd.), included Likert five-point scales, 1–10 rating scales, and ranking questions.

At the beginning of the training program, participants were asked to complete a pre-course survey that included demographic information, such as age, previous course enrollment, handedness (left or right), video game experience, monthly laparoscopic case volume, and a Self-assessment Questionnaire of Laparoscopic Skills. The Laparoscopic Skills Self-Assessment Questionnaire employed a 10-point Likert scale (1 = worst, 10 = best) to evaluate four domains: hand–eye coordination (precise positioning), bimanual coordination, laparoscopic energy device utilization, and suturing proficiency.

The post-test survey inquired about participants' perceptions of the enjoyability, utility, and authenticity of the simulations, as well as the session format. The satisfaction survey employed a 5-point Likert scale to assess satisfaction with the active learning activities based on five characteristics: (1) Usefulness, (2) Productivity, (3) Reasonableness, (4) Appropriateness, and (5) Prospective utility. The combined score ranged from 5 to 25, with 5 being the least satisfied and 25 being the most satisfied. Participants identified the most beneficial simulator training project (e.g., peg transfer, thread passing/navigation, pattern cutting, suturing and knot-tying). Additionally, participants were asked to rank all six training programs from best to worst in terms of the benefits they received: perfusion systems, didactic lectures, ex vivo models, VR simulation, simulator training, and surgical demonstrations. Only the top four ranks were analyzed, with points assigned as follows: 10 points for first rank, 7 for second, 5 for third, and 3 for fourth. The fifth and sixth ranks were not included in the analysis. Mean scores were then calculated for each modality based on the top four ranks.

Phase III: evaluation of validity evidence relevant to performance measures

Starting in April 2024, 37 participants underwent pre- and post-training evaluations involving two standardized laparoscopic tasks. All assessments were conducted via live observation by a panel of three evaluators, all of whom were senior gynecological laparoscopic surgeons with extensive clinical experience. The evaluators were not blinded to participants' pre- or post-training status, as they were members of the training assessment team. Inter-rater reliability was not calculated, as the evaluators provided consensus-based scores during live observation rather than independent ratings. The first task assessed precision tissue dissection using a circular pattern excision with laparoscopic scissors and Kelly clamps on paper-mounted LaparoBowl platforms. Performance was quantified by completion time (capped at 300 s; if exceeded, it was recorded as 300 s) and error analysis, including boundary violations, double-layer transection, material avulsion, or device displacement. The second task assessed suturing and knot-tying competencies using the Suturing Training and Testing model (SUTT1), developed by the European Society for Gynaecological Endoscopy (ESGE) [11, 12]. The task evaluated four domains: (1) technical execution, requiring four interrupted stitches and one randomized 2–1-1 surgical knot using a 20 cm 2–0 suture; (2) spatial precision, requiring needle passage through 0.5 mm tolerance targets with 5 mm suture tails; (3) time efficiency, capped at 900 s (recorded as 900 s if exceeded); and (4) suturing quality, assessed using the ESGE/SUTT1 scoring system. The quality score comprised four components: DOT (accuracy of anatomical trajectory, score 0–10), KNOT (integrity of the three-throw square knot, score 0–2), KNOT TRAUMA (preservation of the suturing board, score 0–2), and CORRECT STITCH (consistency of stitch spacing and tension, score 0–5), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 19.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis of both impact and satisfaction metrics was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Normality was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests. For non-normally distributed data, Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted to examine whether rankings of factor importance were different by cohort, with post hoc pairwise comparisons. Pre-post training changes were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. For normally distributed data, paired-samples t-tests were used. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

A prioritized analysis of training components revealed overall preference for perfused organ modules (average ranking: 6.24), followed by box-trainer simulators (5.13), and ex vivo organ modules (4.07). Stratified by class level (Table 5), senior trainees prioritized perfused organ modules (8.42), ex vivo organ modules (4.74), and surgical broadcasts (4.64); intermediates favored perfused organ modules (5.87), box-trainer simulators (5.36) and ex vivo organ modules (4.15); while juniors emphasized box-trainer simulators (6.61), theoretical instruction (4.35), and perfused organ module (4.76). The rankings of these six programs were largely consistent across the cohorts, except for perfused organ modules (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.001), box-trainer simulators (P = 0.001), and theoretical instruction (P = 0.01). Post hoc tests (Table 6) revealed that compared with the Juniors, the senior and intermediate groups ranked perfused organ modules higher (adjusted P < 0.001), box-trainer simulators (adjusted P = 0.001) and “theoretical instruction” (adjusted P = 0.012) lower. Intermediates ranked “perfused organ modules” higher (adjusted P = 0.005) and “theoretical instruction” lower (adjusted P = 0.050). As demonstrated in Fig. 3A regarding the perceived importance of simulator training modules, approximately 86.61% of trainees prioritized suturing and knot-tying practice as the most critical training component.

For the pattern cutting exercise, post-training completion times were significantly reduced compared to pre-training values (155.00 [54.00–270.00] vs. 230.00 [71.00–300.00], P < 0.001). Subsequent stratified analysis evaluating the association between suturing skill enhancement and participants' baseline characteristics revealed no statistically significant correlations with age, professional seniority, or history of prior training participation (all P > 0.05 by multivariate regression modeling).

Discussion

In this study, we developed an integrated training system for foundational and gynecological laparoscopic skills, combining simulator training, VR modules, theoretical education, and ex vivo porcine organ platforms featuring gynecological surgical models, from salpingectomy, ovarian cystectomy, and salpingo-oophorectomy to myomectomy, total hysterectomy, and pelvic lymphadenectomy. We obtained preliminary evidence of its effectiveness in training gynecologists in laparoscopic surgical skills. In this feasibility and preliminary effectiveness study, the tiered training program was associated with short-term improvements in self-assessed and simulator-based laparoscopic performance. These findings suggest the program’s potential as a fundamental training tool for gynecologists’ laparoscopic surgical skill development, although further studies with control groups and long-term clinical outcomes are needed. In practice, we found that training for the beginner class should be dominated by theoretical lectures and simulator exercises. For the intermediate and advanced classes, priority should be given to more perfusion and ex vivo practices, combined with intensified suture training on simulators as a supplementary means.

Laparoscopic techniques present a steep learning curve for early-career surgeons due to their inherent operational characteristics [13]. Modern surgical education has evolved from the traditional "see one, do one, teach one" paradigm to a "see one, simulate many, do one" model [14], emphasizing the importance of well-designed curricula in developing proper laparoscopic competencies [15]. A nationwide survey of 1,324 surgical residents and attending physicians in China revealed only 30.7% (n = 406) had received standardized laparoscopic training 8. This aligns with our study where 42.86% of participants reported prior laparoscopic training, with significant variation across skill levels: junior (23.53%), intermediate (30.30%), and senior trainees (70.97%). The ascending prevalence of training from junior to senior levels suggests a potential correlation between training completion and surgical skill advancement. Notably, 79.46% of our participants practiced at tertiary hospitals (59.82% at Grade A institutions), indicating greater access to training and higher awareness among physicians in high-level healthcare institutions.

Concurrently, an increasing number of institutions are developing training programs incorporating surgical simulation and assessment tools [16]. Our findings revealed significant variation in the perceived importance of laparoscopic training modalities, strongly correlated with participants' experience levels. For novices, laparoscopic skill acquisition encompasses three-dimensional spatial awareness, hand–eye coordination, and mastery of elongated instruments under restricted tactile feedback (a conclusion consistent with our observations) [17]. Junior trainees in our study relied most heavily on box-trainer simulators, while intermediate trainees ranked them as the second priority, reflecting the core needs for standardized fundamental skill development. Notably, box-trainer simulators maintained higher preference ratings than theoretical instruction and VR modules even among senior surgeons, underscoring that even advanced trainees who can perform independent surgery still require fundamental laparoscopic skills, such as suturing, and may further refine these basic competencies through targeted training. Although numerous studies validate VR's efficacy in laparoscopic training [18, 19], VR was consistently ranked lowest across all cohorts in our investigation. This may be attributed both to our program's inclusion of more realistic ex vivo organ models with perfusion systems and to perceived fidelity limitations in VR simulations. This observation aligns with the conclusion of Jin et al. that VR should not be prioritized in surgical curricula; when implemented, it remains most suitable for novice medical students or junior residents [20]. These findings underscore the imperative for tiered curricula: junior trainees require intensified basic simulator training and theoretical framework development; intermediate trainees benefit from a combination of simulator practice and perfusion system training; while senior trainees benefit most from high-fidelity biological modules to enhance complex surgical skills. Educational strategies must dynamically adapt to trainees' experiential levels to optimize resource allocation and training effectiveness. We acknowledge that the multi-modal design precludes isolation of each component's contribution; therefore, we interpret the program as a bundled intervention, with observed improvements attributed to the combined effect of the entire curriculum.

Notably, 86.61% of trainees identified suturing and knot-tying as the most essential technical components, consistent with existing literature documenting its challenging learning curve in minimally invasive surgery [21, 22]. Our data revealed no correlation between suturing proficiency and age, seniority, or prior training experience (P > 0.05). This is consistent with previous studies. The duration of training is positively correlated with the proficiency of suture techniques, regardless of trainees' baseline laparoscopic experience [23, 24]. This suggests that structured suturing training should be implemented early in surgical education.

The training model's anatomical fidelity directly correlates with its educational value [25]. While ex vivo organ models offer cost-effective alternatives for realistic surgical simulation [26], our study introduces two novel gynecological training modules: (1) perfused ex vivo total hysterectomy simulation system; (2) vascular skeletonization module for lymphadenectomy training. These innovations address the critical gap in specialty-specific laparoscopic training tools. Besides, Intermediate and senior trainees prioritized perfused organ modules for their high-fidelity simulation of real surgical environments, meeting advanced skill development needs. This aligns with Zhang et al.'s success in developing perfused renal models for urological training [27], confirming the efficacy of perfusion-enhanced simulation in surgical education.

This study's primary strengths encompass a rigorously developed training framework created through multidisciplinary expert collaboration, featuring a standardized simulation system that integrates surgical demonstrations, didactic instruction, simulator modules, and live animal model scenarios—collectively forming a reproducible training paradigm. Additionally, the participant cohort encompasses gynecological laparoscopists from tertiary hospitals alongside practitioners from resource-limited primary care institutions, who have minimal surgical volume and significant technical proficiency disparities. The study has several notable limitations. First, the single-center, single-arm design without a control group limits causal inference and generalizability. Additionally, the follow-up was conducted only immediately post-training, which precludes assessment of long-term skill retention. Second, response bias may exist in surveys despite anonymization. Third, objective assessments lacked blinding and inter-rater reliability assessment, and skill transfer to the operating room was not evaluated. Fourth, the questionnaires were expert-reviewed but not formally validated for internal consistency or test–retest reliability. Fifth, the concurrent use of multiple training modalities precludes isolation of each component’s individual contribution. Finally, downstream clinical impacts (e.g., operative time, complications) remain unquantified. Future multi-center, controlled studies with extended follow-up and clinical outcome measures are needed.

In summary, we present an innovative gynecological laparoscopic training system featuring a preliminarily evaluated tiered progression framework—from residents to attending physicians through senior consultants—tailored to stage-specific competency development. Our findings demonstrate promising short-term efficacy. Future validation should focus on objective trainee skill metrics and clinical patient benefits across diverse healthcare settings.
