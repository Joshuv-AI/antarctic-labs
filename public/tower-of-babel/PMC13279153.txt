Spontaneous septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis mimicking acute abdomen: Diagnostic value of MRI
Authors: Meriem Boui, Ahmed Hicham Benomar, Ouijdane Zamani, Meryem Edderai, Zakaria Toufga, Mohamed Abdellaoui, Ouadie El Menaoui, Ayman El Farouki, Driss Benchebba, Bouchaib Chafry, Hassan En-Nouali, Jamal El Fenni, Rachida Saouab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is an uncommon and frequently overlooked condition, typically occurring as a postoperative complication. Spontaneous cases without identifiable risk factors are exceptionally rare and may present with misleading clinical features, often mimicking acute abdominal, gynecological, urological, or hip-related conditions. We report the case of a 59-year-old woman with no prior medical or surgical history who presented with acute febrile hypogastric and suprapubic pain. Initial clinical and laboratory findings suggested a lower abdominal or pelvic inflammatory process, leading to diagnostic uncertainty. Computed tomography revealed inflammatory changes centered on the pubic symphysis, with symphyseal erosions and a retropubic/prevesical fluid collection. Magnetic resonance imaging further characterized the lesion, demonstrating bone marrow edema, soft tissue inflammatory involvement, and an ill-defined fluid collection with surrounding enhancement and caudal extension toward the proximal adductor and perineal soft tissues. Ultrasound-guided aspiration confirmed the diagnosis, yielding approximately 13 mL of purulent material positive for Staphylococcus aureus. Targeted antibiotic therapy resulted in rapid clinical improvement, with follow-up MRI showing marked regression of the collection and residual fluid-sensitive inflammatory changes. This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of spontaneous septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis and illustrates the value of cross-sectional imaging, especially MRI, for early characterization of disease extent, minimally invasive diagnostic confirmation, and post-treatment assessment.

Introduction

Septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is a rare and often underrecognized musculoskeletal infection, most commonly described in postoperative or otherwise predisposed patients [1]. Its clinical presentation is frequently nonspecific and misleading, often mimicking abdominal, gynecological, urological, or hip-related conditions, which may result in diagnostic delay [1,2]. MRI is regarded as the most sensitive imaging modality for early detection of osteoarticular infection, allowing identification of bone marrow edema, soft tissue extension, and abscess formation, whereas image-guided aspiration remains essential for microbiological confirmation and treatment adaptation [3,4]. Spontaneous cases in patients without identifiable risk factors are exceptionally rare. We report a case of spontaneous septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis, emphasizing the diagnostic difficulty and the contribution of multimodality imaging to diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old woman with no significant medical or surgical history presented to the emergency department with progressively worsening hypogastric and suprapubic pain over 1 week, associated with intermittent fever. She denied urinary or gastrointestinal symptoms. On admission, the patient was febrile (39.5°C) with tachycardia (105 beats/min). Physical examination revealed localized tenderness in the suprapubic and anterior pelvic region, without signs of peritoneal irritation. No specific gynecological signs, such as vaginal discharge or adnexal tenderness, were reported. The remainder of the clinical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis of 12,800/mm³ (reference range, 4000-10,000/mm³) and markedly elevated C-reactive protein at 192 mg/L (reference range, <5 mg/L), while urinalysis was normal. Given the febrile lower abdominal and pelvic presentation, the initial diagnostic considerations included atypical appendicitis and a nonspecific pelvic inflammatory or gynecological process.

Because of persistent suprapubic pain with localized anterior pelvic tenderness, pelvic radiographs were obtained and demonstrated irregularity and subtle widening of the pubic symphysis. Abdominal ultrasound excluded appendicitis and adnexal pathology. In view of the persistent diagnostic uncertainty, contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis was performed. Pelvic MRI provided further characterization, demonstrating low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and marked hyperintensity on T2-weighted STIR sequences involving the pubic symphysis, consistent with bone marrow edema (Fig. 2A). These abnormalities were associated with adjacent soft tissue inflammation and a small retropubic/prevesical fluid collection with surrounding inflammatory enhancement and caudal extension into the proximal adductor and anterior perineal soft tissues (Fig. 2B–D). The patient was hospitalized and started on empirical antibiotic therapy. Ultrasound-guided aspiration of the collection yielded approximately 13 mL of purulent material. Microbiological analysis identified Staphylococcus aureus, allowing targeted antimicrobial treatment according to antibiogram results. The clinical and biological course was favorable under adapted therapy. Discussion

Septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is an uncommon entity and a rare location of osteoarticular infection. Clinical recognition remains difficult because presentation is often vague and misleading. In the largest review available, Ross and Hu emphasized the polymorphic nature of this condition, with manifestations ranging from fever and suprapubic pain to gait disturbance and pain on hip motion, all of which may contribute to delayed diagnosis [1]. Most published cases occur in patients with identifiable predisposing factors, particularly pelvic or urological surgery, intense athletic activity, pelvic malignancy, or intravenous drug use [1,2,4,5]. The absence of such factors makes the diagnosis less intuitive and increases the likelihood of initial diagnostic error. Under these circumstances, infection of the pubic symphysis may be mistaken for more common abdominal, gynecological, urological, or hip-related disorders, especially when pelvic pain predominates over locomotor complaints [1,2,5].

Another important differential diagnosis is osteitis pubis, a sterile inflammatory disorder usually associated with repetitive mechanical stress, pelvic surgery, or postpartum changes. Distinguishing osteitis pubis from septic arthritis is essential because therapeutic strategies differ substantially: the former is generally managed conservatively, whereas the latter requires microbiological confirmation and targeted antimicrobial therapy [6]. Imaging is fundamental both for diagnosis and for assessment of local extent. Plain radiographs are often normal or only subtly abnormal at an early stage and become more informative later, when erosions, irregularity of the symphyseal margins, or widening of the joint may appear [2–4,7]. CT is useful for depicting cortical bone destruction and associated collections, and it may also facilitate image-guided aspiration or biopsy [3,4,7]. MRI nevertheless remains the most sensitive technique for early detection, as it accurately demonstrates bone marrow edema, soft tissue inflammation, abscess formation, and extension to adjacent muscular compartments [3,7]. The combination of marrow signal abnormalities centered on the pubic symphysis, surrounding inflammatory soft tissue changes, and a retropubic/prevesical fluid collection with inflammatory enhancement strongly favors the diagnosis of active infection.

Although imaging findings can be highly suggestive, definitive diagnosis still relies on microbiological confirmation. S. aureus is the pathogen most frequently reported in the literature, although Gram-negative and polymicrobial infections may occur depending on the clinical context, especially after pelvic procedures or in complex pelvic disease [1,2,4,5]. Image-guided aspiration therefore represents a major step, both diagnostically and therapeutically, because it allows pathogen identification and adaptation of antibiotic therapy while avoiding unnecessary surgical exploration in selected patients.

Management depends on disease severity and local extent. While surgical debridement may be required in chronic forms, fistulizing disease, or extensive bone destruction, several reports have shown that conservative treatment combining drainage or aspiration with prolonged targeted antibiotic therapy can be effective when diagnosis is established early [2,4,5]. Hansen et al. reported a predominantly surgical experience in a cohort of complex referred patients, yet also underlined the importance of adequate imaging work-up and prolonged antibiotic treatment [4]. Follow-up MRI is also useful in therapeutic monitoring. Regression of the collection and reduction of soft tissue inflammatory changes are reliable indicators of response, whereas mild residual marrow edema or fluid-sensitive hyperintensity may persist despite favorable clinical evolution and should not necessarily be interpreted as treatment failure [3,7].

Overall, this observation highlights 3 practical points: first, septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of febrile pelvic or suprapubic pain, even in the absence of classical risk factors; second, confusion with osteitis pubis or abdominal and gynecological emergencies may delay diagnosis; and third, the combination of cross-sectional imaging and image-guided aspiration provides an effective strategy for diagnosis, microbiological confirmation, treatment planning, and follow-up.

Conclusion

Septic arthritis of the pubic symphysis is a rare but important cause of febrile pelvic pain and may be particularly difficult to recognize when no obvious predisposing factor is present. This case illustrates how multimodality imaging can clarify a misleading clinical presentation, delineate disease extent, and support minimally invasive diagnostic confirmation. Early recognition, microbiological documentation, and appropriately tailored antibiotic therapy may allow successful conservative management and help avoid unnecessary surgery.
