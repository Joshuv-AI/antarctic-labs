Mice lacking NF-ĸB1 undergo premature ovarian aging
Authors: Carolina Lliberos, Seng H Liew, Yasmin Lewis, Nadeen Zerafa, Darcy Ellis, Ashley Mansell, Steve Gerondakis, Karla Hutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemale fertility declines dramatically with age, primarily due to the loss of oocyte number and quality. This physiological event leads to ovarian dysfunction and infertility, irregular cycles and ultimately menopause. Despite its significance, the factors that underlie the natural process of age-related follicle loss, or those that promote premature ovarian aging, remain unknown. Here we show that low levels of chronic inflammation in mice lacking the nuclear factor kappa B transcription factor NF-ĸB1 (Nfkb1−/−) coincide with accelerated depletion of the ovarian reserve, characteristic of premature ovarian aging. While Nfkb1−/− mice enter adult life with normal numbers of primordial follicles, as females age, the primordial follicle pool is depleted more rapidly than in age-matched wild-type controls. Similarly, mice hemizygous for Nfkb1 (Nfkb1±) also exhibit an early loss of the ovarian reserve and a decrease in the number of corpora lutea, consistent with reduced ovulation. Loss of NF-ĸB1 was accompanied by elevated serum cytokine and intra-ovarian inflammatory levels. Overt ovarian fibrosis was not observed in aging Nfkb1−/− mice, indicating that fibrosis may not the mechanism underlying premature follicle depletion. Collectively, these data suggest that loss of NF-ĸB1 and chronic low-grade inflammation may accelerate the age-associated depletion of follicles, leading to early loss of fertility and premature menopause.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12958-026-01574-5.Keywords: Follicle, Aging, Ovary, NF-ĸB, Premature aging, Inflammation

Introduction

Female fertility progressively decreases with age, mainly due to a gradual decline in the quality and number of oocytes [6]. Women are born with a limited reserve of oocytes, which are housed in structures within the ovary termed primordial follicles. Primordial follicles are the precursors of mature hormone-producing follicles and oocytes required for ovulation. From birth onwards, the number of primordial follicles is progressively depleted through normal physiological processes, with the supply of healthy oocytes eventually becoming so low that females become infertile and undergo menopause. In addition to natural ovarian aging, follicles can be depleted much earlier than expected as a result of pathological processes, leading to premature ovarian insufficiency [24]. Women with premature ovarian insufficiency not only experience infertility, but are also at increased risk of developing heart disease and osteoporotic fractures at an early age because of reduced hormone production [33]. Although ovarian function is critical for women’s health, the molecular mechanisms that govern the rate of follicle loss and the age-related decline in ovarian function remain poorly understood.

The NF-κB signal transduction pathway is a conserved signalling network that regulates diverse functions, including differentiation, cell division and survival, immunity and stress responses [34]. Notably, NF-κB is a key transcriptional regulator of inflammation-associated genes, including pro-inflammatory interleukins, chemokines, cytokines and adhesion molecules [16, 34]. The NF-κB pathway is itself activated by pro-inflammatory signals via regulatory loops. In mammals, NF-κB transcription factors comprise homodimers and heterodimers of five related subunits, RelA (p65), NF-κB1 (p105/p50), NF-κB2 (p100/p52), Rel and RelB, with the capacity to activate or repress transcription in a context-dependent manner [16]. RelA and Rel containing dimers are typically activators of pro-inflammatory gene expression. By contrast, the mature NF-ĸB1 subunit p50 lacks an intrinsic capacity to promote transcription, with NF-κB1 homodimers (p50/p50) typically functioning as transcriptional repressors that compete with activating NF-ĸB dimers to determine the levels of NF-ĸB dependent gene expression [34].

Nfkb1−/− mice, which lack both p105, the NF-κB1 precursor protein, and the mature p50 NF-ĸB1 subunit [31], are a well-established model of progressive chronic low grade inflammation and premature aging [2, 12]. The inability of Nfkb1−/− mice to form repressive NF-ĸB1 homodimers, while still retaining a capacity to express activating NF-κB dimers, is believed to elevate the basal NF-κB dependent transcription that contributes to a chronic low-grade inflammatory phenotype [11, 15]. Indeed, Nfkb1−/− mice have abnormally high circulating levels of certain pro-inflammatory cytokines that include IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 and IFN-γ, as well as extensive immune cell infiltrates in many tissues [12, 26, 27, 39]. Notably, this enhanced immune activity is associated with premature aging [2, 12], autoimmune pathology [20, 39], cancer [26] and a shorter life expectancy [2]. Even loss of a single Nfkb1 allele in mice results in inflammation-associated pathology, a finding consistent with the recent observation that certain loss of function heterozygous variants of NFKB1 are associated with inflammatory diseases in humans [4, 13].

Several studies have suggested that chronic inflammatory conditions are associated with early depletion of the ovarian reserve [8, 28, 30], resulting in loss of fertility and premature menopause. Consistent with this hypothesis, mice lacking TNF-α exhibit a prolongation of ovarian function and fertility, accompanied with a preserved ovarian reserve and enhanced ovulation rate, that coincides with increased litter sizes [7]. Similarly, [38] published results showing that mice lacking either IL-1α or IL-1β had a prolonged ovarian lifespan [38]. These works provide evidence that IL-1 and TNF-α-dependent signalling, potentially mediated in part through dysregulated NF-ĸB activation, may be partly responsible for the age-related loss of fertility in mice, and possibly even humans.

Recent studies highlight the emerging importance of NF‑κB signalling in ovarian inflammatory responses and pathology. For example, in a PCOS rat model, NF‑κB signalling was found to be upregulated in ovaries, promoting chronic low-grade inflammation and granulosa cell apoptosis through IL‑6, TNF‑α, and pro-apoptotic pathways [45]. Similarly, NF‑κB canonical pathway activation, including increased NF‑κB1/p105/p50 expression, has been observed in granulosa cells exposed to oxidative stress, linking cytokine secretion and apoptosis and supporting a role for NF‑κB in granulosa cell dysfunction under inflammatory or stress conditions [46]. Interestingly, a recent study using NFκB1C59A mice, which carry a point mutation designed to reduce p50 specifically, reported increased secondary follicles, reduced antral follicles, and decreased corpora lutea, along with reduced circulating TNFα and IL-6 (i.e. reduced inflammation) [22]. However, this study did not assess the ovarian reserve of primordial follicles, or examine intraovarian inflammation, leaving the impact of chronic NF-κB dysregulation and elevated inflammation on ovarian reserve and age-related follicle depletion unexplored.

In this study, we used a comprehensive suite of complementary approaches, including stereology to accurately assess the ovarian reserve and ovarian ageing, combined with Masson trichrome and Picrosirius Red staining, TUNEL, qRT‑PCR, AMH and cytokine ELISAs, and immunohistochemistry, to characterise the specific role of NFKB1 and associated chronic low-grade inflammation in driving intra-ovarian functional and inflammatory changes in Nfkb1⁻/⁻ mice, a genetic model of systemic premature ageing.

Materials and methods

Animals

Litter matched female Nfkb1−/−, Nfkb1± and Nfkb1+/+ (referred to throughout as WT) mice on a C57BL/6 background were bred by inter-crossing Nfkb1± animals in our own facility (Monash University ARL) and provided by Dr Steve Gerondakis (Monash University). The generation of Nfkb1−/− mice has been previously described [31]. Genotyping was performed by PCR using allele-specific primers to distinguish WT and targeted alleles, according to established protocols for this strain (e.g. Jackson Laboratory,Nfkb1tm1Bal)Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled high-barrier facility (Monash University ARL) under a 12 h light–dark cycle, with free access to mouse chow and water. All animal procedures and experiments, performed in accordance with the NHMRC Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals, were approved by the Monash Animal Research Platform Animal Ethics Committee. Mice were euthanized by isoflourane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Ovaries and blood were collected at 2 (young sexually mature), 6 (peak fertility) and 12 months of age (reproductively old); estrous stage was not recorded. No animals were used in this study if they had evidence of skin lesions or significant weight loss.

Quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA isolated from homogenized ovaries using an RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, 74,104) was treated with DNase I (Qiagen, 79,254) and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). RNA (500 ng) was reverse transcribed using a SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, 18,080,051) and Quantitative Real-time PCR performed using a Bio-Rad CFX384 machine (cycling conditions: 2 min at 95ºC, 40 cycles of 5 s at 95ºC, 10 s at 60ºC) and QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Master Mix as indicated by the manufacturer (Qiagen, 208,052). Gapdh was the validated housekeeping control transcript. mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [17]. Results are expressed as fold change in expression compared to WT mice for each age group. The primers used in this study are detailed in Supplemental Table 1.

Histology

Formalin fixed ovaries were embedded in paraffin and serially sectioned at 5 µm intervals for Picrosirius red staining, TUNEL assay and immunohistochemistry. Ovaries for stereology were fixed in Bouin’s solution, embedded and processed in Glycol Methacrylate (GMA) resin and serially sectioned at 20 µm. Every third section was collected and stained with Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) and counterstained with hematoxylin for follicle number assessment.

Primordial and primary follicle quantification

Stereology was used to accurately quantify the primordial and primary follicle numbers, as previously described [25, 29, 43]. Follicle counters were blinded to the genotype. Briefly, stereology was performed using an Olympus BX50 microscope (Tokyo, Japan) mounted with an Autoscan stage (Autoscan Systems Pty Ltd, Melbourne, Australia) and a × 100 oil immersion objective. The stage was controlled by StereoInvestigator software (Version 2018.2.2, MBF Bioscience 2015, MicroBrightField, Inc., Williston, Vermont). Every third 20-µm resin section was counted. Primordial and primary follicle numbers were determined by multiplying the raw counts (Q) by all three sampling fractions (1/f1, 1/f2 and 1/f3) [29, 43]. Only follicles with the oocyte nucleus clearly visible were counted and identified. Representative images of follicles were captured on an Olympus DotSlide using an Olympus XC10 camera.

Quantification of growing follicles, atretic follicles, and corpora lutea

Every ninth section was used to quantify the number of secondary, antral and atretic follicles using a 10 × objective under a Nikon light microscope. Follicles were classified according to previous publications [25, 29, 43], and were only counted if the oocyte nucleus was clearly visible. In brief, secondary follicles were classified by the presence of more than one layer of granulosa cells with no antrum, while antral follicles were identified by the presence of an antral cavity. Atretic follicles were identified when more than 10% of granulosa cells appeared pyknotic (indicative of apoptosis). To avoid double counting, the number of corpora lutea was determined in every third section encompassing the whole ovary. Secondary and antral follicle numbers were obtained by multiplying the sum of the raw follicle count for each section of a single ovary by nine. Raw counts of corpora lutea were not adjusted.

Picrosirius red staining

Paraffin embedded ovarian sections (four to nine sections per animal) were deparaffinised in histolene, rehydrated in an ethanol gradient (100, 70 and 30%) and then immersed in a Picrosirius red staining solution (0.1% w/v of Sirius red F3BA in a saturated aqueous solution of picric acid) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were washed three times in 0.5% glacial acetic acid, rapidly dehydrated using 100% ethanol, immersed in histolene and finally mounted using DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, 100,579). Slides were dried overnight, and whole tissue section images were captured on the DotSlide at × 10 magnification using an Olympus XC10 camera at the Monash Micro Imaging facility (MMI) (Clayton, Australia). The area of positive staining was quantified as previously described [5]. Briefly, ImageJ was used to quantify the area of positive stained ovarian tissue above a threshold that was set based on the staining in the WT animal. The same threshold value was used for all images analysed.

Masson trichrome staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was carried out by the Monash Histology Platform on paraffin-embedded sections. After deparaffinisation in histolene, slides were rehydrated through an ethanol gradient (100, 70 and 30%). Nuclear staining was achieved with Weigert’s iron haematoxylin. Cytoplasm and muscle fibres were stained with Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin and sections were immersed for in phosphomolybdic–phosphotungstic acid to stain collagen fibres blue. After differentiation in 1% acetic acid, the slides were dehydrated stepwise in 70 and 100% ethanol, cleared in histolene, and mounted with DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, 100,579). Slides were dried overnight, and whole tissue section images were captured on the Olympus Provis AX70 microscope at the Monash Micro Imaging facility (MMI) (Clayton, Australia).

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis was detected in 5 μm paraffin embedded ovarian Sects. (4–8 sections per ovary) using the ApopTag Peroxidase In Situ Apoptosis Detection Kit (Merck Millipore, S7100), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptotic cells were visualized by the addition of 0.05% DAB chromagen (Agilent, K3468). Sections were counterstained with Harris hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted using DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, 100579). Ovarian sections were visualized using an Olympus Provis AX70 microscope and tissues in which TdT enzyme was omitted were used as a negative control. Follicles were classified as positive if the oocyte and/or ≥ 2 granulosa cells were positive.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on formalin fixed, paraffin embedded, serially sectioned ovaries at 5 µm intervals. Four to eight ovarian tissue sections per animal were taken from the middle and edges of the ovary and then deparaffinized in histolene, rehydrated in an ethanol gradient and subjected to microwave antigen retrieval for 10 min in 0.01 M citrate acid (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidases were quenched in 0.3% H2O2. Sections were then blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature and incubated with primary antibody against Cleaved-Caspase-3 (1:200, Cell Signaling, 9661S) and α-Smooth Muscle Actin (1:4000, Abcam, ab7817) overnight at 4ºC. Sections were incubated with biotinylated goat secondary antibody against rabbit IgG (1:500, Vector Laboratories, BP-9100) or mouse IgG (1:500, Vector Laboratories, BA-9200) at room temperature, followed by an incubation with avidin–biotin peroxidase complex (Vectastein, Maravai LifeSciences, PK-6100). Peroxidase activity was visualized using 0.05% DAB chromagen (Agilent, K3468) and sections were counterstained with Harris hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted using DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, 100579). Ovarian sections were visualized using an Olympus Provis AX70 microscope and tissues in which the primary antibody was omitted were used as a negative control.

Serum levels of anti-Müllerian hormone

Serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) concentration was determined using the AMH Gen II ELISA assay kit (Beckman Coulter, California, A79766) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. WT and Nfkb1−/− serum samples were assayed in duplicate and the absorbance measured using the CLARIOstar microplate reader (BMG Labtechset, Ortenberg, Germany).

Cytokine measurement

Levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-18 in the serum were quantified by specific murine ELISA kits (Biolegend, 431307 (IL-6) and 430907 (TNF-α) and Abcam, ab216165 (IL-18)) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum samples were assayed in duplicate, with the CLARIOstar microplate reader (BMG Labtechset, Ortenberg, Germany) used to measure the absorbance at 450 and 570 nm.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, California, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. Data was normality evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analysed using unpaired Student’s t-test, while the Mann–Whitney test was chosen for data that was not normally distributed. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. N’s refer to animal number.

Results

Primordial and primary follicles are prematurely depleted in Nfkb1−/− mice

We first charactered the ovaries from WT and Nfkb1−/− mice across the normal reproductive lifespan at 2, 6 and 12 months of age (Fig. 1A). We have previously reported an analyses of follicle numbers in WT mice across this reproductive lifespan [18]. At 2 months of age, reproductively young mice have abundant follicles. By 6 months, mice remain fertile but show a substantial decline in follicle numbers, and by 12 months they are considered reproductively aged, with very few follicles and loss of fertility.

Ovarian morphology was similar in WT and Nfkb1−/− mice at each age (Fig. 1B). Follicles at all stages of development were observed at 2 and 6 months, and ovarian aging was grossly apparent at 12 months of age, characterised by a reduction in ovarian size, with few follicles evident (Fig. 1B). Further evaluation revealed that primordial and primary follicle numbers were similar in reproductively young 2-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− mice (Fig. 1C, D), suggesting that mice lacking NF-ĸB1 begin reproductive life with the normal follicular endowment. However, significantly fewer primordial and primary follicles were observed in Nfkb1−/− compared to WT mice at 6 months of age, consistent with accelerated ovarian aging. By 12 months of age, the number of primordial and primary follicles remaining in the ovary were very low in both groups of mice, with the effect of losing NF-ĸB1 no longer evident (Fig. 1B-D).

Given the striking reduction of primordial and primary follicles in Nfkb1−/− compared to WT mice at 6 months of age, we wondered if loss of a single Nfkb1 allele would also impact follicle numbers. Indeed, primordial follicles were significantly depleted in 6-month-old Nfkb1± mice compared to WT controls (Supplemental Fig. 1A), while the number of primary follicles showed a trend towards a decrease that did not reach significance (Supplemental Fig. 1B) (p > 0.05).

Growing follicles were not prematurely depleted in Nfkb1−/− mice

To assess the ability of follicles to continue growth and development towards the mature hormone producing and ovulatory stages, secondary and antral follicles were also counted in 2, 6 and 12-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− ovaries (Fig. 2A, B). Follicle death (atresia) was also determined using morphological criteria (Fig. 2C). No significant changes were observed in the number of secondary and atretic follicles (Fig. 2A, C). Interestingly, the number of antral follicles was increased at 2 months in Nfkb1−/− mice compared to WT mice, but no differences were observed at 6 and 12 months of age (Fig. 2B). Similarly, at 6 months of age, there were no significant differences in secondary, antral, or atretic follicle numbers in Nfkb1± mice when compared to WT controls (Supplemental Fig. 1C, D, F).

To investigate the possibility that genetic suppression of NF-ĸB1 might impact the ovulatory process, corpora lutea (remnants of follicles that transiently remain in the ovary after ovulation) were counted in 2, 6 and 12-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− mice (Fig. 2D). No genotype differences were observed in corpora lutea numbers at each age, indicating that the number of ovulations was unaffected by loss of NF-ĸB1. Unexpectedly however, in 6-month-old mice hemizygous for Nfkb1, the number of corpora lutea was significantly lower than in age-matched WT mice (Supplemental Fig. 1E). Thus, loss of one allele appears to negatively impact the number of follicles that ovulate.

Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) is used clinically as a biomarker of the ovarian reserve, and low serum AMH levels for age in women are indicative of premature ovarian aging [14]. To investigate the impact NF-ĸB1 loss has on AMH levels and ovarian aging, serum was collected from 6 and 12-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice. A significant decrease in serum AMH concentration was observed in 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice compared to age-matched WT animals (Fig. 2E). Given AMH is produced by primary, secondary and small antral follicles, this finding is consistent with primary follicle numbers being significantly lower in 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice relative to WT counterparts (Fig. 1D). No differences in serum AMH levels between 12-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− mice were found (Fig. 2E).

Follicle atresia was normal in Nfkb1−/− mice

To gain greater insight into secondary and antral follicular atresia and depletion at 2 and 6 months of age, the TUNEL assay and cleaved caspase-3 (CC-3) immunostaining was performed to monitor cell death (Fig. 3A, B). Positive staining was mainly localised to granulosa cells from secondary and antral follicles. No significant difference was observed in the proportion of TUNEL-positive and CC-3-positive follicles between 2-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− mice, or between 6-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− mice (Fig. 3A, B). While a trend towards an increase was observed in the percentage of TUNEL- and CC3-positive antral follicles from Nfkb1−/− mice compared to WT mice, it did not reach statistical significance.

Systemic inflammation is elevated in Nfkb1−/− mice

Previous studies indicate that loss of NF-ĸB1 transcriptional repression in Nfkb1−/− mice can lead to the constitutive activation of canonical NF-ĸB signalling and upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes [3, 12, 16, 26]. Consistent with chronic pathway activation, we observed increased phospho-RelA staining in ovaries of Nfkb1−/− mice compared to controls (Supplemental Fig. 2). We next assessed systemic cytokine levels in WT and Nfkb1−/− mice during reproductive aging (Fig. 4A-D). Both IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations were significantly higher in 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Fig. 4B, D). At 12 months of age, however, levels of circulating IL-6 and TNF-α were not significantly different in WT and Nfkb1−/− mice (Fig. 4B, D). The concentration of IL-18 did not show any change in Nfkb1−/− compared to WT mice (Fig. 4C).

The intra-ovarian inflammatory response is elevated in Nfkb1−/− mice

To investigate the effect of NF-ĸB1 loss on inflammation within the ovary, mRNA levels of the major pro-inflammatory NF-ĸB regulated genes Tnfa, Il1a, Il1b and Il6 were examined in 6 and 12-month-old WT and Nfkb1−/− ovaries (Fig. 4E-H). Expression levels of Tnfa, Il1a and Il6 were significantly increased in 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 4E, F, H). Tnfa, Il1a and Il1b expression was found to be similar in WT and Nfkb1−/− mice at 12 months of age, while Il6 remained elevated at that age (Fig. 4H).

The expression pattern of Csf1 and Csf2 were examined next. These cytokines are involved in the regulation of both macrophages and granulocytes. Csf1 and Csf2 were both significantly increased in 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− compared to WT mice (Fig. 4I, J). mRNA levels of Ccl5, a chemokine that mediates leukocyte recruitment to inflammatory sites, was also measured, with gene expression significantly increased in ovaries from 6-month-old Nfkb1−/− mice compared to the WT group (Fig. 4K).

Ovarian fibrosis was not altered in Nfkb1−/− mice

Fibrosis is the consequence of continuous or unresolved chronic inflammation and is considered a major cause of ovarian dysfunction [47]. To determine whether ovarian fibrosis was affected by the elevated inflammation associated with NF-κB1 loss, Picrosirius Red staining was used to visualize type I and III collagen fibrils in ovaries from 6- and 12-month-old WT and Nfkb1⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 5A, B). Notably, very little Picrosirius Red staining was observed in ovaries from WT and Nfkb1−/− mice at 6 months of age (when follicle depletion was already apparent) in any of the sections analysed, thus staining not quantified at this age. At 12 months of age, Picrosirius Red staining could be visually detected in most sections and when quantified, a trend was detected towards an increase in the percentage of Picrosirius Red-positive stained ovarian area from Nfkb1−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 5B), but, it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0714). We also investigated gene expression levels of collagen I (Col1a1), collagen III (Col3a1) and collagen IV (Col4a1) (Fig. 5C-F). No differences were observed between WT and Nfkb1−/− mice at any age for all the genes analysed. Finally, these tissues were also stained for α-Smooth Muscle Actin and Masson Trichrome, additional markers of fibrosis, with no obvious differences evident (Fig. 5F). While we cannot exclude subtle or localised fibrotic changes, we did not detect overtly increased fibrosis in Nfkb1⁻/⁻ ovaries using these complementary and quantitative methods consistent with those used in established studies of ovarian fibrosis during ageing [5]..

Discussion

This study demonstrates that loss of NF-κB1 (Nfkb1−/−) is associated with elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and NF-kB target genes both systemically and within the ovary. Elevated cytokine expression coincided with a reduction in primordial and primary follicles together with decreased circulating AMH levels, consistent with premature ovarian aging. These findings highlight the importance of NF-κB1 in ovarian biology, and suggest that inflammation may influence the maintenance of the ovarian reserve and female reproductive longevity.

Although the precise mechanism of primordial follicle loss was not directly determined, two possibilities exist: accelerated activation of primordial follicles, or apoptosis of oocytes within these follicles followed by atresia. The first scenario is less likely, as increased activation would be expected to result in more primary follicles, which was not observed, in fact we saw a significant decrease in primary follicles. However, we acknowledge that follicle activation dynamics cannot be fully excluded based on static follicle counts alone, as primary follicle numbers reflect a balance between activation, growth progression, and survival. The second scenario (apoptosis of primordial follicle oocytes) may be more plausible, particularly given the elevated intra-ovarian TNF-α expression and previous demonstration that TNF-α can directly induce apoptosis in this follicle class [42]. The absence of detectable CC3 or TUNEL-positive primordial follicles in this study does not rule out apoptosis, as the peak window of cell death may have been missed, or low-level, cumulative follicle loss over time may be difficult to detect at single time points. Indeed, other studies have reported significant depletion of primordial follicles in the absence of detectable apoptosis within this population [41].

Our data indicate that loss of NF-κB1 primarily impacts the maintenance of the primordial follicle pool. The absence of clear effects in Nfkb1−/− mice on later-stage follicle quality possibly reflects two factors. First, follicles that successfully activate and progress may represent a selected population that has survived earlier depletion, thereby appearing phenotypically normal at later stages. Second, the key impact of NF-κB1 loss may be on follicle survival and activation dynamics, rather than on the morphology or viability of growing follicles once established.

Interestingly, we observed reduced serum AMH levels in Nfkb1−/− mice compared to controls at 6 months of age. AMH is produced predominantly by granulosa cells of growing follicles, particularly secondary and small antral follicles, but is also expressed by primary follicles in mice. In our study, we did not observe significant changes in secondary or antral follicle numbers, but we did detect a reduction in primary follicles. Although primary follicles produce less AMH per follicle than later-stage follicles, they are substantially more abundant. Therefore, depletion of the primary follicle pool in Nfkb1⁻/⁻ mice may contribute to the observed reduction in serum AMH. In addition, subtle alterations in granulosa cell function or follicle health, not detectable by morphological assessment, may also contribute to reduced AMH production from growing follicles.

In addition to TNF-α, we found that Il6 mRNA levels were elevated in ovaries from Nfkb1−/− mice compared to controls. This finding is important given IL-6 can drive an acute inflammatory state into a chronic inflammatory, pro-fibrotic condition [10]. Notably, other studies have reported the contribution of NF-κB to liver fibrosis [21, 27] and a recent paper showed elevated inflammatory markers in the tendons of Nfkb1−/− mice together with increased collagen deposition and expression of matrix-related genes (Col1a1, Col3a1), indicative of fibrosis [3]. Fibrosis has also been proposed to contribute to the normal ovarian aging process [1, 5, 37]. Interestingly however, while a trend towards an increase in fibrosis levels was observed in 12-month-old Nfkb1−/− ovaries compared to WT ovaries using Picrosirius Red staining, it did not reach statistical significance and little Picrosirius Red staining was observed in either group at 6 months, when follicle loss was already evident. Furthermore, intra-ovarian expression levels of type I, III and IV collagens did not show any significant change between Nfkb1−/− mice and WT mice. While we cannot exclude subtle or localised fibrotic changes, our findings suggest that premature depletion of the ovarian reserve in this model may not be associated with overt fibrosis..

IL-6 is also considered a hallmark cytokine of several age-related pathologies [23]. Recent studies show that reproductive ageing in mice is accompanied by increased ovarian inflammation, suggesting that inflammatory signalling may be a causal driver of the normal ovarian ageing process [5, 18]. Compared with young ovaries, reproductively old ovaries display hallmarks of chronic inflammation, including elevated expression of Tnfa, Il1b, Ccl2, Ccl5 and Il6, increased IL-6 protein levels, and the appearance of multinucleated macrophage giant cells [5, 18]. Consistently, aged C57BL/6 mice also show increased circulating IL-6 and IL-18, together with higher proportions of several ovarian immune cell populations [18]. These findings reinforce the concept that ovarian ageing is closely linked to chronic, low-grade inflammation, and highlight IL-6 as a possible mediator, potentially connecting NF-κB1 signalling, immune activation, and the progressive depletion of the ovarian reserve.

Interestingly, we also observed accelerated depletion of primordial follicles in Nfkb1 heterozygotes (Nfkb1±), indicating that loss of a single allele can impact the ovarian reserve. This finding is consistent with prior reports that Nfkb1 haploinsufficiency produces physiological phenotypes [2, 3]. For example, Bernal et al. reported reduced survival in Nfkb1± mice compared with wild-type controls, with a further reduction in Nfkb1−/− animals [2]. Best et al. [3] found reduced expression of the NF-κB subunits p50 and its p105 precursor in Nfkb1± mice relative to wild type, whereas both proteins are absent in knockouts. Changes in the availability of p50/p105 are likely to alter the composition of NF-κB dimers: reduced levels of inhibitory p50-containing homodimers and heterodimers may shift signalling toward RelA-containing, transactivating dimers, and Best et al. reported increased total and phospho-RelA in heterozygotes, a change associated with enhanced canonical NF-κB activity. Notably, at 6 months of age, Nfkb1± mice displayed a reduction in primordial follicle numbers and a decrease in corpora lutea, whereas in Nfkb1−/− mice corpora lutea numbers were not significantly altered at the same age, though trending down. Whilst we do not know why this is, it may reflect a non-linear gene dosage effect of NF-κB1 on ovarian function, where partial loss of NF-κB1 differentially affects ovulatory output. Alternatively, this difference may relate to biological variability in ovulation (including estrous cycle stage at collection), or reduced statistical sensitivity for detecting changes in ovulatory markers.Furthermore, although ovarian cytokines were not measured in heterozygotes, it is possible that Nfkb1 haploinsufficiency elevates pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, although potentially to a lesser extent than full knockouts. Future studies directly assessing cytokines in Nfkb1± ovaries will clarify whether their phenotype reflects a graded inflammatory response to reduced NF-κB1 dosage or distinct tissue-specific mechanisms.

A notable avenue for future research involves examining the implications of loss of function NF-κB1 heterozygous mutations in women, particularly concerning fertility. While current literature predominantly focuses on the immune dysregulation associated with NF-κB1 mutations [19], such as those leading to common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), the potential impact on ovarian function remains underexplored. Large‑scale human genetic studies of age at natural menopause have identified multiple loci linked to reproductive ageing and highlighted NF‑κB signalling as an enriched pathway [35], although NFKB1 itself has not been identified as an individual genetic determinant of menopause timing in women, underscoring a gap in our understanding of how NF‑κB1 contributes to ovarian reserve dynamics and reproductive ageing. Given the likely role of NF-κB signalling in ovarian physiology, including folliculogenesis and regulation of inflammatory responses, it is important to investigate the reproductive consequences of NF-κB1 haploinsufficiency in women. Such studies could provide valuable insights into fertility genetics and help inform clinical care.

Whilst this model is relevant to human conditions characterised by systemic low-grade inflammation, use of global Nfkb1 deletionprecludes discrimination between systemic and ovary intrinsic effects, and prevents identification of the specific cell types responsible for the observed ovarian and inflammatory phenotypes. Nfkb1−/− mice exhibited elevated systemic cytokine levels consistent with chronic low-grade inflammation reported in this model [12, 26, 27], and intra-ovarian inflammation was significantly higher, as indicated by increased expression of NF-κB dependent genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il1a, Tnfa, Il6). Within the ovary, cytokines may be expressed and secreted by multiple cell types, including macrophages, which are the most abundant immune cells in the ovary and play key roles in normal ovarian biology [36, 44]. Other ovarian somatic cells, such as granulosa cells, theca cells, and stromal fibroblasts, can also produce pro-inflammatory cytokines under certain conditions, contributing to local immune signalling and follicle regulation [9, 32, 40]. Future studies using cell type-specific knockouts will allow dissection of the precise cellular contributions and further clarify NF-κB1’s role in ovarian biology.

Together, these findings indicate that NFκB1-dependent regulation of ovarian function is highly context dependent, with distinct consequences arising from selective disruption of p50 processing versus complete loss of NFκB1. While our data do not establish a direct causal link between inflammation and follicle loss, they identify chronic low-grade inflammation and premature ageing as coincident features of accelerated ovarian reserve depletion in Nfkb1⁻/⁻ mice. This work therefore extends prior studies by establishing the ovary, and specifically the primordial follicle pool, as a previously underexplored target of pathology in the setting of chronic inflammatory ageing. While beyond the scope of these focused analyses, future studies integrating single cell large‑scale transcriptomic approaches to define additional NF‑κB1 target genes and downstream pathways within different cellular subsets in the ovary, could provide further insight into the other NF‑κB1‑regulated networks contributing to ovarian ageing and reserve dynamics. Similarly, although we show clear evidence of increased intra-ovarian inflammatory signalling, the specific immune cell populations contributing to this response, and their spatial distribution within the ovary, remain important areas for future investigation.

Interestingly, there was an absence of significant differences in primordial follicle number between genotypes at 12 months, which likely reflects convergence of both WT and Nfkb1-deficient mice towards a severely depleted ovarian reserve at reproductive senescence, rather than resolution of the phenotype. This highlights that the impact of NF-κB1 loss is most evident during the mid-reproductive window, when differences in follicle dynamics are still detectable. The lack of intermediate timepoints between 6 and 12 months limits finer resolution of the temporal progression of follicle depletion, which may help further define the onset and duration of the accelerated ageing phenotype.

In conclusion, our findings identify NF-κB1 as a key regulator of ovarian immune homeostasis and follicle preservation during reproductive aging. Loss of NF-κB1 led to heightened systemic and intra-ovarian inflammation, including elevated TNF-α and IL-6, and was associated with accelerated depletion of the ovarian reserve. These data suggest that chronic low-grade inflammation, driven in part by dysregulated NF-κB signalling, may be an important contributor to age-related ovarian decline. However, given the many roles of the NF-κB signalling pathway in cellular function and stress responses, we cannot rule out the possibility that follicle depletion in Nfkb1−/− mice involves mechanisms beyond inflammation. While further work is needed to pinpoint the specific cellular drivers and causal pathways, our study highlights NF-κB1 as a potential molecular safeguard of the ovarian reserve and a target for interventions aimed at extending reproductive lifespan.
