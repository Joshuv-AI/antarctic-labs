 Validation of a Custom Ball-on-Ring Apparatus and
 Consideration of Common Issues for Use in Further Testing

Abstract

 Ceramic materials are well known for their high hardness and strength but are
limited in their application due to low toughness and sudden failure. As a potential
solution, inspiration can be taken from dental enamel nanostructure, where undulating
rods cause cracks to branch or deflect, increasing the energy needed to cause total
fracture of a ceramic part. To test this bio-inspired material, a proper testing apparatus
needed to be created and tested to verify its accuracy. For this project, a bespoke ball on
ring testing apparatus was created and tested using both conventionally sintered alumina
disks and purchased alumina disks to validate its accuracy. By comparing the Weibull
distribution of rupture strengths measured by the tests to literature values, it was shown
that the testing frame had a wide distribution of strength which did not align with literature
values on the lower end. Through fractography, it was found that some samples fractured
from the contact stress induced by the ball indenter, which could not be used to calculate
rupture strength. This fracture was often linked to low stress to failure, which was initiated
by a flaw on the surface near the indenter which acted as a stress concentrator. Removal of
these samples from the data set increased the accuracy of the reported rupture strength
values for the ceramic. Considering the equations for the magnitude of contact and flexural
strength, along with observations of initiating flaws, several measures can be taken for
testing the bio-inspired composite. These measures include proper polishing of both sides
of the sample, reducing sample thickness, and potentially using a softer indenter material.

1 Introduction

 Modern ceramics encounter a significant challenge ‒ while their high hardness and
elastic modulus exceed those of most materials, their low fracture toughness can result in
sudden and catastrophic failure with minimal warning [1, 2]. The inherent characteristics of
ceramics, including strong atomic bonds and high Peierls-Nabarro stress [3], severely limit
dislocation movement, contributing to their low fracture toughness. To address this issue,
current research focuses on designing innovative microstructures and composites inspired
by natural materials. Highly mineralized biological materials have garnered considerable

attention due to their superior properties compared to those of traditional ceramics.
Advances in novel manufacturing techniques, such as 3D printing, have drawn inspiration
from these surprisingly tough natural microstructures [4]. To assess if these structures are
effective, however, a well-known stress state is required to calculate and understand the
rupture strength of any bio-inspired or mimetic material.

 Using a dental enamel inspired structure, a novel ceramic composite has been
created using additively manufactured Yttria Stabilized Zirconia (YSZ) rods in an alumina
matrix. However, measurements of rupture strength in additively manufactured ceramic
components are both expensive and difficult, requiring specialized machines for common
tests such as a three-point bend test [5]. These bend tests also often induce stresses on
the edge of the sample, where defects are more common, and more likely to initiate failure,
skewing measured rupture strength. To address this issue, a custom Ball-on-Ring (BoR)
testing apparatus was made to induce a biaxial flexural stress state on the samples while
excluding the edges. BoR testing has been shown to be accurate when compared to well-
known and used rupture tests, such as three- or four-point bend tests [5,6]. However,
because the apparatus used in this work was custom-made, it must be tested for accuracy
before being used to assess the novel ceramic composite. This paper focuses on validating
the testing apparatus and examines potential failure mechanisms within the bio-inspired
ceramic composites that could invalidate the tests.

2 Experimental Procedure

2.1 | Mechanical Testing

 A custom designed BoR apparatus was fabricated using machined and heat treated
17-4 tool steel. A 2 mm tungsten carbide ball was used as the indenter. The BoR apparatus
is used with an Instron load frame. The BoR testing apparatus was used as it induces a
known biaxial stress state on the sample face opposite the indenter (mimicking a flexural
biaxial state a structural ceramic may encounter in use), excludes the edges of the ceramic
(which may contain defects which act as severe stress concentrators), and allows for a
calculatable rupture strength (assuming fracture originates from the biaxial stress region).

2.2 | 3D Printing and Sample Preparation

 To validate the accuracy of the custom frame, the rupture strength of sintered
alumina disks was measured and compared to literature reported values. For the sintered
alumina samples, 800 nm alumina powder was loaded into a 20 mm graphite die lined with
graphite foil. Graphite dies are lined with graphite foil in electric field assisted sintering
(EFAS) systems to ensure uniform heat distribution, reduce friction and sticking, protect
against chemical reactions and contamination, maintain electrical conductivity, improve
surface finish, and facilitate easy removal of the sintered sample. The samples were
sintered via EFAS following the heating profile in Table 1. The Micro-Spark Plasma Sintering
System, or DCS-5, is a 5-ton bench-scale machine custom designed for smaller millimeter
to centimeter sized specimens (Figure 1).

 The resulting samples were about 20 mm in diameter, and 7 mm thick. The samples
were then cut in half using a slow speed saw to around 3.5 mm thick. One side of the
samples was ground to 600 grit to try and remove surface defects. The side ground to 600
grit was placed in the testing frame opposite of the indenter as the biaxial flexural surface.

 For the creation of the ceramic composite, a Hyrel 30M (Hyrel 3D, Atlanta, Georgia
USA) direct ink write (DIW) 3D printer was used. Each print was made with commercially
available photocurable YSZ resin (Admatec, Alkmaar, Netherlands). Each rod was printed
separately and cured using a low-power 405 nm ultraviolet light pen, curing each layer as it
was printed. All the rods were aligned by hand on a circular base made of the same YSZ
material, as shown in the X-ray Computed Tomography (XCT) scan in Figure 2 .

Figure 2: XCT scan of the YSZ rods after sintering (top and bottom left), and inside of the
Alumina matrix (bottom right) .

 Following the work of [7], the prints were debound using a microwave oven at 600 °C
to reduce debinding time, then conventionally sintered using a CM 1716-BL furnace (CM
Furnaces Inc., Bloomfield, NJ). The microwave source is a high-power, large format
magnetron operating at 2.45 GHz with a maximum power of 2100 watts (Model BP-210,
Microwave Research Associates, Carol Stream, IL). The rods were then placed in a 20 mm
graphite die lined with graphite foil, and 800 nm alumina powder was added to surround
the sintered rods, aided by a sonic chamber to ensure there were no large gaps between

the rods and the powder. Each sample was then sintered using the same sintering profile in
Table 1.

3.3 | Fractography

 The equations used to calculate the rupture strength of the ceramic are entirely
reliant on the sample fracturing at or near the side of the sample opposite of the indenter.
Failure outside these areas cannot be considered due to a differing stress state. Due to
this, assessment of the fracture surfaces is necessary to determine the origin is in the
biaxial stress region and potentially determine the cause of failure. Following ASTM C1322
[8], all broken ceramics were analyzed with the instigating flaw determined when possible.
A Keyence digital microscope was used for all fractography. Care was taken to identify the
direction of the hackle, aiming to identify where fracture originated. As the samples are
polycrystalline, identification of a mirror zone was often not feasible, and initiating flaw is
not always identifiable using the optical microscope. Further work can be done using
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to try and identify initiating flaws and mirror zone size
in sample fracture.

4.1 Hertzian Stress and Fracture

 When conducting fractography, it was found that the samples with the lowest
calculated rupture strength exhibited hackle originating from the side the indenter pressed

on, not the expected origin of the opposing side. Further fractography showed the samples
fractured with a conical shape, although the size of the cone did seem to be related to the
load to fracture. This conical fracture is shown in Figure 3

 From the acquired data, excluding the samples which fractured due to Hertzian
stress, the Weibull modulus was found to be 10.53 and the characteristic life was found to
be 372.3 MPa MPa. A Weibull plot of this data is shown in Figure 5.

 In comparison to literature values for alumina, the range of rupture strength
obtained from our BoR apparatus had a range of around 300 MPa to 430 MPa, with a

333-375.6 MPa are reported for the characteristic life depending on sintering method and
additive build orientation. Modulus varies widely, ranging from 7.5 to 20.4 [10]. From the
literature values, the tested sintered alumina is most like the “LCM 94% alumina after wet
hydrogen fire”, which had a characteristic life of 375.6 MPa, and a Weibull modulus of 14.2
[10].

4.3 | Discussion

 The data confirms the accuracy of our BoR apparatus, with reported values aligning
well with those found in the literature for sintered alumina. These values are particularly
like those of wet hydrogen fire sintered samples, likely due to the comparable
environments in the two sintering methods. However, a primary concern from our testing
arises when samples do not fracture under biaxial stress as anticipated due to surface
flaws. This issue could potentially invalidate the testing of the ceramic composite.

 As previously mentioned, samples that fracture due to contact stress cannot be
used for calculations because they break under a different stress state. Consequently, it is
essential to investigate why some samples fractured from indenter contact. The samples
that failed due to Hertzian contact all exhibited low calculated biaxial stresses, indicating
the presence of a significant stress concentrator near the Hertzian contact that initiated
cracking. Although not always visible, the two samples with the lowest biaxial stress
revealed large flaws under the optical microscope, supporting this theory. These two
samples are shown in Figure 7.

Figure 7: Hertzian fracture in two samples caused by a large inclusion (left) and several
notches or surface voids at the top (right).

 The samples containing the YSZ rods present an issue with this, where the tops and
bottoms of the samples are more likely to act as stress concentrators due to narrowing of
the rods, or alumina not fully filling in to the bottom of the samples. Removal of these areas
could limit stress concentrators that might induce a premature fracture.

 In consideration of the composite to be tested, it is likely that near the top and
bottom of the rods in the composite there will be stress concentrators such as pores or
lack of fusion between the rods and alumina that can initiate this Hertzian failure. From the
how the alumina samples have fractured, and the equations related to the maximum
stresses on the sample, suggestions to reduce the chance of Hertzian fracture are as
follows:

 1. Cut the composites to remove the tips and bottoms of the rods. These areas are
 likely to have more defects due to how the rods curve around these parts. This will
 remove some of the stress concentrators.
 2. Polish both sides of the sample to decrease surface defects near the indenter.
 3. Reduce the thickness of the samples. The biaxial stress is directly related to the
 inverse of the thickness squared. This means reducing thickness of the sample will
 drastically increase the biaxial stress, while not increasing the Hertzian stress.
 4. If Hertzian contact continues to be an issue, using a softer indenter (e.g., hardened
 steel) will decrease the Hertzian stress on the sample.

 The most feasible of these solutions is the first two, which will be done before any
composite samples are tested. Removing a few mm of thickness at the top and bottom of
the 3D printed rods will reduce the thickness of the sample. If issues persist, the ball
indenter may need to be changed to further reduce Hertzian contact stress. Further work is
required to understand if the composite successfully strengthens the alumina, but through
consideration of the fractured alumina and verification of the BoR apparatus, possible
issues have been identified, with solutions to be tried before any testing is done on the
composite to reduce the chance of an unusable test.

Summary

The validation of a custom-designed BoR testing apparatus for evaluating the biaxial
flexural strength of bio-inspired ceramic composites was performed. The apparatus was
tested using both conventionally sintered and commercially available alumina disks to
ensure its accuracy. By comparing the Weibull distribution of rupture strengths obtained
from the BoR tests to literature values, the study demonstrated that the testing frame
produced results consistent with previously reported data. However, samples fracturing
due to Hertzian contact stress were identified as a significant concern, as these fractures
cannot be used to calculate rupture strength accurately. The paper discusses potential
improvements to mitigate Hertzian stress-induced fractures, such as polishing both sides
of the samples, reducing sample thickness, and using a softer indenter material. The
validated apparatus and the proposed adjustments aim to enhance the reliability of testing
bio-inspired ceramic composites, providing a robust method for assessing their
mechanical properties.
