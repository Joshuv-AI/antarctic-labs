Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fibrosis 5 Score and the Risk of Liver-Related Events in the General Population
Authors: Byeong Geun Song, Goeun Park, Myung Ji Goh, Wonseok Kang, Geum-Youn Gwak, Yong-Han Paik, Moon Seok Choi, Joon Hyeok Lee, Dong Hyun Sinn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsThe metabolic dysfunction-associated fibrosis 5 (MAF-5) tool has been proposed for identifying individuals at high risk of liver fibrosis, but its ability to predict liver-related events (LREs) remains unknown. We aimed to evaluate the ability of MAF-5 to predict LREs and assess whether modifications could enhance its predictive capacity.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 62,625 adults without cancer, organ transplantation, chronic viral hepatitis, or heavy alcohol intake was followed for LREs (hepatocellular carcinoma and/or liver cirrhosis complications). The MAF-5 score was calculated and compared with other noninvasive liver fibrosis biomarkers. We also assessed whether modifying the MAF-5 score could improve LRE prediction.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 11.2 years, 147 patients developed LREs. The MAF-5 scores were used to stratify participants by LRE risk into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories, with incidence rates of 0.108, 0.576, and 1.520 cases per 1,000 person-years, respectively. Age was identified as an independent risk factor for LREs, and therefore, we developed an age-modified MAF-5 (aMAF-5) score, which showed improved performance (C-index 0.870 vs 0.818; integrated area under the curve 0.858 vs 0.784). Within the same MAF-5 category, LRE risk varied according to the aMAF-5 score. The use of “either positive” criteria improved sensitivity (from 0.412 to 0.765) and decreased specificity (from 0.944 to 0.830), while “both positive” criteria improved specificity and reduced sensitivity. Both scores performed well regardless of age, sex, or metabolic syndrome status.ConclusionsThe MAF-5 score allows effective stratification of LRE risk. The aMAF-5 score further improves risk stratification. These scores identify individuals at risk of LREs who may benefit from enhanced surveillance.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Liver fibrosis, Liver-related events, Risk assessment, Predictive learning models

INTRODUCTION

Liver disease is a major global health burden, accounting for 4% of deaths worldwide.1 Deaths are largely attributable to complications of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma,1 which are collectively described as liver-related events (LREs). Early and timely identification of individuals at high risk of LREs is of great importance, as this may allow for active intervention and management to decrease the risk of LREs. Notably, LREs develop mostly in individuals with chronic liver diseases, such as chronic viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver diseases, and steatotic liver diseases.2-4 Screening recommendations exist for viral hepatitis and alcohol use disorders.5-7 However, whether steatotic liver disease warrants screening is controversial, as steatotic liver disease is highly prevalent, but the absolute risk of LREs is very low.8 To date, there is an unmet need for a method to identify individuals at high risk of LREs among those without chronic viral hepatitis or heavy alcohol use.

Recently, the metabolic dysfunction-associated fibrosis 5 (MAF-5) score was proposed as a tool to identify individuals at high risk of liver fibrosis using five simple variables: waist circumference, body mass index (BMI), aspartate aminotransferase level, platelet counts, and diabetes.9 However, although the MAF-5 score showed good performance in identifying individuals with liver fibrosis, which is a strong risk factor for LREs,10 its ability to identify and/or stratify individuals at risk of LREs has not been evaluated. Hence, in this study, we analyzed whether the recently proposed MAF-5 score could be used to identify individuals at risk of LREs. We also compared its performance to that of other noninvasive biomarkers of liver fibrosis and evaluated whether modification of the MAF-5 score could further enhance its performance in identifying individuals at risk of LREs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Samsung Medical Center in Seoul, South Korea, using data from 62,625 adults aged 20 years or older who underwent health screening examinations between January 2001 and December 2016. From the initial 112,493 participants, we excluded those with chronic viral hepatitis B (n=4,932), chronic viral hepatitis C (n=944), heavy alcohol intake (420 g/week for males, 350 g/week for females; n=898), current cancer or a history of cancer (n=2,690), or history of organ transplantation (n=23). Exclusion criteria were not mutually exclusive, and participants could meet multiple criteria simultaneously. Additional exclusion criteria included missing information on any of the five variables used to calculate the MAF-5 score (n=26,819), those with LREs within 6 months of enrollment (n=8), and those with follow-up periods shorter than 6 months (n=12,990) (Fig. 1). The final cohort of 62,625 participants was followed up until the first occurrence of an LRE or the last hospital visit, whichever occurred first. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (IRB approval number: 2025-01-022). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

2. Variables

The MAF-5 score was calculated for each individual and used to classify them into the low (<0), intermediate (0 to 1), or high (≥1) risk groups.9 Noninvasive biomarkers of liver fibrosis were calculated, including the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index,11 aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI),12 and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease fibrosis score (NFS), as previously described.13 In addition, we tested whether modifying the MAF-5 score by incorporating a clinically relevant variable would enhance the performance in predicting LREs. Age is a significant factor for liver disease; we tested its statistical significance in our cohort and found that adding age to the MAF-5 score substantially improved its predictive accuracy. This modified score was termed the age-modified MAF-5 (aMAF-5) score. The aMAF-5 score was defined as the linear predictor from the Cox regression model that included the MAF-5 score and age and was calculated as 0.648×MAF-5+0.087×age. The aMAF-5 scores ranged from –5.084 to 6.807. After analyzing the distribution of LRE cases across various thresholds, the 85th percentile (1.419) was selected as the optimal cutoff value to balance sensitivity and specificity while maintaining clinically meaningful risk stratification. This resulted in 53,231 participants being classified as low risk and 9,394 as high risk.

Metabolic dysfunction was defined according to the new nomenclature for steatotic liver disease as the presence of a BMI ≥23 kg/m2, increased waist circumference (>90 cm for males and >85 cm for females), presence of diabetes (fasting glucose level ≥100 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c level of 5.7% [39 mmol/mol], type 2 diabetes, or treatment for type 2 diabetes), hypertension (blood pressure of 130/85 mm Hg or specific antihypertensive drug treatment), or dyslipidemia (plasma triglyceride level ≥1.7 mmol/L [150 mg/dL], plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level <1.0 mmol/L [40 mg/dL] for males or <1.3 mmol/L [50 mg/dL] for females, or lipid-lowering treatment,14,15 with modifications for the BMI and waist circumference cutoffs to define overweight and abdominal obesity according to regional guidelines).16

Chronic viral hepatitis B and C were defined based on serological testing performed during health screening examinations, with positive hepatitis B surface antigen and positive anti-hepatitis C virus antibody, respectively. Alcohol consumption was assessed using standardized self-administered questionnaires that collected information on both frequency (times/week) and amount of alcohol consumed on each occasion. Participants were categorized into three groups: none (no alcohol consumption), moderate (<140 g/week for females and <210 g/week for males), and significant (140–210 g/week for females and 210–420 g/week for males).

3. Outcome

The outcome variable was the presence of LREs during follow-up. In this study, LREs were specifically defined as the development of hepatocellular carcinoma or complications of liver cirrhosis, including ascites, variceal bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy, representing hard clinical outcomes. The diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma was made according to regional guidelines, with the diagnosis being based on imaging or histological findings.15 Follow-up data were collected from electronic medical records at Samsung Medical Center, including subsequent health screenings and clinical visits. The outcomes were confirmed through electronic medical record chart reviews by independent hepatologists blinded to the exposure scores.

4. Statistical analysis

The predictive performance of noninvasive biomarkers (MAF-5 score, FIB-4 index, APRI, NFS, and aMAF-5 score) was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and the integrated area under the curve (AUC). Harrell’s C-index was calculated to evaluate the discriminative ability of each biomarker in predicting time-to-event outcomes, with bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) used to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The integrated AUC was computed to assess the overall predictive accuracy across the entire follow-up period. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the dynamic predictive performance during the follow-up period, with the AUC values calculated at 5 and 10 years. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated at 5 and 10 years, with 95% CIs estimated using bootstrapping methods (1,000 iterations). To enhance the predictive performance of the MAF-5 score, we developed an aMAF-5 score. Age was incorporated into the original MAF-5 model, and the linear predictor from the Cox proportional hazards model was used as the aMAF-5 score. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Participants were classified into the high- and low-risk groups using the 85th percentile of the score as the cutoff value.

Subgroup analysis was performed to determine whether the performance of noninvasive biomarkers differed by subgroup, based on age (≤60 years vs >60 years), sex (male vs female), and metabolic dysfunction (yes vs no). The cumulative incidence rates of LREs were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared among the three groups (low, intermediate, and high MAF-5 scores) using the log-rank test. The cumulative incidence rates of LREs were compared using low and high aMAF-5 scores within the same MAF-5 score groups.

Differences in baseline characteristics between participants with and without metabolic dysfunction were compared using the Student t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Continuous variables were presented as the mean±standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The “survival” package was used for survival analyses, Cox proportional hazards regression, and Harrell’s C-index calculations; the “risksetROC” package was used for time-dependent ROC analyses.

RESULTS

1. Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study population (Table 1) showed that individuals with metabolic dysfunction were older, predominantly male, and had a higher BMI and waist circumference. The prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus and serum aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and triglyceride levels were higher and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were lower in individuals with metabolic dysfunction. The distribution of MAF-5 categories differed significantly between the groups, with a higher proportion of individuals with metabolic dysfunction in the intermediate-risk (9.7% vs 1.2%) and high-risk (8.2% vs 0.5%) categories.

2. Performance of noninvasive biomarkers for LREs

During a median follow-up of 11.2 years, 147 participants developed LREs (incidence rate: 0.22 cases per 1,000 person-years). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significant differences in the LRE risk across the three MAF-5 categories (log-rank p<0.001) (Fig. 2A). The incidence rates were 0.108, 0.576, and 1.520 cases per 1,000 person-years for the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories, respectively. The MAF-5 score showed a high C-index (0.818; 95% CI, 0.778 to 0.859) and integrated AUC (0.784; 95% CI, 0.779 to 0.790) (Table 2). Compared with the MAF-5 score, the FIB-4 index showed a higher C-index (0.835; 95% CI, 0.796 to 0.874), and the NFS showed a higher integrated AUC (0.795; 95% CI, 0.792 to 0.799). When the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were assessed, the MAF-5 score showed the highest sensitivity (0.412; 95% CI, 0.250 to 0.568) but the lowest specificity (0.930; 95% CI, 0.926 to 0.935) for LREs at 5 years compared with the FIB-4 index, APRI, and NFS, when a high cutoff for fibrosis was used (Table 3). When a lower cutoff for liver fibrosis was used, the MAF-5 score showed a lower sensitivity (0.618; 95% CI, 0.455 to 0.781) than the FIB-4 index (0.735; 95% CI, 0.571 to 0.884) but a higher specificity (0.852; 95% CI, 0.845 to 0.858) than the FIB-4 index (0.744; 95% CI, 0.737 to 0.752) for LREs at 5 years (Table 3). Similarly, the MAF-5 score showed the highest sensitivity but the lowest specificity when a high cutoff for fibrosis was used, and the FIB-4 index showed the highest sensitivity and lowest specificity for LREs at 10 years (Table 3).

3. Performance of noninvasive biomarkers for LREs by subgroups

When LREs were assessed by subgroup, LREs were observed in 74 participants aged ≤60 years, 73 participants aged >60 years, 101 male participants, 46 female participants, 130 participants with metabolic dysfunction, and 17 participants without metabolic dysfunction. The incidence rate, in cases per 1,000 person-years, was 0.13 for patients aged ≤60 years, 0.87 for those aged >60 years, 0.30 for males, 0.14 for females, 0.27 for participants with metabolic dysfunction, and 0.09 for participants without metabolic dysfunction. Subgroup analyses showed good performance of the MAF-5 score across different population subsets (Supplementary Table 1). The C-index of the MAF-5 score was lowest for participants aged ≤60 years (0.778; 95% CI, 0.707 to 0.849) and highest for participants without metabolic dysfunction (0.826; 95% CI, 0.694 to 0.958). The integrated AUC of the MAF-5 score was lowest for participants aged >60 years (0.803; 95% CI, 0.705 to 0.880) and highest for participants without metabolic dysfunction (0.921; 95% CI, 0.812 to 0.971).

4. LRE risk stratification using the aMAF-5 score or combining the MAF-5 and aMAF-5 scores

The cumulative incidence of LREs was higher for participants with high aMAF-5 scores than for those with low aMAF-5 scores (Fig. 2B). The incidence rate was 0.087 cases per 1,000 person-years for the low-risk category and 1.020 cases per 1,000 person-years for the high-risk category. Comparing the aMAF-5 score with other noninvasive biomarkers, the aMAF-5 score showed the highest C-index (0.870; 95% CI, 0.837 to 0.904) and integrated AUC (0.858; 95% CI, 0.854 to 0.861) among the tested biomarkers (Table 2). This superior performance was consistent at 5 (AUC, 0.860; 95% CI, 0.856 to 0.863) and 10 (AUC, 0.851; 95% CI, 0.847 to 0.855) years. Time-dependent ROC analysis further demonstrated that the aMAF-5 score maintained consistently higher AUCs than the other biomarkers throughout the follow-up period (Supplementary Fig. 1). The aMAF-5 score showed the highest performance regardless of the subgroup analyzed (Supplementary Table 1).

When participants were classified using the MAF-5 and aMAF-5 scores, the risk of LREs differed according to the aMAF-5 score within the same MAF-5 category (Fig. 3). Among participants with low MAF-5 scores (n=54,278), 50,313 (92.7%) had low aMAF-5 scores, and their risk of LREs was lower than that of those with high aMAF-5 scores (n=3,965, 7.3%) (0.074 vs 0.564 cases per 1,000 person-years for low and high aMAF-5 scores, respectively; p<0.001) (Fig. 3A). In the intermediate MAF-5 group (n=4,578), 50.8% had high aMAF-5 scores and 49.2% had low aMAF-5 scores; the LRE risk differed between the two groups (0.343 vs 0.808 cases per 1,000 person-years for low and high aMAF-5 scores, respectively; p=0.032) (Fig. 3B). Among participants with high MAF-5 scores (n=3,769), 17.8% had low aMAF-5 scores, and the LRE risk was lower for participants with low aMAF-5 scores than for participants with high aMAF-5 scores (0.309 vs 1.780 cases per 1,000 person-years for low and high aMAF-5 scores, respectively; p=0.005) (Fig. 3C).

When the aMAF-5 and MAF-5 scores were used together, using two criteria with either as positive (MAF-5 ≥1 or high aMAF-5) improved the sensitivity (0.412 to 0.765) with a trade-off in specificity (0.944 to 0.830), compared to using MAF-5 ≥1 alone. Using two criteria with either as positive (MAF-5 ≥0 or high aMAF-5) improved the sensitivity (0.618 to 0.853) with a trade-off in specificity (0.852 to 0.789), compared to using MAF-5 ≥0 alone. Using both criteria as positive (both MAF-5 and aMAF-5 positive), the specificity improved with a trade-off in sensitivity (Table 3). The cumulative incidence curves of LREs stratified by combined MAF-5 and aMAF-5 strategies (both negative, either positive, and both positive) showed risk stratification across the three categories, illustrating the complementary value of these two scores (Supplementary Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that the MAF-5 score could stratify the risk of developing LREs in participants without chronic viral hepatitis or heavy alcohol use. The performance of the MAF-5 score in predicting LREs was good, with a high C-index (0.818; 95% CI, 0.778 to 0.859) and a high integrated AUC (0.784; 95% CI, 0.779 to 0.790). This suggests that the MAF-5 score, which uses five simple variables, is a valuable tool for screening the general population to stratify the risk of LREs and can serve as a referral strategy. However, several noninvasive biomarkers for liver fibrosis, including the FIB-4 index, APRI, and NFS, also use simple variables. When the performance of MAF-5 was compared to that of the other noninvasive biomarkers, the MAF-5 did not outperform these biomarkers and showed a comparable C-index and integrated AUC (Table 2). Therefore, we performed an additional analysis to determine whether modifications to the MAF-5 score could further enhance its performance. We found that age is a strong risk factor for LREs and that adding age to the MAF-5 score, the aMAF-5 score, could further enhance the ability to predict LREs. The aMAF-5 score showed the best performance, as indicated by the highest C-index and integrated AUC values (Table 2).

Old age is a strong risk factor for developing hepatocellular carcinoma and complications of liver cirrhosis.17,18 The outcome of this study was the hard endpoint of liver disease (hepatocellular carcinoma and cirrhosis complications) rather than a surrogate endpoint (liver fibrosis), which may explain why the aMAF-5 score outperformed the MAF-5 score. In the original development and validation study,9 the MAF-5 score was highlighted for its age-independent scoring, which might be advantageous for use with young people.19 However, in this study, we found that the MAF-5 score performed similarly in participants aged ≤60 and >60 years (Table 3). In addition, the aMAF-5 score showed a better C-index and integrated AUC than the MAF-5 score in both age groups. This finding supports the use of age as a covariate to enhance the predictive performance of the MAF-5 score for LREs. When participants were further stratified according to the aMAF-5 score within the same MAF-5 category, the aMAF-5 score could further stratify the risk of LREs within the low, intermediate, and high MAF-5 categories. When the aMAF-5 and MAF-5 scores were used together, using either as a positive criterion, the aMAF-5 score improved the sensitivity with a trade-off in specificity, and when using both as positive criteria, the aMAF-5 score improved the specificity with a trade-off in sensitivity compared to using the MAF-5 score alone. The aMAF-5 score also outperformed other noninvasive biomarkers of fibrosis, including the FIB-4 index, APRI, and NFS. This indicates that the aMAF-5 score, another simple risk score, is useful for stratifying the individual risk of LREs and can be used as a referral tool.

The MAF-5 score was originally developed and validated in participants with metabolic dysfunction. Although LREs are more prevalent in participants with metabolic dysfunction,20,21 their incidence is not zero in those without metabolic dysfunction. In this study, we included participants with and without metabolic dysfunction and found an incidence rate of 0.27 cases per 1,000 person-years for participants with metabolic syndrome and 0.09 cases per 1,000 person-years for participants without metabolic syndrome. In the subgroup analysis stratified by the presence of metabolic syndrome, both MAF-5 and aMAF-5 scores performed well in participants with and without metabolic dysfunction (Supplementary Table 1). The MAF-5 score contains variables that reflect metabolic dysfunction (waist circumference, BMI, and diabetes). Participants without metabolic dysfunction had waist circumference and BMI values within the normal range. Nevertheless, the cutoffs for identifying metabolic abnormalities are arbitrary, and the association between cardiometabolic risk factors and cardiovascular disease risk is continuous, without clear thresholds.22 Hence, screening or referral tools that identify individuals at risk of LREs, regardless of the presence of metabolic syndrome, are needed. Our findings add to the current knowledge that the MAF-5 and aMAF-5 scores are tools that can be used, regardless of the presence of metabolic dysfunction.

This study had some limitations. The major purpose of developing the MAF-5 score was to identify individuals at high risk of liver fibrosis9 and not for the prediction of LREs. We lacked information on liver fibrosis, either by liver biopsy or elastography, when the MAF-5 score was calculated; hence, we could not assess the predictive performance for liver fibrosis. Additionally, our definition of LREs in this study (hepatocellular carcinoma and decompensation events) is narrower than the broader definition often used in the literature, which may include the development of liver fibrosis or cirrhosis itself. We used this narrow definition because fibrosis assessment data were not available in our cohort. As prevention of LREs is a hard clinical endpoint of screening asymptomatic individuals, the use of LREs as an endpoint has advantages. However, unlike liver fibrosis, which is a variable that is present during noninvasive score assessments, LREs develop during follow-up. Several factors may have affected the risk of LREs during follow-up. We calculated noninvasive biomarkers using variables at cohort entry. However, the MAF-5 score, aMAF-5 score, APRI, FIB-4 index, and NFS are not fixed variables, and can show dynamic changes during follow-up. Dynamic changes in noninvasive variables during follow-up are linked to different risks of outcome.23 Hence, the main purpose of using the MAF-5 score (for liver fibrosis) or aMAF-5 score (for prediction of LREs) should be considered when using these noninvasive variables. This cohort comprised all Asians, and further evaluation in other ethnic groups is required. Another limitation is that while we excluded chronic viral hepatitis and heavy alcohol use based on screening examination data, other less common causes of liver disease, such as autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cirrhosis, and cardiac hepatopathy, could not be systematically identified or excluded. Our health screening data did not capture diagnoses made at outside institutions, limiting our ability to exclude individuals with these conditions. These unidentified conditions may have contributed to some LRE cases, potentially introducing residual confounding. However, given the low prevalence of these conditions in the general population, their impact on our overall findings is likely to be limited.

In summary, we found that the MAF-5 score could identify and stratify the risk of LREs in participants without viral hepatitis or heavy alcohol use. The performance of the MAF-5 score in LRE prediction was comparable to that of other noninvasive biomarkers (e.g., FIB-4 index, APRI, and NFS). When age was added to the MAF-5 score (aMAF-5 score), the predictive performance for LREs improved and outperformed other noninvasive biomarkers. These findings suggest that the aMAF-5 score is a useful tool for the prediction of LREs in the general population.
