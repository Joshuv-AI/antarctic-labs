Characterization of OsNrx3i1, the first intron of rice OsNrx3 gene, as a candidate intron-mediated enhancement element for synthetic promoter engineering
Authors: Mengmeng Liu, Chenbo Gong, Jing Zhang, Yongjun Lin, Fei Zhou
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Intron-mediated enhancement (IME) is a potent endogenous mechanism for boosting gene expression, yet few IME elements function efficiently across distantly related plant species. Here, we characterize the first intron of the rice OsNrx3 gene (OsNrx3i1) as a candidate IME element. OsNrx3i1 significantly enhanced gene expression in rice protoplasts and stable transgenic plants, and also showed detectable activity in the dicot Nicotiana benthamiana in transient assays. The enhancement effect was position-dependent, requiring insertion within the 5′ transcribed region, and functionally distinct from typical enhancers. We further identified a repressive upstream open reading frame (uORF) in its native sequence; disruption of this uORF and embedding the intron into an optimized 5′ UTR scaffold, unlocked its full potential. Benchmarking against established IME elements (cat-1 intron, AtTub6i1, OsSodCc1i1) revealed that OsNrx3i1 performs competitively in monocots, but all tested introns exhibited context-dependent and host-specific efficacy. When engineered into minimal synthetic promoters, OsNrx3i1 increased their activity by up to 65-fold in rice protoplasts. This work presents OsNrx3i1 as a promising and engineerable genetic module, providing a foundation for its future exploration and application in plant synthetic biology.

Introduction

Introns, the non-coding sequences that are transcribed but not translated and are widely present in eukaryotic genomes, represent a rich yet underexplored repository of regulatory elements in plant synthetic biology. Despite the energetic cost associated with their transcription and splicing [1], introns are evolutionarily conserved, indicating that they perform crucial regulatory and functional roles within the cell [2,3]. Studies have shown that introns regulate gene expression through multiple mechanisms, including alternative splicing to generate diverse transcript isoforms, protection of genomic integrity [4] and enhancement of genomic stability [5]. Intron number also correlates with enhanced mRNA stability, as evidenced in species ranging from plants to mammals [[6], [7], [8]]. Introns can function as enhancer elements to stimulate gene expression [[9], [10], [11]], provide alternative promoters [[11], [12], [13], [14]], and regulate gene expression in a tissue-specific or developmentally specific manner [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

A phenomenon of particular interest in the field of genetic engineering is IME, in which the presence of an intron within the transcribed region can significantly enhance mRNA accumulation and translational efficiency—often by several orders of magnitude—compared to intronless counterparts [21,22]. Notably, this enhancement is often influenced by the intron's position and orientation within the transcript, a feature that distinguishes IME from classical enhancer elements. IME has been observed across a broad range of organisms, including mammals, plants, insects, and fungi (reviewed by Refs. [[23], [24], [25], [26]]).

IME represents a powerful endogenous strategy for elevating gene expression and has been successfully harnessed in transgenic constructs and synthetic promoters. Examples include modified maize ubi1 introns used to enhance transgene expression in rice and maize [27], the first intron of a soybean eEF1A gene incorporated into synthetic promoters [28], and the rice Act1 [29] and Tub6 [30] introns employed in bidirectional promoter systems [31]. These applications highlight the potential of IME elements as performance-enhancing modules for genetic circuits.

However, a major hurdle in the modular design of plant genetic systems is the limited taxonomic portability of most known IME elements. For instance, the first intron of the castor bean catalase gene (cat-1) functions in monocots but fails to enhance expression in dicots such as tobacco due to inefficient splicing [32]. Similarly, the first introns of the rice sodCc2 [33] and OsTub6 genes enhance expression but are ineffective in Arabidopsis [34]. This “host-range” restriction severely impedes the development of standardized, cross-platform biological parts. The scarcity of such versatile tools creates a significant gap in the plant synthetic biology toolkit, forcing engineers to re-optimize the expression systems for each new host and limiting the predictability and transferability of genetic constructs.

In this study, we identified and characterized the first intron of the rice (Oryza sativa) OsNrx3 gene, termed OsNrx3i1. Our results indicate that OsNrx3i1 could significantly boost expression in monocot systems—including rice protoplasts and stable transgenic plants. Intriguingly, enhancement activity was also observed in the dicot Nicotiana benthamiana via transient assays, suggesting its potential for broader applicability. Furthermore, we show that the enhancing effect of OsNrx3i1 is position-dependent and can be effectively engineered into minimal synthetic promoters to substantially increase their activity. This work presents a foundational characterization of OsNrx3i1 as a candidate IME element and explores its potential utility in synthetic promoter design.

Results

Natural variation in OsNrx3i1 correlates with differential gene expression

In our previous pan-genome analysis of nuclear organelle DNA (NORG) presence/absence variations (PAVs) across the genus Oryza [35], we identified a 10.5-kb nuclear mitochondrial DNA (NUMT) fragment, ONG0401190, located within the first intron of the OsNrx3 gene in a subset of indica rice varieties. Based on this structural variation, we classified OsNrx3 into three haplotypes: Type1 (1.7 kb), Type2 (1.9 kb), and Type3 (12 kb), with the Type3 haplotype containing the inserted ONG0401190 fragment. (Fig. 1A). Further analysis of 33 rice accessions from the pan-genome dataset [36] revealed 14 Type1, 5 Type2, and 14 Type3 varieties. RNA-seq analysis indicated that the expression levels of OsNrx3 in Type1 accessions were significantly higher than those in Type3 accessions (p < 0.05 in shoots; Student's t-test; Fig. 1B). Notably, examination of RNA-seq reads spanning the exon–intron boundaries confirmed that the large NUMT insertion in Type3 did not disrupt canonical splice sites or generate aberrant transcript isoforms (Fig. S1). We further validated this via RT-PCR using primers flanking the first intron, which consistently produced the correctly spliced product from Type3 transcripts (Fig. S2). Consistent with the transcriptome data, Zhenshan 97 (ZS97), which carries the Type3 haplotype, showed significantly lower OsNrx3 expression compared to the other accessions (Fig. 1A and C). These results support a correlation between structural variation in the first intron and OsNrx3 expression levels.

To investigate the regulatory potential of this region, we constructed a putative regulatory sequence (PRS) of OsNrx3 comprising a 96-bp segment from the start codon of the first exon, the entire first intron, and a 21-bp segment of the second exon (Fig. 2A). This fragment was cloned into a dual-luciferase reporter system to assess its regulatory activity. Both PRStype1 and PRStype2 significantly enhanced reporter gene expression, with no significant difference observed between them (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the PRStype3, which contains the large NUMT insertion, completely lacked this enhancer activity (Fig. 2B). OsNrx3i1 is required for strong enhancement activity

To assess the contribution of the intron itself and rule out potential effects from the additional 39-amino-acid peptide encoded by the original OsNrx3 PRStype1 sequence, we first mutated its start codon. However, this modification inadvertently introduced a 54-bp (17-amino acid) uORF that overlapped with the downstream luciferase (LUC) coding sequences (CDS), resulting in strong translational suppression of LUC. To isolate the specific effect of the intronic sequence, we subsequently disrupted this uORF, generating the optimized construct, OsNrx3 PRSmut1. To further evaluate the role of the intron relative to the flanking exon sequences, we generated two deletion variants: PRStype1-intron-del (lacking the intron) and PRSexon-del (retaining the intron sequence but lacking the flanking exons). Notably, compared to the promoter-only control, the PRStype1-intron-del construct still exhibited an approximately 2.1-fold increase in luciferase activity (Fig. 3A), indicating that the flanking exon sequences themselves possess basal expression-enhancing capacity. The intron-only construct (PRSexon-del) also conferred a ∼2.4-fold enhancement, demonstrating that OsNrx3i1 harbors intrinsic IME activity independent of exonic context. However, both the intron-deleted and exon-deleted configurations failed to recapitulate the robust enhancement observed with the full PRStype1 construct (∼32.7-fold, Fig. 3A). These results indicate that while OsNrx3i1 possesses inherent enhancement capacity, the presence of its native flanking exon sequences is critical for achieving maximal IME activity, underscoring the context-dependent nature of this regulatory module.

OsNrx3i1 enhances gene expression in monocots and shows a lower activity in a dicot system

We next evaluated the enhancement effect of PRSmut1 in vivo using Agrobacterium-mediated stable transformation in rice and transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves, with GUS as the reporter (Fig. 3B). In stable transgenic rice plants, the presence of the element increased CaMV 35S-driven GUS activity by approximately 3.2-fold (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the same construct yielded a more modest ∼1.5-fold increase in GUS activity in N. benthamiana leaves under transient expression conditions (Fig. 3E), indicating a weaker enhancement effect in this dicot system compared to stable rice transformants. This discrepancy suggests that the efficacy of OsNrx3i1 may be influenced by experimental platforms, developmental context, or species-specific factors.

In stable rice transformants, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the mRNA expression of the PRSmut1 reporter construct (pDX2181-35SPRS) was significantly higher (on average 8.1-fold, P < 0.01) than that of the promoter-only control (pDX2181-35S, Fig. 3D). The more pronounced increase in transcript abundance compared to the corresponding increase in GUS activity suggests that the element primarily acts to elevate mRNA levels, with its effect potentially being modulated at the post-transcriptional or translational level in stable transformants. In N. benthamiana, however, qRT-PCR detected only a modest increase in transcript levels that was statistically insignificant (Fig. 3F). To investigate whether splicing efficiency underlies this dicot-specific attenuation, we performed RT-PCR using primers flanking OsNrx3i1. In stable transgenic rice, the intron was efficiently and accurately spliced, yielding a single major band corresponding to the mature mRNA. Conversely, in N. benthamiana leaves, RT-PCR revealed a substantial proportion of mis-spliced products, indicating a marked reduction in splicing efficiency (Fig. S3). We propose that the weak enhancement in the dicot host likely stems from a combination of inefficient intron removal and the presence of latent translational repressive elements within the PRSmut1 sequence (e.g., the potential uORF discussed later). Together, these findings indicate that while OsNrx3i1 retains detectable activity in a dicot context, its full IME potential is constrained by species-specific splicing machinery and local sequence architecture.

OsNrx3i1 exhibits position-dependent enhancement, a hallmark of IME

To investigate how OsNrx3i1 enhances gene expression, we first tested whether it functions as a classical enhancer or promoter. When PRSmut1 or OsNrx3i1 was placed upstream of the CaMV 35S promoter, neither construct significantly increased luciferase expression compared to the promoter-only control (Fig. 4). This result indicates that neither the PRSmut1 nor OsNrx3i1 function as typical, position-independent enhancers under these conditions.Fig. 4Position Dependency of OsNrx3i1. Position Dependency of OsNrx3i1. Schematic of the expression constructs and results from dual-luciferase reporter assays in rice protoplasts testing the regulatory activity of the OsNrx3i1.

We then assessed whether the sequence itself possesses intrinsic promoter activity. When PRSmut1 was tested in the absence of any upstream promoter, it drove a low but detectable level of luciferase expression (∼10-fold above background). However, this activity was significantly lower than that driven by CaMV 35S promoter (Fig. S4). These data suggest that the basal promoter activity within the element is insufficient to account for the strong enhancement observed when it is positioned within the transcribed region.

To determine if the enhancement by OsNrx3i1 depends on its location within the transcript, we inserted OsNrx3i1 at the 5′ end, middle, or 3′ end of the LUC ORF (designated Insert 5′, Insert Mid, and Insert 3′, respectively). A significant increase in luciferase activity was observed only when the intron was positioned at the 5′ end (Insert 5′), with statistically significant differences compared to the mid and 3′ positions (p < 0.05, Fig. 4). This positional specificity, particularly the dependence on proximity to the transcription start site, is a hallmark of IME.

Together, the strict position-dependence, coupled with the lack of classical enhancer activity when placed upstream of the promoter, is a defining characteristic of IME, distinguishing it from other regulatory mechanisms.

A mini uORF in the native sequence may attenuate translational efficiency

The disparity between strong enhancement in protoplasts and moderate effects in stable transformations, coupled with the fact that the increase in mRNA levels exceeded the corresponding increase in reporter activity in transgenic rice (Fig. 3C, Fig. 5C and D), led us to hypothesize the involvement of post-transcriptional regulation. Sequence analysis of the PRSmut1 5′ UTR revealed a hexamer (ATGTGA), beginning at the 40th nucleotide, which could encode a potential 1-aa uORF (Fig. 5A). To test its functional impact, we generated PRSmut2, a truncated version lacking the first 45 nucleotides of PRSmut1 (including the ATGTGA hexamer). Using dual-luciferase assays in rice protoplasts, we found that PRSmut2 achieved a 1.6-fold higher reporter expression compared to the original PRSmut1 (Fig. 5B and C). However, to obtain definitive proof without the confounding effects of sequence deletion, we constructed PRSmut3, in which the ATGTGA hexamer was precisely mutated to ATCTGA via site-directed mutagenesis. Building upon these findings, we investigated whether eliminating translational initiation altogether could further unmask latent regulatory potential. We generated an intron-deleted derivative of this optimized sequence, designated PRSmut3-exon-only. Surprisingly, this construct showed a 4.9-fold enhancement over the promoter-only control, substantially exceeding the ∼2-fold activity observed with the original PRStype1-intron-del (Fig. 5B and C). This mechanistic divergence is informative: the native PRStype1 exon sequence encodes a 39-aa N-terminal peptide, which likely imposes a translational burden or interferes with ribosome scanning. In contrast, the precise mutations in PRSmut3 not only disrupt the uORF but also prevent translation of this 39-aa extension, effectively converting the region into a pure, non-translated 5′ UTR (Fig. 5A). The robust 4.9-fold enhancement demonstrates that once translational interference is removed, the exon-derived 5′ UTR harbors intrinsic expression-enhancing capacity. Together, these results reveal that the native sequence harbors dual translational repressive features, and their targeted disruption is essential to unlock the full potential of both exon and intron modules.

To assess whether this optimization strategy is transferable to dicots, we evaluated the same series of constructs in N. benthamiana using dual-luciferase assays. The results revealed a distinct regulatory pattern: while PRSmut1 showed only a marginal ∼1.3-fold enhancement over the promoter-only control, stepwise disruption of translational repressive elements progressively improved activity—PRSmut3 achieved ∼2.3-fold, and PRSmut2 reached ∼4.4-fold enhancement (Fig. 5B and D). This trend confirms that the 1-aa uORF also imposes translational constraints in tobacco, and its removal can partially rescue expression. Strikingly, however, deletion of the intron in the optimized background (PRSmut3-exon-only) abolished the enhancement effect, yielding activity indistinguishable from the promoter-only control (Fig. 5B and C). This contrasts sharply with the rice system, where the intron-less PRSmut3-exon-only construct retained strong activity. In tobacco, the presence of OsNrx3i1 is a prerequisite for any detectable expression boost. Together, these results reveal that while translational repression by uORFs is a conserved feature across monocots and dicots, the functional dependency on intronic context is species-specific, underscoring the need for host-tailored optimization when deploying OsNrx3i1-based modules.

Comparative Benchmarking of OsNrx3i1 against established IME elements

To systematically evaluate the performance of OsNrx3i1 relative to previously characterized IME elements, we conducted a head-to-head comparison in both rice protoplasts and N. benthamiana. We selected three well-documented introns: (i) the first intron of the castor bean catalase gene (cat-1 intron), which enhances expression in monocots but not dicots [33]; (ii) the first intron of Arabidopsis β-tubulin 6 (AtTub6i1), reported to function in both monocots and dicots [34]; and (iii) the first intron of rice cytosolic superoxide dismutase (OsSodCc1i1), which shows monocot-specific activity [33].

To ensure a fair comparison under a unified regulatory architecture, we replaced OsNrx3i1 in the optimized PRSmut3-exon backbone (uORF-disrupted, non-translated 5′ UTR context) with each of the three test introns, generating PRSmut3-exon-cat1, PRSmut3-exon-AtTub6i1, and PRSmut3-exon-OsSodCc1i1 constructs (Fig. 5B). Dual-luciferase assays in rice protoplasts revealed that all four introns conferred substantial enhancement over the promoter-only control (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, AtTub6i1 achieved the highest activity (∼77-fold), slightly exceeding OsNrx3i1 (∼68-fold), followed by OsSodCc1i1 (∼65-fold) and cat-1 intron (∼55-fold) (Fig. 5B and C). This indicates that, when embedded in the optimized OsNrx3-derived 5′ UTR scaffold, multiple IME elements can drive robust expression in monocots, with AtTub6i1 exhibiting marginally superior performance under these conditions.

To test whether the flanking exon context influences intron efficacy, we evaluated AtTub6i1 in its native exon background (AtTub6 PRS containing its endogenous 5′ and 3′ exon segments). Strikingly, AtTub6i1 conferred only ∼30.8-fold enhancement in its native context—less than half the activity observed in the PRSmut3-exon backbone (Fig. 5B and C). This suggests that the OsNrx3-derived 5′ UTR provides a more permissive or synergistic architectural environment for IME function, at least for AtTub6i1, highlighting the importance of exon–intron combinatorial optimization in synthetic promoter design.

We next assessed the same construct series in N. benthamiana leaves via transient agroinfiltration. In the PRSmut3-exon context, OsNrx3i1, cat-1 intron and AtTub6i1 conferred modest but significant enhancement (∼2.27, ∼2.93-fold and ∼2.34-fold, respectively, Fig. 5B and D), whereas OsSodCc1i1 unexpectedly suppressed reporter expression to ∼0.16-fold of the promoter-only control (Fig. 5B and D). Consistent with the rice data, AtTub6i1 exhibited reduced activity in its native exon context in tobacco (∼1.5-fold) compared to the PRSmut3-exon backbone (∼2.3-fold) (Fig. 5B and D).

Together, these results establish two key principles: (1) the PRSmut3-exon sequence serves as a high-performance, portable 5′ UTR scaffold that can amplify the activity of diverse IME introns, particularly in monocots; and (2) the magnitude of IME-driven enhancement is profoundly shaped by the combinatorial compatibility between intron and flanking exon/UTR sequences, with optimal configurations being host-specific. These findings provide a rational framework for the modular engineering of cross-species expression cassettes.

Structural determinant of OsNrx3i1 enhancement activity

To map the functional sequences of OsNrx3i1, we divided it into three segments—Part1, Part2, and Part3—based on sequence conservation and potential regulatory elements (Fig. 5A). A series of truncations within the OsNrx3 PRSmut2 backbone were generated, each preserving the core splicing signals (specifically, the 5 nucleotides upstream and 31 nucleotides downstream of the branch point). Transient expression analysis in rice protoplasts revealed that deletion of Part1 significantly reduced the enhancement activity compared to the full-length OsNrx3 PRSmut2 (Fig. 6). In contrast, constructs lacking either Part2 or Part3 retained enhancement levels comparable to that conferred by OsNrx3 PRSmut2 (Fig. 6). The finding that Parts 2 and 3 are dispensable for full activity suggests that the core IME signal resides predominantly in Part1. It is possible that Parts 2 and 3 contain redundant or context-dependent elements that are compensated by the strong enhancer in Part1 under our experimental conditions. Engineering of OsNrx3i1 into synthetic promoters broadens their expression potency

Having established the key features of OsNrx3 PRS and OsNrx3i1 in enhancing gene expression, we next explored the potential of OsNrx3i1 as a modular component by engineering it into synthetic promoter systems. The OsNrx3 PRSmut1 was fused to two computationally designed minimal synthetic promoters, minSyn105 and minSyn108 [37]. In the rice protoplast assay, the results demonstrated that the inclusion of OsNrx3 PRSmut1 enhanced the activity of these synthetic promoters by 50- to 65-fold (Fig. 7A and B). Notably, the activity of minSyn108 with OsNrx3 PRSmut1 was comparable to that of the CaMV 35S promoter, while minSyn105 with OsNrx3 PRSmut1 exhibited even 3.1-fold higher activity than that of the CaMV 35S promoter (Fig. 7A and B).Fig. 7The enhancement effect of OsNrx3i1 combined with Synthetic promoter minSyn105 and minSyn108. The enhancement effect of OsNrx3i1 combined with Synthetic promoter minSyn105 and minSyn108. Schematic of the expression constructs and results from dual-luciferase reporter assays in (A-B) rice protoplasts and (C-D) N. benthamiana leaves testing the regulatory activity of the OsNrx3i1.

We next compared the enhancement effects of OsNrx3i1 with a known enhancing 5′ UTR element, ADHE, from the rice alcohol dehydrogenase gene (OsADH) [38]. To evaluate the performance of the intron in a heterologous context, we generated a chimeric construct by inserting the OsNrx3i1 intron into the ADHE sequence. To ensure efficient splicing, the insertion was precisely made at a site conforming to the consensus splice-site motif (AG|A/G), creating an AG|GT … AG|A junction. When fused to the minimal promoters, ADHE alone provided a modest 1.7- to 2.6-fold increase in expression (Fig. 7A and B). Further insertion of OsNrx3i1 into the ADHE context boosted expression by 39- to 57-fold, (Fig. 7A and B), indicating that OsNrx3i1 retains significant enhancing activity within a heterologous 5′ UTR architecture, although the highest activity was still achieved with the OsNrx3 PRSmut1 configuration.

To further validate the combined effect of OsNrx3i1 and synthetic promoters in dicots, we performed Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation in N. benthamiana leaves. In contrast to the results observed in rice protoplasts, the fusion of minSyn105 and minSyn108 with either OsNrx3 PRSmut1 or ADHE did not lead to a significant increase in expression in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 7C and D). However, the incorporation of OsNrx3i1 into the ADHE element resulted in 2.0- and 4.2-fold enhancement in expression compared to minSyn105 and minSyn108, respectively (Fig. 7C and D). In N. benthamiana, the activity of the minSyn108 promoter fused with ADHE-OsNrx3i1 reached a level that was not statistically different from the strong constitutive CaMV 35S promoter (Fig. 7D). Although the fold-enhancement in dicots is considerably lower than that observed in rice, these findings demonstrate that the incorporation of OsNrx3i1 into a heterologous 5′ UTR context can expand the tunable activity range of synthetic promoters, providing a strategy to achieve high-level expression in dicots comparable to the 35S promoter.

Collectively, these results show that OsNrx3i1 can be integrated with synthetic promoters and other regulatory elements to create functional expression cassettes. The enhancement observed in monocot cells, coupled with the effect achievable in a dicot through specific configurations, highlights its potential as a versatile component for plant expression engineering, which also underscoring context-dependent differences in its performance.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a naturally occurring NUMT insertion in the first intron of OsNrx3 that correlates with reduced gene expression. Subsequent functional characterization demonstrated that this intronic region, OsNrx3i1, possesses potent enhancement activity. Unlike conventional regulatory elements, OsNrx3i1 showed neither typical enhancer nor promoter activity. Rather, we observed that its regulatory function depends critically on its position within the transcribed region, with maximal enhancement occurring when positioned near the 5′ end of the coding sequence (Fig. 4). These findings align with established models of IME function [25,26], while expanding the repertoire of known IME elements in monocot species, providing a valuable new tool for plant genetic engineering.

The notable aspect of OsNrx3i1 is its detectable activity in both monocot (rice) and dicot (N. benthamiana) systems (Fig. 3, Fig. 5B–D). To systematically evaluate its performance, we benchmarked OsNrx3i1 against three well-documented IME introns (cat-1 intron [32], AtTub6i1 [34], and OsSodCc1i1 [33]) within a unified regulatory architecture. In rice protoplasts, all four introns conferred substantial enhancement (Fig. 5B and C). However, in N. benthamiana, cat-1 intron and AtTub6i1 yielded only modest enhancement (∼2.3–2.9-fold), while OsSodCc1i1 unexpectedly suppressed expression (∼0.16-fold) (Fig. 5B and D). These results indicate that IME functionality is not an intrinsic, universally portable property, but rather emerges from host-specific splicing machinery, trans-acting factor availability, and sequence context. The moderate enhancement observed in dicots compared to the robust effect in monocots points to species-specific optimizations that could be explored in the future, for instance, by adjusting the flanking sequences or codon context to maximize its activity in different hosts.

A clear discrepancy between mRNA accumulation and GUS activity in transgenic rice using the unmodified PRSmut1 led us to identify a 1-aa uORF that limited the efficiency of ribosome scanning. This was further supported by the improved activity of PRSmut2 and PRSmut3, the truncated forms lacking the uORF, in rice protoplasts (Fig. 5A–C). This result is consistent with the known translational suppressive roles of uORFs [39]. Precise disruption of the uORF start codon and elimination of the 39-aa N-terminal peptide converted the flanking region into a pure, non-translated 5′ UTR scaffold (Fig. 5A). Remarkably, this optimized scaffold (PRSmut3-exon) not only elevated OsNrx3i1-driven expression to ∼68-fold in rice but also amplified the activity of heterologous IME introns (e.g., AtTub6i1 reached ∼77-fold, substantially exceeding its ∼30.8-fold activity in its native exon context) (Fig. 5B and C). Conversely, in tobacco, deletion of OsNrx3i1 from this optimized scaffold abolished enhancement entirely (Fig. 5B and D), indicating that in dicots, the intron itself is indispensable for providing co-transcriptional or mRNA stability cues that the 5′ UTR alone cannot supply. Together, these findings highlight a critical principle in genetic part design: the optimization of natural elements often requires the removal of embedded negative regulatory features. This iterative build-test-improve process is central to synthetic biology and was key to unlocking the full potential of OsNrx3i1.

The IME effect of OsNrx3i1 appears to be highly splicing-dependent, as the observed weak enhancement in tobacco leaves likely stems from low splicing efficiency. This is consistent with previous reports on the cat-1 intron [32] and OsSodCc1i1 [33], where monocot introns were found to splice inefficiently in dicot systems, thereby suppressing IME. Recent research indicates that well-spliced introns generally promote gene expression [40], and the integration of exogenous introns with native internal splice sites can restore IME once effective splicing is achieved [41]. Mechanistically, an intron may directly facilitate transcription initiation by recruiting general transcription factors to the promoter or indirectly by modulating the chromatin structure in the promoter region [4]. However, the relationship between splicing and IME remains complex, as intron retention can lead to translational errors and nonsense-mediated decay (NMD).

Regarding the differential effects of PRSmut2 and PRSmut3 between monocots and dicots, we further analyzed the exon sequences for common non-AUG translation initiation sites (TIS), such as CUG, ACG, and GUG [42,43]. We identified three CUG, one ACG, and one GUG sites in the PRSmut3 exon, and two CUG sites in the PRSmut2 exon. We hypothesize that these non-AUG TIS may trigger translational inhibition in tobacco leaves, whereas the impact of such uORFs might be less pronounced in rice protoplasts than in planta. In the rice protoplast system, the inhibitory effect of these non-AUG TIS in PRSmut3 was not significant; instead, the additional 45-bp 5′ UTR sequence in PRSmut3 (compared to PRSmut2) may have contributed to enhanced mRNA stability.

To elucidate the functional determinants of OsNrx3i1, we performed a systematic truncation analysis. Our results pinpointed the 5′ segment (Part1) as the core region responsible for the majority of the IME activity (Figs. 5A and 6). Notably, a comprehensive motif analysis revealed that OsNrx3i1 lacks several canonical IME motifs previously identified in other species (Fig. 5A), including the 16 motifs linked to enhancement in Arabidopsis and the characterized sequences TTNGATYTG [44] and CGATT [45]. While OsNrx3i1 contains 14 of the 81 conserved hexamers identified by Back and Walther [46], its primary features consist of three poly-U stretches and 14 CT-rich stretches (Fig. 5A). Such sequences have been reported to contribute to IME in both 5′ UTRs and introns without compromising splicing efficiency [40,[47], [48], [49], [50]]. The reliance on these elements rather than canonical motifs indicates that OsNrx3i1 may operate through novel sequence features or structural configurations. This expands the known mechanistic repertoire of IME and provides new clues for the rational design of synthetic introns.

Stable and transient gene expression technologies are fundamental platforms for plant synthetic biology and functional genomics. A key challenge in these systems is the need for a diverse toolkit of regulatory elements. Reusing the same promoter in multi-gene constructs often triggers gene silencing [51], while uniform expression of all pathway enzymes can lead to metabolic imbalances. Natural promoters often suffer from limited strength, leaky expression, and poor portability, while synthetic promoters can lack sufficient activity [[52], [53], [54]]. Therefore, a toolbox of promoters with varying strengths is essential for fine-tuning transgene expression [55].

Our study directly addresses this need. By integrating OsNrx3i1 into minimal synthetic promoters (minSyn105 and minSyn108), we created promoters that rival or even surpass the constitutive CaMV 35S promoter (Fig. 7A–D). Furthermore, combining OsNrx3i1 with other regulatory modules like the ADH 5′ UTR generated a spectrum of expression strengths (Fig. 7A–D). This modularity provides researchers with a powerful toolkit to fine-tune gene expression, which is essential for metabolic pathway engineering, multi-gene stacking, and applications requiring precise protein dosage. Indeed, recent work has shown that incorporating introns into Cas9 significantly boosts editing efficiency in plants [56], underscoring the broad potential of elements like OsNrx3i1 in advanced biotechnological applications. As IME elements typically function within the transcribed region and exhibit reduced promiscuous enhancer activity, they offer higher specificity and a lower risk of unintended transcriptional activation compared to classical enhancers [25], making IME elements highly suitable for applications in synthetic biology and gene stacking strategies.

In conclusion, we have identified and characterized a rice intron, OsNrx3i1, that exhibits IME-like properties and shows potential for cross-species applications. Its ability to boost the performance of diverse synthetic promoters opens the possibility for designing strong, specific, or stable expression systems in plants. The rational design process that leads to the uORF-free OsNrx3 PRSmut2 and ADHE-OsNrx3i1 fusion exemplifies the iterative build-test-improve cycle central to synthetic biology. This work lays a foundation for further investigation into its mechanism, optimization, and practical deployment as a genetic module for plant biotechnology.

Methods

Genome resources and genotype characterization of OsNrx3

The genome sequences of O. sativa L. cv. Nipponbare [57] were obtained from Ensembl Plants (http://plants.ensembl.org/). The genomes of O. sativa L. cv. Minghui 63 and Zhenshan 97 were downloaded from Rice Genome Annotation Project database (RIGW; http://rice.hzau.edu.cn/) [58,59], while the genomes of O. sativa L. cv. Azucena and N22 were acquired from Rice Gene Index (https://riceome.hzau.edu.cn) [60,61]. Additional O. sativa genomes used for OsNrx3 genotype identification were obtained from the Rice Research Commons database (RiceRC; https://ricerc.sicau.edu.cn/) [36].

Gene annotations for O. sativa L. cv. Nipponbare were downloaded from NCBI GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_001433935.1/). OsNrx3 corresponds to NCBI Gene ID: LOC4336932, and its transcript is annotated as NCBI GenBank Accession XM_015778233.3. The full-length OsNrx3 gene sequence, along with 2 kb upstream and 500 bp downstream regions, was aligned against other genomes using BLASTN [62] to retrieve corresponding OsNrx3 sequences. All retrieved OsNrx3 gene sequences were then aligned with the reference O. sativa L. cv. Nipponbare sequence using NUCmer [63], and genotypes were assigned based on sequence similarity and structural variation.

RNA-seq-based gene expression analysis

RNA-seq datasets used for expression analysis were downloaded from the National Genomics Data Center (https://bigd.big.ac.cn/?lang=en) under the BioProject number PRJCA002103. Reads were aligned to the O. sativa Nipponbare reference genome (IRGSP-1.0) using HISAT2 [64]. Transcript abundance was quantified using Salmon [65], and was reported in transcripts per million (TPM) or other compatible units accordingly. The aligned reads in BAM format were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, version 2.16.0) [66]. To illustrate the alternative splicing events and junction reads of specific genes, Sashimi plots were generated within IGV using the “Sashimi Plot” function to show the distribution and frequency of splice junctions across genomic regions. To detect the splicing of OsNrx3 transcript, RT-PCR was performed using the primers OsNrx3-rF: 5′-GAGACGGCAGAAGGTGTTGA-3′ and OsNrx3-rR: 5′-CCAGTGTCAAAAGGCAAGGC-3′.

Vector construction

The binary vector pGreenII-0800-LUC was linearized using restriction enzymes BamHI and NcoI. Two oligonucleotides, PGreen-GGsiteF and PGreen-GGsiteR, were designed to form sticky ends compatible with the BamHI and NcoI sites upon annealing. These annealed duplexes were then directly ligated into the linearized backbone using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) to replace the original restriction sites with two BsaI sites, resulting in the construct pGreenII-0800-LUC-C. The LUC expression cassette from pGreenII-0800-LUC-C was then cloned into pUC19-EcoRI–HindIII, in which BsaI sites were mutated, to construct the vector pU-LUC. LUC-based reporter vectors were subsequently built using either pGreenII-0800-LUC-C or pU-LUC as backbone vectors. All vector construction primers are listed in the Supplementary file.

The GUS reporter constructs were generated using pDX2181 as the backbone. Vector construction primers for GUS are also provided in the Supplementary file.

Rice protoplast transient transformation

Rice protoplasts were isolated from 15-day-old etiolated seedlings, following a previously described protocol [67]. For transient transfection, a total of 2 μg plasmid DNA was used for vectors based on pGreenII-0800-LUC-C. When using pU-LUC-derived vectors, a ratio of 2 μg pU-LUC vectors to 50 ng pU-rLUC internal control vector was applied for transfection of 100 μL protoplast suspension. Protoplasts were incubated in the dark for 12 h at room temperature before subsequently measuring luciferase activity.

Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression assay in N. benthamiana

Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101(pSoup-p19) harboring the corresponding expression vectors was cultured in LB liquid medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) at 28°C for 24 h at 200 rpm. Cells were pelleted at 4000× g for 5 min, resuspended in infiltration buffer (100 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES-KOH, pH 5.6) to an OD600 of 0.2–0.5, and incubated in the dark for 3 h before infiltration. The bacterial suspension was then infiltrated into the abaxial side of N. benthamiana leaves using a needleless syringe.

Luciferase activity measurements

Luciferase activity was assessed using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Cat. No. E1910). The measurements were performed using a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Rice protoplasts were collected after 12 h dark incubation, centrifuged at 200× g, and resuspended in 50 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer (1×), followed by vortexing for 10 s to lyse cells. The Dual-Luciferase assay was then carried out according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For N. benthamiana leaves sampled 2 days post-agroinfiltration, 1 cm leaf discs were collected using a cork borer, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into fine powder. The tissue powder was resuspended in 50 μL Passive Lysis Buffer (1×), vortexed for 10 s, and luciferase activity was measured as described above.

Agrobacterium-mediated rice transformation

The rice cultivar O. sativa L. cv. Zhonghua 11 was used for genetic transformation. After dehusking, seeds were surface sterilized with sodium hypochlorite solution and washed thoroughly with sterilized water. Agrobacterium-mediated rice transformation was conducted following the standard procedure outlined by established methods [68].

Expression analysis of transgenic plants

Genomic DNA was extracted using the TPS method, total RNA was extracted using TransZol (TransGen, Cat. No. ET101-01), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen, Cat. No. 11141ES10). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Cat. No. 11184ES08). TBC1 domain family member 22A (LOC_Os09g34040) was used as an internal reference gene with primers qTBC-F: 5′-TGGTCATGTTCCTTCAGCAC-3′ and qTBC-R: 5′-GACTTGGCGAGCTTTTGAAC-3′. The primers for GUS were: GUS-qF: 5′-CGGCAATAACATACGGCGTG-3′ and GUS-qR: 5′-TACGGCAAAGTGTGGGTCAA-3′. To detect the splicing of OsNrx3i1 in PRSmut1-GUS plants, RT-PCR was performed using the primers PRS5′-F: 5′-CATACCACAGTTGGCTGGAGT-3′ and GUS-sR: 5′-CGATCCAGACTGAATGCCCA-3′.

GUS activity quantification

For GUS activity quantification, 200–500 mg of leaf tissue was collected. For transgenic rice plants, leaves from 20-day-old seedlings were used; for N. benthamiana, 1 cm discs were sampled from infiltrated leaves 2 days after agroinfiltration. Leaf tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Protein Quantification Kit (Yeasen, Cat. No. 20202ES76). GUS activity was quantified following a previously described protocol [69], with minor modifications.
