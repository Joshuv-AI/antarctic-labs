Enhancers integrate microenvironmental signals in muscle stem cells during regeneration in health, disease, and aging
Authors: Sarah Hachmer, F. Jeffrey Dilworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective skeletal muscle regeneration requires muscle stem cells (MuSCs) to continuously interpret and respond to signals from their surrounding microenvironment. These niche-derived cues, including inflammatory, extracellular matrix, paracrine, metabolic, and biomechanical signals, direct MuSC progression through quiescence, activation, proliferation, and differentiation by reshaping gene expression programs. Increasing evidence suggests that transcriptional enhancers serve as a key regulatory interface through which environmental information is translated into transcriptional output. Enhancer activity is governed by the coordinated action of lineage-defining transcription factors, histone modifiers, chromatin remodelers, transcriptional coactivators, and architectural proteins that together regulate chromatin accessibility, enhancer-promoter communication, and gene activation. Recent work has shown that enhancer landscapes and three-dimensional genome organization are highly dynamic during muscle regeneration and become altered in aging and disease. In this review, we examine how enhancer-associated mechanisms enable MuSCs to interpret niche-derived signals, highlighting the roles of transcription factor networks, chromatin remodeling complexes, and enhancer-promoter interactions in coordinating gene expression. We further discuss how disruption of enhancer regulation contributes to impaired regeneration in aging and muscular dystrophy, where altered chromatin states and genome organization lead to aberrant transcriptional responses. Understanding how these regulatory elements integrate complex environmental signals will be essential for defining the mechanisms underlying muscle regeneration and may provide new avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Background: Muscle regeneration and its dependency on the microenvironment

Skeletal muscle regeneration is the process of repairing damaged muscle tissue. The primary cell type involved in this process is resident muscle stem cells (MuSCs), also called satellite cells [1]. Under homeostatic conditions, MuSCs reside on myofibers between the basal lamina and the sarcolemma [2]. In response to extrinsic signals indicating damage, MuSCs exit the quiescent state and become activated. During cell division, the activated MuSCs proliferate rapidly to form a pool of myogenic progenitor cells, which then fuse to form multinucleated myofibers [3]. Through this process, MuSCs are able to effectively repair damaged muscle tissue. While MuSCs are the primary cell type responsible for generating new muscle, they are not the only contributors to regeneration. Effective muscle regeneration relies on coordinated interactions between MuSCs and other cell types within the niche, as well as signals from the surrounding microenvironment [4]. These include inflammatory cells (macrophages and neutrophils), fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), and endothelial cells [4, 5]. Together these cell types establish a dynamic environment that supports and regulates regeneration.

MuSCs interpret and respond to a wide range of signaling cues, including but not limited to autocrine, paracrine, and juxtacrine signals, as well as circulating systemic factors, metabolic and oxygen-dependent signals, extracellular matrix-associated cues and biomechanical inputs [4–9]. These responses involve changes to gene expression programs, which are controlled by epigenetic mechanisms [10]. Epigenetic regulation enables cells to maintain lineage identity while retaining the flexibility to respond to environmental stimuli, allowing for the tight control of MuSCs activation and progression through the regeneration pathway [6]. One key mechanism of epigenetic regulation is through transcriptional enhancers; cis-acting DNA regulatory elements that promote gene expression. The activation of enhancers and resulting transcription output is governed by the coordinated action of numerous proteins, including transcription factors, chromatin remodelers, and histone-modifying enzymes [10]. As many of these proteins are targets of signaling pathways, enhancer regulation provides an important mechanism through which cells can interpret and integrate environmental cues.

A central question that emerges from the current literature is how MuSCs interpret and respond to the diverse and dynamic signals present within the regenerative microenvironment and translate these cues into coordinated gene expression programs. Here we examine emerging evidence that enhancer-associated mechanisms may provide a framework through which MuSCs integrate niche-derived signals during regeneration, aging, and disease. We propose that enhancers serve as a central regulatory interface between the niche and the genome, integrating extracellular signals through transcription factors, chromatin regulators, and three-dimensional genome organization to generate context-dependent transcriptional responses during muscle regeneration.

In this framework, signals such as inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and biomechanical cues converge on signal-responsive transcription factors and chromatin-associated proteins, including factors such as p38 MAPK effectors, MEF2s, SMADs, STATs, and TEAD family members (Fig. 1). These proteins are recruited to regulatory regions where they act alongside lineage-determining transcription factors, such as MyoD and Pax7, to modulate enhancer activity [11–13]. Importantly, enhancers do not function in isolation, but are often organized into clusters of regulatory elements, including super-enhancers, that reside within higher-order chromatin domains and participate in long-range interactions with target gene promoters [14, 15]. Within this context, enhancer activity appears to be highly dependent on cell state [15]. Quiescent MuSCs maintain a chromatin landscape enriched for poised or permissive regulatory elements that allow for rapid activation upon injury, while activation and progression through the regeneration pathway are associated with the activation of enhancer elements and dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility [16–19]. This suggests a model in which the response to environmental signaling is not strictly deterministic, but instead reflects the combined influence of multiple signals acting on a pre-existing regulatory landscape. Such a framework allows MuSCs to flexibly respond to changes in the niche while maintaining lineage identity.

While there is strong evidence that individual signaling pathways regulate enhancer-associated proteins and chromatin structure [11, 20], direct evidence for the integration of multiple niche-derived signals at specific enhancer elements remains limited. Most current studies examine signaling pathways in isolation or infer convergence through motif analysis and pathway enrichment, rather than directly testing how multiple signals act together to regulate enhancer activity and chromatin architecture in vivo. Addressing this gap will be important for fully understanding how MuSCs coordinate complex signaling environments during regeneration.

Below we present evidence supporting the role for enhancers serving as a central interface between the microenvironment and gene regulation, enabling MuSCs to interpret diverse signals and translate them into the transcriptional programs required for effective muscle regeneration. We also highlight how disruption of this regulatory system may contribute to impaired regeneration in aging and disease.

What are enhancers and how do they serve as a platform for signals

Enhancers are cis-acting regulatory regions within genomic DNA containing clusters of transcription factor binding sites, and are associated with nucleosomes marked with transcription-related histone modifications [21–25]. These regions can exist in distinct functional states, broadly classified as repressed, primed, poised, active or silent [26]. Primed enhancers are marked only by H3K4me1 [27, 28], while poised and active enhancers are bivalently marked with H3K4me1 and H3K27me3, or H3K4me1 and H3K27ac, respectively [27–29]. Primed, poised, and active enhancers are generally associated with more accessible chromatin states [27]. Conversely, repressed and silent enhancers are associated with more closed chromatin conformations and identified by repressive chromatin marks, such as H3K27me3 and H3K9me2/3 [26, 30–32].

Poised enhancers are marked by H3K4me1 and H3K27me3, creating a chromatin signature containing both active and repressive histone marks [27, 29]. The methyltransferase-containing polycomb group (PcG) complex, polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), is responsible for establishing H3K27me3 chromatin marks [33, 34]. The poising of enhancers allows for quick transition to an active state, permitting rapid and context-dependent transcriptional responses [35]. This activation depends on the removal of the H3K27me3 mark by lysine demethylase 6 (KDM6) proteins, which allows the acetyltransferases p300/CBP to be recruited to enhancers and place H3K27ac modifications [36, 37].

Enhancer activation is mediated by the coordinated recruitment and activity of multiple classes of proteins. Current models suggest that lineage-determining transcription factors bind to specific DNA motifs and facilitate the recruitment of chromatin-modifying enzymes, including histone H3K4 monomethyltransferases such as KMT2C/D and histone acetyltransferases such as p300/CBP [38–40]. The deposition of H3K27ac marks active regulatory regions and promotes the recruitment of additional effector proteins. Bromodomain-containing proteins, such as Brd4 and components of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, recognize acetylated histones and contribute to enhancer function through distinct mechanisms [41–44]. SWI/SNF functions to mobilize nucleosomes, increasing chromatin accessibility to allow transcription factor binding. Brd4 acts as a scaffolding factor to facilitate interactions between transcription factors, coactivator complexes such as Mediator and positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb), as well as RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) [45–49].

In addition to local chromatin regulation, the function of enhancers is closely linked to three-dimensional genome organization. Through DNA looping and higher-order chromatin organization, enhancers can interact with distal promoters to regulate gene expression [40, 50–52]. Architectural proteins such as CTCF, Cohesin, and Ldb1 contribute to the formation of chromatin loops that bring enhancers into proximity with their target promoters [16, 53–55]. These interactions often occur within defined chromatin domains and can involve clusters of enhancers, including super-enhancers, that coordinate the expression of genes associated with cell identity. This higher-order organization provides a structural framework that enables coordinated regulation of gene expression and may facilitate the integration of multiple regulatory inputs.

Active enhancers are frequently associated with RNA Pol II binding and bidirectional transcription of enhancer RNAs (eRNAs), non-coding transcripts that have emerged as functional regulators of gene expression [14, 56–59]. While H3K27ac is a widely used marker of enhancer activation, eRNA transcription is increasingly recognized as a robust indicator of enhancer activity [58, 59]. KMT2C/D contribute to RNA Pol II recruitment and eRNA production through mechanisms that are largely independent of H3K4me1 deposition [60]. Beyond serving as markers of active enhancers, eRNAs can directly modulate transcription through interactions with chromatin modifiers, transcription factors, and components of the promoter-proximal pausing machinery, including P-TEFb and NELF, thereby promoting productive transcriptional elongation [61–70]. In skeletal muscle, the MyoD distal regulatory region generates the eRNA MUNC (DRReRNA) which functions alongside MyoD to regulate the expression of genes required for myogenic differentiation [62–65]. Together, these findings suggest that eRNAs provide an additional layer of enhancer-mediated regulation that may contribute to the rapid and context-dependent transcriptional responses required for MuSCs to interpret signals from the regenerative microenvironment.

At a fundamental level, all cells share the same genetic material, and differential gene expression is the primary mechanism that defines cell identity and function. Enhancers play a central role in this process by enabling cell-type specific and context-dependent gene regulation. The activation of enhancers is directed by lineage-specific transcription factors, which target regulatory regions associated with genes that define cellular identity [17, 38, 71]. However, enhancer activity is not solely determined by lineage factors. The large number of proteins involved in enhancer regulation, including cofactors, chromatin remodelers, histone-modifying enzymes, and architectural proteins, provides multiple points of regulation by signaling pathways.

As a result, enhancers are well-positioned to function as platforms for interpreting and translating signals from the microenvironment. By integrating inputs from signaling pathways at the level of transcription factor recruitment, chromatin modification, and three-dimensional genome organization, enhancers can modulate gene expression programs in a manner that is both cell-type specific and responsive to external cues.

How does the enhancer landscape change in muscle regeneration

The cell-specificity of enhancer activation and resulting regulation of gene expression suggests that these regulatory regions are highly dynamic, enabling cells to respond to environmental stimuli and transition through the distinct cell states required for muscle regeneration. Advances in genomic and epigenomic technologies have made it possible to examine not only local chromatin features, but also the three-dimensional organization of the genome, providing new insight into how enhancer landscapes are remodeled during regeneration.

Studies investigating chromatin architecture have revealed that large-scale structural features of the genome are dynamically regulated throughout the regeneration process. For example, Zhao et al. identified changes in topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries and chromatin looping in isolated muscle stem cells, demonstrating that genome organization is not static but is actively remodeled during lineage progression [16]. Consistent with this, additional work has shown that chromatin compaction and enhancer-promoter loop dynamics are closely associated with transitions between quiescent, activated, and differentiating states [16, 19, 72]. Notably, Boonsanay et al. demonstrated that Suv4-20h1-mediated H4K20me2 maintains MuSC quiescence by promoting compaction of a distal regulatory element at the MyoD locus, with loss of Suv4-20h1 leading to spontaneous MuSC activation [19]. These findings suggest that changes in three-dimensional genome organization contribute to the regulation of gene expression by altering the accessibility of regulatory elements and their interactions with target promoters.

At the level of regulatory elements, quiescent MuSCs are characterized by a distinct chromatin landscape that undergoes widespread remodeling upon activation, particularly at distal regulatory regions [17, 19]. Notably, quiescent MuSCs already possess an extensive repertoire of active enhancers marked by H3K27ac, and activation is associated with substantial remodeling of this enhancer landscape, including loss of activity at some enhancers and the establishment or strengthening of others that support regenerative gene expression programs [73]. Complementing these chromatin-level observations, transcriptional profiling studies have emphasized the rapid and stage-specific nature of these programs, with temporally regulated transcriptional programs accompanied by coordinated alterations in histone modifications that correspond to distinct stages of regeneration [17, 18, 74–76]. Together, these studies highlight the close relationship between chromatin state and gene expression and support a model in which muscle regeneration is accompanied by coordinated changes in chromatin accessibility, enhancer landscape organization, and three-dimensional genome architecture. Within this framework, enhancers emerge as highly dynamic regulatory elements that are both responsive to environmental cues and central to the execution of stage-specific gene expression programs. These properties position enhancer landscapes as key elements through which niche-derived signals can be integrated to drive cell state transitions during regeneration. However, how specific microenvironment signals drive these changes at defined enhancer elements remains to be fully understood.

Super-enhancers as regulatory hubs

Typical enhancers are defined as distal regulatory regions enriched for transcription factor binding motifs and transcription-associated histone modifications. Super-enhancers are extended clusters of such regulatory elements and are characterized by high occupancy of enhancer-associated proteins, including transcription factors, Mediator, p300/CBP, and Brd4 [14, 77]. Genes regulated by super-enhancers are frequently associated with cell identity, as these regions often control the expression of lineage-defining transcription factors across diverse cell types, including myogenic cells [77]. This relationship is supported by large-scale analyses identifying super-enhancers across multiple tissues, which consistently link these regions to genes that define cell-type specific transcriptional programs [14].

Beyond their role in amplifying gene expression, super-enhancers provide a framework for organizing regulatory inputs. Their extended structure and high density of bound factors suggest that they may function as hubs for coordinating transcriptional regulation, integrating inputs from multiple transcription factors and co-regulators to drive robust and context-dependent gene expression [78].

Within the context of muscle, early studies using C2C12 cells demonstrated that super-enhancers are dynamically regulated during myogenic differentiation [79]. These regulatory regions are largely stage-specific between myoblasts and myotubes and are associated with the expression of genes that define each cellular state. Mechanistically, MyoD and FoxO3 cooperate at super-enhancers to promote H3K27ac accumulation and activation of the myogenic gene expression program [79]. This finding is particularly notable because it illustrates how multiple transcription factors can converge on the same regulatory regions to coordinate enhancer activity and cell state transitions [80]. While these studies provide important insight into the mechanisms governing super-enhancer function in myogenic systems, they are largely based on in vitro models, and further work is needed to determine how super-enhancers integrate niche-derived signals within MuSCs during regeneration in vivo.

More recent studies highlight the role of super-enhancers within endogenous muscle tissue. For example, the fast myosin gene locus contains multiple Myh genes, the majority of which are regulated by a shared super-enhancer [81]. In this system, dynamic and competitive interactions between promoters and the super-enhancer contribute to the spatial patterning of muscle fiber types. This provides a clear example of how super-enhancers can coordinate the regulation of multiple genes within a locus through structured enhancer-promoter interactions.

Together, these findings support a model in which super-enhancers act not only as strong regulatory elements, but also as organizational hubs that coordinate transcription factor binding, chromatin structure, and gene expression. Within the broader enhancer landscape, super-enhancers are key sites where lineage-specific programs and signaling inputs converge, positioning them as vital regulatory nodes that integrate microenvironmental signals during muscle regeneration.

Signal mechanisms and their impact on enhancer regulation in muscle regeneration

Muscle regeneration occurs within a complex and continuously evolving microenvironment, requiring MuSCs to interpret and respond to diverse signaling inputs. Transcriptomic analyses highlight the sensitivity of MuSCs to these environmental conditions; for example, comparison of cells isolated in the presence or absence of blood reveals distinct gene expression programs despite similar activation states [74]. These observations underscore the importance of mechanisms that enable cells to translate external cues into coordinated transcriptional responses. As enhancer activity plays a central role in regulating gene expression, these regulatory elements are well-positioned to mediate the effects of niche-derived signals. In the following sections, we highlight key classes of microenvironmental signals and their connections to enhancer-associated regulatory mechanisms.

ECM and fibrogenic signaling

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a fundamental component of the muscle niche, providing both structural support and signaling cues that influence MuSC behavior [82]. Quiescent MuSCs are surrounded by the basal lamina and produce ECM factors themselves to help create the unique niche that these cells reside in during homeostasis [2, 83, 84]. During this quiescent state, ECM-derived signals contribute to the maintenance of stem cell identity; for example, collagen-mediated activation of the calcitonin receptor (CalcR) on MuSCs promotes quiescence through inhibition of transcriptional programs associated with activation [83, 85]. Conversely, in response to injury, fibroblasts and FAPs become major cellular contributors to ECM production and remodeling to shape the signaling landscape require for MuSC expansion [86].

ECM-derived signals influence MuSC behavior through multiple mechanisms, including the release of growth factors, integrin signaling, and changes in tissue mechanics. These pathways ultimately converge on transcription factors and chromatin-associated proteins that regulate enhancer activity. Among these pathways, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling provides one of the best-characterized examples linking ECM-derived cues to enhancer-mediated gene regulation. FGF ligands are embedded into the ECM during homeostasis [87]. During regeneration, ECM remodeling releases signaling factors, including FGF ligands, which activate receptor tyrosine kinases and stimulate downstream pathways such as p38 signaling [82, 84, 88–92]. Activation of p38 is associated with MuSC activation and proliferation, and has a well-established role in regulating myogenic gene expression.

Mechanistically, p38 signaling influences enhancer activity through modulation of transcription factor function and chromatin-associated proteins [11, 93–97]. A central target of this pathway is the myogenic transcription factor MyoD, which binds predominantly at distal regulatory regions and is therefore closely associated with enhancer function [72]. p38 promotes MyoD activity by regulating the phosphorylation and function of cofactors such as E-proteins, facilitating stable DNA binding and transcriptional activation [98–102]. Transcriptional activity of MyoD requires the formation of heterodimers with an E-protein cofactor (such as E47), which allows for stable binding at target motifs [98–101, 103]. The formation of the MyoD/E47 heterodimer is favored by p38-dependent phosphorylation of E47 to permit DNA binding [102]. Once bound to enhancers, MyoD contributes to establishing enhancer promoter loops through interactions with the architectural protein CTCF [71, 72, 104–106]. In addition, p38 signaling regulates enhancer-associated machinery, including MEF2 transcription factors, and the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex through phosphorylation of components such as BAF60c [95, 102, 107].

These studies support a model in which ECM-derived signals are transduced through kinase pathways to directly modulate enhancer-associated proteins, thereby influencing chromatin accessibility, enhancer activity, and downstream gene expression programs.

Juxtacrine signaling

Juxtacrine signaling within the muscle niche provides an additional layer of regulation that maintains MuSC identity and function. Notch signaling is a well-characterized example of this mechanism, playing a critical role in preserving quiescence by regulating the transcription factor Pax7 [108]. Activation of Notch signaling leads to cleavage of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which functions as a transcriptional cofactor in complex with RBP-Jκ to regulate target gene expression [109–112]. The NICD and transcriptional cofactors are then able to directly regulate the expression of Pax7 through binding at upstream enhancer regions [108].

Within the context of enhancer regulation, Pax7 acts as a key mediator linking signaling to chromatin state. Pax7 binds predominantly at distal regulatory regions and is associated with the establishment of active enhancer marks, including H3K4me1 and H3K27ac [113]. The binding of Pax7 influences the recruitment of H3K4 histone methyltransferase complex members, specifically at regulatory regions of myogenic-specific genes, such as Myf5, directly impacting activation of gene transcription [13]. These interactions are accompanied by increased chromatin accessibility and contribute to the formation of enhancer–promoter loops, highlighting a role for Pax7 in organizing both local chromatin structure and higher-order genome architecture [16, 54, 113]. Thus, Notch signaling provides an example of how a cell-contact-dependent niche cue can preserve stem cell identity by acting through enhancer regulation of Pax7 and Pax7-dependent chromatin remodeling.

Inflammatory signaling

Following muscle injury, damaged myofibers and infiltrating immune cells release cytokines and chemokines that establish a proinflammatory environment. These signals are essential for effective regeneration, but also require precise regulation to ensure proper MuSC function. Tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) is a key mediator of this response and has been shown to influence both MuSC activation and differentiation [114–116].

At the level of chromatin regulation, TNFα signaling impacts enhancer activity through multiple mechanisms. Activation of p38 MAPK links inflammatory signaling to previously described pathways regulating MyoD and chromatin remodeling complexes. In parallel, TNFα promotes recruitment of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) to loci such as Pax7, leading to deposition of the repressive H3K27me3 mark and suppression of quiescence-associated genes [114].

Removal of repressive chromatin marks provides an additional mechanism for enhancer activation in this context. Histone demethylases of the KDM6 family, including JMJD3 and UTX, remove H3K27me3 and have distinct roles during muscle regeneration [117]. JMJD3 is particularly important for early responses to inflammatory signaling, regulating genes such as Has2 that contribute to remodeling of the extracellular environment and feedback signaling to MuSCs [117]. In contrast, UTX functions in later stages to promote differentiation-associated gene expression [118].

These findings support a model in which inflammatory signals dynamically regulate enhancer activity through both repression and activation of chromatin states, enabling context-dependent control of gene expression during regeneration.

Biomechanical signaling

Biomechanical properties of the muscle niche, including stiffness, stretch, and tissue architecture, also contribute to the regulation of MuSC behavior [9, 119]. After removal of damaged myofibers, structural features such as “ghost fibers” provide a scaffold that supports cell proliferation and fusion, and may contribute to the spatial organization of regeneration [120, 121]. At the molecular level, biomechanical signals are transduced through mechanosensitive pathways involving the transcriptional cofactors such as YAP and TAZ [122, 123]. In response to increased stiffness, YAP/TAZ translocate to the nucleus, where they interact with TEAD family transcription factors to regulate gene expression. These factors play important roles in controlling MuSC activation, proliferation, and differentiation [85, 124]. Importantly, TEAD factors are enriched at enhancer regions and function in cooperation with myogenic transcription factors such as MyoD to regulate gene expression programs [125–127]. This suggests that biomechanical signals can influence enhancer activity by recruiting mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators.

While current studies support a model linking mechanical cues to transcriptional regulation through YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling, the extent to which these pathways directly reshape enhancer landscapes in vivo remains to be fully defined.

Together, these examples illustrate how diverse microenvironmental signals converge on common regulatory mechanisms to control MuSC gene expression. Despite originating from distinct sources, including extracellular matrix components, neighboring cells, inflammatory cues, and biomechanical inputs, these pathways frequently act through shared classes of transcription factors and chromatin-associated proteins to modulate enhancer activity. This convergence suggests that enhancers serve as central regulatory nodes where multiple signaling pathways can be integrated to produce coordinated transcriptional responses. Notably, many of these studies have examined signaling pathways in isolation, and it remains unclear how simultaneous inputs are combined at specific regulatory elements in vivo. Addressing how multiple signals are integrated at the level of enhancer landscapes and three-dimensional genome organization will be important for understanding how MuSCs interpret complex niche environments to drive appropriate cell fate decisions during regeneration.

Implications for diseases and aging

Under healthy conditions, efficient muscle regeneration depends on coordinated signaling between MuSCs and the surrounding niche, enabling precise control of gene expression programs. Disruption of these signaling mechanisms can impair MuSC function and contribute to disease and age-related decline. Growing evidence suggests that many of these defects converge at the level of enhancer regulation, where alterations in chromatin state, transcription factor activity, and genome organization disrupt a cell's ability to appropriately interpret environmental cues.

Muscular dystrophy

Muscular dystrophies are characterized by impaired MuSC function and defective regeneration. Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), an X-linked disorder caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene, provides a well-studied model for examining how disease-associated changes in the microenvironment affect MuSC behavior [128]. Studies using human cells and animal models highlight a role for enhancer dysregulation in this context. In cultured human MuSCs, Massenet et al. show that DMD-derived cells progressively lose myogenic identity and adopt a fibroblast-like transcriptional program, accompanied by altered expression of key regulatory factors, including MEF2B [129]. Restoration of MEF2B expression partially rescues this phenotype, suggesting that disruption of transcriptional regulatory networks contributes to disease progression. However, these studies are largely performed in vitro, limiting insight into how these mechanisms function within the complex in vivo niche.

In mouse models, enhancer-associated proteins have been directly implicated in disease pathology. In the mdx model, inhibition of the bromodomain protein Brd4 improves the muscle phenotype, reducing inflammation and fibrosis while enhancing regeneration [130]. Mechanistically, Brd4 regulates transcriptional programs associated with reactive oxygen species signaling and cytokine production, linking enhancer activity to the inflammatory environment that characterizes dystrophic muscle.

Additional work highlights the importance of enhancer-promoter interactions in maintaining proper MuSC function. Loss of the transcription factor YY1 in the mdx model disrupts enhancer-promoter looping, altering communication between MuSCs and the niche and promoting infiltration by immune and fibrogenic cells [131]. Together, these findings suggest that in DMD, dysregulation of enhancer activity and chromatin architecture impairs the integration of niche-derived signals, contributing to defective regeneration.

Aging

A decline in MuSC function is also a hallmark of aging and is associated with both intrinsic changes in stem cells and alterations in the surrounding microenvironment [132]. Similar to disease states, aging is accompanied by widespread changes in chromatin organization and enhancer activity that impact gene expression programs [16, 17, 75, 76, 133–135]. At the level of histone modifications, aged MuSCs exhibit increased levels of the active enhancer mark H3K27ac, particularly at regions associated with extracellular matrix and fibrogenic gene programs [134]. This is accompanied by increased Brd4-dependent transcription, linking enhancer activation to altered niche signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of BET proteins in aged mouse models partially restores myogenic capacity, suggesting that aberrant enhancer activation contributes to functional decline [134]. Aging is also associated with dysregulation of H3K27me3 accumulation, both at enhancers and promoter regions [75, 133, 136].

Aging is accompanied by changes in higher-order chromatin structure, including altered chromatin looping interactions that affect enhancer-promoter communication and gene expression [16]. These changes occur alongside reductions in heterochromatin and changes in TAD organization [16, 76]. In parallel, aged quiescent MuSCs exhibit increased chromatin accessibility, particularly at distal regulatory regions, resulting in a chromatin landscape resembling an early-activated state [17, 133]. Rather than simply increasing gene expression, aging may erode the precision of enhancer-mediated responses, potentially causing MuSCs to occupy a partially activated, transcriptionally noisy state that diminishes their ability to execute coordinated regenerative programs.

Together, these findings support a model in which both disease and aging disrupt muscle regeneration through alterations in enhancer-mediated gene regulation. Changes in transcription factor activity, chromatin modifications, and three-dimensional genome organization converge to impair enhancer function, leading to inappropriate or imprecise transcriptional responses to microenvironmental signals. This disruption of enhancer-based integration provides a unifying framework to understand how diverse pathological conditions impair MuSC function.

These insights also highlight enhancer-associated proteins and regulatory mechanisms as potential therapeutic targets. However, a key challenge moving forward will be to define how disease- and age-associated changes in the niche are integrated at specific enhancer elements in vivo, and how restoring proper enhancer function may improve regenerative outcomes.

Future directions

Current research highlights a strong interplay between microenvironmental signaling and the regulation of gene expression during muscle regeneration. While significant progress has been made in defining individual signaling pathways and their downstream targets, the mechanisms by which diverse niche-derived signals are integrated to produce coordinated transcriptional responses remain incompletely understood. In particular, although there is clear evidence that signaling pathways converge on key transcription factors such as MyoD, how these inputs are combined within the broader context of enhancer regulation and chromatin architecture remains to be fully defined.

A major challenge moving forward will be to determine how multiple signaling inputs are simultaneously interpreted at specific enhancer elements in vivo. Most current studies examine signaling pathways in isolation or infer convergence through computational analyses, rather than directly testing how combinations of signals influence enhancer activity, chromatin accessibility, and enhancer-promoter interactions. Addressing this question will be critical to understanding how MuSCs generate precise, context-dependent gene expression programs in response to complex niche environments.

Advances in multi-omics technologies provide new opportunities to address these questions. Single-cell approaches, including scRNA-seq in combination with computational frameworks such as CellChat and NicheNet, can be used to infer intercellular communication within the niche [137, 138]. In parallel, multimodal techniques such as TEA-seq and multi-CUT&Tag enable the simultaneous profiling of chromatin accessibility, transcriptional output, and protein-chromatin interactions within the same cells [139, 140]. Integration of these approaches with methods that capture three-dimensional genome organization will be particularly important for linking signaling inputs to changes in enhancer landscapes and chromatin architecture. Together, future studies that combine these approaches will be essential for bridging the current gap between niche-derived signaling and enhancer-mediated gene regulation, and for defining how these processes are coordinated to drive effective muscle regeneration.

Conclusions

Skeletal muscle regeneration depends on the ability of MuSCs to interpret and respond to signals from a dynamic and complex microenvironment. These responses are mediated by changes in gene expression programs tightly regulated by epigenetic mechanisms. In this context, enhancers emerge as central regulatory elements that link extracellular signals to transcriptional output.

Here we have highlighted our current understanding of how enhancer activity is controlled by the coordinated action of transcription factors, chromatin-associated proteins, and higher-order genome organization, and how these features enable enhancers to function as platforms for integrating diverse signaling inputs. This framework provides a unifying model for explaining how MuSCs generate context-dependent transcriptional responses during regeneration and how disruption of these processes contributes to impaired regeneration in aging and disease.

While our understanding of the intricate role for enhancers in regeneration is in its infancy, a deeper appreciation of how enhancer landscapes integrate microenvironmental signals will be critical for advancing our understanding of muscle biology and may provide new opportunities for therapeutic intervention.
