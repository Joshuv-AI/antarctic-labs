The association between smart elderly care and subjective well-being among older adults in China: the mediating roles of health status and social activities
Authors: Keyi Huang, Huashuai Chen, Jing Zhou, Qianrui Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnder the initiative of China’s “Internet+” strategy, smart elderly care reflects the government’s capacity to bridge the digital divide and provide inclusive care services for older adults. Though studies have identified positive associations between smart elderly care and health outcomes, empirical research examining its impact on older adults’ subjective well-being remains limited.MethodsThis study utilizes data from the 2021 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, N = 8,950), which includes multifaceted information on individuals aged 65 and above, covering their personal characteristics, health status, family information, and community elderly care services. A logit model is employed to investigate the association between smart elderly care and SWB. The mediating effects are tested using the bootstrap resampling method. To address endogeneity concerns, instrumental variable (IV) approaches including IV-Probit and 2SLS are applied as robustness checks.ResultsThe results reveal a positive correlation between smart elderly care and older adults’ subjective well-being (β = 0.101, p < 0.01). This association is partially mediated by both health status (proportion mediated: 38.47%) and social activities (proportion mediated: 2.65%). Heterogeneity analyses show that this positive association holds consistently across both rural and urban older adults, and is more pronounced among those with lower digital literacy.ConclusionsThis study challenges the prevailing assumption that the “digital divide inevitably isolates the older adults from participating in a digitized society.” By integrating digital technologies into older adults care services, we demonstrate that smart elderly care is positively associated with bridging technological barriers and addressing the unmet needs of aging populations. These findings further propose a synergistic mechanism—policy innovation, technological adaptation, and service optimization, to build an inclusive older adults care ecosystem, offering evidence-based insights for policymakers in aging societies to balance equity and efficiency in digital transformation.Keywords: Chinese older adults, Smart elderly care, Subjective well-being, Digital divide

Background

Population aging poses significant challenges to global social security and healthcare systems [1, 2]. As the world’s most populous country, China is experiencing an exceptionally rapid aging process [3]. It is projected that the number of Chinese adults aged 60 and above will rise from 290 million in 2023 to 450 million by 2050, accounting for approximately 33% of the national population [4]. This massive and growing older adults population has elevated older adults care as a critical social issue for urgent resolution. Under the combined pressures of changing family structures, increased population mobility, and a mismatch between the supply and demand of elderly care services, China’s traditional family-centered model of eldercare faces severe challenges [5, 6]. Consequently, certain vulnerable groups—such as rural left-behind older adults, urban empty-nesters, and functionally impaired older adults—experience significant gaps in care, which may threaten their Subjective Well-being. Therefore, establishing efficient older adults care models to enhance SWB has become an urgent priority for the Chinese government [7]. This study aims to clarify the association between Smart Elderly Care and Subjective Well-being among older adults, and to provide important insights for enabling older adults to benefit from the opportunities offered by the digital era and for promoting active aging.

Subjective Well-being (SWB) is a concrete indicator for evaluating individual utility [8], and with the intensification of population aging, the SWB of older adults has increasingly attracted scholarly attention. First, as a subjective evaluation of life experiences, SWB is commonly measured using positive indicators such as life satisfaction, happiness, and quality of life [9–11], negative indicators such as depression and loneliness [12, 13], and more complex methods based on real-time affective analysis [8, 14]. Second, regarding influencing factors, Resource Theory and Activity Theory are the two most representative frameworks in studies of SWB among older adults [15–17]. On the one hand, Resource Theory emphasizes that adequate and stable health, social, and economic resources can effectively compensate for the natural decline in physiological functioning, social roles, and economic income in older age, alleviating associated anxiety and discomfort and providing a foundation for supporting SWB [18, 19]. Among these resources, health status is a key determinant of SWB in older adults [20, 21]. On the other hand, Activity Theory posits that active social participation provides older adults with a sense of self-worth and social recognition, serving as an important avenue for achieving positive experiences in later life [22, 23]. In particular, higher levels of social activities are associated with enhancing personal perception and social acknowledgment, mitigate depression and anxiety resulting from functional decline or loneliness, and foster a more positive attitude toward future life [24, 25].

It is worth noting that the recent rapid development of digital technologies has provided new explanatory pathways for studying the Subjective Well-being of older adults from a non-income perspective. Previous studies have shown that smart home technologies [26], smartphone use, and Internet usage [27] are positively associated with the quality of life of older adults and may be associated with lower loneliness and depression in later life [28, 29]. However, existing evidence primarily focuses on the associations of specific technologies or devices at a micro level, with limited attention to SWB from a broader perspective of elderly care service models.

To fill this research gap, this study adopts a macro-level perspective of Smart Elderly Care and constructs an integrated analytical framework (see Fig. 1). Based on Resource Theory and Activity Theory, this framework extends the work of Dai et al. [15] and examines how Smart Elderly Care is associated with SWB by supplementing health resources for older adults and providing opportunities for participation in social activities. Generally, Smart Elderly Care refers to the use of modern information technologies, such as the Internet, big data, and intelligent devices, to deliver convenient, personalized, and efficient services to community-dwelling older adults, thereby improving their independent living ability and overall quality of life [30, 31]. Unlike in Western countries, Smart Elderly Care in China is primarily policy-driven and has emerged as part of the national “Internet+” strategy [32]. Essentially, it integrates digital technologies with elderly care services to effectively coordinate resources across communities, hospitals, and households, establishing a novel service model centered on family and community. This model not only combines older adults’ needs for health management, emotional support, and social activities—addressing daily care and basic emotional needs—but also facilitates a shift from a supply-oriented to a demand-oriented approach in traditional elderly care, enhancing the alignment of community public services. Research has confirmed that higher levels of community service alignment are associated with stronger improvements in the SWB of older adults.

Association between smart elderly care and subjective well-being

According to Fig. 1, this study posits that Smart Elderly Care is associated with SWB of older adults through two primary pathways: health status and social activities. First, drawing on Resource Theory, we hypothesize that health status mediates the relationship between Smart Elderly Care and SWB. Smart Elderly Care may be associated with the accessibility and efficiency of medical and nursing services for older adults through real-time health monitoring and big data analytics [33, 34], thereby potentially supporting both their physical and mental health [28, 35]. Health, as the foundation for maintaining independent living and a positive mindset in later life [36, 37], is a crucial prerequisite for higher SWB. Second, based on Activity Theory, we hypothesize that social activities constitute another key mediating pathway. High levels of social participation are closely associated with SWB among older adults. Smart Elderly Care may be associated with new opportunities for engagement, particularly for those who are disadvantaged in accessing elderly care services, through online cultural and recreational programs, social interactions, and personalized activities [38]. These opportunities may effectively expand social networks and provide alternative emotional support [39, 40], thereby enriching life purpose and potentially supporting overall well-being. However, due to the persistent “digital divide” among older adults [41–43], whether Smart Elderly Care is associated with bridging this gap and supporting SWB remains an underexplored issue. Based on the above theoretical analysis, this study includes three hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Smart Elderly Care is positively associated with older adults’ SWB.

Methods

Participants

This study utilized data from the 2021 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), organized by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development at Peking University. The longitudinal survey covers 23 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions, representing approximately 85% of China’s total population, thus ensuring strong national representativeness [44]. The baseline survey began in 1998, with follow-up waves conducted every 2–3 years. In the subsequent seven follow-up waves, surviving participants are re-interviewed, and new participants are added to replace deceased or lost respondents, maintaining stability in regional, age, and gender distributions across waves and effectively mitigating sample selection bias [45].

For this study, the initial sample drawn from the 2021 CLHLS data included 13,777 older adults aged 65 and above, with comprehensive information on individual characteristics, health status, family dynamics, and community-based Smart Elderly Care services. To ensure the reliability of the empirical results, we first conducted regressions based on the complete sample of 8,950 respondents with no missing values on any key variables (including SEC, SWB, and all covariates). To address potential selection bias caused by missing data, we further applied Multiple Imputation (MI) to the full sample of 13,777 respondents as a robustness check.

As shown in Table 1, the analytical sample, compared with the excluded sample, consisted of older adults who were generally younger, better educated, more likely to be married, and had higher levels of health insurance coverage, digital literacy, and social participation. As these characteristics are generally positively associated with subjective well-being, the observed association between smart elderly care and subjective well-being may be slightly overestimated. However, the minimal differences in the prevalence of SEC and SWB before and after sample deletion indicate that missing value removal had limited impact on sample representativeness.

Variables

Subjective well-being (SWB)

SWB was measured in this study using the CLHLS survey question, “How do you feel about your current life?” Life satisfaction indicators have been shown to effectively reflect individual evaluations of SWB [24, 46, 47]. This item responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale: (0 = very bad, 4 = very good), with higher scores indicating greater SWB.

As shown in Table 2, over 70% of respondents reported positive evaluations (“good” or “very good”), while negative evaluations (“bad” or “very bad”) accounted for only 2.35%. To avoid the potential impact of extreme skewness in the dependent variable on model estimation and to focus on the policy goal of achieving high SWB, the scale was dichotomized: respondents answering “good” or “very good” were coded as 1, and all others as 0. Additionally, to ensure the robustness of our findings, the original five-point ordinal scale was used in robustness analyses reported in (Table 9).

Smart elderly care

Smart Elderly Care (SEC) was defined in this study based on a new item in the 2021 CLHLS survey: “Does your community provide assistance for older adults to use online services, such as online meal ordering, medical appointment booking, shopping, or ride-hailing?” Respondents who answered “yes” were coded as 1, indicating that their community provides Smart Elderly Care services; those who answered “no” were coded as 0, indicating that the community does not provide such services. The key feature of this definition is that it captures a community-led “proxy model” of Smart Elderly Care. Under this model, older adults can access SEC services through offline channels, such as community registration or phone assistance, which enhances the accessibility and coverage of community-based public services. This approach positions Smart Elderly Care as an potential bridge to help older adults overcome the digital divide.

Covariates

Three covariate categories are included: (1) Demographics: Age, gender (Male = 1), marital status (Married = 1), urban/rural residence (Urban = 1). (2) Health behaviors & informal support: Smoking (Yes = 1), alcohol use (Yes = 1), living arrangement (With family = 1, Alone = 2, Institution Institution = 3), number of surviving children, household size, emotional closeness to children (Confiding persons including children = 1), caregiving support (Frequent contact with children = 1). (3) Socio-economic status: Education years, digital literacy (Regular internet use = 1), pension (Yes = 1), health insurance (Yes = 1), perceived local economic status (Above average = 1).

Mediating variables

Based on the mediating mechanisms which are grounded in resource theory and activity theory [16, 17, 23], we choose two mediators: First, health status, which is measured via the CLHLS question: “How do you rate your current health?”. Responses were dichotomized: “Good” or “Very good” coded as 1, while “Very poor,” “Poor,” or “Fair”, coded as 0. Second, social activity definition follows scholars’ classifications based on five types of high-engagement activities covered in the CLHLS survey: outdoor activities, organized social events, board or card games, regular exercise, and travel [24, 44]. Binary variables were constructed for these activities. Specifically, for regular exercise and travel, participants were coded as 1 if they engaged in the activity and 0 otherwise. For the first three types of high-engagement social activities, responses were coded as binary variable which are attributed as 0 if response is “Monthly/occasionally/never”, and as 1 if “Almost daily/weekly”. If participants engaged in more than two highly participated social activities, the binary variable social activities are coded as 1, else = 0.

Statistical analysis

All analyses are conducted using Stata 18.0. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample distribution. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to preliminarily examine the associations between variables. A binary Logit regression model was constructed to investigate the relationship between SEC and SWB. To explore the underlying mechanisms, the Bootstrap method with 1,000 resamples was employed to estimate the mediating effects of health status and social activities. Interaction terms were included in heterogeneity analyses to examine whether the associations of SEC vary across different subgroups of Chinese older adults. In addition, a series of robustness checks—including instrumental variable approaches, endogeneity tests, alternative variable measurements, and weighted regressions—were performed to ensure the reliability of the findings. All models controlled for city fixed effects and used cluster-robust standard errors at the city level.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the older adults aged 65 and above

Table 3 presents the characteristics of 8,950 Chinese adults aged 65 and above. Octogenarians (age ≥ 85 years) accounts for 48.7%, indicating a substantial proportion of the “oldest-seniors.” The sex distribution is balanced (47.6% male), and the average education duration years are 2.5 years. A total of 71.1% reside in urban areas, while 79.4% co-reside with children and 18.9% lived alone, reflecting family-centered older adults care norms. These sample characteristics align with prior aging studies, confirming sample representativeness. However, only 7.4% interviewees are regular internet users, reflecting the persistence of the “digital divide” among seniors. For mediators, 48.7% self-rated their health as good/very good, and only 13.2% frequently engaged in social activities, suggesting suboptimal levels in health and social engagement that may serve as potential pathways associated with smart elderly care.

Heterogeneity analysis

To examine whether the association between SEC and SWB varies across different subgroups, interaction terms between SEC and digital literacy, pension insurance, health insurance, and current residence are included.

The results in Table 7 indicate that the positive association between SEC and SWB is generally robust but is moderated by digital literacy. Specifically, the association is stronger among older adults with lower digital literacy (β = -0.090, p < 0.05), suggesting that SEC services may effectively benefit digitally disadvantaged older adults, potentially helping them bridge the “digital divide” and demonstrating clear inclusive value. In contrast, the moderating effects of pension insurance, health insurance, and place of residence were not statistically significant, indicating that the positive link between SEC and SWB is broadly consistent across older adults with different levels of social security and living environments. These findings further support the potential applicability of SEC as an inclusive social policy.

Instrumental variable approach

To address potential endogeneity issues, instrumental variable approaches including IV-Probit and 2SLS were applied as robustness checks. The IV-Probit model was selected as the primary estimator due to the binary nature of the dependent variable, while 2SLS models with and without city fixed effects were estimated for consistency with the main specification. We selected two instruments: the interaction between the number of internet connections per 10,000 people in the respondent’s city in 2010 and current community Smart Elderly Care (IV1), and the mean SEC level of other communities within the same county/district (IV2). IV1 reflects the long-term influence of early city-level internet infrastructure on the capacity to develop current SEC. While historical internet access is closely related to the construction of SEC, it is unlikely to directly affect the current SWB of Chinese older adults; its effect operates primarily through the availability of SEC services. IV2 accounts for potential spillover or imitation effects of SEC within the region, as the SEC level of neighboring communities may influence the development of SEC in the focal community. However, due to administrative and service boundaries between communities, SEC in other communities is unlikely to directly affect the SWB of residents in the focal community, satisfying the exogeneity assumption.

As shown in Table 8, Model 1 and 2 present the results from IV-Probit and 2SLS models using both IV1 and IV2. The strong first-stage F-statistics (485.20 and 833.89) and the Hansen J p-values (0.483 and 0.403) confirm the relevance and validity of the instruments. Model 3 reports 2SLS results with city fixed effects. In this specification, IV2 is omitted due to collinearity with the city dummies, as its variation primarily occurs at the city level. Therefore, we rely solely on IV1, which remains a strong instrument (First-stage F-statistic = 48.91). Across all three specifications, the coefficient for SEC is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level, demonstrating the robustness of our main findings to different IV estimators and instrument sets.

Robustness test

To assess the robustness of the findings, several supplementary analyses were conducted as shown in (Table 9). First, regarding the measurement of the dependent variable, an ordered Logit model was applied to the original five-point SWB scale. As shown in Model 1 of Table 9, the positive association between SEC and SWB remained significant (β = 0.500, p < 0.01), indicating that dichotomizing the SWB scale did not affect the main conclusions. Second, to test the measurement robustness of the key explanatory variable and to distinguish between the “community-agent” and “self-service” modes, we employed an alternative measure of “actual use of Smart Elderly Care services.” This analysis excluded missing values and was restricted to the valid sample with reported use status (N = 785). Model 2 showed that older adults who actually used Smart Elderly Care services reported higher levels of subjective well-being (β = 0.062, p < 0.1). However, this subsample was significantly more advantaged than the main sample in terms of education, health status, and urban residence (see Appendix Table 10), indicating potential selection bias and limiting generalizability. Third, to address potential representativeness bias due to the uneven urban-rural distribution of the sample (71.2% urban), probability weights based on the Seventh National Population Census were applied in a weighted subsample regression. As shown in Models 3 and 4, SEC is positively and significantly associated with SWB in both rural (β = 0.936, p < 0.01) and urban (β = 0.422, p < 0.01) subsamples, demonstrating the universal applicability of SEC’s beneficial association across geographic contexts. Finally, to mitigate potential sample selection bias caused by missing data, this study applied Multiple Imputation (MI) to handle missing values and then re-estimated the regressions. The results of Model 5 show that smart elderly care remains positively and significantly associated with older adults’ subjective well-being after imputation, indicating the robustness of the findings. Overall, the positive association between smart elderly care and older adults’ well-being is consistently supported across different specifications.

Discussion

This study utilizes data from the 2021 China Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) and guided by the frameworks of resource theory and activity theory [15], examined the relationship and underlying mechanisms between SEC and SWB among Chinese older adults. The findings indicate that SEC is significantly positively associated with SWB, with older adults in communities providing SEC reporting higher levels of SWB, consistent with Hypothesis 1. This finding aligns with existing evidence on technology-enabled older adults care and well-being [26]. For instance, smart elderly care platforms may reduce medical burdens for older adults with chronic diseases and their families, thereby potentially improving health status [28, 48]. In addition, the utilization of information and communication technologies is associated with lower loneliness [29]. Collectively, advancements in smart elderly care services such as smartphones, smart homes and telemedicine may play a pivotal role in supporting older adults well-being [49, 50].

Furthermore, this study tested the underlying mechanisms linking SEC to SWB using mediation models and quantified the distinct contributions of these pathways to SWB. Bootstrap mediation analysis identifies indirect effects of 38.47% through health status and of 2.65% through social activity participation, validating Hypothesis 2 and 3. According to resource theory, individuals are intrinsically motivated to maintain and acquire health and social resources [51]. SEC may supplements critical material and social resources for older adults through timely and convenient services such as online medical consultations and smart safety monitoring, potentially alleviating daily life stress caused by physiological decline and enhancing their sense of control and security—foundational elements for SWB. Mediation analysis showed that the health pathway accounted for 38.47% of the total effect, consistent with the core expectation of resource theory, thereby supporting Hypothesis 2 and indicating that health status mediates the relationship between SEC and SWB. The social activity pathway accounted for 2.65% of the total effect, although smaller in magnitude, was also statistically significant, suggesting that SEC promotes social participation and expands support networks through providing activity platforms and interaction channels, thereby enhancing psychological satisfaction and sense of belonging. This finding supports activity theory, which emphasizes that sustained social interaction is crucial for maintaining SWB among older adults [23].

The relatively weak mediating effect of the social pathway (2.65%) may reflect the current development stage and policy orientation of SEC in China. Given the large aging population, community-based SEC services have so far prioritized essential health management and daily care needs (e.g., online appointment booking, meal delivery), while features aimed at enhancing social participation and psychological well-being remain underdeveloped. This policy-driven focus may help explain why the social pathway contributes only modestly to older adults’ subjective well-being in our study. In contrast, technology-driven models in Western countries often emphasize social interaction and mental well-being. These differences highlight not only sociocultural variations but also the role of policy and service design in shaping the associations of technological applications.

Moreover, heterogeneity analysis revealed that the positive association between SEC and SWB is more pronounced among older adults with lower digital literacy, indicating that this model has particular significance in promoting digital inclusion and social equity. Traditionally, limited digital literacy is believed to restrict older adults’ benefits from technology and may even exacerbate social marginalization [52]. However, the Chinese SEC system examined in this study primarily relies on a community-based proxy model, allowing older adults to access online services such as appointment booking, health monitoring, and meal delivery through assistance from community staff or relatives without directly operating complex devices. This proxy model shifts technological complexity from individuals to the community level, enabling digitally disadvantaged older adults to indirectly engage in the digital environment, thereby supporting their SWB. This finding empirically challenges the conventional assumption that the digital divide necessarily excludes older adults and provides practical guidance for implementing inclusive SEC in developing countries with generally low digital literacy.

Theoretically, this study deepens the understanding of how smart elderly care services are associated with well-being. First, it highlights the critical role of SEC in bridging the digital divide and providing alternative support, challenging the notion that older adults’ digital literacy inherently limits technology access. Second, the asymmetric associations of the health and social pathways indicate directions for future service optimization: while consolidating healthcare services, there is a need to develop social activities tailored to older adults with advanced age and low digital literacy. Third, the Chinese case demonstrates the unique characteristics of policy-driven SEC, offering new perspectives for research on aging in different institutional contexts.

This study recognizes three limitations. First, cross-sectional CLHLS data constrain causal inference. Second, the measurement of SEC based on whether communities provide online services is relatively simplified and may not fully reflect differences in service breadth and intensity, potentially underestimating regional disparities. Future studies could construct multidimensional indicators encompassing service content, usage frequency, and technology adaptability to more comprehensively assess the associations of SEC on SWB. Finally, the transferability of SEC across different institutional contexts warrants further exploration to provide more generalizable policy guidance for digital governance in aging societies worldwide.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence for the potential of smart elderly care in supporting older adults’ subjective well-being. Specifically, smart elderly care is positively associated with subjective well-being through associations with health status and promoting social activities. The development of inclusive models that prioritize improving digital literacy, ensuring equitable access to services, and implementing cost-effective solutions is crucial for tackling global aging challenges. Notably, China’s policy-driven framework—a tripartite synergy of policy innovation, technological adaptation, and service optimization—highlights institutional strengths that differ from Western market-centric approaches. This provides actionable insights for aging societies in developing countries to achieve technological equity through institutional support.
