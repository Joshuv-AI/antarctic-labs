When BMI exceeds 50: what ten years of follow-up reveal after bariatric surgery (BARI-10-POL study)
Authors: Agata Czerwińska, Izabela Karpińska, Natalia Dowgiałło-Gornowicz, Paweł Jaworski, Piotr Major, Paula Franczak, Paula Franczak, Anna Kloczkowska, Paweł Lech, Michał Orłowski, Monika ProczkoStepaniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with severe obesity and BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² represent a high-risk group for metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS), and long-term outcome data in this population remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and long-term efficacy of MBS in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m². A retrospective multicenter observational study including patients who underwent MBS between 2007 and 2014 and completed at least 10 years of follow-up. Patients were stratified into two groups based on preoperative BMI (< 50 vs. ≥50 kg/m²). Outcomes included long-term weight loss (%TWL, %EWL), remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension (HT), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and gastroesophageal disease (GERD); perioperative complications, and revisional procedures. Eighty-nine patients had baseline BMI ≥ 50 kg/m². Mean follow-up was 11.10 years. Patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² more frequently reached ≥ 20% TWL (77.53 vs. 53.15%, p < 0.001). The proportion of patients achieving ≥ 50% EWL in group with ≥ 50 BMI was 58.43% and results were comparable between groups. Median final BMI remained higher in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group (36.33 vs. 32.27 kg/m², p < 0.001). Remission rates in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group were 59.1% for T2DM, 61.5% for HT, 57.1% for OSA, and 75.0% for GERD, with no significant differences between groups. 30-day postoperative complications occurred in 7.86% of patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m²; this value was not significantly different from the lower-BMI group. Revisional surgery was required more frequently in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² (43.82 vs. 19.90%, p < 0.001), predominantly due to weight regain. MBS in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² results in durable weight loss and significant improvement of obesity-associated diseases. However, patients with obesity class IV and higher remain at a elevated risk of revisional surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58057-1.

Introduction

Obesity is one of the most serious public health problems worldwide1,2. Its prevalence is associated with a significantly increased risk of developing obesity-associated diseases, including but not limited to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension (HT), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), cardiovascular diseases, osteoarthritis, and certain types of cancer, all of which substantially reduce life expectancy3,4.

Among patients with excess body weight, obesity class IV, V, and higher pose a significant clinical challenge. Patients with these types of obesity suffer from multiple obesity-associated disorders that are often poorly controlled5. A study by Kitahara et al. demonstrated a trend toward an increasing risk of mortality with rising BMI levels. Compared with normal-weight individuals, obesity class IV was associated with up to 9.8 years of life lost6.

Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) has been shown to be the most effective and durable treatment for patients with severe obesity7–10. However, patients with a BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² are considered high-risk surgical candidates, with an increased risk of perioperative complications, technical difficulties during surgery, and a higher frequency of rehospitalization8,11,12. A recent study including more than 173,000 participants demonstrated that patients with obesity class IV and higher have a 2.5-fold higher risk of 30-day postoperative mortality following bariatric surgery5. Moreover, some studies suggest that these patients may exhibit a suboptimal response to surgical treatment13.

Long-term studies evaluating MBS outcomes beyond 10 years are scarce. Given that obesity is a chronic disease, understanding long-term outcomes is more meaningful than focusing solely on short-term results. As the prevalence of obesity continues to rise, it can be assumed that the number of patients with obesity class IV and higher will also increase14,15. A better understanding of the specific challenges associated with treating this high-risk, high-BMI patient population is therefore necessary to optimize care and achieve the best possible outcomes. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the long-term outcomes of MBS in patients with a BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² and to compare its safety and efficacy with patients with lower BMI.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a retrospective observational study including patients aged 18–65 years who underwent primary metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) at five bariatric centres in Poland between February 2007 and December 2014, who had at least 10 years of postoperative follow-up. Patients were excluded if data for variables of interest were incomplete or if they were lost to follow-up.

The study is part of the Bariatric Ten-Year Outcomes in Poland (BARI-10-POL) project, conducted under the patronage of the Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Chapter of the Association of Polish Surgeons16–19.

Patients were categorized into two groups based on preoperative BMI: BMI < 50 kg/m² and BMI ≥ 50 kg/m². The BMI threshold of ≥ 50 kg/m² was selected as it is recognized in the previous literature as a subgroup associated with increased perioperative risk, higher postoperative morbidity, and greater long-term treatment complexity5,8,11,12. Therefore, this cutoff was chosen to distinguish a clinically meaningful high-risk population.

Data collected included patient demographics, anthropometric measurements, obesity-associated disorders, and details of the primary surgical procedure (type and duration of MBS). Outcomes of interest included weight loss outcomes; the impact of surgical treatment on selected obesity-related diseases: T2DM, HT, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and OSA; perioperative morbidity related to the primary procedure, and the revisional procedures. Finally, outcome differences between the two BMI groups were analyzed.

Ethics

All data were fully anonymized. Informed consent for surgical treatment and the anonymous use of patient data was obtained from all participants prior to surgery. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Warmia and Mazury in Olsztyn (10/2024).

Surgical techniques and perioperative care

Four types of bariatric procedures were performed laparoscopically: sleeve gastrectomy (SG), Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), and adjustable gastric banding (AGB). Surgical techniques were performed in accordance with established guidelines20. SG was carried out using a 36-French bougie, beginning 4–6 cm from the pylorus. In RYGB, the biliopancreatic limb was approximately 100 cm in length and the Roux limb approximately 150 cm. In OAGB, the biliopancreatic limb measured approximately 200 cm from the ligament of Treitz.

All patients were assessed, qualified, and prepared for surgery in accordance with the Polish Guidelines for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery21. Perioperative care was based on the 15-item Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol22.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome of bariatric treatment was defined as body weight at a minimum of 10 years after the initial procedure. Weight change was expressed as percentage total weight loss (%TWL) and percentage excess weight loss (%EWL), calculated using previously described formulas23. Ideal body weight was defined as that corresponding to a BMI of 25 kg/m². Successful weight loss was defined as ≥ 20% TWL and ≥ 50% EWL. The status of obesity-associated conditions, including T2DM, HT, OSA, and GERD, was assessed at the end of follow-up. Remission of T2DM was defined as discontinuation of preoperative pharmacological treatment and normal measures of glucose metabolism (HbA1c < 6.4%, fasting blood glucose [FBG] < 125 mg/dL). Remission of HT was defined as blood pressure (BP) < 140/90 mmHg without the use of antihypertensive medication. Clinical resolution of OSA was defined by the discontinuation of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy and the absence of self-reported OSA-related symptoms, without routine polysomnographic confirmation. GERD resolution was defined as the complete and persistent relief of reflux symptoms without the need for ongoing proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy. Additionally, the lowest weight during the entire follow up, the rate and types of 30-day postoperative complications following primary MBS, as well as the rate and indications for revisional (revision and conversion) procedures, were analyzed. The results were pooled collectively for all surgical procedures, since the objective of the study was to assess long-term treatment outcomes in a defined patient group rather than to perform a procedure-specific analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Lilliefors correction. Comparisons between the BMI < 50 kg/m² and BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² groups were performed using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test or χ² test with Fisher’s correction for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Additionally, to assess factors influencing clinically relevant outcomes, univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of achieving ≥ 20% TWL and undergoing revisional surgery in the overall cohort. Clinically relevant variables, including age, sex, baseline BMI, obesity-related comorbidities (T2DM, HT, and OSA), type of bariatric procedure, and follow-up duration, were first evaluated using univariable logistic regression analyses. Variables found to be statistically significant in univariable analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable models. Procedure type was analyzed as a categorical variable including SG, RYGB, OAGB, and AGB, with SG used as the reference category. For each variable, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For analyses of revisional surgery, complete separation was observed for the AGB category, as all patients undergoing AGB required revisional surgery. Therefore, Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was used for both univariable and multivariable analyses evaluating predictors of revisional surgery.

Moreover, a sensitivity analysis comparing final BMI between patients with and without revisional surgery in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² subgroup was performed and presented as Supplementary Table 1. Due to non-normal distribution in at least one subgroup in the Shapiro–Wilk test, comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software version 13.3 (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA) and Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 1703 patients underwent primary bariatric procedures across five participating centres between February 2007 and December 2014. Of these, 1218 patients were excluded due to loss to follow-up or missing data. This stringent application of exclusion criteria inevitably reduced the study population. Ultimately, 486 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Eighty-nine patients had a baseline BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² (group 1), and 397 patients had a baseline BMI < 50 kg/m² (group 2). The median baseline BMI was 53.26 kg/m² in group 1 and 41.73 kg/m² in group 2, with corresponding median baseline body weights of 158 kg and 115 kg, respectively. The incidence of baseline obesity-associated diseases was comparable between groups, except for GERD, which was more prevalent in group 2. SG was the most commonly performed procedure in both groups, with similar proportions (62.92% vs. 65.74%). RYGB was performed more frequently in group 1 (p = 0.005), whereas OAGB was more common in group 2 (p = 0.001). The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 11.10 years (range 10.61–12.0 years).

Outcomes

The median operative time in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group was 90 min (range 60–110 min). A non-significant trend toward longer operative time was observed in this group (p = 0.051), Table 2. 30-day postoperative complications occurred in 7.86% of patients in group 1 and included three cases of rhabdomyolysis (3.37%), two cases of leakage (2.24%), one case of gastroparesis (1.12%), one case of postoperative bleeding (1.12%). Although the complication rate was higher in group 1, the difference did not reach statistical significance.

Analysis of weight loss outcomes demonstrated that the lowest postoperative BMI was achieved at a median of 1 year after surgery, followed by gradual weight regain in both groups. The median minimum BMI was higher in group 1 than in group 2 (33.46 kg/m² vs. 28.69 kg/m²), as was the median final BMI (36.33 kg/m² vs. 32.27 kg/m²). These differences were statistically significant.

The median %TWL in group 1 was 32.58% and was significantly higher than in group 2 (p < 0.001), as was the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 20% TWL (77.53% vs. 53.15%, p < 0.001). In group 1, 58.43% of patients achieved ≥ 50% EWL, with a median %EWL of 63%.

Analysis of obesity-associated disorders revealed remission rates in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group of 59.09% for T2DM, 75.00% for GERD, 61.54% for HT, and 57.14% for OSA. Differences in remission rates between groups were not statistically significant.

The revision rate in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² was 43.82%, more than twice that observed in group 2 (p < 0.001). There were 39 revision or conversion procedures performed in group 1, 31 (34.83%) were due to recurrent weight gain, four (4.49%) due to insufficient weight loss, and four (4.49%) due to persistent GERD; one patient (1.12%) had mixed indications (persistent GERD and recurrent weight gain). Revisional procedures were required in 100% of patients after AGB, 53% after SG, and 9.25% after RYGB in group 1. No patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² who underwent OAGB required revisional surgery.

In the multivariable model for ≥ 20% TWL, higher baseline BMI remained independently associated with a greater likelihood of achieving successful weight loss (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.08–1.15, p < 0.0001). OAGB was also associated with a higher probability of achieving ≥ 20% TWL compared with SG (OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.44–5.00, p = 0.02), whereas longer follow-up duration was associated with a lower probability of maintaining ≥ 20% TWL (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.76–0.996, p = 0.04). In the multivariable model for revisional surgery, higher baseline BMI remained independently associated with an increased risk of revisional procedures (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.12, p < 0.0001). Female sex and longer follow-up duration were also identified as independent predictors of revisional surgery. With regard to the type of surgery, AGB was associated with a significantly increased risk of revisional surgery (OR 92.29, 95% CI 5.32–1600.14, p = 0.002). In contrast, RYGB was associated with a significantly lower risk of revisional surgery compared with SG (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.54, p = 0.002). Discussion

MBS is an intervention aimed at weight loss and regression of obesity-related disorders in any of its stages24. The debate on the balance between perioperative risk and potential benefit of surgical treatment in patients with the most advanced obesity should be particularly thorough. Taking into account the chronic nature of obesity, studies based on long-term follow-up after MBS, exceeding 5 years, are especially valuable. The findings of this retrospective, multicenter study provide an overview of long-term MBS outcomes in individuals with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² in Poland compared with patients with BMI < 50 kg/m². The time from the primary procedure reached 10 years in all patients, and in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m², the mean follow-up duration was even 11.79 years. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to analyze outcomes after such an extended follow-up in this specific population.

Patients with obesity must cope not only with excess body weight but also with obesity-related diseases and their complications. The literature contains numerous studies describing a positive association between BMI, the number of obesity-associated diseases, and the risk of their more severe course25,26. In our cohort, we found no significant differences in the prevalence of T2DM, HT, and OSA between the groups; however, patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² were more likely to require insulin therapy and to use three or more antihypertensive medications. Unexpectedly, GERD was more common among patients with BMI < 50 kg/m².

The predominant procedure performed in both groups was SG, reflecting current trends in Poland and worldwide2,27,28. In both groups, more than half of the patients underwent SG. The second most common procedure in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group was RYGB. OAGB, considered technically less complex than RYGB, was more frequently selected in patients with BMI < 50 kg/m², making it the second most commonly performed procedure in this group. Some patients underwent AGB as their primary procedure. This distribution of MBS procedures reflects the fact that the analyzed cohort originates from the early period of bariatric surgery development in Poland. According to current recommendations, AGB is no longer considered standard clinical practice in the treatment of obesity in Poland, mainly due to its high revision rate21.

Published data examining the relationship between complication rates and obesity severity are inconsistent. Some studies report no correlation between BMI and postoperative complications29–31. However, a recent study including 173,110 patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m², using multivariable analysis, demonstrated that such a high BMI remains an independent risk factor for serious complications and represents the strongest independent predictor of 30-day postoperative mortality5. Mittermair et al. reported a 33.3% complication rate after AGB in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m², emphasizing the need for these patients to be operated on by experienced bariatric surgeons32. Soong et al., in their work, report a complication rate of 3.1% for SG, 17.7% for RYGB, and 8.5% for OAGB in the analysis of patients with type IV obesity and higher33. In our cohort, seven cases of complications occurred in group 1 (7.86%). Although the complication rate was higher than in patients with lower BMI, the difference did not reach statistical significance. The most severe complication was anastomotic leakage (2.24%) which, according to the study by Smith et al., requires surgical intervention in up to 89% of cases34. In our study, rhabdomyolysis was defined as a creatine kinase (CK) level exceeding five times the upper limit of normal. The observed rate of rhabdomyolysis in our cohort was 3.37%. The reported incidence of rhabdomyolysis in the literature varies depending on the diagnostic criteria applied35–38. The biochemical definition used in our study may have influenced the observed incidence. The high incidence of rhabdomyolysis in our cohort may also be explained by the risk profile of the studied population, particularly patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m², who are more susceptible to prolonged operative times, pressure-related muscle injury, and reduced tissue perfusion. Gao et al., in a meta-analysis, revealed a positive association between higher BMI and the prevalence of rhabdomyolysis37. Therefore, preventive measures such as careful intraoperative positioning, limiting operative time, adequate padding, and early postoperative mobilization are of particular importance in this group.

Reduction in BMI is one of the parameters used to evaluate the clinical response to MBS and long-term postoperative follow-up. In our previous study, in which ≥ 50% EWL was used as the definition of optimal clinical response, we demonstrated no association between baseline BMI and treatment success in multivariable analysis17. The current analysis similarly revealed no significant differences in %EWL or in the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 50% EWL between BMI groups. Analyzing patients with such high BMI, Mantziari et al. reported 28.3% TWL at 10 years after RYGB, while Soong et al. demonstrated 40.8% TWL for OAGB, 35.1% for SG, and 37.2% for RYGB at 5-year follow-up33,39. In our analysis of all MBS procedures combined, the mean %TWL was significantly higher in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² subgroup, reaching 32.58%. It should be emphasized that this is a single-factor analysis. The currently used definition of a positive weight loss outcome, considered independent of baseline body weight, is ≥ 20% TWL15. According to this criterion, more than three-quarters of patients in the BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² group achieved a positive long-term outcome, a proportion significantly higher than in group 2. Nevertheless, despite these favorable results, the median final BMI in group 1 (36.33 kg/m²) still met the criteria for class II obesity. It is important to recognize that, in this patient population, the primary, realistic goal of treatment is not normalization of BMI, but rather the reduction of obesity-related risks and the improvement of quality of life.

It should be emphasized that the reported weight loss outcomes reflect the entire study cohort and include both patients who underwent a single primary procedure and those who required revisional surgery. This approach was intended to capture real-world long-term outcomes in this high-risk population, where staged or multi-step treatment is common. However, the inclusion of patients undergoing revisional procedures may have influenced the magnitude of weight loss observed, and this should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

Another key component in evaluating the effectiveness of bariatric treatment is influence on the obesity-associated diseases. Analysis of this aspect revealed that patients with obesity class IV and higher achieved remission rates of 59.09% for T2DM, 75.00% for GERD, 61.54% for HT, and 57.14% for OSA. Remission rates were comparable between group 1 and group 2. Mantziari et al. reported a 39% remission rate of T2DM at 10 years after RYGB in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² and similarly found no differences compared with patients with BMI < 50 kg/m²39. Meanwhile, Van der Laan et al. observed 60% remission of HT and 66.7% remission of OSA among patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² at 5 years after OAGB.40.

Soong et al. reported an overall revision rate of 5.4% in a population of patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² after OAGB, SG and RYGB33. A recent meta-analysis presented SG as the procedure most frequently requiring a revisional procedure41. In our cohort, the revision rate in group 1 was more than twice as high as in the control group. This finding emphasizes that both patients and surgeons should be aware that the treatment of advanced obesity may require more than one surgical intervention, particularly when the primary procedure is SG, as more than half of patients undergoing SG in group 1 required revision. Our data were collected during a period when AGB was among the standard surgical procedures. All patients who underwent AGB, regardless of BMI group, required revisional surgery. No patients after OAGB required revisional surgery; however, it should be noted that the OAGB subgroup included only a very small number of patients (n = 3), which limits the ability to draw reliable conclusions. Further studies with long-term follow-up are needed to accurately determine the revision rate in a population with such a high BMI.

Multivariable analysis of factors influencing long-term outcomes demonstrated that higher baseline BMI was associated with both a greater likelihood of achieving an optimal clinical response and a higher risk of requiring revisional surgery. The association between higher baseline BMI and achieving ≥ 20% TWL observed in our study should be interpreted in the context of the weight-loss metric used. As discussed earlier, %TWL is considered less dependent on baseline BMI, which may partially explain these findings. Previous studies evaluating other weight-loss endpoints have generally demonstrated less favorable outcomes among patients with higher preoperative BMI42,43.

Additionally, longer follow-up duration was associated with a lower likelihood of maintaining successful weight loss and a higher risk of revisional procedures. These findings underscore the importance of lifelong follow-up, which enables continuous patient monitoring and timely implementation of interventions aimed at supporting sustained weight loss.

Female sex was also identified as an independent predictor of revisional surgery. Previous studies have reported a predominance of female patients among cohorts undergoing revisional bariatric surgery44. However, it remains unclear whether female sex represents an independent risk factor for revision or rather reflects the overall predominance of women among patients undergoing bariatric procedures. Therefore, sex-related differences in revisional surgery rates should be interpreted with caution.

The analysis also suggested a beneficial effect of OAGB on maintaining an optimal clinical response. However, although the regression analyses were performed in the overall study cohort, conclusions regarding OAGB specifically in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² should still be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of such patients in this subgroup.

AGB remained associated with a higher risk of revisional surgery. Nevertheless, the interpretation of the association between AGB and revisional surgery requires caution. In our cohort, all patients who underwent AGB ultimately required revisional surgery, resulting in complete separation in conventional logistic regression models. To address this issue, Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was applied. Although AGB remained strongly associated with revisional surgery, the relatively small number of patients in this subgroup resulted in wide confidence intervals, thereby limiting the precision of the estimate. Moreover, the lower risk of revisional surgery observed in the RYGB subgroup may indicate a potential advantage of this procedure during surgical decision-making and patient qualification. Nevertheless, these findings require confirmation in further prospective studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its multicenter retrospective observational design may introduce bias related to data collection and differences in perioperative care between centers. Another bias is introduced by the follow-up rate achieved which was 28.5%. The relatively low follow-up rate, a common issue in bariatric surgery, may result in overestimation of weight loss and obesity-associated diseases resolution, as patients with the highest compliance were analyzed. The assessment of OSA remission was based on clinical evaluation without routine polysomnographic confirmation, which may introduce subjectivity and limit the accuracy of the reported outcomes.

Moreover, in the present study there was a very small number of patients undergoing OAGB in the ≥ 50 BMI group limits the reliability of procedure-specific estimates and precludes drawing definitive conclusions regarding outcomes associated with this procedure. OAGB cases were retained in both regression models because procedure type was included as a clinically relevant covariate and all procedure categories performed in the analyzed cohort were incorporated into the analyses. However, we want to emphasize, that due to the very small sample size, the resulting estimates for the OAGB subgroup should be interpreted with extreme caution.

An important aspect of long-term outcomes after metabolic bariatric surgery is the occurrence of nutritional deficiencies, including iron, vitamin B12, and vitamin D. In the present study, these parameters were not systematically collected across participating centres and were not included in the analysis, which limits the comprehensive assessment of long-term safety. Given the ≥ 10-year follow-up period, the absence of standardized nutritional data represents a relevant limitation and should be considered when interpreting the results. As highlighted by Brethauer et al., standardized reporting of bariatric outcomes should include nutritional parameters, which were not captured in the present analysis23.

Another limitation is that outcomes were reported collectively for all procedure types, as the study was not powered to compare individual procedures in terms of efficacy or complications, but rather intended to guide the initial decision between surgeon and patient on the candidacy for bariatric surgical intervention. Additionally, the inclusion of patients who underwent revisional surgery should be considered when interpreting the results, as it may have influenced the observed weight loss and long-term outcomes.

Despite these limitations, we believe that our findings provide valuable insight into the long-term surgical management of patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m².

Conclusions

MBS in patients with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m² is a treatment modality with a high safety profile that provides satisfactory and durable outcomes in terms of both weight loss and remission of obesity-related diseases. However, both surgeons and patients should be aware that final BMI values remain higher than in patients with lower baseline BMI and that there is a high risk of revisional surgery, mainly due to recurrent weight gain, insufficient weight loss, or severe GERD. These findings highlight the need for careful patient selection, long-term follow-up, and individualized surgical strategies in this population.
