ABSTRACT
We previously surveyed professionals in the photovoltaic industry to understand interests in
education and training for performance modeling of photovoltaic (PV) power systems. We found
that most professionals are self-taught and rely on a variety of public sources of technical materials.
Available formal education, such as courses or certification programs, either lacks detail or is
focused on software user training.

Here we report a companion survey of instructors at universities offering courses that address PV
performance modeling. We confirm our earlier finding that most courses lack the level of detail
sought by the professional community. We recommend that the PV Performance Modeling
Collaborative (PVPMC), or a university, sponsor a committee to produce a curriculum outline for
PV system performance modeling, and that the PVPMC solicit industry for well-documented
examples of PV system designs and accompanying performance models.

INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF FINDINGS

Photovoltaic (PV) performance modeling translates observed or modeling weather to electrical
power, considering location, system design and component characteristics. Performance modeling is
integral to design, valuation, operations and maintenance of PV power systems.

Performance modeling is multidisciplinary, requiring some understanding of earth and atmospheric
sciences, electrical engineering, solid state physics and mathematical modeling. Our survey [1] of PV
modeling practitioners found that PV performance modeling is overwhelmingly learned on the job,
from colleagues or through self-study using online resources. The survey found substantial interest
in in-depth education in PV performance modeling.

We also sought to understand the formal education available for PV performance modeling. We
identified U.S. and non-U.S. universities offering courses that address the topic. We invited faculty at
these institutions to respond to eight questions to understand the scope and content of offered
courses; institutional home; means of instruction; type and source of instruction materials; and gaps
in instructional materials.

This report summarizes responses to the questionnaire sent to faculty and our findings about
opportunities to improve formal education for PV performance modelers.

2.1. Question 1: Do you offer courses that teach either PV system
 performance modeling, or PV system design or valuation?
 Please identify the course by catalog number, if available. Please indicate if the courses
 are typically for undergraduates or graduates.

 If syllabi are available, please attach or link to a document. We will use the syllabi and
 course descriptions to understand the learning objectives and level of detail offered in
 each course.

Table 1 summarizes responses to Question 1, and lists courses identified in responses. After
reviewing syllabi, we conclude that UL’s ENG432G and DTU’s 34554 courses approach the level of
detail described as necessary by PV modeling practitioners [1]. Other courses cover the material to
an incomplete or lesser degree of detail. For example, many of these courses teach solar position
calculations, details of solar cell operations, and may also discuss processes that affect PV module
output such as the spectral content of irradiance. But all omit some significant aspects of PV system
performance modeling, such as effects of shading, single axis tracker positioning, and inverter
operations. Often, these courses delegate instruction in system-level modeling to learning to use
software such as the System Advisor Model (SAM).

2.2. Question 2: Which academic departments and colleges provide faculty
 for these courses?

PV system design and performance modeling courses are offered through a variety of engineering
and science departments, with the majority housed within engineering colleges. Faculty expertise
spans a similar range, from materials, photonics, electrical engineering and physics.

Aside from the programs at UL and DTU, current instruction is not likely to produce future
instructors with in-depth understanding of PV system modeling. This seems intuitive in retrospect
since most instructors teaching these courses are also self-taught in the subject, without the benefit
of detailed curricula.

Departments and multi-disciplinary programs that sponsor courses or provide faculty include:

 • Mechanical Engineering (multiple institutions)

 • Electrical and Computer Engineering
 • Electrical Sustainable Energy
 • Electrical and Photonics Engineering
 • Materials Science and Engineering
 • Physics and Astronomy
 • General Engineering
 • Clean Energy Systems

2.3. Question 3: What is the institution’s objective for offering these
 courses?

Institutions primarily offer PV system design and performance modeling courses to prepare students
for careers in the solar and renewable energy industries and to support broader educational
programs in engineering and sustainable energy. Many courses serve as integral components of
larger academic programs, including:

 • Renewable Energy minors.
 • Alternative Energy Engineering bachelor's degrees.
 • Mechanical Engineering and Electrical & Computer Engineering degree concentrations.
 • Power systems specialization tracks.
 • Sustainable Energy master's programs.
Courses are often required or are core or technical electives within these programs.
Except for UL, responses do not indicate intent to prepare students for professional certifications
(e.g., NABCEP Associate credentials).

2.4. Question 4: How are the courses delivered? Please indicate all that
 apply.
 a. In-person instructional lectures/laboratory sessions
 b. Self-paced learning with recorded materials
 c. Project-based learning
 d. Self-directed course with faculty advisor
 e. Other delivery model (please describe)
In-person instructional lectures and laboratory sessions are the dominant delivery methods, used by
nearly all institutions. A substantial number of courses incorporate project-based learning, allowing
students to apply concepts through practical design, analysis, or research projects to allow for
experiential learning alongside classroom instruction.

 14

Less commonly, institutions reported using self-paced learning with recorded materials, typically as a
supplement to in-person instruction rather than as a standalone delivery model.
Only one response indicated an online delivery model. One response indicated that the course
consisted of self-directed learning through independent research.

Responses indicate that instructors rely on a broad mix of instructional resources, with most using
multiple resource types, including textbooks, exercises, real-world datasets, software tools, and
industry engagement. Frequently cited texts include:

 • Brownson, J. R. S. (2013). Solar energy conversion systems. Academic Press.
 • Kalogirou, S. A. (2023). Solar energy engineering: Processes and systems (3rd ed.). Academic
 Press.
 • Gevorkian, P. (2011). Large-scale solar power system design: An engineering guide for grid-
 connected solar power generation. McGraw-Hill Education.
 • Duffie, J. A., & Beckman, W. A. (2020). Solar engineering of thermal processes,
 photovoltaics and wind (5th ed.). John Wiley & Sons.
 • Şen, Z. (2008). Solar energy fundamentals and modeling techniques: Atmosphere,
 environment, climate change and renewable energy. Springer Science & Business Media.
 • Dunlop, J. P. (2012). Photovoltaic systems (3rd ed.). American Technical Publishers.
 • Goswami, D. Y. (2022). Principles of solar engineering (4th ed.). CRC Press.
Some instructors mentioned relying on public data sets, to include the NSRDB, SURFRAD
measurements, and the CEC equipment lists.

Most courses appear to leverage free or commercial software. Software mentioned in responses
include SAM, pvlib-python, PVsyst, PVWatts, PVGIS, PlantPredict, PlantDesign and RatedPower.
These tools are used to teach system design, performance modeling, and energy production analysis.

Guest lecturers from industry and partner organizations are sometimes invited to provide
professional perspectives and expose students to current industry practices. Some instructors
mention supplementing classroom instruction with field trips to utility-scale PV installations.

2.6. Question 6: What instructional resources do you wish were available?

Responses indicate three types of requests for instructional resources: a textbook, additional
software, and case studies or benchmark datasets.

The most common request is for a comprehensive, dedicated textbook covering PV system design
and modeling in detail. Available texts either lack detail in performance modeling, lack detail in
system design, or are somewhat out of date for current design practices and available technology.
Responses indicate a willingness to leverage recorded instructional content, e.g., YouTube, to
supplement available texts.

Two requests for software emerge from the responses:

 • An accessible bifacial irradiance ray-tracing tool, comparable in ease of use to PVWatts.
 • Open-source PV layout and site-design software capable of creating layouts on maps as an
 alternative to commercial design packages.
A third recurring request is for detailed examples, such as well-documented utility-scale PV project
designs; complete modeling workflows, including assumptions, rationale for model and data choices
and results; and benchmark PV system datasets that students can use to validate and compare their
modeling results.

3. FINDINGS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
Our earlier survey of PV modelers [1] indicated broad interest in more detailed education and
training than is generally provided. Modelers expressed a need to understand in detail the full range
of topics that bear on estimates of system output ([1], Table 1), including, for example: solar cell
physics, irradiance modeling, techniques to represent power and energy losses, and treatment of
uncertainty. Most of the courses identified in responses to the educational questionnaire do not
cover the range of topics in the detail desired. Two courses (taught at UL and DTU) provide
sufficient depth. We note that both are part of a group of courses that cover other necessary topics,
such as PV system design.

 Finding: There is demand and need for a course sequence designed to educate professional
 PV system modelers. In our judgment, system design and performance modeling should be
 taught together, and the scope seems greater than can be adequately covered in a single
 course. The course sequence at University of Louisiana at Lafayette (ENGR431G (design)
 and ENGR432G (modeling) could serve as a model for course development.

One reason for the lack of in-depth instruction in PV performance modeling is the absence of a
comprehensive text. Available texts address portions of the scope of PV modeling, but none cover
the full range of topics. Instructors also indicate a lack of well-documented examples comprising
validated PV system designs and worked-through system modeling. All instructors indicated use of
software in their courses; they note the lack of an open-source PV system design platform as an
alternative to commercial software.

 Finding: There is a need for additional instructional resources, including 1) a comprehensive
 text, 2) well-documented examples, and 3) system design software with educational licenses.

We observe that most instructors teaching PV system modeling come with backgrounds in other,
subjects, for example, solar cell physics, optics, or power system engineering. The instructors likely
also learned PV system modeling “on the job” either as professionals or by teaching related material.

 Finding: Instructors would benefit from opportunities for professional development to
 broaden expertise in the range of topics involved with PV system modeling.

We recommend the following actions:

 Action: Form an educational committee to write a curriculum outline for PV performance
 modeling. The committee could be chartered through the PVPMC [2] or led by university
 faculty with participation by both parties.

The curriculum outline could guide lesson plans for specific courses. Once lesson plans mature,
lecture notes and accompanying materials could serve as the basis for a comprehensive text.

 Action: Through the PVPMC, solicit industry for well-documented examples of PV system
 designs with complete PV system models.

Finally, we recommend that institutions recognize that large number of potential students who are
working professionals, and design courses for remote enrolment and instruction.
