Covid-19 pneumonia in lung transplant recipients admitted to the intensive care unit: the COLT-ICU French Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Madeleine Thès, Thibaut Genty, Nathalie Zappella, Charles Tacquard, Guillaume Tachon, Sami Hraiech, Cédric Bretonniere, Sylvie Paulus, Laure Crognier, Hadrien Rozé, Sebastien Repplinger, Jérôme Le Pavec, Fadel Elie, François Stéphan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung transplant (LTx) recipients are at high risk for severe COVID-19. Few data on morbidity and mortality in LTx recipients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for COVID-19 are available.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective study in the nine accredited adult LTx centers in France from March 2020 to December 2022. The primary objective was to identify risk factors for mortality in LTx recipients admitted to the ICU for severe COVID-19. We also described treatment strategies before and during the ICU stay, ventilation strategies, and medium-term changes in lung function tests in survivors.ResultsOf the 84 included patients, 39 (46.4%) died in the ICU. Factors independently associated with ICU mortality were time >30 months since LTx (OR, 5.52; 95%CI, 1.66–20.30; P=0.007) and intubation (OR, 4.90; 95%CI, 1.39–19.73; P=0.017). Among the 54 intubated patients, mortality was 59% both in those with first-line intubation (n=22) and in those with intubation after failure of non-invasive strategies (n=32). Mortality was 80% with intubation after ≥5 days of non-invasive strategies. The use of specific anti-SARS-CoV-2 treatments was not different between survivors and non-survivors. Lung function tests worsened significantly after the infection.ConclusionsThe ICU mortality rate among LTx recipients with severe COVID-19 pneumonia was high. Although intubation was associated with mortality, the very high mortality when intubation was delayed by ≥5 days indicates a need for rapidly identifying those patients who will not recover with non-invasive strategies. Specific anti-SARS-CoV-2 treatments were not associated with decreased mortality.

Introduction

Immunocompromised patients are at high risk for developing severe forms of COVID-19 pneumonia due to SARS-CoV-2.1, 2 Among them, solid-organ transplant recipients more often require admission to the hospital and ICU compared to the general population (39.5% vs. 6.0%) and (29.1% vs. 15.5%, respectively).3

In lung transplant (LTx) recipients hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia during the pandemic, mortality ranged from 13% to 46% in observational studies.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, these were small, heterogeneous, single-center studies with short follow-ups. Overall mortality in LTx recipients with COVID-19 is probably far lower, since half the patients have mild or no symptoms.9 In a multicenter cohort study of solid organ transplant recipients with COVID-19, compared to recipients of other organs, LTx recipients more often required hospitalization (75% vs.66%) and had a higher mortality rate (24% vs. 16%).10 Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) was independently associated with death.10 Among LTx recipients hospitalized for COVID-19, about 50% required ICU admission, and in-ICU mortality ranged from 50% to 100%.3, 10 This considerable variability in mortality may be ascribable to the small number of patients in studies of ICU patients with LTx and COVID-19.

The aim of this study was to obtain a large cohort of LTx recipients admitted to the ICU for COVID-19 in order to improve knowledge of outcomes. The primary objective was to identify risk factors for mortality. The secondary objectives were to evaluate ventilation strategies and their associations with outcomes and, in survivors, to determine whether lung function test results were poorer than before the infection.

Material and methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study in the nine accredited, adult, LTx centers in France. All adult LTx recipients with PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection who were admitted to an ICU in these centers between March 2020 and December 2022 were enrolled. Follow-up was until death in the ICU or, in survivors, 6 months after ICU discharge.

The study was approved by our institutional review board (Groupe Éthique Recherche Médicale, #00012157). In compliance with French law, only patients who had no objection to having their de-identified data used for the study were included.

Data collection

Data were collected from patient electronic or paper files, including hospital discharge reports from ICUs and pulmonology departments and outpatient visit notes. All data were collected at each center in an electronic case-report form via the secure RedCap web application (projectredcap.org). All patients were taking a combination immunosuppressive regimen including an anticalcineurin, an antimetabolite, and a corticosteroid.

For each patient, we collected age, sex, weight, and height; transplantation details such as date, type (single or double lung, combined organs), underlying diagnosis, and post-transplant events (humoral rejection, cellular rejection, bronchial or infectious complications, CLAD). Biopsy-confirmed cellular rejection and CLAD were defined as a persistent ≥20% decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) from baseline, independently from a change in forced vital capacity (FVC).11, 12 Humoral rejection was defined by the detection of donor-specific antibodies warranting the initiation of specific treatment. The results of the most recent lung function tests (LFT) conducted within 4 months before the COVID-19 infection were also collected.

Specific anti-COVID-19 treatments administered before and during the ICU stay and vaccination status at ICU admission were recorded. Clinical and radiological data at diagnosis were collected, as well as the first-line strategy for acute respiratory failure (intubation, noninvasive ventilation [NIV], high-flow nasal oxygen [HFNO], or conventional oxygen therapy [COT]). Hemodynamic failure was defined as the need for vasopressor support, cardiogenic failure as the need for inotropic support, hepatic failure as a prothrombin time<50% in the absence of active bleeding; and renal failure according to the KDIGO classification.13 Bleeding complications were defined as clinical bleeding requiring hemostatic surgery, embolization, or transfusion of packed red blood cells. Thrombotic complications were defined as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or catheter thrombosis documented by computed tomography (CT) or Doppler ultrasound. Survivors underwent LFT 6 months after ICU discharge.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ICU mortality. The secondary outcomes were the strategies used to treat the acute respiratory failure and the LFT results 6 months after ICU discharge in survivors.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean±SD if normally distributed and as median [interquartile range] otherwise. Comparisons were with Student’s test for normally distributed variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for skewed variables. Categorical variables were described as n (%) and compared by applying Fisher's exact test. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to assess time from enrollment to death according to ventilatory strategy, with comparison using the log-rank test.

To identify factors associated with mortality, we first performed univariate logistic regression analyses. Variables with a p-value <0.1 in univariate analysis were then considered for inclusion in the multivariate model (age, time since transplantation >30 months, endotracheal intubation, SAPS II score, and pre-transplant FEV1). In addition, variables deemed clinically relevant based on prior knowledge and clinical judgment were also included, regardless of their univariate significance.

A multivariate logistic regression model was subsequently constructed to identify independent predictors of mortality.

Odds ratios (OR) were computed with their 95% confidence intervals (95%CI).

Statistical analyses were performed using the R program (version 4.3.1, www.r-project.org). P values <0.05 were taken to indicate statistical significance.

Of the 84 patients, 39 (46.4%) died during their ICU stay. Post-LTx complications and LFT results within 4 months before COVID-19 were not significantly different between survivors and non-survivors. Age, time since LTx, and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score-II (SAPSII) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score at admission had significantly higher values in the non-survivors than in the survivors.

Therapeutic Strategy Before and During ICU Stay

The proportion of patients who received dexamethasone or other specific anti-COVID treatments before or during the ICU stay was not significantly different between survivors and non-survivors. The mortality rate was not significantly different between the groups admitted in 2020 vs. 2021–2022.

Strategy for treating acute respiratory failure

Intubation was performed in 54 (64%) patients including 22 (40.8%) as the first-line strategy and 32 (59.2%) after failure of HFNO and/or NIV. ICU mortality in intubated patients was 32/54 (59.3%) (Figure 1). The median times from symptom onset and from ICU admission to intubation were 12 [7–15] days and 1 [0–4] day, respectively. Discussion

In our multicenter retrospective cohort study of 84 LTx recipients with severe COVID-19 requiring ICU admission, ICU mortality was 46.4%. Intubation and an interval of more than 30 months between LTx and COVID-19 were independently associated with ICU mortality. Treatments targeting COVID-19 were not significantly associated with ICU mortality but were heterogeneous and non-standardized. Intubation delayed by 5 days or more was associated with higher mortality and the non-statistically significant nature of the difference should be interpreted in the light of the small sample size. Finally, in survivors, LFTs showed significant declines in FEV1 and FVC.

To the best of our knowledge, ours is the largest cohort specifically addressing ICU mortality in LTx recipients with severe COVID-19. The participation of all accredited LTx centers in France produced comprehensive data on the management of these patients.

ICU mortality in our cohort was significantly higher than 25%–30% rate reported in the general population admitted to the ICU for COVID-19.14, 15, 16 Interestingly, the severity scores at ICU admission were comparable to those reported on non-transplant patients.14 Thus, greater acute illness severity was probably not the main contributor to the higher mortality. LTx recipients are also at higher risk for death from severe COVID-19 compared to other solid organ transplant recipients.8, 17 In a retrospective study comparing SOT recipients to non-transplanted patients with COVID-19 initially admitted to conventional wards, overall mortality was 46.7% in the LTx recipients, that is, similar to ICU mortality in our study.17

Intubation is a well-established risk factor for mortality in the general population with severe COVID-19.18 Our study confirms that a need for intubation is a major risk factor for mortality in LTx recipients. The second independent risk factor for mortality identified in our cohort was having COVID-19 more than 30 months after LTx. Several mechanisms may explain this association. Patients with longer post-transplant duration are more likely to develop chronic lung allograft dysfunction and have reduced pulmonary reserve, which may predispose them to worse outcomes during severe respiratory infections. In addition, long-term comorbidities and frailty may further contribute to increased mortality risk. Another potential explanation may be the long-term persistence of immunosuppression, which could promote severe viral infections and impair viral clearance.19 Also, some immunosuppressants have in vitro antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties that might translate into a dampening of the runaway inflammation seen in COVID-19.20, 21 Thus, the higher target levels of tacrolimus and cyclosporine in the first month after LTx might mitigate the cytokine storm syndrome characteristic of severe COVID-19.22, 23, 24

COVID-19 vaccination was not associated with lower ICU mortality. Of note, only a third of patients had received the three recommended doses. Recruitment for our study started in March 2020, a full year before a vaccine became available. Also, in a retrospective study, only 16% of LTx recipients developed a vaccine response.25 Dexamethasone, which has demonstrated efficacy in the general population with severe COVID-19,26 was not associated with lower ICU mortality in our cohort. However, the sample size was small, as less than half our patients received dexamethasone therapy before or during the ICU stay, probably because all LTx recipients are on long-term corticosteroid therapy as part of their immunosuppressive regimen. None of the other treatments targeting SARS-CoV-2 was associated with a difference in mortality but the numbers of patients treated were extremely small, resulting in very low statistical power.

Two-thirds of our patients were intubated. This proportion is similar to that in the general population of patients admitted to the ICU for severe COVID-19.14, 27 The median time of 1 day between ICU admission and intubation was also similar to data from the general population.14 Deciding whether and when to intubate is a major challenge. ICU mortality was not significantly different with immediate intubation and intubation after HFNO an/or NIV failure and not intubating was associated with lower mortality. However, disease severity probably differed across these three groups, hindering the interpretation of the results. Delaying intubation may be associated with higher mortality.28, 29 In our cohort, intubation after 5 days or more of HFNO and/or NIV was not associated with higher mortality probably due to the very small sample size. Delayed intubation after failure of non-invasive respiratory support has been associated with increased mortality, likely reflecting a more advanced stage of disease. This observation may be explained by progressive respiratory deterioration, including the potential contribution of patient self-inflicted lung injury and worsening parenchymal damage.28, 30

In immunocompromised patients, delayed intubation may also hinder timely microbiological documentation and appropriate treatment of bacterial superinfection or viral co-infection.

In the general population with severe COVID-19, HFNO decreased the intubation rate, but delayed intubation after HFNO failure was associated with higher mortality and more pulmonary sequelae.31, 32, 33

In the survivors, lung function testing 6 months after ICU discharge showed significant declines in FEV1 and FVC compared to the results before the COVID-19 episode. A retrospective study of 80 patients with COVID-19, including only 4 with critical illness but no intubation and 16 with severe illness, showed no significant difference in LFT results before and after COVID-19.34 Several studies focusing on severe COVID-19 in the general population found abnormal LFT results shortly after the disease followed by overall normalization within 12 months.35 In 74 LTx recipients, however, LFT results recovered only partially 3–6 months after COVID-19.36 The proportion of patients with a greater than 20% FEV1 decline was 16% in non-admitted patients, 30% in patients admitted to conventional wards, and 57% in those admitted to the ICU.36 In our cohort, 22.2% of patients had a greater than 20% FEV1 decline 6 months after ICU discharge.

The retrospective data collection is the main limitation of our study. Several biases related to this cohort design should be acknowledged, including collinearity in the analysis of risk factors and confounding introduced by patients who died under do not intubate orders. Second, some LTx recipients who had COVID-19 may have been admitted to ICUs not located in their transplant center and therefore not included in our study. Third, given the multicenter recruitment, the absence early in the pandemic of recommendations for managing patients admitted to the ICU for COVID-19, and the changes in treatment strategies as the pandemic unfolded, the treatments used were heterogeneous. The result was very small sample sizes for some treatments, with very low power for detecting significant differences. Finally, all participating centers were in France, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

In LTx recipients, severe COVID-19 requiring ICU admission led to high mortality and, in survivors, to a decline in lung function 6 months after ICU discharge. Intubation and an interval of more than 30 months between LTx and COVID-19 were independent risk factors for death. Although ICU mortality was not significantly different between patients intubated immediately and those intubated after failure of HFNO and/or NIV, mortality was very high in patients intubated after prolonged non-invasive management. Thus, determining the optimal time for intubation is a key concern.
