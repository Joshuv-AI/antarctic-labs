Light, heavy, primordial: exploring the diversity of black hole seeding and growth mechanisms in the JWST era
Pratika Dayal

Abstract
The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed a puzzling population of massive black holes in the first billion years, many of which are over-massive compared to their hosts (obese black holes), and reside in metal-poor hosts, posing a challenge for theoretical models at these early epochs. In this work, we compare the observational properties of astrophysically-seeded black holes using the delphi semi-analytic model and cosmologically-seeded primordial black holes (PBHs) using the phanes analytic model. We explore the growth of light ( ∼ 100 ​ M ⊙ \sim 100\mathrm{M}_{\odot} ) and heavy ( ∼ 10 3 − 5 ​ M ⊙ \sim 10^{3-5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot} ) seeds through mergers and accretion (both Eddington-limited and at super-Eddington rates) in the astrophysical scenario; PBHs (seeded between 10 0.5 − 6 ​ M ⊙ 10^{0.5-6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot} ) only grow through accretion at sub-Eddington rates. Comparing to observables at z ∼ 5 − 10 z\sim 5-10 , the only model that can be ruled out is the one where we allow Eddington-limited accretion onto light seeds, given their inability of growing to the massive masses observed at these early epochs. The observed high values of the black hole mass-stellar mass relation ( 0.3 − 1 0.3-1 ) can be reproduced by both PBHs and heavy seeds accreting at super-Eddington rates. However, only the PBH and Eddington-limited heavy seeding models can simultaneously reproduce the observed black hole masses ( M B ​ H \mathrm{M}_{BH} ), stellar masses ( M ∗ M_{*} ), and extremely low host metallicities ( Z ≤ 0.01 ​ Z ⊙ Z\leq 0.01\mathrm{Z}_{\odot} ) inferred at z ∼ 7 − 10 z\sim 7-10 . Crucially, we find PBHs show decrease in the black hole mass-stellar mass ratio with increasing halo mass at all redshifts, contrary to any astrophysical black hole model, in addition to inhabiting lower-mass halos. Quantitatively, selecting systems at z ∼ 7 z\sim 7 with M B ​ H / M ∗ > 0.1 \mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}>0.1 and bolometric luminosities ∼ 10 44 − 46 ​ e ​ r ​ g ​ s − 1 \sim 10^{44-46}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1} that show a negative black hole to stellar mass ratio and reside in 10 9 − 11 ​ M ⊙ 10^{9-11}\mathrm{M}_{\odot} halos offer a promising clustering-based discriminant of PBH seeding models.

Introduction

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has been revolutionary in shedding light on the emergence of black holes in the first billion years of the Universe. These incredible observations have given rise to a number of puzzles, including: (i) the existence of black holes as massive as
10^{8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
within the first billion years, at redshifts
z\sim 8-10

(
Kokorev et al. 2023
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Kovács et al. 2024
)
; (ii) black holes that are inferred to be over-massive compared to the stellar masses of their hosts, with black hole to stellar mass ratios at high as
30-100\%
at
z\sim 5-10

(
Übler et al. 2023
;
Kokorev et al. 2023
;
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Kovács et al. 2024
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Kocevski et al. 2025
;
Juodžbalis et al. 2025a
;
Napolitano et al. 2025
)
; (iii) black holes hosted in galaxies that are inferred to be metal poor with metallicity values
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.1-0.01\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}

(
Maiolino et al. 2025a
;
Tripodi et al. 2025
)
; and (iv) the X-ray weakness of such early black holes
(
Yue et al. 2024
;
Ananna et al. 2024
;
Maiolino et al. 2025b
;
Tortosa et al. 2026
;
Comastri et al. 2026
;
Mazzolari et al. 2026
)
.

Observationally a number of caveats remain which concern the inferred black hole masses, bolometric luminosities and stellar masses. For example, black hole masses are typically inferred by applying local calibrations to single-epoch measurements of emission lines at high-redshifts. A number of works note that the high velocities indicated by Balmer line broadening could arise due to outflows
(
Baggen et al. 2024
;
Matthee et al. 2024
)
and single-epoch local calibrators could over-estimate black hole masses by as much as an order of magnitude
(
Lupi et al. 2024
;
King 2024
)
. On the other hand, dynamical mass measurements yield black hole masses very similar to those inferred from virial estimates, although as of now, these are limited to one lensed black hole at
z=7.04

(
Juodžbalis et al. 2025b
)
. Further black hole bolometric luminosities are typically inferred from the H
{\alpha}
luminosity after applying dust corrections. This has been a particularly challenging issue for the class of black holes called “little red dots” that are selected on the basis of their “V-shaped” spectral energy distribution (blue in the ultraviolet and red in the optical), and compact morphology
(
Kocevski et al. 2023
;
Greene et al. 2024
;
Setton et al. 2025
)
. Spectroscopy of two such little red dots at redshifts of
z=3.1
and
4.46
have been used to infer bolometric luminosities that are lower by a factor of ten compared to those from local relations
(
Greene et al. 2026
)
. The stellar mass, too, remains a matter of the debate, especially so in systems that host black holes. Stellar masses are inferred by fitting spectral energy distributions (SEDs) to photometry - assumptions on the initial mass function (IMF), burstiness, and the relative contribution from the black hole and stellar components all play a role in the final inferred stellar mass. For example, changing the slope of the IMF alone is sufficient to change the inferred stellar mass by an order of magnitude
(
Wang et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Further confusing the picture, clustering estimates of 5 faint broad line H
{\alpha}
emitters have been used to infer host stellar masses that are about 40 times lower than those inferred from galaxy-only SED fits
(
Matthee et al. 2025
)
.

With such observational caveats in mind, if one proceeds to take the observations at face value, the prevalence of massive and “obese” early black holes (with respect to the host stellar masses) pose a crucial challenge to theoretical models. A number of theoretical approaches have been involved to explain such observations which range from: (i) episodic super-Eddington accretion on to light- or heavy-seeds
(
Schneider et al. 2023
;
Maiolino et al. 2024
;
Volonteri et al. 2025
;
Hu et al. 2025
)
although other works seem to rule out super-Eddington accretion as a solution, at least for little red dots
(
Sacchi and Bogdán 2025
)
; (ii) extremely efficient accretion- and merger-driven growth of intermediate-to-heavy seeds
(
Prole et al. 2024
;
Cáceres-Burgos et al. 2026
)
; (iii) such systems being the initial phases in the growth of heavy seeds
(
Natarajan et al. 2024
;
Pacucci et al. 2026
)
; (iv) selection biases resulting in observations preferentially selecting systems where the black hole outshines the host
(
Volonteri et al. 2023
;
Ziparo et al. 2026
)
; (v) baryons being inefficient in star formation
(
Maiolino et al. 2024
)
; or (vi) such objects indicating rapidly spinning black holes
(
Inayoshi and Ichikawa 2024
;
Cáceres-Burgos et al. 2026
)
. Such astrophysical solutions have recently been supplemented by theoretical models that look to “cosmological” primordial black holes (PBHs) as a possible solution
(
Dayal 2024
;
Dayal and Maiolino 2026
;
Ziparo et al. 2025
;
Prole et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
)
. A crucial difference between astrophysical seeding models and cosmological primordial black holes is that whilst the former form in halos generated from standard initial density perturbations, the latter can act as the seeds of structure formation, assembling their halos around themselves. Interestingly, primordial black hole models naturally predict extremely low metallicities for black hole hosts, in addition to yielding systems with high black hole-to-stellar mass ratios
(
Dayal 2024
;
Maiolino et al. 2025a
;
Dayal and Maiolino 2026
)
. This has made them an attractive alternative to explaining these puzzling early systems.

In this work, we aim at comparing the observational properties of astrophysical and cosmologically seeded black holes. In terms of astrophysically-seeded black holes, we explore light (
100\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) and heavy (
10^{3-5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) seeding models, that can grow both at Eddington and super-Eddington rates. For cosmologically-seeded PBHs, we explore a mass spectrum extending between
10^{0.5-6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
where we cap accretion at
0.25
times the Eddington rate, driven by black hole observations at
z\sim 10-10.4
. We start by detailing the models used in Sec.
2
. We discuss the global demographics of early black holes - including the redshift evolution of the black hole mass function and bolometric luminosity function - in Secs.
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively. We then discuss the black hole mass-stellar mass relation in Sec.
4.1
, the metallicity evolution of black holes hosts in Sec.
4.2
and the properties of their host halos in Sec.
4.3
before ending with conclusions in Sec.
5
.

We adopt a
{\mathord{\hbox{\char 3\relax}}}
CDM model with dark energy, dark matter and baryonic densities in units of the critical density as
{\mathord{\hbox{\char 10\relax}}}_{{\mathord{\hbox{\char 3\relax}}}}=0.673

{\mathord{\hbox{\char 10\relax}}}_{m}=0.315

2
The Theoretical model

In this work, we use two main pathways of black hole seeding and growth: (i) “astrophysical” - where halos are seeded with either heavy (
10^{3-5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) or light (
100\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) black hole seeds formed through astrophysical processes. The seeding and growth of such astrophysical black holes is followed using the
delphi
semi-analytic model
(
Dayal et al. 2019
;
Dayal et al. 2025
;
Cáceres-Burgos et al. 2026
)
; (ii) “cosmological” - where primordial black holes seeded (by e.g. phase-transitions) at the time of the Big Bang or inflation act as the seeds of structure formation, assembling their halos around themselves. We use the
phanes
analytic model
(
Dayal 2024
;
Dayal and Maiolino 2026
)
in this case. We briefly describe these models and interested readers are referred to the papers referenced here for complete details. All of the models used here are summarised in Table
1
.

2.2
PHANES analytic model for primordial black holes

PBHs generated soon after the Big Bang

(
Hawking 1971
;
Carr and Hawking 1974
;
Carr 2005
;
Carr and Green 2024
)
can act as the “seeds of structure formation”. In this scenario, the Coulomb effect of a single black hole generates an initial density fluctuation that can grow through gravitational instability
(
Hoyle and Narlikar 1966
;
Carr and Rees 1984
;
Carr and Silk 2018
, e.g.)
. Such PBHs have already shown to naturally yield high
M_{\mathrm{B}H}-M_{*}
ratios
(
Dayal 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Ziparo et al. 2025
)
and low-metallicity values for the host
(
Dayal and Maiolino 2026
)
that are generally incompatible with astrophysically-motivated black hole seeding mechanisms
(
Maiolino et al. 2025a
)
.

We use a power-law form of the PBH mass spectrum between
10^{0.5-6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
such that
{dn}/{dm}={\kappa}m^{-{\alpha}}

(
Carr 1975
;
Harada et al. 2016
)
, where we use a value of
{\alpha}=2
. The normalisation,
{\kappa}
, is determined using two of the highest redshift black hole candidates, UHZ1 and GHZ9 at
z\sim 10
and
10.4
, respectively, that are inferred to have a number density of
10^{-5.27}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}

(
Kovács et al. 2024
;
Bogdán et al. 2024
)
and a PBH seed mass of
10^{3-4-3.9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

(
Dayal 2024
)
. In this model, individual PBHs start by assembling dark matter linearly around themselves
(
Carr and Silk 2018
)
. Assuming this process starts at
z_{\mathrm{m}req}=3400
, the redshift of matter-radiation equality, by redshift
z_{\mathrm{h}}=34
, a PBH of mass
M_{\mathrm{P}BH}
can bind a halo mass
M_{h}
which is two orders of magnitude larger than the PBH mass. At this point, the halo comes to dominate the gravitational potential - we assume the halo
then starts growing non-linearly by accreting dark matter from the intergalactic medium (IGM). To estimate these growth rates, we use the average halo accretion rates from high-resolution N-body (dark matter only) simulations
(
Trac et al. 2015
)
.

In order to model baryons, we assume only halos with a baryonic over-density of
{\delta}_{b}=200
(with a corresponding halo mass of
M_{h}^{\mathrm{m}inb}
) can bind baryons at any redshift. Once a halo exceeds this value, it attains an initial gas mass of
M_{g}^{i}=({\mathord{\hbox{\char 10\relax}}}_{b}/{\mathord{\hbox{\char 10\relax}}}_{m})M_{h}
, a part of which can be accreted onto the black hole and fuel star formation. The mass accreted by a black hole in a given time-step is calculated as

(2)

Here,

is the free-fall timescale for the gas in the halo and we use a constant time-step of
{\mathord{\hbox{\char 1\relax}}}t=30
Myrs throughout this work. For the sake of simplicity, we limit our calculations to a black hole spin value of
s=0
; we refer interested readers to
Dayal and Maiolino 2026
for results on black holes with spin values of
s=+1
and
s=-1
. In terms of star formation, at any given time-step, the “effective” instantaneous star formation efficiency is calculated as

. I.e. the effective star formation efficiency is the minimum between the star formation efficiency that produces enough SNII energy to “unbind” the remainder of the gas (
f^{\mathrm{ej}}_{*}
), and the ratio between the time-step and the free-fall time. We account for the impact of both SNII and black hole feedback on the gas mass. We require weak feedback coupling of both black hole (
f_{\mathrm{B}H}^{w}\sim 10^{-3}
) and star formation energies (

3
Redshift evolution of global demographics of early black holes

In this section we start by discussing the redshift evolution of two of the key global demographics for early black holes, for all the models considered in this work: the black hole mass function and the bolometric luminosity function.

3.1
Redshift evolution of the black hole mass function

We start by discussing the redshift evolution of the black hole mass function (BHMF), shown in Fig.
1
, for all the models considered in this work. Globally, the BHMF shows an increase in both its amplitude and mass range with decreasing redshift as continually larger black holes assemble through mergers and accretion with cosmic time in the astrophysical scenarios, and accretion only in the PBH model.

In the
delphi
model, black holes are seeded starting at
z\sim 40
(when the first halos form) down to
z\sim 13
. The number densities of newly formed black hole seeds increase with decreasing redshift due to the continual formation of light halos. By
z\sim 15
, even the earliest black holes have only had about 200 Myr to grow. As a result, by this redshift all of the astrophysical seeding models show the early stages of black hole assembly. Both of the heavy seeding models (
hs-edd1
and
hs-edd5
) show very similar BHMFs that range between
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{3-6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
; the
hs-edd5
has a slight heavy tail extending out to
10^{6.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for the rarest objects with a number density of about
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. These BHMFs peak at a mass of about
10^{5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, falling off on either side. With their much lower initial black hole masses, light seeding models (
ls-edd1
and
ls-edd5
) show a peak at about
100\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and attain maximum masses
\sim 10^{3.5-3.75}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at a number density of
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. With their much longer histories and larger range of seed masses, even at
z\sim 15
, PBHs extend over 6 orders of magnitude in mass (
10^{2-8.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), exceeding the range shown by both the heavy and light seeding models. Their number densities exceed all of the astrophysical seeding models for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}10^{6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at these early epochs - they attain masses of about
10^{7.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for number densities of about
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
.

By
z\sim 10
, there is a clear difference in black hole growth in the Eddington-limited versus super-Eddington accretion models in both the light and heavy seeding scenarios, specially at the massive end. This is driven by the fact that black holes in halos above the critical halo limit (
M_{\mathrm{b}h}^{\mathrm{c}rit}
) can accrete at the minimum between
10\%
of the available gas mass, up to the allowed Eddington fraction. Most massive black holes accrete at
f_{\mathrm{E}dd}>1
in the super-Eddington models resulting in black holes as massive as
10^{8}\penalty\ (10^{9.5})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for the
ls-edd5
(
hs-edd5
) models at a number density of
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. At the massive end, in fact, heavy seeds accreting at the Eddington limit (
hs-edd1
model) yield a BHMF comparable to light seeds that can grow at super-Eddington rates (
ls-edd5
) - for a black hole mass of
10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, both these models predict two orders of magnitude fewer black holes than the maximal
hs-edd5
model. With light seeds limited by Eddington accretion, the
ls-edd1
model yields the lower limit to the BHMF, reaching maximum masses of only about
10^{5.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. PBHs predict a BHMF that lies between the Eddington-limited and super-Eddington models for heavy seeds for black hole masses larger than
10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. However, this model predicts two orders of magnitude larger number densities for intermediate-mass black holes (
10^{4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) compared to any astrophysical seeding model. We remind the reader that the number densities of PBHs have been determined by matching to the masses (
10^{7.5-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) and number densities (
\sim 10^{-5.5}\penalty\ {\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
) of the most massive black holes observed at
z\sim 10

(
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Kovács et al. 2024
)
.

By
z\sim 7
, growing black holes in the heavy-seed astrophysical models (
hs-edd1
and
hs-edd5
) generally accrete a fraction of the available gas mass (since this is lower than the fractional Eddington mass). This results in a convergence of the BHMFs - for example, for a black hole mass of
10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, the number densities predicted by these two models are only different by a factor 2. The black holes in these models grow to masses of about
10^{10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for number densities of about
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. While black holes with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
still grow slower in the
ls-edd5
model, for higher masses, that BHMF lies between the heavy Eddington-limited and super-Eddington models and reaches out to
10^{10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. Again, the
ls-edd1
model shows the lower limit to the growth of light black holes, only reaching maximal masses of about
10^{7.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for a number density of
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. Interestingly, the PBH BHMF shows a clear excess - by about two orders of magnitude - of intermediate-mass black holes at
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{3-4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
compared to any astrophysical seeding model; at
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{5-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, it predicts results that are very similar to the heavy seeding models. We also find both the heavy seeding (
hs-edd1
and
hs-edd5
) and PBH models to be in excellent agreement with the observationally-inferred BHMF at
z\sim 7

(
Fei et al. 2026
)
. Light seeding models predict a much flatter slope than the observations although the
ls-edd5
model is in accord with data for black holes with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}10^{6.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
; given the slower growth of light-seeded Eddington limited black holes, these observations can already be used to rule out the
ls-edd1
model. We briefly note that given our “heavy seeds” have a range of masses between
10^{3-5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, we do not find any specific peak in the BHMF; this is in contrast to the results of
Jeon et al. 2025
who find a well-defined peak at
10^{5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, possibly since all of their heavy seeds are initiated with this fixed mass.

The fact that an increasing fraction of black holes grow by accreting a fraction of the available gas mass yields a greater convergence between all of the astrophysical seeding models considered here by
z\sim 5
as shown in panel (d) of the same figure. For example for a black hole mass of
10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, the heavy-seed Eddington-limited and super-Eddington models and light-seed super-Eddington models show convergent results; the
ls-edd1
model yields a number density that is lower by only a factor 8. The BHMFs from these different models all extend between
10^{3.5-10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
- the most crucial difference lies at the low-mass end where the light seeds models predict a tail of black holes extending down to
10^{2}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
in increasing numbers. These are black holes starved of growth due to a lack of gas in the low-mass halos they reside in. The PBH model persists in predicting an over-abundance of intermediate-mass black holes (with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), by about 3 orders of magnitude, compared to any of the astrophysical seeding models. Whilst the heavy (light) seeding models are in reasonable agreement with the data for black holes with
10^{4.5-5.5}\penalty\ (10^{6-7})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, they all predict the existence of massive black holes (
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}10^{7.5-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) that have yet to be observed. The PBH model yields the closet-match to the bulk of the black holes observed with the JWST, and the slope for the most massive black holes (
\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}10^{8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) observed by combining data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Canada-France High-z Quasar Survey
(
Willott et al. 2010
)
.

Globally, as of now, the only model that can be ruled out is the is the
ls-edd1
model (light seeds accreting at the Eddington-limit) since it under-predicts the data at
z\sim 7
. We note observations at
z\sim 4.5-7
are probing BHMF down to
10^{5.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and are expected, with further lensing observations, to go down to even lower masses. These would provide a definite means of, at least, distinguishing between astrophysical and cosmological seeding models given the over-abundance of the latter at low masses (
\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) at
z\sim 5-10
.

3.2
Redshift evolution of the bolometric luminosity function (BLF)

We now discuss the redshift evolution of the bolometric luminosity function, the results of which are shown in Fig.
2
. In our work, in order to compare with observations, the bolometric luminosity of a black hole (with
f_{\mathrm{E}dd}>0.01
) is calculated as
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}={\epsilon}_{r}\,{\mathord{\hbox{\char 1\relax}}}\mathrm{M}_{BH},c^{2}{\mathord{\hbox{\char 1\relax}}}t^{-1}
, where
c
is the speed of light and
{\mathord{\hbox{\char 1\relax}}}\mathrm{M}_{BH}
is the increase in the black hole mass in the time-step of
{\mathord{\hbox{\char 1\relax}}}t={\mathrm{3}0\penalty\ Myr}
. For values of
f_{\mathrm{E}dd}<0.01
, the bulk of the thermal energy is carried into the black hole rather than being radiated
(
Narayan and Yi 1994
;
Yuan and Narayan 2014
)
. As a result, we consider such inefficiently accreting black holes to produce no luminosity - this has no bearing on our results though
(
Cáceres-Burgos et al. 2026
, for a discussion see)
. With these calculations, at
z\sim 15
, the BLF from putative heavy (light) seeding models only extends to
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{44-45}\penalty\ (\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{42})\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
for a number density corresponding to
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. Given their overall larger masses and accretion rates, the PBH BLF dominates at all
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{40-46.5}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
at this redshift and shows bolomteric luminosities as high as
10^{45.5}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
for a number density of
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. By
z\sim 10
, the growth of black hole seeds leads to a corresponding increase in the BLF: while heavy seeding models converge for
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{44}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
, the fast growth of massive seeds in super-Eddington model (
hs-edd5
) results in it yielding the upper limit to the BLF. For a number density of
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
, this model predicts
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{47.5}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
compared to the 30-times lower value of
10^{46}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
in the
hs-edd1
model. Accreting at super-Eddington rates, black holes in the
ls-edd5
model show a very similar BLF compared to the
hs-edd1
model, specially at the bright-end. With Eddington-limited growth onto light seeds, the
ls-edd1
model yields the lower limit to the BLF with maximum values as low as
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{43}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
for
10^{-8}{\mathrm{c}Mpc^{-3}}
. With its over-abundance of intermediate mass black holes, the PBH BLF exceeds the amplitude of any of the astrophysical seeding models: for
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{43}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
, the PBH model predicts two orders of magnitude more black holes compared to any of the astrophysical seeding models. We also compare to the lower-limit on the BLF from two sources (UHZ1 and GHZ9) inferred at this redshift
(
Kovács et al. 2024
)
. As seen, the heavy seeding, super-Eddington accretion model (
hs-edd5
) is the only one in accord with this data point. Although in agreement with the mass estimates, the PBH model lies slightly below this point. This is because observations assume Eddington-limited accretion to convert between the black hole mass and its bolometric luminosity - however, PBHs accrete at sub-Eddington limits (
f_{\mathrm{E}dd}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.25
) in our model. Confirmation of such high number densities of luminous black holes at these early epochs would allow ruling out all light-seeding models, in addition to Eddington-limited heavy-seeding ones.

4
Physical properties of early black holes and their hosts

We now discuss the detailed physical relations between black holes and their hosts, in the different models studied in this work, in what follows.

Relation between black holes and host stellar masses

We start by noting a positive correlation between the black hole, stellar and halo masses, irrespective of the model considered. A key difference between these models, however, is that given the co-evolution of the black hole and its halo in PBH models, black holes of a given mass typically occupy halos that are less massive compared to any of the astrophysical seeding models. For example, black holes of
10^{6-8}\penalty\ (10^{8-9})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
occupy halos with
M_{h}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{9}\penalty\ (10^{9-10})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

.

4.2
The metallicity evolution of early black holes

We now discuss the relation between the gas-phase metallicity of the host and the black hole mass, colour-coded as a function of the stellar mass, in Fig.
4
. Recent observations have yielded metallicities of 3 black hole hosts between
z\sim 7-10

(
Maiolino et al. 2025a
;
Tripodi et al. 2025
;
Napolitano et al. 2025
)
. All of these works use different methodologies to infer the metallicity:
Maiolino et al. 2025a
infer an extremely low value of
4\times 10^{-3}\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
based on the weakness of the [OIII]5007 emission line relative to the narrow H
{\beta}
emission.
Napolitano et al. 2025
use Ne3O2 ratio-based calibrations to derive a value
\sim 0.01-0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
and
Tripodi et al. 2025
use direct electron temperature methods to derive
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
.

Theoretically, the gas-phase metallicity scales with the stellar mass - and hence the black hole mass given the
\mathrm{M}_{BH}-M_{*}
relation discussed in Sec.
4.1
above; the slope of the
Z-\mathrm{M}_{BH}
relation naturally depends on the exact model considered. At
z\sim 10
, in the
hs-edd1
model, light black holes (
\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) are hosted in putative galaxies (
M_{*}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) which have low values of metallicity (
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.03\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
). This is because the small amount of metals produced by stars are either astrated into further star formation/black hole accretion or lost due to feedback. The metallicity value increases only very slightly with black hole mass - this is because the metals produced by star formation are balanced by those lost through astration and outflows. In this model, the maximum metallicity value is about
0.05\penalty\ \mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{8-9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
hosted in galaxies with
M_{*}\sim 10^{8-10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. Black holes naturally build mass faster in the
hs-edd5
model - as a result this model shows a larger scatter in the
\mathrm{M}_{BH}-Z
relation as compared to the
hs-edd1
model. Here too, the metallicity in light black hole systems (
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}10^{6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) is less than a few percent of the solar value and shows a peak at
\sim 0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for
\sim 10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
back holes hosted in
M_{*}\sim 10^{7-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
systems. It settles back to about
0.03\penalty\ \mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for more massive black holes given metal loss in astration and feedback. With the smallest mass range for black hole growth, the
ls-edd1
model shows
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.02\penalty\ \mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
. While light seeds grow up to
10^{9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
allowing super-Eddington accretion, their metallicity trend is rather flat for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{5-9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
showing
Z\sim 0.01\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
. Finally, given their longest assembly histories and low-accretion and star formation rates, PBHs are as metal enriched as
0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
even at
z\sim 10
. We also compare these results to the observations from
Tripodi et al. 2025
and
Napolitano et al. 2025
.
Only the
hs-edd1
and
ls-edd5
models yield black hole masses, stellar masses and metallicities in agreement with the lower metallicity value (
0.01\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
) inferred by
Napolitano et al. 2025
. However, none of the astrophysical seeding models yield the right combination of black hole mass, stellar mass and metallicity compared to
Tripodi et al. 2025
: while the
hs-edd1
,
hs-edd5
and
ls-edd5
models show metallicity and black hole masses in agreement with the observations, they predicts a lower stellar mass (
10^{7-9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) as compared to the observations, where
M_{*}\sim 10^{9-10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. The PBH model is the
only
one that reproduces the observations along all three axes, at least compared to
Tripodi et al. 2025
. Even this model predicts a slightly different stellar mass (
10^{9-10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) compared to the value of
10^{8-9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
obtained by
Napolitano et al. 2025
; these authors however caution the stellar mass can vary by a factor 2 depending on the black hole contribution to the SED.

The growth of both the black hole and stellar components by
z\sim 7
results in a corresponding build-up of the metal mass for all models. The metallicity essentially scales with the black hole mass for the
hs-edd1
,
hs-edd5
and
ls-edd5
models, albeit with varying degrees of scatter. In the heavy seeding models, systems with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
(
M_{*}\sim 10^{7-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) show
Z\sim 0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
, increasing to
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}0.5\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
(
M_{*}\sim 10^{8-9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
). Given the fast growth of heavy seeds, the
hs-edd5
model shows the largest scatter in the black hole mass-metallicity relation. The metallicity trend is again quite flat for the
ls-edd1
model which shows
Z\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}\;$}0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for all black hole masses
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{2-8.5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. The
ls-edd5
model yields similar results to the
hs-edd1
model, with
Z\sim 0.1\penalty\ (\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}0.5)\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{7}\penalty\ (10^{8})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. Finally, the PBH model shows metallicity values that increase from about
0.03-0.1\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
as the black hole mass increases from
10^{7}-10^{9}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
with associated values of
M_{*}\sim 10^{9-10}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. At this redshift, we compare to the observations from
Maiolino et al. 2025a
who infer an upper limit of
0.01\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
to the metallicity for a system with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{7.1}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and
M_{*}\sim 10^{7.1-8}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. As seen, the only two models that are in agreement along all three axes are
hs-edd1
and the PBH model.

By
z\sim 5
, all astrophysical seeding models show similar black hole mass-metallicity relations where the metallicity has a value of
0.1\penalty\ (\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}0.5)\mathrm{Z}_{\odot}
for
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{7}\penalty\ (10^{8})\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

for

Overall, only the
hs-edd1
and PBH models are in broad accord with the combinations of metallicity, black hole mass and stellar mass available at

reproduce all of the observed data points simultaneously.

4.3
The host halos of early black holes

As seen from Sec.
4.1
above, one of the strongest differences between astrophysical and cosmological black hole seeding models lies in the halo masses they inhabit. We now compare the black hole-stellar mass ratio as a function of the underlying host halo mass, for different bolometric luminosity limits, as shown in Fig.
5
. Our key aim is to look for observational imprints that can help distinguish between the different seeding and growth models studied in this work.

We select systems with a ratio of
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}>0.1
and
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{44-46}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
as a
gedankenexperiment
. We start by noting that, given the suppressed growth of Eddington-limited light seeds, black holes in the
ls-edd1
model never fulfil these requirements. We also note that in astrophysical seeding models, the black hole to stellar mass ratio increases with increasing host halo mass. This is driven by the deeper potentials and larger gas masses in increasingly massive halos that allow copious amounts of star formation, and accretion onto the black hole. However, the black hole to stellar mass ratio
decreases
with increasing halo mass in PBH models where the black hole pre-dates both the halo and stellar mass. Light PBHs are able to assemble light halos that are inefficient at star formation, pushing up the
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}
ratio. As PBHs grow larger halos around themselves, these can support increasing amounts of star formation - the resulting large stellar masses lead to a decrease in the
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}
value.

5
Conclusions and discussion

In this work, we have compared the observational properties of astrophysically- and cosmologically-seeded black holes in the first billion years of the Universe. We use the
delphi
semi-analytic model for light (
\sim 100\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) and heavy (
\sim 10^{3-5}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) astrophysical seeds, and the
phanes
analytic model for primordial black holes (PBHs;
\sim 10^{0.5-6}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
). Our key findings are as follows:

•

All models predict a black hole mass function that grows both in amplitude and mass range with decreasing redshift as larger black holes assemble through accretion and mergers in the astrophysical models, and accretion alone in the PBH scenario. PBHs predict an overabundance of intermediate-mass black holes (
\sim 10^{3-4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) by 2-3 orders of magnitude relative to astrophysical models at
\sim 5-10
, whilst remaining consistent with observations at the massive end. The
ls-edd1
model (light Eddington-limited seeds) is the only astrophysical model that can be ruled out given its under-prediction of the observed BHMF at
z\sim 7
. We caution the PBH mass spectrum has been calibrated using two black holes at
z\sim 10-10.4
. Estimates of the PBH mass spectrum from first principles are crucially required in order to test these cosmological scenarios.

•

The bolometric luminosity function too builds up with time as increasingly larger black holes assemble. At
z\sim 10
, only the heavy-seed super-Eddington model (
hs-edd5
) and the PBH model are consistent with the observational lower limits from UHZ1 and GHZ9, providing a strong test of seeding and growth models at these early epochs. By
z\sim 5-7
, all of the models except Eddington limited light seeds (
ls-edd1
) reproduce the observed bolometric luminosity functions within uncertainties. We caution bolometric luminosity corrections (used to convert Balmer line luminosities to a bolometric luminosity) are under revision, given the increasing wavelength ranges being probed for early black holes. Statistical samples of complete black hole SEDs will be crucial in determining the black hole mass/Balmer line-bolometric corrections at these early epochs.

•

The black hole mass scales with both the stellar mass and halo mass for all models at
z\sim 5-10
; the
ls-edd5
and PBH models show the steepest and shallowest slopes of the black hole mass-stellar mass relation, respectively. The high black hole mass to stellar mass ratios (
\sim 0.3-1
) inferred from JWST at
z\sim 5-10
pose a challenge for black hole models: light seeding models are unable to explain the presence of systems with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim M_{*}
at
z\sim 10
, even in the super-Eddington case. While the heavy seeding Eddington-limited model can reproduce the bulk of the objects, it can not explain the existence of black holes with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}\sim 10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
that are over-massive with respect to their hosts, with
M_{*}<10^{7}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, at
z\sim 7-10
. Overall, only the
hs-edd5
and PBH models are in accord with the data at all
z\sim 5-10
. We also note the
lm-edd1
and PBH models show the largest and smallest halo mass-to-black hole mass ratios, respectively.

•

Metallicity scales with both black hole and stellar masses. Due to their lowest black hole to halo mass relations, galaxies in PBH models are the most affected by feedback and therefore show the lowest metallicity values for a given black hole mass, or stellar mass at
z\sim 5-7
. Indeed, only the PBH model is consistent with
all three axes
of the black hole mass-stellar mass-metallicity relation observed by
Tripodi et al. 2025
and
Maiolino et al. 2025a
. However, it yields a slightly higher stellar mass than that inferred for the object observed by
Napolitano et al. 2025
which is in better agreement with the
hs-edd1
model. Globally, only the
hs-edd1
and PBH models are in broad agreement with the metallicity relations at
z\sim 5-10
.

•

PBH-seeded black holes inhabit systematically lower-mass halos than astrophysically-seeded ones of equivalent mass. The PBH model uniquely predicts a negative
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}
trend with halo mass, in contrast to all astrophysical models where the value of
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}
increases with halo mass. At
z\sim 7
, selecting systems with
\mathrm{M}_{BH}/M_{*}>0.1
and
L_{\mathrm{B}ol}\sim 10^{44-46}\mathrm{e}rg\penalty\ s^{-1}
that show a
negative
black hole to stellar mass ratio and reside in
10^{9-11}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
halos can be used as possible indicators of PBH seeding models.

There are a number of caveats that we now reiterate. The first concerns observationally-inferred quantities including the black hole mass, stellar mass, and metallicities, pinning down which urgently require statistical samples of black hole SEDs and host metallicities. In terms of theoretical assumptions, black holes in the first billion years probably arise from a mix of astrophysical (light and heavy) seeds, and cosmological seeds, complicating the interpretations presented here. We also caution that the mass spectrum of PBHs remains an open problem. We have also assumed super-Eddington accretion rates purely based on the available gas mass. As shown by a number of works, given that the Eddington limit assumes spherical accretion, super-Eddington accretion rates are possible, at least for extended periods, via geometrically thick disk accretion modes
(
Davis and Tchekhovskoy 2020
;
Safarzadeh and Haiman 2020
)
which needs to be explored further in semi-analytic models. In this work, we have also limited ourselves to non-spinning black holes; we aim to expand our calculations to include spinning systems in the future. We have assumed coupling between black hole feedback and all of the gas content of the halo, an assumption we hope to refine in future works. Finally, it is important to note that whilst the clustering of halos formed from primordial density perturbations is well known, the clustering of structures seeded by PBHs critically depends on their exact formation mechanism
(
Matsubara et al. 2019
;
Animali and Vennin 2024
, e.g.)
and will likely be quite different from that in “standard” structure formation.

Overall, the only model that can be conclusively ruled out as of now is one where all black holes are seeded by light seeds that can accrete at Eddington-limited rates. In general, the
hs-edd5
and PBH models are most consistent with the lower limits on the bolometric luminosity function at
z\sim 10
, while the
hs-edd1
and PBH models are in better agreement with the metallicities inferred for early systems. Future observations will be crucial in distinguishing between astrophysical and cosmological seeding models. These include: (i) an over-abundance of low-mass black holes
\sim 10^{3-4}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
in the black hole mass function using gravitational lensing; (ii) extremely low metallicities for systems with high black hole to stellar mass ratios; (iii) negative black hole to stellar mass ratios as a function of the halo mass. Clustering signals via forthcoming
Roman
and
Euclid
observations will be crucial in obtaining hints on the halo mass; and (iv) deep X-ray observations with e.g.
NewAthena
to verify the black hole nature of these early systems.
