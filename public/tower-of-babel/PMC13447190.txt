Prognostic Value of Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring in Patients Admitted With Acute Decompensated Heart Failure: A Prospective Observational Study From a Tertiary Referral Centre
Authors: Tanmay Boob, Pooja Vyas, Kewal Kanabar, Krutika Patel, Ravina Modi, Mohit Khare, Iva Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesHeart failure (HF) remains a major cause of morbidity and frequent readmissions in India. Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) offers dynamic evaluation of blood pressure (BP) patterns, BP variability and morning blood pressure surge (MBPS) which may provide prognostic value in patients with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF).MethodsA prospective observational study enrolling 300 patients admitted with ADHF. After stabilization and prior to discharge, all patients underwent ABPM and were categorized based on dipper patterns. Clinical outcomes, including mortality and re-hospitalization at 3 months, were assessed. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and multivariable Cox regression.ResultsThe mean age of the cohort was 60.2 years; 68% were male. Most patients had HF with reduced ejection fraction (84%), and 57% were hypertensive. Dipper patterns were distributed: normal dipper 51%, non-dipper 36%, reverse dipper 11%, and extreme dipper 2%. At 3 months, mortality was 5.3% and re-hospitalization in 44% of patients. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed increased mortality in reverse dippers, highest average real variability percentile, and low/high MBPS groups (p<0.0001). Multivariable Cox regression identified extreme dipper (hazard ratio [HR], 9.28; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.85–46.49; p=0.007), reverse dipper (HR, 7.53; 95% CI, 2.47–22.10; p=0.01), low systolic BP (HR, 3.70; 95% CI, 1.22–11.22; p=0.02) associated to mortality.ConclusionsABPM is a practical tool for risk stratification in HF. Abnormal nocturnal BP dipper patterns and increased BP variability were associated with increased adverse short-term outcomes in ADHF. Discharge ABPM may aid early risk stratification, while other circadian BP abnormalities primarily reflect disease severity rather than independent risk.Keywords: Heart failure, Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, Mortality, India, Prognosis

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure (HF) remains one of the most significant contributors to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. In India, the burden is increasing rapidly due to rising prevalence of risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and ischemic heart disease.1) While significant advances have been made in pharmacologic and device-based therapies, accurate prognostication in HF patients is essential for optimizing management and resource allocation.

This study investigates the prognostic value of ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) in patients hospitalized with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF). We aimed to identify associations between ABPM assessment of circadian blood pressure (BP), BP variability, morning blood pressure surge (MBPS) and clinical outcomes, including mortality and rehospitalisation at 3 months. Evidence linking these ABPM-derived parameters with short-term outcomes in HF remains limited.

Compensatory neurohormonal changes in HF alter BP and its pattern as well as heart rate. These changes lead to poor control of hypertension, which can cause the decompensation of HF. Unlike traditional BP measurements, ABPM provides a comprehensive 24-hour assessment of circadian BP behaviour, including nocturnal dipping patterns, BP variability, nocturnal hypertension, and MBPS parameters that reflect autonomic and neurohormonal regulation.2) This method allows healthcare providers to capture variations in BP that occur during daily activities, sleep, and exercise, offering a more nuanced understanding of a patient’s hemodynamic status. In HF management, maintaining optimal BP is crucial. Both hypertension and hypotension can exacerbate HF symptoms and lead to adverse outcomes.

As healthcare continues to embrace technology and personalized approaches, ABPM is poised to play an increasingly integral role in optimizing HF care and fostering better patient engagement in managing their health.3)

ABPM provides a more comprehensive picture of BP dynamics compared to office measurements.4,5) It captures diurnal variations, detects masked or white-coat hypertension, and allows classification of circadian BP patterns (normal dipper, non-dipper, extreme dipper, and reverse dipper). These patterns have prognostic implications in hypertensive patients and are increasingly being explored in HF.6)

METHODS

Study design and population

This prospective observational study was conducted at tertiary care cardiac Institute over two years (July 2022–July 2024), India. All patients who were hospitalized for ADHF underwent ABPM study after stabilization prior to discharge.

The present study was approved by institutional ethics committee of the U. N. Mehta Institute of Cardiology and Research Centre (UNMICRC/CARDIO/2022/11). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2024.

Patients hospitalized with ADHF and those who were hemodynamically stable prior to discharge were included in the study. Definition of clinical stabilization: prior to ABPM, all patients were required to meet strict criteria for clinical stabilization, defined as: (1) resolution of resting dyspnoea and orthopnoea (New York Heart Association class return to baseline or Class II); (2) discontinuation of all intravenous diuretics, inotropes, and vasodilators for at least 24 hours; (3) transition to stable oral optimal medical therapy (OMT); and (4) absence of clinical signs of congestion (e.g., elevated jugular venous pressure, rales). Patients who had peripheral arterial occlusive disease and incomplete ABPM data were excluded from the study. In addition, individuals who were receiving inotropic support, intravenous diuretic infusion, or requiring supplemental oxygen continuously even at the time of discharge (failure to wean off) were excluded from the study. This exclusion was necessary to ensure the technical validity of ABPM, which requires an ambulatory setting free from intravenous tethers.

Data collection

Baseline demographic, clinical, and echocardiographic data were recorded for all participants. ABPM was conducted using a validated device over a 24-hour period. Based on the nocturnal decline in systolic blood pressure (SBP), patients were classified into four dipping patterns. A normal dipper pattern was defined as a 10–20% reduction in night-time SBP, whereas a non-dipper pattern was identified when the reduction was less than 10%. Patients showing an increase in nocturnal SBP were categorized as reverse dippers, and those with a reduction greater than 20% were classified as extreme dippers. In ABPM, the MBPS was defined as the difference between morning BP (measured 2 hours after awakening) and the lowest night-time BP and BP variability can be defined as the average variation of BP throughout the day.7) MBPS and average real variability (ARV) were categorized using specific percentile cut-offs at the <25th (low), 25–90th (intermediate), and >90th percentiles defined as high MBPS or high ARV. Our study had limited number of mortality events, we opted simple and clinically interpretable classification using the <25th and >90th percentile thresholds to identify low and markedly elevated phenotypes while preserving statistical stability.

According to the World Health Organization/International Society and Federation of Cardiology definition of ischemic cardiomyopathy was diagnosed in patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of less than 40% resulting from obstructive or recanalized coronary artery disease. Non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy (DCMP) was defined by the presence of a fractional shortening of less than 25% or an ejection fraction (EF) below 40% (both >2 standard deviation [SD]), along with a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter greater than 117% of the predicted value (112% corrected for age and body surface area), after excluding any identifiable cause of myocardial disease. Peripartum cardiomyopathy was defined as new-onset maternal HF with systolic dysfunction (LVEF <45%) occurring in the last month of pregnancy or within the first five months postpartum. HF phenotypes were classified according to LVEF as follows: heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; LVEF ≤40%), heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF; LVEF 41–49%), and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF; LVEF ≥50%). Etiological classification, including ischemic cardiomyopathy, DCMP, hypertensive HF, and restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCMP), was performed independently of EF-based phenotype classification. Worsening HF was characterized by deterioration of signs and symptoms in a patient with previously chronic and stable HF, despite background therapy, necessitating urgent escalation of treatment, such as hospitalization, emergency department visit, or outpatient intravenous diuretic therapy.

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients were followed for 3 months post-discharge. The primary endpoint of the study was all-cause mortality. Secondary endpoints included (1) rehospitalization for worsening HF and (2) cerebrovascular events (non-fatal stroke or transient ischemic attack).

Data availability

The data associated with the paper are not publicly available but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Data are located in controlled data storage at our institute.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). MedCalc® version 23 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium) was used for survival analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and categorical variables as percentages. Comparisons between survivors and non-survivors were made using t-tests and χ2 tests. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox-proportional hazards regression were used to evaluate the predictors of all-cause mortality. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patients with high ARV-SBP (>17.6 mmHg) and high ARV-DBP (>16 mmHg) had markedly reduced survival, with 3-month survival rates of 54.8% and 57.3%, respectively (Figure 1B and C). In adjusted, 24-hour SBP variability (ARV): ARV showed a strong unadjusted prognostic significance; however, its effect was attenuated after multivariate adjustment, suggesting confounding by dipping status and baseline BP (hazard ratio [HR], 1.05; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.61–1.79; p=0.86; and HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 0.89–2.58; p=0.13). Severe left ventricular dysfunction demonstrated an unstable and non-significant effect with extremely wide CIs reflecting sparse events rather than absence of prognostic relevance. Due to small number of events, we minimized overfitting by restricting the multivariable model of clinically relevant predictors, selected based on univariate significance and biological plausibility, and by avoiding collinearity among ABPM variables.

DISCUSSION

This prospective study reinforces and extends existing evidence supporting the prognostic value of ABPM in patients hospitalized with ADHF, particularly during the early post-discharge vulnerable phase. We observed that abnormal circadian BP patterns—particularly reverse and extreme dipping—were associated with increased short-term mortality and re-hospitalization. These findings are consistent with earlier studies that have reported non-dipping and reverse dipping as markers of increased sympathetic activity and autonomic dysfunction in HF patients.1)

The pathophysiological basis for these associations lies in the disrupted autonomic regulation common in advanced HF. Normally, nocturnal dipping reflects healthy autonomic balance with reduced sympathetic tone at night. In contrast, reverse dipping has been linked to increased night time sympathetic activity, endothelial dysfunction, and heightened renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation—all of which contribute to myocardial stress, impaired diastolic function, and adverse remodelling. Extreme dipping, although classically considered protective in hypertension, may paradoxically signal underlying frailty, over diuresis, or baroreflex sensitivity impairment in HF patients, leading to nocturnal hypoperfusion and end-organ ischemia. Most previous studies evaluating ABPM in HF have focused on chronic, stable populations. Our study specifically examined patients during the acute-to-chronic transition phase at hospital discharge- a period recognized as the “vulnerable phase,” characterized by heightened autonomic instability and a markedly increased risk of early adverse events, yet data on ABPM’s utility specifically at this timepoint is scarce in the Indian population. Our findings align with Kotti et al.1) and other study, who demonstrated the prognostic adverse impact of non-dipping and reverse-dipping patterns in admitted stable HF cases. However, their study excluded cases with HFpEF, our cohort included the full spectrum of HF phenotypes and including HFpEF a substantially larger sample size, enhancing the generalizability of our results.

Our finding of low daytime SBP as a predictor of mortality highlights the hemodynamic instability of these patients. It reflects not only poor cardiac output and poor perfusion but also the presence of advanced disease requiring cautious therapeutic modulation. Li et al.6) demonstrated that ambulatory BP monitoring can uncover masked systolic hypotension in patients with HF, while Zhang et al.8) reported that visit-to-visit BP variability is significantly associated with adverse outcomes in HFpEF.

ABPM measurement at discharge captures acute hemodynamic transitions distinct from chronic stable HF. This transitional period—marked by resolving congestion, recent diuresis, and medication titration—creates transient autonomic instability. Notably, reverse-dipping patterns persisted despite clinical euvolemia and OMT, indicating failure of autonomic recovery and RAAS activation rather than a fixed trait. This residual abnormality is clinically significant: it identified patients at highest risk for short-term mortality, arrhythmias and readmission. Thus, discharge ABPM serves as a functional assessment of hemodynamic stability and autonomic readiness, directly informing post-discharge risk and management intensity. Reverse dipping may indicate sympathetic over activity, blunted baroreceptor response, and higher afterload during diastole—factors contributing to adverse remodelling and progression of HF. Our results align with previous studies that suggest reverse dipping reflects autonomic imbalance, increased sympathetic tone at night, and poor cardiovascular outcomes. A study by Kario et al.9) emphasized that reverse dipping is linked to increased nocturnal sympathetic activity, endothelial dysfunction, and poor outcomes in hypertensive and HF populations. Similarly, Solomon et al.10) showed that both reverse and extreme dipping patterns were associated with higher all-cause mortality and HF readmissions in a multicentre HF cohort. Eguchi et al.11) also noted that extreme dipping could represent abnormal autonomic responses in high-risk cardiovascular patients, especially in those with underlying HF.11) These findings are similar to our findings.

An additional finding of our study we discovered an important difference between ambulatory BP indices in their prognostic behaviour. In survival analysis, higher systolic and diastolic ARV showed an association with increased mortality; however, these associations diminished after adjusting for confounding factors in a multi-variable analysis. One finding that was independent of all other variables was that individuals who had a lower MBPS had a higher risk of mortality. This finding indicates that impaired circadian rhythm of BP is the critical predictor of mortality in HF. Unlike in hypertension and the general population, where an exaggerated morning surge is harmful, a preserved morning surge in stabilized HF may reflect intact autonomic responsiveness and cardiovascular reserve. Conversely, a blunted or absent surge likely represents an advance level of myocardial dysfunction, sympathetic exhaustion and thus indicates a more devastating prognosis. Elevated ARV, appears to reflects hemodynamic instability closely linked to short-term BP fluctuations that are strongly correlated with disease severity, dipping pattern, and baseline BP. These findings were supported by Taş et al.12) and Komori et al.13) and Israel et al.14) ABPM detected masked and nocturnal hypertension in patients who appeared normotensive during routine assessment, emphasizing its value in uncovering clinically relevant circadian abnormalities. These observations are supported by prior work from Sarafidis et al.15) and Sogunuru et al.,7) supports this, showing that ABPM identifies nocturnal hypertension and variability that correlate with worsening HF and hospitalisations.

Beyond circadian BP patterns, our study reaffirmed known predictors of poor outcomes in HF: low LVEF, older age, low SBP on admission, presence of diabetes, and hypertension. These factors have consistently been shown in large registries such as Acute Decompensated Heart Failure National Registry and the European Society of Cardiology-HF to predict adverse prognosis in HF.16,17) Clinically, these results suggest that discharge ABPM may aid in early post-discharge risk stratification. Identification of reverse or extreme dipping patterns and impaired morning surge could help guide closer outpatient monitoring and individualized therapeutic strategies. However, given the observational design and modest sample size, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, multicentre cohorts before routine implementation can be recommended.

This study has some limitations: (1) First, this was a single-centre study, and therefore the findings may not be generalizable to other populations. (2) The follow-up period was relatively short (3 months), and ABPM was performed only once prior to discharge, precluding assessment of dynamic circadian recovery. (3) Limited number of events in small population affected the stability and precision of multivariate estimates. External validation in larger, multi-centre populations with longer follow-up and serial ABPM assessments is warranted.

In this prospective observational study, we found that ABPM provides critical prognostic information in hospitalized patients with ADHF. Abnormal nocturnal BP dipper patterns, low daytime SBP were associated with short-term mortality and adverse clinical outcomes. Increased BP variability further identified high-risk patients, whereas a preserved MBPS appear to reflect advanced disease severity rather than independent risk factors. These findings suggest that discharge-phase ABPM may aid in early risk stratification during the vulnerable transition period. However, given the single-centre design, relatively short follow-up duration, and absence of serial ABPM assessments or reproducibility evaluations, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further large, multi-centric studies and meta-analyses are warranted to clarify the role of ABPM in discharge planning and personalized HF management.
