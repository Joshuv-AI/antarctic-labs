Assessment of knowledge and attitudes of pregnant women toward pregnancy-related risk factors in Mosul City, Iraq
Authors: Shukur A. Mahmood, Ehab Mohammed Shakir, Nadia A. Jamal, Fahad M. Mohammed Taqi, Atheer A. Rashad, Nasir M. Younis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Reducing negative pregnancy outcomes depends on maternal knowledge of pregnancy-related risk factors. Developing efficient maternal health interventions in post-conflict Mosul, Iraq, where the healthcare system has been significantly compromised, will depend on assessing pregnant women’s knowledge and attitudes regarding pregnancy risks. This study sought to evaluate knowledge and attitudes about pregnancy-related risk factors among pregnant women in Mosul City, Iraq, as well as to identify the socio-demographic factors influencing this knowledge.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted from January to April 2024 in Mosul City, Iraq. From January to April 2024, 420 pregnant women attending antenatal clinics in Mosul participated. Socio-demographic information was gathered using a standardized questionnaire; knowledge and attitudes regarding pregnancy risk factors were also evaluated using this tool. Knowledge scores were calculated and analyzed in relation to demographic variables using appropriate statistical tests.RESULTS:The mean knowledge score was 15.63 ± 5.72 out of a possible 30 points, indicating moderate knowledge levels. Only 23.8% of participants demonstrated good knowledge. Higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with higher education level (P < 0.001), urban residence (P = 0.003), higher income (P = 0.001), and receiving antenatal care in private facilities (P = 0.002). Most women recognized risks associated with hypertension (76.4%) and diabetes (72.9%), but fewer recognized the significance of fetal movement changes (42.3%) and vaginal bleeding (53.1%). While 87.3% of participants had positive attitudes toward seeking medical care for complications, 47.6% held misconceptions about culturally rooted risk factors, such as dietary taboos and traditional remedies.CONCLUSION:Pregnant women in Mosul demonstrated moderate knowledge of pregnancy-related risk factors with significant educational and socioeconomic disparities.

Introduction

Pregnancy and delivery, though natural processes, have inherent risks that could lead to undesirable outcomes for newborns and mothers. It has been estimated by the World Health Organization that in 2017, around 295,000 women lost their lives during pregnancy or delivery, of which 94% occurred in low- and middle-income countries.[1] Early identification of danger signals and timely medical intervention can prevent the majority of deaths. Women’s understanding of risk factors for pregnancy and warning signs needing immediate medical treatment is crucial to the prevention of maternal deaths.[2] Sanctions, wars, and political turmoil have led to the Iraqi healthcare system facing unprecedented crises over the last few decades. During the 2014–2017 war, the second-largest city in Iraq, Mosul, was hit by the destruction of its healthcare infrastructure.[3] Provision of maternal health care has been heavily affected by the destruction of healthcare facilities, lack of healthcare workers, and interruption of healthcare services. Recent studies have shown that Iraq still has a very high rate of maternal mortality of approximately 79 deaths per 100,000 live births; this rate would likely be higher in conflict-ridden regions like Mosul.[4] Promoting positive health-seeking behaviors and lowering negative pregnancy outcomes depend much on maternal health literacy, especially knowledge of pregnancy risk factors. Several studies from various nations have linked women’s awareness of obstetric danger signs to better birth preparedness, complication readiness, and enhanced maternal and neonatal outcomes.[56] Research, meanwhile, has revealed that knowledge levels differ greatly among various groups and are shaped by variables including education, socioeconomic status, location of residence, and quality of antenatal care.[7] Formulating successful maternal health interventions in post-conflict Mosul must be guided by the attitudes and knowledge of pregnant women regarding pregnancy-related risk factors. Certain obstacles to maternal health care-seeking behaviors and knowledge may have resulted from the disruption of health services, population displacement, and exposure to psychological trauma among the population.[8] Furthermore, traditional wisdom and practices may play a role in shaping the perceptions and reactions of women toward complications of pregnancy.[9] Though various studies have explored maternal knowledge across different regions of Iraq,[10] current data for Mosul, particularly after the conflict years, are limited. Exploration of the existing status of maternal knowledge is of paramount importance for designing healthcare policies and interventions, considering the unique post-conflict challenges and ongoing recovery efforts of the city.[11] Thus, this study sought to evaluate the knowledge and attitudes of pregnant women in Mosul City, Iraq, toward pregnancy-related risk factors and to determine socio-demographic variables influencing this knowledge. The results of this study will help to support evidence-based plans to raise maternal health literacy and, therefore, maternal and neonatal outcomes in this at-risk group.[12]

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from January to April 2024 in Mosul City, Iraq. The study was carried out in five major healthcare facilities providing antenatal care services: Al-Khansa Teaching Hospital, Al-Batool Teaching Hospital, Mosul General Hospital, Ibn Sina Teaching Hospital, and Al-Zahraa Primary Healthcare Center. These facilities were selected to represent different healthcare levels (primary, secondary, and tertiary) and geographical areas (east and west Mosul) to ensure a diverse and representative sample.

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula with the following assumptions: 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% expected proportion of good knowledge (used to maximize the sample size due to the lack of previous studies in this specific population). With an additional 10% to account for nonresponse, the final sample size was determined to be 422 pregnant women. Participants at each facility were chosen using a methodical random sampling approach. A sampling interval was determined depending on the average daily attendance of pregnant women at each site; every nth woman was contacted for participation until the needed sample size was reached.

Pregnant women aged 18 years and older attending antenatal care at the chosen healthcare sites during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Women with significant medical or obstetric complications needing emergency treatment, those with cognitive disabilities impairing their ability to answer the questionnaire, and those who chose not to participate were excluded from the study.

Data collection tools and technique

A thorough literature study served as the basis for a structured questionnaire created from earlier comparable research (5, 6, 13), with changes to fit the regional setting. To guarantee accuracy, the questionnaire was translated into Arabic and back-translated. Clarity, understanding, and cultural appropriateness were evaluated among 20 pregnant women (not part of the final sample) before testing; later, changes were made as required. The questionnaire consisted of three sections: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge assessment, and attitude assessment. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, educational level, profession, monthly family income, residence (urban/rural), parity, gestational age, and source of antenatal care. The knowledge assessment section included warning signs, medical conditions complicating pregnancy, and behaviors influencing pregnancy outcomes. This section had 30 items measuring knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors, with “yes,” “no,” or “don’t know” as possible answers for each item. A correct answer was scored as 1 point, while incorrect answers and “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. The total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 30, with scores categorized as poor (0–10), moderate (11–20), and good (21–30). The attitude assessment section included 15 statements assessing attitudes toward pregnancy risk factors and health-seeking behaviors, with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” Attitude scores were calculated and categorized as positive (≥75% of maximum score), neutral (50–74%), or negative (<50%).

Data collection procedure

Six trained female research assistants with backgrounds in nursing or midwifery conducted the data collection. They received a 2-day training on the objectives of the study, questionnaire administration, ethical considerations, and interview techniques. The questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews in a private setting within each healthcare facility to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Before each interview, the purpose of the study was explained to potential participants, and informed consent was obtained. Interviews lasted approximately 20–30 min and were conducted in Arabic. For participants with limited literacy, the questions were read aloud, and responses were recorded by the research assistants.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Mosul Medical Technical Institute of Northern Technical University (approval number: MMTRC-2025-020; September 25, 2025). Permission was also obtained from the Nineveh Health Directorate and the administrative offices of each participating healthcare facility. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and the right to withdraw at any time without affecting their healthcare services. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were kept secure and confidential, in compliance with the terms and conditions of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration.

Data analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Knowledge and attitude scores were calculated as described in the “Data collection tools and technique” section. Bivariate analysis using independent t-test and one-way analysis of variance was performed to assess the associations between knowledge scores and socio-demographic characteristics. Variables with P < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate linear regression model to identify independent predictors of knowledge scores. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationships among knowledge and attitude scores. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Of the 422 pregnant women approached, 420 completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 99.5%. The mean age of participants was 27.8 ± 5.9 years, with the majority (56.4%) falling in the 25–34 years age group. Regarding educational status, 12.1% had no formal education, 27.4% had primary education, 34.5% had secondary education, and 26.0% had university education or higher. Most participants (72.6%) were housewives, and 62.1% resided in urban areas. In terms of obstetric history, 33.3% were primigravida, and the mean gestational age was 26.5 ± 8.2 weeks.

Table 2 presents the participants’ knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors. The mean knowledge score was 15.63 ± 5.72 out of a possible 30 points, indicating moderate knowledge levels overall. When categorized, 25.2% (n = 106) of participants had poor knowledge, 51.0% (n = 214) had moderate knowledge, and 23.8% (n = 100) had good knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors. Table 2 presents participants’ knowledge regarding specific pregnancy risk factors. The most widely recognized risk factors were hypertension in pregnancy (76.4%), diabetes in pregnancy (72.9%), and severe headache (70.7%). Conversely, the least recognized risk factors were changes in fetal movement (42.3%), leaking amniotic fluid without labor (45.0%), and excessive vomiting (48.3%). Knowledge levels varied noticeably across different categories of risk factors. Participants demonstrated better knowledge about medical conditions complicating pregnancy (mean score 66.2% of maximum) compared to danger signs requiring immediate attention (53.9%) and behavioral/environmental risk factors (59.6%). Regarding attitudes toward pregnancy-related risk factors and appropriate health-seeking behaviors, 67.1% (n = 282) of participants demonstrated positive attitudes, 26.2% (n = 110) had neutral attitudes, and 6.7% (n = 28) had negative attitudes. The majority of participants (87.3%) agreed or strongly agreed that seeking medical care for pregnancy complications is essential. Similarly, 83.6% recognized the importance of regular antenatal visits for monitoring pregnancy risks. However, 47.6% of participants held misconceptions about culturally rooted risk factors, such as dietary taboos and traditional remedies. Additionally, 41.9% believed that some pregnancy complications are normal and do not require medical attention, while 39.3% indicated that family members should be the primary decision-makers regarding seeking care for pregnancy complications.

Bivariate analysis [Table 3] revealed significant associations between knowledge scores and several sociodemographic factors. Participants with higher education levels had significantly higher knowledge scores (P < 0.001), with university-educated women scoring an average of 20.33 ± 4.27 compared to 9.27 ± 3.18 for women with no formal education. Urban residents scored significantly higher than rural residents (16.85 ± 5.53 vs. 13.67 ± 5.56, P < 0.001). Employed women had significantly higher knowledge scores (19.16 ± 4.71) compared to housewives (14.37 ± 5.51) and students (17.81 ± 5.02) (P < 0.001). Knowledge scores also increased with higher monthly family income (P < 0.001) and advanced gestational age (P = 0.017). Women receiving antenatal care exclusively at private facilities or at both public and private facilities scored significantly higher than those attending only public facilities (P < 0.001). No statistically significant associations were found between knowledge scores and age (P = 0.073) or parity (P = 0.089), although primigravida women tended to have slightly higher scores than multiparous women.

In the multivariate linear regression analysis [Table 4], educational level, residence, monthly family income, and source of antenatal care remained independent predictors of knowledge scores after adjusting for other variables. Educational level had the strongest association, with university education increasing knowledge scores by 9.68 points compared to no formal education (β = 9.68, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 8.05–11.31, P < 0.001). Urban residence was associated with a 1.57-point increase in knowledge score compared to rural residence (β = 1.57, 95% CI: 0.54–2.60, P = 0.003). Higher monthly family income was associated with increased knowledge scores, with women earning >1,000,000 IQD (Iraqi Dinar) scoring 1.85 points higher than those earning <500,000 IQD (β = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.69–3.01, P = 0.001). Women receiving antenatal care exclusively from private facilities scored 2.05 points higher than those attending only public facilities (β = 2.05, 95% CI: 0.74–3.36, P = 0.002). The full regression model explained 47.3% of the variance in knowledge scores (adjusted R² = 0.473). A moderate positive correlation was found between knowledge and attitude scores (r = 0.56, P < 0.001), indicating that women with higher knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors were more likely to have positive attitudes toward appropriate health-seeking behaviors. Among women with good knowledge, 91.0% had positive attitudes, compared to 69.6% of those with moderate knowledge and only 37.7% of those with poor knowledge.

Discussion

This study assessed the knowledge and attitudes of pregnant women toward pregnancy-related risk factors in Mosul City, Iraq, and identified socio-demographic factors influencing this knowledge. Our findings revealed moderate knowledge levels overall, with significant disparities across socio-demographic groups. Only about one-quarter of participants demonstrated good knowledge, highlighting considerable room for improvement in maternal health literacy in this post-conflict setting. Regarding knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors among pregnant women in Mosul, the mean knowledge score of 15.63 ± 5.72 (52.1% of the maximum possible score) suggests moderate knowledge levels. This figure aligns with findings from several other middle-income countries, including Ethiopia (54.1%),[13] Tanzania (57.9%),[6] and Egypt (55.8%),[14] but is lower than the number determined in some higher-income Arab countries, like Jordan (66.4%)[15] and Saudi Arabia (61.2%).[7] In Post-conflict Mosul, the healthcare system remains ravaged with limited access to health education; therefore, the moderate level of knowledge observed is most probably attributable to these factors, as maternal health services are still in the recovery stage. Our findings revealed greater awareness of the medical conditions that worsen pregnancy than of the danger signs that require urgent attention. This trend is alarming, as avoiding maternal hospitalization and death requires timely recognition of danger signs and prompt medical care. Notably, the awareness of critical danger signs, such as changes in fetal movement (42.3%), leaking amniotic fluid (45.0%), and vaginal bleeding (53.1%) is low, although these symptoms can indicate serious complications requiring immediate management. Similar knowledge gaps about danger signals have been found in studies from other developing countries,[613] suggesting a widespread need for targeted education around these critical warning signs. Hypertension (76.4%) and diabetes in pregnancy (72.9%) were the most often acknowledged risk factors, perhaps reflecting the growing frequency and public knowledge of these diseases in Iraq (16). The relatively high knowledge of behavioral risk factors like smoking (70.0%) and alcohol use (68.8%) during pregnancy also suggests effective public health communication about these particular hazards. Regarding attitudes toward pregnancy-related risk factors, the results were encouraging: 87.3% of respondents had mostly favorable views on seeking medical treatment for pregnancy problems and 83.6% on the need for consistent antenatal visits. These findings indicate a basis for positive health-seeking behaviors. However, some myths and cultural beliefs continue to cause difficulties for maternal health promotion. Almost half (47.6%) of the respondents had misconceptions connected to cultural beliefs about pregnancy risks, which could affect healthcare choices and postpone suitable care-seeking. Particularly concerning is the finding that 41.9% of respondents considered that certain pregnancy problems are normal and do not need medical attention, which could delay care-seeking for possibly serious conditions. The significant percentage (39.3%) of those who identified family members as main decision-makers about care-seeking also shows the influence of family dynamics on maternal healthcare use in this setting. Similar results have been documented in other conventional societies where family hierarchies, especially the impact of mothers-in-law and husbands, greatly affect women’s healthcare choices.[715] The moderate positive correlation (r = 0.56) between knowledge and attitude scores implies that increasing knowledge about pregnancy-related risk factors could help to foster more favorable attitudes toward suitable health-seeking behaviors. Documented in earlier research,[16] this finding emphasizes the need for health education in fostering good maternal health behaviors. We identified a number of socio-demographic factors strongly associated with the awareness of risk factors during pregnancy. Compared to those who had not received formal education, women who had completed university-level education showed higher levels of awareness of pregnancy risk factors. This result is consistent with a high number of studies in other settings that have repeatedly shown a positive correlation between educational level and knowledge of maternal health.[17] Increased level of education presumably improves women’s capacity to seek, comprehend, and use health information, thereby emphasizing the general societal gains of education for women. The urban–rural gap in knowledge scores noted, where urban residents recorded better knowledge, could reflect inequalities in access to health care services, health information, and educational opportunities. Studies in other nations[18] have also noted such urban advantages, thereby underlining the need for targeted interventions to address rural disadvantages in maternal health knowledge. Greater exposure to health services, sources of information, and perhaps higher levels of education among women who are better off financially could explain the positive link between higher income and higher knowledge scores. Economic status has been linked with maternal health knowledge and outcomes in other settings,[19] and this therefore supports the link between socioeconomic factors and health literacy. The result that females who only attended private facilities had greater knowledge scores than those attending only public institutions could be an indication of variation in the quality and comprehensiveness of antenatal education received during the consultation. Private Iraqi hospitals sometimes provide longer consultations and more individualized care, which could enable more extensive health education.[2021] Women who go to private centers may also be more educated and wealthier, so they would be more likely to score higher on knowledge tests. In contrast to other studies that have shown positive correlations with these variables, our research did not yield significant associations between parity and knowledge scores or age.[2223] This may indicate that in the context of Mosul, educational and socioeconomic factors are more powerful determiners of maternal knowledge related to health than reproductive experience or age.

Limitations and recommendations

Many women were reluctant to participate in the study due to household chores, time constraints, and the high volume of patients at healthcare facilities. So, targeted educational interventions, especially for vulnerable groups with lower education and socioeconomic status, are recommended to improve maternal knowledge and attitudes, potentially reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.

Conclusion

The study revealed that pregnant women in Mosul demonstrated moderate knowledge of pregnancy-related risk factors with significant educational and socioeconomic disparities. Our research did not yield significant associations between parity and knowledge scores or age. This may indicate that in the context of Mosul, educational and socioeconomic factors are more powerful determiners of maternal knowledge related to health than reproductive experience or age.
