A Variable-Metric Non-monotone Line Search Method for Mixed Variational Inequalities and Equilibrium Problems
Mohammed Alshahrani

Abstract
We propose a scaled gap-function method for variational inequalities, mixed variational inequalities, and equilibrium problems over a closed convex set. The method drives a gap function to zero by combining a variable-metric scaled projection step with a modified non-monotone Armijo line search. The construction rests on a structural identity: the scaled projection step is exactly the maximizer that defines the Fukushima regularized gap, so the search direction is simultaneously the algorithmic step and the gap-defining direction. For variational and mixed variational inequalities with a strongly monotone, Lipschitz operator we establish global convergence and an R-linear rate, under a fixed or a controlled-change variable metric. The rate follows from the strong-monotonicity contraction and the resulting explicit gap error bound. For equilibrium problems we obtain global convergence and a gap error bound (Mastroeni’s gap). Numerical experiments on controlled problems confirm the convergence guarantees and the predicted rate, and compare the method with standard extragradient and gap-descent methods. The combination of variable-metric scaling, a modified non-monotone line search, and gap-function descent appears to be new for these problem classes.



Keywords
: mixed variational inequality; equilibrium problem; gap function;
variable metric; non-monotone line search.

1. Introduction

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let
F\colon K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an operator.
The
variational inequality
problem
\mathrm{VI}(F,K)
is to find
x^{*}\in K
such that

\langle F(x^{*}),\,y-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall\,y\in K.

(
\mathrm{VI}(F,K)
)

Adding a proper, convex, lower semicontinuous term
\varphi\colon K\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
gives the

mixed variational inequality

\mathrm{MVI}(F,\varphi,K)
: find
x^{*}\in K
with

\langle F(x^{*}),\,y-x^{*}\rangle+\varphi(y)-\varphi(x^{*})\geq 0\qquad\forall\,y\in K.

(
\mathrm{MVI}(F,\varphi,K)
)

More generally, a bifunction
G\colon K\times K\to\mathbb{R}
with
G(x,x)=0
defines the

equilibrium problem

\mathrm{EP}(G,K)
(in the sense of Blum–Oettli
[
5
]
): find
x^{*}\in K
with

G(x^{*},y)\geq 0\qquad\forall\,y\in K.

(
\mathrm{EP}(G,K)
)

Problem

EP

(
G
,
K
)

subsumes the other two: taking
G(x,y)=\langle F(x),\,y-x\rangle
recovers

VI

(
F
,
K
)

,
and adding
\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)
recovers

MVI

(
F
,
φ
,
K
)

. It also unifies convex optimization, saddle-point
problems, Nash equilibria, and complementarity problems within a single format
[
13
,
29
]
.

A recurring difficulty in these problems is that the natural projection map
x\mapsto P_{K}(x-\alpha F(x))

is a contraction only when
F
is strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous. For merely monotone or
pseudomonotone
F
it is at best nonexpansive, so the plain projection iteration can cycle and fail to
converge. Remedies include
extragradient steps
[
20
,
8
]
, projection-and-contraction
schemes
[
34
]
, and reformulations through
gap
functions whose global
minimizers are exactly the solutions
[
14
,
25
]
.

Ansari et al.
[
2
]
introduced the scaled gradient modified
non-monotone line search method for the constrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in K}f(x)
. Their method combines (i) a
variable-metric scaled projection

step

y_{k}=P_{K,\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}\!\big(x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{D}_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\big),\qquad d_{k}=y_{k}-x_{k},

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is a symmetric positive definite scaling matrix and
P_{K,\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}
is the
projection in the metric induced by
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}
; and (ii) a
modified non-monotone Armijo line
search

f(x_{k}+\lambda_{k}d_{k})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}+\delta_{1}\lambda_{k}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle-\delta_{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2},

(2)

Here the reference value
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is a moving average of past objective
values
[
36
,
17
]
, and the term
-\delta_{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}

is a quadratic correction in the spirit of
[
27
,
33
]
. Under standard
assumptions the method converges globally for pseudoconvex
f
and attains an
R-linear
rate when
f

is strongly quasiconvex. The matrix
\mathcal{D}_{k}
defines the variable metric of the step.
Equation (
1
) premultiplies the gradient by
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and projects in the metric induced by

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}
. It reduces to the Euclidean projected-gradient step when
\mathcal{D}_{k}=I
, and to a preconditioned
(quasi-Newton or spectral) step when
\mathcal{D}_{k}
approximates
[\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})]^{-1}
. The non-monotone
test enforces sufficient decrease against the moving-average reference
\mathcal{T}_{k}
rather than against

f(x_{k})
. This allows non-monotone iterates with
f(x_{k+1})>f(x_{k})

[
16
]
.

The optimality condition for
\min_{x\in K}f
at a pseudoconvex
f
is precisely
\mathrm{VI}(\nabla f,K)
,
and the step (
1
) is the scaled fixed-point map of that variational inequality. Their scheme is therefore a
variational-inequality method whose operator is the gradient
\nabla f
.

We remove the gradient assumption. We replace
\nabla f
by a general operator
F
(or by a bifunction

G
) and replace the objective

\mathrm{MVI}
, and
\mathrm{EP}
that keeps the scaled projection and the modified non-monotone line
search intact. The key is an identity, made precise in
Section
3
. The Fukushima regularized gap
[
14
]
in the scaled metric,

g_{\alpha}(x)=\max_{y\in K}\Big\{\langle F(x),\,x-y\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\Big\},

is uniquely maximized at the scaled projection point
y_{\alpha}(x)=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}(x-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x))

of (
1
)
[
14
]

is
simultaneously the algorithmic step and the gap-defining direction, and the iteration becomes a descent method

Our method draws on three lines of work—variable-metric scaling, the modified non-monotone line
search, and gap-function descent. Each is mature on its own, but to our knowledge they have not been
combined for
\mathrm{VI}
,
\mathrm{MVI}
, and

Scaled gradient projection originated in imaging and constrained
optimization
[

The non-monotone Armijo technique has been developed almost entirely

or

12
]

Gap-descent methods for these problems use exact or standard monotone line
searches

,
25
,
4
]
. The closest precedent is Crisci
et al.
[
10
]
, who join scaling with a non-monotone Armijo rule. Their method remains
within optimization and uses no operator and no gap function.

Gap and D-gap functions reformulate variational inequalities
and equilibrium problems as differentiable optimization

[
14
,
30
,
35
,
25
,
4
]
,
and an error bound of the form

[

,

]
. Such gap-error-bound linear rates are
currently established only for variational inequalities
[

,
38
]
. For
equilibrium problems a genuine geometric (q-linear) rate is available through the proximal method of

[
18

. That rate comes from a proximal contraction

Lara et al.
[
21
]
provides only a non-geometric “type of” rate under diminishing

[

We work under strong monotonicity and make the following contributions:

A unified

2.

The algorithm (Section

,

\mathrm{EP}

Global convergence (

4.

An R-linear rate for the strongly monotone variational inequality
(Theorem

(Remark

A numerical study (Section

.

Proof.

(i)

By (

x^{*}=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}(z)\quad\text{iff}\quad\langle x^{*}-z,\,\mathcal{D}^{-1}(x^{*}-w)\rangle\leq 0\quad\text{for all }w\in K.

With

one has
x^{*}-z=\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x^{*})
, so the condition becomes

\alpha\langle F(x^{*}),\,x^{*}-w\rangle\leq 0,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\langle F(x^{*}),\,w-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }w\in K,

which means that
x^{*}
solves

VI

(
F
,
K
)

and therefore, by Proposition

,
g_{\alpha}(x^{*})=0
.

(ii)

The map

is convex with
\psi(x^{*})=0
, so

x^{*}
minimizes
\psi
over

if and only if
x^{*}
solves

EP

(
G
,
K
)

. Indeed, if

for all

, then
\psi(y)\geq 0=\psi(x^{*})
for all
y\in K
. Conversely, if

x^{*}
minimizes
\psi
over
K

and set

for

gives

0\leq\psi(y_{t})-\psi(x^{*})=G(x^{*},y_{t})+\tfrac{t^{2}}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\leq t\,G(x^{*},y)+\tfrac{t^{2}}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2},

and dividing by

x^{*}
solves

EP

(
G
,
K
)

;
equivalently

Each inner subproblem has a strictly convex (resp. concave) objective, so its minimizer (resp. maximizer)—the algorithm’s step

(resp.

). Here

is the gap-defining direction, which lets a single algorithmic template

Example 5.2
(A genuine VI satisfying Assumptions

–

Let

•

Strong monotonicity, modulus

•

Lipschitz,

•

A genuine VI:

(Proposition

Since

By Definition

\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle\ \leq\ -\,m\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2},

i.e. Assumption

holds with

Problem

(

, because

is strongly monotone. By

Here

coercive, so the sublevel set

The analysis below uses the following set and estimates. Set

where

is the scaled projection step (

g_{\alpha}(x)=-\langle F(x),\,d\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2},\qquad d=y_{\alpha}(x)-x,

so

0\ \leq\ g_{\alpha}(x)\ \leq\ -\langle F(x),\,d\rangle\ \leq\ \lVert F(x)\rVert\,\lVert d\rVert.

(12)

Lemma 5.3
(Line search and sufficient decrease)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
:

(i)

the line search terminates and

\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{\min}:=\min\{\kappa,\lambda_{\max},\sigma\bar{\lambda}\}>0
, where
\kappa
is the
direction constant of Assumption
4.2
(here
\kappa=m
by (
11
)) and

\bar{\lambda}=(1-\delta_{1})\kappa/(L_{g}/2+\delta_{2}).

(ii)

g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}
for all
k
, and
\{\mathcal{T}_{k}\}
is nonincreasing.

(iii)

\mathcal{T}_{k}-\mathcal{T}_{k+1}\geq(1-\eta_{\max})\,\rho\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}
with

\rho=\delta_{1}\kappa\lambda_{\min}>0
.

Proof.

We argue by induction that
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}
for all
k
. This holds at
k=0
because

\mathcal{T}_{0}=g_{\alpha}(x_{0})
. Assume it at index
k
, so
x_{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

(i)

For
0<\lambda\leq\lambda_{\max}\leq 1
the trial point
x_{k}+\lambda d_{k}
lies on

[x_{k},y_{\alpha}(x_{k})]\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{0}
, so the descent lemma for the
L_{g}
-Lipschitz gradient on

\mathcal{C}_{0}
gives

g_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\lambda d_{k})\ \leq\ g_{\alpha}(x_{k})+\lambda\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle+\tfrac{L_{g}}{2}\lambda^{2}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}.

With
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}
and
v_{k}=\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k})
, the test (
10
) holds
whenever

\big(\tfrac{L_{g}}{2}+\delta_{2}\big)\lambda\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ (1-\delta_{1})\big(-\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle\big),

which by (
11
) is true for all
0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}
. Hence the backtracking terminates.
Its first trial is
\min\{s_{k},\lambda_{\max}\}\geq\min\{\kappa,\lambda_{\max}\}
and the trials shrink by

\sigma\in(0,1)
. A trial fails only when it exceeds
\bar{\lambda}
, so the accepted step satisfies

\lambda_{k}\geq\min\{\kappa,\lambda_{\max},\sigma\bar{\lambda}\}=\lambda_{\min}
.

(ii)

The accepted step obeys (
10
), hence

g_{\alpha}(x_{k+1})\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{k}+\delta_{1}\lambda_{k}\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle-\delta_{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{k}.

Therefore
\mathcal{T}_{k+1}=\eta_{k+1}\mathcal{T}_{k}+(1-\eta_{k+1})g_{\alpha}(x_{k+1})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}
, and

\mathcal{T}_{k+1}-g_{\alpha}(x_{k+1})=\eta_{k+1}\big(\mathcal{T}_{k}-g_{\alpha}(x_{k+1})\big)\geq 0.

This closes the
induction and shows
\{\mathcal{T}_{k}\}
is nonincreasing.

(iii)

Using (
11
),
\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{\min}
, and
\eta_{k+1}\leq\eta_{\max}
,

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{T}_{k}-\mathcal{T}_{k+1}&=&(1-\eta_{k+1})\big(\mathcal{T}_{k}-g_{\alpha}(x_{k+1})\big)\\
&\geq&\ (1-\eta_{\max})\big(-\delta_{1}\lambda_{k}\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle\big)\\
&\geq&\ (1-\eta_{\max})\,\delta_{1}\kappa\lambda_{\min}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}=(1-\eta_{\max})\,\rho\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}.\end{array}

(Reusability of Lemma

The proof of Lemma

uses only that

is nonnegative and

with

-Lipschitz gradient on

Theorem 5.5
(Global convergence, V1)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, the series
\sum_{k}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}
is convergent and
the iterates
x_{k}
converge to the unique solution
x^{*}
of

VI

(
F
,
K
)

, with
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\to 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
(iii), summed over
k=0,\dots,N
and telescoped,

(1-\eta_{\max})\,\rho\sum_{k=0}^{N}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{N+1}\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{0},

because
\mathcal{T}_{N+1}\geq g_{\alpha}(x_{N+1})\geq 0
. Letting

gives
\sum_{k}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}<\infty
,
hence
\lVert d_{k}\rVert\to 0
. By (

),

g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq\lVert F(x_{k})\rVert\lVert d_{k}\rVert\leq M_{F}\lVert d_{k}\rVert\to 0.

Finally, the error bound

gives

\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert^{2}\leq g_{\alpha}(x_{k})/\tau\to 0
, so

.

Corollary 5.6
(Best-iterate sublinear rate)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, for every

and, by (

),

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq N}g_{\alpha}(x_{k})=O(1/\sqrt{N})
. The bound is informative only when the direction
constant is positive (here
\kappa=m>0
, so
\rho=\delta_{1}\kappa\lambda_{\min}>0
). By
Remark
5.4
it holds for any merit satisfying the direction condition, so a merely
monotone operator, for which the direction condition holds only with

, is not covered.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem

gives
\displaystyle(1-\eta_{\max})\,\rho\sum_{k=0}^{N}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\leq\mathcal{T}_{0},
and
\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq N}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}

The gap estimate follows from

Theorem 5.7
(R-linear convergence, V1)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, suppose moreover that

\alpha\in\big(0,\,2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})\big)
, and set

q=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2\alpha m}{\mu}+\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2}}\in[0,1),\qquad\theta=1-\lambda_{\min}(1-q)\in(0,1).

Then the iterates
x_{k}
converge to
x^{*}
R-linearly with ratio
\theta
, that is,

\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ \mu\,\theta^{k}\,\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert\qquad\text{for all }k,

(13)

and the gap converges R-linearly with the same ratio,
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq C_{g}\,\theta^{k}
for some
C_{g}>0
.

Proof.

Set
e_{k}=x_{k}-x^{*}
and write
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
. The step map

T(x)=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}(x-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x))=y_{\alpha}(x)
has
x^{*}
as a fixed point by
Proposition
3.1
, and
y_{k}=T(x_{k})
. By Remark
2.2
,
\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}
and

\lVert x\rVert_{*}
lie in
[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\lVert x\rVert,\,\sqrt{\mu}\,\lVert x\rVert]
for every
x
.

We first show that
T
contracts in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}
. As
P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
is nonexpansive in

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}
,

\lVert y_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}^{2}\leq\lVert e_{k}-\alpha\mathcal{D}\big(F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\big)\rVert_{*}^{2}=\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}^{2}-2\alpha\langle e_{k},\,F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\rangle+\alpha^{2}\lVert F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}.

(14)

Strong monotonicity and
L
-Lipschitz continuity, together with the norm bounds above, give

\langle e_{k},\,F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\rangle\geq\tfrac{m}{\mu}\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}^{2},\qquad\lVert F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\leq\mu^{2}L^{2}\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}^{2}.

(15)

Substituting (
15
) into (
14
) yields

\lVert y_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ q\,\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*},\qquad q^{2}=1-\tfrac{2\alpha m}{\mu}+\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2},

(16)

and
q<1
because
\alpha<2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})
. Since
x_{k+1}=(1-\lambda_{k})x_{k}+\lambda_{k}y_{k}
with

\lambda_{k}\in[\lambda_{\min},1]
, the relaxed step inherits the contraction (
16
):

\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert_{*}\leq(1-\lambda_{k})\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}+\lambda_{k}\lVert y_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\leq\big(1-\lambda_{k}(1-q)\big)\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}\leq\theta\,\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}.

(17)

Iterating (
17
) gives
\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}\leq\theta^{k}\lVert e_{0}\rVert_{*}
. Converting back to the
Euclidean norm,

\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\leq\sqrt{\mu}\,\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{*}\leq\sqrt{\mu}\,\theta^{k}\lVert e_{0}\rVert_{*}\leq\mu\,\theta^{k}\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert,

which is (
13
). Finally,
\lVert d_{k}\rVert\leq\big(1+\mu(1+\alpha\mu L)\big)\lVert e_{k}\rVert
by Lipschitz
continuity of
y_{\alpha}
together with
y_{\alpha}(x^{*})=x^{*}
, so (
12
) gives
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq M_{F}\lVert d_{k}\rVert\leq C_{g}\,\theta^{k}
.

Remark 5.8

The R-linear rate does not come from the sufficient decrease of Lemma
5.3
alone. Its
per-step estimate
\mathcal{T}_{k}-\mathcal{T}_{k+1}\geq(1-\eta_{\max})\rho\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}
is quadratic in the
residual, so it controls only
\sum_{k}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}
and gives the sublinear rate of
Corollary
5.6
. An R-linear rate from this estimate alone would need the per-step decrease
to be proportional to
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})
, that is, a bound
g_{\alpha}(x)\leq C\lVert d\rVert^{2}
. No such bound holds:
by (
6
) and (
12
),

\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\ \leq\ g_{\alpha}(x)\ \leq\ \lVert F(x)\rVert\,\lVert d\rVert,

and the linear upper bound is sharp at a solution
x^{*}
on the boundary of
K
, where
d=0
but

\nabla g_{\alpha}(x^{*})=F(x^{*})\neq 0
(Remark
2.10
).

The R-linear rate of Theorem
5.7
comes instead from the contraction (
16
), which
strong monotonicity provides. With the error bound (
9

Remark 5.9

5.1
Error bound and merit equivalence

Theorem
5.7
measures convergence through the regularized gap
g_{\alpha}
. We now relate this gap to the other quantities that measure progress toward the solution, chiefly the
distance and the residual. The next proposition answers this, and the same relations are reused for
equilibrium problems (Section
5.4
).

Proposition 5.11
(Error bound and merit equivalence, V1)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, suppose moreover that

\alpha\in\big(0,2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})\big)
, and set
q=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2\alpha m}{\mu}+\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2}}\in[0,1)
.
Write
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
. For
x\in K
and
t>0
, the scaled residual at step size
t
is

d_{t}(x)=y_{t}(x)-x,\qquad y_{t}(x)=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}\!\big(x-t\mathcal{D}F(x)\big).

In particular,
d_{\alpha}(x)=y_{\alpha}(x)-x
is the residual
d
of (
6
), with

d_{k}=d_{\alpha}(x_{k})
. Then the following hold for all
x\in K
.

(i)

The scaled residual satisfies,

(1-q)\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*},\qquad\lVert d_{t}(x)\rVert\ \leq\ \big(1+\mu(1+t\mu L)\big)\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert\quad(t>0).

(18)

(ii)

The regularized gap is an explicit error bound for the distance to
S=\{x^{*}\}
,

g_{\alpha}(x)\ \geq\ \tau\,\dist(x,S)^{2},\qquad\tau=\frac{(1-q)^{2}}{2\alpha\mu}.

(19)

(iii)

For
0<\alpha<\beta
the D-gap satisfies,

\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\big)\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}\ \leq\ g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\ \leq\ \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\big)\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}.

(20)

(iv)

If moreover
\beta<2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})
, then
\dist(x,S)^{2}
,
\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert^{2}
,

\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert^{2}
, and
g_{\alpha\beta}(x)
are mutually equivalent on
K
, i.e. there are constants

0<\underline{c}\leq\overline{c}
such that

\underline{c}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}\ \leq\ \lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert^{2},\ \ \lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert^{2},\ \ g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\ \leq\ \overline{c}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}\qquad(x\in K).

Proof.

(i)

The proof of Theorem
5.7
shows that

\lVert y_{\alpha}(x)-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\leq q\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*},\quad\text{where}\quad y_{\alpha}(x^{*})=x^{*}.

Therefore

\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}+\lVert y_{\alpha}(x)-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}+q\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*},

which rearranges to the first inequality of (
18
). For the second, fix
t>0
. Since
x^{*}

solves

VI

(
F
,
K
)

, it is a fixed point of the scaled projection step at every step size, so

y_{t}(x^{*})=x^{*}
(Proposition
3.1
). The projection
P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
is Lipschitz with
constant
\mu
in the Euclidean norm (Remark
2.4
, since
\mathcal{D}^{-1}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}
). Writing

u=x-t\mathcal{D}F(x)
and
u^{*}=x^{*}-t\mathcal{D}F(x^{*})
, so that
y_{t}(x)=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}(u)
and

y_{t}(x^{*})=P_{K,\mathcal{D}^{-1}}(u^{*})=x^{*}
,

\lVert y_{t}(x)-x^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ \mu\,\lVert u-u^{*}\rVert\ =\ \mu\,\lVert(x-x^{*})-t\mathcal{D}\big(F(x)-F(x^{*})\big)\rVert.

By the triangle inequality,
\lVert\mathcal{D}\rVert\leq\mu
(Remark
2.2
), and the
L
-Lipschitz
continuity of
F
,

\lVert u-u^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ \lVert x-x^{*}\rVert+t\,\lVert\mathcal{D}\rVert\,\lVert F(x)-F(x^{*})\rVert\ \leq\ (1+t\mu L)\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert,

so that
\lVert y_{t}(x)-x^{*}\rVert\leq\mu(1+t\mu L)\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert
. Finally

d_{t}(x)=\big(y_{t}(x)-x^{*}\big)-\big(x-x^{*}\big)
, and the triangle inequality gives

\lVert d_{t}(x)\rVert\ \leq\ \lVert y_{t}(x)-x^{*}\rVert+\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ \big(1+\mu(1+t\mu L)\big)\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert,

which is the second inequality of (
18
).

(ii)

By the lower bound (
6
), the first inequality of (
18
), and

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{\mu}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}
,

g_{\alpha}(x)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}\ \geq\ \tfrac{(1-q)^{2}}{2\alpha}\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}^{2}\ \geq\ \tfrac{(1-q)^{2}}{2\alpha\mu}\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert^{2},

which is (
19
).

(iii)

Write
\Phi_{t}(y)=\langle F(x),\,x-y\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2t}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}
for the
t
-objective of
(
4
), so that
g_{t}(x)=\max_{y\in K}\Phi_{t}(y)=\Phi_{t}(y_{t}(x))
. Since
y_{t}(x)
is the maximizer and

\lVert y_{t}(x)-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}=\lVert d_{t}(x)\rVert_{*}
,

g_{\alpha}(x)=\langle F(x),\,x-y_{\alpha}(x)\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2},\qquad g_{\beta}(x)=\langle F(x),\,x-y_{\beta}(x)\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}.

For the lower bound,
y_{\alpha}(x)\in K
is feasible for the
\beta
-maximization, so

g_{\beta}(x)\ \geq\ \Phi_{\beta}(y_{\alpha}(x))\ =\ \langle F(x),\,x-y_{\alpha}(x)\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}.

Subtracting the identity for
g_{\alpha}(x)
, the inner-product terms cancel and

g_{\alpha\beta}(x)=g_{\beta}(x)-g_{\alpha}(x)\ \geq\ \Big(\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\Big)\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big)\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2},

the lower bound of (
20
). Symmetrically,
y_{\beta}(x)\in K
is feasible for the

\alpha
-maximization, so

g_{\alpha}(x)\geq\Phi_{\alpha}(y_{\beta}(x))=\langle F(x),\,x-y_{\beta}(x)\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}
.
Subtracting this from the identity for
g_{\beta}(x)
gives

g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\ \leq\ \Big(\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\Big)\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big)\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert_{*}^{2},

the upper bound.

(iv)

Recall
\dist(x,S)=\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert
. By the contraction estimate in the proof of
Theorem
5.7
, applied at step size
t
,

\lVert y_{t}(x)-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ q_{t}\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*},\qquad q_{t}=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2tm}{\mu}+t^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2}},

and
q_{t}<1
precisely when
t<2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})
, which holds for both
t=\alpha
and
t=\beta
. As in (i), the
triangle inequality gives the residual lower bound
(1-q_{t})\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\leq\lVert d_{t}(x)\rVert_{*}
. Combining it
with the upper bound of (i) and the norm equivalence (Remark
2.2
) gives, for

t\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
,

\frac{(1-q_{t})^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}\ \leq\ \lVert d_{t}(x)\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ \big(1+\mu(1+t\mu L)\big)^{2}\,\dist(x,S)^{2},

(21)

so
\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert^{2}
and
\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert^{2}
are each equivalent to
\dist(x,S)^{2}
. Combining
(
20
) with the residual bounds yields

\frac{(1-q_{\alpha})^{2}}{2\mu}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big)\dist(x,S)^{2}\ \leq\ g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\ \leq\ \frac{\mu}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big)\big(1+\mu(1+\beta\mu L)\big)^{2}\dist(x,S)^{2},

(22)

so
g_{\alpha\beta}(x)
is equivalent to
\dist(x,S)^{2}
too. Taking
\underline{c}
to be the least and

\overline{c}
the greatest of the positive constants in (
21
) and (
22
) gives

\underline{c}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}\leq\lVert d_{\alpha}(x)\rVert^{2},\lVert d_{\beta}(x)\rVert^{2},g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\leq\overline{c}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}.

Corollary 5.12
(R-linear in every natural measure)

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
5.7
, the distance
\dist(x_{k},S)
, the residual

\lVert d_{\alpha}(x_{k})\rVert
, and the regularized gap
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})
converge to
0
R-linearly with ratio

\theta
, while the squared residual and the D-gap
g_{\alpha\beta}(x_{k})
(for
\alpha<\beta<2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})
)
converge with ratio
\theta^{2}
.

Proof.

Theorem
5.7
gives
\dist(x_{k},S)=\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\leq\mu\theta^{k}\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert=O(\theta^{k})
, so the
distance converges R-linearly with ratio
\theta
. The residual and the gap inherit this rate: the upper
bound in (
18
) gives
\lVert d_{\alpha}(x_{k})\rVert\leq\big(1+\mu(1+\alpha\mu L)\big)\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert=O(\theta^{k})
, and (
12
) gives
g_{\alpha}(x_{k})\leq M_{F}\lVert d_{\alpha}(x_{k})\rVert=O(\theta^{k})
.

Squaring,
\lVert d_{\alpha}(x_{k})\rVert^{2}=O(\theta^{2k})
. For the D-gap, the upper bound in
(
20
), the norm equivalence
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}^{2}\leq\mu\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}
, and the residual
bound at
t=\beta
give

g_{\alpha\beta}(x_{k})\leq\tfrac{\mu}{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\big)\lVert d_{\beta}(x_{k})\rVert^{2}=O(\theta^{2k})
.
Hence the squared residual and the D-gap converge with ratio
\theta^{2}
.

5.2
Variable metric

Theorems
5.5
–
5.7
fix the scaling matrix. We now let it vary with
k
, taking

\mathcal{D}_{k}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}
, and write
g_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{k}}
for the gap (
4
) with
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{k}
. The
contraction behind Theorem
5.7
uses only the spectral bounds of
\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}
, so it holds with the
same
q
in each
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}
-norm. The moving-average reference of the line search compares values of the
changing merit
g_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{k}}
, so here we drive the iteration with a fixed relaxation step

\lambda\in(0,1]
instead. The next theorem shows that a summable change in the scaling matrices then
preserves the R-linear rate, inflating only its constant.

Theorem 5.13
(Variable metric, V1)

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, suppose moreover that
\alpha\in(0,2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2}))
and

\sum_{k\geq 0}\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert<\infty
in the spectral norm. Run the relaxed iteration

x_{k+1}=(1-\lambda)x_{k}+\lambda\,y_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{k}}(x_{k})
with a fixed step
\lambda\in(0,1]
, and set

q=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2\alpha m}{\mu}+\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2}}\in[0,1),\qquad\theta=1-\lambda(1-q)\in(0,1),

and
M=\exp\!\big(\mu\sum_{k\geq 0}\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert\big)
. Then
x_{k}
converges to
x^{*}

R-linearly with ratio
\theta
, that is,

\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ \mu\sqrt{M}\,\theta^{k}\,\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert\qquad\text{for all }k.

When
\mathcal{D}_{k}\equiv\mathcal{D}
,
M=1
and the bound reduces to Theorem
5.7
.

Proof.

Let
V_{k}=\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}^{2}
. We bound
V_{k+1}
in two steps: the step map contracts in the
current
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}
-norm, and changing the scaling matrix perturbs
V
by a summable factor.

In the
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}
-norm, the contraction in the proof of Theorem
5.7
applies with

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Its constant
q
uses only the spectral bounds
[1/\mu,\mu]
, not the particular
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, so

\lVert y_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}\ \leq\ q\,\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}

holds for every
k
. With the fixed step
x_{k+1}=(1-\lambda)x_{k}+\lambda y_{k}
,
\lambda\in(0,1]
, convexity
of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}
gives

\lVert x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}\ \leq\ \big(1-\lambda(1-q)\big)\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}\ =\ \theta\,\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}.

(23)

Writing
\epsilon_{k}=\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert
and using
\lVert v\rVert^{2}\leq\mu\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}^{2}

(Remark
2.2
) together with (
23
),

V_{k+1}=\lVert x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}}^{2}\ \leq\ \lVert x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}}^{2}+\epsilon_{k}\lVert x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ (1+\mu\epsilon_{k})\,\theta^{2}V_{k}.

Iterating this recursion from
V_{0}
gives

V_{k}\ \leq\ \Big(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu\epsilon_{j})\Big)\theta^{2k}V_{0}.

Since
\sum_{j}\epsilon_{j}<\infty
and
1+t\leq e^{t}
, the product is bounded uniformly in
k
,

\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu\epsilon_{j})\ \leq\ \prod_{j\geq 0}(1+\mu\epsilon_{j})\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(\mu\sum_{j\geq 0}\epsilon_{j}\Big)\ =\ M,

so
V_{k}\leq M\theta^{2k}V_{0}
. Converting back to the Euclidean norm by Remark
2.2
,
we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ \mu V_{k}&\leq&\ \mu M\theta^{2k}V_{0}\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\mu M\theta^{2k}\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}_{0}^{-1}}^{2}\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&\mu^{2}M\theta^{2k}\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert^{2}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{array}

and taking square roots gives
\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\leq\mu\sqrt{M}\,\theta^{k}\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert
.

Remark 5.14

The hypothesis
\sum_{k}\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert<\infty
makes the consecutive changes summable, so
the inverse scaling matrices converge,
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\to\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}
for some
\bar{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}
. It implies the
variable-metric condition

(1+\eta_{k})\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\succeq\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1},\quad\sum_{k}\eta_{k}<\infty,

of Combettes–Vũ
[
9
]
, where
A\succeq B
means
A-B
is positive semidefinite and

\eta_{k}=\mu\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert.

Under it the variation of the scaling matrix costs nothing in the rate: the bound of
Theorem
5.13
keeps the fixed-metric ratio
\theta
and inflates only the constant, by

\sqrt{M}=\exp\!\big(\tfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_{k}\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert\big)
.

5.3
Mixed variational inequalities

The smooth-gap route (V1) used
g_{\alpha}\in C^{1}
, which fails when the nonsmooth term
\varphi
is
present. The strong-monotone rate, however, rests on the contraction of the step map, and this carries
over once the projection is replaced by the scaled resolvent. For

MVI

(
F
,
φ
,
K
)

the inner
subproblem is solved by the scaled forward–backward step

y_{\alpha}(x)=\prox^{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}_{\alpha(\varphi+\iota_{K})}\!\big(x-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x)\big)\in\argmin_{y\in K}\Big\{\langle F(x),\,y-x\rangle+\varphi(y)+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\Big\},

with
\iota_{K}
the indicator of
K
and
\prox^{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
the scaled proximal operator
(Definition
2.5
). The MVI gap is

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x)=-\min_{y\in K}\Big\{\langle F(x),\,y-x\rangle+\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\Big\}\ \geq\ 0,

vanishing iff
x
solves

MVI

(
F
,
φ
,
K
)

(Proposition
3.1
(ii) with

G(x,y)=\langle F(x),\,y-x\rangle+\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)
, convex in
y
). The optimality of
y_{\alpha}(x)
for
the subproblem gives (cf. the VI bound (
6
))
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}

with
d=y_{\alpha}(x)-x
.

Theorem 5.15
(Mixed VI, strongly monotone)

Under Assumption
4.1
, suppose moreover that
F
is strongly monotone with modulus
m
and

L
-Lipschitz,
\varphi
is proper, convex, and lower semicontinuous, and
\alpha\in(0,2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2}))
. Let
the scaling matrices be fixed (
\mathcal{D}_{k}\equiv\mathcal{D}
) or vary with
\sum_{k}\lVert\mathcal{D}_{k+1}^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert<\infty
,
take a fixed relaxation step
\lambda\in(0,1]
, and set

q=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2\alpha m}{\mu}+\alpha^{2}\mu^{2}L^{2}}\in[0,1),\qquad\theta=1-\lambda(1-q)\in(0,1).

Then

MVI

(
F
,
φ
,
K
)

has a unique solution
x^{*}
, and the relaxed iteration

x_{k+1}=(1-\lambda)x_{k}+\lambda\,y_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{k}}(x_{k})
has the following properties.

(i)

The iterates converge to
x^{*}
R-linearly with ratio
\theta
,

\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\ \leq\ C\,\theta^{k}\,\lVert x_{0}-x^{*}\rVert\qquad\text{for all }k,

where
C=\mu
when
\mathcal{D}_{k}\equiv\mathcal{D}
and
C=\mu\sqrt{M}
under the controlled-change condition, with
M
as
in Theorem
5.13
. In particular
\lVert d_{k}\rVert
and
\dist(x_{k},S)
converge to
0

R-linearly with ratio
\theta
.

(ii)

If moreover
\partial\varphi
is bounded on the iterate set, for instance
\varphi
is Lipschitz
near
S
, then
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x_{k})\to 0
R-linearly with ratio
\theta
.

Proof.

Write
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}
and let

T(x)=y_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}}(x)=\prox^{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}_{\alpha(\varphi+\iota_{K})}(x-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x))
be the step map.

The scaled proximal map
\prox^{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}_{\alpha(\varphi+\iota_{K})}
is the resolvent of

\alpha\mathcal{D}\,\partial(\varphi+\iota_{K})
, hence nonexpansive in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}

[
9
]
. By strong
monotonicity and
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
F
together with the spectral bounds (the estimates
(
15
)), the forward map
I-\alpha\mathcal{D}F
satisfies

\lVert(x-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x))-(x^{\prime}-\alpha\mathcal{D}F(x^{\prime}))\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ q\,\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert_{*}\qquad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in K.

Composing the two maps gives
\lVert T(x)-T(x^{\prime})\rVert_{*}\leq q\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert_{*}
, and
q<1
because

\alpha<2m/(\mu^{3}L^{2})
.

As
K
is closed it is complete in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}
, so by Banach’s theorem
T
has a unique fixed point,
which by Proposition
3.1
(ii) is the unique solution
x^{*}
of

MVI

(
F
,
φ
,
K
)

. Taking
x^{\prime}=x^{*}
in
the contraction gives
\lVert y_{\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{k}}(x_{k})-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\leq q\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}
, and the fixed relaxation
step inherits it,

\lVert x_{k+1}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \big(1-\lambda(1-q)\big)\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ =\ \theta\,\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert_{*},

as in (
17
). Iterating and converting to the Euclidean norm gives part (i) with
C=\mu
.
Under the controlled-change condition the argument of Theorem
5.13
replaces
\mu
by

\mu\sqrt{M}
.

For the residual, write
d(x)=T(x)-x
and
d_{k}=d(x_{k})
. Using
T(x^{*})=x^{*}
, the triangle inequality and the
contraction give

\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \lVert d(x)\rVert_{*}+\lVert T(x)-x^{*}\rVert_{*}\ \leq\ \lVert d(x)\rVert_{*}+q\,\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*},

so
\lVert d(x)\rVert_{*}\geq(1-q)\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert_{*}
for all
x\in K
(as in Proposition
5.11
). At the
iterates this makes
\lVert d_{k}\rVert
and
\dist(x_{k},S)
R-linear, and with the subproblem lower bound

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}
it yields the error bound

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert d(x)\rVert_{*}^{2}\ \geq\ \tfrac{(1-q)^{2}}{2\alpha\mu}\,\dist(x,S)^{2}\qquad\forall\,x\in K.

For part (ii), at the minimizer
y_{\alpha}(x)
drop the nonnegative regularizer and apply the subgradient
inequality for
\varphi
at
x
(any
\xi\in\partial\varphi(x)
),

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x)\ \leq\ \langle F(x),\,x-y_{\alpha}(x)\rangle+\varphi(x)-\varphi(y_{\alpha}(x))\ \leq\ \langle F(x)+\xi,\,x-y_{\alpha}(x)\rangle\ \leq\ \lVert F(x)+\xi\rVert\,\lVert d(x)\rVert.

If
\partial\varphi
is bounded along the iterates, the right side at
x=x_{k}
is
O(\theta^{k})
, so

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}(x_{k})\to 0
R-linearly.

Remark 5.16

When
\varphi
is nonsmooth,
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}
is not
C^{1}
, so the smooth-gap certificate (V1)
does not apply. The rate instead comes from the resolvent contraction with a fixed relaxation step

\lambda\in(0,1]
, or a sufficient-decrease test on the computable gap value
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{MVI}}
. A smooth merit enabling a gradient-based line search is available
through the forward–backward envelope, which we do not pursue here.

We illustrate Theorem
5.15
in the next example with a nonsmooth

penalty.

Example 5.17
(A mixed VI satisfying Theorem

)

Keep

,

,

, on

from Example

5.4
Equilibrium problems

Throughout this subsection
G\colon K\times K\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
G(x,x)=0
with
G(x,\cdot)
convex for

x\in K
, and
G
is differentiable with
\nabla_{1}G,\nabla_{2}G
continuous on
K\times K

is then a continuously differentiable gap function
for

EP

(
G
,

)

The inner minimization defining

has the unique minimizer

y_{\alpha}(x)\in\argmin_{y\in K}\Big\{G(x,y)+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}\Big\},\qquad d=y_{\alpha}(x)-x.

By convexity of

,

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x)\leq-G(x,y_{\alpha}(x))\leq\langle-\nabla_{2}G(x,x),\,d\rangle\leq\lVert\nabla_{2}G(x,x)\rVert\,\lVert d\rVert,

(24)

the EP analogue of (

).

Theorem 5.18
(Equilibrium problems: global convergence and error bound)

Suppose Assumption
4.1
holds with the EP gap
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}
as merit, that

\nabla g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}
is Lipschitz on a bounded convex set
\mathcal{C}_{0}
containing every segment

[x_{k},y_{\alpha}(x_{k})]
(automatic when
\nabla_{1}G,\nabla_{2}G
are locally Lipschitz, the iterates being
bounded), and that the direction condition (Assumption
4.2
) holds with constant

\kappa
. Then the SGFM iteration with certificate
v_{k}=\nabla g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})
has the following
properties.

(i)

\sum_{k}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}<\infty
, hence
\lVert d_{k}\rVert\to 0
and
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})\to 0
, and every
accumulation point of
\{x_{k}\}
solves

EP

(
G
,
K
)

.

(ii)

If moreover
G
is strongly monotone with modulus
c
(Definition
2.7
) and

\alpha>\mu/(2c)
, then

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x)\ \geq\ \big(c-\tfrac{\mu}{2\alpha}\big)\dist(x,S)^{2}\qquad\forall\,x\in K,

so
x_{k}\to x^{*}
, the unique solution of

EP

(
G
,
K
)

.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.13
,
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}\in C^{1}
, and by hypothesis
\nabla g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}

is Lipschitz on
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and the direction condition holds,

\langle\nabla g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k}),\,d_{k}\rangle\leq-\kappa\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}
. Lemma
5.3
therefore
applies with merit
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}
and direction constant
\kappa

(Remark
5.4
): the line search returns
\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{\min}
, the reference

\{\mathcal{T}_{k}\}
is nonincreasing, and

\mathcal{T}_{k}-\mathcal{T}_{k+1}\ \geq\ (1-\eta_{\max})\,\delta_{1}\kappa\lambda_{\min}\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}.

Summing over
k=0,\dots,N
and using
\mathcal{T}_{N+1}\geq 0
,

(1-\eta_{\max})\,\delta_{1}\kappa\lambda_{\min}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{0}-\mathcal{T}_{N+1}\ \leq\ \mathcal{T}_{0},

so
\sum_{k}\lVert d_{k}\rVert^{2}<\infty
and
\lVert d_{k}\rVert\to 0
.

By coercivity (Assumption
4.1
) the sublevel set
\{g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}\leq\mathcal{T}_{0}\}
is
bounded, and
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})\leq\mathcal{T}_{k}\leq\mathcal{T}_{0}
keeps the iterates in it, so
\{x_{k}\}

is bounded. Continuity of
\nabla_{2}G
then bounds
\lVert\nabla_{2}G(x_{k},x_{k})\rVert
, and (
24
) gives

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})\ \leq\ \lVert\nabla_{2}G(x_{k},x_{k})\rVert\,\lVert d_{k}\rVert\ \longrightarrow\ 0.

Since
y_{\alpha}
is continuous (Berge’s maximum theorem: the inner objective is jointly continuous by the standing hypotheses of this
subsection and strongly convex in
y
) and
y_{\alpha}(x)=x
iff
x
solves

EP

(
G
,
K
)

, any accumulation point

\bar{x}=\lim_{j}x_{k_{j}}
of the bounded sequence satisfies
y_{\alpha}(\bar{x})-\bar{x}=\lim_{j}d_{k_{j}}=0
, hence
solves

EP

(
G
,
K
)

. This proves part (i).

For part (ii), apply Mastroeni’s error bound
[
25
, Prop. 4.2]
to the regularizer

H(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert x-y\rVert_{\mathcal{D}^{-1}}^{2}
. It is symmetric, and
\nabla H
is Lipschitz in each
argument with modulus
\mu/\alpha
, which is below
2c
exactly when
\alpha>\mu/(2c)
. Under strong
monotonicity of
G
with modulus
c
it yields

g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x)\ \geq\ \big(c-\tfrac{\mu}{2\alpha}\big)\dist(x,S)^{2}\qquad\forall\,x\in K.

With
g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})\to 0
from part (i) and
c-\tfrac{\mu}{2\alpha}>0
,

\dist(x_{k},S)^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{g_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{EP}}(x_{k})}{\,c-\tfrac{\mu}{2\alpha}\,}\ \longrightarrow\ 0,

so
x_{k}\to x^{*}
, the unique solution (uniqueness by strong monotonicity).

Remark 5.19

The direction condition (Assumption
4.2
) holds for any differentiable, strongly monotone
G
, the
equilibrium bifunctions that encode VI and MVI under strong monotonicity of
F
being the special
case
[
25

. The scaled regularizer

.

Remark 5.21
(Toward an EP rate)

The error bound is the EP analogue of (
19

Remark 5.22
(Scope and extensions)

Beyond Section

6.4
Setup

Implementation

The methods are implemented in Julia 1.12.6. Each run uses a single thread on an Intel Core i9-9900K
(3.6 GHz) with 32 GB of memory, under Windows 11.

Performance measures

For each run we record whether it converged, the number of iterations, the number of operator evaluations,
and the running time. The operator-evaluation count is the common measure of work: for SGFM it counts the
operator and inner-solve calls, and for the competitors the inner-subproblem solves, which dominate their
cost, so all methods are compared on the same axis. Along the run we record the residual
r
defined below,
the gap
g_{\alpha}
, and the distance
\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert
when
x^{*}
is known. Each problem uses a fixed random
seed, and all methods start from the same points, so the runs are reproducible.

Stopping rule

All methods stop at the same accuracy. For a variational inequality we use the natural residual

r(x)=\lVert x-P_{K}\!\bigl(x-F(x)\bigr)\rVert,

which is zero exactly at a solution. For an equilibrium problem we use the same residual with
F(x)

replaced by
\nabla_{2}G(x,x)
, the gradient of
G
in its second argument at
y=x
; this matches
r
when
the bifunction encodes a variational inequality. A run stops when
r(x_{k})\leq\varepsilon
with

\varepsilon=10^{-6}
, or at the iteration cap. Each method has its own internal residual, but reporting the
common
r
makes “converged” mean the same accuracy for every method. The residual
r
is zero only at a
solution and is, up to constants, equivalent to the gap
g_{\alpha}
and to
\dist(x_{k},S)
under the
standing assumptions (cf. Section
5.1
); stopping on it is therefore consistent with the gap-
and distance-based rate of Theorem
5.7
.

Iteration cap

The cap follows from the rate. An R-linear residual
r_{k}\leq Cq^{k}
reaches
\varepsilon
in about

\log(C/\varepsilon)/\log(1/q)
steps. We take a fixed multiple of this estimate with a conservative
q
,
so the cap does not cut off a method that would otherwise converge. The value used in each experiment is
stated with that experiment.

Parameters

The free parameters of each method (Table
3
) are fixed once and reused in every experiment.
They come from a search on the test problems, run the same way for every method: a one-at-a-time sweep first
shows how sensitive each parameter is, then a Latin-hypercube search over the joint ranges selects the
setting. The score is the same for all methods — first the fraction of runs that converge, then the number
of operator evaluations — and the best setting is kept. Table
3
reports that setting. For SGFM the line-search constants
\delta_{1}=10^{-3}
,
\delta_{2}=10^{-4}
and the step cap

\lambda_{\max}=1
are kept at standard values, not searched.

Starting points

Each problem is run from five fixed feasible starting points, the same five for every method: the lower and
upper corners of the box, its centre, a further corner alternating between the lower and upper bounds, and
one seeded interior point. The corners are the points farthest from the interior solution, so convergence
from them is the direct test of the global statements (Experiment 1).

Convergence and rate

The starting point changes neither whether the method converges nor how fast. Figure

The contraction is geometric and holds throughout the run, not only near the solution. On a logarithmic

to

(Figure

the residual drops by a fixed factor at every step across seven orders of magnitude. The one departure is a

6.6
The variable metric

On a badly conditioned operator the metric decides the speed, and the diagonal metric wins when the
conditioning lies on the diagonal. Figure
4
runs SGFM with the diagonal
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and with

\mathcal{D}_{k}=I
on the affine operator conditioned at
10^{3}
. Both residuals are straight lines, so both converge
R-linearly as Theorem
5.13
guarantees, but the diagonal metric reaches
10^{-6}
in about

150
iterations against about
1300
for the Euclidean step, almost ten times fewer. The diagonal metric
rescales each coordinate by its own curvature, so the method sees a much smaller effective conditioning.
The size of the gain therefore depends on where the ill-conditioning sits. A diagonal metric removes
conditioning carried on the diagonal of the operator and cannot touch conditioning carried by the
off-diagonal coupling. This is why it helps so much here, where the conditioning is diagonal by construction,
and why a projection method slows on problems whose difficulty is rotational instead.

6.7
The modified non-monotone line search

On these smooth strongly monotone problems the non-monotone line search neither speeds the method up nor
slows it down, as the construction predicts. Figure

non-monotone terms come into play only when a step would raise the gap, and a smooth strongly monotone
operator never forces that. The experiment thus measures the cost of the modification
and finds it negligible, leaving the relaxation available for the non-monotone problems it is designed for.

The gap and the D-gap measure progress in the same way. Figure

6.9
Comparison with standard methods

SGFM is on par with the specialized methods on the equilibrium and mixed problems and is slower than the
extragradient method on the variational ones. Tables
4
and
5
give the
median over the five starts.

On the variational problems (Table
4
) the extragradient method is the faster solver, and
the gap between the two grows as the operator weakens. On the well-conditioned strongly monotone instance the
counts are close,
94
iterations for the extragradient method against
118
for SGFM. On the weakly monotone
instance the extragradient method needs
157
iterations and SGFM needs about
8000
, because the extragradient
step stays effective as the modulus
m
shrinks while the SGFM rate falls off with
m
, as
Figure
3
already showed. On the non-monotone Kojima–Shindo problem SGFM does not reach the
tolerance, while the extragradient method reaches one of the problem’s several solutions. Both results follow
the theory. SGFM rests on a strong-monotonicity contraction, and the extragradient method is built for plain
monotone and some non-monotone operators.

On the equilibrium problems (Table
5
) SGFM is even with the specialized methods where its
theory applies. On the strongly monotone instance SGFM and Mastroeni’s gap descent both converge in
81

iterations and the D-gap method in
110
. Mastroeni’s method uses fewer operator evaluations,
163
against

326
, because SGFM does more work per step in its inner solve and line search, so equal iteration counts do
not mean equal cost. On the merely monotone Nash–Cournot instance only the D-gap method converges, the
regime it was designed for. On the mixed problem SGFM solves the
\ell_{1}
-regularized instance in
73

iterations using the fixed relaxation step in place of the line search.

SGFM is not the fastest method on any single class, and the comparison does not aim to make it one. Each
competitor is specialized to a single class and tuned for it. What the experiments show is that one method,
carrying one analysis, gives the proved guarantees across all three classes: global convergence and the
R-linear rate under strong monotonicity, the same rate under the variable metric, and the gap error bound.
The price of that reach is the per-iteration work of the gap-based step, and on the variational problems a
higher iteration count than a method built for them alone.

7. Conclusion

We extended the scaled gradient modified non-monotone line search method of Ansari et al.
[
2
]
from optimization to
variational inequalities, mixed variational inequalities, and equilibrium problems, through a gap-function
formulation in which the scaled projection coincides with the gap-defining maximizer. For variational and
mixed variational inequalities with a strongly monotone Lipschitz operator the analysis yields global
convergence and an R-linear rate via a gap error bound, under a fixed or a controlled-change variable
metric. For equilibrium problems it yields global convergence and an error bound, with the R-linear rate
reduced to such a bound and left open. The numerical experiments confirm these guarantees on controlled
problems. The residual contracts R-linearly under strong monotonicity, the diagonal metric is about an order
of magnitude faster on ill-conditioned operators, and the method converges from every start across all three
classes. Against specialized methods SGFM is competitive on the equilibrium and mixed problems and slower than
the extragradient method on the variational ones, the price of a single framework that covers all three.

Several questions are left open, each flagged in the analysis. The first is the equilibrium rate. The
Mastroeni-gap error bound of Theorem
5.18
controls the distance to the solution but is only first
order in the residual, so a geometric rate is not automatic. It would follow from a contraction of the
auxiliary-equilibrium map under a Lipschitz-type condition on
G
, once the conflicting requirements on

\alpha
are reconciled—the error bound needs
\alpha
large, a contraction typically small
(Remark
5.21
). The second is the pseudomonotone and nonsmooth range. When the operator is merely
pseudomonotone or the data nonsmooth, the smooth-gap descent identity fails, and the line search moves from
the gap certificate (V1) to a separating-hyperplane test on the natural residual (V2), carrying the
non-monotone reference onto the residual rather than the gap (Remark
5.22
). The third is the
strongly quasiconvex class of the base method: because the gap does not inherit strong quasiconvexity
(Remark
2.17
), an R-linear rate there must come from a gap error bound derived directly from strong
quasiconvexity of
G(x,\cdot)
. Extensions to quasi-variational inequalities and to a Hilbert-space setting,
where the contraction and error-bound arguments use no compactness, are longer-term.
