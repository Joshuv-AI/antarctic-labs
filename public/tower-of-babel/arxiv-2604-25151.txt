Abstract
. We record several consequences of the Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem in the language of rational power series and connect them to a rigidity theorem for Lambert series. First, the zero set of the coefficient sequence of a rational function is eventually periodic. Second, if all sufficiently large prime-index coefficients vanish, then after subtracting a polynomial the function is a finite sum of rational functions in proper powers z d z^{d} , although in general not a single such function. Third, if a rational power series has infinitely many zero coefficients, then among the poles of minimum modulus there must be a root-of-unity relation. We then prove the following Lambert-series rigidity statement: if γ = ( γ n ) n ≥ 1 \gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1} is eventually linearly recurrent and L γ ​ ( z ) := ∑ n ≥ 1 γ n ​ z n 1 − z n L_{\gamma}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}} is rational, then γ \gamma is finitely supported. The proof uses the same finite-place setup as Skolem-Mahler-Lech, but in the Lambert setting the divisor-sum identity produces the contradiction before any p p -adic interpolation step is needed.

1.
Introduction

Let

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]].

When
F
is rational, the coefficient sequence
(a_{n})
satisfies a constant-coefficient linear recurrence from some point on, and conversely every eventually linearly recurrent sequence has rational generating function. The classical Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem says that the zero set

Z(F):=\{n\geq 0:a_{n}=0\}

is eventually periodic: it is a finite set together with finitely many arithmetic progressions. We use this in two elementary ways. First, if all sufficiently large prime-index coefficients vanish, then the eventual support of
(a_{n})
can only occupy residue classes that are not coprime to the ambient modulus; this yields a decomposition of
F
into a finite sum of rational functions in proper powers
z^{d}
. Second, infinitely many zero coefficients force a root-of-unity relation among the dominant characteristic roots, or equivalently among the poles of minimum modulus.

The questions considered here grew out of Lambert-series identities in Rivin’s work on free groups
[
9
]
and from the rigidity problem for the operator

(T_{\gamma}G)(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}G(z^{n}).

Given a sequence
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, define its Lambert series by

L_{\gamma}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}}.

Expanding each summand geometrically gives

(1)

L_{\gamma}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}b_{m}z^{m},\qquad b_{m}:=\sum_{d\mid m}\gamma_{d}.

Thus the coefficients of
L_{\gamma}
are the divisor sums of
\gamma
.

Our main theorem says that the recurrence world is rigid.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{C}
, and put

b_{n}:=\sum_{d\mid n}\gamma_{d}\qquad(n\geq 1).

If both
\gamma
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are eventually linearly recurrent, then
\gamma
is finitely supported.

The main generating-function consequence is immediate.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{C}
, and write

\Gamma_{\gamma}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}z^{n},\qquad L_{\gamma}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}}.

If both
\Gamma_{\gamma}(z)
and
L_{\gamma}(z)
are rational, then
\Gamma_{\gamma}(z)
is a polynomial. Equivalently, among sequences with rational ordinary generating function, the only ones whose Lambert series is rational are the finitely supported sequences.

For classical background on Lambert series see Hardy and Wright
[
6
]
and the survey of Schmidt
[
10
]
. For linear recurrence sequences see Everest, van der Poorten, Shparlinski, and Ward
[
5
]
. For the Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem and its variants see Lech
[

]
, Bell

]
, Adamczewski and Bell
[

]
, and van der Poorten and Schlickewei
[

.

2.
Linear recurrences and the Skolem-Mahler-Lech viewpoint

We say that a sequence
u=(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
eventually linearly recurrent
if there exist
r\geq 1
, coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
with
c_{r}\neq 0
, and an index
N
such that

(2)

u_{n+r}=c_{1}u_{n+r-1}+\cdots+c_{r}u_{n}\qquad(n\geq N).

If one may take
N=0
, we say that the recurrence holds
from the start
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a complex sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

The generating function
\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}z^{n}
is rational.

(2)

The sequence
(u_{n})
is eventually linearly recurrent.

Moreover, if
(u_{n})
is eventually linearly recurrent, then one can write

u=v+f,

where
f
is finitely supported and
v
is linearly recurrent from the start.

Proof.

The equivalence is classical; see, for example,
[
5
, Chapter 3]
. For the final assertion, choose
N,r
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
with
c_{r}\neq 0
such that (
2
) holds for all
n\geq N
. Define
v_{n}:=u_{n}
for
n\geq N
. Since
c_{r}\neq 0
, the recurrence can be solved backward:

v_{n}=c_{r}^{-1}\bigl(v_{n+r}-c_{1}v_{n+r-1}-\cdots-c_{r-1}v_{n+1}\bigr)\qquad(0\leq n<N).

Then
v
satisfies the same recurrence for every
n\geq 0
, and
f:=u-v
is finitely supported.
∎

Theorem 2.2

(Skolem-Mahler-Lech for rational power series)
.

Let

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}(z).

Then the zero set

Z(F):=\{n\geq 0:a_{n}=0\}

is a finite union of arithmetic progressions together with a finite set. In particular,
Z(F)
is eventually periodic.

Proof sketch.

By Lemma
2.1
, after subtracting a polynomial we may assume that
(a_{n})
satisfies a recurrence from the start, so that

a_{n}=\ell(A^{n}v)\qquad(n\geq 0)

for some matrix
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{r}(K)
, vector
v\in K^{r}
, and linear form
\ell
, where
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is a finitely generated field over
\mathbf{Q}
containing all the data.

Choose a good nonarchimedean place of
K
at which the entries of
A
,
A^{-1}
,
v
, and
\ell
are integral. Passing to the corresponding completion, let
\pi
be a uniformizer. Since the residue field is finite and
\bar{A}
is invertible, some power
A^{M}
satisfies

A^{M}=I+\pi C

with
C
integral.

For each residue class
j\bmod M
, the subsequence
a_{Mn+j}
is interpolated by a
p
-adic analytic function on
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
:

f_{j}(x):=\ell\bigl(A^{j}(A^{M})^{x}v\bigr),\qquad(A^{M})^{x}:=\sum_{m\geq 0}\binom{x}{m}(A^{M}-I)^{m}.

The binomial series converges because
A^{M}-I
is
\pi
-adically small, and for every integer
n\geq 0
one has
f_{j}(n)=a_{Mn+j}
.

If
a_{Mn+j}=0
for infinitely many
n
, then
f_{j}
has infinitely many zeros in
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
, hence vanishes identically by Strassmann’s theorem. Therefore the entire progression
Mn+j
belongs to
Z(F)
. Since there are only finitely many residue classes modulo
M
, the zero set is a finite union of arithmetic progressions up to finitely many initial exceptions. See
[
7
]
,
[
3
]
, or
[
1
, §1]
for fuller accounts.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

A standard refinement says that if the recurrence is
nondegenerate
, meaning that no quotient of two distinct characteristic roots is a root of unity, then the sequence has only finitely many zeros; see, for instance,
[
5
, Chapter 3]
or
[
8
]
. The arithmetic progressions in Theorem
2.2
arise precisely from root-of-unity relations among characteristic roots.

3.
Two consequences for rational power series

We first record the structural consequence for prime-index coefficients.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}(z),

and assume that
a_{p}=0
for all sufficiently large primes
p
. Then there exist a polynomial
P(z)
, integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}>1
, and rational functions
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\in\mathbb{C}(z)
such that

F(z)=P(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}(z^{d_{j}}).

In general one cannot take a single
d>1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, the support

S:=\{n\geq 0:a_{n}\neq 0\}

is eventually periodic. Thus there exist
k\geq 1
, a finite set
E\subset\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and residues
r_{1},\dots,r_{t}\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

n\in S\iff n\equiv r_{j}\pmod{k}\ \text{for some }j.

If one of the residues
r_{j}
is coprime to
k
, then Dirichlet’s theorem gives infinitely many primes
p\equiv r_{j}\pmod{k}

[
6
, Theorem 15]
. For all sufficiently large such primes we would have
a_{p}\neq 0
, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence every eventual support residue class satisfies

\gcd(r_{j},k)>1.

Let
P(z)
collect the finitely many terms with indices in
E
and those below the eventual periodic range. For each residue class
r\bmod k
, define

F_{r}(z):=\sum_{n\equiv r\,({\rm mod}\,k)}a_{n}z^{n}.

By the roots-of-unity filter,

F_{r}(z)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^{-jr}F(\omega^{j}z),\qquad\omega=e^{2\pi i/k},

so each
F_{r}
is rational. Writing

F_{r}(z)=z^{r}G_{r}(z^{k})

for a rational function
G_{r}
, we obtain

F(z)=P(z)+\sum_{\gcd(r,k)>1}z^{r}G_{r}(z^{k}).

If
d_{r}:=\gcd(r,k)>1
, then
r=d_{r}r^{\prime}
and
k=d_{r}k^{\prime}
, so

z^{r}G_{r}(z^{k})=(z^{d_{r}})^{r^{\prime}}G_{r}\bigl((z^{d_{r}})^{k^{\prime}}\bigr),

which is a rational function of
z^{d_{r}}
. This yields the required decomposition.
∎

Example 3.2
.

The single-
d
version is false. Consider

F(z)=\frac{z^{4}}{1-z^{2}}+\frac{z^{9}}{1-z^{3}}.

Its coefficients vanish at every prime index:
a_{2}=a_{3}=0
, and for every prime
p>3
one has
p\not\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and
p\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, hence
a_{p}=0
. But
F
is not of the form
P(z)+G(z^{d})
for a single
d>1
, because its support contains infinitely many powers of
2
and infinitely many powers of
3
.

We next isolate the dominant-pole obstruction to infinitely many zeros.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}(z),

and suppose that
a_{n}=0
for infinitely many
n
. Then among the poles of
F
having minimum modulus, at least two have quotient a root of unity.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, there exist integers
M\geq 1
and
r\geq 0
such that

a_{Mn+r}=0\qquad(n\geq 0)

after discarding finitely many initial terms. Write the coefficient sequence in exponential-polynomial form

a_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}P_{i}(n)\lambda_{i}^{n},

where the
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
are distinct and the
P_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[n]
are nonzero. Let

\rho:=\max_{i}|\lambda_{i}|.

The poles of minimum modulus are precisely the reciprocals of those
\lambda_{i}
with modulus
\rho
.

Expanding along the progression
Mn+r
gives

0=a_{Mn+r}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}Q_{i}(n)(\lambda_{i}^{M})^{n},

where
Q_{i}(n)=\lambda_{i}^{r}P_{i}(Mn+r)
is a nonzero polynomial. Suppose that among the dominant roots
|\lambda_{i}|=\rho
, the numbers
\lambda_{i}^{M}
are pairwise distinct. Grouping together equal bases among the
\lambda_{i}^{M}
, we obtain an identity

\sum_{\mu}R_{\mu}(n)\mu^{n}=0\qquad(n\geq 0),

with distinct nonzero bases
\mu
and polynomial coefficients
R_{\mu}
. Such an exponential-polynomial representation is unique; equivalently, the corresponding partial-fraction expansion of the generating function is unique. Hence every
R_{\mu}
must vanish identically. In particular the dominant terms vanish, contradicting the nonzero polynomials
Q_{i}
attached to them. Therefore two dominant roots satisfy

\lambda_{i}^{M}=\lambda_{j}^{M},

so
\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{j}
is a root of unity. Passing to reciprocals gives the corresponding statement for poles.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Irrationally related dominant poles may coexist with infinitely many zeros, but they are not the mechanism producing those zeros. Fix
\theta/\pi\notin\mathbf{Q}
and set

a_{n}=(1-(-1)^{n})(1+e^{in\theta}).

Then
a_{2m}=0
for every
m
, while

\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}=\frac{1}{1-z}+\frac{1}{1-e^{i\theta}z}-\frac{1}{1+z}-\frac{1}{1+e^{i\theta}z}.

The four poles all have modulus
1
, and the ratio between
1
and
e^{-i\theta}
is not a root of unity. Nevertheless, there are root-of-unity pairs
1,-1
and
e^{-i\theta},-e^{-i\theta}
, and these are what force the vanishing on the even progression.

4.
Finite specialization and periodicity over finite fields

The proof of the Lambert-series theorem uses the same finite-place setup as the proof sketch of Theorem
2.2
, but it only needs reduction to a finite residue field.

Lemma 4.1

(finite specialization)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{C}
be a finitely generated domain over
\mathbf{Z}
, and let
s\in A\setminus\{0\}
. Then there exist a finite field
k
and a ring homomorphism

\varphi:A[s^{-1}]\longrightarrow k.

In particular, one may reduce any finite amount of data from
A[s^{-1}]
modulo a finite field while keeping the image of
s
nonzero.

Proof.

The localization
A[s^{-1}]
is a nonzero finitely generated
\mathbf{Z}
-algebra, so it has a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. Then
k:=A[s^{-1}]/\mathfrak{m}
is a field finitely generated as a
\mathbf{Z}
-algebra. Its characteristic is some prime
p
, and
k
is therefore a finitely generated algebra over
\mathbf{F}_{p}
. Since
k
is also a field, Zariski’s lemma implies that
k
is a finite extension of
\mathbf{F}_{p}
, hence a finite field; see, for example, Atiyah–Macdonald
[
2
]
or Eisenbud
[
4
]
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(periodicity over finite fields)
.

Let
k
be a finite field.

(1)

If a sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}\subset k
satisfies a recurrence

u_{n+r}=a_{1}u_{n+r-1}+\cdots+a_{r}u_{n}\qquad(n\geq 0)

with
a_{r}\neq 0
, then
(u_{n})
is periodic.

(2)

If a sequence
(w_{n})_{n\geq 0}\subset k
satisfies a recurrence

w_{n+s}=d_{1}w_{n+s-1}+\cdots+d_{s}w_{n}\qquad(n\geq N)

for some
N
, then
(w_{n})
is eventually periodic.

Proof.

For the first claim, set

U_{n}:=(u_{n},u_{n+1},\dots,u_{n+r-1})\in k^{r}.

Then
U_{n+1}=MU_{n}
, where
M
is the companion matrix of the recurrence. Since
\det M=(-1)^{r+1}a_{r}\neq 0
, we have
M\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(k)
. The group
\mathrm{GL}_{r}(k)
is finite, so
M^{T}=I
for some
T\geq 1
, and therefore
U_{n+T}=U_{n}
for every
n\geq 0
.

For the second claim, form the state vectors

W_{n}:=(w_{n},w_{n+1},\dots,w_{n+s-1})\in k^{s}\qquad(n\geq N).

These evolve by a deterministic map
W_{n+1}=\Psi(W_{n})
on the finite set
k^{s}
. Hence the orbit of
W_{N}
is eventually periodic, and so is
(w_{n})
.
∎

5.
Proof of the Lambert-series rigidity theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Assume that
\gamma
and
b
are eventually linearly recurrent, where

b_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}\gamma_{d}.

We must show that
\gamma
is finitely supported.

By Lemma
2.1
, write
\gamma=\eta+f
with
f
finitely supported and
\eta
linearly recurrent from the start. Then

\sum_{d\mid n}\eta_{d}=b_{n}-\sum_{d\mid n}f_{d}.

The second term on the right is periodic, because if
f_{d}=0
for
d>N
then

\sum_{d\mid n}f_{d}=\sum_{d\leq N}f_{d}\,\mathbf{1}_{d\mid n}.

Hence the divisor-sum sequence of
\eta
is still eventually linearly recurrent. Since
\gamma
is finitely supported if and only if
\eta
is, we may replace
\gamma
by
\eta
and assume from now on that

(3)

\gamma_{n+r}=c_{1}\gamma_{n+r-1}+\cdots+c_{r}\gamma_{n}\qquad(n\geq 1)

for some
r\geq 1
and
c_{r}\neq 0
.

Suppose for contradiction that
\gamma
is not finitely supported. Then
\gamma\neq 0
, so by Möbius inversion there exists
m\geq 1
such that

(4)

S:=\sum_{d\mid m}\gamma_{d}\neq 0.

Indeed, if all such divisor sums were zero, then

\gamma_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\sum_{e\mid n/d}\gamma_{e}=0\qquad(n\geq 1).

Let
N,s
and
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
be such that

(5)

b_{n+s}=d_{1}b_{n+s-1}+\cdots+d_{s}b_{n}\qquad(n\geq N).

Choose a finitely generated domain
A\subset\mathbb{C}
over
\mathbf{Z}
containing

\{c_{1},\dots,c_{r},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r},d_{1},\dots,d_{s},b_{N},\dots,b_{N+s-1},S\}.

By (
3
) and (
5
), every
\gamma_{n}
and every
b_{n}
lies in
A[c_{r}^{-1}]
. Applying Lemma
4.1
to the localization

R:=A[(c_{r}S)^{-1}],

we obtain a finite field
k
and a homomorphism

\varphi:R\to k.

Write bars for reduction via
\varphi
.

The reduced sequence
\bar{\gamma}
satisfies the recurrence (
3
) over
k
with trailing coefficient
\bar{c}_{r}\neq 0
. By Lemma
4.2
(1),
\bar{\gamma}
is periodic; let
T_{\gamma}
be a period. The reduced sequence
\bar{b}
satisfies (
5
) for all
n\geq N
, so by Lemma
4.2
(2) it is eventually periodic; let
T_{b}
be a period valid for all
n\geq N_{0}
.

Let
T
be a common multiple of
T_{\gamma}
and
T_{b}
. By Dirichlet’s theorem, there exist infinitely many rational primes
q
with
q\equiv 1\pmod{T}
; see, for example,
[
6
, Theorem 15]
. Choose one such prime satisfying also
q\nmid m
and
mq\geq N_{0}
.

Since
mq^{2}\equiv mq\pmod{T_{b}}
and both indices are at least
N_{0}
, eventual periodicity gives

(6)

\bar{b}_{mq^{2}}=\bar{b}_{mq}.

On the other hand, because
q\nmid m
,

b_{mq^{2}}-b_{mq}=\sum_{d\mid mq^{2}}\gamma_{d}-\sum_{d\mid mq}\gamma_{d}=\sum_{e\mid m}\gamma_{eq^{2}}.

Reducing modulo
k
and using
q^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{T_{\gamma}}
, we obtain

\bar{b}_{mq^{2}}-\bar{b}_{mq}=\sum_{e\mid m}\bar{\gamma}_{eq^{2}}=\sum_{e\mid m}\bar{\gamma}_{e}=\overline{S}.

But
S
was inverted in
R
, so
\overline{S}\neq 0
in
k
. This contradicts (
6
). Therefore
\gamma
must be finitely supported.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

A sequence has rational ordinary generating function if and only if it is eventually linearly recurrent; see
[
5
, Chapter 3]
. Apply Theorem
1.1
to
\gamma
and to the coefficient sequence
b
of
L_{\gamma}
given by (
1
). If
\gamma
is finitely supported, then both
\Gamma_{\gamma}(z)
and
L_{\gamma}(z)
are obviously rational, and
\Gamma_{\gamma}(z)
is a polynomial.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is very close in spirit to the proof sketch of Theorem
2.2
. In both arguments one reduces a recurrence to a finite field, obtains periodicity or eventual periodicity there, and then compares values along carefully chosen arithmetic patterns. The difference is that Skolem-Mahler-Lech passes from periodicity modulo a finite place to identically vanishing subsequences by means of
p
-adic interpolation and Strassmann’s theorem. In the Lambert setting the special identity

b_{mq^{2}}-b_{mq}=\sum_{e\mid m}\gamma_{eq^{2}}

already produces a fixed nonzero quantity after reduction, so the contradiction is obtained before any
p
-adic analytic step is needed.

6.
Further corollaries and examples

We first record a partial consequence of the prime-index vanishing theorem. It is weaker than Theorem
1.1
, but it already shows that prime and prime-square coefficients cannot behave independently.

Proposition 6.1

(prime and prime-square coefficients)
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{C}
, and put

b_{n}:=\sum_{d\mid n}\gamma_{d}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Assume that both
\gamma
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are eventually linearly recurrent. Define

c_{n}:=b_{n}-\gamma_{n}-\gamma_{1}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Then
c=(c_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is eventually linearly recurrent and satisfies

c_{p}=0

for every prime
p
. Consequently,

\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}z^{n}=P(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}(z^{d_{j}})

for some polynomial
P(z)
, integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}>1
, and rational functions
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\in\mathbb{C}(z)
. In particular,

\gamma_{p}=0,\qquad b_{p}=\gamma_{1}

for all sufficiently large primes
p
. If
\gamma_{1}=0
, then both
\gamma_{p}
and
b_{p}
vanish for all sufficiently large primes
p
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
and
b
are eventually linearly recurrent, so is
c
. For a prime
p
we have

c_{p}=b_{p}-\gamma_{p}-\gamma_{1}=(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{p})-\gamma_{p}-\gamma_{1}=0.

Applying Proposition
3.1
to the rational generating function
\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}z^{n}
yields the stated decomposition.

By the proof of Proposition
3.1
, there exist an integer
k\geq 1
and a finite set of residue classes
R\subset\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

c_{n}\neq 0\implies n\equiv r\pmod{k}\text{ for some }r\in R,\qquad\gcd(r,k)>1.

If
p
is a sufficiently large prime with
p\nmid k
, then
p^{2}
is coprime to
k
, so
p^{2}
lies in none of the eventual support classes. Hence
c_{p^{2}}=0
. On the other hand,

c_{p^{2}}=b_{p^{2}}-\gamma_{p^{2}}-\gamma_{1}=(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{p}+\gamma_{p^{2}})-\gamma_{p^{2}}-\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{p}.

Therefore
\gamma_{p}=0
for all sufficiently large primes
p
, and then

b_{p}=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{p}=\gamma_{1}

for all sufficiently large primes
p
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Proposition
6.1
is a useful first layer of Theorem
1.1
: comparing prime and prime-square coefficients forces vanishing at prime indices. What it does
not
do by itself is relate
\gamma_{eq^{2}}
back to
\gamma_{e}
for fixed
e
. That extra step is precisely what the finite-place argument in the proof of Theorem
1.1
supplies after reduction modulo a suitable finite field.

The original operator question can be phrased as follows. Given
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, define

(T_{\gamma}G)(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}G(z^{n}),

whenever the series makes sense formally near
z=0
. Since

T_{\gamma}\!\left(\frac{z}{1-z}\right)=L_{\gamma}(z),

one test function already suffices in the recurrent setting.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be eventually linearly recurrent. If

T_{\gamma}\!\left(\frac{z}{1-z}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}}

is rational, then
\gamma
is finitely supported. In particular, if
T_{\gamma}
sends every rational function with poles at roots of unity to a rational function, then
\gamma
is finitely supported.

Corollary 6.4

(periodic coefficients)
.

If
\gamma
is periodic and
L_{\gamma}(z)
is rational, then
\gamma
is identically zero.

Proof.

A periodic sequence is linearly recurrent from the start. If it is finitely supported, it must be the zero sequence. Now apply Corollary
1.2
.
∎

Corollary 6.5

(Fibonacci example)
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the Fibonacci sequence. Then

\sum_{n\geq 1}F_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{1-z^{n}}

is not a rational function.

Proof.

The Fibonacci sequence is linearly recurrent and not finitely supported, so Corollary
1.2
applies.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is naturally nonarchimedean. One can read Lemma
4.1
as the choice of a good finite place of the finitely generated field generated by the recurrence data. After reduction at that place, recurrent sequences become periodic or eventually periodic, and the prime-power comparison
mq
versus
mq^{2}
forces a fixed nonzero divisor sum to vanish. The argument does not require the coefficients of the recurrence to be algebraic; finite generation over
\mathbf{Z}
is enough.
