Patient and diagnosis delays among migrant tuberculosis patients in suburban Shanghai, China: a 10-year retrospective study
Authors: Zichun Wang, Ziqing Qiao, Lixin Rao, Chunhua Xu, Jing Wang, Zhou Yu, Qing Wang, Weixing Shi, Xin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze the status of patient delay (PD) and diagnosis delay (DD) and explore factors associated with delays among migrant tuberculosis (TB) patients in suburban Shanghai from 2015 to 2024, aiming to provide a scientific basis for targeted TB control strategies.MethodsA retrospective surveillance-based study was conducted using pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) case data from Fengxian District, Shanghai, obtained from the Tuberculosis Information Management System. Statistical analyses, including the Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, multivariable logistic regression, and generalized least squares (GLS) analysis, were performed to examine factors associated with delays. Joinpoint regression was used to analyze temporal trends.ResultsA total of 3,178 PTB cases (1,573 locals and 1,605 migrants) were included. Compared with locals, migrants exhibited a significantly shorter median PD (8 vs. 11 days; P < 0.001) but a longer median DD (9 vs. 7 days; P < 0.001). Regarding PD, a significant upward trend was observed among migrants from 2017 to 2024 (Annual Percentage Change [APC] = 5.28%, P = 0.015). PD was more likely among female patients, those aged 30–69 years, and those seeking first diagnosis outside Fengxian. DD was less likely among patients seeking first diagnosis outside the district, but more likely among those with negative bacteriological results.ConclusionMigrants in suburban Shanghai are characterized by a pattern of “shorter PD but prolonged DD.” However, the rising trend in PD and the complex effects of cross-district care seeking highlight the urgent need for strengthened inter-district coordination and improved molecular diagnostics at the district level.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13492-3.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a critical global public health challenge. Despite the World Health Organization’s (WHO) vision to end the TB epidemic by 2035 [1], China—a high-burden country—reported an estimated 696,000 incident cases in 2024, ranking third globally [2]. Amidst rapid urbanization, large-scale population mobility not only complicates control efforts but also acts as a primary driver of cross-regional transmission [3]. Currently, China’s internal migrant population stands at 376 million, accounting for 26% of the total population [4]. Notably, this demographic has become a critical focal point for TB control, contributing nearly one-third of registered cases nationwide [5], a figure that exceeds 70% in megacities like Shanghai [6].

Current TB control strategies rely heavily on passive case finding based on symptom-driven visits, the effectiveness of which hinges on patients seeking care proactively and in a timely manner [7–8]. However, realizing this goal is particularly challenging among migrants. Constrained by barriers such as a lack of local health insurance reimbursement, concerns over income loss due to work absenteeism, and relatively lower health literacy, migrants face more formidable access barriers than locals [9]. Recent literature indicates that in high-burden countries, such prevalent patient delay (PD) and diagnosis delay (DD) not only exacerbate transmission risks and disease severity but also lead to poor treatment outcomes, posing a significant challenge to control efforts [10–12].

Although previous studies have explored disparities and heterogeneity in TB delays among migrants in certain regions of China [13–14], there remains a paucity of long-term longitudinal surveillance evidence from rapidly urbanizing suburban industrial zones—specifically those facing a structural mismatch between limited health resource allocation and surging population demands. Fengxian District represents a typical suburb characterized by “population inversion” (where migrants outnumber locals), with migrants accounting for 54.7% of the total population [15]. Unlike central urban districts, this area concentrates a large number of young migrants employed in manufacturing. Their high mobility and congregate living conditions not only result in a reported TB incidence significantly higher than that of local residents [16] but also markedly increase the difficulty of epidemic management. This complex demographic structure places significant strain on a local public health system that has not yet fully adapted to these rapid changes.

To address this gap, this study utilizes ten years of TB registry and surveillance data (2015–2024) from Fengxian District to investigate the long-term trends and key determinants of patient and diagnostic delays among migrants, in comparison with local residents. The findings aim to provide a scientific basis for optimizing active TB screening strategies and health resource allocation in resource-constrained peri-urban areas.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This retrospective study was conducted in Fengxian District, a rapidly urbanizing suburb in southern Shanghai. As of 2024, the district reported a resident population of 1.31 million, with migrants accounting for 54.7% (717,000) [15]. TB control operates under Shanghai’s “tripartite model” (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, hospital, and community), mandating that all suspected cases identified at non-designated clinics be referred to the designated hospital. Notably, Fengxian relies on a single designated secondary hospital for diagnosis and treatment. This differs from Shanghai’s central districts, which are served by 25 designated tertiary hospitals equipped with more advanced resources. Regarding policy, most migrants lack local health insurance, resulting in coverage gaps for adjunctive examinations and hospitalization compared to insured residents.

Data collection

Data were sourced from the Shanghai Municipal Statistical Survey System for Disease Control and Prevention. This system employs standardized electronic reporting, maintaining a data completeness rate exceeding 99%. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, residency, and location of the first-diagnosis facility), clinical information (bacteriological status, treatment category, and case origination), and key diagnostic and treatment time points (dates of symptom onset, first medical visit, and confirmed diagnosis). In accordance with diagnostic criteria [17], tuberculous pleurisy cases were included in the pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) statistics to ensure study consistency, given that their diagnosis, treatment, and prevention and control strategies align closely with those for pulmonary tuberculosis.

Study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

We initially extracted all PTB cases registered in Fengxian District, Shanghai, between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2024. The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with active PTB, including tuberculous pleurisy. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) confirmed non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection; (2) other forms of extrapulmonary tuberculosis; (3) incomplete records with missing key dates; and (4) logical inconsistencies in critical temporal data resulting in negative time intervals (e.g., a diagnosis date recorded prior to the initial healthcare visit). The final analytic cohort consisted of 3,178 patients. The detailed case selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Definitions

Delays

In this study, PD was defined as the interval between symptom onset and the first visit to a medical institution, and DD was defined as the interval between the initial medical consultation and confirmation of PTB diagnosis at a designated facility. For analysis, a 14-day cut-off was applied to both PD and DD in accordance with relevant guidance and previous studies [18–20].

Bacteriological results

Bacteriologically positive cases were defined by a positive result in sputum smear microscopy, culture, or molecular testing.

Case origination

(1) Passive case finding: Patients who self-presented at healthcare facilities due to symptoms; (2) Active case finding: Patients identified through organized screening programs (e.g., contact tracing, screening of high-risk groups) or via referrals following incidental findings during routine health examinations. In Fengxian District, this specifically comprised mandatory pre-employment chest X-ray screening for workers in the manufacturing sector, symptom screening and contact tracing in workplaces and schools, and routine community-based health examinations for the elderly (aged ≥ 60 years).

Treatment category

(1) New: Those who had never received anti-tuberculosis drugs or had been treated for less than one month; (2) Retreated: Those who had previously received anti-tuberculosis treatment for one month or longer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3). Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or chi-square test, as appropriate. To identify factors associated with PD and DD, we used two complementary modeling approaches: multivariable logistic regression with a predefined 14-day cutoff and generalized least squares (GLS) models with log-transformed delay days as continuous outcomes. Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature [20–21] and clinical plausibility. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 indicating no multicollinearity. Model fit and discrimination for the logistic models were evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), respectively. Temporal trends were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0; Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA). The optimal number of joinpoints was determined based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and statistical significance was assessed using a Monte Carlo permutation test (4,499 permutations). Trends were quantified as the annual percentage change (APC).

Sample size and missing data

Given the retrospective design using district-wide surveillance data, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, we included all eligible cases registered over the 10-year study period to maximize statistical power. The final sample size (N = 3,178) was considered sufficient for the intended multivariable analyses. As missing data for key variables were minimal (< 2%), a complete-case analysis approach was adopted.

Results

Characteristics of local patients compared with those of migrant patients

A total of 3,178 PTB cases were registered in Fengxian District during the study period (2015–2024), comprising 1,605 (50.5%) migrants and 1,573 (49.5%) local residents. Compared with locals, migrants exhibited a significantly shorter PD (median, 8 vs. 11 days; P < 0.001) and a lower proportion of delays exceeding 14 days (36.7% vs. 42.2%; P = 0.002). In contrast, the median DD was significantly longer among migrants than among locals (9 vs. 7 days; P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Patient delays and diagnosis delays in different years

Joinpoint regression analysis identified distinct temporal trends. Among the overall PTB patients, no significant trend was observed for DD; however, PD exhibited a significant upward trajectory from 2020 to 2024 (APC = 8.04%, P = 0.022). Stratified analyses revealed that for migrants, PD increased significantly between 2017 and 2024 (APC = 5.28%, P = 0.015). Conversely, among locals, a significant increase in DD was observed from 2017 to 2024 (APC = 5.6%, P = 0.045) (Fig. 2).

Trends in patient choices at first-diagnosis hospitals, 2015–2024

Joinpoint regression analysis identified distinct monotonic trends from 2015 to 2024 (0 joinpoints). The proportion of patients seeking first-diagnosis within Fengxian demonstrated a consistent, significant decline among both migrants (APC = − 9.21%, P < 0.001) and local residents (APC = − 8.30%, P < 0.001). In contrast, the proportion of patients seeking care in other districts exhibited a significant upward trend for both groups (Migrants: APC = 9.46%, P < 0.001; Local residents: APC = 6.32%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with patient and diagnosis delays in the general population

In the general population, multivariable logistic regression showed that female (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 1.271, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.084–1.490), age 30–49 years (AOR = 1.273, 95% CI: 1.044–1.553), age 50–69 years (AOR = 1.391, 95% CI: 1.122–1.725), and first-diagnosis in other districts (AOR = 1.200, 95% CI: 1.037–1.388) were associated with higher odds of PD, whereas active case finding was associated with lower odds of PD (AOR = 0.325, 95% CI: 0.188–0.532). For DD, age ≥ 70 years (AOR = 1.466, 95% CI: 1.105–1.945) and negative bacteriological results (AOR = 1.987, 95% CI: 1.694–2.330) were associated with higher odds, while first-diagnosis in other districts was associated with lower odds (AOR = 0.520, 95% CI: 0.444–0.609) (Table 2). The AUCs were 0.560 (95% CI: 0.540–0.580) for PD and 0.632 (95% CI: 0.610–0.653) for DD. GLS analyses yielded broadly similar patterns of association (Supplementary Table S1).

Factors associated with delays among migrants

Among migrants, female (AOR = 1.375, 95% CI: 1.104–1.712), age 30–49 years (AOR = 1.298, 95% CI: 1.031–1.634), age 50–69 years (AOR = 1.599, 95% CI: 1.191–2.145), and first-diagnosis in other districts (AOR = 1.248, 95% CI: 1.015–1.534) were associated with higher odds of PD, whereas negative bacteriological results were associated with lower odds of PD (AOR = 0.785, 95% CI: 0.638–0.965). Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with patient and diagnosis delays among migrants

Factors associated with delays among locals

Among locals, no variables were significantly associated with PD in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. For DD, first-diagnosis in other districts was associated with lower odds of delay (AOR = 0.410, 95% CI: 0.326–0.514), whereas negative bacteriological results were associated with higher odds (AOR = 1.859, 95% CI: 1.478–2.343) (Supplementary Table S3). The AUC for the DD model was 0.648 (95% CI: 0.617–0.678). The GLS models confirmed the main associations for DD and additionally suggested a possible age-related pattern for PD (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Drawing on a decade of surveillance data from Fengxian District, Shanghai, this study examined the long-term trends and associated factors of PD and DD among patients with TB, with a particular focus on migrants. Overall, migrants exhibited a distinct pattern of “shorter PD but longer DD.” This finding diverges from studies in western China [22], indicating that healthcare-seeking behaviors among migrants in resource-rich megacities may differ from those in less-developed settings.

One hypothesis that may be relevant to the shorter PD observed among migrants is the “healthy migrant effect”. Our data showed that the median age of migrants was significantly lower than that of locals. This age distribution aligns with the regional demographic pattern, where the general migrant population is predominantly concentrated in the 21–50 age group [23]. This pattern is consistent with a self-selection mechanism where younger, healthier individuals migrate for work [24]. To maintain workforce participation, this population may be more likely to seek care promptly after symptom onset [25]. However, given the absence of direct data on clinical severity and comorbidities, this explanation remains hypothesis-generating and warrants further verification, ideally with additional clinical indicators.

It is noteworthy that despite a low baseline, PD among migrants showed a significant upward trend from 2017 to 2024, paralleling the annual rise in cross-district care-seeking within this group. This pattern coincided with increasing patient flow toward central urban areas in pursuit of higher-quality services [26–27]. As a far-suburban district, the objective disparity in medical resources between Fengxian and downtown Shanghai may be associated with a “siphon effect” [28]. Regardless of whether driven by proximity or preference, cross-district care-seeking may be linked to a longer diagnostic pathway due to additional travel burdens and potential inefficiencies in cross-regional referrals and information sharing [29]. This underscores the importance of strengthening inter-regional coordination in TB care.

Female patients and working-age adults (30–69 years) were more likely to experience PD, which may be associated with domestic burdens [30] and work-health conflicts [31], respectively. To address this, workplace-based education and screening strategies may help proactively reach these time-constrained populations and bridge the gap in passive case finding.

Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic may have been associated with changes in delay trends, with PD rising markedly post-2020. This increase may have been related to fear of nosocomial infection [32] and symptom overlap between TB and COVID-19 [33]. These findings highlight the importance of establishing differentiated screening pathways and enhancing public education during health emergencies.

The persistently higher DD among migrants suggests potential structural challenges within the healthcare system. From a clinical perspective, negative bacteriological results were associated with longer DD, as these cases often require additional examinations and clinical reassessment before confirmation, a process consistent with a longer time to diagnosis. Limited diagnostic capacity in primary care [34] and socioeconomic barriers (e.g., insurance barriers and work conflicts) [35] may also be associated with longer diagnostic timelines.

Limitations

This study used long-term surveillance data to fill the knowledge gap regarding TB delays among migrants in megacity suburbs. However, findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations: First, the determination of delay times may be subject to misclassification and recall bias, particularly for symptom onset dates. Second, the surveillance data lacked key clinical and social variables—such as specific diagnostic modality, symptom severity, comorbidities (e.g., HIV), health insurance type, income level, and distance to care—limiting our ability to fully control for potential confounding. Third, the absence of qualitative data makes it difficult to deeply analyze the psychological and social mechanisms behind care-seeking decisions. Fourth, as this study was confined to a suburban district of Shanghai, caution should be exercised when extrapolating results to economically less developed regions. Fifth, given the observational and retrospective design, our findings should be interpreted as epidemiological associations rather than causal effects. In addition, because multiple subgroup analyses were conducted, these findings may be subject to an increased risk of multiple testing and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The modest AUC values indicate limited model discrimination and explanatory ability; therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting these models, and they should not be used for individual-level prediction.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found that migrant TB patients in Fengxian District exhibited a pattern of “shorter PD but prolonged DD,” despite an upward trend in PD in recent years. Our findings suggest that cross-district care seeking and negative bacteriological results were associated with these delays and may help identify areas for improving TB service delivery. Potential measures include expanding molecular diagnostics (e.g., GeneXpert), strengthening inter-district information sharing and referral, and implementing targeted interventions, such as extended clinic hours or workplace screenings, for groups more likely to experience delay.
