Ringmaster LMO : Asynchronous Linear Minimization Oracle Momentum Method
Abdurakhmon Sadiev, Artavazd Maranjyan, Ivan Ilin, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
Muon has recently emerged as a strong alternative to AdamW for training neural networks, with encouraging large-scale pretraining results and growing evidence that matrix-structured updates can be faster in practice. Yet Muon, and more generally Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) based methods, are typically used synchronously. This is problematic in heterogeneous distributed systems, where workers complete gradient computations at different speeds and synchronous training must repeatedly wait for slower workers. In this work, we introduce Ringmaster LMO , an asynchronous LMO-based momentum method for unconstrained stochastic nonconvex optimization. Our method builds on the delay-thresholding idea of Ringmaster ASGD . For SGD -type methods, Ringmaster ASGD achieves optimal time complexity by discarding overly stale gradients. Ringmaster LMO extends this mechanism to general LMO-based updates. We establish convergence guarantees under generalized ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smoothness and further develop a parameter-agnostic variant with decreasing stepsizes and adaptive delay thresholds. Finally, we translate our iteration guarantees into time complexity bounds under heterogeneous worker computation times. In the classical Euclidean smooth setting, these bounds recover the optimal time complexity of Ringmaster ASGD . Experiments on stochastic quadratic problems and NanoChat language-model pretraining show that the advantages of Ringmaster LMO grow with system heterogeneity and that the method outperforms strong synchronous and asynchronous baselines.

Introduction

For more than a decade,
Adam

(
25
)
has been the default choice for training large neural networks, with its decoupled weight-decay variant
AdamW

(
31
)
more recently becoming the default variant.
For matrix-valued layers, however, these methods treat each weight matrix as a vector, rather than explicitly using its matrix structure.
Recently,
22
proposed the
Muon
optimizer, showing that using the matrix structure of hidden-layer weights can be beneficial.

Muon
forms the update direction from the matrix gradient via approximate orthogonalization.
This matrix-structured update has led to strong empirical results, including the
nanoGPT
speedrun where
Muon
improves on the
AdamW
baseline
(
21
)
.
Recent pretraining benchmarks also report that
Muon
can be substantially more compute-efficient than
AdamW

(
44
;
30
;
51
)
.
More generally,
Muon
fits into the framework of linear minimization oracle (LMO)-based optimizers introduced by
40

This viewpoint makes the geometry of the update explicit: the choice of norm determines the update direction, and for matrix parameters the spectral-norm ball recovers the orthogonalized direction used by
Muon
.

Despite this progress, LMO-based optimizers are still typically used within synchronous training pipelines.
When several workers compute gradients in parallel, the usual synchronous implementation must wait for the slowest worker before forming the next update.
Asynchronous methods, going back at least to
HOGWILD!

(
42
)
, avoid this synchronization barrier by updating the model whenever a worker returns a gradient.
The price is delay: a gradient arriving at iteration
k
may have been computed at an older iterate with delay
\delta_{k}\geq 0
.
Recent analyses have clarified the effect of such delays for asynchronous
SGD

(
49
;
37
;
34
)
.
These results, however, are built for
SGD
-type updates in Euclidean setting, and do not directly apply to LMO-based optimizers.

This leaves the central question of this paper:

Can asynchronous LMO-based methods recover the optimal time complexity known for Euclidean setting.

We show that this can be done.
Building on the delay-thresholding mechanism of
Ringmaster ASGD

(
37
)
and the LMO-based momentum framework of
40
, we introduce an asynchronous LMO-based method for unconstrained stochastic nonconvex optimization.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first asynchronous LMO-based method with time complexity guarantees under heterogeneous worker computation times.
In the classical Euclidean smooth setting, the bound recovers the optimal time complexity of
Ringmaster ASGD
.

Related work

We review the prior work most closely related to our setting.

LMO-based and matrix-structured optimizers.

The empirical success of

Subsequent work has refined this theory under generalized smoothness

These works are synchronous: they do not analyze delayed gradients produced by asynchronous workers, nor do they provide time complexity guarantees under heterogeneous computation times.

Asynchronous stochastic optimization.

Asynchronous stochastic optimization removes the synchronization barrier by updating the model whenever a worker returns a gradient
(
47
;
1
;
42
;
38
)
.
The main analytical difficulty is delay: the gradient used at iteration

Classical analyses control this error through bounded-delay assumptions

;

, while more recent work uses delay-adaptive stepsizes

;

;

For heterogeneous worker speeds, the relevant objective is time complexity rather than only counting iterations.

(

)
as well as variance-reduced parallel stochastic optimization
(

)
.

Closest baselines and parameter agnosticism.

The closest asynchronous baseline to our method is

Overall, our work connects these three lines by extending delay-thresholding from Euclidean

2
Preliminaries and setup

This section fixes the optimization model, the LMO geometry, the distributed computation model, and the assumptions used in the analysis.

2.1
Optimization model and LMO geometry

We study the nonconvex stochastic optimization problem

\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(x)\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(x;\xi)\right]\right\},

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the model parameter,
\mathcal{D}
is the data distribution,
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}
is a data sample, and
f(x;\xi)
is the corresponding loss.
Let
\left\|\cdot\right\|
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Following
40
, the update direction is defined through a linear minimization oracle (LMO) over the unit ball of this norm:

\operatorname{lmo}(y)\in\argmin_{u~:~\left\|u\right\|\leq 1}\left\langle y,u\right\rangle\quad\text{for }y\neq 0,\qquad\operatorname{lmo}(0)\coloneqq 0.

When the minimizer is not unique, any minimizer may be chosen.
This definition implies that the magnitude of the update is always bounded because
\left\|\operatorname{lmo}(y)\right\|\leq 1
.

2.2
Function and oracle assumptions

We use the following assumptions throughout the convergence analysis.

Assumption 2.1

(Lower boundedness)
.

There exists
f^{*}>-\infty
such that
f(x)\geq f^{*}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We define
\Delta_{0}\coloneqq f(x_{0})-f^{*},
where
x_{0}
is the starting point of the optimization methods.

Assumption 2.2

(Unbiased stochastic gradients with bounded variance)
.

For every
\xi
, the function
f(x;\xi)
is differentiable with respect to its first argument
x
.
Moreover, the stochastic gradients are unbiased and have bounded variance
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\nabla f(x;\xi)\right]=\nabla f(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Assumption 2.3

(
(L_{0},L_{1})
–smoothness)
.

There exist constants
L_{0},L_{1}\geq 0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|_{*}\leq\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\right\|_{*}\right)\left\|x-y\right\|.

Here
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}
denotes the dual norm associated with
\left\|\cdot\right\|
, i.e.

\left\|y\right\|_{*}\coloneqq\sup_{u~:~\left\|u\right\|\leq 1}\left\langle y,u\right\rangle.

2.3
reduces to the standard
L_{0}
-smoothness condition with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|
when
L_{1}=0
.
When
L_{1}>0
, the effective local smoothness constant is allowed to grow with the gradient norm, which motivates generalized smoothness models for objectives arising in modern machine learning
(
8
;
41
)
.

2.2
controls stochastic-gradient noise in the Euclidean norm, whereas
2.3
is stated in the norm induced by
\left\|\cdot\right\|
and its dual.
We denote by

\rho\coloneqq\sup_{z\neq 0}\frac{\left\|z\right\|_{*}}{\left\|z\right\|_{2}}

the corresponding norm-equivalence constant, so that
\left\|z\right\|_{*}\leq\rho\left\|z\right\|_{2}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.3
Distributed computation model

We consider a distributed setting with
n
workers.
We write
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Each worker
i
has access to the same data distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
To model heterogeneous worker speeds, we use the fixed computation model of
39
.
We extend the analysis to arbitrarily changing computation times in
Appendix

E
, while keeping the fixed model in the main text for a clearer presentation.

Assumption 2.4

(Fixed computation model)
.

For each worker
i\in[n]
, computing a stochastic gradient
\nabla f(x;\xi)
takes
\tau_{i}>0
seconds.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}
.
For
m\in[n]
, define

H_{m}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{-1},

the harmonic mean of the first
m
computation times
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m}
.

Since
\tau_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\tau_{n}
, the sequence
H_{m}
is non-decreasing in
m
.

Throughout this paper, we assume zero-latency communication between the workers and the server, adopting the standard modeling assumption used in prior asynchronous optimization literature
(
39
;
26
;
49
;
37
)
.
While communication costs are undeniably important in practice, modeling them properly requires the adoption of specific mechanisms like compression or local steps.
Incorporating those techniques introduces a separate set of theoretical challenges that are largely orthogonal to our primary objective: understanding asynchronous dynamics in the presence of heterogeneous computation speeds.

method

Parameter-agnostic case

We now turn to the parameter-agnostic case and begin with its iteration complexity.

Theorem 4.4

(Parameter-agnostic iteration complexity; proof in

Suppose Assumptions

In the Euclidean case, our parameter-agnostic bound matches that of

Time complexity

We now analyze the time complexity of

in the parameter-agnostic case, where the threshold

This case is slightly more difficult than the fixed-threshold case below.

Lemma 4.5

(Time complexity for a square-root delay threshold; proof in
Section

D.2
)
.

Consider

1
with delay-threshold sequence given in

4.4

Under

Proof sketch.

We group iterations by the integer threshold level

\mathcal{K}_{r}\coloneqq\{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}\mid\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor=r\}

∎

4.4

Theorem 4.6

(Total time complexity)
.

Under the assumptions and parameter choices of

4.4

be the number of iterations needed to reach an

Up to logarithmic factors, this bound has the same worker-heterogeneity dependence as

6
Conclusion

We introduced
Ringmaster LMO
, an asynchronous LMO-based momentum method for stochastic nonconvex optimization, together with a parameter-agnostic variant.
Our analysis establishes iteration and time complexity guarantees under generalized
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness and shows that, in the classical Euclidean smooth setting, the time bound recovers the optimal time complexity known for
Ringmaster ASGD
.
Experiments on stochastic quadratics and NanoChat pretraining further show that the benefits of asynchrony become more pronounced as worker-speed heterogeneity increases.

An important limitation of our analysis is that it assumes a homogeneous data distribution across workers; extending both the method and the theory to data-heterogeneous distributed settings is a natural next step.
Another limitation is that lower bounds for the asynchronous time complexity of general LMO-based methods in arbitrary norms remain open.
This question is challenging because matching lower bounds are not known even for the corresponding synchronous case. Developing such lower bounds for the parameter-agnostic case appears even harder.

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right)\exp\left(L_{1}\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|\right)\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})+\eta_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\operatorname{lmo}(m_{k+1})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right)\exp\left(L_{1}\eta_{k}\right)\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})+\eta_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-m_{k+1},\operatorname{lmo}(m_{k+1})\right\rangle+\eta_{k}\left\langle m_{k+1},\operatorname{lmo}(m_{k+1})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right)\exp\left(L_{1}\eta_{k}\right)\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})+\eta_{k}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-m_{k+1}\right\|_{*}-\eta_{k}\left\|m_{k+1}\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right)\exp\left(L_{1}\eta_{k}\right)\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})+2\eta_{k}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-m_{k+1}\right\|_{*}-\eta_{k}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right)\exp\left(L_{1}\eta_{k}\right)\eta_{k}^{2}~.

In the steps above, the second inequality follows from the property
\left\|\operatorname{lmo}(\cdot)\right\|\leq 1
.
The third inequality applies Hölder’s inequality alongside the definition of the linear minimization oracle (which implies
\left\langle m_{k+1},\operatorname{lmo}(m_{k+1})\right\rangle=-\left\|m_{k+1}\right\|_{*}
).
Finally, the last step utilizes the triangle inequality.

Rearranging the terms to isolate
\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}
and summing over
k=0,\dots,K-1
yields the desired result.
∎

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}{K}}+\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}+L_{1}\Delta_{0}}{K}+\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{L_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rho\sigma+\frac{L_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\Delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}(\rho\sigma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}+\frac{\rho\sigma}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}{K}}+\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}}{K}+\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{L_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rho\sigma+\frac{L_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\Delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}(\rho\sigma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}+\frac{\rho\sigma}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\right).

Therefore, iteration complexity is defined as

K_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{L_{0}\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}+\frac{(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

∎

\displaystyle\leq 528L_{1}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{3}\exp(4L_{1}\hat{\eta}k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}})k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}.

Since
k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\leq 70L_{1}\hat{\eta}
, we obtain the coarse uniform bound:

\text{⑥}\leq 36960L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{280\hat{\eta}^{2}L_{1}^{2}}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}.

Bounding
⑦
.

The second term mimics the structure of the first but scales with
L_{1}
and the initial gradient norm. Following the exact same integral approximation:

⑦

\displaystyle\leq 33L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}-1}\frac{1}{(k+1)}(4\hat{\eta}k^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}-3\hat{\eta})\exp\left(L_{1}\hat{\eta}\left(\frac{3}{(k+1)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}+4k^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}-3\right)\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 132L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}-1}\frac{1}{(k+1)^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}}\exp(L_{1}\hat{\eta}\cdot 4k^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 132L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{3}\exp(4L_{1}\hat{\eta}k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}})\int_{1}^{k_{0}}\frac{1}{t^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}}\,dt\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 528L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{3}\exp(4L_{1}\hat{\eta}k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}})k_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right].

Again, evaluating at the threshold
k_{0}
, we obtain:

\text{⑦}\leq 36960L_{1}^{3}\hat{\eta}^{4}\exp(280L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right].

Bounding
⑧
.

The third term lacks the
r_{k}
multiplier, rendering it a pure harmonic series weighted by an exponential. Bounding the exponential globally by its value at
k=0
and applying the standard harmonic bound
\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\frac{1}{k+1}\leq\log(k_{0})+1
:

⑧

\displaystyle\leq 33L_{1}\hat{\eta}^{2}\exp(3L_{1}\hat{\eta})\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\frac{1}{(k+1)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 33L_{1}\hat{\eta}^{2}\exp(3L_{1}\hat{\eta})(\log k_{0}+1){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 132L_{1}\hat{\eta}^{2}\exp(3L_{1}\hat{\eta})\max\{1,\log\lceil 70L_{1}\hat{\eta}\rceil\}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right].

Final assembly of the bound.

Combining
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{3}
, moving the
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]
to the left-hand side, and inserting our explicit bounds for the transient phase (
\text{⑥},\text{⑦},\text{⑧}
), we yield the global inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}+\frac{3}{2}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{L_{1}\hat{\eta}}+36L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{L_{1}\hat{\eta}}(\log K+1)

\displaystyle\quad+72L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{1.03L_{1}\hat{\eta}}(\log K+1)+11\rho\sigma\hat{\eta}(\log K+1)

\displaystyle\quad+36960L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{280\hat{\eta}^{2}L_{1}^{2}}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+36960L_{1}^{3}\hat{\eta}^{4}e^{280L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+132L_{1}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{3L_{1}\hat{\eta}}\max\{1,\log\lceil 70L_{1}\hat{\eta}\rceil\}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\right].

By Lemma
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle 4\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}^{2}}{L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}}\geq\begin{cases}\frac{\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}^{2}}{2L_{0}},&\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\leq\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}};\\
\frac{\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}}{2L_{1}},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus, we have

\left\|\nabla f(x_{0})\right\|_{*}\leq\max\left\{8L_{1}\Delta_{0},\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\right\}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}+\frac{3}{2}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{L_{1}\hat{\eta}}+36L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{L_{1}\hat{\eta}}(\log K+1)

\displaystyle\quad+72L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{1.03L_{1}\hat{\eta}}(\log K+1)+11\rho\sigma\hat{\eta}(\log K+1)

\displaystyle\quad+36960L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{280\hat{\eta}^{2}L_{1}^{2}}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+295680L_{1}^{4}\hat{\eta}^{4}e^{280L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}}\Delta_{0}+36960L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{280L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+1056L_{1}^{2}\hat{\eta}^{2}e^{3L_{1}\hat{\eta}}\max\{1,\log\lceil 70L_{1}\hat{\eta}\rceil\}\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle\quad+132e^{3L_{1}\hat{\eta}}\max\{1,\log\lceil 70L_{1}\hat{\eta}\rceil\}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}.

Standard smoothness.

In this case we have
L_{1}=0
. Thus, taking
\hat{\eta}=\eta
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}+11\rho\sigma\eta(\log K+1)+228L_{0}\eta^{2}(\log K+1)~.

Dividing by
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}
, collecting the terms and consolidating coefficients using Big-
\mathcal{O}
notation, we arrive at the envelope
\Psi(L_{0},0)=\nicefrac{{\Delta_{0}}}{{\eta}}+\rho\sigma+L_{0}\eta
.
Finally, bounding the minimum gradient by the weighted average over
K
steps:

\min_{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\Psi(L_{0},0)\log K}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}\right).

Generalized smoothness:
L_{1}
is unknown.

In this case we have
L_{1}>0
. Thus, taking
\hat{\eta}=\nicefrac{{\eta}}{{17}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(e^{L_{1}^{2}\eta^{2}}\Delta_{0}+\rho\sigma\hat{\eta}(\log K+1)+e^{L_{1}^{2}\eta^{2}}L_{0}\hat{\eta}^{2}(\log K+1)\right).

Dividing by
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}
and collecting the terms, we arrive at the envelope
\Psi(L_{0},L_{1})
. Finally, bounding the minimum gradient by the weighted average over
K
steps:

\min_{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\Psi(L_{0},L_{1})\log K}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}\right),

which completes the proof.

Generalized smoothness:
L_{1}
is known.

Thus, taking
\hat{\eta}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{17L_{1}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta_{0}+\rho\frac{\sigma}{L_{1}}(\log K+1)+\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}^{2}}(\log K+1)\right).

Dividing by
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}
and collecting the terms, we arrive at the envelope
\Psi(L_{0},L_{1})=L_{1}\Delta_{0}+\rho\sigma+\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}
. Finally, bounding the minimum gradient by the weighted average over

\min_{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\Psi(L_{0},L_{1})\log K}{K^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Time complexity analysis under fixed computation times

We now prove the time complexity bounds under
2.4
.
We start with the case of a fixed delay threshold.

Lemma
4.2

(Duration of
R
updates
(
37
, Lemma 4.1)
)
.

Let the delay threshold of
Algorithm

1
be fixed as
R_{k}\equiv R
.
Under
2.4
, the time needed to complete any
R
consecutive updates is at most

t(R)\coloneqq 2\min_{m\in[n]}H_{m}\left(1+\frac{R}{m}\right).

As an immediate corollary, if the threshold remains fixed at
R
for the first
K
updates, then partitioning these updates into
\lceil\nicefrac{{K}}{{R}}\rceil
consecutive blocks and applying
Lemma

4.2
yields

T(K)\leq\left\lceil\frac{K}{R}\right\rceil t(R)~.

This blockwise estimate is the key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

4.3
.

D.1
Proof of
Theorem

4.3

We restate the theorem for convenience.

Theorem
4.3

(Fixed-parameter total time complexity)
.

Under the assumptions and parameter choices of
Theorem

4.1
, under
2.4
, the time complexity of reaching an
\varepsilon
-stationary point satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\min_{m\in[n]}H_{m}\left(\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{m\varepsilon^{4}}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{mL_{0}\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{m\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right).

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon^{2}},

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{L_{1}\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{L_{0}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}},

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{L_{1}^{2}\Delta_{0}(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{L_{0}\varepsilon^{2}},

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{(\rho\sigma)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

and let
\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}+C_{\varepsilon}+D_{\varepsilon}+E_{\varepsilon}
.
By
Theorem

4.1
, it suffices to take

K_{\varepsilon}=c\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}
for a universal constant
c\geq 1
.

Recall that

\alpha=\begin{cases}1,&\sigma=0,\\
\min\left\{1,\frac{\Delta_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}L_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\rho\sigma K_{\varepsilon}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\right\},&\sigma>0,\end{cases}\qquad R=\frac{1}{\alpha}~.

We partition the
K_{\varepsilon}
updates into
\lceil\nicefrac{{K_{\varepsilon}}}{{R}}\rceil
consecutive blocks and apply
Lemma

4.2
.
This gives

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\lceil\frac{K_{\varepsilon}}{R}\right\rceil t(R)=2\left\lceil K_{\varepsilon}\alpha\right\rceil\min_{m\in[n]}H_{m}\left(1+\frac{1}{m\alpha}\right).

Up to constants hidden in
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
, we can ignore the ceiling, so the last inequality gives

T_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(\min_{m\in[n]}H_{m}\left(K_{\varepsilon}\alpha+\frac{K_{\varepsilon}}{m}\right)\right).

It remains to bound
K_{\varepsilon}\alpha
.
Since
K_{\varepsilon}=c\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}
, it suffices to bound
\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}\alpha
.
If
\sigma=0
, then
C_{\varepsilon}=D_{\varepsilon}=E_{\varepsilon}=0
and
\alpha=1
, so

\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}\alpha=A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}
.
If
\sigma>0
, then
\alpha\leq 1
and

(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon})\alpha\leq A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}~.

Moreover, since
c\geq 1
and
C_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon},E_{\varepsilon}
are terms in
\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}
,
we have
C_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon},E_{\varepsilon}\leq K_{\varepsilon}
, and

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}}=A_{\varepsilon}~,

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq D_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}}\leq D_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}}=B_{\varepsilon}~,

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq E_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}}\leq E_{\varepsilon}\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\rho\sigma\sqrt{E_{\varepsilon}}}=\frac{\sqrt{L_{0}\Delta_{0}}}{\varepsilon}=:G_{\varepsilon}~.

Hence
\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}\alpha=\mathcal{O}(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}+G_{\varepsilon})
, and therefore

K_{\varepsilon}\alpha=\mathcal{O}(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}+G_{\varepsilon})
.
Also,

\frac{K_{\varepsilon}}{m}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}+C_{\varepsilon}+D_{\varepsilon}+E_{\varepsilon}}{m}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}+\frac{C_{\varepsilon}+D_{\varepsilon}+E_{\varepsilon}}{m}\right),

where the last equality uses
m\geq 1
.
Combining the last three bounds gives the claimed bound.
∎

The parameter-agnostic case is slightly more delicate, since the threshold does not remain fixed throughout the run.
To handle this, we extend the same blockwise argument to a non-decreasing threshold sequence.

Lemma D.1

(Time for non-decreasing adaptive threshold)
.

Let
K\geq 1
, and let
R_{k}\geq 1
be a non-decreasing delay threshold.
For each integer
r\in\{\lfloor R_{0}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor\}
, let

\mathcal{K}_{r}\coloneqq\{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}\mid\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor=r\}

denote the corresponding block of iterations, and define

N_{r}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{r}|}{r}\right\rceil.

Then the time needed by
Algorithm

1
to complete its first
K
updates satisfies

T(K)\leq\sum_{r=\lfloor R_{0}\rfloor}^{\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor}N_{r}\,t(r)~.

Proof.

Split the iterations
\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}
into the blocks
\mathcal{K}_{r}
.
Since
R_{k}
is non-decreasing, all iterations in each
\mathcal{K}_{r}
appear next to each other in time.
For a fixed
r
, partition
\mathcal{K}_{r}
into

N_{r}=\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{r}|}{r}\right\rceil

consecutive groups, each containing at most
r
iterations.
Throughout each such group,
\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor=r
, and hence
R_{k}\geq r
.
Replacing the actual threshold by the fixed threshold
r
can only make the rule more conservative.
If a group contains exactly
r
iterations, then
Lemma

4.2
shows that it is completed within time at most
t(r)
.
If the last group contains fewer than
r
iterations, its duration is still at most
t(r)
, since completing fewer than
r
updates cannot take longer than completing
r
updates under the same threshold.
Therefore, the total time spent on all iterations in
\mathcal{K}_{r}
is at most
N_{r}t(r)
.
Summing this bound over all values of
r
gives

T(K)\leq\sum_{r=\lfloor R_{0}\rfloor}^{\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor}N_{r}\,t(r)~.

∎

D.2
Proof of
Lemma

4.5

We now specialize
Lemma

D.1
to the square-root threshold schedule.

Lemma
4.5

(Time complexity for square-root threshold)
.

Let the delay threshold be
R_{0}=1
and
R_{k}=\sqrt{k}
for
k\geq 1
.
Under
2.4
, for any
K\geq 1
, the time needed to complete
K
iterations satisfies

T(K)=\mathcal{O}\left(\min_{m\in[n]}H_{m}\left(\sqrt{K}+\frac{K}{m}\right)\right).

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

D.1
with
R_{0}=1
and
R_{k}=\sqrt{k}
for
k\geq 1
.
For any integer
r\geq 1
and any
k\geq 1
, the condition
\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor=r
is equivalent to

r^{2}\leq k<(r+1)^{2}.

The extra iteration
k=0
belongs to
\mathcal{K}_{1}
.
Therefore, for every
r\geq 1
,

|\mathcal{K}_{r}|\leq 2r+2~.

Hence,

N_{r}=\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{r}|}{r}\right\rceil\leq\left\lceil\frac{2r+2}{r}\right\rceil\leq 4~.

Let
r_{\max}\coloneqq\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor

Since

, it follows that

∎

Appendix E
Time complexity under time-varying computation rates

2.4
assumes that each worker has a constant gradient-computation time throughout the run.
This leads to explicit time complexity bounds in terms of the harmonic means
H_{m}
, but it does not capture clusters whose worker speeds change over time because of interruptions or fluctuating load
(
12
;
28
;
36
)
.
In this section we therefore move to a more general model with time-varying computation rates.

E.1
Universal computation model

We use the universal computation model of
50
.

Assumption E.1

(Universal computation model)
.

For each worker
i\in[n]
, there exists a nonnegative function

p_{i}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

that is continuous almost everywhere.
For any
0\leq T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, the number of stochastic gradients completed by worker
i
during the interval
[T_{1},T_{2}]
is

N_{i}(T_{1},T_{2})\coloneqq\left\lfloor\int_{T_{1}}^{T_{2}}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor.

This assumption includes
2.4
as a special case: if
p_{i}(s)=1/\tau_{i}
for all
s\geq 0
, then

N_{i}(T_{1},T_{2})=\left\lfloor\frac{T_{2}-T_{1}}{\tau_{i}}\right\rfloor.

E.2
Fixed-parameter schedule

We begin with the fixed-parameter regime, which is the counterpart of
Theorem

4.3
under
E.1
.
Unlike in the fixed computation model, the duration of a block of
R
updates now depends on the starting time of that block.
For this reason, the resulting time bound is recursive rather than explicit.

Lemma E.2

(Duration of
R
updates under the universal computation model
(
37
, Lemma 5.1)
)
.

Assume
E.1
.
Let the delay threshold of
Algorithm

1
be fixed as
R_{k}\equiv R
.
Assume that an iteration starts at time
T_{0}
.
Then the next
R
updates of
Algorithm

1
are completed within elapsed time

t(R;T_{0})\coloneqq\min\left\{t\geq 0:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{4}\int_{T_{0}}^{T_{0}+t}p_{i}(s)\,ds\right\rfloor\geq R\right\}.

Theorem E.3

(Fixed-parameter time complexity under the universal computation model)
.

Under the assumptions and parameter choices of
Theorem

4.1
, let
K_{\varepsilon}
be the number of iterations needed to reach an
\varepsilon
-stationary point, and let
R=1/\alpha
be the corresponding fixed delay threshold. Then, under
E.1
, the time complexity of reaching an
\varepsilon
-stationary point satisfies

T_{\varepsilon}\leq T_{\lceil K_{\varepsilon}/R\rceil},

where the sequence
\{T_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is defined recursively by

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle T_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T_{k-1}+t(R;T_{k-1}),\qquad k\geq 1.

Proof.

Partition the first
K_{\varepsilon}
updates into
\lceil K_{\varepsilon}/R\rceil
consecutive blocks of length at most
R
. Applying
Lemma

E.2
blockwise yields the stated recursion and therefore the bound

T_{\varepsilon}\leq T_{\lceil K_{\varepsilon}/R\rceil}.

∎

Here
K_{\varepsilon}
denotes the iteration complexity from
Theorem

4.1
, namely, the number of updates needed to reach an
\varepsilon
-stationary point. Thus the theorem converts the iteration bound into a time bound by grouping these
K_{\varepsilon}
updates into blocks of size
R
and tracking their completion times through the recursion above. In the classical smooth case
L_{1}=0
, the iteration complexity from
Theorem

4.1
has the same
\varepsilon
-dependence as
Ringmaster ASGD
, up to the norm-equivalence factor
\rho
. In the Euclidean setting, where

, this recovers the
Ringmaster ASGD
scaling and hence the corresponding optimal time complexity.
We next extend
Lemma

E.2
to non-decreasing threshold sequences.

E.3
Adaptive threshold schedule

We now turn to the parameter-agnostic schedule, where the threshold increases with the iteration counter.
We first derive a generic recursive bound for arbitrary non-decreasing thresholds and then specialize it to the square-root rule
R_{0}=1
and
R_{k}=\sqrt{k}
for

.

Lemma E.4

(Time for general adaptive threshold under the universal computation model)
.

Let
R_{k}
be a non-decreasing delay threshold.
For each integer
r\in\{\lfloor R_{0}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor\}
, let

\mathcal{K}_{r}\coloneqq\{k\mid\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor=r\}

denote the block of iterations where the threshold is constant.
For every such
r

\mathcal{K}_{r}
into

N_{r}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{r}|}{r}\right\rceil

r

Let

Denote by

the threshold value attached to the

Define the sequence

recursively by

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle T_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T_{j-1}+t(r_{j};T_{j-1}),\qquad j=1,\dots,B.

Then the time needed to complete the first
K

T(K)\leq T_{B}.

Proof.

Partition the iterations
\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}
into disjoint blocks

according to the integer value of
\lfloor R_{k}\rfloor

Since
R_{k}
is non-decreasing, all iterations in each

r

Replacing the actual threshold by

Hence
Lemma

E.2
implies that, if this sub-block starts at time
T
, then all its iterations are completed within time at most
t(r;T)
.

Now enumerate all sub-blocks in chronological order and let
r_{j}
be the threshold associated with the
j
-th one.
Starting from time
T_{0}=0
, the first sub-block is completed by time at most

T_{1}=T_{0}+t(r_{1};T_{0}).

Repeating the same argument inductively, if the first
j-1
sub-blocks are completed by time at most
T_{j-1}
, then the
j
-th sub-block is completed by time at most

T_{j}=T_{j-1}+t(r_{j};T_{j-1}).

After all

B=\sum_{r=\lfloor R_{0}\rfloor}^{\lfloor R_{K-1}\rfloor}\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{r}|}{r}\right\rceil

sub-blocks are processed, all first
K
iterations have been completed. Therefore,

T(K)\leq T_{B}.

∎

This is the universal-model analogue of
Lemma

D.1
: it reduces the analysis of a non-decreasing threshold sequence to a recursion over threshold-constant sub-blocks.

Lemma E.5

(Square-root threshold under the universal computation model)
.

Let
R_{0}=1
and
R_{k}=\sqrt{k}
for
k\geq 1
.
Under
E.1
, the time needed to complete the first
K
iterations of
Algorithm

1
is bounded by

T(K)\leq S_{3\lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor+1},

where the sequence
\{S_{j}\}_{j=0}^{3\lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor+1}
is defined recursively as

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{j-1}+t\!\left(\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{3}\right\rceil\right\};S_{j-1}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,3\lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor+1.

Proof.

We apply
Lemma

E.4
with
R_{0}=1
and
R_{k}=\sqrt{k}
for
k\geq 1
.
For every integer
r\geq 1
and every
k\geq 1
, the condition
\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor=r
is equivalent to

r^{2}\leq k<(r+1)^{2},

The extra iteration
k=0
belongs to
\mathcal{K}_{1}
. Hence

|\mathcal{K}_{r}|\leq 2r+2

for all
r\geq 1
. Therefore
N_{1}\leq 4
, while
N_{r}\leq 3
for all
r\geq 2
.

Let
r_{\max}\coloneqq\lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor
.
Hence the total number of sub-blocks is at most

B\leq 4+3(r_{\max}-1)=3r_{\max}+1.

Moreover, after the first threshold level, each new level contributes at most three additional sub-blocks, so the threshold attached to the
j
-th sub-block is at most

\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{3}\right\rceil\right\}.

Therefore, using the monotonicity of
t(r;T)
in
r
, the recursion from
Lemma

E.4
is dominated by

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{j-1}+t\!\left(\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{3}\right\rceil\right\};S_{j-1}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,3r_{\max}+1,

and the resulting completion time satisfies

T(K)\leq S_{3r_{\max}+1}=S_{3\lfloor\sqrt{K}\rfloor+1}.

∎

Theorem E.6

(Parameter-agnostic time complexity under the universal computation model)
.

Consider
Algorithm

1
with

\alpha_{0}=1,\qquad\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\quad(k\geq 1),\qquad R_{0}=1,\qquad R_{k}=\sqrt{k}\quad(k\geq 1),\qquad\eta_{k}=\frac{\eta}{(k+1)^{3/4}}

Let

K_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Psi^{4}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)

be the iteration complexity from
Theorem

4.4
.
Then, under
E.1
, the time complexity of reaching an
\varepsilon
-stationary point satisfies

T_{\varepsilon}\leq S_{3\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor+1}=S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\Psi^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})},

Here and below,
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
hides logarithmic factors in
1/\varepsilon
.
The sequence
\{S_{j}\}
is defined recursively by

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{j-1}+t\!\left(\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{3}\right\rceil\right\};S_{j-1}\right).

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.4
, the method reaches an
\varepsilon
-stationary point within

K_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Psi^{4}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)

iterations.
Applying
Lemma

E.5
with
K=K_{\varepsilon}
yields

T_{\varepsilon}\leq S_{3\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor+1}.

Since
\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\Psi^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})

∎

E.4
Comparison with Ringmaster ASGD

We briefly compare the recursive time bounds obtained for
Ringmaster ASGD
and
Ringmaster LMO
under the universal computation model.
Because the duration function
t(r;T)
depends on both the block size
r
and the starting time
T
, this comparison is necessarily weaker than in the fixed computation model.

For
Ringmaster ASGD
, the iteration complexity in
37
is

\bar{K}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{48L\Delta\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right\rceil.

Take the fixed threshold

R\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil.

Applying the fixed-threshold bound above with this choice of
R
gives

T_{\varepsilon}^{\rm Ringmaster}\leq T^{(R)}_{N_{\rm Ringmaster}},

where

N_{\rm Ringmaster}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\bar{K}}{R}\right\rceil

and the sequence
\{T^{(R)}_{k}\}
is defined by

\displaystyle T^{(R)}_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle T^{(R)}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T^{(R)}_{k-1}+t(R;T^{(R)}_{k-1}).

Since
\bar{K}=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2}L\Delta/\varepsilon^{4})
and
R=\Theta(\sigma/\varepsilon^{2})
, we obtain

N_{\rm Ringmaster}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma L\Delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

For our method, the iteration complexity from
Theorem

4.4
is

K_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Psi^{4}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right),

and
Theorem

E.6
yields the time bound

T_{\varepsilon}\leq S_{N_{\rm ours}},

where

N_{\rm ours}\coloneqq 3\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor+1

and the sequence
\{S_{j}\}
is defined by

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{j-1}+t\!\left(\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{3}\right\rceil\right\};S_{j-1}\right).

Since
\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\Psi^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
, this gives

N_{\rm ours}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Psi^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Thus, from the point of view of the
\varepsilon
-dependence, the two methods are already quite close: both require on the order of
1/\varepsilon^{2}
recursion steps, up to logarithmic factors for our method.
Their problem-dependent prefactors are different, so this should be interpreted only as an
\varepsilon
-scaling comparison.

The difference lies in how these blocks are formed.

Ringmaster ASGD
uses the same threshold
R
in every block, while our method uses the increasing thresholds

1,1,1,1,2,2,2,3,3,3,\dots,\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor,\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor,\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor.

Because the quantity
t(r;T)
depends on both the threshold
r
and the starting time
T
, the universal computation model does not by itself give a constant-factor comparison between

T^{(R)}_{N_{\rm Ringmaster}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{N_{\rm ours}}.

Indeed, the worker speeds may change arbitrarily with time, so two recursions with a similar number of steps need not be within a constant factor of each other.

Therefore, in the fully general universal model, the clean conclusion is that both methods involve about
1/\varepsilon^{2}
recursion steps, but we cannot claim that our bound matches the
Ringmaster ASGD
bound up to a universal constant without extra assumptions on the computation-rate functions
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

If one wants a genuine constant-factor time comparison, a sufficient extra condition is that block durations of the relevant size remain stable under shifts of the starting time.
Let

R_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\coloneqq\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}}\rfloor,

and assume that there exists a constant
C_{\star}\geq 1
such that

t(R_{\varepsilon}^{\star};T)\leq C_{\star}\,t(R_{\varepsilon}^{\star};0)\qquad\text{for all }T\geq 0.

Since

\left\lceil\frac{j}{3}\right\rceil\leq R_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\qquad\text{for }j=1,\dots,N_{\rm ours},

and
t(r;T)
is nondecreasing in
r
, every increment in the recursion for
\{S_{j}\}
satisfies

t\!\left(\left\lceil\frac{j}{3}\right\rceil;S_{j-1}\right)\leq t(R_{\varepsilon}^{\star};S_{j-1})\leq C_{\star}\,t(R_{\varepsilon}^{\star};0).

Therefore,

T_{\varepsilon}\leq S_{N_{\rm ours}}\leq C_{\star}\,N_{\rm ours}\,t(R_{\varepsilon}^{\star};0)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Psi^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,t(\sqrt{K_{\varepsilon}};0)\right).

In other words, under this localized start-time stability assumption, our adaptive schedule has the same time scaling as a fixed-threshold scheme at the natural block size
R_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
, up to constant and logarithmic factors.
