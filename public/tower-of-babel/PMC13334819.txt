Real-world safety and efficacy of enzalutamide versus darolutamide in nonmetastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: K. Ishimoto, N. Fujita, S. Kato, Y. Kawashima, M. Shinohara, R. Tabata, F. Yoneyama, R. Tanaka, T. Oishi, H. Miura, K. Togashi, K. Okita, H. Horiguchi, T. Tanaka, D. Noro, Y. Suzuki, S. Sato, C. Ohyama, S. Hatakeyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough enzalutamide and darolutamide share a common mechanism of action targeting the androgen receptor pathway, they differ in their molecular structure and pharmacological properties, which may influence their efficacy and safety. However, real-world evidence directly comparing these agents in patients with nonmetastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) remains limited.Materials and methodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, data from 198 patients who received enzalutamide or darolutamide as their first androgen receptor signaling inhibitor (ARSI) during treatment of nmCRPC were analyzed. Adverse events (AEs) and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) responses were compared. Progression of PSA and development of metastases were assessed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model, adjusting for death as a competing risk.ResultsIn the cohort, 94 (47%) patients received enzalutamide and 104 (53%) received darolutamide. Incidence rates of any-grade AEs and grade ≥3 AEs were similar between the two groups (P = 0.968 and P = 1.000, respectively). The rates of any PSA response, decline in PSA ≥50%, and decline in PSA ≥90% did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.130, P = 0.687, and P = 0.171, respectively). In multivariable analyses, ARSI type was not significantly associated with progression of PSA [reference, enzalutamide; subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR), 1.153; P = 0.620] or development of metastases (reference, enzalutamide; SHR, 0.815; P = 0.510).ConclusionsEnzalutamide and darolutamide had comparable safety and oncological outcomes in patients with nmCRPC. These findings support the use of either agent as an appropriate first ARSI treatment.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PC) is one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies in males worldwide.1 Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is the standard systemic treatment of patients with recurrent disease following local therapy, as well as for those with advanced PC.2,3 Although ADT provides effective tumor control in the early phase, in a substantial proportion of patients, the PC eventually progresses to castration-resistant PC (CRPC).4,5

Among these patients, nonmetastatic CRPC (nmCRPC) represents a clinically important disease state because patients in this phase are at high risk of developing metastatic CRPC (mCRPC), which is associated with poor survival.6 The advent of novel androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARSIs) has markedly improved disease control in patients with nmCRPC, and they have become an essential component of contemporary management strategies.7, 8, 9 Among the available ARSIs, enzalutamide and darolutamide are the most frequently used agents in clinical practice for patients with nmCRPC.10 Although these agents share a common mechanism of action, targeting the androgen receptor pathway,11 they differ in molecular structure and pharmacological properties.12 These differences may influence the efficacy and safety profiles in patients with nmCRPC. However, real-world evidence directly comparing enzalutamide and darolutamide remains limited, and the available studies are constrained by small sample sizes or reliance on large administrative or electronic medical record databases,13, 14, 15 which may introduce inherent methodological limitations.

Thus, in the present real-world study, the aim was to compare the safety and efficacy of enzalutamide and darolutamide as the first ARSI treatment in patients with nmCRPC, to address the current evidence gap in clinical practice.

Patients and methods

Patient selection

In this multicenter retrospective study, data from 368 consecutive patients with nmCRPC who were treated at 12 participating hospitals between June 2001 and September 2025 were assessed. A total of 170 patients were excluded for the following reasons: (i) receipt of abiraterone acetate (n = 33) or apalutamide (n = 26) as the initial ARSI, and (ii) treatment limited to first-generation antiandrogens and/or docetaxel during the nmCRPC phase (n = 111). Ultimately, data from 198 patients who received enzalutamide or darolutamide as the first ARSI treatment were included in the final analyses. nmCRPC was defined as a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level >1 ng/ml, a castrate testosterone level <50 ng/dl, and the absence of metastatic lesions on conventional imaging (computed tomography or bone scintigraphy).16

Evaluation of variables

The following clinical variables were assessed: age and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) at the diagnosis of nmCRPC; PSA level at initial PC diagnosis; biopsy Gleason score; clinical tumor and node stages at initial diagnosis; history of radical treatment; clinical node stage at the diagnosis of nmCRPC; and PSA doubling time (PSADT). PSADT was calculated using the Sloan Kettering method, which requires at least three PSA measurements of ≥0.2 ng/ml obtained at intervals of at least 1 month within the 12 months preceding the diagnosis of nmCRPC.17 In addition, the relative dose intensities (RDIs) of enzalutamide and darolutamide were calculated to evaluate treatment exposure. RDI was defined as the ratio of the actual cumulative dose delivered to the planned cumulative dose over a specified treatment period, expressed as a percentage. The planned dose was defined as the standard approved daily dose (enzalutamide, 160 mg/day; darolutamide, 600 mg twice daily). Dose reduction, temporary interruption, and treatment delay were incorporated into the calculation of the actual cumulative dose. RDI was calculated for each patient during the first ARSI treatment.

Treatment

All patients included in this study had received continuous ADT, consisting of bilateral orchiectomy or medical castration with luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonists or antagonists, throughout the course of PC treatment. In Japan, enzalutamide and darolutamide became available for clinical use in patients with nmCRPC in May 2014 and January 2020, respectively. The selection of the ARSI for nmCRPC treatment was determined at the discretion of the treating clinician. Patients were categorized into two groups: those who received enzalutamide as the first ARSI (enzalutamide group) and those who received darolutamide as the first ARSI (darolutamide group).

Outcomes

Adverse events (AEs) associated with enzalutamide or darolutamide were documented by treating physicians during routine clinical practice and assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. AE data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records by trained investigators at each participating institution using a standardized electronic case report form. To ensure consistency across institutions and minimize potential misclassification or data entry errors, all extracted data were reviewed and cross-checked for internal consistency by the corresponding author and an additional investigator. No independent external adjudication committee was involved.

For oncological outcomes, change in PSA at 1 and 3 months after initiation of ARSI treatment, the maximum change in PSA during ARSI treatment, and the rates of any PSA response, a decline in PSA ≥50%, and a decline in PSA ≥90% were compared between the two groups. In addition, the cumulative incidences of progression of PSA and development of metastases were compared between the groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), and EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan). Quantitative variables are presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)], and differences between the two groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test, as appropriate.

The optimal PSADT cut-off value for development of metastases was determined using a receiver operating characteristic curve. Given the substantial differences in all-cause mortality between the two groups, the cumulative incidences of progression of PSA and development of metastases were estimated using a competing-risks framework, treating death before these events as a competing event. Comparisons of cumulative incidence functions were carried out using Gray’s test. Multivariable analyses for progression of PSA and development of metastases were subsequently conducted using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model, adjusting for death as a competing risk.18

Subgroup analyses were carried out to determine PSA progression-free survival (PSA-PFS) and metastasis-free survival (MFS). Subgroups were defined by age (<80 years versus ≥80 years), biopsy Gleason score (<8 versus ≥8), and PSADT (below versus above the optimal cut-off value). Cox proportional hazards models were used for subgroup analyses to estimate cause-specific hazards and to provide a straightforward interpretation of treatment effects within each subgroup, whereas Fine-Gray models were reserved for primary multivariable analyses to appropriately account for competing risks.

Progression of PSA was defined as an increase in the PSA level of >25% and >2 ng/ml above the nadir, confirmed by progression at two timepoints at least 3 weeks apart.16 Statistical significance was defined as P <0.05.

AEs, adverse events.

AEs associated with ARSI treatment

The most common any-grade AE associated with enzalutamide was general malaise or fatigue, followed by anorexia and liver injury. Similarly, for darolutamide, the most frequent any-grade AE was general malaise or fatigue, followed by liver injury (Figure 1B). The incidence rates of any-grade AEs did not differ significantly between the two groups (Figure 1C). Grade ≥3 AEs occurred in one patient in the enzalutamide group and two patients in the darolutamide group, with no significant difference between the groups (Figure 1D). The grade ≥3 AE observed in the enzalutamide group was hypertension, whereas the grade ≥3 AEs observed in the darolutamide group were liver injury and anorexia. Five patients in each group discontinued ARSI treatment because of AEs, with no significant difference between the groups (Figure 1E). In the enzalutamide group, the AEs leading to treatment discontinuation were nausea (n = 2), liver injury (n = 2), and skin rash (n = 1), whereas in the darolutamide group, the reasons for discontinuation were liver injury (n = 3), nausea (n = 1), and anorexia (n = 1).

In the subgroup analyses, there were no significant differences in PSA-PFS or MFS between the enzalutamide and darolutamide groups across any of the predefined subgroups, including age (<80 years versus ≥80 years), biopsy Gleason score (<8 versus ≥8), and PSADT (<3.3 versus ≥3.3 months; Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100734). Discussion

This study comprehensively compared the safety and efficacy of enzalutamide and darolutamide in patients with nmCRPC using real-world clinical data. Our findings indicate that both agents provide comparable safety profiles and oncological outcomes, with no meaningful differences in PSA response, progression of PSA, or development of metastases. The subgroup analyses further supported the consistency of these results across clinically relevant subgroups of patients. From a clinical perspective, the present results suggest that neither agent demonstrated a clearly superior safety or efficacy profile in routine clinical practice, and treatment selection may therefore be individualized. However, given the retrospective and nonrandomized nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered definitive evidence of therapeutic equivalence between the two agents. Overall, the present findings provide practical real-world insights into treatment selection for patients with nmCRPC, while also highlighting the need for prospective comparative studies to better define potential differences between enzalutamide and darolutamide.

In contrast to our results, earlier real-world studies have suggested potential differences in the oncological outcomes between the two agents. A retrospective observational study using health claims data from acute care hospitals in Japan evaluated 418 patients treated with darolutamide and 1898 treated with enzalutamide, reporting that darolutamide was associated with a significantly longer time to mCRPC progression (HR 0.66; 95% CI 0.61-0.72).14 Similarly, a retrospective analysis of electronic medical records from a large urology practice network in the United States, which included 362 patients receiving darolutamide and 382 receiving enzalutamide, demonstrated a lower risk of mCRPC progression among patients treated with darolutamide (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.43-0.82).15 However, these studies were based on administrative claims or electronic medical record databases, which may be subject to misclassification of disease status and have incomplete clinical information, including PSA values, Gleason scores, and RDI, and they may lack detailed documentation of progression-related events. The present study also has some limitations, which will be discussed below. Nevertheless, the present findings underscore the requirement for prospective studies with standardized outcome definitions, comprehensive clinical datasets, and appropriate adjustments for treatment exposure to clarify potential differences in oncological outcomes between enzalutamide and darolutamide in patients with nmCRPC.

The findings regarding the safety profile should also be noted. Similar to oncological outcomes, evidence comparing the safety of enzalutamide and darolutamide is limited and is largely derived from electronic medical record databases or network meta-analyses.15,19,20 In a systematic review and network meta-analysis by Mori et al, darolutamide had the lowest rates across all AE outcomes compared with enzalutamide.20 Another systematic review and network meta-analysis similarly reported that darolutamide had the highest surface under the cumulative ranking curve values for overall AEs and grade ≥3 AEs, indicating the lowest probability of AEs.19 These findings may be explained by the low penetration of the blood-brain barrier by darolutamide and low binding affinity for γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors.21, 22, 23 Furthermore, unlike enzalutamide, darolutamide does not inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes,24 and is therefore less likely to cause drug–drug interactions.25 However, in the aforementioned real-world retrospective analysis from a large urology practice network in the United States, the frequencies of any AEs and AEs of special interest, including fatigue, rash, and cognitive or memory impairment, were comparable between enzalutamide and darolutamide.15 These results are consistent with our findings, suggesting that potential safety differences inferred from pharmacological properties or indirect comparative evidence should be interpreted with caution, as clinical tolerability appears broadly similar between the two agents. Further studies with prospective designs and standardized assessments of AEs are required to clarify potential differences in safety profiles between enzalutamide and darolutamide in patients with nmCRPC.

In this study cohort, substantial differences in all-cause mortality were observed between the enzalutamide and darolutamide groups. Detailed causes of death are presented in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100734. In both groups, PC was the leading cause of death; therefore, the marked difference in all-cause mortality was primarily driven by differences in cancer-specific mortality. In contrast, although cardiovascular disease has been suggested as a potential concern with enzalutamide because of possible drug–drug interactions affecting concomitant cardiovascular medications,26,27 cardiovascular mortality was relatively uncommon in the present cohort and did not appear to be the major contributor to the observed mortality difference between the two groups. Because the cumulative incidence rates of PSA progression and metastasis development did not differ significantly between the groups, the observed difference in mortality may at least partly be attributable to the longer follow-up duration in the enzalutamide group. In Japan, enzalutamide and darolutamide became available for clinical use in patients with nmCRPC in May 2014 and January 2020, respectively; consequently, enzalutamide was introduced earlier in clinical practice, resulting in a longer observation period compared with darolutamide (Table 1; median follow-up duration: 57 versus 42 months). Moreover, the poorer ECOG PS in the enzalutamide group may also have contributed to the observed differences in mortality (Table 1; ECOG PS ≥1: 42% versus 20%).

This study had several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design precludes definitive causal inferences because selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors could not be fully eliminated. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study may have contributed to underreporting of low-grade AEs, particularly subjective symptoms that are susceptible to interphysician variability in documentation practices. While clinically significant AEs leading to treatment discontinuation or dose modification were likely captured reliably, milder events may not have been documented consistently across participating institutions. Secondly, the interval between nmCRPC diagnosis and initiation of the first ARSI treatment varied among patients, which may have influenced progression of the disease and oncological outcomes. Although this variability reflects real-world clinical practice, heterogeneity in treatment timing may have introduced bias. Thirdly, the present analysis focused exclusively on outcomes associated with the first ARSI treatment; therefore, the comparative effectiveness of enzalutamide and darolutamide when used as a second or later ARSI treatment was not assessed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that enzalutamide and darolutamide offer comparable safety and oncological outcomes in patients with nmCRPC. The results support the use of either agent as an appropriate first ARSI treatment. Further prospective studies are required to validate these findings.
