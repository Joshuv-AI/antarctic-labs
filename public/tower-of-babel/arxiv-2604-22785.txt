CoFi-PGMA: Counterfactual Policy Gradients under Filtered Feedback for Multi-Agent LLMs
Stela Tong, Elai Ben-Gal

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) deployments increasingly rely on multi-agent architectures in which multiple models either compete through routing mechanisms or collaborate to produce a final answer. In both settings, the learning signal received by each agent is filtered by the system mechanism. Routing produces selection-gated feedback where only the chosen response is evaluated, while collaboration produces shared rewards that obscure the individual contribution of each agent. As a result, standard RLHF objectives designed for a single deployed policy become misspecified. We introduce CoFi-PGMA (Counterfactual Policy Gradients under Filtered Feedback for Multi-Agent LLMs), a unified framework for learning under filtered feedback in multi-agent LLM systems. Our approach derives a counterfactual per-agent training objective based on marginal contribution, which corrects the learning signal under both routing and collaborative mechanisms. For routing systems, the objective corresponds to off-policy corrections for selection-gated feedback, while for collaborative systems it reduces to leave-one-out difference rewards for credit assignment. We further analyze how softmax routing induces risk-sensitive incentives and provide practical training algorithms that integrate counterfactual estimators, multiturn-aware rewards, and policy optimization methods, and demonstrate the approach on a real-world reasoning dataset.



1
Introduction

Many deployed LLM products are no longer a single monolithic policy. Instead, they are
multi-agent systems
in which multiple specialized models are invoked and a system-level mechanism decides what the user ultimately sees. Two common industrial patterns are (i)
routing
, where a controller selects among experts (e.g., choosing between different specialist models) and only the selected response is delivered, and (ii)
collaboration
, where roles such as planner–solver–critic–editor jointly produce one final answer via a composition rule. In both patterns the learning signal received by any single agent is
filtered
by the mechanism: under routing, reward is observed only for the selected output (selection-gated feedback); under collaboration, a single final reward is shared across agents, obscuring individual contributions (credit assignment).

Standard reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) objectives are designed for a single deployed policy receiving direct feedback. In multi-agent systems, however, the system mechanism induces filtered feedback that makes naive per-agent RLHF either statistically biased (because unselected actions are never evaluated) or strategically misaligned (because shared reward does not reflect each agent’s marginal contribution). Our main novelty lies in introducing Counterfactual Policy Gradients under Filtered Feedback for Multi-Agent LLMs (CoFi-PGMA), a unified framework for learning under filtered feedback in multi-agent LLM systems. We instantiate this framework through two concrete mechanisms, competitive routing and collaborative generation, and evaluate the approach on a real-world reasoning dataset.

Concretely, we train each agent using a counterfactual advantage signal that measures its marginal contribution to the final outcome, defined as the difference between the observed system reward and the reward that would have been obtained if the agent had not participated. In routing systems, this corresponds to removing the agent from the candidate set; in collaborative systems, it corresponds to replacing the agent’s contribution with a baseline. We instantiate this objective using practical estimators—doubly robust estimators for selection-gated routing and leave-one-out estimators for collaboration—together with multiturn-aware rewards and GRPO optimization. Although motivated by RLHF systems, the framework applies more broadly to multi-agent reinforcement learning settings where feedback is filtered by a system-level mechanism such as routing or aggregation.

Related work

RLHF

RLHF has become a
dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human
preferences by learning reward models from comparison data and
optimizing policies accordingly
(
ouyang2022training
)
. Recent
methods explore improved optimization procedures for large-scale LLM
alignment, such as GRPO, which stabilizes policy updates during
training
(
shao2024deepseekmath
)
. Other work studies reward
formulations that capture longer-horizon conversational effects,
including multiturn-aware rewards that evaluate the downstream impact
of intermediate responses in dialogue systems

(
wu2025collabllm
)
. These methods provide effective tools for aligning LLM behavior with
human preferences. However, most formulations assume a single deployed
policy and do not account for the filtered feedback mechanisms that
arise in multi-agent LLM systems.

Multi-agent RL

The multi-agent reinforcement learning literature studies the
problem of credit assignment in cooperative systems, where a global
reward must be decomposed into meaningful learning signals for
individual agents. Prior work proposes mechanisms such as difference
rewards and counterfactual baselines that estimate each agent’s
marginal contribution to overall system performance

(
tumer2000collective
;
foerster2018counterfactual
)
. These ideas provide principled tools for attributing outcomes in multi-agent
settings but are mathematically limited to simplified environments and do not
capture more general settings with filtered feedback.

Contextual Bandits and Routing

Routing mechanisms used in multi-agent LLM systems resemble
contextual bandit settings with selection-gated feedback, where only
the reward of the chosen action is observed. This partial-feedback
setting has been extensively studied in off-policy evaluation and
learning from logged bandit data, where techniques such as importance
sampling and doubly robust estimators are used to correct for biased
feedback logs
(
dudik2011doubly
)
.

Our work connects these strands by studying learning in multi-agent
LLM systems under filtered feedback and deriving counterfactual
per-agent training objectives that correct the learning signal in both
routing and collaborative settings.

3
Framework for RLHF under Filtered Feedback

We introduce a unified framework for RLHF in multi-agent systems with filtered feedback.
The framework formalizes both routing and collaboration mechanisms
and defines counterfactual marginal contribution as the correct learning signal.

3.1
Interaction model

We model a conversation as an episodic process of length
T
with histories

h_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
. At turn
t
,
K
agents sequentially generate proposals

a_{t}^{(1:K)}=(a_{t}^{(1)},\dots,a_{t}^{(K)})
according to a joint policy

\pi_{\theta}
. We assume the
joint proposal distribution
admits the
autoregressive factorization

\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(1:K)}\mid h_{t})=\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{t}^{(1)}\mid h_{t})\prod_{i=2}^{K}\pi_{\theta_{i}}\!\left(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)}\right),

(1)

so that agent
i
may condition on the proposals produced by earlier agents
within the same turn. This formulation captures common multi-agent pipelines such as
planner–solver–critic architectures, where later agents observe earlier
outputs. The frequently used independent-agent model is recovered as the special case

\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})=\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t}),
which yields the product factorization

\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(1:K)}\mid h_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t}).

(2)

In the remainder of the paper, we use the autoregressive formulation
1
to model
collaborative generation
pipelines where agents can observe
earlier proposals, and the independent factorization (
2
) to model

competitive routing
settings where agents generate proposals without access to
others’ outputs.

A
mechanism

M
maps the set of proposals to the deployed output
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
:

y_{t}=M(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},\xi_{t}),

where
\xi_{t}
captures any mechanism randomness (e.g., stochastic routing). The environment/user then transitions as
h_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid h_{t},y_{t})
. A scalar feedback signal is computed on the deployed output,
r_{t}=r(h_{t},y_{t})
, and the return is
R_{1:T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
.

Let the
reward allocation rule
of the mechanism be

\alpha_{i}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},\xi_{t})\in[0,1]\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}=1,

which specifies the fraction of the deployed reward assigned to agent
i
. The expected utility of agent
i
at turn
t
is defined as

U_{i}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\alpha_{i}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},\xi_{t})\,r(h_{t},y_{t})\right],

where the expectation is over proposal generation and mechanism randomness.

The
system objective
is

J_{\mathrm{sys}}(\theta_{1:K}):=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r(h_{t},y_{t})].

We keep
r(\cdot)
abstract here; in implementation we will instantiate
r
using a concrete reward construction (e.g., multiturn-aware reward).

Filtered feedback as partial observability of potential outcomes

For any fixed
(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},\xi_{t})
, define the deployed outcome
y_{t}
and reward
r_{t}=r(h_{t},y_{t})
. Learning does not necessarily observe all
potential outcomes
such as
r(h_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})
for alternative outputs
y_{t}^{\prime}
that were not deployed. We formalize this with a logging map
\Lambda
that produces an observation
o_{t}
used for learning,

o_{t}=\Lambda(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},y_{t},r_{t},\xi_{t}).

We say the system exhibits
filtered feedback
when
o_{t}
contains only a strict subset of the information required to evaluate counterfactual outcomes, i.e., when
r(h_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})
is unobserved for many
y_{t}^{\prime}
induced by alternative proposals or alternative mechanism decisions. Routing and collaboration are two canonical mechanisms that induce filtered feedback in distinct ways.

Competitive routing

We use the independent-agent model (
2
) in this setting. Each agent proposes a complete candidate answer
a_{t}^{(i)}
. A router selects an index
I_{t}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
with conditional probabilities

p_{i}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}):=\Pr(I_{t}=i\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}),

and deploys
y_{t}=a_{t}^{(I_{t})}
, yielding reward
r_{t}=r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})})
. The key filtering is that the system observes
r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})})
but not
r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})
for
j\neq I_{t}
. In competitive routing the allocation rule is

\alpha_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{I_{t}=i\}
,
so the utility reduces to
U_{i}=\mathbb{E}[p_{i}\,r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})]
.

For example, a common router parameterization scores each candidate via
s_{\phi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})
and applies softmax with temperature
\tau
(optionally mixed with uniform exploration
\epsilon
):

p_{i}\;=\;(1-\epsilon)\frac{\exp(s_{\phi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(s_{\phi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})/\tau)}+\epsilon\frac{1}{K}.

(3)

This is a contextual bandit with a large action space (the action is the selected candidate) and selection-gated reward logging.

Proposition 1

(Softmax routing induces risk-sensitive incentives)
.

Assume the router probabilities
p_{i}
are given by Eq. (
3
)
and that the score is a function of the candidate reward,

s_{\phi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})=\sigma(r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)}))

for some nonconstant function
\sigma
.
Let
r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1)}),\dots,r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(K)})
be independent conditional
on
h_{t}
. Then the expected utility

U_{i}=\mathbb{E}[p_{i}r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})]

cannot be characterized by the mean reward

\mathbb{E}[r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})]
alone.

Proposition
1
shows that under softmax routing an
agent’s expected utility

U_{i}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[p_{i}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})\,r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(i)})\right]

depends on the full set of candidate rewards, since the routing
probabilities depend on the relative scores of all candidates. However,
the observed feedback is selection-gated: we only observe

r_{t}=r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})}),I_{t}\sim p(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}),

while the counterfactual rewards
r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})
for
j\neq I_{t}

remain unobserved. This creates a mismatch between the objective, which
depends on all candidates, and the observed data, which reveals only one,
leading to a partially observed learning problem. As a result, naive use
of the observed reward yields a biased estimate of
U_{i}
, and learning
must reason about counterfactual rewards of unselected candidates.

Collaborative generation

Agents produce contributions that are combined into one final deployed answer. Concretely, the mechanism is an aggregation rule
M
and the deployed output is
y_{t}=M(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})
, with the shared reward
r_{t}=r(h_{t},y_{t})
. This corresponds to the allocation rule
\alpha_{i}=1/K
,
so that
U_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}[r(h_{t},y_{t})]
. Here the filtering is not that some reward is missing (we still observe
r_{t}
), but that the observed scalar
r_{t}
does not decompose into per-agent contributions; each agent receives a shared reward that conflates its own effect with other agents and interaction terms induced by
M
.

Proposition 2

(Shared reward does not identify individual contribution)
.

Fix
t
and
h_{t}
. There exist two collaborative mechanisms, given by aggregation
rules
M,M^{\prime}
and reward functions
r,r^{\prime}
, such that the resulting deployed rewards
have the same distribution,

r(h_{t},M(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}r^{\prime}(h_{t},M^{\prime}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})),

but agent
i
has different counterfactual marginal contributions under the two
mechanisms. Consequently, the shared utility

U_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}[r(h_{t},y_{t})]

does not identify the contribution of agent
i
.

Proposition
2
implies that shared rewards do not
provide sufficient information to attribute the final outcome to
individual agents. In particular, the same observed reward may arise
from fundamentally different underlying contributions, so the learning
signal does not identify whether agent
i
improved or degraded the
outcome. As a result, agents may receive positive updates even when they
free-ride on the contributions of others, or negative updates when they
are beneficial but offset by other agents or the aggregation mechanism. Moreover, the shared reward conflates the effects of all agents and
interaction terms induced by
M
, introducing substantial variance in
the per-agent learning signal. This leads to noisy and unstable updates,
since fluctuations in other agents’ actions or in the aggregation
mechanism are attributed to agent
i
. Consequently, shared reward
provides both a misaligned and high-variance signal for credit assignment.

3.2
Counterfactual Marginal Contribution

Motivated by the limitations identified above, we introduce a baseline distribution
q_{i}(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})
that generates a
replacement
proposal

\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}\sim q_{i}(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})

for agent
i
(e.g., a null message, a reference model, or a fixed template). Let

z_{t}:=(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})

denote the information available before agent
i
acts. To construct the counterfactual outcome without agent
i
, we replace the proposal of agent
i
with
\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}
and generate downstream proposals sequentially under the counterfactual prefix.

Formally, define the counterfactual proposal sequence
a_{t}^{(1:K),-i}
by

a_{t}^{(1:i-1),-i}:=a_{t}^{(1:i-1)},a_{t}^{(i),-i}:=\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)},

and for
j>i
,

a_{t}^{(j),-i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{j}}(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:j-1),-i}).
The counterfactual deployed output is

y_{t}^{-i}:=M(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K),-i},\xi_{t}^{-i})
and
r_{t}^{-i}:=r(h_{t},y_{t}^{-i}),

where
\xi_{t}^{-i}
denotes fresh mechanism randomness independent of the realized action
a_{t}^{(i)}
.

Let

G_{t}:=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}

denote the return-to-go from turn
t
onward. Define the counterfactual rewards
r_{u}^{-i}
for
u\geq t
recursively by
running the same mechanism and environment dynamics under the counterfactual
proposal sequence, starting from the counterfactual output
y_{t}^{-i}
.
The corresponding counterfactual return-to-go is

G_{t}^{-i}:=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}^{-i}.

Definition 1

(Counterfactual Marginal Contribution)
.

The counterfactual marginal contribution of agent
i
at turn
t
is

\Delta_{i,t}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}\,\middle|\,z_{t},a_{t}^{(i)}\right].

Note that when proposals are generated independently in the
competitive routing
case, the conditioning prefix
vanishes and
z_{t}=h_{t}
. Then the counterfactual trajectory
reduces to a leave-one-out construction without downstream resampling.

3.3
Counterfactual Policy Gradient

We now characterize the policy gradient when each agent is trained using counterfactual marginal contribution.

Theorem 1

(Counterfactual policy gradient under autoregressive multi-agent policies)
.

Assume the joint proposal distribution factorizes autoregressively
as in (
1
). For each agent
i
, assume

\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})

is continuously differentiable in
\theta_{i}
,
the rewards are bounded, and policies are differentiable. Let
q_{i}(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})
be a replacement distribution
independent of
\theta_{i}
. Then

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}J_{\mathrm{sys}}(\theta_{1:K})=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})\,\Delta_{i,t}\Bigg].

Theorem
1
shows that counterfactual marginal contribution
provides a valid policy-gradient signal even when agents act sequentially.
In contrast, using the return-to-go
G_{t}
as the learning signal is
problematic in multi-agent settings with filtered feedback. In the routing setting, the issue is statistical bias. The objective
depends on the rewards of all candidate actions, but the observed
feedback is selection-gated, which is only the reward of the deployed candidate
is observed. As a result, naive updates using
\nabla\log\pi_{i}\cdot G_{t}

do not provide an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the true objective,
since the counterfactual rewards of unselected candidates are missing.
Counterfactual marginal contribution corrects this by explicitly
accounting for the full candidate set through off-policy estimation. In the collaborative setting, the issue is variance and misattribution.
The shared return
G_{t}
conflates the contribution of agent
i
with
stochastic effects from other agents and the aggregation mechanism.
Concretely, writing
G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}=\Delta_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t},
where
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i,t}\mid z_{t},a_{t}^{(i)}]=0,

the gradient term
\nabla\log\pi_{i}\cdot G_{t}
includes a component

\nabla\log\pi_{i}\cdot G_{t}^{-i}
that is independent of agent
i
’s
action and contributes zero only in expectation, but induces substantial
variance in finite samples. In both cases,
\Delta_{i,t}
isolates the causal effect of agent
i
.
For routing, it restores unbiasedness by correcting for missing
counterfactual feedback; for collaboration, it removes
action-independent variability and reduces variance, which will be shown in Proposition
3
. Thus, it yields a
higher signal-to-noise ratio and more effective credit assignment under
filtered feedback.

Proposition 3

(Counterfactual marginal contribution filters action-independent noise)
.

Fix agent
i
and turn
t
, and let

\psi_{i,t}:=\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid z_{t}).
Then

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i,t}G_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i,t}\Delta_{i,t}],
and

\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,t}G_{t})=\mathrm{Var}\!\bigl(\psi_{i,t}(G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i})\bigr)+\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,t}G_{t}^{-i})+2\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(\psi_{i,t}(G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}),\,\psi_{i,t}G_{t}^{-i}\bigr).

In particular, if

\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,t}G_{t}^{-i})+2\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\bigl(\psi_{i,t}(G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}),\,\psi_{i,t}G_{t}^{-i}\bigr)>0,

then

\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,t}\Delta_{i,t})\;\leq\;\mathrm{Var}\!\bigl(\psi_{i,t}(G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i})\bigr)\;<\;\mathrm{Var}(\psi_{i,t}G_{t}).

Proposition
3
shows that the shared return
G_{t}
can be decomposed into a signal component
G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}
and an
action-independent component
G_{t}^{-i}
arising from other agents and
the aggregation mechanism. The latter contributes variance to the
gradient estimator but does not affect its expectation, since it is
independent of agent
i
’s action. The variance reduction condition above is mild. It requires only that the
action-independent component does not cancel the signal in the covariance
term. In particular, it fails only under pathological dependence between
the baseline return
G_{t}^{-i}
and the signal
G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}
.
Intuitively, such a scenario would require the rest of the system to be
systematically anti-aligned with agent
i
’s marginal contribution,
which is unlikely in practice.

Competitive routing: DR estimation under selection-gated logging

In routing we observe only
(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},I_{t},r_{t})
with

r_{t}=r(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})})
and propensities

p_{j}=\Pr(I_{t}=j\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})
. Define
G_{t}^{(j)}
as the conditional expected return-to-go if candidate
j
were deployed at turn
t
while the rest of the trajectory follows the same policies and environment dynamics, i.e.,

G_{t}^{(j)}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[G_{t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},I_{t}=j\right].

To estimate the unobserved candidate return-to-go

G_{t}^{(j)}
corresponding to deploying candidate
j
,
we use contextual-bandit estimators.
Let
\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})
be a learned predictor of

G_{t}^{(j)}
.
The doubly-robust estimate of the return-to-go of candidate
j
is

\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})+\frac{\mathbf{1}\{I_{t}=j\}}{p_{j}}\big(G_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})\big).

Thus, if candidate
j
is not selected (
j\neq I_{t}
),
the estimate reduces to the model prediction

\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)}).
If candidate
j
is selected (
j=I_{t}
),
the estimator uses the observed return-to-go to correct the prediction

\widehat{G}_{t}^{(I_{t})}=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})})+\frac{1}{p_{I_{t}}}\big(G_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(I_{t})})\big).
Therefore, unobserved candidate returns rely on the learned predictor,
while the observed return-to-go provides an importance-weighted
correction for the selected candidate.

Corollary 1

(Unbiased routing marginal contribution)
.

Assume the routing setting with independent candidate proposals, and Let

\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}-\sum_{j\neq i}p_{j}^{-i}\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)},

where
p_{j}^{-i}
are the router probabilities after removing agent
i
.
If either the propensities
p_{j}
are correct or

\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})=G_{t}^{(j)}
,
then

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=\mathbb{E}[G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}],
which equals
\Delta_{i,t}
in the routing setting..

While the unbiasedness guarantee of the doubly robust estimator requires either correct propensities or a correct outcome model, in practice both components may be misspecified. This is the typical regime in real-world systems. Importantly, the doubly robust estimator is known to remain more stable than either pure importance sampling or pure model-based estimation under joint misspecification, often achieving a favorable bias–variance tradeoff. In our setting, where both the router and the reward predictor are learned and evolving, this robustness is particularly important, as it mitigates errors arising from imperfect propensity estimation and prediction models. Empirically, this manifests in Section
4
as improved learning stability and reduced variance compared to winner-take-all or purely model-based baselines.

Collaborative generation: leave-one-out counterfactual evaluation

In collaboration, we use the autoregressive counterfactual construction defined
above. Conditional on the pre-action information

z_{t}=(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)}),

we replace agent
i
’s proposal with

\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}\sim q_{i}(\cdot\mid z_{t}),

resample all downstream proposals
a_{t}^{(j),-i}
for
j>i
under the
counterfactual prefix, and compute the resulting counterfactual return-to-go

G_{t}^{-i}
.

Corollary 2

(Unbiased leave-one-out credit)
.

Let

\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}=G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}.

Then

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}\mid z_{t},a_{t}^{(i)}]=\Delta_{i,t}.

3.4
Single-Turn Specialization

Many multi-agent LLM systems operate as a single decision problem at each
prompt. In this case the trajectory reduces to one turn and the return-to-go
simplifies to the immediate reward. Let the agents produce proposals

a^{(1:K)}
at history
h
, and let the mechanism deploy an output

y=M(h,a^{(1:K)})
with reward
r(h,y)
. The system objective becomes

J_{\mathrm{sys}}(\theta_{1:K})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r(h,y)\right].

The deployed reward under the full system is

G:=r(h,y).

To construct the counterfactual outcome without agent
i
, we replace its
proposal by a baseline draw

\tilde{a}^{(i)}\sim q_{i}(\cdot\mid h,a^{(1:i-1)})

and define the counterfactual proposal profile

a^{(1:K),-i}
by keeping the prefix fixed and inserting
\tilde{a}^{(i)}

(resampling downstream proposals when the policy is autoregressive).
Let

G^{-i}:=r\!\left(h,M(h,a^{(1:K),-i})\right)

denote the corresponding counterfactual reward.
The marginal contribution of agent

becomes

Under the policy factorization in (

In

with probabilities

In

settings the mechanism aggregates all proposals into

and

G^{-i}=r(h,M(h,a^{(1:K),-i}))

5
Conclusion

We study RLHF for multi-agent LLM systems in which the feedback available to each agent is filtered by a system-level mechanism. Our main contribution is CoFi-PGMA, a counterfactual policy-gradient formulation based on marginal contribution. The framework provides practical estimators for two common settings: doubly robust correction for competitive routing and leave-one-out credit assignment for collaborative generation, yielding a simple training recipe that integrates naturally with GRPO and a learned router. Our GSM8K experiments provide initial empirical support for this framework. In a competitive-routing setup with three LoRA-based agents, DR-GRPO outperforms both a frozen single-agent reference and a winner-take-all routed baseline. The corrected objective improves exact-match accuracy, reduces router regret relative to an oracle over the sampled candidate set, and yields better reward-predictor calibration and stronger specialization. These results are consistent with the view that naive winner-take-all training is misspecified under selection-gated feedback, whereas counterfactual correction recovers a more informative learning signal.

A key limitation of this work is that our empirical evaluation remains small-scale, and several extensions remain for future work. First, the routing experiments should be expanded to larger models, larger training sets, and multiple seeds. Second, the collaborative setting, where filtered feedback appears as shared reward rather than selection-gated logging, should be evaluated empirically using the leave-one-out estimator developed in the paper. Third, richer reward models, including long horizon MR metrics as introduced by Wu et. al.
wu2025collabllm
, and stronger routers may further improve performance and help disentangle gains from better candidate generation versus better routing. More broadly, our results suggest that as LLM systems increasingly rely on mixtures of experts, tool-using pipelines, and multi-agent decompositions, training objectives must account explicitly for the mechanism that mediates feedback. Counterfactual credit assignment provides one principled way to do so.

p_{\theta}(\tau)=\rho(h_{1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\Bigg[\pi_{\theta_{1}}(a_{t}^{(1)}\mid h_{t})\prod_{j=2}^{K}\pi_{\theta_{j}}(a_{t}^{(j)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:j-1)})\Bigg]\,p(\xi_{t},y_{t},r_{t},h_{t+1}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}),

Applying the log-derivative identity,

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}p_{\theta}(\tau)=p_{\theta}(\tau)\,\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log p_{\theta}(\tau),

gives

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}J_{\mathrm{sys}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Big(\sum_{u=1}^{T}r_{u}\Big)\,\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log p_{\theta}(\tau)\right].

Since
\theta_{i}
appears only in the policy of agent
i

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log p_{\theta}(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)}).

Substituting this into the previous equation,

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}J_{\mathrm{sys}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})\Big(\sum_{u=1}^{T}r_{u}\Big)\right].

i
acts at turn
t

z_{t}=(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:i-1)})

and earlier randomness. Therefore,

is generated

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid z_{t})\,G_{t}^{-i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[G_{t}^{-i}\cdot 0\right]

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\{I_{t}=j\}}{p_{j}}G_{t}\,\middle|\,h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}\right]=\frac{1}{p_{j}}\Pr(I_{t}=j\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})\mathbb{E}[G_{t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)},I_{t}=j]=G_{t}^{(j)}.

Also,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\{I_{t}=j\}}{p_{j}}\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})\,\middle|\,h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}\right]=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)}),

because
\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})
is measurable with respect to
(h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)})
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})+G_{t}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t}^{(j)})=G_{t}^{(j)}.

Thus in either case,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=G_{t}^{(j)}.

Now by definition,

\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}-\sum_{j\neq i}p_{j}^{-i}\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}.

Taking conditional expectation and using linearity,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]-\sum_{j\neq i}p_{j}^{-i}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}].

Substituting the unbiasedness of each
\widehat{G}_{t}^{(j)}
,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}G_{t}^{(j)}-\sum_{j\neq i}p_{j}^{-i}G_{t}^{(j)}.

The first term is exactly the conditional expected return-to-go under the actual router, and the second is the conditional expected return-to-go under the router with agent
i
removed. Hence

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Delta}_{i,t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}]=\mathbb{E}[G_{t}-G_{t}^{-i}\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{(1:K)}].

This proves the corollary.
∎

Appendix G
Implementation with GRPO and MR

GRPO with filtered-feedback correction

Fix a history
h_{t}
and draw
N
joint proposal samples

a_{t,n}^{(1:K)}
from the joint policy
\pi_{\theta}
using the
autoregressive factorization in (
1
).
For each sample
n
, the mechanism produces a deployed output

y_{t,n}

and the corresponding return-to-go

G_{t,n}.
For each agent
i
we construct a scalar training signal

\widetilde{G}_{i,n}
,
and compute the group-normalized advantage
[
2
]

\widehat{A}_{i,n}=\frac{\widetilde{G}_{i,n}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\widetilde{G}_{i,n^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n^{\prime}}(\widetilde{G}_{i,n^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n^{\prime\prime}}\widetilde{G}_{i,n^{\prime\prime}})^{2}}+\delta}.

We then optimize the clipped GRPO objective
[
2
]

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\theta_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{h_{t},n,t}\!\left[\min\!\Big(\rho_{i,n,t}\widehat{A}_{i,n},\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,n,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,n}\Big)\right]-\beta\mathbb{E}_{h_{t}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{i}}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right],

where

\rho_{i,n,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta_{i}}(w_{i,n,t}\mid h_{t},w_{i,n,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{i}^{\mathrm{old}}}(w_{i,n,t}\mid h_{t},w_{i,n,<t})}.
The only mechanism-specific component is the training signal

\widetilde{G}_{i,n}
, which estimates the marginal contribution

\Delta_{i,t}
.

Competitive routing

For each joint sample
n
, the router selects

I_{t,n}\sim p(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t,n}^{(1:K)})

and deploys
y_{t,n}=a_{t,I_{t,n},n}
, yielding return-to-go
G_{t,n}
.
Since rewards for unselected candidates are unobserved, we learn a
predictor
\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a)
and construct doubly robust estimates

\widehat{G}_{j,n}=\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t,j,n})+\frac{\mathbf{1}\{I_{t,n}=j\}}{p_{j,n}}\left(G_{t,n}-\widehat{\mu}_{\psi}(h_{t},a_{t,j,n})\right).

Let
p^{-i}_{j,n}
denote router probabilities after removing agent
i
.
The training signal is

\widetilde{G}_{i,n}=\sum_{j}p_{j,n}\widehat{G}_{j,n}-\sum_{j\neq i}p^{-i}_{j,n}\widehat{G}_{j,n}.

Collaboration generation

For each sample
n
we observe
G_{t,n}
.
To evaluate the counterfactual without agent
i
, we replace its proposal
with
\tilde{a}_{t,i,n}\sim q_{i}(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t,n}^{(1:i-1)})
,
resample downstream proposals, and compute the counterfactual return

G_{t,n}^{-i}
. The training signal is

\widetilde{G}_{i,n}=G_{t,n}-G_{t,n}^{-i}.

MR instantiation

The theoretical objective uses the return-to-go

G_{t}=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}

as the training signal. Computing
G_{t}
exactly would require
simulating the full future dialogue trajectory after each response,
which is typically infeasible. Instead we approximate the expected
return-to-go using a multiturn-aware evaluation metric
[
3
]
.

For a history–response pair
(h_{t},y_{t})
we estimate the expected
terminal dialogue quality by sampling future trajectories

\tau\sim p(\cdot\mid h_{t},y_{t})

and evaluating the completed dialogue with a terminal scoring
function
\mathcal{R}
:

\mathrm{MR}(h_{t},y_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot\mid h_{t},y_{t})}\big[\mathcal{R}((h_{t},y_{t})\oplus\tau)\big].

This quantity approximates the downstream return-to-go,

\mathrm{MR}(h_{t},y_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}[G_{t}\mid h_{t},y_{t}]
.
In practice we define the training reward as

r(h_{t},y_{t}):=\mathrm{MR}(h_{t},y_{t}).
When the interaction reduces to a single decision step, the return-to-go
equals the immediate reward and the MR estimator collapses to

r(h,y):=\mathcal{R}(h,y).
