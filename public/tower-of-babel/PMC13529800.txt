Vagus nerve–dependent antidepressant effects of ineupatorolide B via gut microbiota modulation in a mouse model of depression
Authors: Siming Wang, Yuqian Zhang, Ningning Wu, Siqi Wang, Yuhe Ma, Weichen Zhang, Guiying Li, Kenji Hashimoto, Zhiyu Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ineupatorolide B (InB), a sesquiterpene lactone with anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor properties, has not previously been evaluated for antidepressant effects. Given the central role of the gut–brain axis in stress-related mood disorders, we investigated whether InB alleviates depression-like phenotypes in mice exposed to chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) through microbiota- and vagus-dependent mechanisms. CSDS-susceptible mice received vehicle, fluoxetine, or InB and were assessed using behavioral paradigms, plasma inflammatory cytokine measurements, and analyses of synaptic-related protein expression in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). CSDS induced robust depression-like behaviors, elevated interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, caused splenomegaly, and reduced PSD-95 and BDNF expression in the mPFC. Repeated InB administration significantly reversed these behavioral, inflammatory, and synaptic abnormalities. 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that CSDS disrupted gut microbial α- and β-diversity, whereas InB treatment largely normalized these alterations and restored depression-associated short-chain fatty acids, including isobutyric and valeric acid, consistent with reestablishment of microbial metabolic homeostasis. Importantly, subdiaphragmatic vagotomy abolished the antidepressant-like, anti-inflammatory, and synaptic effects of InB, demonstrating that its efficacy requires intact vagus nerve–mediated gut–brain signaling. Together, these findings identify vagus-dependent modulation of the microbiota–immune–synaptic axis as a mechanistic pathway through which InB exerts antidepressant effects and highlight this compound as a potential microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategy for depression.

Introduction

Depression is a highly prevalent and disabling mood disorder characterized by persistent low mood and loss of interest or pleasure. Its global burden continues to rise, influenced by socioeconomic stressors and recent large-scale disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Mental health disorders, particularly depression, account for a substantial proportion of suicide deaths worldwide, contributing to approximately 70–80% of suicides in high-income countries and around 50% in low- and middle-income countries [1].

First-line pharmacological treatments include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Although effective for some patients, these agents are limited by delayed therapeutic onset, incomplete response rates, prolonged treatment duration, and adverse effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances and sexual dysfunction, all of which may reduce adherence and overall quality of life [2, 3]. These limitations underscore the need for mechanistically novel and better-tolerated therapeutic strategies. In this context, plant-derived bioactive compounds have attracted increasing interest because of their potential anti-inflammatory, neuromodulatory, and microbiota-regulating properties [4].

Inula cappa (Buchanan-Hamilton ex D. Don) Pruski & Anderberg, also known as Duhaldea cappa [5], is a medicinal shrub widely used in traditional medicine. One of its principal bioactive constituents, ineupatorolide B (InB), is a sesquiterpene lactone with well-documented anti-inflammatory activity mediated by suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production at cellular and tissue levels [5]. Despite these pharmacological properties, the potential antidepressant effects and neurobiological mechanisms of InB have not been examined in preclinical models.

Accumulating evidence implicates the vagus nerve–dependent gut–brain axis as a critical regulator of stress-related mood disorders. Alterations in gut microbial composition can promote systemic inflammation, disrupt neurotransmitter signaling, and contribute to depressive phenotypes [6–10]. The vagus nerve provides a major bidirectional communication pathway linking intestinal microbial activity with central neural circuits that regulate mood and stress responsiveness [11]. Restoration of gut microbial homeostasis or vagal signaling has been shown to alleviate depression-like behaviors in both preclinical and clinical contexts [12]. However, whether the anti-inflammatory properties of InB engage microbiota-dependent and vagus-mediated mechanisms remains unknown.

Here, we investigated the antidepressant potential of InB in a chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) mouse model. We first examined whether repeated InB administration alleviates depression-like behaviors and systemic inflammation. We next assessed its impact on gut microbiota composition and microbial metabolites using 16S rRNA sequencing and short-chain fatty acid profiling. Finally, we assessed whether subdiaphragmatic vagotomy (SDV) would abolish the effects of InB, given that SDV has previously been shown to block depression-like behaviors induced by the oral administration of depression-associated microbes [8, 10] or by fecal microbiota transplantation from mice exhibiting depression-like phenotypes [13].

Materials and methods

Animals

Thirty-six male adult C57BL/6 mice (10 weeks old, 21–33 g) and twenty CD1 mice (17 weeks old, >37 g) were purchased from Beijing West River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were kept in a controlled environment with a 12:12-hour light/dark cycle, where the lights were set to turn on at 20:00. The temperature was maintained at 26 ± 1 °C, and the humidity level was kept at 60%. They had free access to food and water.

Ethical approval

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hebei University (approval number: IACUC-HBU2024M062), following the regulations established by the Hebei University Animal Centre. Every effort was taken to minimize discomfort to the animals.

Drugs and drug administration

The SSRI fluoxetine hydrochloride (Flu; 10 mg/kg, Cayman 14418, USA) was diluted in sterile physiological saline as a positive control [13, 14]. InB was isolated and extracted at the College of Pharmacy, Hebei Medical University, and then mixed with 0.5% (w/v) sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) to prepare 10 mg/kg. Mice in the negative control group were administered with 0.5% CMC (10 mL/kg). These drugs were given through oral gavage (o.g.).

Experimental design

Experiment A: In the chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) model, C57BL/6 mice were subjected to various CD1 aggressor mice for 10 min daily, for 10 days (days 1–10), as detailed previously [8–10, 15]. After 24 h of CSDS, the resident CD1 mice and the intruder C57BL/6 mice were placed in cages opposite each other, separated by a perforated organic glass partition allowing visual, olfactory, and auditory interactions, followed by a 24-hour isolation. Starting on day 11, mice received daily oral administrations of Flu, InB, or CMC at 8:00 a.m. for 9 consecutive days (days 11–19).

The mice were assigned into four groups: (i) Control (no CSDS, n = 9); (ii) Negative (CSDS + 0.5% CMC [10 mL/kg], n = 9); (iii) Flu (CSDS + Flu [10 mg/kg], n = 9); and (iv) InB (CSDS + InB [10 mg/kg], n = 9). The dose of 10 mg/kg for both Flu and InB was selected based on previous studies [16–19]. They were subjected to behavioral tests as follows: the social interaction test (SIT) on day 11, the locomotion test (LMT) and tail suspension test (TST) on day 17, the forced swimming test (FST) on day 18, and the 1% sucrose preference test (SPT) on day 19. Fecal samples were collected on day 19. Heart blood samples were obtained from anesthetized mice and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 3 min; the resulting plasma was stored at −80 °C. Medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) samples were also collected and kept at −80 °C.

Experiment B: Sham surgery (Sham) or SDV was performed under 5% isoflurane anesthesia. The abdominal hair was removed, and the esophagus was exposed under a microscope. The dorsal and ventral vagus nerves of the esophagus were severed. The muscles and skin were sutured after the procedure, and the mice were placed in a clean cage until they recovered from anesthesia. Mice were then maintained in their cages for 14 days (days 1–14) [8, 10]. Starting on day 14, C57BL/6 mice were subjected to CSDS, similar to Experiment A, by being exposed daily to a different CD1 aggressor mouse for 10 min over a 10-day period (days 14–23). After the stress protocol ended on day 23, mice were housed individually for 24 h. Starting on day 24, the mice received daily oral administrations of Flu, InB, or CMC at 8:00 a.m. for 9 consecutive days (days 24–32).

The mice were classified into four groups: (i) Sham group (n = 9): Sham surgery, no CSDS; (ii) Vehicle group (n = 9): SDV + CSDS + 0.5% CMC (10 mL/kg); (iii) Flu group (n = 9): SDV + CSDS + Flu (10 mg/kg); and (iv) InB group (n = 9): SDV + CSDS + InB (10 mg/kg). Five behavioral tests were conducted: the SIT on day 24, the LMT and TST on day 30, the FST on day 31, and the SPT on day 32. On day 33, plasma samples were obtained by centrifuging blood at 3000 × g for 3 min and then stored at −80 °C. mPFC samples were also obtained and kept at −80 °C.

Behavioral tests

Social interaction test (SIT)

After the final social defeat stress event, the SIT was performed at 8:00 a.m. the following day, after which they were placed in an interaction chamber (42 cm³) that contained a smaller cage (11 × 3.5 × 20 cm) on one side. Each mouse was first observed for 2.5 min without an aggressor present, and then another 2.5 min after an unfamiliar aggressor mouse was placed into the smaller cage. The duration of the mouse’s interactions before and after the aggressor’s introduction was recorded. The SIT was used to identify CSDS-susceptible mice [15]. The SIT data are shown in Supplemental Figure S1.

Locomotion test (LMT)

Mice were placed in a 40 cm² arena with transparent walls and tracked for 60 min using Noldus software (Wageningen, Netherlands) to assess their locomotor activity. The arena was disinfected with 75% ethanol (v/v) between each test [15].

Tail suspension test (TST)

Each mouse’s tail was taped approximately 3 cm from its tip and suspended within a black, opaque box (20 × 20 × 35 cm). The duration of immobility was recorded and analyzed using Noldus software [15].

Forced swimming test (FST)

The FST is a well-established behavioral assay used to assess depression-like behavior [20]. Each mouse was placed individually into a transparent cylindrical container (23 cm high × 10 cm in diameter) filled with warm water (23 ± 1 °C) to a depth of 15 cm. Immobility was recorded using Noldus software over 6 min. Mice initially swam vigorously but tended to become more immobile over time.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

Mice were trained with a 1% sucrose solution for 48 h before testing. Each mouse was housed individually, and the position of the water bottle was switched every 12 h to prevent any location-based bias in sucrose preference. Mice were deprived of food and water 4 h before the experiment. Next, two bottles were placed in the cage: one filled with a 1% sucrose solution and the other with water. The mice were given free access to both bottles for 1 h, after which the bottles were reweighed. The sucrose preference index was calculated as (sucrose consumption/total fluid consumption) × 100%, providing a measure of each mouse’s preference for sucrose [21].

Molecular docking

The relevant targets of InB were screened by TCMSP database (Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database) and Swiss Target Prediction database. Using “Depression” as the keyword, from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), OMIM (https://www.omim.org), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org) and DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com) were found to be Depression as the target, and the corresponding target was obtained through integration. The gene target obtained by InB was deduplicated and the depression target was obtained by Venny (2.1.0). Using STRING (https://cn.String-db.org), the intersection gene targets of InB and depression were analyzed online, and the “Homo sapiens” species were selected to obtain their PPI maps, which were optimized by Cytoscape (3.10.3) software.

The protein sequence was derived from the UniProt database. Since no experimentally resolved structure was available, the protein’s three-dimensional (3D) structure was predicted using AlphaFold2 and then energy-optimized with Rosetta Relax. Water molecules were removed and hydrogens were added to the resulting model using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (PyMOL). The compound structure was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Docking simulations were carried out on the CB-DOCK2 online server (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/blinddock.php) in a blind docking approach [22]. CB-DOCK2 uses artificial neural networks to detect binding pockets and employs AutoDock Vina for docking calculations. The docked complexes were evaluated based on binding energy, followed by interaction analysis and docking site assessment to verify binding stability for subsequent dynamics studies. Interactions between the ligand and receptor were analyzed with the Python Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) online server (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web), and the 3D conformations of ligand-receptor complexes were visualized with PyMOL.

Measurement of IL-6 and TNF-α levels

The plasma levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) (RK00008; ab clone, USA) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (RK00027; ab clone, USA) were measured using their corresponding ELISA kits.

Western blot analysis

Protein was extracted from mPFC tissue using a commercial kit (SD-001; Invent Biotechnologies Inc., USA), and its concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit (PC0020; Solarbio). An equal amount of protein (35 μg per sample) was loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). After blocking, membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against glutamate receptor 1 (GluA1) (SD2010, 1:500; HUABIO, China), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (SJ12-09, 1:500; HUABIO, China), PSD-95 (SR38-09, 1:500; HUABIO, China), and β-actin (AC026, 1:10,000; ABclonal, USA). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit DyLight 800 secondary antibody (S9002, 1:1000; Report) at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a fluorescence imaging system and analyzed with Image J 1.0 (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Cell culture and processing

BV2 cells are an immortalized cell line derived from mouse microglia. Mouse BV2 cells (CL-0493, Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and penicillin (50 units/mL) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 10⁷ cells/well and cultured until they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. After a 1-hour pretreatment with InB (2 and 4 µM), cells were exposed to a culture medium enriched with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 200 µM) and corticosterone (200 µM) for an additional 24 h.

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was evaluated with the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate/propidium iodide (FITC/PI) staining kit (Sigma-Aldrich, No. 46950-50MG-F). BV2 cells were plated in a 6-well plate and treated according to the established protocol. The treated cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and reconstituted in 1× binding buffer. The cells were then incubated with FITC Annexin V and PI for 15 min at 25 °C in the dark. Following staining, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur).

16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) analysis

Fecal samples were processed by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR with the forward primer 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and the reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). After quantification, the microbiome data were analyzed using QIIME2 2019.4 (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.4/tutorials/) [23], with slight modifications to the official tutorial procedures.

Measurement of short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Fecal samples were collected as previously reported [8–10, 15, 24] and stored in sterile microtubes at −80 °C. SCFAs were measured by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). SCFA analysis was performed using gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) with an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a DB-FFAP column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, USA).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed utilizing SPSS Statistics 20 (SPSS, Tokyo, Japan) and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Fecal sample data were analyzed with QIIME2 (2019.4) and R scripts. Weight data were evaluated using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test for multiple comparisons. For comparing the four groups, a one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD test or the Kruskal-Wallis test were employed. The α-diversity of the gut microbiota and the relative abundance of bacteria at various taxonomic levels were examined using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. Gut microbiota abundance and distribution were analyzed with algorithms or linear discriminant analysis of effect size (LEfSe) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). The correlation between gut microbiota and SCFAs was assessed using Spearman’s correlation analysis [25]. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of InB on the depression-like phenotype

The experimental procedure for Experiment A is illustrated in Fig. 1A. CSDS modeling and Flu or InB administration did not significantly affect body weight (Fig. 1B). However, CSDS did not impair the motor function of the mice (Fig. 1C). Compared with the control group, CSDS prolonged immobility times in the TST and FST, effects that were not observed in the Flu- or InB-treated groups (Fig. 1D–E). In the SPT, CSDS significantly reduced the sucrose preference relative to the InB group (Fig. 1F). Taken together, these results suggest that both InB and Flu exhibit antidepressant-like effects in the CSDS model. CSDS exhibited significantly high plasma levels of TNF-α and IL-6, while InB and Flu treatments substantially reduced the levels of these cytokines, with InB showing a more pronounced effect than Flu (Fig. 1G–H). In addition, CSDS caused splenomegaly [19, 26, 27], which was not observed in the InB group (Fig. 1I–J), likely due to the anti-inflammatory properties of InB [10]. Furthermore, Flu and InB treatments significantly upregulated GluA1, PSD-95, and BDNF expression in the mPFC of CSDS mice (Fig. 1K–M). The Annexin V/PI and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays showed that corticosterone/H₂O₂ treatment significantly increased the cell apoptosis rate relative to the control group. The influence of InB on gut microbiota

CSDS mice exhibited an imbalance in their gut microbiota, consistent with previous findings [9, 20, 27–29]. α-diversity and β-diversity were analyzed to determine the diversity and composition of the gut microbiota across the four groups. LEfSe analysis revealed marked differences in microbial abundances among the four groups (Fig. 4I). Hierarchical clustering showed that the CSDS and control groups clustered distinctly, a divergence not observed in the InB group (Fig. 4J). Further clustering analyses revealed that after CSDS, the microbial community in the InB group more closely resembled that of the control group (Fig. 4K).

Additional examination at the phylum level demonstrated marked differences in gut microbiota composition among the four groups (Fig. S2A). Particularly, CSDS mice exhibited significantly increased levels of Bacteroidetes and significantly reduced levels of TM7, Tenericutes, and Proteobacteria (Fig. S2B–E). Nevertheless, this pattern was absent in the InB group, implying that InB treatment may restore the gut microbiota composition to a state closer to that of the control group.

Fecal SCFA contents differed among the four groups. Relative to the control group, CSDS mice exhibited significantly reduced levels of isobutyric and valeric acids, a change not observed in the InB group. Furthermore, propionic acid levels differed significantly between the InB and CSDS groups (Fig. S3A–C). Subsequently, logistic regression and correlation analyses were performed to explore the relationship between gut microbiota and SCFAs. At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes were negatively associated with isobutyric acid (r = −0.4427, P = 0.0069), whereas TM7 exhibited a positive correlation with isobutyric acid (r = 0.3468, P = 0.0382) (Fig. S3D–E).

Discussion

The present study reveals several key findings. First, repeated administration of InB significantly alleviated depression-like behaviors in the CSDS mouse model, as indicated by reduced immobility in the TST and FST and restoration of sucrose preference. Second, InB decreased systemic inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α), prevented splenomegaly, and normalized synaptic-related proteins (GluA1, PSD-95, and BDNF) in the mPFC, suggesting coordinated anti-inflammatory and synaptic regulatory effects. Third, InB reversed CSDS-induced disruptions in gut microbiota diversity and composition and restored SCFA levels, particularly isobutyric and valeric acid, supporting modulation of the gut–brain axis. Fourth, subdiaphragmatic vagotomy abolished the antidepressant and anti-inflammatory effects of InB, demonstrating a requirement for vagus nerve–mediated gut–brain signaling. Collectively, these findings position InB as a biologically active compound capable of mitigating stress-induced depressive phenotypes through coordinated regulation of immune, microbial, and neural pathways.

Our data support a model in which InB reshapes gut microbial composition, suppresses systemic inflammation, and restores synaptic plasticity markers in the mPFC. Although causal relationships between specific microbial taxa and behavioral outcomes remain unresolved, the convergence of microbiota normalization, cytokine reduction, and synaptic restoration supports an integrated microbiota–immune–synaptic mechanism. Notably, fluoxetine increased synaptic proteins without reducing plasma IL-6 in CSDS mice [15], whereas InB simultaneously reduced inflammatory cytokines and restored synaptic protein expression, suggesting engagement of inflammation–synaptic crosstalk potentially mediated through microbiota-dependent pathways.

The antidepressant effects of InB may therefore arise primarily from peripheral gut–vagus–immune signaling rather than direct central pharmacological action. A key limitation is the absence of pharmacokinetic evidence demonstrating blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration; thus, the behavioral effects cannot currently be attributed to direct central exposure. Instead, normalization of systemic inflammation and microbiota-derived metabolites supports a mechanism whereby InB influences brain function through vagal afferent and neuroimmune communication.

Consistent with this interpretation, the protective effects of InB in BV2 microglial cells should be viewed as evidence of intrinsic anti-inflammatory potential, rather than proof of direct microglial modulation in vivo. Peripheral suppression of inflammatory signaling and restoration of microbial metabolites may indirectly regulate central microglial activation and synaptic plasticity without requiring BBB penetration. Future studies integrating brain pharmacokinetics, metabolite tracing, vagal manipulation, and microbiota-targeted approaches will be required to determine whether InB acts exclusively through peripheral signaling or also possesses central bioavailability.

Accumulating evidence indicates that patients with depression exhibit reduced SCFA levels—including butyric, acetic, isobutyric, and valeric acid—which may influence neuronal activity through immune and neuroendocrine mechanisms [30]. SCFAs exert anti-inflammatory effects via G-protein-coupled receptors such as GPR41 and GPR43 and can attenuate systemic inflammation [31]. They also stabilize hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function, cross the BBB, and modulate stress-related neuroendocrine signaling [32]. In the present study, InB restored CSDS-induced reductions in SCFAs, consistent with recovery of microbial metabolic homeostasis and downstream behavioral benefits.

Nevertheless, fecal SCFA concentrations represent indirect metabolic indices influenced by absorption, host metabolism, and intestinal transit. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as supportive correlates rather than definitive mediators. Future studies measuring circulating and brain SCFA levels, together with receptor-level analyses of GPR41/43 signaling, will be necessary to establish causal links between SCFA regulation and antidepressant effects.

Interestingly, Proteobacteria abundance was not normalized by InB despite improvements in behavior, inflammation, synaptic proteins, and SCFAs. Because Proteobacteria represent a minor yet immunologically relevant fraction, this observation suggests that global taxonomic normalization is not required for antidepressant efficacy. Instead, selective modulation of functional microbial pathways—including SCFA production, immune signaling, and vagus-mediated communication—may be sufficient to restore behavioral homeostasis. This interpretation aligns with emerging views that microbial metabolites and neural signaling, rather than overall community structure, are primary determinants of neuropsychiatric outcomes and supports development of precision microbiota-targeted interventions.

The vagus nerve serves as a central conduit of gut–brain communication, transmitting inflammatory and metabolic signals from the gastrointestinal tract to the brain [11]. Vagal afferent activity can promote neuroinflammatory signaling, microglial activation, and synaptic dysregulation, thereby contributing to depression-like behaviors [11, 33–35], while stress-induced HPA axis activation further amplifies immune responses [36]. Prior studies demonstrated vagus-dependent behavioral effects of Lactobacillus rhamnosus (JB-1) [37] and depression-like phenotypes induced by microbiota transfer from CSDS-susceptible mice that are attenuated by SDV [8, 10, 12, 38]. Consistent with this framework, SDV abolished the behavioral, inflammatory, and synaptic effects of InB, confirming a requirement for vagus-dependent microbiota–brain signaling.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. The molecular pathways downstream of vagus-dependent microbiota signaling require further clarification, including the roles of specific metabolites and afferent neural circuits. Generalizability beyond the CSDS model remains uncertain, and additional stress paradigms should be examined. Brain-region specificity beyond the mPFC, central inflammatory signaling, and causal microbiota manipulations (e.g., fecal transplantation or gnotobiotic models) also warrant investigation. The absence of treatment-only control groups limits interpretation of drug-specific effects independent of stress, and future pharmacokinetic studies are needed to determine central exposure of InB. Finally, the exclusive use of male mice restricts translational generalizability, highlighting the importance of sex-inclusive experimental designs.

To enhance interpretive rigor, the present conclusions prioritize experimentally supported microbiota- and vagus-dependent mechanisms, whereas exploratory analyses such as network pharmacology and in vitro assays are regarded as hypothesis-generating.

In conclusion, InB alleviates depression-like behaviors in CSDS-susceptible mice by modulating gut microbiota composition, restoring SCFA metabolism, suppressing systemic inflammation, and enhancing synaptic plasticity in the mPFC. These effects require intact vagus-dependent gut–brain signaling. Together, the findings identify InB as a microbiota-targeted therapeutic candidate and support modulation of microbiota–immune–neural interactions as a translational strategy for depression.
