Equilibrating continuous-variable open quantum systems using stochastic classical trajectories in path-integral space
William H. D. Moore, Stuart C. Althorpe

Abstract
Beyond the weak-coupling limit, open quantum systems equilibrate to a highly entangled thermal state. For continuous-variable systems, this state can be written explicitly as an imaginary-time phase-space path integral, in which the positions are directly entangled with the bath, and the momenta are correlated with the positions through a phase term. Here, we ask to what extent this state can be reached by propagating stochastic classical trajectories in path-integral phase space. Surprisingly, we find that the trajectories equilibrate to the exact quantum equilibrium state, recovering the purely imaginary momentum–position correlation in the phase term. The trajectories are generated using a recently derived Matsubara generalized Langevin equation, which produces the imaginary correlations by evolving the stochastic variables into the complex plane. This makes the dynamics numerically unstable, but we are nonetheless able to demonstrate the equilibration of a quartic oscillator coupled to a white-noise bath. These unexpected findings could lead to new approximate methodologies for simulating continuous-variable open quantum systems.

For an open quantum system, a non-equilibrium reduced density matrix (obtained by tracing the full density matrix over the locally equilibrated bath states) will equilibrate to the fully entangled equilibrium state after propagating for long enough with the exact quantum time-evolution operator. For a continuous-variable system, this equilibrium state can be written out explicitly in terms of imaginary-time path-integral phase-space variables
23
;
1
;
17
. The entanglement appears as a Gaussian term reducing the amplitude of thermal fluctuations around the path centroids in response to the bath coupling. Crucially, the system momenta and positions are correlated through a phase term.

In this article we investigate whether it is possible to equilibrate to this state by propagating stochastic classical trajectories in path-integral phase space. It is well known that this can be done for the position marginal, using the well-established technique of path-integral molecular dynamics, whereby a thermostat is attached to a fictitious ‘ring-polymer’ Hamiltonian
16
;
11
. However, generating the full momentum–position distribution from classical trajectories would appear to be impossible on account of the phase, which stochastic classical dynamics cannot be expected to generate. Surprisingly, we show in this article that this is not the case: stochastic classical trajectories can, at least in principle, and sometimes in practice (depending on numerical stability), equilibrate to the exact quantum equilibrium state.

The key is to generate the trajectories using a generalised Langevin equation (GLE) in path-integral phase-space, which was derived recently in Ref.
17
using Matsubara dynamics. ‘Matsubara dynamics’ in this context refers to a semiclassical approximation to the exact quantum dynamics for a continuous system, which arises when initially smooth imaginary-time Feynman paths are assumed to remain smooth for all time
1
;
8
. This sole approximation is sufficient to collapse the exact quantum dynamics to an ensemble of classical trajectories in the extended path-integral phase space. Ref.
17
showed that, when applied to a system–bath Hamiltonian of the form of Eq. (
1
), Matsubara dynamics can be used to derive a GLE in the path-integral phase space of the system.

In Ref.
17
it was assumed that, although this GLE conserves the quantum Boltzmann distribution, it would not be capable of taking an arbitrary initial distribution and equilibrating it, on account of the momentum--position phase
1
1

1

To compensate for this, Ref.
17

t=0
using analytic continuation.

Here, we show that it does precisely this, by evolving the system into the complex plane such that the resulting distribution naturally includes the phase and correctly describes the imaginary correlations between the momenta and positions. To prove that the Matsubara GLE has this property, we map it onto an auxiliary space in which the stochastic dynamics becomes Markovian, from which we construct a Fokker–Planck equation, the stationary solution of which is then shown to marginalise to the exact quantum equilibrium state.

II
Classical background

II.1
Generalised Langevin equations

Consider an open classical system with the usual system–bath Hamiltonian
23
;
22

H(p,q,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x})=H_{\text{s}}(p,q)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\Bigg[\frac{p_{\alpha}^{2}}{2m_{\alpha}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}\left(x_{\alpha}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}q\right)^{2}\Bigg]

(3)

where

H_{\text{s}}(p,q)=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(q)

(4)

are generated from a spectral density

J(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\frac{c_{\alpha}^{2}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\alpha}).

The dynamics of the system coordinates

can be propagated using a generalised Langevin equation (GLE)

where

and

\displaystyle\zeta(t-s)

\displaystyle=\beta\langle R(s)R(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\frac{c_{\alpha}^{2}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}\cos{\left(\omega_{\alpha}(t-s)\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\cos\left(\omega(t-s)\right).

(8)

This equation is the fluctuation–dissipation relation
10
that ensures that an arbitrary, normalised, system probability density evolves to the equilibrium distribution
Z^{-1}\exp[-\beta H_{\text{s}}(p,q)]
as

.

In the special case of an ohmic spectral density

J(\omega)=m\gamma\omega

(9)

the noise
R(t)
becomes white (and will then be written

), such that

\zeta(t-s)=\beta\langle W(s)W(t)\rangle=2m\gamma\,\delta(t-s)

(10)

and the dynamics becomes Markovian, with Eq. (
6
) reducing to the Langevin equation

m\ddot{q}(t)=-V^{\prime}[q(t)]-m\gamma\dot{q}(t)+W(t).

(11)

II.2
Fokker–Planck equations for Markovian dynamics

A stochastic, Markovian, equation of motion such as Eq. (
11
) is equivalent to a deterministic Fokker–Planck equation (FPE) describing the time-evolution of the probability density
\rho(p,q,t)
. There is a standard procedure for generating the FPE
24
;
18
. The stochastic equations of motion are put into the form

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(t))+\mathbf{F}(t)

(12)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)
is a vector composed of the
n

and

F_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}\eta_{j}(t)

(13)

in which
\eta_{j}(t)
are white noise-variables with zero mean and unit variance.
The FPE corresponding to Eq. (

\frac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}=\left[-\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{\Theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\right)\right]\rho(\mathbf{x},t)

(14)

where

\Theta_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{ik}\alpha_{jk}.

(15)

Applying this procedure to Eq. (
11
) yields the well-known Klein–Kramers equation

\frac{\partial\rho(p,q,t)}{\partial t}=\bigg[-\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\frac{p}{m}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{p}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{q}}\\
+\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left(p+\frac{m}{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\right)\bigg]\rho(p,q,t).

(16)

II.3
Fokker–Planck equations for non-Markovian dynamics

To obtain the FPE corresponding to a non-Markovian stochastic dynamics, we need to map onto an auxiliary space in which the dynamics is Markovian

. To illustrate this procedure, consider the GLE of Eq. (
6
) with a Debye–Drude spectral density

The first step is to introduce a stochastic variable

When

\displaystyle=\frac{p(t)}{m}

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V(q(t))}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q}+z(t)

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}z(t)-\gamma\omega_{c}p(t)+\omega_{c}W(t)

Since the dynamics of Eq. (

The stationary solution

III
Imaginary-time Path integrals

III.1
Equilibrium distributions

For an isolated system, with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{s}}
, the position marginal of the path-integral equilibrium distribution is
16
;
5

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf q})

\displaystyle={1\over Z}\prod_{i=l}^{N}\langle q_{l}|e^{-\beta_{N}\hat{H}_{\text{s}}}|q_{l+1}\rangle

\displaystyle={1\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{N}\left[U_{N}({\bf q})+S_{N}({\bf q})\right]}

(28)

where
\beta_{N}=\beta/N
,
q_{N+1}\equiv q_{1}
, and

\displaystyle U_{N}({\bf q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{N}V(q_{l})

(29a)

\displaystyle S_{N}({\bf q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{N}{(q_{l+1}-q_{l})^{2}m\over 2\beta_{N}\hbar^{2}}.

(29b)

The imaginary-time Feynman paths
{\bf q}\equiv\left\{q_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
are Wiener paths in imaginary time
\tau=0\to\beta\hbar
which resemble jagged loops, often nicknamed ‘ring polymers’.

The paths can be Fourier-smoothed into differentiable functions of
\tau
by approximating them as
4

q\left(\tau\right)=Q_{0}+\sqrt{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\widetilde{M}}\left[Q_{n}\sin\omega_{n}\tau+Q_{\bar{n}}\cos\omega_{n}\tau\right]

(30)

with
\widetilde{M}=(M-1)/2

2
2

2

We assume throughout that
M
is odd.
, where
M
is the number of Fourier modes, and

\displaystyle\omega_{n}={2n\pi\over\beta\hbar}

(31)

are the Matsubara frequencies. Formally, this smoothing is produced by making
M
finite, then taking the limit
N\to\infty
in the distribution of Eq. (
III.1
). The mode
Q_{0}
is often referred to as the ‘centroid’ and the modes

as the ‘Matsubara modes’
1
;
8

\beta\to 0
. [Note that in the above and in what follows we use the convention that positive/negative values of

On smoothing the paths, Eq. (

\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf Q})={1\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left[U_{M}({\bf Q})+S_{M}({\bf Q})\right]}

where

\displaystyle U_{M}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle={1\over\beta\hbar}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,V[q(\tau)]

(33a)

\displaystyle S_{M}({\bf Q})

\displaystyle={m\over 2\beta\hbar}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left({\partial q(\tau)\over\partial\tau}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={m\over 2}\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\omega_{n}^{2}Q_{n}^{2}.

(33b)

One can obtain the fully correlated position–momentum distribution from Eq. (

\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf P},{\bf Q})={1\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left[H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})-i\theta_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})\right]}

where

\displaystyle H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})

\displaystyle={1\over\beta\hbar}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}\!d\tau\,\left({p^{2}(\tau)\over 2m}+V[q(\tau)]\right)

\displaystyle={{\bf P}^{2}\over 2m}+U_{M}({\bf Q})

(35)

\displaystyle\theta_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=-{1\over\beta\hbar}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}\!d\tau\,{\partial q(\tau)\over\partial\tau}p(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\omega_{n}Q_{\bar{n}}P_{n}

(36)

and
p(\tau)

are defined by analogy with Eq. (
30
).
We will refer to

as the ‘Matsubara Hamiltonian’ and
\theta_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})
as the ‘Matsubara phase’. It is well known

;
6

III.2
Matsubara dynamics

Under real-time quantum evolution, a function which initially depends only on the smooth Matsubara paths
p(\tau)
and
q(\tau)
, or equivalently, the Matsubara modes
({\bf P},{\bf Q})
, rapidly becomes dependent also on the jagged, discontinuous modes that have been excluded from Eq. (
30
)—unless
V(q)
is harmonic. This is because the exact time evolution is generated by applying
N
replicas of the von-Neumann–Liouville operator, one at each of the discrete imaginary time-slices in Eq. (
III.1
). However, it is possible to project the jagged modes out of this operator, to ensure that an initially smooth function remains smooth for all time
1
;
8
. The application of this constraint makes the dynamics classical in the extended phase space
({\bf P},{\bf Q})
, where the trajectories follow the equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle={\partial H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})\over\partial P_{n}}={P_{n}\over m}

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=-{\partial H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})\over\partial Q_{n}}=-{\partial U_{M}({\bf Q})\over\partial Q_{n}}

(37)

in which
H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
is the Matsubara Hamiltonian of Eq. (
III.1
). We will refer to this approximate dynamics as ‘Matsubara dynamics’.

Despite being classical, Matsubara dynamics conserves the quantum Boltzmann distribution of Eq. (
34
). This is because
H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
is conserved from Eq. (
III.2
), and
\theta_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
is conserved because it is the momentum conjugate to a continuous symmetry transformation

\tau\to\tau+\tau_{0}

(38)

under which
H_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
is invariant. This last property follows from the periodicity of the imaginary-time paths, which allows one to write

\frac{\partial H_{M}}{\partial\tau_{0}}=0.

(39)

Additional properties of this imaginary-time-translation symmetry are given in Appendix
A
. Two key relations that will be used later in Sec.
V
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U_{M}}{\partial\tau_{0}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\omega_{n}Q_{\bar{n}}\frac{\partial U_{M}}{\partial Q_{n}}=0

(40)

and

\displaystyle R_{n}\left(\tau_{0}+\frac{\beta\hbar}{4n}\right)

\displaystyle=-R_{\bar{n}}\left(\tau_{0}\right)

\displaystyle R_{\bar{n}}\left(\tau_{0}+\frac{\beta\hbar}{4n}\right)

\displaystyle=R_{n}\left(\tau_{0}\right)

(41)

where

P

The reduced phase-space equilibrium distribution is

is now the system contribution to the total Matsubara phase, the bath contribution having been integrated out to give the second term in Eq. (

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\frac{c_{\alpha}^{2}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}\frac{s}{s^{2}+\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}

IV.2
Matsubara generalised Langevin equation

Reference
17

B

-\left[Q_{n}(0)K_{n}(t)-iQ_{\bar{n}}(0)L_{n}(t)\right]

(45)

where

\displaystyle f^{(n)}_{M}(\mathbf{Q})=-{\partial U_{M}(\mathbf{Q})\over\partial Q_{n}}

(46)

and the complex-valued noise is

+\left(\xi_{\alpha n}+i\frac{\omega_{n}}{\omega_{\alpha n}}\lambda_{\alpha\bar{n}}\right)\sin{\omega_{\alpha}t}\bigg]

(47)

where
\lambda_{\alpha n}
and
\xi_{\alpha n}
are Gaussian random variates with zero mean and unit variance and
\omega_{\alpha n}^{2}=\omega_{\alpha}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}
. The memory kernels of

are

\displaystyle\langle R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(s)R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(t-s)}{\beta}-\frac{K_{n}(t-s)}{\beta}

\displaystyle\langle R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(s)R_{\bar{n}}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{iL_{n}(t-s)}{\beta}

\displaystyle\langle R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(s)R_{n^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\quad|n|\neq|n^{\prime}|

(48)

where

\displaystyle K_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{n}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\frac{\cos\omega t}{\omega^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}}

(49a)

\displaystyle L_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{n}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,J(\omega)\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}}.

(49b)

Equation (
45
) differs from the GLE of Ref.
17
only in the form of the transient driving terms in the second line.
These terms result from locally equilibrating the bath modes around the system coordinate at time

and cannot be removed by adjusting the initial distribution of the bath modes. They arise because the non-Markovian
R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)
lacks history at

45

V.1
White-noise spectral density

For a white-noise spectral density, neglecting the transient terms, Eq. (

\displaystyle\langle R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(s)R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)\rangle

V.1.3
The imaginary momentum–position correlation

The Matsubara Langevin equation of Eq. (
50
) produces the equilibrium distribution

\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
without explicitly invoking the phase
\exp[-i\theta_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})]
. In other words, an
expectation value of some property
A({\bf P},{\bf Q})
can be evaluated using

\int\!d{\bf P}\int\!d{\bf Q}\,\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf P},{\bf Q})A({\bf P},{\bf Q})=\\
\lim_{N_{\text{t}}\to\infty}{1\over N_{\text{t}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{t}}}A({\bf P}(t_{i}),{\bf Q}(t_{i}))

(67)

where the phase-space points
\left\{{\bf P}(t_{i}),{\bf Q}(t_{i})\right\}
are taken at
N_{\text{t}}
times
t_{i}
from a trajectory propagated using Eq. (
50
). The phase is included naturally in the stationary distribution as a result of the complex-valued random force
R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
which pushes the phase-space variables away from the real axis, such that
P_{n}
and
Q_{\bar{n}}
maintain the purely imaginary correlation given by
\theta_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
.

Another way of looking at this is that the propagation of Eq. (
50
) has the effect of analytically continuing the distribution
\rho_{\text{eq}}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
into the complex plane, such that the phase no longer appears. It therefore eliminates the dreaded ‘phase problem’ but has replaced it with another numerical difficulty, since classical dynamics in the complex plane is well known to be numerically unstable. From Eq. (
V.1.1
), we can see that the instability is likely to be tamed by increasing
\gamma
and worsened by increasing
|n|
. This suggests that any attempt to propagate trajectories using Eq. (
50
) will be numerically stable only if
M
is sufficiently small.

V.1.4
A simple numerical test

VI
Conclusions

It is surprising that stochastic classical trajectories in an extended space can equilibrate to the exact quantum equilibrium state, correctly including the purely imaginary correlations between the momentum and position variables. Nonetheless, there are no free lunches, and the stochastic classical dynamics produces these correlations by evolving the trajectories into the complex plane. Inevitably, this leads to numerical instability, so we are not advocating the use of this approach as a practical method. It may, however, be useful as a starting point from which to derive more approximate and practical methods for simulating continuous-variable open quantum dynamics.

(86)

so that

\frac{\partial R_{n}}{\partial\tau_{0}}\equiv\frac{\partial R_{n}(0)}{\partial\tau_{0}}=-\omega_{n}R_{\bar{n}}.

(87)

Subsituting this last expression (with
R\to Q
) into

{\partial U_{M}({\bf Q})\over\partial\tau_{0}}=0=\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}{\partial Q_{n}\over\partial\tau_{0}}{\partial U_{M}({\bf Q})\over\partial Q_{n}}

(88)

gives Eq. (
40
).

Appendix B
Derivation of the Matsubara GLE

The Matsubara Hamiltonian corresponding to Eq. (
3
) is

H_{M}^{\text{tot}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},{\bf X})=H_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\Bigg[\frac{P_{\alpha,n}^{2}}{2m_{\alpha}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}\left(X_{\alpha,n}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}Q_{n}\right)^{2}\Bigg]

(89)

where
(P_{\alpha,n},X_{\alpha,n})
are the Matsubara modes corresponding to bath variables
(p_{\alpha},x_{\alpha})
. To obtain an initial distribution in which the bath is locally equilibrated around the system variables, and the system is prepared in some arbitrary initial density
\rho_{0}
, we start with the full system–bath equilibrium distribution

\rho_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{X})={1\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left[H_{M}^{\text{tot}}({\bf P},{\bf Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{X})-i\theta^{\text{tot}}_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{X})\right]}

(90)

where

\theta^{\text{tot}}_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},\mathbf{X})=\theta_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})\\
+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\omega_{n}X_{\alpha,{\bar{n}}}P_{\alpha,n}.

(91)

We then make the substitution

P_{\alpha,n}=\Pi_{\alpha,n}+im_{\alpha}\omega_{n}X_{\alpha\bar{n}}

(92)

which is equivalent to shifting the momentum integration contour so as to eliminate the bath contribution to the phase, giving

\rho_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q},\mathbf{\Pi}_{b},\mathbf{X})={1\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left[F_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q})+B_{M}({\bf\Pi}_{b},{\bf X};{\bf Q})-i\theta_{M}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})\right]}

(93)

where

B_{M}({\bf\Pi}_{b},{\bf X};{\bf Q})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}\sum_{n=-\widetilde{M}}^{\widetilde{M}}\left(\frac{\Pi_{\alpha,n}^{2}}{2m_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha n}^{2}{\overline{X}}_{\alpha,n}^{2}\right)

(94)

and

{\overline{X}}_{\alpha,n}\equiv X_{\alpha,n}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha n}^{2}}Q_{n}.

(95)

Replacing the system-dependent part of Eq. (
93
) by
\rho_{0}({\bf P},{\bf Q})
, we then obtain

\rho_{M}({\bf P},{\bf Q},\mathbf{\Pi}_{b},\mathbf{X})={\rho_{0}({\bf P},{\bf Q})\over\cal{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta B_{M}({\bf\Pi}_{b},{\bf X};{\bf Q})}

(96)

which describes an initial distribution in which the bath variables
X_{\alpha,n}
are equilibrated about the system coordinate
Q_{n}
, and the system distribution is arbitrary.

From Eq. (
III.2
),
H_{M}^{\text{tot}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q},{\bf P}_{\text{b}},{\bf X})
generates the Matsubara-dynamics equations of motion

\displaystyle m\ddot{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle=f^{(n)}_{M}(\mathbf{Q})+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}c_{\alpha}\left(X_{\alpha n}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}Q_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\ddot{X}_{\alpha n}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{\alpha}^{2}\left(X_{\alpha n}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}Q_{n}\right)

(97)

with
f^{(n)}_{M}(\mathbf{Q})
defined in Eq. (
46
).
Solving for the bath dynamics up to time
t
, we obtain

m\ddot{Q}_{n}(t)=f^{(n)}_{M}(\mathbf{Q}(t))-\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\zeta(t-s)\dot{Q}_{n}(s)\\
+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}c_{\alpha}\left[\left(X_{\alpha,n}-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}Q_{n}\right)\cos{\omega_{\alpha}t}+\frac{P_{\alpha,n}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}}\sin{\omega_{\alpha}t}\right]

(98)

with
\zeta(t)
given in Eq. (
II.1
),
X_{\alpha,n}\equiv X_{\alpha,n}(t=0)
and similarly for

Q_{n}
and
P_{\alpha,n}
. Converting from
{\bf P}_{\text{b}}
to
{\bf\Pi}_{\text{b}}
, we then obtain

m\ddot{Q}_{n}(t)=f^{(n)}_{M}(\mathbf{Q}(t))-\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\zeta(t-s)\dot{Q}_{n}(s)\\
+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n_{b}}c_{\alpha}\bigg[{\overline{X}}_{\alpha,n}\cos{\omega_{\alpha}t}+\left(\frac{\Pi_{\alpha,n}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}}+i\frac{\omega_{n}}{\omega_{\alpha}}{\overline{X}}_{\alpha,\bar{n}}\right)\sin{\omega_{\alpha}t}\\
-\frac{c_{\alpha}\omega_{n}}{m_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha n}^{2}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}}{\omega_{\alpha}}Q_{n}\cos\omega_{\alpha}t-iQ_{\bar{n}}\sin\omega_{\alpha}t\right)\bigg].

(99)

Comparison of the variances of
{\bf\Pi}_{\text{b}}
and
{\bf X}
in Eq. (
96
) with Eq. (
47
) shows that the first three terms in the sum correspond to
R_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(t)
in the GLE [Eq. (
45
)]; comparison with Eq. (
49
) shows that the last two terms correspond to
-\left[Q_{n}K_{n}(t)-iQ_{\bar{n}}L_{n}(t)\right]
.
