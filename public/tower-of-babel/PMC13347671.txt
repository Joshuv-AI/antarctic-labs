Propagation Process of Noncaking Coal Dust Explosion Impact Airflow and Generation Characteristics of CO and CO2 in Vertical Space
Authors: Tianqi Liu, Fengshuo Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The explosion hazards of noncaking coal dust have received widespread attention. To obtain the characteristics of noncaking coal dust explosions in vertical space, research has been conducted on the impact airflow and toxic and harmful gas products. It is found that the impact airflow velocity first increases and then decreases. t = 45 ms is a sufficient reaction time inside the vertical space, and the impact airflow velocity has reaches its maximum. The expansion of the high-temperature region and the high-speed airflow promote the propagation of CO, expanding the toxic range of CO. A certain concentration of CO2 is generated in the ignition region, with a mass concentration of over 0.08%. This indicates that in the initial stage of a coal dust explosion, the amount of CO2 generated has reached a certain concentration, posing a potential threat to the environment and personnel safety. The region of y = 0.13∼0.3 m is affected by the rising impact airflow and wall effects, and the CO2 generated in the central region of the pipe is much lower than that generated in the wall region. This phenomenon reveals the uneven distribution of gas products during the explosion process, which is of great significance for understanding the dynamics of explosions and the propagation mechanism of gas products.

Introduction

Coal is a very important energy source in China, accounting for a significant proportion of the country’s energy consumption structure. In the process of coal mining, the generation of dust is inevitable. , Noncaking coal is a common type of bituminous coal that has undergone significant oxidation in its early stages of formation. When heated, there is basically no production of gelatinous bodies. Noncaking coal has a high moisture content, may also contain a certain amount of secondary humic acid, and has a high oxygen content, some as high as 10% or more. Once the dust in a limited space reaches a certain concentration and is ignited by appropriate energy, it is prone to explosive disasters, which are extremely dangerous. − Even worse, the impact airflow and toxic pollutants generated by explosions can lead to secondary disasters, severe pollution, and large-scale deaths. − Therefore, in order to prevent secondary disasters and reduce the casualties and property losses caused by dust explosions, it is necessary to study the impact airflow generated by dust explosions and further investigate the characteristics of toxic and harmful products generated by dust explosions.

Dust explosions are different from combustible gas explosions. Their explosion reactions have both similarities and certain differences. − In the explosion process of combustible gas, there is basically no participation of particulate matter, mainly reacting with the surrounding gas phase. However, the reaction process of a dust explosion is more complex, including the two-phase reaction of solid particles and the surrounding gas phase. − After dust particles are ignited, a large amount of combustible volatile gases are released, and the combustible gases and particles explode together, releasing a large amount of energy. Taking noncaking coal as an example, the carbon content of noncaking coal is generally between 75% and 85%. It is mainly produced in the northwest region of China. Related studies have shown that under the same conditions, the hazards of dust explosions are greater than those of combustible gas explosions. − Noncaking coal is mainly used for gasification and power generation and can also be used as power and civilian fuel; therefore, the explosion risk of noncaking coal deserves attention.

At present, compared to using experimental methods to study the physical suppression process of dust explosions in vertical pipes, numerical simulation methods have more advantages in operability and visibility. − The numerical simulation method based on computational fluid dynamics theory can restore the chemical reaction process of dust explosion, helping us predict the flame propagation characteristics and evolution laws of the instantaneous process of dust explosion in time and space. − The numerical simulation research on dust explosion characteristics mainly focuses on small-scale experimental instruments such as Hartmann tubes, 20 L spheres, and horizontal combustion tubes, and the simulation of dust explosions. − There has been relatively little research on the simulation of explosion hazards caused by coal dust explosions. At the same time, current research mainly reports on the propagation process and acceleration mechanism of the dust explosion flames. There are few reports on the propagation of impact airflow, and further research is needed on the gas products generated by the explosions. Relevant in-depth research is of great significance for further understanding the hazards of powder explosions.

From the above, it can be concluded that more research is needed on the hazards of destructive disasters caused by coal dust explosions. The author of this article discussed the influencing factors of explosion intensity in previous studies, revealed the correlation between dust cloud ignition and explosion, identified the formation conditions and propagation characteristics of dust clouds, and conducted small-scale coal dust explosion research. − The above research and achievements have provided a solid foundation for further research in this article. In view of this, this article selects mathematical models related to dust explosion propagation characteristics for suspended noncaking coal in a vertical pipeline, constructs a geometric model of the vertical pipeline using ICEM software, and divides the grid. The innovation of this article lies in the analysis of the flow field characteristics of coal dust explosion impact airflow and the discussion of the concentration of toxic and harmful gases generated after the explosion, which is of great significance for identifying the risk of injury in explosion accidents. Based on computational fluid dynamics theory, simulation software Fluent is used to simulate the propagation characteristics of the impact airflow velocity and the generation characteristics of toxic and harmful products after the explosion of noncaking coal in Hartmann pipe, providing simulation experimental support for further research on the hazards of noncaking coal explosions in confined spaces.

Dust Explosion Experimental System

Figure shows the experimental process and apparatus. First, the sample preparation involves coarse grinding, fine grinding, screening, and drying of coal samples to obtain noncaking coal dust particle samples with an average particle size of 75 μm. The experimental setup includes a mortar, ball mill, electric vibrating screen, and a drying oven. Next, ignition tests are conducted by placing noncaking coal dust samples into an experimental device for testing the ignition process and explosion propagation of industrial dust. The device is semienclosed and vertical, commonly known internationally as a Hartmann tube. The experimental setup consists of a Hartmann tube body, ignition electrode, diffuser, air compressor, and control panel. The Hartmann tube has a volume of 1.2 L and is made of quartz glass. The ignition electrodes are distributed on both sides of the Hartmann tube, located one-third of the distance from the bottom. Precise pulse ignition is used, with an ignition energy range of 1∼2000 mJ. The measurement accuracy is high, and it has high stability and anti-interference ability. The bottom of the tube is connected to the diffuser, which is used to spray and disperse dust, creating a suspended dust cloud with a certain turbulence level inside the tube.

Numerical Simulation Model

Numerical Method and Computational Grid

The diffusion, ignition, and explosion processes of dust inside a vertical pipeline are complex and transient processes. Considering the characteristics of the open upper end of the container, we believe that the gas phase inside the container is a continuum and cannot be compressed. Euler and Lagrangian methods are chosen to describe the coupling interaction between the gas phase and the particle phase, respectively. In addition, the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy of these two phases is alternately solved using a bidirectional coupling algorithm. The combustion model is selected based on the Eddy breakup algorithm for component transport, and the reaction rate is controlled by turbulence intensity. On the basis of chemical reaction kinetics and fluid mechanics, control equations were established for mass conservation, energy conservation, momentum conservation, and chemical reaction equilibrium. The turbulent combustion process of coal dust volatiles is described using a vortex dissipation model. The turbulent flow process of the gas phase is described by the equations of turbulent kinetic energy and turbulent energy dissipation rate.

The height of the vertical pipeline is 0.3 m, the inner diameter is 0.068 m, and the volume is 1.2 L. A three-dimensional unstructured grid was established for the experimental setup, and the specific details of the tube, ignition electrode, and gas nozzle were optimized and modeled according to the dimensions of the experimental setup. The total number of grid cells was 71279. For the convenience of describing geometric positions, X-, Y-, and Z-coordinate axes were established, with O as the coordinate origin. The flow field data of the explosion space need to be obtained by setting monitoring points. Five observation points, P1(0, 0.2, 0), P2(0, 0.225, 0), P3(0, 0.25, 0), P4(0, 0.275, 0), and P5(0, 0.3, 0), were set to detect changes in the velocity of the impact airflow inside the vertical pipeline after ignition, as shown in Figure . The reason for choosing these five observation points is that they are all located in the middle and upper parts of the pipe, which can well reflect the flow field characteristics above the explosion space. After grid independence testing, the grid size met the accuracy requirements of the study and provide good computational economy.

Parameter Settings

This article selects noncaking coal samples for combustion process simulation, with an ignition delay time of 1 s and a total dust mass of 3 g. The intermediate product of dust combustion is CO, and the final products are CO2 and H2O. The combustion-supporting component is O2. If the reaction is not complete, toxic CO gas will be generated. The average particle size of the dust is set to 75 μm. To accelerate iterative convergence, the underrelaxation factors for pressure, momentum, and turbulence terms are set to 0.5, 0.2, and 0.7, respectively. The initial temperature of the nonignition area and the inner wall of the vertical pipeline is 300 K. Considering the influence of gravity, its direction is along the negative Y-axis with a magnitude of 9.8 m/s2. The calculation domain is the gas flow space inside the pipe. The lower wall of the calculation domain is set as the velocity inlet in the dust spraying stage, but as the wall in the reaction stage. The upper wall is set as the pressure outlet, and the remaining walls are set as non slip boundaries. All walls are adiabatic.

In order to optimize the calculation process, save calculation time, and improve calculation accuracy, the following assumptions are made:Dust particles are considered regular spheres.There are collisions and thermal radiation among particles.The gas phase is reactive and incompressible.There is an interaction between the gas phase and the discrete phase during the diffusion process.

Reliability Analysis of Numerical Models

The contour lines of the flame temperature T at different times t after simulated ignition are shown in Figure . It can be seen that after the noncaking coal is ignited by the ignition electrode located at a height of 0.1 m, the heat and kinetic energy generated by the combustion quickly spread outward, sweeping and igniting the surrounding noncaking coal particles. At 10 ms, the flame has already propagated 0.02 m upward from the 0.1 m position, and at the same time, due to gravity, the flame has spread downward by 0.02 m. As the amount of noncaking coal added to the reaction increases, the high-temperature zone gradually expands, and the speed of flame expansion upward is much greater than the speed of flame propagation downward. At 20 ms, the flame propagates 0.04 m upward from the 0.1 m position and 0.025 m downward; at 30 ms, the upper end of the flame reached 0.16 m, the lower end spread by 0.03 m, and the flame no longer spread downward thereafter. As the combustion range expands, the flame reaches the device wall at 40 ms, and the temperature of the flame propagating along the wall is higher than that of the adjacent areas. At this time, the flame height reached 0.22 m. The combustion range continues to expand, and the reaction zone increases. At 45 ms, the entire area from the lower end of the flame at 0.07 m to the upper end of the tube belongs to the combustion-reaction stage. At this time, the height of the upper end of the flame reaches 0.27 m. At 50 ms, the upper end of the flame reaches the tube at the top of the device.

Isocontour map of flame temperature of noncaking coal dust explosion at different times.

The flame height is compared as a control parameter with experimental data to verify the reliability of the simulation. The measured and simulated values of flame height, h, at different times after ignition are shown in Table and Figure . The simulated values of flame height during the combustion reaction stage of noncaking coal dust in the simulation results are all higher than the measured values, which is due to the simplification of wall heat transfer in the reaction vessel during the numerical simulation process, resulting in higher simulated values. The maximum error is 5.26%, which occurs at t = 10 ms after ignition. It is believed that the simulation result error is within an acceptable range, and the numerical simulation model is reliable.

Results and Discussion

Propagation Characteristics of Noncaking Coal Dust Explosion Impact Airflow Velocity in Vertical Pipe

Figure shows the variation of the impact airflow velocity in the XOY direction with time t after ignition. At t = 10 ms, the direction of the impact airflow inside the tube propagates upward and downward, with the reaction area at y = 0.1m as the core. The downward-propagating impact airflow rebounds upward after hitting the bottom of the device, and an airflow vortex appears in the area at y = 0∼0.05 m at the bottom. At this moment, the maximum velocity of the impact airflow inside the tube is 1.2 m/s, which occurs at the upper part of the device. At t = 20 ms, the velocity of the impact airflow generated in the reaction area increases, and the downward-propagating impact airflow further stabilizes in the vortex of the airflow in the bottom y = 0∼0.05 m area. At this moment, the area with the highest impact airflow velocity in the tube is around y = 0.15 m, and the maximum velocity reaches 2.4 m/s. At t = 30 ms, the vortex of the airflow still exists in the area of y = 0–0.05 m at the bottom, and the closer the flame is to y = 0.05 m, the greater the influence of the vortex, and the downward propagation speed gradually slows down. At this time, the area with the highest impact airflow velocity in the tube is at y = 0.15∼0.20 m, and the maximum velocity reaches 4.2 m/s. At t = 40 ms, the downward-propagating impact airflow gradually decreases until it is completely engulfed by the vortex of the airflow in the area of y = 0∼0.05 m at the bottom. At this point, y = 0.05 m becomes the boundary between the upward and downward airflow, and the downward airflow is difficult to affect the propagation of flames. At this time, the area with the highest impact airflow velocity in the tube is y = 0.18∼0.27 m, and the maximum velocity reaches 12 m/s. At t = 45 ms, due to the high temperature generated in the reaction area, a local low-pressure area was formed inside the device, and the cold-state field at the bottom of the device becames a high-pressure area. The upward airflow generated by the pressure difference gradually merges into the upward airflow, and the flame no longer propagates downward. At this time, the area with the highest impact airflow velocity in the tube is y = 0.22∼0.30 m, and the maximum velocity exceeds 24 m/s. At t = 50 ms, the bottom vortex continues to decrease but has not completely disappeared. At this time, the area with the highest impact airflow velocity inside the tube is at y = 0.25∼0.30 m, and the maximum velocity exceeds 12 m/s.

Impact airflow velocity in vertical tube (XOY direction).

Further investigation was conducted on the propagation characteristics of the impact airflow velocity inside the tubes. The variation of the impact airflow velocity V with time t after ignition was measured at five observation points: P1(0, 0.2, 0), P2(0, 0.225, 0), P3(0, 0.25, 0), P4(0, 0.275, 0), and P5(0, 0.3, 0), as shown in Figure . As time changes, the velocity of the impact airflow shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, and as the observation height increases, the maximum measured impact airflow velocity also continues to increase. At P4, the maximum impact airflow velocity was measured to be 24 m/s at five positions, which occurred 45 ms after ignition. The maximum impact airflow velocity at P5 was lower than those at P4 and P3, and the reason for not continuing to rise may be due to the influence of backflow from the pipe mouth. At the same time, it can be seen that the time for different observation points to reach the maximum impact airflow velocity increases with the height. The impact airflow generated by noncaking coal dust explosion propagates upward along the vertical pipeline, and the velocity continues to increase. After reaching a height of 0.275 m, it is affected by the backflow at the outlet and loses some of its velocity. Then, the impact airflow flows out of the experimental device, and the airflow velocity inside the device suddenly decreases.

Impact airflow velocity at different positions along the central axis at different times.

Based on the above analysis, it is believed that the time t = 45 ms is the sufficient reaction time inside the tube, and the velocity of the noncaking coal dust explosion impact airflow has reached its maximum value. At this moment, seven observation sections perpendicular to the Y-axis were made, located at y = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3 m, respectively, as shown in Figure a. It can be seen that the impact airflow velocity at the cross-sections of y = 0, 0.05, and 0.1 m is relatively slow, less than 4 m/s. The velocity of the impact airflow passing through the cross-sections at y = 0.15 and 0.2 m gradually increases, and due to the wall effect, the velocity in the central region is significantly higher than that near the wall. The impact airflow velocity passing through the cross-section at y = 0.25 and 0.3 m reaches its maximum, with a maximum impact airflow velocity of 24 m/s. Based on the velocity cloud map of the impact airflow at different heights inside the vertical tube, the interior of the vertical pipeline is divided into three regions at the sufficient reaction time according to the impact airflow, as shown in Figure b. The area of y = 0∼0.1 m is a low-speed zone, where the gas flow velocity is low and there are cyclonic eddies, causing significant airflow disturbance and making it difficult for dust to participate in the reaction. The area of y = 0.1∼0.23 m is the velocity mutation zone, where there is a large difference in the velocity of the impact airflow. The lowest velocity is less than 4 m/s, and the maximum velocity exceeds 18 m/s. The high-speed airflow promotes the propagation of high-temperature gas inside the pipe, and the chemical reaction of dust is intense in this area. The area with y = 0.23∼0.3 m is a high-speed zone, and the gas flow velocity in the area is all higher than 14 m/s, with the highest speed reaching 24 m/s. This indicates that the impact airflow generated by the noncaking coal dust explosion has considerable explosion hazard, and corresponding measures need to be formulated to respond reasonably.

Airflow propagation speed when the impact airflow reaches its maximum (45 ms). (a) Impact airflow velocity at different heights. (b) Impact airflow velocity at z = 0 m cross-section.

When the impact airflow velocity of the coal powder explosion reaches its maximum, the reaction process of coal powder is very intense and complex. This study provides a detailed description of the distribution of impact airflow velocity during a coal dust explosion in a vertical pipeline. According to the velocity distribution, the pipe is divided into three different regions, each with its own specific airflow characteristics and coal powder reaction behavior. At the same time, in combination with the explosion impact airflow velocity curve, it can be seen that the impact airflow velocity curve first goes through a gentle upward stage. During this stage, the coal dust cloud in the vertical pipeline is activated by the ignition energy released from the ignition electrode. The coal dust cloud particles near the ignition electrode begin to pyrolyze and release volatile combustible gases. The gas inside the vertical pipeline is heated and expanded, and the flow rate gradually increases. After t = 30 ms, the volatile combustible gases released by the coal dust particles inside the tube converge into a cloud, reaching the lowest explosive concentration and being ignited. At the same time, more combustible coal dust particles are involved in the explosion, forming a chain reaction of coal dust explosion, and the velocity of the impact airflow begins to rapidly increase. Finally, as a large amount of combustible coal dust is consumed, it cannot be replenished in a limited space in a timely manner, and the airflow velocity rapidly decreases after reaching its peak.

At the same time, an observation section perpendicular to the Z-axis was made, located at z = 0 m, passing through the central axis of the vertical tube, as shown in Figure b and Figure b.

First, the CO mass fraction cloud map can be observed, as shown in Figure . Figure a shows that the mass fraction of CO in the cross-section at y = 0 and 0.045 m is 0%. Due to the influence of the rising airflow, CO gas with a density greater than that of air that should have settled, did not appear at the lower end of the container. The CO concentration displayed at three cross-sections of y = 0.09, 0.135, and 0.18 m gradually decreases, with the highest CO concentration shown at the 0.09 m cross-section. The local CO mass fraction reaches 0.06%, and the CO concentration in the central area is significantly higher than that on the wall due to the wall effect. The three cross-sections at y = 0.225, 0.27, and 0.3 m show a gradual increase in CO concentration, with the highest CO concentration at y = 0.3 m and a local CO mass fraction of over 0.05%. Based on Figure b, it can be seen that there is almost no CO in the low-speed region of y = 0∼0.07 m. The ignition region of y = 0.07∼0.13 m has the highest CO concentration, with a mass concentration of over 0.06%. The CO distribution in the region of y = 0.13∼0.3 m is uniform, and CO is almost present throughout the entire region. The expansion of the high-temperature region and the flow of high-speed gas promote the propagation of CO inside the tube, expanding the toxic range of CO.

Then, the CO2 mass fraction cloud map was observed, as shown in Figure . Figure a shows that under the influence of the rising airflow, the mass concentration of CO2 measured at the observation cross-sections y = 0 and 0.045 m at the lower end of the space is consistent with the mass concentration of CO2 in the air, both below 0.04%. There is almost no change in mass concentration in this area. At y = 0.09 m, the mass concentration of CO2 in the center of the cross-section begins to be higher than that in the air. Removing 0.04% mass concentration of CO2 gas from the air can result in a CO2 mass concentration of 0.08% or more in the central area. The cloud maps of CO2 mass concentration measured at three cross-sections of y = 0.135, 0.18, and 0.225 m show that, as the height increases, the CO2 generation area in the center of the vertical tube gradually decreases. Due to the wall effect, the mass concentration of CO2 generated at the wall of the tube is much higher than in other areas inside the vertical tube, reaching 0.1%. Combined with the mass fraction cloud map of CO generation, the wall effect at y = 0.12∼0.25 m intensifies the combustion reaction of noncaking coal, and CO is completely consumed and converted into CO2, which explains why the mass fraction of CO generated here is 0% and the mass fraction of CO2 generated is 0.1%. The cross-sections at y = 0.225, 0.27, and 0.3 m show a gradual decrease in the concentration of CO2 generated. Based on Figure b, it can be seen that there is almost no CO2 generation in the low-speed region of y = 0∼0.07 m. A certain concentration of CO2 is generated in the ignition region of y = 0.07∼0.13 m, with a mass concentration of over 0.08%. The region of y = 0.13∼0.3 m is affected by the rising impact airflow and wall effects, and the CO2 generated in the central region of the pipe is much lower than that generated in the wall region.

Further research was conducted on the concentration changes of CO and CO2 products in the subsidence area of the explosion source (y = 0.08∼0.1 m), as shown in Figure . The coordinates of the observation points are (0, 0.08, 0), (0, 0.09, 0), and (0, 0.1, 0). Figure a records the variation of CO mass fraction. It can be seen that the fluctuation of CO mass fraction is quite severe, which is due to the fact that CO, as an intermediate product of noncaking coal explosion, is not only generated when the reaction is incomplete, but also acts as a reactant during the reaction process. The trend of CO mass fraction at a height of 0.08 m is to first increase and then decrease, with a maximum of 0.055%. The trend of CO mass fraction variation in the height range of 0.09∼0.1 m generally shows a rapid increase followed by a decrease and then an increase to reach the highest concentration before a decrease. The peak concentration occurs at 0.09 at 50 ms, reaching 0.065%. According to the Chinese Indoor Air Quality Standard GB/T 18883–2022, the maximum average concentration of indoor CO gas is 10 mg/m, which to some extent reflects that the toxicity of CO is unacceptable to the human body. When a person is in an environment with a CO mass fraction exceeding 0.04% for more than an hour, dizziness will occur, which poses considerable danger and toxicity.

Changes in CO and CO2 concentrations in settling areas at different times. (a) CO mass fraction. (b) CO2 mass fraction.

Figure b records the generation of CO2 mass fraction at the monitoring point inside the vertical tube after removing the CO2 mass fraction in the air. It can be seen that the CO2 mass fraction at a height of 0.08 m shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, with a maximum of 0.0575%. The generation trend of CO2 in the height range of 0.09∼0.1 m generally shows a rapid increase followed by a slowdown in the growth rate. After the growth rate increases again and reaches the highest concentration, it decreases at 0.09 m and continues to increase after decreasing at 0.1 m. The peak concentration appears at 0.1 at 48 ms, reaching 0.115%. According to the Chinese Indoor Air Quality Standard GB/T 18883–2022, the average concentration of indoor CO2 cannot exceed 0.1%. When a person is in an environment with a CO2 mass fraction exceeding 0.1%, they may feel dull, have difficulty concentrating, and experience palpitations, which are quite dangerous and toxic.

Based on the generation curves of CO and CO2, it can be inferred that near the explosion source area in the vertical tube, due to the small explosion area induced by the ignition electrode after ignition, the noncaking coal dust involved in the explosion is not fully mixed with the air. The first step is incomplete combustion, which produces CO and CO2 and quickly reaches the reaction limit at the moment when the slope of the generation curve reaches its maximum, which is also the reason for the first rise of the generation curve. Then the reaction enters the plateau period, in order to activate more noncaking coal to participate in the combustion and explosion reaction inside the tube, the CO generated in the previous reaction is gradually consumed, and the CO2 generation curve continues to rise, but the rate slows down significantly. At the same time, the heat released by the CO reaction gradually increases the number of noncaking coal particles participating in the reaction, and the reaction area gradually expands. Because of the release of heat from CO participating in the reaction, the high-temperature area of coal dust explosion inside the tube gradually expands. After 30 ms, the volatile combustible gas cloud is ignited, causing an increase in the chain reaction rate of combustible coal dust, which turns the combustion reaction inside the vertical tube into an incomplete combustion reaction again. At this stage, the reaction inside the vertical tube is the most intense, and the strongest impact airflow is also formed. At 30 ms after ignition, the CO generation curve rises again, and the CO2 generation curve rate increases again. This is due to the increase in the mass of combustible noncaking coal dust and the chain reaction rate, which causes the combustion reaction inside the vertical tube to enter the incomplete combustion reaction again. At this stage, the reaction inside the vertical tube is the most intense, and the strongest impact airflow is also formed. Finally, as the combustible and noncaking coal in the area reacts completely, the CO and CO2 generation curves enter the final stage, during which CO and oxygen are gradually consumed, and CO2 continues to be generated but at a slower rate. The decrease in the CO2 mass concentration at y = 0.09 m in the final stage may be due to the influence of the rising impact airflow, which carries away the CO2 generated here, resulting in a decrease in the CO2 mass concentration. At a distance of 0.08 m from the center of the explosion source, the effects mentioned above are relatively small. After ignition, it is mainly affected by the rising impact airflow and begins to decrease after reaching a concentration peak.

The generation curve of CO and CO2 during coal powder explosion reflects the entire process from ignition, incomplete combustion, and intensified chain reaction to the end of combustion. This process involves the mixing of coal powder and oxygen, the kinetics of combustion reactions, and the influence of impact airflow on product distribution. By analyzing these reaction mechanisms, the characteristics and influencing factors of coal powder explosions can be better understood. In the early stage of ignition, because of the small explosion area induced by the ignition electrode and insufficient mixing of coal powder and air, incomplete combustion occurs first. As the reaction continues, the CO generated by the reaction is gradually consumed, and the CO2 generation curve continues to rise. At the same time, the heat released by the reaction gradually increases the number of coal dust particles participating in the reaction, and the reaction area expands. Subsequently, the combustible gas cloud is ignited, causing the combustion reaction inside the vertical tube to once again become an incomplete combustion reaction, forming the strongest impact airflow. Finally, as the combustible coal dust in the area reacts completely, the reduction of combustibles limits the continuation of the reaction. The reaction between CO and oxygen is gradually consumed, and CO2 continues to be generated but at a very slow rate.

Conclusions

The velocity of the noncaking coal dust explosion impact airflow shows a trend of increasing first and then decreasing with time. This means that in the early stages of the explosion, the velocity of the impact airflow rapidly increases and gradually decreases after reaching its peak. Meanwhile, the height of the observation point is closely related to the maximum measured impact airflow velocity, and the higher the observation point, the greater the impact airflow velocity. Especially at 45 ms, the impact airflow velocity of the coal dust explosion inside the tube reaches its maximum, indicating that this is a critical time point in the explosion process.

The CO distribution in the region of y = 0.13∼0.3 m is uniform, and CO is almost present throughout the entire region. The expansion of the high-temperature region and the flow of high-speed gas promote the propagation of CO inside the tube, expanding the toxic range of CO. This indicates that the distribution and propagation of CO are significantly influenced by the reaction rate and airflow velocity after the explosion.

A certain concentration of CO2 is generated in the ignition region, with a mass concentration of over 0.08%. The region of y = 0.13∼0.3 m is affected by the rising impact airflow and wall effects, and the CO2 generated in the central region of the pipe is much lower than that generated in the wall region. This indicates that the generation and distribution of CO2 are not uniform and are influenced by both the reaction rate and the tube structure.
