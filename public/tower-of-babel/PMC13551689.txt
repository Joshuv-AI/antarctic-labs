Incidence and risk factors for postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiahui Liang, Zhiqiang He, Min Luo, Min Liang, Liujuan Sha, Lifen Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative vascular crisis is one of the most serious complications in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors. To date, no consensus has been reached regarding the factors influencing its occurrence. This study aimed to investigate the incidence and influencing factors of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors through a meta-analysis.MethodsA systematic search of nine Chinese and English literature databases was conducted from database inception to August 31, 2025, to identify studies on the incidence and influencing factors of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors. Two researchers independently performed literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment. Data were analyzed using Stata 17.0 and RevMan 5.4.1 software. Sensitivity analysis, publication bias assessment, and subgroup analysis were also performed.ResultsA total of 27 studies identified 44 influencing factors, and 13 factors were reported in at least two independent studies with complete data. The average score of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale included in the study was ≥ 7, indicating relatively high quality. Finally, the combined incidence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors was 14.4%, and 12 influencing factors were obtained: diabetes, hypertension, oral infection, decreased fibrinogen, decreased ALB, ALB < 35g/L, preoperative radiotherapy, surgical history in the recipient area, anastomotic veins < 2, history of alcohol abuse, smoking history, and gender.ConclusionThe pooled incidence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral cancer was 14.41%, indicating a relatively high risk in this population. Clinicians should develop differentiated prevention and management strategies for high-risk patients based on relevant influencing factors to improve monitoring accuracy and reduce the occurrence of vascular crisis.Registration numberCRD420251123018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08961-9.Keywords: Oral and maxillofacial tumor, Vascular crisis, Influencing factors, Meta-analysis, Systematic review

Introduction

Oral cancer, a major subtype of head and neck cancer, is one of the most common malignancies in the head and neck region [1]. The latest global statistics indicate that approximately 389,800 new cases of oral cancer are diagnosed each year [2]. Only 40%—50% of patients can survive five years after diagnosis [3]. Increased exposure to risk factors such as tobacco use and excessive alcohol consumption has made the epidemiological situation of oral cancer in China more complex. In 2021, the number of deaths, incidence rates, and prevalence rates of oral cancer in China were 238,000, 41,600 and 215,100 respectively [3]. Given the large number of patients, surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality; however, the extensive tissue defects resulting from surgery need to be reconstructed using flap techniques to restore local function and appearance [4]. However, flap survival is threatened by vascular crisis [5]. Studies have shown that vascular crisis caused by obstruction of the vascular anastomotic circulation is the main cause of free flap failure, with an incidence ranging from 3.30% to 29.51% [6, 7]. It not only significantly increases the risk of reoperation but may also lead to failure of local vascular reconstruction or impaired recovery of maxillofacial function, thereby reducing patients’ quality of life.

Although previous studies have investigated the incidence, underlying mechanisms, and clinical characteristics of vascular crisis, consensus has not yet been reached regarding its major influencing factors. Evidence suggests that diabetes, a history of preoperative radiotherapy, the method of vascular anastomosis, and surgical duration are independent risk factors for vascular crisis [8]. However, other studies have reported that these factors are not significantly associated with the occurrence of vascular crisis [9]. The conclusions of existing studies often show substantial heterogeneity because of differences in sample size, variations in research methods, and inconsistencies in outcome assessment criteria, which limit their generalizability and applicability.Therefore, to further clarify the relevant influencing factors, it is necessary to synthesize existing evidence on the incidence and influencing factors of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral cancer through a systematic review and meta-analysis. This approach may help identify the multidimensional factors associated with vascular crisis and provide evidence-based support for developing precise clinical prevention strategies and personalized intervention measures.

Materials and methods

Definition of postoperative vascular crisis

Vascular crisis refers to a circulatory disorder caused by various factors following vascular anastomosis of a free tissue flap, with thrombosis at the anastomotic site being the primary cause. Vascular crises can be classified as venous, arterial, or combined arteriovenous crises, and the vast majority occur within 72 h after surgery [10].

Protocol and registration

This study followed the "Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses" (PRISMA) guideline and has registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251123018).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

(1) Inclusion criteria: ① Original research articles; ② Cross-sectional studies, case–control studies and cohort studies;③ Research on the incidence rate and influencing factors of patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors. In the present study, the term "oral cancer" refersto malignant tumors arising in the oral cavity. Someincluded studies used the broader term "head andneck cancer"; however, only data related to oralcancer were extracted and analyzed when available. (2) Exclusion criteria: ① Studies where the full text cannot be obtained or where there are duplicates; For studies with incomplete data, even after sending an email to the corresponding author, complete data still cannot be obtained. ③ Research with relatively low methodological quality (Newcastle–Ottawa Scale score < 6); ④ Review, commentary or conference papers; ⑤ Non-Chinese and non-English literature.

Literature retrieval strategy

Computer searches were conducted in Pubmed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, China Science and Technology Journal Database and SinoMed literature database were proposed, and the search time limit for both databases was established until August 31, 2025. Literature retrieval was conducted by combining free words with subject words. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Literature screening

Two researchers (J L and Z H) independently screened the literature retrieved from Endnote. After the initial deduplication, the title and abstract are read for the initial exclusion. Then, the literature that does not meet the inclusion criteria is excluded again by reading the main text. Finally, the included literature is determined through the full-text quality evaluation. Any disputes are handled by a third party (L P).

Data extraction and quality assessment

This study prioritized the extraction of odds ratios (ORs) derived from multivariate regression analyses in the original studies. Data were extracted from the main text, tables, and figures of each article, including the year of publication, first author, country, study setting, study population, study design, diagnostic criteria, number of vascular crisis cases, total sample size, and influencing factors. Before formal data extraction, the research team pilot-tested the data extraction form and selected a small number of representative studies for preliminary extraction to ensure that all members had a consistent understanding of the extracted items. To minimize potential duplication of patient populations, especially among studies conducted in China, we assessed possible overlap by comparing study institutions, recruitment periods, study settings, sample sizes, author groups, and reported patient characteristics. No clearly duplicated cohort was identified. Two researchers (J L and Z H) independently extracted the data and conducted cross-checks. In case of any objection, the decision shall be made by the third party (L P). The quality of cohort studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11], with a total score of 9. A score of ≥ 6 was considered high-quality and included in the study. Two researchers (J L and Z H) independently evaluated the methodological quality. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved discrepancies were adjudicated by a third reviewer (L P).

Statistical analysis

In this study, RevMan 5.4.1 and stata 17.0 were used for the main data synthesis and heterogeneity test, and Stata 17.0 was used to assist in the sensitivity analysis and publication bias test. To ensure the reliability and interpretability of the quantitative synthesis, only factors reported in at least two independent studies with complete extractable data were included in the meta-analysis. Factors reported in only one study were summarized qualitatively but were not pooled quantitatively, because a single study does not allow estimation of between-study heterogeneity or a meaningful pooled effect. The combination of effect sizes was achieved by extracting the odds ratios of each study and their 95% confidence intervals, converting them to natural logarithms, and then using the inverse variance method for weighted combination. Select the effect model based on the heterogeneity test results: when I2 ≤ 50% or P > 0.1, use the Fixed Effects Model (FEM); otherwise, choose the Random Effects Model (REM). To test the robustness of the combined results, we used the "one-retention method" for sensitivity analysis and effect model transformation analysis. The potential publication bias was comprehensively evaluated by using funnel plot visual assessment, Egger's test and clipping method. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To investigate the heterogeneity in the incidence of vascular crisis after oral cancer surgery and its associated risk factors (diabetes, oral infection), we conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses based on country, study design, and publication year.

Results

Literature retrieval results

A total of 2,660 relevant records were initially identified through the systematic literature search. After duplicate removal, 909 duplicate records were excluded. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were screened, and 57 articles were retained for further assessment. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, the full texts of the remaining articles were then carefully reviewed. In addition, relevant references were traced using the snowballing method to further supplement and screen the literature. Finally, 27 studies were included, comprising 17 Chinese-language studies [5, 8, 9, 12–25] and 10 English-language studies [3, 6, 7, 10, 26–31]. Basic characteristics and quality of the included literature

Based on the available literature information, this study systematically summarized the basic characteristics of the included studies. Regarding geographical distribution, most studies were conducted in China, with a total of 23 articles, while the remaining four studies were conducted in the United Kingdom [31], France [26], Japan [6] and India [28]. All included studies were published between 2014 and 2025. Regarding study design, three studies were prospective cohort studies, whereas the remaining studies were retrospective. Among them, the studies by Zheng [10] and Li [9] further adopted a 1:1 matched case–control design. The incidence of flap vascular crisis reported across the included studies ranged from 3.30% to 29.51%. During data extraction, we also recorded whether each study provided a definition of postoperative vascular crisis and the diagnostic time window used. It should be noted that 12 included studies did not provide a clear definition of vascular crisis, whereas the remaining studies used different diagnostic windows, such as 72 h, 3 days, or 7 days after surgery. This inconsistency was recognized as a potential methodological source of heterogeneity and was considered when interpreting the pooled results. After preliminary extraction and organization, 44 risk factors were identified by consensus. However, only 13 factors were reported in at least two independent studies with complete data and therefore met the criteria for subsequent meta-analysis. The results of the methodological quality assessment showed that the scores ranged from 6 to 8 points. Except for the studies by Xia [19] and Ishimaru [6], all included studies were considered to be of high quality. The incidence and risk factors of vascular crisis

The meta-analysis results (Fig. 2) showed substantial heterogeneity in the incidence of postoperative vascular crisis among patients with oral cancer across the included studies(I2 = 78%, P < 0.001). Therefore, a random-effects model was used, and the pooled incidence was 14.41% (95%CI: 10.23%—18.59%, P < 0.001).

Incidence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumor

A total of 12 factors are significantly associated with the occurrence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral cancer (Table 3). The effect values from high to low are as follows: Hypertension (OR = 10.46, 95%CI: 2.13–51.33, P = 0.004), male (OR = 7.26, 95%CI: 2.68–19.67, P < 0.001), oral infection (OR = 4.59, 95%CI: 2.71–7.78, P < 0.001)、ALB < 35 g/L (OR = 4.52, 95%CI: 1.91–10.74, P = 0.0006), diabetes (OR = 3.08, 95%CI: 2.23–4.24, P < 0.001), surgical history in the recipient area (OR = 3.13, 95%CI: 1.47–6.67, P = 0.003), preoperative radiotherapy (OR = 2.31, 95%CI: 1.61–3.33, P < 0.001), low fibrinogen level (OR = 2.02, 95%CI: 1.03–3.95, P = 0.04), history of alcohol abuse (OR = 1.83, 95%CI: 1.19–2.82, P = 0.006), anastomotic veins < 2 (OR = 1.74, 95%CI: 1.26–2.41, P = 0.0008), low ALB level (OR = 1.54, 95%CI: 1.27–1.87, P < 0.001) and smoking history (OR = 1.55, 95%CI: 1.39–1.74, P < 0.001), The correlations were all statistically significant. In contrast, there was no significant statistical association between age < 60 years and the occurrence of vascular crisis (OR = 4.15, 95%CI: 0.41–42.18, P = 0.23).

Sensitivity analysis

According to the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis of the incidence of vascular crisis (Fig. 3), the pooled effect estimate fluctuated between 0.08 and 0.14 after each study was sequentially excluded. The highest estimate, 0.14, was observed after excluding the study by Ishimaru et al. However, in all cases, the upper and lower limits of the 95% CI did not cross the null line, and the direction of the confidence interval was consistent with that of the overall pooled effect. These findings indicate that the overall effect estimate was stable and was not directionally altered by the exclusion of any single study. This indicates that the meta-analysis conclusion regarding the incidence of vascular crisis was relatively robust, and the statistical inference was not substantially influenced by any individual study. The sensitivity analysis based on random-effects and fixed-effect models (Table 3) showed that, except for history of alcohol abuse (OR = 2.50, 95%CI: 0.78–8.00, P = 0.12), low ALB levels (OR = 2.46, 95%CI: 1.01–6.00, P = 0.05), and age < 60 years (OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 1.44–2.12, P < 0.001), the pooled ORs and statistical significance of most other risk factors were generally consistent across different models. These findings suggest that this part of the results was relatively insensitive to model selection and that the overall conclusions of the meta-analysis were stable.

Publication bias test

Publication bias was assessed for the included studies. The funnel plots (Fig. 4A and B) showed asymmetrical distributions of study points for the incidence of vascular crisis and diabetes-related factors, suggesting the possible presence of publication bias. Further quantitative assessment was performed using Egger’s linear regression test. The results showed that the intercept was 2.40 for the incidence analysis (95% CI: 1.01–3.78, P = 0.001) and 0.99 for the diabetes analysis (95% CI: 0.59–1.39, P < 0.001), suggesting the presence of significant small-study effects. To evaluate the impact of publication bias on the pooled effect estimates, the non-parametric trim-and-fill method was used. The results indicated that 9 and 7 potentially missing studies were imputed in the funnel plots for the incidence analysis and diabetes analysis, respectively (Fig. 5). After adjustment, the pooled effect estimates were as follows: incidence, 0.196 (95% CI: 0.151–0.240); and diabetes, OR = 1.75 (95% CI: 1.48–2.03). Compared with the original estimates, the trim-and-fill-adjusted estimates changed only slightly, and the statistical significance remained unchanged. These findings suggest that although this meta-analysis may have been affected by publication bias, the overall direction of the conclusion was not altered. Nevertheless, caution is still warranted when interpreting the pooled effect estimates.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis showed that there were significant differences in the incidence of vascular crisis among different stratifications, detailed in Table 4. Stratified by country, the incidence of mergers in Chinese studies was significantly higher than that in non-Chinese studies (16.08% vs. 5.23%, P = 0.001), and the internal heterogeneity within Chinese studies was higher (I2 = 76.7%). Stratified by research design, the incidence of comorbidities in retrospective studies was higher than that in prospective studies (15.31% vs. 7.25%, P = 0.022), and retrospective studies had greater heterogeneity (I2 = 79.8%). Stratified by publication year, the incidence of studies published in 2020 and later was higher than that before 2020 (16.11% vs. 10.66%, P = 0.176), and there was moderate to high heterogeneity within both groups. The association strength of diabetes risk factors varies among different subgroups. In Chinese studies, the risk of vascular crisis was higher in diabetic patients (OR = 3.45, 95%CI: 2.40–4.96), and the heterogeneity was greater (I2 = 72%). Studies after 2020 showed a strong risk association (OR = 6.71, low heterogeneity), while those before 2020 had a weak association (OR = 1.72, moderate heterogeneity).The association with oral infections also showed significant differences. Studies after 2020 demonstrated a higher OR value (14.16, 95%CI: 6.89–29.07), with no heterogeneity. However, there was no statistically significant association in the studies before 2020 (OR = 1.83, 95%CI: 0.94–3.58), and the heterogeneity was relatively high. Due to the insufficiency of data for non-Chinese subgroups, no country-specific stratified analysis was conducted.

Discussion

The pooled incidence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial tumors was 14.41% (95% CI: 10.23% ~ 18.59%), which is higher than previously reported levels. A study comprising 1,786 patients, reported an incidence ranging from 4.9% to 5.8% [10], while Zheng et al. (2025) [32] reported a pooled incidence of 5% in their meta-analysis. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, differences in diagnostic criteria may have contributed, some studies relied solely on clinical manifestations, whereas others did not report their diagnostic criteria at all, and broader definitions may have overestimated the true incidence. Second, the majority of studies did not clearly report the follow-up timeframes, despite the fact that flap crisis predominantly occurs within the first 72 h postoperatively [10]. Variations in follow-up duration directly affect the detection rate. Finally, some included studies had small sample sizes (N < 100) [9, 13, 20, 22, 23, 25], and small-sample studies tend to report more extreme incidence estimates. Therefore, future prospective studies adopting standardized diagnostic criteria are warranted to provide more accurate baseline incidence estimates. Based on the qualitative description of the influencing factors, all relevant influencing factors can be classified into four categories: disease-related factors, laboratory test indicators, treatment factors and sociodemographic factors.

Disease-related factors associated with the occurrence of vascular crisis

The results of this study showed that patients with diabetes and oral infection had a higher risk of vascular crisis, which is consistent with previous studies [6, 9, 12–16, 19–24, 27, 29]. This increased risk may be related to diabetes-induced local and systemic inflammatory responses, which can subsequently lead to vascular endothelial dysfunction [33]. Diabetes may also interact with oral infection by impairing immune function, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection and further exacerbating vascular endothelial injury [33, 34]. In addition, hypertension was also identified as an influencing factor in this meta-analysis. Hypertension increases the mechanical load on the vascular wall, causing substantial structural and functional changes in systemic microvessels, promoting arteriosclerosis and vascular endothelial damage, and increasing the likelihood of vascular crisis [35]. Some pooled estimates, such as the association between hypertension and postoperative vascular crisis, had wide confidence intervals, indicating limited precision and possible instability of the effect estimates. These results may have been influenced by small sample sizes, sparse event data, residual confounding, and differences in study design. Therefore, these factors should be interpreted as potential associated factors rather than definitive causal risk factors.

Laboratory test indicators associated with the occurrence of vascular crisis

Studies have shown that decreased fibrinogen and albumin levels, particularly ALB < 35 g/L, are factors associated with vascular crisis. This finding is biologically plausible, as these laboratory indicators not only reflect the overall health status of patients but may also increase the risk of vascular crisis through multiple mechanisms, including effects on blood coagulation, immune function, nutritional status, and vascular permeability [36]. One possible explanation is that fibrinogen is the precursor of fibrin, which forms the main structural framework of blood clots and participates in tissue repair and wound healing. Therefore, decreased fibrinogen levels may impair the coagulation process, thereby increasing the risk of bleeding and vascular complications [36]. In addition, albumin has multiple biological functions, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic effects. Low albumin levels are usually accompanied by malnutrition, impaired immune function, and chronic inflammatory responses, which may adversely affect postoperative recovery and further increase the risk of postoperative vascular crisis [37].

Treatment- and lifestyle-related factors associated with the occurrence of vascular crisis

The results showed that radiotherapy was identified as one of the factors associated with vascular crisis. This may be because radiotherapy, as an acute stimulus, can induce acute inflammatory responses and trigger complex changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby leading to excessive local inflammation [38, 39]. A history of surgery in the recipient site was also identified as an influencing factor, possibly due to vascular injury and poor postoperative recovery after multiple surgeries or extensive resection and reconstruction in the recipient area [7]. The number of anastomotic veins was also associated with vascular crisis, which may be attributed to the importance of more than two venous anastomoses in ensuring venous return and vascular stability.A history of alcohol abuse was also identified as a factor associated with vascular crisis. This may be related to oxidative stress caused by alcohol metabolites, which can directly damage vascular endothelial cells. In addition, alcohol abuse is often accompanied by malnutrition and abnormal liver function, which may further increase the risk of vascular crisis [40]. Smoking was also identified as a risk factor, possibly because smoking is significantly associated with changes in the oral bacterial community and may have adverse effects on both the cardiovascular system and oral health [41, 42].

Sociodemographic factors associated with the occurrence of vascular crisis

The results showed that sex was also an influencing factor. Male patients had a higher incidence of vascular crisis than female patients, which may be related to the higher prevalence of smoking and heavy alcohol consumption among men [43]. In addition, estrogen may exert a protective effect on the arterial endothelium, thereby reducing the risk of vascular crisis in women [44].

Additionally, we classify the above factors into modifiable, potentially modifiable, and non-modifiable factors to enhance the clinical applicability of our findings. Disease-related factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and oral infection may be considered potentially modifiable or optimizable through preoperative assessment and perioperative management. Laboratory indicators, including decreased fibrinogen levels, decreased albumin levels, and ALB < 35 g/L, may reflect coagulation, nutritional, and inflammatory status and may also provide opportunities for preoperative optimization. Among treatment-related factors, the number of anastomotic veins may be considered a potentially modifiable intraoperative factor, whereas preoperative radiotherapy and recipient-site surgical history are less modifiable historical factors but may still be useful for risk stratification. Smoking and alcohol abuse were classified as modifiable lifestyle-related factors, whereas sex was considered a non-modifiable demographic factor.

Potential sources of heterogeneity

Egger’s test indicated the presence of publication bias, suggesting that small-study effects may have led to an overestimation of the effect estimates. Although adjustment using the trim-and-fill method did not substantially alter the direction of the conclusions, potential bias may still affect the accuracy of the results, particularly in assessing the association between diabetes and postoperative vascular crisis. Nevertheless, diabetes remained clinically relevant as a risk factor. The pooled estimates may still be affected by small-study effects or selective reporting. Therefore, the strength of the conclusions should be interpreted with caution in clinical decision-making, particularly for risk factors supported by a limited number of studies. Future studies should aim to minimize potential bias and further validate these findings.The subgroup analysis indicated that the incidence of vascular crisis and the strength of its associations with diabetes and oral infection may be substantially influenced by country or region, study design, and publication year, potentially reflecting changes in diagnostic criteria or reporting practices over time. These factors may represent important sources of the substantial heterogeneity observed in the primary analysis. Notably, the markedly increased risk estimates for oral infection in studies published after 2020 may reflect either a deeper understanding of its pathophysiological role or improved diagnostic sensitivity in recent years, warranting greater clinical attention.

Furthermore, inconsistencies in the definition of postoperative vascular crisis, including the absence of a clear definition, variations in diagnostic criteria, and differences in follow-up timeframes, represent a potentially important source of heterogeneity in this meta-analysis. In addition, unmeasured clinical heterogeneity among the included studies, such as tumor type, reconstruction technique, flap type, surgical complexity, and perioperative protocols, may also substantially affect the interpretation of the pooled effect estimates and limit the depth of subgroup analyses. Insufficient reporting of these key variables in the original studies hindered our ability to quantitatively explore their specific impacts through detailed subgroup analyses. Therefore, the pooled effects obtained in this analysis should be regarded as average estimates across different clinical scenarios. Future studies should focus on standardizing the reporting of these potential factors to clarify the consistency or differences in outcomes across different patient subgroups and technical approaches.

Limitations and implications

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, most of the included studies were case–control or cohort studies, which generally provide a relatively low level of evidence. In addition, differences in data quality across studies may have contributed to instability in the statistical significance of the analytical results. Moreover, the conclusions regarding some individual influencing factors were inconsistent across different statistical models, which may be related to differences in study design, patient populations, and the specific variables included. Second, although this study covered multiple relevant factors, some studies had poor data quality and incomplete reporting of original indicators, which limited further exploration of certain influencing factors. Third, most of the included studies were conducted in China, which may have introduced regional bias and limited the broader applicability and cross-regional generalizability of the findings. We state that although this study provides useful evidence regarding postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral cancer, the generalizability of the pooled estimates to other countries and healthcare systems should be interpreted with caution. We also highlight the need for future multicenter studies from different countries and regions to validate these findings. Finally, because the search languages were limited to Chinese and English and the search strategy had certain restrictions, some relevant studies may have been missed, thereby affecting the comprehensiveness of the results.

Implications

Future research should prioritize large-sample prospective cohort studies, multicenter registry studies, and standardized outcome definitions to enhance the external validity and clinical applicability of the findings. Large sample sizes and multicenter designs can improve the stability and generalizability of results by including patient populations from different regions and ethnic backgrounds. Standardized outcome definitions are essential for reducing heterogeneity across studies, particularly in the assessment of postoperative complications and treatment outcomes. In addition, future studies should emphasize the collection of high-quality raw data, especially in multicenter settings, to ensure data consistency and completeness. Unified standard operating procedures and strict data quality control can effectively reduce data loss and inconsistency, thereby enhancing the credibility and clinical utility of the research findings.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the combined incidence of postoperative vascular crisis in patients with oral and maxillofacial malignant tumors was 14.4%. The influencing factors include disease-related factors (such as diabetes, hypertension, oral infection), laboratory test indicators (such as decreased fibrinogen, decreased albumin, ALB < 35 g/L), treatment-related factors (such as preoperative radiotherapy, history of surgery in the recipient area, number of anastomotic veins < 2), and sociodemography factors (such as history of alcohol abuse, smoking history, gender). These factors not only provide important basis for the risk assessment of vascular crises, but also offer potential targets for individualized clinical intervention.
