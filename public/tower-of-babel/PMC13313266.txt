Using peripheral blood indicators and T cells subsets to evaluate the efficacy of immunotherapy for advanced and recurrent cervical cancer
Authors: Jing Bai, Xiao-Li Cui, Si-Jia Bo, Li Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo identify predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy response in advanced/recurrent cervical cancer by evaluating peripheral blood indicators including T-cell subsets and serum biomarkers. This prospective study (June 2022–July 2024) enrolled 50 patients with stage III-IVa or recurrent cervical cancer receiving first-line immunotherapy combined with chemo/radiotherapy from Qingdao Central Hospital network. We analyzed baseline CD4+/CD8+ T-cell percentages and post-treatment (after one course) levels of CA125, SCCA, T-cell subsets, and PD-1 expression on T-cells using logistic regression (treatment efficacy，comparing 36 responders vs. 14 non-responders) and Cox regression (prognosis, with disease progression or death events occurring in 26 patients). Treatment response was significantly associated with: (1) baseline CD4+ T-cell percentage and (2) post-treatment CA125/SCCA levels, CD8+ T-cell percentage, and PD-1 expression on both CD4+/CD8+ T-cells (all p < .05). Prognostic factors identified by Cox regression included these same markers plus post-treatment CD4+ T-cell percentage. Optimal responders showed: high baseline CD4+ T-cells, low post-treatment CA125/SCCA, elevated CD4+/CD8+ T-cells, and reduced PD-1 expression. Peripheral T-cell subsets and serum biomarkers show significant associations with predicted immunotherapy outcomes. High baseline CD4+ T-cells with post-treatment normalization of immune markers (increased T-cells, decreased PD-1+ cells, and tumor antigens) are associated with a higher likelihood of benefiting from immunotherapy. It should be noted that this study has limitations, including a modest sample size and the assessment of biomarkers at only a single post-treatment time point, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. These peripheral blood biomarkers may represent a minimally invasive approach for exploring treatment dynamics and are considered worthy of further investigation as potential adjuncts in the context of personalized immunotherapy for advanced cervical cancer. However, their predictive performance and clinical utility remain to be validated in larger, independent cohorts before any clinical application can be considered.KEYWORDS: Advanced and recurrent cervical cancer, immune checkpoint inhibitors, T cell subsets, efficacy, prognosis

Introduction

In China, cervical cancer is the most common malignancy among the major female reproductive tract cancers and is the fourth leading cause of cancer death among women. Recent data from population-based registries indicate that, particularly in rural areas, both the incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer have been increasing,1 likely due to gaps in screening and lower HPV vaccination coverage. Advanced and recurrent cervical cancer remain prevalent,2 and for these patients, the treatment typically involves concurrent chemoradiotherapy, but the clinical outcomes are often not ideal.3 The management of cervical cancer has advanced significantly over recent decades, driven by improvements in diagnosis, surgical techniques, and adjuvant therapies. As highlighted by D’Augè et al., strategic and personalized approaches are paramount, even in early-stage disease, to optimize oncological outcomes and preserve quality of life.4 These advancements underscore a broader shift toward precision medicine in cervical cancer care. However, despite these progresses, the therapeutic landscape for advanced and recurrent cervical cancer remains challenging.2,3 This stark contrast between the management of early-stage and advanced-stage disease highlights the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies in the latter setting. While immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy brings new hope, some patients do not benefit from immunotherapy, and the remission rate for monotherapy is low. Combining chemoradiotherapy with immunotherapy may be necessary to improve survival outcomes, but such a strategy can also increase adverse reactions.5,6 When severe immune-related adverse reactions occur, hyperprogressive disease may develop, which shortens survival.7 Patient screening is essential in immunotherapy as it optimizes treatment efficiency and minimizes risks and adverse events.

The efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced and recurrent cervical cancer has been firmly established by the phase 3 KEYNOTE-826 trial, which demonstrated that pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab significantly improved overall survival and progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with persistent, recurrent, or metastatic cervical cancer.8–10 In the final analysis, the addition of pembrolizumab reduced the risk of death by 37% (HR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.52–0.77) and extended median overall survival from 16.8 to 26.4 months in the intention-to-treat population, with consistent benefits observed across subgroups defined by bevacizumab use, platinum choice, and histologic type.8,9 These findings have established pembrolizumab-based combinations as a standard-of-care first-line treatment for this patient population and underscore the value of identifying biomarkers that can further optimize patient selection.

There is accumulating evidence that peripheral PD-1 expression and T-cell subset dynamics are associated with immune responses across different tumor types, as comprehensively reviewed in the context of checkpoint inhibitor therapy.11 The peripheral blood provides an accessible window to the systemic immune landscape. Notably, several studies have specifically shown that the abundance and phenotype of peripheral PD-1+ CD8+ T cells correlate with anti-PD-1 treatment response. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Kamphorst et al. demonstrated that the proliferation of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood after PD-1-targeted therapy was associated with clinical response.12 Furthermore, Huang et al. reported that a higher frequency of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in the periphery was linked to better overall survival in melanoma patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting its potential as a dynamic biomarker.13 These foundational studies across malignancies provide a strong rationale for investigating similar peripheral immune correlates in cervical cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy.

In cervical cancer, emerging studies have begun to explore circulating immune biomarkers. While most investigations have focused on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and PD-L1 expression in tumor tissue, a growing body of evidence highlights the relevance of peripheral immune profiling. For example, alterations in peripheral T-cell subsets-such as decreased CD4+/CD8+ ratios or elevated PD-1 expression on circulating T cells – have been reported in cervical cancer patients and may reflect systemic immune dysregulation. Moreover, changes in peripheral PD-1+ T cells during treatment have been suggested to correlate with therapeutic response. In particular, a decrease in PD-1+ CD8+ T cells after immunotherapy has been associated with favorable outcomes in several solid tumors, though data specific to cervical cancer remain limited. These observations support the potential utility of serial peripheral blood monitoring to capture dynamic immune shifts that may predict treatment efficacy and prognosis in cervical cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade.

Biomarkers suitable for accurately predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy and the prognosis are limited. PD-L1 remains the most widely used predictive biomarker in clinical practice.14 However, since the expression of PD-L1 is highly dependent on the specific characteristics of the tumor tissue, tissue sampling may not fully capture the complex, dynamic expression patterns of PD-L1.15,16 The spatiotemporal heterogeneity in PD-L1 expression can detract from the stability and accuracy of its detection for predicting immunotherapy outcomes.17,18 Thus, while tissue-based markers like PD-L1 and TILs offer a snapshot of the local tumor immune microenvironment, peripheral immune profiling provides a dynamic, systemic perspective that is amenable to serial monitoring. Peripheral blood testing, also referred to as a “liquid biopsy,” offers several significant advantages in both clinical and research settings. It is minimally invasive, safe, and cost-effective. Repeated sampling over time is feasible, which enables dynamic monitoring and helps to capture tumor heterogeneity. As a result, it has been increasingly developed and adopted as a biomarker for immunotherapy in recent years.19,20

In this study, we aimed to identify reliable peripheral blood biomarkers to guide immunotherapy in patients with advanced and recurrent cervical cancer. We evaluated selected serum tumor biomarkers and T cell subsets before and after therapy. The findings enabled us to distinguish between protective and risk factors, thereby enhancing patient screening and informing the optimal use of immunotherapy in this challenging population.

Materials and methods

Study design

Patients were prospectively enrolled from June 2022 to July 2024 at Qingdao Central Hospital and its partner hospitals. Eligible patients were (1) 18 to 70 y of age, (2) diagnosed with advanced or recurrent cervical squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, or adenosquamous carcinoma, and (3) receiving immunotherapy combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy as systemic anti-tumor treatment. Patients were excluded if they (1) had active autoimmune diseases requiring systemic management, (2) were receiving other approved systemic immunomodulators or using high doses of hormones or other immunosuppressants long term, or (3) had an expected survival time of less than 12 weeks. Additionally, patients were included only if they had complete clinical data along with follow-up information for at least 10 months. This study received approval from the Ethics Review Board of Qingdao Central Hospital (approval No. LW202215) and was conducted in strict accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was received from all patients.

Patient data

The basic data included age, diagnosis, pathological type, ECOG score, clinical stage, family history of tumors, and treatment plan. We summarized their peripheral blood indicators and T lymphocyte subsets before and after one course of therapy, which were referred to hereafter as baseline and treatment, respectively. The peripheral blood indicators included carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) and squamous-cell carcinoma antigen (SCCA), and the T lymphocyte subsets included the percentage of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells and the PD-1 expression rate on CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells.

The response to immunotherapy was evaluated every 3–4 weeks according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1 for immune-based therapeutics (iRECIST1.1). The remission status at 6 months was determined from a comprehensive evaluation of the patient from the first medication to 6 months of follow-up. Patients were deemed to have a complete response (CR) if all detectable signs of cancer disappeared after the treatment, as evaluated by imaging, physical examination, and lab tests. A partial response (PR) was noted if the treatment led to a substantial decrease in the size or number of cancerous lesions. Patients were classified as having a stable disease (SD) if the tumor size remained relatively unchanged for more than 6 months and no response (NR) if disease progression occurred within 6 months of starting medication. For all patients, progression-free survival (PFS) was used as the prognostic indicator, which was measured as the time from the start of the first immunotherapy to either disease progression (according to imaging) or death from any cause.

Blood samples

Peripheral venous blood was collected into vacuum tubes containing an anticoagulant 3 d before the first immunotherapy session (baseline) and within 48 hours after the completion of one course of therapy (treatment). The tubes were inverted to lyse the red blood cells with hemolysin. The PD-1 expression of T cells was determined by flow cytometry using an EasyCell flow cytometer (Shenzhen Wellgrow Technology Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Data were analyzed using Navios CytExpert (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). After lysis, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prepare a single-cell suspension. For each patient, paired baseline and post-treatment PBMC samples were processed and cryopreserved under identical conditions and subsequently analyzed in the same batch to minimize inter-run variability. The resulting peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples were resuspended in cell freezing medium and immediately stored at −80°C for long-term preservation until batch analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

For the detection of T-cell subsets and PD-1 expression, frozen PBMC samples were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath, washed with PBS., and assessed for viability using trypan blue exclusion. Only samples with viability >85% were used; three samples (3/106, 2.8%) were excluded due to viability <70% and were not included in the final analysis.

Staining and acquisition reproducibility: Intra-assay reproducibility was assessed by staining and acquiring the same healthy donor PBMC sample in triplicate within each run. The coefficient of variation (CV) for key markers across replicates was as follows: CD4+ T cells: 3.2%, CD8+ T cells: 4.1%, PD-1+ CD4+: 6.8%, PD-1+ CD8+: 7.3%. The CV for each marker was calculated as (standard deviation/mean) × 100% using the triplicate measurements from the same donor sample. All CVs were below 10%, indicating acceptable reproducibility.

Blinding: Flow cytometry data acquisition and analysis were performed by two operators who were blinded to the clinical outcomes and group assignments (responder vs non-responder, baseline vs post-treatment) to minimize bias.

For cell surface staining, approximately 1 × 106 cells per sample were incubated with a pre-optimized panel of fluorescently conjugated anti-human antibodies (see Table 1) for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark, including a viability dye (7-AAD) to exclude dead cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA)and R4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Categorical data were expressed as case count (ratio) and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn to determine the cut-off values of peripheral blood indicators and T lymphocyte subsets in predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy. Curves were generated using R software (version 4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the pROC package. The 95% confidence intervals for the AUC were calculated using 1000 stratified bootstrap replicates. All ROC plots include shaded 95% confidence bands and are labeled with the corresponding AUC and 95% CI (Figure 1). Model Reliability and Events per Variable (EPV): To ensure the robustness of the multivariate regression models, we calculated the Events per Variable (EPV). A common heuristic in regression modeling is that an Events per Variable (EPV) value of 10 or greater is desirable for stable model estimates and to mitigate the risk of overfitting. To improve model stability and reduce overfitting, we limited the number of predictors in each multivariate model to a maximum of four variables, selected based on clinical relevance and univariate significance (P < .10). The number of outcome events and the EPV for the primary multivariate models are reported in Table 3. The EPV values for our key models were as follows: logistic regression (response) EPV = 9.0, Cox regression (PFS) EPV = 6.5. The following clinical variables were pre-specified for inclusion in all multivariate models due to their established prognostic importance in cervical cancer and immunotherapy response: Age (<55 vs ≥55 y), FIGO stage (III vs IV), Histological type (squamous vs non-squamous).In addition to these pre-specified variables, empirical selection was performed for the immune and serum biomarkers (e.g., T-cell subsets, PD-1 expression, CA125, SCCA). A variable was considered for inclusion in the multivariate model if it showed an association with the outcome in univariate analysis at a significance level of P < .10. This approach allowed us to identify potentially relevant biomarkers while controlling for key clinical confounders. To maintain model parsimony and avoid overfitting, the number of empirically selected variables in each final multivariate model was limited to a maximum of four. This restriction was applied after ensuring that the pre-specified clinical variables were retained in the model. Variables with established clinical importance (e.g., age, stage) or those showing an association with the outcome in univariate analysis at a significance level of P < .10 were considered candidates for the multivariate models. Results

Patient information

Fifty patients were enrolled in this study, all of whom were receiving PD-1 immunotherapy in combination with either chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Their 2018 FIGO staging varied from III to IVa, and their ECOG scores were ≤2. Among them, 23 patients (46.0%) were aged 55 y or older, and 27 patients (54.0%) were less than 55 y old. The breakdown of cancer types was as follows: 41 patients (82.0%) had squamous cell carcinoma, 6 patients (12.0%) had adenocarcinoma, and 3 patients (6.0%) had adenosquamous carcinoma. The last follow-up was on December 19, 2024, and the follow-up duration ranged from 266 to 660 d.

After the therapy, 14 patients (28.0%) were classified as NR. The other 36 (72.0%) patients, collectively referred to as Group R, were categorized as either PR or SD. There was no significant difference in age, clinical pathological characteristics, and laboratory test results between Group NR and Group R (P > .05), indicating that the baseline was comparable (Table 4). Grade 2 or lower immune-related adverse events (irAEs) occurred to 26 patients (52.0%), primarily affecting the endocrine, skin, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal systems.

Correlation between immunotherapy efficacy and indicators

PD-1 expression was calculated as the percentage of PD-1-positive cells within the CD4+ or CD8+ T-cell compartments, as defined by FMO-based gating.

Compared to Group NR, Group R had significantly higher CD4+ cell percentage both before and after the treatment (P < .05), and its percentage of CD8+ cells was significantly higher only after the treatment (P < .01). After the treatment, the PD-1 expression rates on CD4+ and CD8+ cells were significantly lower for Group R (P < .01). According to the ROC curves (Figure 1), the cutoff value of the baseline CD4+ T cell percentage was 26.95%. For the indicators measured post-treatment, the respective cutoff values were as follows: CD4+ T cell percentage, 28.79%; CD8+ T cell percentage, 36.76%; PD-1 expression rate on CD4+ T cells, 6.75%; PD-1 expression rate on CD8+ T cells, 8.37%. It is important to note that these cut-off values, while statistically significant in our cohort, are exploratory and data-driven. Their generalizability and optimal clinical application require confirmation in larger, independent validation studies.

The Area Under the Curve (AUC) with 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CL) for each ROC analysis used to derive these cutoffs is presented in Table 5. All biomarkers showed significant discriminatory power (all AUC > 0.7), with post-treatment CD8+ T cell percentage and PD-1 expression on CD4+ T cells demonstrating the highest predictive accuracy for treatment response (AUC = 0.812 and 0.827, respectively).

The biological indicators that differed significantly between the two groups were examined further by logistic regression analysis. Table 6 shows that before the treatment, Group R had a significantly higher (P = .018) percentage of CD4+ T cells but did not differ from Group NR in the percentage of CD8+ T cells (P = .783). After the treatment, Group R had significantly lower (P < .05) CA125, SCCA, and PD-1 expression rates on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. While Group R had a significantly higher (P = .009) percentage of CD8+ T cells than Group NR after the treatment, no such difference existed in the percentage of CD4+ T cells (P = .142). That is, the apparent difference in the percentage of CD4+ T cells between the two groups (Table 4) disappeared since it was not an independent predictor of the outcome once other factors were considered. Hence, regarding the efficacy of immunotherapy, the protective factors included high levels of CD4+ T cell percentage before the treatment and high levels of CD8+ T cell percentage after the treatment, whereas the risk factors included high levels of tumor biomarkers (CA125 and SCCA) and PD-1 expression rates (on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) after the treatment.

Discussion

Advanced and recurrent cervical cancer remain formidable clinical challenges largely because the treatment options often yield only modest efficacy.21 Recent years have witnessed a paradigm shift with the integration of novel targeted agents and immunotherapies. As highlighted by recent evidence, the treatment landscape is evolving beyond traditional modalities to include antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) such as tisotumab vedotin, and combination strategies leveraging immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) like pembrolizumab, which have shown promising efficacy in clinical trials.22 These advancements pave the way for a more tailored approach to treatment. Our study identifies several readily accessible peripheral blood biomarkers, including T-cell subsets and PD-1 expression levels, which may contribute to the ongoing effort to develop personalized treatment approaches for advanced cervical cancer. However, these findings are preliminary and necessitate rigorous external validation. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a promising advancement in the treatment of advanced and recurrent cervical cancer. They are increasingly adopted as a first-line therapy for metastatic, recurrent, and persistent disease, and have significantly extended both overall and progression-free survival.23–25 However, some patients fail to benefit from immunotherapy, which highlights the need for robust biomarkers to inform treatment decisions. Recent investigations have examined the dynamic fluctuations in peripheral blood indicators during immunotherapy. Researchers are also exploring the associations between the phenotypic and functional status of the circulating immune cells and treatment response, to thus uncover predictive markers that could distinguish responders from non-responders and ultimately improve patient outcomes.26–29 Elevated PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells has been associated with cellular dysfunction, immune tolerance, and tumor escape, and PD-1 is a hallmark of T-cell exhaustion. Exhausted T cells are a dysfunctional subset that arises primarily under conditions of chronic antigen exposure where continuous stimulation drives a gradual loss of T cell effector functions. When T cells are exhausted, they exhibit reduced functional capabilities, including diminished production and secretion of key effector molecules such as cytokines, granzymes, and perforin.30 Hence, assessing the PD-1 expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells is critical for evaluating immune function. The utility of peripheral T-cell subsets and PD-1 expression as dynamic biomarkers for immunotherapy response has been increasingly recognized across multiple tumor types. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Kamphorst et al.9 demonstrated that the proliferative response of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood following anti-PD-1 therapy was associated with clinical response, with early proliferation serving as an indicator of effective immune reinvigoration. Similarly, Huang et al. showed in melanoma patients that the ratio of T-cell invigoration to tumor burden correlated with response to pembrolizumab, and that baseline peripheral PD-1+ CD8+ T-cell frequency was associated31 with overall survival.13 In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, serial monitoring of circulating CD8+ T cells expressing PD-1 and other exhaustion markers has been shown to reflect intratumoral immune activity and predict treatment outcomes. Furthermore, in gastric cancer, an increase in peripheral CD8+ T-cell count early during immunotherapy was reported to correlate with favorable prognosis.32 Collectively, these studies across diverse solid tumors support the concept that peripheral T-cell dynamics – particularly changes in CD8+ T-cell abundance and PD-1 expression – serve as accessible, real-time indicators of systemic immune engagement with checkpoint blockade. These findings provided the rationale for investigating similar immune correlates in cervical cancer, where data on peripheral biomarkers remain comparatively limited.

In this study, we analyzed the clinical data, pathological data, and routine tumor markers of patients with advanced and recurrent cervical cancer undergoing immunotherapy. By controlling for confounding factors, we identified factors associated with the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment and patient prognosis, thereby providing new ideas for dynamic treatment monitoring.

Compared to Group NR, Group R had higher baseline levels of CD4+T cell percentage. Presumably, with higher levels of CD4+ T cells, the immune response was mobilized more effectively, and immune cells were activated to recognize tumor cells. CD8+ T cells are cytotoxic T cells that mainly participate in the immune response to tumor cells. After one course of therapy, compared to Group NR, Group R had a higher CD8+ T cell percentage, which may be related to the positive selection of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) of human thymic epithelial cells after immunotherapy. An increased number of CD8+ T cells that recognize antigens presented by MHC class I molecules generally correlates with enhanced cytotoxicity against tumor cells, although the detailed mechanisms are not yet fully understood and remain an area of active research. In addition, compared to Group NR, Group R had significantly lower CA125 and SCCA, likely because therapy effectively activated the body’s immune system and reduced the tumor burden. Group R also had lower post-treatment PD-1 expression rates on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, indicating that the medication effectively blocked PD-1 receptors and led to better response. However, in the multivariate Cox regression analysis of PFS, no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups in the PD-1 expression rate on CD4+ and CD8+ cells (P > .05), for which there may be two reasons. First, immunotherapy drugs block PD-1 receptors at different times and in different tissues, and there can be timing issues with the collected blood samples. Second, patients may have varying adverse immune reactions to immunotherapy, and such reactions can trigger further immune responses, leading to fluctuations in PD-1 expression levels. Regarding the prognosis of immunotherapy, independent predictors of PFS included baseline CD4+ T cell percentage and post-treatment CA125, SCCA, CD4+ T cell percentage, and CD8+ T cell percentage.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although this is a multicenter prospective cohort study, only a small number of patients with recurrent and advanced cervical cancer opted for immunotherapy and were enrolled. The small patient pool may have introduced selection bias and information bias. Furthermore, the optimal cut-off values for the peripheral blood biomarkers were determined in an exploratory manner using ROC curve analysis on the present dataset. While this approach is standard for initial biomarker development, it can lead to over-optimism regarding their performance, and these specific numerical thresholds need to be prospectively validated before clinical implementation. Second, while we implemented rigorous quality control measures – including blinded flow cytometry analysis, intra-assay CV monitoring, and standardized batch processing – the use of cryopreserved PBMCs and the modest sample size may still introduce variability that could affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, the choice of immunotherapy drug, treatment cycle, and follow-up time depended on individual conditions and varied across patients. In addition, we only measured the changes after one course of therapy, which may be too short to capture long-term disease stability. Future studies need to include larger cohorts, consider multiple therapy courses, and have longer follow-up periods to validate the present findings. Most importantly, the exploratory cut-off values and multivariate models derived from this single-center cohort require confirmation through external validation in independent, larger patient populations before any consideration for clinical translation.

To contextualize our findings within the broader field of immuno-oncology biomarkers, we compared the effect sizes (AUC) and optimal cut-offs of our key peripheral blood indicators with those reported in published studies across different tumor types, including any available cervical cancer series. It is important to interpret such cross-study comparisons with caution due to substantial variations in pre-analytical factors, flow cytometry panels, gating strategies, and patient populations. For instance, the predictive power of post-treatment CD8+ T cell percentage (AUC = 0.812) and PD-1 expression on CD4+ T cells (AUC = 0.827) in our cohort is broadly comparable to the discriminatory accuracy reported for similar immune biomarkers in studies of NSCLC and melanoma.11,12,19 Similarly, the optimal cut-off for PD-1+ CD8+ T cells identified here (8.37%) falls within the wide range reported for other malignancies (e.g., 5–15% in some cohorts), although direct numerical comparison is confounded by methodological differences. While data from peripheral blood in cervical cancer remains scarce, our study provides crucial, novel benchmarks for future research. This comparative analysis suggests that certain peripheral immune signatures may have trans-tumor predictive value, but it also underscores the critical need for standardized assays and reporting to enable meaningful cross-study comparisons and clinical translation.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that specific peripheral immune and serum biomarkers – including baseline CD4+ T-cell percentage, early changes in CD8+ T-cell percentage, PD-1 expression, and tumor antigens (CA125/SCCA) – show significant associations with treatment response and progression-free survival in patients receiving immunotherapy for advanced cervical cancer. These readily accessible parameters may reflect the dynamic interplay between the host immune system and tumor burden. However, the predictive utility of these biomarkers and the optimal cut-offs identified must be rigorously tested in prospective, multicenter validation studies before they can be considered for clinical application. If validated, such a minimally invasive profiling strategy could serve as a complementary approach to monitor treatment dynamics and inform personalized management in this challenging patient population.

Future studies should be directed along several clear paths to validate and extend our findings. First, external validation in a prospective, multi-center cohort of advanced cervical cancer patients is essential to confirm the generalizability of these peripheral blood biomarkers. Second, incorporating more frequent serial blood sampling would allow for a truly dynamic assessment of immune changes. The integration of validated biomarkers into a clinically usable risk-score could ultimately guide personalized immunotherapy management, but this remains a goal for future research. Finally, incorporating more frequent serial blood sampling would allow for a truly dynamic assessment of immune changes, enabling the construction of longitudinal trajectories that may be more powerful than single time-point measurements for predicting long-term outcomes and capturing the evolution of treatment resistance. The integration of these validated biomarkers into a clinically usable risk-score could ultimately guide timely treatment switches and personalize immunotherapy management for cervical cancer patients.

Potential Clinical Implications: If validated in larger prospective cohorts, the peripheral blood biomarkers identified in this study could inform clinical decision-making at several key junctures. First, prior to treatment initiation, a high baseline CD4+ T-cell percentage might help identify patients with a more favorable systemic immune landscape, who are thus more likely to benefit from first-line immunotherapy combinations, aiding in patient stratification. Second, early during therapy (e.g., after the first cycle), a composite profile-characterized by a decline in CA125/SCCA, an increase in CD8+ T-cells, and a reduction in PD-1 expression on T cells-could serve as an early indicator of response. This might allow for the early continuation of effective treatment in responders and prompt consideration of alternative strategies in non-responders, potentially sparing patients from prolonged ineffective therapy and its associated toxicity. Finally, serial monitoring of these biomarkers could provide a minimally invasive means to track disease status and immune activation dynamically, complementing radiographic assessments.

Statistical Considerations for Biomarker Validation: Our analysis involved the evaluation of multiple biomarkers and their associations with treatment outcomes. While we employed bootstrap resampling for internal validation and limited the number of predictors in multivariate models, the multiple comparisons inherent in exploratory biomarker research increase the risk of false-positive findings. Furthermore, the development of optimal cut-offs from the same dataset used to evaluate performance, combined with our modest sample size, elevates the risk of overfitting-where models perform well on the derivation cohort but fail to generalize. To mitigate these issues in future validation studies, the application of penalized regression techniques (e.g., LASSO or ridge regression) will be crucial. These methods inherently perform variable selection and shrinkage of coefficients, providing a more robust approach for identifying a parsimonious set of biomarkers from a larger candidate panel while controlling for overfitting, before proceeding to traditional regression modeling or clinical score development in independent cohorts.

Our findings on peripheral blood biomarkers align with the pressing need for accessible tools to guide tailored-treatment in this new era of advanced cervical cancer management.
