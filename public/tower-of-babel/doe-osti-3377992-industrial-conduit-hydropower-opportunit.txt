 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

Industrial conduit hydropower involves generating electricity from flowing water in existing industrial
water supply, process flow, or wastewater discharges. The broader range of conduit hydropower involves
powering existing water infrastructure in municipal, industrial, or agricultural systems. Whereas
municipal and agricultural water systems have been retrofitted somewhat broadly in the United States,
there are very few examples of industrial conduit hydropower. As stated in Kao et al. (2022), “conduit
hydropower opportunities associated with industrial conduits are the least understood […] industrial
developments are likely to be particularly efficient and cost-effective since they are typically eligible for
on-site net-metering.” Therefore, additional insight into industrial opportunities is needed.

Traditionally, conduit hydropower involves installing small turbines (e.g., pump-as-turbine [PAT]
systems) in pressurized pipelines to capture what is otherwise unutilized excess water pressure. Within
public water supply systems (which have both municipal and industrial applications), such retrofits are
most easily accomplished by bypassing a pressure reducing valve (PRV) for energy recovery. Other
forms of municipal and industrial conduit hydropower can involve capturing energy using wastewater
flow turbines that harvest gravity-driven or pumped discharges and tailrace energy recovery in cooling or
dewatering systems with substantial outflows. Many projects benefit from offsetting on-site power
consumption, which optimizes the value of the electricity generated and may simplify interconnection.

Because this form of hydropower involves the development of no new dams or diversions, it has a
reduced environmental impact and benefits from a streamlined regulatory process whereby a project can
earn a qualifying conduit facility designation to make it exempt from Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC) licensing requirements.1 Furthermore, some state and federal grant funding and
energy tax credits are available to help make conduit hydropower more affordable, and net metering
enables additional benefits (DeNeale et al., 2025).2 Depending on flow reliability and capital costs,
conduit-based hydropower systems often achieve paybacks within three to ten years.3 These projects
reduce electricity expenses, require no fuel inputs, and offer reliable and predictable generation where
flows are stable. They also advance sustainability by lowering grid purchases while turning existing
infrastructure into productive assets.

Conduit hydropower operates on the same fundamental principle as steam turbine systems commonly
used in industrial steam networks. In both cases, a turbine is installed in parallel with a PRV to convert
excess pressure energy into electricity while maintaining the required downstream pressure. Steam
turbines, typically small backpressure or condensing units, are widely used in paper mills, food and
beverage processing plants, and district heating systems to generate onsite power from steam that would
otherwise be throttled (DOE, 2015). Systems in the 50 kW to 2 MW range have demonstrated high
reliability and attractive payback periods under continuous operation. The broad deployment of steam
turbines across the industrial sector illustrates the proven potential for energy recovery at existing

 2. DEVELOPING INDUSTRIAL CONDUIT HYDROPOWER

In terms of development process, the same general process shown in Figure 3 and presented in more
detail in DeNeale et al. (2025) applies for industrial conduit hydropower development. Developing
conduit hydropower can be much quicker than traditional hydropower thanks to a more streamlined
regulatory process stemming from the Hydropower Regulatory Efficiency Act of 20134 (HREA) and the
removal of such projects from Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) jurisdiction, referred to as
qualifying conduit facilities. Removing the uncertainty, time, and cost associated with a FERC license or
exemption has allowed projects to be developed more quickly. Additionally, the minimal construction due
to the utilization of existing infrastructure and the limited ground disturbance activities accelerate
development.

However, as identified through a prior workshop on opportunities and challenges for conduit hydropower
held in April 2024 and documented in DeNeale et al. (2024) and DeNeale et al. (2025), challenges remain
throughout the process—namely, stakeholder engagement, other permitting requirements, and power
sales—and industrial facility operations could further complicate development. It is also worth noting that
some of the human factors affecting industrial conduit hydropower consideration may make development
more challenging. For instance, the employee structure and dynamics of industrial facilities may make
long payback periods more challenging to justify to management, especially when other factors like plant
efficiency improvement or heat recovery may be more attractive.

Across all industries, systems with existing PRVs likely have the greatest potential for development due
to the ease of implementation, especially for systems with high pressure reduction. Also, systems with
high and consistent discharge flows could be more viable.

Although it is generally similar to other forms of conduit hydropower, industrial conduit hydropower
development requires understanding of a few specific considerations. The following features are crucially
important when considering industrial conduit hydropower development.

 • Ownership and control: industrial systems are privately owned and operated by a single industrial
 entity, with development decisions made to support internal business objectives.

 • Operational priorities: industrial systems prioritize process reliability and product quality and
 must integrate hydropower without disrupting core facility operations.

2.1 SUBSECTOR HYDROPOWER APPLICATIONS

The United States Geological Survey (USGS) estimates water use in the United States and publishes a
report every 5 years documenting the latest data (Medalie et al., 2025; Dieter et al., 2018). The USGS also
differentiates between self-supplied water and public-supply water; a diagram of various select water use
concepts is shown in Figure 4. Figure 5 plots US water use by source (groundwater or surface water), end
use (crop irrigation, public supply, and thermoelectric power), and region, and Table 3 provides quantities
of water withdrawals for 2015. Importantly, USGS defines the term public-supply water use as water
withdrawn by public and private water suppliers that furnish water to at least 25 people or have a
minimum of 15 connections; this same definition is used by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
for a public water system. Public suppliers provide water for various uses, such as domestic, commercial,
industrial, thermoelectric power, and public use. Because public-supply water is subject to more federal
and state regulations, data and information are more readily available than they are for self-supplied
water. Water supply for industrial applications can thus come from either public or private supply, with
the source supply impacting the availability of public data.

2.1.1 Aquaculture

Aquaculture facilities, particularly fish hatcheries, rely on steady flows of clean water that is circulated
through tanks or raceways before being discharged. Because these systems are typically designed for
continuous operation and involve minimal consumptive use, they offer excellent potential for conduit
hydropower. Water can be harnessed both at the intake, where gravity supply lines often bring water from
elevated sources, and at the discharge, where effluent flows return to rivers or lakes.

The combination of consistent flow, predictable operation, and existing pipeline infrastructure makes
aquaculture a strong candidate for small-scale hydropower installations. The main constraints are
regulatory in nature because facilities must maintain water quality and meet fish health standards.
However, technically, these sites align well with qualifying conduit definitions.

Additional information on conduit hydropower for the aquaculture industry is provided in Appendix A.1.

2.1.2 Food and Beverage Processing (Manufacturing)

Food and beverage processing facilities use water for a wide range of purposes, including ingredient
preparation, sanitation, and cooking. Water quality and hygiene standards are particularly stringent, which
often limits opportunities for reuse within the plant. Similar to installations in the municipal sector,
turbine integration needs to adhere to certain standards (e.g., NSF/ANSI 61 for drinking water safety).
While volumes can be substantial, the suitability for conduit hydropower depends on the configuration of
the facility and whether elevation differences exist among intake, process, and discharge points.
Depending on the water supply source (e.g., municipal) and its pressure, an existing PRV could be
bypassed via a PAT.

Potential also exists in wastewater outflows, especially where plants are located above receiving
waterways. As a result, conduit hydropower opportunities in food and beverage processing are more site-
specific, and there are no known existing installations.

Additional information on conduit hydropower for the food and beverage processing industry is provided
in Appendix A.2.

2.1.3 Pulp and Paper Manufacturing

The pulp and paper sector is one of the most water-intensive industries, using large volumes of water for
pulping, bleaching, cooling, and cleaning processes. Because much of this water is returned after
treatment, the sector already invests heavily in dedicated treatment systems and extensive piping
infrastructure. Conduit hydropower can be incorporated either at supply points, where pressure reduction
devices are often needed, or at discharge points, where treated water flows back to natural waterways.

This industry’s long history with hydropower and the presence of large, centralized facilities make it
particularly suitable for conduit installations. The main challenge for adding conventional hydropower
lies in the economics of retrofitting older mills or maintaining traditional larger-scale turbine equipment,
which is less likely to be the case with conduit hydropower.

2.1.4   Mining

Mining operations frequently move significant volumes of water, either as part of ore processing or
through dewatering activities. Treatment plants are often built to handle contaminated water before it is
released back into natural waterways. These treatment discharges, particularly where mine sites are
located in mountainous terrain, can provide favorable conditions for conduit hydropower.

The sector is especially attractive where remediation or treatment is ongoing, since hydropower can offset
part of the operating energy demand. Although flows can be variable, the presence of head differences
between ponds, tunnels, and treatment systems may create sufficient pressure to integrate small turbines.
Mining therefore represents one of the more promising non-traditional industrial contexts for conduit
applications.

Additional information on conduit hydropower for the mining industry is provided in Appendix A.4.

2.1.5 Thermoelectric Power

Thermoelectric power plants are among the largest industrial water users, drawing immense volumes for
steam condensation and cooling. After use, this water is often discharged through canals, pipes, or cooling
towers, sometimes over substantial drops. These discharge points can host conduit hydropower
installations, especially where flows are consistent and elevation differences exist.

Because these facilities already manage large, engineered water pathways, the integration of turbines is
technically straightforward. The primary challenges are economic and operational, as plant operators may
prioritize thermal efficiency over secondary generation and may be moving away from water-intensive
once-through cooling. Still, the scale of flows makes this sector a strong candidate for significant conduit
hydropower potential.

Additional information on conduit hydropower for the thermoelectric power industry is provided in
Appendix A.5.

2.1.6 Desalination

Desalination plants pressurize seawater or brackish water to separate fresh water from salts, creating a
residual brine stream still at high pressure. This brine reject provides an opportunity for energy recovery,
historically through turbines and increasingly through pressure exchanger technologies. Because
desalination inherently involves high pressures and engineered conduits, hydropower integration is
technically straightforward. Modern plants often adopt dedicated energy recovery devices, which serve
the same purpose as conduit hydropower. As desalination continues to expand, especially in arid regions,
these recovery systems will remain a critical element of sustainable plant design.

Additional information on conduit hydropower for the desalination industry is provided in Appendix A.6.

2.1.7 Data centers

Data centers increasingly represent a unique industrial water user due to their reliance on large-scale
cooling systems. Depending on the cooling technology, water may be evaporated in cooling towers,
circulated in closed loops, or used in liquid immersion systems. In many cases, the resulting blowdown or
discharge from cooling towers is sent to municipal systems or released with minimal treatment. Conduit
hydropower opportunities in data centers are limited compared to more traditional industries because
flows are smaller and typically lack significant head. However, in sites with large-scale water circulation,
especially those located on sloped terrain or connected to municipal supply pipelines with pressure
reduction points, small hydropower systems could be integrated. As water and energy use in data centers
grows, niche opportunities for conduit applications may emerge, particularly in facilities exploring hybrid
or reclaimed water cooling systems.

 4. SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

This report presents an early-stage assessment of the potential for integrating hydropower generation
within existing industrial water infrastructure. This form of energy, referred to as conduit hydropower,
relies on water already being moved for operational or regulatory purposes within industrial facilities. It
offers an opportunity to recover energy without the need for new dams or significant environmental
disruption. Industrial facilities in the United States use vast amounts of water for processing, cooling,
cleaning, and other operational needs. This water often flows under pressure or experiences changes in
elevation that can be harnessed to generate electricity using turbines, much like in traditional hydropower
systems but applied to facility infrastructure. Despite its technical feasibility, conduit hydropower has
received little attention in the industrial sector, largely due to a lack of data, case studies, and awareness
of the technical and economic feasibility of adding hydropower to existing facilities. This report is a
crucial step in the exploration of where and how such systems might be deployed.

Ongoing research is underway to more rigorously assess conduit hydropower opportunities within the US
manufacturing sector, and there are plans to expand the analysis to other energy-intensive industries. This
effort, funded by the DOE Industrial Technologies Office, aims to develop improved methods for
estimating technical and BTM generation potential using facility-level water and energy data. Early
findings indicate that data availability and quality remain significant constraints, particularly with respect
to site-specific flow rates, pressures, and operational timing. Preliminary screening suggests that where
adequate data exist, estimates of industrial conduit hydropower potential may substantially exceed the
2022 ORNL national assessment of conduit hydropower potential for the industrial sector, highlighting
both the promise of the resource and the need for more comprehensive data collection and validation.

The scoping assessment also outlines several critical research gaps that could be filled to accelerate the
development of industrial conduit hydropower in the US. Chief among them is the lack of detailed data on
how water is used and managed in industrial processes. Without information on flow rates, pressure, and
timing, it is difficult to assess whether water systems are suitable for hydropower applications. Similarly,
there is limited facility-level data on water intake and wastewater discharge, both of which are essential
for evaluating site-specific energy recovery potential. There is also a need for better methods to
extrapolate available data to sector-wide or regional hydropower estimates. In parallel, electricity
consumption data for these sectors must be gathered to determine how hydropower generation aligns with
energy use.

Furthermore, limited engagement with industry stakeholders means that key operational details,
challenges, and priorities are often missed. More direct collaboration would provide valuable insights and
inform feasibility assessments. Creating well-documented case studies and decision-support tools and
providing lab-led technical assistance could help move the assessment from idea to implementation.
Addressing these gaps is essential for advancing conduit hydropower as a viable source of on-site energy
recovery across the industrial sector.

In conclusion, while conduit hydropower remains largely untapped in the US industrial sector, the
analysis presented in this report suggests that it could become a meaningful contributor to on-site energy
generation. Capturing even a fraction of the estimated potential could support energy resilience, reduce
emissions, and lower operating costs for facilities, particularly if paired with broader efforts to modernize
industrial water and energy infrastructure. Further research and data collection will be necessary to refine
these estimates and to catalyze development, but the opportunity appears both technically viable and
worthy of national attention.

A.2. Food Processing, Chemical, Metals, Petroleum (Manufacturing)

Other industrial manufacturing processes such as food processing, chemical processing, and primary
metals and petroleum refineries account for about 4% of US water withdrawals. Per conversations with
multiple hydro equipment vendors, hydro inquiries have been made by food processing and other
industrial facilities over the years, but to their knowledge none of the projects have been built. In general,
these inquiries were regarding a pressure reducing valve located on a municipal water supply entering the
facility. There is anecdotal evidence of one very small project constructed on a wastewater injection
system for a pharmaceutical company, but there are no records of the project with FERC.

Several inquiries were also made to turbine manufacturers regarding water used in the fracking process
for natural gas extraction. Large volumes of water are withdrawn and reinjected into deep wells. The idea
of adding hydropower to this process has not yet proved feasible.14

Project cited “industrial use.” The turbine is located adjacent to a PRV on the water supply inlet to an

A.4. Mining

The mining industry accounts for about 1% of US water withdrawals. Due to the remote nature of some
mining operations, they are commonly powered using on-site fossil fuel generation. Lowering operating
costs by using on-site renewable generation is of interest to mining companies. According to Rocky
Mountain Institute, mining companies have installed or announced 4.9 GW of renewable energy projects
by the end of 2019, all of which are wind and solar.16 The potential in this sector could include conduit
hydropower within the mining process, or any other collocated small hydro, NPD, or run of river projects.

Water treatment facilities at mines hold the same potential as municipal water or wastewater treatment
facilities. In general, a mining water treatment plant will receive water through an existing pipeline from a
discharge point, and clean water will return to a natural waterway. Hydropower potential will exist where
the supply (e.g., a tailings pond) is higher in elevation than the treatment facility, or where the discharge
is higher than the receiving water.

FERC information on mining projects may be limited as it is likely that some are operating off the
national grid and were not under FERC jurisdiction (such as the Summitville Mine example below). The
Pine Creek Mine Tunnel project (P-12532) is pending a FERC license, and the 250 kW Pine Creek Water
Discharge 1 & 2 project received an exemption in 2011.

A.5. Thermoelectric Power

Water withdrawals associated with the generation of thermoelectric power account for the greatest
percentage of US water withdrawals in any sector. The main demand for water within a thermoelectric
power plant is for condensing steam. Thermoelectric power generation typically converts the energy in a
fuel source (fossil, nuclear, or biomass) to steam and then uses the steam to drive a turbine-generator.
After the steam is exhausted from the turbine it is condensed and recycled for use in the production of
steam again. Since the condensate must be cooled as much as possible to reduce backpressure on the
turbine, this recycling of the steam is a critical process in the efficiency of the plant.

An opportunity for conduit hydropower exists within the water delivery infrastructure if a significant drop
occurs on either the supply or discharge canals. We are not aware of any existing projects in the US; the
two following examples are projects in Poland and Spain.

A.6. Desalination Plants

On average there are 50 to 75 significant desalination projects per year in the United States with an
average capacity of approximately 1 million gal per day.20 The majority of these new desalination plants
use reverse osmosis to separate water from dissolved salts through semi-permeable membranes under
high pressures. The residue of liquid water containing salt, still at high pressure, can be passed through a
turbine to recover part of the energy used for the initial compression. Desalination is among the most
energy-intensive processes in the water sector, with seawater reverse osmosis typically requiring
5–7 kWh of electricity per cubic meter of water produced (Al-Karaghouli & Kazmerski, 2013).

Energy recovery devices are now standard components of modern desalination plants, where they capture
the pressure energy remaining in the brine stream leaving reverse osmosis membranes. Rather than
allowing this high-pressure flow to be dissipated through throttling, devices such as pressure exchangers,
turbochargers, and small turbines transfer or convert the energy to assist in pressurizing the incoming
feedwater, significantly reducing overall power demand. Although the principle of recovering pressure
energy is similar to conduit hydropower, the recovered energy in desalination systems is typically used to
offset pumping energy within a closed hydraulic circuit rather than to generate electricity through a
turbine-generator set.

A.7. Wastewater Facilities

Municipal and industrial wastewater facilities have the opportunity for conduit hydropower using the
treated water discharged back into a natural waterway. There is discharge infrastructure available, and
therefore the hydropower potential is essentially the same at a municipal wastewater facility as it is at an
industrial wastewater facility. The opportunity will only exist where the receiving waterway is at a
significantly lower elevation than the discharge point.

Two examples of conduit hydropower on municipal wastewater treatment facilities in the US are Deer
Island in Massachusetts (2 MW) and Point Loma in California (1,350 kW), both discharge to the ocean.
International examples include Toronto, Canada (750 kW), Sydney, Australia (4.5 MW) and Yorkshire,
UK (180 kW).
