From Parasite to Therapy: Immunomodulatory Effects of a Trichuris trichiura Derived Recombinant Protein in Allergic Airway Disease
Authors: Jessica Cristiane da Conceição de Andrade, Eduardo Santos da Silva, Jennifer Emily Anunciação Sousa, Jade Liz Ferreira Mendes Souza, Carolina Melo Orrico‐Ferreira, Lorena Miranda Souza, Raphael Chagas Silva, Silvia Letícia Bispo dos Santos, Antônio Márcio Santana Fernandes, João Vitor Borges Rios, Paulo Emílio Oliveira Cruz, Vítor Lima Miranda Melo, Emília Maria Medeiros de Andrade Belitardo, Luís Gustavo Carvalho Pacheco, Carina Silva Pinheiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary• C4299 reduced airway Th2 cytokines and enhanced regulatory mediators in allergic mice.• C4299 expanded antigen‐responsive Tregs and shifted antibody responses towards IgG2a.

To the Editor,

Helminth‐derived molecules have attracted considerable interest as a potential source of therapeutic biomolecules for allergic diseases due to their ability in inducing regulatory immune responses and counterbalance type 2 inflammation [1, 2]. Helminths have co‐evolved with the human immune system and can promote immune tolerance through regulatory cytokines such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β and the expansion of regulatory T cells [3]. These properties have inspired the search for parasite‐derived molecules that could reproduce such immunomodulatory effects without infection.

Trichuris trichiura, a soil‐transmitted helminth infecting nearly 500 million people worldwide, represents a potential source of immunomodulatory proteins [2]. Proteomic and transcriptomic analyses of this parasite have identified several candidate molecules with regulatory properties [2, 4]. Among them, C4299 protein has been previously produced in its recombinant form and displayed the ability to induce IL‐10 production in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, suggesting tolerogenic potential [2]. However, its immunomodulatory activity in vivo has not yet been explored. In this study, we investigated the in vitro immunomodulatory effects of the recombinant C4299 on THP‐1‐derived macrophages, alongside its in vivo impact in a murine model of allergic airway inflammation induced by the mite Blomia tropicalis, a primary allergen source in tropical regions [5, 6, 7].

Stimulation with recombinant C4299 did not compromise THP‐1‐derived macrophages' viability and induced secretion of IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐10 and IFN‐γ (Figure 1A). The induction of IL‐10 was particularly notable given its central role in immune tolerance and allergen immunotherapy [3, 5, 8]. These findings demonstrate that C4299 is biologically active and capable of stimulating innate immune responses towards a regulatory profile, consistent with similar outcomes observed in our previous study with peripheral blood mononuclear cells from mite‐allergic individuals [2].

To evaluate its effects in vivo, A/J mice were sensitized and challenged with B. tropicalis extract and treated with the recombinant C4299, placebo (only phosphate‐buffered saline) or dexamethasone via subcutaneous injections [5, 6, 7]. In the lungs, C4299 significantly reduced Th2‐associated cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5 and IL‐13) and similarly decreased pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF and IL‐1β), while increasing IL‐10 and TGF‐β levels (Figure 1B). Cytokine levels were also quantified in BAL fluid, confirming the local immunomodulatory effects of C4299. Considering that regulatory T cells are central mediators of immune tolerance, we next examined splenic regulatory responses. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that splenocytes from C4299‐treated mice displayed increased frequencies of CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ regulatory T cells following restimulation with C4299 (Figure 1C). This increase reached levels comparable to those observed with dexamethasone (Figure 1C) and was more pronounced after restimulation with the recombinant protein than with B. tropicalis extract, suggesting an antigen‐responsive regulatory mechanism. Consistently, antibodies responses have been also selectively modulated. Treatment with C4299 significantly decreased IgG1 and increased IgG2a levels, resulting in an elevated IgG2a/IgG1 ratio, but IgE levels against Blomia tropicalis remained unchanged (Figure 1D). In fact, this antibody pattern is consistent with immune deviation away from Th2 responses and towards a Th1‐associated profile [3, 5, 8]. Importantly, C4299 appeared to induce a more selective immunoregulatory effect than dexamethasone. Similar immunomodulatory findings were achieved when splenocytes were restimulated with B. tropicalis extract. These findings indicate attenuation of local Th2 inflammation accompanied by enhancement of regulatory mediators in the airways, a pattern consistent with immune deviation sought in allergen immunotherapy [5, 8, 9]. Additional findings corroborate the regulatory immune response; analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid demonstrated that C4299 significantly reduced total leukocyte infiltration compared with placebo. Although reductions in eosinophil numbers and eosinophil peroxidase activity did not reach statistical significance, cytokine profiles revealed a clear immunomodulatory pattern. Splenocyte cultures restimulated with C4299 produced increased levels of IL‐10, TGF‐β and IFN‐γ while reducing IL‐1β, particularly in cells derived from C4299‐treated animals. Together, these results suggest that C4299 promotes systemic regulatory and Th1‐associated immune pathways [3, 5, 8, 9].

Collectively, these findings identify C4299 as a helminth‐derived recombinant protein with relevant anti‐allergic activity. In this model, C4299 reduced airway leukocyte infiltration, downregulated IL‐5 and IL‐13, increased IL‐10 and TGF‐β locally in the lungs, enhanced antigen‐responsive Foxp3+ regulatory T cells and shifted antibody responses towards IgG2a. The concomitant induction of IL‐10, TGF‐β, and IFN‐γ suggests immune rebalancing rather than nonspecific immunosuppression [1, 3, 5].

Although the lack of significant IgE reduction and the use of an acute murine model represent limitations, these results support C4299 as a promising candidate for the development of a helminth‐derived immunomodulatory biopharmaceutical or as a potential adjuvant for allergen‐specific immunotherapy strategies [2, 8].
