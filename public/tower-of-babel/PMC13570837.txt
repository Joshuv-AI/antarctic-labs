Characteristics and Future Perspectives of Available Electronic Health Record Databases in Japan
Authors: Yi Piao, Yuya Kimijima, Michihiko Nakamura, Nobutomo Matsui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global use of real-world data (RWD) for drug approval and post-marketing surveillance has increased significantly. During 2023 and 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration issued industry guidance, and the European Medicines Agency released real-world evidence framework reports to support regulatory decision making. In Japan, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency promotes the use of RWD, with collective efforts from the government and related industries. This report focuses on electronic health record (EHR) databases in Japan, aiming to (1) organize the characteristics of data generated from such databases, and (2) identify the challenges when using EHR databases for regulatory decision making. This report conducted an internet-based Google search using the keywords ‘electronic medical record database’ with the condition that the results include any of the keywords ‘Japan’ or ‘real-world data’. Online interviews were conducted with representatives from eight EHR database companies identified as being utilized in Japan, focusing on the characteristics of each EHR database, challenges in their utilization, and future perspectives. The characteristics and limitations of current EHR databases were summarized; they offer broad coverage in terms of patient backgrounds and easier access to clinical laboratory values but are unable to track patients who transfer between hospitals due to lack of data linkage, and there may be loss of information during data abstraction. Data collection methods were mostly retrospective. Critically, there is no overall standardization between the EHR databases. Currently, EHR studies are primarily observational to verify efficacy and safety in real clinical practice and are gradually being used for post-marketing surveillance to support regulatory decision making in Japan. Addressing operational, technical, and methodological challenges in using EHR databases for regulatory approval applications requires efforts from multiple stakeholders, including industry, academia, and government.

Key Points

Japanese electronic health record databases provide rich clinical data but face persistent challenges in linkage, standardization, and outcome validation.Their regulatory use remains largely limited to post-marketing surveillance, with variable levels of regulatory readiness.Advancing regulatory use will require coordinated efforts to strengthen validation frameworks, interoperability, and alignment with international standards.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a global increase in the use of real-world data (RWD) for drug approval applications and post-marketing evidence generation. RWD are data relating to patient health status and/or the delivery of health care routinely collected from a variety of sources [1]. In the United States, the construction and utilization of medical information databases such as electronic health records (EHR), claims data, and registry data has advanced significantly [1]. There are several instances where such data have been used for new drug applications. For example, palbociclib received an expanded indication for male breast cancer [2], and entrectinib was approved for ROS1 non-small cell lung cancer [3] using RWD to complement clinical trial data and as an external control.

To utilize RWD as supporting information for regulatory decision making, it is essential to ensure data quality, specifically its completeness, consistency, and accuracy [1]. Discussions on data quality have been grounded in data used in actual applications, and in 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued industry guidance [4]. In 2023, the European Medicines Agency published a real-world evidence (RWE) [1] framework to support European Union (EU) regulatory decision making [5]. This report provided recommendations to address identified opportunities and challenges, such as further efforts to make relevant information available on data sources, data quality, and methodological aspects to support regulatory decision making.

In Japan, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) is promoting the use of RWD. The PMDA has issued guidance on using registries in new drug applications [6], covering reliability, quality management, and EHR data use. Additionally, a recent article outlines the PMDA’s perspective on using RWD/RWE as external controls for new drug approvals in Japan, aiming to promote appropriate drug development based on RWD/RWE and to facilitate proper discussion about RWD/RWE utilization with the PMDA [7]. To date, eight new drug applications have utilized RWD, and several post-marketing database surveillance studies have been conducted [7]. Meanwhile, the PMDA has also published its perspective on the regulatory use of RWD/RWE, including the assessment of drug safety risks and the impact of regulatory actions at the post-marketing stage [8]. As the importance of RWD for regulatory decision making gains attention, examples of RWD utilization in Japan’s post-marketing surveillance (PMS) include several database PMS studies as of April 20, 2025 [9]. PMS is Japan’s own regulatory post-marketing research, crucial for post-market drug safety monitoring.

The 2022 Basic Policy on Economic and Fiscal Management proposed initiatives like a national medical information platform and EHR standardization. To support this, the government launched the Medical DX Promotion Headquarters to advance secondary use of medical data. However, most RWD are not yet collected with regulatory use in mind, except for some registries like SCRUM-Japan. A paper published by the AMED Shibata Group proposes adaptive monitoring for existing registries [10]. This method combines minimal routine monitoring with additional checks once regulatory use is planned. EHR databases, which enable source data monitoring, are well-suited for this approach [11]. Therefore, this report focuses on EHR databases. However, outside Japan, EHR use in clinical trials has sometimes been rejected due to data insufficiency, disease misclassification, and confounding factors [12, 13].

This review aims to organize and describe the characteristics of currently available EHR databases in Japan from several key perspectives relevant to regulatory use, including data completeness, interoperability, linkage capabilities, and regulatory readiness. By adopting these perspectives as an organizing framework, the manuscript seeks to provide a structured overview of the Japanese EHR data landscape and to identify common challenges and future directions, rather than to assess the relative merits of individual databases.

Research Methodology

An internet-based Google search was conducted to identify eligible EHR data sources using the keywords as inclusion criteria (1) ‘electronic medical record database’, limited to results containing either (2) ‘Japan’ or (3) ‘real-world data’. No time restrictions were applied. Databases identified through this process were subsequently screened for relevance. In addition, databases were excluded if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (1) the database was not an EHR database; (2) the number of contracted hospitals was not disclosed as of April 2025, when the search was conducted; (3) the disease area covered by the database was limited; or (4) qualitative interviews could not be conducted.

As shown in Fig. 1, 347 websites were identified through this search; among these, 13 EHR databases currently utilized in Japan were identified. In accordance with the exclusion criteria, five EHR databases were excluded. The remaining eight EHR databases were selected after confirming their utilization in Japan and obtaining agreement to participate in the study. Among the identified databases, those that agreed to participate were queried regarding their status, challenges, and service roadmap. Online interviews were conducted with representatives from eight EHR database vendors that met the criteria. The interviews were conducted in an online format by three researchers using the following standardized set of five predefined questions: (1) What are the key characteristics of your database? (2) Is your database intended to be regulatory grade or non-regulatory grade? (3) What initiatives are you undertaking to support regulatory submissions? (4) What challenges do you face in aiming for regulatory use? (5) What future plans do you have to address these challenges? Information obtained from the interviews was descriptively summarized, rather than quantitatively analyzed, reflecting the exploratory nature of this review. To enhance the reproducibility and reliability of the findings, the summarized interview content and corresponding database descriptions were independently reviewed and discussed among the authors to ensure consistency in interpretation. In addition, to confirm the factual accuracy of the collected information, the summarized descriptions of each database were reviewed and verified by the respective database vendors. Characteristics and Limitations of Current EHR Databases in Japan

Kawakami summarized the categories and characteristics of real-world EHR databases in Japan [14]. Compared with other data sources, EHR databases have a fundamental advantage of broad coverage in terms of patient age groups, disease areas, and disease activity, as well as easier access to clinical laboratory values. However, limitations exist, such as the inability to track patients transferring between hospitals due to a lack of data linkage between hospitals, difficulty merging data from multiple facilities due to a lack of standardization, and loss of information during data abstraction. This is because electronic medical records contain a significant amount of unstructured data in the findings section, making it difficult to extract uniform data. Furthermore, the absence of database linkage means patient data cannot be traced, especially when patients transfer between hospitals during ongoing treatment.

To provide a structured overview of Japanese EHR databases, including eight EHR databases in Japan that have been actively utilized as of October 2024, we organized the databases along several key dimensions. Table 1 provides a structured overview of key database characteristics to support systematic understanding, rather than a direct comparative evaluation. Cross-cutting topics such as standardization, data quality management, and outcome validation are addressed in the main text for clarity and context. These include basic database characteristics (such as operating organization and data collection methods), the scope of clinical information captured, and technical capabilities relevant to RWD generation, including longitudinal capture of care, data linkage, and handling of unstructured data. For the data collection items under ‘Relevance of the Data Source’, detailed clinical laboratory values and patient background information were available across all databases. Further details for some databases are available at the website of the Japanese Society for Pharmacoepidemiology [15]. Within EHR databases, there are two types of data: structured, which follows a predetermined input format, and unstructured, which does not. An example of unstructured data is free-text clinical notes written by physicians, which cannot be uniformly analyzed and thus require new analytical methods. In addition to information obtained from published sources, limited expert interviews were conducted to support consistent interpretation of database characteristics. These interviews were used solely to clarify publicly available information and to contextualize technical features across databases. CyberOncology

CyberOncology is operated by PRiME-R, with affiliated hospitals primarily being university and cancer center hospitals, making the database strong in the oncology field [16]. The data extraction method involves linking the database system to the EHRs of affiliated facilities and supporting input into the EHR databases. The system allows standardized medical information to be selected and entered from a list using a pull-down menu format, structuring and accumulating the data. This enables the construction of a structured database and the utilization of RWD in medical institutions [17]. As of January 2026, data linkage is not available, but Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW)-defined hospital-based cancer registry data and Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) data are captured. Participating hospitals are beginning to apply the technology to process unstructured data such as pathology and radiology reports automatically. They have a track record of implementing abstractors and outcome validation. The output data are currently being adapted to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) [18].

Millennial Medical Record

Millennial Medical Record is operated by Life Data Initiative (LDI) and NTT Data. Under the Next-Generation Medical Infrastructure Act,1 LDI is certified as an authorized anonymized medical information creation entity [20]. LDI entrusts part of its operations to NTT Data, which is certified as an ‘Authorized Medical Information Handling Entrusted Entity’ by the government. They collect, accumulate, process, and provide medical information. In addition to the traditional use of medical information, they are one of the first in Japan to work on structuring text information from reports and utilizing it for clinical research through required AI approaches (natural language processing). The database contains EHR-derived information, including laboratory values necessary for outcome research measuring the effects of drugs and treatments and PMS analyzing adverse effects, in addition to claims data and DPC data. However, to date, there is no track record of utilizing the data for PMS or regulatory submissions. The standard data model for output data is Medical Markup Language.

Flatiron Health

The Flatiron Health Japan Research Database is an EHR-based oncology RWD platform operated by Flatiron Health K.K. [21]. It extracts structured and unstructured information from the EHR databases and departmental systems of medical institutions, standardizes and abstracts the data, and constructs a database. Originally established with a focus on providing US nationwide oncology RWD, which have supported multiple regulatory approvals, Flatiron Health now provides global RWD across the US, UK, Germany, and Japan; however, the scope of this manuscript is limited to the situation in Japan. Its site network primarily includes large medical institutions such as cancer centers and university hospitals. As of January 2025, its network consisted of five participating medical institutions, and it plans to expand to approximately 10 sites by the end of 2026. Leveraging rigorous data processing methodologies established by its parent company, Flatiron Health, Inc., and adapting these to meet the local legal, ethical, and clinical context, Flatiron Health K.K. processes both structured and unstructured data from the EHR databases and related department systems of each medical institution. This is achieved using harmonization and technology-enabled abstraction approaches. It then curates the data according to predefined data models, providing standardized RWD that is harmonized across countries. For unstructured data, including pathology and radiology reports, physicians’ clinical notes, progress notes, and scanned documents such as referral letters from other hospitals, the database employs specialized software to perform abstraction processing according to predefined procedures [22]. Additionally, in accordance with Flatiron Health’s Data Quality Framework, continuous multistage quality control and validation are conducted [23].

Real World Data (RWD)

The RWD database is operated by Real World Data Co., Ltd. and JMDC Inc., in collaboration with the Health, Clinic, and Education Information Evaluation Institute, which anonymizes and aggregates data collected from medical institutions and local governments. The database covers a wide range of healthcare provider types, from primary care clinics to hospitals with over 1000 beds and collects data from both acute and chronic care hospitals [24]. In addition to the existing database, they have recently initiated efforts for PMS with supplementary data collection. This involves developing a system that integrates with EHR databases for PMS targeting patients at the time of their visits, to augment the accumulated data from routine clinical practice (Hybrid PMS) [25]. While the existing database had limitations in available outcomes, the Hybrid PMS enables additional outcome collection. Subsequently, consultations were held with regulatory authorities regarding database use, database reliability investigations, and epidemiological surveys.

Japan Medical Data Survey (JAMDAS)

JAMDAS is a cloud-based EHR RWD platform independently developed by M3 [26]. Its primary partners are clinics, and it covers a wide range of disease areas. The data collection approach involves processing and extracting data in real time from each clinic using a standardized cloud-based EHR database and seeks to ensure that no personal information is included. One of its key features is that it is the largest cloud-based EHR database in Japan, offering excellent timeliness, with the ability to extract data as recent as the previous day’s from participating sites. Additionally, it can extract unstructured data through text mining. Utilization in PMS or regulatory decision making is currently under consideration.

TXP-Data Warehouse (DWH)

The Next Stage Series is managed by TXP Medical, aggregating comprehensive data from core hospitals across Japanese regions. It includes detailed structured data from emergency departments and intensive care units (ICUs), alongside extensive medical records and laboratory data encompassing over 900 items from all hospital departments [27]. This robust dataset is made available to companies for statistical analysis. For emergency departments and ICUs, the database provides structured data on comorbidities, regular medications, activities of daily living indicators, social backgrounds, minute-by-minute vital signs and intervention records, and clinical diagnoses, which often differ from claim-based disease names. By integrating this information with laboratory data, the platform facilitates in-depth analysis of complex acute conditions, including ICU-specific diseases. Additionally, TXP Medical is advancing the automation of registry entries using large language models. This innovation would enable the extraction and structuring of data from unstructured medical record entries, significantly enhancing registry accuracy and efficiency.

MID-NET®

MID-NET® is managed and operated by the PMDA with the cooperation of the participating medical institutions to enhance safety measures. MID-NET® has introduced a mechanism to control and assure quality and is managed and operated in accordance with the quality management plan. Currently, more than 30 hospitals across nine locations nationwide are participating. Outcome validation using this database is progressing, with 14 outcome definitions validated so far. Additionally, one of the features of MID-NET® is its ability to be used in conjunction with the National Hospital Organization’s Clinical Data Aggregation platform, which is operated by the National Hospital Organization [28].

Japan Medical Association Medical Information Management Organization (J-MIMO)

The J-MIMO database is operated by J-MIMO under the Next Generation Medical Infrastructure Act [29]. J-MIMO uniquely collects data in cooperation with national regional medical associations and national regional medical coordination councils, gathering electronic medical record data from hospitals nationwide and integrating it with nursing care data and health checkup data. J-MIMO has several key focus areas for database enhancement. Soon, it will strengthen collection of unstructured and image data, apply AI technology to analyze free-text data, and link to disease registries and national databases such as the National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan. Its mission is to contribute to improving patient and resident health through collaboration with hospitals, research institutes, and local governments on data utilization.

From the survey results shown in Table 1, it was found that each vendor’s EHR has unique characteristics. Data collection methods incorporated not only retrospective but also prospective data collection. Only databases certified under the Next Generation Medical Infrastructure Act are linked to public databases such as the National Database and Nursing Care Database. J-MIMO and Millennial Medical Record have such certifications, and they already have experience with multiple outcome validations. Regarding the format of output data, it was confirmed that multiple standards are used among EHR databases in Japan, with no overall standardization.

Challenges for the Future Regulatory Use of RWD and Recommendations from Working Group 1 (RWD/RWE), PhRMA Japan Medical Affairs Committee

From the perspective of PhRMA Working Group 1, this section outlines recommendations that address system-wide challenges and recommendations in Japan, rather than database-specific issues.

Globally, regulatory authorities are accelerating initiatives to utilize EHR data for regulatory decision making. In Japan, based on the Medical DX Reiwa Vision 2030, the MHLW is advancing the development of a nationwide medical information platform [30]. Under this initiative, an EHR information-sharing service is being established to enable the standardized exchange of three types of clinical documents and six categories of information using the international interoperability standard HL7 FHIR. Importantly, this platform is expected to support not only primary clinical use but also secondary use—such as policy development and pharmaceutical research—within a dedicated secondary-use infrastructure.

Concurrently, a Cabinet Office-led study group on the Promotion of Medical Information Utilization has been driving institutional discussions, including regulatory and legal reforms, to position secondary use of medical information as an explicit policy objective. These discussions have actively referenced the European Health Data Space (EHDS) as a leading international regulatory framework for health data utilization [31].

The EHDS aims to establish a common foundation and harmonized rules for the secure and efficient use of health data across the EU. Core principles were articulated in the Towards the European Health Data Space 1 (TEHDAS1) framework [32], followed by the development of concrete governance models, guidelines, and technical specifications through multiple pilot initiatives under TEHDAS2 [33]. Among these, the Data Analysis and Real-World Interrogation Network (DARWIN EU)—led by the European Medicines Agency—represents a landmark regulatory use case [34]. DARWIN EU was established to deliver high-quality, secure RWE to support regulatory decision making on medicines. It enables rapid federated analyses across the EU through harmonization of participating data sources using the OMOP CDM.

These international developments provide important reference models for Japan. However, translating such frameworks into effective regulatory use of RWD in Japan requires addressing several structural and methodological challenges. The PhRMA Japan Medical Affairs Committee Working Group 1 (RWD/RWE) has identified key issues and corresponding recommendations, which are summarized below.

Enhancing Reliability Through Strengthening Computable Phenotypes (Outcome Validation)

Outcome validation verifies whether patient outcomes, defined by specific criteria, accurately reflect clinical reality, typically through medical record review [1]. In Japan, such validation is rare, with MID-NET® being a notable exception. However, revisions to the Next Generation Medical Infrastructure Act now permit verification using pseudonymized medical information, enabling regulatory-grade data accuracy [35]. The Act’s guidelines require outcome data from at least one million individuals for certification, promoting the availability of large-scale EHR databases. This is expected to facilitate broader outcome validation. Nevertheless, interviews with database vendors reveal that validation frameworks are not yet in place, and outcome data are not being systematically collected. Since pharmaceutical companies typically conduct outcome validation on a project basis, and medical institutions see limited benefit, routine validation remains difficult. A sustainable system offering clear incentives to institutions is needed, supported by collaboration among industry, government, and academia. The MHLW and PMDA’s Consortium for Promotion of Medical Information Database discussed such efforts, including public sharing of validation results via platforms like the Outcome Definition Repository, leveraging the utilization of overseas validation methods, and using AI to streamline validation processes [31]. Other measures discussed included leveraging outcome validation results from overseas and utilizing AI abstraction to improve the efficiency of implementation [4, 36].

Integrated Analysis of Multiple Data Sources Via Distributed Data Networks

In utilizing RWD, a distributed and federated approach that appropriately links multiple data sources is crucial for building a large-scale database to enhance generalizability. However, when considering the linkage (comparison and integration) of data sources, challenges related to privacy protection and data standardization cannot be avoided. To address these challenges, several countries have implemented and achieved results with a system called ‘Federation’ for distributed data linkage and utilization.

Elkordy et al. defined federated analytics as “a privacy-preserving framework for computing data analytics over multiple remote parties (e.g., mobile devices) or siloed institutional entities (e.g., hospitals, banks) without sharing the data among parties” [37]. While federated analytics excels in privacy protection, it is crucial that each data source is maintained in accordance with common rules, referred to as the CDM. To work with RWD across multiple sources, data may need to be put into a common format, sometimes referred to as a CDM, with common representation (terminologies, vocabularies, coding schemes [1]), with well-known examples such as Sentinel and OMOP CDM. Currently, the data standards of various domestic data vendors are inconsistent and not aligned with international standards. This could become a challenge when linking databases for RWD utilization in the future. This is not solely a domestic issue: international discussion is required, and is ongoing in some cases, on the unification of data standards.

One successful case is DARWIN EU [4]. In DARWIN EU, each data source is converted to the OMOP CDM with the clear objective of supplying evidence to support regulatory decisions on medicines. The coordination center executes federated analysis in response to research questions defined by the authorities, with a clear division of roles, allowing multiple analyses and studies to be conducted effectively.

Abstracting Data from Unstructured Data Sources

There is a growing trend to convert unstructured data into structured data using the latest AI technologies such as natural language processing and machine learning for effective utilization. However, regulatory authorities have currently issued no clear rules regarding the use of such advanced technologies for data utilization, and it would be desirable for the government to issue guidance. This issue is in the process of being developed worldwide, and some organizations have touted the usefulness of using AI technology for RWD [38].

When viewed in comparison with international frameworks such as the EHDS and DARWIN EU, the challenges identified in Sects. 4.1–4.3 highlight several structural gaps specific to Japan. In international initiatives, outcome validation, data standardization using a CDM, and governance for secondary use are increasingly positioned as integral components of regulatory-ready data infrastructures. By contrast, in Japan, outcome validation using EHR data remains limited to a small number of initiatives, data standardization and computable phenotype development vary across databases, and the implementation of distributed data networks suitable for large-scale regulatory analyses is still at an early stage. Similarly, while international frameworks are actively defining regulatory expectations and governance models for the secondary use of unstructured data and advanced analytics, including natural language processing, corresponding guidance and standardized practices in Japan are still evolving.

These differences underscore the need for coordinated, cross-stakeholder efforts in Japan to align ongoing national initiatives with international practices, particularly in strengthening outcome validation frameworks, promoting interoperability through data standardization, and clarifying regulatory pathways for the secondary use of EHR-based real-world data.

Conclusion

This report identified several available EHR databases in Japan and summarized their strengths and limitations. Currently, EHR databases are primarily used for PMS to support regulatory decision making. To address the operational, technical, and methodological challenges to enhance the quality of evidence generated and the consistency of regulatory decision making requires effort from multiple stakeholders including industry, academia, and government. Utilization of EHR databases is expected to contribute to faster development cycles, thereby reducing drug lag/drug loss.
