Application of health belief model (HBM) in health sciences and medical researches: systematic review on African and Asian countries
Authors: Sina Temesgen Tolera, Ebisa Zerihun, Gari Hunduma, Shiferaw Letta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Health Belief Model (HBM) is a widely used psychological framework for explaining and predicting individual’s health related attitudes, belief, and behaviors. Although the model has been applied across various fields of health and medical sciences, the extent of its global use, particularly across African and Asian countries has not been well documented.ObjectiveThis systematic review aimed to assess the application of HBM in health sciences and medical research conducted between 2010 and 2025 in Africa and Asian countries.MethodsThe review followed the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Searches were conducted using keywords “Health Belief Model” combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR) across major databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library and PsycINFO. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed research articles applying the HBM within African and Asian contexts.ResultFrom a total of 1,421 identified records, 77 studies met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 61% (n = 47) were conducted in Africa countries and 39% (n = 30) in Asian countries. Most studies (64.94%) employed cross-sectional quantitative design, while 12% used mixed-methods approaches. Random controlled trial account for 9.11% of studies, quasi-experimental design for 7%, and qualitative approaches (including focus groups and in-depth interviews) for 6%. Overall, 85.71% of the studies applied all core components of HBM. Fifteen studies examined COVID-19 vaccine acceptance, nine focused on HIV/AIDS testing, counseling and prevention, and eleven studies explored non-communicable diseases prevention. Additionally, six studies assessed women’s adherence to breast cancer screening, and four studies examined cervical cancer screening behaviours using HBM.ConclusionA current systematic review demonstrates that the HBM remain a valuable framework for understanding and influencing health behaviors across diverse settings. Its application in Africa and Asian research highlights its usefulness in guiding targeted interventions that address perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, and cues to action, ultimately contributing to improved health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28281-5.Keywords: Health belief model, Application, Systematic review, Public health, Africa, Asia

What is known about the topic

The health belief model (HBM) is a psychological framework used to explain and predict health-related behaviors by examining a person’s perceptions of health threats and the benefits of action. Its application involves evaluating an individual’s perceived susceptibility to a health threat, perceived severity of the illness, perceived benefits of taking action, and perceived barriers to action. It extensively known and employed by health sciences researchers as well health behavioral to explain why some people adopt healthy behaviors while others do not. It is well-known because it gives public health practitioners a framework for creating health education initiatives and interventions that target individual perceptions, including emphasizing how serious an illness is or how simple it is to adopt a better lifestyle. Also, the concept has been used to encourage activities like cancer screening, vaccines, and managing chronic diseases by addressing potential hurdles and highlighting advantages. Additionally, it can shed light on the reasons behind the success or failure of particular health efforts as well as how to improve the persuasiveness of health information.

What the current systematic review adds?

Over the past few decades, there has been a notable increase in research utilizing the HBM. So that the recent systematic evaluations indicate the remains a widely used basic framework for understanding and promoting health behaviors, with numerous ongoing studies applying it to diverse health interventions, including some that involve media campaigns. The HBM is a prominent theoretical paradigm for designing interventions related to illness prevention and health promotion. Additionally, it directs educational programs for the management of chronic conditions like diabetes, weight control, and medication adherence for cardiovascular patients. Many applications incorporate educational programs meant to increase knowledge and beliefs. For example, HBM-based education has been utilized in recent research to enhance healthy behaviors among pregnant women and breast self-examination habits among visually impaired women.

What the current review adds on policy and practical implication?

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is still a useful basic framework for comprehending and encouraging preventive health behaviors, but it works best when combined with other theories and used in particular social and cultural situations. Thus, Policy implications and practical concentrate on leveraging the HBM to build targeted communication strategies and overcome structural constraints.

Introduction

The health belief model (HBM) is a psychological paradigm used in social psychology to explain and forecast people’s potentially harmful attitudes, behaviors, and beliefs about their health [1]. The concept, which was created in the 1950s by social psychologists at the US Public Health Service, looks at how beliefs about one’s vulnerability to disease, the seriousness of illnesses, the advantages of preventive treatment, and obstacles to healthcare affect behavior. To comprehend and encourage participation in health-protective behaviors, the HBM is frequently utilized in health behavior research and public health initiatives [1, 2]. Additionally, it combines ideas that are related to the trans theoretical model, such as self-efficacy, or confidence in one’s capacity to act, and highlights the role that stimuli or cues to action, such health campaigns or medical advice, have in encouraging behavior change [3].

The HBM, one of the earliest theories of health behavior [3], was created in the 1950s at the U.S. Public Health Service by social psychologists Howard et al. [4].Despite mobile X-ray cars visiting communities, few people were being screened for tuberculosis (TB), which alarmed researchers and medical professionals at the time [5]. The model has been used more recently to understand intentions to vaccinate [6] preventive measures to stop the spread of COVID-19 at a social gathering [7], responses to disease symptoms [1], compliance with medical regimens [8], lifestyle behaviors, and behaviors related to chronic illnesses, which may necessitate both initial behavior modification and long-term behavior maintenance [1].

The HBM theoretical constructs originate from theories in Cognitive Psychology [9]. In early twentieth century, cognitive theorists believed that reinforcements operated by affecting expectations rather than by affecting behavior straightly [10]. The expectation is that a certain health action could prevents the condition for which people consider they might be at risk [9]. The following constructs of the HBM are proposed to vary between individuals and predict engagement in health-related behaviors [1]. Under HMB, modifying variables in HBM are individual characteristics, including demographic, psychosocial, and structural variables, can affect perceptions (i.e., perceived seriousness, susceptibility, benefits, and barriers) of health-related behaviors. Demographic variables include age, sex, race, ethnicity, and education, among others [1, 8]. Among the psychosocial factors are peer and reference group pressure, social class, and personality. Among other things, structural variables include earlier exposure to the disease and awareness about it [3].

Health Belief Model (HBM) is adapted from Champion and Skinner [11] along Rosenstock [2] of Historical origins of the health belief model.

Perceived susceptibility, which is also referred to as perceived vulnerability Tajeri Moghadam et al. [12], refers to an individual’s belief in the possibility of getting infected with a disease or health condition [11]. and has both explanatory and predictive value [13].

Perceived severity is the seriousness with which an individual feels they might contract a disease or that they may suffer consequences if they don’t take any action [11]. Hence, according to Becker [13] for individuals to take action, they should believe that not taking action would lead to not only sickness but serious consequences. The subjective evaluation of the severity of a health issue and its possible repercussions is known as perceived severity [1, 8]. The combination of both perceived susceptibility and severity is referred to as perceived threat [11].This is because when one combines the likelihood of contracting a disease and the possibility that the infection could be serious, then this presents itself as a threat to the individual. Others may acknowledge the possibility that they could develop the illness, but believe it is unlikely [2].

Perceived benefit is the belief that actions taken by the individual are likely to lead to a reduction in the threat posed by the disease [11]. This refers to one feeling that by taking action the likelihood of getting infected with a disease will be reduced and that the seriousness of the infection will be reduced. The perceived advantages of acting also have an impact on the third category of health-related behaviors [8]. Perceived barriers refer to the negative feelings that may impede one from adopting the desired behavior [11]. for instance, cost related to the action one may need to take. Individuals weigh their actions and behaviors on a cost-benefit scale where consideration of danger, pain, and side effects among others are considered Cues to action refer to factors that may trigger a certain behavior or decision in an individual. They are reminders to individuals that they need to act in a certain way [11]. Cues to action can be classified as either internal or external [1, 8]. Examples of external cues that promote engagement in health-related activities include events or information from close relatives, the media, or medical professionals. Self-efficacy was not have been part of the early designs of HBM but was added later to account for an individual’s belief that their behavior will lead to expected outcomes [11].

Despite the widespread application of the HBM in health behavior research, existing evidence remains fragmented, particularly in African and Asian countries. This systematic review aimed to synthesize and critically evaluate how HBM constructs are operationalized in health sciences and medical research, their effectiveness in predicting health behaviors and their consistency across contexts.

Methods

Review protocol

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2021) Updated guidelines [14]. A predefined review protocol was developed prior to the study to ensure transparency and methodological rigor; however, it was not it was not prospectively registered in a public registry such as PROSPERO.

An adapted PICO framework guided the review:

Screening strategies

In the current systematic review, screening was conducted at title, abstract and full-text levels based on eligibility criteria including countries and publication years. It was carried out by a minimum of four independent reviewers to reduce bias. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. In case where agreement could not be reached, a fifth reviewer was consulted to resolve conflicts. Initial screening removes extraneous research.

Searching databases and strategies

The databases used in this review were PubMed, Global health, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and PsycINFO. These were selected to ensure comprehensive coverage of biomedical, public health, psychological and interdisciplinary literature relevant to HBM research, thereby minimizing the risk of missing relevant studies. Google Scholar and other regional indexing platforms were excluded due to limitations in search reproducibility, lack of standardized indexing, and a higher likelihood of retrieving non peer-reviewed, thereby ensuring methodological rigor and transparency.

The search strategy was developed by breaking the research question into key concepts, creating search terms (keywords and subject headings), and combining them using Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT). Core search terms included “health science”, “Health Belief Model,” “health behavior,” “public health,” “health promotion,” “health education,” and “health care,” combined with geographical terms such as “Africa” and “Asia,” including individual country names within these regions. A comprehensive search approach was employed, including database searching, truncation and wildcards, forward and backward citation tracking, and manual screening of reference lists to identify additional relevant studies. Pilot testing of the search strategies was conducted in PubMed and Scopus to evaluate effectiveness of search terms identify relevant synonyms and ensure retrieval of key studies and was refined to improve sensitivity and specificity before application across all databases.

Data extraction and information synthesizes

An extraction form made as a spreadsheet in Microsoft Excel Office version 2019 was used by the authors to extract data. The author names, year, continents/nation, participant-forwarded measurement actions, and quality evaluation were all included in this data. Information synthesis for a systematic review in this review involved merging the findings from evaluated research using either qualitative or quantitative methods to arrive at more general conclusions. This procedure, data after data extraction, entails actions like finding recurring themes, classifying related data, and applying techniques like thematic analysis for qualitative data or meta-analysis for quantitative data. As a result, the synthesis data is then displayed in tables, charts, or textual descriptions, frequently with a discussion of the general conclusions, constraints, and ramifications.

Inclusion and exclusion

The full text, abstracts, and gray papers from any medical, biological, or health science were used in conjunction with the health belief model. The years covered for the last fifteen years (2010–2025) to capture recent, methodologically robust evidence reflecting modern healthcare environments, including advances in digital health and the impact of emerging public health challenges. Every population that was examined for this study was included. This review did not use the full text, abstract, or grey documents from non-health sciences and medical or biomedical sciences along with the health belief model.

Data quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for observational studies [15] was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies. Nine criteria—1 = affirmative, 2 = no, 3 = unclear, and 4 = not applicable—were created for observational studies, including cross-sectional research. Then rate of records either high publication risk or low paper quality if it obtained fewer than five out of nine “yes” responses; five to seven responses are considered medium; and eight or nine responses are low risk.

Application of study designs and HBM components

In the current systematic review, fifty (64.94%) studies were applied cross sectional study methods through quantitative approaches alone for the application of HBM during their studies. Approximately nine (11.69%) of this research applied HBM using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Five (6.49%) research employed qualitative techniques including focus groups and in-depth interviews, seven (9.11%) used the random control trial method, and six used quasi-experimental techniques. Across studies, the sixty-six (85.71%) applied all HBM construct, indicating broad conceptual adoption.

However, analytical examination revealed variability in how these constructs were operationalized and their relative influence on health behaviors. Constructs such as perceived vulnerability, perceived severity, perceived benefits and self-efficacy were most consistently associated with positive health related behaviors and interventions outcome. The eleven (14.29%) of the studies were not explicitly identified the components of HBM (Table 2, SF3). On the other side, as compared other HBM components, about 12 studies didn’t include the cue to action during their assessment (Table 2).

Discussion

The current systematic review was to assess the application of Health Belief Model (HBM) in health sciences and medical researches across African and Asian countries. The two continents, Asia and Africa, were chosen because they have a lot in common when it comes to the advancement of health research, mainly because they are both developing economies with sizable populations and similar health issues. Furthermore, both areas are heavily burdened by non-communicable diseases including cancer, diabetes, and cardiovascular disorders as well as communicable diseases like HIV/AIDS and malaria. Health research agendas must be intricate and varied due to this dual burden. According to the systematic review, the HBM was frequently used in studies carried out in Asian and African countries. It found that researchers in these areas frequently use the HBM framework to explain and influence health behaviors such as COVID-19 vaccine acceptance; HIV/AIDS testing, counseling and prevention; and NCD and for other health related issues as the preventive strategies.

The current review found that the HBM is still a useful foundation for comprehending and encouraging healthy habits. By acknowledging the influence of social, economic, environmental, and other factors on health attitudes, incorporating social determinants of health into the model may provide a fuller understanding of how human behavior is evolving. This can be accomplished by modifying interventions in accordance with cultural values to increase their applicability and efficacy [93]. The previous studies found that delivering personalized health messages with modern technology could improve health promotion and prevention and increase the model’s applicability to modern lifestyle choices [94].

Based on HBM application, fifteen studies were found that HBM was used for the COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in which they found from Kenya [17], Ethiopia [35, 36], Sudan [57, 58], Ghana [60], Egypt [65, 68], Morocco [76, 79], Zambia [87], China [90] and India [91, 92]. According to these findings, citizens must accept the COVID-19 immunization in order to reduce and slow the virus’s spread. Despite the fact that the HBM is one of the most popular models for comprehending vaccination behavior against COVID-19 sickness, the domains of the HBM were not sufficiently exploited to investigate citizen acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine. The current systematic review consistent to the previous systematic review conducted in USA found that the HBM applied to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy [95]. On the other side, eight studies were used HBM for HIV/AIDS testing, counselling and prevention among social groups, which were accessed from Kenya [16, 18], Tanzania [43, 45], Nigeria [48], South Africa [54, 55] and Iran [63]. These studies almost proved that the HBM is a helpful framework for creating interventions to support HIV/AIDS testing, counseling, and prevention practices within social groups by recognizing and addressing significant psychological factors that influence health decisions.

Furthermore, twelve studies obtained from Ethiopia [27, 28, 34], Vietnam [29], Iran [38, 72], Kenya [41], Tanzania [42], Saudi Arabia [52], South Africa [56], Egypt [66], China [85] were aimed to use HBM for non-communicable diseases (NCD) and preventive though exercise such as weight management, hypertensive and type 2 diabetes management. According to these studies, using the HBM to prevent NCDs has shown that preventive practices are highly correlated with its basic elements. Although some research points out a drawback in the model’s emphasis on individual psychology over more general societal circumstances, the results emphasize the significance of individual beliefs in determining health activities.

Moreover, the six studies conducted in Iran [24], Egypt [26], Ethiopia [30], Somalia [53], Iran [71] and China [82] were used HBM for women information with breast cancer screening. Furthermore, the three studies conducted in Saudi Arabia [23], Ethiopia [31], Turkey [39] were used HBM for women information with cervical cancer screening. These studies also showed that the HBM is employed because it clarifies how an individual’s perceptions of risk, severity, rewards, and barriers affect their choice to get screened for cervical cancer. By addressing these particular attitudes and factors, HBM-based interventions can raise screening rates. The current review found that applying the health belief model to cervical cancer screening uptake commonly practiced has been seen among women [96].

The study conducted in Iran [19–21] focused on HBM application for female adolescents and pregnant women in social groups. The study conducted in Ethiopia [32, 33] were aimed about women those gave births for the last 12 months on perception on institutional delivery service utilization and birth community-based health insurance. The other studies obtained from Gambia [51] and Mali [74] were used HBM for the prevention of malaria among the children. Policy and practical implication of the review

Systematic reviews suggest policies should employ HBM to guide public health activities and communication. HBM constructs can be used by policymakers to create focused, successful disease preventive communication initiatives. The most commonly significant indicators across research are perceived benefits and self-efficacy. Policies should encourage interventions that highlight the advantages of a behavior change and increase confidence in people’s capacity to perform that behavior (e.g., self-efficacy programs). The HBM can help uncover cultural and demographic elements that influence health beliefs, enabling the formulation of culturally appropriate public policies and messaging. By concentrating money on program elements with the strongest impact evidence, decision-makers may allocate resources more effectively by knowing which HBM constructs have the greatest influence. The HBM alone may not fully explain all health behaviors. For a more thorough approach to changing health-related behavior, policymakers should support the integration of the HBM with other theoretical frameworks like Resource Theory or the Theory of Planned Behavior.

On the other hand, systematic reviews highlight a number of useful implications for program designers and practitioners. The HBM has been a useful and popular framework for creating interventions for the past few decades. Its six fundamental constructs can be used by practitioners to organize educational programs. Additionally, with a heavy emphasis on perceived advantages and self-efficacy, practitioners should create teaching materials that address the particular beliefs proven to be most important in systematic reviews. The concept has been used to promote adherence to numerous health activities, from medication use in chronic disease to cancer screenings. Reviews emphasize the necessity for academics and practitioners to clearly describe interventions based on HBM, enabling for improved identification of useful components and future replication of successful program. The model’s usefulness can vary by context. When applying HBM constructs, practitioners should take the particular disease or behavior into account.

Strengths and limitation

The current systematic review has certain strengths. Due to their shared status as emerging economies with sizable populations and similar health challenges, Africa and Asia share important similarities in the development of health research, which is why the current systematic review used a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases to ensure extensive coverage of existing literature. There were several pertinent articles from various sources. High-quality, relevant research was included since predetermined criteria were used during the selection process. The data extraction technique was independently performed by four reviewers, increasing the reliability of the returned information.

On the other sides, it has certain limitations: The HBM remains a widely used foundational framework in health research due to its effectiveness in predicting health-related behaviors, but current reviews highlight limitations and the need to integrate it with other theories and account for broader social and environmental factors. The review has certain limitations. The first is that the limited, although growing, data from systematic reviews does not fully support the effectiveness of the HBM as the basis for all health behavior interventions. The second conclusion is the therapies that concentrate on the four cores HBM constructs perceived vulnerability, severity, benefits, and self-efficacy tend to be more successful. The third problem is that some research highlights the model’s shortcomings, including its static nature and the requirement to incorporate environmental, social, and economic factors for better predictive potential.

Conclusion

The current systematic review concludes that the HBM is still a very helpful foundation for understanding and encouraging health research methods by Asian and African researchers. By acknowledging the influence of social, economic, environmental, and other factors on health attitudes, incorporating social determinants of health into the model may provide a fuller understanding of how human behavior is evolving. This can be accomplished by modifying interventions in accordance with cultural values to increase their applicability and efficacy. The current systematic review of health studies indicates that the HBM has significant implications for predicting and changing health habits. Important ramifications include using the idea to develop targeted health therapies that address each person’s perceptions of susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, and barriers to action, as well as using signals to action to promote behavior change. The implications for health research and intervention included predicting health behaviors, developing targeted interventions, improving health communication, adding other components, applying to a range of activities, and determining efficacy.
