Safety and Pharmacokinetics of Olamkicept Doses up to 2400 mg in Healthy Japanese Men: Phase I, Randomized, Placebo‐Controlled, and Double‐Blind Trial
Authors: Ryuzo Hanada, Katharina Bruzelius, Michael Falkenberg, Lars‐Erik Kyhl, Signe Møgelmose, Arjun Ravi, Philippe Pinton, Naomichi Yoneshima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe first‐in‐class, fully human fusion protein, olamkicept, selectively inhibits trans interleukin‐6 signaling. Previously, the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and immunogenicity of olamkicept following single ascending doses (SAD) up to 750 mg and multiple ascending doses (MAD) up to 600 mg daily were assessed. To assess higher doses, parallel trials of olamkicept up to 2400 mg daily were separately performed in healthy European and Japanese adult men. In this phase I, single‐center, within‐group randomized, double‐blind trial, Japanese participants were randomized to SAD olamkicept infusions (1200, 1800, and 2400 mg) or placebo. The primary objective was to assess the safety of higher doses of olamkicept based on treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), vital signs, electrocardiograms (ECG), and clinical chemistry. PK parameters were included as secondary outcomes. Immunogenicity was not assessed in the present Japanese cohort. Twenty‐four participants were randomized. Four TEAEs and no adverse drug reactions were reported. All TEAEs were mild and resolved without intervention. There were no treatment or dose related trends in TEAEs, vital signs, ECG, clinical chemistry, hematology, hemostasis, or urinalysis. A single infusion of olamkicept achieved measurable serum concentrations for up to 28 days after treatment and exposure was dose proportional. In conclusion, single doses of olamkicept up to 2400 mg have a favorable safety profile and dose proportional PK in healthy Japanese men. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), which include Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are characterized by chronic intestinal inflammation resulting from abnormal immune responses to intestinal microorganisms [1]. It is possible that a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental influences, including diet, infection, or exposure to chemicals/toxins, induce adverse changes in the intestinal microbiome promoting a pro‐inflammatory environment [1, 2]. In Japan between 1990 and 2021, Global Burden of Disease study data indicated that the age‐adjusted prevalence of IBD increased from 26.5 to 28.4 per 100,000 people [3]. In 2021 globally, IBD affected > 3,800,000 people, with > 375,000 new cases, > 42,000 deaths, and > 1,500,000 years of healthy life lost [4]. In Japan in 2021, the age‐adjusted incidence (1.9 and 2.0 per 100,000) and prevalence (28.6 and 28.2 per 100,000) of IBD were similar in women and men, respectively [3]. While the introduction of novel, targeted immunomodulatory treatments has broadened the therapeutic options available to IBD patients, limited efficacy, loss of response to treatment, and adverse effects arising from chronic immunomodulation highlight an ongoing unmet medical need [5]. There is therefore a requirement for identifying new mechanisms for therapeutic targeting.

Interleukin 6 (IL‐6) is a cytokine that has been validated as a therapeutic target in several inflammatory diseases [6]. It signals via two main pathways, namely, classic (cis) and trans. Classic IL‐6 signaling involves IL‐6 binding to the cell membrane‐bound IL‐6 receptor (mIL‐6R), expressed by the majority of lymphocytes, hepatocytes, and osteoclasts [7]. Binding of IL‐6 to mIL‐6R recruits transmembrane glycoprotein 130 (gp130). The resulting hexameric complex can activate the following intracellular signaling pathways: signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K) [8]. The classic IL‐6 signaling pathway has been associated with an anti‐inflammatory phenotype in T cells and promotes intestinal regeneration through trophic effects on epithelial cells [7, 9, 10]. In trans IL‐6 signaling, complexes of the IL‐6 and soluble IL‐6 receptor (sIL‐6R) bind to ubiquitously expressed membrane‐bound gp130 [7]. Trans IL‐6 signaling may induce pro‐inflammatory immune responses and has been associated with the development of autoimmune disorders [7]. Selective blockade of IL‐6 trans‐signaling neutralizes pro‐inflammatory IL‐6/sIL‐6R activity while preserving cis signaling through mIL‐6R. Thus, widespread inflammatory responses are decreased without impairing the anti‐inflammatory and regenerative functions of the cis pathway [11].

Olamkicept is a first‐in‐class, fully human fusion protein that binds to and neutralizes the IL‐6/sIL‐6R complex, thereby specifically inhibiting trans IL‐6 signaling [12, 13]. Phase I, first‐in‐human trials demonstrated that single intravenous doses of up to 750 mg and multiple doses up to 600 mg (administered once weekly for 4 weeks) were safe and well tolerated [14]. Olamkicept showed dose‐dependent PK with an elimination half‐life of approximately 5 days; accumulation was not observed during multiple dosing. The area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) and maximum observed serum concentration increased proportionally with increasing doses, while systemic clearance, terminal half‐life, and volume distribution during the terminal phase were dose independent and comparable among the 75 mg and higher dose groups [14]. Zhang et al. [12] examined olamkicept induction therapy at 300 and 600 mg in patients with UC. They found a mean steady‐state maximum serum concentration after the sixth 600 mg dose of 185.0 μg/mL, mean steady‐state volume of distribution of 14.8 L, and mean steady‐state clearance of 0.1 L/h [12]. Exposure to olamkicept was similar in men and women (unpublished data). An exploratory pharmacodynamic (PD) assay involving ex vivo stimulation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells indicated that STAT3 phosphorylation was fully abrogated at concentrations ≥ 1 μg/mL. PK modeling indicated that doses of 300–600 mg administered biweekly could maintain trough concentrations of ~1 μg/mL. In subsequent phase II trials involving patients with active IBD, promising evidence of efficacy was observed at the 600 mg dose level [15]. Exploratory exposure response analyses of the data from these trials revealed a trend to higher levels of efficacy with higher trough concentrations (unpublished data). However, modeling of exposure data suggested that trough concentrations of 1 μg/mL could not be maintained with 600 mg administered at a Q4w dosing interval (unpublished data). These analyses indicated the necessity to further explore the safety, tolerability, and PK of higher doses before embarking on subsequent efficacy trials. Dose response data was generated from an in silico mechanistic model of colonic inflammation yielding doses that were studied in the present trial: 1200, 1800, and 2400 mg [16].

Ethnic differences have the potential to impact the safety, PK, or dose requirements for therapeutic drugs [17]. Bridging studies are performed to establish whether therapeutic drugs demonstrate similar properties across different ethnic populations [18]. When evaluating new therapeutic drugs, Japan's Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency will consider bridging data from global studies, particularly if supported by a domestic Japanese bridging trial [19, 20]. Bridging studies are relevant for antibody‐based biologics, such as olamkicept, which generally show minimal ethnic variability in PK, safety, and immunogenicity [21, 22].

Therefore, parallel phase I studies were performed in both Western and Japanese populations to evaluate potential ethnic differences in the safety and PK of higher doses of olamkicept. This trial was performed in healthy Japanese men to address the need for local safety and PK data for olamkicept at dose levels beyond those currently evaluated in the first‐in‐human phase I trial [14] and to support the findings from the more recent phase I trial, which examined higher doses of olamkicept, both of which were performed in Western populations [23]. Single intravenous (IV) infusions of olamkicept 1200, 1800, and 2400 mg were tested based on preclinical studies, phase I and II trials, and modeling data [12, 14, 15, 24, 25]. Data from this trial will support future trials in IBD.

Methods

Study Design

This was a double‐blind, phase I, within‐group randomized trial investigating the safety and PK of olamkicept in healthy Japanese men (NCT06515834, Figure S1). The trial was performed at SOUSEIKAI Sumida Hospital in Tokyo. Before the start of the trial, the protocol and all participant materials were approved by an independent institutional review board. A Clinical Trial Notification was submitted with no clinical hold issued before the trial started. The conduct of the trial reflected the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) Good Clinical Practice guidelines and Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were advised that they could withdraw from the trial for any reason and were informed about potential risks and benefits. All participants provided written informed consent before the trial started.

Study Population

Due to the lack of non‐clinical data to support the selected dose levels in women, healthy Japanese men between the ages of 18 and 45 years with no history of chronic inflammatory disease were recruited. Key reasons for trial exclusion included any history of clinically significant medical conditions, alcohol abuse, or drug abuse. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are included in Table S1.

Randomization and Blinding

An electronic data capture system was used for randomization and treatment allocation. The trial investigators, associated trial team, and participants were blinded to treatment allocation. To maintain blinding, unblinded pharmacy staff prepared trial treatments for randomized participants using visually identical IV infusion bags.

Treatment

Olamkicept was reconstituted using 5 mL of water for injection and was made up to 250 mL using 0.9% saline solution. Participants in the placebo group were infused with 250 mL of 0.9% saline solution only. A single dose of olamkicept 1200, 1800, or 2400 mg or placebo was infused intravenously over 2 h.

Participants in each dose group were randomized to olamkicept or placebo in a 3:1 ratio. Sentinel dosing was performed for each dose group. The first two participants in each dose were randomized 1:1 to olamkicept or placebo, and safety and tolerability data were evaluated for 48 h before the remaining six participants in each group were randomized 5:1 to olamkicept or placebo to preserve the overall 3:1 ratio. The safety, tolerability, and exposure of olamkicept for each dose level were evaluated by an independent safety review committee (SRC). A minimum of 10 days of safety and tolerability data and 4 days of systemic exposure data were needed for any SRC decision to allow the next dose group to be treated.

Screening took place between 28 and 2 days prior to randomization. The trial included a residential stay from Days −1 to 3. Participants were randomized and olamkicept or placebo was administered on Day 1. Ambulatory visits were scheduled for Days 4, 5, 8, and 11, and follow‐up visits were scheduled for Days 15, 22, 29, and 36 ± 2.

Primary Endpoints

The primary objective of the trial was to assess the safety and tolerability of olamkicept after SAD IV infusions. The number, type, intensity, and causality of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were recorded (Figure S1). Change from baseline in vital signs, 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), hematology, clinical chemistry, hemostasis, and urinalysis was also examined (Figure S1).

Secondary PK Endpoints

Blood samples for the PK analysis were collected just prior to the olamkicept infusion and at 0.5, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 96 h and Days 8 and 11 after the start of the infusion (Figure S1). During the follow‐up period, blood samples were taken on Days 15, 22, and 29. The following PK parameters were investigated: area under the concentration‐time curve from dosing to infinity (AUCinf), area under the concentration‐time curve to last measurable concentration (AUClast), observed serum concentration at end of infusion (C eoi), baseline adjusted maximum observed concentration (C max), time of maximum observed concentration (t max), terminal half‐life (t ½), mean residence time (MRT), total clearance (CL), and apparent volume of distribution at steady‐state (V ss).

PK Analysis

Blood samples were analyzed by using a validated sandwich immunoassay with electrochemiluminescence detection (Meso Scale Discovery system) with a mouse anti‐gp130 capture antibody, a ruthenium‐tagged anti‐olamkicept F(ab)2 fragment as detection component, and a lower/upper limit of quantification of 0.375/1440 μg olamkicept/mL serum. The PK analysis was performed using non‐compartmental methods.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for data reporting. Data from placebo groups were pooled. All randomized participants who received one dose of the investigational medical product (IMP) were included in the full analysis set (FAS). IMP exposure, treatment compliance, and baseline characteristics (demographics, medical history, prior and concomitant medication, and physical examination) were described based on the FAS population. Participants in the FAS without major protocol deviations, such as significant noncompliance or other serious unforeseen deviations, and analyzed by actual treatment received were included in the per protocol (PP) analysis set. PK endpoints were described based on the PP analysis set. Safety descriptions were based on the safety analysis set (SAS), which included all treated participants, analyzed by the actual treatment they received. No formal sample size calculations were performed.

Results

Patient Disposition

A total of 146 potential participants were screened, of which 122 failed screening and 24 were randomized to treatment (Table S2). Screening failures reflected eligibility criteria not being met (52.7%), withdrawal by participant (4.1%), and other reasons (26.7%). All 24 randomized participants were exposed to olamkicept or placebo, and all completed the trial. The FAS, PP, and SAS populations were identical and included all randomized participants.

Baseline Characteristics

All participants were Japanese. Demographic and baseline characteristics were comparable between dose groups. The mean age was 24.8 years, and the mean BMI was 20.3 kg/m2 in the overall population (Table 1). Previous medical history reported by two participants, including one case of lower limb fracture and one case of meniscus injury, was not thought to be clinically relevant for their participation in the trial.

Prior and Concomitant Medication

No prior medications were reported. Concomitant medications, namely, analgesics and cough and cold preparations, were reported for one participant who received 1200 mg olamkicept.

Exposure and Treatment Compliance

Treatment compliance was 100%, as all participants received their scheduled single‐dose infusions of olamkicept or placebo.

Safety

There were no treatment‐ or dose‐related trends across dose groups. There were no serious or severe TEAEs. In total, four TEAEs were reported by three participants: three in the olamkicept 1200 mg group and one in the placebo group (Table 2; Table S3). No single TEAE was reported by > 1 participant. No adverse drug reactions (ADRs) were reported. All TEAEs were mild and resolved without intervention.

Clinical Laboratory Findings

Clinical chemistry, hematology, hemostasis, urinalysis, vital signs, ECG, and physical testing identified no treatment‐ or dose‐related shifts or changes from baseline. Occasional abnormal results were reported; however, none were considered clinically relevant.

PK

A single infusion of olamkicept achieved measurable serum concentrations for up to 28 days after treatment (Figure 1). Mean t max was 2.1 h and mean t ½ was 5.4–6.0 days (Table 3). Exposure to olamkicept, measured by C max or AUCinf, increased dose proportionally across 1200 and 2400 mg (Figure 2). The distribution and clearance of olamkicept were comparable across doses, with mean V ss ranging from 6.9–8.8 L and mean CL ranging from 1.8–2.1 L/day (Table 3).

Discussion

This phase I trial examined the safety, tolerability, and PK of single IV infusions of olamkicept administered at doses of 1200, 1800, or 2400 mg or placebo in healthy Japanese men. A previous phase I trial in Germany examined either single (0.75–750 mg) or four multiple doses (75–600 mg; once weekly) of olamkicept in healthy men and women and people with CD [14]. The safety and PK of higher SAD and MAD doses of olamkicept up to 2400 mg have also been examined in a more recent phase I trial in Germany [23]. Although cross‐trial comparisons are limited by potential differences in study designs and populations, the positive safety and PK profiles found in this trial are consistent with previous phase I trials [14, 23].

Four TEAEs, namely, ventricular tachycardia, diarrhea, COVID‐19, and headache, were reported in the current trial; all were mild in severity, and none were considered serious or related to treatment (Table S3). None of the TEAEs resulted in treatment discontinuation or death. There were no TEAEs or clinical laboratory findings that would typically reflect classic IL‐6 inhibition [26], such as gastrointestinal tract perforation, myelotoxicity, or hepatotoxicity. The safety profile of SAD olamkicept in the Japanese participants was comparable to the safety profile reported in the German phase I trial, in which four TEAEs, namely, diarrhea, jaw fracture, tonsilitis/nasopharyngitis, and infusion‐site hematoma, and one ADR (diarrhea) were reported [23]. Finally, there were no dose‐ or concentration‐related TEAEs, clinical laboratory results, vital signs, or physical examination findings in the Japanese trial participants, which is also consistent with the previous phase I trials [14, 23].

The PK of olamkicept demonstrated linearity over 1200–2400 mg, which was also observed in European men who received single doses of olamkicept [23]. The average C max and AUC were numerically greater in Japanese men compared to their EU counterparts (mean C max at 1200, 1800, and 2400 mg doses: 336.0, 613.2, and 890.8 vs. 340.5, 441.2, and 687.7; mean AUCinf at 1200, 1800, and 2400 mg doses: 587.7, 978.7, and 1335.6 vs. 522.1, 699.2, and 1016.7). It is possible that this observation could reflect a discrepancy in the weight of Japanese and European men (mean weight 58.7 kg and 79.8 kg, respectively), although it cannot be concluded based on the small sample size studied. With similar absolute clearance and distribution, a fixed dose results in higher exposure per kilogram of body weight. This pattern is typical for antibody‐based biologics and does not indicate intrinsic ethnic PK differences. Nonetheless, this finding and its potential clinical significance should be explored in larger efficacy studies that incorporate population PK analyses. Apart from C max and AUC, the other mean PK measurements were comparable between this study and the European study by Ignatenko et al. [23] (Table S4).

These data support the consistency of safety and PK data for olamkicept up to 2400 mg across ethnic groups. The German phase I trial, which included SAD and MAD dosing of olamkicept up to 2400 mg, demonstrated the safety, pharmacokinetics, lack of accumulation, and the favorable immunogenicity profile of olamkicept in a healthy population [23]. By providing a bridge between phase I data in both Western and Japanese populations, this trial supports the use of similar olamkicept dosing regimens in Japanese and Western patients.

The limitations of this trial include that it was a single‐dose study performed in a restricted number of healthy Japanese volunteers and that no immunogenicity or efficacy endpoints were included. The limited population size, while typical for phase I trials, may have restricted the statistical power to detect rare adverse events. Generalizability to the broader patient population may be limited by the inclusion of healthy participants as they do not reflect the state of disease, comorbidities, or concomitant medications present in the target patient population with IBD. This study was limited to healthy Japanese men. This approach is consistent with ethical and regulatory guidelines for early‐phase studies, where unknown risks at higher dose levels may warrant initial testing in men only. Future studies will include female participants once sufficient safety data are available.

Immunogenicity was not tested in this trial as the likelihood of detecting antibodies following single‐dose administration is low. The phase I trial in Germany identified treatment‐related olamkicept anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) in 22% of patients in the MAD cohort following treatment; however, no dose dependency was observed [23]. There is also no evidence that the development of ADA affects the PK profile of olamkicept [15]. Based on these data, while the likelihood of detecting clinically relevant immunogenicity in Japanese patients is low, it will still need to be confirmed by clinical trials. Therefore, further clinical trials are required to demonstrate the efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity of olamkicept in Japanese patients with IBD.

In conclusion, this trial in healthy Japanese men demonstrates that single doses of olamkicept up to 2400 mg have a positive safety and PK profile that is comparable to that observed in a Western population [23]. These findings provide an example of best practice in global drug development, demonstrating how parallel early‐phase studies can help accelerate access to innovative therapies across regions. Based on these positive phase I results, further human trials are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of higher doses of olamkicept up to 2400 mg.
