From Reflex to Reason: Does New-Onset Heart Failure Warrant a Routine Ischemic Evaluation?
Authors: Minju Han, Jeehoon Kang
Published: 2026

Ischemic heart disease remains one of the most important and potentially actionable causes of heart failure. For this reason, current heart failure guidelines support evaluation for possible ischemic heart disease when the result may clarify etiology and guide management, particularly when coronary artery disease is clinically suspected and the patient would be a candidate for ischemia resolution by revascularization.1,2,3) Of note, guidelines support selective, indication-driven testing, and do not mandate a reflexive coronary work-up for every patient with new heart failure. The clinical value of ischemic evaluation depends on a sequence of events: the patient must have a meaningful pre-test probability of actionable coronary artery disease, the test must identify disease accurately and safely, and the finding must translate into a treatment that the patient can tolerate and that improves an outcome.

In this issue of the International Journal of Heart Failure, the authors examine the association between early ischemic evaluation and long-term mortality in a nationwide Danish cohort of 61,475 patients with new-onset heart failure and no known coronary artery disease, prior revascularization, or acute myocardial infarction at presentation. Only 20% underwent ischemic evaluation within 90 days of heart failure diagnosis.4) Those who were evaluated were younger and had fewer comorbidities than those who were not, underscoring the degree to which real-world testing already reflects clinician selection. After inverse probability weighting, ischemic evaluation was associated with a modestly lower long-term mortality risk in the overall cohort (hazard ratio, 0.92). However, the association was heterogeneous, with more benefit in men than in women, in middle-aged patients than in the very young or very old. The benefit was absent among patients older than 85 years.

Interpreting this modest association requires attention to the study variable of interest in this analysis. The study compares patients according to whether an ischemic evaluation was performed, rather than according to what the evaluation revealed or the treatment it prompted. Whether obstructive coronary artery disease was found, whether the anatomy was appropriate for revascularization, and whether revascularization or intensified secondary prevention followed were not captured. The association therefore reflects the decision to test rather than any specific treatment that followed. The modest beneficial impact of this association could correspond to a small benefit in selected patients, along with residual confounding from the better health of those tested, or both. This is consistent with the broader evidence base. Randomized trials of revascularization, the principal therapeutic consequence of ischemic evaluation, have shown limited mortality benefit in comparable populations. Percutaneous revascularization added little to medical therapy in ischemic left ventricular dysfunction in REVIVED-BCIS2,5) and surgical revascularization in STICH improved survival only over 10 years of follow-up in a relatively young cohort.6,7) Trials of antithrombotic therapy in heart failure without another clear indication have not demonstrated a clear survival advantage for aspirin.8,9) Within this same Danish cohort, fewer than one in 5 evaluated patients underwent revascularization.10) Given this evidence, a large survival benefit from ischemic evaluation alone would be unlikely. The modest overall association and the nearly neutral estimates from the inverse-probability-weighted analysis are in line with these earlier findings.

The heterogeneity across subgroups should be interpreted with similar caution. The most apparent benefit shows in men and middle-aged patients, who have the highest prevalence of obstructive coronary artery disease. However, this benefit may reflect unmeasured differences between patients rather than the ischemia evaluation itself, leading to a smaller benefit in the sensitivity analyses. The neutral association in women is unlikely to indicate that ischemia is unimportant; women more often have non-obstructive coronary artery disease and coronary microvascular dysfunction, which are less readily identified by conventional angiography and less likely to be corrected by epicardial revascularization.11) In patients older than 85 years, competing non-cardiovascular mortality and frailty weigh heavily on prognosis, and defining the coronary anatomy will less often change the clinical course.12) The subgroup findings are therefore best viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as a definitive map of where testing does or does not help. Several features of the data warrant caution. As the authors acknowledge, administrative data cannot distinguish reduced from preserved ejection fraction, quantify angina or ischemic burden, or capture the frailty and renal function that influence both the decision to test and the outcome. The exclusion of patients who died within 90 days removes the earliest and highest-risk period after diagnosis. These limitations are inherent to registry-based research and temper how definitively the findings can be read.

Taken together, these findings suggest that routine, reflexive testing for ischemia applied uniformly to every patient with new-onset heart failure is unlikely to yield consistent benefit. These findings support the selective, individualized approach that current guidelines already recommend. In practice, the evaluation should begin before the test is ordered: How likely is this patient to have obstructive coronary artery disease?; is the phenotype heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, with mildly reduced ejection fraction, or with preserved ejection fraction?; do symptoms, electrocardiogram, cardiac biomarkers, or imaging studies point to ischemia?; could the patient undergo revascularization or secondary prevention?; and would the result change management in a way consistent with the patient’s goals? When the answers favor testing, ischemic evaluation would remain central. When they do not, routine testing risks becoming reflex rather than reason. A reasonable approach is to pursue ischemic evaluation not simply because heart failure is newly diagnosed, but because its result is likely to influence subsequent management.
