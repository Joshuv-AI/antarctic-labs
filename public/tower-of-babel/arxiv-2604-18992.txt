Estimating galactic foreground with the population of resolved galactic binaries
and Qing-Guo Huang

Abstract
The stochastic gravitational wave background in the mHz band is a key target for future spaceborne interferometers. Detecting such a signal presents multiple challenges for data processing, especially complicated by the presence of numerous compact binaries in our galaxy. The superposition of gravitational waves from their inspiral stages creates a confusion foreground that need to be estimated accurately. In this work, we derive the variation in the intensity of detector response to this foreground by analyzing the spatial distribution of binary systems. Subsequently, we search for an injected stochastic background using the modeled foreground within Taiji Data Challenge II. With some assumptions about the statistical properties of foreground, the results show that the approach of describing foreground based on the population properties of resolved Galactic binaries can yield preliminary feasible results.



1
Introduction

Space-based gravitational wave (GW) detectors are facilities relying on laser transmission and interferometry between
several spacecraft.
By operating in space, such observatories get rid of the seismic and gravity gradient noise that limits ground-based detectors.
The longer arm lengths and quieter environment open a unique observational window in the millihertz band.
Representative missions such as the
Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA)
[
4
,
6
]
, Taiji
[
14
,
25
]

and TianQin
[
24
]
are all scheduled for launch in the 2030s.
They will enable the detection of GWs from massive black hole binaries
[
22
]
,
extreme mass-ratio inspirals
[
11
]
, Galactic binaries (GBs)
[
20
]
and potentially primordial GWs
[
26
,
10
,
33
]
.

Among these diverse GW sources, compact binary stars are a particularly promising class.
Based on current observational knowledge, there are
\mathcal{O}(10^{7})
binary systems in the Milky Way and nearby galaxies,
which are expected to emit quasi-monochromatic GWs in frequency band of space detectors
[
29
]
.
A large fraction of these are double white dwarfs.
About
10^{4}
binaries are expected to be detectable as individual sources,
making them the most numerous GW sources in the space mission.
The remaining unresolved binaries collectively form a persistent stochastic GW signal.
While this is a scientifically valuable observation that reveals Galactic structure
[
12
]
,
it acts as another source of confusion noise, reducing detector sensitivity and hindering the detection of other GW sources.
Therefore, accurate modeling and estimation of the confusion foreground are essential.

A historical approach involves subtracting GBs with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) above a threshold iteratively
and then fitting the residual with an empirical analytical spectrum
[
31
,
16
]
.
This procedure relies on some idealizations unattainable in practice:
The resolved parameters of GBs are set to the true values which are unknown in reality.
The results of parameter estimation contain errors inevitably and

[
27
]
demonstrates that the confusion noise is reduced after taking the errors into account.
Additionally, modeling the foreground spectrum requires prior knowledge of the instrumental noise spectrum.
In the prototype global fitting analysis
[
23
]
,
the confusion foreground estimation is indeed performed independently of GB identification.
The power spectral density (PSD) of the combination of confusion foreground and instrumental noise is fitted using interpolation.
In fact, due to the anisotropic distribution of GBs and motion of space-based detectors,
the foreground exhibits a cyclostationary modulation of its amplitude.
The above methods focus on the frequency-domain spectrum,
little is known about this modulation effect in the time domain because of the stationary assumption adopted.
In contrast,
[
8
]
treats the foreground as an anisotropic stochastic GW signal and then
calculates the detector response based on a known structure of the Milky Way,
which is believed to be well constrained by the resolved GBs (RGBs)
[
2
]
.
In this work, we check and adopt this method for the first time.
Trying to determine the amplitude of foreground using the RGBs in Taiji Data Challenge (TDC) II simulation datasets
[
9
]
.
We analyze the characteristics of parameter distribution in this dataset
, describe the foreground as an anisotropic background, calculate the detector response,
and finally conduct a search for the injected isotropic stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB).

2
Foreground and GB population

The frequency evolution of GB is relatively slow and we use a simple waveform from
[
19
]
.
In the source frame, it is given by

h_{+}(t)=A(1+\cos^{2}\iota)\cos\left[\varphi(t)\right],\quad h_{\times}(t)=2A\cos\iota\sin\left[\varphi(t)\right].

(2.1)

where the phase takes a Taylor expansion:

\varphi(t)=2\pi\left(f_{0}t+\frac{1}{2}\dot{f}_{0}t^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\ddot{f}_{0}t^{3}\right)+\varphi_{0},\quad\ddot{f}_{0}=\frac{11}{3}\frac{\dot{f}_{0}^{2}}{f_{0}}.

(2.2)

In the subsequent discussion on the population distribution, we prefer to parameterize with chirp mass
\mathcal{M}
.
For systems evolving solely under gravity,

A=\frac{2(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}(\pi f_{0})^{2/3}}{c^{4}d_{L}},\quad\dot{f}_{0}=\frac{96\pi^{8/3}(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}f_{0}^{11/3}}{5c^{5}}.

(2.3)

Although the signal from unresolved GBs (UGBs) is so strong that it is termed a “foreground”,
it is intrinsically random and statistical methods should be adopted.
We will model this foreground with the convention of anisotropic SGWB,
whose quadratic expectation is

\langle h_{A}(f,\boldsymbol{n})h_{A^{\prime}}^{*}(f^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{4}S_{c}(f,\boldsymbol{n})\delta_{AA^{\prime}}\delta(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}).

(2.4)

This implies that the foreground is Gaussian, stationary and unpolarized.
It must be noted, however, that these assumptions are not strictly valid.
For example,
[
28
]
shows the presence of non-Gaussianity in residuals of global fitting pipeline
hinders accurate estimation of the SGWB.
The inspiral frequency of a GB evolves, increasing gradually for a system dominated by GW radiation.
However, for the space mission with lifetime of several years, stability may be a feasible assumption.
Therefore, we retain the above assumptions to simplify the data processing.
Besides, another commonly adopted assumption for anisotropic SGWB is that
the angular distribution of power is independent of frequency,
which means

S_{c}(f,\boldsymbol{n})=H(f)P(\boldsymbol{n}).

(2.5)

Since the foreground is a superposition of GWs from individual GBs.

S_{c}(f,\boldsymbol{n})
is determined by their population.
The above assumptions about SGWB force us to adopt a population distribution which can be decomposed as

p(\mathcal{M},f_{0})p(\boldsymbol{n},d_{L})p(\iota)p(\varphi_{0})p(\psi).

(2.6)

The independence between position and
\{\mathcal{M},f_{0}\}
follows from eq. (
2.5
).
Independence of
\{\iota,\,\varphi_{0},\,\psi\}
is due to both dataset simulation and physical reasons:
These parameters are usually not considered in a galactic dynamic simulation but drawn from hypothetical uniform distributions
with respect to
\cos\iota
,
\varphi_{0}
and
\psi
, which assure that the background is unpolarized and unrelated between different directions.
Replacing the total waveform with the sum of all GBs in eq. (
2.4
), we obtain

\langle h_{A}(f,\boldsymbol{n})h_{A^{\prime}}^{*}(f^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\sum_{i,j}h_{A,i}(f)h_{A^{\prime},j}^{*}(f^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}_{i})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}_{j})\rangle

(2.7)

Once treating GBs as independent samples drawn from eq. (
2.6
) and replacing the ensemble average with the expectation,
the cross correlation terms from
i\neq j
vanish. We show in appendix
A
that

\begin{gathered}P(\boldsymbol{n})=\int\mathrm{d}d_{L}\,\frac{p(\boldsymbol{n},d_{L})}{d_{L}^{2}},\\
H(f)=\frac{8N}{5}\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}\,\mathcal{A}^{2}(\mathcal{M},f)p(\mathcal{M},f),\end{gathered}

(2.8)

where
N
is the total number of GBs and
\mathcal{A}=Ad_{L}
.

4
Search for SGWB

We work with the
AET
TDI combinations. The correlation spectrum between two channels is

\langle\Delta_{I}(f)\Delta_{J}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\delta(f-f^{\prime})\left[N_{IJ}(f)+\Gamma_{IJ}(f)S_{h}(f)+\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{n}\,\gamma(f,\boldsymbol{n})S_{c}(f,\boldsymbol{n})\right],

(4.1)

where
N_{IJ}
denotes the instrumental noise, and
S_{h}(f)
is the injected isotropic SGWB.
The isotropic overlap function is

\Gamma_{IJ}(f)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{n}\,\gamma(f,\boldsymbol{n}).

(4.2)

We employ a pixel basis on the sphere using
healpy

[
34
,
13
]
.
The integral is discretized as a sum over the skymap pixels.

nside
of
18
is adopted in our work.

The angular distribution
P(\boldsymbol{n})
is fixed to previous result of RGB population analysis.
This is achieved by counting the number of sources in each pixel and averaging them with
d_{L}^{-2}
as the weight.
However, a problem arises here.
Since we do not introduce a prior model for the structure of Milky Way,
the sky map is obtained directly with the position of simulated GBs.
In some sky regions where the number of GBs is insufficient,
there is a significant deviation between sampling and statistical average.
Specifically, these directions may exhibit excessively bright noise.
Therefore, we have set a threshold of
5
GBs:
Sky regions containing fewer than this number of GBs are not be involved in the calculation of foreground.

H(f)
and the parameters in the isotropic SGWB spectrum will be inferred from the simulated data within a Bayesian framework.
Due to variations of armlength and orientation of detectors, the observed TDI variables are non-stationary.
Therefore, following
[

, we split the original data into segments. Each segments last for
5

assume the stability of statistical properties in each segment.
The likelihood is

\begin{gathered}\ln p(s|C)=-\sum_{i,f}\left[\mathrm{det}(C_{i}(f))+s_{i}^{\dagger}(f)C_{i}^{-1}(f)s_{i}(f)\right],\\
s_{i}(f)=\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}(f)&E_{i}(f)&T_{i}(f)\end{bmatrix}^{T},\end{gathered}

(4.3)

where
i
indexes the time segment and the components of covariance

are given by eq. (
4.1

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have searched for the SGWB amidst the confusion foreground generated by the superposition of
GWs from GBs.
Our method decomposes the foreground into two independent parts:
the spectrum in the frequency domain and the modulation effect in the time domain.
For the former, we employ a flexible interpolation and trans-dimension sampling to fit it.
For the latter, we first synthesize the positional information of binary star systems in the dataset
to derive their distribution characteristics,
and then use the response pattern of detectors to calculate this modulation effect.

Most unknown parameters can be recovered with a CL of
2\sigma
.
This error is not significant but can be perceived to some extent.
This is likely related to the assumptions we made when describing the foreground spectrum.
Some information is smoothed out during the process of transforming individual system parameters into statistical distributions.
When calculating the energy spectrum, we assume Gaussianity and independence among different sources.
In regions with a small number of sources, this assumption may not hold.
From an observational perspective,
the operational average of background PSD can only be achieve by averaging data taken at different times.
The expectation of a probability distribution, on the other hand, assumes a ensemble average across multiple galaxy systems,
and the two are not equivalent.
Therefore, deviations in the energy spectrum calculated from statistical properties are understandable.
Furthermore, due to selection effects of detection,
the distribution characteristics of the resolvable dataset may not even represent the distribution model of unknown sources accurately.

Nevertheless, the current approach may still retain practical value.
In a complete data analysis process, the results of parameter estimation of resolved GB systems also contain errors.
Hence, the spatial distribution model should also be accompanied by uncertainty,
and we need to compare this uncertainty with the detection capability of the detector to draw more accurate conclusions.
Then, we will consider whether it is really necessary to take the selection effect into account.
This requires us to further establish a process that combines GB identification with foreground estimation.

Appendix A
Spectrum of foreground

The method we use to relate the energy spectrum and population is taken from
[
5
]
,
which focuses on coalescing compact binaries.
We make several modifications to adapt it to continuous GB signals.
In section
2
, we have shown that

\langle h_{A}(f,\boldsymbol{n})h_{A^{\prime}}^{*}(f^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})\rangle\sim N\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}\mathrm{d}f_{0}\,p(\mathcal{M},f_{0})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\mathrm{d}d_{L}\,p(\boldsymbol{n}_{i},d_{L})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}_{i})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}_{i})\\
\int\mathrm{d}\iota\,p(\iota)\int\mathrm{d}\varphi_{0}\,p(\varphi_{0})\int\mathrm{d}\psi\,p(\psi)h_{A}(f)h_{A^{\prime}}^{*}(f^{\prime}).

(A.1)

Note that
h_{A}
on the right side represents the waveform of a single system
and depends on the integrated variables.
We have omitted most of index
i
from parameters for simplicity.

We can explicitly express the dependence of waveform on the polarization angle
\psi
as

\sum_{A}R_{AB}(2\psi)h_{B}(f).

(A.2)

That is, we redefine
h_{B}
to denote the particular waveform of
\psi=0
.
We will frequently use similar techniques in subsequent derivations to ease complex notations.
The matrix
R_{AB}
is

R_{AB}(2\psi)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos 2\psi&-\sin 2\psi\\
\sin 2\psi&\cos 2\psi\end{bmatrix}_{AB}=\delta_{AB}\cos 2\psi-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{AB}^{2}\sin 2\psi,

(A.3)

where
\sigma^{2}_{AB}
is the second Pauli matrix.
Marginalize over
\psi
gives

\frac{1}{2}\delta_{AA^{\prime}}\sum_{B}h_{B}(f)h_{B}^{*}(f^{\prime})+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sigma_{AA^{\prime}}^{2}\left[h_{+}(f)h_{\times}^{*}(f^{\prime})-h_{\times}(f)h_{+}^{*}(f^{\prime})\right]

(A.4)

Since we assume that the foreground is stationary, we neglect the derivations of initial frequency
and treat the GW signal as monochromatic. The waveform in frequency domain is

h_{+}(f)=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{d_{L}}\delta(f-f_{0})\frac{1+\cos^{2}\iota}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{0}},\quad h_{\times}(f)=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{d_{L}}\delta(f-f_{0})\cos\iota\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\varphi_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2})},

(A.5)

where we have ignored the negative frequencies.
Because waveform appeared in eq. (
A.4
) always couples with its complex conjugation,

\varphi_{0}
cancels out, and marginalizing over it leaves the result unchanged.

When marginalize over
\iota
, the term containing Pauli matrix in eq. (
A.4
) vanishes due to the integral of an odd function and
the first item yield a coefficient

\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}(\cos\iota)\,\left[\left(\frac{1+\cos^{2}\iota}{2}\right)^{2}+\cos^{2}\iota\right]=\frac{4}{5}.

(A.6)

Thus the integrand becomes

\frac{2}{5}\delta_{AA^{\prime}}\delta(f-f_{0})\delta(f^{\prime}-f_{0})\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}}{d_{L}}\right)^{2}.

(A.7)

Using

\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}_{i})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}_{i})=\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}_{i}),

(A.8)

and

\delta(f-f_{0})\delta(f^{\prime}-f_{0})=\delta(f-f^{\prime})\delta(f-f_{0}).

(A.9)

We complete the integration and the final result is

\frac{2}{5}\delta_{AA^{\prime}}\delta(f-f^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})\int\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}^{2}(\mathcal{M},f)p(\mathcal{M},f)\int\mathrm{d}d_{L}\,\frac{p(\boldsymbol{n},d_{L})}{d_{L}^{2}}.

(A.10)

Comparing with eq. (
2.4
) leads to eq. (
2.8
).
