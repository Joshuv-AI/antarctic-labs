Risk-Controlled Post-Processing of Decision Policies
Sunay Joshi, Tao Wang, Hamed Hassani, Edgar Dobriban

Abstract
Predictive models are often deployed through existing decision policies that stakeholders are reluctant to change unless a risk constraint requires intervention. We study risk-controlled post-processing: given a deterministic baseline policy, choose a new policy that maximizes agreement with the baseline subject to a chance constraint on a user-specified loss. At the population level, we show that the optimal policy has a threshold structure: it follows the baseline except on contexts where switching to the oracle fallback policy yields a large reduction in conditional violation risk. At the finite-sample level, given a fitted fallback policy and score, we develop a post-processing algorithm that uses calibration data to select a threshold. Leveraging tools from algorithmic stability and stochastic processes, we show that under regularity conditions, in the i.i.d. setting, the expected excess risk of the post-processed policy is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / n ) O(\log n/n) . In the special case when an exact-safe fallback policy is available, the algorithm achieves precise expected risk control under exchangeability. In this setting, we also give high-probability near-optimality guarantees on the post-processed policy. Experiments on a COVID-19 radiograph diagnosis task, an LLM routing problem, and a synthetic multiclass decision task show that targeted post-processing can meet or nearly meet risk budgets while preserving substantially more agreement with the baseline than score-blind random mixing.

Introduction

Machine learning systems are increasingly used not only to predict outcomes, but also to recommend actions. In many illustrations, however, the deployed action rule is not an unconstrained optimizer. It may be a legacy policy, a clinical workflow, a rule approved by a regulator, or a model whose behavior is already familiar to users. In such settings, it is natural to ask for a
post-processor
: a lightweight wrapper that changes the baseline action only when needed to satisfy a risk constraint.

This paper studies a simple but expressive version of this problem. We are given a deterministic baseline policy
\pi_{0}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
, a base loss
\ell(a,y)
measuring the consequence of taking action
a
when the outcome is
y
, a loss cutoff
c
, and a risk budget
\varepsilon
. The loss cutoff defines the violation event
\{\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c\}
, and the constraint requires the violation risk
\mathbb{P}(\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c)
to be at most the risk budget
\varepsilon
. The goal is to construct a policy
\pi
that agrees with
\pi_{0}
as often as possible while satisfying this chance constraint. Thus, the objective encodes fidelity to the existing policy, while the constraint encodes safety, reliability, or domain-specific acceptability.

We begin by demonstrating that the population problem admits an explicit oracle solution: the optimal policy switches from the baseline policy to a fallback policy exactly when the oracle score exceeds a threshold.
Since the oracle fallback policy and scores are unknown, we propose a
finite-sample conformalized post-processing algorithm (
Algorithm

1
). After plugging-in for the unknown conditional label distribution, a held-out calibration set is used to select a switching threshold.
In general, the violation loss can be
non-monotone, so we leverage tools from algorithmic stability and stochastic processes to prove an
O(\log n/n)
expected excess violation risk guarantee under regularity conditions.
When an exact-safe fallback policy is available,
monotonicity is restored, yielding precise expected risk control, assuming only data
exchangeability. Experiments on a COVID-19 radiograph diagnosis task, an LLM
thinking-mode routing problem, and a synthetic multiclass task show that the algorithm meets or
nearly meets the desired risk budgets while preserving more agreement with the
baseline than score-blind random mixing.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are as follows.

•

We formulate risk-controlled policy post-processing as an
agreement-maximization problem subject to a chance constraint.

•

We characterize the population-optimal policy, showing that it switches away from the baseline exactly on contexts with the largest oracle scores.

•

We propose a finite-sample algorithm (
Algorithm

1
) to post-process the baseline policy, switching from the baseline policy to a fitted fallback policy only on high-score contexts.

•

We analyze our algorithm in two regimes. With an
exact-safe fallback policy, we prove a finite-sample risk control guarantee under exchangeability,
and show near-optimal agreement with the baseline.
For a general fitted fallback, the violation loss is non-monotone, and under regularity conditions and i.i.d. sampling, we bound the excess risk by
O(\log n/n)
via rank stability. Our techniques may be of interest in other non-monotone calibration problems.

•

We evaluate the method on a COVID-19 radiograph diagnosis task, an LLM routing problem, and a synthetic multiclass task, demonstrating that the algorithm meets or nearly meets risk budgets while preserving more agreement with the baseline than score-blind random mixing.

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{P}(\pi(X)=\pi_{0}(X))\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{P}\big(\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c\big)\leq\varepsilon.

3.2
Population-level optimal policy

In this section, we solve the optimization problem in
Equation

1
. We begin with definitions. Define the
conditional violation risk function

g:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]

by

g(a,x):=\mathbb{P}\big(\ell(a,Y)\geq c\mid X=x\big)
for all
(a,x)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}
.
Note that the violation risk can be written as

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c\big)=\mathbb{E}[g(\pi(X),X)]
.
Define the conditional violation risk of the baseline policy
g_{0}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
as
g_{0}(x):=g(\pi_{0}(x),x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Define the minimal conditional violation risk
g_{*}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
as

g_{*}(x):=\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g(a,x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Because
\mathcal{A}
is finite, after deterministic tie-breaking there exists a measurable selector

\pi_{*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}

such that for each
x
,

\pi_{*}(x)\in\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g(a,x)
, with the convention that
\pi_{*}(x)=\pi_{0}(x)
whenever

\pi_{0}(x)\in\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g(a,x)
.
We call
\pi_{*}
the
oracle fallback policy.
Define the
oracle score

\Delta:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
by

\Delta(x):=g_{0}(x)-g_{*}(x)

for
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
In words,
\Delta(x)
is the conditional excess violation risk of keeping the baseline action rather than using the oracle fallback policy.

The following result characterizes the population-optimal post-processor. It
shows that the optimal policy has a
threshold structure:
it preserves the
baseline
\pi_{0}
on low-score contexts, and switches to the oracle fallback
policy
\pi_{*}
on high-score contexts, in such a way that the risk constraint is tight.

Theorem 3.1

(Population-level solution to
Equation

1
)
.

Assume that the marginal distribution
P_{X}
is atomless.
Let
G_{*}:=\mathbb{E}[g_{*}(X)]
,
G_{0}:=\mathbb{E}[g_{0}(X)]
, and
B:=\varepsilon-G_{*}
.
If
G_{*}>\varepsilon
, then
Equation

1
is infeasible. If
G_{0}\leq\varepsilon
, then

\pi_{0}
is optimal. In the non-trivial case
G_{*}\leq\varepsilon<G_{0}
, define

\tau:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,1]:\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta(X)\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(X)<t\}\right]+t\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)=t)\geq B\right\}.

If
B=0
, an optimal deterministic policy is

\pi^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{0}(x),&\Delta(x)=0,\\
\pi_{*}(x),&\Delta(x)>0.\end{cases}

If
B>0
, let
s:=\left(B-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta(X)\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(X)<\tau\}\right]\right)/\tau
.
Then
s\in[0,\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)=\tau)]
, and there exists a measurable set

E\subseteq\{x:\Delta(x)=\tau\}
with
P_{X}(E)=s
. An optimal deterministic policy is

\pi^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{0}(x),&x\in\{\Delta<\tau\}\cup E,\\
\pi_{*}(x),&x\in\{\Delta\geq\tau\}\setminus E.\end{cases}

Moreover, for the optimal policy in the non-trivial case, we have
\mathbb{P}\big(\ell(\pi^{*}(X),Y)\geq c\big)=\varepsilon
and the agreement with the baseline is

\mathbb{P}(\pi^{*}(X)=\pi_{0}(X))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)=0),&B=0,\\
\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau)+s,&B>0.\end{cases}

The proof of the above result is provided in Section
D.1
.

4.1
Stability-based risk control guarantee

Algorithm

1
bears a resemblance to the general Conformal Risk Control (CRC) algorithm
(
4
)
, for which strong distribution-free risk control guarantees are readily available.
However, a crucial assumption behind the CRC guarantee is the monotonicity of the loss function with respect to the calibration threshold.
In our setting, due to the fact that we must estimate the fallback policy
\hat{\pi}_{*}
, the violation loss
L(x,y;\tau)
is not necessarily monotone in
\tau
.
This presents significant technical challenges in the analysis of
Algorithm

1
, which we tackle via recently-introduced
stability techniques
in the Conformal Prediction literature
(
6
)
.

Consider an exchangeable
augmented sample

Z_{i}:i\in[n+1]
consisting of
n
calibration datapoints and an additional test point
Z_{n+1}=(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
.
For a subset
S\subseteq[n+1]

Z_{S}:=\{Z_{i}:i\in S\}
for the set of observations with indices in
S
.
We define the
augmented threshold

\hat{\tau}_{1:(n+1)}
as the threshold obtained by running
Algorithm

1
on the augmented sample
Z_{[n+1]}
.
Also, given
i\in[n+1]
, we define the

\hat{\tau}_{-i}
as the threshold obtained by running
Algorithm

1
on the leave-one-out sample
Z_{[n+1]\setminus\{i\}}
. (In particular, note that
\hat{\tau}_{-(n+1)}
is the threshold we deploy in practice.)

The key quantity in our analysis is the

of our algorithm, which is defined as the maximum displacement between the rank of
\hat{\tau}_{1:(n+1)}
and the rank of
\hat{\tau}_{-i}
among the scores
\hat{\Delta}_{j}:j\in[n+1]

.

4.3
Near-optimality of the finite-sample policy

Finally, in the exact-safe fallback regime of
Section

4.2
, under mild regularity conditions, we show that the post-processed policy is
nearly optimal
for the problem in
Equation

1
. That is, the post-processor preserves nearly as
much agreement with the baseline as the oracle policy.
The sub-optimality relative to the oracle comes from two sources: finite-sample
calibration noise, and the error incurred by using the fitted score

\hat{\Delta}
instead of the oracle score
\Delta
.
This is captured by the following quantity:

\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u):=\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|>u)+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}(|\Delta(X)-t|\leq u).

Denote
(t)_{+}:=\max\{0,t\}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(Near-optimality of exact-safe fallback post-processing)
.

\delta\in(0,1)

6
Discussion

We studied the problem of post-processing a baseline policy to enforce a chance constraint on a downstream loss.
After characterizing the population optimum,
we showed small excess violation risk under regularity conditions, and proved exact risk control and near-optimality when the fallback is exact-safe.
The experiments demonstrated that our algorithm achieves risk control with minimally invasive post-processing, comparing favorably to a random mixing baseline.
Future directions include generalizing the framework to randomized policies and continuous action spaces.

Appendix A
Additional related work

Conformal prediction and conformal risk control.

Conformal prediction provides finite-sample distribution-free predictive guarantees under exchangeability
(
32
;
26
;
5
;
2
)
. Conformal risk control extends these ideas from miscoverage to more general user-specified risks
(
8
;
3
;
4
)
. The standard one-dimensional conformal risk-control argument relies on monotonicity of the loss in a tuning parameter. Our exact-safe fallback result fits exactly into this paradigm, while plug-in calibration leads to non-monotone losses. Recent work has begun to study non-monotone risk control via stability or finite-grid analyses
(
6
;
1
)
; our analysis is specialized to policy post-processing and exploits the rank stability of the selected threshold.

Decision-aware conformal methods.

Several recent works calibrate uncertainty estimates or decision rules directly for downstream decision-making. Conformal predictive decision-making and conformal decision theory calibrate decisions rather than only prediction sets
(
31
;
22
)
. Decision-focused uncertainty quantification and conformal calibration for optimization under uncertainty incorporate downstream costs and robust-optimization objectives into the construction of uncertainty sets
(
13
;
36
;
24
;
18
)
. Our focus is complementary: rather than learning a new decision rule or uncertainty set from scratch, we seek the least intrusive post-processing of a fixed baseline policy that satisfies a chance constraint.

Reject-option and selective prediction.

The special case in which the post-processor replaces a risky prediction by an abstention, referral, or additional-test action is closely related to classification with a reject option, originating with Chow’s optimal error–reject tradeoff
(
10
)
. Later work developed convex surrogates and learning-theoretic analyses for reject-option classifiers
(
7
;
12
)
, and recent surveys organize this broader literature around ambiguity and novelty rejection
(
16
)
. Our formulation differs in two ways: it allows an arbitrary finite action space and loss matrix, and it treats the baseline policy as an object to be preserved unless risk control requires otherwise.

Prediction, optimization, and decision-making under uncertainty.

Our population objective is also connected to classical statistical decision theory
(
33
)
, predict-then-optimize pipelines
(
15
)
, and optimization under uncertainty
(
20
)
. The distinctive feature of the present work is the distribution-free calibration layer: the post-processor may use a fitted probabilistic model to rank interventions, but the selected threshold is calibrated using held-out data to control the risk budget.

C.3
Full near-optimality theorem

Theorem C.5

(Near-optimality of exact-safe fallback post-processing)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. Suppose the following conditions hold.

1.

The fitted fallback policy is exact-safe:

\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\hat{\pi}_{*}(x),y)\geq c\}=0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}.

2.

The calibration and test observations
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n+1}
are i.i.d.

3.

The oracle risk curve

R^{*}(\tau):=\mathbb{P}(\ell(\pi_{0}(X),Y)\geq c,\Delta(X)<\tau)

for
\tau\in[0,1]
has an interior crossing: there exists
\tau^{*}\in(0,1)
satisfying

R^{*}(\tau^{*})=\varepsilon
. Moreover, there are constants
C_{R}>0
and

\rho\in(0,\min\{\tau^{*},1-\tau^{*}\})
such that

|R^{*}(\tau)-R^{*}(\tau^{*})|\geq C_{R}|\tau-\tau^{*}|

whenever
|\tau-\tau^{*}|\leq\rho
.

4.

The oracle agreement curve is locally Lipschitz at
\tau^{*}
: there
is a constant
L_{J}<\infty
such that

\big|\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau)-\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau^{*})\big|\leq L_{J}|\tau-\tau^{*}|

whenever
|\tau-\tau^{*}|\leq\rho
.

Define

\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u):=\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|>u)+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{P}(|\Delta(X)-t|\leq u),\qquad\varepsilon_{\Delta}:=\inf_{u>0}\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u).

Also define

\hat{p}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\pi_{0}(X_{i}),Y_{i})\geq c\},\qquad\varepsilon_{1,n}(\delta):=\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\log(4/\delta)}{2n}},

and

\varepsilon_{n}(\delta):=\frac{n}{n+1}\varepsilon_{1,n}(\delta)+\frac{1}{n+1}+\varepsilon_{\Delta},\qquad\nu_{n}(\delta):=C_{R}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{n}(\delta)+\frac{1}{n+1}\right),

\eta_{n}(\delta):=L_{J}\nu_{n}(\delta)+\varepsilon_{\Delta}.

With probability at least
1-\delta
over the calibration data, if
\nu_{n}(\delta)\leq\rho
, then

\left(J^{*}-\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{\pi}(X;\hat{\tau})=\pi_{0}(X)\bigr)\right)_{+}\leq\eta_{n}(\delta),

where
J^{*}:=\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau^{*})
is the optimal population agreement value for the problem in

Equation

1
.
Consequently, we have the bound

\left(J^{*}-\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{\pi}(X;\hat{\tau})=\pi_{0}(X)\bigr)\right)_{+}\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{C_{5}}{n+1}+C_{6}\varepsilon_{\Delta},

where
C_{4},C_{5},C_{6}
are defined in
Section

C.1
.
In addition, whenever
\varepsilon_{\Delta}\to 0
, the sub-optimality positive-part is
O(n^{-1/2})+O(\varepsilon_{\Delta})
.

The proof of the above result is provided in
Section

D.9
.

Remark C.6

(Threshold-disagreement modulus)
.

The quantity
\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u)
measures how often thresholding

\hat{\Delta}(X)
can disagree with thresholding the oracle score
\Delta(X)
,
uniformly over all thresholds. Indeed, for any
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\Delta}(X)<\tau\}\neq\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(X)<\tau\}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|>u)+\mathbb{P}(|\Delta(X)-\tau|\leq u)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u).

The first term is the score estimation error, while the second is the mass of
oracle scores within distance
u
of a threshold, i.e., points whose decision
is sensitive to perturbations of size
u
. Thus

\varepsilon_{\Delta}=\inf_{u>0}\mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(u)
is a uniform thresholding error
bound. If
|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|\leq a_{n}
a.s. and

\Delta(X)
has bounded density, then
\varepsilon_{\Delta}=O(a_{n})
.

D.1
Proof of the population oracle

Proof.

Let
\mu:=P_{X}
. For any measurable deterministic policy
\pi
, define

S(\pi):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\pi(x)=\pi_{0}(x)\}
.
Construct a new policy
\tilde{\pi}
by

\tilde{\pi}(x):=\begin{cases}\pi_{0}(x),&x\in S(\pi),\\
\pi_{*}(x),&x\notin S(\pi).\end{cases}

Then pointwise
\mathbf{1}\{\tilde{\pi}(x)=\pi_{0}(x)\}\geq\mathbf{1}\{\pi(x)=\pi_{0}(x)\}
, hence

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\pi}(X)=\pi_{0}(X))\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi(X)=\pi_{0}(X))
.
On the other hand, for each
x\notin S(\pi)
we have
\pi(x)\neq\pi_{0}(x)
and by optimality of
\pi_{*}(x)
,

g(\pi_{*}(x),x)=g_{*}(x)\leq g(\pi(x),x)
. Therefore
g(\tilde{\pi}(x),x)\leq g(\pi(x),x)
for all
x
, and

\mathbb{P}(\ell(\tilde{\pi}(X),Y)\geq c)=\mathbb{E}[g(\tilde{\pi}(X),X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[g(\pi(X),X)]=\mathbb{P}(\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c)
.
Thus if
\pi
is feasible, then so is
\tilde{\pi}
, and
\tilde{\pi}
achieves at least as large an objective value. Hence some optimum lies in the two-action class.

For any measurable set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, define the two-action policy

\pi_{S}(x):=\begin{cases}\pi_{0}(x),&x\in S,\\
\pi_{*}(x),&x\notin S.\end{cases}

Its agreement rate is

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{S}(X)=\pi_{0}(X))=\mu(S)+\mu(S^{c}\cap\{\Delta=0\})=\mu(S\cup\{\Delta=0\}),

Indeed,
\Delta(x)=0
if and only if
g_{0}(x)=g_{*}(x)
, which holds if and only if

\pi_{0}(x)\in\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g(a,x)
; by the tie-breaking convention for

\pi_{*}
, this is equivalent to
\pi_{*}(x)=\pi_{0}(x)
.
Its risk is

\mathbb{P}(\ell(\pi_{S}(X),Y)\geq c)=\mathbb{E}[g(\pi_{S}(X),X)]=\int_{S}g_{0}\,d\mu+\int_{S^{c}}g_{*}\,d\mu=G_{*}+\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu.

Since
\Delta=0
on
\{\Delta=0\}
, we have
\int_{S\cup\{\Delta=0\}}\Delta\,d\mu=\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu
. Thus, for the set-optimization problem below we may restrict without loss of generality to sets
S
that contain
\{\Delta=0\}
, in which case
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{S}(X)=\pi_{0}(X))=\mu(S)
.
Therefore
Equation

1
is equivalent to the pure set problem

\max_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X}\text{ measurable}}\ \mu(S)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu\leq B.

(8)

If
B<0
, (
8
) is infeasible. If
B\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Delta\,d\mu
(equivalently
G_{0}\leq\varepsilon
), then
S=\mathcal{X}
is optimal and corresponds to
\pi_{0}
.
Henceforth assume
0\leq B<\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Delta\,d\mu
.

If
B=0
, feasibility forces
\Delta=0

\mu
-a.e. on
S
, so every feasible
S
satisfies
S\subseteq\{\Delta=0\}
up to
\mu
-null sets. Thus
S^{*}:=\{\Delta=0\}
is feasible and maximizes
\mu(S)
, proving optimality in this case (and in particular
\tau=0
by
Section

C.8
).

Now assume
B>0
. By
Section

C.8
, we have
\tau\in(0,1]
.

(a) A universal upper bound.

Fix any
\lambda\geq 0
. For any feasible
S
with
\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu\leq B
,

\mu(S)=\int_{S}1\,d\mu=\int_{S}(1-\lambda\Delta)\,d\mu+\lambda\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu\leq\int_{S}(1-\lambda\Delta)\,d\mu+\lambda B.

Moreover, for any set
S
,

\int_{S}(1-\lambda\Delta)\,d\mu\leq\int_{S}(1-\lambda\Delta)_{+}\,d\mu\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1-\lambda\Delta)_{+}\,d\mu,

and equality is attained by taking
S=\{1-\lambda\Delta>0\}\cup E_{0}
for any measurable
E_{0}\subseteq\{1-\lambda\Delta=0\}
.
Thus every feasible
S
satisfies

\mu(S)\leq J(\lambda):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1-\lambda\Delta)_{+}\,d\mu+\lambda B,

and hence

\sup\Big\{\mu(S):\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu\leq B\Big\}\leq\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}J(\lambda).

(b) Choose
\lambda^{*}=1/\tau
and construct
S^{*}
attaining the bound.

By
Section

C.8
,

\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu\ \leq\ B\ \leq\ \int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu+\tau\,\mu(\Delta=\tau),

which implies
s=\frac{B-\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu}{\tau}\in[0,\mu(\Delta=\tau)]
.
Since
\mu
is atomless, by Sierpiński’s theorem
(
27
)
: for any measurable
A
and any
u\in[0,\mu(A)]
, there exists a measurable
E\subseteq A
with
\mu(E)=u
.
Apply this with
A=\{\Delta=\tau\}
and
u=s
to obtain
E\subseteq\{\Delta=\tau\}
with
\mu(E)=s
, and define
S^{*}:=\{\Delta<\tau\}\cup E
.
Then
\Delta=\tau
on
E
, so

\int_{S^{*}}\Delta\,d\mu=\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu+\int_{E}\Delta\,d\mu=\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu+\tau\,\mu(E)=\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu+\tau s=B,

so
S^{*}
is feasible and the constraint is tight.

Now set
\lambda^{*}:=1/\tau
. Observe that

(1-\lambda^{*}\Delta)_{+}=\Big(1-\frac{\Delta}{\tau}\Big)_{+}=\mathbf{1}\{\Delta<\tau\}\Big(1-\frac{\Delta}{\tau}\Big),

so

\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1-\lambda^{*}\Delta)_{+}\,d\mu=\mu(\Delta<\tau)-\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu.

Therefore

\displaystyle J(\lambda^{*})

\displaystyle=\mu(\Delta<\tau)-\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu+\frac{B}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mu(\Delta<\tau)+\frac{B-\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu}{\tau}=\mu(\Delta<\tau)+s=\mu(S^{*}).

Combining with the bound
\mu(S)\leq J(\lambda^{*})
for all feasible
S
shows that
S^{*}
is optimal for (
8
). Translating back, the corresponding deterministic policy
\pi^{*}:=\pi_{S^{*}}
is optimal for
Equation

1
, with
\mathbb{P}\bigl(\ell(\pi^{*}(X),Y)\geq c\bigr)=\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{P}(\pi^{*}(X)=\pi_{0}(X))=\mu(\Delta<\tau)+\mu(E)=\mu(\Delta<\tau)+s
.
∎

Similarly, for

By

for all

\sum_{t=r_{i}}^{r_{i}+h_{i}-1}W_{(t)}\leq 1,

Thus, if

(9)

Proof of expected risk control from rank stability

Proof.

Exchangeability gives

\mathbb{E}\big[L(Z_{N};\hat{\tau}_{-N})\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\right].

By

C.2
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{1:N})+\frac{2K}{N}.

On the event
E_{\mathrm{start}}
from the proof of
Section

4.1
, the threshold
\tau=0
is
\hat{R}_{1:N}^{+}
-feasible for all sufficiently large

, so that

. Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{1:N})+1\leq(N+1)\varepsilon

D.8
Proof of the exact-safe fallback theorem

Proof.

Exact-safety gives, for every
z=(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and every

\tau\in[0,1]\cup\{\top\}
,

L(z;\tau)=\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\pi_{0}(x),y)\geq c\}\,\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\Delta}(x)<\tau\}.

Thus
L(z;\tau)
is non-decreasing in the extended threshold order.

As in
Section

C.2
, let
N:=n+1
, and consider an augmented sample
Z_{1:N}
.
For each
i\in[N]
, let
\hat{\tau}_{-i}
be the threshold
selected by
Algorithm

1
with

\hat{\pi}_{*}=\pi_{\mathrm{safe}}
from the leave-one-out sample

Z_{[N]\setminus\{i\}}
. By exchangeability,

\mathbb{E}\big[L(Z_{N};\hat{\tau}_{-N})\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\right].

Next, define the (un-bumped) empirical risk over the augmented sample as

\hat{R}_{1:N}(\tau):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}L(Z_{j};\tau)

for
\tau\in[0,1]\cup\{\top\}
.
Let

\mathcal{T}_{1:N}:=\{0,\top\}\cup\{\hat{\Delta}(X_{i}):i\in[N]\}
, and define the modified threshold

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{1:N}^{\circ}:=\max\left\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{1:N}:\hat{R}_{1:N}(\tau)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(10)

where the maximum is taken in the extended order.
We say a threshold
\tau\in[0,1]\cup\{\top\}
is
\hat{R}_{1:N}
-feasible
if
\hat{R}_{1:N}(\tau)\leq\varepsilon
.
Note that
\tau=0
is
\hat{R}_{1:N}
-feasible because
L(z;0)\equiv 0
.

Fix
i\in[N]
. If the set of
\hat{R}_{-i}^{+}
-feasible indices is non-empty, then since
\hat{\tau}_{-i}
is
\hat{R}_{-i}^{+}
-feasible, we deduce

\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}L(Z_{j};\hat{\tau}_{-i})+1\leq N\varepsilon.

Since
L(Z_{j};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\leq 1
, it follows that

\sum_{j=1}^{N}L(Z_{j};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\leq N\varepsilon
.
On the other hand, if the set of
\hat{R}_{-i}^{+}
-feasible indices is empty, then
\hat{\tau}_{-i}=0
by convention and the same
inequality holds because
L(z;0)\equiv 0
. Therefore each

\hat{\tau}_{-i}
is
\hat{R}_{1:N}
-feasible.
Consequently, by the monotonicity of
\hat{R}_{1:N}(\tau)
in
\tau
, we have

\hat{\tau}_{-i}\leq\hat{\tau}_{1:N}^{\circ}
for every
i\in[N]
. As a result,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{-i})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(Z_{i};\hat{\tau}_{1:N}^{\circ})\leq\varepsilon.

Taking expectations proves

\mathbb{E}[L(Z_{N};\hat{\tau}_{-N})]\leq\varepsilon
. Since
\hat{\tau}_{-N}
is the
threshold selected from the calibration observations
Z_{[n]}
, this implies the result.
∎

D.9
Proof of the near-optimality theorem

Proof.

By the exact-safety assumption, we have

L(X,Y;\tau)=\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\pi_{0}(X),Y)\geq c\}\,\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\Delta}(X)<\tau\}

a.s. for
\tau\in[0,1]
. Let

\hat{R}(\tau)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(X_{i},Y_{i};\tau)
and

R(\tau)=\mathbb{E}[L(X_{i},Y_{i};\tau)]
.
Given
\tau\in[0,1]\cup\{\top\}
, we say that
\tau
is
\hat{R}^{+}
-feasible
if
\hat{R}^{+}(\tau)\leq\varepsilon
.
By
Section

C.4
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}|\hat{R}(\tau)-R(\tau)|\leq\varepsilon_{1,n}(\delta).

We work on this event.

The oracle safe-action loss is

L^{*}(X,Y;\tau)=\mathbf{1}\{\ell(\pi_{0}(X),Y)\geq c\}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(X)<\tau\}
.
For
\tau\in[0,1]
, set

E_{\tau}:=\{\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\Delta}(X)<\tau\}\neq\mathbf{1}\{\Delta(X)<\tau\}\}.

Since the two losses can differ only on
E_{\tau}
,

|R(\tau)-R^{*}(\tau)|\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{\tau}).

For any
u>0
, if
E_{\tau}
holds and
|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|\leq u
, then

|\Delta(X)-\tau|\leq u
. Hence

E_{\tau}\subseteq\{|\hat{\Delta}(X)-\Delta(X)|>u\}\cup\{|\Delta(X)-\tau|\leq u\}.

Taking the supremum over
\tau\in[0,1]
and then the infimum over
u>0
gives

\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}|R(\tau)-R^{*}(\tau)|\leq\varepsilon_{\Delta}.

The same containment, applied directly to the two threshold indicators, gives

\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}\big|\mathbb{P}(\hat{\Delta}(X)<\tau)-\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau)\big|\leq\varepsilon_{\Delta}.

Because
\hat{R}^{+}(\tau)=\frac{n}{n+1}\hat{R}(\tau)+\frac{1}{n+1}
and
R^{*}(\tau)\in[0,1]
, on the same event

\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}|\hat{R}^{+}(\tau)-R^{*}(\tau)|\leq\frac{n}{n+1}\varepsilon_{1,n}(\delta)+\frac{1}{n+1}+\varepsilon_{\Delta}=\varepsilon_{n}(\delta).

Let
\nu=\nu_{n}(\delta)
. By assumption,
\nu\leq\rho
, so both
\tau^{*}-\nu
and
\tau^{*}+\nu
lie in
[0,1]
and the local margin condition applies to them. Since
R^{*}(\tau^{*})=\varepsilon
and
R^{*}
is non-decreasing,

R^{*}(\tau^{*}-\nu)\leq\varepsilon-C_{R}\nu,\qquad R^{*}(\tau^{*}+\nu)\geq\varepsilon+C_{R}\nu.

The definition of
\nu
gives
C_{R}\nu=\varepsilon_{n}(\delta)+1/(n+1)
. Hence

\hat{R}^{+}(\tau^{*}-\nu)\leq\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n+1}<\varepsilon,\qquad\hat{R}^{+}(\tau^{*}+\nu)\geq\varepsilon+\frac{1}{n+1}>\varepsilon.

Note that
\hat{R}^{+}
is non-decreasing in the extended threshold order. The algorithm selects over the grid
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{0,\top\}\cup\{\hat{\Delta}(X_{i}):i\in[n]\}
, while the two thresholds
\tau^{*}-\nu
and
\tau^{*}+\nu
need not themselves be grid points. Since
\tau^{*}-\nu
is
\hat{R}^{+}
-feasible, the smallest grid point
t^{+}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfying
t^{+}\geq\tau^{*}-\nu
has the same empirical loss as
\tau^{*}-\nu
unless it is
\top
, in which case all calibration scores are already below
\tau^{*}-\nu
and the same conclusion holds. Hence
t^{+}
is grid-feasible, so the selected threshold satisfies
\hat{\tau}\geq t^{+}\geq\tau^{*}-\nu
. Since
\tau^{*}+\nu
is not
\hat{R}^{+}
-feasible, monotonicity makes every grid threshold to its right (including
\top
) infeasible; hence
\hat{\tau}\leq\tau^{*}+\nu
. In particular, on this event
\hat{\tau}\in[0,1]
,
\tau^{*}-\nu\leq\hat{\tau}\leq\tau^{*}+\nu
, and

|\hat{\tau}-\tau^{*}|\leq\nu_{n}(\delta)
.

It remains to translate threshold error into an agreement shortfall. Write
\mu:=P_{X}
. In the exact-safe setting, any feasible policy
\pi
with agreement set
A_{\pi}:=\{x:\pi(x)=\pi_{0}(x)\}
satisfies

\int_{A_{\pi}}\Delta\,d\mu=\int_{A_{\pi}}g_{0}\,d\mu\leq\mathbb{P}(\ell(\pi(X),Y)\geq c)\leq\varepsilon.

Thus the agreement of any feasible policy is bounded by the value of the set problem

\max\{\mu(S):\int_{S}\Delta\,d\mu\leq\varepsilon\}
.
The set
S^{*}=\{\Delta<\tau^{*}\}
is feasible and tight because exact-safety gives

R^{*}(\tau^{*})=\int_{S^{*}}\Delta\,d\mu=\varepsilon
, and the policy that keeps
\pi_{0}
on
S^{*}
and uses
\pi_{\mathrm{safe}}
elsewhere attains risk
\varepsilon
and agreement at least
\mu(S^{*})
. For any feasible
S
,

\mu(S)\leq\int_{S}\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{\tau^{*}}\right)d\mu+\frac{\varepsilon}{\tau^{*}}\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{\tau^{*}}\right)_{+}d\mu+\frac{\varepsilon}{\tau^{*}}=\mu(\Delta<\tau^{*})+\frac{\varepsilon-R^{*}(\tau^{*})}{\tau^{*}}=J^{*},

where the equality uses
\tau^{*}>0
and

\left(1-\Delta/\tau^{*}\right)_{+}=\mathbf{1}\{\Delta<\tau^{*}\}(1-\Delta/\tau^{*})
. Thus
S^{*}
is optimal, and the optimal population agreement is

J^{*}=\mu(S^{*})=\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau^{*})
. The deployed policy agrees with the baseline at least on the event

\{\hat{\Delta}(X)<\hat{\tau}\}
, so

\displaystyle J^{*}-\mathbb{P}(\hat{\pi}(X;\hat{\tau})=\pi_{0}(X))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau^{*})-\mathbb{P}(\hat{\Delta}(X)<\hat{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq\big|\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\tau^{*})-\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\hat{\tau})\big|

\displaystyle\quad+\big|\mathbb{P}(\Delta(X)<\hat{\tau})-\mathbb{P}(\hat{\Delta}(X)<\hat{\tau})\big|

\displaystyle\leq L_{J}|\hat{\tau}-\tau^{*}|+\varepsilon_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq L_{J}\nu_{n}(\delta)+\varepsilon_{\Delta}.

Since the right-hand side is non-negative, the same inequality implies the asserted positive-part shortfall bound.
Unpacking the definition of
\nu_{n}(\delta)
and bounding
\hat{p}\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(J^{*}-\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{\pi}(X;\hat{\tau})=\pi_{0}(X)\bigr)\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{J}}{C_{R}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}+\frac{2}{n+1}\right]+\left(1+\frac{L_{J}}{C_{R}}\right)\varepsilon_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{C_{5}}{n+1}+C_{6}\varepsilon_{\Delta},

as desired.
∎

D.10
Proof of the exact-safe fallback concentration corollary

Proof.

If
p=0
, then
W_{i}=0
a.s. for each
i\in[n]
and result follows. Assume
p>0
.
Define the
left conditional distribution function

F(t):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}<t\mid W_{1}=1)
for
t\in[0,1]
.
On
\{m\geq 1\}
, define

\hat{F}_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\mathbf{1}\{Z_{i}<t\}
; on
\{m=0\}
, set
\hat{F}_{m}(t):=0
.
The population and empirical risks satisfy

R(\tau)=pF(\tau)
and
\hat{R}(\tau)=\hat{p}\,\hat{F}_{m}(\tau)

for all
\tau\in[0,1]
. Hence

\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}|\hat{R}(\tau)-R(\tau)|\leq|\hat{p}-p|+\hat{p}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat{F}_{m}(t)-F(t)|.

By Hoeffding’s inequality
(
17
)
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

|\hat{p}-p|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}.

Conditional on the index set
S:=\{i\in[n]:W_{i}=1\}
and conditional on
m=|S|\geq 1
, the selected scores
(Z_{i})_{i\in S}
are i.i.d. from the conditional law of
Z_{1}
given
W_{1}=1
. The standard Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality for half-lines
(-\infty,t]

(
23
)
also controls strict half-lines
(-\infty,t)
by taking left limits. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat{F}_{m}(t)-F(t)|>\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}}\;\middle|\;S\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}

for every such
S
. On
\{m=0\}
, the product

\hat{p}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat{F}_{m}(t)-F(t)|
is zero by definition. Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\hat{p}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat{F}_{m}(t)-F(t)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}.

A union bound completes the proof.
∎

D.11
Proof of the suffix low-sum block bound

Proof.

Fix
h\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and a start index
r\in\{i_{0},\dots,n-h+1\}
. Let

Y_{r,h}:=\sum_{t=r}^{r+h-1}S_{t}
.
For any
\theta>0
, Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}(Y_{r,h}\leq b)\leq e^{\theta b}\prod_{t=r}^{r+h-1}\mathbb{E}[e^{-\theta S_{t}}].

Since
S_{t}\in[-1,1]
, Hoeffding’s lemma
(
17
)
yields

\mathbb{E}[e^{-\theta S_{t}}]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\theta\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]\right)\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\theta\lambda\right),

so

\mathbb{P}(Y_{r,h}\leq b)\leq\exp(\theta b+h\theta^{2}/2-\theta\lambda h)
.
Optimizing over
\theta>0
gives

\mathbb{P}(Y_{r,h}\leq b)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\lambda h-b)_{+})^{2}}{2h}\right).

A union bound over the possible start indices
r
and block lengths
m
gives

\mathbb{P}\big(M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)\geq h\big)\leq\sum_{m=h}^{n}n\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\lambda m-b)_{+}^{2}}{2m}\right).

Since
M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)
is non-negative and integer-valued, we may write

\mathbb{E}[M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)]=\sum_{h=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)\geq h)

and split the sum at

h_{0}:=\left\lceil 16\lambda^{-2}\log n+2b/\lambda\right\rceil
.
The contribution up to and including
h=h_{0}
is at most
h_{0}
.
For
h>h_{0}
, we have
h\geq 2b/\lambda
, hence
\lambda h-b\geq\lambda h/2
.
Thus, the tail sum can be bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{h=h_{0}+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\!\left(M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)\geq h\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=h_{0}+1}^{n}\sum_{m=h}^{n}ne^{-\lambda^{2}m/8}

\displaystyle=n\sum_{m=h_{0}+1}^{n}(m-h_{0})e^{-\lambda^{2}m/8}\leq\frac{ne^{-\lambda^{2}(h_{0}+1)/8}}{(1-e^{-\lambda^{2}/8})^{2}},

which by

n\exp(-\lambda^{2}h_{0}/8)\leq{1}/{n}
is bounded by one for sufficiently large
n
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[M^{(b)}_{i_{0}}(n)]\leq h_{0}+1\leq 16\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\log n+\frac{2b}{\lambda}+2
for all sufficiently large
n
.
∎

D.12
Proof of the right-endpoint exclusion lemma

Proof.

For each fixed
i\in[N]
,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{0,-i}]=p_{0}\geq\varepsilon+\xi
, and Hoeffding’s inequality
(
17
)
gives

\mathbb{P}(E_{\mathrm{int},-i}^{c})\leq e^{-(N-1)\xi^{2}/2}.

A union bound over
i\in[N]
yields

\mathbb{P}(E_{\mathrm{int}}^{c})\leq Ne^{-(N-1)\xi^{2}/2}.

On
E_{\mathrm{int}}
, averaging the inequalities

\sum_{k\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}I^{0}_{k}>(N-1)(\varepsilon+\xi/2)
over
i
gives

\sum_{k=1}^{N}I^{0}_{k}>N(\varepsilon+\xi/2)
.

Therefore

\displaystyle T_{N+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{N}(I^{s}_{(t)}-\varepsilon)+\sum_{t=1}^{N}W_{(t)}+1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(I^{0}_{k}-\varepsilon)+1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle>\frac{N\xi}{2}+1-\varepsilon.

For all sufficiently large
N
, this is larger than
1
. Hence

T_{N+1}>1.

By
Section

C.2
,

T_{N}\geq T_{N+1}-1>0.

Thus both indices
N
and
N+1
are
\hat{R}^{+}_{1:N}
-infeasible, and consequently

\hat{j}<N.

Given
i\in[N]
, a similar argument shows that
\hat{j}_{-i}<N
.
Indeed, on
E_{\mathrm{int},-i}
,

\displaystyle T^{-i}_{N+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in[N]\setminus\{q_{i}\}}(I^{s}_{(t)}-\varepsilon)+\sum_{t\in[N]\setminus\{q_{i}\}}W_{(t)}+1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}(I^{0}_{k}-\varepsilon)+1-\varepsilon>\frac{(N-1)\xi}{2}+1-\varepsilon.

For all sufficiently large
N
, this is larger than
1
, so

T^{-i}_{N+1}>1.

Let

b_{i}:=\max\bigl\{\mathcal{J}_{-i}\setminus\{N+1\}\bigr\}.

By

D.13
Proof of the concomitants lemma

Proof.

Let
Q_{z}
be a regular conditional law of
U_{1}
given
Z_{1}=z
. Conditional on the full vector
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
, the variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are independent with conditional laws
Q_{Z_{1}},\dots,Q_{Z_{n}}
. The ranking permutation
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is a measurable function of
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
because ties have probability zero. Therefore, conditional on the full vector,
U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)}
are independent with laws
Q_{Z_{(1)}},\dots,Q_{Z_{(n)}}
. Since this product law depends on the
Z
-vector only through the order statistics, conditioning further only on
\mathcal{G}=\sigma(Z_{(1)},\dots,Z_{(n)})
gives the same product law. This proves both the conditional independence and the stated conditional marginal distributions.
∎

D.14
Proof of the threshold lemma for the population oracle

Proof.

Since
\Delta\geq 0
and
\Delta
is integrable, the sets
\{\Delta\leq t\}
are increasing in
t
, so
F
is non-decreasing and
F(0)=0
.
Moreover, if
s_{n}\downarrow t
, then
\Delta\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\leq s_{n}\}\to\Delta\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\leq t\}
pointwise and is dominated by
\Delta
, so
F
is right-continuous by dominated convergence.
Since
g_{0},g_{*}\in[0,1]
, we have
0\leq\Delta\leq 1
, and therefore

F(1)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Delta\,d\mu=\mathbb{E}[\Delta(X)]>B.

Hence the set
\{t\in[0,1]:F(t)\geq B\}
is non-empty and
\tau\leq 1
.

If
B=0
, then
0\in\{t:F(t)\geq B\}
, so
\tau=0
. Conversely, if
\tau=0
, then by the definition of the infimum there exists a sequence
u_{n}\downarrow 0
with
F(u_{n})\geq B
for all
n
. By right-continuity,

F(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(u_{n})\geq B.

Since
F(0)=0
and
B\geq 0
, it follows that
B=0
.
If
B>0
, then
F(0)=0<B
, so
\tau>0
; finiteness has been shown above.

Finally, assume
B>0
so that
\tau>0
, and take any sequence
t_{n}\uparrow\tau
with
0\leq t_{n}<\tau
for all
n
(e.g.
t_{n}:=\max\{0,\tau-1/n\}
). Then
\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\leq t_{n}\}\uparrow\mathbf{1}\{\Delta<\tau\}
, so

F(\tau-):=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(t_{n})=\int_{\{\Delta<\tau\}}\Delta\,d\mu.

By definition of
\tau
, we have
F(t)<B
for all
t<\tau
, hence
F(\tau-)\leq B
. On the other hand, by definition of the infimum there exists a sequence
u_{n}\downarrow\tau
with
F(u_{n})\geq B
for all
n
, so right-continuity yields
F(\tau)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(u_{n})\geq B
. Finally,
F(\tau)=F(\tau-)+\tau\,\mu(\Delta=\tau)
, yielding the stated inequality.
∎
