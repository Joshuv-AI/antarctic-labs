Perinatal Diagnosis of Generalized Arterial Calcification of Infancy: First Genetically Confirmed ENPP1 Case in an Egyptian Fetus
Authors: Alshaimaa Tarek, Wael Tohamy, Hanan Hany, Mohamed K Khalifa, Khaled R Gaber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeneralized Arterial Calcification of Infancy (GACI) is a rare genetic vascular disease characterized by the early onset (between in utero and infancy) of extensive calcification and stenosis of the large and medium-sized arteries. Presentation is typically with respiratory distress, congestive heart failure, and systemic hypertension. With approximately 300 cases reported worldwide in the medical literature. The prevalence is unknown; however, based on the carrier frequency of the recognized pathogenic variants, a frequency of 1 in 200,000 has been suggested. The autosomal recessive form of GACI disorder is caused by mutations in the ENPP1 or ABCC6 genes. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first genetically confirmed case of GACI in an Egyptian fetus detected during the perinatal period. In this report, we describe a case of GACI in a fetus with a pathogenic ENPP1 gene mutation at 29 weeks of gestation. Ultrasound examination revealed aortic and pulmonary valve stenosis, biventricular hypertrophy, and a hypercalcified aorta and ductal arch. Genetic testing identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the ENPP1 gene: c.749C>T (p.Pro250Leu). In conclusion, early diagnosis of GACI is vital due to its severe prognosis and early symptom onset. Expert fetal echocardiography plays a key role in detecting arterial calcification during pregnancy. Genetic testing enhances diagnostic accuracy, informs treatment strategies, and supports family counselling.Keywords: Arterial calcification, Perinatal, GAICI, ENP11, Egyptian case

Introduction

Generalized arterial calcification of infancy (GACI) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder that affects around 1 in 200,000 pregnant women.1 It marked by arterial calcium deposition and intimal proliferation causing vascular stenosis during childhood. During pregnancy, ultrasound examination can be used to detect the disease.2 Affected neonates usually suffer from hypertension, edema, respiratory distress, cyanosis, cardiomegaly, and heart failure.3

GACI is classified into type 1 and type 2 based on the underlying gene variant.4 In type 1 disease, mutations in ENPP1 lead to loss of the enzyme that normally breaks down extracellular ATP into AMP and inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi). Because PPi is a natural blocker of abnormal mineral buildup, its reduction removes this protective effect and allows calcification to occur more easily.5 This subtype represents the majority of cases, accounting for approximately 75%. In type 2, mutations in the ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 6 (ABCC6) gene lead to loss of the ABCC6 protein, which disrupts extracellular ATP transport and reduces PPi levels. It comprises around 10% of cases.6

Most patients with GACI die from cardiovascular complications within the first year, with a 55% mortality rate before six months, reflecting the poor prognosis of the disease. Early and appropriate intervention may slow disease progression.1 Bisphosphonates are currently the most frequently reported treatment for GACI.7

In this report, we describe a rare case of generalized arterial calcification of infancy (GACI) in an Egyptian infant who initially presented with cardiomegaly, pericardial effusion, and arterial calcification. Genetic testing revealed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the ENPP1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of GACI type 1.

Clinical presentation

A 23-year-old asymptomatic pregnant primigravida in a consanguineous marriage with no significant family history was referred for evaluation due to heart abnormalities detected on fetal echocardiography. 3D and 4D ultrasounds at GA 29 revealed abnormalities in the cardiac axis, a single atrium, pericardial effusion, and an abnormal four-chamber view. Doppler evaluation showed a three-vessel umbilical cord (two arteries and one vein), with the umbilical artery demonstrating normal unidirectional flow and an average Resistive Index (RI) of 0.54. A detailed scan revealed a single intrauterine female fetus with aortic and pulmonary valve stenosis, post-stenotic ectasia, and biventricular hypertrophy associated with a hypercalcified aorta and ductal arch (Fig. 1). Moderate pericardial effusion was noted: 3 mm on the left side and 8 mm on the right.

Ultrasonography showing the Hypercalcified aorta.

No additional visceral, morphological, or somatic structures were observed. No subtle signs of aneuploidy. Mild polyhydramnios, with an amniotic fluid index (AFI) of 32 cm. The middle cerebral artery (MCA's) showed a normal Resistive Index (RI) of 0.59. The mean Pulsatility Index (Pl) was within normal limits. Doppler assessment of the uterine arteries showed no pre-diastolic notching. Ultrasound findings were suggestive of expected arterial calcification.

The patient was advised to undergo molecular testing; however, unexpected fetal demise occurred.

Therefore, molecular analysis of the product of conception was recommended.

Patients and methods

Sample from the product of conception were collected and processed for genetic analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted following standard laboratory procedures.

For library preparation and sequencing, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was done using the Twist Bioscience Comprehensive Exome kit with an added mitochondrial DNA spike-in. DNA libraries were made following the manufacturer's instructions, which included fragmentation, end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The libraries were then measured, combined, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform at Macrogen Inc., Korea, producing paired-end reads with high coverage of the targeted regions.

Bioinformatics Analysis and Variant Prioritization: Raw sequencing data were processed through the Inheritance BFX pipeline. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM, and variant calling was performed with GATK. Variants were annotated with population frequency data (gnomAD), pathogenicity predictions (PolyPhen-2, SIFT, CADD), and clinical significance databases (ClinVar, HGMD).

Variant prioritization focused on pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes associated with cardiovascular, metabolic, or calcification disorders; rare variants (allele frequency < 1%); predicted deleterious variants by multiple in silico tools; and inheritance pattern analysis in the context of consanguinity, with emphasis on homozygous variants. Candidate variants identified by WES were subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Discussion

Generalized Arterial Calcification of Infancy (GACI) or Idiopathic Infantile Arterial Calcification (IIAC), is a rare, autosomal recessive disorder marked by calcium deposits in the arterial wall leads to vessel narrowing, hypertension, heart failure, and early death. Since its first description in 1899, approximately 300 cases have been reported worldwide, underscoring its rarity and poor prognosis.8

The disorder is most often associated with pathogenic variants in the ENPP1 gene. Approximately 80% of affected individuals carry ENPP1 mutations that impair the synthesis of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), a critical inhibitor of vascular calcification.9

GACI can present in utero or within hours of birth, termed “early onset,” or within the first few months of life, termed “late onset”. Chong et al. described perinatal cases of GACI detected shortly after birth,3 while Nagar et al. reported prenatal diagnosis at 27 weeks of gestation using ultrasonography.10 In our case, echocardiography revealed cardiac abnormalities at 29 weeks of gestation. Both 4D and 2D Doppler imaging showed cardiac abnormalities, including an abnormal four-chamber view, altered cardiac axis, a single atrium, cardiomegaly, and moderate pericardial effusion. A detailed scan identified aortic and pulmonary valve stenosis, biventricular hypertrophy, and a hypercalcified aorta and ductal arch. No additional visceral, morphological, or somatic flaws. There were no subtle signs of aneuploidy. Genetic testing confirmed a homozygous pathogenic variant in ENPP1, establishing the diagnosis of GACI. To our knowledge, this represents the first genetically confirmed case of GACI reported in an Egyptian fetus, highlighting the importance of molecular diagnostics in regions with high rates of consanguinity. Genetic testing in our case represents a homozygous pathogenic variant in ENPP1 gene (c.749C>T; p.Pro250Leu). This missense mutation substitutes proline with leucine at codon 250 of the ENPP1 protein. According to ClinVar (Variation ID: 1076487), the variant is classified as pathogenic. This variant is present in population databases (rs754659608, gnomAD 0.008%).

Early detection of GACI through molecular testing and advanced fetal echocardiography is critical, as timely diagnosis facilitates accurate genetic counselling, guides reproductive planning, and allows consideration of emerging therapeutic strategies.2

Although there are still few therapeutic options available, early administration of bisphosphonates has been shown to reduce the progression of the disease and, in some cases, enhance survival when given early.11

Clinical cases highlight that not all cases have the same outcomes, since Brunod et al.12 described an infant with a novel ENPP1 mutation who showed a reduction in arterial calcifications but continued to suffer from severe hypertension and vascular calcification.12 On the other hand, a case of a preterm infant with GACI who survived after receiving bisphosphonate treatment and an early diagnosis, showing the possible advantages of timely intervention and appropriate treatment.13

By showing that GACI can be identified during the perinatal period through fetal echocardiography and confirmed by genetic testing, our case contributes to the literature.

Early detection of a homozygous ENPP1 mutation within a consanguineous background highlights the critical role of genetic counselling in people where autosomal recessive conditions are more common. The advantage of early recognition of GACI enables families to informed reproductive decisions, such as preimplantation genetic testing, and provides accurate information about prognosis and recurrence risk. Because congenital heart defects and central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities may arise from the same underlying genetic mutation or associated malformations, clinical follow up is recommended.

Conclusion

Early diagnosis of Generalized Arterial Calcification of Infancy (GACI) is vital due to its severe prognosis and early symptom onset. Therefore, fetal echocardiography performed by an expert has an important role in antenatal detection of cardiac abnormalities associated with GACI. Furthermore, genetic testing offers important valued information on GACI and other calcification-related disorders, which enhances diagnostic accuracy, informs treatment strategies, and supports family counselling. For future pregnancies, it is highly recommended to consider prenatal genetic screening to evaluate the chance of recurrence and encourage wise reproductive choices.
