Number of Teeth and Posterior Occlusal Pairs by Sociodemographic Factors in the Finnish Adult Population, Results From the Healthy Finland 2023 Study
Authors: Irene Uski, Konsta Naumanen, Ritva Näpänkangas, Ulla Harjunmaa, Anna Liisa Suominen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTooth count and edentulism have long served as key indicators in oral health research, though edentulism is becoming rarer in Western societies. In contrast, occlusal pairs as an alternative measure, and their association with sociodemographic factors, have received far less attention.ObjectivesAim of the study was to gain population‐level information on the number of teeth and posterior occlusal pairs (POPs) in the Finnish adult population and whether they are associated with sociodemographic factors.MethodsStudy employed data from the nationwide Healthy Finland 2023 cross‐sectional study. Participants aged 20 years and over (n = 1798) were examined clinically by trained dentists. Outcome variables were number of teeth and POPs. Sociodemographic explanatory factors were age, sex, marital status, education level, area of residence, income and urbanization. Associations were tested with Poisson regression analyses.ResultsPrevalence of edentulism was 4.2%. Participants had an average of 24.8 teeth (SD 7.3) and 6.5 POPs (SD 2.8). Those aged 20–54 years had almost complete dentition (mean 27.1–29.1 teeth). Older age, lower education, lower income, and being widowed were associated with a lower number of teeth and POPs. Being unmarried or living in Eastern or Northern Finland was associated with a lower number of teeth, whereas rural residence was associated with fewer POPs.ConclusionFinnish adult oral health has continued improving in terms of number of teeth and prevalence of edentulism. However, better sociodemographic status is still associated with higher tooth count and POPs, education level having the strongest association.Keywords: edentulism, healthy finland, number of teeth, posterior occlusal pairs, sociodemographic factors

Introduction

Oral health is a fundamental component of general health and well‐being, and across‐population disparities in oral health reflect broader socioeconomic inequalities [1]. These disparities are not random; they reflect the social determinants of health that shape living conditions and access to care [1, 2]. Number of teeth and the prevalence edentulism and their change over time at the population level has been one of the most commonly used indicators of oral health and a standard measure in studies on social inequalities. However, they are considered a relatively crude metric because neither accounts for functional aspects or underlying oral disease processes [3, 4, 5, 6].

Global data highlight that oral health inequalities extend beyond tooth count to include functional limitations, such as impaired chewing and eating ability, disproportionately affecting disadvantaged populations [7]. European data show that these functional limitations are more common in countries with less redistributive welfare systems and among older, socioeconomically vulnerable populations [8]. Socioeconomic inequalities in oral health are present in all European welfare regimes, yet they are not the lowest in the Scandinavian model despite their better overall oral health [9].

International evidence consistently shows that education, income, and place of residence are linked to risk of tooth loss and edentulism [10, 11]. People with lower income tend to have worse oral health, influenced by factors such as access to dental care, health behaviours, education, stress, early life conditions, and broader social and policy environments [12]. According to the most recent population‐based study conducted in Finland, lower educational level, higher age, male sex, being unmarried or widowed, and having a native language other than Finnish or Swedish were independently associated with poorer dental and periodontal health [13]. In Finland, previous national surveys have shown a consistent decline in edentulism and a gradual increase in the number of remaining teeth among dentate adults over the past decades [14, 15, 16].

A reduction in posterior occlusal pairs (POPs) has been linked to an increased risk of tooth loss in older adults particularly in the anterior region due to increased occlusal pressure on remaining teeth [17]. Lower POPs can impair chewing ability and overall oral function, which may lead to a decline in oral health‐related quality of life, even when the anterior teeth remain intact [18]. Moreover, recent research indicates that POP count may be more important for cognitive health than overall tooth count [19].

The aim of this study was to examine the number of teeth and POPs and compare how sociodemographic factors are associated with them in the adult Finnish population. Understanding whether socioeconomic factors associate not only with the number of teeth but also with the presence of occlusal pairs can offer a more nuanced view of oral health disparities and inform strategies to reduce inequalities, especially in a context where complete edentulism is declining.

Materials and Methods

The study employed data from the Healthy Finland 2023 Survey, a cross‐sectional national study aimed at assessing the health and well‐being of Finnish adults aged 20 years and over [20]. Stratified random sampling was applied to account for regional differences and demographic factors [21]. The survey included questionnaires and a clinical health examination. As part of the health examination, 3522 participants were invited to undergo a clinical oral health examination conducted between January and July 2023. The clinical oral health examination included 1798 patients, with a participation rate of 51.6%. The oral health examination protocol was designed to ensure comparability with earlier Finnish population studies (Mini‐Finland, Health 2000, Health 2011) and has been described in detail by Suominen et al. [13].

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity with approval from the Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District Regional Committee (HUS/900/2022). The Healthy Finland 2023 study's ethical guidelines are described in the cohort profile [21].

Clinical Oral Examination

The oral variables included in the present study were clinically determined number of teeth and POPs, as described in detail by Suominen et al. [13]. In the analysis, only teeth recorded as missing were treated as missing. Roots were recorded as present teeth and dental implants were recorded as missing teeth. Complete edentulism was determined as all teeth missing. Dentures were removed for occlusal assessment which was conducted with the patient biting into maximum intercuspation without guidance from the examiner. Occlusal measurements were only performed on participants who had posterior teeth (molars or premolars) present in at least one quadrant of the upper or lower jaw. Occlusal contacts were recorded by identifying antagonist tooth pairs, including molars and premolars. Two occluding teeth resulted in one occlusal pair. A canine was accepted as an antagonist in a POP. If teeth were aligned but did not make contact due to an open bite, they were still recorded as pairs. In this analysis, implants and tooth‐supported fixed dental prostheses were included, but residual roots were excluded. Maximum number for POPs was 10: five pairs on the left and five pairs on the right. In data processing, edentulous participants and participants who had complete denture in either jaw were considered as having no occlusal pairs.

Sociodemographic Factors

Sociodemographic factors included in this study were sex, age, marital status, education level, area of residence (collaborative area), income bracket and urbanization. Background factors excluding education level and income bracket were derived from the Digital and Population Data Services Agency [21]. Education level and income bracket were asked in the questionnaire. Participants were asked how many years of education they had received. Education level was considered basic if the participant had a maximum of 9 years of schooling. Middle education was defined as 9–12 years in school and higher education as over 12 years in school. In the questionnaire participants were asked to evaluate their household yearly income before tax reductions on a five‐level scale; less than 15 000 €, 15 001–35 000 €, 35 001–55 000 €, 55 001–75 000 € or more than 75 000 €. Finland is divided into five collaborative areas for regional coordination, development and cooperation of social and healthcare services [22]. The collaborative areas (Southern Finland, Western Finland, Inland Finland, Eastern Finland and Northern Finland) were used as geographical areas of residence in this study as illustrated in Figure 1. Participant residency in either urban or rural areas was based on the urban–rural classification by Finnish Environment Institute in which settlements with 15 000 inhabitants or more are considered urban [23].

Statistics

Sociodemographic factors were tested in relation to age group using the Chi‐square test. Poisson regression analyses were performed to test associations between the outcome variables (number of teeth and POPs) and explanatory sociodemographic factors (sex, age, marital status, education level, collaborative area, income bracket and urbanization). Before the regression analysis, bivariate correlations were tested between the explanatory variables with Pearson Correlation (continuous variable, age) or Spearman's Correlation (ordinal variables, all other variables). All correlation coefficients were found to be less than 0.6 and all explanatory variables were eligible for inclusion in the multivariate models. Regression analysis included edentulous participants. To obtain nationally representative results for the target population, the Healthy Finland survey used a stratified cluster sampling design. To take into account this and by using weight coefficients to also account for the non‐response rate, the data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0.2.0 (20) and SAS Callable SUDAAN software (Release 11.0).

Chat gpt was used to optimize text length in discussion section and to create pictures to graphical abstract.

All the edentulous participants were over 55 years old: 11.0% of participants aged 65–74 years and 18.2% of participants aged 75 years or older were edentulous. Prevalence of edentulism was highest in Northern and Eastern Finland collaborative areas (Figure 1).

Discussion

Sociodemographic background factors showed partly similar associations for number of teeth and POPs. Age was negatively associated with number of teeth and POPs. Education and income were positively associated with number of teeth and POPs. Number of teeth was lower in older, widowed and unmarried participants and those living in Eastern or Northern Finland, whereas number of POPs was lower in older, widowed and unmarried participants and those living in rural areas.

Sex

Sex was not significantly associated with tooth count or POPs. In previous studies, edentulism and tooth loss have both been more prevalent among Finnish women than men [15, 24, 25]. In the Mini‐Finland study (1977–1980) men averaged 14.6 teeth and women 11.1, including edentulous participants [16]. In 2000, sex differences still persisted for edentulism but not for tooth count [15]. By 2011, no sex differences were detected [14]. Similar trends have been seen elsewhere: Southern Sweden (2007–2008) showed no differences in number of teeth or edentulism [26]; Norway (2018–2019) reported higher edentulism in older women [27]; Switzerland saw diminishing edentulism differences from 1992 to 2012 [28]; in the US women had slightly higher edentulism from 1957 to 2012 [29]. Also in Brazil (2010), women were less likely to have ≥ 20 teeth [30]. In New Zealand (2009), women aged 65–74 retained more teeth than men, while no sex differences were found in China (2005) [31].

Age

Higher age was associated with fewer teeth and lower POPs. Overall, Finns now retain more natural teeth than ever before. Most participants aged 20–44 had complete dentition, an improvement from earlier studies [14]. Tooth numbers improved across all age groups compared to Mini‐Finland, Health 2000, and Health 2011 [15, 16, 32]. In europe (2013), the mean number of teeth in participants 50 years or older ranged from 14.3 (Estonia) to 24.5 (Sweden), most below 20 teeth [33]. Chinese adults (2005) had more teeth than New Zealanders (2009) [31]. A South Korean study for years 2000–2015 and a Swedish study for years 1973–2013 reported fewer teeth with age and more teeth in younger cohorts [34, 35]. In the US, edentulism declined across successive cohorts between years 1957–2012 [29].

Marital Status

Compared to being married, being unmarried, widowed or of unknown marital status was linked to fewer teeth, and widowhood was associated with fewer POPs. Similar patterns were found in Mexico in 2002–2003, where married individuals had less edentulism than widowed or divorced [36], and in Brazil in 2010, where widowed participants were less likely to have ≥ 20 teeth [30]. In Japan in 2022, older adults without a spouse had fewer teeth, partly explained by better oral health habits among those with a spouse, especially men [37]. Korean data also showed higher tooth counts among participants with a spouse in 2010–2012 [38]. In general in Finland in 2000, married participants were reported to have less general health problems compared to participants without a spouse [39].

Educational Level and Income

In the current study, lower educational level and income showed associations with a lower number of teeth and POPs, aligning with previous research [11, 25, 40]. In the UK, each additional year of education reduced the likelihood of edentulism [10]. In Switzerland (1992–2012), the number of missing teeth decreased with higher education [28]. Similar associations were observed in China and South Africa (2007–2010) [41]. In Brazil, functional dentition (≥ 20 teeth) was more prevalent among more educated and higher income participants in 2010 [30]. Chilean adults with higher education had more teeth and lower edentulism in 2003 and 2016–17 [42]. In Korea (2010–2012), participants with more teeth had a higher prevalence for longer education [38].

In Finland, the public dental service is affordable but there are limitations in its capacity resulting in waiting times of several months before getting non‐urgent treatment [43]. Private dental service has high out‐of‐pocket costs but provides service within days, although private dental service providers are more commonly available in urban areas. In Finland (1997), lower income was linked to edentulism among older adults, especially in Eastern Finland [44]. In Brazil, higher income correlated with lower edentulism prevalence [40]. In Denmark (1998–1999), higher income was associated with better dental status, though no such link was observed in Sweden at the same time among older adults [45].

Area of Residence and Urbanization

Southern Finland, including the capital Helsinki, is the most densely populated and prosperous area of Finland and where 45% of participants lived. Residence in Eastern or Northern Finland was associated with lower tooth count, but not with fewer POPs. POP count was significantly lower in rural compared to urban areas, while the number of teeth showed no significant association with rural residency. This aligns with earlier findings showing higher edentulism and fewer teeth in these regions [24, 25, 44]. In Health 2000, participants in Southern Finland had the best general health and the worst health was in Eastern Finland [39]. POP associations have not been previously reported. Geographical differences in edentulism have also been observed in the US where edentulism prevalences were higher in states with higher poverty [29]. In Switzerland (2002–2012), no urban–rural differences were found in missing teeth, though fixed prostheses and implants were slightly more common in urban areas [28]. In low‐ and middle‐income countries, urbanization has shown mixed associations: in South Africa and Ghana, edentulism was lower in rural areas, attributed to limited dental workforce (less extractions) and lower sugar availability, whereas in China, edentulism was higher in rural areas [41]. In Korea, urban residents were more likely to have higher tooth counts [38].

Edentulism

Edentulism prevalence was low (4.2%) and all edentulous participants were over 55 years old. In Finland, edentulism prevalence declined from 30% in the Mini‐Finland study (1977–1980) to 15% in Health 2000 and 9%–10% in Health 2011 [15, 16, 32]. In the present study, 11.3% of participants aged 60 years or older were edentulous. Globally, prevalence among adults over 50 ranged from 3.0%–21.7% in lower‐ and upper‐middle‐income countries [46], 5.8%–2.2% in Swiss over 15 years olds (1992–2012) [28], and dropped from 13% to 1% in Jönköping, Sweden (1983–2013) in adults 20 years and older [35].

POPs

POP counts aligned with tooth numbers, though associations with sociodemographic factors differed. Roots were recorded as present teeth but not occlusal antagonists, and implants as missing teeth but existing antagonists, partly explaining regression differences. POPs may better reflect impaired oral function than tooth count, as a single missing tooth eliminates an occlusal pair. Rural residence was linked with fewer POPs but not with fewer teeth.

Natural tooth count reflects oral health history, while occlusal pairs—natural or artificial—reflect current function. As complete edentulism becomes rare, adding POPs to epidemiological studies may improve functional assessment. A Brazilian study validated both measures for estimating dental configurations and occlusal contacts [47].

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of the study is its careful design and population representativeness; weighted data should correct for non‐attendance as described by Suominen et al. [13]. The methodology matches earlier national studies, facilitating comparisons. A limitation was the clinical examination sites being only in health care centres, not in participants' homes. In Sweden and other Scandinavian studies, non‐respondents were older and more often edentulous [26, 45]. Many elderly, the main edentulous group, likely reside in care facilities and did not attend; Norwegian data confirm poorer oral health among nursing home residents [27]. Earlier Finnish studies conducted home visits for non‐attendees [15, 32, 48].

Conclusion

Based on this study, the oral health of Finnish adults has improved compared to previous national surveys conducted in 1977–2011 investigating the number of teeth and edentulism. The mean number of teeth among the Finnish population is slightly higher compared to other European countries, although it remains lower than that observed in Sweden. Oral health in Finland is still associated with better sociodemographic background factors, education having the strongest positive effect. Using POPs as an alternative measure to the number of teeth could provide more information on oral function at the population level.
