Impact of Diabetes on the Efficacy of Low‐Voltage Area Ablation for Persistent Atrial Fibrillation: A Subanalysis of the SUPPRESS‐AF Trial
Authors: Tatsuhisa Ozaki, Koichi Inoue, Tsuyoshi Mishima, Takuya Ohashi, Satoshi Osaki, Yasunori Ueda, Masaharu Masuda, Akihiro Sunaga, Nobuaki Tanaka, Tetsuya Watanabe, Hitoshi Minamiguchi, Yasuyuki Egami, Takafumi Oka, Tomoko Minamisaka, Takashi Kanda, Masato Okada, Masato Kawasaki, Yasuhiro Matsuda, Koji Tanaka, Nobuhiko Makino, Hirota Kida, Shungo Hikoso, Tomoharu Dohi, Yohei Sotomi, Yasushi Sakata, the OCVC‐SUPPRESS‐AF investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe potential value of low‐voltage area (LVA) ablation for persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) has been suggested. However, its efficacy determinants remain unclear. We investigated the impact of diabetes on the outcomes of LVA ablation in patients with persistent AF.MethodsThis post hoc subanalysis of the multicenter randomized controlled trial SUPPRESS‐AF (Efficacy and Safety of Low‐Voltage‐Guided Ablation for Recurrence Prevention Compared With Pulmonary Vein Isolation Alone in Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation) included patients with persistent AF undergoing initial ablation. Following pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), patients with LVAs were randomized to PVI+LVA‐ablation or PVI alone. We compared recurrence‐free survival between patients with and without diabetes. The primary end point was freedom from AF/atrial tachycardia recurrence.ResultsOf 1347 patients with persistent AF, 343 (25.5%) had left atrial LVAs. The nondiabetic group comprised 264 patients (136 and 128 in the PVI‐alone and PVI+LVA‐ablation arms, respectively), and the diabetic group comprised 77 patients (35 and 42 in each arm, respectively). No significant difference was observed in LVA extent between the groups. Overall recurrence rates were also similar (44% versus 42%; P=0.658). In the nondiabetic group, the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm showed significantly lower recurrence rates compared with the PVI‐alone arm (35% versus 49%; P=0.018). Conversely, patients with diabetes exhibited higher recurrence rates in the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm compared with those in the PVI‐alone arm (48% versus 40%; P=0.290). A significant interaction was observed between diabetes status and LVA ablation efficacy (P for interaction=0.041).ConclusionsIn this exploratory post hoc analysis, LVA ablation may benefit patients without diabetes, whereas no clear benefit, and a possible signal of futility, was observed in patients with diabetes.Keywords: catheter ablation, diabetes, low‐voltage area, persistent atrial fibrillation, randomized controlled trialSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

The success rate of catheter ablation for persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) with pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) alone remains suboptimal. 1 , 2 , 3 Left atrial low‐voltage area (LVA) ablation has emerged as a potential strategy for improving outcomes. Although previous studies on the efficacy of LVA ablation have shown conflicting results, 4 , 5 , 6 our recent SUPPRESS‐AF (Efficacy and Safety of Low‐Voltage‐Guided Ablation for Recurrence Prevention Compared With Pulmonary Vein Isolation Alone in Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation) randomized controlled trial did not demonstrate superiority in patients receiving additional LVA ablation compared with those receiving PVI alone. 7 Appropriate patient selection may be crucial for understanding this outcome. Notably, prespecified subgroup analyses suggested a significant interaction between diabetes status and the efficacy of LVA ablation.

Diabetes is a well‐established independent risk factor for AF development and for cardioembolic stroke in patients with AF, 8 , 9 and it has been shown to promote left atrial fibrosis and the formation of LVAs. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 However, despite these well‐documented structural changes, it remains unclear whether the presence of diabetes modifies the clinical efficacy of substrate‐based ablation strategies, such as LVA ablation. Prior studies have not specifically examined how diabetes‐related atrial remodeling influences procedural success or recurrence patterns following targeted substrate modification.

Accordingly, we hypothesized that the efficacy of substrate modification by LVA ablation may differ based on diabetes status. Specifically, we predicted that patients without diabetes would benefit from targeted substrate modification, whereas in patients with diabetes, the diffuse and heterogeneous atrial remodeling might attenuate or even negate the therapeutic effect of LVA ablation. The aim of this study was therefore to clarify the impact of diabetes on the effectiveness of LVA ablation in patients with persistent AF, given the significant interaction observed in the SUPPRESS‐AF randomized controlled trial.

METHODS

The data sets analyzed in the current study are not publicly available owing to institutional and ethical restrictions. However, deidentified data may be made available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and with appropriate institutional approvals.

Ethics

The study conforms to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of each hospital.

Consent

All patients provided written informed consent to participate.

Study Design

This study was a post hoc analysis of the SUPPRESS‐AF trial (UMIN000035940). The original SUPPRESS‐AF trial was a prospective, investigator‐initiated, multicenter, randomized, open‐label trial conducted by Osaka Cardiovascular Conference Arrhythmia Investigators at 8 hospitals. The inclusion criteria were adults aged ≥20 years (including both men and women), who were undergoing their first ablation for persistent AF (as defined below), with adequate cognitive capacity to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were a left atrial diameter of ≥55 mm, a history of cardiac surgery, valvular AF, hemodialysis, contraindication to ablation, contraindication to anticoagulant therapy, history of stroke or systemic embolism within the past 6 months, treatable cause of AF, pregnancy, and the physician's judgment of unsuitability for enrollment.

Patients with left atrial LVAs (as defined below) were eligible and subsequently enrolled in this study. They were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to PVI alone (PVI‐alone group) or LVA ablation after PVI (PVI+LVA‐ablation group) immediately after voltage mapping. Randomization allocation was computer generated. The present post hoc study focused on the difference in the effectiveness of PVI alone versus PVI+LVA ablation between patients without and with diabetes (as defined below).

Definitions

Persistent AF was defined as continuous AF lasting for >7 days. Long‐standing persistent AF was defined as continuous AF lasting for >12 months.

Low‐voltage area (LVA) was defined as a region with a bipolar peak‐to‐peak voltage <0.5 mV. The presence of LVA was defined as a total LVA area ≥5 cm2 of the left atrial surface on the voltage map after PVI.

The history of diabetes was assessed before the procedure according to the Japanese guidelines at that time. Diabetes was defined as follows: (1) 2 measurements meeting the diagnostic criteria (fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2‐hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL during an oral glucose tolerance test, random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%); or (2) 1 measurement meeting these criteria in the presence of symptoms of hyperglycemia and/or definite diabetic retinopathy. 14 , 15

Study Procedure

Catheter ablation was performed using a carefully established procedural protocol to minimize variability among facilities and operators. An electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO 3; Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) was used for catheter navigation, ablation guidance, and mapping. Ipsilateral encircling PVI was performed in all patients using an open‐irrigated ablation catheter with a contact force sensor (Thermocool Smarttouch SF; Biosense Webster). A Visitag Surpoint module (Biosense Webster) guided radiofrequency energy application. Target Visitag Surpoint was set at ≥425 for the anterior wall and ≥375 for the posterior wall, with an interlesion distance of ≤4 mm. Throughout the ablation procedure, the following Visitag module settings were used: (1) catheter stability range of motion <2 mm, (2) catheter stability duration >5 seconds, and (3) contact force <5 g (time <25%). After waiting for ≥20 minutes, both entrance and exit blocks were confirmed at each ipsilaterally isolated pulmonary vein. Electrical cardioversion was performed in cases in which AF persisted until the end of the PVI procedure.

Following PVI, left atrial voltage mapping was performed with 100‐ppm pacing from the high right atrium. Magnetic sensor‐enabled multielectrode mapping catheters with a 1‐mm electrode size (Lasso Nav or Pentaray; Biosense Webster) were used. Mapping points were automatically acquired using the Confidense Module (Biosense Webster) until all color gaps on the voltage map were filled in the following settings: cycle length filtering, ±30 ms; local activation time stability, 3 ms; position stability, 2 mm; density, 1 mm; tissue proximity indicator, off; and fill and color interpolation threshold, 10 mm. LVAs were identified as regions with a bipolar peak‐to‐peak voltage of <0.5 mV. The scar level was set to 0.05 mV. Moreover, the area of the LVA was manually measured using the area measurement tool of the CARTO system, and when the total area of the LVA exceeded 5 cm2, left atrial LVA was considered present.

In the PVI+LVA‐ablation group, homogenization ablation covering all LVAs was performed, except for scar areas, where mapping points with a voltage <0.05 mV were concentrated. LVAs located at the posterior wall were isolated from the roof and bottom lines. Each radiofrequency application was guided by a Visitag Surpoint of ≥350 with an interlesion distance of <6 mm. Notably, ablation was omitted at sites where ablation could impair the physiological electrical conduction system or damage collateral structures, including the esophagus.

After the completion of these procedures, AF/atrial tachycardia (AT) was induced by atrial burst stimulation and intravenous administration of isoproterenol. The induced AT and nonpulmonary vein AF triggers were ablated at the discretion of the attending operator. In addition, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation was permitted when a tricuspid isthmus‐dependent atrial flutter was observed.

Follow‐Up and End Points

The patients were followed up for 12 months and were required to visit the cardiology outpatient clinic at 6 and 12 months for medical assessment. A standard 12‐lead ECG in the supine resting position and a 24‐hour Holter ECG during daily life were performed before each outpatient visit. The Holter ECG was recorded using 2 leads: CM5 and NASA. In addition, symptom‐driven ECG recordings for 30 seconds were performed twice daily using a portable ECG (HCG 901 or HCG 801; Omron, Kyoto, Japan) for 6 to 12 months. Patients were instructed to place the electrodes in contact with the index finger and anterior chest, several centimeters below the nipple.

The primary end point of this study was AF/AT recurrence. AF/AT recurrence was defined as 1 of the following events: (1) AF/AT indicated on a scheduled or symptom‐triggered ECG or (2) AF/AT of at least 30‐second duration on Holter ECG monitoring. AF/AT episodes during the 3 months after initial or repeat ablation were not included as recurrence events (blanking period). Antiarrhythmic drug use was not recommended for 3 months after ablation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.68; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 16 Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are reported as means (SDs) and were compared using the Student t‐test, the Mann–Whitney U test, or the paired t‐test, as appropriate. The recurrence rate was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the comparison of survival curves between the PVI‐alone and PVI+LVA‐ablation groups in each cohort was performed using the log‐rank test. We used a Cox proportional hazards model to estimate the impact of the PVI+LVA‐ablation strategy compared with that of the PVI alone strategy in both groups. To address a potential imbalance with long‐standing persistent AF (LSPAF), we additionally performed multivariable Cox analyses, including LSPAF as a covariate. We also conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with persistent AF excluding LSPAF, repeating Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses. The interaction between the ablation strategy and diabetes was also assessed. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 1347 patients who underwent initial ablation for persistent AF between June 2019 and August 2022, 343 (25.5%) with left atrial LVAs were eligible for randomization. Eligible patients were divided into 2 groups based on the presence of diabetes: the nondiabetic group, which consisted of 264 patients (136 in the PVI‐alone arm and 128 in the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm), and the diabetic group, which consisted of 77 patients (35 in the PVI‐alone arm and 42 in the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm) (Figure 1). Compared with the patients in the nondiabetic group, the patients with diabetes were more frequently men and had a higher body mass index, a greater prevalence of dyslipidemia and cardiomyopathy, and elevated congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years, diabetes mellitus, stroke/transient ischemic attack/thromboembolism, vascular disease, age 65 to 74 years, and sex category (CHA2DS2‐VASc scores. The diabetic cohort also demonstrated lower diastolic blood pressure, more frequent use of antiplatelet drugs, higher hemoglobin levels, and increased interventricular septal thickness (Table 1). These differences are summarized in Table S1. In the nondiabetic group, patients who underwent PVI+LVA ablation and those who underwent PVI alone exhibited significant differences with regard to anticoagulant therapy. In the diabetic group, although baseline characteristics were broadly comparable, LSPAF was more frequent in the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm (Table 2).

Procedure‐Related Findings

Procedure‐related findings are presented in Table 3 and Table S2. Notably, no significant difference was observed in the extent of LVA between the diabetic and nondiabetic groups, and the frequency of AT ablation was comparable between the groups (Table 3). In the nondiabetic group, the left atrial surface area was wider, and ablation for regular AT was more frequently performed in the PVI+LVA‐ablation group than in the PVI‐alone group (Table S2). Additionally, the total procedure time, total radiofrequency application time, and total applied energy were significantly greater in the PVI+LVA‐ablation group than in the PVI‐alone group, regardless of diabetes (Table S2).

Clinical End Points and Subanalysis

During the follow‐up period, 17 patients (5.3%) were lost to follow‐up, and overall follow‐up completeness was 94.7%, consistent with the parent SUPPRESS‐AF trial.

The overall AF/AT recurrence rate was comparable between diabetic and nondiabetic groups (44% versus 42%; log‐rank P=0.658; hazard ratio=1.065 [95% CI, 0.73–1.56]; P=0.749) (Figure 2). However, analysis based on diabetes status revealed that the impact of additional ablation differed substantially between the groups. In the nondiabetic group, the recurrence rate of AF/AT was significantly lower in the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm than in the PVI‐alone arm (35% versus 49%; log‐rank P=0.018; hazard ratio=0.642 [95% CI, 0.44–0.94]; P=0.021) (Figure 3A). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the recurrence rate between the PVI‐alone arm and the PVI+LVA‐ablation arm in the diabetic group (40% versus 48%; log‐rank P=0.290; hazard ratio=1.42 [95% CI, 0.72–2.82]; P=0.313) (Figure 3B). Moreover, a significant interaction was observed between the ablation strategy and the presence of diabetes (P for interaction=0.041).

To account for the imbalance in LSPAF between treatment arms, we performed an exploratory multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis including AF type (LSPAF versus persistent AF), diabetes status, ablation strategy, and their interaction. In this adjusted model, LSPAF emerged as an independent predictor of recurrence (hazard ratio=2.07 [95% CI, 1.43–2.98]; P<0.001), whereas the main effect of LVA ablation remained significant in patients without diabetes (hazard ratio=0.65 [95% CI, 0.44–0.94]; P=0.02). The interaction term between diabetes status and LVA ablation showed a consistent direction toward attenuation of treatment efficacy in patients with diabetes; however, it did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (hazard ratio for interaction=2.06 [95% CI, 0.94–4.52]; P=0.07), likely reflecting the limited sample size in the diabetic subgroup and the resulting wide 95% CIs (Table S3). In sensitivity analyses excluding LSPAF, the benefit of additional LVA ablation remained evident in the nondiabetic cohort (31% versus 46%; log‐rank P=0.021; hazard ratio=0.60 [95% CI, 0.38–0.93]; P=0.021), whereas no clear difference was observed in the diabetic cohort (39% versus 38%; log‐rank P=0.267; hazard ratio=1.22 [95% CI, 0.56–2.68]; P=0.267) (Figure S1).

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

In the overall cohort of the SUPPRESS‐AF trial, the addition of LVA ablation did not significantly reduce AF/AT recurrence at 1 year. 7 Given this neutral result, it is important to identify subgroups who may benefit from this strategy. This post hoc subanalysis examined whether diabetes modifies the efficacy of LVA ablation in addition to PVI.

The main findings can be summarized as follows: (1) in patients with persistent AF without diabetes, LVA ablation in addition to PVI significantly reduced AF/AT recurrence compared with PVI alone; (2) in patients with diabetes, no clear benefit of additional LVA ablation was observed, and recurrence rates were numerically higher, suggesting potential futility of this strategy in this subgroup; (3) the interaction analysis (P for interaction=0.041) suggests that diabetes may modify the effect of additional LVA ablation on recurrence, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size, potential residual confounding, and the exploratory nature of the analysis; and (4) the increased recurrence rate in patients with diabetes receiving LVA ablation was predominantly driven by a higher incidence of AT recurrence.

These findings provide important insights into patient stratification for the LVA ablation strategy in persistent AF and highlight the potential effect of diabetes on ablation outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first study to suggest that the efficacy of LVA ablation may be modified by diabetes status in a randomized controlled trial setting.

Impact of LVA Ablation on Persistent AF

Several randomized controlled trials investigating left atrial LVA ablation have been conducted; however, their results have been inconsistent, and no definitive conclusion has been reached on its efficacy. 4 , 5 , 6 To overcome this limitation, the SUPPRESS‐AF trial used a unique design, randomizing only patients with documented left atrial LVAs on voltage mapping after PVI, thereby enabling the efficient evaluation of additional LVA ablation effects in patients with persistent AF. 7 Although the primary analysis yielded a neutral outcome, the present subanalysis suggests that the therapeutic value of substrate modification is not uniform and may be influenced by underlying comorbidities, particularly diabetes.

Diabetes as a Risk Factor for LVA Formation

Diabetes is an independent risk factor for atrial LVA formation. 17 , 18 Additionally, poor glycemic control increases the prevalence of LVAs. 19 The pathophysiological mechanisms are complex and involve multiple pathways. First, chronic hyperglycemia leads to advanced glycation end‐product accumulation and chronic inflammation, promoting myocardial fibrosis and collagen cross‐linking. 20 Second, gap junction dysfunction and ion channel abnormalities result in decreased electrical conduction velocity and heterogeneous conduction patterns. 12 , 21 , 22 Third, microvascular dysfunction causes local ischemia and tissue necrosis, accelerating fibrosis and leading to more extensive LVA formation. 23 Collectively, these factors contribute to the development of LVAs and the creation of substrates for AF and AT.

Differential Effects of LVA Ablation in Patients With Diabetes

One of the most notable findings of our subanalysis is the borderline interaction between diabetes status and the efficacy of LVA ablation (P for interaction=0.041). This effect modification was observed despite no significant difference in the baseline extent of LVAs between patients with and without diabetes (Table 3). Although age also showed a marginal interaction (P for interaction=0.047), other clinical factors, such as sex and left atrial size, did not substantially modify the treatment effect. Among the subgroups tested, diabetes status showed a distinct pattern of effect modification (Figure S2).

We previously conducted the Effect of Extensive Ablation on Recurrence in Patients with Persistent Atrial Fibrillation Treated (EARNEST) PVI trial, which evaluated the effectiveness of additional ablation in patients with persistent AF. 24 Subanalyses of that trial demonstrate that additional ablation offered greater benefit in the presence of factors indicative of an advanced arrhythmogenic substrate, such as older age, prolonged AF duration, more significant mitral regurgitation, and a higher diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, persistent form of atrial fibrillation, left atrial diameter >45 mm, age >65 years, female sex, and hypertension (DR‐FLASH) score, a composite clinical score developed to estimate the severity of atrial arrhythmogenic substrate. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 By comparison, a diabetes‐focused subanalysis suggested that additional ablation beyond PVI did not provide incremental benefit in patients with diabetes, whereas a significant reduction in AF recurrence was observed in those without diabetes. 29 This contrast is consistent with our findings and suggests that diabetes may differ fundamentally from other substrate‐related factors, potentially representing a distinct phenotype of atrial cardiomyopathy in which conventional substrate modification is less effective.

The differential effect of LVA ablation between patients with and without diabetes may reflect differences in underlying atrial cardiomyopathy and myocardial substrate characteristics within or adjacent to LVAs.

From the perspective of atrial cardiomyopathy, in conditions such as hypertension and heart failure, elevated left atrial pressure promotes atrial remodeling, leading to regional myocardial degeneration owing to heterogeneous wall stress distribution according to the Laplace law, particularly in thin or dilated areas. This spatial heterogeneity may lead to nonuniform (patchy) structural and electrical remodeling within the atria. Such regionally distributed fibrotic changes can be delineated as discrete LVAs by bipolar voltage mapping and are therefore amenable to targeted ablation.

In contrast to hemodynamic‐driven remodeling, diabetes as a metabolic disorder may promote diffuse atrial myocardial damage, leading to widespread arrhythmogenic substrate formation beyond the LVA regions. 30 Mechanistically, diabetes has been associated with diffuse interstitial fibrosis driven by chronic metabolic impairment. This interstitial remodeling may impair myocardial conduction while preserving bipolar voltage amplitude and thereby result in functionally abnormal but electrically “normal‐appearing” myocardium. As a result, conventional voltage‐based mapping may underestimate the extent of the arrhythmogenic substrate in diabetic atria, thereby limiting the effectiveness of LVA‐guided ablation strategies.

In the present study, patients with diabetes exhibited higher recurrence rates following LVA ablation; however, this difference was attributable not to AF recurrence but to an increased incidence of AT (Figure 4C and 4D). Notably, among the patients who underwent PVI alone, the incidence of AT was similar between the diabetic and nondiabetic groups (Figure 4C), suggesting that differences in atrial cardiomyopathy alone do not fully account for the observed increase in AT. LVA ablation was associated with a higher incidence of AT specifically in patients with diabetes, but not in those without diabetes (Figure 4C), indicating that the local electrophysiological properties of myocardium within or adjacent to the LVA likely play a critical role in determining susceptibility to AT through interaction with ablation lesions.

Two potential mechanisms may explain the increased AT occurrence in patients with diabetes. First, reentrant circuits may more readily form between ablated LVA regions and anatomic barriers (such as PVI lesions or valve annuli), possibly because of the presence of adjacent arrhythmogenic substrate. Second, the standard ablation protocol (Visitag Surpoint ≥350 with an interlesion distance <6 mm) may be insufficient to achieve complete substrate modification in diabetic atria, resulting in residual conduction pathways that facilitate AT.

Pathologic Heterogeneity in LVA

Although LVA presents an electrophysiological phenotype, the underlying pathology is heterogeneous. Histopathologic examination of atrial biopsies by Takahashi et al 31 demonstrated that LVA formation involves multiple histologic changes, including fibrosis, intercellular space expansion, myofibrillar loss, and alterations in myocardial nuclear density. Diabetes may influence both the histopathologic features and their spatial heterogeneity, thereby altering the substrate properties of LVA. This pathologic heterogeneity appears to influence the efficacy of LVA ablation. Therefore, patient selection for an LVA ablation strategy should incorporate both quantitative parameters (presence and extent of LVAs) and a qualitative assessment of the underlying pathologic substrate. Although further investigation is warranted to delineate the specific pathophysiological features of diabetes‐related LVA, our findings suggest that diabetes status serves as a valuable marker for the qualitative assessment of LVA, enabling the stratification of patients who may benefit from LVA ablation.

Future Perspective

Although our current findings suggest limited efficacy of conventional LVA ablation in patients with diabetes, future investigations should identify modified approaches that could provide therapeutic benefits in this population. Although our study was limited to conventional voltage amplitude measurements, future studies should evaluate additional electrophysiological parameters, such as conduction delay and fractionated electrograms, which may provide valuable insights into the pathologic substrate of diabetes‐related LVA. Although we adopted standard protocols for the ablation settings and LVA definition (<0.5 mV), patients with diabetes may benefit from modified approaches, such as more intensive ablation of the LVA to avoid AT recurrence or more extensive substrate modification, including areas adjacent to conventional LVA or intermediate voltage areas (e.g., 0.5–1.0 mV). These considerations warrant further investigation to optimize ablation strategies in patients with diabetes.

Study Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, data on glycemic control, diabetes duration, and therapeutic regimens were unavailable. Second, because the study population exclusively comprises East Asian individuals, the generalizability of the results to other ethnic groups requires careful consideration. Third, although treatment allocation was randomized in the parent trial, the present analysis represents a post hoc subgroup analysis and may therefore be affected by chance imbalances in prognostic factors, including LSPAF. To address this concern, we performed multivariable analyses adjusting for LSPAF and conducted sensitivity analyses. Although these analyses demonstrated overall consistency in the direction of the findings, statistical precision remained limited because of the small sample size, particularly in the diabetes subgroup. Importantly, the interaction between diabetes status and ablation strategy reached only borderline statistical significance (P for interaction=0.041) in the context of multiple subgroup comparisons, and the possibility of a chance finding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, intraprocedural electrophysiological end points, such as AF termination or changes in AF cycle length during LVA ablation, were not systematically evaluated. Consequently, the immediate impact of LVA ablation on the arrhythmogenic substrate could not be assessed, and we cannot definitively determine whether the increased incidence of AT in patients with diabetes resulted from incomplete substrate modification or the creation of new proarrhythmic circuits. Additionally, the absence of continuous monitoring with implantable loop recorders may have led to an underestimation of asymptomatic recurrences. Finally, as this was an exploratory post hoc subgroup analysis without a prespecified power calculation for the interaction effect, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis generating and require validation in future prospective studies.

CONCLUSIONS

In this post hoc exploratory analysis, diabetes was associated with differential outcomes following LVA ablation in patients with persistent AF. Although a potential benefit of LVA ablation was suggested in patients without diabetes, no clear benefit, and a possible signal of futility with increased AT recurrence, was observed in patients with diabetes. Given the borderline interaction signal and the potential for residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis generating. Further prospective studies are warranted to clarify whether diabetes truly modifies the efficacy of substrate‐based ablation strategies.
