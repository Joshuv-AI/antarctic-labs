Navigating social isolation: a qualitative study of Iranian mothers of autistic children
Authors: Atefeh Shamsi, Imane Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis qualitative study explored the social isolation experiences reported by mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran.MethodsTwenty-two mothers of autistic children aged 4–12 years participated in semi-structured interviews conducted in Yazd, Iran. Data were analyzed inductively using conventional content analysis. The reporting of the study was strengthened using the COREQ checklist, including additional details on the interviewer, recruitment, interview procedures, coding, trustworthiness, and consistency between the thematic table and the Results section.ResultsOne main theme, social isolation, was developed from participants’ accounts. Three categories were identified: stigma and social judgment, inadequate formal and informal support, and caregiving burden and defensive withdrawal. The twelve subcategories reported in the thematic table are now aligned with the Results section. Participants’ accounts suggested that social isolation was linked mainly to stigmatizing social responses, community misunderstanding, limited family and service support, therapy-related demands, and mothers’ protective withdrawal from social settings.ConclusionsIn this sample of 22 mothers in Yazd, social isolation appeared to be shaped by the interaction between autism-related caregiving demands and the surrounding social and cultural environment. The findings support the need for culturally sensitive public awareness, family education, and accessible support services. Because this was a single-city qualitative study based on mothers’ self-reported experiences, the findings should be transferred cautiously to other Iranian or international contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05016-0.

Lay abstract

This study explored how 22 mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran, described their experiences of social isolation. Mothers reported that isolation was often linked to judgmental gazes, insensitive comments, misconceptions about autism, limited understanding from family or community members, and difficulty accessing practical support. They also described the demanding nature of therapy schedules and the need to withdraw from some social situations to protect themselves and their children from stressful reactions. These findings should not be interpreted as proving that autism itself causes isolation. Rather, participants' accounts suggest that social responses to autism and insufficient support played an important role in their experiences. The study was conducted in one Iranian city; therefore, the findings may be relevant to similar contexts but should not be assumed to represent all Iranian families of autistic children.

Introduction

Autism is a complex developmental condition characterized by differences in social communication, interaction, behavior, sensory processing, and daily functioning. Globally, approximately 1 in 100 children is estimated to meet diagnostic criteria for autism [1]. Although autism is lifelong, the well-being of autistic children and their families is substantially influenced by the social environment, access to support, and the degree to which communities understand and accommodate neurodevelopmental differences.

Mothers of autistic children often experience distinctive caregiving responsibilities related to therapy coordination, advocacy, family roles, and social expectations. In many settings, mothers are expected to organize health care and educational support while also managing public reactions to autism-related behaviors. These demands may be associated with emotional exhaustion, reduced social participation, and lower quality of life [2, 3]. However, qualitative research is needed to clarify how mothers themselves describe the social processes through which isolation develops.

In Iran, expectations regarding motherhood and child behavior are influenced by family-centered values, community interdependence, and concerns about social reputation. Mothers of autistic children may encounter cultural taboos around neurodevelopmental differences, pressure to maintain family honor, and limited public understanding of autism. Extended family members may also play influential roles in child-rearing decisions and in mothers’ access to emotional or practical support [4, 5]. Understanding how these cultural factors shape mothers’ social experiences is essential for developing contextually appropriate support interventions.

Previous research has examined stigma, stress, and support needs among parents of autistic children in several cultural contexts [2, 4–8]. Iranian studies have also identified treatment-related challenges, diagnostic delays, family stress, and support needs [4, 5, 9]. Nevertheless, fewer studies have focused specifically on the social interaction experiences through which mothers of autistic children in Iran describe becoming socially isolated. This study therefore aimed to explore the social isolation experiences of mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran, and to identify the social, familial, service-related, and cultural factors that participants perceived as contributing to isolation.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study used conventional content analysis to explore the social interaction and social isolation experiences of mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran. Conventional content analysis was selected because it allows categories and themes to be derived inductively from participants’ accounts when existing theory is insufficient to explain the phenomenon in a specific context [10]. The study was conducted between September 2023 and February 2024. Reporting was strengthened in accordance with the COREQ checklist for interviews and focus groups [11].

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited from therapy centers, special education schools, and autism parent associations in Yazd to obtain variation in mothers’ social and service experiences. Purposive sampling was used. Eligible participants were mothers who met the following criteria: (a) being the mother of a child aged 4–12 years with a confirmed autism diagnosis; (b) the child was currently receiving at least one formal therapeutic service, such as behavioral therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, or a combination of services; (c) the mother was the primary caregiver; and (d) the mother was willing to share her experiences in a Persian-language interview. Autism diagnosis was verified through review of children’s available medical records, confirming diagnosis by a pediatric psychiatrist or developmental pediatrician using DSM-5 criteria.

Mothers of children with only suspected, but not formally confirmed, autism were excluded. Mothers who were unable to communicate in Persian were also excluded. Potential participants were informed about the study through participating centers and parent associations and were then contacted by the research team if they expressed interest. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached. The number of eligible mothers who declined participation or did not respond to the invitation was not formally recorded; this is acknowledged as a reporting limitation.

Research team and reflexivity

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author, a female nursing researcher with training in qualitative interviewing and experience in qualitative health research. The interviewer was affiliated with an academic research center and was not involved in the participants’ clinical care or their children’s therapy services. No relationship was established with participants before recruitment. Participants were informed that the purpose of the study was to understand mothers’ social interaction experiences and perceived sources of social isolation. Before and during data collection, the interviewer maintained reflexive notes to record assumptions related to her professional background, expectations about caregiving burden, and possible influence on probing during interviews.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted in Persian at locations selected by participants, most commonly in a private room at therapy centers or at the researcher’s university. Interviews lasted between 45 and 90 min. Only the participant and interviewer were involved in the interview conversation. When interviews were conducted in institutional settings, privacy was maintained and no service provider took part in the interview. Each participant completed one interview; repeat interviews were not conducted. All interviews were audio-recorded with written consent and transcribed verbatim in Persian. Brief field notes were made after interviews to document contextual observations and reflexive impressions.

The interview guide was developed based on a review of relevant literature and input from three professionals: a child psychiatrist, a special education teacher, and a clinical psychologist. The guide was piloted with two mothers who were not included in the final sample, and wording was refined for clarity. The guide included open questions and probes, such as:

“Can you describe your daily routine and how your child’s diagnosis affects your social life?”

“Can you describe your daily routine and how your child’s diagnosis affects your social life?”

“Can you tell me about experiences in which you felt judged or misunderstood because of your child’s autism?”

“Can you tell me about experiences in which you felt judged or misunderstood because of your child’s autism?”

“What kinds of support have been most helpful to you? What kinds of support have been lacking?”

“How has your relationship with family, friends, and community changed since your child’s diagnosis?”

Thematic saturation was defined as the point at which no substantially new codes, categories, or interpretive insights emerged from additional interviews. The research team judged that saturation had been reached after 18 interviews, and four further interviews were conducted to confirm the stability of the analytic structure. Full transcripts were not routinely returned to participants for correction; however, a brief summary of emerging categories was discussed with two participants as part of member checking.

Translation procedures

Participant quotations presented in this manuscript were translated from Persian into English by the first author and reviewed by a professional translator for linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence. Translation aimed to preserve meaning while ensuring natural English expression. Where an English wording risked altering cultural nuance, the Persian transcript was consulted and the translation was revised.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis following Hsieh and Shannon [10]. Analysis proceeded through five iterative stages: (1) repeated reading of transcripts to achieve immersion; (2) line-by-line open coding of meaning units relevant to social interaction, isolation, support, stigma, and caregiving demands; (3) comparison of codes across transcripts; (4) clustering related codes into subcategories and categories; and (5) development of an overarching theme that integrated the categories. No predetermined theme list was imposed; the final thematic structure was derived inductively from the interview data. Coding and category development were managed using a structured coding matrix. Two researchers independently coded all transcripts. Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. The final coding tree, consisting of one main theme, three categories, and twelve subcategories, is reported in Table 2 and is used consistently throughout the Results and Discussion.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Trustworthiness was strengthened using strategies informed by Guba and Lincoln [12]. Credibility was supported through prolonged engagement with the transcripts, peer debriefing, consensus coding, and member checking with two participants. Transferability was supported by describing the study setting, sampling strategy, participant characteristics, and cultural context. Dependability was enhanced by documenting key analytic decisions in an audit trail. Confirmability was supported through reflexive notes and independent coding by two researchers. The use of participant quotations throughout the Results was intended to show the link between data and interpretations.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.SSU.REC.1403.122). All participants provided written informed consent before data collection. Participants were informed of the study aims, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Pseudonyms or participant numbers are used throughout the manuscript.

Results

Participant characteristics

Twenty-two mothers participated in this study. Their ages ranged from 26 to 45 years (mean = 34.5 years). Nine participants were employed outside the home, and 13 were housewives or unemployed. The educational levels of participants ranged from primary education to university degree. All participants were married. The ages of their autistic children ranged from 4 to 12 years (mean = 7.2 years). Regarding autism presentation, 14 children were verbal and 8 were non-verbal or minimally verbal. Eight children had co-occurring intellectual developmental disorders and 6 had co-occurring attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Duration since diagnosis ranged from 1 to 8 years.(Table 1).

Category 1: Stigma and social judgment

This category described mothers’ experiences of being watched, criticized, pitied, blamed, or avoided because of public interpretations of their children’s autism-related behaviors. Mothers did not describe isolation as a simple consequence of autism diagnosis alone; rather, their accounts emphasized how social reactions to autism shaped their withdrawal from public and family spaces.

Subcategory 1.1: Judgmental gazes and stares

Mothers frequently described public staring as one of the earliest and most persistent forms of social judgment. These gazes were experienced not as neutral observation but as criticism of both the child and the mother’s parenting.

“Going to the park used to be one of our favorite things to do, but now the constant stares of others make us avoid it. People do not understand our situation, and their judgment makes me feel like a failure.” (Participant 12).

“Going to the park used to be one of our favorite things to do, but now the constant stares of others make us avoid it. People do not understand our situation, and their judgment makes me feel like a failure.” (Participant 12).

“Every time my son has a meltdown at the supermarket, I feel the judgmental stares of people around us. They do not realize he is not being naughty; it is the nature of this condition.” (Participant 18).

“Every time my son has a meltdown at the supermarket, I feel the judgmental stares of people around us. They do not realize he is not being naughty; it is the nature of this condition.” (Participant 18).

“When my daughter exhibits hand-flapping mannerisms, others at the park stare at us. I can feel the judgment in their eyes, as if they believe I am an inferior parent.” (Participant 21).

“When my daughter exhibits hand-flapping mannerisms, others at the park stare at us. I can feel the judgment in their eyes, as if they believe I am an inferior parent.” (Participant 21).

These accounts show that mothers interpreted staring as a stigmatizing social response that made ordinary community activities emotionally unsafe.

Subcategory 1.2: Offensive and insensitive comments

Mothers also reported direct comments that framed their children’s behaviors as misbehavior or their own parenting as inadequate. Even comments intended as sympathy were sometimes experienced as pity and reinforced a sense of difference.

“Several times in restaurants or stores, my son has been judged and criticized for being loud or behaving differently. For example, once a woman angrily told me to control my child. These encounters are very upsetting and make me feel like my son and I are different from everyone else.” (Participant 8).

“Several times in restaurants or stores, my son has been judged and criticized for being loud or behaving differently. For example, once a woman angrily told me to control my child. These encounters are very upsetting and make me feel like my son and I are different from everyone else.” (Participant 8).

“Some of my friends looked at me with pity and said things like, ‘It is so hard’ or ‘I hope he gets better.’ Instead of being comforting, these comments only made me feel more ashamed and embarrassed.” (Participant 6).

“Some of my friends looked at me with pity and said things like, ‘It is so hard’ or ‘I hope he gets better.’ Instead of being comforting, these comments only made me feel more ashamed and embarrassed.” (Participant 6).

“People often look at me with sad eyes, as if my child is a burden, rather than seeing the joy and unique perspective he brings to my life.” (Participant 20).

“People often look at me with sad eyes, as if my child is a burden, rather than seeing the joy and unique perspective he brings to my life.” (Participant 20).

These quotations indicate that insensitive comments, blame, and pity can all operate as social judgments that contribute to mothers’ reluctance to participate in public life.

Subcategory 1.3: Social exclusion and avoidance

Participants described losing social relationships, being avoided by others, or avoiding events themselves in anticipation of negative responses.

“Since the time my son was diagnosed with autism, my friends have slowly vanished. They would make excuses to leave parties we went to, trying to get away as quickly as possible. We thought that we could no longer be a part of their group, and it has been really hard to keep friendships like before.” (Participant 2).

“Since the time my son was diagnosed with autism, my friends have slowly vanished. They would make excuses to leave parties we went to, trying to get away as quickly as possible. We thought that we could no longer be a part of their group, and it has been really hard to keep friendships like before.” (Participant 2).

“I cannot even do simple things like grocery shopping anymore because of people’s hurtful stares and sometimes rude comments. I am tired of constantly having to explain autism to others. I miss being able to do a normal grocery run.” (Participant 5).

“I cannot even do simple things like grocery shopping anymore because of people’s hurtful stares and sometimes rude comments. I am tired of constantly having to explain autism to others. I miss being able to do a normal grocery run.” (Participant 5).

“When we go out, my son’s behavior is not predictable, and I can see people giving us strange looks. It becomes extremely stressful socializing, so I try to avoid it whenever possible.” (Participant 15).

“When we go out, my son’s behavior is not predictable, and I can see people giving us strange looks. It becomes extremely stressful socializing, so I try to avoid it whenever possible.” (Participant 15).

These experiences show how social exclusion was produced through both other people’s avoidance and mothers’ anticipatory avoidance of settings perceived as judgmental.

Subcategory 1.4: Internalized pressure to conceal diagnosis

Some mothers described concealment as a strategy for reducing stigma. This concealment was not presented as a preference for privacy alone; it was linked to fear of stereotyping, family expectations, and the social consequences of disclosure.

“There are a lot of biases toward autism, and so I conceal my daughter’s condition from individuals who are not aware of our circumstances. It appears that discussing the matter and becoming more aware only makes society despise us more and causes us to be stereotyped in another manner.” (Participant 16).

“There are a lot of biases toward autism, and so I conceal my daughter’s condition from individuals who are not aware of our circumstances. It appears that discussing the matter and becoming more aware only makes society despise us more and causes us to be stereotyped in another manner.” (Participant 16).

“My family thinks that if they make people aware that my child has autism, people will avoid them. They want me to tell people that my child has ADHD instead of autism when I am interacting with others.” (Participant 11).

“My family thinks that if they make people aware that my child has autism, people will avoid them. They want me to tell people that my child has ADHD instead of autism when I am interacting with others.” (Participant 11).

“I have decided to keep our family life very private because I do not think I can stand people’s stares and comments. Better not be in public view.” (Participant 7).

“I have decided to keep our family life very private because I do not think I can stand people’s stares and comments. Better not be in public view.” (Participant 7).

Concealment therefore functioned as a protective response to anticipated stigma, but it also reduced opportunities for support and open communication.

Category 2: Inadequate formal and informal support

This revised category reports support-related data more explicitly. It includes mothers’ accounts of limited understanding from relatives and friends, community misinformation, hesitation to seek support because of stigma, and fragmented professional guidance or service access.

Subcategory 2.1: Limited understanding within family and friendship networks

Participants commonly described family members and friends as central to social life but not always as reliable sources of understanding. Some relatives interpreted mothers’ decisions to avoid gatherings as overprotection or exaggeration.

“Even my family does not fully understand. They think I am overreacting when I decline invitations because they do not see the daily challenges we face. Living with a child with autism is like living in a different world.” (Participant 14).

“Even my family does not fully understand. They think I am overreacting when I decline invitations because they do not see the daily challenges we face. Living with a child with autism is like living in a different world.” (Participant 14).

“Before my daughter’s diagnosis, our weekends were full of family gatherings, but gradually these visits became exhausting, and we had to cancel family plans. Explaining autism to everyone who has not experienced it is difficult, and sometimes I feel judged.” (Participant 7).

“Before my daughter’s diagnosis, our weekends were full of family gatherings, but gradually these visits became exhausting, and we had to cancel family plans. Explaining autism to everyone who has not experienced it is difficult, and sometimes I feel judged.” (Participant 7).

In these accounts, lack of informal support was not limited to absence of help; it also included relatives’ limited recognition of the daily work involved in caregiving.

Subcategory 2.2: Inadequate community support and misinformation

Community misinformation about autism shaped mothers’ experiences of support. Mothers described assumptions that autistic children were emotionally detached, undisciplined, or less affected because their condition was not physically visible.

“Other people tend to think that because my son does not make eye contact, he does not care. Really, he is very emotional and sensitive, but he may not appear to be.” (Participant 13).

“Other people tend to think that because my son does not make eye contact, he does not care. Really, he is very emotional and sensitive, but he may not appear to be.” (Participant 13).

“Some people think that because my child does not have visible physical symptoms, her condition must not be that serious. They do not see the daily battles we face with sensory and communication issues.” (Participant 19).

“Some people think that because my child does not have visible physical symptoms, her condition must not be that serious. They do not see the daily battles we face with sensory and communication issues.” (Participant 19).

“I wish people would stop pitying us and instead ask how they can help or learn more about autism.” (Participant 2).

“I wish people would stop pitying us and instead ask how they can help or learn more about autism.” (Participant 2).

These accounts suggest that misinformation reduced meaningful community support and contributed to mothers’ sense that they needed to explain or defend their children repeatedly.

Subcategory 2.3: Barriers to support seeking

Stigma also affected mothers’ willingness to use available support resources. Some mothers avoided support groups or community services because participation itself could reveal the diagnosis or expose them to judgment.

“I was introduced to support resources by the association, but I avoided joining support groups because I was worried about what people would think of me.” (Participant 15).

“I was introduced to support resources by the association, but I avoided joining support groups because I was worried about what people would think of me.” (Participant 15).

This subcategory shows that the presence of support resources does not necessarily mean that mothers experience them as accessible. Social safety, confidentiality, and stigma reduction were described as important conditions for support seeking.

Subcategory 2.4: Fragmented professional guidance and service access

Mothers also described service-related difficulties, including the burden of navigating multiple professionals and the confusion created by inconsistent advice. These experiences contributed to the perception that support systems were fragmented rather than coordinated.

“We interviewed several experts, each with their type of guidance for doing better at our current social and family relationship, but none of them presented a better situation that we felt was heading in the right direction. It only made us more confused as to what the right approach was.” (Participant 10).

“We interviewed several experts, each with their type of guidance for doing better at our current social and family relationship, but none of them presented a better situation that we felt was heading in the right direction. It only made us more confused as to what the right approach was.” (Participant 10).

“I have to plan everything around therapy sessions and procedures for my son. Socializing is an unheard-of luxury.” (Participant 9).

“I have to plan everything around therapy sessions and procedures for my son. Socializing is an unheard-of luxury.” (Participant 9).

The support gap therefore included both insufficient informal understanding and practical difficulties in obtaining coordinated, accessible, and usable professional support.

Category 3: Caregiving burden and defensive withdrawal

The third category captured how therapy schedules, constant vigilance, emotional fatigue, and anticipated public judgment shaped mothers’ withdrawal from social life. The label was revised to emphasize that withdrawal was often defensive rather than passive.

Subcategory 3.1: Exhausting therapy and care routines

Mothers described their schedules as being organized around therapy sessions, home routines, and their children’s daily needs. These demands limited time for friendships and social participation.

“I used to hang out with a small group of friends, but now I rarely see them. My child’s therapies and routines take up so much time that I do not socialize as much anymore.” (Participant 10).

“I used to hang out with a small group of friends, but now I rarely see them. My child’s therapies and routines take up so much time that I do not socialize as much anymore.” (Participant 10).

“I have to plan everything around therapy sessions and procedures for my son. Socializing is an unheard-of luxury.” (Participant 9).

“I have to plan everything around therapy sessions and procedures for my son. Socializing is an unheard-of luxury.” (Participant 9).

Therapy-related demands were therefore connected to isolation not only because they consumed time, but also because they required continuous planning and reduced flexibility.

Subcategory 3.2: Restricted personal time and reduced social participation

Participants reported that caregiving substantially reduced their personal time, hobbies, and peer relationships.

“Ever since my son was diagnosed with autism, I felt as though my whole life had changed. I could no longer go out with my peers or pursue any hobbies. I was busy twenty-four seven, taking care of my son and feeling very much alone.” (Participant 4).

“Ever since my son was diagnosed with autism, I felt as though my whole life had changed. I could no longer go out with my peers or pursue any hobbies. I was busy twenty-four seven, taking care of my son and feeling very much alone.” (Participant 4).

“I have to be constantly aware of my son’s needs, which means that I cannot completely relax and enjoy being part of a social setting. My body is there, but not my mind.” (Participant 17).

“I have to be constantly aware of my son’s needs, which means that I cannot completely relax and enjoy being part of a social setting. My body is there, but not my mind.” (Participant 17).

These accounts illustrate how mothers could be physically present in social spaces while feeling unable to participate fully because of ongoing responsibility and vigilance.

Subcategory 3.3: Emotional exhaustion and constant vigilance

The unpredictability of social situations produced emotional exhaustion. Mothers described preparing for possible negative reactions and monitoring their children continuously.

“I never know how my daughter will react in social situations, so I avoid them to prevent any potential unpleasant incidents.” (Participant 14).

“I never know how my daughter will react in social situations, so I avoid them to prevent any potential unpleasant incidents.” (Participant 14).

“No matter what we try with therapies and social skills interventions, my son simply cannot find friendships. It feels like our efforts are always fruitless.” (Participant 22).

“No matter what we try with therapies and social skills interventions, my son simply cannot find friendships. It feels like our efforts are always fruitless.” (Participant 22).

The emotional burden was intensified when mothers perceived that their efforts to support their children’s social participation did not lead to acceptance from others.

Subcategory 3.4: Seclusion as a protective strategy

For several mothers, withdrawal from social settings became a strategy for reducing immediate stress and protecting the child and family from judgment.

“We have pretty much stopped going out as much as possible and avoid situations where we are going to be the center of attention.” (Participant 10).

“We have pretty much stopped going out as much as possible and avoid situations where we are going to be the center of attention.” (Participant 10).

“We used to attend gatherings, but the constant staring or whispering bothered me a lot. Now, we only go out when it is necessary and have decided it is better to focus on our daily routines.” (Participant 7).

“We used to attend gatherings, but the constant staring or whispering bothered me a lot. Now, we only go out when it is necessary and have decided it is better to focus on our daily routines.” (Participant 7).

Although seclusion reduced exposure to stigmatizing interactions, it also reinforced mothers’ longer-term isolation from community life.

Discussion

This study explored social isolation as described by 22 mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran. The revised findings show one main theme, social isolation, with three categories: stigma and social judgment, inadequate formal and informal support, and caregiving burden and defensive withdrawal. In response to reviewer concerns, the thematic table, Results headings, and Discussion now use the same terminology and analytic structure.

The findings should be interpreted as participants’ accounts and as qualitative interpretations of those accounts, not as causal evidence. Mothers’ narratives suggested that much of their isolation was linked to stigmatizing social responses, community misunderstanding, insufficient support, and the cumulative demands of caregiving. This interpretation is consistent with prior research showing that parents of autistic children may experience courtesy stigma, social rejection, and reduced participation in community life [12–15].

Stigma and social judgment

The first category highlighted how judgmental gazes, insensitive comments, social avoidance, and concealment pressure shaped mothers’ social withdrawal. Mothers frequently described being treated as inadequate parents when their children behaved differently in public. These findings are aligned with stigma research showing that autism-related behaviors may be interpreted through culturally available stereotypes and that parents can experience both felt and enacted stigma [13, 14]. In the present study, stigma was not only a public phenomenon; it also entered family relationships through pressure to conceal the diagnosis or relabel autism as another condition perceived as less socially threatening.

The revised manuscript avoids stating that autism itself does or does not cause isolation. Instead, the data are interpreted as showing that mothers perceived social responses to autism as central to their isolation. This distinction is important because it shifts attention from individual blame toward the need for more informed and accepting communities.

Inadequate formal and informal support

The second category was revised substantially because the previous subcategories overlapped with stigma. The revised category now focuses on support-related findings: limited family and friendship understanding, community misinformation, barriers to support seeking, and fragmented professional guidance or service access. This revision better reflects the data and directly addresses the reviewer’s concern that support systems must be supported by formal and informal support evidence.

Informal support was especially important in this context because family networks are central to social life and caregiving. When relatives minimized mothers’ concerns or interpreted avoidance of gatherings as overreaction, mothers felt unsupported even within close relationships. Prior research on social support among mothers of autistic children indicates that both network size and the perceived effectiveness of support are related to maternal well-being [16]. Therefore, interventions should consider not only whether support exists, but also whether mothers experience it as informed, respectful, and practically useful.

Professional and service support also emerged as fragmented. Participants described multiple appointments, inconsistent guidance, and difficulty integrating therapy demands with family and social life. Iranian literature has similarly identified diagnostic, treatment, and support-related challenges among families of autistic children [4, 5, 9]. These findings suggest that accessible, coordinated, and culturally sensitive services may reduce some of the burden that currently falls on mothers.

Caregiving burden and defensive withdrawal

The third category captured the time, emotional labor, and vigilance associated with caregiving. Mothers reported that therapy routines and constant monitoring reduced personal time and limited social participation. Previous research has documented elevated parenting stress among parents of autistic children [17–20]. The present study extends this evidence by showing how mothers described stress as socially situated: exhaustion was intensified when public environments were perceived as judgmental or unsupportive.

The phrase defensive withdrawal is used to emphasize that seclusion was often an active protective strategy rather than simple passivity. Mothers withdrew to avoid public criticism, reduce stress for their children, and protect family dignity. However, this strategy can produce a cycle in which avoidance reduces immediate distress but increases longer-term isolation.

Cultural context and transferability

The findings need to be understood within the cultural and social context of Yazd, Iran. Participants described family reputation, social expectations regarding child behavior, and pressure to conceal diagnosis as important contextual factors. These findings may be relevant to other settings in which family reputation, community visibility, and extended family judgments strongly shape caregiving experiences. However, the study should not be interpreted as representing all Iranian mothers of autistic children. Iran is socially, economically, ethnically, and geographically diverse, and mothers in other regions may experience different forms of support, stigma, or service access.

Implications for practice and policy

The implications of this study should be interpreted cautiously because of the qualitative, single-city design. First, public education about autism may help reduce stigmatizing gazes, parental blame, and misinformation. Such education should avoid deficit-focused messaging and should emphasize respectful interaction with autistic children and their families. Second, support programs should include extended family members where culturally appropriate, because relatives may strongly influence mothers’ decisions about disclosure, social participation, and therapy. Third, support groups and parent associations should address mothers’ concerns about confidentiality and stigma so that support-seeking feels socially safe. Fourth, autism-related services should be better coordinated to reduce the practical burden of fragmented professional guidance and therapy schedules.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that directly affect interpretation of the conclusions. First, the study was conducted in one city, Yazd, and involved 22 mothers recruited through therapy centers, special education schools, and parent associations. Therefore, the findings may be transferable to similar contexts but cannot be assumed to represent all Iranian families of autistic children. Second, only mothers were included; fathers, autistic children, siblings, extended family members, and professionals may have different perspectives. Third, the study relied on self-reported experiences and may be affected by recall bias, social desirability, or the emotional salience of difficult events. Fourth, autism presentation and co-occurring conditions were described using available records and maternal report rather than standardized assessment conducted for this study. Fifth, the number of eligible mothers who declined participation was not formally recorded. Sixth, full transcripts were not routinely returned to participants for correction, although member checking was conducted with two participants. Seventh, the interview guide was developed with professional input but without expert-by-experience co-production with autistic people or mothers of autistic children. Finally, as a qualitative study, the findings identify perceived links and interpretive patterns; they do not establish causal relationships between autism, stigma, support, and social isolation.

Conclusion

In this qualitative study of 22 mothers of autistic children in Yazd, Iran, participants described social isolation as a process shaped by stigma and social judgment, inadequate formal and informal support, and caregiving burden with defensive withdrawal. Mothers’ accounts suggested that social responses to autism, community misunderstanding, and limited support were central to their experiences of isolation. These findings support culturally sensitive public awareness, family education, coordinated services, and socially safe support options for mothers of autistic children. Because the study was conducted in one city and included only mothers, the findings should be transferred cautiously and should be examined in future research across diverse Iranian regions and family perspectives.
