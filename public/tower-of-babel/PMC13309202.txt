A previously healthy 56-year-old woman presented to urgent care in late July with a 4-day history of fever and headache with associated nausea and vomiting. She was sent home with a diagnosis of nonspecific viral illness after her symptoms improved with supportive management and was advised to return to hospital if her symptoms worsened. Three days later, she was found unconscious and transported to an emergency department in Hamilton, Ontario. Collateral history revealed that her symptoms had started about 1 week after visiting a lake near Haliburton, Ontario, where she was bitten by mosquitoes.

On arrival, the patient’s temperature was 39.5°C and her Glasgow Coma Scale score was 3. She was intubated for airway protection. In the intensive care unit, her neurologic examination was notable for coma and bilateral Hoffmann sign. The results of a lumbar puncture showed neutrophilic pleocytosis with a concurrent blood leukocytosis of 24 × 109/L (Table 1).

Our initial differential diagnosis included bacterial meningitis caused by Streptococcus or Listeria, early herpes simplex virus (HSV) encephalitis, and encephalitis from a mosquito-borne virus such as West Nile virus (WNV). We started empiric antibiotics pending further results from the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Figure 1). We considered that metabolic causes of encephalitis were unlikely because no important acid-base or electrolyte abnormalities were present (on admission: creatinine of 253 μmol/L, sodium 140 mmol/L, chloride 101 mmol/L, potassium 3.7 mmol/L, creatine kinase 897 U/L, venous blood gas pH 7.38, partial pressure of carbon dioxide 43 mm Hg, bicarbonate 26 mmol/L), and liver and thyroid function were normal. We attributed the patient’s elevated serum creatinine to dehydration and rhabdomyolysis caused by lying still as the result of coma.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the patient’s brain showed asymmetric, T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) signal intensities, most prominent in the left medial temporal lobe with external capsule involvement (Figure 2A, Figure 2B). Though nonspecific, these imaging findings were compatible with viral encephalitis caused by HSV, WNV, or eastern equine encephalitis (EEE) virus.

Because of our high suspicion for HSV encephalitis and the unremarkable initial infectious workup (Figure 1), we repeated a lumbar puncture 4 days later, in case the results were falsely negative. Repeat CSF revealed lymphocytic pleocytosis and a further increase in the protein level; bacterial, HSV, varicella, and enterovirus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests were negative. On day 6, we discontinued empiric antibiotics and sent samples for further infectious disease serology, including HIV, syphilis, Lyme disease, Powassan virus, and EEE virus (Figure 1). Despite 2 negative HSV PCR tests, we continued acyclovir because the MRI features were suggestive of HSV encephalitis and we found no other clear diagnosis. To assess for autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis, we ordered a CSF antibody panel, which was negative. On day 10, we started empiric intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) owing to a lack of neurologic improvement.

On day 13, we repeated MRI, which showed worsening encephalitis with progression of T2 FLAIR signal intensities (Figure 2C, Figure 2D). Screening serology for EEE virus using a hemagglutination inhibition assay for total antibodies returned positive (titre of 1:160) on day 13. At this point, we discontinued acyclovir because of worsening acute kidney injury that required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and 2 negative HSV PCR tests. We ordered confirmatory testing (neutralizing antibody plus PCR) for EEE virus and made a definitive diagnosis of EEE 17 days after admission because of positive neutralizing antibody titres of 1:80 and persistently reactive screening serology (titre of 1:160); the PCR test was negative.

Multiple video electroencephalograms consistently showed persistent, isoelectric brain activity, suggesting severe, irreversible cortical brain injury. Despite supportive management with CRRT, steroids, and IVIG, the patient remained comatose. Given the diagnosis of neuroinvasive EEE with irreversible neurologic damage, we withdrew life support 60 days after presentation, after extensive goals-of-care discussions with the patient’s family.

Discussion

Eastern equine encephalitis virus is an Alphavirus with a 12-kilobase, single-stranded RNA genome that is endemic to regions east of the Mississippi River in the United States, Central and Eastern Ontario (Wellington, Simcoe, Renfrew, Leeds and Grenville, and Ottawa regions), and southern Quebec (Lanaudière, Montérégie, Laurentides, and Outaouais regions).1 Eastern equine encephalitis virus is maintained within an enzootic cycle that involves mosquitos and birds, with occasional spillover into dead-end hosts, such as humans or horses.1 In particular, Culiseta melanura is the main mosquito vector, and birds such as the American robin, tufted titmouse, and black-capped chickadee serve as the main reservoir.1 Field studies suggest that C. melanura exhibit nocturnal feeding behaviours with peak activity during dawn and dusk hours.2

The first human case of EEE virus was identified in 1938 in Massachusetts, with cases typically occurring in late summer and early fall.3 In recent years, sporadic outbreaks in horses followed by human cases have increased in frequency and have spread further north into Northern New England and Canada.4 As of 2025, we are aware of only 3 human cases having been reported in Canada, but a lack of clinical suspicion and testing may result in an underestimation of the true incidence.5 Furthermore, animal surveillance suggests that the geographic spread of EEE virus is far greater than what is predicted by human cases alone.3 Climate change may increase the risk and geographic distribution of EEE virus in ways that are difficult to predict, such as changes in temperature and precipitation, and denser mosquito populations.4

Initial symptoms of EEE typically develop after a 4-to-10-day incubation period and are often nonspecific, including fever, headache, nausea, vomiting, and lymphadenopathy lasting up to 2 weeks.6 Whereas most infections are asymptomatic or mild, severe neuroinvasive EEE can develop rapidly, with altered mental status, seizures, and focal neurologic deficits, and often progresses to coma or death.7 The initial symptoms noted by our patient were likely a mild form of EEE that later developed into neuroinvasive disease. Overall, the annual incidence of EEE in humans is low, and of these cases, fewer than 5% are neuroinvasive.6 The average incidence of neuroinvasive disease is reported as 0.03 cases per million people in the US.3 Neuroinvasive EEE has a high case fatality rate (30% to 41%), with many survivors left with long-term neurologic sequelae, making it one of the most lethal mosquito-borne diseases in North America.6,8 Risk factors for neuroinvasive disease include age (> 50 yr and < 15 yr) and immunocompromised states.6

Given the nonspecific clinical features of EEE, laboratory testing and imaging are critical for diagnosis. Radiographic features are variable but are almost always abnormal in neuroinvasive EEE; the most common MRI findings are asymmetric T2 FLAIR hyperintensities in the internal and external capsule, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cortical regions.8 However, this imaging pattern is nonspecific and can also be seen in other mosquito-borne encephalitides, such as WNV, Japanese, and St. Louis encephalitis. 8 Herpes simplex virus or varicella-zoster virus encephalitis are key diagnoses to rule out when EEE is considered, because they may respond to treatment with acyclovir. A predominantly neutrophilic pleocytosis is most often seen in the CSF of patients with EEE, whereas other types of viral encephalitis, such as HSV encephalitis, usually cause lymphocytic pleocytosis.9 Currently, the gold standard diagnosis for EEE virus infection is serologic testing. If the screening serology using hemagglutination inhibition for total immunoglobulins M and G against EEE virus is positive (titre ≥ 1:10), a confirmatory test of EEE virus–specific neutralizing antibodies via plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) should be ordered.10 Testing with PCR is not a sensitive method for diagnosis; a positive result suggests active or recent infection but does not correlate with disease severity, and a negative PCR result does not rule out the diagnosis.10

Our patient had typical diagnostic features of EEE, with a neutrophil-predominant CSF pleocytosis and prominent T2 FLAIR hyperintensities on MRI. Initial screening antibody and confirmatory PRNT testing were positive. Polymerase chain reaction for EEE virus was negative, suggesting established infection. Given the rarity of EEE virus, testing is limited to specialized centres; we sent samples to Public Health Ontario and the Winnipeg National Microbiology Laboratory. Currently, there are no effective evidence-based treatments available for EEE, with management being largely supportive. One small, retrospective observational study showed a possible benefit of early, empiric IVIG; however, the mortality and morbidity benefits of this treatment are debated and have been observed only in case reports.9 Early administration of IVIG is challenging because of delays in diagnosis due to infrequent consideration of EEE and the time required to obtain confirmational serology. There is little evidence that steroids are effective for patients with EEE or other categories of encephalitis, with a recent meta-analysis showing that steroids in other types of viral encephalitis did not provide a clear survival benefit.11 Our patient’s coma did not improve with steroids or IVIG. However, having a definitive diagnosis of EEE allowed the medical team to have realistic, evidence-based conversations with the patient’s family about expected prognosis.

Although there is a vaccine for EEE virus in horses, no approved vaccine is available for human use. With the lack of effective treatment options, disease prevention remains the most important measure for reducing morbidity and mortality associated with EEE. Preventive strategies largely rely on public health measures such as public education, use of insect repellant and protective clothing, disease surveillance in horses, and, if needed, control of mosquito populations. Improved access to confirmatory testing may allow for earlier intervention, which may improve patient outcomes.6

Neuroinvasive EEE is associated with a high mortality rate and long-term neurologic morbidity in the uncommon survivors. Given the nonspecific clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings of encephalitis, clinicians should consider broad viral serology testing that includes vector-borne diseases such as EEE virus, WNV, and Powassan for patients presenting with encephalitis in the late summer and early fall.
