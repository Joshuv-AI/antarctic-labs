Marker-trait association analysis revealed loci linked to salinity tolerance in a worldwide collection of safflower
Authors: Fatemeh Saeidnia, Mohammad Mahdi Majidi, Fatemeh Ebrahimi, Soroor Mansoori, Hossein Ali Zarei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present investigation was designed to identify molecular marker loci linked to essential agronomic traits and oil content under contrasting salinity regimes, and to evaluate the stability of these associations within a globally representative safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) germplasm collection. Over two successive years, 100 accessions were evaluated, generating 341 polymorphic amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) markers using 10 EcoRI/MseI primer combinations. These markers were subsequently employed in association mapping analyses encompassing 17 agronomic traits and their derived indices. Population structure assessment revealed three genetically distinct subgroups within the panel. Mixed linear model (MLM) analysis facilitated the identification of 140 significant MTAs under non-saline conditions and 128 MTAs under salinity stress. Several loci exhibited associations with multiple traits, suggesting either pleiotropic regulatory functions or tight genetic linkage. Markers M61/E37-1, M61/E37-16, M62/E41-15, M60/E38-9, M51/E41-31, M61/E40-20, and M47/E37-9 were consistently associated with salinity tolerance across environments. After confirmation, these MTAs represent valuable resources for marker-assisted selection and for the targeted introgression of favorable alleles into safflower breeding programs adapted to both optimal and saline conditions. Furthermore, the results provide a critical framework for fine-scale genetic mapping and the eventual isolation of causal genes and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) that confer salinity tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56459-9.

Introduction

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.), a member of the Asteraceae family, is a multipurpose and economically important crop cultivated for diverse applications across agricultural, nutritional, medicinal, and industrial sectors1,2. In addition to its role as a source of edible oil, it provides medicinal and ornamental derivatives, leafy vegetables, and livestock feed. The seeds contain 28–40% oil, notable for its exceptionally high proportion of unsaturated fatty acids, which impart both nutritional value and commercial significance3. A prominent agronomic advantage of safflower is its deep and well-developed taproot system, which enables efficient extraction of water and mineral nutrients from lower soil strata4,5. This adaptation confers superior tolerance to drought and salinity compared with many other oilseed crops4. Consequently, safflower has been widely adopted in arid and semi-arid regions, where severe environmental stresses substantially constrain the productivity of conventional food and cash crops1.

Salinity constitutes one of the most pervasive abiotic stresses that constrain agricultural productivity worldwide and pose major challenges for sustainable crop management6. Elevated salt concentrations impair plant growth primarily by disturbing ionic and osmotic equilibrium, inducing nutrient imbalances, and disrupting essential physiological processes. These disruptions collectively suppress plant growth, inhibit metabolic activity, and substantially reduce photosynthetic efficiency7. For sustainable safflower cultivation, the development of genotypes capable of sustaining productivity under saline conditions is therefore a critical priority8. Elucidating the molecular and genetic mechanisms underlying salt tolerance is essential for advancing breeding strategies and designing effective agronomic interventions.

Plant adaptation to salinity is governed by a complex regulatory network that integrates stress perception, signal transduction, ion transport and homeostasis, osmotic adjustment, detoxification mechanisms, and the modulation of growth and developmental processes9. One promising strategy for enhancing salinity tolerance involves the identification of molecular markers associated with adaptive traits, which can subsequently be utilized in marker-assisted selection (MAS). MAS represents an efficient breeding approach by enabling the indirect selection of traits that are difficult to phenotype or that manifest only at specific developmental stages10,11. The efficacy of this approach depends on the discovery of molecular markers that are closely associated with the functional genes or quantitative trait loci (QTLs). Once validated, these marker–trait associations provide valuable resources for the precise introgression of favorable alleles into elite germplasm, thereby enhancing salinity tolerance12.

The integration of molecular markers into breeding programs accelerates the identification of genes of interest across the genome, significantly reducing both time and cost13. Among the molecular marker techniques currently available, amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) analysis is recognized as a powerful, reproducible, and extremely informative method, capable of detecting extensive genetic polymorphism within a single assay without requiring prior sequence information for primer construction2. AFLP has broad utility, including the monitoring of agronomic trait inheritance, genetic disorder diagnosis, pedigree reconstruction, DNA fingerprinting, and the identification of markers linked to agriculturally important traits. A major advantage of AFLP relative to other fingerprinting approaches lies in its capacity to survey genome-wide polymorphism with high reproducibility and resolution2. Consequently, AFLP markers have been extensively applied to investigate genetic diversity and population structure in a wide range of plant species, including safflower14–16. Despite these advances, progress in safflower genetic improvement through marker-assisted breeding has remained limited, and the identification of trait-associated genetic loci remains underexplored2,3.

The foundation of marker-assisted selection (MAS) resides in the precise identification of marker–trait associations (MTAs), which function as indispensable tools for improving varietal performance and expediting genetic gain17. Two primary strategies have been established for dissecting complex quantitative traits: linkage analysis and association mapping18,19. Conventional quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping has long been utilized for trait dissection; however, this approach is often constrained by limited resolution, extensive resource demands, and the necessity of large segregating populations20. More recently, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have emerged as a robust alternative for detecting MTAs in natural plant populations21,22. In contrast to linkage mapping, GWAS provides finer mapping resolution, allows the exploration of a broader allelic spectrum, and reduces both temporal and resource-related constraints23.

A critical requirement in association studies is the control of population structure to minimize false-positive associations and mitigate both Type I and Type II errors. This is commonly addressed through mixed-model statistical frameworks24,25. Association mapping typically employs two main analytical models: the general linear model (GLM), which incorporates population structure as a covariate, and the mixed linear model (MLM), which combines population structure (Q) with kinship (K) to achieve greater statistical accuracy and reliability2. Amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) markers, in particular, have been effectively applied to assess population structure, characterize genetic diversity, construct linkage maps, agronomic trait mapping, and parentage assignment14.

The increasing necessity to expand crop cultivation onto salt-affected marginal soils underscores the importance of characterizing critical agronomic traits and identifying genotypes capable of sustaining productivity under salinity stress. While several quantitative trait loci (QTL)-based linkage studies have been done in safflower26,27, association mapping aimed at uncovering genetic loci governing complex quantitative traits, such as salt tolerance, remains comparatively limited. Crucially, based on our knowledge, no prior genome-wide association study (GWAS) has been conducted on safflower under explicit salinity stress conditions. To address this gap, the present study was undertaken on a core collection with three principal objectives: first, to establish the optimal population genetic structure for conducting association analysis within a globally representative safflower collection; second, to identify genetic loci linked to important agronomic traits and salinity tolerance under both control and saline conditions through amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) markers; and third, to detect stable marker loci that can serve as robust predictors of agronomic performance and salt tolerance across diverse environmental conditions.

Results

Phenotyping

To assess the impact of salinity stress on safflower performance, a comprehensive evaluation of agro-morphological and yield-related traits was conducted across diverse environmental conditions. Considerable differences were observed among genotypes, environmental regimes (control and saline), and their interactions for most measured traits (data not shown), indicating a pronounced genotype × environment interaction effect. Notably, phenological traits, such as days to flowering (DF) and days to maturity (DM), exhibited relative stability, whereas the mean performance of all other evaluated traits declined markedly under saline conditions. In particular, seed yield (SY) and oil yield (OY) were severely affected by salinity, with average reductions of 46% and 35%, respectively, underscoring the detrimental effects of high salinity on safflower productivity (Table 1). These findings highlight the necessity of assessing both yield components and stress tolerance indices to identify genotypes capable of maintaining performance under adverse environmental conditions.

The genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) demonstrated substantial variability among the assessed traits. Under non-stress conditions, GCV values ranged from 2.49% for oil percentage (OP) to 17.89% for the number of capitula per plant (NCP), whereas under salinity stress, values ranged from 2.82% for DM to 14.67% for the number of branches per plant (NBP) (Table 1). This pattern indicates that salinity stress altered the distribution of genetic variation across traits. Genetic variability for DF, DM, NBP, NCP, and capitulum weight (CW) was higher in the control environment than under salt stress, suggesting that these traits are strongly influenced by environmental conditions. Conversely, traits associated with seed yield and oil content displayed greater variability under salinity, reflecting differential genetic responses to stress and highlighting the potential for selecting tolerant genotypes based on these traits.

Estimates of broad-sense heritability (h²b) were moderate to high for all evaluated traits across both environments, indicating that the majority of these traits are largely determined by genetic factors and can be effectively targeted in breeding programs. Under control conditions, h²b values ranged from 36.37% for seed yield (SY) to 80.49% for days to flowering (DF), whereas under saline conditions, they ranged from 27.34% for SY to 78.33% for days to maturity (DM) (Table 1). With the exception of the number of seeds per capitulum (NSC), heritability estimates were consistently higher under non-stress conditions than under salinity stress, suggesting that environmental stress can suppress the expression of genetic potential for several traits. These observations highlight the necessity of evaluating traits under both control and stress conditions to facilitate the effective selection of salt-tolerant germplasm.

Correlation analyses revealed a significant positive association between the stress tolerance index (STI) and the yield stability index (YSI), indicating that genotypes exhibiting both high stability and salinity tolerance can be efficiently selected using these indices. Within this context, genotypes 32, 46, 51, 52, and 60 were identified as promising candidates, demonstrating superior performance across both indices (Fig. 1). These genotypes represent valuable parental lines for enhancing salt tolerance while maintaining yield stability in breeding programs.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was done separately under control and saline conditions to provide a comprehensive assessment of the relationships among genotypes and traits. The first two principal components accounted for approximately 69% and 67% of the total variation under control and saline conditions, respectively (Fig. 2). Under non-stress conditions, the first principal component (PC1), explaining 43.28% of the variance, was strongly associated with seed yield and its components, including NBP, NCP, and CW, and was therefore designated the “productivity component.” The second principal component (PC2), accounting for 26.30% of variation, exhibited negative correlations with DF and DM and was interpreted as the “phenological component.” Consequently, selecting genotypes with high scores for both PC1 and PC2 could simultaneously enhance seed and oil yield while promoting earliness, providing a practical framework for concurrent improvement of productivity and phenology in safflower (Fig. 2a).

Under salinity stress, PC1 was heavily weighted by seed yield, yield components, and stress tolerance indices, reflecting the “yield potential and salinity tolerance” dimension. PC2 showed a positive association with YSI and negative correlations with TOL and SSI, highlighting the trade-off between yield stability and stress susceptibility. These results indicate that genotypes with high scores for both PC1 and PC2 under saline conditions possess the optimal combination of yield performance and stress resilience, rendering them ideal candidates for breeding programs targeting marginal, salt-affected environments (Fig. 2b). Collectively, the integration of PCA with stress indices and heritability estimates provides a robust framework for identifying genotypes that combine high productivity, stability, and salinity tolerance, thereby facilitating informed selection decisions in safflower breeding under both favorable and adverse environmental conditions.

Genotyping

Analysis of the ten selected AFLP primer combinations produced a total of 454 amplified fragments, of which 341 were polymorphic (Table 2). The number of fragments generated per primer pair varied from 30 (M47/E37) to 38 (M51/E32, M61/E32, M61/E40), with an overall mean of 34.1 fragments per combination. The percentage of polymorphic bands varied from 64.50% for M60/E38 to 83.72% for M47/E40 and M61/E37, yielding an average polymorphism rate of 75.13%. The informativeness of each marker was evaluated using the polymorphism information content (PIC), which ranged from 0.23 (M62/E40 and M61/E37) to 0.35 (M60/E38), with a mean PIC of 0.27 across all markers. Marker index (MI) values, reflecting the overall discriminatory capacity of the markers, ranged from 7.33 for M62/E40 to a maximum of 11.15 for M47/E40. Additionally, Shannon’s diversity index was calculated to further assess marker informativeness, with M47/E40 exhibiting the highest value (1.51) and M51/E32 the lowest (0.39) (Table 2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the selected AFLP primer combinations were highly effective in detecting genetic polymorphism and provided robust information for downstream analyses of population structure and marker–trait associations.

Population structure and association analysis

Before performing population structure and association analysis, quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated for marker–trait associations under each salinity condition to evaluate the adequacy of the mixed linear model (MLM). The plots demonstrated close adherence of observed p-values to the expected distribution under the null hypothesis, with deviation occurring only at the extreme tail which may indicate overcorrection or the relatively small sample size of our cohort for genome-wide association analysis. This pattern confirms that the MLM efficiently controlled population structure and kinship effects, thereby minimizing spurious associations and supporting the reliability of the detected MTAs (Figs. 3 and 4). To elucidate the population structure of the safflower panel, the optimal number of subpopulations (K) was determined by evaluating both the maximum likelihood estimates and ΔK values. ΔK was plotted against consecutive K values according to Evanno et al.28. As presented in Table 3; Figs. 5 and 6, the analysis identified three genetically distinct subpopulations within the safflower collection, corresponding to the highest ΔK value observed at K = 3.

Marker–trait associations (MTAs) were examined using a general linear model (GLM) and mixed linear model (MLM), assessing relationships between AFLP markers and mean phenotypic trait values across years and salinity conditions. Under non-stress conditions, 198 AFLP markers exhibited significant and tentative associations with the evaluated traits at a probability threshold of 0.05 using the GLM, with P-values < 0.01 and false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied (data not shown). The proportion of phenotypic variance explained (coefficient of determination, R²) for individual traits ranged from 0.04% to 0.15%. Under salinity stress, 220 markers displayed significant and tentative correlations with the measured traits at the same probability level, with R² values spanning 0.04 to 0.14% (data not shown). Incorporation of the kinship matrix into the MLM accounted for relatedness among genotypes, resulting in fewer significant tentative associations relative to the GLM. Specifically, under non-stress conditions, 140 markers exhibited significant tentative associations, whereas 128 markers were significant under salinity stress at the 0.05 probability level (Supplementary Table S1 online). The proportion of phenotypic variance explained by individual markers ranged from 0.52% to 5.60% under normal conditions and from 0.97% to 10.27% under salt stress (Supplementary Table S1 online). Additionally, association analyses were performed for stress tolerance and susceptibility indices, revealing that 73 and 61 markers were tentatively associated with these indices using the GLM and MLM models, respectively (Table 4).

Both GLM and MLM analyses indicated that several markers were simultaneously associated with multiple traits. Under salinity stress, this marker maintained significant correlations with capitulum diameter (CD), TSW, oil percentage (OP), and oil yield (OY) in both models. Marker M61/E37-4 exhibited significant associations with the number of branches per plant (NBP), number of capitula per plant (NCP), capitulum weight (CW), and seed yield (SY) under non-stress conditions (Supplementary Table S1 online). Under saline conditions, MLM analysis identified markers M47/E37-5 and M60/E38-9 as significantly associated with CW, SY, and OP. Marker M62/E40-23 was linked to NBP and NCP under normal conditions and to DF and CD under salt stress. Marker M62/E41-3 exhibited significant associations with CW and TSW under non-stress conditions, as well as with NBP, NSC, and OP under salinity stress. Additionally, markers M47/E40-10 and M61/E40-30 were significantly associated with NCP and CW at normal situation, whereas M51/E41-18, M51/E41-28, and M62/E41-17 displayed significant correlations with TSW and OP under saline conditions (Supplementary Table S1 online).

With respect to stress-related indices, markers M61/E37-1, M61/E37-16, M62/E41-15, M60/E38-9, and M51/E41-31 exhibited significant associations with mean productivity (MP), geometric mean productivity (GMP), and stress tolerance index (STI). Moreover, markers M61/E40-20 and M47/E37-9 demonstrated significant correlations with MP, GMP, STI, yield stability index (YSI), and stress susceptibility index (SSI) (Table 4).

To identify robust and environmentally stable MTAs, associations were analyzed independently under each salinity condition. The MLM model revealed eight MTAs exhibiting consistent expression across both normal and saline environments. For example, markers M51/E41-13 and M61/E37-22 were strongly and stably associated with plant height (PH) under both conditions. Similarly, markers M61/E40-30 and M61/E32-9 maintained consistent correlations with NCP, and marker M61/E37-1 showed substantial and reproducible associations with seed yield (SY) across both environments (Supplementary Table S1 online). Collectively, these findings highlight a subset of markers with potential utility in marker-assisted selection, enabling the simultaneous improvement of agronomic performance and salinity tolerance in safflower.

Discussion

Analyses of genetic variability and molecular markers offer essential insights into the potential for enhancing agronomic performance and stress tolerance in crop species. The pronounced genetic variation detected among the evaluated safflower accessions across all measured traits reflects intrinsic differences in the underlying genes regulating yield, its components, and salinity tolerance. This genetic diversity facilitates the identification and selection of accessions with varying levels of tolerance to salinity stress. In addition, the results revealed that both environmental conditions and genotype × environment interactions significantly influenced the majority of traits under both control and saline conditions. These findings emphasize the strong environmental dependence of trait expression and underscore the importance of conducting marker–trait association analyses separately for each growth environment.

Exposure to salinity stress led to reductions in nearly all measured parameters, with the exception of days to flowering (DF) and days to maturity (DM). Consistent with previous reports, decreases in plant growth and yield under saline conditions are primarily attributed to osmotic and ionic stress effects9,29,30. In the present study, salt stress resulted in a 46% decline in seed yield and a 35% reduction in oil yield. The accumulation of salts in the soil reduces soil water potential, thereby limiting water and nutrient uptake, inducing osmotic stress, and consequently decreasing yield3,31. These findings are consistent with previous reports in safflower as well as in other crop species32,33.

The considerable genetic variation detected across all assessed traits underscores a substantial potential for genetic enhancement among the evaluated accessions. Furthermore, higher genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) values under non-stress conditions relative to salinity stress suggest that salt stress reduces the genetic variability of most traits, implying that selection may be more effective under favorable environmental conditions. All examined traits exhibited moderate to high broad-sense heritability estimates, indicating that genetic improvement through selection is feasible and supporting the identification of reliable marker–trait associations34. Additionally, the significant positive correlation between the stress tolerance index (STI) and the yield stability index (YSI) suggests that selection for salinity tolerance concurrently promotes stable performance, thereby enabling simultaneous enhancement of stress tolerance and phenotypic stability. Certain accessions were identified as superior based on their high STI and YSI values.

The high proportion of polymorphic loci detected in this study demonstrates the effectiveness of AFLP markers for differentiating safflower accessions. These findings are consistent with previous reports on safflower genetic variation2,14,16. Notably, the primer combination M47/E40 exhibited the highest percentage of polymorphism, along with elevated polymorphism information content (PIC), marker index (MI), and resolving power (RP), indicating its high informativeness and reliability for accession identification and differentiation.

Analysis of population structure within the safflower panel revealed the existence of three genetically distinct clusters, reflecting underlying stratification among the evaluated accessions. Marker–trait association (MTA) analyses conducted under both non-stress and salinity stress conditions demonstrated that the mixed linear model (MLM) identified fewer significant and tentative associations compared to the general linear model (GLM). The AFLP markers detected through the MLM represent the most reliable candidates for future marker-assisted selection (MAS) programs. The integration of both the Q-matrix (population structure) and K-matrix (kinship) into the MLM effectively mitigates inflation in the coefficient of determination, thereby providing a robust correction for population structure effects35.

Considerable variation in MTAs was observed between normal and salinity stress conditions, suggesting that a greater number of candidate regions contribute to trait regulation under non-stress conditions relative to saline environments. The proportion of phenotypic variance accounted for by individual markers was relatively low, ranging from 0.52% to 5.60% under control conditions and from 0.85% to 10.27% under salinity stress. These modest R² values are consistent with the polygenic nature of the evaluated traits, the presence of candidate regions exerting small quantitative effects, rare allelic variants, and complex epistatic interactions35. These findings align with previous reports that observed similar patterns in safflower2.

Both GLM and MLM analyses revealed that certain AFLP markers were significantly associated with multiple traits simultaneously, highlighting their potential utility as candidate regions in concurrent improvement of agronomic and quality characteristics2,36,37. Such multi-trait associations may arise from co-expression of quantitative trait loci (e-QTLs)36,38. For instance, under non-stress conditions, marker M61/E32-1 was significantly correlated with days to flowering (DF), number of seeds per capitulum (NSC), and thousand-seed weight (TSW). Under salinity stress, the same marker exhibited significant associations with capitulum diameter (CD), TSW, oil percentage (OP), and oil yield (OY) in both GLM and MLM analyses. Similarly, marker M61/E37-4 was associated with the number of branches per plant (NBP), number of capitula per plant (NCP), capitulum weight (CW), and seed yield (SY) under normal conditions. Additionally, MLM analysis revealed that marker M62/E40-23 was linked to NBP and NCP under control conditions and to DF and CD under saline stress, whereas M62/E41-3 exhibited associations with CW and TSW under normal conditions and with NBP, NSC, and OP under stress. These multi-trait associations likely result from pleiotropic effects or the influence of closely linked genes affecting multiple traits37,39.

A comprehensive understanding of the genetic basis of salinity tolerance necessitates the integration of molecular marker data with phenotypic evaluations to pinpoint candidate genomic regions underlying effective stress responses40. Using the MLM approach, 61 candidate genomic regions were identified as significantly associated with salinity tolerance and susceptibility indices. Among these, markers M61/E37-1, M61/E37-16, M62/E41-15, M60/E38-9, and M51/E41-31 were significantly linked to mean productivity (MP), geometric mean productivity (GMP), and the stress tolerance index (STI). Furthermore, markers M61/E40-20 and M47/E37-9 demonstrated significant correlations with MP, GMP, STI, yield stability index (YSI), and stress susceptibility index (SSI). These candidate genomic regions may harbor a gene influencing salinity tolerance and represent promising targets for enhancing salinity tolerance in safflower, pending functional validation of their roles in regulating stress-responsive traits. Before its application in breeding programs, this association would need to be validated in independent genetic populations, and the AFLP fragment would ideally need to be converted into a sequence-specific marker, such as an SNP, for more efficient use in selection.

Environmental conditions likely contributed to the observed differences in MTAs between normal and saline conditions41. The results suggest that either distinct genes may govern the same trait under different conditions or that gene expression levels may vary depending on the environment38. Notably, eight markers were identified that consistently exhibited associations with multiple traits across both environmental contexts. As reported by Diapari et al.42, markers consistently detected in two or more independent environments are considered more reliable and robust than those observed in a single environment, reinforcing their potential utility for breeding applications.

A key limitation of our study is the sample size of the association panel (N = 100). As noted in the methods section, this size, while valuable for capturing global diversity, may be under-powered for a comprehensive GWAS. This likely resulted in the non-detection of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) with minor effects on the agronomic traits and oil content under contrasting salinity conditions. The marker-trait associations we report should therefore be interpreted as identifying the most prominent, major-effect loci. Our findings represent a crucial first step and a valuable foundation for future genetic studies in safflower. To capture the full spectrum of genetic loci, including those with small effects, future research should utilize larger and potentially more diverse populations, combined with high-density genotyping platforms.

In conclusion, association analysis has emerged as a robust approach for the identification and characterization of genes and molecular markers associated with complex agronomic and economically important traits. This study provides a detailed phenotypic characterization of a globally diverse safflower panel under contrasting salinity conditions. Before performing population structure and association analysis, Q–Q plots were also examined for signs of test statistic deflation and to evaluate the adequacy of the mixed linear model (MLM). A slight downward deviation from the expected distribution was interpreted as an indication of conservative model behavior, reflecting effective control of population structure and relatedness rather than model inadequacy. Using a preliminary AFLP-based analysis, we have identified several genomic regions putatively associated with essential agronomic traits and oil content. While these associations require validation and fine-mapping with high-density SNP markers, our findings establish a valuable phenotypic and genetic foundation. This work highlights key target regions of the safflower genome, paving the way for future genomic-assisted breeding efforts aimed at improving salinity tolerance in this important crop. The level of polymorphism observed within the evaluated germplasm was sufficient to capture significant genetic variation for the measured traits. Analysis of population structure further revealed the presence of three genetically distinct safflower subpopulations, indicating underlying stratification within the panel. Implementation of a mixed linear model (MLM) facilitated the identification of 140 and 128 significant putative markers–trait associations (MTAs) at control and saline conditions, respectively, of which seven MTAs were specifically associated with salinity tolerance. These findings demonstrate that AFLP markers constitute a valuable resource for improving salt tolerance in future safflower breeding programs. Several markers were significantly and putatively correlated with key agronomic traits, underscoring their potential utility in crop improvement strategies. The environmental specificity observed for numerous MTAs highlights the critical influence of genotype × environment interactions on association analyses. Nevertheless, eight MTAs displayed consistent expression across both non-stress and saline conditions, indicating their robustness and reliability. This preliminary investigation successfully identified several major AFLP marker loci associated with key agronomic traits and oil content in safflower under saline and non-saline conditions. While the study’s power to detect small-effect loci was limited by the sample size, these findings provide a foundational framework for future marker-assisted selection programs and more comprehensive genomic studies aimed at enhancing salinity tolerance and yield in this important oilseed crop.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field experiment

The field experiment was conducted across two consecutive growing seasons (2020 and 2021) at the Research Farm of Isfahan University of Technology, Lavark, Najaf-Abad, Isfahan, Iran (32°\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$30^{\prime}$$\end{document} N, 51°\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20^{\prime}$$\end{document} E; 1630 m above mean sea level). The site is defined by a mean annual temperature of 14.5 °C and an average annual rainfall of 140 mm. The region experiences hot and dry summers, making supplemental irrigation essential for crop cultivation.

A total of 100 safflower accessions were evaluated, representing a global diversity panel. This collection comprised 17 Iranian genotypes originating from distinct agro-ecological zones within the country and 83 exotic accessions obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the Leibniz Institute of Plant Genetics and Crop Plant Research (IPK) gene bank (Supplementary Table S2 online). Field trials were established in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications under both saline and non-saline conditions. In each repetition, seeds from each accession were sown in two rows of one meter in length, with an inter-row spacing of 25 cm and intra-row spacing of 20 cm, while adjacent plots were separated by 75 cm. Irrigation was performed using a surface drip tape system. Under non-stress conditions, plants were irrigated with fresh water until the branching stage, at which point salinity treatments were initiated.

Salt stress was imposed through the gradual elevation of irrigation water salinity. Initially, plots received normal water with an electrical conductivity (ECw) of 3 dSm− 1 until the rosette stage. Salinity was then progressively increased to 7 dSm− 1 during the subsequent irrigation phase and further raised to 16 dSm− 1 in the final phase, beginning after the heading stage. Soil moisture levels within the root zone were regularly monitored to ensure uniform stress imposition. Prior to each irrigation event, the required volume of water and the precise amount of salt necessary to achieve the target salinity were calculated. Water samples were collected during every irrigation to validate salinity levels, and salts were dissolved and applied incrementally to maintain consistent stress intensity across the experimental period.

Phenotyping

Over the two-year experimental period, eleven agro-morphological and quality-related traits were systematically evaluated under both saline and non-saline conditions for each experimental plot. The assessed traits included days to 50% flowering (DF, days), days to 90% maturity (DM, days), plant height (PH, cm), number of branches per plant (NBP), number of capitula per plant (NCP), number of seeds per capitulum (NSC), capitulum diameter (CD, cm), capitulum weight (CW, g), seed yield per plant (SY, g), thousand-seed weight (TSW, g), and oil percentage (OP, %). Phenological parameters (DF and DM) were recorded based on plot means, whereas the remaining morphological attributes were measured from ten randomly selected plants within the central rows of each genotype, and their mean values were applied in subsequent statistical analyses. Seed oil content was determined using a near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy device (Inframatic 8620, Perten Instruments, Germany) and expressed as a percentage. Oil yield per plant was subsequently derived by multiplying the seed oil percentage with the corresponding seed yield per plant. To discriminate salt-tolerant from salt-sensitive accessions, six indices of stress tolerance and susceptibility were calculated based on seed yield under control and saline conditions. These indices comprised the tolerance index (TOL)43, mean productivity (MP), geometric mean productivity (GMP)44, stress tolerance index (STI)44, stress susceptibility index (SSI)45, and yield stability index (YSI)46.

Genotyping

Genomic DNA extraction from young safflower leaf tissues was performed using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol as outlined by Saghai-Maroof et al.47. The concentration and purity of the isolated DNA were evaluated through electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels and spectrophotometric measurements. Genotyping was carried out following the amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) method described by Vos et al.48. From an initial screening of 20 EcoRI/MseI primer combinations, the 10 most informative pairs were selected based on their ability to generate a high number of polymorphic bands.

Data were processed using BioCalculator software (v3.2; QIAGEN), where AFLP polymorphisms were scored in a binary matrix, designating the presence of bands as “1” and their absence as “0.” Only polymorphic bands that were clearly resolvable and reproducible across replicates were included in the final analysis.

Statistical analyses

Phenotypic data analysis

Prior to conducting the analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to evaluate the normality distribution of data. The homogeneity of residual variances across experimental years was subsequently assessed using Bartlett’s test, ensuring the comparability of datasets prior to their combination across the two growing seasons. Variance components were then estimated through a combined ANOVA implemented with the PROC MIXED of SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Within this framework, environmental effects were treated as fixed factors, whereas genotype and year were considered random factors. Whenever the F-test revealed statistically significant differences among treatments, pairwise comparisons of means were performed using Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test, applying a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Genotypic correlations among traits were calculated using the PROC CORR procedure in SAS. Broad-sense heritability (h²b) estimation was done following the method of Hallauer and Miranda49.

Data collected under both normal and salinity stress conditions across years were subjected to ANOVA, separately. Estimation of variance and covariance components was performed for each environment using the PROC MIXED in SAS. Broad-sense heritability was then calculated on a mean phenotypic basis, averaged across repetitions and years under both control and salinity stress conditions. Furthermore, the extent of genetic variation was quantified by calculating the genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) as described by Falconer and Mackay50. In addition, to reduce the multidimensionality of the dataset and to assess the relationships among safflower genotypes and the full set of measured traits, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted based on a correlation matrix in Statgraphics software, version 17.251.

Molecular data analysis

Polymorphic amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) markers were scored as binary data, with the presence of a band assigned a value of ‘1’ and its absence a value of ‘0’. For each AFLP marker, a series of descriptive statistics were calculated to evaluate marker informativeness. Population structure and marker–trait association analysis

Before performing population structure and association analysis, quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated under each salinity condition by in-house R scripts to assess potential inflation and evaluate the adequacy of the mixed linear model (MLM). Genome-wide distribution of the test statistic indicated no evidence for population stratification (Q-Q plots). A slight downward deviation from the expected distribution was interpreted as an indication of conservative model behavior, reflecting effective control of population structure and relatedness rather than model inadequacy. Population structure was examined using amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) marker data in STRUCTURE version 2.3.4, allowing the germplasm collection to be classified into genetically distinct subpopulations. The analysis was conducted under an admixture model, with a burn-in of 10,000 iterations followed by 100,000 repetitions of the Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) algorithm. The membership of each genotype was run for the range of genetic clusters (K) from K = 2 to K = 10, with five independent runs conducted for each K value to ensure reliability. The ΔK method of Evanno et al.28, was used to determine the optimal number of subpopulations, as carried out in STRUCTURE HARVESTER56. This approach calculates the rate of change in the log probability of data across successive K values, providing an objective measure for identifying the optimal population structure.

Association analysis was conducted within a mixed linear model (MLM) framework24, to compute P-values for MTAs using TASSEL version 4.2.157. Phenotypic mean values (P-matrix) for each trait were employed to detect significant associations under both control and salinity stress conditions. As achieving uniform salt stress across field plots is notoriously difficult, therefore, we calculated best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) using the lme4 package58 for each trait and then association analysis based on MLM model was performed on the BLUP values to minimize spatial/environmental noise. To account for potential confounding effects arising from population structure, the Q-matrix obtained from STRUCTURE at the optimal K value was incorporated into the MLM as a covariate. Additionally, a kinship matrix was calculated based on the results of marker genotype data using TASSEL version 4.2.157. To control for multiple testing, the false discovery rate (FDR) was applied using the QVALUE package in R59, ensuring a rigorous and reliable identification of marker–trait associations.

Although our panel of 100 accessions provides a globally representative sample of safflower germplasm, we acknowledge that this sample size is relatively modest for a genome-wide association study. Consequently, the statistical power to detect loci with small phenotypic effects is limited, and the study is susceptible to a higher false-negative rate. Therefore, the marker-trait associations identified in this study are more likely to represent loci with moderate to large effects on the evaluated traits.
