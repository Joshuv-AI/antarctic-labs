 Phase 1 Summary
The objective of this project was to design, seek necessary approvals, build and operate a large-
scale pilot sorbent-based post combustion carbon capture system (CCS) at a coal fired power
generation facility. TDA’s CCS uses a highly stable, low-cost, high-capacity physical adsorbent to
effectively remove CO2 via a combination vacuum and concentration swing adsorption (VCSA)
process. The CCS is integrated with the power plant flue gas exhaust, which is rich in CO2 (~13%
vol. CO2) and removes more than 90% of the plant’s overall carbon emissions.

The project included multiple phases, and we were to design (Phase 1), seek necessary approvals
(Phase 2), build (Phase 3) and operate (Phase 4) a large-scale pilot sorbent-based CCS at the
Wyoming Integrated Test Center (ITC) site located outside the Dry Fork Power Station (Gillette,
WY). However, the project got terminated midway through the Phase 1 work and this final
technical report summarizes the project progress through its termination.

TDA’s large-scale pilot system will: (i) separate carbon emissions from a stream at an industrial
(power generation) facility, and (ii) validate >90% (threshold) and >95% (objective) carbon
capture efficiencies with >95% CO2 purity under real exhaust conditions. In Phase I, TDA and its
partners were working on a front-end engineering design (FEED) study for a commercial-scale,
advanced carbon capture system capable of processing up to 23 MW (roughly 200,000 MTPY)
net CO2 with 90+% carbon capture efficiency and 95+% CO2 purity. The host site WITC is attached
to Basin Electric’s Dry Fork Station rated at 405 MW and currently releases 2.7 million MTPY of
CO2. The pilot-scale CCS unit will be installed at the ITC’s large test bay which is already equipped
to deliver up to 23 MWe of flue gas.

In the FEED study, we worked with GR2 Engineering, a highly specialized Engineering
Procurement and Construction (EPC) firm, to complete the design of balance of plant, manage
the FEED tasks and specifying the specialty equipment (e.g., valves, vacuum system) used in the
carbon capture module. We also collaborated with our technology licensor/business partner SLB
(previously Schlumberger) to carry out a business case analysis and in the commercialization of
the concept. Wyoming ITC will host the project. We also worked with University of Colorado,
Denver (UCD) to complete a Life Cycle Analysis (LCA) for the pilot-scale plant and the full-scale
system. We also carried out a techno-economic assessment.

As other projects selected by OCED under this program, the Project is terminated during the
Phase 1 stage, without fully completing an engineering design package for the capture system.

3 Overall Project Description and Description of Phase 1 Activities
3.1 Project Objective
The objective of this project is to design, seek necessary approvals, build, and operate a large-scale
pilot sorbent-based post-combustion carbon capture system (CCS) at a coal-ﬁred power generation
facility. TDA’s CCS uses a highly-stable, low-cost, high-capacity physical adsorbent to effectively
remove CO2 via a combination vacuum and concentration swing adsorption (VCSA) process. The
CCS is integrated with the power plant ﬂue gas exhaust, which is rich in CO2 (~13% vol. CO2) and
removes more than 90% of the plant’s overall carbon emissions. In this project we will design (Phase
1), seek necessary approvals (Phase 2), build (Phase 3), and operate (Phase 4) a large-scale pilot
sorbent-based CCS at the Wyoming Integrated Test Center (ITC) site located outside the Dry Fork
Power Station (Gillette, WY). The proposed large-scale pilot system will: (i) separate carbon dioxide
emissions from a stream at an industrial (power generation) facility and (ii) validate >90% (threshold)
and >95% (objective) carbon capture efficiencies with >95% CO2 purity under real exhaust
conditions.

3.2 Description of Phase 1 Activities
In Phase I, TDA and its partners worked on a front-end engineering design (FEED) study for a
commercial-scale, advanced carbon capture system capable of processing approximately 158,000
metric tons of carbon dioxide (CO2) per annum (MTPA) with at least 90% carbon capture efficiency
and 95% CO2 purity. The host site Wyoming ITC is attached to Basin Electric’s Dry Fork Power Station
(rated at 405 MW) and currently releases 2.7 million MTPY of CO2. The pilot-scale CCS unit will be
installed at the ITC’s large test bay which is already equipped to deliver up to 23 MWe of ﬂue gas. The
test site is only 0.5 miles away from a CO2 storage site that is qualiﬁed as a carbon capture HUB
under the Wyoming CarbonSAFE program (DE-FE0031891), which will be evaluated as a potential
site for sequestration.
In the FEED study, we worked with GR2 Engineering, a highly specialized Engineering Procurement
and Construction (EPC) ﬁrm, to complete the balance of plant design, manage the FEED tasks, and
specify the specialty equipment (e.g., valves, vacuum system) used in the carbon capture module.
We also collaborated with Schlumberger Technology Corporation (STC) to conduct market and
commercialization analyses. Wyoming ITC will host the project. We worked with Dr. Arunprakash
Karunanithi from the University of Colorado, Denver to complete a lifecycle analysis (LCA) for both
the pilot-scale and the full-scale CCS. We worked with Dr. Ashok Rao to complete a
technoeconomic assessment, and we will work with the University of Wyoming’s School of
Engineering Resources to implement Phase 1 Community Beneﬁt (CB) Commitments and develop
plans for future phase CB activities.
3.3 Technical Scope Summary
The technical scope of work for the large-scale pilot plant was designed toproceed through a stage-
gate engineering and design process. The work is divided into four phases with speciﬁc goals that
must be met at the end of each phase to proceed as described below. The ﬁrst phase involves
planning for necessary agreements and permits to fund and operate the plant and engineering to
develop a Class 3 estimate for the pilot plant cost. The second phase includes securing the
agreements for ﬁnancing and operating the plant, obtaining the necessary construction permits,
completing a NEPA review for the Phase 3 and 4 activities, and completing the detailed engineering
to develop a Class 1 estimate for the pilot plant cost. The pilot plant will be constructed,
commissioned, and started up in the third phase of the project. The fourth phase of the project will
be the regular operation of the pilot plant for a one-year demonstration to obtain performance data
that will be used to validate the process and scale-up models and to reﬁne the TEA, LCA, and
business plans for commercial deployment of the technology.
