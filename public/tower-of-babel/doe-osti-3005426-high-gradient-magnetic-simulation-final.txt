 ABSTRACT

Over the course of nearly 2 years, the goal of this project has been to identify methods for the separation
of uranium oxide particles from other geologic materials. The primary motivation is to assess methods for
enhancing the extraction and throughput of actinide particles from environmental samples, enabling the
evaluation of these trace actinides through more traditional methods such as thermal ionization mass
spectrometry or secondary ion mass spectrometry. The methodology adopted was magnetic separation,
because actinide particles and their oxides are paramagnetic (i.e., slightly attracted to a magnetic field),
whereas many geologic materials are diamagnetic (i.e., slightly repulsed by a magnetic field). These
diamagnetic materials include the silica, alumina, and aluminosilicate species that make up the vast
majority of the geologic matrix. Numerous experiments have been run, and experimental procedures have
been refined to accomplish such separations. All experiments in this project used uranium oxide particles,
chiefly UOx, where x is slightly greater than 2. Analytical measurements of various particle types were
obtained using single-particle inductively-coupled-plasma time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Modeling
efforts were employed to evaluate contributions of individual forces acting on particles that affect their
retention.

In prior work and early in this project, the team confirmed that UO2 particles were strongly retained in a
magnetic filter. However, removing retained UO2 particles for subsequent evaluation was a struggle, and
the major contributions of this project have been in addressing this difficulty. The modeling effort
identified that, in addition to the magnetic force, the van der Waals force is significant in this removal
phase. The van der Waals force is much stronger for UO2 particles than it is for soil materials such as
Al2O3, SiO2, or even Fe2O3. Hence, it represents not only a difficulty to overcome but also an opportunity
to accomplish greater separation from other particles. We achieved reasonable success by incorporating
sonication (vibration) into the removal phase procedures, obtaining significant separation of uranium
oxides from other materials.

During the Independent Review in January 2025, several recommendations from the review panel were
adopted. These recommendations have guided experiments for most of this year and included running
replicate experiments to identify uncertainties, pursuing enhanced methods for removal (recovery) from
the filter, and running some experiments using U3O8 instead of UO2. We have also identified several
improvements that could be made, in future work, to refine the methodology and create a more robust and
useful tool for extracting actinide samples from environmental matrices.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Environmental sampling is a principal tool used by the International Atomic Energy Agency and others to
monitor the operation of nuclear facilities. Sometimes the actinide particles of interest are mixed with
large amounts of innocuous soil material, making it difficult or expensive to evaluate the actinides
themselves. The goal of this project has been to deliver actinide particles mixed with far fewer geologic
particles to facilitate the direct and convenient evaluation of the actinides themselves through established
methods such as thermal ionization mass spectrometry or secondary ionization mass spectrometry. It is
essential that the UO2 particles remain intact, with their original chemical form, isotopic content, and
general morphology. This requirement precludes any processing that would modify or destroy the
particles, such as dissolution in HNO3.

This project primarily involves uranium oxides and the oxides of Si, Al, Fe, Mg, and other geological
materials. It could well be extended to oxides of other actinides, but no steps were taken to do so. Most
experiments were performed with a certified reference material, Arizona road dust (ARD), containing
known quantities of SiO2, Al2O3, and Fe2O3. In the end, the project team successfully separated uranium

 1

oxide from other materials. However, this separation was not as straightforward as anticipated at the
beginning of the project and involved additional separation steps aside from the use of the magnetic field.

The methodology used to achieve this result relies on passing a particulate slurry through a magnetized
filter on which the paramagnetic UO2 particles are preferentially retained over the diamagnetic soil
materials. Previous work had demonstrated this retention, and it was further demonstrated in the first year
of this project. However, we encountered difficulties in recovering the uranium particles from the filter,
and addressing this issue became a major component of the work during the project’s second year, which
is described in this report.

Many improvements and accomplishments from the project’s first year continued to provide a strong
foundation for work in the second year:

 The apparatus, redesigned based on electromagnetic field simulations, continued to provide excellent
 particle capture. The filter housing developed a leak halfway through the year and had to be replaced.

 Analytical measurements using single-particle inductively-coupled-plasma time-of-flight mass
 spectrometry (SP-ICP-TOF-MS) provided reliable quantitative measurements for all components.

 Development of a simulation model aided considerably in understanding the capture and retention
 phenomena, and additional simulations led to enhanced methods for recovery from the filter.

Early in the Q2 of FY 2025 (January 16, 2025) we held an Independent Review that highlighted several
areas of weakness which we endeavored to address during the remainder of the year:

 We identified several standard cases that would form the basis for remaining experimental work.

 Replicate measurements were used to identify basic patterns and their uncertainties.

 Extra effort was applied to address the difficulty in removing uranium particles from the filter;
 methods used included high flow rates and sonication to induce vibration of the filter fibers.

 The modeling effort was used to identify some of the difficulties and help us focus on solutions to the
 removal issue.

 We undertook a few experiments with U3O8 in addition to UO2.

The modeling results identified that the principal difficulty in removing uranium particles from the filter
was due to the van der Waals forces. These results were somewhat unexpected, because we originally
anticipated that removing the filter from the magnetic field would release the particles to be carried off by
a wash solution. When the particles were not released or were released in limited amounts, we looked for
causes and investigated additional modifications to our procedures. Thus, the original project plan,
consistent with the name High Gradient Magnetic Separation, turned out to be an incomplete description
of the forces and the procedures needed to achieve an effective separation.

Overall, this project has accomplished all the goals planned at its start. It has spawned two papers for the
open literature and two conference presentations; the primary authors were a postdoctoral research
associate and a junior staff member, both of whom were integral to the success of the project. This report
primarily addresses the accomplishments of the second year (FY 2025); it particularly emphasizes work
since the Independent Review and complements the annual report from the first year (ORNL/SPR-
2024/3721).

 2. APPARATUS REDESIGN

The principal design of the magnetic filtration apparatus has remained largely unchanged from the time of
its initial fabrication in FY 2024 Q2 (see FY 2024 Q2 and annual report ORNL/SPR-2024/3721 for
additional details; it is also summarized in Appendix A of this report). However, the material used in the
fabrication of the filter flow channel has been changed from polycarbonate to aluminum. This change was
made because of a structural weakness that was discovered in the polycarbonate channel. Specifically, the
polycarbonate channel was prone to cracking at the mouth of the filter over time due to stress from
handling. Constructing the flow channel from aluminum eliminates this concern because aluminum
provides significantly increased strength without affecting the magnetic field. All experiments in Q3 and
Q4 of FY 2025 were performed using this new filter housing and no change in filter performance was
detected.

 3. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Basic experimental procedures remained unchanged from those used during the previous year and were
documented thoroughly in the FY 2024 report (ORNL/SPR-2024/3721). Briefly, submicron uranium
oxide powders were prepared in separate batches by grinding and fractionation. A slurry of UOx, ZrO2
grinding media, and ethanol was ground in a ball mill for several days. Fractionation was done in a
column of ethanol to separate submicron uranium particles from larger particulates. The fractionated UOx
particles were then dried under a low flow of N2 and weighed on a four-digit balance. They were then
collected for feed preparation, which involved weighing samples of uranium oxide powder (nominally
10 mg) and ARD (~100 mg). The particles were added to 1 L deionized water (DI) (18 MΩ·cm) along
with 1 mL Triton X surfactant.

In response to the Independent Review, we committed to running replicate experiments to establish the
quantitative uncertainties of the results. We also ran a few experiments with U3O8 in place of UO2.
Through qualitative evaluation of many previous results, we adopted a standard experimental procedure
involving recirculation runs through the magnetized filter followed by rinses both in and out of the
magnetic field.

3.1 REPLICATE TESTS OF UO2 AND U3O8 COLLECTION ON MAGNETIZED FILTER

Two types of replicate experiments are described in this report: sequential and nonsequential. The two
groups of experiments used similar methods but were intended to elucidate different aspects of magnetic
recovery. Specifically, nonsequential experiments evaluated the efficacy of various enhanced flushing
techniques, whereas sequential experiments examined recovery from enriched/depleted feed suspensions
(e.g., recovery from a depleted feed after an initial round of retention on the filter). The experimental
protocol for nonsequential experiments is described in this section, and the protocol for the sequential
experiments is provided in Section 3.1.1. Before beginning each experiment, an initial sample was always
taken from the feed suspension to ensure that the starting conditions were known. In each nonsequential
experiment, the feed underwent two retention steps in which the particle suspension was recirculated
through the filter at a flow rate of 1 L/min for a total of 20 min per step. The first retention step was out of
magnet, whereas the second step was in the magnetic field. Samples were taken from the particle
suspension at time intervals of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min during each retention step. Once these
steps were completed, the filter was emptied of solution, which was returned to the feed bottle and
discarded after sampling.

The retention steps were followed by several flushes to remove material retained on the filter. In the first
flush, 1 L DI was passed once through the filter at a low flow rate of 0.125 L/min with the filter in the

 3

magnetic field. A sample of the effluent was taken after the whole volume was passed through the filter.
Next, the filter was taken out of the magnetic field, and another 1 L of clean DI was passed once through
the filter at 0.125 L/min. Again, a sample of the effluent was taken. Finally, the experiment was
concluded with a recirculating flush with the filter outside the magnetic field. A 1 L bottle of clean DI
was recirculated through the filter at a flow rate of 2 L/min for a total of 12 min. A sample was taken
every 3 min to observe the elution of suspended particles. In these tests, either UO2 or U3O8 was used in
the initial feed, as indicated in the results and supplemental information.

In these experiments, the project team tested various enhancements to flushing the filter, including a high
flow rate, sonication, and high ionic strength (2 M NaCl). The test matrix is summarized in Table 1.

3.1.1 Sequential Loading of High- and Low-Content UO2 Suspensions

An additional set of sequential tests was performed to investigate how repeated passes through the filter
might enhance the concentration of U/Si. Two extremes were chosen that would have very low and high
U/ARD ratios in the feed. For the low U/ARD case, the depleted feed was used because it was expected
to have an exceptionally low U/ARD ratio due to the selective capture of uranium in the first pass through
the filter. The suspension collected during the first out-of-filter flush was chosen as the feed for the high
U/ARD case because the first out-of-magnet flush typically achieved the highest U recovery rates and
highest U/ARD ratios. Thus, from a replicate set of three tests, three more tests with a low U
concentration feed and three more with a high U concentration feed were generated. Because of the
number of sequential tests being performed, the sampling protocol was simplified. A single 20 min in-
magnet recirculating retention step was used to load the filter. Additionally, samples were taken from the
effluent only at the end of each filtration step rather than at discrete time intervals. Although the first two
low flow rate flushing steps were not changed, an additional high flow rate (2 L/min) recirculating flush
was added. The first recirculating flush was performed for 10 min with the filter in an ultrasonic bath. A
hand-held sonicator was also applied to the portion of the filter (a little less than half) that extended out of
the bath. After this sonicating flush, another high flow rate flush with DI water only was performed for 10
min. Sonication was also used during a final nitric acid rinse to ensure that no carryover of particles
would occur between experiments. The test matrix is summarized in Table 2.

Samples were analyzed using SP-ICP-TOF-MS as described in previous reports and publications. The
samples were also submitted for bulk inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry analyses.

 4. RESULTS

The results for triplicate experiments obtained to date are presented in this section. They are separated
into two steps—retention on the filter and recovery from the filter.

4.1 DEPOSITION ON THE FILTER AND RECOVERY OF UO2 AT HIGH FLOW RATE

The first triplicate tests were carried out using UO2 and ARD in suspension, with the concentration of
ARD about 10 times that of UO2, as described in Table 1. The suspension was run through the filter first
while out of the magnetic field, and then in the field. Samples were collected during both filtration steps,
as well as during the flushing steps that followed. For the final flushing step, a high flow rate of 2 L/min
was used. These data are summarized in Figure 1 and are provided in their entirety in Appendix B. To aid
in interpretation, the figure shows the fraction of the total particle population that is made up of U-bearing
particles (i.e., a value of 0.1 indicates that 10% of the particles in suspension contain uranium). The
elements analyzed, in approximate order of abundance, included Al, Ti, Fe, Si, Cs, Ce, and Au. The signal
from U was about the same as the signal from Si in the feed suspension. The signal from Al was about an
order of magnitude higher than the signal from Si and U and about five times the signal from Fe. The
elements Ce, Cs, and Au were present only in trace quantities. Hence, this report focuses on the ratios of
Al, Si, and Fe with respect to U, as did previous analyses. The analyses presented here do not include O,
the main electronegative element in the oxide system.

Figure 1 shows that the fraction of U in the initial feed was about 10% of the total, as expected. In the
effluents arising during the loading of the filter, the U concentration dropped by a factor 10, both when
the filter was in the magnet and when it was out of the magnet. Data from the ARD components also
indicated some retention in the magnet, although not to as great an extent, except for Ce. The bar chart in
Figure 1 shows the U fraction in the effluents obtained during the various flushing steps. In all but the
second run, the U ratio was higher in the flushes than in the feed, even for the in-magnet flush. Out of the
magnet, the removal of U was improved, and, after 12 min of the recirculating flush, the effluent was
enhanced by a factor of 2–10. The data show scatter, which arose primarily from the sampling protocol,
which became more precise as the experiments continued. The team was initially concerned that flushing
with HNO3 might compromise the Al parts in the filter housing but through testing determined that the
risk was low relative to the benefit gained by ensuring that all U was removed after each experiment.

4.1.1 Deposition on the Filter and Recovery of U3O8 at High Flow Rate and with Sonication

For the sonicated flush experiments, U3O8 (at a nominal concentration of 10 mg/L) was used instead of
UOx with no change in ARD concentration (100 mg/L). These tests were also performed in triplicate, and
the results are summarized in Figure 2. Again, the figure shows the fraction of the total particle
population that is made up of U-bearing particles for each of the sampling intervals. The first sample
shown is for the feed, and subsequent samples are for flushes, as indicated in the figure. The U3O8
introduced additional minor components that were evident with SP-ICP-TOF-MS, including Y, Cr, Mn,
and Pb, all at very low concentrations. Detailed results are presented in Appendix B.

It is evident that, in contrast to the previous series, a large variability was present in the measured feed
concentrations. The reason for such a swing is unclear, but Triton X was not used in the first of these
tests, and the low measured value suggests that the particles were not as well distributed in that feed. The
third test evidently had a significantly higher U concentration than the other tests in this series. Because
the weighing of the samples was precise, this difference may have arisen from inadvertent experimental
factors related to, for example, the reuse of vessels for feed preparation. Thus, focusing on changes in
U/ARD ratios for a particular test series is important to understand the behavior of the different types of
particles.

In these tests, much of the U was removed from the magnet in early flushes. In the timed recirculation
flush, much of the U was removed after 6 min, although the drop in ratio was not steep, and significant U
was still collected after 12 min of recirculation. Note that the use of sonication shifted the peak of U

No systematic differences were evident between the behavior of UO2 and U3O8 particles within the
uncertainty of the measurements. Qualitatively, the experimental results described in Sections 4.1 and
4.1.1 are similar.

4.1.2 Deposition on the Filter and Recovery of UO2 at High Flow Rate and with Sonication

Two replicate tests were conducted with a NaCl rinse with the possibility that the higher ionic strength
might weaken the forces attaching the UO2 to the demagnetized filter material, as suggested by some
members of the Independent Review panel. The results of these experiments are shown in Figure 3 in the
same format as the results for the previous tests. The U/ARD ratios in the feed suspension were high for
both tests, suggesting that there may have been some carryover contamination from previous studies. The
initial amounts of U in the feed were about 10 times the amounts measured in previous tests. However,
this occurrence should not affect interpretation of the results.

4.2 SEQUENTIAL TESTING PRELIMINARY RESULTS

Results from the analysis of sequential testing samples became available only recently, and they have not
been fully considered. Both single particle and bulk analyses data were recorded. The bulk data are
provided in Figure 4 for the nine replicate runs. In the figure, F indicates the feed, C indicates the effluent
after the feed was passed through the magnetized filter, 1 indicates the DI rinse with the filter in the
magnetic field, 2 indicates a pass through the DI rinse with the filter out of the magnetic field, R indicates
a recirculation test at high flow rate (2 L/min), S indicates a recirculation with sonication, and H indicates
a final HNO3 rinse of the filter with sonication. Note that the vertical axis is a log plot, so the increase in
U/Al ratio during the recirculation and sonication runs for the first six experiments is notable. For the last
runs, however, there was a decrease in U/Al ratio that needs further investigation. A small amount of Al
from the filter housing may have dissolved during the flush with HNO3 and may have affected the bulk
analyses.

Figure 5 shows the U/Al and U/Fe ratios for the SP-ICP-TOF-MS results for the last three runs, 1H–3H.
The single particle results show that the relative amount of U in particulate form increased during the
recirculation/sonication runs for both Fe and Al. These results contrast with the results from the latter
bulk runs and corroborate previous results that a high flow rate and sonication effectively increase the
ratio of U/ARD recovered from the filter.

 5. MODELING

5.1 TRAJECTORY SIMULATION METHODS

A complete description of the trajectory model is provided in Appendix C. This model is an extension of
the one described in Section 4 of the FY 2024 annual report for this project and includes an additional
consideration for the electrostatic force. The main thrust of this year’s modeling effort was to investigate
each force’s contribution to particle capture. These simulations also examined differences between the
capture of uranium oxide and soil matrix species (namely silica and hematite).

5.1.1 Simulation Results

To better understand the forces acting on the particles and their effects on the filter’s capture efficiency,
stochastic trajectory simulations were performed using the methods described in Appendix C. In
considering the trajectory of a particle toward a single ferromagnetic wire, we found that the two most
important forces acting on the particle are hydrodynamic drag and Brownian motion. This result was
expected given the small size (diameter ≤ 2 μm) of the simulated particles. Particle capture, in contrast, is
largely dictated by the relative strengths of the attractive magnetic and van der Waals forces as well as the

 12

repulsive electrostatic force. Moreover, Brownian motion, although it is not a direct capture force, still
indirectly enhances capture by influencing the particle trajectory. The gravitational/buoyancy force,
conversely, plays a relatively minor role in both particle trajectory and capture.

In particular, the capture of uranium oxide particles (Figure 6a) varies considerably with particle size
based on the interplay between the different forces acting on the particle. For small particles (e.g., 0.1 and
0.2 μm particles), the simulated capture efficiency of the magnetic filter is largely a product of Brownian
motion, which facilitates capture in conjunction with the magnetic force. For example, removing
Brownian motion from the capture simulation for 0.1 μm particles reduces the estimated capture
efficiency of the filter from approximately 95% to just 34%, with essentially all the remaining capture
directly attributable to the magnetic force. As particle size increases, however, Brownian motion becomes
less important, resulting in lower simulated capture rates for larger uranium particles. This finding agrees
with experimental findings from our prior work, which showed that smaller uranium oxide particles were
not only captured at a higher rate but also had a greater portion of their capture tied to the magnetic
mechanism than large particles.1,2 As shown in Figure 7, nonmagnetic capture tied to the van der Waals
force becomes more prominent as particle size increases, with up to 40% of simulated capture directly
attributable to the van der Waals force at a particle diameter of 0.6 μm. This fraction decreases somewhat
for the largest particles simulated because of the growing influence of the magnetic force, which increases
as a function of particle volume. Overall, these simulations tend to underpredict the effect of nonmagnetic
capture, which accounted for 60% of the filter’s experimental uranium oxide capture at flow rates of 250
mL/min or less (Section 3 in the FY 2024 annual report).

This underprediction may be tied partly to an underestimation of the van der Waals force because of the
assumption that the particles and filter wires are smooth spheres and cylinders, respectively. Artificially
increasing the strength of the van der Waals force does slightly increase simulated nonmagnetic capture
(e.g., from 40% to 47% of all capture for 0.6 μm particles), but this change alone does not fully explain
the underprediction of nonmagnetic capture in these simulations. Alternatively, the importance of the van
der Waals force on particle capture could be increased by increasing the assumed strength of the
electrostatic force, but this change would simultaneously reduce overall capture, leading to an
underprediction of the total capture rate. Increasing the strength of the van der Waals force alongside
increasing the strength of the electrostatic force could offset this loss of capture. Another possibility is
that the electrostatic force acting on the uranium oxide particles is attractive rather than repulsive as
previously assumed (Figure 5). Nevertheless, because the surface potential of the uranium oxide particles
is unknown, these considerations must be evaluated using additional experiments. Additionally, other
nonmagnetic forces may also contribute to the capture of uranium oxide particles and could be
investigated in future work.

For hematite and silica particles (Figure 6b), nonmagnetic capture accounts for a negligible component of
the overall simulated capture rate. This finding is in good agreement with our experimental findings from
filtration testing at 250 mL/min (Section 3 in the FY 2024 annual report) and can be attributed to the
larger particle size (i.e., stronger magnetic force and weaker contribution from Brownian motion) and the
weaker van der Waals force acting on these particles (i.e., Hamaker constants for silica, hematite, and
uranium oxide are 4.6, 39, and 150 zJ, respectively). Additionally, the simulated overall capture
efficiencies for hematite and silica are expected to be approximately 64% and 18%, respectively, based on
an approximate size distribution derived from the specifications supplied by Beckman Coulter
(Appendix B in the FY 2024 annual report). These results are also in good agreement with the
experimental filtration values reported in the 2024 annual report. Specifically, the estimated capture
efficiency of iron (primarily hematite in ARD) was about 65%, whereas the estimated capture efficiency
of silicon (primarily silica in ARD) was around 15% at a flow rate of 250 mL/min.

 6. POTENTIAL IMPROVEMENTS AND FUTURE WORK

6.1 CONSIDERATIONS FOR PARTICLE RECOVERY

Although high gradient magnetic separation has been shown, both in this and prior reports, to be a highly
effective means of capturing uranium particles from suspensions that contain soil materials, the removal
of uranium particles from the magnetic filter remains a consistent challenge. Various filter washing
methods (e.g., backwashing at a high flow rate and backwashing with sonication) have been used to
improve particle recovery rates, but a significant fraction of the particles captured are still retained. As
shown in the FY 2024 annual report, these particles tend to be retained through direct contact with the
stainless steel fibers used in the magnetic filter. At the particle–wire surface-to-surface distance in
question, the principal force acting on the retained particles is expected to be the van der Waals force
(Figure 8).

 Figure 8. Estimated absolute values for the magnetic and van der Waals forces acting on a uranium oxide
particle with a diameter of 1.0 μm as a function of the particle–wire surface-to-surface distance. The magnetic
and van der Waals forces are equal at a distance of approximately 1.69 μm. At shorter distances, the van der Waals
 force dominates.

This phenomenon, in part, helps to explain the relatively high rate of uranium oxide particle retention.
Mathematical estimates of the van der Waals force, particle–wire relationship in Eq. C6 (Appendix C),
are directly correlated with the material’s Hamaker constant. Uranium oxide has a significantly higher
Hamaker constant (150 zJ) than many prominent ARD species (e.g., silica’s Hamaker constant is 4.6 zJ),
which results in a proportional increase in the van der Waals force. Additionally, although the effect is
muted at close range, particle size is inversely proportional to the van der Waals force. Because the ARD
particles are larger than the uranium oxide particles (≤ 4 and ≤ 1 μm, respectively), the uranium oxide
particles are subject to a slightly stronger van der Waals force.

Though the strength of the van der Waals force acting on the uranium oxide particles presents a challenge
to recovery, it nevertheless could also be leveraged to attain a higher uranium selectivity. Sonication
coupled with a high eluant flow rate was demonstrated to remove U-bearing particles from the filter after
the less tightly bound ARD particles had been removed. Specifically, different removal rates could
potentially be achieved by precisely controlling the intensity and frequency of sonication to first target the
removal of soil particles that are relatively weakly retained. Once these particles are removed, the
intensity of the applied sonication could be increased to remove the uranium oxide retained on the filter.
Additionally, because the van der Waals force dominates at short range, this method could even facilitate
in-magnet recovery, eliminating the need for both in- and out-of-magnet recovery steps.

 15

6.1.1 Next Generation Apparatus Design

To achieve the aforementioned goals, the current apparatus design would need to be changed in two
significant ways. First, the apparatus would need to be designed in such a way that it could be fully
submersed in a sonication bath with tunable power and ultrasonic frequency. Use of a sonication bath was
shown in these experiments to be a much better means of delivering acoustic energy to the filter than the
handheld device. Second, the magnetic housing would need to have a more open design to reduce
dampening of the ultrasonic waves. This, in turn, would also require a redesign of the magnet array to
ensure that the applied magnetic field strength is not reduced. Additionally, the sonication medium is
another consideration because extended periods of submersion in water may lead to corrosion of the
magnetic filtration apparatus. For instance, in the field, filter loading would likely take place with the
assembly in the sonication bath, even if it is not turned on, to reduce the need for handling the apparatus.
Moreover, the use of dedicated sonication gels or oils may improve sonication performance.

 7. CONCLUSIONS AND PROJECT SUMMARY

During the second year of this project, it was firmly established that high gradient magnetic separation
does a thorough job of retaining uranium oxide particles in the magnetic filter. This retention is a result of
several forces acting on the particles, notably the magnetic force and Brownian motion (for small particles
< 1 µm). The van der Waals force is also involved in the retention step but is most significant after
retention is complete and the filter is removed from the magnetic field.

The recovery of uranium oxide particles from the filter proved to be a laborious process. Even after
removal from the magnetic field and a rinse solution circulated at high flow rate, about half of the
particles remained. Sonication was quite helpful in removing much of this residual material. Because of
the very high van der Waals forces for uranium oxides, the fraction of the residual particles that were
uranium was quite high. This effect was totally unexpected at the beginning of the project and took
considerable time and effort to identify and overcome. However, the van der Waals force appears to be at
least as significant as the magnetic force in separating UOx particles from most other materials.

Over the course of this project, considerable progress has been made in understanding the forces at play
and producing an apparatus and procedure capable of accomplishing the desired separation of actinide
particles. The technology readiness level of TRL = 5 has been accomplished as anticipated and is
summarized in Table 3. The details of the technology readiness assessment are provided in Appendix D.

The efficiency of a single filter cell (i.e., a volume of space containing a single filter fiber) can then be
derived using the trajectory analysis. This derivation is carried out by repeatedly simulating the trajectory
of particles entering the filter cell at various starting locations to ascertain the frequency with which
particles are captured. Particles that enter the cell at a point closer to the centerline, for example, tend to
be captured more often than those entering at a point closer to the cell edge. In most cases, in fact, capture
occurs within a relatively small band of starting positions near the cell centerline; most of the filter cell
yields negligible capture frequencies. Assuming that particles can enter the filter cell at any location with
equal likelihood, one can use an adequate assessment of the capture probability within the region where
capture is significant to determine cell efficiency. Limiting the scope of the simulation in this way helps
to mitigate the increased computational cost incurred when Brownian diffusion is incorporated into the
trajectory analysis (purely deterministic trajectory models only need to simulate particle movement at
each entry point once because no variation is present in the simulation). After the single cell efficiency (

Critical Technology Elements (A technology element is “critical” if the system being acquired depends on the technology element to
meet operational requirements (with acceptable development, cost and schedule; and with acceptable production and operations costs) and if
the technology element or its application is either new or novel.)
  Filter retention (essential functional element, modified for novel application)
  Recovery from filter (essential functional element, new feature, new environment)
  Measurement and assessment (essential functional element, requires novel approach)
  Optimal design (essential functional element, novel materials, new environment)

Assessment Justification (Provide any comments or considerations you used in making your assessment.)
Filter Retention. This step combines both laboratory procedure and hardware. Regarding the latter, filter
retention has been greatly enhanced by redesign and fabrication of a new filter apparatus, which has much higher
magnetic field strength. This apparatus qualifies as “engineering-scale prototype,” at TRL=6 in Table 1 (column
3) of the TRA Guide. However, the procedures only qualify as “analytical procedures and components
integrated,” corresponding to TRL=4 in Table 1 (column 2) of the Guide. Both of these are involved, creating a
hybrid value for overall retention step of TRL=5.

Recovery from Filter. Recovery from filter has been significantly improved by incorporating high flows and
sonication, however these have not been optimized. The apparatus that was TRL=6 for retention is only TRL=4
for recovery from the filter. Sonication is not integral to the apparatus as designed, but was imposed using a small
hand-held sonicator, indicating “basic hardware integrated into lab-scale experiments,” i.e. TRL=4 in Table 1 of
the Guide (column 3). Similarly, the procedures for removal constitute “basic procedures and instrumentation
integrated,” corresponding to TRL=4 in Table 1 of the Guide (column 1). This provides an overall composite
value of TRL=4.

Measurement and Assessment. Measurement and assessment has been greatly aided by collaboration with
another NA-22 project at ORNL, Single-Particle ICP Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (SP-ICP-TOF-MS).
This method has been used almost exclusively for our analytical measurements, resulting in good comparisons
between actinides and soil components. TRL=6. The reason that we list a higher TRL than the SP-ICP-TOF-MS
project is that for all practical purposes, we utilize it as a “black box,” where we supply samples and it returns
measurements. We are not concerned with efficiency, dilutions, preparations, calibrations, automation, or
operational performance. We are somewhat concerned with uncertainty and error but assume that such
assessments have been made and operations optimized. The development of the method must be concerned with
such issues, and because that development is still incomplete, their assessment of TRL=4 is different than ours.

Optimization. Full design capabilities are still rudimentary, awaiting optimization of sonication in apparatus and
procedures. The magnetic filter was optimized for particle retention, but the sonication needs to be optimized for
selective (i.e. actinide) particle recovery from the filter. Both the hardware and laboratory procedures reflect basic
capabilities, i.e., “basic analytical procedures and components integrated,” and “basic hardware components
integrated,” corresponding to TRL=4 in Table 1 of the Guide. Hence, assessment is TRL=4.

Overall. The assignment of an overall score of TRL=5 for the entire project is a synthesis of the TRL values for
each of the CTE’s described above.
