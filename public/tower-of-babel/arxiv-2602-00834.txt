A Minimum Variance Path Principle for Accurate and Stable Score-Based Density Ratio Estimation
Wei Chen, Jiacheng Li, Shigui Li, Zhiqi Lin, Junmei Yang, John Paisley, Delu Zeng

Abstract
Score-based methods are powerful across machine learning, but they face a paradox: theoretically path-independent, yet practically path-dependent. We resolve this by proving that practical training objectives differ from the ideal, ground-truth objective by a crucial, overlooked term: the path variance of the score function. We propose the MVP ( M imum V ariance P ath) Principle to minimize this path variance. Our key contribution is deriving a closed-form expression for the variance, making optimization tractable. By parameterizing the path with a flexible Kumaraswamy Mixture Model, our method learns data-adaptive, low-variance paths without heuristic manual selection. This principled optimization of the complete objective yields more accurate and stable estimators, establishing new state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks and providing a general framework for optimizing score-based interpolation.

Bridging the Gap: Our Contribution.

We propose the first DRE framework that goes beyond fixed or heuristic paths by explicitly learning an optimal path through a theoretically grounded objective. Our analysis shows that this objective (the path variance) is the missing term in the ideal score-matching loss that prior DRE methods have overlooked. By parameterizing the path with a flexible KMM and optimizing it with its path variance, we convert the intractable functional search into a tractable, low-dimensional optimization. Our method, MVP, thus adapts the path schedule to the data distributions, achieving more accurate density ratio estimation.

4
Minimum Path Variance Principle

Our proposed method, MVP, is designed to resolve an important discrepancy between the theory and practice of score-based DRE. In this section, we detail the core components of our method: the derivation of the analytical path variance and our flexible KMM-based path parameterization.

4.1
The Path Variance: An Overlooked Term in the Objective

Our analysis begins by relating the expected density ratio estimation error to the score-matching objective. We consider the estimation error,

\Delta({\bm{x}})=|\log r({\bm{x}})-\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}})|^{2},\quad{\bm{x}}\sim p_{1},

(7)

where the true and estimated log-density ratios are given by
\log r({\bm{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\mathrm{d}t
and
\log\hat{r}({\bm{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\mathrm{d}t
. The lemma below provides an explicit upper bound for this error.

Lemma
\thetheorem
.

Let
\{p_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be a probability path connecting
p_{0}
and
p_{1}

Section

A.1

\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\Delta({\bm{x}})\right]\leq\left(\frac{e^{nL}-1}{nL}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\right)^{m}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{m}},

(8)

where
\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)=s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)
is the error of the score model, the exponents satisfy
\tfrac{1}{n}+\tfrac{1}{m}=1
, and
L
is the Lipschitz constant in
Section

A.1
.

Relationship to Existing Error Bounds.

Our derivation in

4.1

71

However, important differences remain. Their bound is expressed in terms of statistical divergence between the true and estimated density ratios, whereas our

4.1

71

\mathcal{V}

The proof is presented in
Section

A.2

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}]\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left|\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

{theorem}

[Minimum Path Variance Principle]

In the loss decomposition (

\mathcal{V}\triangleq\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}))\mathrm{d}t

\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}[\Delta({\bm{x}})]\leq e^{L}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{STSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}))\mathrm{d}t\right].

This establishes the
Min

V

P

MVP
) principle

\mathcal{V}

4.2
Minimizing the Path Variance via KMM Parameterization

The analytical expressions in
Section

4.1
transform the problem of minimizing the path variance into a functional optimization problem over the space of valid path schedules
(\alpha,\beta)
. To solve this tractably, we introduce a flexible, low-dimensional parameterization for the path that satisfies the necessary boundary conditions and monotonicity constraints by construction. This transforms an infinite-dimensional functional optimization into a low-dimensional parametric one, offering superior stability and tractability.

A Function Class with Built-in Constraints.

A valid schedule
\{\alpha(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
must satisfy the boundary conditions
\alpha(0)=1,\alpha(1)=0
and be monotonically decreasing. Instead of enforcing these properties with external constraints, we select a function class that satisfies them inherently. We achieve this by defining
\alpha
based on the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a probability distribution defined on
[0,1]
. A CDF,
F(t)
, is by definition a monotonically increasing function from
0
to
1
. Therefore, the transformation
1-F(t)
yields a function that is monotonically decreasing from
1
to
0
, matching our requirements for
\{\alpha(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
.

The Kumaraswamy Mixture Model (KMM).

While a single distribution on
[0,1]
(e.g., Beta or Kumaraswamy) could define a valid path, its expressive power is limited, being unimodal. To construct a significantly more flexible function class, we employ a
Kumaraswamy Mixture Model (KMM)

(
34
;
55
)
. The Kumaraswamy distribution is chosen over the more common Beta distribution for its simple and closed-form CDF, which allows for efficient and stable computation of derivatives. By taking a weighted sum of several Kumaraswamy components, we can create complex, multimodal probability density shapes, which in turn allows the path schedule to have varying rates of change to optimally minimize variance.

Formally, we define a
K
-component KMM, parameterized by
{\bm{\phi}}=\{w_{k},a_{k},b_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, via its probability density function (PDF) and CDF,

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\cdot\mathrm{KS}(t;a_{k},b_{k}),\quad F_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\left[1-(1-t^{a_{k}})^{b_{k}}\right],

(13)

where
\mathrm{KS}(t;a,b)=abt^{a-1}(1-t^{a})^{b-1}
is the Kumaraswamy PDF,
w_{k}
are mixture weights satisfying
\sum w_{k}=1
, and
a_{k},b_{k}>0
are shape parameters.

Final Construction and Guaranteed Properties.

We now define our learnable path schedule
\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)
using the KMM’s CDF. The derivative
\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)
is simply the negative of the KMM’s PDF,

\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=1-F_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t),\quad\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=-p_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t).

(14)

This construction guarantees the following desired properties without external enforcement:

•

Boundary Conditions:
\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(0)=1-F_{{\bm{\phi}}}(0)=1
and
\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(1)=1-F_{{\bm{\phi}}}(1)=0
.

•

Monotonicity:
\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)
is guaranteed to be monotonically decreasing since
p_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)\geq 0
.

•

Smoothness:
\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,1)
, ensuring a smooth probability flow.

The coupling schedule
\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)
and its derivative follow from the constraint selected below.

Affine:

\displaystyle\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)+\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=1-\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t),\quad\dot{\beta}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=-\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t).

(15)

Spherical:

\displaystyle\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}+\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}},\quad\dot{\beta}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)=-\tfrac{\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)}{\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)}.

(16)

Substituting into the path variance expressions in
Section

4.1
, we recast the functional optimization as a finite-dimensional problem over the KMM parameters
{\bm{\phi}}
, namely minimizing
\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}]
.

Parameterization for Unconstrained Optimization.

To perform gradient-based optimization for
{\bm{\phi}}
, we must handle the constraints on the KMM parameters (
w_{k}>0,\sum w_{k}=1,a_{k}>0,b_{k}>0
). We use a standard reparameterization technique, optimizing a set of unconstrained latent variables
\hat{{\bm{\phi}}}=\{\hat{w}_{k},\hat{a}_{k},\hat{b}_{k}\}
which are mapped to valid KMM parameters via softmax and softplus transformations,

w_{k}=\frac{e^{\hat{w}_{k}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{\hat{w}_{k}}},\quad a_{k}=\text{softplus}(\hat{a}_{k})=\log(1+e^{\hat{a}_{k}}),\quad b_{k}=\text{softplus}(\hat{b}_{k}).

(17)

Conclusions and Future Works

This paper addresses the key paradox in score-based density ratio estimation (DRE) that estimator performance strongly depends on the chosen path schedule while being theoretically path-invariant.
Our analysis reveals that this discrepancy arises from an overlooked path variance term in training objectives. Based on this observation, we established the
Min
imum
P
ath
V
ariance (MVP) principle, arguing that this term should be explicitly minimized rather than ignored. We then proposed MVP, a framework that derives a closed-form expression for path variance and directly optimizes it. By parameterizing the path with a Kumaraswamy Mixture (KMM) Model, MVP learns a data-adaptive low-variance path. This eliminates the need for heuristic path selection and ensures closer alignment with the ideal score-matching objective. Experiments demonstrate that MVP achieves SOTA performance in estimation error and stability across challenging DRE benchmarks.

Future Works.

The MVP principle is general and could benefit other score-based generative models by learning optimal noise schedules to improve sample quality and stability. Beyond accuracy, future research could focus on computational efficiency, for example by leveraging optimal transport–based path formulations or adopting lighter parameterizations to further accelerate training. We believe that explicitly optimizing the geometry of interpolation paths will continue to afford significant advances in density ratio estimation and related score-based methods.

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\left(s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s^{(t)}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}},t)\right)\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}1^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\quad(\text{Cauchy-Schwarz inequality})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Taking expectations over
p_{1}({\bm{x}})
and applying Tonelli’s theorem (the integrand is nonnegative) gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\Delta({\bm{x}})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.\quad\left(\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\geq 0\right)

(21)

Next, we rewrite the inner expectation with respect to
p_{1}
in terms of
p_{t}
. Using the change of measure:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}p_{1}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot\frac{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot\exp\left(\log p_{1}({\bm{x}})-\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot\exp\left(\int^{1}_{t}\partial_{s}\log p_{s}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot\exp\left(\int^{1}_{t}\left|\partial_{s}\log p_{s}({\bm{x}})\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\quad(\star)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot e^{L(1-t)}\right],\quad(\star\star)

where
(\star)
follows since
\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\geq 0
a.e., and
(\star\star)
follows under
Section

A.1
.

Substituting
Equation

22
into
Equation

21
yields the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}\left[\Delta({\bm{x}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\cdot e^{L(1-t)}\right]\mathrm{d}t

(23)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}e^{L(1-t)}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{nL(1-t)}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\right)^{m}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e^{nL}-1}{nL}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\right)^{m}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{m}},

where the second inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality with
\tfrac{1}{n}+\tfrac{1}{m}=1
. Different choices of
(n,m)
yield different bounds:

•

(n=\infty,m=1)
:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}[\Delta({\bm{x}})]\leq e^{L}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}]\mathrm{d}t,

(24)

which preserves the training loss in its original integral form, at the cost of a looser exponential factor
e^{L}
.

•

(n=2,m=2)
(Cauchy–Schwarz):

\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}})}[\Delta({\bm{x}})]\leq\left(\frac{e^{2L}-1}{2L}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}[\epsilon({\bm{x}},t)^{2}]\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(25)

which yields a tighter
L
-dependent factor but replaces the training loss with an
L^{2}
-type form.

•

General
(n,m)
: one may also choose intermediate
(n,m)
to trade off between the looseness of the exponential factor
\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{nL(1-t)}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/n}
and the form of the training loss norm.

This finishes the proof.

∎

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right),

since

A.4
Proof of
Section

4.1

See
4.1

Proof.

Let
s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
be the true time score. We provide the derivation for each case separately. The core strategy for both is to apply the law of total variance to decompose
\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)
and then analyze each component.

Case 1 (DI).

For the DI case, the interpolant is
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\alpha(t){\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\mathbf{x}}_{1}
, where
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{0}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{x}};{\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\sim p_{1}({\bm{x}})
. The transition kernel for a given
{\bm{x}}_{1}
is
p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{x}};\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1},\alpha(t)^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
.

By the law of total variance, we can decompose the variance term:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)\right]}_{\text{Term (I)}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right]\right)}_{\text{Term (II)}}.

We first compute the inner expectation required for Term (II). The conditional time score is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left[-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)-d\log\alpha(t)-\frac{\|{\bm{x}}-\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}}{2\alpha(t)^{2}}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=-d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}+\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{3}}\|{\bm{x}}-\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{2}}({\bm{x}}-\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle=-d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}+\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{3}}\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{2}}{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1},

where we have defined the centered sample
{\bm{z}}={\bm{x}}-\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{z}};{\bm{0}},\alpha(t)^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})
.

Using the moments
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}]={\bm{0}}
and
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}]=d\alpha(t)^{2}
, the inner expectation simplifies to:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right]=-d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}+\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{3}}\left(d\alpha(t)^{2}\right)+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}]^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1}=0.

(32)

Since the conditional expectation is zero for any
{\bm{x}}_{1}
, its variance, Term (II), is also zero.
The total variance thus simplifies entirely to Term (I)
. Furthermore, because the conditional mean of the time score is zero, its conditional variance equals its conditional second moment:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)^{2}\right]

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\left(\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{3}}\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}-d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{2}}{\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{3}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{4}]+\left(d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}\right)^{2}-2d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{4}}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}]+\left(\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}[({\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{6}}d(d+2)\alpha(t)^{4}+d^{2}\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}-2d\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{4}}(d\alpha(t)^{2})+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{4}}(\alpha(t)^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\left(d(d+2)+d^{2}-2d^{2}\right)+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2d\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}.

Here, we use the standard Gaussian moments:
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{4}]=d(d+2)\alpha(t)^{4}
,
\mathbb{E}[({\bm{z}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1})^{2}]=\alpha(t)^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}
, and that all cross-terms involving odd moments of
{\bm{z}}
are zero.

Finally, substituting this result into (I) and taking the expectation over
p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})
yields the total variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\frac{2d\dot{\alpha}_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}+\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2d\dot{\alpha}_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}+\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}\right].

Case 2 (DDBI).

For the DDBI case, the interpolant is
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\alpha(t){\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\mathbf{x}}_{1}+\sigma_{t}{\mathbf{z}}
, with
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{0}
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\sim p_{1}
, and noise schedule
\sigma_{t}^{2}=t(1-t)\gamma^{2}+(\alpha(t)^{2}+\beta(t)^{2})\varepsilon
. The transition kernel conditioned on both endpoints is
p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{\mu}}_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
, where
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=\alpha(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}
.

The law of total variance is now applied by conditioning on the pair
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)\right]}_{\text{Term (I)}}

(35)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\right]\right)}_{\text{Term (II)}}.

Let
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=\alpha(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}
and
\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}=\dot{\alpha}(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}(t){\bm{x}}_{1}
. The conditional time score is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left[-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2})-\frac{\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|^{2}}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})+\frac{({\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t})^{\top}\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

Letting
{\bm{z}}={\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})
with
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}]=d\sigma_{t}^{2}
, we take the conditional expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}]}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})+\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}]^{\top}\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

(37)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{d\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})=0.

As in the DI case, the conditional expectation is zero, and thus Term (II) is zero and the total variance reduces to the Term I. Similarly, the conditional variance equals the conditional second moment:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{d}{2}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{{\bm{z}}^{\top}\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{d^{2}}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\quad(\star)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{4}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}-\frac{d^{2}}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{4(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{4}}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{4}]+\frac{1}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\mathbb{E}[({\bm{z}}^{\top}\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})^{2}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{d^{2}}{4}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}d(d+2)(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}{4(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{4}}+\frac{\|\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}\|^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}\quad(\star\star)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{4}+\frac{d(d+2)}{4}\right)\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\|\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-d^{2}+d^{2}+2d)}{4}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\|\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\|\dot{\alpha}(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},

(38)

where
(\star)
holds because the cross-terms involving odd moments of the centered Gaussian
{\bm{z}}
are zero and the rest term satisfies
\mathbb{E}[2AB]=2A\cdot\mathbb{E}[B]=\frac{d^{2}}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}
with
A=\frac{d}{2}\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},B=\frac{\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}
.
(\star\star)
holds with the moments
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{z}}\|^{4}]=d(d+2)(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}
and the quadratic form
\mathbb{E}[({\bm{z}}^{\top}\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t})^{2}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\|\dot{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}\|^{2}
.

Taking the final expectation over
p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
and
p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})
gives the total variance:

\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)=\frac{d}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p_{0},p_{1}}\left\|\dot{\alpha}(t){\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}(t){\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(39)

This completes the proof for the DDBI case.
∎

A.5
Control of the Lipschitz Constant via Minimum Variance Path Principle

Remark
\thetheorem
.

The connection between path variance
\mathcal{V}[\alpha,\beta]
and the Lipschitz constant
L
in
Section

A.1
is primarily
empirical
and
heuristic
in nature, which is why we present it as a remark rather than a formal proposition. In practice, minimizing
\mathcal{V}
tends to produce smoother paths, and empirically correlates with reduced values of
L
.

Argument.

Our heuristic is motivated by the observation that both
\mathcal{V}
and the time score magnitude depend on the path derivatives
\dot{\alpha}(t)
and
\dot{\beta}(t)
. Specifically, the closed-form expressions for
\mathcal{V}
in
Section

4.1
are explicit functionals of squared path velocities (e.g.,
\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2}
,
\dot{\beta}(t)^{2}
, or
\|\dot{\alpha}(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}
). Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{V}
discourages paths with large instantaneous velocities.

Because our KMM parameterization yields smooth, bounded paths (see
Equation

14
), extreme velocity spikes are structurally unlikely. In such a regular function class, minimizing the integrated squared velocity
\mathcal{V}

approximately suppresses
the maximum path velocity, which in turn appears to help keep
L
from becoming excessively large in practice (see
Section

A.1
for detailed derivations).

This behavior is consistently observed in our experiments: the optimized paths (see
Figures

5
,
6
,
7
and
8
in
Section

B.6
) are visibly smooth. We emphasize that this is a
phenomenological observation
, not a theoretical guarantee, and further analysis of the precise relationship between path geometry and
L
remains an open question.
∎

A.6
Proof of the Equivalence of Time Score Matching Objectives

Proposition
\thetheorem
.

Let
p_{t}
be the marginal distribution induced by the path schedule
\{\alpha(t),\beta(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
. For any fixed schedule, all three objectives share the same optimum:

s_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
.

•

Time Score Matching (TSM):

\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2}.

(40)

•

Conditional Time Score Matching (CTSM):

\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{CTSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})p({\bm{y}})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2},

(41)

where
{\bm{y}}
is an instance of the conditioning variable
{\mathbf{y}}
(
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{x}}_{1}
for the DI,
{\mathbf{y}}=({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}}_{1})
for the DDBI).

•

Sliced Time Score Matching (STSM):

\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{STSM}}({\bm{\theta}})\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[\partial_{t}s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\!+\!\frac{1}{2}s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right]\!+\!\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{boundary}},

(42)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{boundary}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{0},0)\!-\!s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{1},1)\right]
.

Proof.

We prove that all three objectives are minimized when
s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
by showing their differences are constants independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
.

Part 1: Relating CTSM and TSM.

We begin with the CTSM objective. For fixed
t
, the inner expectation in CTSM can be expanded using the identity
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}({\bm{z}})+\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}]^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTSM}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{y}})-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2}\right].

(43)

For a given
t
and sample
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, we analyze the inner expectation over
{\bm{y}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})-\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)+\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2}\right]\quad(\star)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})-\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right\|^{2}\right]+\left|\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})\right)+\left|\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2},

where
(\star)
holds under
\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})]
.
The key insight is that the conditional expectation
\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
yields the marginal score:

\mu({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})\right]=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}).

(45)

Substituting this back yields the final relationship:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})\right)\right]}_{V_{\text{cond}}}.

(46)

Since
V_{\text{cond}}
is independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
, both objectives share the same minimizer.

Part 2: Relating STSM and TSM.

The STSM objective is derived via integration by parts from the TSM objective. The standard derivation shows that
(
11
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{STSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(t)}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right|^{2}}_{V_{\text{marg}}}.

(47)

Since
V_{\text{marg}}
is independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
and is a constant for a given path schedule, the optimum is shared.

Finally, since
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\text{const}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{STSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\text{const}
, all three objectives are minimized by the same model
s_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
.
∎

B.2
Experimental Settings for Objectives

Our experimental framework follows the theoretical analysis in
Section

4.1
. For each interpolant class (DI and DDBI), we have two primary objectives: 1) the minimization of the path variance
\mathcal{V}[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}]
to learn an optimal path schedule, and 2) the minimization of a joint score matching loss to train the joint score model
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq[s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}})}({\bm{x}},t),s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)]
. Here,
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}})}
and
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}
are the data and time score models, aiming to approximate the data score
s^{({\bm{x}})}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq\partial_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
and time score
s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
. The superscripts
(t)
and
({\bm{x}})
are labels indicating “time” and “data”, not derivatives.
We use these notations consistently throughout the paper.
This section provides the precise mathematical formulations for these objectives as implemented in our experiments.

B.2.1
Commonly Used Objectives

In this section, we introduce three objectives for training the time score model.

Time Score Matching (TSM).

To approximate the true marginal time score
s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)
, the time score model
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)
is trained by minimizing the ideal, but intractable, time score-matching loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left|s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}.

(56)

Sliced Time Score Matching (STSM).

Since the target score
s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)
is unknown, this objective cannot be optimized directly. A standard technique, as shown in
11
, is to apply integration by parts to derive a tractable objective. This equivalent training loss, which is termed as the sliced time score matching (STSM), is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{STSM}}({\bm{\theta}})\!=\!2\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{0},0)\!-\!s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{1},1)\right]\!+\!\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left[2\partial_{t}s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\!+\!s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)^{2}\right],

(57)

where the first term denote the boundary conditions of
s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}
,
\partial_{t}s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}
is the time derivative of
s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}
, and
p(t)
is a timestep distribution over
(0,1)
(see
Section

B.2.5
for details).

Conditional Time Score Matching (CTSM).

In the limit of infinite data and model capacity, the time score model
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}
exactly recovers the time score
s^{(t)}
by minimizing the conditional time score matching (CTSM) objective
(
71
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})p({\bm{y}})}\left|\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}})-s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2},

(58)

where
p(t)
is a timestep distribution (see
Section

B.2.5
for details),
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is induced from DI (see
Equation

2
) or DDBI (see
Equation

3
), and
{\bm{y}}
is an instance of the conditioning variable
{\mathbf{y}}
(
{\mathbf{y}}\triangleq{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
for DI,
{\mathbf{y}}\triangleq({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}}_{1})
for DDBI).

We Use CTSM.

As shown in
Section

A.6
, all these three objectives are equivalent, guaranteeing that the learned model satisfies

s_{{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
.
Hence, we adopt CTSM for training since (i) the conditional score is available in closed form, (ii) no derivatives are required, and (iii) the score model is evaluated only once per batch.

B.2.2
Total Objective for DRE with the DI

Path Variance Objective.

For the DI setting, we learn the path parameters
{\bm{\phi}}
of our KMM-based schedule by minimizing the analytical variance given in
Section

4.1
:

\min_{{\bm{\phi}}}\quad\mathcal{V}_{\text{DI}}\left[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{2d\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}}{\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}}+\frac{\dot{\beta}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}}{\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t.

(59)

Joint Score Matching Objective.

We adopt the joint training strategy from
11
;
71
, where the joint score network
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}},t)\triangleq[s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}})}({\bm{x}},t),s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)]
is trained to simultaneously predict both the conditional time score and the conditional data score. The network thus outputs a scalar time score
s^{(t)}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}
and a vector data score
s^{({\bm{x}})}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The time score target is
s^{(t)}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})\triangleq\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})
, given by:

s^{(t)}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})=\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)}{\alpha(t)}\left(\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}-d\right)+\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)}{\alpha(t)}{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1}.

(60)

The data score target is
s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1})
. This is given by:

s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})=-\frac{{\bm{x}}_{t}-\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha(t){\bm{x}}_{0}}{\alpha(t)^{2}}=-\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\alpha(t)}.

(61)

Thus, the conditional joint score matching objective (CJSM) is to minimize the sum of the mean squared errors for both scores:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CJSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left|s^{({\bm{x}},t)}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})-s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2},

(62)

where
s^{({\bm{x}},t)}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{1})=[s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t};{\bm{x}}_{1}),s^{(t)}_{\text{DI}}({\bm{x}}_{t};{\bm{x}}_{1})]
is the joint score target and
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=[s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}})}({\bm{x}}_{t},t),s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)]
is the joint score model.

Total Objective.

The total objective is the weighted sum of the path variance objective and the joint score matching objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\phi}})=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CJSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{V}_{\text{DI}}\left[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}\right],\quad\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1,

(63)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the weighting coefficients determined by the Soft Optimal Uncertainty Weighting (UW-SO) method (see
Section

B.2.4
for details).

B.2.3
Total Objective for DRE with the DDBI

Path Variance Objective.

Similarly, for the DDBI setting, the KMM path parameters
{\bm{\phi}}
are learned by minimizing the corresponding analytical variance from
Section

4.1
:

\min_{{\bm{\phi}}}\quad\mathcal{V}_{\text{DDBI}}\left[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{d}{2}\frac{(\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2})^{2}}{(\sigma_{t}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left\|\dot{\alpha}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t){\bm{x}}_{1}\right\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

(64)

where
\sigma_{t}^{2}=t(1-t)\gamma^{2}+(\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2}+\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}(t)^{2})\varepsilon
,
\gamma\geq 0
is the noise factor, and
\varepsilon
is a small number.

Joint Score Matching Objective.

Following the same joint training strategy, the network predicts both the conditional time and data scores. The target scores are conditioned on the endpoints
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})
.

The time score target is
s^{(t)}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\triangleq\partial_{t}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})
, given by:

s^{(t)}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})=\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(\frac{\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}-d\right)+\frac{(\dot{\alpha}(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\dot{\beta}(t){\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{z}}}{\sigma_{t}}.

(65)

The data score target is
s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})
, which simplifies to:

s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})=-\frac{{\bm{x}}_{t}-(\alpha(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1})}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}=-\frac{\sigma_{t}{\bm{z}}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}=-\frac{{\bm{z}}}{\sigma_{t}}.

(66)

In these expressions,
\sigma_{t}^{2}=t(1-t)\gamma^{2}+(\alpha(t)^{2}+\beta(t)^{2})\varepsilon
and
{\bm{z}}=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{t}-(\alpha(t){\bm{x}}_{0}+\beta(t){\bm{x}}_{1})}{\sigma_{t}}
.

Similarly, the CJSM objective is to minimize the sum of the mean squared errors for both scores:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CJSM}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})p_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\left|s^{({\bm{x}},t)}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})-s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\right|^{2},

(67)

where
s^{({\bm{x}},t)}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})=[s^{({\bm{x}})}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1}),s^{(t)}_{\text{DDBI}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t;{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1})]
is the joint score target and
s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}},t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=[s_{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{x}})}({\bm{x}}_{t},t),s_{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)]
is the joint score model.

Total Objective.

Similarly, the total objective is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\phi}})=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CJSM}}({\bm{\theta}})+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{V}_{\text{DDBI}}\left[\alpha_{{\bm{\phi}}},\beta_{{\bm{\phi}}}\right],\quad\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1.

(68)

B.2.4
Adaptive Objective Weighting Trick

We balance heterogeneous training objectives, including the path and the joint score matching objectives, using the soft optimal uncertainty weighting (UW-SO) method of
33
, an analytical refinement of the uncertainty-based weighting framework of
30
.
UW-SO computes closed-form weights at every optimization step, eliminating the need for learned noise parameters while preserving the benefits of uncertainty-aware balancing.

Given
J
loss terms
\{\mathcal{L}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, we compute their normalized weights as

\lambda_{j}=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{1}{T(\mathcal{L}_{j}+\epsilon)}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\exp\left(\frac{1}{T(\mathcal{L}_{j}+\epsilon)}\right)},

(69)

where
\epsilon>0
ensures numerical stability and
T>0
is a softmax temperature (we set
T=2
by default).
The final training objective is a stop-gradient weighted sum:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\texttt{sg}(\lambda_{j})\mathcal{L}_{j},

(70)

where
sg
denotes the stop-gradient operation, preventing the weights from affecting the backward pass.
This yields a parameter-free, stable, and easily reproducible loss combination strategy, which we adopt as the default in all experiments.

B.2.5
Variance-based Importance Sampling for Timesteps

Rather than sampling
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
, we adopt a variance-based importance sampler inspired by
56
;
71
, which improves training stability by focusing computational effort on time steps where the ground-truth score signal is more reliable (i.e., has lower variance).

We utilize this sampler to fundamentally simplify the training objective. We start with the objective containing the optimal inverse-variance weighting function,
\lambda(t)\propto 1/\left(\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\varepsilon\right)
, and employ importance sampling by drawing time steps
t\sim p(t)
. The resulting objective is proportional to:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}\propto\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t)}\left[\frac{\lambda(t)}{p(t)}\cdot\left|s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}\right].

(71)

By setting the sampling distribution
p(t)
to be directly proportional to this optimal weighting function:

p(t)\propto\lambda(t)\propto\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}({\bm{x}})}\left(s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right)+\varepsilon},

(72)

the importance sampling factor
\frac{\lambda(t)}{p(t)}
becomes a constant,
\frac{1}{C}
. This results in an effectively uniform weighting of the squared error term:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{TSM}}\propto\frac{1}{C}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t)}\left[\left|s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}\right]\propto\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t)}\left[\left|s^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)-s_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}},t)\right|^{2}\right].

(73)

This mathematically guarantees the benefits of optimal weighting while maintaining an objective free of explicit, non-unity weighting functions.

Analytical expressions of the variance are available for DI and DDBI (
Section

4.1
). In practice, we pre-compute variances on a time grid and sample
t
from the resulting categorical distribution, updating it periodically as the path schedule
\big(\alpha_{\bm{\phi}}(t),\beta_{\bm{\phi}}(t)\big)
evolves.

B.3
Experimental Settings for the Kumaraswamy Distribution

A Stable Kumaraswamy.

The Kumaraswamy distribution suffers from severe numerical issues near the
[0,1]
boundaries, where direct evaluation of
t^{a}
or
(1-t^{a})^{b}
leads to catastrophic cancellation and unstable gradients
(
65
)
. To address this, we compute powers as,

x^{y}=\exp\left(y\cdot\mathrm{log1p}(x-1)\right),

(74)

which preserves precision by avoiding subtraction of nearly equal numbers. This stable power function is applied to the CDF and PDF (
Equation

13
), ensuring reliable gradients during training.

Initialization Strategy for Mixture Components.

To prevent component collapse and encourage specialization, we initialize the
K
Kumaraswamy components with modes spread across
[0,1]
. For each
\tau_{k}=\frac{k+0.5}{K+1}
, the shape parameters
(a_{k},b_{k})
are set asymmetrically:

(a_{k},b_{k})=\begin{cases}(1.5+0.5k,3.0+2.0(K-k))&\text{if }\tau_{k}<0.5,\\
(3.0+2.0k,1.5+0.5(K-k))&\text{if }\tau_{k}\geq 0.5.\end{cases}

(75)

This design produces right-skewed components (
a_{k}<b_{k}
) for
\tau_{k}<0.5
and left-skewed components (
a_{k}>b_{k}
) for
\tau_{k}\geq 0.5
, ensuring that their modes

\mathrm{mode}(k)=\left(\frac{a_{k}-1}{a_{k}b_{k}-1}\right)^{1/a_{k}},\qquad a_{k}>1,b_{k}>1,

(76)

are distributed across
[0,1]
. The weights are initialized uniformly,
w_{k}=1/K
. Such diversity-oriented initialization accelerates convergence and avoids degenerate solutions
(
14
)
.

B.4
Experimental Settings and Results for
f

Mutual Information (MI) Estimation.

MI quantifies the statistical dependence between two random variables
{\mathbf{x}}\sim p({\bm{x}})
and
{\mathbf{y}}\sim q({\bm{y}})
, and is defined as

\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\left[\log\frac{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{p({\bm{x}})q({\bm{y}})}\right]
.
MI estimation naturally reduces to DRE since
\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
involves the expectation of a log density ratio.

Details on Geometrically Pathological Distributions.

To further probe the robustness of our method, we evaluate it on a suite of five MI estimation tasks involving geometrically pathological distributions. These tasks, inspired by the benchmark suite from
13
, are specifically designed to challenge the underlying assumptions of many standard estimators.

1.

Edge-Singular Gaussian.

This task assesses model stability in edge-case correlation scenarios. We consider two jointly Gaussian random variables,
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},[[1,\rho],[\rho,1]])
. The mutual information, given by
\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=-0.5\log(1-\rho^{2})
, becomes numerically challenging at the extremes of the correlation coefficient
\rho
. As
\rho\to 1^{-}
, the distributions become nearly degenerate and collinear, leading to numerical divergence in many estimators. Conversely, as
\rho\to 0^{+}
, the dependency signal becomes vanishingly weak, testing the model’s sensitivity and its ability to avoid vanishing gradients.

2.

Half-Cube Map.

This distribution challenges models with heavy-tailed data. We begin with two correlated Gaussian variables and apply the homeomorphism
\text{half-cube}({\mathbf{z}})=|{\mathbf{z}}|^{3/2}\mathrm{sign}({\mathbf{z}})
to each. The resulting variables,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=\text{half-cube}({\mathbf{x}})
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\text{half-cube}({\mathbf{y}})
, have significantly heavier tails than the original Gaussians. While this transformation preserves the mutual information, i.e.,
\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime};{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})=\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})
, the distorted, heavy-tailed geometry can be difficult for non-parametric methods that rely on local density estimation.

3.

Asinh Mapping.

This task tests the model’s ability to handle distributions with highly concentrated densities. We start with two independent standard Gaussian random variables,
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
, and apply the inverse hyperbolic sine transformation,
\mathrm{asinh}({\mathbf{z}})=\log({\mathbf{z}}+\sqrt{{\mathbf{z}}^{2}+1})
, to each. The resulting variables,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=\mathrm{asinh}({\mathbf{x}})
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\mathrm{asinh}({\mathbf{y}})
, form our two distributions. This mapping compresses the tails of the original Gaussian distributions into a central region with extremely high curvature, creating sharp density peaks that can cause gradient instability and pose significant challenges for kernel-based methods.

4.

Additive Noise.

This scenario is designed to evaluate performance on distributions with fragmented support and sharp discontinuities. Let
{\mathbf{x}}
be a random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{U}({\bm{0}},{\bm{1}})
, and let
{\mathbf{n}}
be an independent noise variable,
{\mathbf{n}}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. We define the second variable as
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{n}}
. The resulting joint distribution is piecewise-constant and possesses sharp, non-differentiable boundaries. This structure violates the smoothness assumptions underlying many score-based estimators, providing a stringent test of model robustness. The true MI is given by
\text{MI}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=\log(2\epsilon)+0.5
for
\epsilon\leq 0.5
.

5.

Gamma-Exponential.

This task features a complex, non-linear dependency structure. We define a hierarchical relationship where the first variable
{\mathbf{x}}
is drawn from a Gamma distribution,
{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\rho,1)
. The second variable
{\mathbf{y}}
is then drawn from an Exponential distribution whose rate parameter is given by the value of
{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}\sim\mathrm{Exponential}({\bm{x}})
. The resulting joint distribution is highly asymmetric and non-Gaussian. The true mutual information is
I({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{y}})=\psi(\rho+1)-\log(\rho)
, where
\psi
is the digamma function.

The results in
Table

6(e)
can be summarized as follows:
(1) On the Edge-Singular Gaussian task, MVP (both affine and spherical) attains the lowest or near-lowest MSE, especially under extreme correlations (
\rho\to 0,\pm 1
). On the Half-Cube Map task with heavy tails, the spherical constraint yields stable and accurate estimates, outperforming all baselines.
(2) On the Asinh Mapping task with concentrated densities and sharp curvature, MVP again achieves the lowest error, while baselines such as VP struggle with density peaks.
(3) The largest gains appear on the Additive Noise and Gamma–Exponential tasks. With sharp discontinuities and complex non-linear dependencies, fixed-path methods degrade severely, whereas MVP, particularly with the spherical constraint, achieves dramatically lower MSE, often by an order of magnitude.

Overall, no fixed path is uniformly effective across all five benchmarks, with performance varying by data geometry. In contrast, MVP consistently delivers state-of-the-art accuracy by optimizing path variance, producing data-adaptive trajectories tailored to each problem’s structure.

High-dimensional & High-discrepancy Distributions.

To rigorously test model performance under extreme conditions, we designed an experiment focused on MI estimation between two high-dimensional Gaussian distributions with a large discrepancy. This setup is intended to specifically invoke the “density-chasm” problem, a known failure mode for DRE-based methods where the path between the two distributions traverses a region of vanishingly low density
(
51
)
. The goal is to evaluate whether our path optimization strategy can effectively navigate this chasm where fixed paths may fail.
We define the two distributions as
p_{0}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
and
p_{1}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\Sigma)
. The covariance matrix
\Sigma
is constructed to be block-diagonal, where each
2\times 2
block along the diagonal is given by
\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}
, with
\rho=0.5
. This structure creates strong pairwise correlations within each block while ensuring that the blocks themselves are independent. This results in a highly ill-conditioned and low-rank covariance matrix, a known challenge for score-based DRE methods
(
11
)
.
The experiments are conducted across several dimensions,
d\in\{40,80,120,160\}
. As the dimension increases, so does the true MI between the distributions, and thus the discrepancy, with corresponding MI values of approximately
\{10,20,30,40\}
nats. This allows us to systematically evaluate model robustness as the density-chasm problem becomes progressively more severe.

B.5
Experimental Settings and Results for Density Estimation

Structured and Multi-modal Distributions.

We use a suite of six 2D synthetic datasets to evaluate the model’s ability to learn complex and structured distributions. Five of these are standard benchmarks used in
8
, while the tree dataset follows the setup from
24
. These datasets are specifically chosen to probe different failure modes of density models.

•

Disconnected Topologies.

The circles and rings datasets test the model’s ability to capture distributions with multiple, disconnected components and assign zero density to the regions between them.

•

Intricate Structures.

The 2spirals and pinwheel datasets feature highly structured, non-linear manifolds that require the model to learn complex, curving paths.

•

Discontinuous and Branching Densities.

The checkerboard dataset presents a particularly difficult challenge with its discontinuous, grid-like density. The tree dataset is designed to assess a model’s capacity to generate sharp, branching topological structures, a known challenge for many generative models.

Together, these datasets form a comprehensive testbed for evaluating the flexibility and robustness of a density estimation method.

Density estimation on six structured and multi-modal datasets. MVP successfully learns a data-adaptive path tailored to the specific manifold of each dataset.

Real-world Tabular Distributions.

We evaluate on five tabular datasets widely used in density estimation
(
17
)
, covering domains from particle physics to image patches. These datasets pose challenging, non-Gaussian structures with unknown generative processes and complex correlations, making them suitable for testing model expressiveness. We follow the preprocessing and splits of
17
for fair comparison.
