Designing of a conserved subunit multiepitope vaccine candidate against Francisella tularensis Schu S4 using immunoinformatics
Authors: Sheikh Sunzid Ahmed, M Oliur Rahman, Miruna Banu, Momtaz Begum, Mohammad Z Ahmed, Mohammad Ajmal Ali, Joongku Lee, Kaiser Hamid, Ali S Alqahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrancisella tularensis Schu S4 is the most virulent strain responsible for tularemia, a highly infectious zoonotic disease. Despite its potential as a pathogen of concern, no effective vaccine has been developed for this pathogen. In this study, two multi-epitope subunit vaccine candidates were designed targeting the virulence factors DacD and DsbA to reduce disease severity caused by F. tularensis Schu S4. Using a comprehensive immunoinformatics approach, the most efficient B- and T-cell epitopes were identified from conserved sequences and linked with adjuvants and linkers to generate two vaccine constructs. These constructs were modeled, refined, and validated through molecular docking against Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs) and MHC alleles, followed by 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations. Their immunogenic potential and expression efficiency were further evaluated through immune simulations and computational cloning into the pET30a( +) vector. Both the constructed vaccine candidates, TulaVac1 and TulaVac2, showed high antigenicity, immunogenicity, stability, and solubility. The epitope conservancy was nearly 100%, with global population coverage rates of 73.19% and 99.74% for MHC class I and II epitopes, respectively. Docking analyses revealed six CTL epitopes with binding energies of ≤ -9.0 kcal/mol, while vaccine-TLR and MHC docking suggested superior performance of TulaVac1. MM/GBSA analysis yielded favorable binding energies ranging from − 87.22 to − 161.30 kcal/mol, MD simulations confirmed the stable interaction for 200 ns, and immune simulations showed robust immune responses with satisfactory codon adaptation. These findings position TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 as promising candidates for effective prophylaxis against tularemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58834-y.Keywords: Francisella tularensis Schu S4, Vaccine, Tularemia, Epitopes, ImmunoinformaticsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Francisella tularensis is the causative agent of tularemia, a sporadic and potentially fatal zoonotic disease commonly referred to as “rabbit fever” in humans. The name “tularemia” refers to the disease’s geographic origin, which was first discovered in Tulare County, California, in 19111. This bacterium is considered an exceptionally virulent pathogen, as exposure to as few as 10 colony-forming units may cause fatal infection in humans2. Owing to its high virulence and ease of aerosol dissemination, F. tularensis was prioritized in offensive biological weapons programs by both the United States and the Soviet Union during the Cold War3,4. Concerns regarding its potential use as a biological warfare agent have re-emerged, highlighting the urgent need for effective vaccines and countermeasures5. F. tularensis Schu S4 is considered the most virulent strain and is widely used as a reference strain6. Various transmission routes, including direct skin contact with infected animals, ingestion of contaminated food or water, bites from infected arthropods, and inhalation of infectious aerosols, can lead to severe and sometimes fatal illness7. Depending on the mode of transmission, tularemia can present with a variety of clinical symptoms, most commonly fever, fatigue, and acute systemic symptoms, which can rapidly worsen if left untreated. Due to its high virulence, aerosolization capability, and history of weaponization, F. tularensis remains a major focus of research for vaccine development and public health countermeasures8.

Tularemia is a globally distributed zoonotic disease with sporadic outbreaks across North America, Europe, and parts of Asia. In the United States alone, 2,462 cases were reported between 2011 and 2022, with an average annual incidence of 0.064 cases per 100,000 population (https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/). Across Europe, over 1,100 confirmed cases were reported in 2016, with endemic circulation in countries such as Sweden, Finland, and Spain (https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/publications-data/tularaemia-annual-epidemiological-report-2016). In the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region, seroprevalence studies indicate that approximately 6.2% of individuals have been exposed to F. tularensis, highlighting its underreported burden. Although tularemia is generally associated with a low-case fatality rate of less than 2%, mortality can increase up to 24% depending on the infecting strain and clinical manifestation, and may reach as high as 60% in untreated cases9. These findings underscore the global health significance of tularemia and the need for effective preventive strategies.

Tularemia can manifest in various ways depending on the pathway of infection. The most common form of tularemia is ulcerogenlandular, characterized by enlarged lymph nodes and a skin ulcer, typically caused by exposure to infected animals, ticks, or deer flies. The glandular form, which lacks an ulcer, resembles ulceroglandular tularemia. Oculoglandular tularemia occurs when the bacteria enter through the eye, causing eye irritation and swollen lymph nodes near the ear, often due to handling infected animals8. Oropharyngeal tularemia can develop after ingestion of contaminated food or water and is typically characterized by oral ulcers, cervical lymphadenopathy, and throat pain. Pneumonic tularemia represents the most severe clinical form of the disease and may develop from untreated cases or following inhalation of contaminated particles, presenting with respiratory symptoms including cough and chest pain. Unlike other forms of tularemia, which typically exhibit localized indications, typhoidal tularemia manifests with widespread symptoms3. Most naturally acquired cases are treatable with success if identified early. This pathogen is classified as a Class A biothreat agent by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)10.

Epidemic outbreaks of tularemia in various regions underscore the urgent need for ongoing research into effective vaccines and treatments. In Finland, Russia, and Sweden, outbreaks are frequently associated with wildlife and tick bites. In the western and south-central United States, the disease is often linked to contact with infected animals and ticks. Between 2007 and 2020, significant tularemia outbreaks in Spain affected both domestic and wild animals, including dogs, foxes, wolves, voles, mice, gray shrews, sheep, and river crayfish, as well as humans. Major human outbreaks occurred in 2007–2009 and 2014–2015, with continued cases reported in 2020. Lagomorphs showed intermittent positive cases, with renewed positivity in 2014, 2017, and 2019–2020, indicating their potential role in disease transmission. Additionally, F. tularensis was identified in new hosts, such as the shrew Crocidura russula and wild rabbits in Spain. Serological positivity was detected in sheep and dogs at various times, while river crayfish and environmental water samples tested negative. These findings emphasize the need for continued research to develop effective vaccines and treatments10–12.

Several antigenic determinants of F. tularensis have been investigated for vaccine development. Among these, outer membrane proteins (OMPs), lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and virulence-associated proteins such as IglC, Tul4, and FopA have demonstrated strong immunogenic potential in previous studies. These antigens play critical roles in host–pathogen interaction, intracellular survival, and immune evasion13. However, many of these targets show variability or limited protective efficacy when used individually. In contrast, the DacD and DsbA proteins selected in this study are highly conserved and are directly involved in cell wall integrity and the maturation of virulence factor, making them promising candidates for multi-epitope vaccine design. DacD, a D-alanyl-D-alanine carboxypeptidase, plays a pivotal role in peptidoglycan biosynthesis and cell wall maturation. This protein is essential for maintaining the structural integrity and division of F. tularensis Schu S4 cells, particularly under adverse conditions. Inhibiting DacD could cause cell wall defects and weaken the pathogen, thereby enhancing vaccine effectiveness. DsbA, a thiol-disulfide oxidoreductase, facilitates disulfide bond formation in extracytoplasmic proteins, a process essential for the proper folding and stability of virulence factors necessary for infection and immune evasion. Targeting DsbA can disrupt the assembly of these virulence determinants, thereby impairing the pathogen’s ability to establish infection. Both DacD and DsbA are highly conserved across virulent strains, including F. tularensis Schu S4, making their derived epitopes promising candidates for a subunit vaccine14,15. The Schu S4 strain was selected as the reference strain due to its well-characterized genome and its status as the most virulent type strain of F. tularensis. Notably, Schu S4 is particularly infectious, with as few as 10 CFU capable of causing fatal disease in humans, emphasizing its importance in clinical research and biodefense preparedness16. To ensure broader applicability, the present study focused on highly conserved virulence-associated proteins (DacD and DsbA), which are preserved across multiple virulent strains. This approach may enhance the ability of the proposed vaccine candidates to induce broad protective immunity against diverse circulating strains rather than limiting protection to one specific strain.

The immunoinformatic approach offers a significant advantage in designing a multi-epitope subunit vaccine against F. tularensis Schu S4 strain. Advances in bioinformatics and the availability of proteomic and genomic data have enabled the precise design of subunit vaccines, despite the fact that conventional vaccine development approaches are often costly and labor-intensive17. Immunoinformatics enables the careful selection of antigenic portions of the pathogen’s proteins, focusing on epitopes that elicit a strong immune response while avoiding toxic or overly immunogenic regions. This approach results in a vaccine that is not only safer but also more targeted and effective. In addition, the ability to quickly and accurately predict epitopes using computational tools accelerates the vaccine design process, providing a faster and more cost-effective alternative to conventional methods and ensuring robust protection against the extremely virulent F. tularensis Schu S418. Recent immunoinformatics-based studies have applied similar strategies for vaccine design against F. tularensis, although with certain limitations. For instance, Moradkasani et al.19 developed a vaccine with multiple epitopes based on OMPs and proposed new drug targets; however, their study primarily relied on surface-exposed and hypothetical proteins, which may limit functional reliability and translational applicability. Similarly, Khan et al.20 employed a structural proteomics-guided approach using the proteome of F. novicida, a less virulent strain, which may not fully reflect the complex pathogenic features of the highly virulent Schu S4 strain. In another study, Suleman et al.21 designed a vaccine targeting functional and structural proteins such as FopA, OmpA, PilA, Tul4, IglC, and MglA, demonstrating promising immunogenicity and stability; however, their analysis was limited to a narrower set of antigenic targets and primarily focused on a single Toll-like receptor pathway, potentially restricting the breadth of immune activation. Immunoinformatics approaches have also been effectively employed to design prospective vaccines against a range of pathogens, including Nipah virus, Helicobacter pylori, Jamestown Canyon virus, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis22–25. Recent work has explored the complete proteome of the less virulent F. novicida strain for the design of a multi-epitope vaccine against tularemia20. However, research specifically targeting the highly virulent F. tularensis Schu S4 strain remains limited, particularly studies focusing on conserved virulence-associated proteins such as DacD and DsbA.

Therefore, we designed the present study to develop and evaluate two multi-epitope subunit vaccine candidates against F. tularensis Schu S4 using a comprehensive immunoinformatics approach. Specifically, the study focused on the conserved and functionally important virulence-associated proteins DacD and DsbA as potential antigenic sources for epitope-based vaccine design. To achieve this objective, B- and T-cell epitopes were selected based on antigenicity, immunogenicity, safety, and conservancy, followed by global population coverage analysis. The shortlisted epitopes were subsequently integrated into vaccine constructs and further evaluated through structural modeling, molecular docking with immune receptors and MHC alleles, MM/GBSA binding energy calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, immune simulation, and in silico cloning. By integrating these complementary computational approaches, this study proposes robust and potentially broad-spectrum vaccine candidates and provides a more comprehensive framework for rational vaccine design against tularemia.

Materials and methods

Sequence acquisition and conserved sequence assessment: The NCBI database was used to extract sequence data for DacD and DsbA (Tables S1 and S2). The selection of DacD and DsbA proteins was based on their established roles in virulence, involvement in essential bacterial processes, and high sequence conservation across virulent F. tularensis strains. Notably, DacD is essential for virulence and cell wall integrity, while DsbA is involved in the proper folding and maturation of virulence-associated proteins14,15, making them suitable targets for epitope-based vaccine design. The sequences were then aligned via the MSA module in MEGA1126, with the MUSCLE algorithm27 with default parameter settings. Two conserved sequences were obtained for DacD and DsbA, and subsequently evaluated for various properties. After sequence acquisition, all analyses were performed following the standard pipeline28. The physicochemical properties were assessed employing the ExPaSy ProtParam tool29, antigenicity using VaxiJen 2.030, allergenicity using AllerTop 2.031, transmembrane helices using TMHMM 2.032, human homology using BLASTp of NCBI33, solubility using Protein-Sol34, subcellular localization using PSLpred35, virulence factor identification using the VICMpred server36, and signal peptide identification using SignalP 4.1 server37. All tools were run using default parameters unless otherwise specified. For the human homology assessment, the BLASTp search was restricted to Homo sapiens (taxid: 9606) with an e-threshold of 0.05 and all other settings were kept as default.

Selection of T-cell epitopes: The NetCTL 1.2 server was used to identify cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL) epitopes, with a combined score as the selection criterion before making final predictions38. The prediction was performed using the A1 supertype, with a threshold value of 0.75 for epitope identification, and default weights of 0.15 and 0.05 for C-terminal cleavage and TAP transport efficiency, respectively. Epitopes were prioritized according to their overall combined scores. For predicting helper T-lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes, the IEDB MHC-II binding prediction server was employed, using the full HLA reference set and NN-align 2.3 method for accurate predictions. Peptides of 15 amino acids were used for epitope prediction, and candidates were shortlisted according to their adjusted percentile rank. Antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity and human homology of both CTL and HTL epitopes were assessed through VaxiJen 2.0, AllerTop 2.0, ToxinPred and BLASTp tools30,31,33,39. The shortlisted epitopes were assessed for immunogenic potential using the IEDB immunogenicity server. The IEDB MHC-I binding prediction server was used to detect interacting MHC-I alleles for the selected CTL epitopes. For HTL epitopes, their cytokine-stimulating ability was assessed using IFN-epitope and IL4pred servers40,41.

Selection of B-cell epitopes: Linear B-lymphocyte (LBL) epitopes were predicted using the ABCpred server with a threshold value of 0.51, and a peptide length of 12 amino acids42. The predicted LBL epitopes were further analyzed using the iBCE-EL server to estimate epitope probability43. Additional screening was performed to assess antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and similarity to human proteins using VaxiJen 2.0, AllerTOP 2.0, ToxinPred, and BLASTp, respectively30,31,33,39.

Epitope conservancy and population coverage assessment: The conservation of the predicted CTL and HTL epitopes was evaluated using the IEDB epitope conservancy tool. Population coverage was subsequently estimated through the IEDB population coverage server, with default parameters for both analyses17.

MHC cluster analysis: MHC-I and MHC-II alleles associated with the selected epitopes were grouped using the MHCcluster 2.0 server44. For MHC-I clustering, the NetMHCpan-2.8 approach was used with prevalent and well-characterized HLA alleles, while MHC-II clustering was performed using representative HLA-DR alleles.

Formulation of vaccine constructs: Two vaccine constructs were designed by incorporating the shortlisted epitopes. Multiple constructs were developed to comparatively evaluate the effects of epitope arrangement and sequence organization on immunogenicity, structural stability, and receptor-binding efficiency, as recommended in previous immunoinformatics-based vaccine design studies45,46. In both constructs, 50S ribosomal protein L7/L12 (NCBI accession: P9WHE3) was used as an adjuvant and fused to the N-terminal region through an EAAAK linker. The selected LBL, CTL, and HTL epitopes were linked using KK, AAY, and GPGPG linkers, respectively.

Post-formulation evaluation of the vaccine candidates: The ExPASy ProtParam server was used to evaluate the physicochemical characteristics of both vaccine constructs29. To predict antigenicity, allergenicity, solubility, transmembrane helices, and signal peptides, the VaxiJen 2.0, AllerTop 2.0, Protein-Sol, TMHMM 2.0, and SignalP 4.1 servers were used respectively30–32,34,37. To minimize potential autoimmune risks, the constructs were screened for sequence similarity against the human proteome using NCBI BLASTp33.

Secondary structure assessment: Secondary structural elements of both vaccine constructs were predicted using the PSIPRED 4.0 server under default settings47. The server predicted the proportion of key secondary structural features, including random coils, alpha-helices, and beta-strands.

3D structure modeling, refinement, and validation: 3D models were generated using the AlphaFold 2 pipeline on the Google Colab platform48. The predicted models were refined using the GalaxyRefine server49. Following refinement, the models were assessed by Ramachandran plot study using the SAVES server, while additional structural quality verification was performed with ProSA-Web50.

Identification of discontinuous B-lymphocytic epitopes: Conformational B-cell epitopes within the designed vaccine candidates were predicted using the ElliPro server with default parameters. The predictions are based on an adapted version of Thornton’s technique, which considers the protein index (PI), clustering of neighboring residues, and overall protein structure51.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulation: Molecular docking was performed for both CTL epitopes and vaccine constructs. During epitope docking, different HLA alleles were used as the receptors. For the vaccine constructs, TLR3 and TLR4 were used as additional receptors alongside HLA alleles. TLR3 and TLR4 were chosen because of their well-established roles in innate immune recognition and activation of antiviral and antibacterial immune responses. TLR3 detects double-stranded RNA and promotes type I interferon responses, while TLR4 senses pathogen-associated molecular patterns and triggers pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. These receptors are widely employed in immunoinformatics-based vaccine studies to evaluate vaccine–host immune receptor interactions and assess whether the vaccine constructs can stimulate both innate and adaptive immune responses52,53. All receptors were fetched from the PDB database. Prior to epitope docking, the 3D models were constructed and refined using Pep-Fold 3 and GalaxyRefine servers49,54. Following refinement, epitope docking was performed using PyRx 0.8 tool55. The designed candidates were docked with TLR receptors and HLA alleles using three different web servers: ClusPro 2.0, HawkDock, and HDOCK56–58. Visualization and molecular interaction assessments were conducted using DS Visualizer, and the interaction analysis of the vaccine-complexes was explored through the PDBsum tool59. The free binding energy for the candidates complexed with TLR3 and TLR4 was calculated employing the HawkDock server with default parameter settings57.

Normal mode analysis (NMA) and all-atom simulation were performed to evaluate the structural compactness and stability of the designed candidates. The constructs complexed with TLR3 and TLR4 were subjected to molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. NMA was conducted using the iMODS server60. All-atom simulation was processed via Schrödinger 2020–1 package, spanning a timescale of 200 ns45. The simulation systems were placed in orthorhombic periodic boxes and hydrated using the SPC water model. Energy minimization of the fully solvated complexes was then performed with the OPLS2005 force field under the standard Desmond parameters. MD simulations were carried out in the NPT ensemble, maintaining the system at 300 K and 1 atm pressure. During the simulation, trajectory frames were saved every 200 ps, while energy information was collected at 1.2 ps intervals.

Immune simulation: Immunological responses were simulated using the C-ImmSim server, with the default random seed, simulation volume set to 50, and 1000 simulation steps61. Three vaccine doses were modeled at time steps 1, 84, and 168. The simulation was carried out using an agent-based framework that represents immune cell interactions.

Adaptation of codons and computational cloning: Codon usage was optimized through the Codon Optimization Tool available on the VectorBuilder server, using E. coli K-12 substrain MG1655 as the expression host. The resulting optimized sequences were then collected and inserted computationally into the E. coli pET30a( +) vector using SnapGene 7.2.117. The pET30a( +) was chosen based on its well-established use in high-level recombinant protein expression in E. coli, facilitating efficient transcription, translation, and purification of heterologous proteins. This vector system is widely employed in vaccine development studies to evaluate the expression potential of designed constructs, thereby supporting downstream experimental validation46,62.

Overall, the methodology followed a stepwise immunoinformatics pipeline. Initially, conserved virulence-associated proteins (DacD and DsbA) were prioritized and studied for their physicochemical and immunological features. Subsequently, B-cell and T-cell epitopes were determined and screened based on antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and host homology. The shortlisted epitopes were further evaluated for conservancy and population coverage, followed by clustering analysis of interacting MHC alleles. The prioritized epitopes were subsequently integrated with suitable adjuvants and linkers to design multi-epitope vaccine constructs. The constructs were subsequently subjected to structural modeling, refinement, and validation.

Molecular docking with immune receptors and MHC alleles, along with binding energy calculations and MD simulations, was carried out to evaluate stability and interaction. Finally, immune simulation along with codon optimization and in silico cloning, was carried out to examine both immune response potential and expression suitability. Results

Sequence acquisition and conserved sequences assessment: A total of 30 D-alanyl-D-alanine carboxypeptidase (DacD) sequences and eight thiol-disulfide oxidoreductase (DsbA) sequences were retrieved from NCBI. Multiple sequence alignment detected two conserved sequences for DacD and DsbA proteins, where the conserved sequence of the DacD proteins was longer than that of the DsbA proteins (Table 1). Theoretical isoelectric point (pI) analysis indicated that the DacD conserved sequence exhibits a basic nature (8.98), whereas the DsbA conserved sequence demonstrates an acidic nature (4.95). With higher extinction coefficients, the conserved sequence of DacD proteins demonstrated a greater number of aromatic residues or disulfide bonds compared to the DsbA sequence. Both the DacD and DsbA sequences revealed an instability index lower than 40, and were suggested to be stable. The aliphatic index suggested that the DacD sequence has greater thermostability than the DsbA sequence. The hydrophilic nature of both sequences was supported by their negative grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) values. The antigenicity, allergenicity, transmembrane topology and host homology studies revealed satisfactory results. The solubility index showed that the DacD conserved sequence is insoluble, while the DsbA sequence is soluble. Both sequences were predicted to be periplasmic and were classified as pathogenic, with positive virulence factor predictions. In addition, both the conserved sequences showed presence of signal peptides (Table 1).

Selection of T-cell epitopes: Twelve CTL and 10 HTL epitopes were selected for further vaccine development from a comprehensive list. Six CTL epitopes were identified from the DacD conserved sequence, and an additional six from the DsbA conserved sequence (Table 2). All shortlisted CTL epitopes were predicted to be antigenic, non-allergenic, and non-toxic. None of them showed host-homology with human proteome, and all exhibited positive results in immunogenicity studies. Additionally, the CTL epitopes demonstrated strong binding properties to MHC-I alleles, with IC50 values lower than 500 nM (Table S3).

Epitope conservancy, population coverage, and MHC cluster analysis: The epitope conservancy analysis yielded favorable results for both CTL and HTL epitopes. CTL epitopes conservancy ranged from 88.89% to 100%, with all but two epitopes showing 100% conservancy (Table 2). For HTL epitopes, conservancy ranged from 73.33% to 100%, with all but three epitopes demonstrating 100% conservancy (Table 3). Population coverage analysis revealed 73.19% coverage for MHC class I alleles, 99.74% coverage for MHC class II alleles, and 99.93% coverage for the combined class I and II alleles (Fig. 2A–C). The cluster analysis provided insights into their interaction profiles, with 18 MHC-I alleles in the first cluster (Fig. 2D) and seven MHC-II alleles in the second cluster (Fig. 2E).

Formulation of the vaccine candidates: Two vaccine constructs were formulated by incorporating linkers and adjuvants with the shortlisted CTL, HTL and LBL epitopes (Fig. 3). The constructs were named TulaVac1 and TulaVac2, each consisting of 388 amino acids (Table S5). In both constructs, the epitopes were derived from the conserved DacD and DsbA sequences.

Post-formulation evaluation of the vaccine candidates: Despite having same number of amino acids, TulaVac2 was slightly heavier than TulaVac1 (Table 5). Both constructs were acidic in nature, based on their theoretical isoelectric points. The instability index and aliphatic index further supported their vaccine-candidacy. The GRAVY value was positive for TulaVac1 and negative for TulaVac2, indicating potential hydrophobic and hydrophilic characteristics, respectively. Both candidates were found to be soluble, non-allergenic, and antigenic (Fig. S1). Additionally, no transmembrane helices or signal peptides were identified in either construct (Fig. S2).

Discussion

While various strategies have been employed in immunoinformatics-based vaccine design, two common approaches include genome-wide antigen screening and selection based on known antigens. Genome-driven studies, such as those by Basmenj et al.63 and Koupaei et al.64, begin with the entire bacterial genome to identify potential antigenic candidates through multiple computational filtering steps. This approach is advantageous in discovering novel antigens and providing a comprehensive overview of potential vaccine targets. However, it is computationally intensive and may lead to the inclusion of hypothetical or poorly characterized proteins, which can limit biological reliability and translational applicability. In contrast, the present study adopts a targeted strategy by focusing on well-characterized and conserved virulence-associated proteins, DacD and DsbA, which play critical roles in cell wall integrity and protein folding in F. tularensis Schu S4. This approach ensures functional relevance, reduces uncertainty associated with uncharacterized proteins, and enables more precise epitope selection45. Nevertheless, it may overlook novel antigenic candidates that could be identified through genome-wide screening. Therefore, our method complements genome-based approaches by prioritizing biologically validated targets while integrating comprehensive immunoinformatics analyses, thereby providing a more focused and reliable framework for vaccine design against highly virulent pathogens.

Recent studies have also employed integrative immunoinformatics and reverse vaccinology approaches for multi-epitope vaccine design. For instance, Roy et al.65 utilized a pan-genome-based strategy to identify conserved antigenic targets and designed multiple vaccine constructs, ultimately selecting the most stable candidate based on structural and immunological parameters. Similarly, Biswas et al.66 applied a combined pan-genome and reverse vaccinology pipeline to design a multi-epitope vaccine against human cytomegalovirus, demonstrating strong receptor binding affinity and minimal structural fluctuations during molecular simulations. In comparison, the present study adopts a targeted approach by focusing on well-characterized virulence-associated proteins (DacD and DsbA), ensuring functional relevance while maintaining comparable stability, immunogenicity, and receptor-binding efficiency of the vaccine constructs.

In the present investigation, CTL and linear B-cell epitopes were predicted using NetCTL 1.2 and ABCpred servers, respectively, rather than relying solely on Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) tools. NetCTL 1.2 integrates multiple predictive components, including MHC class I binding, proteasomal cleavage, and TAP transport efficiency, thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of cytotoxic T-cell epitope candidates. Similarly, ABCpred is based on an artificial neural network for predicting linear B-cell epitopes and has been widely used for sequence-based epitope identification. Although IEDB provides robust and widely validated prediction algorithms for both B- and T-cell epitopes, it comprises multiple independent modules rather than a single integrated predictor. In this study, NetCTL and ABCpred were selected to obtain complementary predictions using specialized, method-specific approaches. Additional validation steps (e.g., antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and human homology screening) were applied to ensure reliability and reduce false positives. This combined strategy is consistent with commonly adopted practices in immunoinformatics-based vaccine design studies63,67.

In the present study, epitope conservancy was assessed via the IEDB epitope conservancy analysis server, which is widely used in immunoinformatics-based vaccine design studies. In addition to IEDB, previous studies have utilized alternative approaches for conservancy analysis. For instance, Basmenj et al.68 applied a Java-based algorithm to assess epitope conservancy across viral variants. Several recent studies, including Islam et al.55 and Bhattacharjee et al.69, have also used the same conservancy tool for similar analyses. The use of the IEDB platform in the present work is consistent with these studies and provides a standardized and reproducible framework for evaluating epitope conservation across multiple sequences, thereby facilitating comparison with previously reported findings. Moreover, the IEDB tool offers a user-friendly interface, accessibility without the need for custom programming, and integration with other immunoinformatics resources, making it suitable for efficient and large-scale epitope conservancy analysis.

Population coverage analysis is a critical parameter for evaluating the global applicability and translational potential of epitope-based vaccines. In the present study, the combined population coverage for MHC class I and II epitopes reached 99.93%, indicating near-universal coverage across diverse human populations. This value is notably higher than those reported in several recent studies, where global population coverage values of approximately 90.42% (Coxsackievirus A10), 97.41% (HIV subtype C), and 97.98% (HIV infection) were observed for different multi-epitope vaccine constructs70–72. The comparatively higher coverage observed in the present study suggests that the shortlisted epitopes interact with a broader range of HLA alleles, thereby enhancing the possibility of effective immune responses across genetically diverse populations. Such extensive coverage is particularly important for pathogens with widespread geographical distribution and heterogeneous host immune backgrounds.

The predicted epitopes were assembled with linkers and an adjuvant to generate the TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 constructs (Fig. 3). In this study, 50S ribosomal protein L7/L12 was selected as a built-in adjuvant due to its strong immunostimulatory properties and established application in multi-epitope vaccine design. Bacterial protein-based adjuvants, such as L7/L12, function as carrier molecules that enhance antigen presentation and activate innate immune responses through pattern recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors, thereby promoting adaptive immunity. This approach is supported by previous studies, in which L7/L12 was successfully used to improve immunogenicity and stability in multi-epitope vaccine constructs against Vibrio harveyi and Trypanosoma cruzi39,55. The EAAAK linker was employed to connect the adjuvant to the epitope regions due to its rigid α-helical conformation, which ensures structural separation and minimizes functional interference between domains. This rigidity supports correct folding and helps maintain the functional integrity of both the adjuvant and epitopes, making it preferable to flexible linkers for separating domains73,74. The epitopes were linked using KK, AAY, and GPGPG sequences to enhance B-cell exposure, facilitate CTL processing, and promote HTL presentation, respectively, thereby optimizing the overall immune response. The post-formulation performance of TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 is consistent with findings from previous studies75,76.

Structural evaluation of TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 revealed a predominance of α-helical content, which is associated with enhanced structural stability and favorable protein–protein interactions, supporting their suitability for vaccine design (Fig. 4). The comparatively higher α-helix proportion in TulaVac2 suggests a potentially more stable conformation under physiological conditions, while the presence of β-strands and random coils contributes to necessary structural flexibility77. The validity of the predicted tertiary structures was further confirmed through Z-score and Ramachandran plot analyses, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 5)78,79. Additionally, the detection of multiple discontinuous B-cell epitopes in both TulaVac1 (14) and TulaVac2 (10) indicates strong potential for effective antibody recognition and humoral immune activation, in line with studies highlighting the importance of conformational epitopes in enhancing vaccine efficacy17,75.

Molecular docking results showed that both TulaVac constructs formed strong associations with the selected immune receptors, with consistently favorable energy scores observed across multiple docking platforms (ClusPro, HawkDock, and HDOCK). The use of a multi-server docking approach in our study enhances the robustness and reliability of the interaction analysis by minimizing method-specific bias and enabling cross-validation of binding affinity. In comparison, previous studies have primarily relied on single-server docking; for example, a recent multi-epitope HIV vaccine study reported strong interactions with TLR2 and TLR3 using ClusPro, with binding energies of − 1401.5 and − 1078.6 kcal/mol, respectively, supported by additional binding affinity calculations72. While both studies demonstrate favorable receptor engagement, the multi-platform docking strategy employed in the present investigation provides a more comprehensive evaluation of binding stability. The MM/GBSA study unveiled a higher mean free binding energy for the TulaVac2 construct (-136.29 kcal/mol) compared to TulaVac1 (-114.20 kcal/mol), targeting TLR3 and TLR4 receptors. Sarkar et al.17 conducted a free binding energy analysis on the HCOV vaccine constructs targeting TLR2 and TLR4 receptors, reporting a mean free binding energy of -109.44 kcal/ mol. This result is less favorable compared to the results of our study, underscoring the stronger binding affinity of the TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 constructs. These findings further bolster the potential of TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 as promising vaccine candidates.

Molecular dynamics simulation using NMA analysis revealed significant stability of the two vaccine-TLR complexes. Sarkar et al.17 reported an eigenvalue of 1.278806e-07 for the HCOV vaccine construct, which is lower than that of TulaVac1 (5.946739e-06) and TulaVac2 (3.661191e-07). The higher eigenvalues of the TulaVac constructs indicates greater stability and resistance to deformation compared to the HCOV construct (Fig. 9). Moreover, the 200 ns simulation trajectories further bolstered the candidacy of TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 by demonstrating minimal fluctuations. Khan et al.80 conducted a 10 ns all-atom simulation of vaccine-TLR complexes to assess the stability of a Helicobacter pylori vaccine candidate. Similarly, in a very recent study, a 50 ns simulation was performed to evaluate the structural integrity of a Leishmania major vaccine-TLR11 complex79. In contrast, our study extended the simulation time to 200 ns, providing a more comprehensive analysis of the compactness and stability of the TulaVac-TLR complexes over a substantially longer period (Fig. 10). The longer simulation period enabled evaluation of structural changes, complex stability, and dynamic behavior of the vaccine constructs, providing key information for judging their durability and potential effectiveness as vaccine candidates81. The immune simulation results closely mirrored those of recent studies78,82. After each vaccination, a strong humoral and cell-mediated immune response was observed, as evidenced by increased levels of helper T-cells, plasma B-cells, memory B-cells, and cytotoxic T-cells, and various antibodies. The low Simpson index (D) indicated a diverse and well-rounded immune response, ensuring that no single epitope dominated the immune response (Fig. 11J,T)62. The CAI is an important indicator of potential expression efficiency in a host system. The CAI value of 0.94 for both TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 suggests favorable codon optimization for expression in E. coli strain K-12. Computational cloning analysis further supported their potential for efficient expression, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 12A,B)17,82,84.

The in silico design of the TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 vaccine constructs is subject to several limitations. One key challenge is the lack of standardized benchmarks, which can affect the reliability of prediction methods and the accuracy of computational analyses due to insufficient datasets. In addition, some of the computational tools employed in this study are based on earlier algorithms which, despite their established use and continued validation, may not fully reflect recent advancements in predictive modeling and could influence the precision of epitope selection and immune response prediction. Furthermore, the proposed vaccine constructs require validation through wet-lab bioassays to assess their safety, efficacy, and immunogenicity against the pathogenic strain Schu S4. Such validation is essential to confirm their ability to elicit a protective immune response and establish long-term immunological memory. While computational predictions provide valuable insights, the real-world applicability and effectiveness of TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 ultimately depend on further experimental validation in both in vivo and in vitro systems.

Conclusion

Multi-epitope vaccines offer a promising approach for combating bacterial infections and cancer, supported by computational biology and immunoinformatics for rapid, systematic, and cost-effective design. In this study, we developed two multi-epitope vaccine constructs, TulaVac1 and TulaVac2, against Francisella tularensis Schu S4 by targeting the virulence-associated proteins DacD and DsbA. Both constructs incorporated predicted CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes and showed favorable results in physicochemical evaluation, population coverage analysis, and 3D structural validation. Docking analysis indicated strong interactions with selected HLA alleles and Toll-like receptors, while normal mode analysis, all-atom MD simulations, and immune simulations supported their structural stability and potential immunogenicity. Although these in silico findings are promising, in vitro and in vivo studies are required to confirm their safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy. Overall, this study provides a rational immunoinformatics-based framework for multi-epitope vaccine design and highlights TulaVac1 and TulaVac2 as potential candidates for future tularemia vaccine development.
