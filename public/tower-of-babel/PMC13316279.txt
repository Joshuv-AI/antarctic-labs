Pathogenesis and genome characterization of translucent post-larvae disease-causing Vibrio parahaemolyticus in Litopenaeus vannamei
Authors: Ramya Kumar, Athiya Azzahidah, Yik How Teoh, Shin-Jen Lin, Arun K Dhar, Shih-Shun Lin, Hao-Ching Wang, Chu-Fang Lo, Han-Ching Wang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Over the past decade, global shrimp production has expanded markedly, with Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) remaining the predominant species cultured worldwide. In 2023, Southeast Asian countries were among the leading producers, contributing ∼840,000 t (GOAL, 2022). However, disease outbreaks, both emerging and re-emerging, continue to adversely affect shrimp aquaculture. Between 2009 and 2018, white spot disease (WSD) and acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND) were estimated to have caused annual economic losses approaching US$4 billion (Shinn et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2022a). Consequently, research has increasingly focused on improving production sustainability, elucidating shrimp immune responses and deciphering host–pathogen–environment interactions.

Among recently emerging diseases affecting Pacific white shrimp, Translucent Post-larvae Disease (TPD), also known as glass post-larvae disease (GPD), was first reported in China in 2020 (Zou et al., 2020). TPD primarily affects post-larval stages (P6-P12) and can result in mortality rates of up to 90% within 3 d Clinically, infected shrimp have pale or colorless hepatopancreas and digestive tracts, producing a characteristic translucent body appearance (Zou et al., 2020). The causative agent was identified as V. parahaemolyticus strain Vp JS20200428004–2 isolated from moribund shrimp (Liu et al., 2023a). Although TPD shares certain pathological features with AHPND, TPD-associated strains lack the pirA and pirB binary toxin genes that define AHPND-causing Vibrio spp. (Zou et al., 2020).

Histopathological analyses of TPD-infected shrimp revealed necrosis and sloughing of hepatopancreatic and intestinal epithelial cells (Liu et al., 2023a) and hemocytic enteritis in the midgut (Dinh-Hung et al., 2025). Whole-genome sequencing of strain Vp JS20200428004–2 identified a ∼187-kbp virulence plasmid encoding the TPD-associated genes vhvp1 and vhvp2 (Zou et al., 2020). Three primer sets targeting vhvp1 and the 5′ and 3′ regions of vhvp2 (vhvp2–1 and vhvp2–2) were subsequently developed for molecular detection. Notably, TPD-associated plasmids have also been detected in V. natriegens and V. campbellii, and experimental infection with these strains resulted in high mortality in shrimp (Jia et al., 2024), suggesting potential horizontal dissemination of TPD virulence determinants. Despite these advances, pathogenic mechanisms underlying TPD, host immune responses to infection and effective mitigation strategies all remain incompletely understood.

In the present study, post-larval L. vannamei exhibiting TPD-like clinical signs were obtained from a commercial source. A pathogenic V. parahaemolyticus (VP) strain, designated P40, was isolated from cephalothorax homogenates and screened for TPD-associated virulence genes (vhvp1, vhvp2–1, and vhvp2–2) by colony PCR. Immersion challenge with strain P40 reproduced typical TPD clinical signs and high mortality, confirming its pathogenicity and leading to its designation as a TPD-causing VP strain. A single-colony isolate from strain P40, designated P40.49, was subsequently used for experimental infection assays to evaluate bacterial virulence gene expression at both genomic DNA and transcript levels. Histopathological analyses were done to characterize tissue damage, and host immune responses were examined in hemocytes, stomach, and intestine. In addition, we assessed the protective potential of dietary supplementation with perilla powder (PP), derived from Perilla frutescens (Lamiaceae), a plant widely cultivated in East Asia (Hou et al., 2022). Shrimp were fed a 1% PP-supplemented diet for 14 d prior to TPD challenge to evaluate its effects on disease progression and TPD virulence gene expression. Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing of strain P40.49 confirmed the presence of a TPD-associated virulence plasmid encoding vhvp1 and vhvp2. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated clustering of strain P40.49 with previously reported TPD-causing strains, including AG1 and Vp JS20200428004–2. Comparative genomic analysis using BRIG identified additional unique genes located on TPD virulence plasmids that may serve as potential diagnostic markers beyond vhvp1 and vhvp2.

Material and methods

Isolation and PCR screening of TPD-causing bacteria strain

Shrimp (L. vannamei) displaying clinical signs of TPD were obtained from local farms in Taiwan and subjected to bacterial isolation, as described below. The shrimp body surface was sterilized with 75% ethanol and the cephalothorax region including stomach and hepatopancreas was aseptically excised. Collected tissues were homogenized in 100 μL of TSB (Tryptic Soy Broth) supplemented with 2% NaCl (TSB + 2% NaCl); thereafter, 50 μL of homogenate was spread onto thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose (TCBS) and tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates, followed by incubation at 30 °C overnight. Single colonies were screened by PCR using TPD-specific primers targeting vhvp1, vhvp2–1 and vhvp2–2 (Table 1). Colonies positive for all three targets were cultured overnight at 30 °C in TSB + 2% NaCl. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (5000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) and genomic DNA was extracted using a DTAB/CTAB DNA extraction kit (GeneReach Biotechnology Corp.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted gDNA served as the template for PCR amplification. PCR was performed using Taq DNA polymerase (Bioman). Amplification of vhvp1, vhvp2–1 and vhvp2–2 was done under the following conditions: 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 50 s; and finally, 72 °C for 7 min. For amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene, cycling conditions were 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min 40 s; followed by 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were resolved on 2% agarose gels, with the 16S rRNA gene serving as an internal control. TPD-negative Vibrio strains (N10, N37, and N15) and the TPD-positive Vibrio strain (P40) were selected for subsequent immersion challenge assays to evaluate TPD pathogenicity. For amplicon sequencing, the purified PCR products were submitted to Genomics BioSci & Tech Co., Ltd. (Taiwan) for Sanger sequencing.

Experimental animals and bacterial strains

Litopenaeus vannamei shrimp (∼1 g average body weight) were obtained from the Department of Aquaculture, National Pingtung University of Science and Technology (NPUST). Prior to immersion challenge experiments, shrimp were acclimated for 1 d in sterilized seawater at 20 ppt salinity and 27 °C. Shrimp without visible clinical signs were selected for the experiment. The shrimp source is routinely monitored through random diagnostic screening for seven major shrimp pathogens, including EMS/AHPND, EHP, WSSV, IHHNV, YHV, TSV, and MBV using the POCKIT™ Central detection system (GeneReach Biotechnology Corp.). Nevertheless, pre-challenge screening for background Vibrio spp. or V. parahaemolyticus was not performed for the shrimp used in this experiment. The seawater used in this study was obtained from a commercial source and adjusted to a salinity of 20 ppt before use. Each tank was filled with 30 L of prepared seawater and disinfected with bleach for 6–12 h. Residual chlorine was then neutralized with sodium thiosulfate before shrimp were introduced. Because the seawater was disinfected prior to use, bacterial testing of the seawater was not performed before the TPD challenge.

For the initial immersion challenge, bacterial inoculum was prepared from V. parahaemolyticus strains isolated from the cephalothorax region of TPD-like shrimp, as described in Section 2.1. These included TPD-negative strains (N10, N37, and N15) and a TPD-positive strain (P40). Following experimental validation, a single-colony isolate from strain P40 was designated P40.49 and used for subsequent infection assays. Similarly, single-colony isolates of TPD-negative strains were obtained and designated N37.13 and N15.31. Vibrio spp. strains used in this study included TPD-negative strains (N10, N37, N37.13, N15, and N15.31) and TPD-positive strains (P40 and P40.49). All strains were maintained on TCBS agar at 30 °C. Bacterial inoculum were prepared by culturing each strain in TSB + 2% NaCl at 30 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. The isolated bacterial strains P40.49, N10, N37.13, and N15.31 were subjected to whole genome sequencing as described in Section 2.8.

Immersion challenge with TPD-negative and TPD-causing Vibrio spp

Immersion challenge assays were conducted with modifications based on a previous AHPND protocol (Kumar et al., 2025). Briefly, 3 mL of each overnight bacterial culture was inoculated into 300 mL of TSB + 2% NaCl and incubated at 30 °C for approximately 1.5 h, after which cultures were adjusted to a bacterial density of OD600 = 0.1- 0.15. Individual bacterial cultures were mixed with 900 mL of seawater from the shrimp tanks to achieve an approximate final concentration of 106 CFU/mL. The challenge was performed in square plastic tubs measuring 24 cm × 24 cm × 9 cm, containing 900 mL of seawater (20 ppt). Shrimp (n = 35 per group) were immersed in the bacterial suspension for 15 min. For the control group, shrimp were immersed in 900 mL of seawater containing 100 mL of TSB + 2% NaCl. Following the immersion challenge, shrimp were transferred back to their respective tanks, and 300 mL of the corresponding immersion suspension was added to each tank to maintain a bacterial density of approximately 104 CFU/mL. Tissue samples were collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-infection (hpi), with three shrimp pooled per sample and four pooled samples collected per group. (3 shrimp/ pool and 4 samples/ group).

Detection of genome copy numbers of TPD-causing bacteria

Genomic DNA was extracted from the gills, hepatopancreas (Hep), intestine (Ins), muscle (Mus), stomach (Stm), pleopods (PL), and hemocytes (Hcy) of TPD-infected shrimp using the DTAB/CTAB DNA extraction kit (GeneReach Biotechnology Corp.), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The copy number of VHVP2 was normalized to host genome copy number. As the IQ REAL™ TPD Quantitative System (GeneReach Biotechnology Corp.) does not contain an internal control for a shrimp reference gene, host genome copies were quantified independently using the IQ REAL™ WSSV Quantitative System (GeneReach Biotechnology Corp.) for normalization purposes. qPCR assays were performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Statistical differences in bacterial genome copy numbers among tissues were analyzed using Student’s t-test. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from shrimp gills, hepatopancreas, intestine, muscle, stomach, pleopods, and hemocytes using REzol™ C&T reagent (Protech Technology, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nano-200 micro-spectrophotometer (Allsheng Instruments, Taiwan). A total of 2.5 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). The resulting cDNA was used for qPCR analysis of target genes listed in Table 1, performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) using KAPA SYBR® FAST Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems). Gene expression levels were normalized to the internal control genes (V. parahaemolyticus 16S rRNA or shrimp EF1α) and calculated using the 2-ΔCT method. Subsequently, the empirical rule was applied to identify and exclude statistical outliers from the dataset (Tseng et al., 2019). To minimize the impact of extreme individual variation, samples were pooled (3 shrimp per pool, 4 pooled samples per group). Given the natural biological variability among genetically heterogeneous shrimp, data are presented as mean ± 1 SD to reflect the normal distribution of the dataset. Statistical significance between groups was determined using Student’s t-test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

Shrimp histopathology

Samples for histopathological analysis were processed as described (Ng et al., 2018). Shrimp (n = 4 per time point) collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hpi were injected with 1 mL of Davidson’s fixative into the cephalothorax and abdominal segments. After injection, shrimp were immersed in 5–10 mL of Davidson’s fixative for 24 h, followed by transfer to fresh fixative and incubation at room temperature for an additional 24 h. Following fixation, shrimp were transferred to 5 mL of 75% ethanol for long-term storage at room temperature. Specimens were then longitudinally sectioned, placed into tissue cassettes and processed for paraffin embedding, microtome sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining before examining using a light microscope (Olympus BX53).

Perilla powder (PP) supplemented feeding trial

Fresh perilla plants, including stems and leaves, were dried at 85 °C until the residual moisture content reached approximately 8%. The dried material was subsequently pulverized into a fine perilla powder (PP) using a patented high-velocity collision plate technology (Huang, 2009; US Patent No US 7472,852 B2). This continuous pulverization system produces ultrafine particles with an average size of approximately 2.5 μm while minimizing heat generation, thereby preserving the physicochemical properties of the dry material. The resulting fine PP was used for coating commercial shrimp feed.

The feeding efficacy of PP-supplemented diets was subsequently evaluated in a shrimp feeding trial. Commercial shrimp feed was supplemented with 1% PP by soaking the appropriate amount of feed in a 1% PP solution. The control diet (without PP) was prepared in parallel using distilled water. Treated feeds were incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow PP absorption, then evenly spread onto sterile petri dishes and dried at 37 °C for 16 h. Pacific white shrimp (∼1–2 g average body weight) were fed the 1% PP-supplemented or control diet at 5% of body weight twice daily for 14 d. To maintain water quality, water exchange was performed once every 3 d. After the feeding period, shrimp were subjected to immersion challenge with the TPD-causing VP strain P40.49, as described in Section 2.3. Shrimp stomach and intestine tissues were collected at 48 hpi for TPD detection.

Whole genome sequencing - de novo assembly and gene prediction

Genomic DNA from four Vibrio spp. strains isolated from TPD-infected shrimp, including the TPD-positive strain P40.49 and three TPD-negative strains (N10, N15.31, and N37.13), was extracted and subjected to Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing. Raw ONT reads were adapter-trimmed using Porechop v0.2.3 (Wick et al., 2017) and quality-filtered with Japsa v1.9–01a (https://github.com/mdcao/japsa/). Thereafter, de novo genome assemblies were generated using Flye v2.5 (Kolmogorov et al., 2019), followed by long-read polishing with Racon v1.4.9 (Vaser et al., 2017) and neural network–based polishing using Medaka v0.10 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka). Residual base-level errors were corrected using Illumina short reads with NextPolish v1.1.0 (Hu et al., 2020). Circularization and origin normalization were performed using Circlator v1.5.5 (Hunt et al., 2015). Assembly quality was assessed using QUAST v4.6.3 (Gurevich et al., 2013), and gene prediction and prokaryotic genome annotation were conducted with Prokka v1.12 (Seemann, 2014). All sequencing, assembly, and annotation procedures were performed by Genomics BioSci & Tech Co., Ltd., Taiwan. The genome sequences of strains N10, N37.13, N15.31, and P40.49 generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI under BioProject ID PRJNA1434679.

Comparative analysis of virulence-associated plasmids was performed using representative plasmid sequences from V. parahaemolyticus strains VpJs4–2, P40.49, and AG1. The AG1 and VpJs4–2 strain plasmid sequences were retrieved from NCBI and annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 to generate GenBank files, whereas the P40.49 plasmid annotation was provided by Genomics BioSci & Tech Co., Ltd. (Taiwan). Pairwise and multiple gene cluster comparisons among plasmids were performed using Clinker v0.0.27 (Gilchrist and Chooi, 2020). Clinker carries out global alignments of translated protein sequences from all coding sequences (CDSs) within each plasmid using the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix, with a gap opening penalty of –10 and a gap extension penalty of –0.5. Homologous gene matches were defined using a minimum amino acid sequence identity threshold of 90%. Linear comparative maps were then generated using Clinker’s integrated visualization module, which links homologous genes between plasmids with ribbons and colors genes according to their cluster assignments.

Phylogenetic analysis of the TPD-causing VP strain P40.49

To resolve phylogenetic relationships of V. parahaemolyticus and related Vibrio spp., whole-genome–based phylogenomic analysis was performed using the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) (Meier-Kolthoff and Göker, 2019). A total of 38 genomes were analyzed, including four strains from this study (TPD-positive: P40.49; TPD-negative: N37.13, N15.31, and N10) and 34 publicly available Vibrio spp. genomes retrieved from the NCBI database.

Pairwise intergenomic distances were calculated using the Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) approach with the recommended distance formula d5 and the Greedy-with-Trimming algorithm (Meier-Kolthoff et al., 2013). Digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values and confidence intervals were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) version 4.0 with default settings (Meier-Kolthoff et al., 2013; 2022). Species clustering was performed in TYGS using a 70% dDDH threshold around each type strain, whereby genomes sharing ≥70% dDDH were assigned to the corresponding species cluster. The intergenomic distance matrix was used to infer a phylogenomic tree using the balanced minimum evolution method implemented in FastME v2.1.6.1, with subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) for topology optimization (Lefort et al., 2015). Branch support was estimated using 100 GBDP pseudo-bootstrap replicates, and support values ≥60% were reported. The final phylogram was midpoint-rooted, with branch lengths scaled according to GBDP distance units.

Whole-genome comparative analysis using BRIG for identification of unique genes

Whole-genome comparisons were performed using BRIG (BLAST Ring Image Generator) (Alikhan et al., 2011) to evaluate genomic similarity between TPD-positive and TPD-negative strains and to identify coding sequences (CDSs) unique to TPD-positive strains. The complete genome of the TPD-positive strain Vp JS20200428004–2 was used as the reference, whereas genomes of strain P40.49 and 12 TPD-negative strains (N15.31, N37.13, N10, 5HP, 3HP, S02, BB22OP, CDC_K4557, FDA_R31, M1–1, RIMD2210633, and UCM-V493) were used as queries. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using BLASTn, and sequence identity values were visualized in circular BRIG plots. Regions with <70% sequence identity were displayed as gaps. To identify CDSs unique to TPD-positive strains, predicted protein sequences of strain P40.49 were aligned against the Vp JS20200428004–2 reference genome using tblastn. A custom Python script was applied to filter results, retaining CDSs present in both TPD-positive strains but absent in all TPD-negative strains. The final set of unique CDSs was annotated and visualized in the outermost rings of the BRIG plots. Selected CDSs were subsequently used for primer design to enable molecular detection of TPD-causing bacteria.

Primer design and validation of TPD-specific molecular markers

Based on comparative genomic analysis, five unique CDSs associated with putative virulence-related pathways were selected for primer design. Candidate genes included (i) TcToxin A-5139a (vhvp1), (ii) YenB-5140b (vhvp2), (iii) Mutator-TNS, (iv) DG-PDE-5147, and (v) ParA-5298. Primer sets were designed to evaluate the presence of these genes in infected shrimp samples and bacterial isolates (Table 1). The designed primers were applied to screen genomic DNA extracted from experimentally TPD-infected shrimp stomach and intestine tissues collected at 24 hpi and TPD-causing V. parahaemolyticus strain (P40.49), and TPD-negative strains (N10, N15.31, and N37.13). In addition, AHPND-causing strains (3HP and 5HP), non-AHPND strains (S02 and M1–1) were included for specificity assessment. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene (Bac16S) and shrimp EF1α were used as internal control genes for bacterial and shrimp-derived samples, respectively. For amplicon sequencing, the purified PCR products were submitted to Genomics BioSci & Tech Co., Ltd. (Taiwan) for Sanger sequencing.

Results

Isolation of TPD-causing bacteria from TPD-like shrimp and validation of pathogenicity

Shrimp obtained from a commercial source had pale hepatopancreas and translucent body coloration, consistent with TPD-like clinical signs (Fig. 1A). The cephalothorax tissues of the shrimp were used for bacterial isolation on TCBS agar plates. Single colonies were screened by PCR using TPD-specific primers targeting vhvp1, vhvp2–1, and vhvp2–2 (Fig. S1). Strains yielding positive amplification were designated as TPD-positive strain P40 (Fig. 1B), whereas colonies lacking amplification were designated as TPD-negative strains (N10, N37, and N15). Experimental immersion challenge was performed using the isolated TPD-positive (P40) and TPD-negative strains. At 24 hpi, shrimp challenged with strain P40 had pale hepatopancreas and translucent body appearance compared to TSB-treated controls (Fig. 1C). Survival analysis demonstrated significantly reduced survival in shrimp challenged with strain P40 (∼22% at 96 hpi), whereas shrimp challenged with TPD-negative strains (N10, N37, N15) or TSB control had markedly higher survival rates (Fig. 1D). Stomach and hepatopancreas tissues collected at 24 hpi from shrimp infected with strain P40 were subjected to PCR analysis. TPD-associated genes vhvp1, vhvp2–1, and vhvp2–2 were detected in stomach samples, whereas only vhvp1 and vhvp2–2 were detected in hepatopancreas samples (Fig. 1E). Following confirmation of pathogenicity, a single-colony isolate of strain P40 was selected and designated P40.49 for subsequent infection experiments. Corresponding single-colony isolates of TPD-negative strains were designated N37.13 and N15.31. All isolates were verified by PCR using TPD-specific primers (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, PCR analysis demonstrated that TPD-causing strains did not harbor the pirA and pirB toxin genes (Fig. 1F). Amplicons of vhvp1, vhvp2–1, vhvp2–2 genes were subjected to Sanger sequencing to confirm the identity of the amplified target sequences.

Tissue distribution of TPD-causing strain P40.49 following immersion challenge

To determine temporal and tissue distributions of the TPD-causing strain P40.49, shrimp were subjected to immersion challenge and seven tissues (gill, hepatopancreas, intestine, muscle, stomach, pleopod, and hemocytes) were collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hpi. PCR detection was performed using primer sets targeting vhvp1, vhvp2–1, and vhvp2–2. At 6 hpi, vhvp1 was strongly detected in gill, pleopod and hemocyte samples, whereas detection in other tissues was weak or inconsistent (Fig. 2A). By 12 and 24 hpi, vhvp1 was consistently detected across most examined tissues. At 48 hpi, signal intensity was markedly reduced compared to earlier time points. Detection of vhvp2–1 was limited at 6 and 24–48 hpi, with clearer amplification observed at 12 hpi in several tissues (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the detection pattern of vhvp2–2 largely paralleled that of vhvp1, with broader tissue distribution at 12–24 hpi and reduced signal intensity at 48 hpi (Fig. 2C). Genomic DNA from strain P40.49 served as the positive control. Considering Vibrio spp. is an enteric pathogen, hepatopancreas, intestine, and stomach would be candidate tissue samples for TPD detection particularly during the early phase of infection.

Temporal and tissue-specific distribution of TPD-causing strain P40.49 determined by qPCR

To further validate the PCR findings, genomic DNA from seven tissues collected at various time points post-infection was analyzed by qPCR targeting the vhvp2 gene to determine bacterial genome copy numbers. At 6 hpi, the stomach had the highest vhvp2 copy numbers, followed by pleopod and gill (Fig. 3A). By 12 hpi, gill had the highest bacterial load, with elevated copy numbers also detected in intestine and pleopod (Fig. 3B). At 24 hpi, intestine had markedly higher vhvp2 copy numbers compared to all other tissues, followed by stomach (Fig. 3C). At 48 hpi, intestine remained the predominant tissue, with hepatopancreas and muscle having the next highest copy numbers (Fig. 3D). mRNA analysis showed that vhvp-1 (Fig. S2), vhvp2–1 (Fig. S3), and vhvp2–2 (Fig. S4) were upregulated in shrimp challenged with the TPD-positive strain P40.49 compared to TPD-negative strains or the TSB control. These data revealed a dynamic shift in bacterial distribution over the course of infection, with early enrichment in stomach and gill, followed by progressive accumulation in intestine at later stages. The consistent detection of vhvp2 in stomach, hepatopancreas, and intestine across multiple time points supported their suitability as diagnostic target tissues for TPD detection.

Histopathological analysis of hepatopancreas and intestine in TPD-infected shrimp

Histological examination revealed normal morphology in the hepatopancreas of healthy shrimp. In contrast, hepatopancreas tissues from shrimp challenged with strain P40.49 had epithelial cell sloughing and hemocytic infiltration at early time points (6 and 12 hpi). At later stages (24 and 48 hpi), severe epithelial necrosis, extensive hemocytic infiltration, and bacterial cells were observed (Fig. 4A). Although hepatopancreatic necrosis resembled pathological features reported in AHPND, distinct alterations were evident in the intestine. At early stages (6 and 12 hpi), intestinal tissues had epithelial sloughing and hemocytic infiltration. By 24 and 48 hpi, pronounced epithelial dislodgement into the intestinal lumen was observed, accompanied by extensive tissue disruption (Fig. 4B). Therefore, isolated TPD-causing V. parahaemolyticus strain P40.49 induced characteristic pathological lesions in both hepatopancreas and intestine consistent with TPD-associated tissue damage.

TPD-infected tissues exhibit epithelial sloughing (red arrows), hemocytic infiltration (black arrows), and the presence of bacterial cells within hepatopancreatic tubules (black asterisks). (B) Representative intestinal sections from healthy and TPD-infected shrimp. Immune gene expression in shrimp challenged with TPD-causing bacteria P40.49

To assess host immune responses following TPD infection, key components of the prophenoloxidase (ProPO) system and genes involved in related immune pathways were analyzed. Genes examined included lipopolysaccharide- and β−1,3-glucan-binding protein (LGBP), prophenoloxidase-activating enzyme 1 (PPAE1), proPO, serpin7, toll-like, the antimicrobial peptide penaeidin3a (Pen3a), and antioxidant-related genes MnSOD2 and ROS-mod. In hemocytes of TPD-infected shrimp, PPAE1, toll-like, MnSOD2, and ROS-mod were significantly upregulated at 24 hpi, whereas Pen3a was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5A). In stomach tissue, expression of LGBP, serpin7, MnSOD2, and ROS-mod was significantly reduced, whereas Pen3a showed increased expression (Fig. 5B). In intestine tissue, several immune-related genes, including LGBP, toll-like, Pen3a, MnSOD2, and ROS-mod, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these results indicated tissue-specific modulation of immune and antioxidant-related genes in response to TPD infection, particularly in hemocytes and intestine.

Effect of PP-supplemented feed on TPD progression

Shrimp were fed a 1% PP-supplemented diet for 14 d prior to challenge with strain P40.49. Compared to shrimp fed the control commercial diet (No PP), PP-treated shrimp collected at 48 hpi had attenuated TPD-associated clinical signs (Fig. 6A). Genomic DNA extracted from stomach and intestine tissues collected at 48 hpi was subjected to PCR analysis targeting vhvp1, vhvp2–1, and vhvp2–2. In stomach tissues, shrimp from the No PP group had clear amplification of all three TPD-associated genes, whereas the 1% PP-treated group displayed reduced detection intensity (Fig. 6B). In intestinal tissues, no apparent difference in gene detection patterns was observed between the No PP and PP-treated groups (Fig. 6B). Based on the current data, the protective effect of PP against TPD in shrimp remains inconclusive. Although PP-treated shrimp showed some potential improvement, the available evidence is insufficient to clearly demonstrate that PP provides effective protection against TPD. In addition, the present experiment evaluated disease outcomes only within 24 h after infection; therefore, longer-term challenge studies are required to further determine whether PP can reduce TPD severity and improve shrimp survival over an extended infection period.

Whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of TPD-causing strain P40.49

Whole-genome sequencing of strain P40.49 revealed two circular chromosomes (chromosome I: ∼3.50 Mb; chromosome II: ∼1.92 Mb) and three plasmids, including a ∼69-kb TPD-associated virulence plasmid (CFPCKFLB_3) harboring vhvp1 and vhvp2 (Fig. 7A). Comparative genomic analysis demonstrated high overall sequence similarity between strain P40.49 and the reference TPD-causing strain Vp JS20200428004–2. The 69-kbp virulence plasmid (CFPCKFLB_3) was successfully assembled as a circular molecule based on Nanopore long-read sequencing. Comparative synteny analysis with the 187-kbp plasmid of strain Vp JS20200428004–2 and the 69-kbp plasmid of strain AG1, shown in Fig. S5 demonstrated that CFPCKFLB_3 retains the conserved virulence-associated region but lacks several non-virulence accessory regions found in the larger reference plasmid. Genome-based phylogenetic reconstruction using the TYGS platform placed the four strains sequenced in this study into distinct clades within the genus Vibrio spp. (Fig. 7B). Strain P40.49 clustered robustly within the V. parahaemolyticus lineage, grouping with TPD-causing strains AG1 (Outside China) and Vp JS20200428004–2 (China) with maximal bootstrap support. Consistently, digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH, formula d₄) values between P40.49 and AG1 reached 100.0%, and between P40.49 and Vp JS20200428004–2 reached 99.3%, both exceeding the 70% species delineation threshold. In contrast, the three TPD-negative strains were positioned outside the V. parahaemolyticus cluster. Strains N15.31 and N37.13 grouped with V. campbellii BoB-53, sharing dDDH values of 77.7% relative to the type strain and 100.0% between each other, supporting their classification within V. campbellii. Strain N10 clustered within the V. owensii lineage and shared a dDDH value of 74.5% with V. owensii XSBZ03, consistent with species-level assignment. Collectively, these results confirmed that TPD-causing strain P40.49 belongs to V. parahaemolyticus, whereas the TPD-negative strains represent distinct but closely related Vibrio spp., including V. campbellii and V. owensii.

Comparative genomic identification and validation of unique sequences in TPD-causing strain P40.49 and related TPD-positive isolates

BRIG-based whole-genome comparison revealed high genomic similarity among TPD-positive strains (Vp JS20200428004–2, AG1, and P40.49), whereas TPD-negative strains had multiple large gaps and low-identity regions, indicating substantial structural divergence (Fig. 8A). Coding sequences (CDSs) conserved among TPD-positive strains but absent in all TPD-negative strains were considered candidate TPD-specific genes. From these unique CDSs, 14 putative virulence-associated genes were selected for further evaluation. PCR screening was performed using genomic DNA from experimentally infected shrimp stomach and intestine tissues collected 24 hpi and TPD-causing strain (P40.49), TPD-negative strains (N10, N15.31, and N37.13), AHPND-causing strains (3HP and 5HP), non-AHPND strains (S02 and M1–1). Based on primer specificity and amplification patterns, five genes were shortlisted: ParA-5298 (chromosome partitioning), TcToxin A-5139a (vhvp1) and YenB-5140b (vhvp2) (bacterial secretion system), Mutator-TNS (DNA transposition), and DG-PDE-5147 (c-di-GMP signaling pathway associated with biofilm regulation). PCR analysis demonstrated specific amplification in TPD-causing strains and TPD-infected shrimp samples for all genes except Mutator-TNS, which had inconsistent specificity (Fig. 8B). Therefore, TcToxin A-5139a, YenB-5140b, DG-PDE-5147, and ParA-5298 represent potential molecular markers for TPD detection, complementing the established markers vhvp1 and vhvp2. Amplicons of TcToxin A-5139a, YenB-5140b, Mutator-TNS, DG-PDE-5147, and ParA-5298 were subjected to Sanger sequencing to confirm the identity of the amplified target sequences.

Discussion

Shrimp aquaculture continues to have recurrent disease outbreaks, particularly bacterial epidemics caused by Vibrio spp. Vibriosis remains a persistent threat in both shrimp farms and hatcheries, leading to substantial economic losses worldwide (Sanches-Fernandes et al., 2022). Many Vibrio spp. function as opportunistic marine pathogens that can acquire virulence plasmids encoding potent toxins, as exemplified by AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus strains (Han et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015; Lai et al., 2015; Dong et al., 2017). Pathogenic Vibrio spp. associated with major shrimp diseases include V. parahaemolyticus, V. alginolyticus, V. harveyi, V. owensii, V. campbellii, V. vulnificus, and V. splendidus (Ina-Salwany et al., 2019; Amatul-Samahah et al., 2020; de Souza Valente and Wan, 2021; Flegel, 2012), highlighting the genomic plasticity and adaptive potential of this genus in aquaculture environments.

In 2019, TPD emerged as a severe hatchery disease in China, causing mortality rates exceeding 90% within 24–48 h in shrimp post-larvae (Zou et al., 2020). TPD primarily affects L. vannamei post-larvae (PL6-PL12) and is characterized by reduced feeding, pale hepatopancreas, and a translucent body appearance. Zou et al. (2020) subsequently isolated the causative bacterium and identified it as V. parahaemolyticus strain Vp JS20200428004–2, harboring a ∼187-kb virulence plasmid (Zou et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023a). Recent genomic and proteomic analyses further revealed that protein fractions exceeding 100 kDa were lethal to shrimp post-larvae and reproduced classical TPD histopathological lesions in the hepatopancreas and midgut (Liu et al., 2023a). Mass spectrometry identified Vibrio high virulent proteins vhvp1 and vhvp2 as key virulence determinants encoded by the plasmid-borne genes vhvp1 and vhvp2. Despite these advances, the pathogenic mechanisms underlying TPD infection and host tissue tropism remain incompletely understood.

In the present study, cephalothorax tissues from TPD-suspected shrimp obtained from a commercial source were used to isolate a TPD-causing V. parahaemolyticus strain, designated P40.49 (Fig. 1B). Genomic DNA analysis confirmed the presence of vhvp1 and vhvp2, whereas the AHPND-associated binary toxin genes pirA and pirB were absent (Fig. 1F), clearly distinguishing this strain from AHPND-causing isolates. Experimental immersion challenge with strain P40.49 resulted in ∼88% cumulative mortality at 96 hpi (Fig. 1D), accompanied by classical TPD clinical manifestations including pale hepatopancreas and empty digestive tract (Fig. 1C), consistent with previous reports (Zou et al., 2020).

Detection of vhvp1 and vhvp2 in stomach and hepatopancreas tissues of experimentally infected shrimp further confirmed active colonization and plasmid gene presence (Fig. 1E). To determine optimal diagnostic tissues, seven tissues were sampled at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hpi following experimental infection. Both endpoint PCR and quantitative genome copy analysis demonstrated consistently higher detection signals in the stomach, hepatopancreas, and intestine across multiple time points (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), confirming these tissues are reliable targets for early and sustained detection of TPD-associated bacteria in infected shrimp.

Histopathological examination of TPD-infected shrimp revealed progressive degeneration of the hepatopancreas characterized by epithelial sloughing, hemocytic infiltration, and bacterial colonization features consistent with classical TPD pathology (Fig. 4A) and in agreement with previous reports (Zou et al., 2020; Jia et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a; Yu et al., 2022b). The hepatopancreatic lesions observed in TPD have similarities with those described in AHPND, including acute necrosis and hemocytic infiltration (Tran et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015; Lai et al., 2015; Han et al., 2015). However, a distinguishing pathological feature of TPD is in the intestine. In the present study, intestinal tissues exhibited enterocyte sloughing and hemocytic infiltration at early stages, followed by pronounced epithelial dislodgement into the lumen at later stages (Fig. 4B). Previous investigations reported characteristic TPD-associated lesions in both the hepatopancreas and midgut (Zou et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023a). Hepatopancreatic alterations include epithelial sloughing, acute necrosis, tubular atrophy, reduction of B and R cells, and nuclear pyknosis (Zou et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023a; Yu et al., 2022b). Intestinal pathology has been described as epithelial sloughing, luminal narrowing, and bacterial accumulation within the lumen (Liu et al., 2023a; Yang et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2024). More recently, Srisala et al. (2025) reported midgut smooth muscle necrosis (MSNN), characterized by nuclear pyknosis and karyorrhexis of smooth muscle cells in TPD-infected shrimp. Similar pathology has also been observed in experimentally challenged TPD-infected shrimp (Mai et al., 2025).

Interestingly, hemocytic infiltration has not been consistently emphasized as a hallmark of TPD in all previously published papers. In contrast, our observations revealed marked hemocytic infiltration in both hepatopancreas and intestine at 24 hpi (Fig. 4), suggesting that host inflammatory responses may be more pronounced than previously recognized. Further investigations, particularly at earlier infection time points, are warranted to delineate the temporal progression and mechanistic basis of these pathological alterations.

These histopathological signs in the hepatopancreas and intestine might indicate an increased immune response that was evident in hemocytes, stomach and the intestine of the TPD-infected shrimp (Fig. 5). In shrimp, the ProPO and Toll signalling pathways have an important role in shrimp immunity. The ProPO cascade is initiated by pattern-recognition protein (PRP) such as LGBP that activates the PPAE1 that in turn activates ProPO that leads to melanization and transcriptional regulation of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) like Peneadin3a (Amparyup et al., 2013). Upregulation of LGBP gene expression is associated with Gram-negative bacteria, mainly V. parahaemolyticus that cause AHPND (Amparyup et al., 2009). In this study, ProPO and PPAE1 expression was present in hemocytes but not in stomach and intestine of the TPD-infected tissues (Fig. 5). In P. monodon ProPO cascade, downregulation of ProPO leads to downregulation of peneadin3a, LGBP and PPAE (Amparyup et al., 2009), with a similar trend in hemocytes of TPD-infected shrimp (Fig. 5A). Toll-like genes were significantly upregulated in TPD-infected shrimp hemocytes, stomach and intestine suggesting that the Toll-signalling pathway has a key role in shrimp immunity against TPD infection (Fig. 5). As TPD infection progressed superoxide dismutase (MnSOD2) and ROS modulator gene expression were significantly higher in hemocytes and intestine suggesting that TPD might induce an increased oxidative stress and trigger an immune response (Fig. 5). The anti-inflammatory genes MnSOD2 and ROS modulator are known critical components of shrimp innate immune response.

In shrimp aquaculture, conventional disease management strategies have largely relied on strict biosecurity measures and antibiotics. However, increasing antimicrobial resistance has prompted the exploration of alternative and sustainable intervention approaches (Liu et al., 2023b). For TPD control, recent studies have investigated natural antimicrobial formulations such as AquaAura, a blend of organic acids, olive extracts, and citrus components which were reported to reduce mortality in TPD-infected shrimp (Stef et al., 2025).

The current findings underscored the growing interest in plant-derived compounds as functional feed additives for disease mitigation. In the present study, we evaluated perilla powder (PP) as a dietary supplement. PP has previously demonstrated immunomodulatory potential against AHPND infection (unpublished data). Phytochemical analyses indicated that perilla contains diverse bioactive constituents, including terpenoids, peptides, tocopherols, coumarins, flavonoids, fatty acids, and phytosterols (Huang et al., 2023). Several of these compounds have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, particularly terpenoids and peptides, which modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways (Huang et al., 2023). In mammalian models, perilla-derived compounds were found to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and regulate superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Edward et al., 2022), as well as suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and tumor proliferation (Liu et al., 2023b). Additionally, certain perilla phytochemicals have antibacterial activity by disrupting bacterial cell envelope integrity and inhibiting essential enzymatic functions (Huang et al., 2023).

Following 14 d of dietary supplementation with 1% PP, shrimp were challenged with the TPD-causing strain. Notably, the PP-treated group had attenuated clinical manifestations of TPD and reduced detection of vhvp1 and vhvp2 in stomach tissue compared to the control group (Fig. 6). Therefore, we inferred that PP supplementation may interfere with early bacterial colonization or reduce plasmid-associated virulence gene presence in the stomach. Nevertheless, mechanisms underlying this protective effect remain to be clarified. Taken together, PP may show preliminary protective potential against TPD; however, the current evidence is insufficient to confirm a clear protective effect. Because disease progression was monitored only up to 24 h after infection, longer-term challenge studies with survival analysis, bacterial load quantification, and histopathology are needed. Future studies should also clarify whether PP acts through antibacterial activity, immune modulation, or changes in gut microbiome dynamics.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of strain P40.49 confirmed its classification within V. parahaemolyticus, with an approximately 5.6 Mb genome composed of two chromosomes and a ∼69 kb plasmid harboring the virulence genes vhvp1 and vhvp2 (Fig. 7A). These genomic features are consistent with previously reported TPD-causing strains (Liu et al., 2023a; Mai et al., 2025). Notably, although the TPD-associated plasmid in the reference strain Vp JS20200428004–2 was reported to be ∼187 kb (Zou et al., 2020), the plasmid size observed in strain P40.49 (∼69 kb) closely resembles that of the AG1 strain (Mai et al., 2025). Correspondingly, digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) analysis revealed 100% similarity between P40.49 and AG1, supporting their close genomic relationship. The successful circularization of CFPCKFLB_3 and its syntenic relationship with the plasmids of strains Vp JS20200428004–2 and AG1 support the proposed plasmid slimming process, in which the virulence-associated region is retained while several non-virulence accessory regions found in the larger reference plasmid are lost (Fig. S4). Genome-based phylogenetic analysis further demonstrated that the TPD-positive strain P40.49 clustered tightly with previously characterized TPD-causing strains AG1 and Vp JS20200428004–2 (Zou et al., 2020; Mai et al., 2025), whereas the TPD-negative isolates grouped with V. campbellii (N37.13, N15.31) and V. owensii (N10) (Fig. 7B).

Comparative genomic analysis using BRIG identified unique coding sequences (CDSs) specific to TPD-causing strains (P40.49, AG1, Vp JS20200428004–2) when compared against TPD-negative strains (N10, N37.13, N15.31), AHPND-causing strains (3HP, 5HP, M1–1), and non-pathogenic V. parahaemolyticus isolates. Among the candidate genes, five loci, TcToxinA (vhvp1), YenB (vhvp2), Mutator-TNS, DG-PDE, and ParA were selected for validation (Fig. 8). PCR screening demonstrated that all loci except Mutator-TNS were specifically detected in TPD-infected shrimp samples and TPD-positive bacterial isolates, indicating their potential utility as molecular markers for TPD diagnosis.

Functionally, TcToxinA (TcA; vhvp1) has been identified as a principal virulence determinant in TPD-causing strains (Liu et al., 2023a). TcA toxin is proposed to bind to host cell membrane receptors and induces epithelial degeneration, and its secretion is considered critical for TPD pathogenesis (Liu et al., 2023a; Dinh-Hung et al., 2025). Supporting this hypothesis, Yang et al. (2023) demonstrated that immersion challenge with partially purified VTc toxin reproduced TPD clinical signs, whereas deletion of Tc toxin–encoding genes abolished pathogenicity. LC-MS/MS analysis further confirmed that the >100 kDa protein bands corresponded to TcA, TcB, and TcC subunits of the Tc toxin complex, underscoring the central role of this tripartite toxin in disease progression (Yang et al., 2023).

Recent studies detected emergence of novel TPD-causing Vibrio strains, highlighting ongoing plasmid evolution and horizontal dissemination of virulence determinants (Wang et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) harboring Tc toxin genes have been identified in newly characterized pathogenic strains such as JD2305, suggesting dynamic reshaping of TPD-associated plasmids (Jin et al., 2026). Moreover, a recently described strain V. parahaemolyticus (JM20210818) had enhanced virulence, with transcriptome profiling indicating downregulation of GPX4 and induction of ferroptosis, thereby disrupting host epithelial barriers and exacerbating pathogenesis (Wang et al., 2026). Therefore, we inferred that TPD-associated plasmids undergo adaptive evolution, potentially increasing virulence and expanding host-pathogen interaction complexity in shrimp aquaculture systems.

In conclusion, in this study, we successfully isolated a pathogenic bacterium from post-larval shrimp exhibiting high mortality and confirmed (whole-genome sequencing). The bacterial isolate represents a TPD-causing V. parahaemolyticus strain, designated P40.49. Genomic characterization revealed a virulence plasmid encoding vhvp1 and vhvp2, and phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that strain P40.49 clusters tightly with previously reported TPD-causing V. parahaemolyticus strains, reinforcing its taxonomic identity and virulence potential. Experimental immersion challenge with strain P40.49 resulted in rapid and high mortality, accompanied by significant increases in bacterial genome copy numbers in the stomach, hepatopancreas, and intestine as early as 6 hpi. These findings indicated that early bacterial colonization and virulence gene presence preferentially occurred in digestive tissues, establishing them as primary targets for early TPD detection. Histopathological analyses further revealed progressive epithelial sloughing, necrosis, bacterial colonization, and hemocytic infiltration in both hepatopancreas and intestine, suggesting that toxin-mediated epithelial damage and host inflammatory responses jointly drive tissue degeneration. Comparative genomic analysis identified four additional unique genes located on the TPD-associated plasmid that were specifically detected in TPD-infected shrimp samples and TPD-causing strains. These loci may function as auxiliary virulence determinants or plasmid maintenance elements and represent promising molecular markers for TPD diagnosis beyond vhvp1 and vhvp2. Importantly, dietary supplementation with perilla powder (PP) was associated with reduced TPD-associated clinical manifestations and decreased detection of virulence genes in stomach tissue, suggesting that PP may attenuate bacterial colonization or modulate host responses during early infection. Although the precise mechanisms remain to be clarified, PP supplementation may partially suppress or mitigate TPD-induced tissue damage, potentially through antimicrobial, antioxidant, or immunomodulatory effects. Taken together, TPD pathogenesis appeared to involve: (i) rapid colonization of digestive tissues, (ii) plasmid-mediated toxin activity leading to epithelial destruction, (iii) subsequent inflammatory and oxidative host responses, and (iv) progressive tissue collapse culminating in high mortality. Dietary PP supplementation may prevent early colonization or elicit host-response to alleviate disease severity. A deeper mechanistic understanding of these processes will not only facilitate development of rapid and precise diagnostic tools but also inform targeted biosecurity and nutritional intervention strategies to mitigate the spread and impact of TPD in shrimp aquaculture.
