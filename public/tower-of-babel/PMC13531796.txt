Psychometric testing of the abdominal surgery impact scale in the Turkish population
Authors: Emel Cihan, Serap Sayar, Emine Acar Uysal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostsurgery recovery is a multifaceted and dynamic process. Recent reports recommend using condition-specific patient-reported outcome measures to evaluate surgical recovery. This study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the Abdominal Surgery Impact Scale (ASIS) in the Turkish population.MethodsThis methodological study included 181 patients who primarily underwent minor or intermediate abdominal procedures. The scale was applied twice: in the early postoperative period and one month postoperatively. Content validity, construct validity, item analysis, internal consistency, and responsiveness were evaluated.ResultsMost participants underwent hernia surgery (44.2%) or cholecystectomy (41.4%); only 7.7% had major surgery. Responsiveness analysis showed that mean total ASIS-TR scores increased significantly (p < 0.001) from the early postoperative period (77.71 ± 23.60) to one month postoperatively (116.08 ± 14.50). The ceiling effect of the one-month postoperative scale was 40.3%. Item–total correlations ranged from 0.33 to 0.75. Internal consistency was high (McDonald’s ω = 0.91, Cronbach’s α = 0.91), with domain ω values ranging from 0.51 for Visceral Function to 0.94 for Psychological Function. The fit indices at the two time points supported construct validity. Early fit index values were as follows: χ2/df = 2.08, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, GFI = 0.87.ConclusionThe ASIS-TR demonstrated acceptable to high reliability, validity, and responsiveness. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the predominance of minor surgical procedures, the presence of a ceiling effect, and the low reliability of the Visceral Function domain. Future research involving multicentre recruitment should be conducted with patient populations undergoing major gastrointestinal surgeries, and convergent validity and test–retest reliability should be evaluated.Keywords: Reliability and validity, Psychometrics, Postoperative recovery, Abdominal surgery, Cholecystectomy, Hernia, Turkish

Background

Recovery can be defined as regaining one’s physical or psychological health or returning to normal health after being ill or injured. It may also be understood as regaining strength, eliminating illness, and achieving improvements in health, vitality, and quality of life while ensuring the absence of recurring disorders with a return to daily routines [1]. Recovery after abdominal surgery is a subjective, multifaceted, long-term, complex, and dynamic process that encompasses various dimensions of health. It generally follows a standard trajectory marked by a sharp decline in health status and loss of function immediately after surgery, with a gradual return to the preoperative baseline and complete functional recovery over an uncertain period lasting weeks to months, or even longer [2]. A concept analysis study described abdominal surgery recovery as ‘the process of returning to physical health and fitness (without post-operative complications), alleviating unpleasant conditions, and becoming mentally healthy and emotionally stable, as seen by appropriate emotional expression, more engagement in social interactions, and independence and confidence about achieving daily routines and activities’ [1]. Although numerous studies have explored interventions aimed at enhancing postoperative recovery, such as minimally invasive surgical techniques or enhanced recovery protocols, these investigations often rely on conventional outcome measures rather than patient-reported recovery [3–5]. Two recent meta-analyses compared the surgical outcomes of minimally invasive surgery (MIS) and open surgery in studies of colorectal cancer patients involving [5] and not involving [3] older patients, including operative time, mortality, length of hospital stay (LOS), complication rates, readmission, and reoperation. These meta-analyses highlighted how studies of abdominal surgery often prioritise operative and postoperative endpoints such as complications, LOS, and perioperative outcomes. Moreover, significant discrepancies in reported outcomes across studies, which limit the scope of meta‑analyses, indicate that MIS or technical success alone does not adequately explain patient recovery [3, 5]. This underscores the need for recovery assessments based on patient-reported outcome measure (PROMs). The surgical outcome parameters considered in studies to date may not adequately reflect the full scope of the recovery process or capture the patient’s subjective experience. Standardised measurement tools are needed to assess recovery from the patient’s perspective [6]. In addition to traditional surgical outcome indicators, condition- or disease-specific quality-of-life and recovery measures are now gaining increasing importance in evaluating the effectiveness of surgical interventions. Consistent with the principles of patient-centred care, the use of PROMs to assess postoperative recovery is strongly recommended [7–9]. A recently published international consensus-based core outcome set recommended the use of a condition-specific PROM in the context of gastrointestinal recovery [10]. PROMs enable the recovery process to be monitored using data reported directly by patients, without interpretation or filtering by clinicians or third parties [11]. This approach not only enhances the accuracy of recovery assessment but also positions patients as active participants and primary stakeholders in their own recovery journeys, supporting the principles of patient-centred care [6, 7, 11]. The Abdominal Surgery Impact Scale (ASIS), developed by Urbach et al. in 2006 [12], is one such instrument designed to evaluate short-term quality of life in the context of postoperative surgical recovery following abdominal surgery. It encompasses six domains: Physical Limitations, Functional Impairment, Pain, Visceral Function, Sleep, and Psychological Function [12]. The ASIS domains comprehensively assess the different stages of recovery, which have important sub-features. In the early stage of recovery, the primary focus is the ability to regulate reflexes and bodily movement. During the intermediate stage of recovery, movement abilities, the capacity to carry out basic activities of daily living, the restoration of gastrointestinal functions, physical and emotional well-being, and decreased pain are all prioritised. The final stage of recovery is characterised by the successful completion of routine duties, the regaining of independence, and full participation in social interactions [1].

The psychometric validation of PROM scales across diverse populations ensures standardised, reliable, and reproducible measures of postoperative health status, and their translation and cultural validation further strengthen their roles as endpoints in assessing perioperative interventions [6]. The use of standardised recovery measurement tools also enables global comparisons of study results. Methodological studies of the ASIS have been carried out in German [13] and Chinese [14] populations. The present study aimed to adapt the ASIS to Turkish to assess recovery following gastrointestinal surgery in the Turkish population (TR) and to evaluate the scale’s psychometric properties, including validity and reliability.

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

This study utilised a methodological design and was conducted in the general surgery clinic of a university-affiliated training and research hospital between March and June 2023. The study population consisted of patients scheduled to undergo abdominal surgery; most of these cases entailed minor to intermediate surgery. Participants who met the following inclusion criteria were selected through convenience sampling: patients aged > 18 years who underwent either open or laparoscopic abdominal surgery and who provided informed consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included the need for reoperation, pregnancy, American Society of Anesthesiologists scores of IV or higher, active psychiatric illness, and the inability to speak or understand Turkish.

Sample size

In the literature, the recommended sample size for psychometric validation studies is five to ten times the number of items in the scale or at least 100 participants [15, 16].

Since the ASIS comprises 18 items, a total sample of 204 patients was recruited to satisfy the aforementioned recommendation.

Data collection

Data collection tools were administered to patients who agreed to participate in the study after obtaining ethics committee approval from the relevant institutional board and informed verbal and written consent from all patients.

Data were collected using a sociodemographic characteristics form and the ASIS. The ASIS was administered twice: once during the early postoperative period (most often on postoperative day 1 or 2), and again one month after surgery. For patients who underwent cancer surgery, the scale was first administered on postoperative day 4 or 5, closer to discharge, to allow for clinical stabilisation. While patients undergoing minor surgeries are generally discharged more quickly, patients undergoing major surgeries may be discharged after longer stays. Furthermore, in cases of major surgery, pain levels may still be high 1–2 days postoperatively and patients may not have started eating or become mobile yet. For these reasons, the initial administration of the ASIS was performed close to each patient’s time of discharge in this study to avoid biased results or responses. The initial postoperative data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted in patients’ hospital rooms and one-month follow-up data were obtained via telephone interviews. Patients who did not respond after at least three telephone calls within two weeks were excluded from follow-up.

Outcome measures

Sociodemographic characteristics form

This form included questions on sociodemographic variables such as age, gender, and education, as well as clinical characteristics including disease type, surgical procedure, operation type, anaesthesia type, and LOS. Clinical data were extracted from patients’ medical records.

Abdominal Surgery Impact Scale (ASIS)

The ASIS was developed in 2006 by Urbach et al. [12] to measure short-term quality of life in the specific context of postoperative surgical recovery and to evaluate the postoperative recovery processes of patients undergoing abdominal surgery. It is a 7-point Likert-type scale consisting of 18 items across six domains: Physical Limitations, Functional Impairment, Pain, Visceral Function, Sleep, and Psychological Function. In the original reliability analysis, the Cronbach’s α value of the scale was 0.91, while domain-specific α values ranged from 0.79 to 0.90. Factor analysis results indicated acceptable model fit indices, including root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.08, standardised root mean squared residual (SRMR) = 0.05, and Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) = 0.89. Total and domain scores are calculated by summing item responses, with total scores ranging from 18 to 126. Higher scores indicate better recovery and quality of life, whereas lower scores reflect poorer recovery. The scale has no established cut-off value [12].

Validity process

Language validity

Translation and back-translation methods were employed in adapting the scale. The scale was first translated from English into Turkish by two professional translators. The research team evaluated both of the forward translations for each item. They compared them to check for meaning and concept consistency, discussed the most appropriate translation for each item, and formed a pre-final Turkish scale. The pre-final scale was back-translated by a different professional translator who was blinded to the original scale. The research team then checked the back-translated scale for similarity with the original scale. Based on input from the research team, the ASIS-TR scale was finalised.

Face validity

A pilot study was conducted with 30 patients. These patients provided opinions regarding the clarity and comprehensibility of the scale items [17–19]. The average time to complete the scale was 2.52 min (range: 0.57–3.57 min). Based on patient feedback, necessary adjustments were made.

Content validity

An expert review was conducted using the Polit and Beck technique [20]. A group of 12 experts reviewed the revised ASIS-TR and rated each item’s clarity and relevance. This expert panel consisted of three general surgery specialists (surgeons), three surgical nurses specialising in general surgery, and six nurse academics in the field of surgical nursing. These experts rated each item’s relevance to the construct being measured and provided feedback regarding potential redundancies or omissions. The item-level content validity index (CVI) and the scale-level CVI were calculated in accordance with the framework of Polit and Beck [20], yielding a scale-level CVI value of 0.99. The scale was finalised by incorporating both expert recommendations and patient feedback, with wording modifications accordingly made for several items. Data from the pilot study were excluded from the main research sample.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 27.0 version. The normality of continuous variables was assessed both visually with histograms and statistically with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and skewness and kurtosis values. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Initially, a total of 204 patients completed the early postoperative questionnaire. However, 23 patients were lost to follow-up at one month after surgery because they had not responded to any telephone calls. A total of 181 patients (response rate: 88%) completed the one-month assessment. The data from these 181 patients who completed both the early postoperative questionnaire and the one-month questionnaire were included in the study’s complete-case analysis. The data from the 23 patients who completed the early postoperative questionnaire but did not complete the one-month questionnaire were excluded from the analysis. Upon comparing the baseline characteristics of one-month responders and non-responders, no statistically significant differences were found for any sociodemographic or clinical variables.

Reliability analyses

Reliability was evaluated using item–total correlation coefficients, lower–upper 27% group analysis, floor and ceiling effects, and internal consistency. Internal consistency was assessed by calculating McDonald’s omega (ω) and Cronbach’s alpha (α). Item discrimination was tested by comparing the upper 27% and lower 27% of the scores (i.e., individuals with the highest total scale scores versus individuals with the lowest total scale scores) using independent-samples t-tests.

Responsiveness analysis

Responsiveness was analysed by comparing early postoperative and one-month postoperative scores using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to determine changes in recovery over time. The rank-biserial correlation coefficient with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for effect size was calculated for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Test values of 0.10–0.30 are interpreted as indicating a small effect (low-level relationship), 0.30–0.50 a moderate effect (moderate-level relationship), and 0.50 and above a large effect (high-level relationship) [21, 22]. Utilising distribution‑based analysis, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the scale was determined.

Validity analyses

For construct validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using Analysis of Moment Structures (AMOS) software to verify the original structure of the scale. The model was tested separately at the early postoperative and one-month postoperative time points. The paired nature of the data was handled simply as two repeated applications. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is recommended for aims such as determining factor structures for newly developed scales. However, it is not mandatory in cross-cultural scale adaptation studies [18, 23]. In the present study, EFA was not performed because it was planned to preserve the domain structure of the original scale without changes. In previous international studies in which this scale was used, it was similarly planned that the domains would be maintained with items similar to those of the original scale. Therefore, CFA was performed to validate the original structure Fig. 1 and Fig. 2. The maximum likelihood method was chosen for estimation. The model fit was evaluated using fit indices. There are three questions in each domain in the model and no modifications were made. Factor loadings were calculated. Sampling adequacy before factor analysis was assessed with the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. To support convergent and divergent validity, the average variance explained (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) values for the CFA sample group were calculated. For the hypothesis testing, the existence of a negative correlation between a patient’s LOS and ASIS-TR score was hypothesised. The correlation between LOS and ASIS-TR score was subsequently assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. 12

Responsiveness outcomes

A statistically significant difference was observed between the early postoperative and one-month postoperative total ASIS-TR scores with an effect size rank-biserial correlation (rrb) of − 0.986 (95% CI: − 0.990 to − 0.980) (p < 0.001) (Table 3). All domain scores at one month postoperatively were significantly higher than those obtained during the early postoperative period (p < 0.001), with effect sizes ranging from − 0.701 to − 0.977. The MCID for the scale was determined to be 6.29 (95% CI: 0.25–12.33).

Validity outcomes

The CFA outcomes indicated acceptable fit indices. The early postoperative scale fit indices were obtained as follows: relative chi-square (CMIN/df) = 2.08, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.06, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.93, incremental fit index (IFI) = 0.93, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) = 0.91, and GFI = 0.86. Standardised factor loadings ranged from 0.53 to 0.97. The one-month postoperative scale fit indices were as follows: chi-square (CMIN/df) = 1.81, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.06, CFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, and GFI = 0.87. Standardised factor loadings ranged from 0.73 to 0.99. The KMO value was 0.86 and Bartlett’s test was significant (p < 0.001).

The correlation among factors ranged from 0.34 to 0.84. For the total scale and the scale’s domains, AVE ranged from 0.586 to 0.902 and CR values ranged from 0.846 to 0.965 (Table 4). Statistically significant weak negative correlations were found between LOS and both the early postoperative ASIS-TR total score (r = − 0.17, p = 0.022) (Table 4) and the one-month ASIS-TR total score (r = − 0.27, p < 0.001). No statistically significant difference was found between early postoperative ASIS-TR scores according to surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic) (p = 0.227) or type of surgery (p = 0.473).

Discussion

This study has confirmed that the Turkish version of the ASIS (ASIS-TR) exhibits acceptable-to-high reliability, validity, and responsiveness. The minimum item–total correlation coefficient threshold for item reliability is generally accepted as > 0.25 [24]. All item–total correlations of the ASIS-TR exceed this value, indicating robust reliability. High internal consistency coefficients, reflected by total McDonald’s ω and Cronbach’s α values, further suggest strong reliability. McDonald’s ω is based on factor analysis, whereas Cronbach’s α primarily relies on average inter-item correlations. McDonald’s ω is a more robust measure than α when the assumptions underlying α are disrupted. McDonald’s ω considers the strength of the relationship between items. Therefore, ω is generally considered a more appropriate and reliable indicator of internal consistency. Both α and ω values range from 0 to 1, with internal consistency typically regarded as acceptable when the estimate is 0.70 or higher [25]. In contrast, McDonald’s ω coefficients below 0.65 and Cronbach’s α values below 0.60 indicate low reliability [18, 24, 26]. The Chinese version of the ASIS-Revised had 14 items in total, its Cronbach’s α coefficient was found to be 0.71, and the α values of the different domains ranged between 0.96 and 0.98 [14]. In the validation study of the German version of the ASIS, the overall Cronbach’s α was reported as 0.85, with domain-specific values ranging from 0.45 to 0.88 [13]. The McDonald’s ω and Cronbach’s α values for the ASIS-TR total scale and most of its domains demonstrated high reliability; however, the reliability coefficient for the Visceral Function domain in the early postoperative period was low. The α coefficient is affected by the number of items on the test, the item measurement variance, and the total test measurement variance. The variance differs according to the sample characteristics. Thus, sample characteristics can directly affect measurement reliability and the α value may vary indirectly depending on whether the group with which a measurement is made is homogeneous or heterogeneous [23, 24]. These values may show low reliability due to sample characteristics and variability is to be expected. Each domain of the ASIS consists of three items. Item 10 (‘I am not able to move my bowels normally’) has the lowest item–total correlation and is located in the Visceral Function domain. Even if this item is deleted, the α value remains low, and ω cannot be estimated with item deletion because the number of items is less than four. Furthermore, the structure of the original scale was not to be altered. Item deletion was accordingly not performed. Additionally, items 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 15 did not significantly distinguish between the group with the lower 27% of scores and the group with the upper 27%, and item 10 overlapped with the Visceral Function domain, which possessed lower reliability. The Visceral Function domain assesses bowel function, thirst, and appetite in patients. The lower reliability of this domain may be related to clinical and sample characteristics. The study sample predominantly included patients who had undergone hernia repair and cholecystectomy surgery, procedures that are typically associated with minimal tissue damage and smaller incisions. Furthermore, the early postoperative assessment was conducted at variable time points, with the scale generally administered on postoperative day 1–2 for hernia repair and cholecystectomy surgeries versus day 4–5 for cancer-related surgeries. This variability in timing may have affected recovery scores. The patients who underwent minor surgery resumed liquids and regular diets earlier, experienced shorter times to flatus and bowel movement, and were discharged earlier postoperatively compared to patients undergoing more extensive gastrointestinal surgeries [27]. In contrast, the recovery of bowel function after colorectal cancer surgery can be considerably prolonged, often taking between 3 and 9 days postoperatively [28, 29]. Postoperative feeding time in patients who had gastrointestinal cancer surgery at our institution typically commenced on postoperative day 3 or after the passage of flatus. Consequently, thirst and appetite levels in these patients may have differed significantly from those of hernia patients. This may explain the lower reliability observed for the Visceral Function domain. For this reason, domain-level Visceral Function scores should be interpreted with caution in the early postoperative period, particularly as the present sample consisted predominantly of minor surgical procedures. A recent meta-analysis compared surgical outcomes (intraoperative blood loss/transfusion rate, operative time, disease-free/survival time, LOS, complication rates, etc.) in patients who underwent liver resection with MIS or open surgery. Although the studies were heterogeneous in design, MIS yielded superior results in terms of postoperative complications and LOS [4]. Abdominal surgery populations are also heterogeneous; the choice between MIS versus open surgical approaches materially affects postoperative recovery-related proxies such as LOS and complications. Psychometric validation of the ASIS-TR should eventually be performed with broader and more demanding abdominal surgery cohorts. Furthermore, when the one-month ω value was examined in this study, the slightly increased value (0.66) reflected a shift toward an acceptable-to-borderline level. This borderline improvement should be interpreted as partial mitigation. Future studies are recommended to evaluate the reliability and validity of the ASIS-TR in samples including patients undergoing gastrointestinal cancer surgery, bariatric surgery, and other abdominal procedures involving larger incisions. Longer follow-up durations are also needed.

There was a significant difference between the lower 27% and upper 27% groups in total scale scores, indicating that the items of the scale can distinguish patient groups with low scores. This finding supports the scale’s reliability.

The one-month ASIS-TR scores were significantly higher than the early postoperative scores with large effect sizes, indicating improved recovery and better quality of life over time and strong responsiveness. This finding is consistent with the literature, as postoperative recovery levels in abdominal surgery patients have been previously reported to exceed baseline levels [30, 31]. Clausen et al. examined the relationship between glycaemic profiles and recovery quality after emergency abdominal surgery, finding that the quality of recovery at one month postoperatively was higher than baseline values [30]. Similarly, Pelliccioni et al. investigated the impact of physiotherapy on long-term postoperative recovery using the ASIS and reported a 35% increase in recovery rates [31]. Our findings support the ability of the ASIS to detect changes in recovery status following abdominal surgery sensitively. The effect size values indicated an exceptionally strong and statistically significant negative association for ASIS‑TR scores. The narrow confidence interval reflects the high precision and robustness of this effect estimate. Furthermore, an increase of ≥ 6.29 points on the scale indicates a clinically meaningful and noticeable improvement in patients’ recovery, and this MCID value may help clinicians interpret postoperative changes more effectively. However, the wide 95% CI (0.25–12.33) suggests potential heterogeneity within the study population or variability in patient‑reported perceptions of recovery.

Floor and ceiling effects were examined to assess the scale’s general validity and reliability. It is recommended that floor and ceiling effects be below 15% [32]. In this study, the ceiling effect was found to exceed the value recommended in the literature. This may have reduced the validity and reliability of the results, making it difficult to distinguish between patients whose recovery levels range from poor to excellent. Further research involving longer follow-up periods, more granular time points, and more complex surgical cases is recommended.

CFA was performed separately at each of the two time points to verify the original structure of the scale. Bartlett’s test was used to determine whether the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis and whether it was statistically significant enough to proceed. Sampling adequacy for factor analysis was evaluated using the KMO test, for which values above 0.90 are considered excellent [18, 24]. In this study, the KMO value was ideal and Bartlett’s test was statistically significant. This indicated that the final analysed sample of 181 patients was adequate for CFA. Moreover, all standardised factor loadings were above the threshold of 0.50 [24]. A relative chi-square value below 5 is considered indicative of a good fit [18, 24]. RMSEA values below 0.05 indicate excellent fit, while values up to 0.08 suggest acceptable fit [33]. CFI and IFI values above 0.90 are deemed acceptable, with values over 0.95 considered excellent [18, 33, 34]. GFI values greater than 0.90 are considered good and values greater than 0.85 are considered acceptable. Finally, RMR and SRMR values closer to zero indicate a better fit, with SRMR values below 0.08 indicative of a good fit [33]. However, these thresholds provide general guidelines rather than strict rules, as they may vary depending on the model’s complexity, sample size, and specific research context [33]. When we evaluated the results of CFA for both the early postoperative period and the first postoperative month, the outcomes indicated good fit according to CMIN/df and SRMR, excellent fit according to CFI/IFI, and acceptable fit according to RMSEA. The GFI values were within the borderline-to-acceptable range, indicating that the model demonstrated good-to-acceptable overall fit.

The fact that the obtained AVE values were above the accepted threshold of 0.50 and that the CR values were greater than the threshold of 0.70 supports the validity of the scale. It was observed that the AVE values for the scale domains were greater than the maximum shared variance (MSV) value for that dimension (AVE > MSV) and that the CR values for the domains were greater than the AVE values (CR > AVE) [35]. These findings indicate that convergent/divergent validity was established. Thus, based on these obtained values, the scale is acceptable in terms of its structural validity and reliability.

In the hypothesis testing, the finding of a statistically significant but weak negative correlation between LOS and the total ASIS-TR score obtained in the early postoperative period supports the validity of the scale. However, LOS is a limited external criterion and the correlation is weak. Therefore, caution is advised while interpreting this result. Future studies are recommended to examine convergent validity using tools such as the Quality of Recovery (QoR) or Gastrointestinal Quality of Life Index (GIQLI) scales.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single centre using convenience sampling, which limited the generalisability, increased the risk of selection bias, and restricted the external validity. Future research should be conducted with multicentre recruitment and probability-based sampling. Furthermore, the early postoperative assessment of the present study was conducted at different time points; the scale was administered on postoperative day 1–2 for patients who underwent minor surgeries versus day 4–5 for cancer patients. This variability may have introduced measurement bias and affected recovery scores. Telephone-based follow-up at one month may have also been subject to interviewer-related response bias.

The sample predominantly consisted of patients undergoing minor procedures such as hernia surgery and cholecystectomy, with a relatively small proportion of major abdominal surgeries such as gastrointestinal cancer or bariatric surgery procedures. Therefore, the sample is not representative of patients undergoing ‘abdominal surgery’ in a broader sense. This imbalance limits the generalisability of the psychometric findings to the broader abdominal surgery population. Furthermore, sample characteristics affect construct validity and reliability results. Considering that patients undergoing major surgery generally have larger incisions and prolonged or more complex recovery trajectories compared to those undergoing minor surgery, the present results may not fully reflect the postoperative recovery experiences of oncological, colorectal, or bariatric surgery patient groups. This limitation affects the study’s reported reliability, generalisability, and construct validity. For example, the reliability coefficient for Visceral Function was low among the scale’s early postoperative results. Future research is recommended to specifically test the reliability and validity of the scale in populations undergoing major gastrointestinal surgery (colorectal, stomach, pancreatic cancer, bariatric surgery, etc.).

Furthermore, test–retest reliability (temporal stability) and convergent validity (with a standard measure) were not assessed. It is recommended that test–retest reliability assessments be performed to determine whether measured properties remain stable over time and whether the conditions of the first and second applications are the same or similar. Ideally, scales should be reapplied at intervals of one to four weeks [18, 32]. In the present study, it was assumed that the recovery status could change from poor to good within two weeks after surgery and test–retest validity was not considered while planning the research. Future studies should examine test–retest reliability in the short term (i.e., intervals of 1–4 weeks) and evaluate convergent validity using scales such as the QoR or the GIQLI. Finally, future research should consider anchor-based approaches to establish clinically meaningful change.

Conclusion

The reliability, validity, and responsiveness values of the ASIS-TR ranged from acceptable to high in this study. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously considering a procedure distribution dominated by minor surgical procedures (e.g., hernia surgery and cholecystectomy), a ceiling effect at one month, a lack of convergent validity, the low reliability of the Visceral Function domain, and limited evidence for generalisability to broader abdominal surgery. Patients’ early postoperative recovery levels were assessed as moderate to moderate-high while one-month postoperative recovery levels were significantly higher, indicating marked improvement over the course of the immediate postoperative period. The scale not only captures immediate postoperative quality of life and recovery; it may also serve as a valuable tool for appraising the effectiveness of nursing interventions. It may support postoperative monitoring and discharge assessment through identification of patients eligible for early discharge following elective or emergency abdominal surgery during surgical rounds, which would reduce unnecessary hospital stays. This scale should be used to evaluate the effectiveness of postoperative nursing care services. However, its utility among patients undergoing major surgery remains to be established.

Given the existence of cross-cultural differences in postoperative care and recovery, the scale’s inclusion in future cross-cultural adaptation, validation, and outcome measurement studies is recommended. Such efforts could increase the scale’s use on a global level and ensure that surgical patients’ recovery outcomes are assessed in a more consistent manner that takes cultural context into account.
