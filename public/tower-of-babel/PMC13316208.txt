Bionic black phosphorus nanosheets confer dual protection against nephrocalcinosis-induced kidney injury via ROS scavenging and NETosis inhibition
Authors: Yu He, Xiaoqi Yang, Yongke Bai, Xiaozhuo Ba, Tao Ye, Zichen Zhong, Zhiqiang Chen, Heng Li, Kun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcium oxalate (CaOx) nephrocalcinosis is a chronic kidney disease marked by CaOx crystal deposition, oxidative stress, neutrophil infiltration, and renal tubular epithelial cell injury. To date, no targeted therapies are clinically available for this condition. Antioxidant nanomaterials capable of scavenging excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) offer a promising approach for treating CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury. Neutrophil-mediated delivery systems have been leveraged to transport nanomaterials to inflamed kidneys for acute kidney injury therapy, and their targeting specificity can be further augmented through the functionalization of cell membranes with targeting peptides. Herein, we developed a biomimetic nanoplatform by loading DNase I onto black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) and coating them with a neutrophil membrane modified with a CD44-targeting peptide (PNM@D@BP), aiming to ameliorate renal CaOx crystal deposition and the consequent kidney injury. The engineered neutrophil membrane coating conferred the nanocarrier with the ability to home to sites of renal injury and inflammation. PNM@D@BP exhibited potent antioxidative activity, effectively eliminating CaOx crystal-induced ROS. In both in vivo and in vitro settings, PNM@D@BP was shown to suppress the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Moreover, RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analyses revealed that PNM@D@BP protects against CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury by modulating oxidative stress and neutrophil-driven inflammatory responses. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of PNM@D@BP for the treatment of CaOx kidney stones.

Introduction

Kidney stones affect approximately 10% of the global adult population [1], with calcium oxalate (CaOx) stones being the most prevalent type [2]. CaOx nephrocalcinosis, characterized by progressive CaOx crystal deposition in the renal parenchyma, is a chronic kidney disease that can lead to persistent renal tubular injury, inflammation, and ultimately irreversible decline in renal function [3,4]. Despite its high prevalence and significant long-term morbidity, the therapeutic options currently available for this condition remain limited, highlighting an urgent clinical need for effective and targeted interventions.

Current pharmacotherapy for the management and prevention of stones primarily includes thiazide diuretics, potassium citrate, allopurinol, and tiopronin [5]. However, the prolonged utilization of these medications may result in undesirable side effects, diminished patient compliance, and increased financial burden [6]. More importantly, none of these agents directly address the core pathological processes—namely, oxidative stress and inflammatory injury—that drive CaOx crystal-induced renal damage. Although certain plant extracts with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties have demonstrated potential antilithogenic activities in preclinical studies, their clinical application has yet to be realized because of their lack of targeting specificity and limited bioavailability [[7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, there is a pressing necessity to develop a new category of therapeutics that directly targets the fundamental pathological mechanisms of CaOx nephrocalcinosis with high antilithogenic efficacy, minimal adverse effects, and precise renal targeting.

Accumulating evidence has elucidated that oxidative stress and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation play central roles in the pathogenesis of CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury [10,11]. Specifically, CaOx crystals stimulate renal tubular epithelial cells to produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via NADPH oxidase and mitochondrial pathways [12]. This overproduction of ROS, coupled with a concomitant decrease in antioxidant capacity, results in severe oxidative stress, which in turn causes renal tubular epithelial cell injury and death [13]. Injured tubular cells not only lose their normal physiological function but also provide adhesion sites for CaOx crystal attachment and fixation, thereby fueling a vicious cycle that promotes the initiation and progression of CaOx nephrocalcinosis[[14], [15], [16], [17]]. In parallel, CaOx crystal stimulation triggers the recruitment of neutrophils into the renal interstitial inflammatory microenvironment [18]. Upon activation, these neutrophils release NETs—web-like DNA structures decorated with granular proteins such as myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE)—which further amplify local inflammation and exacerbate renal tissue damage [[19], [20], [21]]. Thus, the dual processes of oxidative stress and NETosis represent two mechanistically intertwined and therapeutically targetable pathways in CaOx nephrocalcinosis. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at simultaneously scavenging excessive ROS and inhibiting NETs formation are expected to break this pathological cycle and confer robust renoprotection.

In recent years, antioxidant nanomaterials have emerged as a promising strategy for alleviating kidney injury by virtue of their efficient ROS-scavenging capabilities [22,23]. Among these, black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs), a subtype of two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials, have attracted considerable attention owing to their high surface-to-volume ratio, broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging activity, and excellent biocompatibility[[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Notably, BPNSs have been demonstrated to effectively mitigate acute kidney injury (AKI) in mice by acting as potent ROS scavengers [29]. Furthermore, the unique photodegradable property of black phosphorus, which enables its gradual transformation from the elemental form into biocompatible phosphorus oxides under physiological conditions, further underscores its favorable safety profile and therapeutic potential for chronic kidney diseases [30]. In parallel, given that extracellular DNA constitutes the primary structural framework of NETs, the administration of deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) to enzymatically degrade these DNA scaffolds has been validated as a viable approach to mitigate NETs-mediated tissue damage in various inflammatory disease models[[31], [32], [33], [34]]. However, effective delivery of these therapeutic agents specifically to the injured kidney remains a significant challenge. To address this, neutrophil-mediated delivery systems have been developed to exploit the innate inflammation-homing properties of neutrophils, enabling targeted transport of nanomaterials to inflamed kidneys [35]. Moreover, the incorporation of targeting peptides onto cell membranes has proven to be an effective strategy to further enhance the targeting specificity of nanocarriers toward diseased tissues [36,37].

Herein, we developed a biomimetic nanoplatform by loading DNase I onto black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) and subsequently coating them with a neutrophil membrane functionalized with a CD44-targeting peptide (PNM@D@BP), aiming to simultaneously scavenge ROS and inhibit NETosis for the treatment of CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the neutrophil membrane coating, modified with a CD44-targeting peptide, endows the nanocarrier with the ability to specifically home to sites of renal injury and inflammation, thereby significantly enhancing its renal accumulation and retention. PNM@D@BP exhibited potent antioxidative activity, effectively eliminating CaOx crystal-induced ROS in both in vitro and in vivo settings. Moreover, PNM@D@BP was validated to suppress NETs formation, thereby attenuating neutrophil-driven inflammatory responses. Furthermore, RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analyses revealed that PNM@D@BP confers protection against CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury by modulating oxidative stress and neutrophil-mediated inflammatory pathways. Collectively, these findings highlight the considerable therapeutic potential of PNM@D@BP as a promising candidate for the targeted treatment of CaOx kidney stones.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the mechanism of PNM@D@BP for accelerating CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury.

Results and discussion

The preparation and characterization of PNM@D@BP

In this study, PNM@D@BP were fabricated as shown Fig. 2A. Initially, bulk black phosphorus was exfoliated into BPNSs via sonication, which were subsequently functionalized with NH2-PEG-NH2 to yield PEGylated BPNSs (BP@PEG). Thereafter, DNase I was loaded onto the BP@PEG nanocarriers to form the drug-loaded complex (D@BP@PEG). Simultaneously, HL-60 cells were differentiated into a neutrophil-like phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 1A), and their membranes were isolated and then modified with targeting peptides to obtain peptide-functionalized neutrophil membrane (PNM). Ultimately, the PNM were coated onto the surface of the D@BP@PEG complex via a co-extrusion process, thereby fabricating the final biomimetic nanoparticle, designated as PNM@D@BP. The morphology of BP, BP@PEG, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 1B. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements revealed that the average thickness of the materials increased progressively from BP to D@BP and then to PNM@D@BP (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 1C and 1D). The energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of PNM@D@BP was performed to determine its elemental composition. The appearance of characteristic peaks for carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) confirms the successful synthesis of the final product (Fig. 2D). The products were subsequently characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The emergence of characteristic absorption peaks confirmed the successful modification with PEG and the loading of DNase I (Fig. 2E). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms that P, N, C, and O are the main elements (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the products were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2G). Changes in both the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential further confirmed the successful synthesis of PNM@D@BP. SDS-PAGE demonstrated that PNM@D@BP faithfully reproduced the protein profile of the native neutrophil-like cell membrane, thereby establishing the presence of neutrophil-specific proteins (Fig. 2H). Western blot detected the neutrophil signature membrane proteins CD11a and CD18 on the PNM@D@BP (Fig. 2I). Drug release studies demonstrated that approximately 52% of the loaded DNase I was released within 7 days in PBS at pH 7.4 (Fig. 2J). CaOx crystals stimulate renal tubular epithelial cells to produce excessive ROS, leading to cellular injury. Therefore, the in vitro free radical scavenging capacity of PNM@D@BP was investigated. Accordingly, the antioxidant capacity of PNM@D@BP was evaluated using 2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assays. The scavenging rates against both ABTS and DPPH radicals increased in a concentration-dependent manner with rising concentrations of PNM@D@BP, indicating that PNM@D@BP possesses potent and broad-spectrum antioxidant activity in vitro (Fig. 2K and L). Furthermore, we directly measured the enzymatic activity of DNase I before loading and after release from PNM@D@BP using a fluorescence-based DNase I activity assay. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1E, the released DNase I retained potent enzymatic activity comparable to that of free DNase I, indicating that the mild fabrication process (4°C sonication and co-extrusion) and the release conditions did not significantly compromise the bioactivity of the enzyme. Collectively, these data confirm that PNM@D@BP was successfully synthesized and is characterized by effective in vitro antioxidant properties.Fig. 2The preparation and characterization of PNM@D@BP. Renal targeting of PNM@D@BP in vivo and cellular uptake in vitro

CD44 is a cell-adhesion molecule that is ubiquitously expressed on leukocytes and parenchymal cells [38]. It functions as a key molecular bridge in kidney stone formation, where its binding to osteopontin (OPN) or matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) facilitates crystal adhesion, internalization, and growth, thereby promoting stone aggregation [39]. IHC analysis revealed an upregulation of CD44 in the kidneys of mice with CaOx nephrocalcinosis compared to the control group (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the IHC findings, Western Blot analysis further confirmed the elevated CD44 expression in CaOx monohydrate (COM)-stimulated HK-2 cells (Fig. 3B). Accordingly, we engineered a neutrophil membrane-coated nanocarrier by modifying the membranes with CD44-targeting peptide (Cys-(DSPE-PEG2K-Mal)-KPSSPPEE), ultimately aiming to achieve superior kidney-targeting capability. The characterization of CD44-targeting peptide is shown in Fig. 3C and D. To assess the biodistribution of PNM@D@BP in vivo, Cy5.5-labeled BP and Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP were intravenously administered to CaOx nephrocalcinosis mice via the tail vein (Fig. 3E). The distribution of PNM@D@BP was observed using a PekinElmer IVIS spectrum imaging system. In mice treated with Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP, fluorescence signals were monitored using in vivo fluorescence imaging at 2-, 12-, and 48-h post-injection. The fluorescence intensity reached a maximum at 2 h after administration and showed a significant decrease beyond 48 h following PNM@D@BP injection. These results indicate that PNM@D@BP can be effectively cleared from the body after performing its function, suggesting a favorable biosafety profile. A strong Cy5.5 fluorescence signal was detected primarily in the liver and kidneys, indicating these organs as the main routes for nanoparticle processing and elimination (Fig. 3F and G). To examine the uptake of Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP in renal tubules, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) labeling of AQP-1 in mouse kidney tissues. In nephrocalcinosis mice administered Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP, a markedly stronger fluorescence signal was detected in proximal tubular cells (Fig. 3H). This indicates that the CD44-targeting peptide-anchored neutrophil membrane coating enables D@BP to home in on renal injury sites, promote accumulation in tubular epithelial cells, and extend retention within compromised tubules. The cellular uptake of PNM@D@BP was assessed using flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. COM-stimulated HK-2 cells were incubated with Cy5.5-labeled BP or Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP for 2 or 6 h. Flow cytometry demonstrated the enhanced uptake, showing higher accumulation of PNM@D@BP in HK-2 cells than BP (Fig. 3I and J). Consistently, confocal imaging further revealed a significantly stronger fluorescence signal in the PNM@D@BP-treated group compared to the BP group (Fig. 3K). These findings indicate that coating BP with a CD44-targeting peptide-anchored neutrophil membrane potentiates its accumulation in target HK-2 cells under in vitro conditions. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PNM@D@BP effectively targets renal injury and inflammatory sites, thereby significantly enhancing its renal accumulation and ultimately achieving efficient drug bioavailability. Notably, the CD44-targeted peptide-modified neutrophil membrane coating constitutes a key innovative feature of this nanoplatform, distinguishing it from conventional nanosystems. This membrane-based modification endows PNM@D@BP with dual-target recognition capacity: endogenous neutrophil membrane proteins mediate adhesion to inflamed vascular endothelium through interactions with ICAM-1, whereas the conjugated CD44-targeting peptide confers specific binding affinity toward CD44-overexpressing tubular epithelial cells within injured renal regions. This synergistic targeting mechanism not only enhances renal accumulation but also prolongs local retention via receptor-mediated anchoring, thereby minimizing rapid urinary excretion. Moreover, CD44 ligation promotes efficient receptor-mediated endocytosis, substantially improving the intracellular bioavailability of the loaded therapeutic agents. By contrast, uncoated nanoparticles are prone to rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system and lack injury-targeting specificity, while non-targeted D@BP depends predominantly on passive accumulation. Biosafety of PNM@D@BP

The biosafety of nanomaterials is critical to their application. To evaluate the in vivo biosafety of PNM@D@BP, male C57BL/6J mice received daily injections of either phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or PNM@D@BP for seven consecutive days. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, and lungs, were then collected from each group for histological analysis by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. The results showed that PNM@D@BP did not induce any significant signs of toxicity in the examined organs (Fig. 4A). Serum biochemical analysis showed no significant differences in the levels of aspartate alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) between mice treated with different concentrations of PNM@D@BP and the PBS control group (Fig. 4B and C). The counts of white blood cells (WBCs), red blood cells (RBCs), platelets (PLTs), and the concentration of hemoglobin (HGB) remained comparable between the PBS control and PNM@D@BP treatment groups (Fig. 4D–G). Then, the biocompatibility of PNM@D@BP was evaluated in vitro using a hemolysis test and CCK-8 assays. In the in vitro hemolysis test, PNM@D@BP demonstrated excellent blood compatibility, with its hemolysis rate significantly lower than the limit value stipulated by the safety standards for biological materials (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, CCK-8 assays and live/dead cell staining confirmed that PNM@D@BP exhibited no obvious cytotoxicity at a concentration of 200 μg/mL (Fig. 4I, Figure S7A and Figure S7B). Overall, these findings proved that PNM@D@BP has excellent biosafety in vivo and in vitro. The safety assessment in this study was limited to a 7-day observation period. Although PNM@D@BP has been demonstrated to exhibit favorable acute biosafety, its chronic toxicity and long-term cumulative effects remain to be further investigated. As the primary metabolic organ for phosphorus-containing compounds and the major excretion route for nanomaterials, the liver and kidney, respectively, warrant particular attention in subsequent long-term studies. PNM@D@BP alleviated renal CaOx crystal deposition and CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury in vivo

Renal tubular epithelial cells injury has been reported to be a key factor causing the formation of CaOx nephrocalcinosis [14,15]. To verify the effects of PNM@D@BP on renal CaOx crystals deposition and CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury in CaOx nephrocalcinosis mouse model, we subjected mice kidneys to further assays (Fig. 5A). The deposition of CaOx crystals in the mice kidney was assessed using polarized light microscopy and Von Kossa staining. Fig. 5B shows crystal deposition of each group under a polarized light optical microscope. In the PBS group, no crystal deposition was observed. Under low magnification, numerous highly refractive CaOx crystals were observed in both the renal cortex and medulla of kidney in the Gly + PBS group. Treatment with BP, DNase I, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP significantly reduced renal CaOx crystal deposition, with the most pronounced effect observed in the PNM@D@BP group (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Furthermore, Von Kossa staining corroborated the findings from polarized light optical microscopy, demonstrating that PNM@D@BP treatment markedly reduced CaOx crystal deposition in the mouse model of nephrocalcinosis (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 2B). To further evaluate the impact of PNM@D@BP on CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury, we assessed renal pathology using a panel of methods, including periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1). PAS revealed that all treatments (BP, DNase I, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP) exerted a significant renoprotective effect against CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury, among which PNM@D@BP was the most effective (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 2D, all treatments (BP, DNase I, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP) markedly reduced tubular cell death, among which PNM@D@BP was the most effective. In line with the TUNEL and PAS results, PNM@D@BP treatment also downregulated the expression of KIM-1, as evidenced by IHC staining in the nephrocalcinosis mice model (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 2E). Moreover, PNM@D@BP treatment markedly reduced serum levels of creatinine, BUN, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the nephrocalcinosis mice model (Fig. 5G, H, I). In addition, PNM@D@BP treatment markedly increased serum level of glutathione (GSH) compared to Gly group (Fig. 5J). The above results revealed PNM@D@BP could alleviate renal CaOx crystals deposition and CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury in vivo, and demonstrating its high antilithogenic efficacy. BPNSs has been reported to act as ROS scavengers to mitigate acute kidney injury in mice [29]. It has also been established that DNase I confers protection against acute kidney injury through the inhibition of NETs [33]. In this study, we report that the combined application of BPNSs and DNase I exerts dual therapeutic effects—ROS scavenging and NETs inhibition—effectively alleviating both renal CaOx crystal deposition and crystal-induced kidney injury.

PNM@D@BP attenuated oxidative stress level in the CaOx nephrocalcinosis mouse model

The pathogenesis of CaOx kidney stones involves a vicious cycle: oxalate and/or CaOx crystals initiate renal damage by stimulating ROS in renal tubular epithelial cells, which induces inflammation and injury; These pathological changes then fuel a vicious cycle by facilitating crystal aggregation and growth, promoting stone formation [40,41]. Therefore, scavenging excessive ROS to attenuate renal oxidative stress level represents a key strategy to alleviate kidney injury and interrupt this disease process. The potent ROS-scavenging activity of PNM@D@BP primarily arises from its core component-BPNSs. Benefiting from the unique puckered lattice structure and abundant lone-pair electrons on phosphorus atoms, BPNSs can directly react with and neutralize a broad spectrum of reactive oxygen species, including hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anions (O2•-), exhibiting intrinsic catalase-like and superoxide dismutase-like activities. Compared with conventional antioxidants, BPNSs possess a significantly higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, enabling efficient adsorption and elimination of ROS in an enzyme-independent manner, thereby achieving sustained and broad-spectrum ROS scavenging. Furthermore, the loading of DNase I and coating of neutrophil membranes do not compromise the inherent ROS-scavenging properties of BPNSs. Instead, the targeted delivery conferred by PNM modification ensures the precise accumulation of BPNSs at inflammatory and injured sites, thereby maximizing their local antioxidative efficacy. To evaluate the in vivo ROS-scavenging capacity of PNM@D@BP, we performed a series of experiments using a mouse model of glyoxylate-induced CaOx nephrocalcinosis. To evaluate renal ROS generation, we performed 8-OHdG staining on kidney tissue sections. As evidenced by in vivo immunofluorescence, treatment with BP, DNase I, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP significantly reduced renal ROS production, with the most pronounced effect observed in the PNM@D@BP group (Fig. 6A Supplementary Fig. 3A). Therapeutic mechanisms of PNM@D@BP on CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury

The PNM@D@BP exhibits promising efficacy in inhibiting CaOx crystal deposition and mitigating renal injury. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. To address this, we conducted high-throughput RNA sequencing on renal tissues from CaOx nephrocalcinosis mice treated with either PNM@D@BP or PBS. To evaluate the overall transcriptional variation among samples, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted. As shown in Fig. 10A, the PCA plot demonstrates a significant separation between the PNM@D@BP-treated group and the PBS-treated group along the first principal component (PC1). Samples within each group clustered closely, indicating high experimental reproducibility. The volcano plot analysis revealed 1895 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two treatment groups, among which 918 were upregulated and 977 were downregulated (Fig. 10B). Subsequent analysis of DEGs revealed that, compared to the PNM@D@BP-treated group, the expression levels of genes such as Lcn2, Il1rn, and Nfkbia were significantly upregulated in the PBS-treated group, suggesting that PNM@D@BP treatment may suppress inflammatory response. Concurrently, the expression of oxidative stress-related genes, including Mt1, Mt2, and Nqo1, was also markedly higher in the PBS-treated group than in the PNM@D@BP-treated group. Furthermore, genes associated with NETs formation, such as ltf, H3c2, H3c6, H3c7, and Prtn3, showed significantly elevated expression in the PBS-treated group, indicating that PNM@D@BP treatment may suppress NETs formation (Fig. 10C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed alterations in biological functions related to the acute inflammatory response and oxidative processes (Fig. 10D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed the concurrent activation of the KEAP1-NRF2 signaling pathway and the NF-κB pathway, alongside perturbations in apoptotic processes and neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 10E and F). Collectively, within the CaOx nephrocalcinosis mice, the activation of inflammatory responses and oxidative stress promotes the formation NETs. Conclusion

Our study suggests that PNM@D@BP holds great potential for managing CaOx crystal-induced kidney injury, with in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrating that its therapeutic mechanisms involve ROS scavenging, reduction of neutrophil infiltration, and inhibition of NETosis, thereby offering a promising application for treating CaOx kidney stones. Nevertheless, several limitations should be addressed in future research to further validate and enhance its significance. First, our findings are currently based solely on an acute glyoxylate-induced mouse model of CaOx nephrocalcinosis, which does not fully recapitulate the chronic, progressive nature of human nephrolithiasis; therefore, validation in other animal models and more chronic stone formation models with extended observation periods is necessary to evaluate long-term efficacy, safety, chronic toxicity, immunogenicity, and durability of the anti-stone effect. Additionally, translating nanoparticle-based therapies like PNM@D@BP into clinical practice faces practical hurdles, including scalable production, cost, and regulatory clearance. Addressing these limitations should be a priority for future multidisciplinary nanomedicine research before any potential clinical translation.

Synthesis of PNM@D@BP

BPNSs were prepared through a liquid phase exfoliation method as described previously [46]. Briefly, HL-60 cells were cultured in medium containing 1.25% DMSO for one week to induce neutrophil-like differentiation, followed by isolation of their cell membranes [47]. Bulk BP powder was dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and subjected to probe sonication for 12 h in an ice-water bath, with the ice replenished every hour. The resulting brown dispersion was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to remove unexfoliated black phosphorus. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to remove NMP. The obtained precipitate was collected as BPNSs. An appropriate amount of deionized water was added to wash the BPNSs, which were then redispersed in water for subsequent use. NH2-PEG-NH2 and BPNSs were dispersed in deionized water, followed by bath sonication for 30 min and subsequent magnetic stirring at 1000 rpm at room temperature for 4 h. The resulting mixture was transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C to remove unbound NH2-PEG-NH2. The BPNSs were washed twice with deionized water using the same centrifugation protocol. The final precipitate was redispersed in water to obtain PEGylated BPNSs (BP@PEG). Subsequently, BP@PEG was mixed with DNase I and stirred in the dark for 12 h to allow the loading of DNase I onto BP@PEG. The DNase I-loaded BP@PEG was collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, washed twice with deionized water to remove free DNase I, and finally obtained as DNase I-loaded BP@PEG (D@BP@PEG). Targeting peptides and neutrophil-like cell membrane (NM) were dispersed in deionized water, ultrasonicated at 4°C for 3 min, and then stirred for 1 h to obtain peptide-modified neutrophil membrane (PNM). The above PNM solution was mixed with D@BP@PEG and ultrasonicated at 4°C for 3 min. The mixture was co-extruded using a liposome extruder, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The collected precipitate was redispersed in deionized water, thereby coating the PNM onto the surface of D@BP@PEG to obtain the final product, PNM@D@BP.

Characterization of PNM@D@BP

The morphology and microstructure of the prepared materials were characterized by TEM (Thermo Scientific Talos F200i) and AFM (Bruker-Dimension Edge). The layered structure of BP nanosheets and the subsequent PNM coating were observed by TEM. The AFM was employed to determine the thickness and surface topography of the prepared materials. EDS mapping was conducted to confirm the uniform distribution of characteristic elements and the successful coating of organic components in the final PNM@D@BP composite. The chemical composition and surface modifications were analyzed by FTIR (Thermo Fisher-Nicolet iS50) and XPS (Thermo Fisher-K-Alpha). FTIR spectra of BP, BP@PEG, D@BP, and PNM@D@BP were recorded to identify characteristic functional groups and verify the stepwise surface conjugation and membrane coating. The hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge of the nanoparticles at different stages of preparation were measured by DLS and zeta potential analysis (Malvern Zetasizer nano ZS90, UK), respectively. The successful coating of the platelet-neutrophil hybrid membrane was confirmed by protein analysis. SDS-PAGE was performed to compare the protein profiles of NM, PNM, and PNM@D@BP. Furthermore, Western blot analysis was used to detect the presence of characteristic neutrophil membrane markers, CD11a and CD18, in NM, PNM, and PNM@D@BP. To evaluate drug release, an appropriate amount of PNM@D@BP was placed into a dialysis bag and immersed in PBS buffer at 37°C. At predetermined time points, samples that had been continuously agitated in an orbital shaker incubator were collected for analysis, and an equal volume of fresh medium was replenished. The release amount of DNase I was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter DU730, USA). Ethics of animals

All animal experiments were carried out in strict compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols were formally approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJH-202408001).

Animals and treatments

6-8-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Research Center of Hubei. All mice were raised in the Animal Facilities of Tongji Hospital according to specific pathogen free standards, and allowed free access to food and water. Thirty mice were randomly divided into six groups: PBS group (PBS, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal injection with phosphate-buffered saline (200 μL) every day for one week; CaOx nephrocalcinosis mouse model group (Gly + PBS, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal glyoxylate (75 mg/kg) and PBS (200 μL)every day for one week [48]. BP treatment group (Gly + BP, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal glyoxylate (75 mg/kg) every day for one week. Additionally, they received BPNSs (2 mg/kg) via tail vein injection on days 2, 4, and 6. DNase I treatment group (Gly + DNase I, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal glyoxylate (75 mg/kg) every day for one week. Additionally, they received DNase I (10000 U/kg) via tail vein injection on days 2, 4, and 6. D@BP treatment group (Gly + D@BP, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal glyoxylate (75 mg/kg) every day for one week. Additionally, they received D@BP (equivalent to 2 mg/kg BP and 10000 U/kg DNase I) via tail vein injection on days 2, 4, and 6. PNM@D@BP treatment group (Gly + PNM@D@BP, n = 5), mice received intraperitoneal glyoxylate (75 mg/kg) every day for one week. Additionally, they received PNM@D@BP (equivalent to 2 mg/kg BP and 10000 U/kg DNase I) via tail vein injection on days 2, 4, and 6. On day 8, all mice were euthanized, and kidney samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent analysis.

Fluorescence imaging and biodistribution analysis

C57BL/6J mice with CaOx nephrocalcinosis were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 5 per group) and administered either Cy5.5-labeled BP or Cy5.5-labeled PNM@D@BP, respectively. In vivo fluorescence imaging was conducted at 2-, 12-, and 48-h post-injection using a PekinElmer IVIS spectrum imaging system. To assess biodistribution, the mice were euthanized at the aforementioned time points post-injection. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested for ex vivo imaging and subsequent semiquantitative analysis.

Blood physiochemical assays

Blood samples were collected from the eyeball immediately after euthanasia. Serum levels of AST, ALT, BUN, and CRE were evaluated by Chemistry Analyzer (Chemray 240, Rayto, China). The level of LDH released by the different groups was quantified according to the manufacturer's protocol. Additionally, a panel of standard hematological parameters was assessed, including RBCs, WBCs, PLTs, and HGB.

HE staining

Paraffin-embedded heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were sectioned and stained with HE using standard procedure.

Detection of kidney CaOx crystals

We visualized and analyzed CaOx crystal deposition with a polarized light microscope. Additionally, Von Kossa staining was also performed to further confirm CaOx crystals deposition as described in previous study [49]. Image J software was employed to quantify the percentage area of crystal deposition per section.

PAS staining

To evaluate tubular injury, paraffin-embedded kidneys sections were subjected to PAS following the manufacturer's protocol. Ten non-overlapping microscopic fields (200×) were randomly selected and scored for injury severity based on an established scoring system: 0, none; 1, <25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 51–75%; and 4, >75% [50].

Tunnel staining

To evaluate renal cell death, we conducted TUNEL staining with a commercial kit and visualized the results by fluorescence microscopy. The extent of cell death was expressed as the percentage area per section, which was quantified using Image J software.

IHC

Immunohistochemical staining for proteins KIM-1, IL-1β, HO-1, SOD2, and CAT was performed on paraffin-embedded kidney sections. After incubation with specific primary antibodies (anti-KIM-1, 1:1000; anti-IL-1β, 1:1000; anti-HO-1, 1:500; anti-SOD2, 1:1000, and anti-CAT, 1:1000.) overnight at 4°C, the sections were treated with respective horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 0.5 h at 37°C. Signals were developed using an Envision HRP Polymer system, visualized under a microscope (×200 magnification), and quantified with Image J software to determine protein expression levels.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 according to manufacturer's instructions.

Cell culture

HK-2 cells were acquired from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China) and grown in DMEM/F12 medium augmented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) were isolated from the renal cortex of 6-8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice as described previously [7]. In brief, cortical tissues were minced and digested with 1 mg/mL type I collagenase at 37°C for 30 min to dissociate the cells. Subsequently, the cell suspension was subjected to purification via Percoll gradient density centrifugation. Bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) were isolated from the tibia and femur of 6-8-week-old C57BL/6 mice following an established protocol [51]. Briefly, the bone marrow was flushed with ice-cold PBS supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Subsequently, neutrophils were purified by negative selection using a Percoll isolation kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following washing, the purified cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for subsequent in vitro studies. A Transwell co-culture system was employed to investigate the functional changes of neutrophils in the context of COM-stimulated TECs. Briefly, TECs seeded in the lower chamber were stimulated with 100 μg/mL COM, while BMDNs were introduced into the upper chamber, followed by 24 h of co-incubation.

HK-2 cells groups and treatments

To establish an in vitro kidney injury model, HK-2 cells were treated with or without PNM@D@BP for 2 h, and then incubated with 100 mg/ml of CaOx monohydrate (COM) stimulation for 24h. HK-2 cells were divided into the following six groups: PBS group (PBS), HK-2 cells were cultured with DMEM medium and treated with PBS (20 μL) for 24h; COM stimulating group (COM + PBS), HK-2 cells were treated with COM (100 mg/mL) and PBS (20 μL) stimulation for 24h; BP treatment group (COM + BP), HK-2 cells were subjected BP (20 μg/mL) pretreatment for 2h, and then treated with COM (100 mg/mL) stimulation for 24h; DNase I treatment group (COM + DNase I), HK-2 cells were subjected to DNase I (100 U/mL) pretreatment for 2h, and then treated with COM (100 mg/mL) stimulation for 24h; D@BP treatment group (COM + D@BP), HK-2 cells were subjected to D@BP (equivalent to 20 μg/mL BP and 100 U/mL DNase I) pretreatment for 2h, and then treated with COM (100 mg/mL) stimulation for 24h; PNM@D@BP treatment group (COM + PNM@D@BP), HK-2 cells were subjected to PNM@D@BP (equivalent to 20 μg/mL BP and 100 U/mL DNase I) pretreatment for 2h, and then treated with COM (100 mg/mL) stimulation for 24h.

Detection of free radical scavenging capability

The free radical scavenging capacity of the PNM@D@BP was assessed through a series of well-established in vitro assays. ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging activities were determined using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's protocols. In brief, varying concentrations of PNM@D@BP (0.1–5 μg/mL) were added to the respective reaction solutions. Following a specified incubation period, the absorbance or fluorescence intensity was measured with a spectrophotometer to quantify the scavenging activity.

Detection of ROS in HK-2 cells

We used fluorescent microscope and flow cytometry to detect ROS in HK-2 cells. Briefly, treated HK-2 cells were washed and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 0.5h at 37°C. Then, a fluorescent microscope was used to evaluate the levels of intracellular ROS. Additionally, flow cytometry was used to further measure the intracellular ROS levels as described previously [52].

Determination of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured with the TMRE fluorescent probe (Beyotime, C2001). Specifically, treated HK-2 cells were washed and subsequently incubated with TMRE working solution at 37°C for 45 min. After staining, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and the relative fluorescence intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software.

DHE fluorescence

Treated HK-2 cells were washed and subsequently incubated with 5 μM DHE at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS, the cells were observed using a fluorescence microscope. The relative fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

Measurement of GSH and MDA levels

HK-2 cells were seeded into 6-well plates for 24 h. HK-2 cells were treated with BP, DNase I, D@BP, or PNM@D@BP in combination with COM (100 mg/mL) for another 24 h. The supernatants were collected and analyzed using GSH and MDA assay kits following manufacturer's instructions.

Cell viability assay

HK-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates for 24 h. HK-2 cells were treated with BP, DNase I, D@BP, or PNM@D@BP in combination with COM (100 mg/mL) for another 24 h. Then, 10 μL CCK-8 was added to each well and incubated for 1h 30min. The OD values were detected by a microplate reader at wave length of 450 nm to calculate the cell viability. Meanwhile, the cytotoxicity of PNM@D@BP was also evaluated using the Calcein/PI Cell Viability Assay Kit following the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunofluorescence

Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, kidney sections were blocked with goat serum and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against CitH3 (1:200), Ly6G (1:100) and MPO (1:150). Subsequently, the sections were stained with species-appropriate FITC- or CY3-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100) and DAPI for nuclear visualization. All images were captured with a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot

Protein was extracted from cells and separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were first blocked with BSA and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies, including CD11a (180 kDa; 1:2000), CD18 (85 kDa; 1:1000), ATP1A1 (110 kDa; 1:5000), CitH3 (15 kDa; 1:1000), MPO (60 kDa; 1:2000), NE (35 kDa; 1:2000) and β-actin (42 kDa; 1:5000). After washing, the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS system (Bio-Rad). Protein expression levels were quantified to β-actin. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA sequencing

Following collection from C57BL/6J mice, fresh renal tissues were trimmed of connective and adipose tissue and rinsed with ice-cold RNase-free saline. The samples were then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 h before long-term storage at −80°C. Total RNA was extracted from the preserved renal tissues for transcriptome sequencing. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by LC-Bio Technology (China). The raw RNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the SangerBox platform (China).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using two-tailed Student's t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). The value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
