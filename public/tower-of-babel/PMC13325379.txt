Comparative Adsorption Behavior of Orange Peel Powder and Its Chars for Dye Removal
Authors: Priya Pal, Mark Lynch, Peter Harris, Kamrun Nahar, Paulomi Polly Burey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the efficacy of orange peel powder (OP), a readily available agri-food waste, and its muffle furnace char (MF) and nitrogen-assisted char (NA), for crystal violet (CV) adsorption. Char samples analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis, Fourier-transform infrared spectrometry, scanning electron microscopy, and proximate analysis showed substantial structural changes. The impacts on absorption of pH, adsorbent, contact time, dye concentration, and temperature were evaluated. Orange peel powder showed maximum adsorption capacity (49.35 mg g–1, Langmuir isotherm), compared to those of MF (19.05 mg g–1) and NA (12.87 mg g–1), due to its abundant surface sites and functional groups. Enhanced pH tolerance and process stability and other parameters were exhibited by MF and NA but with reduced adsorption capacity for CV. All adsorbents conformed to the Langmuir isotherm and exhibited pseudo-second order kinetics, indicative of monolayer chemisorption. Crystal violet adsorption on OP was exothermic and spontaneous at ambient temperature, while MF and NA exhibited endothermic and temperature-dependent behavior. Although orange peel exhibited superior adsorption capacity, chars have practical benefits for effective dye removal, facilitating waste valorization and sustainable water management.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization has led to substantial economic and social benefits, worldwide. It has also posed enormous challenges for water quality management. As urban areas grow, both the amount and variety of pollutants infiltrating surface water and groundwater systems have increased significantly, putting pressure on conventional water treatment facilities and rendering the water supplies of several communities susceptible to contamination. The complexity of wastewater generated by urban and industrial activities containing synthetic chemicals, dyes, and heavy metals frequently surpasses the treatment capabilities of current facilities, posing ongoing risks to potable water, aquatic ecosystems, and the overall environmental sustainability.

A range of organic pollutants, including pesticides, herbicides, industrial byproducts, dyes, and household wastes, may enter the environment posing a risk to ecosystems and human populations. One such pollutant, crystal violet (CV), was chosen as a representative of such pollutants for study as it is persistent and readily detected in water samples, due to its high molar absorptivity in the visible spectrum enabling accurate detection at low concentrations using UV–Vis spectrophotometry. Additionally, CV may act as a potential model contaminant for cationic pollutants in laboratory-scale studies.

Concurrent with these escalating pollution issues, the increasing accumulation of agri-food waste, including orange peels, poses a global management problem. Significant amounts of orange peel are produced annually as a byproduct of fruit juice processing. These nutrient-rich wastes are typically disposed of in landfills or incinerated, leading to environmental and logistical issues. Due to a high abundance of cellulose, hemicellulose, and various surface functional groups, orange peel has great potential as an absorbent. The beneficial features of orange peel for pollutant adsorption and water purification are increasingly recognized in sustainability and waste valorization research. A range of studies have been done with physically and chemically modified orange peel to enhance adsorption capacity and selectivity. − Pyrolysis (thermal decomposition in the absence of oxygen) converts orange peel into biochar, a porous, carbon-rich material with enhanced adsorption properties. These enhanced properties are attributed to an increase in surface area and active sites resulting from restructuring of the cellular matrix.

Comparison of different pyrolysis methods on the adsorption performance of agri-food wastes, such as orange peel, is lacking. This is particularly evident when these materials are used without consideration of their inherent adsorption properties, despite the growing interest in biobased adsorbents for water treatment. Most previous studies have focused on either unmodified biomass or materials modified by chemical treatments, often overlooking the impact of various pyrolysis approaches, such as muffle furnace or nitrogen-assisted processes on char structure, surface chemistry, and contaminant removal efficacy. , These approaches are rarely subjected to a thorough comparison of their effects on adsorption kinetics, equilibrium, and thermodynamic behavior.

This study provides an extensive overview of raw and structurally different chars made from orange peels using pyrolysis methods. This work enhances understanding of sustainable waste valorization by examining the capacities and possible mechanisms for CV uptake, allowing for optimization of nonchemically activated bioadsorbent designed for environmental applications. It also evaluates kinetic, isothermal, and thermodynamic behaviors of orange peel and derived chars, to enable production of improved agri-food waste-derived adsorbents for pollution adsorption.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used were analytical grade. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%, pellets anhydrous), crystal violet (C25H30ClN3; M.wt. 407.99 g mol–1), and sodium chloride (NaCl; M.wt. 58.44 g mol–1) were used as received. Crystal violet stock solutions were prepared in Milli-Q-deionized water and diluted as needed. Initial pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH.

Adsorbent Preparation and Characterization

Orange peel, collected from a local fruit shop, was washed thoroughly with deionized water to eliminate surface impurities. Clean peels were dried in a Sunbeam DT6000 Food Lab Electronic Dehydrator at 60 °C for 24 h. The dried peel was ground using a Nutribullet household grinder and sieved through a range of standard 75 μm–1.18 mm meshes to ensure uniform particle size between 300 μm and 1.18 mm across all adsorbent samples. Further removal of residual moisture, from the sieved orange peel powder (OP), was achieved overnight in a Labec ODWF14 laboratory oven. To examine the influence of process conditions, two different methods were used to convert the dried powder into char. For nitrogen-assisted charring (NA), OP was placed in a Rio Grande kiln model CS2 and heated to 600 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min–1 holding for 1 h under a continuous nitrogen flow rate of 400 mL min–1. For muffle furnace charring (MF), ceramic crucibles were lidded, and the same temperature and heating conditions as above were applied in a Nabertherm P330 muffle furnace without nitrogen flow. All prepared chars were cooled inside the furnace and stored in airtight containers for further use.

Proximate Analysis

Orange powder and its chars were assessed for their proximate composition by weighing samples using an electronic balance (HR-250AZ; A&D Co., Ltd., Japan) with 0.0001 g precision. Moisture content, total, volatile, and ash content were determined using Standard Method 2540G. Fixed carbon was calculated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) in conjunction with standard method 2540G.

Physicochemical Characterization

Chars were comprehensively characterized to evaluate their thermal stability, surface functionality, morphology, and textural properties.

Thermal stability and decomposition profiles were assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and were performed using a TA SDT650 thermogravimetric analyzer. Samples were heated at a rate of 10 °C min–1 to 110 °C and held for 5 min to remove moisture, followed by heating to 900 °C at the same rate and held for 25 min before cooling. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted using an IRAffinity-1S spectrometer in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode over a range of 400–4000 cm–1 to identify surface functional groups. Spectra were recorded between 400 cm–1 and 4000 cm–1 in attenuated total reflectance mode using an IRAffinity-1S FTIR spectrometer.

Surface morphology of the orange peel powder and derived chars was examined using a JEOL JSM-7000 scanning electron microscope (SEM). Additionally, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was employed to determine the specific surface area by degassing samples (0.1–0.4 g) at 100 °C for 8 h in a Tristar 3000 analyzer.

Point of Zero Charge and Effect of pH

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) was determined using the salt addition method. In a series of 100 mL Schott bottles, 0.1 g of the sample was added to each containing 25 mL of 0.1 M NaCl solution with initial pH (pHi) values (2–12) prepared in triplicates and adjusted by 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions kept under shaking at room temperature for 24 h. The final pH (pHf) values of the supernatant liquid were noted, and a plot of pHi vs pHf was used to determine the pHPZC.

The effects of pH on absorption were assessed using over for a range of pH dye solutions, i.e., 2, 4, 7, 10, and 12 were prepared by adjusting the pH using 0.1 M NaOH or 0.1 M HCl as required. Then, 100 mg each of OP, NA, and MF sample was added to each solution bottles containing 25 mL of 50 mg L–1 CV, followed by shaking at 120 rpm for 1 h in a shaker water bath at 25 °C. Solutions were filtered and the concentration of the residual CV in the filtrate was determined at λmax (590 nm) by UV VIS spectrophotometry (Cecil CE2000 series).

Batch Adsorption Studies

Each batch adsorption experiment was conducted in 250 mL Schott bottles under similar conditions: 100 mg of the sample was mixed with 25 mL of 50 mg L–1 CV, agitated at 120 rpm for 1 h in a shaking water bath at 25 °C without pH adjustment. The solution was then filtered using a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene syringe filter and the concentration of the residual CV in the filtrate was determined at λmax (590 nm) by UV VIS spectrophotometry. The CV removal effectiveness (%) and the CV adsorption capacity (mg g–1) were calculated using eqs and , respectively. 1 CVremoval(%)=C0−CeC0×100 2 qe=(C0−Ce)×Vm where C 0 and C e are the initial and equilibrium concentration of CV solution (mg L–1), respectively, m is the mass (g) adsorbent, and V is the volume of solution (L). All experiments were done in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Adsorbent Dose Effect

The capacity of the adsorbent for a given initial concentration of dye solutions was determined from the adsorbent dosage, by adding 25 mL of dye solution with an initial dye concentration of 50 mg L–1. The absorption capacities for OP, NA, and MF char addition on the amount of dye adsorbed were determined by varying amounts of absorbent (0.48, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9 g L–1), at a contact time of 1 h at 25 °C, with a shaking speed of 120 rpm. Solutions were filtered and the concentration of residual CV was determined at λmax (590 nm) using UV VIS spectrophotometry.

Adsorption Kinetics

To examine the effect of contact time, batch adsorption studies were conducted using 50 mg L–1 CV in glass bottles at an adsorbent dose of either 4 g L–1, or 100 mg of the sample in 25 mL, in a shaking water bath at 120 rpm for 5 min to 2 h at 25 °C. Samples were collected at 5, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min intervals and filtered, and the residual CV concentration of the filtrate was determined at the absorbance max (590 nm). Absorption kinetics of CV onto OP, NA, and MF chars were evaluated by plotting nonlinear expressions for Lagergren pseudo-first order (eq S1) and Ho-McKay’s pseudo-second order (eq S2) presented in SF 1.1.

To predict any potential rate-limiting steps in the adsorption progression, intraparticle diffusion was evaluated using the Weber–Morris model. It predicted the pore or internal diffusion stipulated by employing the relationship between specific sorption (q t) and time (t 0.5) presented in eq S3 (SF 1.1).

Adsorption Isotherms

Adsorption isotherms were determined using a constant dose of 4 g L–1 sample of OP, NA, and MF chars in separate glass bottles in a series of increasing concentrations of dye from 10 mg L–1 to 200 mg L–1 at 25 °C on a shaking water bath at 120 rpm until equilibrium was achieved. Solutions were filtered, and the residual CV concentration of the filtrate was determined at absorbance max (590 nm). These isotherms relate the dye concentration on the char surface to its equilibrium concentration in solution. Equilibrium experimental data for CV adsorption onto OP, NA, and MF char were studied using three isotherms; Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models whose nonlinear expressions are given in eqs S4–S6 (SF 1.2), respectively.

Adsorption Thermodynamics

The thermodynamic stability and exothermic characteristics of the adsorption process require the calculation of various thermodynamic parameters such as change in the Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), entropy (ΔS°), and enthalpy (ΔH°), which were measured using eqs S7 and S8 (SF 1.3) developed from a linearized Van’t Hoff equation. It was not converted to a nonlinearized form as studied by Lima et al. (2020), which showed both linearized and nonlinearized forms show the same thermodynamic values.

Desorption Studies

The desorption and regeneration experiments were conducted in water, 0.1 M HCl, and 0.1 M NaOH. A 25 mL aliquot of 10 mg L–1 CV was treated with 4 g L–1 adsorbent dose for 1 h at 120 rpm. The dye-loaded adsorbent was thoroughly washed to remove the unadsorbed dye. These were shaken with 25 mL of different desorbing agents and equilibrated for 1 h. The desorbed dye concentration was measured, and percent desorption was calculated.

Statistical and Error Analysis

Data was analyzed using Microsoft Excel Version 2601 with RealStats add-in. The sample size was too small to determine whether the data was normally distributed. Therefore, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess significance within data sets of >2 groups, and Tukey-HSD was used for pairwise analysis. An α value of 0.05 was applied for all statistical analyses. Modeling was conducted using Microsoft Excel Version 2601 with the Solver add-in. Equations were fit to data using the least-squares approach, utilizing the solver function to minimize the sum of squared error. To further validate the kinetic and isotherm models beyond the coefficient of determination (R 2), nonlinear regression analysis was performed using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. Three additional statistical error functions were employed: the chi-squared test (χ2), the sum of squared errors (SSE), and the residual root-mean-square error (RMSE) to analyze the data.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Adsorbents

Proximate Analysis

Pyrolysis of orange peel powder (OP) caused major compositional shifts in the resulting chars (Table ). The marked decrease in moisture content, together with the drop in the fraction of volatile solids in total solids (VS/TS falling from about 93% in OP to 81% in MF and 74% in NA), indicates dehydration and progressive devolatilization during pyrolysis, which are key objectives in biochar production for stability and storage. The increased ash content in both chars is due to the concentration of inorganic constituents as organics are volatilized. Notably, the substantial rise in fixed carbon in NA and MF chars reflects aromatic condensation, which is directly associated with enhanced adsorptive performance. The observed char yields also align with orange peel and other fruit wastes subjected to similar pyrolysis conditions.

Thermograms

Thermogravimetric analysis of OP and its chars consistently show three distinct stages. Moisture loss occurred between 40 and 130 °C, major decomposition of organic matter (mainly hemicellulose and cellulose) from around 150 to 370 °C, and ash and residue formation at higher temperatures up to 900 °C, which can be largely attributed to lignin degradation, followed by ash formation on the introduction of oxygen. Orange peel chars exhibited a higher thermal stability than the raw powder, as reflected by a smaller weight loss across the temperature range and a larger remaining residue at 900 °C (Figure ), due to the chars having undergone most of their thermal decomposition during pyrolysis, resulting in thermally resistant carbon structures remaining. This stability is shown by lower mass loss and increased residue at 900 °C in TGA, which indicates that chars can withstand multiple thermal regenerations and resist operational stresses without significant structural deterioration.

Thermogravimetric analysis of OP and its chars along with marked stages.

Functional Group Assessment

The FTIR analysis of samples (Figure ) showed a strong broad band at around 3430 cm–1 prominent in OP, which was almost absent in both NA and MF. This indicates O–H stretching vibrations characteristic of water and hydroxyl groups present in alcohols, phenols, and carboxylic acids within lignocellulosic material. Peaks at 2920 cm–1 and 2850 cm–1, corresponding to asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching, respectively, were observed in the raw powder but were absent in the charred samples, due to the destruction of aliphatic structures during pyrolysis. Furthermore, the bands at 1750 cm–1 (CO stretching in carboxylic acids, esters, aldehydes, and ketones) and at 1600 cm–1 (CC vibrations of aromatic rings) were reduced or eliminated after charring. These changes signify dehydration, decarbonylation, and the loss of aliphatic structures, which are distinctive features of thermal degradation in biomass. , The increased intensity of the CC band postcharring indicates increased aromaticity and the possible formation of graphitic domains, which correlate with greater thermal stability seen in TGA.

FTIR spectra for OP and its chars (A) before adsorption and (B) after adsorption.

Surface Morphology

Thermal treatment changed the surface morphology of the adsorbents. Orange peel powder (Figure A) had a solid, compact, fibrous surface with limited visible porosity, consistent with volatile and oxygenated functional groups measured by proximate analysis and FTIR. The intact lignocellulosic network offers multiple potential adsorption sites throughout the fiber bundles. In contrast, the muffle-furnace char (Figure B) displays a more coarse, fragmented structure characterized by larger pores and channels resulting from devolatilization and partial collapse of the biomass matrix, consistent with its increased fixed-carbon content and improved thermal stability. Nitrogen-assisted char (Figure C) exhibits thinner, more wrinkled sheet-like structures and a more open, layered network, suggesting increased interior porosity and reduced oxidative damage due to charring in an inert atmosphere. The progressive morphological changes from OP to MF and NA are consistent with changes observed in pH tolerance, thermogravimetric behavior, and pHPZC measurements, signifying a shift from a highly functionalized, less stable surface to more carbon-rich, structurally robust adsorbents (Figure ).

Scanning electron microscopy of: (A) Orange peel powder, (B) nitrogen-assisted orange peel char, and (C) muffle furnace orange peel char.

Surface Area Analysis

The dried orange peel (OP) showed a moderate surface area of 5.98 m2 g–1 with a total pore volume of 4.89 × 10–3 cm3 g–1 (Figure ), whereas MF developed a higher surface area than the raw peel (6.97 m2 g–1) and the largest pore volume (5.55 × 10–3 cm3 g–1). In contrast, NA at 600 °C under N2 exhibited the lowest BET surface area (2.36 m2 g–1) and a slightly reduced pore volume (3.44 × 10–3 cm3 g–1). The relatively low N2-BET areas of these chars can be attributed to incomplete opening of pores during carbonization, including matrix collapse, deposition of tars, and increased ash content of NA that can block or narrow pores. Noncombustible structures are more abundant in nonwoody plants such as OP than in woody biomass, and lower combustible fractions are known to yield fewer surface pores during carbonization. , This likely contributes to the comparatively low surface areas of the biochars in this study relative to those produced from agri-wastes with higher combustible contents. Changes in the BET surface area could be associated with structural ordering during pyrolysis. As temperatures increase, carbon structures may become more organized due to graphitization or thermal annealing, which can compete with pore formation and reduce the accessible surface area. , Factors such as surface smoothing observed in SEM, sintering, the reduction of oxygen-containing functional groups seen in FTIR spectra, the transition of micropores to meso/macropores, aging, longer residence time, and exposure to air during pyrolysis have all been reported to contribute to a decrease in the BET surface area. − Therefore, a reduction in surface area at higher pyrolysis intensity may indicate not just pore collapse or expansion but also the ordering and smoothing of the char surface.

Point of Zero Charge and pH Effects

Characteristics and adsorption characteristics of chars are affected by pH. A distinctly acidic pHPZC (3.15) is exhibited by OP, whereas both MF and NA showed near-neutral to mildly basic pHPZC values of 7.5 and 7.2, respectively (Figure A). This shift to higher pHPZC after pyrolysis indicates loss of acidic surface groups and enhanced negative surface charge at neutral pH, which favors the adsorption of cationic dyes. − Surfaces with pHPZC below the solution pH will have negative charges, promoting cationic dye adsorption (Figure B), while surfaces with higher pHPZC are less effective. The OP showed ΔpH curve approaching zero which can be attributed to saturation of surface O– sites and limited buffering capacity at extreme alkalinity leading to apparent second zero charge point that is an artifact rather than a true multisite pHPZC. Both the adsorbent and adsorbate have functional groups capable of protonation or deprotonation, resulting in varying surface charges depending on the pH of the solution. These changes in surface charge facilitate electrostatic interactions, either attractive or repulsive, between charged dye molecules and the adsorbent surface, which influences adsorption efficiency.

(A) Point of zero charge (pHPZC) of OP and its chars and (B) effect of pH on removal of CV by OP and its chars (4 g L–1 adsorbent dose with 50 mg L–1 dye).

CV desorption can be explained by pHPZC. When the solution pH is above the pHPZC, deprotonation results in negative surface charges, facilitating the electrostatic adsorption of cations. In contrast, a pH below the pHPZC produces protonated positive surfaces that repel cations, reducing efficiency. At pH 9–10, where pH >pHPZC, surfaces are deprotonated, increasing attraction. At pH values >11, OP decreases due to competition with OH– ions or dye aggregation, but MF and NA have a stable negative charge due to their higher pHPZC. The chars’ neutral-basic pHPZC improves pH tolerance and CV uptake compared to acidic OP at alkaline pH, due to electrostatic force modulation of surface charge.

Adsorption Studies

Effect of the Adsorbent Dose

Dye removal increased with the adsorbent dose across all materials. Near-complete removal (>95%) of CV was achieved as the adsorbent dose increased from 0.48 to 6 g L–1 (Figure ). The improvement in removal percentage with a higher adsorbent dose was expected and is likely due to the increased availability of active adsorption sites.

Effect of the adsorbent dose on % removal of CV by NA, MF, and OP and their respective adsorption capacities (mg g–1).

Conversely, the adsorption capacity (q e) decreased for all materials as the adsorbent dose increased. This inverse relationship arises from the calculation of the adsorption capacity per unit mass of the adsorbent (eq ). At higher doses, a greater number of adsorbent sites remain unsaturated, while the total dye available for adsorption remains unchanged. The trend fits with the literature suggesting that an increase in adsorbent mass may lead to a decrease in the utilization efficiency of available sites, leading to a lower capacity per gram despite an overall higher total removal.

Effect of Contact Time

Adsorption of CV with respect to the exposure time showed a range of behaviors. Removal values of 86.87% (OP) and 76.42% (MF) were achieved at 10 and 15 min, respectively, while NA achieved only 65.29% after 1 h (Figure ). In contrast, a rise to 87.74% in MF occurred over 2 h, which is likely related to a secondary adsorption involving intraparticle diffusion or the slow exposure of new adsorption sites, as shown by the multistage adsorption kinetics observed in porous adsorbents. , This behavior is due to the initial high availability of active sites on the adsorbent surface, resulting in a rapid uptake. Following the initial phase, unfilled surface sites become increasingly difficult to occupy due to repulsive interactions between adsorbed solute particles on the solid surface and those in the bulk phase. Untreated OP rapidly adsorbed CV, making it appropriate for short-contact-time applications, but it may not be suitable for larger systems due to swelling, mechanical instability, and potential release of dissolved organics and particles in untreated biomass. Despite a slower initial absorption, NA and MF chars improve structural integrity, leaching, and regeneration compatibility. ,

Effect of contact time on adsorption at 50 mg L–1 CV with 4 g L–1 of OP, MF, and NA.

Effect of Initial Dye Concentration

As the initial concentration of CV increased, the adsorption capacity of the materials increases (Figure ). At low dye concentrations, the initial CV concentration remains within the sorption capacity of all three samples, so removal efficiencies are high. As the concentration rises, the number of dye molecules begins to exceed the finite number of active sites. Therefore, progressive site saturation drives a decline in percentage removal. Even at 200 mg L–1, OP and MF still remove 81.27% and 43.37% of the dye, respectively, but NA falls to only 25.68%. The steeper gradient for NA indicates that it has fewer functional sites, a smaller effective surface area, or weaker dye surface interactions, causing it to reach saturation more rapidly than OP or MF as the dye concentration increases. Thus, for unadjusted pH, OP has a higher removal rate than MF and NA with a gradual increase in dye concentration.

Effect of initial concentration on % removal of CV by NA, MF, and OP and their respective adsorption capacities (mg g–1).

Effect of Temperature

Increasing temperature negatively impacted adsorption onto OP but positively impacted chars. Maximum adsorption occurred at 25 °C for OP and 65 °C for MF and NA, respectively (Figure ). Temperature is a key adsorption variable because it alters adsorbent surface chemistry and controls the mobility and solubility of dye molecules in solution. As temperature rises, solution viscosity decreases and the diffusion of adsorbate molecules across the external film and into the internal pore network increases. Chars at elevated temperatures show enhanced dye mobility and greater CV removal, indicative of an endothermic process. For OP, a slight decrease in adsorption with rising temperature suggests that adsorption of CV onto OP is favorable at lower temperatures due to weak Van Der Waal’s forces and physisorption, exhibiting an exothermic adsorption. Increased kinetic energy at elevated temperatures provides vibration energy to overcome these weak interactions causing active sites of the OP to release dye ions from the adsorbent surface. , These temperature-response patterns have significant importance for applications and material selection. Since OP demonstrates a decrease in CV uptake with rising temperature, achieving optimal removal at 25 °C, it is most effective for ambient or cooler wastewaters. Conversely, NA and MF achieve optimal removal at elevated temperatures and so are useful for heated industrial effluents or streams at elevated temperatures. ,

Adsorption Kinetics

The rate at which adsorbates move from the aqueous phase to the adsorbent surface is essential in contaminant removal. Kinetic studies provide information concerning how long it takes the system to reach target removal efficiencies and equilibrium. , In this study, a pseudo-first-order model showed coefficients of determination (R 2) of 0.982, 0.954, and 0.959 for OP, MF, and NA, respectively (Figure ). The model was adhered to, implying that CV absorption on OP and chars is driven by first-order behavior but that some additional kinetic models are required to fully describe the adsorption efficiency.

Pseudo-first order kinetics of OP and its chars for 50 mg L–1 CV and 4 g L–1 adsorbent.

The pseudo-second-order model accurately represented the experimental data. Values for R 2 were 0.990, 0.9751, and 0.9913 for OP, MF, and NA, respectively (Figure ). Crystal violet adsorption on OP and chars was driven by chemisorption, with the rate being closely linked to active site accessibility and surface functionality rather than bulk dye concentration. This suggests that these adsorbents achieve a high removal efficiency by ensuring sufficient contact time to optimize access to reactive sites.

Pseudo-second-order kinetics of OP and its chars for 50 mg L–1 CV and 4 g L–1 adsorbents.

The intraparticle diffusion model indicated that adsorption proceeded by different controlling mechanisms. A rapid initial multistep adsorption was exhibited by NA on surface sites with a R 2 value of 0.7716, followed by slower diffusion into internal spaces. MF showed a moderate role for pore diffusion with a R 2 of 0.4636, while OP had the highest adsorption capacity and had R 2 of 0.6401, indicating that surface adsorption and boundary-layer effects have a greater importance (Figure ). These results indicate that OP can achieve large absorption capacities, but hydrodynamics and mixing must be carefully controlled to overcome mass-transfer restrictions. The nonzero intercepts for all adsorbents confirm that intraparticle diffusion is not the only rate-controlling step. The larger c values for OP (8.9782) indicate a greater boundary layer effect and surface adsorption dominance, consistent with its abundant surface functional groups that rapidly bind cationic CV onto external surfaces. The higher k id and lower c values for NA (k id = 0.5321, c = 3.5832) suggest enhanced pore diffusion, attributable to NA’s more pronounced internal porosity and fewer surface binding sites. MF exhibits intermediate behavior with intermediate structural characteristics.

Intraparticle diffusion kinetic model of OP and its chars for 50 mg L–1 CV and 4 g L–1 adsorbents.

Kinetic data (Table ) give the information needed to optimize operating conditions. The pseudo-first-order model fits NA well, whereas OP conformed to the pseudo-second-order model with the highest adsorption capacity. Intraparticle diffusion constants (k id and c) indicate strong surface adsorption and pore diffusion, allowing dye uptake at shorter contact times. In contrast, MF showed reduced intraparticle diffusion and capacity, requiring a longer contact duration to effectively remove CV. Thus, the most promising option for small-scale CV treatment units is OP, with NA as another option when fast initial uptake beyond maximal capacity is required.

Adsorption Isotherms

Langmuir Isotherm

The Langmuir adsorption isotherm defines a homogeneous monolayer of dye molecules on the adsorbent surface, influencing adsorbate reactivity. Adsorbed molecules remain immobile and do not interact with neighbors. As seen in Figure , the separation factor R L characterizes the isotherm and adsorption type, with R L values from 0 to 1 indicative of favorable adsorption. The maximum adsorption capacity (Q max) obtained for OP may be due to inherent porosity, intact structural features, and active sites. In contrast, the lower Q max values for MF and NA are consistent with their higher fixed-carbon content and smoother FTIR spectra, suggesting loss or transformation of surface functional groups during charring. The Langmuir isotherm constant, K L, values for NA, MF, and OP were 0.1277, 0.3378, and 0.0686, respectively, indicating that equilibrium sorption for all three of 50 mg L–1 dye solutions was favorable as 0 < R L < 1. The high R 2 indicates that adsorption of dyes onto OP and its chars follow the Langmuir isotherm.

Isotherm models: Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin for (A) OP, (B) MF, and (C) NA.

To show the potential of OP and its chars in the present study, a comparison with other adsorbents for removal of CV dye was performed (Table ) by comparing the maximum adsorption capacities of different adsorbents using the Langmuir model. The Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity of OP (49.36 mg g–1) is competitive with many unmodified or minimally processed agricultural waste adsorbents reported in the literature. For instance, Sen et al., in 2024, studied ball-milled royal palm leaf sheath, which achieved a comparable 42.39 mg g–1 for CV. Freundlich Isotherm

The Freundlich isotherm describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Adsorption data was analyzed by plotting q e against C e, including the standard deviation. The Freundlich plots for NA, MF, and OP exhibit clear linearity, with R 2 values of 0.985 for OP, 0.917 for MF, and 0.907 for NA, implying an acceptable fit of the model across all samples (Figure ). The Freundlich constant K F measures adsorption affinity, with MF exhibiting a higher K F value of 6.791 mg g–1, indicating an enhanced capacity for CV uptake. The surface heterogeneity factor (1/n) is less than 1 for each material, indicating favorable adsorption. Results indicate multilayer adsorption and distinct site affinities. Adsorption in the orange peel–CV separation system can occur as homogeneous or heterogeneous and may involve monolayer or multilayer arrangements.

Temkin Isotherm

The Temkin isotherm model involves adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and proposes that the heat of adsorption decreases linearly with increasing coverage, indicating energetic heterogeneity on the adsorbent surface. The adsorbate is adsorbed on energetic and nonequivalent adsorption sites on the adsorbent surface, with the more energetic sites acting as initial adsorption points. The nonlinear Temkin isotherm constants B and A were determined from the plots of q e versus C e (Figure ). High coefficients of determination, R 2 values, suggest that the adsorption process could be due to heterogeneous surface coverage. This is in good agreement with the results of the Langmuir isotherm for CV dye. Equilibrium data (Table ) is critical for efficient system design, operation, and control.

Adsorption Thermodynamics

Thermodynamic parameters may explain how adsorbate molecules interact with a porous solid surface and whether adsorption is favored at higher or lower temperatures. Each adsorbent in this study performed distinct yet complementary functions (Figure ). The endothermic and entropy-driven adsorption of MF (ΔH° ≈66.7 kJ mol–1, substantial positive ΔS°, and negative ΔG° at all tested temperatures) corroborates the observed increase in CV removal with temperature, indicating that MF is most effective in warm or mildly heated effluents, where elevated temperatures enhance both spontaneity and adsorption capacity. In contrast, the mildly exothermic and spontaneous adsorption on OP (ΔH° ≈−11.1 kJ mol–1, negative ΔS°, and ΔG° ≈−1.1 kJ mol–1) is consistent with the slight decrease in removal at higher temperatures, suggesting that OP is better suited to treating ambient or cooler wastewaters, such as laboratory effluents or small textile discharges, without additional heating. A positive ΔH° and ΔS° but marginally nonspontaneous ΔG° at ambient temperatures for NA becomes spontaneous at ≥45 °C and is more sensitive and therefore less attractive as a primary adsorbent. The small magnitude of ΔG° falls within the near equilibrium where adsorption occurs with lower affinity. Therefore, NA’s lower removal efficiency (65%) compared to OP and MF and the observed improvement in NA performance at elevated temperatures confirm the entropy-driven, endothermic nature of the process. Proposed Adsorption Mechanism

A conceptual schematic illustration (Figure ) summarizes how the dominant adsorption pathways for crystal violet (CV) change as orange peel is converted to char. It shows the four primary interaction mechanisms whose relative contributions shift markedly with the adsorbent composition.

Proposed adsorption mechanism and pyrolysis shift of OP and its chars.

In orange peel powder (OP), the low point of zero charge (pHPZC = 3.15) means that at pH 7, the surface is strongly negatively charged; therefore, electrostatic attraction between the surface and the cationic dye is the main driving force. This is further reinforced by hydrogen bonding as the FTIR spectrum of OP shows abundant hydroxyl and carbonyl groups that can interact with the CV molecule.

After pyrolysis at 600 °C (MF and NA), most functional groups are removed and the pHPZC shifts to values close to neutrality (7.5 and 7.2); thus, electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding become less important. The fixed-carbon content and the presence of aromatic CC bands increase markedly, indicating the development of graphitic domains. On these char surfaces, adsorption will be dominated by π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of CV and the aromatic carbon framework, with NA further modifying electron donor–acceptor interactions. van der Waals forces and physisorption provide a background contribution in all cases. The moderately exothermic enthalpy for OP (ΔH° ≈−11 kJ mol–1) suggests mixed physical and chemical adsorption, while the strongly endothermic enthalpies for MF and NA (ΔH° ≈+66 kJ mol–1) are consistent with mainly chemisorptive binding on aromatic domains. Overall, pyrolysis shifts from electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding in OP to π–π-dominated adsorption in MF and NA, which is in line with the pseudo-second-order kinetics seen for all three materials.

Future Implications

The distinct behavior of orange peel powder and its chars in this investigation indicates unique yet useful functions for practical pollutant removal. Orange peel powder exhibited the highest crystal violet adsorption capacity, indicating its application as a sacrificial, primary adsorbent for cationic effluents from small textile facilities or laboratories. In contrast, muffle-furnace and nitrogen-assisted chars demonstrated reduced adsorption capacities but had improved tolerance to pH and temperature fluctuations, supporting the idea that the char’s physicochemical stability is ideal for prolonged filtration and column applications in variable wastewater environments. Their endothermic, temperature-favored absorption indicates that char-based beds may be incorporated into warm or heat recovery stages of treatment to utilize waste heat, whereas the exothermic absorption for OP is more appropriate for room-temperature batch or contact-tank systems. Future studies will focus on fixed-bed and continuous flow experiments that combine a high-capacity OP with downstream char columns, assessing performance, deterioration, cost analysis, and applicability to actual effluents. A comprehensive study comparing adsorption properties with anionic dyes and multipollutant systems for char-based adsorption will also be beneficial. It is anticipated that by carefully utilizing the advantages of both raw orange peel powder and its chars in structured treatment systems, contaminated wastewater can be effectively controlled with a single, locally sourced biomass, promoting circular economic goals while offering cheap and practical options for independent and small-scale operations.
