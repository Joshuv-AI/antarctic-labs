Biomass-based thermally tunable dual afterglow with room temperature daylight visibility
Authors: Haozhou Sun, Luyao Wang, Sitong Guo, Min Wang, Yingxiang Zhai, Weiming Yin, Shouxin Liu, Jian Li, Zhijun Chen, Shujun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomass-derived long-persistent afterglow materials that simultaneously deliver thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) and room-temperature phosphorescence (RTP) are highly sought after but remain challenging to achieve, particularly when daylight-visible RTP and high-temperature-tolerant, color-tunable TADF are required. Herein, we develop a thermally tunable RTP-TADF dual-mode afterglow material featuring thermochromic green-to-blue emission by using boric acid (BA) to covalently crosslink natural ellagic acid (EA) and cellulose (Cell) in situ during a facile air-drying process. The resulting EA@Cell, with B-O-C-rigidified structural network, exhibits long-persistent green RTP that remains visible to the naked eye for up to 8 s under daylight conditions upon ultraviolet excitation. Notably, EA@Cell exhibits thermally enhanced TADF emission across 293–413 K, with a 127-fold increase in afterglow lifetime, and maintains its structural integrity and emissive robustness even at temperatures as high as 453 K. As a practical demonstration of EA@Cell, the solution of EA and BA is formulated into daylight-visible RTP inks and thermochromic afterglow inks, enabling low-cost cellulose-based anti-counterfeiting, high-temperature thermal sensing, and dynamic information encryption.Subject terms: Sustainability, Polymers, Bioinspired materials

Introduction

In the recent decade, organic afterglow materials with long lifetimes and dynamic stimuli responsiveness, including multi-channel light responses1–3, stimulus-triggered emission changes (e.g., humidity and temperature)4–7, and color tunability8–12, have attracted widespread interest because of their promising applications in optoelectronic technologies, anti-counterfeiting, and information security13–18. Afterglow emission is primarily governed by two luminescent mechanisms: room-temperature phosphorescence (RTP) and thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF), in which emissive processes arise from intersystem crossing (ISC) and reverse intersystem crossing (RISC), respectively, between the singlet (S1) and triplet (T1) excited states19–21, resulting in long-lived luminescence decay. Nevertheless, conventional afterglow materials are limited to a single emissive mode, where RTP or TADF exhibits a characteristic temperature-dependent quenching or activation response22–25. When both emissive pathways are simultaneously accessible within the same material, their thermally driven synergistic modulation enables thermochromic afterglow (TCAG), manifesting as pronounced color evolution with temperature. The advent of afterglow materials that unite RTP-TADF dual-mode emission has created valuable opportunities for high-level dynamic information encryption that relies on temperature modulation26,27.

Commonly, to realize efficient RTP-TADF dual-mode emission, several key principles should be considered: one is to generate abundant T1 excitons by employing metal-organic hybrids and/or introducing heteroatoms to promote ISC, which is essential for achieving long-lived RTP28–30; another strategy is to secure a fast RISC rate from T1 to S1 by reducing the singlet-triplet energy gap (ΔEST) via enhanced conjugation and donor–acceptor (D-A) structures, thereby enabling efficient TADF31,32; and the third is to stabilize excited T1 states by suppressing non-radiative migration and decay pathways, which can be achieved by rigidifying the chromophore microenvironment through molecular confinement strategies33–35. Building on these principles, numerous RTP-TADF dual-mode and dual-color afterglow materials have been developed, including metal-organic hybrids, organic molecules, carbon dots, and diverse polymer composites36–43. Nevertheless, RTP-TADF dual-mode afterglow materials encounter a critical challenge: T1 excitons are highly susceptible to thermal stimulation and readily undergo rapid deactivation44,45. This limited thermal stability severely restricts their ability to maintain long-lived TADF at elevated temperatures or to exhibit thermally enhanced TADF emission, ultimately compromising the operational robustness of RTP-TADF materials with dynamic color outputs. Moreover, the short lifetimes of most organic phosphors limit RTP visibility to dark environments, substantially limiting their practicality for convenient, rapid anti-counterfeiting applications. Beyond these photophysical constraints, the chromophores used in most thermally tunable dual-afterglow materials are still petrochemical in origin, and their fabrication typically involves complex synthetic routes, expensive precursors, and hazardous solvents, which significantly hinder large-scale and environmentally friendly production44,46–48.

Motivated by these challenges, we developed a thermochromic RTP-TADF dual-mode afterglow material by integrating natural phenolic ellagic acid (EA), boric acid (BA), and cellulose (Cell) via a facile air-drying strategy, denoted as EA@Cell (Fig. 1a). EA is a naturally derived polyphenolic molecule featuring carbonyl-containing dilactone moieties conjugated with fused aromatic rings. It is hypothesized that the presence of these lactone rings not only increases molecular rigidity and planarity but also restricts intramolecular motion, thereby helping to suppress non-radiative decay from the T1 and S1 states and promoting efficient afterglow emission. Complementarily, BA was strategically incorporated for its ability to readily establish boric ester linkages with hydroxyl-rich species, including EA and Cell, thereby rigidifying the local microenvironment, suppressing non-radiative decay, and promoting RTP-TADF afterglow behavior. The resulting EA@Cell maintains structural robustness across 293–453 K, while delivering long-lived daylight-visible RTP (Fig. 1b) and thermally enhanced TADF over a notably wide temperature window of 293–413 K (Fig. 1c). The photoluminescence characteristics of EA@Cell and the underlying mechanisms were comprehensively elucidated. As a proof of concept, EA@Cell was further demonstrated for anti-counterfeiting and thermal sensing applications.

Results

Characterization and thermochromic emission properties of EA@Cell

EA@Cell was facilely prepared via an efficient one-pot reaction, in which EA and BA were integrated with cellulose nanofibrils (CNF) during air drying (Fig. 1a). Scanning electron microscope imaging and elemental mapping revealed that EA@Cell featured a dense and well-defined laminar structure with a uniform distribution of B atoms throughout the matrix (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, the molecular-level interactions in EA@Cell were elucidated using 11B NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 2). Pristine BA featured characteristic B-O resonances at 17.8 ppm. In contrast, these signals in EA@Cell exhibited a pronounced blue shift to 16.4 ppm, indicating the formation of B-O-C complexes49–52. Moreover, the appearance of tetravalent B-O-C resonances at 1.4 ppm in the ¹¹B NMR spectra of EA@Cell, EA/BA, and BA/Cell provided convincing evidence for the formation of covalent boric ester bonds between BA and the hydroxyl groups of Cell as well as the phenolic moieties of EA molecules49–52. To further elucidate the chemical crosslinking characteristics in EA@Cell, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. 3). Deconvolution of the O 1 s core-level spectra revealed that, upon integration of BA, the relative band area percentage of the C-OH content in Cell decreased by 17.4%. In contrast, the C-OH content in EA@Cell remained nearly identical to that in BA/Cell. These results indicate that EA primarily formed B-O-C linkages with BA, enabling BA to serve as a molecular bridge between Cell and EA. Integrating these spectroscopic findings with the morphological evidence, we conclude that the resulting covalent “bridges” consolidate the network architecture, reinforcing the overall structural integrity of EA@Cell.

To elucidate the emissive characteristics of EA@Cell, its photophysical properties were investigated. The excitation-phosphorescence mapping spectrum identified 390 nm as the optimal excitation wavelength (Supplementary Fig. 4). Upon 390 nm excitation at room temperature (i.e., 293 K), EA@Cell exhibited prompt fluorescence centered at 450 nm with a lifetime of 6.55 ns, along with delayed phosphorescence emission centered at 525 nm with a lifetime of 1303.7 ms (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 5). Impressively, the bright green RTP of EA@Cell remained visible to the naked eye for up to 8 s under daylight after the UV excitation was switched off (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Video 1). Motivated by this prominent daylight-visible afterglow, the luminescence efficiency of EA@Cell was further quantified by photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) measurements, affording a total PLQY of 16.06%, including fluorescence and phosphorescence contributions of 5.76% and 10.3%, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Most notably, EA@Cell exhibited pronounced thermally regulated dual-emission response: with increasing temperature, the previously absent fluorescence contribution in its afterglow spectrum gradually became activated and intensified in both intensity (Supplementary Fig. 6) and lifetime (Fig. 2c). The emission decay profiles of EA@Cell at different temperatures are presented in Supplementary Fig. 7, and the corresponding fitting results are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. In contrast, the phosphorescence emission at 525 nm simultaneously weakened (Fig. 2d), revealing a temperature-driven rechanneling of excitons from the T₁ state back to the S₁ manifold via an activated RISC process. At 413 K, the 450 nm delayed fluorescence reached its peak intensity with a maximum lifetime of 152.8 ms (Supplementary Video 2), representing a 127-fold increase relative to 293 K, whereas the 525 nm phosphorescence continued to diminish (Supplementary Fig. 6). Ultimately, at 453 K, the afterglow spectrum evolved into an exclusively TADF-dominated emission (Fig. 2a). Meanwhile, the afterglow color underwent a pronounced thermochromic transition, with 393 K marking the critical threshold at which the emission shifted sharply from bright green to intense blue (Fig. 1c), a change further delineated by the corresponding evolution of the CIE chromaticity coordinates (Fig. 2b). Time-resolved photoluminescence spectra corroborated this well-defined dual-afterglow behavior, resolving two distinct afterglow peaks at 413 K that were assigned to TADF and phosphorescence, respectively, and confirming long-lived afterglow emission with lifetimes up to 656 ms (Fig. 2e). In addition, the thermally modulated dual-color afterglow emission remained robust over repeated heat-cool-heat cycles (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 8), which is consistent with the high thermal stability of EA@Cell, as confirmed by a weight loss of only 5% at 453 K (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results validated that temperature-regulated RTP-TADF dual-mode and dual-color luminescence was realized in EA@Cell, and that thermally enhanced TADF emission was achieved. The thermally enhanced TADF lifetime observed in EA@Cell can be attributed to two factors. First, the RISC rate constant (kRISC) increases significantly with temperature, from 3.7×10−3 s−1 at 293 K to 2.21 s−1 at 413 K (Supplementary Table 1), allowing the RISC process to compete more effectively with the non-radiative decay of S1 and T1. Similar temperature-enhanced TADF lifetimes have also been reported for synthetic aromatic molecules confined in matrices such as carbon dots and polymers34,53. Second, upon chemical crosslinking with BA, the non-radiative decay rate constant of T1 (knrT) decreases significantly from 42.3 s−1 in EA/Cell (prepared by air-drying an EA/CNF dispersion) to 0.68 s−1 in EA@Cell, while the radiative decay rate constant of T1 (krT) decreases from 0.311 s−1 to 0.079 s−1. These results indicate that the boric ester linkages in EA@Cell effectively stabilize T1 excitons, thereby maintaining a sustained T1 reservoir for the continuous repopulation of S1 via RISC, rather than allowing T1 excitons to undergo non-radiative deactivation or a direct radiative transition to the ground state. These combined effects facilitate the RISC process from T1 to S1, ultimately leading to the thermally enhanced TADF lifetime observed between 293 and 413 K.

Temperature-modulated dual-mode and dual-color emissive mechanism of EA@Cell

To elucidate the emission mechanism of EA@Cell, the photophysical properties of EA and BA were comprehensively investigated to establish their individual emissive characteristics and provide a basis for comparison with the integrated material. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, EA exhibited a dominant UV-Vis absorption band in the 320-400 nm region, with a molar absorption coefficient of 1.52 × 104 M−1 cm−1 at 390 nm (Supplementary Table 3). Upon excitation at 390 nm, EA exhibited phosphorescence-dominated afterglow emission at 77 K as well as 293 K, with the RTP emission band centered at 545 nm. In terms of BA, a dominant blue afterglow emission band centered at 415-420 nm was observed across 293-453 K (Supplementary Fig. 11). Nevertheless, the emission signatures of EA and BA are fundamentally different from that of EA@Cell, which is characterized by a thermally tunable dual-mode RTP-TADF afterglow. These results indicate that the luminescence behavior of EA@Cell cannot be rationalized by the individual emissions of EA and BA, but instead points to the formation of a new emissive species within the integrated EA/BA/Cell crosslinked matrix.

To determine the emissive chromophore in EA@Cell and assign the afterglow bands shown in Fig. 2a, a series of control samples, including EA-doped BA materials (EA/BA), EA-doped CNF materials (EA/Cell), and BA-doped CNF materials (BA/Cell), were further characterized by variable-temperature emission spectroscopy and phosphorescence excitation spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 12). The emission decay profiles of EA/BA, EA/Cell, and BA/Cell at different temperatures are presented in Supplementary Figs. 13–15. Strikingly, EA/BA reproduced the RTP emission profile of EA@Cell, featuring a phosphorescence band centered at 525 nm (Fig. 3a) with a lifetime of 675.3 ms and, more importantly, a nearly identical phosphorescence excitation spectrum, with a maximum at 395 nm, compared with 390 nm for EA@Cell (Supplementary Fig. 12). Moreover, although the EA/Cell control sample exhibited a comparable phosphorescence excitation spectrum, with a maximum at 375 nm, its RTP lifetime was drastically shortened to 23.5 ms. In contrast, BA/Cell exhibited an RTP band centered at 520 nm, but its excitation spectrum was centered at 310 nm, markedly different from those of the EA-integrated materials. These results provide compelling evidence that the 525 nm phosphorescence band of EA@Cell originates from an EA/BA emissive chromophore with a molar absorption coefficient of 1.57×104 M−1 cm−1 at 390 nm (Supplementary Table 3), while the corresponding photoexcitation is predominantly mediated by the EA moieties. Compared with the 450 nm TADF emission of EA@Cell and the 415-420 nm emission band of BA, the EA/BA chromophore exhibited thermally tunable dual-mode RTP-TADF afterglow, with a TADF band centered at 465 nm (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 12) and a maximum lifetime of 23 ms at 393 K (Supplementary Fig. 13). In sharp contrast, BA/Cell showed only a modest redshift of the TADF band to 435 nm relative to BA, whereas EA/Cell exhibited essentially no temperature-responsive TADF emission. These results pinpoint that BA underlies the temperature-enhanced TADF behavior, whereas EA/BA serves as the emissive chromophore governing the RTP-TADF dual delayed-emission characteristics of EA@Cell. The BA used in this work, with a stated purity of 99.5%, was further examined by liquid 11B NMR spectroscopy, which revealed only a single resonance at 19.4 ppm assignable to boric acid (Supplementary Fig. 16)54. To exclude the possible influence of BA impurities (unknown) on the afterglow emission of EA@Cell, BA was purified by recrystallization according to a literature-reported procedure55. The variable-temperature afterglow spectra of purified BA (Supplementary Fig. 17) are similar to those of raw BA (Supplementary Fig. 11). More importantly, EA@Cell prepared using the purified BA exhibited emission spectra identical to those prepared from the unpurified BA (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). In addition, BA samples with different purities (Aladdin: 99%, 99.5%, 99.99%, and 99.998%) were used as controls for the fabrication of EA@Cell. All resulting samples exhibited essentially identical dual RTP-TADF emission profiles, with RTP centered at 525 nm at 293 K and TADF centered at 450 nm at 453 K (Supplementary Fig. 18c, d). These results further support that the EA/BA chromophore is responsible for the characteristic afterglow behavior of EA@Cell, rather than impurities in BA.

Previous 11B NMR spectra revealed characteristic signals of covalent B-O-C linkages, including tetravalent B-O-C linkages formed between BA and EA (Supplementary Fig. 2). The covalent integration of the EA/BA chromophores and the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the B-O-C linkages are anticipated to narrow the excited-state energy levels (i.e., ΔEST value) and enable more effective ISC and RISC processes56,57. To probe this hypothesis, the ΔEST values of EA/BA, EA/Cell, BA/Cell, and EA@Cell were compared. Notably, ΔEST decreased from 0.52 eV for EA/Cell to 0.45 eV for BA/Cell upon boric ester linkage formation (Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Table 4), and further to 0.34 eV for EA@Cell (Fig. 3b) as successive boric ester linkages formed with the phenolic moieties of EA molecules. As expected, the EA/BA chromophore exhibited a ΔEST value of 0.38 eV, close to that of EA@Cell, and in agreement with the established criterion that a ΔEST below 0.4 eV is favorable for promoting the RISC process required for TADF emission58. In addition, the experimentally supported formation of diverse B-O-C linkages in the EA/BA chromophore promoted further confirmation of its chemical structure, for which mono-, di-, tri-, or tetravalent linkage modes between EA and BA were hypothesized. To examine the proposed linkage structures and the associated excited-state electronic features, density functional theory calculations were performed. It was found that the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) constant, ξ (S1, T1), generally increased with the degree of boric ester linkage in EA/BA, and reaches the highest value of 0.7307 cm−1 for the tetravalent-linked structure, compared with 0.0074, 0.0017, 0.0147 cm−1 for other configurations (Supplementary Fig. 20). This result indicates that the tetravalent boric ester-linked structure is the most favorable EA/BA configuration for enhancing SOC, further facilitating the ISC and RISC processes to achieve effective RTP-TADF dual-afterglow behavior. Meanwhile, SOC ξ (T1, S0) decreases overall with increasing degree of boric ester linkage, and the tetravalent-linked EA/BA exhibits a relatively low value of 0.0296 cm−1, compared with 0.065, 0.063, 0.0155 cm−1 for the other configurations (Supplementary Table 5). This result indicates that, in the tetravalent-linked structure, T1 excitons preferentially undergo up-conversion to the S1 state rather than direct radiative decay to the ground state, which is crucial for sustaining the RISC channel and ultimately contributes to the effective TADF behavior of EA@Cell.

Variable-temperature Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) measurements were conducted to confirm the determinative role of B-O-C linkages in governing the temperature-responsive emission of EA@Cell. As shown in Fig. 3c, the 1058 cm⁻¹ band, assigned to the B-O-C linkage, exhibited an overall temperature-stable profile below 373 K, consistent with the phosphorescence-dominated emission behavior. Upon heating above 373 K, however, this band underwent a subtle redshift with a noticeable decrease in integrated area, indicating the possible involvement of thermally enhanced molecular vibrations and/or microscopic chemical change within the local B-O-C environment. Interestingly, this thermally driven spectral transition aligns with the thermochromic threshold, coinciding with the green-to-blue afterglow switch. To further examine the evolution of the B-O-C linkages, heating-cooling cycling experiments were conducted, followed by FTIR analysis (Fig. 3d). Notably, the shift of the band at 1058 cm⁻¹ was reversible within a single heating-cooling cycle, indicating that the decrease in the integrated area of the B-O-C band mainly originated from a thermally induced perturbation of the B-O-C vibrational environment. In contrast, an accumulated band shift at 293 K was observed after repeated heating-cooling cycles, indicating the involvement of a microscopic dynamic chemical equilibrium accompanied by progressive network reorganization, potentially associated with the repeated dissociation/reformation and local rearrangement of B-O-C linkages. Nevertheless, EA@Cell maintained robust thermal stability throughout this process (Supplementary Fig. 9), and its afterglow emission profile remained essentially unchanged after repeated thermal cycling (Supplementary Fig. 8). Meanwhile, the variable-temperature X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed that increasing temperature reduced the crystalline domains of the cellulose matrix (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 6). These results indicate that, on one hand, the thermally stable B-O-C linkages in EA@Cell preserve the rigidified microstructure required for long-lived phosphorescence, with a lifetime still reaching 524.1 ms at 373 K. On the other hand, the more relaxed B-O-C bonding arrangement at elevated temperatures renders the EA/BA chromophore more susceptible to undergo RISC to the S1 state, resulting in efficient TADF emission59–61.

Having established the dynamic function of the B-O-C linkage in the RTP-TADF dual-mode emission of EA@Cell, the intermolecular interactions among EA, Cell, and BA were further investigated. Notably, integration of natural EA into BA/Cell appreciably enhances the overall afterglow performance, delivering substantially improved thermal tolerance and strengthened, prolonged RTP and TADF in EA@Cell relative to BA/Cell (Fig. 3f). To gain deeper mechanistic insight into this enhancement, the underlying electronic structure was further examined at the molecular orbital level by analyzing the energy levels and orbital distributions of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) (Supplementary Fig. 21). Comparison with the BA/Cell model further highlights the essential role of EA in defining the electronic structure of EA@Cell. In BA/Cell, the frontier molecular orbitals are mainly localized around the boric ester linkage and Cell moiety, with a large HOMO-LUMO gap of 6.90 eV, indicating that BA/Cell itself does not form an effective low-energy emissive chromophore. In contrast, after integration with EA, both the HOMO and LUMO become predominantly localized on the EA moieties, with slight extension onto the boric ester linkage and negligible contribution from Cell. Meanwhile, the calculated HOMO-LUMO gap is markedly reduced to 4.17 eV. These results demonstrate that EA is pivotal in reconstructing the frontier electronic structure and facilitating low-energy excitation, thus establishing the crosslinked EA/BA as the emissive chromophore of EA@Cell, whereas BA serves as a structural bridge that modulates the electronic structure. The role of Cell is to rigidify the chemically crosslinked EA/BA chromophore within a dense, entangled cellulose fibril network, thereby suppressing non-radiative decay from the T1 and S1 states (Supplementary Table 1) and mitigating quenching induced by oxygen and humidity. Therefore, the PLQY, RTP-TADF intensity, and lifetime were all significantly enhanced after integration of the EA/BA chromophore into the Cell matrix (Supplementary Fig. 22). As a result, EA@Cell exhibited an expanded temperature regime for thermally enhanced TADF lifetime compared with EA/BA, with the upper temperature limit increasing from 393 K to 413 K.

Overall, the mechanism underlying the long-lived, daylight-visible RTP and the thermally enhanced TADF can be explained as follows: BA covalently crosslinks EA and Cell through B-O-C linkages, constructing a rigid and tightly confined molecular environment in EA@Cell that facilitates the ISC process of EA/BA chromophores, stabilizes T1 excitons, and enables long-lived phosphorescence under daylight with high quantum yield. Meanwhile, BA underpines the temperature-enhanced TADF behavior of EA@Cell, while EA further reconstructs the frontier electronic structure and establishes a lower-energy emissive center, promoting efficient RISC and enabling TADF emission. The synergistically integrated EA@Cell affords an RTP lifetime that not only exceeds those of previously reported simply-prepared biomass-based RTP materials utilizing natural and modified natural chromophores, but is also comparable to those achieved with synthetic chromophores (Supplementary Fig. 23). Moreover, EA@Cell maintains TADF emission up to 453 K, surpassing the thermal stability limits of most TADF materials (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Table 7). More importantly, this design strategy for EA@Cell is applicable to various natural phenolics (e.g., tannic acid (TA) and syringic acid (SA)) (Supplementary Fig. 24). Nevertheless, EA@Cell exhibited more favorable afterglow performance than TA@Cell and SA@Cell, with longer RTP and TADF lifetimes; for comparison, TA@Cell exhibited an RTP lifetime of 896.2 ms and a TADF lifetime of 29.1 ms at 413 K. These results support the generality of the design strategy for biomass-based afterglow materials with thermally tunable dual afterglow, and further highlight the structural advantages of EA, especially its carbonyl-containing dilactone structure conjugated with fused aromatic rings.

Potential applications of EA@Cell

Inspired by the pronounced TCAG properties of EA@Cell, we further utilized the EA/BA aqueous solution as a functional ink to inscribe a “TADF” pattern onto Cell-based paper, establishing a paper-based printing methodology endowed with TCAG signatures (Fig. 4a). The TCAG response was evaluated by positioning the printed paper on a temperature-regulated platform and recording the afterglow behavior at different temperatures immediately after cessation of UV excitation. At a relatively low temperature of 333 K, the printed “TADF” pattern emitted a vivid green afterglow persisting for several seconds. Upon elevating the temperature from 333 to 453 K, the afterglow lifetime progressively shortened and the emission steadily shifted from green to cyan and ultimately blue, evidencing a pronounced thermochromic evolution (Fig. 4b). Notably, when the TCAG paper was placed on a high-temperature surface (Fig. 4c), the spatial heat distribution could be directly mapped through afterglow imaging, where hotter zones manifested as blue emission and cooler areas preserved green luminescence, consistent with infrared thermal sensing. This temperature-visualizable platform offers an innovative approach for real-time thermal mapping and presents promising opportunities for mitigating localized thermal failure in sophisticated electronic and photonic devices.

3D printing is widely recognized as one of the fastest and most cost-effective manufacturing methods. Demand for 3D-printed light-emitting structures is growing across a wide range of applications, including high-level information encryption and energy storage62,63. In this work, EA@Cell composites can be conveniently integrated with functional polymer matrices (e.g., polylactic acid (PLA)) using 3D printing techniques to fabricate daylight-visible afterglow constructs with well-controlled architectures (Fig. 4d). Taking advantage of the fact that EA and BA are water-soluble, the EA/BA ink demonstrates the feasibility of fabricating diverse anti-counterfeiting patterns on filter paper using inkjet printing (Fig. 4e). These patterns, invisible in daylight, exhibited long-lived and daylight-visible RTP emission after switching off the 365 nm UV light. The phosphorescent patterns arise from BA-mediated in situ rigidification of EA/BA chromophores on cellulose. The afterglow emission from paper treated with the EA/BA solution is also visible in daylight (Fig. 4e). Significantly, the humidity-responsive phosphorescence of EA@Cell (Supplementary Fig. 25) enables the creation of rewritable phosphorescent paper, in which water serves as the ink for advanced information encryption. As shown in Fig. 4e, the word “CNF” appeared on the phosphorescent paper because water quenched the luminescent emission. The encrypted information can be erased by simply removing the water through thermal drying, allowing the phosphorescent paper to be reused. In addition, the ultra-long and high-intensity afterglow emission of phosphorescent paper can significantly enhance the readability of text content that is not visible in the dark (Fig. 4e). Therefore, the aforementioned design strategy for EA@Cell proves effective for developing sustainable anti-counterfeiting applications on Cell-based materials and further enables a low-cost, rewritable medium for thermal sensing, temperature mapping, and dynamic information display.

From an application perspective, the photostability and biodegradability of the biomass-based EA@Cell were further evaluated. Notably, even after storage under ambient laboratory conditions for one year, EA@Cell largely preserved its dual-mode RTP-TADF and dual-color emission characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 26). In particular, the RTP and TADF lifetimes measured at 413 K showed only slight decreases, from 1303.7 ms to 1265.2 ms and from 152.9 ms to 132.2 ms, respectively, demonstrating robust photostability of EA@Cell. The biodegradability of EA@Cell was further evaluated under simulated environmental conditions. In sharp contrast to conventional poly(vinyl chloride), the EA@Cell film underwent pronounced degradation and was nearly completely decomposed within one month (Supplementary Fig. 27), highlighting the favorable biodegradable and end-of-life sustainability of EA@Cell for potential practical applications.

Discussion

In this work, we develop EA@Cell, a temperature-regulated dual-afterglow material featuring daylight-visible RTP and thermally enhanced TADF emission. EA@Cell is achieved through the straightforward integration of BA and natural phenolic EA into the Cell matrix via air drying. The success of the fabrication relies on the formation of covalent B-O-C boric ester linkages between BA and the phenolic moieties of EA molecules within EA@Cell, which establish a rigid local network and enable bright green phosphorescence visible to the naked eye for up to 8 s under daylight conditions. Consistent with this structural role, molecular-level investigations reveal that these covalent B-O-C linkages also narrow the ΔEST of the EA/BA chromophores, promote the RISC process to rechannel T₁ excitons into the S₁ manifold, and effectively suppress non-radiative decay, resulting in thermally enhanced TADF with intense blue emission. Natural EA substantially improves overall afterglow performance, affording enhanced thermal tolerance and longer-lived RTP and TADF. The TADF emission at 450 nm exhibits a long lifetime of up to 153 ms at 413 K, with a temperature tolerance reaching 453 K, outperforming most organic TADF materials. This biomass-driven, thermally tunable dual-afterglow material offers opportunities for secure information protection and high-temperature thermal sensing.

Methods

Materials

CNF (oxidized by 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl radical) was purchased from NanoFC (Zhongshan, China). Boric acid (99%, 99.5%, 99.99% and 99.998%), ellagic acid (96%), tannic acid (95%), syringic acid (99%), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (98%) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). PLA pellets (Ingeo Biopolymer 4032D) were supplied by NatureWorks (Ravago, Belgium). DI water was produced using a Smart-RO ultrapure water system (Hitech Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Preparation of EA@Cell

10 g of CNF dispersion (1 wt% solid content), 2 mg of EA (2 wt% of CNF), and 15 mg of BA (15 wt% of CNF) were added to 10 mL DI water. The pH was adjusted to 11 using 0.1 M NaOH, and the mixture was dispersed under intense stirring for 5 min. The uniform EA@Cell dispersion was poured into a 5 × 5 cm plastic mold and allowed to air dry. The dried sample was peeled off and stored in a sealed bag before characterizations, and its water content was determined to be 2.9% by the gravimetric method after oven drying at 105 °C. The EA/Cell and other control samples were prepared under the same experimental conditions as EA@Cell (Supplementary Table 8).

Preparation of EA/BA inks and RTP-TADF papers

To prepare EA/BA ink, 2 mg of EA and 15 mg of BA were dissolved in 10 mL DI water by adjusting the pH to 11 using 0.1 M NaOH solution. For inkjet printing, the ink was loaded into the cartridges on a commercial inkjet printer (HP Deskjet 2332). To fabricate RTP-TADF anti-counterfeiting paper, filter paper was immersed in EA/BA ink for 10 s. After that, the treated filter paper was removed and dried in a blast-drying oven at 60 °C for 1 min.

Preparation of 3D RTP materials using FDM 3D printing

The EA@Cell sample was ground into powder, and 5 g of the powder was mixed with 495 g of PLA pellets to obtain an EA@Cell/PLA mixture. The mixture was then extruded and pelletized at 180 °C to produce EA@Cell/PLA pellets. Subsequently, the pellets were processed using a single-screw extruder (SMJX25) at 175 °C to produce filaments with a diameter of 1.75 mm. Finally, 3D constructs were fabricated using a DDKUN FDM 3D printer (6610D) under computer-controlled programs.
