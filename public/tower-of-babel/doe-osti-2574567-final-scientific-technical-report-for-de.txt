Final Scientific/Technical Report Summary
Award Number: DE-SC0025089
Recipient: RookStack LLC
Principal Investigator: Mark Ladd
Project Title: Development of an Artificial Intelligence Solution for Residential Heating and
Cooling Equipment Sizing to Reduce Equipment Oversizing
Project Period: July 22, 2024 – July 21, 2025

Purpose of the Research
This project aimed to develop and validate an AI solution to predict heating and cooling design
loads for residential buildings. The goal was to offer a more accurate and scalable alternative to
the Manual J method, which often results in oversized HVAC systems.

Research Approach
RookStack developed a predictive model trained on over 6,800 homes. To validate the
RookStack model, Purdue University independently estimated heating and cooling loads for 75
homes using three different methods:

 • Thermostat-Based Method: using smart thermostat runtime data and a thermal circuit
 model.
 • Utility Bill Method: using historical fuel usage and outdoor temperatures.
 • Manual J Load Calculations: conducted either in-person or remotely by professionals

These estimates were compared to the RookStack predictions to assess accuracy and
performance.

Findings
 • The RookStack model most often produced heating load predictions between the lower-
 bound Thermostat and Utility methods and the upper-bound Manual J estimates.
 • Thermostat and Utility Bill methods were consistent but likely underestimate true
 design loads due to not accounting for design wind conditions or internal heat gains.
 • Manual J load calculations frequently overestimated loads and were subject to
 variability based on provider approach.
 • The RookStack method showed a mean difference of 13 kBTU/hr from the Thermostat-
 based method, significantly less than the 32 kBTU/hr mean difference found for Manual
 J load calculations.

Potential Applications
The RookStack method presents a scalable, low-burden alternative for residential HVAC load
sizing. It can reduce system oversizing, lower installation costs and reduce stress on the
electrical grid.

1. Abstract
 This project developed and validated data-driven methods to estimate residential heating
 and cooling loads, aiming to reduce oversizing of HVAC equipment. RookStack LLC
 (RookStack) developed machine learning-based models to predict home heating and cooling
 loads. Purdue University independently validated the accuracy of RookStack’s predictions on
 75 homes across the United States by comparing them with loads derived from smart
 thermostat runtime data, utility bills, and Manual J calculations. Results show the RookStack
 predictions were consistent relative to the thermostat and utility bills methods with less
 oversizing than the current industry standard Manual J method.

2. Objectives
 • Develop machine learning models to predict residential heating and cooling loads.
 • Collect heating and cooling loads from 60–100 homes using smart thermostats, utility
 bills, and detailed Manual J load calculations.
 • Assess and compare the accuracy of RookStack’s data-driven predictions with industry-
 standard Manual J methods.

3. Technical Approach

3.1 RookStack Load Estimation Model
RookStack developed a grey box artificial intelligence application to predict residential design
heating and cooling loads using publicly available data and a short homeowner survey. The
RookStack models were developed using trade secret methods and a training dataset of over
6,800 homes. RookStack heating and cooling load predictions were made for all 75 homes in
the study.

3.2 Purdue Load Estimation Validation Methods
To validate RookStack’s predictions, Purdue University developed multiple independent
approaches to estimate the ground-truth design loads for participating homes.

3.2.1 Thermostat-Based Load Estimation
Purdue used smart thermostat runtime data from enrolled homes to estimate the actual
thermal load experienced during a heating design day. The developed method uses historical
data from smart thermostats to train the parameters of the building envelope thermal circuit
model. Ecobee smart thermostats keep a record of indoor temperature, humidity, and
equipment runtime every 5 minutes. The measurements of interest were the indoor
temperature, outdoor temperature, and how long the primary heating equipment has been
running in each five-minute measurement window. The equipment heat output was determined
from photos of the equipment nameplate provided by study participants. For homes with
primary heating equipment that provides a constant supply of heat to the house whenever it’s
turned on, the amount of heat supplied to the house in each measurement window can be
directly inferred. A thermal circuit model was then developed and extrapolated to design
heating conditions (or interpolated if the historical record contains design conditions) to derive
the design heating load estimate for the building. Data was available to generate thermostat-
based load estimates for all 75 of the homes in the study.

3.2.2 Utility Bill Method
This method used monthly fuel usage from utility bills to estimate average building thermal
resistance using a simplified steady-state heat balance. Using the heating efficiency from the
equipment nameplate heat input relative to heating fuel usage was determined. Outdoor air
temperature data were matched to billing periods, and effective thermal conductance was
inferred by solving Q = UA(ΔT). These inferred UA values were used to back-calculate the peak
heating load under ASHRAE 99% design day conditions. Utility bill data was provided for all 75
homes in the study however data quality allowed for the method to be used on only 74 of the
75 of the homes in the study.

3.2.3 Manual J Load Calculations
Manual J load calculations were performed by professionals offering this service in the local
market of participant homes. Finding local professionals to perform Manual J load calculations
was a significant challenge. In some locations we were unable to find any professionals offering

Manual J load calculations. In one location the local professional hired to perform Manual J load
calculations provided this service using real estate listing data without ever visiting the homes.
We had not expected this and going forward we sought out service providers that agreed to visit
the homes and perform Manual J load calculations in-person. However, in locations where we
could not find any service providers to do Manual J load calculations in-person, we eventually
used remote providers to expand the dataset. Ultimately, we were able to get in-person
Manual J calculations for 33 homes and another 47 Manual J calculations were performed
remotely. For 9 of the homes, we had both in-person and remote Manual J calculations
performed to allow for comparison between the different methods. In total we were able to
obtain at least one Manual J load calculations on 71 of the 75 homes in the study.

4. Results

4.1 Overview of Data Collection Outcomes
From 303 responses to our outreach for study participants requests, 75 were included in the
final analysis. Homes were excluded from the study most often due to not having the necessary
heating system configuration (1 or 2 stage gas furnace with Ecobee smart thermostat) for the
Thermostat-Based method or for being non-responsive. Participants were incentivized to
participate in the study with electronic gift cards. Participants were sent gift cards ranging from
$10-$50 depending on the complexity of the action requested. Actions that received gift card
incentives included completing a housing characteristics survey, providing thermostat/utility
bill/equipment nameplate data and allowing access to their home for in-person Manual J load
calculations. Funding for electronic gift cards came from a grant from the State of Indiana.

4.3 Cooling Load Estimate Results

Cooling load estimates were not possible using the Thermostat and Bill methods as those
methods are derived from measurements of heat inputs into the home. Thus, the only available

comparison for the RookStack cooling load predictions were the Manual J load calculations. To
assess the performance of the RookStack cooling load predictions they were compared to
Manual J calculations performed remotely, in-person and the 40 homes with the closest
Thermostat Method and Manual J heating loads. The group of 40 homes with the closest
Thermostat Method and Manual J heating loads were selected to evaluate the RookStack
cooling load predictions because they were considered to be the most accurate of the Manual J
cooling loads.

5. Discussion

5.1 Identifying Ground Truth
Purdue concluded that the Thermostat-Based Load Estimation and Utility Bill Method provided
the most reliable reference points due to their basis in real home performance data. However,
these methods also likely underestimate true design loads because they do not consider design
wind speeds and do not subtract internal heat gains from occupants and appliances, which were
likely present during measurement periods. Consequently, while both methods reflect
measured performance, they tend to underpredict peak design loads. The Thermostat-Based
Load Estimation Method was considered to be the most accurate measurement of the heating
load for the home because it was a direct measurement of the heat input into the home. For
this reason, the Thermostat-Based Load Estimation Method was used for identifying homes with

the top performing Manual J load calculations for evaluation of the RookStack cooling load
predictions.

5.2 Performance of Manual J Load Calculations
Manual J results were useful but showed variability and a tendency to overestimate loads.
Figure 4 shows that the mean difference between the Manual J heating loads and the
Thermostat Method was 32 kBTU/hr. For some homes this difference was over 100 kBTU/hr.

 Figure 4: Comparison of Manual J and Thermostat Method Heating Load Estimates

This aligns with to the widespread oversizing seen in other assessments of installed HVAC
equipment sizing. Conservative assumptions and contractor-to-contractor inconsistency may
explain the inflated estimates.

5.3 Performance of the RookStack Method
The RookStack predictions most often fell between the likely underestimated Thermostat and
Utility methods and the likely overestimated Manual J results. Figure 5 shows that the mean
difference between the RookStack heating load predictions and the Thermostat Method was 13
kBTU/hr. For one home this difference was over 50 kBTU/hr. These differences are significantly
less than was found for the Manual J load calculations.

 Figure 5: Comparison of RookStack and Thermostat Method Heating Load Estimates

On balance, the RookStack method seems to provide a reasonable estimate of actual home
loads—offering a data-driven, scalable alternative that can reduce oversizing while maintaining
adequate system capacity.

A paper covering the Purdue University team findings during this project is in the process of
being written by Alex Lee and Professor Kevin Kircher of Purdue University. Their target for
submission of this paper for publication is Fall of 2025.

6. Conclusions
The project successfully met its objectives. RookStack developed machine learning models to
predict heating and cooling loads. Purdue collected heating and cooling loads from 75 homes to
assess the accuracy of the RookStack prediction method. The RookStack prediction method
demonstrated feasibility and accuracy when validated against measured loads and the current
standard practice Manual J method. This work shows that with modest user input HVAC load
sizing can be streamlined without sacrificing accuracy. These findings support the development
of a scalable software application to help homeowners, contractors and electric utilities avoid
oversized systems, lower costs and reduce strain on the electric grid.
