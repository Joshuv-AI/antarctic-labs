Phosphorus digestibility of copra meal for broiler chickens fed different levels of non-phytate phosphorus in the pre-experimental period☆
Authors: V.S. Haetinger, O. Adeola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early-life nutritional interventions in broilers may modulate subsequent digestive capacity, however, the impact of phosphorus (P) nutrition during the starter period on basal endogenous losses (BEL) of P and the P digestibility of feedstuff fed in the subsequent phase remains poorly defined. This study evaluated the influence of pre-experimental non-phytate P (nPP) concentrations on the BEL of P and the standardized P digestibility of a potential alternative feedstuff, copra meal (CM), with or without phytase supplementation. The experiment employed a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement in a split-plot design. During the pre-experimental phase (d 0 to 18 post hatching), birds were assigned to two starter diets containing either deficient (3.0 g/kg) or adequate (4.5 g/kg) nPP levels (24 replicates; 8 birds/cage). In the subsequent three days, three grower diets were administered to 8 replicates: two diets utilizing CM as the sole P source (supplemented with or without 1,000 FYT/kg of phytase) with 6 birds/cage, and one P-free diet that served to correct for BEL of P with 10 birds/cage. Statistical analysis was performed using a randomized complete block in a split-plot design with starter diets as main plots and grower diets as subplots, and the P-free diet was analyzed separately. No significant interactions were found for growth performance or digestibility data. Broilers conditioned on the deficient-nPP starter diet exhibited greater (P < 0.05) standardized P digestibility of CM (80%) compared to the adequate-nPP starter (77%), phytase inclusion enhanced (P < 0.05) these values to 88% and 83%, respectively. The BEL of P and Ca were estimated at 116 and 204 mg/kg of dry matter intake, respectively, and were unaffected by the starter diet nPP concentration. In conclusion, while starter nPP levels did not modulate the BEL of P or Ca, they significantly influenced P digestibility. These findings suggest that pre-experimental P nutrition should be standardized when evaluating the digestibility of feed ingredients. Furthermore, copra meal had high P digestibility that was optimized by phytase supplementation.

Introduction

Nutritional evaluations of potential alternative feed ingredients to corn and soybean meal are critical for balancing feed costs, growth performance, profitability, and environmental sustainability of chicken meat production (Matton et al., 2025). While the details of phosphorus (P) evaluation studies with broiler chickens are still being developed and evaluated (Mutucumarana et al., 2015; Dilelis et al., 2021; Mutucumarana and Ravindran, 2021; Haetinger and Adeola, 2025), implementing digestible P matrix in poultry formulations can offer significant improvements in precision nutrition such as reducing the reliance on inorganic phosphates, mitigating bone abnormalities due to P deficiency or Ca:P imbalances, e.g. tibial dyschondroplasia and bone deformities, and reducing nutrient excretion (Adedokun and Adeola, 2013; WPSA, 2013; Rodehutscord et al., 2017).

One factor that can influence P digestibility values is the level of non-phytate P (nPP) in the pre-experimental diet (Rodehutscord et al., 2017; Haetinger et al., 2024). Broiler chickens have shown a capacity to enhance their digestion and absorption of P when fed diets with nPP concentration below recommended values, which may influence the results of P digestibility studies. The mechanism of this physiological adaptation is reportedly via enhanced endogenous phytase activity (Zeller et al., 2015; Sommerfeld et al., 2018, 2019), in combination with upregulation of active transport of P (Yan et al., 2005; Huber et al., 2006, 2015). These adaptations resulted in higher apparent P digestibility in birds fed a deficient-nPP pre-experimental diet compared with birds fed adequate levels of nPP (Rousseau et al., 2016; Haetinger et al., 2024). However, these studies only found an effect of pre-experimental diet nPP level on the apparent ileal P digestibility in the subsequent period, with no effect on the regression-derived P digestibility of soybean meal (Haetinger et al., 2024), which provides some correction for specific and basal endogenous losses.

Since feeding a diet with deficient levels of nPP during the pre-experimental period can alter intestinal P digestion and absorption, we hypothesized that the level of nPP in the pre-experimental diet could also affect the amount of P recovered at the end of the ileum when birds are fed a P-free diet i.e. basal endogenous losses (BEL) of P, which would impact the standardized digestibility of P of ingredients. However, the impact of pre-experimental diet formulation on endogenous losses of P, and standardized digestibility of P for broiler chickens has not yet been investigated.

Copra meal (CM) is a byproduct of coconut oil production and is readily available at a lower cost than corn and soybean meal in regions such as Indonesia, the Philippines, and certain areas in Central and South America where coconut production is present at a large scale, thus its inclusion in broiler diets could be a viable alternative to reduce feed costs and mitigate wastage of this byproduct (Sundu et al., 2009). The ME of CM for broiler chickens has been reported to vary from 1,658 to 3,727 kcal/kg (Sundu et al., 2009; Haetinger et al., 2021; Rostagno et al., 2024), this variation can be attributed to the different methodologies used in the studies as well as differences in the source of coconuts and the processing methods used to yield CM. However, data about CM P content and digestibility is scant. Reports by Stein et al. (2015) and Rostagno et al. (2024) indicate that CM contains about 6 g/kg of total P, and between 2.2 to 3 g/kg of P not bound to phytic acid. While there are no reports of P digestibility of CM for broilers, Rostagno et al. (2024) reported 35% digestibility of P for pigs, and Stein et al. (2015) found that the standardized total tract P digestibility of CM for pigs was 70.6%, and supplementation of phytase enhanced this value to 90.3%. Indicating that CM may be able to contribute a significant amount of digestible P, which can be enhanced by dietary supplementation of phytase. However, investigation of the digestibility of P in CM with or without the supplementation of exogenous phytase in poultry has not been reported.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to determine the apparent and standardized ileal P digestibility of copra meal with or without exogenous phytase supplementation for broiler chickens, and identify whether the pre-experimental diet non-phytate P concentration affects these values and basal endogenous losses of P.

Materials and methods

The Purdue University Animal Care and Use Committee approved all protocols used in the study. Part of the results of this study were presented at the 2026 International Poultry Scientific Forum, Atlanta, GA (Haetinger and Adeola, 2026).

Experimental design

The experiment consisted of a split-plot design with two starter diets as main plots and three grower diets as subplots. Treatments were organized in a factorial arrangement of 2 × 3 with 2 pre-experimental starter diets formulated to contain 3.0 g/kg nPP (deficient) or 4.5 g/kg nPP (adequate) fed for 18 d, and 3 experimental diets fed for the subsequent 3 d, consisting of a P-free diet (PFD), a basal diet with CM as the only source of P, and basal diet with 1,000 FYT/kg of phytase Novozymes Hiphorius GT 10 (Table 1; Supplementary Table 1). The adequate nPP level in the starter diet was based on the nPP recommendations of the NRC (1994) and the deficient nPP level was based on previous studies investigating nPP depletions (Rousseau et al., 2016; Omotoso et al., 2023; Haetinger et al., 2024). The diets, except the PFD, were formulated to maintain the Ca: total P ratio at 1.5 in all diets by varying the limestone concentration, which was coarsely ground. The grower diets contained chromic oxide (Cr2O3) as an indigestible index marker at 5 g/kg in the form of a 20% premix with cornstarch. Bird and digesta sampling

A total of 384 Cobb 500 (Cobb-Vantress Inc., Siloam Springs, AR) male broiler chicks were individually tagged with identification numbers, weighed and allotted on d 0 post hatching to one of 2 corn-soybean meal-based starter diets with 3.0 or 4.5 g/kg nPP, each starter treatment had 24 replicate cages of 8 birds per cage, in a randomized complete block design with initial BW as the blocking factor. Birds were raised in electrically heated battery brooders (model SB 4 T, Alternative Design Manufacturing, Siloam Springs, AR), and the temperature was controlled to decrease 1°C every 2 d from 35°C on d 0 post hatching. Light was provided 23 h per day, and birds had unlimited access to feed and water throughout the study. All diets were fed in mash form.

On d 18, feed and all birds were weighed, and 176 birds were selected from each starter to be distributed into the 3 experimental grower diets, with 8 replications. To ensure an adequate amount of ileal digesta samples, the PFD treatments had 10 birds per cage whereas the other treatments had 6. Birds were selected for the experimental period such that the ratio of the average body weight of the selected birds in each starter matched the ratio of the average body weight of all birds on d 18.

The grower diets were then fed for the subsequent 3 d; excreta samples were collected on the last 2 d by lining collection pans with waxed paper. On d 21 post hatching all 352 birds were weighed and euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and dissected to excise the ileum, defined as the section of the distal small intestine that starts at the Meckel’s diverticulum and ends at the ileocecal junction. Distilled water was used to flush the ileal digesta out of the distal two-thirds of the ileum. The ileal digesta samples collected were pooled by cage and immediately stored at −20°C.

Laboratory analyses

After being freeze-dried, samples of ileal digesta and excreta were processed through a coffee grinder to pass a 0.5-mm screen. The ash content was established by heating samples at 600°C over 6 h in a muffle furnace (AOAC, 2006, method 942.05).

Chromium concentrations in the grower diets, ileal digesta, and excreta were analyzed by wet-ash digestion using nitric and perchloric acids following the protocol by Fenton and Fenton (1979) and measuring concentrations with a spectrophotometer (Spark 10 M; Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). This same digested material was used to determine P concentrations via spectrophotometry at an absorbance of 630 nm. Finally, Ca levels were measured using flame atomic absorption spectrometry (Varian Spectr. AA 220FS; Varian Australia Pty Ltd., Victoria, Australia) after diluting the samples in a solution of deionized water, HCl, and 0.5% LaCl3⬤7H2O.

Additionally, ileal digesta, experimental diets and copra meal were analyzed for nitrogen using the combustion method (TruMac N; LECO Corp., St. Joseph, MI; method 990.03; AOAC, 2006), and diets and copra meal were analyzed for phytic acid by using the ferric precipitation colorimetric method described by Thompson and Erdman (1982). Grower diets were analyzed for phytase activity by the Magnetic Immuno‑Chromatographic Test (Willard et al., 2022) by Novonesis® Durham, NC, USA.

Calculations and statistical analysis

The apparent total tract utilization (ATTU) and apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of P, Ca, N, and ash in the diets were calculated using the index method as described by (Kong Adeola, 2014).AIDorATTU(%)=100−[(Cri×PoCro×Pi)×100]

For ileal digestibility calculations, Cri stands for the Cr concentration in the diets and Cro is the concentration of Cr in ileal digesta; Pi is the P, Ca, N, or ash concentration in the diet, and Po is the P, Ca, N, or ash concentration in ileal digesta. For total tract digestibility calculations, the nutrient concentration in excreta was used instead of ileal digesta.

The basal endogenous losses and total tract excretion of P or Ca were calculated using the analyzed Cr, P, and Ca concentrations in the ileal digesta and excreta of birds fed the P-free diets as follows:BELortotaltractexcretion=(CriCro)×Po

Subsequently the BEL values were used to calculate the standardized ileal digestibility (SID), and the total tract excretion values were used to calculate the standardized total tract utilization (STTU) of P and Ca using the following equation:SIDP(%)=AIDP+(100*BELPi)

In addition, P and Ca intake were calculated as the product of the DM concentration, feed intake, and P or Ca concentration in the diet, and the digestible P or Ca intake were calculated by multiplying the intake by the AID of the respective mineral.

The GLM procedure of SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used to conduct the statistical analysis, after first confirming data and residual normal distribution and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. The results were treated as a randomized complete block design for analysis, with the grower period data analyzed as a split-plot experiment as described by (Haetinger et al., 2024).

For this analysis, the main effect of starter diet nPP level was tested using the interaction between starter diet and block as the error term, and the main effect of the grower diet was assessed with the interaction between grower diet and block as error term. The interaction between starter and grower diets was evaluated using the three-way interaction of starter, grower, and block as the error term. In case of no significant two-way interaction, the main effects were considered. The PFD treatments were analyzed separately for the effect of pre-experimental starter diet as a randomized complete block design. Significance was set at P < 0.05 and a tendency was considered when 0.1 > P > 0.05.

Discussion

Although there were no significant interactions on digestibility metrics, there were significant effects of the pre-experimental starter diet nPP level on AID and SID of P and Ca, as well as on ATTU and STTU of P, which were all higher in birds fed the deficient nPP starter diet. However, there were no effects of starter diet on the BEL of P or Ca. These results indicate that the production and reabsorption of endogenous secretions of P and Ca are not affected by the level of nPP and Ca in the pre-experimental diet, but the digestion and absorption of dietary P and Ca are enhanced when birds are fed reduced nPP and Ca in the pre-experimental diet. The lack of effect of the starter diet nPP concentration on the BEL of P and Ca suggests a functional divergence between homeostatic regulation and basal physiological maintenance. Previous research indicates that when broiler chickens are fed P-deficient diets, endogenous phytase activity is enhanced (Zeller et al., 2015; Sommerfeld et al., 2018, 2019) and expression of Type IIb sodium-phosphate cotransporter (NaPi-IIb) is upregulated in the brush border membrane to maximize dietary P absorption (Yan et al., 2005; Huber et al., 2015). However, basal endogenous P losses are primarily composed of pancreatic and biliary secretions, mucin, and desquamated intestinal epithelial cells, which have variable degrees of breakdown and reabsorption in the gut (Mutucumarana and Ravindran, 2021). Because these components are largely a function of dry matter intake and intestinal cell turnover rather than a response to mineral status, they remain relatively constant when expressed per kg of dry matter intake (DMI). This implies that while the bird’s efficiency in absorbing dietary P can be modulated by P nutrition, the obligatory loss of P associated with metabolic maintenance is not.

The standardized ileal digestibility of P was 5% higher and that of Ca was 21.5% higher in birds that were fed the reduced nPP pre-experimental starter diet, and the total tract utilization of P followed the same trend, which can be attributed to intestinal adaptations via hormonal regulation with parathyroid hormone and calcitriol to enhance intestinal endogenous phytase activity (Zeller et al., 2015; Sommerfeld et al., 2018, 2019) and absorption of Ca and phosphate (Yan et al., 2005; Huber et al., 2015; Novotny et al., 2023). These adaptations influenced the P and Ca digestibility values when using the standardized digestibility method, whereas a low nPP pre-experimental diet was found to not affect the digestibility values when using the regression method to determine P digestibility in soybean meal (Haetinger et al., 2024). Although the standardized method is less laborious and requires less resources than the regression method, the standardized method uses only one inclusion level of the test ingredient whereas the regression method relies on at least three inclusion levels (WPSA, 2013). The different protocols employed in each of these methods can cause variable digestibility results for the same feed ingredient when comparing the two, as shown by Dilelis et al. (2021), reporting that the ileal digestibility of P in fish meal was 45.5% using the SID, and 25.5% using the regression method. Similarly, Anwar et al. (2018) compared the regression, direct and difference method to estimate the Ca digestibility of dicalcium phosphate and reported significant differences, ranging from 13 to 34% of Ca digestibility. Thus, as the poultry industry transitions to digestible P and Ca matrices, standardization of methodology is paramount to obtain comparable digestibility values of feedstuffs to assemble feed composition tables in the future.

In birds that received the adequate nPP diet during the starter period, the AID of P in CM was 76% in the diet without phytase and 81.1% when phytase was supplemented, the SID of P in CM was estimated at 77.4%, and 82.5% when phytase was supplemented in the diet. Although the calculated phytase supplementation was 1,000 FYT/kg, the analyzed phytase activity was 2,140 FYT/kg. These digestibility values are relatively high compared with the ranges of values of SID of P previously reported in other feed ingredients such as canola meal from 32% to 58.3% (Babatunde and Adeola, 2021; Rostagno et al., 2024; Venter et al., 2024), corn from 41% to 52.2% (Babatunde et al., 2020; Rostagno et al., 2024), and soybean meal from 45% to 65.4% (Babatunde et al., 2020; Rostagno et al., 2024; Venter et al., 2024). In contrast, the SID of P of corn and soybean meal reported by Lee and Kong (2024) were 73.1% and 88.4%, respectively. Babatunde et al. (2020) also reported that the SID of P in corn was 69.9% and in soybean meal was 82.8% when fed in a diet supplemented with 1,000 FYT/kg of phytase, putting the value for soybean meal close to the one found for CM in our study. The studies by Babatunde et al. (2020) and Venter et al. (2024) evaluated the SID of P of feed ingredients with and without 1,000 FYT/kg and found that the phytase supplementation enhanced digestibility of P by a minimum of 27% in different ingredients such as canola meal, corn and soybean meal, whereas in our study we observed only an 8.4% increase in SID of P of CM with phytase supplementation. The proportion of phytic acid P, the substrate of phytase, represents 65%, 75% and 63% of the total P in canola meal, corn and soybean meal, respectively (NRC, 2012; Rostagno et al., 2024), which are much higher than the 23% in CM, thus partly explaining the difference in phytase efficacy.

Previous studies reported that phytic acid P in CM was 4.1 g/kg representing 65% of the total P (Rostagno et al., 2024). Contrastingly, phytic acid P in the CM used in our study was only 1.2 g/kg, which is 23% of the total P. This difference illustrates the divergence in the composition of CM that could be due to the different regions where it is produced, since different cultivars, crop years, soil characteristics and irrigation can influence phytate P composition in crops (Cossa et al., 2000). In addition, differences in the production process including oil extraction, endosperm separation, and the cooking process may affect the phytate portion thus affecting P digestibility (Sundu et al., 2009; Haetinger and Adeola, 2023). The variability in composition is one of the factors that limit the practical inclusion rate of CM in broiler chicken diets. In addition, CM can have high fat content, high concentrations of soluble and insoluble fiber, and elevated water holding capacity (Sundu et al., 2009; Stein et al., 2015). But previous studies indicated that physical treatment of CM, such as pelleting and crumbling, in combination with exogenous enzyme supplementation can enhance the nutritional value of CM and may allow dietary inclusions of up to 30% without compromising growth (Sundu et al., 2009; Diarra and Anand, 2020). Our results are comparatively consistent with these reports as we found that supplementing phytase enhanced the feeding value of CM by increasing the digestibility of P.

The respective ileal endogenous losses of birds fed the nPP-deficient or -adequate pre-experimental diet were 110 or 121 mg P/kg DMI, and 202 or 206 mg Ca/kg DMI, which did not differ between the two pre-experimental starter groups. Babatunde and Adeola (2021) reported that the BEL of P were 197 mg/kg DMI in 13-d-old broiler chickens, which were significantly higher than in 21-d-old broiler chickens (158 mg/kg DMI). The latter is relatively close to the values found in our study in 21-d-old broiler chickens (average of 116 mg/kg DMI). The value found in our study is also similar to those reported by (Dilelis et al., 2021) of 129 mg/kg DMI, and (Anwar and Ravindran, 2020) of 133 mg/kg DMI. However, the BEL of P and Ca found in this study are different than values that we reported in a recent study of 224 mg P/kg DMI, and 99 mg Ca/kg DMI in 22-d-old birds fed similar diet formulations (Haetinger and Adeola, 2025), the reasons for these divergences are unknown.

There was a marked discrepancy between the large, 520% increase in total tract Ca excretion (1,272 mg/kg DMI) relative to ileal Ca BEL (204 mg/kg DMI), that contrasted with only 120% increase for total tract P excretion (262 mg/kg DMI) and ileal P BEL(116 mg/kg DMI), indicating that when birds are fed diets devoid of P and Ca, urinary Ca flux is disproportionately enhanced. This is consistent with previous reports of P and Ca excretion (Haetinger and Adeola, 2025), and may be driven by mobilization of hydroxyapatite from bone reserves to maintain circulating ionized Ca levels, and because Ca constitutes a significantly larger portion of bone tissue than P, resorption releases a substantially higher amount of Ca into circulation thus excess Ca is excreted via the urine (Proszkowiec-Weglarz and Angel, 2013). However, the precise endocrine signaling governing this differential renal clearance under concurrent deficiency remains to be tested.

In conclusion, the apparent and standardized ileal digestibility of P in copra meal for broiler chickens were affected by pre-experimental starter diet nPP concentration and by supplementation of phytase independently. However, basal endogenous losses of P and Ca were not affected by the pre-experimental diet nPP concentration. Therefore, these results suggest that starter diet mineral levels must be accounted for when evaluating subsequent digestibility values. For birds fed nPP concentration that met their requirements during the starter phase, the SID of P in copra meal was 77.4%, and 82.5% when phytase was supplemented. Future research expanding the database of P and Ca digestibility values to other alternative feedstuffs is essential to optimize mineral nutrition and broaden the range of ingredients with established precise nutrient digestibility data.
