Mechanistic study of FA-PG3 nanoparticles promoting adipose browning through the PPARγ and PRDM16-UCP1 pathway in severe pediatric obesity
Authors: Xiaoli Zhang, Yuan Tian, Shengqun Xu, Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood-derived bioactive compounds offer an attractive and sustainable approach to metabolic disease prevention, yet mechanistic evidence supporting their efficacy in pediatric obesity remains limited. This study investigates the thermogenic potential of ferulic acid (FA), a plant-derived phenolic compound, when delivered via a dendrimer-based nanocarrier. Using an integrative strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, cellular assays, animal models, and clinical biomarker analysis, we demonstrate that FA-loaded generation 3 PAMAM nanoparticles (FA-PG3) effectively activate adipose tissue thermogenesis. Computational analyses identified UCP1 as a central molecular target of FA, with PPARγ and PRDM16 acting as critical upstream regulators. FA-PG3 nanoparticles exhibited optimized nanoscale properties and sustained FA release. In vitro, FA-PG3 promoted beige adipocyte differentiation, enhanced mitochondrial respiration, and increased thermogenic gene expression. In vivo, FA-PG3 improved energy expenditure, insulin sensitivity, and adipose browning in obese mice, while UCP1 silencing largely abolished these metabolic benefits. Notably, children with severe obesity exhibited significantly lower circulating levels of browning-associated adipokines, supporting the clinical relevance of targeting thermogenic pathways. Our findings highlight a previously underappreciated food-derived, nano-enabled strategy for metabolic reprogramming and position FA-PG3 as a promising translational candidate for addressing pediatric obesity through adipose tissue thermogenesis. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cell biology

Introduction

With changes in lifestyle and excessive energy intake, childhood obesity has become increasingly prevalent. The number of children with severe obesity, commonly defined as BMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile for age and sex or BMI ≥35 kg/m², has risen markedly, posing a major threat to pediatric health1–3. They commonly present with insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and disrupted glucose and lipid metabolism, which markedly elevates their risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases in adulthood4. Current clinical interventions primarily include dietary regulation, exercise prescriptions, and behavioral therapies; however, pharmacological treatment in children is limited by concerns regarding both efficacy and safety, and often suffers from poor compliance5. Therefore, developing novel, mechanism-driven, safe, and effective therapies has become a key priority in managing severe pediatric obesity6. In recent years, enhancing energy expenditure and promoting metabolic remodeling of adipose tissue have emerged as promising avenues for obesity treatment7. Among these, inducing the “browning” of adipocytes to boost thermogenic and metabolic activity has shown solid biological rationale and promising clinical potential8.

Adipocytes are generally classified into white adipocytes, which mainly store energy, and brown/beige adipocytes, which are responsible for heat production. Browning is the conversion of white adipocytes into metabolically active beige adipocytes through mitochondrial UCP1 expression and activation9. UCP1 promotes non-shivering thermogenesis and energy expenditure by modulating mitochondrial proton flux and is regarded as a hallmark molecule of adipocyte browning10. Numerous studies have demonstrated that upregulation of UCP1 not only enhances energy metabolism in adipose tissue but also improves insulin sensitivity and reduces inflammatory status11,12. Transcription factors such as PPARγ and PRDM16 play essential roles in stimulating UCP1 expression and represent critical targets for promoting adipose metabolic remodeling13,14. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies targeting UCP1 and its upstream regulatory networks have become a promising breakthrough in current anti-obesity research.

Ferulic acid (FA) is a natural plant-derived phenolic compound known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory properties, and has attracted increasing attention in recent years for the treatment of metabolic syndrome15. Evidence suggests that FA can stimulate the PPARγ pathway, influencing adipocyte differentiation and metabolism, and has been proposed as a candidate to induce adipocyte browning16,17. However, its poor water solubility and low in vivo stability have limited its sustained pharmacological activity18. Poly(amidoamine) dendrimers (PAMAM), a class of cationic nanocarriers, possess well-defined structures, high surface modifiability, and strong drug-loading capacity, which significantly enhance the stability and targeting efficiency of natural small molecules19. Recent studies have shown that generation 3 PAMAM (PG3) preferentially accumulates in visceral adipose tissue, enhances systemic energy expenditure, and ameliorates obesity-associated metabolic dysfunction20. In addition, PG3 have been reported to alleviate obesity-associated chronic inflammation and ectopic lipid deposition, thereby improving metabolic homeostasis21. Therefore, developing ferulic acid-loaded generation 3 poly(amidoamine) dendrimer (FA-PG3) for targeted regulation of lipid metabolism holds significant research and translational potential.

This study aimed to construct FA-PG3 nanoparticles and to systematically evaluate their biological effects on activating the PPARγ/PRDM16 pathway, upregulating UCP1 expression, promoting adipocyte browning, and enhancing energy expenditure. By elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms of FA-PG3 in lipid metabolism regulation, this study provides a theoretical basis for precise interventions in obesity, particularly severe obesity in children. This strategy integrates bioactive natural compounds with a nanomaterial-based delivery platform, offering a promising balance of safety, targeting specificity, and improved bioavailability—characteristics crucial for translational application. Specifically, in pediatric populations, the development of a low-toxicity, efficient, and controllable intervention is of great significance for improving obesity-related metabolic disorders and halting disease progression. This study not only expands the theoretical foundation for adipose metabolism regulation but also offers scientific evidence for the development of novel nanomedicine systems based on natural compounds, potentially contributing to new therapeutic strategies for pediatric metabolic diseases.

Results

Network pharmacology screening identified FA as a promoter of UCP1 activation via the PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling pathway

To identify natural compounds that may promote adipocyte browning and increase energy expenditure, we applied an integrated strategy combining network pharmacology and transcriptomic sequencing, as shown in the screening workflow (Fig. 1A). First, active compounds from Angelica sinensis were retrieved from the TCMSP database. Based on the criteria of OB > 50% and DL > 0.05, the 13 compounds with the highest pharmacological scores were retained. Among these, FA was identified as a core candidate due to its previously reported effects on improving obesity-related metabolic disorders22–24 (Table 1).

Next, beige adipocytes were treated with FA, and transcriptomic sequencing was performed on both the FA-treated and control groups. Differential expression analysis identified 180 DEGs in the FA-treated group, including 45 upregulated and 135 downregulated genes (Fig. 1B). A heatmap based on these DEGs revealed distinct clustering patterns between the control and FA groups, indicating a clear separation in gene expression profiles (Fig. S1A).

We searched the GeneCards database using the keyword “UCP1 activation” and identified a total of 4964 UCP1-related genes. By intersecting these genes with the DEGs, 40 potential UCP1-associated target genes were identified (Fig. 1C). A PPI network was then generated using the STRING database, and hub targets were ranked according to degree centrality (Fig. S1B). Among them, PPARγ showed the highest interaction score, while PPARγ, PRDM16, and UCP1 were positioned at the core of the network, indicating possible direct or indirect regulatory links among these proteins (Fig. 1D).

To verify the binding potential of FA with UCP1 and its regulatory factors, molecular docking was performed using AutoDockTools 1.5.7 and Vina 1.2.7. The 2D and 3D structures of FA were obtained from the PubChem database (Fig. S1C, D), and the crystal structure of UCP1 was retrieved from the PDB database (Fig. S1E). Water molecules were removed and protein structures optimized using PyMOL 2.5.5. Hydrogen atoms were added and partial charges calculated using AutoDockTools 1.5.7. The docking grid was defined, and the docking center was set within the active site of UCP1.

Molecular docking showed that FA bound strongly to UCP1, with a binding free energy of −5.5 kcal/mol, mainly through hydrogen bonds with ARG83 and SER146 (Fig. S1F). FA also exhibited favorable binding to PPARγ (−6.7 kcal/mol) by forming stable hydrophobic interactions within its ligand-binding domain. In addition, FA bound to PRDM16 with a free energy of −5.9 kcal/mol, suggesting that it may enhance UCP1 expression and brown fat activation through the PPARγ/PRDM16 pathway (Fig. S1G, H).

To explore the potential regulatory effects of FA on UCP1-associated genes, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the 40 overlapping genes. GO analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched in BP related to adipocyte differentiation and thermogenesis (Fig. 1E). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the candidate targets were mainly involved in the PPAR signaling pathway and thermogenesis-related pathways (Fig. 1F).

In summary, this study identified FA as a candidate compound through network pharmacology. Functional enrichment and molecular docking analyses further validated that FA may promote UCP1 activation via the PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling pathway, thereby facilitating adipocyte browning and enhancing energy expenditure.

FA promotes UCP1 expression and enhances adipocyte browning by activating the PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling axis

To investigate the effect of ferulic acid (FA) on UCP1 expression in adipocytes, in vitro experiments were conducted using a beige adipocyte model derived from 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (Fig. 2A). We first verified the differentiation of beige adipocytes using Oil Red O staining. As shown in Fig. 2A, compared to undifferentiated 3T3-L1 preadipocytes, the induced cells exhibited a progressive accumulation of multilocular lipid droplets surrounding the nucleus, with an eccentric nuclear localization, indicating maturation toward a beige adipocyte phenotype (Fig. 2B). At the cellular level, three groups were established: Control, FA, and FA + GW9662. RT-qPCR showed that UCP1, PPARγ, and PRDM16 expression were significantly increased in the FA group compared with the Control group. When cells were co-treated with the PPARγ antagonist GW9662, the FA-induced upregulation of UCP1 was partially attenuated, indicating that FA may regulate UCP1 through the PPARγ/PRDM16 pathway (Fig. 2C). This finding was further confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 2D, E).

To assess the effect of FA on adipocyte browning, lipid accumulation was evaluated using Oil Red O staining. Compared with the Control group, FA-treated adipocytes exhibited reduced Oil Red O staining intensity, indicating enhanced lipid breakdown. However, this effect was attenuated following GW9662 treatment (Fig. 2F). Mitochondrial function analysis showed that OCR and ATP levels were significantly elevated in the FA group compared with the Control, whereas both were markedly reduced following GW9662 treatment (Fig. 2G, H). These results further support the role of FA in promoting adipocyte browning and enhancing energy expenditure.

FA promotes UCP1 expression and enhances adipocyte browning in mouse model of obesity via the PPARγ/PRDM16 pathway

Subsequently, an HFD-induced mouse model of obesity was established, upon which FA intervention experiments were conducted. The overall experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 3A. The experimental groups included a normal diet control group (NC), an HFD model group, an HFD + FA treatment group, and an HFD + FA + PPARγ antagonist group (HFD + FA + GW9662). Compared with the NC group, mice fed the HFD showed typical obese phenotypes, including significantly increased WAT mass and fat index, whereas BAT weight remained unchanged, confirming successful model establishment (Figure. S2A–C). After treatment with FA and/or GW9662, H&E staining showed that FA increased BAT volume and weight relative to the HFD group, suggesting enhanced adipose browning and beige adipocyte formation. In contrast, these effects were markedly attenuated in the HFD + FA + GW9662 group compared with the HFD + FA group (Fig. 3B–D).

WB analysis demonstrated that protein levels of UCP1, PPARγ, and PRDM16 were significantly decreased in the HFD group compared with the NC group. FA treatment markedly increased their expression, whereas co-treatment with GW9662 significantly attenuated these effects relative to the FA group, indicating that FA promotes adipocyte browning through activation of browning-related markers (Fig. 3E–H). With respect to energy metabolism, metabolic cage analysis showed that the HFD group had a significantly lower basal metabolic rate than the NC group. FA treatment markedly increased VO2 VCO2 and heat production compared with the HFD group, suggesting enhanced energy expenditure, whereas this effect was significantly weakened by GW9662 co-treatment (Fig. 3I–K). In the GTT, FA-treated mice displayed significantly improved glucose clearance and enhanced insulin sensitivity, while GW9662 treatment markedly diminished these positive metabolic regulatory effects (Fig. 3L). Similarly, ITT results indicated impaired insulin responsiveness in the HFD group, as reflected by a slower decline in blood glucose. FA treatment produced the greatest glucose reduction, whereas the FA + GW9662 group showed a response similar to that of the HFD group, indicating that GW9662 partially reversed the insulin-sensitizing effect of FA (Fig. 3M). Compared with the NC group, the HFD group showed markedly worsened metabolic parameters. FA treatment significantly improved glucose, insulin, cholesterol, triglyceride, and free fatty acid levels relative to the HFD group, whereas co-treatment with GW9662 partially reversed these improvements (Fig. S2D–H). In addition, body weight was significantly higher in HFD-fed mice than in NC mice, while FA treatment led to a gradual reduction in body weight; this effect was partly attenuated in the HFD + FA + GW9662 group (Fig. S2I).

These findings collectively suggest that FA intervention not only promotes adipose tissue browning at the histomorphological level but also significantly improves systemic metabolic status. GW9662 treatment, while inhibiting PPARγ activity, simultaneously abolished FA-induced UCP1 upregulation and the associated metabolic improvements, indicating that the effects of FA may be mediated through the PPARγ signaling pathway.

Successful fabrication of FA-PG3 nanoparticles and validation of their stability and sustained release properties

In this study, a functional nanoparticle platform capable of efficiently delivering FA was developed based on the highly modifiable and nanoscale architecture of PG3. FA-PG3 were successfully prepared, and their physicochemical characteristics, stability, and in vitro release behavior were systematically evaluated to investigate their potential mechanism of action in adipocytes (Fig. 4A). DLS analysis revealed a significant increase in particle size after FA loading, indicating successful incorporation of FA into the PG3 framework (Fig. 4B). TEM analysis further confirmed that FA-PG3 nanoparticles displayed a uniform spherical shape with smooth surfaces, consistent with the particle size obtained by DLS (Fig. 4C). Zeta potential analysis showed that FA-PG3 nanoparticles had a near-neutral surface charge. Compared to unloaded PG3, the surface charge was reduced, suggesting that FA loading altered the nanoparticle surface properties (Fig. S3A). Moreover, the PDI significantly decreased upon FA incorporation, indicating improved uniformity and dispersion stability (Fig. S3B).

To further evaluate the physiological stability and release characteristics of FA-PG3 nanoparticles, we assessed their stability in different physiological environments (H2O and culture medium). The results showed no significant changes in particle size or zeta potential under these conditions, and no visible aggregation was observed, indicating that the nanoparticles exhibited excellent colloidal stability under physiological conditions (Fig. 4D, E). Moreover, in vitro drug release assays demonstrated a relatively stable and gradually slowing release profile of FA from the FA-PG3 nanoparticles, suggesting that the PG3 matrix effectively regulated the release of FA and prolonged its bioactivity (Fig. 4F).

To verify whether FA-PG3 nanoparticles possess tissue-targeting capabilities, we administered Cy5-labeled PG3 and FA-PG3 via intraperitoneal injection in HFD-induced mice. Within 24 hours, a strong Cy5 fluorescence signal was observed in the abdominal region (Fig. 4G), with the highest intensity localized in eWAT, followed by moderate signals in rWAT, and only faint signals detected in subcutaneous iWAT and interscapular BAT. Additionally, pronounced fluorescence was observed in the liver and kidneys, whereas only low levels were detected in the lungs, spleen, and heart, indicating that while PG3 and FA-PG3 can enter systemic circulation, they predominantly accumulate in visceral adipose tissue and hepatic and renal tissues. This distribution pattern suggests that, although FA-PG3 exhibits preferential accumulation in visceral adipose tissue, it also deposits in metabolic organs such as the liver and kidneys, which is likely attributable to the physiological clearance pathways of the nanoparticles rather than reflecting absolute adipose tissue-specific targeting (Fig. 4H). Tissue section analysis further confirmed that both PG3 and FA-PG3 signals penetrated uniformly throughout the eWAT, rather than remaining confined to the tissue surface. The PBS negative control group showed no detectable autofluorescence background (Fig. S3C). Dual immunofluorescence staining revealed that the PBS negative control group exhibited no spontaneous fluorescence background, and the Cy5 signal co-localized with the adipocyte marker protein Caveolin-1, confirming the successful internalization of FA-PG3 into adipocytes (Fig. S3D).

To evaluate the in vivo safety of FA-PG3, histopathological examinations were performed on major organs. H&E staining showed normal structural integrity of the liver, kidneys, and heart, with no evidence of necrosis, edema, or inflammatory cell infiltration. It should be noted that this histological assessment was conducted at a single time point and therefore cannot exclude the possibility of long-term or subclinical toxicity (Fig. S3E). To further evaluate in vivo safety, serum samples were analyzed for liver function markers. ALT and AST levels showed no significant differences among the PG3, FA-PG3, and PBS groups (Fig. S3F, G). Taken together, these findings demonstrate the successful fabrication of FA-PG3 nanoparticles, with comprehensive characterization confirming favorable physicochemical properties, stability, and sustained-release behavior. FA was effectively loaded onto the PG3 scaffold, and the release profile contributed to improved FA stability. Furthermore, FA-PG3 nanoparticles exhibited pronounced selective accumulation within visceral adipose depots in vivo.

In vitro experiments confirmed that FA-PG3 nanoparticles promoted adipocyte browning and upregulated brown fat marker gene expression

To evaluate the regulatory effect of FA-PG3 nanoparticles on adipocyte browning, we conducted in vitro experiments using a beige adipocyte model induced from 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (Fig. 5A). The study included four groups: Control, unloaded PG3, free FA, and FA-PG3. Cell viability was first evaluated by the CCK-8 assay. Compared with the control group, neither the PG3 group nor the FA-PG3 group showed a significant change in viability, indicating that the nanoparticles had no obvious cytotoxic effects (Fig. S4A).

Scale bar: 50 μm; C RT-qPCR analysis of mRNA expression levels of UCP1, PPARγ, and PRDM16; D OCR measurements in adipocytes across different treatment groups using the Seahorse XF analyzer; E Quantification of intracellular ATP levels; F RT-qPCR analysis of FGF21, irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 gene expression. All experiments were performed in triplicate. ns indicates no statistically significant difference; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 vs. control.

Oil Red O staining was performed to assess lipid accumulation. Compared to the control group, the FA and PG3 groups exhibited a significant reduction in lipid staining intensity, with an even more pronounced decrease observed in the FA-PG3 group. This suggests that FA-PG3 nanoparticles more effectively suppress lipid accumulation and may enhance lipolysis (Fig. 5B). RT-qPCR was then performed to assess the expression of brown adipocyte marker genes. Compared with the Control and PG3 groups, both FA and FA-PG3 treatments significantly increased Ucp1, Pparγ, and Prdm16 expression, with the strongest effect observed in the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 5C). WB analysis further confirmed that protein levels of UCP1, PRDM16, and PPARγ were markedly elevated in both the FA and FA-PG3 groups compared to the Control and PG3 groups, with the highest expression levels observed in the FA-PG3 group (Fig. S4B, C).

To further evaluate the effects of FA-PG3 on energy metabolism, we assessed mitochondrial function indicators, including OCR and ATP content. Seahorse XF analysis showed that OCR was significantly increased in the FA, PG3, and FA-PG3 groups compared with the control group, with the greatest increase observed in the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 5D). ATP levels were also higher in the FA PG3 and FA-PG3 groups than in controls, and the increase was more pronounced in the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 5E).

In addition, based on literature and the GeneCards database, we selected four adipokines—FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4—as representative factors closely associated with adipocyte browning and metabolic regulation25–28. RT-qPCR analysis showed that FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 mRNA levels were significantly increased in the FA, PG3, and FA-PG3 groups compared with the control group, with the greatest upregulation observed in the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 5F). WB analysis confirmed these trends and was consistent with the RT-qPCR findings (Fig. S4D-E).

In summary, FA-PG3 nanoparticles significantly enhanced UCP1 expression, suppressed lipid accumulation, improved mitochondrial function, and promoted the expression of metabolism-related adipokines in vitro, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to free FA.

FA-PG3 nanoparticles promote adipocyte browning and improve metabolic function in a mouse model of obesity

To evaluate the regulatory effects of FA-PG3 nanoparticles on obesity phenotypes and energy metabolism in vivo, we established an HFD-induced mouse model of obesity. The experimental grouping and administration schedule are shown in Fig. 6A. Mice were divided into four groups: PBS, PG3, FA, and FA-PG3. Compared with the PBS group, all treatment groups showed significantly less body weight gain and lower final body weight. Notably, the FA-PG3 group showed a more pronounced reduction in weight gain than either the FA or PG3 group (Fig. 6B).

Histological analysis revealed that, relative to the PBS group, adipocytes in both subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues were smaller, more densely packed, and contained fewer lipid droplets in the PG3, FA, and FA-PG3 groups, with the FA-PG3 group exhibiting the most marked changes (Fig. 6C). WB analysis showed that UCP1, PPARγ, and PRDM16 were significantly upregulated in adipose tissues from both the FA and FA-PG3 groups, with higher expression in the FA-PG3 group than in the FA group (Fig. S5A–D). In addition, the FA-PG3 group exhibited significantly increased protein levels of browning- and energy metabolism-related adipokines, including FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4, compared with the PBS group, and these levels were higher than those in the FA and PG3 groups (Fig. 6D–H).

Energy metabolism assessment showed that VO2 and VCO2 were significantly elevated in the PG3, FA, and FA-PG3 groups compared with the PBS group. Notably, the FA-PG3 group exhibited the greatest increase in basal energy metabolism, suggesting a more pronounced enhancement of energy expenditure (Fig. 6I–L). GTT and ITT results demonstrated improved glucose clearance and enhanced insulin sensitivity in the PG3, FA, and FA-PG3 groups, with the FA-PG3 group showing the most substantial improvement (Fig. S5C, D). Additionally, levels of glucose, insulin, TC, TG, and free fatty acids were significantly reduced in the PG3, FA, and FA-PG3 groups, indicating an amelioration of obesity-related metabolic disturbances (Fig. S5E-I).

In summary, FA-PG3 nanoparticles effectively suppressed weight gain, promoted adipose tissue browning, enhanced energy expenditure, and improved both glucose and lipid metabolic profiles in a mouse model of obesity.

FA-PG3 regulates UCP1 expression to mediate adipocyte browning and energy metabolism

To further clarify the role of UCP1 in FA-PG3-induced adipocyte browning and enhanced energy metabolism, we established a UCP1-stable knockdown cell line using shRNA in 3T3-L1-derived beige adipocytes (Fig. S6A). The shRNA sequence with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. S6B). Two experimental groups were included for comparison: FA-PG3 + sh-NC and FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 (UCP1 knockdown). Oil Red O staining showed that, relative to the Control + sh-NC group, the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group had markedly reduced lipid accumulation and smaller lipid droplets. In contrast, UCP1 knockdown in the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group resulted in significantly increased lipid accumulation and enlarged lipid droplets compared to the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group, indicating that UCP1 knockdown attenuated the regulatory effect of FA-PG3 on lipid metabolism (Fig. S6C, D).

WB analysis showed that protein levels of FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 were significantly upregulated in the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group relative to the Control + sh-NC group, whereas these levels were markedly reduced in the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group. These results suggest that UCP1 acts as a key downstream effector in FA-PG3-mediated adipocyte browning (Fig. S6E, F). To determine whether FA-PG3 regulates UCP1 expression via the PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling pathway, we conducted further analysis of related pathway molecules. Compared with the Control + sh-NC group, the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group showed significantly elevated protein levels of PPARγ and PRDM16. However, no significant difference in PPARγ or PRDM16 expression was observed between the FA-PG3 + sh-NC and FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 groups, suggesting that UCP1 deficiency may not directly affect the PPARγ/PRDM16 pathway (Fig. S6G-H).

In assessments of mitochondrial function, the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group exhibited significantly increased OCR and ATP content compared with the Control + sh-NC group. In contrast, both OCR and ATP levels were markedly reduced in the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group relative to the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group (Fig. S6I, J), indicating that UCP1 deficiency diminished the enhancing effects of FA-PG3 on mitochondrial energy metabolism.

In summary, FA-PG3 can simultaneously activate the PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling pathway and upregulate UCP1 expression. UCP1 acts as a downstream effector of FA-PG3 and is critical for enhancing adipocyte browning and mitochondrial function, while UCP1 deficiency significantly impairs this process.

FA-PG3 nanoparticles improve metabolic phenotypes in mouse model of obesity in a UCP1-dependent manner

To determine the critical role of UCP1 in mediating the metabolic benefits of FA-PG3, we established an obesity model by feeding mice an HFD for 12 weeks. C57BL/6 wild-type and UCP1 knockdown (sh-UCP1) mice were then subjected to FA-PG3 intervention. The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 7A. After 6 weeks of treatment, the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group showed a much weaker reduction in body weight than the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 7B). Histological analysis revealed that, relative to the Control + sh-NC group, visceral adipose tissue in the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group displayed reduced adipocyte size, tighter cellular arrangement, and fewer lipid droplets. In contrast, the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group showed increased adipocyte size, looser arrangement, and more prominent lipid droplet accumulation compared to the FA-PG3 group, indicating that UCP1 knockdown compromised the structural improvements induced by FA-PG3 in adipose tissue (Fig. 7C).

WB analysis further demonstrated that, compared with the Control + sh-NC group, the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group exhibited significantly increased expression of UCP1, PPARγ, and PRDM16. Compared with the FA-PG3 group, UCP1 expression was markedly reduced in the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group, whereas PPARγ and PRDM16 levels remained unchanged (Fig. S7A–D). Additionally, the expression levels of the metabolism-related adipokines FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 were significantly elevated in the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group relative to the Control + sh-NC group, but substantially decreased in the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group compared to the FA-PG3 group, suggesting that UCP1 serves as a key mediator of the metabolic effects of FA-PG3 (Fig. 7D–H).

Metabolic cage analysis revealed that the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group exhibited elevated basal metabolic levels compared to the Control + sh-NC group. In contrast, relative to the FA-PG3 group, the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group showed significantly reduced VO₂ and VCO₂, indicating a decrease in basal metabolic rate (Fig. 7I, J). GTT and ITT results demonstrated that the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group had improved glucose clearance and enhanced insulin sensitivity compared to the Control + sh-NC group. However, in the UCP1-deficient group, both glucose clearance and insulin sensitivity were impaired relative to the FA-PG3 group (Fig. 7K, L). Further blood biochemical analysis revealed that the FA-PG3 + sh-NC group exhibited significantly lower metabolic indices than the Control + sh-NC group. In contrast, the FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group showed markedly increased levels of glucose, insulin, cholesterol, TG, and free fatty acids compared to the FA-PG3 group (Fig. S7E–I), suggesting a worsening of metabolic dysfunction.

Collectively, these findings indicate that the beneficial effects of FA-PG3 on energy metabolism and adipocyte browning in mouse model of obesity are substantially attenuated in the absence of UCP1, highlighting the central regulatory role of UCP1 in this process.

Adipokines are significantly associated with metabolic dysfunction in children with severe obesity

To further validate the clinical relevance of FA-PG3 in regulating adipocyte browning through adipokines, we conducted a serum factor analysis in a pediatric cohort with severe obesity. A total of 206 children with severe obesity and 70 age-matched children with normal BMI were enrolled. Serum samples were collected to measure the expression levels of the adipokines FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4. In addition, metabolic parameters, including BMI, FPG, TG, TC, and insulin levels, were recorded (Fig. 8A).

Expression changes of adipokines in children with severe obesity and their correlations with metabolic indicators.

A Workflow for clinical sample collection and metabolic parameter analysis in children with severe obesity (n = 206) and healthy controls (n = 70); Serum levels of FGF21 (B), irisin (C), BMP7 (D), and NRG4 (E) measured by ELISA in both groups; Pearson correlation analyses showing the associations between FGF21 (F), irisin (G), BMP7 (H), and NRG4 (I) levels and metabolic indicators including BMI, ALT/AST, and insulin sensitivity. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 vs. control.

Since FA-PG3 treatment markedly increased the expression of FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 in brown adipocytes in both cellular and animal experiments, these secreted factors are likely involved in adipose metabolic regulation and were regarded as indicators of obesity-related metabolic status rather than specific browning markers. Accordingly, their serum levels were further evaluated in a clinical cohort to explore associations with metabolic status and establish a translational link between mechanism and phenotype. ELISA results showed that serum FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 levels were significantly lower in the EO group (children with severe obesity) than in the Healthy group, indicating that these browning-related adipokines are suppressed under obese conditions (Fig. 8B–E).

Analysis of metabolic indicators revealed that children in the severe obesity group exhibited significantly higher levels of BMI, TG, LDL-c, FPG, insulin, HOMA-IR, the ALT/AST ratio, and UA compared to the healthy control group. Conversely, HDL-c levels were markedly lower, reflecting a typical pattern of metabolic dysregulation (Fig. S8A–I). Further Pearson correlation analysis showed that serum levels of FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 were significantly negatively correlated with BMI, ALT/AST ratio, and HOMA-IR index (Fig. 8F–I).

In summary, these results reveal that FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 are significantly downregulated in children with severe obesity and are closely associated with metabolic parameters. When integrated with our previous findings that FA-PG3 induces adipocyte browning and upregulates these secreted factors in cellular and animal models, these data suggest an association between browning-related adipokines and metabolic abnormalities in severe pediatric obesity. While providing population-level clues for understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms of obesity-related metabolic disorders, these findings cannot be used to directly infer the clinical efficacy of FA-PG3 or to establish these factors as biomarkers of its therapeutic response.

Discussion

This study focused on developing a metabolic regulatory strategy for severe pediatric obesity by designing and constructing ferulic acid-loaded PAMAM nanoparticles (FA-PG3) and systematically investigating the molecular mechanisms by which they regulate adipocyte browning and improve energy metabolism. The results showed that FA-PG3 treatment simultaneously activated PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling and upregulated UCP1 expression. UCP1, located downstream of PPARγ/PRDM16, acts as a key effector molecule in promoting adipocyte browning and mitochondrial function, significantly enhancing mitochondrial function and thermogenic capacity in adipocytes, and ameliorating obesity-related metabolic abnormalities such as weight gain and insulin resistance in animal models. Furthermore, the specificity of this mechanism was validated using a UCP1 knockdown model. At the population level, the levels of multiple adipokines were markedly reduced in children with severe obesity and were significantly correlated with metabolic indicators, further reinforcing the potential translational value of FA-PG3 in clinical applications. This study provides a novel metabolic intervention strategy combining a natural molecule with a functional nanocarrier, offering strong innovation and practical significance.

Previous studies have widely established UCP1 as a key protein in adipocyte browning and non-shivering thermogenesis; however, most approaches to inducing UCP1 expression have relied on cold exposure or hormonal interventions, which are impractical for long-term use in children29,30. In contrast, this study employed naturally derived FA as an inducer, offering greater safety and acceptability. The construction of a nanoparticle-based delivery system further enhanced its efficacy, enabling more precise and controllable activation of UCP1. Notably, the study not only observed UCP1 upregulation and a browning phenotype but also confirmed their dependence on UCP1 expression. The significantly attenuated effects of FA-PG3 in UCP1 knockdown mice validated the specificity and reliability of the underlying mechanism. Compared with conventional observational studies, this work takes a critical step toward establishing a causal relationship.

FA has been shown in previous studies to possess antioxidant properties and regulate lipid metabolism; however, its low bioavailability and instability in vivo limit its further application18,31,32. In this study, FA-PG3 nanoparticles were successfully prepared using G3 PAMAM as the carrier. These nanoparticles demonstrated high encapsulation efficiency and favorable sustained-release characteristics, while also enhancing cellular uptake and target specificity. They exhibited superior performance in terms of particle size, dispersion stability, and sustained release capacity. FA-PG3 exerted significantly stronger browning effects in vitro compared to free FA, indicating that the carrier system plays a critical role in enhancing pharmacological efficacy. Compared with other common nanocarriers, PAMAM offers advantages such as a well-defined structure and high modifiability, suggesting its potential to serve as a versatile platform for future interventions in metabolic diseases.

This study utilized network pharmacology combined with molecular docking to identify potential targets and signaling pathways of FA based on database analyses. The results highlighted key regulators of lipid metabolism, including PPARγ, PRDM16, and UCP1. Integrated transcriptomic analysis and functional assays confirmed that FA modulates the PPAR signaling axis, which was further validated in both cellular and animal models. This research strategy—from prediction to validation to blockade—strengthened the mechanistic depth and coherence of the study. Additionally, improvements in mitochondrial function and reductions in lipid accumulation provided multidimensional evidence supporting the overall efficacy of FA-PG3 in enhancing adipocyte metabolic performance, indicating that its effects extend beyond gene expression to include actual improvements in metabolic output.

It is worth emphasizing that this study extends to the clinical population level, involving the collection of serum samples from 206 children with severe obesity and 70 healthy controls to evaluate key adipokines, including FGF21, irisin, BMP7, and NRG4. The results reveal that the levels of these browning-promoting factors are consistently lower in children with obesity and are significantly negatively correlated with metabolic indicators such as BMI and insulin resistance index. These findings are highly consistent with the results observed in animal and cellular experiments, thereby reinforcing the translational relevance of the study to real-world populations. This integrative approach, combining basic research with population data, aligns with current trends in translational medicine and provides a solid foundation for the clinical application of future intervention strategies.

At present, interventions for severe obesity in children remain largely dependent on lifestyle modification and behavioral management, while pharmacological options are limited and often accompanied by potential side effects33,34. As a novel strategy combining a naturally derived bioactive compound with a biodegradable nanocarrier system, FA-PG3 demonstrates high biocompatibility and target specificity, making it particularly suitable for use in vulnerable populations such as children35,36. In contrast to systemic metabolic drugs, this approach focuses on remodeling adipose tissue function to enhance energy expenditure from the “energy-consuming end,” thereby minimizing systemic adverse effects. More importantly, the well-defined mechanism of action and promising preclinical outcomes offer both theoretical rationale and experimental support for the advancement of FA-PG3 into preclinical studies and individualized therapeutic applications.

This study has limitations regarding organ safety and tissue specificity. Although no acute histological damage to major organs was observed following repeated administration, tissue distribution assays revealed significant fluorescence signals in the liver and kidneys, suggesting that FA-PG3 may accumulate in these organs via metabolic clearance pathways rather than achieving complete adipose-specific targeting. The current H&E staining assessment represents only a morphological observation at a single time point and cannot exclude the possibility of subclinical toxicity resulting from long-term administration. Given the focus on translational applications for severe pediatric obesity, the long-term biosafety and potential impact on developing organs require further systematic evaluation through repeated-dose toxicity studies, dynamic monitoring of serum biochemical markers, and immunohistochemical analysis. Through the analysis of pediatric serum samples, this study found that levels of FGF21, Irisin, BMP7, and NRG4 were negatively correlated with BMI and metabolic indicators, consistent with trends observed in animal experiments. However, these data are derived from a cross-sectional observational study, which can only reveal statistical correlations; they cannot validate the mechanism of FA-PG3 in humans, nor do they support the use of these factors as predictive biomarkers for therapeutic response. This study primarily assessed thermogenic effects through molecular markers such as UCP1, mitochondrial function assays, and indirect calorimetry using metabolic cages; it did not employ PET-CT or infrared thermography to directly detect adipose tissue thermogenic activity. Future studies should include functional experiments to further validate these findings. Furthermore, circulating Irisin in humans is primarily derived from skeletal muscle tissue37, and its status as a browning marker remains controversial. Therefore, the population data merely suggest an association between metabolic dysregulation of adipose tissue and alterations in secretory factors, providing a hypothetical basis for subsequent research rather than validating therapeutic efficacy. Future prospective interventional studies are required to evaluate the clinical translational potential of FA-PG3.

In summary, this study presents several notable innovations. First, it pioneers the construction of the FA-PG3 nanoparticle system, which improves the in vivo activity and stability of FA. Second, it identifies the molecular targets and signaling pathways involved, and for the first time, confirms the dependency of its action through a UCP1 knockdown model. Third, the experimental design encompasses mechanistic analysis, cellular metabolism, animal phenotyping, and clinical correlation, reflecting both systematic rigor and translational potential. Lastly, by focusing on the unique population of children with severe obesity, the study provides a novel direction for the prevention and treatment of pediatric metabolic disorders. However, several limitations remain. First, the clinical samples were based on cross-sectional data and lacked interventional validation. Second, the long-term toxicity and pharmacokinetic profile of FA-PG3 have not yet been assessed. Third, the tissue distribution and metabolic pathways of the nanoparticles require further clarification. Future research should involve larger population-based studies and comprehensive preclinical evaluations to validate the safety and efficacy of FA-PG3, while also optimizing its carrier structure and targeting capacity to facilitate real-world application.

This study systematically reveals that ferulic acid-loaded PG3 cationic dendrimer nanoparticles (FA-PG3) promote adipocyte browning, enhance mitochondrial function, and increase energy expenditure by activating PPARγ/PRDM16 signaling while simultaneously upregulating UCP1 expression, thereby ultimately ameliorating obesity-related metabolic dysfunction. FA-PG3 exhibited significant biological effects in both cellular and animal models, and its metabolic benefits were dependent on UCP1 expression, confirming it as a novel anti-obesity nanotherapeutic candidate with a well-defined mechanism of action and precise targeting.

Methods

Sample collection and ethical approval

A total of 206 children with severe obesity (BMI > 120% of the 95th percentile for age and sex) under the age of 16 were enrolled in this study, along with 70 healthy children as controls. All participants were recruited from the outpatient and inpatient departments of the Department of Pediatrics, Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University, between January 2023 and June 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (Approval No. 2023PS917K).

Serum sample collection and processing

All serum samples were obtained from residual blood collected during routine clinical testing, either during hospitalization or outpatient visits. Blood was drawn in the early morning after overnight fasting, with a volume not exceeding standard clinical limits (≤3 mL per subject). Samples were collected using vacuum tubes without anticoagulants (BD Vacutainer, 367896, USA). Collected samples were left at room temperature for 30 minutes before centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes (Thermo Scientific Sorvall ST16, USA). The serum fraction was subsequently separated and preserved at −80 °C for analysis.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) detection of adipokines

Serum levels of target adipokines were measured using ELISA kits. FGF21 (PF313) and irisin (PI470) kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China), while BMP7 (CSB-E08122h) and NRG4 (CSB-EL016080HU) kits were obtained from Cusabio (China). Each sample was tested in duplicate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated from each assay.

Measurement of serum metabolic indicators in clinical samples

Fasting glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), uric acid (UA), and creatinine levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Hitachi Ltd., Japan). Fasting insulin (FINS) levels were determined by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Cobas e601, Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). Insulin resistance was assessed using the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA-IR), calculated as FPG × FINS / 22.5.

Correlation and multivariate statistical analyses

The correlations between serum adipokine concentrations, metabolic parameters, and BMI of each subject were analyzed. Data processing was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA). Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed variables, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to non-normally distributed data. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening of active compounds from Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)

Natural bioactive compounds from Angelica sinensis were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP). Screening thresholds were set at oral bioavailability (OB) >50% and drug-likeness (DL) >0.05. OB reflects the capacity of a compound to enter systemic circulation, while DL is used to evaluate the compound’s drug-like properties.

High-throughput sequencing and analysis

3T3-L1-derived beige adipocytes were treated with FA, a bioactive compound identified by TCM screening. Six samples were collected from both the control and FA-treated groups for RNA sequencing. Total RNA was isolated from all 12 samples using a total RNA extraction kit (12183555, Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration was measured with a UV spectrophotometer (BioSpectrometer basic, Eppendorf, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA for library preparation, and sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform. Raw image files were converted into raw reads by base calling. After removal of adapter sequences and low-quality reads with Cutadapt, the remaining high-quality sequences were retained as clean reads. These clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression was quantified in R to generate the expression matrix.

Differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” package in R, with a threshold of |log2FC | > 1 and p < 0.05. Venn diagrams and volcano plots were generated using the Xiantao Academic Online tool (https://www.xiantaozi.com/), and heatmaps were created using the Hiplot platform (https://hiplot.com.cn/).

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted for the identified key targets. GO analysis covered biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) using the Xiantao Academic platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/) with a significance cutoff of p < 0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment was also conducted to identify significantly enriched pathways (p < 0.05), which were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R. Based on the literature, pathways most relevant to adipocyte browning and metabolic regulation were selected for further focus.

Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis

The identified potential therapeutic targets were uploaded to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), selecting Mus musculus as the model organism for PPI analysis. The minimum interaction confidence score was set at 0.500, unconnected nodes were excluded, and all other parameters were kept at their default values. A PPI network of targets potentially involved in obesity treatment was then constructed, and the interaction data were exported. The number of interacting nodes for each protein was subsequently visualized using R software.

Molecular docking simulation

The 2D and 3D chemical structures of FA (Compound CID: 445858) were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and saved in SDF format. Crystal structures of the candidate FA target proteins, including UCP1 (PDB ID: 8HBV), PPARG (PDB ID: 8WFE), and PRDM16 (PDB ID: 2N1I), were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). The 3D structure of FA was built in Chem3D Ultra 14.0 and optimized by energy minimization using the MM2 force field. Receptor proteins were prepared using PyMOL 2.5.7 by removing water molecules and organic ligands. Subsequently, AutoDock Tools 1.5.7 was employed to process the receptor proteins by adding hydrogens, computing charges, and converting both ligand and receptor structures into “.pdbqt” files. Docking grid boxes were defined by setting appropriate center coordinates and grid spacing. Vina 1.2.7 was then used to perform the docking simulations and calculate binding free energy. By integrating the PPI network and docking results, the potential target interactions of FA were identified.

Cell culture and differentiation

3T3-L1 preadipocytes (ATCC, USA, CL-173) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (11965118, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5670701, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; 15140122, Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The 293 T cell line (ATCC, CRL-3216) was cultured in DMEM (11965092, Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS, 10 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 using a Heracell™ Vios 160i CR CO₂ incubator (51033770, Thermo Scientific™, USA).

For beige adipocyte differentiation, 3T3-L1 cells were grown to full confluence and then exposed for 2 days to beige induction medium composed of high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 0.5 mM isobutylmethylxanthine (IBMX; I5879, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1 μM dexamethasone (DEX; D4902, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 10 μg/mL insulin (HY-P0035A, MCE, China), 50 nM triiodothyronine (T3), and 1 μM rosiglitazone38. The medium was then replaced with beige maintenance medium consisting of complete medium supplemented with 10 μg/mL insulin, 50 nM T3, and 1 μM rosiglitazone for an additional 2 days. Cells were maintained in complete medium containing 50 nM T3, with medium changes every 2 days. After 8–12 days, more than 90% of cells had differentiated into mature adipocytes39.

The experimental groups were established as follows: Control group: untreated cells cultured in standard medium; PG3 group: treated with PG3 PAMAM nanoparticles (50 μg/mL); FA group: treated with free FA (50 μM); FA-PG3 group: treated with FA-PG3 nanoparticles at a concentration equivalent to 50 μM FA; FA + GW9662 group: treated with free FA (50 μM) and the PPARγ antagonist GW9662 (10 μM); FA-PG3 + sh-NC group: treated with FA-PG3 nanoparticles and lentiviral vector carrying a non-targeting control shRNA; FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group: treated with FA-PG3 nanoparticles and lentiviral-mediated UCP1 knockdown40.

UCP1 gene knockdown

Potential shRNA target sites for mouse UCP1 were designed based on the corresponding GenBank cDNA sequence. Three shRNA sequences targeting UCP1 were generated, together with a non-targeting shRNA as a negative control (sh-NC). The primer sequences are provided in Table S1, and all oligonucleotides were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral vectors were constructed using the pLKO.1 system, and the control group received the empty negative control vector. Packaging plasmids and target constructs were co-transfected into 293 T cells at 80–90% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668500, Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, the lentiviral supernatant was collected, filtered, and centrifuged, and viral titers were determined using cells in the exponential growth phase.

For infection, cells in logarithmic growth were trypsinized and resuspended to a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL. The cell suspension was seeded into 6-well plates at 2 mL per well, followed by lentiviral transduction at an MOI of 10 (viral titer: 1 × 108 TU/mL) for 48 h. Stable transfectants were then selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (UC0E03, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Knockdown efficiency was confirmed 48 h after transfection by measuring UCP1 expression using RT-qPCR.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined with the CCK-8 assay (CK04, Dojindo, Japan). Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h, the indicated treatments were applied. Following 48 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, after which absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX, USA).

Lipid accumulation analysis

Lipid accumulation was evaluated by Oil Red O staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, China) for 10 min, followed by treatment with 60% isopropanol (109827, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min. The cells were then stained with 0.3% Oil Red O solution (102419, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for 30 min. After three washes with PBS, lipid droplets were observed under a microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany). The retained dye was subsequently dissolved in isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader.

Mitochondrial function assessment

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was determined using a Seahorse XF Analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells were seeded into Seahorse XF 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with bicarbonate-free assay medium, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Oligomycin, FCCP, and antimycin A/rotenone from the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015-100, Agilent, USA) were injected sequentially to monitor dynamic changes in OCR.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content was determined using the ATP Assay Kit (MAK190, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After cell lysis, absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX, USA).

Preparation of nanoparticles

To prepare FA-PG3 nanoparticles, PAMAM generation 3 (PG3) dendrimers (412422, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dispersed in 10 mL of PBS buffer. Separately, 2 mg of FA (PHR1791, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in a 1:1 acetone-methanol mixture and then slowly added dropwise into the PG3 buffer solution. The reaction mixture was magnetically stirred at 37 °C in the dark for 4 h. Residual organic solvents were removed by dialysis, and unencapsulated hydrophobic drug molecules were eliminated using syringe filtration.

The Cy5-labeled FA-PG3 complex was prepared by adding Cy5-labeled PG3 to an HSA aqueous solution at a Cy5-NHS to PG3 mass ratio of 1:50.

Characterization of nanoparticle size and morphology

Particle size distribution and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instrument Ltd., UK) at 25 °C. Measurements included particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential, with a scattering angle of 173°. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and average values for particle diameter and PDI were calculated.

In vitro release assay

The release behavior of FA-PG3 was evaluated using Cy5-labeled complexes. Briefly, 1 mL of the complex (1 mg/mL) was placed in a cellulose dialysis bag (Spectra/Por 2, MWCO 12,000–14,000 Da, Spectrum, USA) and immersed in 9 mL PBS. Samples were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. At predetermined intervals, the dialysis bag was transferred to fresh PBS. The fluorescence intensity of the release medium was measured using a FLUOstar Optima FL microplate reader (Ex/Em: 620/670 nm).

Stability assessment of nanoparticles

To evaluate the stability of the nanoparticles under different physiological conditions, the particles were suspended separately in distilled water (H2O) and high-glucose DMEM medium (11965118, Gibco, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Samples were collected at 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 hours and analyzed by DLS to monitor changes in particle size and zeta potential.

Animal model establishment and experimental grouping

Three-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (SPF grade, purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were housed in an animal facility maintained at 22 ± 2°C under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60% of calories from fat and 345 mg/kg cholesterol (F3282; Bio-Serv, West Chester, PA, USA) and water. Continuous HFD feeding for 10 weeks was used to establish the obesity model41. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (No. 2025PS1727K).

The mice were randomly divided into 12 groups (n = 6): NC group, HFD group, HFD + FA group, HFD + FA + GW9662 group, PBS group (negative control, without Cy5-labeled nanoparticle injection), PG3 group, FA group, FA-PG3 group, Control + sh-NC group, FA-PG3 + sh-NC group, and FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group. After 10 weeks of HFD feeding, mice were randomized and subjected to the indicated interventions. Both the FA monomer and FA-PG3 nanoparticle treatment protocols lasted 6 weeks, with intraperitoneal administration performed three times weekly at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight42. Mice in the GW9662 group received intraperitoneal GW9662 at 10 mg/kg once weekly for 6 weeks43. The FA-PG3 + sh-UCP1 group received lentiviral sh-UCP1 injections twice per week in addition to the FA-PG3 treatment. GTT and ITT were performed in the first week following the completion of the intervention (Week 7). During the administration period, mouse body weight was measured weekly at fixed time points using an analytical balance (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland), and food intake was recorded. Food consumption was calculated by measuring the 24-hour difference in food weight with a precision balance (±0.01 g) to ensure consistent feeding conditions. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess pathological alterations in major organs, including the liver, kidney, and heart.

In vitro tissue distribution and in vivo imaging

Tissue fluorescence was detected using the PerkinElmer IVIS Spectrum imaging system (Living Image Software v4.5.5, USA), and fluorescence signal intensities were recorded. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 μg of Cy5-labeled FA-PG3 or PG3. At 24 hours post-injection, fluorescence imaging was performed using the PerkinElmer IVIS system. On day 3 after injection, the mice were euthanized, and fluorescence signals in various tissues were analyzed using the same system.

Metabolic function tests

Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT): The GTT was performed in the 7th week following model induction. Mice were fasted for 16 hours starting at 8:00 p.m. the night before the test. The following morning, glucose (2 g/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 14431-43-7) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Blood glucose was measured from the tail vein at 0 (baseline), 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes using a glucometer (Breeze 2, Bayer, USA).

Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT): ITT was conducted in the seventh week after model induction, following a 4 h fast. Human insulin (0.75 U/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered intraperitoneally, and tail vein blood glucose levels were recorded at 0, 30, 60, and 120 minutes after injection.

Energy metabolism measurement

In the seventh week after model induction, energy metabolism was evaluated by indirect calorimetry using the Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS, Columbus Instruments, USA). Six mice from each group were housed individually and allowed to acclimate for 8 h before data collection. Oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and heat production were then monitored continuously over a 24 h period. All data were recorded and analyzed automatically using CLAX 5.4 software.

Histological staining and immunohistochemistry analysis

Following euthanasia, subcutaneous inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), retroperitoneal white adipose tissue (rWAT), thigh adipose tissue (tAT), interscapular brown adipose tissue (BAT), liver, heart, and kidneys were rapidly dissected. The volume and weight of each tissue were recorded. Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and then processed for paraffin embedding. Sections of 5 μm were prepared and stained with H&E for histomorphological evaluation. Microscopic images were acquired with an Olympus BX51 system, and adipocyte size was quantified using ImageJ 1.53k.

Lee’s index measurement

At the end of the intervention, mouse body weight and body length (nose-to-anus distance) were measured, and Lee’s index was calculated as: Lee’s Index = [Body weight (g)1/3 × 10] / Body length (cm).

Serum biochemical analysis

After a 6 h fast, blood samples were collected before sacrifice by retro-orbital or abdominal aortic puncture. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C. Commercial ELISA kits were used to determine serum biomarkers, including glucose (S0202M, Beyotime, China), insulin (10-1247-01, Mercodia, Sweden), TC (TR13421, Thermo Scientific, USA), TG (TR22421, Thermo Scientific, USA), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) (299-94301, Fujifilm Wako, Japan), and alanine (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (C009-2-1 / C010-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Following dissection, tissues were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099-100 mL, Beyotime, China) for two days, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Sections were incubated overnight at a 1:250 dilution with Caveolin-1 primary antibody (D46G3, 3267, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After washing, sections were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (A27034, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A PBS negative control group (without Cy5-labeled nanoparticle injection or immunostaining) was included to ensure specificity. Fluorescence co-localization was observed using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Germany), and merged channel images were used to analyze the intracellular distribution of FA-PG3 within adipocytes.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using Trizol reagent (16096020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were assessed at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop LITE spectrophotometer (ND-LITE-PR, Thermo Scientific™, Germany). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047Q, TaKaRa, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (4364344, Applied Biosystems, USA) on an ABI PRISM 7500 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, USA). All primers were synthesized by TaKaRa (Table S2), and β-actin served as the internal control. Relative expression levels were determined by the 2-ΔΔCt method. All RT-qPCR experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Western Blot (WB)

First, tissues or cells were collected. For BAT collection, mice were maintained at 22 ± 2°C under a 12 h light/dark cycle, and all specimens were harvested in the morning to minimize circadian influences on thermogenic gene expression. Only the interscapular BAT depot was dissected, based on its characteristic dark brown appearance and lobulated morphology, while adjacent white adipose tissue was carefully removed. Immediately after isolation, tissues were rinsed with ice-cold saline to eliminate residual blood, blotted dry, and processed within 3 min. Samples designated for Western blotting were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Tissue or cell samples were lysed using enhanced RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (FFP39, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (ST023, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 2 hours to prevent nonspecific binding. After blocking, membranes were incubated with diluted primary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. All primary antibodies were rabbit anti-human (details provided in Table S3). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab6721, 1:2000, Abcam, UK) for 1 hour at room temperature. Equal volumes of solutions A and B from the Pierce™ ECL WB Substrate Kit (32209, Thermo Scientific™, Germany) were mixed in the dark and applied to the membranes. Signal detection was performed using a gel imaging system. Band intensities were captured with the Bio-Rad imaging system (BIO-RAD, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software. β-actin was used as the internal loading control. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical software and data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1) in the RStudio environment (version 2022.12.0-353). Data preprocessing was performed with Perl (version 5.30.0), and figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test, whereas multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. For analyses involving repeated measurements over time, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing was applied. When data were not normally distributed, or variances were unequal, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test was used. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson correlation for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank correlation for non-normally distributed variables. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample sizes were selected according to common practice in adipose browning and energy metabolism studies, together with our preliminary experimental data; no formal a priori power analysis was performed during study design.
