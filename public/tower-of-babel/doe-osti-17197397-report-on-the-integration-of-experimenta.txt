 ABSTRACT

 The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program will accelerate
development, qualification, and deployment of advanced materials and manufacturing technologies to
enable reliable and economical nuclear energy. This effort requires the consideration of environmental
effects on the evolution of properties and microstructures. The ability to obtain representative properties
from neutron irradiations remains limited by the time required to achieve an adequate level of exposure or
displacement damage (displacements per atom). Ion irradiation may enable rapid assessment of radiation-
induced damage to a material. To show that additively manufactured (AM) stainless steel (SS) 316 can be
used in high-temperature nuclear applications, both high-temperature and He generation must be
included. Oak Ridge National Laboratory continued to generate initial predictions of neutron-irradiated
hardness or yield strength up to 10 dpa for laser powder bed fusion-produced (LPBF) AM SS 316 using
ion irradiations with and without co-injection of He and instrumented indentation. Further, the lab, re-
parameterized a simplified rate theory model to examine the impacts of He, damage rate, and temperature
on cavity evolution to guide future work. Instrumented indentation experiments on LPBF SS 316H
demonstrated that a bulk equivalent hardness can be converted to a measurement of yield stress at room
temperature in line with High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) -irradiated steels across irradiation
temperatures from 400°C to 650°C up to 10 dpa, and as a function of either a stress-relief or solution-
annealed heat treatment. This equivalency was enabled through the inclusion of strain hardening in the
conversion of hardness to yield stress and the application of invariance theory to account for the three
orders of magnitude differences in radiation damage rate between the ion irradiation and HFIR
irradiations. The mean-field rate theory (MFRT) was retooled from its initial use case of ferritic steel
systems to austenitic steels. Initial parameterization of the MFRT growth model using cavity
microstructures from ion irradiation at 600°C reproduced the average cavity diameter and root mean cube
of the cavity diameter. Extrapolation from irradiation at 600°C to 400°C confirmed the generalized
functionality of the model for the same damage rate. Using the cavity microstructure from HFIR-
irradiated LPBF SS 316H as input and including the movement and trapping of He, a prediction was
made for HFIR irradiation at 10 dpa. Future characterization on HFIR-irradiated SS 316H will be used to
evaluate the predictions and refine the rate theory model.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The motivation for the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program is to
accelerate development, qualification, and deployment of advanced materials and manufacturing
technologies to enable reliable and economical nuclear energy. Typically, the primary pathway uses
traditional data packages consistent with the American Society of Mechanical Engineers Boiler and
Pressure Vessel Code [1], which does not cover all environmental effects. Therefore, additional testing is
needed to generate environmental data, such as corrosion or radiation effects data, relevant to nuclear
environments. The ability to obtain representative properties from neutron irradiations remains limited by
the time required to achieve an adequate level of exposure or displacement damage (displacements per
atom). Ion irradiation may enable rapid assessment of radiation-induced damage to a material and is
proposed as part of an accelerated materials qualification framework under the AMMT program [2, 3]. As
discussed in previous AMMT reports [4], the primary foci to evaluate the framework would be (1)
integrated microstructure-modeling approaches [5-7] and (2) determining how to extract performance-
relevant data from the ion-irradiated microstructures and benchmark the process with neutron-irradiated
specimens [4, 8]. The yield strength (YS) is a defining performance property, whether via low-
temperature failure modes as estimated through the strength required to fracture [9], high-temperature
failure modes as the boundary for creep, or as a design limit for engineering applications. Thus, YS is
targeted as the figure of merit or property of interest to help demonstrate the Licensing Approach with
Ions and Neutrons [2].

 A-3

In addition to the typical properties necessary for American Society of Mechanical Engineers
qualification of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316 stainless steel (SS 316) [10], nuclear environments
introduce a new challenge: He. Neutron-rich environments produce He through transmutation reactions
such as a two-step 58Ni(n,γ)59Ni(n,α)56Fe reaction relevant to most steels [11]. The accumulation of He
can lead to many degradation modes, including cavity swelling [12], high-temperature He embrittlement
[13-15], and a reduction in creep rupture life. For example, in prior fast-reactor irradiations of austenitic
steels such as SS 316, researchers found that after only accumulating 3 atomic parts per million (appm)
He within the material (an equivalent dose of about 4.5 dpa), the creep rupture life decreased from over
1,000 h when tested at 69 MPa/760°C to less than 200 h [16]. This decrease in creep life was primarily
due to the transmutation-induced He generation within the material, leading to grain boundary decohesion
when He bubbles nucleated on these interfaces. The He production rate is also dependent on the
composition of the material and the energy-flux spectra of the environment [17]. Both high-temperature
and He generation must be included to show that additively manufactured (AM) SS 316 can be used in
high-temperature nuclear applications.

The overall goals of this effort are to integrate ion irradiation, neutron irradiation, and modeling data for
materials developed and tested in AMMT and to demonstrate acceptable performance of AM materials in
irradiation environments to evaluate the irradiation challenge problem [18]. In pursuit of this goal, Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) continued to generate initial predictions of neutron-irradiated
hardness or YS at 10 dpa for LPBF AM 316 steel using ion irradiations and indentation and to re-
parameterize a simplified rate theory model to examine the impacts of He, damage rate, and temperature
on cavity evolution to guide future work. Section 2 describes the experimental work continued from FY25
integrating ion and neutron irradiation data to predict the yield stress at room temperature [8] and
compared with the same alloys irradiated in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). Section 3 takes a
computational approach to integrate ion irradiation data into a mean field rate theory (MFRT) model of
cavity growth to predict microstructure evolution as a function of the displacements per atom,
temperature, and He generation rate. Although these two sections appear distinct, they both contribute to
the ability to use ion irradiation to predict the performance of materials under neutron irradiation.

 2. INTEGRATION OF ION AND NEUTRON DATA THROUGH INDENTATION
 EXPERIMENTS

One of the challenges to accelerate materials qualification efforts and assess the impacts of He using ion
irradiation data is the ability to extract relevant mechanical property information from the thin volume
that is the ion-bombarded surface. Several studies have pursued qualitative and quantitative measures
with varying levels of success: one study reported a strong agreement between ion irradiation and fast-
reactor-irradiated steel [19]. In previous AMMT reports, empirical relationships showed strong promise to
convert bulk-like hardness from instrumented indentation tests to yield stress at room temperature.
Although useful for quick estimations, such relationships lack the physical understanding necessary to
extrapolate across materials and material systems. The work in FY26 took the methodologies from
previous years and refined them to convert directly from bulk-like hardness to yield stress without using
empirical relationships.

2.1 METHODOLOGY

Instrumented indentation testing (colloquially known as nanoindentation) experiments were performed on
a KLA iMicro Nanoindenter dedicated to nuclear fuels and materials at ORNL. Indents were performed in
either a 5 × 5 or a 6 × 6 array (total of either 25 or 36 points) for each specimen at room temperature
using a Berkovich tip and a 50 mN actuator. The indenter performed advanced nanoindentation
techniques using the continuous stiffness method [20, 21]. This technique provides a small oscillation to
the indenter tip and allows for the determination of hardness and the elastic modulus through the entire

 A-3

depth of the indentation test. Instead of a fixed frequency and amplitude as is commonly used in the
literature for irradiated materials [19, 22], this work used a fixed frequency of 110 Hz with a variable
amplitude equal to 10% of the applied load. Thus, as the indenter uses a higher applied load to push to
deeper depths, the corresponding increase in amplitude keeps the signal-to-noise ratio at a minimal level.
The indentation system was calibrated using fused silica, and the reference fused silica was checked
before and after each series of indentations. The data from each individual indent are collated to form
average curves for hardness vs. displacement and elastic modulus vs. displacement. To facilitate analysis,
each average hardness curve was analyzed with the framework of the Nix-Gao law for strain gradient
plasticity to account for size effects [23]. Equation (1) is the original Nix-Gao derivation, and Eq. (2) is
the same equation rewritten to a linear form. In these equations, H is the measured hardness, h is the
displacement by the indenter, h* is a characteristic length depending on the shape of the indenter, and H0
is the bulk equivalent hardness. Although 𝐻02 is multiplied by 1 and h*/h, as the term h*/h approaches
zero (h approaches infinity), this term becomes negligible. Thus, plotting the hardness squared as a
function of the inverse displacement allows for the extrapolation of the linear fit of the data to “infinite”
depth, allowing for the determination of the bulk equivalent hardness, H0.

For this investigation, several variations of LPBF 316H were ion-irradiated at Argonne National
Laboratory using 5 MeV Ni2+ ions at in the temperature range of 300°C–650°C up to 10 dpa. Details of
the ion irradiations were reported previously [24] with the samples produced from LPBF 316H-2 and are
not repeated here. The calculated range and implantation profile using the Stopping and Range of Ions in
Matter (SRIM) program [25] are shown in Figure 1, using Co ions in place of Ni ions because of a known
error in SRIM for Ni ion stopping powers [26]. For this report, the focus of the indentation work is
narrowed to the LPBF 316H specimens (AM 316), in which the materials were identical before irradiation
with either neutrons in HFIR or ions. As with previous sections, nanoindentation was conducted using the
continuous stiffness method with a frequency of 110 Hz. Because the microstructure depth examined is
about 5× the indentation depth [27], the fitting for the Nix-Gao analysis was limited to 100–200 nm from
the surface, corresponding to the depth at which the damage (displacements per atom) was calculated.

2.2 RESULTS OF INSTRUMENTED INDENTATION AND COMPARISON WITH
 PROPERTIES FROM HFIR IRRADIATIONS

Representative Nix-Gao plots for the evolution of hardness as a function of the irradiation temperature for
the stress-relieved (SR, 24 h at 650°C) and solution-annealed (SA, 1 h at 1,100°C) LPBF 316H at 10 dpa
are included in Figure 2 and Figure 3, respectively. The y-intercepts in these plots represent the square of
the bulk hardness, and by comparison, they provide qualitative insight into the changes in hardness. In all
examined specimens, the Nix-Gao plots depict two distinct regions corresponding to the ion-irradiated
volume (up to 250 nm of indentation depth) and the underlying substrate. By fitting Eq. (2) to only the
ion-bombarded volume, the properties of these regions can be compared across irradiation temperatures
and heat treatments. For SR LPBF 316H, the hardness monotonically decreased with increasing
irradiation temperature from 3.1 ± 0.3 GPa to 2.5 ± 0.2 GPa. The SA LPBF 316H exhibited a bell shape
with a peak at 500°C and 3.9 ± 0.2 GPa. Based on the microstructure characterization presented for the
same samples in W.-Y. Chen et al. [24], both heat treatments showed their highest amount of cavity
nucleation at 600°C, indicating cavities are not likely the primary source of hardness. Based on the
qualitative dislocation microstructure, the SA condition irradiated at 500 °C formed a significant density
of dislocations at 10 dpa. The nonirradiated microstructure of the SR condition already contained
dislocation cells from the printing process and likely contributed to the hardness through either the
retention of the dislocation cells or the network of Ni segregation at the former dislocation cell walls [28].
Further detailed characterization is needed to quantify the dislocation loop and line densities and
determine whether these are the primary source of hardness.

With the measured hardness values, a comparison can be made to the same SR and SA conditions
irradiated in HFIR. In FY25, several correlations were derived empirically to convert the bulk equivalent
hardness from instrumented indentation testing to Vickers hardness and then to yield stress at room
temperature [8]. Although useful, these correlations did not have a physical basis and limit the broader
applicability outside of LPBF 316 steels. Several relationships exist to convert hardness into yield stress,
and of these, many imply that work hardening is negligible. The historical justification in irradiated
materials for a small to negligible amount of work hardening stems from previous studies at relatively low
temperatures in which defect motion is low and defect clusters are small [29]. In the paper by Ohr [30],
iron irradiated at low fluences near 90°C exhibited a work hardening coefficient of 0.39 for room
temperature tensile testing. Later work by Farell, Byun, and many others [31-34], demonstrated the work
hardening coefficient decreases with increasing radiation exposure for SS 316 up to 0.89 dpa at irradiation
temperatures <100°C. The tensile properties reported for the SR and SA LPBF 316H [35, 36] line up with
this expectation by having low uniform elongation from room temperature testing for irradiation
temperatures less than 400°C. However, at higher irradiation temperatures, the uniform elongation is
extended and, therefore, has work hardening capability. In this work, an assumption is made that the ion-
irradiated microstructures follow the same patterns and, thus, work hardening must be included in the
conversion from hardness to yield stress.

A one-to-one comparison of ions and neutrons is not a straightforward comparison given the known
effects of damage rate on the evolution of irradiated microstructures. Thus, an invariance relationship was
applied to shift the ion irradiation temperatures to equivalent neutron irradiation temperatures. The
invariance shift was calculated using Eq. 14 in Mansur [38] with several simplifying assumptions. First,
the high dislocation density within the SR LPBF 316H lends itself to the assumption that defect losses are
sink-dominated. Second, the lack of dislocation density in the SA LPBF 316H has defect losses
dominated by mutual annihilation by recombination. Third, the relative sink strengths for interstitials and
vacancies are similar between ion irradiations and neutron irradiations. This common assumption is made
when trying to equate the two microstructures for the prediction of void swelling [39] but may limit the
applicability of invariance theory to all circumstances. The result of these assumptions and calculations is
the shift of the predicted YS from ion irradiation to lower “neutron” temperatures, shown in Figure 7. For
the SR condition, the ion irradiation values sit within the scatter of the neutron dataset and extend the
trend to lower irradiation temperatures. A similar observation is made for the SA condition, though with
some points still resulting in greater yield stress than seems reasonable. Nevertheless, by including the
effects of strain hardening and providing an offset based on invariance theory, strong agreement and
predictive trends were established between accelerated ion irradiations and HFIR irradiations.

3. RATE THEORY INVESTIGATIONS TO EXTRAPOLATE ACROSS ION AND NEUTRON
 CONDITIONS

Although experimental comparisons can provide measurements between ion and neutron irradiations,
physics-driven modeling allows for correlation and, more importantly, prediction of microstructure and
properties under irradiation. Many options are available, and the AMMT program exercises several length
and time scales to evaluate the suitability of accelerated experimentation. MFRT is the approach taken in
this work because it is well suited to predict long-term behavior through calculation speed and relative
simplicity. This methodology is limited in its ability to handle defect cluster nucleation, which is best left
to cluster dynamics approaches [40-44], so it is applied only to growth in this study.

3.1 DESCRIPTION OF THE RATE THEORY MODEL

The -MFRT model used in this study was developed for cavity evolution in ferritic-martensitic steels to
quantitatively predict the cavity size distribution and associated swelling based on a predefined
microstructure and its associated growth under irradiation [45, 46]. A cavity is defined according to
nomenclature described in Taller et al. [47], in which cavities encompass He-stabilized features as
“bubbles” and all others as “voids.” When the process supporting the stabilization is not known, the
generic term of “cavity” is used. A generalized flow chart of the model is included as Figure 8. The model
takes as input many common materials properties, a starting microstructure defined by size distributions
and subsequently sink strengths, and irradiation conditions. Using these parameters, He is partitioned
throughout the microstructural features based on their sink strength and released back into the lattice
based on thermal release. The He delegated to cavities plays a role in solving the cavity growth rate
equation for each part of the cavity distribution, which is then applied to the cavity distribution to shrink
or grow cavities. The new size distributions become the input for the next timestep with associated He
partitioning. The process is repeated until the desired damage level is reached. Further details on the
model are available in other documents [45, 46]. A strong caution is that the model does not include
cavity nucleation mechanisms. The model allows users to input a fixed nucleation rate at a cavity size of
either 0.5 nm for bubbles or 5 nm for voids. This functionality is meant to serve as a stop gap until a
nucleation-driven model can be coupled with this model, similar to the hybrid cluster dynamics-rate
theory model in VanCoevering [48].

 IRRADIATION

As stated previously, the MFRT model takes a provided microstructure and grows the cavity distribution
based on the parameters selected in

Table 1. Development and refinement of the model focused on the LPBF SR 316H condition because this
sample exhibited a relatively large amount of swelling at about 1% at 5 dpa under ion irradiation at 10−3
dpa/s at 600°C with an estimated dislocation density of 1014 m-2. The microstructure as extrapolated from
the model for the cavity distribution at 10 dpa is shown in Figure 9, along with the calculated cavity
growth rate based on the He repartitioning scheme described in Figure 8. Table 2 presents a quantitative
summary of the cavity microstructure. Experimentally, the cavity microstructure grew significantly
between 5 and 10 dpa, and this growth was captured by the model’s parameterization. The average void
radius and root mean cube were found to be a reasonable match between the experimental and predicted
values at 10 dpa. However, the width of the experiment distribution was not found in the model’s
prediction. Examining the calculated growth rate showed that small cavities have the largest growth rate,
whereas larger cavities are slower to grow. This difference in growth rates caused contraction in the
prediction as small cavities grew toward the average, and large cavities did not grow at the same pace
away from the average. Consequently, because the swelling is proportional to the cube of the cavity
diameter, the predicted swelling is lower than the measured cavity swelling. Nevertheless, the comparison
demonstrated that this initial parameterization was sufficient to examine swelling.

10 appm He/dpa without modification of other materials parameters. The predicted cavity size
distribution and the HFIR-irradiation are displayed in Figure 11 along with the calculated growth rate.
The cavity size distribution grew significantly from 2 to 10 dpa, especially in comparison with the higher
damage rate of ion irradiation in Figure 10. This difference was reflected in the cavity growth rate with
cavities having overall larger growth rates under HFIR conditions compared with ion irradiation
conditions with approximately the same shape. This similarity implies the same process is driving the
growth of cavities under both ion and neutron irradiation conditions.

Several limitations identified through the course of this study reduce the utility of the MFRT model in its
current state. As stated earlier in this section, cavity nucleation is treated as an input into the model and
injected into the distribution at either a 0.5 nm diameter for bubble nucleation or a 5 nm diameter for void
nucleation. For cavity distributions with no appreciable density near these sizes, adding a nucleation rate
greater than approximately 1% of the total cavity density resulted in numerical instabilities, and the
system did not converge to a solution. Similarly, numerical instabilities occurred if the He injected into a
single bin of the distribution resulted in overpressurized bubbles. Traditionally, this problem was
alleviated through the inclusion of a dislocation loop punching mechanism [95-97] to grow the bubble
and increase the dislocation sink strength accordingly. This MFRT model considered the dislocation sink
strength as a static value and, thus, cannot include this mechanism in its current iteration. Coevolving
interstitial and vacancy clusters as implemented in many cluster dynamics models [42, 43, 98, 99] could
resolve this numerical problem, albeit with significant effort. Within the limitations of the MFRT model,
the relative importance of physical mechanisms can still be examined even without full prediction
capabilities.

 4. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

The overall goal of this effort in FY26 was to integrate ion irradiation, neutron irradiation, and modeling
data for materials developed and tested in AMMT to evaluate the irradiation challenge problem. In pursuit
of this goal, ORNL continued to generate initial predictions of neutron-irradiated hardness or YS up to
10 dpa for LPBF AM 316 steel using ion irradiations and indentation and developed a simplified rate
theory model to examine the effects of He, damage rate, and temperature on cavity evolution to guide
future work. Instrumented indentation experiments demonstrated that a bulk equivalent hardness can be
converted to a measurement of yield stress at room temperature across a range of temperatures and as a
function of initial heat treatment. This equivalency was enabled through the inclusion of strain hardening
in the conversion of hardness to yield stress and the application of invariance theory to account for the
three orders of magnitude differences in radiation damage rate. The rate theory was re-parameterized
from its initial use case of ferritic steel system to austenitic steels. Initial calibration using cavity
microstructures from ion irradiation at 600°C showed the average cavity diameter and root mean cube of
the cavity diameter can be reproduced using the MFRT growth model. Extrapolation to lower
temperatures confirmed the generalized functionality of the model for the same damage rate. Using the

 A-3

cavity microstructure from HFIR-irradiated LPBF 316H as an input and including the movement and
trapping of He, a prediction was made for HFIR irradiation at 10 dpa. Characterization planned for FY27
on HFIR-irradiated SS 316H will be used to evaluate the predictions and refine the rate theory model.
