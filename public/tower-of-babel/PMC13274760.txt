Dynamic Association Between Health Literacy and Fear of Progression Among Nasopharyngeal Cancer Survivors During the Post‑treatment Survivorship Period: A Multicenter Exploratory Longitudinal Study
Authors: Qin-Mei Lin, Bing-Jin Zhang, Xiao-Mian Qiu, Ai-Ying Chen, Qiu-Yan Zhang, Bi-Hua Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the dynamic association and predictive effect between health literacy and Fear of Progression (FoP) among nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) patients during post‑treatment survivorship period.MethodsA multicenter exploratory longitudinal study design was adopted. A total of 94 newly diagnosed patients with stage III and above non-metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma were enrolled from the Department of Radiotherapy between August 2023 and December 2024.Three follow-up time points were set at 3 months (T1), 6 months (T2), and 12 months (T3) after treatment completion, at which patients’ health literacy and Fear of Progression (FoP) levels were assessed. The correlation between health literacy and FoP, as well as the predictive effect of health literacy on FoP, were further analyzed.ResultsThe mean age of the 94 NPC patients was 48.6 ± 12.3 years. Health literacy remained moderate across the three time points, while FoP was consistently high. Health literacy was significantly negatively correlated with FoP at all time points, with stronger correlations in the resource utilization and threat perception dimensions. The lagged linear model showed health literacy had a significant unadjusted negative predictive association on subsequent FoP. However, after controlling for prior FoP’s inertia effect, health literacy’s independent predictive effect disappeared, while FoP’s inertia remained extremely strong. Dimensional decomposition revealed only health literacy’s resource utilization dimension had a significant protective effect on FoP, with the threat perception dimension marginally significant. Sensitivity analysis confirmed findings’ robustness.ConclusionNPC patients’ FoP showed high temporal stability during the post‑treatment survivorship period; single-modality health literacy interventions failed to alter this chronic psychological state short-term. Clinically, early combined interventions of health literacy enhancement and psychological cognitive restructuring are recommended. Prioritize improving patients’ medical resource utilization capacity, with a multidisciplinary support system to provide sustained health and psychological support during family reintegration.Keywords: fear of progression, FoP, health literacy, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, return to families, exploratory longitudinal study

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant neoplasm arising from the mucosal epithelium of the nasopharynx and has a particularly high prevalence in South and East China. NPC is a major subtype of head and neck cancer with distinct epidemiological and biological features. Oncogenic viruses, particularly Epstein–Barr virus, play a key role in its pathogenesis.1 Molecular and immunological abnormalities, including field cancerization, genetic and epigenetic changes, and an unfavorable tumor microenvironment, also drive the development and progression of head and neck cancers.2 Epidemiological data indicate that NPC in China accounts for 38.29% of global incidence and 40.14% of mortality, both of which exceed international averages.3,4 Advances in therapeutic strategies, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) combined with concurrent chemotherapy, supplemented by individualized approaches such as immunotherapy, targeted therapy, nutritional support, and minimally invasive surgery, have substantially improved patient survival outcomes and quality of life. The current 5-year survival rate has reached approximately 80%.5 Despite these improvements, local recurrence occurs in 10%–15% of patients, with nearly half of recurrences developing within 2 years.6,7 Jiang et al8 reported that fear of cancer recurrence among patients with NPC is significantly elevated and closely associated with overall psychosocial factors. Zheng et al9 further found that NPC survivors experience a strong fear of disease progression, characterized by uncertainty about the disease and a persistent sense of insecurity. Similarly, several longitudinal studies on head and neck cancers, including NPC, have confirmed that fear of cancer recurrence follows a relatively stable and chronic trajectory and may not be fully alleviated during routine follow-up.10,11 Persistent high levels of fear of progression (FoP) are associated with considerable psychological distress, which may adversely affect long-term treatment outcomes and quality of life.12–18

Engagement in healthy behaviors has been shown to reduce psychological distress and enhance overall well-being. Evidence indicates that health literacy, shaped through such behaviors, is closely associated with cancer-related knowledge and health outcomes.19 Limited health literacy has been linked to carcinogenic behaviors, increased reliance on potentially avoidable healthcare utilization (including hospital admissions and emergency services), and greater economic burden.

It is noteworthy that existing interventions targeting FoP among cancer survivors, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, face-to-face counseling, mindfulness- and acceptance-based therapies, and stepped-care models, have demonstrated favorable effects in alleviating FoP and improving health management among survivors of breast cancer and other malignancies.20–22 Similarly, interventions focusing on improving health literacy, such as tailoring health information delivery through the teach-back method and providing self-management training, have also shown effectiveness in enhancing treatment adherence and reducing psychological burden among cancer patients.23 However, the applicability and effectiveness of these interventions in patients with NPC, particularly during the post‑treatment survivorship period, remain to be further investigated.

Although FoP has been recognized as a prominent issue in NPC survivors, most existing studies focus on its prevalence and psychological correlates. To date, few studies have specifically explored the association between health literacy and FoP in NPC patients, especially during the post-treatment survivorship period. The potential predictive effect of health literacy on dynamic changes in FoP remains unclear. This exploratory study collected longitudinal data from patients with NPC at three time points, 3 months (T1), 6 months (T2), and 12 months (T3) after completion of treatment, to examine the association between health literacy and FoP, with the aim of providing evidence-based support for health education, extended nursing care, and psychological interventions.

Participants and Methods

Study Participants

Patients with NPC admitted to the radiotherapy departments of two affiliated hospitals of Fujian Medical University between August 2023 and December 2024 were included in the study. This study was designed as an exploratory longitudinal investigation without prospective sample size calculation.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) age ≥ 18 years; 2) newly confirmed pathological diagnosis of NPC without metastasis, classified as stage III or higher; 3) completion of induction chemotherapy combined with targeted therapy and radical IMRT, followed by entry into the follow-up period; 4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the survey; and 5) absence of psychiatric disorders with the ability to cooperate with study procedures.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) recurrence of NPC during the follow-up period; 2) presence of concomitant malignancies; 3) language impairment or inability to cooperate with survey completion; or 4) discontinuation of treatment or inability to complete the prescribed therapeutic regimen for any reason.

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Approval Number: 2023KY197). A total of 118 eligible NPC patients were initially enrolled. The number of valid participants at each follow-up time point was 113 at T1, 106 at T2, and 94 at T3. Overall, 24 patients were lost to follow-up during the study period, mainly due to loss of contact and inability to complete the survey. A lagged dataset comprising 188 observations was constructed (T1→T2: 94 observations; T2→T3: 94 observations).

Study Methods

Patient General Information Form

Demographic and clinical data were collected using a researcher-designed form. Variables included sex, age, place of residence, marital status, educational attainment, duration of hospitalization, and health insurance coverage.

Health Literacy Assessment

Health literacy is defined as the capacity of individuals to acquire, comprehend, and apply health information to preserve and enhance their health outcomes. In this study, health literacy was evaluated using the revised Health Literacy Belief Scale developed in a Chinese domestic academic thesis.24 This scale was originally developed and validated in Chinese populations, so it is a domestically developed scale rather than a cross‑culturally adapted scale. This scale has been validated and widely applied in multiple cancer populations, including hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer.25–27 Although it has not been specifically validated in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, its robust performance in general oncology populations supports its applicability in the present study. This instrument comprises 48 items across five dimensions: personal health beliefs, perceived self-efficacy, perceived control, perceived resource utilization, and perceived threat. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, resulting in a maximum possible total score of 240 points. Higher scores indicate stronger comprehension and perception of health-related concepts, reflecting greater health literacy. The test–retest reliability of the scale was 0.889, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.935. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the five dimensions (personal health beliefs, perceived self-efficacy, perceived control, perceived resource utilization, and perceived threat) were 0.866, 0.824, 0.791, 0.891, and 0.891, respectively, indicating acceptable internal consistency for both the overall scale and its subscales. Health literacy levels were categorized based on the score index as follows: high (> 75.0%), moderate (50.0–75.0%), or low (< 50.0%). The score index was calculated using the formula: actual score ÷ theoretical maximum score × 100%. The scale underwent cross-cultural adaptation, and its internal consistency was confirmed, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.935.

FoP Survey

The Chinese version of the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF) was used to evaluate FoP. In NPC, patients face a persistent risk of both local recurrence and distant progression, and often experience fear regarding both outcomes. The concept of FoP encompasses fears of recurrence, metastasis, treatment failure, and symptom deterioration, making it broader in scope and more consistent with the clinical experiences of NPC survivors. Therefore, the FoP-Q-SF has been widely recognized and is frequently used as a valid and appropriate instrument for assessing cancer-related fear in this population. The FoP-Q-SF was originally developed by Mehnert et al28 and subsequently translated into Chinese by Qiyun et al29 The instrument comprises 12 items across two dimensions: social/family concerns and physical health concerns. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, producing a total score ranging from 12 to 60. Higher scores indicate greater FoP, with scores ≥ 34 considered clinically significant and above the normative range. Score ranges of 12–23, 24–36, and 37–60 correspond to mild, moderate, and high fear of FoP, respectively. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.883 for the total scale and 0.812 and 0.829 for the social/family and physical health dimensions, respectively.

Data Collection Methods

Questionnaire surveys were conducted at four time points, baseline (T0), 3 months (T1), 6 months (T2), and 12 months (T3) after completion of radiotherapy, using both online and paper-based formats. Prior to distribution, the objectives and significance of the survey were explained through standardized instructions, and questionnaires were provided following informed consent. Upon completion, responses were reviewed for completeness, and any missing information was supplemented on site when necessary.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3, with the significance level set at α = 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, and only participants with complete data at each time point were included in the statistical analysis.

Descriptive Statistics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were summarized. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables were tested for normality; those following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (interquartile range).

Correlation Analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the linear associations between the total score and dimension scores of health literacy and the score of FoP. A correlation heatmap was generated to visually illustrate the strength of the relationships among variables.

Analysis of the Predictive Effect of Health Literacy on FoP

Lagged linear model: A lagged linear model was constructed to examine the predictive effect of health literacy on subsequent FoP. The model was specified as: Fear_t+1 = β0 + β1Health_t + Covariates + ε, where Fear_t+1 represents the FoP score at the subsequent time point and Health_t represents the health literacy score at the preceding time point. To assess potential clustering at the individual level, we first fitted a linear mixed-effects model and calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient. The results showed an intraclass correlation coefficient estimate of < 0.001 and an individual-level variance of < 0.001, indicating negligible between-subject variability (<5%). Based on these findings, we subsequently applied ordinary least squares regression with cluster-robust standard errors at the individual level. This approach provides consistent and robust parameter estimates for longitudinal data while avoiding convergence issues associated with singular fits in linear mixed-effects models.

Autoregressive model: To further control for the temporal inertia effect of FoP, an autoregressive model was constructed to test the independent predictive effect of health literacy: Fear_t+1 = β0+ β1Fear_t + β2Health_t + Covariates + ε. This model assessed whether health literacy independently predicted subsequent FoP after accounting for prior FoP levels. Due to data availability and study design limitations, key clinical variables such as tumor stage, treatment intensity, and treatment-related toxicity were not adjusted for in the model.

Dimension-Specific Analysis of Health Literacy

To evaluate the predictive effects of different dimensions of health literacy on fear of cancer recurrence, two complementary analytical strategies were applied. (1) Primary analysis: each dimension was entered into the model separately to assess its overall association with fear of recurrence. This analysis was considered exploratory in nature. (2) Supplementary analysis: a multivariable model including all five dimensions was constructed as follows: Fear_next = β0+ β1Belief + β2Ability + β3Control + β4Resource + β5Threat + Covariates + ε, where Fear_next represents the total FoP score at the subsequent time point, and Belief, Ability, Control, Resource, and Threat denote the scores of the five health literacy dimensions. This joint model was used to estimate the independent effect of each dimension while accounting for shared variance among them. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, with VIF < 5 indicating an acceptable level of collinearity.

Subgroup Analysis

Stratified analyses were performed according to sex (male/female) and age groups (<40 years, 40–65 years, >65 years) to evaluate the predictive effect of health literacy on FoP within each subgroup. Interaction tests were conducted to determine whether the interactions between sex or age and health literacy were statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings using two approaches: (1) extreme value exclusion, in which outliers were removed and the models refitted; and (2) time-period analysis, in which the effects of health literacy were examined separately for the T1→T2 and T2→T3 follow-up intervals.

Results

Correlation Analysis Between Health Literacy and Fear of Progression

The results of Pearson correlation analysis showed that the total health literacy score was significantly negatively correlated with the total FoP score at all follow-up time points (P < 0.05). The correlation was strongest at T2 (T1: r = −0.22, P < 0.05; T2: r = −0.39, P < 0.01; T3: r = −0.26, P < 0.05), indicating that higher levels of health literacy were associated with lower levels of fear of progression. Further analysis of the correlations between the dimensions of health literacy and FoP revealed that perceived resource utilization (r = −0.30) and perceived threat (r = −0.20) showed relatively stronger associations with FoP. A visual representation of the correlations among variables is presented in the correlation heatmap (Figure 1).

Unadjusted Negative Predictive Association of Health Literacy on Fear of Progression and Its Dynamic Effect After Controlling for Temporal Inertia

The lagged linear model showed that, after adjusting for demographic covariates including sex, age, education level, and monthly income, health literacy at the previous time point significantly negatively predicted FoP at the subsequent time point (β = −0.126, SE = 0.037, z = −3.37, P < 0.001, 95% CI: −0.199 to −0.053). This finding indicates that health literacy has a significant unadjusted negative predictive association on subsequent FoP. The model explained 12.4% of the total variance in FoP (R2 = 0.124). However, this model is subject to omitted variable bias. Given that prior FoP is strongly associated with both health literacy and subsequent fear levels, excluding it may introduce downward bias in the estimated coefficients of health literacy and artificially exaggerate its apparent protective effect. Therefore, this model provides only an exploratory and unadjusted estimate and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality.

However, after constructing an autoregressive model to control for the temporal inertia effect of prior FoP, the independent predictive effect of health literacy on subsequent FoP was no longer significant (β = 0.009, SE = 0.021, z = 0.44, P = 0.660, 95% CI: −0.032 to 0.051). In contrast, the inertia effect of prior FoP was extremely strong (β = 0.857, SE = 0.056, z = 15.22, P < 0.001). This autoregressive model explained 73.5% of the variance in subsequent FoP (R2 = 0.735). These preliminary findings suggest that fear of disease progression in nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients is highly time-stable The apparent protective effect of health literacy observed in unadjusted models is likely an artifact of omitted variable bias. Health literacy alone is unlikely to independently alter this relatively stable and chronic psychological state over the short term. Detailed results are presented in Table 3 and Figures 2–3.

Associations of Health Literacy Dimensions with Fear of Progression

In the exploratory, unadjusted dimension-specific models where each dimension was entered separately, only the resource utilization dimension showed a significant negative association with subsequent FoP (β = −0.215, P = 0.011), while the other four dimensions were not statistically significant. In the adjusted joint model including all five dimensions simultaneously to account for shared variance, resource utilization remained a significant protective factor, whereas all other dimensions remained non-significant. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no evidence of serious collinearity (VIF range: 1.04–1.20). These findings suggest that resource utilization is the key driving component underlying the association between health literacy and FoP, independent of the other dimensions of health literacy. Subgroup Analysis of Health Literacy Effects on Fear of Progression

Subgroup analyses by sex and age group showed that after controlling for the temporal inertia of FoP, the predictive effect of health literacy on FoP was not statistically significant in any subgroup: Male (β = 0.027, P = 0.334), Female (β = −0.011, P = 0.769), Age <40 years (β = −0.010, P = 0.899,), Age 40–65 years (β = 0.004, P = 0.859). The age subgroup of >65 years was excluded from analysis due to an excessively small sample size (10 patients, corresponding to 20 observations). In addition, the 95% confidence interval for the <40 years subgroup (N = 32) was −0.159 to 0.139. The wide interval indicates limited statistical power in this subgroup analysis, reflecting inherent characteristics of the dataset. Interaction tests indicated that the interaction effects of sex × health literacy and age group × health literacy were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), suggesting that the protective effect of health literacy on FoP does not differ across demographic subgroups. Sensitivity Analysis of Health Literacy Effects on Fear of Progression

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the study’s core findings. After removing outliers and refitting the model (n = 187), the independent predictive effect of health literacy on FoP remained non-significant (β = 0.014, P = 0.507). Analyses by follow-up interval showed that during the T1 → T2 interval, health literacy had a negative but non-significant effect on FoP (β = −0.030, P = 0.318). During the T2 → T3 interval, health literacy had a weak positive but non-significant effect (β = 0.019, P = 0.607). The differences in effect between the two follow-up intervals were not statistically significant. These findings indicate that, regardless of outlier removal or follow-up period, the independent predictive effect of health literacy on FoP after controlling for the temporal inertia of FoP remains non-significant, confirming the stability of the main conclusion and ruling out chance effects. We further tested the interaction between time period and health literacy to examine potential heterogeneity between the two intervals (T1→T2 and T2→T3). The interaction term was not statistically significant (β = 0.088, SE = 0.053, P = 0.098, 95% CI: −0.016 to 0.192). Although the effect directions appeared opposite across the two intervals (negative for T1→T2 and positive for T2→T3), neither estimate reached statistical significance, suggesting that the observed direction reversal is more likely attributable to random variation rather than a true time-varying effect. These findings support the robustness of using a pooled lagged dataset in the main analysis. Detailed results are presented in Table 6 and Figure 7.

Discussion

This exploratory multicenter longitudinal study focused on the post‑treatment survivorship period when NPC patients return to family life, exploring the relationship and predictive effect of health literacy on FoP, and further analyzing the roles of core dimensions of health literacy and population heterogeneity. The results reveal the epidemiological characteristics of health literacy and FoP in this population and clarify their dynamic association, providing empirical evidence for clinical health education and psychological interventions for NPC survivors.

Epidemiological Features of Health Literacy and Fear of Progression in NPC Patients During the Post‑treatment Survivorship Period

NPC is a distinct subtype of head and neck cancer closely associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection, genetic susceptibility, and a unique immune microenvironment.1,2 Driven by EBV latency, oncogenic protein expression, and immune evasion, nasopharyngeal carcinoma is characterized by high invasiveness, early regional spread, and a relatively high two-year recurrence risk.30 These biological features help explain why survivors of NPC often experience pronounced and persistent fear of disease progression after treatment. The study showed that at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after curative treatment, NPC patients’ health literacy scores were consistently at a moderate level, while their FoP scores remained high, consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of cancer in China.

Cancer is widely recognized as a lifestyle-related disease, with approximately 50% of cancer-related deaths in China attributable to unhealthy lifestyle factors.31 Preventive strategies are closely associated with individual health literacy levels.19 In a study of 1,643 colorectal cancer survivors, Husson et al32 found that limited health literacy was associated with poorer self-management abilities and reduced quality of life. Health literacy plays a crucial role in addressing health-related challenges in cancer survivors and reducing mortality risk.33 The moderate level of health literacy in NPC patients may relate to insufficient dissemination of NPC-specific health knowledge, limited ability to access and utilize professional medical information, and a lack of targeted health education during the transition from hospital to home. Additionally, the American Cancer Society’s Cancer Survivors Nutrition and Physical Activity Guidelines highlight that developing appropriate nutrition and exercise programs and promoting shared health activities among survivors can improve quality of life and health outcomes.34 Nurses play a key role in facilitating patient engagement and enhancing health literacy.35 A nurse-led, patient-participatory continuity-of-care model is recommended as a strategy to strengthen health literacy, improve perceived quality of care, and ultimately enhance overall quality of life.

In contrast, patients’ FoP remained high, which is closely related to the clinical features of NPC. The local recurrence rate of NPC is 10%–15%, with nearly half of recurrences occurring within two years after treatment.6,7 The uncertainty of recurrence and the potential burden of further treatment impose sustained psychological stress on patients during follow-up, which aligns with research showing that approximately 49% of adult cancer survivors experience clinically significant fear of cancer recurrence.36 Zheng et al9 conducted face-to-face semi-structured interviews with 30 NPC patients who received particle therapy and found strong fear of progression, reflecting patients’ uncertainty and insecurity regarding the disease. This underscores the importance of accurate knowledge about NPC and confidence in treatment efficacy in modulating recurrence fear. Furthermore, elevated FoP may lead to lower quality of life, disrupted daily activities, excessive attention to bodily changes, increased depressive symptoms, and limitations in future planning. These outcomes are influenced by psychological resilience, disease-related cognition, and confidence in treatment effectiveness. Supporting this, Li et al14 found that NPC patients with higher psychological resilience had 34% lower FoP scores than those with lower resilience, as resilience helps individuals cope with disease-related stress and reduces catastrophic thinking about recurrence.

Correlation and Dynamic Predictive Effect of Health Literacy on Fear of Progression

Pearson correlation analysis showed that health literacy was negatively correlated with FoP at all time points, with the strongest correlation observed at 6 months post-treatment (r = −0.39, P < 0.01). This confirms the negative relationship between health literacy and FoP in NPC patients, consistent with findings in breast cancer survivors.37 The lagged linear model further revealed that, after controlling for demographic covariates, health literacy had a significant unadjusted negative predictive association on subsequent FoP, suggesting that improving health literacy in the short term may reduce the risk of FoP. This identifies health literacy as an important protective factor against FoP in NPC patients, consistent with findings in head and neck cancer research by Clarke et al38 However, due to the omission of prior FoP, a strong confounding factor, these results are likely biased. This omitted variable bias distorts the estimated coefficient of health literacy and may generate a spurious “protective effect” that does not reflect a true independent association.

However, after controlling for the inertia effect of prior FoP, the independent predictive effect of health literacy on subsequent FoP disappeared, while the inertia effect of FoP was very strong (β = 0.857, P < 0.001), explaining 73.5% of the variance in subsequent FoP. This is a key novel finding of the present study, indicating that FoP in NPC patients exhibits high temporal stability and, once established, becomes a chronic psychological state. Two longitudinal studies of head and neck cancer patients similarly observed sustained high-FoP trajectories over three years, further confirming the chronic nature of FoP. The inertia effect suggests that once FoP forms, it becomes a self-perpetuating cognitive-emotional state.11,39 According to the cognitive processing model of FoP proposed by Fardell et al,40 FoP is not a simple emotional reaction but a persistent cognitive bias formed through continuous attention to disease recurrence, which provides a theoretical explanation for the inertia effect observed in this study. This cognitive state is difficult to alter through a single health literacy intervention in the short term. These findings enrich our understanding of the dynamic characteristics of FoP among cancer survivors and emphasize that clinical interventions for FoP cannot rely on a single approach. Instead, long-term, multidimensional strategies incorporating psychological cognitive restructuring are required. This is further supported by the unique biological characteristics of head and neck cancers. In EBV-driven NPC, persistent immune surveillance, potential viral reactivation, and delayed treatment-related toxicities generate ongoing disease threat signals, thereby contributing to a stable trajectory of fear of recurrence.11 Accordingly, fear of recurrence reflects not only psychological adjustment processes but also an adaptive response to a persistent, biologically grounded risk of disease recurrence.

Independent Effects of Health Literacy Dimensions on Fear of Progression

Dimension-specific analysis revealed that only the perceived resource utilization dimension of health literacy had a significant protective effect on FoP (β = −0.215, P = 0.011), while the perceived threat dimension showed a marginal effect (P = 0.058). The other dimensions (ie. personal health beliefs, perceived self-efficacy, and perceived control) did not exhibit significant effects. This result identifies perceived resource utilization as the core driver through which health literacy alleviates FoP in NPC patients, a mechanism closely related to the clinical management features of NPC. Due to the extended follow-up period in NPC, patients need to regularly complete imaging exams, blood tests, and adverse effect management. Effectively accessing and utilizing medical resources (eg. professional consultations, standardized follow-up channels, reliable health information) helps patients accurately monitor their health status, reducing uncertainty and, consequently, fear of recurrence.41 In contrast, the other dimensions of health literacy are more reflective of internal cognitive and psychological characteristics. Their potential effect on reducing FoP may require long-term psychological interventions and behavioral training to manifest,40–45 which likely explains why significant effects were not observed during the short-term follow-up of this study.

Subgroup Analysis Results and Clinical Implications

Stratified analyses by sex and age groups showed that, after accounting for the inertia effect of prior FoP, health literacy was not significantly associated with FoP in any subgroup. In addition, no significant interaction was observed between health literacy and either sex or age. Notably, the <40 years subgroup exhibited a wide 95% confidence interval, indicating limited statistical power, while the >65 years subgroup was excluded due to small sample size, which further limits the robustness of age-stratified conclusions. These exploratory findings suggest that the potential association between health literacy and FoP may not meaningfully differ across demographic subgroups in nasopharyngeal carcinoma survivors. Pending confirmation from larger-scale studies, health literacy-based interventions aimed at reducing FoP may be reasonably implemented as a general strategy without mandatory sex- or age-specific tailoring, which could help simplify clinical implementation and improve the efficiency of patient education in this population.

Study Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, sample size and statistical power: This exploratory study was conducted without a prospective sample size calculation. The relatively small sample may be underpowered for dimension-specific and subgroup analyses, and all corresponding results should be interpreted as exploratory. Second, sample size and representativeness: The study included a relatively small sample (94 patients), all recruited from two affiliated hospitals of Fujian Medical University, representing a single geographic region. This limits the generalizability of the findings to NPC patients in other regions of China. Third, follow-up duration and time points: The follow-up lasted only 12 months with three time points, which is insufficient to assess the long-term predictive effect of health literacy on FoP and does not fully capture the dynamic trajectory of FoP in the later follow-up period. Fourth, unadjusted clinical confounding factors: only sociodemographic variables were included as covariates, Important psychosocial factors (eg. social support, coping strategies, family functioning) and clinical factors (eg. tumor stage, treatment intensity, toxicity burden) were not considered. These factors may interact with health literacy and FoP. In addition, health literacy and FoP were measured using single questionnaire scales. The study lacked objective clinical indicators and qualitative methods that could provide deeper validation and explanation of the findings. Lastly, as an exploratory observational observational study, this research only establishes correlations and predictive effects between health literacy and FoP. It does not clarify the specific effectiveness or optimal design of health literacy interventions to reduce FoP. Large-sample, multicenter studies are needed to enhance external validity; longer follow-up is necessary to explore long-term mechanisms. Additional psychosocial and clinical factors should be included to build comprehensive predictive models. Randomized controlled trials should also be conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of health literacy interventions, providing stronger evidence for clinical management of NPC patients.

Future Research Directions

Based on the findings of this exploratory observational study, including the high temporal stability of FoP and the lack of an independent predictive effect of health literacy after accounting for prior FoP inertia, and considering the clinical need for effective FoP management and health literacy improvement in NPC follow-up care, several future research directions and hypotheses are proposed to inform subsequent empirical investigations. It should be emphasized that practice-oriented recommendations in this specific area require validation through randomized controlled trials.

Integrate FoP screening into routine follow-up: Incorporate FoP assessment into standard NPC follow-up protocols to accurately identify patients at high risk of FoP, enabling timely early psychological evaluation and targeted intervention to prevent accumulation of negative emotions.

Conclusion

This exploratory longitudinal study followed 94 NPC patients who completed radical treatment for 12 months to investigate the dynamic relationship between health literacy and FoP during the post‑treatment survivorship period. The results showed that health literacy was at a moderate level, while FoP remained consistently high at all follow-up time points. Health literacy and FoP were negatively correlated across all time points. Health literacy exerted a significant unadjusted negative predictive association on subsequent FoP; however, FoP exhibited high temporal stability, indicating that once established, it becomes a chronic psychological state. Among the five dimensions of health literacy, only perceived resource utilization was a core driver in alleviating FoP. The protective effect of health literacy on FoP showed no significant heterogeneity across sex or age subgroups. These findings indicate a significant dynamic association that health literacy is a key protective factor against FoP in NPC patients, and enhancing patients’ ability to access and utilize medical resources is the primary target to reduce FoP. The high temporal stability of FoP suggests that a single health literacy intervention is insufficient to alter this chronic psychological state. Based on these observational findings, it is reasonable to suggest that clinical practice may consider an early integrated strategy that combines health literacy enhancement with psychological and cognitive support to address the persistent fear of FoP in this population.

Nursing staff should lead targeted health education centered on resource utilization, incorporate FoP screening into routine follow-up, and establish multidisciplinary support systems to provide continuous health and psychological support during the post‑treatment survivorship period. This approach can effectively reduce FoP and improve quality of life among NPC survivors.
