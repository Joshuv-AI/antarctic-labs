 Executive Summary
In October 2024, Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) was tasked to conduct an
independent review of groundwater data and assess the monitored natural attenuation (MNA)
performance related to the Chemicals, Metals, and Pesticides (CMP) Pits Operable Unit (OU)
located in the central portion of the Savannah River Site (SRS). The SRNL assessment of MNA
entailed an independent analysis of groundwater concentration data, groundwater elevation data,
available surface water concentration data, soil concentration data, and relevant historical
geological characterization logs for the CMP Pits OU. Historical data tables and records were
obtained from both the Savannah River Nuclear Solutions - Area Completion Projects (SRNS-
ACP) team and South Carolina State University (SCSU) and condensed into new data sets by
the SRNL project team for more targeted analysis of MNA performance characteristics. All data
pertaining to groundwater concentration, surface water concentration, and groundwater elevation
were restricted to collection dates after any known active remediation for the CMP OU.
The ultimate objective of many remedial strategies, including MNA, is to reduce the potential risk
to environmental and human exposure posed by site contaminants. Natural attenuation achieves
this through the destruction, transformation, or reduction of contaminant concentration or mobility
by passive processes. Typically, these processes are carried out through the biodegradation
caused by microbial species, sorption of contaminants to soil particles, and/or the dispersion of
contaminants throughout an aquifer unit. Following the cessation of active soil vapor recovery in
2009 at the CMP OU, the strategy of MNA with institutional controls for continued remediation
was chosen in agreement with the South Carolina Department Environmental Services (SCDES)
and the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). The effectiveness of volatile organic
compound (VOC) MNA can be evaluated in the terms of lines of evidence (LOE) that indicate this
process taking place (USEPA, 1999). The EPA recognizes three primary lines of evidence which
include:
 1. Clear trends of decreasing contaminant mass or concentration in historical groundwater
 and/or soil chemistry data. Where groundwater contamination is found, these trends
 should not be attributable to plume migration.
 2. Indirect indication of natural attenuation processes using hydrogeologic and geochemical
 data (geochemical footprint). This data may be used to quantify rates of contaminant
 sorption, dilution, or volatilization of contaminants of concern COCs.
 3. Direct demonstration of microbial species, known to degrade COCs, through microcosm
 studies. DNA analysis of microbial samples to qualify the presence of these microbes may
 be used for direct evidence. The presence of degradation compounds, electron donors,
 and electron acceptors may also be used indicate the degradation of COCs via biological
 processes.
The assessment of MNA for the CMP Pits OU focuses on these three lines of evidence.
Remediation Action Objectives (RAOs) were also identified during this assessment. These RAOs
pertain to the site-specific objectives detailed in historical records of decision for the CMP Pits
OU. Upon review of available recorded decisions and annual reporting for active remediation
results and MNA, three RAOs were identified for the CMP Pits OU:
 1. Prevent human exposure to contaminated groundwater above Maximum Contaminant
 Levels (MCLs) or Regulatory Guidelines (RGs).

 vi

 2. Reduce the COC concentrations in the groundwater plume to MCLs and reduce the size
 of the plume footprint.
 3. Prevent discharge of contaminated groundwater to surface water at concentrations above
 MCLs.
All RAOs were found to be currently fulfilled by institutional controls, remediation efforts, and
verifiable NA processes.
Subsets of existing monitoring wells were chosen to define the lateral and vertical extent of the
plume area for all primary COCs. These subsets of wells excluded any monitoring wells outside
of the plume area, prevented redundancy in data analysis, and allowed for a simplified
representation of overall contaminant trends to be made. Monitoring well subsets included source
area wells, intermediate area wells, downgradient wells, and distal wells relative to the
contaminant plume and the former burial unit. The locations of each monitoring well subset
correspond to the COC plume footprint with reference to its assumed direction of movement in
the subsurface. Where applicable, well subsets were created for each aquifer unit as they have
been defined by SRNS Effectiveness Monitoring Reports (EMRs) (e.g. SRNS, 2024). Aquifer units
include the Transmissive Zone (TZ), the Middle Aquifer Zone (MAZ), the Lower Aquifer Zone
(LAZ), and the Gordon Aquifer (GA).
The COCs used for this study include perchloroethylene (PCE), trichloroethylene (TCE), cis-
dichloroethylene (DCE), vinyl chloride (VC), 1-4 dioxane, and lindane. The initial assessment
technique included conventional attenuation calculations as dictated by the EPA first order decay
rate calculation guidance. This involved using data trends to evaluate concentration vs. time and
concentration vs. distance, as outlined in the EPA Groundwater Issue: Calculation and Use of
First-Order Rate Constant for Monitoring Natural Attenuation Studies (USEPA, 1999). The goal
of this approach was to estimate possible compliance point timelines and historical plume
expansion or contraction characteristics. This form of conventional analysis was found to be
unsuitable due the characteristics of the geologic setting and increasing concentrations of primary
contaminants along the primary flow path of the overall plume footprint. Long term data trends
were constructed for each monitoring well using sampling data pertaining to each COC and
groundwater elevation data to gain insight to plume characteristics where the aforementioned
calculations were not applied. Mann-Kendall analysis was performed for all available monitoring
wells with sufficient data to confirm the trends of primary COCs.
New cross section and isoconcentration contour maps were drafted to depict COC plume extent
and migration over time. These figures and maps were focused on the “center line” of plume
migration for primary COCs that leads from the former CMP Pit burial sites, northeast toward the
Pen Branch Wetland area. The vertical extent of COCs was found to be of critical importance for
understanding plume characteristics and the overall migration of contaminants through each
aquifer zone and the semi-confining layers that separate them.
Groundwater, surface water, and soil data collected by ACP and SCSU in the Pen Branch
Wetlands were compiled for study by the SRNL team in conjunction with the overall CMP Pits
MNA assessment. Sampling procedures were reviewed, and recent conclusions made by the
SCSU team were also evaluated. Data gaps identified during both the overall MNA assessment
and Pen Branch wetland assessment pertain to insufficient groundwater and hydraulic gradient
characterization information available along the southern edge of the Pen Branch stream valley.

 vii

Significant conclusions from this evaluation include:
 • Residual contaminant leaching from soils beneath the former burial unit of the CMP Pits
 OU continue to contribute to groundwater concentrations.
 • The 2009 to 2024 data assessment shows plume stabilization or contraction is occurring
 on three of the four plume edges for all COCs.
 • Historical COC data trend analysis shows increasing groundwater concentration trends
 within shallow wells beneath the former burial unit, northeast and downward, to LAZ wells
 proximal to the southern edge of the Pen Branch Wetland from 2009 to 2024.
 • Hydrogeologic conditions in monitoring wells show an overall downward vertical gradient
 in the CMP Pits OU on the southern side of the Pen Branch wetland, which indicate that
 contaminated groundwater in the LAZ may not be in full communication with the Pen
 Branch stream. More data is needed conclude the true influence that COCs within the LAZ
 may have upon surface water in the future.
 • Chlorinated volatile organic compounds (cVOCs) that have reached the shallow soils
 beneath Pen Branch have been adequately remediated by natural attenuation, and
 currently there have been no detections of contaminants within the Pen Branch surface
 waters above regulatory limits.
 • Shallow soil and groundwater sampling data collected by SCSU show declining
 concentrations of cVOCs from 2018 to present, which show that surface waters of the Pen
 Branch are currently protected by the degradation capacity of shallow wetlands soils.
 • Degradation products such as DCE are first seen in transmissive zone wells CMP-37D
 and CMP-38D located within the wetlands, further south of the SCSU sampling locations
 and this provides evidence of natural attenuation in a wider zone surrounding the Pen
 Branch stream.
Evaluation of hydrogeologic and geochemical data (geochemical footprint) as an indirect
indication of natural attenuation processes (LOE 2) showed conditions are better suited to support
aerobic degradation mechanisms over anaerobic mechanisms throughout the wider plume
footprint. Aquifer zones in close proximity to the Pen Branch Wetlands were found to be conducive
to anerobic mechanisms. Measurement of specific conductivity and turbidity throughout the well
network also support that conditions are present that are conducive to degradation of cVOCs.
Addition of DO measurements to the groundwater sampling scheme is recommended to aid in
this evaluation, however.
Recent microbial sampling within the wetlands identified the presence of microbial species known
to degrade COCs, like dehalogenation bacteria, which reinforces the conclusion that PCE and
TCE biodegradation is an appreciable source of natural attenuation within the hyporheic zone of
the Pen Branch wetlands. However, incorporation of further, microbial monitoring/analysis to
better characterize the broader subsurface microbial ecosystem structure and function is
recommended to support EPA’s third LOE for MNA.
Following this independent data assessment of the CMP Pits by SRNL, recommendations for
further investigation or action were formulated for consideration by SRNS. Data gaps that were
found to exist are presented in this study with potential options to address them. Primary findings
include the absence of native bioremediation within impacted aquifer zones of the CMP Pits area
(excluding the Pen Branch wetlands), the vertical movement of COCs from the MAZ to the TZ,
and the tangential interaction of plume migration with the Pen Branch Wetland. Data gaps were

identified for deeper groundwater (primarily LAZ) characterization beneath the Pen Branch
Wetland along the northeastern extent of plume footprint and southern edge of the Pen Branch
Wetland. Filling the identified data gaps would support future remedial decisions.

1.0 Introduction

1.1 Description of Study

In October 2024, SRNL began an independent assessment of ongoing remediation of COCs for
the SRS specific to MNA at the CMP Pits OU. This assessment reviewed the historical
contaminant concentration and groundwater elevation trends following historical active
remediation performed at the CMP Pits. The results of this assessment are used to identify plume
expansion and contraction, and the migration of contaminants, over time. Data gaps and potential
analysis approaches that would support better plume characterization are likewise included in this
report.
1.2 CMP Pits Background

The CMP Pits OU is comprised of seven former, unlined, waste disposal units that received non-
radioactive waste from August 1971 to February 1979 and were designated as a regulated waste
unit on the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act/Comprehensive Environmental Response,
Compensation and Liability Act (RCRA/CERCLA) unit list for the SRS Federal Facility Agreement
in 1989 (SRNS, 2024). The former burial unit is located approximately 1500 ft south of Pen Branch
and situated on a knoll 310 ft above mean sea level. Groundwater occurs approximately 90 feet
below land surface at the CMP Pit location. During active use, the CMP Pits accepted waste laden
with organic chemicals, metals, and pesticides which included polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs).
The pits were excavated to remove waste and contaminated soil, preceding the installation of a
liner, and filled to grade with clean soil in 1984 (SRNS, 2017). Presently, contamination remains
in soils of the vadose zone below the extent of the historical excavated area.
Several campaigns of remedial actions (RAs) and interim remedial actions (IRAs) have taken
place following the original excavation of the CMP Pits OU in 1984. Land surface area
management to control and reduce erosion or surface water intrusion to contaminated soils was
conducted by the emplacement of soil spread cover, riprap spread cover, grass reseeding, and
erosion protection fabric in 1996. Ballast area soils remediation, including further excavations and
enhanced bioremediation of excavated soil, were performed in the years 2000, 2001, and 2005.
Soil vapor extraction (SVE) was employed from 2001 until 2009 and utilized passive, active, and
heated/active soil vapor extraction techniques (SRNS, 2017). Active remediation techniques were
discontinued in 2009 when concentration data in recovery air and subsurface soil samples
indicated that continued operation would produce diminishing returns. MNA was selected as the
following remediation strategy for the CMP Pits OU (SRNS, 2017).
In compliance with the 2004 Record of Decision (ROD), following subsequent active remediation
events until 2009 to further reduce contaminants within the vadose zone, the current CMP Pits
remedial strategy comprises semiannual groundwater and surface water sampling in conjunction
with MNA to address remaining contamination (WSRC, 2004).
1.3 Current Remediation Objectives and Institutional Controls

Upon conclusion of active remediation in 2009 the remedial alternative of MNA was chosen for
the CMP Pits OU. Semi-annual sampling has continued following this decision to fulfil the
monitoring aspect of this remedial alternative with annual reporting of results to the SCDES and

 1

USEPA (SRNS, 2017). Monitoring results have continually been compared to modelling
predictions to fulfil the stated objective of the 2004 Record of Decision to “ensure that the
groundwater discharge does not result in any statistically significant increase of constituents from
the groundwater in the surface water at the point of entry or at any point where there is reason to
believe accumulation of constituents might occur downstream” (WSRC, 2004). Upon review of
available recorded decisions and annual reporting for active remediation results and MNA, three
Remedial Action Objectives (RAOs) were identified for the CMP Pits OU:
 1. Prevent human exposure to contaminated groundwater above MCLs or Regulatory
 Guidelines (RGs). Institutional controls implemented by SRS to restrict public and on-site
 worker access to, or near, the CMP Pits OU and within the SRS boundary were currently
 found to fulfil this objective.
 2. Reduce the COC concentrations in the groundwater plume to MCLs and reduce the size
 of the plume footprint. This objective was found to be ongoing. COC concentrations are
 decreasing in many shallow TZ and MAZ monitoring wells and some LAZ monitoring wells.
 However, a portion of LAZ monitoring results show increasing concentration trends and
 the plume footprint in this aquifer zone has expanded to the north and northeast following
 active remediation cessation in 2009.
 3. Prevent discharge of contaminated groundwater to surface water at concentrations above
 MCLs. To date, this objective has been achieved. No sampling data of surface water
 shows exceedances of MCLs. Appreciable natural degradation of cVOCs, proven to be
 taking place within the Pen Branch wetlands, in combination with previous remediation
 are likely responsible for this objective’s status.

1.4 CMP Pits Local Geology and Hydrostratigraphy

Two aquifers of the Floridan Aquifer System are impacted to differing degrees by COC sources
originating from the CMP Pits OU former burial unit. The aquifers are the Upper Three Runs
Aquifer (UTRA) and the Gordon Aquifer (GA). The Gordon Confining Unit separates the UTRA
and GA. Both the UTRA and GA are composed of unconsolidated marine and fluvial sediments
of the Atlantic Coastal Plain which underlie the SRS (Aadland and Bledsoe, 1990).
The majority of contaminant plume migration from the CMP Pits OU takes place within the UTRA.
MNA processes also take place within this aquifer. The UTRA is divided into three aquifer zones
by two discontinuous clay layers designated the tan clay confining zone (TCCZ) and the tan clay
lower clay (TCLC). The TCCZ and TCLC separate the UTRA into the transmissive zone (TZ),
middle aquifer zone (MAZ), and the lower aquifer zone (LAZ). Both the TCCZ and TCLC are
zones of primarily clayey slit, with low hydraulic conductivity relative to the surrounding soils of
the UTRA. Historically, the TCCZ has not been recognized as a confining layer extensive enough
to be of note when considering the UTRA in the southern portion of the SRS area, with only two
aquifer sub-zones (upper and lower) being recognized and separated by the “tan clay” low-
permeability zone (WSRC, 1995). The TCCZ and TCLC are composed of similar “tan clay” sandy
clay/silt material with hummocky structures. Though thicker on average than the TCCZ, the TCLC
is also considered a semi confining unit that is leaky or even absent in places over much of the
SRS (SRNS, 2015). COC storage in and migration through each semi-confining layer is known to
take place and is routinely monitored by the existing monitoring well network throughout the CMP
Pits OU where samples are collected from the vertical extent of the UTRA. The Gordon Confining
Unit – or “green clay” – separates the UTRA and the Gordon Aquifer as a competent aquitard.

 2

TZ, MAZ, and LAZ sediment is moderately to poorly sorted, coarse to medium-grained sands.
Within the CMP Pits OU, the TZ is predominantly unsaturated. The groundwater surface reaches
this zone intermittently throughout the CMP Pits OU, primarily beneath the former burial unit and
adjacent to the Pen Branch stream. The MAZ and LAZ are the primary saturated aquifer sub-units
where COC migration occurs at the CMP Pits OU, within the UTRA, and are composed of similar
sediments to the TZ.

2.0 Monitored Natural Attenuation Assessment Criteria

The effectiveness of VOC MNA can be evaluated in the terms of LOEs that indicate this process
taking place (USEPA, 1999). The EPA recognizes three primary lines of evidence which include:
 1. Clear trends of decreasing contaminant mass or concentration in historical groundwater
 and/or soil chemistry data. Where groundwater contamination is found, these trends
 should not be attributable to plume migration.
Historical groundwater data that demonstrate a clear and meaningful trend of decreasing
contaminant mass and/or concentration over time and the presence of degradation (daughter)
products at appropriate monitoring points. This typically includes graphical techniques using the
VOC data and statistical tests, such as the Mann-Kendall test.

3.0 Monitored Natural Attenuation Assessment Methodology

3.1 EPA First Order Decay Assessment Criteria

Time-series concentration data at individual wells and plume-wide trends can be used to evaluate
the stability of the area of cVOC impact in groundwater and show that contaminant concentrations
or mass are decreasing over time. Following historical data compilation for monitoring wells,
contaminant trend analysis was performed to determine if a standardized approach to MNA
assessment could be applied to the CMP Pits OU. Using historical groundwater chemistry data
to demonstrate decreasing mass or concentration over time is first tier site-specific information to
be assessed in a MNA effectiveness evaluation (U.S. EPA, 1999). Three forms of first order data
are most useful for MNA assessment.

 4

3.2 Concentration vs. Time Constants

Concentration vs. time constants are used to estimate the length of time concentrations of
contaminants within a single monitoring point will take to decrease to a remediation goal. This
constant was assessed first for this MNA assessment of the CMP Pits OU and were determined
to be of limited utility for the following reasons:

 • These rate constants cannot be applied to wells where contaminant trends are not
 decreasing. The number of increasing contaminant trends seen in wells assessed for each
 aquifer zone reduces the applicability of this technique to wells already known to be on
 the edges of the plume footprint, where contraction or expansion is already assessed
 through trend analysis and visualized in isoconcentration figures.
 • Increasing trends of the same contaminants in source area wells show that new
 contaminant mass has been introduced to the overall plume during the period of
 assessment. These calculations only estimate the time a current trend may take to reach
 a compliance point (ground water protection standard (GWPS) of 5 µg/L for PCE and
 TCE), not how long new contaminant mass may take to influence the data trend of any
 single monitoring well. In summary, this type of decay calculation only provides an
 extrapolation of what a current trend would be if, or until, new contaminant migration
 influences this trend can be made.
Due to these reasons, concentration vs. time constants were of limited utility for the wider CMP
Pits OU MNA assessment.
3.3 Concentration vs. Distance Bulk Attenuation Rate Constants

Concentration vs. distance bulk attenuation constants estimate the distance a plume may travel
before reaching a set remediation goal for a certain contaminant. Contaminant concentrations
and hydraulic gradient values for wells determined to be along the axis of a plume travel path, in
conjunction with hydrogeologic parameters of the aquifer, are used to calculate this travel length.
These rate calculations were found to be of very limited applicability to the CMP Pits OU MNA
assessment for several reasons:
 • Bulk attenuation constants can only be applied to a single plume zone and are designed
 to assess an unconfined aquifer zone with a single contiguous plume. The use of bulk
 attenuation rate constants to assess the TZ is not possible due to the intermittent
 saturation of this aquifer zone.
 • Bulk attenuation constants do not account for COC migration into separated aquifer
 zones. These calculations depend upon a continuance of COCs from a source zone to
 the furthest downgradient monitoring point. A constant calculated in this fashion for any
 single aquifer unit, such as the MAZ, will by its nature be inaccurate as some amount of
 COCs move through the low permeable TCLC and into the LAZ. COCs that are lost
 through the TCLC are then not accounted for in down gradient wells of the MAZ, resulting
 in inaccurate bulk attenuation calculation results. In essence, this method cannot
 differentiate between attenuation, and contaminant mass loss to another aquifer zone.
 • This form of plume duration assessment is also insufficient to account for different
 hydraulic gradient directions within even semi-separated aquifer units. Hydraulic gradient
 and direction differ between the TZ, MAZ, and LAZ of the UTRA. Therefore, a wholistic

 5

 approach that involves all aquifer units to determine a plume travel distance (i.e. the
 removal of the TCCZ and TCLC for overall UTRA assessment) will also provide
 inaccurate results of unknown significance.
Due to these reasons, concentration vs. distance bulk attenuation calculations were not utilized
for the CMP Pits OU MNA assessment.
3.4 Biodegradation Rate Constants

Biodegradation rate constants characterize the impact of biodegradation on contaminant
concentrations within a plume zone. Biodegradation of contaminants within the CMP Pits OU is
known to be primarily limited to areas within the Pen Branch wetlands (SRNS, 2024). Quantifiable
degradation products for the most widespread COC, PCE, are not seen in any significant
quantities upgradient of the wetlands. TCE is largely spread throughout the CMP Pits OU,
however, its presence is not explained by the anerobic degradation of PCE. Shallow source area
wells indicate that TCE is a co-contaminant and not a product of PCE degradation. Additionally,
trend analysis of primary COCs is adequate to determine advection and dispersion processes.
Biodegradation attenuation calculations also typically utilize microbial analysis of the wider plume
area and data of this type is not available at the time of this assessment. Notably, no aerobic
bacteria, capable of cometabolism of cVOCs, characterization is known to have been conducted
within the CMP Pits OU. Bacteria containing the oxygenase enzyme have been documented to
degrade cVOCs through cometabolism (Wilson et. al., 2019). Due to these reasons,
biodegradation rate constants were not utilized for the overall CMP Pits OU MNA assessment.

3.5 Trend Analysis – Concentration Mapping

To develop a model of plume characteristics, an extensive trend analysis was conducted. Using
the appropriate and representative monitoring points available and routine groundwater sample
collection data to track dissolved phase contaminants over time, SRNL investigated the
contaminant trends for the primary COCs for the CMP Pits OU. Concentration and groundwater
elevation vs. time graphs were drafted for all chosen wells within each UTRA aquifer zone to
complete this trend analysis.
New cross sections and isoconcentration maps were created to visualize plume characteristics
over time. An overhead view of lateral COC plume extent is shown in isoconcentration maps for
year 2024 for each UTRA zone. To show the vertical COC plume extent, cross sections were
created using wells along the center line of COC migration, and width cross sections were created
using wells perpendicular to the center line of COC migration.
Multi-year contaminant concentration trends were analyzed for each chosen monitoring well in
each UTRA zone for each primary COC. Grouping of monitoring wells designated ‘source’,
‘intermediate’, ‘distal’, and ‘downgradient’ were created for organizational purposes for MNA
assessment, within each UTRA zone. Each grouping corresponds to differing portions of the
plume footprint.

 • Source zone wells were organized from wells where the highest historical COC
 concentrations have occurred or likely originated (i.e. the former burial unit area). Data
 trends within the source area monitor the potential influx of new contaminant mass into an
 aquifer unit.

 6

 • Intermediate zone wells were organized from wells within the plume footprint and
 downgradient of the source area within the central portion of the plume. Intermediate wells
 monitor the movement of contaminants downgradient of the source zone and can be used
 to differentiate between an expanding plume or a stagnant-migrating plume.
 • Distal zone wells were organized from wells upgradient and side-gradient of the plume
 footprint to determine potential plume expansion and contraction characteristics.
 • Downgradient zone wells were organized from wells on the leading edge of the plume

4.0 Plume Attenuation Assessment

The evaluation of plume attenuation is dependent upon contaminant trend analysis. The nature
of COC concentration trends within the plume footprint provides the most relevant and useful data
for the assessment of MNA processes. Monitoring wells that have years of associated analytical
sampling and groundwater elevation data points provide the necessary information to complete
an overall trend analysis. Where certain trends exist also provides insight into the likelihood and
nature of contaminant migration and MNA processes that may take place within a plume. For
instance, increasing contaminant concentrations over time in monitoring wells known to be within
the historical source zone of COCs, such as adjacent to the former burial unit in this investigation,
may indicate that new contaminant mass is being transferred into the groundwater and the
removal contaminants within shallow soils is incomplete. Conversely, if source area monitoring
well trends of COCs are in decline and more downgradient COC trends are increasing, this may
indicate that degradation of COCs is limited and concentrated areas of the plume are simply
migrating with the hydraulic gradient within an aquifer. If the latter case is identified (where
performance monitoring shows the plume is not stable or shrinking), implementation of
enhancement options to increase attenuation and/or reduce loading may be considered. If no
enhancement options are viable, however, further source and/or primary plume treatment could
be necessary, depending on remedial action objectives (ITRC, 2008).
4.1 Plume Attenuation Assessment – Mann Kendall Analysis

Nonparametric tests such as the Mann-Kendall test are suitable for analyzing time-series
concentration data that do not follow a normal distribution. Nonparametric methods focus on the
location of the probability distribution of the sampled population rather than the specific
parameters of the population. The Mann-Kendall test for trend is a nonparametric test that has no
distribution assumptions, and irregularly spaced measurement periods and missing data are
permitted. The advantage of this approach involves cases where outliers in the data would
produce biased estimates when using parametric trend tests (such as a least squares estimated
slope). The Mann-Kendall statistic (S) measures the trend in the data and can indicate the
potential presence of increasing or decreasing concentrations at a given location. A confidence
in the contaminant trends, which represents the probability that the contaminant concentrations
are increasing or decreasing, can also be estimated with this method.
A trend analysis was performed on specified monitoring wells using data from 2009 to 2024 to
determine the distribution of PCE and TCE concentrations over this length of time. This was
performed for PCE/TCE concentrations for the three UTRA aquifer zones and for all four well
zones (source, intermediate, downgradient, and distal) where applicable. A Mann-Kendall

 7

analysis for all chosen wells was conducted to determine the 2009 to 2024 concentration trends
within COC data. Results of this analysis are presented in Table 2.
A Mann-Kendall analysis is a non-parametric test that evaluates the presence of a monotonic
upward or downward trend in a data set over time. The trend of historical groundwater data over
time is evidence for the evaluation of contaminant attenuation. Concentration data from individual
wells for each COC were utilized for this trend analysis. Upon completion of the test, each well is
assigned a confidence factor and a trend qualifier to signify an increasing trend (I), decreasing
trend (D), or if more data is required to determine a trend (MDN). If a well was determined to meet
the criteria for MDN classification, it was still accounted for in other analysis methods, such as
plume contraction/expansion analysis and iso-concentration/cross section contouring.
All available data was used for this analysis. Where non-detected (ND) values were seen in the
compiled data tables, the following criteria were used.
 • If ND values were less than 10% of the total number of data points, half values for each
 detection limit were used in their place.
 • If ND values exceeded 10%, but were less than 50%, the Mann-Kendall analysis was
 conducted twice, once with omitted ND entries in the data set and then again with half
 detection limit values. The Mann-Kendall analysis results were then compared for any
 significant difference in trend qualifiers or confidence factors. Where differences were
 found, specific analysis and explanation was made between them.
 • If ND values exceeded 50%, a visual analysis of the distribution was conducted. If
 detections of COCs were determined to be skewed to more recent dates, the data set was
 limited to the first detectable concentration date and re-computed.
 • If ND values exceeded 50% and were not determined to be skewed, a Mann-Kendall was
 not performed and a qualifier of MDN was allocated to that data set for the individual well.

5.0 Monitored Natural Attenuation Assessment Methodology – Geochemical Footprint

This line of evidence utilizes hydrogeologic and geochemical data that can be used to
demonstrate, indirectly, the types of natural attenuation processes and the rate at which such
processes will reduce contaminant concentrations to required levels. Example analytes include
competing electron acceptors (e.g., oxygen, sulfate, and nitrate), helpful electron donors (e.g.,
hydrocarbons and hydrogen), and diagnostic indicators/byproducts (e.g., methane and iron).
Geochemical data (temperature, specific conductance, turbidity, pH, oxidation-reduction
potential, and dissolved oxygen) can be used to indicate the presence of appropriate geochemical
conditions in the groundwater. Specifically, these data support a) evaluating key geochemistry
and master variables (e.g. redox, pH, and specific conductivity) along with indicators (such as
dissolved oxygen) to assess how/if the conditions support known natural attenuation processes
and b) determining if geochemical conditions are conducive to attenuation. In anaerobic areas,
conditions must be conducive to reductive dechlorination to assure degradation of parent cVOCs
while surrounding and distal plume conditions can be either anaerobic or aerobic to support
reductive degradation, cometabolism, and direct oxidative metabolism of less chlorinated
daughter products such as DCE and VC. Several additional parameters could be used as
supplemental indicators to determine if there is a reducing environment in anaerobic zones
specifically. These include:

 8

 • Methane: Microorganisms, in the absence of oxygen, will metabolize organic
 contaminants and ultimately produce methane. Elevated levels of methane indicate that
 anaerobic degradation is occurring, and that the environment is suitable for this process.
 • Dissolved iron: High dissolved iron concentrations are indicative of reducing conditions.
 • Ammonia/nitrate: Under strongly reducing conditions, microbial reactions will convert
 nitrate to ammonia.
 • Sulfate: Dissolved sulfate in groundwater may inhibit reductive dechlorination of solvents.
 • Total organic carbon (TOC): A decrease in TOC values suggests that organic compounds
 are being consumed or transformed by microbial activity.
Several of these supplemental indicators (methane and ammonia) are not currently included in
the sampling scheme for CMP Pits but could potentially provide additional support to the trends
being seen if they were to be included moving forward.

6.0 Monitored Natural Attenuation Assessment Methodology – Microbial Degradation

The third line of evidence uses data from groundwater sampling events that directly demonstrate
or quantify the occurrence of a particular attenuation process and/or the ability to degrade the
VOCs. Monitoring for VOC degradation products, microorganisms, and presence of enzymes in
treatment zones provides direct evidence to support use of an MNA strategy. The criteria in Table
3 were evaluated in this assessment to determine if reductive dechlorination and cometabolism
are occurring in the areas where groundwater sampling was conducted.

7.0 Geochemical Footprint- Results and Discussion

Table 4 provides an overarching statistical summary of the current (2025) geochemical data
collected from CMP Pit wells. For each parameter, the summary lists the minimum, median, and
maximum values along with the 1st and 3rd quartile. Data summarized in this table was collected
through field measurements.
The data indicate that conditions may not be well suited to support anaerobic degradation of
cVOCs throughout the plume except for the zone containing the Pen Branch wetlands. All
samples show ORP values that would support aerobic degradation mechanisms (greater than -
25 mv). Wells with ORP values close to the minimum (0.8 mV) may also support alternative
reductive dichlorination species such as Dehalogenimonas, but rapid dechlorination by key
anaerobic biodegradation organisms, like Dehalococcoides, is supported under much lower ORP
conditions (ORP Values < -100 mV) than what is present at the CMP Pits site. However, for cVOC
daughter products like DCE and VC, the intermediate and higher ORP conditions present do
support relatively rapid aerobic degradation. The groundwater conditions are also favorable for
attenuation of 1,4-dioxane. Aerobic cometabolism is the most likely biological attenuation pathway
for that contaminant.
The pH throughout the well network is slightly acidic to circumneutral in most cases. 75% of the
wells had a pH of less than 6.5. Circumneutral to slightly basic pH ranges support anaerobic-
aerobic cometabolism and aerobic direct metabolism, but the more acidic conditions at CMP Pits
(pH < 6.5) may hinder biodegradation mechanisms.
Specific conductivity ranged from 0 to 210 μS/cm. This indicates that there is a relatively low
concentration of dissolved ions and minerals present, which is favorable for biodegradation of

 9

cVOCs. While there isn't a direct impact of conductivity itself on the degradation of VOCs, it can
be an indicator of conditions that might affect VOC degradation processes. The groundwater
specific conductivity at CMP Pits is conducive to degradation of cVOCs.
Turbidity values were generally very low, with three quarters of the measurements falling between
4 and 7.7 NTU. Overall, low turbidity encourages biodegradation mechanisms; thus, supporting
the viability of MNA as a remedy at the CMP Pits.
It is recommended to add DO measurements to the groundwater sampling scheme. This
parameter is a key indicator of whether conditions support known natural attenuation processes
of the contaminants of concern.

11.0 Trend Analysis Key Data Points for MNA Assessment

Historical COC data trend analysis from 2009 to 2024 showed increasing contaminant
concentration trends for several monitoring points within the CMP Pits OU. These wells are
located in each UTRA aquifer zone. Trends indicate that contaminant mass has leached into
groundwater beneath the former CMP Pits and migrated, generally, downward and northward
through each aquifer zone of the UTRA. Though the overall trend analysis does provide an
indication that the hydrologic system can attenuate the mass that has migrated through
groundwater, more data is needed to fully support this conclusion and confirm the primary
mechanism contributing to cVOC attenuation in shallow soils below the stream. Key monitoring
wells and associated COC trend analysis conclusions include:

 • PCE and TCE trends have increased at specific source, intermediate, and downgradient
 portions of the plume.
 • CMP-35D, CMP-59C, CMP-47D, and CMP-32C show clear increasing trends of PCE and
 TCE from 2009 to 2024. Well CMP-64BU shows more recent increases in COC
 concentrations from 2021 to 2024. This string of monitoring wells runs roughly northeast
 from the former burial unit and shows the likely pathway of migration for COCs through
 the UTRA aquifer units of the CMP Pits OU. 1,4-dioxane concentrations also follow this
 pattern within the same monitoring wells. Lindane contaminants have not migrated
 northward beyond well CMP-47D. A notable exception to this larger pattern includes the
 decreasing trend of TCE within well CMP-47D. It is not clear from the data why TCE
 concentrations do not follow the pattern of all other COCs seen at CMP-47D.
 • TZ well CMP-35D is located on the northern edge of the former burial unit and shows
 increasing concentrations of PCE, TCE, 1,4-dioxane, and lindane from 2009 to 2024. The
 CMP-35D screen interval sits above the TCCZ that separates the TZ from the MAZ and
 monitors shallow groundwater beneath the former burial unit. Increases in COC
 concentrations within this well indicate that new mass has continually and increasingly

 leaching from the vadose zone or above soils for all COCs. With new contaminant mass
 entering the UTRA at this well, the vadose zone source of COCs has not been completely
 removed. Conventional assessment of first line evidence is not suitable for the CMP Pits
 OU due to these issues, and MNA performance must be inferred from trend analysis and

• LAZ well CMP-64BU shows the highest downgradient concentrations of PCE, TCE, and

13.0 Pen Branch Stream and Wetland Natural Attenuation Overview

The primary driver for risk of exposure to COCs and/or their off-site migration within the CMP Pits
OU is their entry into surface waters of the Pen Branch stream. A series of shallow groundwater
and hyporheic sediments investigations have been conducted for the Pen Branch wetlands to
determine evidence of COC presence and potential surface water contamination by South
Carolina State University (SCSU), Savannah River Nuclear Solutions (SRNS), and SRNL since
the mid-2000s. In addition, surface water sampling stations operated by SRNS and SCSU monitor
for COC detections within the Pen Branch stream.
The studies conducted within the Pen Branch wetland and current conceptual models for the CMP
Pits OU designate the Pen Branch stream as the end point for COC plume migration for all UTRA
aquifer zones. PCE and TCE detections in soils and groundwater from samples collected
historically by SCSU and SRNS from 2009 to 2024 confirm that COC plumes have reached the
subsurface areas beneath the Pen Branch stream in concentrations above GWPS limits. Newly
installed monitoring wells within the Pen Branch stream by SRNS in 2024 have detected
maximum concentrations of PCE at 95.7 µg/L and TCE at 29.1 µg/L in well CMP-PB-003-D1. A
map presenting newly installed Pen Branch monitoring wells is shown in Figure 35. 2024 PCE
and TCE sampling results for newly installed Pen Branch monitoring wells is presented in Table
8. SCSU historical sampling results show maximum concentrations of PCE and TCE at monitoring
point 5DB80, adjacent to well CMP-PB-003-D1. SCSU monitoring wells and station locations are
presented in Figure 36 and long-term data trends are presented in Figure 37. PCE and TCE
analytical data for monitoring points CMP-PC-003-D1 and 5DB80 appear to mark a primary entry
point for these COCs into shallow Pen Branch sediments based on the collected data.
13.1 Natural Attenuation Sampling Procedure

Data collected from shallow soil borings reported by SCSU from 2017 and 2024 were collected
from manual piezometer installations along the edge of the Pen Branch stream. Piezometers were
constructed from solid polyvinyl chloride (PVC) with no screened interval and manually driven into
the streambed to approximately 70 cm (Williams et. al., 2023). Following pipe installation,
remaining stream water was removed by bailing and a hand auger used to core soil from the pipe
interior for interval sediment samples. Upwelling of hyporheic groundwater was grab sampled
during sediment coring and subsequent groundwater samples were collected by passive diffusion
bag samplers.
Piezometer locations are tightly spaced over 10-15 m in total to monitor small-scale upwelling of
cVOCs along the southern Pen Branch stream edge. Annual sampling is conducted for each
piezometer for long term tracking of primary cVOCs and their breakdown compounds cis-DCE
and VC in the shallow groundwater to detect concentrations prior to discharge to the Pen Branch
stream. Routine surface water sampling is conducted by SCSU and SRNS to track any influx of
cVOCs within the Pen Branch stream for comparison to shallow groundwater results.

 24

The approach to sampling and monitoring within the Pen Branch wetland provides useful and
novel information of the assessment of natural attenuation cVOCs. The tracking of influx cVOCs
from 80 cm below stream surface to as little as 15 cm below stream surface shows very detailed
information of small scale intrusions of these contaminants into the wetland. In addition, the tightly
packed nature of sampling stations to monitor the degradation compounds such as cis-DCE and
VC prove that small upwellings are undergoing natural attenuation as they rise and move
westward along the predominant stream flow direction. Surface water monitoring stations monitor
the end point of cVOCs within the CMP Pits OU, and in conjunction with SRNS monitoring stations
provide adequate characterization of what contaminants do enter surface waters.
13.2 Pen Branch Shallow Soils and Groundwater Sampling Results

Of the nine piezometers installed in 2023 and 2024, soil sampling data collected by SCSU only
shows detections of PCE, TCE, cis-1,2 DCE, and VC in 15 cm depth interval soil samples taken
from monitoring points 5DB80 and 5DZ3 at the plume release zone. Detections of these COCs in
soil for monitoring point 5DB80 show highly elevated concentrations at the 15 cm depth interval
below the streambed, with PCE detections ranging from 5.4 μg/kg in July 2023 to 570 μg/kg in
April 2023, and a detection of 186 μg/kg in April 2024. Conversely, the shallowest (15 cm)
piezometer groundwater or surface water show no detections for PCE, TCE, cis-1,2 DCE, or VC.
Similar results are seen in shallow monitoring well data for newly installed wells completed in
2024 by SRNS. These are very finely interspaced monitoring locations and the dilution of the
shallow groundwater by the Pen Branch surface waters result in a bulk water sample, making
dissolved phase detections of cVOCs in these challenging.
Extensive sediment sampling of hyporheic soils within the Pen Branch wetland and stream edges
have shown strong evidence for natural attenuation along the assumed PCE/TCE plume contact
area with surface waters. Data collected by SCSU and presented in Figure 37 shows decreasing
historical trends of PCE and TCE with the presence of associated breakdown compounds, cis-
DCE and VC. The appearance of these less-chlorinated breakdown products is not seen in
sampling data outside of the Pen Branch wetlands within the CMP-Pits OU in appreciable
concentrations and supports that natural attenuation via biodegradation of PCE/TCE in wetland
soils is taking place. Other intermediate daughter products, like trans-DCE and 1,1-DCE, were
not measured in any of the SCSU-analyzed soil samples (Figure 37). Recent (2024) microbial
sampling results have also detected dehalogenation bacteria such as Dehalogenimonas and
Dehalococcoides (Williams et. al., 2023), which are halo-respiring bacteria species known for
cometabolic and reductive dechlorination of PCE and TCE. This reinforces the conclusion that
PCE and TCE biodegradation is an appreciable source of natural attenuation within the hyporheic
zone of the Pen Branch wetland.
This decrease in parent contaminants in wetland sediments is likely associated with elevated
concentrations of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) that support biodegradation and natural
attenuation of cVOCs. Conversely, a decrease in TOC values over time would suggest that
organic compounds are being consumed or transformed by microbial activity. There is a well-
established organic layer within the wetland area, but data for these monitoring locations has not
been collected to support this evaluation. Organic-rich environments can intercept groundwater
contaminated with chlorinated organics and naturally reduce concentrations and toxicity (Lorah
and Olsen, 1999). A lower redox state typically accompanies higher DOC in wetland environments
where mechanisms like reductive dechlorination are supported/enhanced.

 25

Dissolved iron concentrations are significantly higher in wells along/near Pen Branch (range of
1760 to 5440 µg/L in CMP37D, 39D, 40D, and 43D in 2024 monitoring data) than those
throughout the other portions of the well network (< 100 up to ~ 500 µg/L). These high dissolved
iron concentrations can be indicative of reducing conditions down near the stream and wetlands
that could support attenuation mechanisms. Redox conditions can heavily impact the possible
pathways and rates of biodegradation, as well as serve as an indicator of microbial activity. ORP
levels below ~ 50 mV suggest that reductive pathways might be supported, while levels below -
100 mV are favorable for reductive dechlorination.
Overall, current (measured in 2025) groundwater conditions throughout the CMP Pits network
were oxidizing, with ORP values exceeding 300 mV at some locations (CMP45D, CMP64BU, and
CMP66B) during the last sampling event (May 2025). Redox conditions near or below 50 mV
were only measured at CMP52BU, CMP59C, and CMP37D during the May 2025 event.
CMP52BU and CMP59C are both located at/near the CMP Pits, while CMP37D is along the
stream over 1000 ft away from the other locations. Elevated/increasing TCE and PCE
concentrations at these well locations were also recorded.
Elevated concentrations of both parent (TCE and PCE) VOCs, as well as daughter products (cis-
DCE and VC), were measured from the CMP pits down to the wetlands/Pen Branch. However,
surface water data does not indicate detectable concentrations of these contaminants. Redox
conditions measured at the surface water stations were similar to that of the groundwater
conditions. All surface water ORP measurements were > 140 mV, with CMPSW22 measuring at
257 mV and CMPSW20 at 315 mV.
Advanced tools and metrics to assess the microbial health and efficacy can be used in
combination with traditional metrics like plume status (if the plume is shrinking, static, expanding).
Incorporation of further, microbial monitoring to better characterize the broader subsurface
microbial ecosystem structure and function is recommended. It would also document the
responsiveness of the microbial community to site conditions and the enzymes that are present
to address the specific needs over time. This monitoring would ultimately enhance the
understanding of the local microbial community and its potential for further NA processes.
Verification of these results and a wider panel of microbial species have historically been
performed by SRS external laboratories such as Microbial Insights of Knoxville TN.
In anaerobic zones, validation of the MNA strategy would be marked by the presence and
sequential appearance or disappearance of daughter products and the presence of significant
populations of organisms that attenuate cVOCs in anaerobic conditions. Whereas, in aerobic
zones, validation of the attenuation mechanisms would be marked by low concentrations of
reductive dechlorination daughters and the presence of significant populations of organisms that
have genetic markers for known cometabolic enzymes. Table 9 shows microbial species known
to degrade cVOCs and Table 10 shows the enzymes known to be associated with cVOC
breakdown. The potential presence of microbial species that express certain cometabolic
enzymes could be used to demonstrate biodegradation of cVOCs for the CMP Pits OU.
The degradation of chlorinated ethenes from PCE to VC are documented to be highly dependent
upon available oxygen within an aquifer system (LMS/MND/S25064). Degradation of more highly
chlorinated ethenes, such as PCE and TCE, take place at a relatively higher pace within anerobic
conditions, whereas lower chlorinated ethenes degrade faster in aerobic conditions. The wider
UTRA aquifer zones of the CMP Pits OU are host to largely aerobic conditions, in contrast to
those seen in the shallower sediment layers of the Pen Branch wetland where conditions are

 26

anerobic and thus conducive to TCE and PCE degradation. In these shallower anerobic layers,
significant levels of daughter products are seen. Little data of the exact depth of this interface over
time is available to help strengthen the overall extent of what biodegradation zone exists beneath
the Pen Branch wetland and streambed. Groundwater sampling data collected from TZ wells
CMP-37D and CMP-38D show the first instances of degradation products observed between the
former burial unit and SCSU’s monitoring locations. This indicates the wider and deeper extent of
natural attenuation. The quantification of the attenuation capacity for the Pen Branch wetland,
more extensively than shallow soils adjacent to the stream bed, would provide understanding to
what threshold of cVOCs is a true threat to surface water discharge. Most existing monitoring
points and soil sampling data do not extend beyond 5 ft below the wetland or streambed surface.
If further characterization of MAZ and LAZ groundwater is performed below the Pen Branch
wetland, vertical dissolved oxygen measurements are also recommended. This additional data
would provide for a better conclusion to anaerobic degradation extent.
Compound specific isotope analysis (CSIA) could also be performed to help determine the degree
of cVOC degradation caused by biological processes within the microbial community verses
abiotic transformation processes. CSIA sampling is a valuable addition to the overall analysis of
NA for most cVOC contaminated sites, however this form of analysis is inherently unreliable at
low detection concentrations where background noise can be mistaken as legitimated detections
of chlorine isotopes (ITRC 2013).
13.3 Pen Branch MNA Sampling and Data Assessment Summary

The SRNL assessment is in general agreement with the procedures and analysis of data collected
by SCSU and SRNS within the Pen Branch wetland and stream. Sufficient data has been
collected and analyzed during these assessments to confidently detail the natural attenuation of
cVOCs within shallow wetland soils and groundwater. Existing data gaps were found to include:
 • Further expansion of microbial sampling should be conducted to fully understand the
 possible species that exist within the wetland and their possible impact to cVOC
 degradation. Associated degradation enzymes should also be investigated.
 • Further integration of shallow soils and groundwater data collected by SCSU should be
 performed in conjunction with MAZ and LAZ delineation beneath the Pen Branch.
 Ultimately, SCSU data covers only a small portion of the streambed and wider monitoring
 is only provided by surface water sampling stations. The area proximal to SCSU
 piezometer 5DB80 is likely not the only point of upwelling of cVOCs in the Pen Branch. A
 better understanding of plume proportions that are upwelling from the MAZ and LAZ would
 help to integrate SCSU shallow assessments and point to other areas in need of similar
 investigation.

14.0 Conclusions

An analysis of MNA performance was conducted by SRNL to help determine the continued
viability of this remedial strategy for the CMP Pits OU of the SRS.
Key summary points of the MNA performance review include:

 • Residual contaminant leaching from soils beneath the former burial unit of the CMP Pits
 OU continue to contribute to groundwater concentrations.

 27

 • Historical COC data trend analysis shows increasing concentration trends within
 groundwater within shallow wells beneath the former burial unit, northeast and downward,
 to LAZ wells proximal to the southern edge of the Pen Branch Wetland.
 • Hydrogeologic conditions in monitoring wells show an overall downward vertical gradient
 in the CMP Pits OU on the southern side of the Pen Branch wetland, which indicates that
 contaminated groundwater in the LAZ may not be in full communication with the Pen
 Branch stream. More data is needed conclude the true influence that COCs within the LAZ
 may have upon surface water in the future.
 • cVOCs that have reached the shallow soils beneath Pen Branch have been adequately
 remediated by natural attenuation and there have been no detections of contaminants
 within the Pen Branch surface waters above regulatory limits thus far.
 • Shallow soil and groundwater sampling data collected by SCSU show declining
 concentrations of cVOCs from 2018 to present, which shows that Pen Branch surface
 waters are protected by the degradation capacity of shallow wetland soils.
 • Degradation products such as DCE are first seen in transmissive zone wells CMP-37D
 and CMP-38D located within the wetlands, further south of the SCSU sampling locations.
 This provides evidence of natural attenuation in a wider zone surrounding the Pen Branch
 stream.
 • Expanded microbial sampling and a more complete characterization of natural attenuation
 capacity of deeper zones beneath the Pen Branch wetland would lead to a more
 comprehensive quantification of the capacity for cVOC natural attenuation for the CMP
 Pits OU.
 • Investigation of the hydrogeologic communication between the LAZ and the Pen Branch
 would provide valuable data to determine the full risk of COCs within this aquifer zone to
 surface waters and a more solid basis for continued MNA.
Conventional methods of MNA assessment such as first order decay evaluations were found to
be unsuitable for this assessment. To infer the conditions of contaminants in the subsurface,
extensive Mann-Kendall trend analysis was performed for all wells with applicable data. In
addition, new isoconcentration contour maps and cross sections were drafted to visually present
the estimated extent of the co-mingled plume. Using data trends and contour mapping,
comparisons were made and analyzed using historical groundwater sampling data from 2009 to
2024 where available. During this assessment, NA performance indicators were evaluated, and
possible data gaps were identified. Trend analysis performance was conducted on wells listed in
Table 1. Results of the Mann-Kendall analysis are presented in Table 2, Table 5, and Table 6.
Historical COC data trend analysis showed increasing contaminant concentration trends for
several monitoring points within the CMP Pits OU. These wells are located in each UTRA aquifer
zone with data that extends from 2009 to 2024. Trend analysis indicates that contaminant mass
has leached during the 2009 to 2024 period into groundwater beneath the former CMP Pits and
migrated, generally, downward and northward through each aquifer zone of the UTRA. Wells
CMP-35D, CMP-59C, CMP-47D, and CMP-32C show clear increasing trends of PCE and TCE
from 2009 to 2024. Well CMP-64BU shows more recent increases in COC concentrations from
2021 to 2024. These monitoring points appear to mark a migration pathway of cVOCs from the
former burial unit to the edge of the Pen Branch wetland through all aquifer units of the UTRA.
1,4-dioxane concentrations also follow this general pattern within the same monitoring wells.
Lindane contaminants have not migrated far from the burial area northward beyond well CMP-
47D. Overall, side gradient and distal wells to the east and west of COC plumes, such as CMP-

 28

31C, CMP-54C, CMP-55C and CMP-65B, and wells south of the former burial unit such as CMP-
10D and CMP-11D, show decreasing or stable trends. These trends indicate that plume
expansion for directions other than northeast are not occurring. This serves as strong evidence
that remaining contaminants within the former source area are limited to introduction to the TZ in
the vicinity of well CMP-35D. COC introduction into shallow TZ groundwater from the vadose
zone, or more shallow soils, by groundwater elevation fluctuations or advective processes
carrying contaminants downwards beneath the former burial unit acts as a residual source for the
CMP Pits OU plume the magnitude of which is unknown. Following the cessation of active
remediation in 2009, there has been limited characterization work conducted in the vadose zone
to determine the extent of residual contamination (SRNS, 2022).
The lack of chlorinated solvents degradation products such as DCE and VC in sampling results
indicate that little breakdown of PCE or TCE is occurring and biodegradation in the wider UTRA
is not an appreciable form of NA between the former burial unit and the Pen Branch wetland. This
conclusion is supported by other evaluations of the CMP Pits OU (SRNS, 2024). Advection,
dispersion, and sorption of COCs to soil particles are currently considered the primary drivers of
natural attenuation outside of the Pen Branch wetland (SRNS, 2024).
The increasing trends seen in TZ, MAZ, and LAZ monitoring wells appear in line with SRNS
modelling conducted for the CMP-Pits OU (WSRC, 2002). Increases in COC concentrations have
been predicted within the UTRA by software modelling, utilizing the same historical trend data
available to SRNL, and are likely the result of increasing groundwater elevations within the former
source zone beneath the burial unit dissolving residual contaminants from the vadose zone and
downward migration of COCs from soil zones above the vadose zone.
Depiction of these trends in the form of a series of cross section figures show that plume migration
from the burial trenches downgradient to the northeast through wells CMP-35D, CMP-59C, CMP-
47D, and CMP-32C has continually taken place since 2009. In the proximity of wells CMP-32C
and CMP-51D, COCs appear to mostly migrate downward into the LAZ through the TCLC.
However, concentrations of cVOCs within well CMP-8 indicate that not all contaminants follow
this migration pathway and COCs continue to migrate within the MAZ and saturated portions of
the TZ to the northeast. It is likely that this has created a semi-stratified plume with primary
downgradient concentrations of COCs located within the LAZ.
Within the Pen Branch wetland and stream, multiple rounds of soil, groundwater, and surface
water assessments have been conducted by SCSU and SRNS since the mid-2000s. The SRNL
assessment of the procedures of these sampling events and data analysis conclude that overall
performance is well conducted, and results are of value to the overall MNA performance
assessment of the CMP Pits OU. A minor data gap is the limited number of microbial species
identified thus far. Expanded microbial sampling would likewise expand the characterization of
NA performance in shallow Pen Branch soils. In doing so, a more comprehensive evaluation of
the capability for shallow soil NA to degrade cVOCs can be achieved and would better integrate
wetlands attenuation into the overall MNA strategy for the CMP Pits OU.
Remedial objectives to prevent human exposure and discharge of contaminated groundwater to
surface water are currently being achieved. The isolated nature and highly controlled access to
CMP Pits OU fulfils the objective of institutional controls and land use restrictions. In addition,
surface water monitoring data also show that no COCs are impacting the Pen Branch stream
above MCLs. The objective to reduce groundwater concentrations and plume size was found to

 29

have varied results. COCs within the TZ and MAZ continue to show declining trends in most
monitoring wells will some exceptions in TZ shallow wells CMP-35D and CMP-13D and MAZ wells
CMP-59C and CMP-55C. Plume sizes within the TZ and MAZ also shown stable to declining
footprint sizes. LAZ COC data shows an increasing trend for one or more cVOC in seven of the
twelve monitoring wells analyzed. Plume size has expanded to the north and northeast within the
LAZ following the 2009 cessation of active remediation, but contracted to the east, west, and
south.
There is insufficient data available to identify the extent of cVOC contaminants beneath the Pen
Branch wetland within the LAZ, however the LAZ plume is currently not impacting surface water
in Pen Branch above the GWPS. It is also unclear from the vertical hydraulic data available if
significant portions of the LAZ plume are upwelling to the hyporheic zone of the Pen Branch
stream, or if detections of cVOCs within the shallow soils of the wetlands are primarily fed by
contaminants within the MAZ. The assessment of vertical hydraulic gradient direction to be
generally downward in most areas of the plume away from Pen Branch, suggests that LAZ
contaminants are not likely upwelling in a significant amount. Additionally, the differing hydraulic
gradient directions between the MAZ and LAZ suggest these aquifers do not behave identically.
The determination of which plume migration pathway, and the aquifer unit it occupies, is the
primary source of shallow wetland contamination is a critical part of the overall MNA performance
assessment. If the primary contaminant migration pathway is within the LAZ, this also may
influence the determination of potential risk to surface water contamination. This depends upon
contaminated groundwater communication between the LAZ and the Pen Branch stream, as
dictated by geologic and hydrologic conditions in the subsurface. The LAZ may be the primary
contaminant migration pathway; though, if it does not fully communicate with the Pen Branch and
its surface waters as the wider downward vertical gradient south of the wetlands suggests, the
contaminants within the LAZ groundwater may be considered a lesser threat to surface water
contamination. Available analytical data suggests this may be the case. cVOC concentrations in
wells such as CMP-8B and CMP-64BU, the LAZ monitoring points closest to the Pen Branch
wetland, show trends of stable or increased contaminant concentrations. In contrast, SCSU
shallow soils data show decreasing cVOC concentrations, and surface water sampling locations
have shown no detections of COCs. These contrasting data points would suggest that LAZ
contaminants may not be migrating upward to the Pen Branch Wetland.
Further characterization of the LAZ beneath the Pen Branch of cVOCs concentrations and vertical
hydraulic gradient information can provide the needed data to fully tie the wider CMP Pits OU
plume monitoring data to the highly specialized and detailed information collected in the wetland.
In doing so, quantification of COC influx to areas known to undergo NA and the capacity for NA
to deal with these COCs can be completed.
The sustainability of the MNA remediation strategy for the CMP Pits OU is influenced by several
factors. The factors include:
 1. The quantification of contaminant mass transport into the aquifer system from the vadose
 zone beneath the former burial unit.
 2. Characterization of specific NA processes throughout the plume area, including potential
 aerobic cometabolism of cVOCs and broader anaerobic microbial existence within the Pen
 Branch Wetland, with the goal to better estimate the capacity of cVOC degradation prior
 to reaching surface water of the Pen Branch stream.

 30

These factors would culminate in an estimate of attenuation rates for cVOCs within the CMP Pits
OU. The Interstate Technology and Regulatory Council (ITRC) designates this process as a mass
balance for natural attenuation (ITRC 2008). Mass balance for NA estimation considers variable
such as chemical attenuation, biological attenuation, and physical attenuation to determine the
maximum permissible flux to meet regulatory requirements. An example diagram of this process
is presented in Figure 38. By filling the data gaps such as quantifying vadose zone influx of COCs
and microbial capacity for attenuation of those COCs, full delineation of plume travel pathways
within UTRA aquifer zones, and then subsequently combining them with existing groundwater
flow modelling and routine monitoring efforts conducted by SRNS, a mass balance may be
completed. This mass balance would allow for a refined conceptual model to be used to enhance
the understanding of what risk is presented by measured concentrations of COCs within the
groundwater of the CMP Pits OU and provide evidence for continued MNA as a remediation
strategy.

15.0 Recommended Path Forward or Future Work

Recommendations for the larger MNA process are presented for consideration:
 1. MAZ and LAZ characterization of the cVOC plume northeast of wells CMP-8 and CMP-
 64, beneath the Pen Branch wetland and stream, to fill the data gap and undelineated
 northeastern extent of the LAZ plume. This effort will provide the necessary data to
 delineate what amounts of cVOCs are upwelling, if at all, from this aquifer unit. This data
 would also help target potential other ‘hot spot’ areas similar to those currently investigated
 by SCSU. In addition, filling the data gap between the LAZ and the Pen Branch wetland
 would provide data necessary to understand if the NA seen in wetland soils is a
 sustainable trend that can be relied upon for protection of surface water contamination.
 Quantifying cVOC mobility from the LAZ to the MAZ and TZ can then be compared to
 rates of NA within the wetland soils. In doing so, the NA assessment for the wetland
 conducted by SCSU and the overall aquifer performance of NA can be fully integrated.
 2. Expanded microbial sampling in conjunction with SCSU/SRNS wetland studies are
 recommended to be performed to fully characterize the microbial community that performs
 NA within this area. Full characterization of what species of de-halogenating bacteria
 would enable more accurate assessment of the capabilities that shallow soils in the Pen
 Branch wetlands have for NA. The characterization of aerobic biodegradation of cVOCs
 throughout the wider plume area of the CMP Pits OU would also provide a more
 comprehensive view of the current NA processes taking place. Sampling and analysis for
 microbial expression of certain enzymes may be used to estimate the biodegradation of
 cVOCs outside of the Pen Branch wetland.
 3. Soil borings and soil gas surveys conducted near, and down gradient, of well CMP-35B
 would further elucidate the distribution of residual VOC contamination within the vadose
 zone. Data collected from such an effort could be used to develop a vadose zone specific
 model to better estimate the flux of COCs into shallow TZ groundwater, and better inform
 the current modelling done by SRNS for the CMP Pits OU.
 4. It is recommended to add DO measurements to the routine groundwater sampling scheme
 for all wells in this network. This parameter is a key indicator of whether conditions support
 known natural attenuation processes of cVOCs. DOC/TOC measurements should also be
 added to the monitoring scheme for wells in the wetlands specifically.
