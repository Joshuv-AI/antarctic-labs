Integrin α6-targeted photodynamic therapy potentiates PD-1 blockade in MSS colorectal cancer
Authors: Ting Hu, Qian-Kun Shi, Qiao-Li Wang, Ze-Rong Huang, Wan-Qi Chen, Lin-Na Luo, Kai-Li Xie, Fang Hu, Jie Yang, Jian-Jun Li, Guo-Kai Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer is largely refractory to PD-1 blockade due to its weak immunogenicity and an immune-excluded tumor microenvironment. Here, we developed an integrin α6-targeted photosensitizer, Ce6-RWYD, for tumor-selective photodynamic therapy (PDT). Ce6-RWYD exhibited minimal dark toxicity but induced strong light-activated cytotoxicity, accompanied by plasma membrane damage. Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT also induced mitochondrial dysfunction and immunogenic cell death, while suppressing integrin α6-related downstream signaling. In vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated enhanced tumor accumulation and prolonged retention of Ce6-RWYD compared with the talaporfin sodium (NPe6). In a CT26 subcutaneous tumor model, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT achieved robust antitumor efficacy, remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment, and showed favorable in vivo biosafety. Combination with anti-PD-1 further enhanced antitumor efficacy, accompanied by increased intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and reduced Gr-1⁺ myeloid cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT sensitizes MSS colorectal cancer to PD-1 blockade.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04700-y.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Because CRC often remains asymptomatic in its early stages, the majority of patients are diagnosed at locally advanced or metastatic stages, when tumors are typically not surgically resectable and often exhibit resistance to conventional chemotherapy [2]. Moreover, the nonspecific action of chemotherapeutic agents frequently results in dose-limiting toxicity to healthy tissues. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including antibodies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), have demonstrated remarkable and substantial clinical efficacy in a subset of CRC characterized by mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H). However, clinical outcomes remain limited in microsatellite-stable (MSS) or mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) CRC, which accounts for over 90% of advanced CRC cases [3–5]. Despite extensive efforts to develop immunotherapy-based combination strategies, durable disease control in this subtype continues to be challenging [6]. These unmet needs underscore the urgency for innovative and effective therapeutic strategies for MSS CRC.

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a promising therapeutic modality characterized by high spatial selectivity and potent cytotoxicity [7], and has been applied in the treatment of various malignancies including non-small cell lung cancer, prostate cancer, head and neck cancer, and colorectal cancer [8]. Upon light irradiation, photosensitizers (PSs) transfer energy to ground state molecular oxygen (³O₂), generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS cause oxidative damage to cellular biomolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately resulting in irreversible cellular injury [9, 10]. This damage in turn directly activates tumor cell death through multiple regulated pathways, including autophagy, apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis [11–13]. Notably, PDT can trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD), characterized by damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signals such as calreticulin (CRT) surface exposure, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) nuclear translocation/release, and extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) release. These DAMPs promote dendritic cell activation, enhance antigen presentation, and thereby stimulate a cytotoxic T-cell mediated antitumor immune response [14, 15].

As a central component of PDT, photosensitizers have been extensively engineered to optimize photophysical properties and tumor selectivity [16, 17]. Chlorin e6 (Ce6) is a prototypical second-generation photosensitizer and exhibits high ROS generation efficiency upon irradiation at 650–700 nm [18, 19]. However, its clinical translation is hampered by poor aqueous solubility, limited tumor selectivity, rapid systemic clearance, and nonspecific phototoxicity [20]. To overcome these limitations, third-generation photosensitizers have been constructed by conjugating second-generation photosensitizers with functional moieties such as antibodies, peptides, or nanoparticles to enhance tumor-targeting specificity and pharmacodynamic performance [21].

Integrin α6, encoded by the ITGA6 gene, is a laminin receptor formed through heterodimerization with integrin β1 or β4 [22]. In colorectal cancer, integrin α6 expression is significantly elevated across all disease stages [23]. Beyond CRC, integrin α6 is overexpressed in multiple malignancies and has been associated with tumor progression and unfavorable clinical outcomes [24, 25], supporting its relevance as a clinically meaningful biomarker and an accessible target for tumor imaging and therapy [26–29]. Based on this biological rationale, we previously identified and optimized a linear peptide, RWYD, that specifically targets integrin α6 and binds the α6β4 and α6β1 heterodimers with nanomolar affinity [30].

In this context, we developed an integrin α6-targeted photosensitizer, Ce6-RWYD, to enable tumor-selective PDT with the potential to promote antitumor immunity and improve the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. This conjugate consists of the targeting peptide RWYD covalently linked to the photosensitizer Ce6, wherein RWYD mediates tumor-selective accumulation and Ce6 generating cytotoxic ROS upon 660 nm light irradiation. In this study, we evaluated the antitumor efficacy and underlying mechanisms of PDT mediated by Ce6-RWYD in CRC models both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we examined the ability of Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT to potentiates CRC to PD-1 blockade therapy.

Results

Integrin α6 is highly expressed in CRC

Analysis of transcriptomic data from TCGA colorectal cancer cohorts (COAD and READ; UCSC Xena, HiSeqV2) [31] showed that ITGA6 expression was significantly elevated in CRC tissues across all pathologic stages compared with normal tissues (n = 408 vs. n = 82) (Fig. 1A). Elevated ITGA6 expression was consistently observed across clinicopathological subgroups stratified by TNM stage as well as by individual T, N, and M categories (Fig. 1B-D). This trend was further corroborated in a paired subset of matched tumor and normal samples (n = 41 pairs), which confirmed higher ITGA6 expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 1E).

To validate these transcriptomic findings at the protein level, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on 33 paired clinical CRC and adjacent normal tissue specimens collected from patients at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Representative IHC images demonstrated a gradual increase in staining intensity from negative (–) to strongly positive (+++) patterns (Fig. 1F). Consistent with the TCGA data, integrin α6 expression was higher in CRC tissues than in normal tissues, with significantly increased H-scores (144.5 ± 53.73 vs. 76.75 ± 31.61; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, flow cytometry of CRC cell lines (CT26, MC38, HCT116, and SW480) demonstrated high integrin α6 expression (Figure S1). Collectively, these data indicate that integrin α6 is aberrantly upregulated in CRC and correlated with clinicopathological features, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Characterization of Ce6-RWYD

We synthesized an integrin α6-targeted photosensitizer by conjugating Ce6 with a D-amino-acid-modified RWYD peptide (termed Ce6-DDDDDD-RWYD, hereafter referred to as Ce6-RWYD) (Fig. 2A, S2), with structures confirmed by mass spectrometry (Figure S3).Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that Ce6-RWYD formed into uniform, ultrasmall nanoparticles (Fig. 2B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed a hydrodynamic diameter below 10 nm with a narrow size distribution (Fig. 2C). In agreement, Ce6-RWYD exhibited a negative zeta potential, consistent with the presence of multiple acidic residues (Fig. 2D). UV-vis spectroscopy confirmed that Ce6-RWYD retained the characteristic absorption profile of Ce6, including the Q-band around 660 nm (Fig. 2E). Upon 660 nm irradiation, Ce6-RWYD generated singlet oxygen more efficiently than talaporfin sodium (NPe6), as quantified by singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) fluorescence (Fig. 2F). Uncle thermal ramp analysis showed an aggregation onset temperature (Tagg, static light scattering (SLS) at 473 nm) of approximately 49 °C and a transition midpoint (Tm, barycentric mean (BCM)) of approximately 55 °C, suggesting that Ce6-RWYD does not undergo pronounced aggregation or conformational transition at physiologically relevant temperatures (Fig. 2G, H). Notably, biolayer interferometry (BLI) revealed dose-dependent binding of Ce6-RWYD to integrin α6β1 and α6β4 with nanomolar affinity (KD = 9.84 × 10⁻⁸ M and 5.87 × 10⁻⁸ M, respectively), whereas NPe6 exhibited negligible binding (Fig. 2I, J).

Enhanced cellular uptake and ROS generation of Ce6-RWYD

Effective cellular uptake is a prerequisite for optimal PDT efficacy. We first examined the uptake of Ce6-RWYD in CT26 and HCT116 cells by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3A, only weak Ce6 fluorescence was detected in the PBS and NPe6 groups, whereas Ce6-RWYD exhibited markedly stronger red fluorescence in both cell lines, with nuclei counterstained by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, blue).

Quantitative analysis of CLSM images showed that intracellular Ce6 fluorescence intensity was significantly higher in the Ce6-RWYD group than in the control groups (Fig. 3B). Consistently, flow cytometry revealed a pronounced rightward shift (Fig. 3C, D) and a significantly increased mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in CT26 and HCT116 cells treated with Ce6-RWYD compared with NPe6 (Fig. 3E), suggesting enhanced cellular uptake that may be mediated by integrin α6-targeted delivery.

To further validate the targeting specificity of Ce6-RWYD, we performed a free-ligand competition assay. CLSM imaging revealed strong intracellular Ce6 fluorescence in cells treated with Ce6-RWYD, whereas pre-incubation with a 10-fold molar excess of free RWYD markedly attenuated the fluorescence signal in both CT26 and HCT116 cells (Figure S4A, C). Quantitative analysis of CLSM images confirmed a significant decrease in relative fluorescence intensity following RWYD competition (Figure S4B, D). Consistently, flow cytometry demonstrated a pronounced increase in Ce6 fluorescence after Ce6-RWYD treatment, which was largely abolished by pre-incubation with free RWYD (Figure S4E, F). MFI quantification further verified that competitive blockade significantly reduced Ce6-RWYD uptake in both cell lines (Figure S4G, H).

Given that intracellular ROS generation is a key determinant of PDT efficacy, we next evaluated ROS production following light activation using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA). As shown in Fig. 3F, negligible DCF fluorescence was observed in the PBS + light and NPe6 + light groups, while Ce6-RWYD + light induced strong green fluorescence in both CT26 and HCT116 cells. Quantitative analysis based on CLSM images and flow cytometry further confirmed that Ce6-RWYD + light yielded the highest ROS levels among all groups (Fig. 3G, H).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the specific interaction between RWYD and integrin α6 promotes the cellular internalization of Ce6-RWYD in CRC cells, thereby enabling robust intracellular ROS generation upon light irradiation.

Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induces membrane damage and cell death in vitro

In vitro phototoxicity of Ce6-RWYD was evaluated in CT26 and HCT116 cells using CCK-8 assays, SYTOX Green staining, calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, and Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 4A and Figure S5, Ce6-RWYD exhibited negligible dark toxicity across the tested concentration range in CT26 and HCT116 cells, as well as in normal intestinal epithelial cell lines FHC and NCM. In contrast, upon 660 nm laser irradiation, Ce6-RWYD induced a pronounced, dose-dependent reduction in cell viability in multiple CRC cell lines, including CT26, HCT116, MC38, and SW480 cells, whereas NPe6 displayed markedly weaker phototoxicity. The IC50 values of Ce6-RWYD under irradiation were 2.52 µM for CT26 cells and 2.72 µM for HCT116 cells, suggesting that RWYD-mediated targeting enhances PDT efficacy.

To dynamically monitor PDT induced changes in membrane permeabilization, CRC cells were incubated with different concentrations of Ce6-RWYD, followed by irradiation and SYTOX Green staining. Representative images revealed rapid accumulation of SYTOX Green positive cells after irradiation (Fig. 4B). Stronger fluorescence signals were observed at higher Ce6-RWYD concentrations and longer irradiation durations, accompanied by cell swelling and bubble like morphological alterations. Quantitative analysis showed that Ce6-RWYD + light substantially increased SYTOX Green fluorescence intensity compared with the PBS group (Fig. 4C, D), consistent with increased membrane permeabilization. Consistently, calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining revealed extensive cell death following Ce6-RWYD treatment and subsequent irradiation. Cells treated with PBS + light or NPe6 + light remained predominantly calcein-positive (green), while Ce6-RWYD combined with irradiation resulted in widespread PI-positive (red) staining in both CT26 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 4E). LDH release assays further supported PDT induced plasma membrane damage, as LDH levels were significantly elevated in the Ce6-RWYD + light group compared with the control groups (Fig. 4F).

Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry showed that Ce6-RWYD alone induced minimal cell death, whereas Ce6-RWYD plus light irradiation markedly increased the proportion of dead cells to 80.11% in CT26 cells and 82.5% in HCT116 cells, which was significantly higher than that in the PBS + light and NPe6 + light groups (Figure S6A, B). To further determine whether integrin α6 contributes to Ce6-RWYD induced phototoxicity, CT26 and HCT116 cells with ITGA6 knockout were subjected to Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry after Ce6-RWYD + light treatment (Figure S7A). Ce6-RWYD + light caused only mild cell death in ITGA6 knockout cells, with death rates of 4.38% in CT26 cells (Figure S7B) and 2.97% in HCT116 cells (Figure S7C), indicating markedly attenuated phototoxicity compared with wild type cells. These results suggest that ITGA6 knockout substantially weakens the PDT efficacy of Ce6-RWYD, supporting the contribution of integrin α6 targeting to its enhanced phototoxicity.

Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ICD in vitro

To characterize PDT-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed in CRC cells using the JC-1 probe. Confocal imaging showed that PBS + light, NPe6 + light, and Ce6-RWYD maintained strong red fluorescence in both CT26 and HCT116 cells, indicating preserved ΔΨm (Fig. 5A). In contrast, Ce6-RWYD + light markedly reduced red fluorescence and increased green fluorescence, consistent with mitochondrial depolarization. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed mitochondrial depolarization after Ce6-RWYD + light, as evidenced by a significant increase in JC-1 monomers in the Ce6-RWYD + light group (Fig. 5B, C).

As PDT can induce immunogenic cell death, we next examined canonical ICD hallmarks, including surface CRT exposure, HMGB1 redistribution, and extracellular ATP release [32]. CLSM imaging and corresponding quantitative fluorescence analysis showed markedly enhanced surface CRT exposure after Ce6-RWYD + light compared with the control groups (Fig. 5D, E). Consistently, flow cytometric detection further demonstrated increased surface CRT expression in both CT26 and HCT116 cells, as supported by MFI quantification (Fig. 5F, G). CLSM imaging also showed strong colocalization of HMGB1 with the nucleus in control cells, whereas Ce6-RWYD under irradiation markedly reduced the nuclear overlap of HMGB1 in both CT26 and HCT116 cells (Figure S8A). Quantitative analysis of CLSM images confirmed the reduced nuclear overlap of HMGB1 after Ce6-RWYD + light treatment (Figure S8B). Consistently, Ce6-RWYD + light significantly increased extracellular ATP release in both CT26 and HCT116 cells compared with the control groups (Figure S8C).

Together, these results demonstrate that Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT can induces mitochondrial dysfunction and triggers ICD in CRC cells.

Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT suppresses integrinα6-related downstream signaling

To further characterize the transcriptomic response to Ce6-RWYD upon irradiation, bulk RNA sequencing was performed in CT26 and HCT116 cells. Differential expression analysis using edgeR (|log₂FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05) identified 2066 and 2176 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) following Ce6-RWYD + light in CT26 cells (Fig. 6A, B) and HCT116 cells (Figure S9A, B), respectively. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of downregulated genes highlighted pathways involved in cell adhesion and extracellular matrix-related pathways (Fig. 6C, S9C). Notably, key integrin- and ECM-associated genes, including integrin subunits and multiple collagen isoforms, were markedly downregulated after PDT treatment, indicating attenuation of integrin-mediated adhesion signaling. Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed significant negative enrichment of adhesion related gene sets after treatment, with focal adhesion, ECM receptor interaction, and cell adhesion molecules enriched in CT26 cells (Fig. 6D), and ECM receptor interaction and cell adhesion molecules enriched in HCT116 cells (Figure S9D).

To validate the transcriptomic findings, ITGA6 expression was assessed by qRT-PCR. ITGA6 mRNA levels were significantly reduced following Ce6-RWYD + light, with no significant changes in the control groups (Fig. 6E). At the protein level, Ce6-RWYD + light induced a distinct mobility shift of integrin α6 in CT26 cells, as indicated by the appearance or intensification of a higher-molecular-weight band, whereas integrin α6 protein abundance was clearly reduced in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6F). As a transmembrane glycoprotein, integrin α6 may undergo protein processing or post-translational modification, which could explain the altered banding pattern observed in CT26 cells rather than indicating increased functional integrin α6 signaling. Given the established role of integrin α6 in cell adhesion and focal adhesion related signaling, we further assessed representative downstream signaling molecules. Consistently, Ce6-RWYD + light reduced the phosphorylation levels of FAK, Src, and mTOR, with minimal effects on their total protein expression (Fig. 6F). Quantitative analysis further confirmed inhibition of the FAK/Src axis and downstream mTOR signaling in both CT26 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these findings suggest that Ce6-RWYD + light alters integrin α6 status and disrupts integrin-associated adhesion signaling.

In vivo biodistribution and pharmacokinetic analysis of Ce6-RWYD

To assess the in vivo biodistribution of Ce6-RWYD, BALB/c nude mice and CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were intravenously injected with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD, followed by whole body fluorescence imaging to monitor the Ce6 fluorescence signal in vivo. Whole-body fluorescence imaging of BALB/c nude mice showed prolonged in vivo retention of Ce6-RWYD, with detectable fluorescence persisting for up to 48 h after injection. In contrast, NPe6 was rapidly cleared and became barely detectable by 12 h (Figure S10A). Quantitative analysis revealed that whole-body fluorescence decreased to half of the initial intensity at approximately 24 h for Ce6-RWYD, compared with approximately 6 h for NPe6 (Figure S10B). Ex vivo imaging of major organs collected at 24 h showed predominant fluorescence in the liver and kidneys, with additional signals observed in the gastrointestinal tract, including the stomach and intestine (Figure S10C), indicating liver and kidney involvement in Ce6-RWYD clearance, with possible gastrointestinal excretion.

We further evaluated the tumor targeting and biodistribution of Ce6-RWYD in CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice by in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging at multiple time points. In vivo imaging revealed rapid tumor localization of Ce6-RWYD, with strong fluorescence signals maintained for at least 8 h (Fig. 7A). In contrast, NPe6 produced only weak tumor fluorescence signals that diminished over time. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence signals demonstrated significantly greater intratumoral accumulation of Ce6-RWYD than NPe6 at multiple time points (Fig. 7B). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of the excised major organs and tumors at different time points was performed to evaluate Ce6-RWYD biodistribution. Quantitative analysis showed that the tumor-to-liver fluorescence ratio increased over time and exceeded 1 at 6 h post injection, indicating higher tumor accumulation than hepatic retention at this time point (Figure S11A, B). Accordingly, 6 h post injection was chosen as the irradiation time point for subsequent PDT treatment. Fluorescence signals were consistently observed in the kidneys, suggesting renal clearance of Ce6-RWYD. At 24 h post injection, Ce6-RWYD still showed preferential accumulation in tumor tissue, whereas NPe6 displayed nearly undetectable tumor fluorescence (Fig. 7C, D).

Pharmacokinetic analysis showed that Ce6-RWYD exhibited prolonged systemic retention compared with NPe6 (Fig. 7E–G). Although the elimination half-life (T₁/₂) and clearance (CL) were comparable between the two groups, Ce6-RWYD displayed a markedly higher AUC₀-∞ (173.06 vs. 73.01 h·µg/mL) and mean residence time (MRT₀-∞, 5.03 vs. 2.69 h), indicating prolonged circulation and increased systemic exposure, which may facilitate tumor accumulation.

Together, these data show that Ce6-RWYD exhibits prolonged systemic retention and improved tumor accumulation in vivo, supporting its potential application for targeted PDT in CRC.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and biosafety of Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT

Given the pronounced phototoxicity of Ce6-RWYD in vitro and its favorable tumor accumulation in vivo, we next evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in a subcutaneous CRC model following the treatment schedule illustrated in Fig. 8A. Throughout the treatment period, no significant body weight loss was observed in any group, suggesting good systemic tolerance (Fig. 8B). In contrast, Ce6-RWYD combined with light irradiation significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 8C, D), while the NPe6 + light group showed only moderate tumor growth delay (Fig. 8E, Figure S12). Notably, by day 15, Ce6-RWYD + light achieved almost complete tumor growth inhibition, with an inhibition rate of approximately 98%. One mouse in this group exhibited complete tumor regression (Fig. 8C, red circle).

Histological analyses further corroborated the therapeutic outcome. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed pronounced tumor tissue destruction in the Ce6-RWYD + light group compared with control groups. Immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67 demonstrated a substantial reduction in proliferating tumor cells following Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT, whereas only modest changes were observed in the NPe6 + light group (Fig. 8F). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significantly lower percentage of Ki-67 positive cells in the Ce6-RWYD + light group (Fig. 8G). In parallel, TUNEL staining revealed extensive DNA fragmentation in tumors treated with Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT, indicating robust tumor cell death (Fig. 8F). Immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections and corresponding quantitative analysis further demonstrated robust ICD induction after Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT in vivo. As shown in Fig. 8H-J, Ce6-RWYD + light markedly enhanced CRT exposure in tumor tissues (red fluorescence), whereas HMGB1 fluorescence intensity was substantially reduced (green fluorescence), suggesting HMGB1 translocation from the nucleus following Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT. The release of DAMPs can facilitate antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell activation within the tumor microenvironment.

Tumor infiltrating immune cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, with the gating strategy summarized in Figure S13A. Ce6-RWYD + light treatment markedly reduced the proportions of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Fig. 8K, Figure S13B) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Fig. 8L, Figure S13C) within tumor tissues compared with the control groups. In contrast, the proportions of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 8M, Figure S13D) and dendritic cells (DCs) (Fig. 8N, Figure S13E) were significantly increased after Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT, suggesting enhanced antitumor immune activation and antigen presentation in the tumor microenvironment. To further evaluate cytotoxic T-cell activation, the levels of granzyme B and IFN-γ were measured by ELISA in both plasma and tumor tissues. Ce6-RWYD + light treatment significantly increased granzyme B (Figure S14A, B) and IFN-γ levels (Figure S14C, D) compared with the control groups, indicating enhanced cytotoxic immune activation following Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT.

To further evaluate oxidative damage and changes in downstream integrin-associated signaling in tumors after Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT, immunofluorescence staining for 4HNE, integrin α6, and phosphorylated FAK was performed. Ce6-RWYD + light markedly increased 4HNE fluorescence intensity, indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation after Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT (Figure S15A, B). In contrast, integrin α6 expression (Figure S15A, C)and FAK phosphorylation (Figure S15A, D) were significantly decreased after Ce6-RWYD + light treatment, suggesting suppression of integrin α6-associated adhesion signaling in tumor tissues.

The biosafety profile of Ce6-RWYD was assessed at the end of the treatment period. No significant abnormalities were observed among the treatment groups in routine hematological parameters (Figure S16A) or in serum biochemical indicators of hepatic and renal function, including ALT, AST, UREA, and CREA (Figure S16B). In addition, histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no obvious pathological abnormalities after treatment (Figure S17). Ce6-RWYD also exhibited minimal hemolytic activity across the tested concentrations, supporting its favorable blood compatibility for intravenous administration (Figure S18).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT achieved potent antitumor efficacy, remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment, and showed favorable in vivo biosafety in CT26 tumor-bearing mice.

Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT potentiates PD-1 blockade therapy in MSS CRC

Body weights remained stable throughout the treatment period across all groups, indicating good systemic tolerability (Fig. 9B). Anti-PD-1 + light group produced limited tumor control, whereas Ce6-RWYD + light clearly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 9C). Notably, the combination (Ce6-RWYD + light + anti-PD-1) achieved the strongest antitumor effect, yielding the smallest tumor volumes and tumor weights at the endpoint (Fig. 9D, E), including one case of complete tumor regression (Fig. 9C, red circle).

Discussion

Colorectal cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, and therapeutic options for locally advanced and metastatic CRC remain limited. Most colorectal cancer are microsatellite-stable and derive minimal benefit from PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade [32]. As the disease progresses, lesions often cause bleeding and obstruction. The endoscopic accessibility of colorectal lesions enables precise and repeatable light delivery, supporting the translational potential of PDT for CRC treatment [33]. Accordingly, PDT has been used as a palliative option in advanced CRC to alleviate symptoms and improve local disease control [34]. Beyond local cytotoxicity, PDT can also elicit immunogenic features that may enhance antitumor immunity [35].

However, conventional photosensitizers often exhibit suboptimal tumor selectivity and intratumoral accumulation because they primarily rely on passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect after systemic administration [36, 37]. To improve targeting specificity and therapeutic performance, third-generation photosensitizers have been developed by integrating functional ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, or nanoparticles. Among these, short peptides are particularly attractive owing to their excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, efficient tissue penetration, and rapid systemic clearance [21, 38].

In this study, we developed Ce6-RWYD, a novel integrin α6 targeted photosensitizer for tumor-selective PDT against MSS CRC. Compared with the non-targeted photosensitizer NPe6, Ce6-RWYD showed enhanced cellular uptake, pronounced ROS generation, improved tumor accumulation, and potent antitumor efficacy while maintaining favorable tolerability. Mechanistically, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induced plasma membrane damage, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and ICD activation, while simultaneously suppressing integrin α6-associated downstream signaling. As a monotherapy, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT suppressed tumor growth and remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment. Importantly, these immunomodulatory effects further sensitized MSS CRC to PD-1 blockade therapy.

A notable feature of this work is the selection of integrin α6 as a targeting moiety for PDT. Integrins are cell surface adhesion receptors that link the extracellular matrix to the cytoskeleton and transmit bidirectional signals [22]. Dysregulated integrin signaling contributes to tumor progression, invasion and therapy resistance [39, 40]. Integrin α6 commonly forms heterodimers with β1 or β4 (α6β1/α6β4) and is frequently overexpressed in multiple malignancies [41–43]. In the present study, TCGA analysis and paired clinical specimens confirmed elevated integrin α6 expression in CRC tissues. Building on our previous identification of the RWYD peptide as a high affinity integrin α6 ligand, we constructed the targeted photosensitizer Ce6-RWYD and demonstrated its nanomolar binding affinity toward α6β1 and α6β4.

Functionally, Ce6-RWYD showed markedly enhanced cellular uptake and intracellular ROS generation compared with NPe6, leading to pronounced phototoxicity with minimal dark toxicity. Multiple complementary assays demonstrated substantial plasma membrane damage, mitochondrial depolarization, and cell death following photoactivation. In addition to direct cytotoxicity, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induced canonical ICD associated DAMPs, including CRT exposure, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion, supporting its ability to promote antitumor immune activation. Together, these findings support integrin α6 as a targetable vulnerability in CRC and establish Ce6-RWYD as a targeted PDT platform with both cytotoxic and immunomodulatory properties.

Transcriptomic profiling further revealed that Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induced a distinct transcriptional program characterized by suppression of focal adhesion, ECM receptor interaction, and cell adhesion pathways. Consistently, ITGA6 expression was selectively reduced at the transcript level. At the protein level, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT induced distinct ITGA6 banding patterns in CT26 and HCT116 cells. The broadened upper ITGA6 band observed in CT26 cells likely reflects altered post-translational modification or protein processing, such as glycosylation, rather than enhanced functional ITGA6 expression. This interpretation is supported by the marked reduction in the phosphorylation of FAK, Src, and mTOR in both cell lines, indicating that ITGA6-related downstream signaling was clearly suppressed after Ce6-RWYD + light treatment. Given the central role of integrin FAK/Src signaling in regulating adhesion dynamics and cell motility, suppression of this pathway may contribute to the enhanced locoregional tumor control achieved by Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT.

The favorable in vivo performance of Ce6-RWYD further underscores its translational potential for targeted PDT. Compared with NPe6, Ce6-RWYD exhibited prolonged systemic retention, enhanced tumor targeting, and an improved pharmacokinetic profile, supporting sustained intratumoral drug availability for effective photoactivation. In CT26 tumor-bearing mice, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT achieved potent antitumor efficacy, remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment, and showed favorable in vivo biosafety. Moreover, combining Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT with PD-1 blockade produced superior tumor control compared with either monotherapy alone.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that integrin α6-targeted PDT achieves effective local tumor control while promoting immune activation in MSS CRC. Considering the routine use of endoscopy in CRC diagnosis and management, this strategy may provide a clinically feasible approach for combining locoregional PDT with immunotherapy sensitization in advanced CRC.

Conclusion

This study presents Ce6-RWYD, an integrin α6-targeted photosensitizer engineered for precise and well tolerated PDT. Beyond effective local tumor control, Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT remodels the tumor immune microenvironment toward an immune-permissive state and enhances antitumor immunity. These effects sensitize MSS colorectal cancer to PD-1 blockade, providing a promising strategy to improve immunotherapy responsiveness in this clinically challenging subtype.

Method

Immunohistochemistry of human CRC specimens

Approval for the use of human samples was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, with a waiver of informed consent (No. B2025-670-01). Formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50%). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Antigen retrieval was performed in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with anti-integrin α6 antibody (#ab181551, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. Chromogenic detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Whole-slide images were acquired by a KF-PRO-020 digital pathology slide scanner (Ningbo Konfoong Bioinformation Technology Co., Ltd., China). H-scores were quantified by HALO® quantitative image analysis software (Indica Labs, USA).

Cell lines and animals

Human and mouse colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines (HCT116, SW480, CT26, MC38) and human normal colon epithelial cell lines (FHC, NCM460) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; USA). HCT116 and FHC cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, CT26, MC38, SW480 and NCM460 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.5% penicillin-streptomycin.

The animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (L025501202401002). Four- to six-week old famale BALB/c mice and famale BALB/c nude mice were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and raised under pathogen free conditions.

Synthesis and physicochemical characterization of Ce6-RWYD

Ce6 was obtained from Frontier Scientific. The integrin α6 targeting polypeptide (DDDDDDRWYD) was synthesized by SynPeptide (Nanjing, China). N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) was purchased from Leyan. N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), and anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from MACKLIN.

Ce6-DDDDDDRWYD, abbreviated as Ce6-RWYD, was prepared via a two-step conjugation. Briefly, Ce6 (130 mg) was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO (5 mL) and activated with EDC (119 mg) and NHS (66.7 mg) for 4 h at room temperature in the dark. DDDDDDRWYD (226 mg) dissolved in DMSO was then added dropwise to the activated Ce6 solution, followed by stirring for 24 h. The reaction mixture was dialyzed against water (MWCO 1500 Da) and subsequently lyophilized. The resulting conjugate was confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS; Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Fusion).

Talaporfin sodium (NPe6; #HY-16477, MedChemExpress) served as a control. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). The hydrodynamic diameter (size) and zeta potential of NPe6 and Ce6-RWYD aggregates were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering, respectively, on a Malvern Zetasizer. Thermal stability analysis was performed using an Uncle Multifunctional Protein Stability Analysis System (Uncle-0706; Unchained Labs, USA).

Singlet oxygen (¹O₂) detection

Singlet oxygen (¹O₂) generation was assessed using Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG; #S0067, Beyotime Biotechnology). NPe6 and Ce6-RWYD were prepared at an equivalent Ce6 concentration of 5 µg/mL, and SOSG was added to a final concentration of 5 µM. The mixtures were continuously irradiated with a 660 nm laser (20 mW/cm²), and SOSG fluorescence was monitored at 10 s intervals over 120 s using excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525–530 nm. SOSG without laser irradiation served as a negative control, and PBS-only samples were included for blank correction. The increase in SOSG fluorescence intensity reflects the amount of ¹O₂ generated by photosensitizers.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI)

The interaction between Ce6-RWYD and recombinant human integrin α6 was measured by biolayer interferometry (BLI) using an Octet R8 system (ForteBio, Sartorius) at 30 °C. Streptavidin (SA) biosensors were pre-equilibrated in PBST (1× PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) and used as the running buffer throughout the assay. Biotinylated integrin α6 protein was immobilized onto SA biosensors, followed by baseline stabilization. The sensors were then exposed to NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD at 1, 2, and 4 µM to record the association phase and subsequently transferred to running buffer to monitor dissociation. Sensorgrams were processed and analyzed using Octet Data Analysis software (Sartorius). Baseline-subtracted curves were globally fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to obtain the association rate (ka), dissociation rate (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Flow cytometric analysis of integrin α6 expression

To evaluate the surface expression of integrin α6, CT26, HCT116, MC38, and SW480 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to about 80% confluence. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended as single cell suspensions, followed by staining with a PE-conjugated anti-integrin α6 antibody (#FAB13501P, R&D Systems) or a PE-conjugated rat IgG isotype control (#IC006P, R&D Systems) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After staining, cells were washed three times with cold PBS, resuspended in staining buffer, and analyzed immediately by CytoFLEX LX (Beckman Coulter, USA).

In vitro cellular uptake and ROS generation

Cellular uptake was assessed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry. For CLSM analysis, CT26 and HCT116 cells were seeded in confocal dishes. After 24 h, cells were incubated with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD for 1 h, washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; #C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology). Confocal imaging was performed using a Nikon AX confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Japan). For flow cytometry, CT26 and HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS to remove extracellular compounds, cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry to quantify intracellular Ce6 fluorescence. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used to compare cellular uptake between groups.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; #S0033S, Beyotime Biotechnology), which is oxidized to fluorescent DCF in the presence of ROS. For CLSM imaging, CRC cells were seeded in confocal dishes to about 80% confluence. For flow cytometry, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. After overnight attachment, cells were incubated with PBS, NPe6, or Ce6-RWYD for 1 h. Cells were then irradiated with a 660-nm laser for 1 min, with an additional Ce6-RWYD group maintained in the dark as the non-irradiated control. The laser power density was 20 mW/cm² at the sample surface. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing DCFH-DA (1:5000 in PBS), and cells were incubated for 20 min. DCF fluorescence was immediately imaged by CLSM or quantified by flow cytometry. Confocal images were quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), and flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo (version 10).

Competition with excess free peptide

To determine whether cellular uptake of Ce6-RWYD is specifically mediated by integrin α6, a competition assay using excess free peptide was performed. CT26 and HCT116 cells were seeded in confocal dishes (for microscopy) or 6-well plates (for flow cytometry; 1 × 10⁵ cells per well) and cultured overnight. Cells were pre-incubated with free RWYD peptide (50 µM, a 10-fold molar excess) in serum-free medium for 60 min at 37 °C, followed by addition of Ce6-RWYD to a final concentration of 5 µM and incubation for an additional 1 h. Cells treated with PBS, free RWYD peptide alone, or Ce6-RWYD alone served as controls. For CLSM, cells were fixed, stained with DAPI, and imaged. Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ. For flow cytometry, cells were harvested and analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX system (Beckman Coulter); mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified using FlowJo software.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was evaluated using a CCK-8 kit (Selleck Chemicals, #B34304). CRC cells (CT26, HCT116, SW480, and MC38) and normal colon epithelial cells (FHC and NCM460) were seeded in 96-well plates at 5,000 cells per well and incubated with photosensitizers at indicated concentrations for 1 h. For phototoxicity assays, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (20 mW/cm², 1 min) and cultured for another 24 h. For dark toxicity assays, cells were treated identically without irradiation. CCK-8 reagent was then added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Evaluation of plasma membrane damage

Plasma membrane permeabilization and damage was assessed using SYTOX Green staining (Invitrogen, #S7020) and LDH release assays (Beyotime, #C0017). CT26 and HCT116 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with photosensitizers for 1 h before irradiation with a 660 nm laser (20 mW/cm²). For SYTOX Green imaging, cells were treated with Ce6-RWYD at 3, 5, or 10 µM and irradiated for 30 s or 1 min, followed by immediate addition of SYTOX Green. Fluorescence images were acquired using an IncuCyte S3 live-cell imaging system. For LDH release assays, cells were treated with photosensitizers at 5 µM, irradiated for 1 min, and incubated for 4 h before LDH activity in the supernatant was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Evaluation of PDT-induced cell death

For calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining, CT26 and HCT116 cells were incubated with photosensitizers for 1 h and then irradiated with a 660 nm laser where indicated (20 mW/cm², 1 min). After treatment, cells were stained using a calcein-AM/PI kit (Solarbio, #CA1630) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and imaged by CLSM.

For Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry, CT26 and HCT116 cells were divided into four groups: PBS + light, NPe6 + light, Ce6-RWYD without light, and Ce6-RWYD + light. Cells were incubated with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD at an equivalent Ce6 concentration of 5 µM for 1 h, followed by irradiation with a 660 nm laser where indicated (20 mW/cm², 1 min). After treatment, cells were stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BMS500FI-300) and analyzed by flow cytometry. To further confirm the role of integrin α6 in Ce6-RWYD-mediated PDT, ITGA6- knockout (ITGA6-KO) CT26 and HCT116 cell lines were generated by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing using lentiviral transduction. Knockout efficiency was verified by flow cytometry with a PE-conjugated anti-integrin α6 antibody as described in the “Flow cytometric analysis of integrin α6 expression” section. The verified ITGA6-KO cells were subjected to the same treatment and Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometric analysis to assess the contribution of integrin α6 to Ce6-RWYD-induced phototoxicity.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using an enhanced JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (#C2003S, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with 5 µM NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD for 1 h, washed three times with PBS, and then irradiated with a 660 nm laser (20 mW/cm², 1 min) or kept in the dark as controls. Cells were subsequently incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark and washed with JC-1 staining buffer. JC-1 fluorescence was visualized by CLSM, with red and green fluorescence corresponding to JC-1 aggregates and monomers, respectively. For flow cytometry, JC-1 stained cells were washed and analyzed to quantify the distribution of JC-1 monomers and aggregates.

Detection of ICD-associated DAMPs in vitro

Cell surface CRT exposure was examined by CLSM and flow cytometry. CRC cells were incubated with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD at an equivalent Ce6 concentration of 5 µM for 1 h, followed by irradiation with a 660 nm laser where indicated (20 mW/cm², 1 min) and incubation for another 3 h. For CLSM, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, and stained with an anti-calreticulin antibody (Abcam, #ab92516), followed by an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (BioLegend, #406416). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired by CLSM. For flow cytometry, treated cells were collected, blocked with 5% BSA, and stained with the anti-calreticulin antibody, followed by a PE-conjugated secondary antibody (BioLegend, #406421), and analyzed to quantify surface CRT exposure.

HMGB1 nuclear translocation was assessed by immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, CRC cells were treated as described above. After the 2 h post-irradiation incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated with an anti-HMGB1 antibody (#10829-1-AP, Proteintech), followed by a fluorescent secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired by CLSM. HMGB1 nuclear translocation was evaluated by quantifying the overlap between HMGB1 and DAPI signals.

Extracellular ATP release was measured using an ATP assay kit (#S0027, BioLegend)) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after treatment and post-irradiation incubation, culture supernatants were collected and ATP levels were determined using the kit reagents. Luminescence signals were measured to quantify extracellular ATP release.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting

Total RNA was extracted using the NcmSpin Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (M5106, NCM Biotech), and cDNA was synthesized using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (#A5001, Promega). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q312-03, Vazyme Biotech) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Primers for ITGA6 were as follows: human ITGA6, forward 5′-CGAAACCAAGGTTCTGAGCCCA-3′ and reverse 5′-CTTGGATCTCCACTGAGGCAGT-3′;

mouse Itga6, forward 5′-CATCACGGCTTCTGTGGAGATC-3′ and reverse 5′-CATTGTCGTCTCCACATCCTTCC-3′.

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (#B14002, Selleck Chemicals) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#B15002, Selleck Chemicals). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with SDS loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min before separation by SDS-PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL reagent and imaged on a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. For parallel blots probed separately for phosphorylated and total proteins, the intensity of each target band was first normalized to its corresponding loading control on the same membrane. These normalized values were then expressed relative to those of the PBS-treated control group on their respective blots. Finally, the relative phosphorylation level for each protein was calculated as the ratio of the normalized phospho-protein signal to the normalized total-protein signal.

Primary antibodies included anti-FAK (#12636-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-phospho-FAK (Tyr397, #T55587S, Abmart), anti-Src (#F0262, Selleck Chemicals), anti-phospho-Src (Tyr416, #F0304, Selleck Chemicals), anti-mTOR (#T55306, Abmart), anti-phospho-mTOR (Ser2448, #T56571, Abmart), and anti-integrin α6 (#ab181551, Abcam).

In vivo biodistribution and pharmacokinetic studies

For biodistribution and tumor-targeting studies, BALB/c nude mice or CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were intravenously injected with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD (Ce6 dose: 3 mg/kg). Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed at predetermined time points using an IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). For fluorescence decay analysis in BALB/c nude mice, a consistent whole-body region of interest (ROI) was selected at each time point, and average radiant efficiency was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer). The apparent fluorescence decay half-life was estimated based on the time required for fluorescence intensity to decrease to 50% of the initial signal.

For ex vivo biodistribution analysis, CT26 tumor-bearing mice were sacrificed at indicated time points after injection, and major organs and tumor tissues were harvested for fluorescence imaging under identical acquisition parameters. Tumor-to-liver and tumor-to-kidney fluorescence intensity ratios were calculated to evaluate tumor selectivity over time.

To obtain the pharmacokinetics profiles, healthy BALB/c mice were intravenously injected with NPe6 or Ce6-RWYD (Ce6 dose: 3 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points after injection: 0.017, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and the Ce6 fluorescence intensity was measured. Pharmacokinetic parameters, including elimination half-life (T₁/₂), area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC), mean residence time (MRT), and clearance (CL), were estimated by non-compartmental analysis using the resulting mean concentration-time profile with Phoenix WinNonlin 8.1 software (Certara, USA).

In vivo antitumor efficacy

BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the flank with 1 × 10⁶ CT26 cells suspended in PBS. Ten days after tumor inoculation, mice were randomized into four groups: (1) PBS; (2) Ce6-RWYD (no laser); (3) NPe6 + light; and (4) Ce6-RWYD + light. PBS, NPe6, or Ce6-RWYD (Ce6 dose: 3 mg/kg) was intravenously administered on days 0. Tumors were then irradiated with a 660-nm laser (20 mW/cm²; 1 min on/1 min off, 4 cycles) at 6 h post-injection. Owing to limited light penetration and reduced effective light intensity in vivo, a prolonged irradiation protocol was used for animal experiments. Tumor size was measured every 2 days using an electronic caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as V = length × (width)² / 2. All mice were sacrificed on day 15, and tumors were harvested and photographed.

For combination therapy, CT26-bearing mice were randomized into four groups: PBS + light, anti-PD-1 + light, Ce6-RWYD + light, and Ce6-RWYD + light + anti-PD-1. Mice in the Ce6-RWYD + light and combination groups received intravenous injections of Ce6-RWYD (Ce6 dose: 3 mg/kg) on days 0 and 7, followed by tumor irradiation with a 660-nm laser (20 mW/cm²; 1 min on/1 min off, 4 cycles) at 6 h post-injection. Anti-PD-1 antibody was administered separately from PDT (200 µg per mouse) on days 3, 6, 10, and 13. At the end of the study, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were harvested, photographed and weighed.

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Tumor tissues harvested at the endpoint were minced, digested into single-cell suspensions and stained with Zombie Aqua™ Fixable Viability Dye (#423107, BioLegend). After washing, cells were incubated with anti-CD45 antibody (APC-Cy7, #557659, BD Biosciences) and Fc Block (anti-CD16/CD32) in staining buffer for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Surface staining was performed using the following antibody panels: cDC1 cells, CD11c (BV421, #117343, BioLegend) and MHC-II (I-A/I-E) (BV650, #107641, BioLegend); MDSC, CD11b (PE, #557397, BD) and Gr-1 (BV421, #108445, BioLegend); CD8 + T cells: CD3 (FITC, #100204, BioLegend) and CD8α (AF700, #100730, BioLegend). For Treg analysis, cells were stained with CD4 (PE, #100408, BioLegend), followed by fixation, permeabilization, and intracellular staining with Foxp3 (AF647, #320013, BioLegend) using the True-Nuclear™ Transcription Factor Buffer Set (#424401, BioLegend). Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

IFN-γ and granzyme B quantification by ELISA

Peripheral blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus using EDTA-coated anticoagulant tubes. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at − 80 °C until assayed. Plasma levels of IFN-γ and Granzyme B were measured using the Mouse IFN-γ Precoated ELISA Kit (#1210002, BioLegend) and the Mouse Granzyme B ELISA Kit (#EK1115, BOSTER), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. For intratumoral cytokine measurement, tumor tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (#B14002, Selleck Chemicals) on ice using a tissue homogenizer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Total protein concentration of each supernatant was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Tumor lysates were then assayed for IFN-γ and Granzyme B using the aforementioned ELISA kits. Intratumoral cytokine concentrations were normalized to total protein content and expressed as pg/mg protein.

In vivo biosafety assessment

Systemic biosafety was assessed by monitoring mouse body weight every 2 days throughout treatment. At the endpoint, blood samples were collected for hematological and serum biochemical analyses. For hemolysis assessment, mouse erythrocytes were incubated with Ce6-RWYD at 25, 50, or 100 µg/mL at 37 °C for 2 h. PBS and 1% Triton X-100 were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After centrifugation, absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm was measured, and hemolysis was calculated as: Hemolysis (%) = (A_sample − A_PBS) / (A_Triton − A_PBS) × 100%.

Histological evaluation

For histopathological examination, major organs and tumors were harvested after euthanasia, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, paraffin embedded, and sectioned. Major organ sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess treatment related toxicity. Tumor sections were subjected to H&E staining, Ki-67 immunostaining using an anti-Ki-67 antibody (#GB151141, Servicebio), and TUNEL immunofluorescence staining (#G1505, Servicebio) to evaluate tumor tissue damage, proliferation, and cell death, respectively.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on frozen or paraffin-embedded tumor sections according to the staining purpose. For 4HNE, integrin α6, downstream signaling molecules, and ICD-associated DAMPs, tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and cryosectioned at 7 μm. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with the following primary antibodies on separate tissue sections: anti-4HNE (#bs-6313R, Bioss), PE-conjugated anti-integrin α6 antibody (#12-0495-83, Invitrogen), anti-CRT (#ab92516, Abcam), anti-HMGB1 (#10829-1-AP, Proteintech), or anti-p-FAK (Tyr397, #T55587S, Abmart). For intracellular or nuclear markers, including 4HNE, HMGB1, and p-FAK, sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 before blocking. CRT and p-FAK were detected using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (#A21207, Invitrogen), whereas HMGB1 and 4HNE were detected using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (#A21206, Invitrogen). Integrin α6 was visualized through direct PE conjugation.

For immune cell infiltration, 4 μm paraffin-embedded sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (#P0081, Beyotime Biotechnology) at 95 °C for 10 min, blocked with 2% goat serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody mixtures. CD8⁺ T cells and tumor cells were co-stained using rabbit anti-CD8 (#100744, BioLegend) and anti-pan-cytokeratin (#ab7753, Abcam), whereas Gr-1⁺ myeloid cells and tumor cells were co-stained using rat anti-Gr-1(#108401, BioLegend) and anti-pan-cytokeratin. Sections were then incubated with the corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody mixtures for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Whole-slide images were acquired using a KF-PRO-020 digital pathology slide scanner, and confocal images were acquired using a Nikon AX confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), except for in vivo tumor growth curves and body weight measurements, which are shown as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used when data were approximately normally distributed; otherwise, the two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test was performed, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
