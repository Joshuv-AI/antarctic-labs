Geometric duality, perfect graphs, and the Sierpiński space Thanks: B. Farkas was supported by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF) projects nos. I5918 and PAT5730424
Piotr Borodulin–Nadzieja, Barnabás Farkas, Anna Pelczar–Barwacz

Abstract
. In their classical paper On the stopping time Banach space , Bang and Odell, among a plethora of results concerning the dyadic stopping time space and its dual, presented the first non-trivial example of the duality phenomenon between combinatorial Banach spaces. We give a full characterization of such pairs ( ℱ 0 , ℱ 1 ) (\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1}) of families of finite sets: This duality holds iff there is a perfect graph G G on ℕ \mathbb{N} such that ℱ 0 \mathcal{F}_{0} consists of all finite cliques of G G and ℱ 1 \mathcal{F}_{1} consists of all finite anti-cliques of G G . As it turns out, Lovász’ famous perfect graph theorem is an immediate corollary of this result. Among the many examples of such pairs of families, we investigate a particularly interesting one, when G G is the Sierpiński graph, and study general methods of embedding combinatorial and classical sequence spaces in the generated space, including the Schreier and ℓ p \ell_{p} spaces.

In Section

In Section

In Section

Another interesting consequence of the main theorem is the description of the extreme points of

where

In Section

we study a, in a sense, canonical perfect graph, the

let

be connected by an edge if

, and let

be the family of its finite cliques. Analogous objects, defined on uncountable subsets of

In Section
6
a general method of embedding combinatorial spaces isometrically in the Sierpiński space is presented. In particular, we show that this method applies to all Schreier spaces of finite order; also, that if we just slightly modify the definition of the Schreier sequence, then all the resulting Schreier
∗
spaces embed isometrically in the Sierpiński space.

In Section

In Section

2.
Combinatorial Banach spaces

We begin with a quick overview of these spaces. In general, we define combinatorial spaces over arbitrary non-empty countable, perhaps finite,
\Omega
as follows (see
[
8
,
7
]
): Fix a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[\Omega]^{<\infty}
covering
\Omega
and let
\|x\|_{\mathcal{F}}=\sup\{\sum_{k\in F}|x(k)|:F\in\mathcal{F}\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
and
Y_{\mathcal{F}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\|x\|_{\mathcal{F}}<\infty\}
; to define
X_{\mathcal{F}}
for an infinite
\Omega
, fix an enumeration
\Omega=\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and set

X_{\mathcal{F}}=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\|P_{\{a_{n},a_{n+1},\dots\}}(x)\|_{\mathcal{F}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\big\}.

Obviously, the enumeration itself does not play any role here because

X_{\mathcal{F}}=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\inf\big\{\|P_{\Omega\setminus E}(x)\|_{\mathcal{F}}:E\in[\Omega]^{<\infty}\big\}=0\big\}.

The properties mentioned in the introduction still hold:
X_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq Y_{\mathcal{F}}
equipped with
\|\bullet\|_{\mathcal{F}}
are Banach spaces,
X_{\mathcal{F}}
is the completion of
c_{00}(\Omega)
, and the canonical algebraic basis
(e_{n})=(e_{n})_{n\in\Omega}
of

is a normalized
1
-unconditional basis in
X_{\mathcal{F}}
. When it is necessary, we will write
(e_{\mathcal{F},n})
to clarify the underlying space.

Notice that (i) we can and will always assume that
\mathcal{F}
is hereditary, that is,
E\subseteq F\in\mathcal{F}
implies
E\in\mathcal{F}
, and (ii) working with an arbitrary
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega)

does not lead to more general spaces because
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\bigcup\{[H]^{<\infty}:H\in\mathcal{H}\}\subseteq[\Omega]^{<\infty}
and

for every

.

We will work, for example, over
\Omega=\{1,\dots,n\},\mathbb{N},\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, and the dyadic tree

2^{<\mathbb{N}}=\big\{s:s=\emptyset\,\text{ or }\,s\,\text{ is a }\,\{1,2,\dots,n\}\to\{0,1\}\,\text{function}\,(n\in\mathbb{N})\big\}

but for now, as long as the underlying set does not play any crucial role, let us continue over

and set

\mathrm{FHC}=\mathrm{FHC}(\mathbb{N})=\big\{\mathcal{F}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}:\mathcal{F}\,\text{ is hereditary and covers }\,\mathbb{N}\big\}.

When referring to topological properties of and operations on subsets of

.

Notice that an
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{FHC}
is compact (that is,
\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}
) iff

iff

does not contain infinite

We will consider

Clearly, if

The point is that with the help of the operation

3.
Graph-generated families

In the introduction we mentioned two important examples,
\mathcal{C}=\{C\subseteq 2^{<\mathbb{N}}:C
is a finite chain
\}
and
\mathcal{A}=\{A\subseteq 2^{<\mathbb{N}}:A
is a finite anti-chain
\}
where
2^{<\mathbb{N}}
is considered with the usual partial order,
s\unlhd t
iff
t
extends
s
. Of course, we can work with an arbitrary countable poset
\mathbb{P}=(P,\leq)
and define
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})=\{C\subseteq P:C
is a finite chain in
\mathbb{P}\}
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{P})=\{A\subseteq P:A
is a finite anti-chain in
\mathbb{P}\}
. Moreover, we can go one crucial step further: Given a non-empty countable
V
and a (simple) graph
G=(V,E)
, that is,
E\subseteq[V]^{2}=\{\{a,b\}:a,b\in V,a\neq b\}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(G)

\displaystyle=\big\{C\in[V]^{<\infty}:C\,\text{ is a \emph{clique} in $G$, i.e. }\,[C]^{2}\subseteq E\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(G)

\displaystyle=\big\{A\in[V]^{<\infty}:A\,\text{ is an \emph{anti-clique} in $G$, i.e. }\,[A]^{2}\cap E=\emptyset\big\}.

Given a poset
\mathbb{P}=(P,\leq)
, let
G_{\mathbb{P}}
be its
comparability graph
, that is,
G_{\mathbb{P}}
lives on
P
and
\{p,q\}
is an edge iff
p<q
or
q<p
. It follows that
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{C}(G_{\mathbb{P}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{A}(G_{\mathbb{P}})
.

Obviously, if
C\in\mathcal{C}(G)
and
A\in\mathcal{A}(G)
then
|C\cap A|\leq 1
, moreover,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(G)

\displaystyle=\big\{C\in[V]^{<\infty}:|C\cap A|\leq 1\,\text{ for every }\,A\in\mathcal{A}(G)\big\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(G)

\displaystyle=\big\{A\in[V]^{<\infty}:|A\cap C|\leq 1\,\text{ for every }\,C\in\mathcal{C}(G)\big\}.

This observation leads to the following construction: Given
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{FHC}
define

\mathcal{F}^{\perp}=\big\{E\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}:|E\cap F|\leq 1\,\text{ for every }\,F\in\mathcal{F}\big\}.

Then
\mathcal{F}^{\perp}\in\mathrm{FHC}
as well. In general,
\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}\supseteq\mathcal{F}
always holds but
\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}
can be much larger than
\mathcal{F}
, e.g. with
\mathcal{S}=\{S\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}:S=\emptyset
or

we have

\mathcal{S}^{\perp}=[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}\cup\big\{\{1,n\}:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\,\text{ and }\,\mathcal{S}^{\perp\perp}=\big\{\{1\}\big\}\cup\big[\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}\big]^{<\infty}.

At the same time,
\mathcal{C}(G)^{\perp}=\mathcal{A}(G)
and
\mathcal{A}(G)^{\perp}=\mathcal{C}(G)
, hence
\mathcal{C}(G)^{\perp\perp}=\mathcal{C}(G)
and
\mathcal{A}(G)^{\perp\perp}=\mathcal{A}(G)
for every graph
G
, moreover, the following holds:

Fact 3.1
.

Given
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{FHC}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}^{\perp}
for some
\mathcal{E}\in\mathrm{FHC}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}
.

(iii)

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{C}(G)
for some graph
G
on
\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

(i)
\to
(ii): We know that
\mathcal{E}^{\perp\perp\perp}\supseteq\mathcal{E}^{\perp}
, conversely, if
H\in\mathcal{E}^{\perp\perp\perp}
then

for every

, hence

.

(ii)
\to
(iii): Let
G=(\mathbb{N},[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\cap\mathcal{F})
. Obviously,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(G)
. Conversely, let
C\in\mathcal{C}(G)
and assume on the contrary that

. Then there is an

such that

. In other words,

contains a pair
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp}
but this is impossible because
\{a,b\}
is an edge and hence
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(iii)
\to
(i): We know that
\mathcal{C}(G)=\mathcal{A}(G)^{\perp}
.
∎

\mathbb{N}

Clearly, if

4.

First of all, we reformulate the definition of duality in terms of the canonical basic sequences, show that orthogonality of families is a necessary condition of geometric duality, and that this notion is symmetric:

To show the non-trivial implication in the first statement, assume that

k_{n_{i+1}}
, then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}F_{n_{i},k_{i}}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
.
∎

Question 5.9
.

Does the Sierpiński space contain a copy of
\ell_{1}(c_{0})
?
Is there a reasonable characterization of those Banach spaces with unconditional basis (or those combinatorial spaces) which embed in the Sierpiński space?

6.
Combinatorial spaces emulated in Sierpiński spaces

In this section we study canonical subspaces of the Sierpiński space(s). For example, each of them contains obvious (complemented isometric) copies of
c_{0}
and
\ell_{1}
: Given
f\in\mathrm{Bij}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Q})
, there are both infinite chains and infinite anti-chains with respect to
\leq_{f}
. If
D\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
is infinite, then
(e_{f,n})_{n\in D}
is isometrically equivalent to the basis of
\ell_{1}
, and similarly, if
B\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{f}
is infinite, then
(e_{f,n})_{n\in B}
is isometrically equivalent to the basis of
c_{0}
.

Below we will describe a general scheme of producing subspaces of the Sierpiński space. First of all, notice that the definitions of
\leq_{f}
,
G_{f}
,
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, and
X_{f}
make perfect sense when starting with an injection
\theta:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
instead of a bijection
f
. Moreover, the basis
(e_{\theta,n})
of the resulting combinatorial space
X_{\theta}
is isometrically equivalent to a subbasis of
(e_{f,n})
in a ‘proper’ Sierpiński space
X_{f}
: Simply extend
\mathbb{N}
to a larger copy
\mathbb{N}^{\prime}
of the natural numbers by adding intermediate points and extend
\theta
to a bijection
f
between
\mathbb{N}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. In particular, every Sierpiński space contains a complemented isometric copy of
X_{\theta}
. We will embed certain combinatorial spaces in these ‘partial’ Sierpiński spaces. To emphasize that the underlying set of the combinatorial space
X_{\mathcal{F}}
we wish to embed in an
X_{\theta}
does not play any role (unlike
\mathbb{N}
in the defintion of the Sierpiński spaces), we will work with a general countable
\Omega
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{FHC}(\Omega)
. We say that a pair
((I_{t})_{t\in\Omega},\theta)

emulates

\mathcal{F}
if
(I_{t})
is a sequence of pairwise disjoint non-empty intervals on
\mathbb{N}
,
\theta:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
is an injection, and, with
x_{t}=\sum_{n\in I_{t}}e_{\theta,n}\in X_{\theta}
, for every
E\in[\Omega]^{<\infty}

\Big\|\sum_{t\in E}x_{t}\Big\|_{\theta}=|E|\;\text{ if and only if }\,E\in\mathcal{F}.

If there is such a pair, we say that
\mathcal{F}

admits an emulation
.

Notice that
\|\sum_{t\in E}x_{t}\|_{\theta}=|E|
for
E=\{t\}
means that
\|x_{t}\|_{\theta}=1
, i.e.
\theta
is decreasing on
I_{t}
. In other words, an emulation, for now over an infinite
\Omega
, can be seen as the pair of an enumeration
\Omega=\{t_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and a sequence
(R_{t})
of pairwise disjoint non-empty finite subsets of
\mathbb{Q}
: The enumeration gives us the order of the intervals,
I_{t_{1}}<I_{t_{2}}<\dots
,
|I_{t}|=|R_{t}|
, and
\theta
is the unique function
\bigcup_{t}I_{t}\to\mathbb{Q}
which is decreasing on
I_{t}
and
\theta[I_{t}]=R_{t}
; obviously,
\theta
on
\mathbb{N}\setminus\bigcup_{t}I_{t}
does not play any role. It also follows that
I_{t}
itself is not important but
|I_{t}|
and the order of the intervals; this will be useful later when we will need to move / shift the intervals. In general,

\Big\|\sum_{t\in E}x_{t}\Big\|_{\theta}=\max\Big\{|C|:C\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\,\text{ and }\,C\subseteq\bigcup_{t\in E}I_{t}\Big\}

for every finite
E\subseteq\Omega
, where, of course, if
C\cap I_{t}\neq\emptyset
then
|C\cap I_{t}|=1
. In other words, if we label elements of
R_{t_{n}}
with
n
, then
\|\sum_{t\in E}x_{t}\|_{\theta}
is the length of the longest increasing sequence in
\bigcup_{t\in E}R_{t}
with increasing labels.

Applying these observations, it is easy to check the following:

Fact 6.2
.

If
((I_{t}),\theta)
emulates
\mathcal{F}
, then
(x_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\sim}}(e_{\mathcal{F},t})
; in particular, the Sierpiński space contains an isometric copy of
X_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Example 6.3

(emulating
\bigcup\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathrm{Fh}(\mathcal{Q})
)
.

Let
I_{n}=\{n\}
for each
n
and
\theta:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
be increasing. Then
((I_{n}),\theta)
emulates
[\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}
, and so
(x_{n})
generates a copy of
\ell_{1}
. Similarly, if
\theta
is decreasing, then it emulates
[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}
.

Regarding
\bigcup\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathrm{Fh}(\mathcal{Q})
from Example
3.3
, we show that both of them admit emulations assuming every
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
does (see also the remark below). Let
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{n}V_{n}
be a decomposition into non-empty finite sets,
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathcal{Q}_{n})
be a sequence where
\mathcal{Q}_{n}\in\mathrm{FHC}(V_{n})
, and assume that
((I^{n}_{t})_{t\in V_{n}},\theta_{n})
emulates
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.

We can assume that
I^{1}:=\bigcup_{t\in V_{1}}I^{1}_{t}<I^{2}:=\bigcup_{t\in V_{2}}I^{2}_{t}<\dots
and
\bigcup_{n}I^{n}=\mathbb{N}
. We can also move the ranges of each
\theta_{n}
, if we shift them to the left such that
\theta_{1}[I^{1}]>\theta_{2}[I^{2}]>\dots
, then
((I^{n}_{t}),\bigcup_{n}\theta_{n})
emulates
\bigcup\mathcal{Q}
; if we shift them to the right such that
\theta_{1}[I^{1}]<\theta_{2}[I^{2}]<\dots
, then
((I^{n}_{t}),\bigcup_{n}\theta_{n})
emulates
\mathrm{Fh}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Remark 6.4
.

(Balázs Keszegh)
In general, not every finite family, that is,
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{FHC}(V)
for some finite
V\neq\emptyset
, admits emulations. Moreover, among those which satisfy
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[V]^{\leq 2}
, most of them do not admit emulations. This follows from the observation that in this case we can assume that
|I_{t}|\leq 2
for every
t\in V
, and hence, with
n=|V|
, the number of emulations is
\leq n^{n}n^{2n}
but the number of such, pairwise non-isomorphic families is asymptotically
2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!
. Probably the simplest concrete example of such an
\mathcal{F}
without emulation is
\mathcal{C}(C_{5})
.

Example 6.5

(
\ell_{1}\oplus_{0}\ell_{1}
and
c_{0}\oplus_{1}c_{0}
)
.

Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{F}=[\{0\}\times\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}\cup[\{1\}\times\mathbb{N}]^{<\infty}
, in other words,
\mathcal{F}
is the family generating
\ell_{1}\oplus_{0}\ell_{1}
, the product equipped with the max norm. Of course, Sierpiński spaces contain copies of
\ell_{1}\oplus_{0}\ell_{1}
(because it is isomorphic to
\ell_{1}
). At the same time, it is easy to show that
\mathcal{F}
admits no emulation: Assume on the contrary that
((I_{i,n}),\theta)
emulates
\mathcal{F}
(
i\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
). Ordering the intervals consecutively
J_{k}=I_{i_{k},n_{k}}
,
J_{1}<J_{2}<\dots
, we can find
k<m<k^{\prime}
such that
i_{k}=i_{k^{\prime}}\neq i_{m}
and we may assume that
i_{k}=0
. This means that
\theta[J_{m}]<\theta[J_{k}]
because otherwise
\{(0,n_{k}),(1,n_{m})\}
would belong to
\mathcal{F}
, and similarly
\theta[J_{k^{\prime}}]<\theta[J_{m}]
, and so
\theta[J_{k^{\prime}}]<\theta[J_{k}]
but this is impossible because
\{(0,n_{k}),(0,n_{k^{\prime}})\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

The same holds for the family
\mathcal{F}=[\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}\cup\{\{(0,n),(1,m)\}:n,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
generating
c_{0}\oplus_{1}c_{0}
, the product equipped with the sum norm: Fix
J_{k}<J_{m}<J_{k^{\prime}}<J_{m^{\prime}}
such that
i_{k}=i_{k^{\prime}}=0
and
i_{m}=i_{m^{\prime}}=1
. Then
\theta[J_{m^{\prime}}]<\theta[J_{m}]
because
\{(1,n_{m}),(1,n_{m^{\prime}})\}\notin\mathcal{F}
,
\min\theta[J_{m}]<\max\theta[J_{k^{\prime}}]
because
\{(1,n_{m}),(0,n_{k^{\prime}})\}\in\mathcal{F}
, and
\theta[J_{k^{\prime}}]<\theta[J_{k}]
because
\{(0,n_{k}),(0,n_{k^{\prime}})\}\notin\mathcal{F}
, therefore
\theta[J_{m^{\prime}}]<\theta[J_{k}]
, a contradiction becaue
\{(0,n_{k}),(1,n_{m^{\prime}})\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Question 6.6
.

Does the Sierpiński space contain isometric copies of
\ell_{1}\oplus_{0}\ell_{1}
or
c_{0}\oplus_{1}c_{0}
?

In the rest of this section, we will discuss the Schreier families and their possible emulations (for more details and results on these families and the generated spaces, see e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
11
]
,

[
16
]
). We will need the following (see
[
12
]
for
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{D}
): The
Schreier operation

\mathbf{S}:\mathrm{FHC}\to\mathrm{FHC}
,

\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F})=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigg\{\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}F_{k}:m\in\mathbb{N},F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\emptyset\},\text{ and }\,\{m\}\leq F_{1}<\dots<F_{m}\bigg\},

the
diagonal union operation

\mathbf{D}:\mathrm{FHC}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathrm{FHC}
,

\mathbf{D}\big((\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k}\big)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}\!\upharpoonright\![k,\infty)=\{\emptyset\}\cup\big\{F\colon F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}\setminus\{\emptyset\},k\leq\min F\big\},

and a combination
\mathbf{D}^{*}:\mathrm{FHC}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathrm{FHC}
of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{D}
,

\mathbf{D}^{*}\big((\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k}\big)=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigg\{\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}F_{k}:m\in\mathbb{N},F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}\setminus\{\emptyset\},\text{ and }\,\{m\}\leq F_{m}<\dots<F_{1}\bigg\}.

In order to define the Schreier families, we fix a
ladder system

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\big((\xi^{\gamma}_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}:\gamma\in\mathrm{Lim}(\omega_{1})\big)

where
\mathrm{Lim}(\omega_{1})=\{
limit ordinals below
\omega_{1}\}
and
\xi^{\gamma}_{1}<\xi^{\gamma}_{2}<\dots<\gamma
is unbounded in
\gamma
for each such
\gamma
. Given a ladder system
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, the associated
Schreier
and
Schreier
∗
sequences,
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
are constructed by recursion on
\alpha<\omega_{1}
: Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathcal{S}^{*}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}
, in the successor step let

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha+1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{S}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)\,\text{ and }\,\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha+1}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{S}\big(\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big),

and for a limit
\gamma
let

\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{D}\big(\big(\mathcal{S}_{\xi^{\gamma}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)_{k}\big)\,\text{ and }\,\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{D}^{*}\big(\big(\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\xi^{\gamma}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)_{k}\big).

It is common to assume a bit more about the generating ladder system, namely, we call
\boldsymbol{\xi}

standard
if

\mathcal{S}_{\xi^{\gamma}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\xi^{\gamma}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subseteq\dots\,\text{ is increasing for every }\,\gamma.

It is easy to construct standard ladders by recursion applying the following: An easy induction shows that given an arbitrary ladder system
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\alpha<\beta<\omega_{1}
,

\text{there is an }\,m\in\mathbb{N}\,\text{ such that }\,\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\!\upharpoonright\![m,\infty)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

and hence
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\beta+m}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
. Now, if
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is standard, then so is
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{+}=((\xi^{\gamma}_{k}+1)_{k=1}^{\infty}:\gamma\in\mathrm{Lim}(\omega_{1}))
and it is straightforward to check that

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{+})\,\text{ for every }\,\alpha.

From now on, if we simply write
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha}
, we refer to any families of the form
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
for some standard
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

We will use the following preservation theorem:

Theorem 6.7
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{k}\in\mathrm{FHC}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following holds:

(1)

If
\mathcal{F}
has an emulation
((I_{n}),\theta)
satisfying
I_{1}<I_{2}<\dots
, then so does the family
\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F})
.

(2)

If each
\mathcal{F}_{k}
has an emulation
((I^{k}_{n}),\theta_{k})
such that
I^{k}_{1}<I^{k}_{2}<\dots
, then so does the family

\mathbf{D}^{*}((\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k})
.

Proof.

(1):
We may assume that
\theta[\mathbb{N}]\subseteq(0,1)
. Let
(J_{n})
be the interval partition of
\mathbb{N}
such that
J_{n}=I^{1}_{n}\cup\dots\cup I^{n}_{n}
, where
|I^{k}_{n}|=|I_{n}|
for each
k
and
I^{1}_{n}<I^{2}_{n}<\dots<I^{n}_{n}
. Fix order preserving bijections
g^{k}_{n}\colon I^{k}_{n}\to I_{n}
and define
\theta^{*}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
:

\text{For }\,j\in I^{k}_{n}\subseteq J_{n}\,\text{ let }\,\theta^{*}(j)=\theta\big(g^{k}_{n}(j)\big)-k.

In other words,
\theta^{*}
acts like
\theta
on each
I^{k}_{n}\sim I_{n}
but moves the images to the left of each other as
k
increases. It follows that
\theta^{*}
is decreasing on
J_{n}
.

We claim that
((J_{n}),\theta^{*})
emulates
\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F})
, that is, with
x_{n}=\sum_{j\in J_{n}}x_{\theta^{*},j}\in X_{\theta^{*}}
,

\Big\|\sum_{n\in E}x_{n}\Big\|_{\theta^{*}}=|E|\,\text{ iff }\,E\in\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F}).

First, let
E=F_{1}\cup\dots\cup F_{m}\in\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F})
where
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
and
\{m\}\leq F_{1}<F_{2}<\dots<F_{m}
. We need to find an increasing sequence
(j_{n})_{n\in E}
such that
j_{n}\in J_{n}
and
(\theta^{*}(j_{n}))_{n\in E}
is also increasing.
As
\mathcal{F}
is emulated by
((I_{n}),\theta)
, for each
k
there is an increasing sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in F_{k}}
such that
i_{n}\in I_{n}
and
(\theta(i_{n}))_{n\in F_{k}}
is also increasing. Take the copies of these
i_{n}
from
I^{m-k+1}_{n}
(we know that
k\leq m\leq n
), that is, let
j_{n}\in I^{m-k+1}_{n}\subseteq J_{n}
such that
g^{m-k+1}_{n}(j_{n})=i_{n}
. Then
(j_{n})_{n\in F_{k}}
is increasing, just like

\theta^{*}(j_{n})=\theta\big(g^{m-k+1}_{n}(j_{n})\big)-(m-k+1)=\theta(i_{n})-m+k-1\;(n\in F_{k}).

The concatenation
(j_{n})_{n\in E}
of the sequences
(j_{n})_{n\in F_{k}}
,
k=1,\dots,m
is increasing; we show that its image
(\theta^{*}(j_{n}))_{n\in E}
is also increasing: If
n\in F_{k}
and
n^{\prime}\in F_{k+1}
, then
j_{n}\in I^{m-k+1}_{n}\subseteq J_{n}
and
j_{n^{\prime}}\in I^{m-k}_{n^{\prime}}\subseteq J_{n^{\prime}}
, hence

\theta^{*}(j_{n})=\theta(i_{n})-m+k-1<-m+k<\theta(i_{n^{\prime}})-m+k=\theta^{*}(j_{n^{\prime}}).

Now suppose that
\|\sum_{n\in E}x_{n}\|_{\theta}=|E|
holds for some finite
E\subseteq\mathbb{N}
witnessed by
(j_{n})_{n\in E}
, and let
m=\min(E)
. First of all, notice that if
n\in E\setminus\{m\}
and
j_{n}\in I^{k}_{n}
, then
k\leq m
because otherwise
\theta^{*}(j_{n})=\theta(g^{k}_{n}(j_{n}))-k<-m
and, at the same time, with some
k^{\prime}\leq m
,
\theta^{*}(j_{n})>\theta^{*}(j_{m})=\theta(g^{k^{\prime}}_{m}(j_{m}))-k^{\prime}\geq-m
. For each
k\leq m
define

F_{k}=\big\{n\in E:j_{n}\in I^{m-k+1}_{n}\big\}.

Then
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
because for
n\in F_{k}
both
i_{n}=g^{m-k+1}_{n}(j_{n})
and
\theta(i_{n})=\theta^{*}(j_{n})+m-k+1
are increasing. Some of these sets may be empty but if
n\in F_{k}
and
n^{\prime}\in F_{k^{\prime}}
for some
k<k^{\prime}\leq m
, then

\theta^{*}(j_{n})=\theta(i_{n})-m+k-1<-m+k^{\prime}-1<\theta(i_{n^{\prime}})-m+k^{\prime}-1=\theta^{*}(j_{n^{\prime}})

and hence
F_{k}<F_{k^{\prime}}
. It follows that
E\in\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{F})
.

(2): We can
assume that
\theta_{k}[\mathbb{N}]\subseteq(0,1)
for each
k
. Also, we can rearrange the intervals
I^{k}_{n}
by shifting them along
\mathbb{N}
such that

I^{1}_{n}<\dots<I^{n}_{n}<I^{1}_{n+1}<\dots\,\text{ and }\,J_{n}:=I^{1}_{n}\cup\dots\cup I^{n}_{n}\,\text{ is an interval}.

Define
\theta^{*}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}
as follows:

\text{For }\,j\in I^{k}_{n}\subseteq J_{n}\,\text{ let }\,\theta^{*}(j)=\theta_{k}(j)-k.

The obvious modification of the proof of (1) shows that
((J_{n}),\theta^{*})
emulates
\mathbf{D}^{*}((\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k})
: We pick
j_{n}\in I^{k}_{n}\subseteq J_{n}
, instead of
j_{n}\in I^{m-k+1}_{n}
, and concatenate the sequences
(j_{n})_{n\in F_{k}}
in the reverse order,
(j_{n})_{n\in F_{m}},(j_{n})_{n\in F_{m-1}},\dots,(j_{n})_{n\in F_{1}}
. Similarly, in the second part of the proof, we work with
I^{k}_{n}
instead of
I^{m-k+1}_{n}
.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

When applying Theorem
6.7
to obtain an emulation of
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathcal{S}
from the canonical emulation of
\mathcal{S}_{0}=[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}
,
|J_{n}|=n
for each

and

will be the union of infinitely

. Similarly, if

Since
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\mathcal{S}^{*}_{0}=[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq 1}
has an emulation as required above, we obtain the following by induction:

Corollary 6.9
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
admits an emulation, and so the Sierpiński space contains isometric copies of each Schreier space

of finite order. Furthermore, for every

,
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\alpha}
admits an emulation, and hence the Sierpiński space contains isometric copies of each Schreier
∗
space

.

It is natural to ask the following :

Question 6.10
.

Does the Schreier families

7.
Embedding
\ell_{p}
’s in the Sierpiński space

Let
(X,\|\bullet\|)
be a sequence Banach lattice, that is,
c_{00}\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the unit vectors
(e_{n})
are normalized in
\|\bullet\|
and for any
x\in X
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, if
|y(n)|\leq|x(n)|
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
y\in X
with
\|y\|\leq\|x\|
. In follows that
(e_{n})
is a 1-unconditional basic sequence in
X
, in particular for any
x=\sum_{n}a_{n}x_{n}\in X
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
also
x_{A}=\sum_{n\in A}a_{n}x_{n}\in X
with
\|x_{A}\|_{X}\leq\|x\|_{X}
. Let
S(X)
be the unit sphere in
X
and
\widetilde{B}(X)=B(X)\cap c_{00}
.

Consider a partial order in
\tilde{B}(X)
defined as follows:
x\,\dot{\leqslant}\,y
, for
x,y\in\tilde{B}(X)
, if for any
z\in\tilde{B}(X)
with
\min\supp z>\max\{\max\supp x,\max\supp y\}
, and
x+z\in\tilde{B}(X)
also
y+z\in\tilde{B}(X)
.

Consider the following properties of the basic sequence
(e_{n})\subseteq X
.

(
\alpha
)

for any
x,y\in\tilde{B}(X)
with a finite support, either
x\,\dot{\leqslant}\,y
or
y\,\dot{\leqslant}\,x
,

(
\beta
)

for any
x,y\in\tilde{B}(X)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\max\{\max\supp x,\max\supp y\}<n
, and scalars
a,b\in[0,1]
: if
y\,\dot{\leqslant}\,x+ay_{n}\in\tilde{B}(X)
with
x+(a+b)y_{n}\in\tilde{B}(X)

\tilde{B}(X)\ni y+by_{n}\,\dot{\leqslant}\,x+(a+b)y_{n}

Proposition 7.1
.

Let

be a sequence Banach lattice admitting a function

with the following properties:

(1)

for any

(2)

is additive on disjointly supported vectors,

(3)

.

Then

for any

. Moreover, the unit vector sequence

satisfies
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
.

Proof.

Fix

.

Assume first that

and take

with

\min\supp z>\max\{\max\supp x,\max\supp y\}

and

. Then by (1) and (2) we have

and

. For

in terms of

,
x(v)=1/2
for

, then it can be extended to an extreme point of
B(Y_{G})
:

Theorem 8.1
.

If
G=(V,E)
contains an odd hole
D
, then there is an extreme point of
B(Y_{G})
whose value is
1/2
on each
v\in D
(so it is not terminal) and
\mathrm{ran}(x)\subseteq\{0,1/2,1\}
.

Proof.

We assume that
V=\{v_{0},v_{1},\dots\}
is infinite. We will find the desired extreme point
x
in a recursive way, setting more and more values of
x
. We may assume, without loss of generality, that
G
is connected. Indeed, suppose
that a connected component
C_{0}
contains an odd hole and we are able to find
x_{0}
supported on
C_{0}
. For every other component
C
we can fix a terminal point
x_{C}
supported by this component, and then
x=x_{0}+\sum_{C}x_{C}\in\mathrm{Ext}(B(Y_{G}))
.

Let
D_{0}=D
and define
x(v)=1/2
for each
v\in D_{0}
. At the
(n+1)
st step let

k=\min\big\{j\in\mathbb{N}:v_{j}\in V\setminus D_{n}\,\text{ and }\,v_{j}\,\text{ is connected to a vertex in }\,D_{n}\big\}.

Since
G
is infinite and connected,
k
is well-defined and, with
D_{n+1}=D_{n}\cup\{v_{k}\}
,
\bigcup_{n}D_{n}=V
. At the
(n+1)
st step we define
x(v_{k})
as follows:

•

if there is
v\in D_{n}
connected to
v_{k}
such that
x(v)=1
, then put
x(v_{k})=0
,

•

if there are
v\neq v^{\prime}\in D_{n}
such that
x(v)=x(v^{\prime})=1/2
, and
v,v^{\prime},v_{k}
are pairwise connected, then put
x(v_{k})=0
,

•

if all
v\in D_{n}
connected to
v_{k}
are such that
x(v)=0
, then put
x(v_{k})=1
,

•

otherwise, put
x(v_{k})=1/2
.

We will sketch the proof that
x\in\mathrm{Ext}(B(Y_{G}))
. Let
V_{i}=x^{-1}(i)
for
i\in\{0,1/2,1\}
. Suppose that
z
is such that
\|x\pm z\|_{G}\leq 1
. Notice that
\mathrm{supp}(z)\subseteq V_{1/2}
: Indeed, clearly
\mathrm{supp}(z)\cap V_{1}=\emptyset
, and if
v\in V_{0}
, then it is connected either to a
w\in V_{1}
or it forms a triangle with some
w,w^{\prime}\in V_{1/2}
. In any case,
z(v)=0
follows.

It is easy to see that
V_{1/2}
is connected. We know that
z\!\upharpoonright\!D_{0}\equiv 0
because
x\!\upharpoonright\!D_{0}
is an extreme point of
B(Y_{G\upharpoonright D_{0}})
. Now, fix a
w\in V_{1/2}\setminus D_{0}
with
d(w,D_{0})=n>0
where
d
is the usual distance function on
G\!\upharpoonright\!V_{1/2}
. Then there is a chain
w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=w
in
V_{1/2}
such that
d(w_{i},D_{0})=i
and it follows that
z(w_{n})=-z(w_{n-1})=z(w_{n-2})=\dots=\pm z(w_{0})=0
.
∎

What can we say about odd antiholes?

Theorem 8.2
.

Suppose
G=(V,E)
is an
(2n+1)
-antihole and that
|x(v)|=1/n
for each
v\in V
. Then
x\in\mathrm{Ext}(B(Y_{G}))
.

Proof.

Enumerate
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{2n+1}\}
along the associated cycle and notice that

(1,0,1,0,\dots,1,0,1,0,0)

is the characteristic function of a maximal clique. In fact, all maximal cliques are of size
n
and their characteristic functions are shifts of the one above, enumerate them as
C_{1},\dots,C_{2n+1}
. Let
A
be the
(2n+1)\times(2n+1)
matrix such that its
i
th row is the characteristic function of
C_{i}
. One can show (see
[
18
, Proposition 4.3.9]
) that
\det(A)\neq 0
.
6
6

6

A matrix of this form, that is, when the rows list all possible shifts of a fixed non-zero vector, is called
circulant
. The fact that these matrices are invertible follows from the fact that its eigenvalues are combinations of square
n
-roots of identity where the coefficients are given by a discrete Fourier transform of the inducing vector.

Now fix an
x
such that
|x(v)|=1/n
for each
v
, we show that
x
is an extreme point of
B(Y_{G})
. We can assume that
x(v)=1/n
for each
v
. Let
z\in Y_{G}
be such that
\|x\pm z\|_{G}\leq 1
. Then, with a small enough
\varepsilon>0
, for each maximal clique
C
, we have

1\pm\sum_{v\in C}\varepsilon z(v)=\sum_{v\in C}\big|1/n\pm\varepsilon z(v)\big|=\sum_{v\in C}\big|x(v)\pm\varepsilon z(v)\big|\leq\|x+\varepsilon z\|_{G}\leq 1,

and so
\sum_{v\in C}z(v)=0
, i.e.
Az=0
, hence
z=0
.
∎

Perhaps the extreme points of the above form can be extended to the whole graph containing an odd antihole, like in Theorem
8.1
, but the construction may be much more involved.

Finally, we present an additional application of the idea used in the last proof:

Corollary 8.3
.

For each
q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[-1,1]
there is a graph
G
and an
x\in\mathrm{Ext}(B(Y_{G}))
such that
x(v)=q
for some
v
.

Proof.

We can assume that
q=i/n\in(0,1)
where
i,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 2
. Let
V=D\cup\{w\}
, where
D
is a
(2n+1)
-antihole. Fix a non-maximal clique
C
in
D
of size
n-i
, connect
w
to all vertices in
C
, and define
x
on the resulting graph
G
by
x(v)=1/n
for
v\in D
and
x(w)=i/n
. We claim
that
x\in\mathrm{Ext}(B(Y_{G}))
. Consider the matrix
A
associated to
D
as in the proof of Theorem
8.2
and extend it to a
(2n+2)\times(2n+2)
-matrix
B
,

B=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
y&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

where
y
is a characteristic function of
C
. Then
\det(B)=\det(A)\neq 0
and if
\|x\pm z\|_{G}\leq 1
then, by the same argument as above,
Bz=0
, hence
z=0
.
∎

The above corollary indicates that, in the general case, the extreme points of

may be much more complicated than the terminal points of
