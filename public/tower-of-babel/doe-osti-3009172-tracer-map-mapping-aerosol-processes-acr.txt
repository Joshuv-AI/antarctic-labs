1. Executive Summary

The Houston, TX Metropolitan area was selected for the 2022 Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions
Experiment (TRACER) due to its high convective storm activity and broad range of polluted aerosol
conditions. With the overall TRACER project in mind, we planned TRACER-MAP, a related
investigation of the impacts of convective storms on atmospheric composition, chemistry and aerosol
processing in Houston. Because the Houston metroplex is large and has a wide range of atmospheric
conditions, this project has “MAP-ped” the air chemistry conditions across Houston while atmospheric
scientists working in the TRACER project monitor convective storm activity overhead. Specifically, we
conducted a series of aerosol, gas, and meteorological measurements in the summer of 2022 that matches
measurements from the Department of Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM)
Mobile Facilities. The TRACER-MAP campaign extended from Jul 1 – Sept 3, 2022. TRACER-MAP
included two urban sites: Aldine and University of Houston (UH); two industrial sites: San Jacinto
Battleground and the main DOE TRACER site at the La Porte Municipal Airport (ARM/La Porte AMF);
and one background site in WG Jones State Forest north of Houston.

With the extensive instrumentation payload of the MAQL2 and the diversity of sites across Houston,
TRACER-MAP captured diverse emission, sea breeze and atmospheric chemistry conditions.

2. Project Activities

2.1. TRACER-MAP measurement campaign

Our first objective was to conduct aerosol (particle size, distribution and composition, CCN concentration
and activity, aerosol optical properties), gas (VOC and trace gases) and meteorology (including boundary
layer height) measurements at sites across Houston. PIs contributed to the BAMS article on TRACER led
by Michael Jensen (2025 in press) which listed these activities.

2.1.1 VOC measurement summary
VOCs were measured and averaged to a 5-minute time resolution. Site averages were calculated, which
included all time spent at each site, for data below MDL, a one-third MDL value was substituted (Figure
2). Figure 2 includes VOC measured in the hydronium ion mode, including aromatic, oxygenated and
biogenic VOC and highly reactive VOC (HR-VOC) which were measured in select reagent ion mode for
the PTRMS. The propene mass (m42) likely included additional reactive hydrocarbons (e.g. pentene,
hexene, cyclopentane and cyclohexane), so it’s labeled propene+.

Figure 2 Site averages of VOC measured by PTRMS in Jul-Aug 2022 during TRACER-MAP. Bottom panel includes highly
reactive VOC (HR-VOC; propene plus additional compounds with m42 in the O2+ ionization mode and 1,3-butadiene).

The ARM site had the largest influence from propene+, aromatic compounds including toluene, styrene
and xylenes. Similar to m42, the m75 (hydroxyacetone) appears to include additional compounds at
ARM. In contrast to other sites, the m75 concentration exceeded isoprene at ARM and may have included
isobutanol or methyl acetate. With all these considered (acetone, aromatics, propene+, and m75), ARM
likely has strong industrial influence, which is confirmed by the local emissions inventory. The high
fraction of acetaldehyde likely also indicates some influence of aged air masses as well. Aldine and UH,
both near-urban sites, had similar profiles with motor vehicle, biogenic and volatile chemical product
(VCP, acetone) contributions as well as aged/oxidized VOC (e.g. MVK+MACR, acetaldehyde, MEK and
hydroxyacetone). In contrast, San Jacinto also had a strong industrial signal with high contributions from
acetone, isoprene, MEK, and benzene. Diurnal trends and emissions inventories help to identify these
VOC as point source emissions, as isoprene and MEK had both high overnight concentrations and high
emissions in the inventories. Jones Forest represents a rural/forested site which is evident from high
contributions of biogenic VOC including isoprene and monoterpenes and lower contributions from

aged/oxidized VOC in this source region. The VOC profile is relevant in terms of characterizing source
regimes as well as for understanding new particle formation and growth across Houston. A manuscript is
near completion describing the VOC profiles and upwind emissions inventory across Houston during
TRACER-MAP, with comparisons to historical measurements in the Houston Shipping Channel.
Additionally, a manuscript has been submitted Aerosol Science & Technology (Nov 2025), which
includes VOC data from TRACER-MAP and is led by Jeff Pierce’s group at Colorado State University
(O’Donnell et al).

2.1.3 Meteorology and boundary height summary

information was collected using a Vaisala CL-31 ceilometer. The meteorological regimes present during

measurements from the MAQL-2 trailer. Identification of outflow boundaries (gust fronts) and storm
activity at the site as well as days with a local Seabreeze/Land breeze circulation in the region. The
measurement period included approximately half (52 %) of days with observed outflow boundaries across
the five sites. The statistics for each site are shown in Table 1.

Diel profile of the first retrieved boundary layer height from the ceilometer are shown in Figure 4. The
first retrieved layer height from the ceilometer is often considered the mixing layer height (MH). The
University of Houston campus observed the maximum diel MH of approximately 2100 m. The two
coastal locations, La Porte and Battleground both showed a lower maximum diel MH during the
afternoon (convective) portion of the day with a bimodal peak. Rapid decreases in MH after the afternoon
peak are likely influenced from convective activity shutting off surface heating. A longer-term
measurement would be recommended for validation of measurement profiles.

2.1.4 Particle measurement summary
University of California Researchers used a set of instruments to measure aerosol size distributions and
size-resolved CCN activation efficiency as part of the TRACER-MAP project. Size distributions were
measured using a combination of a scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS; home-built) and an
aerodynamic particle sizer (APS; TSI 3321). The raw data from the two instruments were processed
together to produce merged size distributions spanning the 0.02 to 16 mm combined size range of the two
separately (Figure 5). Size-resolved CCN activation efficiency was measured with a cloud condensation
nuclei counter (CCNc; DMT CCN-100) operated downstream of the differential mobility analyzer of the

SMPS and in parallel with a condensation particle counter (CPC; TSI 3762). The CCNc supersaturation
stepped through supersaturations of 0.15%, 0.47%, and 0.64% between SMPS size scans.

All instruments performed well throughout the study, with only a few short gaps in data collection, as
shown in the figure below of the number and volume concentration size distribution time series. Analysis
has focused primarily on three features of the dataset: i) the influence of meteorology and sampling
location on the frequency and strength of new particle formation (NPF) events, ii) the influence of
Saharan dust on the coarse mode size distribution, and iii) site-to-site and diurnal variability in the particle
hygroscopicity inferred from the CCNc measurements. A manuscript summarizing the dataset and
highlighting those areas of focus is nearing completion and will be submitted soon.

Figure 5 Combined particle count and size distribution measurements.

2.2 Aerosol characteristics in pre- and post-storm scenarios

Our second objective was to determine spatial variability across the Houston metropolitan area of aerosol
characteristics in pre- and post-storm scenarios in terms of particle size, distribution, composition, CCN
activity and optical properties. An example of aerosol composition changes noted for pre- and post-rain
scenarios is included below.

2.2.1 Aerosol impacts pre- and post-storm
An analysis of measurements from the aerosol mass spectrometer (AMS) revealed differences between
pre- and post-storm aerosol for oxidized aerosol (Figure 6). LOOOA is less-oxidized oxygenated organic

2.3 Urban aerosol processing and atmospheric chemistry

For our third objective we wanted to determine how urban aerosol processing differs between pre- and
post-storm atmospheres in terms of particle growth, aging and CCN activity utilizing integration of
observations and modeling.

2.3.1 New particle formation under different atmospheric conditions

We quantified differences in atmospheric conditions (e.g. aerosol composition, gas composition, and
condensation sick) for non-rain and post-rain scenarios (Figure 7). We found that NPF was observed on
both non-rain and rain days (included in the data here), despite wide differences in these atmospheric
conditions.

2.3.2 Atmospheric chemistry modeling efforts during TRACER-MAP

The modeling investigation used the Comprehensive Air Pollution model with Extensions (CAMx)
version 7.31 coupled with simulated meteorology data from the Weather Research and Forecasting
(WRF) model version 4.6. The model used initial and boundary conditions from GEOS-Chem version
14.1.1, and 2019 emissions data from TCEQ to simulate three domains over the contiguous United States
(d01: 12km x 12km), Southeast Texas (d02: 4km x 4km), and the Houston/Galveston region (d03:
1.33km x 1.33km) for the duration of the TRACER-MAP campaign. We tested different configurations
and physics options for the WRF model and found the optimal settings best predicted the conditions
during TRACER-MAP period. To analyze the performance of the model, we used surface wind and
relative humidity data from the TRACER ancillary (ACC) and ARM Mobile Facility (AMF1) sites as
well as trace gases data from the TRACER-MAP Mobile Air Quality Laboratory 2 (MAQL-2) and
Continuous Ambient Monitoring Station (CAMS) surface stations.

Figure 8 Spatial distribution of hourly relative humidity, ozone, and nitrogen oxides (NOx) with wind during the July 12th sea-
breeze event. Dots represent surface observations for ACC site (dark blue) and AMF1 site (purple) from TRACER-AQ, the
MAQL-2 mobile laboratory (teal) from TRACER-MAP, and CAMS surface stations (black) with white dots representing sites
with only wind data.

The final report shows an example of the simulation results for the week of July 10th, 2022. This period
was chosen due to the presence of two sea-breeze events coupled with elevated surface ozone on July 11th
and July 12th with our main focus on the July 12th event. Figure 8 shows relative humidity, Ozone, and
Nitrogen Oxides combined with wind for both the model and observations. Based on the winds and
relative humidity, the model predicts the timing of the sea-breeze passage quite well with both the wind
and relative humidity shifting during the same hour and location as observed. The model does slightly

under-predict wind speeds near the coast for all shown hours with better predictions over land. For
surface ozone, the spatial and temporal variation of the ozone plume over the urban Houston area was
captured quite well in the model. There is a slight over-estimation during the last hour around the Houston
Ship-channel which is most likely due to the slightly over-estimated values over and near the coast. NOx
concentrations also show a good agreement throughout the domain except for one site near the center of
Houston. Due to the site being within close proximity and downwind of a major highway, this
disagreement could be due to the influence of local emissions from individual car plumes.

Overall, the model predicted both the timing of the sea-breeze and its effects on trace gases quite well
when compared to surface observations. The model predicted similar patterns for aerosol speciation such
as sulfate and organic carbon, but fewer sites of aerosol component measurements were available to
evaluate the model. Model sensitivity experiments were also conducted to quantify the effects of
anthropogenic emissions from Houston on trace gases and aerosols during the campaign periods and
different types of meteorological conditions (e.g., sea breeze and convection). The project team is
preparing a manuscript on those modeling results.

2.4 Convective transport impacts on aerosol and VOC

Our fourth objective was to identify potential influences of convective transport (e.g. downdrafts) on
particle size, number and composition through the interpretation of in situ and airborne tracer
measurements.

2.4.1 Case days of convective transport impact
A case day, July 25, 2022, where an outflow boundary impacted surface measurements at the Aldine, TX
site is shown in Figure 9.

 Figure 9 A time-series from July 25, 2022, at the Aldine site showing a rapid ozone enhancement coincident with a
 convective storm outflow boundary passage – highlighted with a gray box.

A significant ozone enhancement of 24ppb over 25 minutes time was observed at 15:22 local time,
coincident with a convective storm outflow boundary passage. Outflow passage was identified using
KHGX radar scans and verified using surface measurements at the site. This enhancement shows a
distinct air mass transition highlighting how convective storm activity can influence surface air quality.

Figure 10 Time series of particle size distributions and VOC with convective storms including precipitation events at the site
indicated by grey boxes.

We observed a few different events on Aug 18- 20 at Aldine, a near-urban site (Figure 10). Each grey box
indicates a frontal passage that included a precipitation event. Changes were observed in the particle size
distributions, with some particle growth events noted. Event B (Aug 19 ~12–04 UTC = 07–23 CDT)
occurred in the morning to midday (12–16 UTC) with a shallow boundary layer and the largest aromatic
VOC peaks co-rising with CO/NO₂ and a brief O₃ titration. In the afternoon (16–22 UTC) the boundary
layer deepens which results in partial dilution of VOCs, however, the aromatic VOCs are still elevated
above the continental baseline. In Event C (Aug 20 ~20 UTC = 15 CDT) there is a deep, well-mixed
boundary layer with lower aromatic VOCs at the surface and higher oxygenated VOCs and O₃ associated
secondary production. Combined these show that time of day controls impact—morning onshore mature
convective storm intersects a shallow BL (strong surface aromatics); the similar afternoon mature
convective storm is diluted/lofted, so primary VOCs are relatively lower. Characterization of these events
is being developed into a manuscript.
