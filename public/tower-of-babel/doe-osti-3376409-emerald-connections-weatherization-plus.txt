Executive Summary
The Emerald Connections Weatherization Plus Health Pilot Program (Healthy Homes Pilot or
the pilot), delivered 97 comprehensive energy retrofits to low-income households in two
underserved communities: Lynn, Massachusetts (57) and the Beacon Hill neighborhood in
Seattle, Washington (40). Designed by Emerald Cities Collaborative (ECC) and funded via a
U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) Enhancement
and Innovation (E&I) grant, ECC created the pilot to expand the reach and range of services
typically available to participating WAP households.

Specifically, the pilot enabled enhanced health and safety (H&S) improvements alongside
energy efficiency and electrification improvements, enabling clients who would otherwise
have had their improvements deferred by WAP to participate. The pilot project installed
eligible measures alongside measures funded by WAP and local utility programs. Eligible
measures included exterior repairs, insulation and air sealing, heating system repairs and
replacements (including heat pumps), mold, lead, and asbestos remediation and pest
control, and lighting and appliance upgrades. The pilot, like WAP, was provided at no
additional cost to income-qualified clients.

ECC designed the pilot to assess two overarching program delivery questions:

1. Can outreach led by community-based organizations (CBOs) reduce barriers to
participation?

2. Does the pilot’s funding and flexibility enable weatherization agencies to address
deferrals and provide more comprehensive services?

To determine the answer to these questions and to identify lessons learned and best
practices to inform future programs, ECC contracted with Resource Innovations, an energy-
focused consulting company. To evaluate the pilot, our team conducted half a dozen
interviews with ECC, participating CBOs, and weatherization agencies. We also conducted
15 in-depth interviews with pilot participants across both states. Our team’s interviews
covered all aspects of the pilot lifecycle, including outreach, enrollment, and income
verification, home energy assessments, measure installation, and home repairs. During each
interview, we also sought to understand implementation challenges and opportunities to
improve upon the pilot’s design for future application.

Key Findings
Based on our interviews with pilot delivery partners and a sample of participants, the
evaluation team offers the following answers to the pilot’s two key program delivery
questions.

1. Can outreach led by CBOs reduce barriers to participation?

The evaluation team’s interviews with CBOs, weatherization agencies, and participants
indicated that the pilot’s CBO-centric approach to recruitment reduced several key barriers
to energy program participation, including:

 • Program awareness. Most interviewed participants were not aware of the energy
 efficiency and energy assistance programs prior to their CBO telling them about the
 opportunity to participate in the pilot. If they were, they did not know if they qualified
 and/or were interested in learning more.
 • Trust concerns. Due to their trusted and long-standing presence in the Lynn and
 Beacon Hill communities, LSN and El Centro’s endorsement of the pilot mitigated
 participants’ anxiety of permitting access and improvements to their home.
 • Enrollment difficulties. Participants universally praised the support they received
 from CBOs throughout their experience in the pilot. Their feedback clearly indicated
 that the personalized “case management” approach taken by CBOs avoided some
 potential participants dropping out of the pilot during the protracted enrollment,
 income verification, and scheduling process.

Despite strong interest and the ability to address the traditional participation barriers above,
CBOs faced several challenges to convert expressions of interest into eligible pilot
participants, including:

 • Interest outpaced eligibility. In Seattle, El Centro reported that the number of
 interest forms received was seven times higher than the number of individuals who
 qualified for the pilot. Some clients did not live in the pilot’s service area, and some
 clients were unable to enroll since pre-enrollment language support was not
 available in an onboarding capacity.
 • Language barriers. Although CBOs often provided in-language materials and
 support, they reported that some participants dropped out when weatherization
 agencies were unable to offer the same consistent in-language assistance during the
 assessment (i.e., certain administrative staff were unable to coordinate or support
 clients). While language support was available, it could not always be arranged
 quickly enough to meet immediate needs. For example, a contractor might return to

 iv

 a site on short notice for a callback, but the weatherization agency may not have been
 able to secure on-site interpretation that same day.

While CBO-centered outreach mitigated many of the traditional barriers described above, it
proved insufficient to meet the pilot’s participation target of 120 participants by February
2026. This was likely because otherwise eligible households required additional assistance
to meet all eligibility criteria, particularly the fuel assistance requirement. Providing this
support required more intensive case management. Given the pilot’s limited duration, CBOs
shifted their focus to supporting applicants who had already been recruited and previously
met all eligibility requirements. At the same time, weatherization partners prioritized
assisting clients already in the pipeline to help ensure that as many households as possible
could ultimately be served.

When it became clear that CBO recruitment alone could not fill the pilot’s participation
pipeline by the expiration of the pilot, Action and OH used pilot funds for clients within their
existing participation timeline. In some cases, these clients were going to be served without
the pilot but received more comprehensive improvements to their homes as a result. In
other cases, specifically in Lynn, the funding flexibility allowed Action to serve clients who
would otherwise have been deferred. ECC saw this shift—from focusing exclusively on CBO
recruitment to supporting eligible households in weatherization partner’s pipeline—as a
preferred alternative to spending more time on CBO recruitment and potentially running
out of time to serve eligible households in the pilot’s identified communities.

Even if the CBOs had successfully enrolled the original target of 120 pilot participants, ECC
noted that volume may have exceeded the implementation capacity of the partner
weatherization agencies, which were concurrently serving their existing customer pipelines.
ECC shared that Action, for example, typically completes approximately 75 DOE WAP
projects each year. Consequently adding 60 CBO-recruited units on top of their existing
pipeline may not have been feasible given the pilot’s limited delivery timeline.

Additionally, OH faced capacity constraints, though pilot’s timing limitation was likely the
most significant challenge. If the program had been able to start earlier or secure a no-cost
extension, it is likely that the target of 120 units could have been met. Leveraged funding
sources also shifted during implementation (e.g., potential repeal of the IRA, utility program
changes, etc.). While the original plan—based on the information available at the time of
application—was to complete 120 units, that goal ultimately proved ambitious given the
funding uncertainties, inflation, and other changes that occurred during the project period.

The evidence suggests that while CBOs were able to identify, recruit, and support the
enrollment of clients that were less likely to otherwise participate, the approach still faces
challenges and, for this specific pilot effort, did not yield a large volume of participants to

 v

meet the pilot’s goals. As a result, CBO-led outreach may be most effective when
implemented as part of a broader, multi-pronged outreach strategy rather than a
standalone recruitment mechanism. Through the pilot, ECC and the pilot partners were able
to increase the depth of interventions by providing more comprehensive and holistic
services. However, the compressed delivery timeline for the pilot makes it difficult to render
a definitive assessment.

2. Does the pilot’s funding and flexibility enable weatherization agencies to address
 deferrals and provide more comprehensive services?

The answer to this pilot delivery and evaluation question differed meaningfully between the
two states the pilot operated in.

In Lynn, the pilot significantly altered how Action’s auditors delivered services to low-income
households in Lynn. The ability to combine the pilot’s flexible E&I funding with existing
funding sources—specifically DOE WAP funds and National Grid resources—enabled Action
to serve households that previously could not be reached through standard program
structures. Once working in these homes, auditors were also able to deliver a broader range
of improvements than would otherwise have been possible. This evaluation identified
multiple benefits of pilot funding, including:

 • “Unlocking” potential. Action staff described pilot funding as effectively “unlocking”
 untapped potential. Specifically, the pilot’s flexibility around health and safety (H&S)
 expenditures allowed Action to address barriers that would normally prevent the use
 of other energy efficiency funding streams. By resolving these barriers, the pilot
 enabled the deployment of WAP and utility funding to access previous unavailable
 savings in these homes.
 • Funding optimization. The availability of flexible funding allowed Action to optimize
 across a broader set of funding sources. As a result, staff could allocate expenditure
 strategically based on all relevant factors such as savings-to-investment ratio
 requirements, program timing, remaining funding availability, and differing
 household eligibility requirements. This flexibility essentially allowed Action to
 “stretch” available program dollars further while maintaining program compliance.
 • Customer experience. The pilot also improved the customer experience by
 providing continuity of service during periods when other funding sources were
 temporarily unavailable. For example, when utility-funded expenditures were paused
 between September and December 2025, pilot funds enabled Action to continue
 delivering services without interruption to clients.

In Seattle, OH offered a different perspective. According to the interviewed OH staff, by the
time pilot funding was available to them, other funding sources (expanded DOE, LIHEAP,

 vi

and state resources) available to them in Seattle already covered many of the previously
identified gaps that the pilot intended to fill. This represented a shift from ECC’s early
conversations with OH, when the weatherization partner considered using pilot funding for
select measures that OH did not provide. In the end, OH opted to not make any changes to
their existing protocol and measure pool. This decision was in part dictated by capacity
concerns and OH’s ability to spend down pilot funding expeditiously without overburdening
staff. As a result of these factors, OH largely deployed pilot funds as additive capital—
enabling the agency to increase production—rather than focus on CBO recruited
participants or offer additional measures it could not already offer. This approach in Seattle,
while practical, provided less insight into the pilot’s ability to further reduce barriers and/or
provide additional services to clients.

Despite their differing implementation experiences, both weatherization agencies
emphasized that the additional funding provided a significant net benefit to the
communities they serve. However, the variation in the agency’s experiences highlights how
regional conditions, particularly the availability and typical use cases of existing funding
sources in each area, can significantly affect the incremental value of pilot funding.

In some contexts, pilot funding primarily expanded program reach and enabled more
comprehensive services (Lynn), while in others it functioned mainly to increase overall
project volume (Seattle). These differences also reflect weatherization agency-level
decisions about how to deploy additional funding. Some agencies may treat similar future
funding sources as an opportunity to reduce barriers to participation by addressing health
and safety issues or other conditions that would typically prevent households from receiving
services. Others integrated the funds into existing program processes and continued to
operate largely within their standard protocols.

It is also important to recognize that government agencies and nonprofit service providers
are not always structurally incentivized or have capacity to expand services when additional
funding becomes available. This is because additional funding can also introduce new
program requirements, compliance expectations, and oversight processes. These additional
administrative responsibilities increased the resource and labor demands associated with
project delivery. This pilot highlighted the administrative balance between additional
resources and additional complexity, as well as how that balance point on that continuum
varies between geographies based on organizational capacity and already available funding
sources.

 WHAT IS A “DEEP” RETROFIT?

 According to the Federal Energy Management Program, a deep energy retrofit “leverages
 whole building approaches and integrative design to maximize energy efficiency and
 emissions reductions.” This may include upgrading roof insulation, installing efficient
 windows, and minimizing outdoor air infiltration by sealing the building envelope. In the case
 of the Healthy Homes pilot, ECC went beyond these energy-focused improvements and
 improved the health, safety, and livability of client’s homes.

1.1 Eligibility
To qualify for the pilot, applicants would need to meet the following criteria:

 - Place of Residence: Applicant must provide verification of their home address (e.g.,
 utility bill, lease agreements, or mortgage payment receipt) and reside in ZIP codes

 10

 within identified communities (Lynn in MA and the Beacon Hill neighborhood in
 Seattle WA)
 - Rentership or Ownership of Home: The applicant can provide documentation that
 reflects their rentership or ownership of a home (Landlord of a 1-4 unit building with
 low-income tenants and a homeowner or renter of a 1–4-unit building)
 - Government-Issued Identification: The applicant can provide at least one form of
 government-issued identification for each person living in the household
 - Income Eligibility: Low-Income (under 60% of State Median Income)

Eligible projects include:

 - Weatherization (i.e., insulation and air sealing)
 - Exterior repairs (roof, siding, trim, gutters etc.)
 - Heating system repairs/replacements (including air source heat pumps)
 - Mold, lead, and asbestos remediation and pest control
 - Lighting and appliance upgrades

Below is more information about each key delivery partner:

El Centro de la Raza (Washington, CBO). El Centro served as the community-based
organization partner to ECC. El Centro primarily leveraged its established community
programs and events to recruit pilot participants. Once engaged in the pilot, the
organization provided potential pilot participants with hands-on application assistance,
including help with documentation and language access, and served as a key intermediary
between participants and the City’s Office of Housing. Throughout the process, El Centro
provided ongoing, trust-based support by coordinating logistics, responding to
participants' questions, and ensuring that households understood and could successfully
navigate each stage of the weatherization process. El Centro experienced a change in pilot
staffing in August 2025, just as participation was set to ramp up. After a six-month vacancy,
El Centro hired a new point-of-contact for the pilot, but the disruption in staffing continuity
and onboarding time for the new contact was a challenge for the pilot.

Seattle Office of Housing (Washington, Weatherization Agency Partner). The Seattle
Office of Housing (OH) partnered with ECC as an implementation partner for the
Washington branch of the pilot. OH administrative staff strategically braided ECC funds into
OH’s existing weatherization and healthy homes programs to increase production capacity
and serve additional households already in the agency’s pipeline. Rather than operating as a
standalone initiative, ECC dollars served as additive funding layered on top of work OH was

 12

already delivering—at a time when other federal, state, and local funding streams were
expanding significantly.1 This approach maximized efficiency, avoided duplication, and
accelerated service delivery. OH played a critical role in stabilizing operations after the
staffing change at El Centro by adapting to evolving requirements and absorbing
operational and administrative responsibilities.

Latino Support Network (Massachusetts, CBO). ECC partnered with The Latino Support
Network (LSN), an organization with deep local trust and longstanding relationships within
Lynn’s Latino community, as the CBO for the pilot in Lynn, Massachusetts. LSN provides
immigration support, housing assistance, technical services, and educational programming
in both English and Spanish to the Lynn community. Neighbor to Neighbor (N2N) is another
local trusted CBO that ECC staff were aware of and introduced LSN to ECC. ECC initiated
the partnership with LSN in April 2023, as part of its effort to identify trusted local
organizations to support a Healthy Homes Weatherization grant. Although LSN had not
previously implemented weatherization pilots, the pilot aligned closely with LSN’s core
mission to advance economic development, affordable housing stability, and community
education. ECC supported LSN’s onboarding by delivering trainings, webinars, quarterly
meetings, and outreach materials that introduced healthy homes concepts, energy
efficiency measures, and pilot requirements. LSN actively built internal capacity by adapting
ECC materials for its community, translating resources into Spanish and five additional
languages, and creating additional visual and educational tools. Staff embedded outreach
and education into existing classes, workshops, and community events, raising awareness,
collecting information on housing needs, and building trust with residents in
familiar, accessible settings.

Action Inc. (Massachusetts, Weatherization Agency Partner). ECC worked with Action,
Inc. as its weatherization partner for the Healthy Homes pilot in Lynn, Massachusetts. ECC
began partnering discussions with Action in late 2022 and officially contracted with the
long-standing weatherization agency in December 2023. As the on-the-ground
implementing agency, Action integrated ECC’s E&I funds into its existing DOE WAP and
utility-funded weatherization programs to deliver comprehensive services to households.
Action managed referrals from LSN, conducted home energy audits, and leveraged multiple
funding streams to maximize impact. Action strategically used ECC funds to offer expanded
health and safety interventions—such as mold remediation, pest control, and critical home
repairs—that traditional weatherization programs could not typically cover.

2 About the Evaluation
Resource Innovations conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the Healthy Homes pilot.

2.1 Evaluation Objectives
Our team identified three objectives for our evaluation of the pilot:

 1. Conduct an evaluability assessment and develop a Healthy Homes pilot-specific
 logic model that supports subsequent evaluation activities and captures the
 intended short-, medium-, and long-run outcomes of the pilot
 2. Collect information from program partners and participants that provides answers to
 the pilot’s key program design questions (i.e., the effectiveness of CBO-driven
 recruitment and the ability of the pilot funding to drive more comprehensive
 services)
 3. Gather additional insights and lessons learned from the delivery of the pilot that can
 inform future programmatic efforts

2.2 Evaluation Activities
To inform this evaluation, Resource Innovations completed the following activities:

 1. Logic Model Development. Working closely with the ECC and pilot partners, our
 team defined key indicators across pilot inputs, outputs, and outcomes associated
 with the program and documented them using a logic model (provided in the
 following subsection)
 2. Pilot Partner Interviews (n=7). Interview with CBOs and weatherization agencies to
 identify the importance of their roles in the delivery of the pilot and to understand
 their perspectives on scalability of the pilot.
 3. Participant Interviews (n=15). Gather information regarding participant
 experiences, feedback, and potential improvements for the future. Because of the
 evaluation’s interest in CBO-based recruitment, our team limited our interviews to
 participants recruited by a CBO (versus participants that received pilot funding but
 were recruited through traditional channels and already in the weatherization agency
 delivery pipeline).

Together, these evaluation activities produced a structured, evidence-based understanding
of pilot performance, challenges, and outcomes. The findings provide practical
recommendations for pilot improvement and support decision-making related to replication
and potential scaling of the Healthy Homes model in the future.

3 Findings
The evaluation team organized the findings from this evaluation into two sections.

In first section (“Answers to Key Program Delivery Questions”), our team uses evaluation
findings to answer the two primary questions that ECC designed the pilot to explore:

 1. Can outreach led by CBOs reduce barriers to participation?

 2. Does the pilot’s funding and flexibility enable weatherization agencies to address
 deferrals and provide more comprehensive services?

In the second section “Topic-Specific Key Findings,” our team summarizes a broader set of
evaluation findings organized by topic (e.g., “Pilot Design” and “Participant Recruitment”). In
this section, our team summarizes CBOs, weatherization agencies, and clients’ experience
participating in the pilot, identifies successes and challenges faced, and, perhaps most
importantly, documents lessons learned for potential application as part of future
programmatic efforts.

sources also shifted during implementation (e.g., potential repeal of the IRA, utility program
changes, etc.). While the original plan—based on the information available at the time of
application—was to complete 120 units, that goal ultimately proved ambitious given the
funding uncertainties, inflation, and other changes that occurred during the project period.

The evidence suggests that while CBOs were able to identify, recruit, and support the
enrollment of clients that were less likely to otherwise participate, the approach still faces
challenges and, for this specific pilot effort, did not yield a large volume of participants to
meet the pilot’s goals. As a result, CBO-led outreach may be most effective when
implemented as part of a broader, multi-pronged outreach strategy rather than a
standalone recruitment mechanism. Through the pilot, ECC and the pilot partners were able
to increase the depth of interventions by providing more comprehensive and holistic
services. However, the compressed delivery timeline for the pilot makes it difficult to render
a definitive assessment.

 2. Does the pilot’s funding and flexibility enable weatherization agencies to
 address deferrals and provide more comprehensive services?

The answer to this pilot delivery and evaluation question differed meaningfully between the
two states the pilot operated in.

In Lynn, the pilot significantly altered how Action’s auditors delivered services to low-income
households in Lynn. The ability to combine the pilot’s flexible E&I funding with existing
funding sources—specifically DOE WAP funds and National Grid resources—enabled Action
to serve households that previously could not be reached through standard program
structures. Once working in these homes, auditors were also able to deliver a broader range
of improvements than would otherwise have been possible. This evaluation identified
multiple benefits of pilot funding, including:

 • “Unlocking” potential. Action staff described pilot funding as effectively “unlocking”
 untapped potential. Specifically, the pilot’s flexibility around health and safety (H&S)

 21

 Findings

 expenditures allowed Action to address barriers that would normally prevent the use
 of other energy efficiency funding streams. By resolving these barriers, the pilot
 enabled the deployment of WAP and utility funding to access previous unavailable
 savings in these homes.
 • Funding optimization. The availability of flexible funding allowed Action to optimize
 across a broader set of funding sources. As a result, staff could allocate expenditure
 strategically based on all relevant factors such as savings-to-investment ratio
 requirements, program timing, remaining funding availability, and differing
 household eligibility requirements. This flexibility essentially allowed Action to
 “stretch” available program dollars further while maintaining program compliance.
 • Customer experience. The pilot also improved the customer experience by
 providing continuity of service during periods when other funding sources were
 temporarily unavailable. For example, when utility-funded expenditures were paused
 between September and December 2025, pilot funds enabled Action to continue
 delivering services without interruption to clients.

In Seattle, OH offered a unique perspective. According to the interviewed OH staff, by the
time pilot funding was available to them, other funding sources (expanded DOE, LIHEAP,
and state resources) available to them in Seattle already covered many of the previously
identified gaps that the pilot intended to fill. This represented a shift from ECC’s early
conversations with OH, when the weatherization partner considered using pilot funding for
select measures that OH did not provide. In the end, OH opted to not make any changes to
their existing protocol and measure pool. This decision was in part dictated by capacity
concerns and OH’s ability to spend down pilot funding expeditiously without overburdening
staff. As a result of these factors, OH largely deployed pilot funds as additive capital—
enabling the agency to increase production—rather than focus on CBO recruited
participants or offer additional measures it could not already offer. This approach in Seattle,
while practical, provided less insight into the pilot’s ability to further reduce barriers and/or
provide additional services to clients.

Despite their differing implementation experiences, both weatherization agencies
emphasized that the additional funding provided a significant net benefit to the
communities they serve. However, the variation in the agency’s experiences highlights how
regional conditions, particularly the availability and typical use cases of existing funding
sources in each area, can significantly affect the incremental value of pilot funding.

In some contexts, pilot funding primarily expanded program reach and enabled more
comprehensive services (Lynn), while in others it functioned mainly to increase overall
project volume (Seattle). These differences also reflect weatherization agency-level
decisions about how to deploy additional funding. Some agencies may treat similar future
funding sources as an opportunity to reduce barriers to participation by addressing health

 22

 Findings

and safety issues or other conditions that would typically prevent households from receiving
services. Others integrated the funds into existing program processes and continued to
operate largely within their standard protocols.

It is also important to recognize that government agencies and nonprofit service providers
are not always structurally incentivized or have capacity to expand services when additional
funding becomes available. This is because additional funding can also introduce new
program requirements, compliance expectations, and oversight processes. These additional
administrative responsibilities increased the resource and labor demands associated with
project delivery. This pilot highlighted the administrative balance between additional
resources and additional complexity, as well as how that balance point on that continuum
varies between geographies based on organizational capacity and already available funding

Summary: ECC designed a pilot that successfully addressed H&S barriers, which enabled

extended the reach of the traditional WAP services while simultaneously offering more
comprehensive energy and H&S improvements.

Lesson Learned: Providing flexible funding to address pre-weatherization H&S barriers can
unlock services for previously deferred households, enabling more equitable program

3.2.2 Establishing Community Partnerships
Summary: The pilot’s success relied on multi-organization partnerships that combined
technical expertise with trusted community relationships.

Lesson Learned: ECC’s experience with these organizations highlights an important lesson
for future CBO-centered program designs: successful implementation requires both strong
CBO interest and adequate weatherization agency capacity within the service territory.

3.2.2.1 Identifying the Right Partners
ECC utilized an open procurement process to identify CBO partners.

In Washington State, four CBOs applied, all located in King County, specifically South
Seattle and the Chinatown/International District. ECC selected El Centro following a review
process with a local advisory group of Seattle-based CBO representatives, recognizing that
El Centro was already a trusted, well-established CBO serving the exact populations the
pilot aimed to reach. ECC also felt the pilot aligned with El Centro’s existing work in
government benefits navigation.

Although they did not formally apply, ECC reported that two additional CBOs—one in Pierce
County and one in Pacific County—initially expressed interest in participating in the pilot.
Both organizations met with ECC to discuss the challenges they encountered during the
proposal development process. Both organizations decided not to apply because their local
weatherization agencies lacked sufficient staffing capacity to support pilot implementation.

More broadly, the experience underscores the importance of identifying motivated partners
with sufficient capacity when launching innovative pilot programs. New program models
often introduce additional administrative requirements and operational complexities,
particularly when attempting to serve previously deferred households or expand service
delivery to new participants. Without adequate staffing and organizational capacity among
implementing partners, these additional demands may limit program participation or deter
otherwise interested organizations from applying. Future CBO-centered initiatives may
benefit from early assessments of implementation partner capacity and stronger
coordination between CBOs and weatherization agencies during program design and
application development.

While ECC primarily implemented the pilot through its selected CBO partners, the initiative
also fostered broader multi-organization collaboration to promote the program and raise
community awareness. For example, in Lynn, ECC co-convened the Lynn Community
Resource Group (CRG), a coalition that includes Lynn Community Health Center, Neighbor

 27

 Findings

to Neighbor, Lynn United for Change, LHAND, and Northeast Legal Aid. Additional
partnerships supported outreach and community engagement activities, including
collaboration with Lynn Community Television (LCTV) for event hosting and media outreach,
as well as joint participation in an Earth Day 2025 event with The Food Project, Resonant
Energy, and the Green Energy Consumers Alliance.

These partnerships and coordination efforts enhanced cross-organizational communication

the participating CBOs and the services they provide. Multi-organization collaboration may
enhance program visibility and community trust, particularly when initiatives are introduced
through established local networks and trusted community institutions.

3.2.3 Recruitment
Summary: CBOs successfully promoted the pilot by integrating outreach into existing
community services, leveraging peer referrals, and providing in-language materials and
support to build trust, reduce skepticism, and improve accessibility for diverse households.

Lesson Learned: CBOs serve clients that use wide range of languages, meaning the
program materials may need to be translated into five or more languages to fully serve a
given community.

3.2.3.1 Outreach Approaches
To promote the pilot in the Beacon Hill community in Seattle, El Centro integrated the pilot's
promotion into its existing service pathways. For example, El Centro had established
programs focused on utility discount enrollment, emergency assistance, and community
resource navigation, which they extended to include the pilot. This alignment leveraged El
Centro’s role as a trusted advisor (i.e., we are already helping you with this, let us also help
you with that) when promoting the pilot to potential participants. El Centro also hosted
several open informational sessions to demystify and simplify the concepts and services
involved in weatherization work to reach potential participants.

LSN also shared information about the pilot as part of its ongoing community activities and
events, with a focus on clearly detailing the eligibility and application requirements.
Participants said they felt comfortable asking LSN staff questions and raising concerns
because it was a trusted organization.

Several participants (in both states) reported that friends who completed the pilot
encouraged them to apply. The evaluation team’s interviews revealed that successful

materials into five additional languages—Portuguese, Haitian Creole, Khmer, Arabic, and

proactively addressed concerns. The overall impact was that language support reduced
fear, improved understanding, and increased accessibility for participating households.
Overall, community-centered pilot models provide the added benefit of leveraging existing
trust and relationships within local communities, which can support more effective outreach
and engagement.

Lesson Learned: Community-based, multilingual case management and consistent

3.2.4.1 Supporting Enrollment
After recruiting a potential participant, CBOs provided ongoing reassurance and step-by-
step guidance throughout the enrollment process. The enrollment process included several
steps, chief among them completing the income verification to confirm eligibility for the
pilot.

Interviewed CBO staff noted, and several interviewed participants confirmed, initially feeling
nervous about sharing information about their income or, later in the participation process,
allowing home visits. CBO staff explained each step of the process and remained available
to participants to answer follow-up questions. According to participants, regular
communication with their CBO contact built trust and reduced anxiety throughout the
process.

To help participants navigate the enrollment barriers and minimize participants falling out of
the pilot’s enrollment process once recruited, both CBOs leaned into a supportive case
management approach. Specifically, LSN staff assisted with filling out interest forms,
followed up with applicants, and supported households in completing LIHEAP applications
and enrolling in National Grid’s Discount Rate program. LSN also advocated on behalf of
participants experiencing delays in service delivery and connected residents to additional
resources beyond weatherization, including tenant rights education, legal aid, and lead
abatement programs. According to LSN, this firsthand approach, while time-consuming and
insufficient for all recruited clients, reduced the number of participants that otherwise would
have dropped out of the enrollment process. The ability to support participants in this
personal and effective way, including by providing in-language support, is a clear benefit of
the pilot’s CBO-centric outreach approach.

Despite these efforts, the CBOs faced several challenges that limited the number of clients
who successfully transitioned from recruited to fully enrolled. These included:

 • Staffing disruptions, including a multi-month coordinator vacancy at El Centro that
 adversely impacted outreach continuity in Seattle and reduced CBO-generated
 enrollment momentum. As a result of the staffing change, ECC lowered El Centro’s
 participant target from 60 to 30 enrollments. However, in the end, only seven
 participants recruited by El Centro enrolled. (As discussed further below, the balance
 of pilots in Seattle came from OH’s list of previously recruited in-progress clients).
 Additionally, as stated previously, this number does not include households
 described earlier who dropped out after facing language barriers and/or were told
 by OH contractors that they would need to cover out-of-pocket costs themselves
 before measures could be installed.

 • Engaging landlords emerged as a critical barrier to serving tenant-occupied

3.2.5 Program Delivery
Summary: The pilot demonstrated that flexible funding and expanded repair scopes can
enable more comprehensive whole-home improvements.

Lesson Learned: Flexible funding and whole-home repair authority can significantly expand
the impact of weatherization programs, but successful implementation requires clearer
operational guidance, early contractor coordination, and sufficient timelines that account for
local program conditions and logistical complexities.

3.2.5.1 Training Materials
Although ECC’s training materials were viewed as informative, auditors described them as
conceptually strong but operationally limited. The pilot authorized measures outside
standard WAP parameters, yet provided limited field-based guidance on evaluating
structural conditions, distinguishing between cosmetic and critical grading issues,
interpreting foundation cracks, determining when roof replacement was appropriate, or
establishing reasonable cost ranges. ECC still developed an extensive measure list which
was helpful for addressing gaps, but it was not feasible to create a single document or
training material that can effectively evaluate all components of a home. Additionally, there
is no single auditor who can effectively evaluate an entire home, since each scenario can be
different and requires unique experience. With this, both auditors and administrative staff
expressed uncertainty about pilot priorities—whether to maximize the number of homes
served or the depth of investment per home—and had to independently interpret how ECC
rules interacted with existing braiding requirements. Auditors recommended staged, hands-
on training with follow-up sessions after field experience to strengthen implementation.
Administrators noted that if the pilot were to be scaled or replicated in the future, it would
be beneficial for ECC to clearly define the pilot’s goals and priorities at the outset.

3.2.5.2 Benefits and Challenges of Additional Funding
Unlike traditional weatherization programs constrained by strict cost ratios, limited barrier
allowances, and efficiency-only requirements, the pilot allowed the weatherization agency
partners to address pre-weatherization H&S barriers that are typically excluded or
insufficiently funded. Auditors emphasized that homes function as integrated systems;
without resolving foundational H&S issues, energy efficiency measures cannot be installed
safely or effectively.

For Action, the pilot fundamentally shifted how Action’s auditors served low-income
households in Lynn. The ability to combine the flexible E&I funding available through the
pilot with the existing DOE WAP and National Grid allowed Action to serve participants they

 33

 Findings

previously could not and once working in those homes, to provide a much wider range of
services. Even modest pilot funding for repairs and health and safety improvements—
estimated at $10,000 per home—enabled auditors to unlock and apply other energy-
efficiency-focused funding streams that otherwise would not have been available. The
results were more complete, whole-home interventions that addressed both energy and
health concerns. When asked directly whether the flexibility or volume of the pilot funds was
more valuable to them, Action’s answer was definitive: flexibility. In the future, pilots and
programs should, to the extent possible, prioritize flexibility over sheer dollar volume when
designing funding structures.

OH reported a unique experience with the pilot. While ECC designed the pilot funding to
address gaps in traditional services and funding sources—an outcome observed in Lynn
through Action—the context in Seattle differed. According to interviewed OH staff, by the
time pilot funding became available, several other funding streams, including expanded
DOE Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) funding, LIHEAP resources, and state
funding, were already covering many of the previously identified service gaps in Seattle. As
previously noted, ECC reported that when El Centro was selected as the CBO partner, OH
indicated they had already conducted substantial weatherization work in the Beacon Hill
neighborhood and were concerned that few remaining households would require services.
This prior program activity may have reduced the potential incremental impact of the pilot in
that area. Additionally, participating weatherization agencies were given flexibility to design
their own pilot budgets. Agencies had the option to allocate funds toward increasing
staffing capacity or expanding service delivery if they determined that additional resources
were needed to increase production. In practice, OH appears to have integrated pilot
funding within its existing operational structure rather than using the funds to significantly
expand service volume or address deferrals. As a result, the pilot may have provided less
incremental benefit in Seattle compared with locations where funding gaps or service
barriers were more pronounced.

Despite their different experiences, both weatherization agencies were clear that the
additional funding was a significant net benefit for the communities they serve. However,
the disparity in the agency’s experience highlights how regional differences, particularly the
availability and use cases of existing funding sources in each area, can significantly affect the
incremental value of pilot funding to improve programmatic reach and comprehensiveness
(rather than simply enabling greater project volume). The differing experiences between
agencies suggest that the incremental value of flexible pilot funding may depend heavily on
local program conditions, including existing funding availability, prior service coverage, and
agency-level implementation decisions.

3.2.5.3 Scheduling Considerations
In Lynn, delays in procuring subcontractors capable of performing new repair scopes
slowed early pilot implementation. Action chose to postpone completing home energy
audits until they could identify and onboard contractors capable of delivering pilot-funded
measures and improvements that fall outside the scope of traditional WAP and utility
program services. This decision was driven by a desire to minimize delays between audit
completion and service delivery. Additionally, Action hoped to onboard contractors who
could cover all the newly added measures, but at times were unsure of which specific
contractors were needed for the work. Program staff were concerned that completing audits
before securing the appropriate contractors could lead to extended waiting times for
participants, which might create frustration and reduce program satisfaction. Staff also
expressed concern about identifying measures needed during audits without having
contractors available to perform the required work. No new contractors were formally
onboarded. ECC instead encouraged Action to collaborate with contractors already within
its existing trade ally network to determine whether they could perform the expanded range
of services required under the pilot. Leveraging existing contractor relationships was viewed
as a more efficient alternative to the more time-intensive process of identifying and
onboarding entirely new contractors. Despite this adjustment, the pilot still experienced a
launch delay during the initial stages.

According to interviewed CBO and agency staff, taking initiative to communicate with
participants was essential for maintaining trust, preventing dropouts, and minimizing
frustration when delays occur. Staff emphasized the importance of setting clear expectations
about program timelines at the outset and providing regular updates throughout the
process—even when there is no considerable progress to report. Consistent communication
was viewed as a key factor in sustaining participant engagement and retention. Staff also
noted that coordinating construction activities presents ongoing operational challenges.
Successful scheduling requires accurate scoping of work, availability of qualified crews, and
close coordination with participants to ensure contractors can access homes when needed.
Specifically, OH field staff reported that they would even encourage participants to be
present during all construction activities to minimize risk of miscommunication between the
participant and the contractors, reduce the incidence of call backs, and ensure
improvements are installed correctly. Given these logistical complexities, weatherization
agencies implementing expanded repair scopes or specialized measures may benefit from
identifying, securing, and onboarding the necessary contractors as early as possible during
the program design or startup phase to minimize implementation delays.

3.2.5.4 Time Constraints & Related Implications
The pilot’s overall delivery timeline was adversely compressed by several factors, including:

 • Late launch (late 2024). As documented in the pilot timeline (Figure 3), ECC’s
 period of performance did not begin until March 2023. The first pilot energy audit
 and measure installation happened in September 2024, which was about 18 months
 after DOE approved ECC’s application and implementation was able to begin. This
 setback was only minor, and only made ECC slightly behind their planned timeline of
 year 1 being program design, year 2 outreach and recruitment, and year 3 program
 implementation. A meaningful ramp-up period for a pilot is not uncommon; there
 was significant pilot material to develop and deploy before the pilot could begin
 serving participants. Regardless of the reasons, the impact of starting projects in late
 2024 meant that the pilot needed to complete all 120 targeted projects in just over a
 year (i.e., before the end of February 2026). While feasible, the remaining pilot
 runway's mean ECC did not have much time to absorb other potential delays.

 • Funding uncertainty (early 2025). As a result of the administration change
 following the 2024 election, there was significant uncertainty regarding the
 continuation of the pilot and the availability of E&I funding. For nearly five months,
 ECC had limited contact with their DOE E&I contact and were hesitant to proceed
 without certainty that pilot spending would be repaid by DOE. The timing of this
 delay, just as the pilot was entering the implementation phase, was particularly
 problematic.

 • Government shutdown (late 2025). While the shutdown was not as impactful as the
 uncertainty generated by the administration change, it further complicated ECC’s
 delivery of the pilot, right as the pilot was gaining momentum heading into the final
 months of the pilot period. For example, the shut down halted the processing of
 LIHEAP applications just as they reopened for the 2026 heating season.

 • Extreme weather conditions (early 2026), specifically in Lynn, caused by multiple
 large snow storms made completing in-progress jobs difficult.

There were three primary implications of the compressed timeline:

 • Goal obtainment. ECC initially set a participation target of 120 households—80 in
 single-family homes and 40 in multifamily units. Pilot funding supported energy
 efficiency and health and safety improvements for 97 households across both types
 of housing. Project costs were higher than initially anticipated, and ECC had expected
 that additional funding sources could be leveraged to support each project. Despite

 36

 Findings

 these constraints, ECC and the participating weatherization agencies were able to
 complete more comprehensive improvements in participating homes than would
 have been possible under standard program funding alone. The lesson learned is
 that although the pilot served fewer households than originally targeted, the
 additional funding enabled deeper, more comprehensive upgrades that addressed
 both energy efficiency and health and safety needs.

• Including Multifamily. As noted in the previous bullet, ECC intended to serve 40
 multifamily participants. However, all 97 eventual participants in the pilot lived in
 single-family residences, and several in Lynn were multi-unit homes with 2+ units. For
 timing reasons listed above, both OH and Action advised ECC that the logistical
 complexity and time required to coordinate with landlords and property
 management companies made serving multifamily buildings infeasible, given the
 pilot’s compressed timeline. Additionally, according to tax assessor data, there were
 only handful of larger MF buildings in the Beacon Hill neighborhood in Seattle that
 could potentially be eligible. Furthermore, outreach and recruitment activities by the
 CBOs did not yield a substantial number of potential clients residing in larger
 multifamily buildings. Through the program design phase, the weatherization
 agencies prioritized working with small-scale MF buildings through the pilot that had
 residential components, instead of commercial equipment, and referrals (where
 feasible) were made to other existing MF programs. Weatherization agencies
 reported differing experiences with landlord engagement. OH staff indicated that
 their organization typically avoids direct landlord engagement, whereas Action
 reported more frequent interaction with landlords during delivery. However, serving
 larger multifamily buildings (five or more units) requires more coordination, including
 additional project management and compliance with distinct program rules and
 requirements. More broadly, both agencies noted that navigating relationships with
 property managers and landlords remains complex. Although OH did not complete
