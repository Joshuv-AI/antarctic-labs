Comparative mitogenomics of Ocnus glacialis reveals lineage-specific evolutionary rates and complex gene rearrangements in Dendrochirotida
Authors: Seong Duk Do, Yeonhui Lee, Jae-Sung Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The order Dendrochirotida (Class Holothuroidea) is a species-rich echinoderm group, yet its internal evolutionary history remains poorly resolved due to limited mitogenomic resources. In this study, we characterized the first complete mitochondrial genome of Ocnus glacialis and conducted comparative analyses to elucidate its phylogenetic position and molecular evolutionary patterns. The circular mitogenome of O. glacialis is 16,776 bp in length, containing the canonical set of 37 genes. Among the analyzed dendrochirotids, O. glacialis exhibited the highest A + T content (70.88%) and a near-zero AT-skew, a compositional profile often linked to lineage-specific evolution in specialized environments. Selection pressure analyses, including branch-model tests, revealed that these compositional features are associated with relaxed purifying selection and an accelerated rate of sequence evolution. Branch-site analyses further identified specific codon sites in cytb, nad2, nad4l, nad5, and nad6 under positive or relaxed constraints. Structurally, O. glacialis displayed the most complex gene rearrangement pattern among the studied species, characterized by multiple tandem duplication-random loss (TDRL) events and extensive intergenic sequences. Furthermore, divergence time estimation suggests that these structural and compositional shifts occurred in tandem with the lineage’s diversification. We propose that these mitogenomic signatures reflect a synergistic outcome of habitat transition toward Arctic cold-water and deep-sea environments, coupled with demographic factors such as reduced effective population sizes inherent to its benthic life history. By resolving taxonomic uncertainties, this study provides a robust temporal and molecular framework for understanding the evolutionary history and ecological diversification of the Ocnus lineage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59110-9.

Introduction

The phylum Echinodermata Klein, 1778, exclusively comprises marine invertebrates found in oceans worldwide. Within the phylum, there are five distinct classes: Asteroidea de Blainville, 1830; Crinoidea Miller, 1821; Echinoidea Schumacher, 1817; Holothuroidea de Blainville, 1834; and Ophiuroidea Gray, 1840. Sea cucumbers, scientifically known as holothuroids, belong to the class Holothuroidea, which comprises approximately 2000 species1. Holothuroids hold ecological significance due to their high species diversity and distribution across a wide range of vertical and horizontal oceanic regions2,3. They also have substantial economic importance as seafoods and traditional medicines in Asia and several countries4,5. Given their prevalence, diverse morphologies, ecological roles, and unique features, such as soft bodies, reduced skeletons, and leathery to gelatinous body walls, extensive molecular taxonomic analyses have been conducted to unravel the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary history of holothurians6–8. Since the 1800s, the complex and unique morphology of holothuroids has led to multiple, often conflicting, hypotheses regarding their morphological phylogeny. Then, Miller et al. reported a molecular phylogeny of 84 species in the Holothuroidae using three mitochondrial and three nuclear genes, and separated them into seven taxa: Apodidia, Elasipodida, Holothuriida, Persiculida, Molpadida, Synallactida, and Dendrochirotida9.

The order Dendrochirotida is characterized by branched (dendritic) oral tentacles and a predominantly suspension-feeding lifestyle10,11. Among the 11 families within this order, Cucumariidae exhibits the highest species diversity12. Based on occurrence records from the Ocean Biodiversity Information System (OBIS), we identified six species within the genus Ocnus: Ocnus brunneus, Ocnus diomedeae, Ocnus lacteus, Ocnus planci, Ocnus pygmaeus, and Ocnus glacialis. Among these, the cucumariid O. glacialis (Ljungman, 1879) is principally found in the deep-sea regions of the Arctic and Northeast Atlantic, with documented occurrences in the Beaufort13 and the Chukchi Sea13,14. In line with these findings, OBIS records indicate that O. glacialis can inhabit cold, deep-sea environments (approximately − 5 to 5 °C) at different depths exceeding 2000 m. In contrast, other Ocnus species are primarily distributed in relatively shallow waters (< 300 m) and temperate environments (approximately 5 to 30 °C) (Figure S1).

Due to its intrinsic features, such as maternal inheritance and a high substitution rate, the mitochondrial genome serves as an effective molecular marker for phylogenetic classification. Recent advances in sequencing technologies have facilitated a rapid increase in mitogenomic data for the class Holothuroidea, leading to more intensive phylogenetic and evolutionary studies15–17. In particular, mitogenome-based analyses have been successfully applied to the orders Apodida and Holothuriida, providing insights into gene arrangement patterns, nucleotide biases, and lineage diversification15,17.

In contrast, mitogenomic resources for the order Dendrochirotida, including the family Cucumariidae, remain relatively scarce, which limits robust phylogenetic inference for this group. Although a few dendrochirotid mitogenomes have been reported recently, comprehensive comparative analyses across diverse taxa are still lacking. Therefore, expanding mitogenomic resources for Dendrochirotida is essential for improving our understanding of their evolutionary history and clarifying relationships within Cucumariidae. Consequently, understanding the phylogenetic relationships and genetic divergence of O. glacialis and other cucumariid species has been constrained by a lack of detailed morphometric data and genomic resources.

To date, no complete mitogenome sequence has been available for O. glacialis, and only partial sequences of the cox1 gene have been reported in the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD; e.g., ASGRD083-18.COI-5P). Currently, the NCBI database contains 55 complete holothuroid mitogenomes (excluding redundant or unannotated entries), of which only nine belong to Dendrochirotida. In this study, we report the complete mitochondrial genome of O. glacialis, increasing the number of available dendrochirotid mitogenomes to ten. By comparing this new dataset with those of nine other dendrochirotid species, we aim to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary patterns within the order Dendrochirotida.

Results

Mitochondrial genome structure of O. glacialis

The mitochondrial genome of O. glacialis consisted of 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), two ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), and a putative control region (CR), with a total length of 16,776 bp (Fig. 1; Table 1). Of the 13 PCGs, atp6, atp8, cox1, cox2, cox3, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4l, nad5, and cytb were encoded on the heavy strand, while only nad6 was located on the light strand. Among the 22 tRNAs, trnA, trnD, trnQ, trnS2, and trnV were encoded on the light strand, whereas the remaining 17 tRNAs were located on the heavy strand. Both rRNA genes were located on the heavy strand. Overall, the mitochondrial genome lengths of the 11 dendrochirotid species analyzed ranged from 15,756 to 17,538 bp, placing the mitogenome of O. glacialis within the intermediate size range (Table S1).

Overlaps, non-coding intergenic regions, and the control region

The lengths of the intergenic nucleotides ranged from 2 to 255 bp, excluding the CR, whereas overlapping areas ranged from 1 to 27 bp (Table 1). Intergenic regions longer than 100 bp were observed between cox1 and nad4l (101 bp), nad6 and cytb (225 bp), trnM and trnA (167 bp), and trnI and nad2 (142 bp). The total length of the intergenic sequences in O. glacialis was 1203 bp, with an A + T content of 81.8%. These sequences accounted for approximately 7.2% of the entire mitochondrial genome of O. glacialis.

The CR was 782 bp in length and contained a single non-coding region. In addition, four types of tandem repeats were observed within this region (Figure S2). In contrast, no evidence of pseudogene remnants, repeat structures, or conserved motifs was detected in other intergenic regions.

Compared with the other nine dendrochirotid species, the presence of intergenic regions longer than 100 bp in O. glacialis was highly unusual. For example, in the intergenic region between the nad6 and cytb genes, all dendrochirotid species except O. glacialis exhibited short conserved sequences: C. anceps had 12 bp (TTATATAAATAA), Phyllophorus proteus (formally known as Neocucumis proteus) had 11 bp (CATTTATATAA), and the remaining seven species shared an identical 8 bp sequence (TATATAAA). In contrast, O. glacialis lacked this conserved sequence and instead possessed a much longer intergenic sequence of 225 bp (Table 1). In addition, trnR was consistently located between the cox1 and nad4l genes in all dendrochirotid species except O. glacialis. At this position, O. glacialis contained a 101 bp intergenic sequence that was unassigned to any known gene. Moreover, whereas other dendrochirotid species exhibited very short intergenic regions (0 to 6 bp), O. glacialis possessed an unusually long intergenic sequence of 142 bp.

Base composition

The mitochondrial genome of O. glacialis showed a base composition of 35.80% adenine (A), 35.08% thymine (T), 17.16% cytosine (C), and 11.96% guanine (G), resulting in an A + T content of 70.88% and a G + C content of 29.12% (Table S2). For the 13 PCGs, the base composition was 32.70% A, 36.88% T, 17.81% C, and 12.61% G, corresponding to an A + T content of 69.57% and a G + C content of 30.43%. Within PCGs, the A + T content was 64.40% at the first codon position and increased markedly to 81.86% at the wobble position, with corresponding G + C contents of 35.60% and 18.14%, respectively. The tRNAs and rRNAs exhibited A + T contents of 71.49% and 69.49%, respectively, whereas the CR showed the highest A + T bias at 74.94%.

Base composition and skew of the complete mitogenomes were compared among 10 Dendrochirotida species (Fig. 2; Table S3). Notably, O. glacialis showed the highest A + T content (70.88%), followed by C. anceps (69.61%) and C. quadrangularis (66.93%). In contrast, the remaining seven species possessed relatively lower and narrower A + T contents, ranging from 63.61% to 65.20%. All species showed positive AT skews ranging from 0.010 to 0.216 and negative GC skews ranging from − 0.178 to -0.336, indicating a consistent nucleotide compositional bias across Dendrochirotida mitogenomes. The highest AT skew was observed in P. violaceus (0.216), while the lowest value was scored in O. glacialis (0.010).

tRNAs and rRNAs

A total of 22 tRNAs were identified in the O. glacialis mitogenome, ranging from 64 to 72 bp in length, with a total tRNA length of 1508 bp (Table 1). Predicted secondary structures of these tRNAs showed the typical cloverleaf configuration, except for trnS1, which lacked the dihydrouridine arm (D arm) (Figure S3).

The mitochondrial genome contained a large rRNA gene (rrnL, 1412 bp) and a small rRNA gene (rrnS, 781 bp). The trnN, trnE, and trnF genes were located between rrnL and rrnS (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Protein-coding genes

All 13 PCGs of O. glacialis mitogenome initiated with the start codon, ATG, a pattern that was also consistently observed in the other analyzed Dendrochirotida species (Table S4). Regarding termination codons in O. glacialis mitogenome, only atp6 and nad6 ended with TAG, while the remaining PCGs terminated with TAA (Table S5). Among the other Dendrochirotida species, start codons were highly conserved, with most PCGs initiating with ATG. However, GTG was used as the start codon for nad4l in C. anceps, nad1 in P. liuwutiensis, and nad2 in C. frondosa, C. miniata, T. gemmata, and P. violaceus. In contrast, termination codons were more variable and included TAA, TAG, and incomplete stop codons (T–) (Table S5).

The 13 PCGs of O. glacialis mitogenome comprised a total of 3,716 codons, representing the lowest codon count among the analyzed Dendrochirotida species (Figure S4A; Table S6). The most frequently used amino acids in O. glacialis were leucine (Leu, 583), isoleucine (Ile, 520), and phenylalanine (Phe, 299), while the least frequently used were cysteine (Cys, 33), lysine (Lys, 58), arginine (Arg, 64), and aspartic acid (Asp, 64), excluding termination codons. Consistent with O. glacialis, most Dendrochirotida species, except C. anceps and C. quadrangularis, showed the highest usage of leucine (Leu, 589–602), isoleucine (Ile, 465–520), and phenylalanine (Phe, 301–332). In contrast, C. anceps and C. quadrangularis most frequently use isoleucine (Ile, 566–568), leucine (Leu, 527–539), and asparagine (Asn, 324–327) (Table S6). Across all examined Dendrochirotida species, cysteine (Cys, 35–40), lysine (Lys, 58), and arginine (Arg, 60–67) were consistently the least frequently used amino acids, mirroring the pattern observed in O. glacialis.

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis of the 13 PCGs of O. glacialis revealed the highest RSCU values for UUA (Leu2, 2.56), CGA (Arg, 2.25), and UCU (Ser2, 2.04), whereas GCG (Ala), ACG (Thr), and CAG (Gln) were not used (RSCU = 0) (Figure S4B; Table S6). Compared with other Dendrochirotida species, O. glacialis exhibited distinctive codon usage patterns (Fig. 3; Table S6). Notably, it was the only species showing a significant bias toward U rather than C at the wobble position with A = 42.7%, U = 39.1%, C = 13.2%, and G = 5.2%, whereas the mean values for the remaining species were A = 51.0%, U = 19.3%, C = 24.0%, and G = 5.6%. For example, histidine codons in O. glacialis mitogenome had RSCU values of 1.45 for CAU and 0.55 for CAC, whereas the remaining species showed CAU values ranging from 0.24 to 0.71 and CAC values from 1.29 to 1.76. Overall, when amino acids were grouped according to their physicochemical properties, O. glacialis showed proportions of 61.87% hydrophobic, 29.06% polar uncharged, 5.27% positively charged, and 3.81% negatively charged residues, whereas the corresponding mean values for the remaining species were 61.17%, 29.46%, 5.30%, and 4.06%, respectively.

Evolutionary analysis of PCGs

To elucidate the overall and gene-specific selection patterns among the 10 dendrochirotid species, the ratios of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (Ka/Ks), denoted as ω, were calculated for the 13 PCGs. All PCGs exhibited ω values below 1.0, indicating that these mitochondrial genes are generally subject to purifying selection across dendrochirotid species (Fig. 4A). Consistent with this result, the one-ratio model (M0), which assumes a single ω ratio across all branches, estimated an overall ω value of 0.085, further supporting the predominance of purifying selection across the dendrochirotid lineages (Fig. 4B).

Among the 13 PCG, atp8 showed the highest ω (0.244 ± 0.115), followed by nad2 (0.091 ± 0.026), whereas the lowest ω values were observed in cox1 (0.032 ± 0.009) and cox3 (0.034 ± 0.015) (Fig. 4A). When comparing ω values between groups, including O. glacialis and those excluding O. glacialis, the mean ω values of all PCGs were consistently higher in the group including O. glacialis, with differences ranging from 0.008 to 0.067. Mann–Whitney U tests revealed statistically significant differences for atp8 and cox3 between the two groups (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A).

The two-ratio model (branch model) indicated that the foreground branch (C. anceps, C. quadrangularis, and O. glacialis) had a higher ω value than the background branch (ωF = 0.1008, ωB = 0.0801). The likelihood ratio test (LRT) showed that the two-ratio model was significantly supported over the one-ratio model (2ΔInL = 22.147, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C).

A branch-site model was applied by designating only O. glacialis as the foreground branch. The LRT indicated that the branch-site alternative model was significantly supported over the null model (2ΔInL = 11.157, P < 0.001), and the alternative model estimated ω = 9.576 for site classes 2a and 2b, which comprised 4.87% of codon sites. Positively selected sites identified by the Bayes empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis with posterior probabilities greater than 0.95 (P < 0.05) were detected in cytb, nad2, nad4l, nad5, and nad6 (Fig. 4D).

Comparative analysis of Kimura two-parameter (K2P) distance among Dendrochirotida species showed that O. glacialis showed the highest overall genetic distance (0.449 ± 0.083), followed by C. quadrangularis (0.377 ± 0.107), C. anceps (0.374 ± 0.114), P. liuwutiensis (0.355 ± 0.112), P. proteus (0.347 ± 0.108), P. fragilis (0.342 ± 0.095), T. gemmata (0.341 ± 0.097), C. frondosa (0.322 ± 0.134), P. violaceus (0.322 ± 0.095), and C. miniata (0.321 ± 0.133), in descending order (Figure S5. Notably, O. glacialis exhibited the highest K2P distances across all genes except nad4. In addition, gene-specific comparisons revealed that atp8 showed the highest K2P distance (0.578 ± 0.098), followed by nad5 (0.435 ± 0.069) and nad4 (0.424 ± 0.076). In contrast, cox1 exhibited the lowest K2P distance (0.223 ± 0.052), with cox3 (0.276 ± 0.069) and cox2 (0.306 ± 0.044) displaying similarly low values.

Phylogenetic analysis of Holothuroidea

Phylogenetic trees were constructed based on a concatenated dataset of 13 PCGs and two rRNA genes from 58 species of Holothuroidea, with two species of Ophiuroidea used as outgroups (Figure S6A and S6B). The maximum likelihood (ML) analysis recovered the topology (Apodida + (Elasipodida + (Dendrochirotida + (Synallactida + Holothuriida)))), which was identical to that obtained from the Bayesian inference (BI) analysis (Figure S6A and S6B). Most nodes in the ML tree were strongly supported, with bootstrap values exceeding 90. However, the clade Holothuriida exhibited relatively low support, with bootstrap values ranging from 35 to 65 (Figure S6A). Similarly, the BI tree also showed weak support for Holothuriida, with a posterior probability of 0.62 (Figure S6B).

The monophyly of Dendrochirotida was strongly supported in both the ML and BI analyses and recovered the topology of (((((C. quadrangularis + C. anceps) + O. glacialis) + (((C. frondosa + C. miniata) + T. gemmata) + P. violaceus)) + (P. proteus + (P. liuwutiensis + P. fragilis)))) (Figure S6A and S6B). Within Dendrochirotida, three major clades were identified: clade 1, comprising (P. proteus + (P. liuwutiensis + P. fragilis)), clade 2, consisting of (((C. frondosa + C. miniata) + T. gemmata) + P. violaceus); and clade 3, including ((C. quadrangularis + C. anceps) + O. glacialis). O. glacialis was recovered as the sister taxon to C. quadrangularis and C. anceps.

Divergence time analysis

Using 13 PCGs, a divergence time tree was retrieved for 48 holothuroid species, excluding duplicate taxa, with 2 ophiuroid species included as outgroups. The overall topology of the resulting tree was consistent with those of the previously reconstructed ML and BI trees (Figure S5A and S5B). The crown ages of the major holothuroid orders were estimated to be 226.93 Ma for Apodida (95% HPD: 150.91–299.55 Ma), 275.14 Ma for Elasipodida (95% HPD: 223.31–323.40 Ma), 224.47 Ma for Dendrochirotida (95% HPD: 219.82–274.27 Ma), 219.54 Ma for Synallactida (95% HPD: 200.08–236.10 Ma), and 173.01 Ma for Holothuriida (95% HPD: 148.42–198.26 Ma) (Fig. 5).

Gene rearrangement in Dendrochirotida

Mitochondrial gene rearrangements of 10 Dendrochirotida species were analyzed using CREx2 and compared with the echinoderm ground pattern (Fig. 6A and B). Compared to the echinoderm mitochondrial ground pattern, an inversion of trnS1 was inferred at the node 1 corresponding to the initial divergence of Dendrochirotida (Fig. 6A). In the echinoderm ground pattern, trnS2, trnS1, trnQ, trnA, trnV, and trnD were located on the light strand, whereas, in all Dendrochirotida species, only trnS2, trnA, trnV, and trnD (excluding trnS1) were located on the light strand. In clade 1, which is composed of P. fragilis, P. liuwutiensis, and P. proteus, a transposition event between trnD and trnM was observed and is inferred to have occurred at node 2.

In clade 2, comprising P. violaceus, T. gemmata, C. miniata, and C. frondosa, P. violaceus showed a gene rearrangement identical to the echinoderm ground pattern, except for the inversion of trnS1 at node 1 (Fig. 6A). In contrast, T. gemmata showed an inversion of trnD, relocating it to the heavy strand. This inversion was absent in C. miniata or C. frondosa, which also share node 6, suggesting that the trnD inversion observed in T. gemmata is a lineage-specific feature that arose during its divergence. Both C. miniata and C. frondosa possessed two CRs and exhibited tRNA gene rearrangements resulting from tandem duplication random loss (TDRL), inferred to have occurred at node 7. In addition, C. frondosa showed a duplication of trnL1 between trnT and the CR.

In clade 3, which is composed of O. glacialis, C. quadrangularis, and C. anceps, C. quadrangularis and C. anceps possess two duplicated CRs, suggesting the occurrence of TDRL events (Fig. 6A). In particular, the rrnS–trnT–trnE–CR block was likely duplicated in C. anceps and inserted downstream of rrnL, followed by the loss of trnT, resulting in the observed mitochondrial gene rearrangement. A subsequent transposition involving rrnS–trnE likely produced the gene order trnI–rrnS–trnE–nad2–rrnL–CR in C. quadrangularis.

Overall, among all Dendrochirotida species, O. glacialis exhibited a notably complex mitochondrial gene rearrangement (Fig. 6A and B). Its gene order differed significantly not only from those of C. quadrangularis and C. anceps, which belong to the same clade, but also from species in other clades (Fig. 6A). CREx2 analysis inferred that this unique gene rearrangement observed in O. glacialis resulted from a combination of one inversion, one transposition, and four TDRL events (Fig. 6B).

To explain the gene arrangement observed in clade 3, we propose two alternative hypotheses. The first hypothesis assumes that a shared TDRL at node 8, the common ancestor of clade 3, after which a transposition occurred in the C. quadrangularis lineage, whereas an additional TDRL and transposition occurred in the O. glacialis (Fig. 6A). The second hypothesis suggests that no TDRL occurred at node 8, the common ancestor of clade 3. Instead, a shared TDRL occurred at node 9, transposition in the C. quadrangularis lineage and independent TDRL and transposition in the O. glacialis lineage (Fig. 6A).

Discussion

Although numerous studies using mitochondrial genomes of Holothuroidea have been published to date, complete mitochondrial genome analyses have been reported for only 7 of the 10 Dendrochirotida species registered in the NCBI database18–24. Moreover, comprehensive comparative studies focusing on Dendrochirotida, which is one of the seven orders comprising the class Holothuroidea, remain insufficient. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the genetic and evolutionary characteristics of O. glacialis, a member of the order Dendrochirotida, by analyzing its complete mitochondrial genome and comparing it with those of nine other Dendrochirotida species with available complete mitochondrial genome data.

Drivers of A + T content bias in the O. glacialis mitogenome

The nucleotide composition of mitochondrial genomes often reflects evolutionary history and environmental adaptation. High A + T content has frequently been reported in taxa inhabiting extreme environments17,25,26. This compositional bias may be interpreted through several non-exclusive mechanisms. First, a shift in nucleotide usage toward A or T, which requires lower energetic costs for synthesis and maintenance compared to G or C, is often suggested27. Second, spontaneous C deamination, an intrinsically occurring process, can ultimately be fixed as C-to-T transitions during DNA replication; adenine deamination to hypoxanthine may also influence strand-specific substitution patterns by promoting A-to-G transitions28–30. Third, asymmetric replication of mitochondrial DNA may further reinforce compositional bias, as prolonged single-stranded exposure of one strand increases its vulnerability to deamination and other forms of damage, leading to strand-specific substitution asymmetry29,31. Such base transitions preferentially accumulate as synonymous mutations at the third codon positions, where functional constraints are relatively relaxed due to the strong purifying selection acting on mitochondrial genomes32.

Consistent with these mechanisms, metazoan mitogenomes generally exhibit high A + T content33 However, the extent of this bias varies across environmental conditions and taxonomic groups. For example, insect mitogenomes typically exhibit particularly high A + T content (approximately 64–87%) compared with other metazoans34. In addition, environmental stressors may increase mutation rates and alter the mutational spectrum, thereby indirectly promoting elevated A + T content in mitogenomes via AT-biased nucleotide substitutions35,36.

The deep-sea environment, characterized by distinctive physicochemical conditions such as high hydrostatic pressure and generally low temperature, imposes substantial physiological stress on organisms37. In holothuroids and some asterozoans, deep-sea species tend to exhibit higher average A + T content compared to their shallow-water counterparts15,17,38–40. However, this trend is not consistently observed in other echinoderm classes, such as Echinoidea, despite their shared ancestry within Echinozoa41,42. These contrasting patterns suggest that the elevated in A + T content cannot be explained solely by environmental factors. Notably, although Holothuroidea and Echinoidea both belong to the clade Echinozoa43, a more consistent increase in A + T content between deep-sea and shallow-water species has been observed in holothuroids15,17,38,41. This suggests that while environmental stressors like high hydrostatic pressure may influence mitogenomic evolution, the resulting compositional shifts are likely mediated by lineage-specific evolutionary strategies.

Although the gene composition of the O. glacialis mitogenome is consistent with that commonly observed in echinoderms45,46, O. glacialis exhibits a markedly elevated A + T content (70.88%) compared to other holothuroid species. Therefore, the consistently high A + T content observed across diverse deep-sea holothuroid lineages, including Apodida, Elasipodida, Synallatida, and Dendrochirotida, suggests that environmental factors may have influenced mitochondrial genome evolution in O. glacialis15,17,38.

To evaluate the relative characteristics of the elevated A + T content observed in O. glacialis, nucleotide composition and compositional asymmetry were compared across 9 dendrochirotids. C. anceps and C. quadrangularis showed the next highest A + T contents after O. glacialis, at 69.61% and 66.93%, respectively. Given that these species were recovered within the same clade, the elevated A + T content may represent a lineage-specific phylogenetic characteristic. However, clear differences were observed in nucleotide compositional asymmetry. Whereas C. anceps and C. quadrangularis exhibited high AT-skew values of 0.203 and 0.202, respectively, O. glacialis showed a value close to zero (0.010), and its GC-skew was also closer to zero than that of the other compared dendrochirotids (− 0.178). Notably, this pattern of high A + T content coupled with an AT bias close to zero has been repeatedly observed in deep-sea holothurians across multiple taxonomic groups, including species from different classes, suggesting that it may represent a common mitogenomic feature of deep-sea lineages17,38. It is plausible that the relatively high A + T content observed in C. anceps and C. quadrangularis also reflects environmental adaptation. However, because they exhibit broad geographic distributions and share similar habitats with many other dendrochirotids, distinguishing between an environmentally driven pattern and an ancestral genomic feature remains challenging. In this context, O. glacialis provides a useful contrast due to its distinct Arctic and deep-sea habitats. Future mitogenomic sequencing of deeper ancestral lineages or more basal related taxa will be essential to fully disentangle the relative contributions of historical ancestry and environmental adaptation to these compositional patterns.

In addition, these results also suggest that, although O. glacialis retains elevated A + T content, the asymmetry between A and T is reduced relative to C. anceps and C. quadrangularis, indicating a greater relative contribution of T. In addition, the shift in GC-skew toward zero suggests that the asymmetry between G and C is also reduced, implying a decrease in the relative excess of C and a corresponding increase in the relative contribution of G. Therefore, even within the same clade, distinct molecular evolutionary mechanisms may have contributed to the formation of A + T content. To test this hypothesis more clearly, additional mitogenome analyses, including shallow-water Ocnus species, will be required to determine whether the elevated A + T content and AT bias observed in O. glacialis reflect adaptation to deep-sea environments, an intrinsic phylogenetic feature of this clade, or a broader compositional characteristic shared across the genus Ocnus.

Synonymous codon bias and compositional evolution in O. glacialis

RSCU analysis across ten dendrochirotid species revealed a ubiquitous preference for A-or U-ending codons. In O. glacialis, however, this bias was exceptionally pronounced at the third codon position (81.8%), characterized specifically by a marked shift toward U-ending codons and a depletion of A- and C-ending codons. This trend aligns with the overall nucleotide skew of the mitogenome and likely reflects asymmetric mutational pressure during mitochondrial replication, where prolonged single-stranded exposure facilitates C-to-U deamination29,31. Similar A/U-biased patterns have been documented in other deep-sea holothuroids, suggesting a common environmental influence on mutational spectra17,38. Furthermore, the widespread preference for A/U-ending codons, particularly driven by the increased contribution of U (T) at the third codon position, appears to be functionally coupled with the elevated overall A + T content of the O. glacialis mitogenome. This pronounced bias toward A/U at the wobble position suggests that directional mutation pressure or selective constraints favoring translation efficiency may have reinforced the genome-wide A + T enrichment, providing further support for molecular adaptation to specialized cold or high-pressure environments.

Despite these significant RSCU shifts, amino acid usage in O. glacialis remained remarkably stable. The proportions of hydrophobic (61.87%) and polar (29.06%) residues were nearly identical to the mean values of other species, indicating that the primary signal of divergence is synonymous rather than functional. Moreover, because mitochondrial translation utilizes a restricted tRNA set capable of “superwobbling,” these RSCU patterns do not necessarily imply an adaptive improvement in translational efficiency44,45. Together with the evidence for relaxed selective pressure in the O. glacialis lineage, the observed codon bias is better interpreted as a byproduct of genome-wide mutational pressure accumulating under relaxed purifying selection, rather than as direct adaptive remodeling of mitochondrial protein properties.

Purifying selection on mitochondrial PCGs

Mitochondrial genes are generally maintained under strong purifying selection (ω < 1) to prevents the accumulation of deleterious nonsynonymous substitutions46–48. Consistent with this, our analysis of 13 PCGs across ten dendrochirotid species yielded ω values well below 1.0, with the one-ratio model (M0) estimating an overall ω of 0.085. This confirms that purifying selection is the predominant force across this order. However, such low values can mask subtle shifts in selective intensity within specific lineages.

Since all genes retained ω values below 1 in this study, to investigate whether the relative deep-sea habitat of O. glacialis influenced its evolutionary trajectory, we conducted lineage-specific analyses, as purifying selective pressure may have been partially relaxed in certain lineages despite the overall persistence of purifying selection49. Interestingly, while most genes remained under purifying selection, the two-ratio branch model indicated a slight increase in the ω value for Clade 3 (including O. glacialis) compared to the background branches (ω = 0.101 vs. 0.080). Furthermore, branch-site model analyses identified specific codon sites in five genes (cytb, nad2, nad4l, nad5, and nad6) with high posterior probabilities (> 0.95), suggesting evolutionary patterns distinct from other lineages.

These signals may reflect a relaxation of purifying selection associated with the physiological constraints of deep-sea environments similar to patterns observed in high-altitude or other deep-sea holothuroids50,51. Recent large-scale comparative analyses have suggested that modest increases in dN/dS are often better explained by demographic factors, such as reduced effective population sizes or restricted geographic ranges, rather than adaptive remodeling51. Comparable signals of relaxed purifying selection have also been reported in other deep-sea holothuroids, suggesting that this pattern may not be unique to O. glacialis17.

In this study, the relative increase in ω values and the lineage-specific signals detected in several genes of O. glacialis may also reflect subtle shifts in selective intensity associated with the physiological constraints imposed by the deep-sea environment. The detection of these signals in genes directly involved in oxidative phosphorylation suggests that altered energetic demands in the deep sea may have exerted specific selective pressures on these functional units. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that background factors such as limited dispersal and population isolation, typical of sedentary dendrochirotids, may also contribute to these observed genetic patterns.

Gene rearrangement patterns and TDRL-mediated evolution

Mitochondrial gene rearrangements serve as potent phylogenetic markers for inversions, transpositions, inverse transpositions, and TDRL events52,53. Gene rearrangements have been widely reported across various species of Holothuroidea17,22,38,54,55. In this study, the consensus phylogenetic trees and the gene order analysis of 10 Dendrochirotida species revealed a common inversion of the trnS1 gene among these species. This phenomenon was also consistently observed in other Holothuroidea55. While clades 1 and 2 exhibit relatively conserved gene orders22,56, the rearrangements in Clade 3 species, C. anceps, C. quadrangularis, and O. glacialis, are significantly more complex and are best explained by the TDRL mechanism.

A key piece of evidence supporting TDRL in C. anceps and C. quadrangularis is the presence of duplicated CRs. Previous studies that lacked CR data often characterized these changes as simple transpositions21,23. However, the structural signature of duplicated CRs strongly suggests a TDRL event involving a large gene cluster. We hypothesize that an ancestral TDRL event occurred in the common ancestor of these two species, followed by lineage-specific secondary modifications, such as the transposition observed in C. quadrangularis.

In O. glacialis, CREx2 analysis indicates a history of repeated structural reorganization, including four TDRL events. Interestingly, the long intergenic regions exceeding 100 bp in O. glacialis may represent ‘molecular fossils’, vestigial sequences that were incompletely excised following duplication60. Thus, the long intergenic regions in O. glacialis may be interpreted as residual fragments left after differential gene loss during TDRL-mediated rearrangement. In contrast, duplicated CRs are preserved in Cucumaria as relatively clear structural signatures of TDRL22, whereas no comparable structural remnants were identified in O. glacialis. These findings suggest that TDRL-mediated rearrangement is a major driver of mitogenomic diversity within Dendrochirotida, with the degree of signature preservation reflecting the evolutionary distance from the initial duplication event.

The intergenic sequences of O. glacialis total 1,203 bp (approximately 7.2% of the mitogenome) and exhibit an exceptionally high A + T content of 81.8%. If these regions are interpreted as residual fragments from repeated TDRL events, their accumulation likely provided a permissive non-coding substrate for mutational bias. Unlike coding regions, which are under strong purifying selection, these non-coding sequences can tolerate neutral substitutions. Consequently, asymmetric mutational processes, such as the deamination-driven C-to-U transitions discussed earlier, could accumulate more readily in these regions, further reinforcing the mitogenome’s overall A + T richness57. This interpretation is also consistent with previous reports showing that some mitochondrial intergenic spacers can evolve very rapidly58.

The repeated TDRL-mediated restructuring in O. glacialis appears linked to an accelerated accumulation of mitochondrial nucleotide substitutions59. In this study, O. glacialis exhibited higher K2P distances across all 13 PCGs, consistent with its markedly elongated branch lengths in both ML and BI phylogenies. While K2P distance and branch length measure different aspects of sequence evolution, both suggest that the O. glacialis lineage has accumulated more substitutions than other dendrochirotids. Previous studies have reported a positive correlation between mitochondrial gene rearrangement rates and nucleotide substitution rates42. A similar relationship between rearrangement frequency and branch length has been observed in other echinoderms, such as Ophiuroidea42. These findings support the interpretation that structural reorganization and substitutional change in O. glacialis are correlated evolutionary patterns. Furthermore, asymmetric mutational processes can influence both the magnitude and direction of nucleotide substitutions. This suggests that the accelerated substitution rate in O. glacialis is intrinsically related to its pronounced AT bias, high A + T content, and altered RSCU patterns.

Biogeographic origin and arctic adaptation of O. glacialis

Previously, it was suggested that the origin of Holothuroidea may have been associated with deep-sea environments17. In the present phylogenetic tree, many of the species belonging to Apodida and Elasipodida, the earliest-diverging orders, are deep-sea dwellers, which is an intriguing pattern consistent with this hypothesis. However, the number of mitogenomes available for both Apodida and Elasipodida remains limited, and many of these studies have focused primarily on species collected from deep-sea habitats, making it difficult to exclude the possibility of sampling bias. Thus, although the concentration of deep-sea species within basal lineages is certainly noteworthy, it remains unclear whether this pattern reflects a genuine signal of the ancestral habitat of holothuroids or is simply an artifact of limited taxon sampling. Resolving this issue will require broader mitogenomes incorporating a wider range of species from both Apodida and Elasipodida.

This uncertainty becomes even more evident within Dendrochirotida. Among the ten dendrochirotid species included in the present study, O. glacialis was the only species specialized for deep-sea environments, while several other Ocnus species have generally been reported from shallow or coastal temperate to warm-temperate habitats60,61. In contrast, O. glacialis has been recorded across Arctic shelf, coastal, and slope environments, showing an exceptional distribution within the genus. This pattern suggests that O. glacialis is unlikely to represent a lineage that simply retained the typical ecology of the genus while expanding northward. Rather, it may represent a lineage that diverged from an ancestrally shallow-water, coastal clade and subsequently adapted to colder and deeper environments around the Arctic. Accordingly, rather than interpreting these data as direct support for a deep-sea origin of Holothuroidea as a whole, it may be more important to consider the possibility that certain holothuroid lineages underwent secondary adaptation from shallow-water ancestors into deep-sea environments. This interpretation is also consistent with the biogeographic background of O. glacialis. Previous studies have suggested that Pacific-origin species are prevalent among Arctic echinoderm faunas, and O. glacialis has likewise been classified as a Pacific-origin species62. This supports the possibility that the ancestral lineage of O. glacialis was associated with the North Pacific and later dispersed into the Arctic.

Based on this biogeographic interpretation, the most plausible route of Arctic colonization by O. glacialis would have been Pacific–Arctic exchange through the Bering Strait. The initial marine opening of the Bering Strait has been estimated to have occurred during the late Miocene, approximately 5.5 Ma, after which marine biotic exchange between the North Pacific and the Arctic became possible63. However, because the Bering Strait was repeatedly opened and closed in response to sea-level fluctuations, the Arctic colonization of the ancestral lineage of O. glacialis is more appropriately interpreted not as a single dispersal event, but as the cumulative result of repeated episodes of dispersal during open phases of the strait, followed by isolation during closed phases64,65. In particular, during glacial periods, sea-level lowering likely exposed the Bering land bridge and restricted direct marine exchange, suggesting that actual Arctic entry most likely occurred during interglacial or relatively high sea-level intervals, with subsequent divergence and adaptation proceeding under isolation during glacial phases. Therefore, O. glacialis may be interpreted as a lineage that entered the Arctic from the Pacific after the opening of the Bering Strait during the Pliocene and subsequently established its present Arctic distribution and phylogenetic distinctiveness through repeated glacial–interglacial cycles.

Meanwhile, although divergence time analysis estimated the split leading to O. glacialis at approximately 203.59 Ma, this date more likely reflects separation within a much deeper ancestral lineage rather than the time at which O. glacialis itself diverged within Ocnus. Because comparable mitogenome data remain very limited for extant Ocnus species, it is currently difficult to estimate directly when O. glacialis diverged from its congeners. Moreover, given that other Ocnus species are primarily distributed in shallow-water or temperate coastal habitats, the cold-adapted and deep-sea habitat specialization of O. glacialis are more likely to represent derived features acquired in a particular lineage after diversification of the genus, rather than ancestral traits of the genus. This interpretation is consistent with the idea that, even within Dendrochirotida, ecological differences among extant species may have arisen through relatively recent lineage diversification rather than at deeper higher-level phylogenetic splits. For example, C. frondosa is distributed in cold North Atlantic waters, whereas C. miniata is known from temperate coastal habitats of the northeastern Pacific, and the divergence time between these two species has been estimated at approximately 23.80 Ma. At least in dendrochirotid lineages, therefore, ecological differences among extant species may have been shaped during more recent processes of lineage divergence and environmental adaptation rather than during ancient ancestral splits. Accordingly, clarifying the precise divergence time of O. glacialis and identifying the stage at which environmental adaptation arose will require broader taxon sampling and additional mitogenomic data across the genus Ocnus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and identification

A single specimen of O. glacialis (Figure S7) was collected from the Beaufort Sea (69°52′N, 139°03′W) using a remotely operated underwater vehicle operated by the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute at a depth of approximately 800 m (Figure S8). Taxonomic identification was conducted using both morphological and molecular criteria. The specimen exhibited general diagnostic features of the family Cucumariidae, including a cylindrical body and a distinct tentacle crown. In addition, occurrence records from the OBIS database were compiled to examine the bathymetric distribution of O. glacialis (Figure S1).

For molecular confirmation, the COI gene was analyzed. Immediately following collection, partial muscle tissue, which is less likely to harbor symbionts compared to the digestive or coelomic systems, was dissected from the specimen and used for whole genomic DNA extraction with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The purified DNA was then stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. To identify the specimen, the mitochondrial COI was amplified using a 20 µL PCR mixture containing the degenerate primers dgLCO1490 and dgHCO2198, 300 ng of genomic DNA, and TOPsimple PCR PreMIX-nTaq (Enzynomics, Seoul, South Korea)66. PCR amplification was performed using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 38 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min, 50 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

The amplified PCR products were sequenced using an ABI 3730XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) by Bionics (Seoul, South Korea). The obtained sequences were queried against the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD), showing 99.9 ~ 100% similarity to the COI sequence of O. glacialis, thereby confirming the specimen as O. glacialis. Consistent results were further obtained from the complete mitogenome generated using next-generation sequencing (NGS), reaffirming the species identification.

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

NGS was performed to obtain the complete mitogenomes of O. glacialis using the HiSeq platform (150 bp; HiSeq X Ten; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). A fragment library was prepared at Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) using the TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, purified genomic DNA was randomly fragmented, followed by ligation of 5′ and 3′ adapters. The prepared library was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform, and paired-end raw reads were subjected to quality assessment using FastQC v0.11.967. After demultiplexing, only reads with matching index pairs were retained. Quality filtering and trimming were performed using Trimmomatic68 to remove adapter sequences, low-quality reads, reads containing more than 10% unknown bases, and ambiguous bases, yielding high-quality reads. From a total of 66,257,656 raw reads, 48,869,256 filtered reads were retained for downstream analyses. De novo assembly of circular mitochondrial contigs was conducted using NOVOplasty69 with multiple k-mer settings with proper sequencing depth (Figure S9). The resulting consensus mitochondrial genome was annotated using MITOS270, and the mitochondrial genome map of O. glacialis was generated using the Proksee web server71.

Genome analysis

Genetic and evolutionary analyses were conducted using the complete mitochondrial genomes of O. glacialis and nine Dendrochirotida species. The analyzed species and their corresponding NCBI accession numbers are as follows: Cercodemas anceps (ON950725), Colochirus quadrangularis (MT108721), Cucumaria frondosa (MZ321055), Cucumaria miniata (AY182376), P. proteus (formally known as N. proteus; MZ305458), Pseudocolochirus violaceus (MT587564), Thyonella gemmata (MZ463652), Phyllophorella liuwutiensis (MN198190), and Phyrella fragilis (MZ305459). Nucleotide composition of the mitogenomes was analyzed using MEGA 11 ver. 11.0.1372. The nucleotide composition of the 13 PCGs was further used to calculate AT-skew and GC-skew according to the formulas: AT-skew = (A - T) / (A + T) and GC-skew = (G - C) / (G + C). The secondary structures of O. glacialis tRNAs were predicted using the tRNAscan-SE73 and visualized using RNAcanvas74. Codon usage and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) for the 13 PCGs across the 10 Dendrochirotida species were calculated using MEGA 11 ver. 11.0.1372.

Mitochondrial gene rearrangements were analyzed using CREx2, which was used to compare gene order patterns and infer possible rearrangement events among the analyzed taxa75. Genetic distances among species were calculated from the 13 PCGs based on the Kimura two-parameter (K2P) model using MEGA 11 ver. 11.0.1372.

In O. glacialis, all intergenic regions, including the control region, were analyzed for possible pseudogene remnants, repetitive sequences, and conserved motifs. BLASTn was used to detect pseudogene-like sequences76, MAFFT ver. 7.490 was used to determine whether these regions showed meaningful alignment with adjacent sequences77, Tandem Repeat Finder was used to identify repeat sequences78, and the MEME Suite was used to search for conserved motifs79. BLASTn, Tandem Repeat Finder, and MEME Suite analyses were performed using their respective web-based implementations.

Selective pressure analysis

To evaluate the selective pressures acting on the 13 PCGs of O. glacialis, the ratios of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions (Ka/Ks) were calculated. In general, a ω greater than 1 indicates positive selection (or accelerated evolution), a ratio equal to 1 suggests neutral selection, whereas a ratio less than 1 is indicative of purifying selection or functional constraint49.

For ω estimation, the amino acid sequence of the 13 PCGs from 10 dendrochirotid species were translated into MEGA 11 ver. 11.0.13 72 using the echinoderm mitochondrial translation table. The translated amino acid sequences were aligned using MAFFT ver. 7.49077 with the L-INS-i algorithm with 1000 iterative refinements (maxiterate = 1000). The aligned amino acid sequences were back-translated into codon-based nucleotide alignments using PAL2NAL80. Subsequently, Ka and Ks values for each gene were calculated using the KaKs_calculator 3.081.

Statistical analysis of ω values between the group including O. glacialis and the group excluding O. glacialis was performed using the SPSS software package (version 28.0, IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, IL, USA). The homogeneity of variance between the two comparison groups was assessed using Levene’s test. Subsequently, differences in the ω values between these groups were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test.

To further investigate lineage-specific selective pressures in more detail, additional codon-based analyses were performed using the codeml program in the PAML ver. 4.10.1082. The aligned sequences of the 13 PCGs were concatenated into a single PHYLIP file for subsequent analyses by concatenator ver. 0.2.1.

Selective pressure analyses were conducted using the M0, branch model, and branch-site model. The M0 was used to estimate the overall selective pressure acting on mitochondrial PCGs. To examine whether selective pressure differed in the O. glacialis clade, the branch model was applied by designating C. anceps, C. quadrangularis, and O. glacialis as the foreground branch and the remaining 7 dendrochirotid species as the background branch. The one-ratio model and two-ratio model were compared using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to determine whether the ω differed significantly between foreground and background branches.

To identify positively selected sites along O. glacialis, the branch-site model was applied. The branch-site model was applied by designating O. glacialis as the foreground branch and the remaining 9 dendrochirotid species as the background branch. The LRT was used to compare the branch-site model with the corresponding null model, in which Ka/Ks ratio was fixed at 1. Bayes Empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis was used to calculate posterior probabilities and identify codon sites under positive selection.

All likelihood ratio tests were performed by comparing twice the log-likelihood difference (2ΔInL) between nested models and evaluating significance using a chi-square distribution.

Phylogenetic analysis

To evaluate the phylogenetic relationships within Holothuroidea, including O. glacialis, phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using maximum-likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) approaches. A total of 60 complete mitochondrial genomes were analyzed, comprising holothuroids (3 Apodida, 14 Holothuriida, 22 Synallactida, 11 Dendrochirotida, and 8 Elasipodida) and ophiuroids as outgroups (2 Ophiurida). Detailed information on the species included in the phylogenetic analyses is provided in Table S1.

Sequences of the 13 PCGs and two rRNA genes were aligned using the L-INS-i algorithm with 1,000 iterative refinements (maxiterate = 1000) implemented in MAFFT ver. 7.49077. Poorly aligned regions and gaps were removed using trimAl ver. 1.483. The curated alignments were used to generate two datasets: one consisting of the 13 PCGs and another combining the 13 PCGs with the two rRNA genes, using SequenceMatrix ver. 1.8.1, and subsequently converted to NEXUS format84. The best-fit nucleotide substitution models for each gene were determined using ModelFinder implemented in IQ-TREE2 ver. 2.0.7 (Table S7)85. ML phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using IQ-TREE2 ver. 2.0.786 under the optimal substitution models selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates to assess node support. BI phylogenetic analysis was conducted using MrBayes ver. 3.2.787, applying the best-fit substitution model selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were conducted for 10,000,000 generations with four chains each, sampling every 100 generations. Convergence and effective sample size (ESS) values were assessed using Tracer ver. 1.7.288, and all parameters exhibited ESS values greater than 200. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in using LogCombiner ver. 2.7, and a consensus tree was generated with TreeAnnotator ver. 2.7.689. Both ML and BI trees were visualized and edited using FigTree ver. 1.4.490.

Divergence time analysis

Divergence time analysis was conducted based on the mitochondrial genome dataset used in the phylogenetic analysis, after excluding duplicate taxa. The final dataset comprised 50 complete mitochondrial genomes, including 48 Holothuroidea species and 2 Ophiuroidea species used as outgroups. The holothuroid taxa consisted of 3 Apodida, 12 Holothuriida, 14 Synallactida, 11 Dendrochirotida, and 8 Elasipodida species.

For divergence time estimation, the 13 PCGs from each species were analyzed as separate partitions using BEAST v2.7.789. The same substitution model (GTR), strict clock model, and Yule process tree prior were applied to all gene partitions. To calibrate divergence times, fossil-based age constraints from previous studies were applied to the crown node of Holothuroidea, the stem node of Elasipodida, and the stem node of Holothuriidae, respectively, and each calibration point was assigned a lognormal prior7. The calibration ages were based on the midpoint values of 435 Ma (431–449 Ma), 389.5 Ma (385–394 Ma), and 250.5 Ma (244–257 Ma), respectively.

MCMC analyses were run independently four times for 50,000,000 generations, with parameters sampled every 100 generations. The resulting log and tree files were combined using LogCombiner, and 30% of each run was discarded as burn-in. ESS values were assessed in Tracer, and all parameters showed ESS values greater than 200 35. A maximum clade credibility (MCC) consensus tree was then generated using TreeAnnotator and visualized in FigTree v1.4.489,90. Uncertainty in divergence time estimates was represented by 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals.
