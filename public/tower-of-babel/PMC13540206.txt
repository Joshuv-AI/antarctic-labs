Rethinking protein choices: the association between the isocaloric substitution of dietary protein sources and dietary acid load in hemodialysis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatemeh Navab, Houri Heshamtipour, Shayesteh Keyhanpour, Maryam Najarian, Sahar Foshati, Mojgan Mortazavi, Mohammad Hossein Rouhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemodialysis (HD) patients experience high morbidity due to metabolic acidosis and malnutrition. Dietary acid load (DAL), largely determined by protein source, is a key modifiable factor in acid-base balance. However, the relationship between protein source substitutions and DAL, considering nutritional status, remains unclear. We investigated associations between exchanging dietary protein sources and DAL in HD patients.MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study enrolled 307 maintenance HD patients. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire, and serum albumin was measured from blood samples. Isocaloric substitutions of 1 g/day protein from one source for another were evaluated using generalized linear models with simultaneous inclusion of both protein sources, adjusted for total energy intake and relevant confounders.ResultsIsocaloric substitution of red/processed meat with dairy, legume, or nut protein was significantly associated with increased odds of higher DAL. Conversely, replacing dairy protein with red/processed or white meat, as well as exchanging legume or nut protein for red/processed or white meat, was associated with reduced odds of higher DAL. These associations were markedly modified by nutritional status assessed by serum albumin. In patients with lower albumin levels (< 3.83 g/dL), substitutions involving red/processed meat replacement with plant-based proteins showed stronger associations with Potential renal acid load (PRAL), an estimate of the renal acid excretory requirement imposed by diet, with the most pronounced protective association observed when nut protein was replaced with red/processed meat. In patients with higher albumin levels (≥ 3.84 g/dL), replacement of white protein with dairy, legume, or nut protein uniformly increased PRAL odds, while substituting legume or nut protein with red/processed or white meat consistently exerted a protective association against elevated dietary acid load.ConclusionProtein source substitutions are independently associated with DAL in hemodialysis patients, with effect directions and magnitudes varying by specific food combinations and substantially modified by nutritional status. These findings support personalized dietary strategies integrating both protein source selection and individual patient characteristics for optimized acid-base management in this high-risk population.

Introduction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a growing public health challenge [1]. Patients with EKRD require renal replacement therapies (RRT) including hemodialysis (HD), which accounts for approximately 69% of all RRT [2]. Hemodialysis patients may experience several complications, including acid accumulation and malnutrition [3–5]. As previous studies have shown, dietary intake can affect both acid–base balance and nutritional status [6–8]. Dietary acid load (DAL) is a measure commonly used to estimate the acid-producing potential of the diet. Dietary acid load was estimated using two validated methods: potential renal acid load (PRAL), defined as the estimated renal acid excretory requirement imposed by diet, and net endogenous acid production (NEAP), defined as the estimated total endogenous acid generation driven by protein versus alkali intake. Higher values indicate greater acid-producing potential [9, 10].

According to the 2020 National Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guideline, people undergoing HD are recommended to consume 1 to 1.2 g/kg/day of protein [11]. The source of protein intake is one of the important factors that can affect DAL [12]; therefore, it can have a significant impact on maintaining acid-base balance in HD patients. A post hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial by Müller et al. indicated that, in healthy individuals, consumption of plant-based proteins is associated with a lower DAL compared to animal-based proteins such as meat [13]. Moreover, a review by Remer et al., investigated the relation of dietary protein with DAL and concluded that increasing protein intake along with fruits and vegetables can help bone health [14]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has been conducted to examine how replacing one protein source with another may affect DAL in HD patients.

On the other hand, malnutrition, indicated by low albumin, is associated with acid accumulation in the patient’s body [15]. In other words, as malnutrition worsens, acidity rises, and when acidity is high, it can worsen malnutrition [16]. While some studies have noted this association, the potential interaction between these two factors has not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, in this study, we aim to investigate the association between substituting different protein sources and DAL in HD patients. We also want to see whether this association differs between patients who are malnourished and those who are not.

Method

Study design and population

This multi-center cross-sectional study was conducted in Isfahan, Iran, from September 2021 to March 2022. This study included 307 maintenance HD patients, who were recruited from five different HD centers in Isfahan. The inclusion criteria of our study were as follows: receiving maintenance HD for at least 3 months; age ≥ 18 years; and demonstrating the ability and readiness to participate in our investigation. In contrast, individuals with a daily energy intake above 4200 kcal (17,573 kJ) or below 800 kcal (3,347 kJ) were excluded from the study [17]. Prior to the initiation of the study, all participants were informed about the study’s purposes and procedures, and subsequently signed informed consent forms. The Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol (IR.MUI.PHANUT.REC.1404.168).

Dietary assessment

To evaluate long-term dietary intakes, participants completed a 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) during face-to-face interviews conducted by trained dietitians. Participants reported both the frequency of consumption and the usual portion size for each listed food item. Portion sizes were quantified using standard household measures and subsequently converted to grams for analysis [18]. A nine-category scale was used to assess dietary frequency, ranging from “never or less than once per month” to “six or more times per day”. Dietary intake data were analyzed using Nutritionist IV software (First DataBank, San Bruno, CA, USA), which is based primarily on the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Food Composition Database. The nutrient composition of reported food items was derived from the USDA food composition tables embedded within the software. For local foods not available in the database, appropriate substitutions or standardized recipes were applied to ensure accurate nutrient estimation. The reliability and validity FFQ have been previously confirmed in the Iranian population. The correlation coefficient between FFQ and mean of twelve 24 h dietary recalls was 0.65 [19].

Anthropometric measurements

Participants’ height was measured in a standard standing position without shoes, using a non-stretchable tape measure, and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). Dry weight was assessed after the dialysis session while patients wore light clothing, using a calibrated digital scale with a precision of 0.1 kg (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). Dry weight was defined as the lowest post-dialysis weight at which the patients demonstrated no clinical signs or symptoms of hypovolemia or hypervolemia [22]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing dry weight in kilograms by the height in meters squared. Waist circumference (WC) was determined with an accuracy of 0.1 cm using a non-elastic tape placed at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest [23].

Assessment of malnutrition

Serum albumin level was used as the biomarker of malnutrition. Blood samples were collected in a fasting state before dialysis, and serum was separated by centrifugation (2000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C). Serum albumin concentrations were measured using the bromocresol green (BCG) method, a colorimetric test commonly used in clinical laboratories. Analysis was performed with an automated biochemistry analyzer. All measurements were conducted in a single laboratory following standard quality control procedures, and the results were reported in grams per deciliter (g/dL). To explore whether nutritional status, as indicated by serum albumin, modifies the association between food group substitutions and dietary acid load indices, we performed stratified analyses based on median albumin level. Albumin was used as a proxy for overall nutritional and inflammatory status, recognizing that it does not directly influence the biochemical calculation of PRAL or NEAP.

Assessment of hemodialysis quality

The quality of hemodialysis was assessed using the Urea Reduction Ratio (URR), a widely accepted indicator of dialysis adequacy. Assessment of other variables

Demographic information, including age, occupation, and marital status, was gathered through participant self-report, whereas clinical data such as dialysis vintage, cause of kidney failure, duration and frequency of dialysis sessions, comorbidities, and medications were extracted from hospital records.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and by examining skewness and kurtosis values [25]. Variables that were not normally distributed were expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and analyzed using appropriate non-parametric statistical tests. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and qualitative data were reported as percentage. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the independent samples t-test, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. To control for covariates, adjusted analyses were conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Dietary intakes were reported as intake per 1000 kcal. According to the previous study [26], PRAL and NEAP were dichotomized based on the median-cut method for regression analyses. To quantify the association between a 1 g/day substitution of food groups and the odds of elevated PRAL and NEAP levels, multivariate logistic regression models were fitted using generalized linear modeling (GLM). The 1 g/day substitution effect was estimated using an isocaloric substitution modeling approach. Protein from both sources was simultaneously entered into the regression model. Under this parameterization, increasing protein from Source B by 1 g/day while holding total protein and energy constant necessarily corresponds to a 1 g/day decrease in protein from Source (A) Therefore, the substitution effect was calculated as the difference between regression coefficients (βB − βA), representing the expected change in outcome associated with replacing 1 g/day of protein from Source A with protein from Source (B) Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to evaluate the associations between food group substitutions and elevated PRAL and NEAP levels. The analyses were stratified according to the median value of serum albumin. All models were adjusted for potential confounders including age, sex, total energy intake, urea reduction ratio, marital and employment status.

Results

The general characteristics of the study participants, stratified by PRAL and NEAP levels, are summarized in Table 1. Our findings demonstrated a significant predominance of male participants in the lower PRAL group. Furthermore, unemployment was more prevalent among individuals with higher PRAL, whereas those with lower PRAL were predominantly retired or self-employed. In addition, the percentage of married patients was higher in the lower NEAP group. On the other hand, higher URR values were observed in patients with greater PRAL. No significant differences were observed in other characteristics across the various levels of PRAL and NEAP.

The association between the odds of increased PRAL as well as NEAP and the isocaloric substitution of one gram per day (1 g/d) of different protein sources with another is presented in Table 3. An increased odds of greater PRAL and NEAP was observed when 1 g/d of red/processed meat was isocalorically replaced with dairy protein, legume protein, or nut protein. Moreover, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d white protein with dairy protein and legume protein was associated with greater odds of NEAP> median. In addition to dairy protein and legume protein, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d white protein with nut protein was associated with higher odds of greater PRAL. Conversely, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of dairy protein with other animal protein sources was associated with lower odds of greater PRAL and NEAP. Specifically, isocalorically substituting 1 g/d of dairy protein with red/processed meat reduced odds of higher PRAL and NEAP. Similarly, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of dairy protein with white meat protein was associated a significant reduction in odds of higher PRAL and NEAP. Furthermore, isocalorically substituting plant-based protein resources with animal-based protein resources was associated with decreased odds of greater PRAL and NEAP. Isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of legume protein with red/processed meat reduced odds of greater PRAL and NEAP. Similarly, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of legume protein with white meat protein was associated with reduced odds of higher PRAL and NEAP. However, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of legume protein with nut protein was associated with increased odds of NEAP> median. Moreover, a significant reduction in odds of higher PRAL and NEAP was observed when 1 g/d of nut protein was isocalorically replaced with red/processed meat. Likewise, isocalorically replacing 1 g/d of nut protein with white meat protein was associated with reduced odds of PRAL> median.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the substitution of red and processed meats in place of dairy products, legumes, and nuts was associated with an increased odds of higher endogenous acid production. Conversely, replacing just 1 gram per day of protein from red, processed, or white meat with dairy protein resulted in a reduction in DAL. Additionally, the consumption of white meat in place of dairy products, legumes, or nuts was found to increase the indicators of DAL. Notably, legume intake contributed to a reduction in acid production when used as a substitute for red, processed, or white meats.

In individuals without malnutrition, as determined by normal serum albumin levels, the substitution of alternative protein sources, such as dairy, nuts, and legumes for red and processed meat, as well as white meat, resulted in a reduction of the PRAL. Among malnourished individuals, replacing red and processed meat with other protein sources, excluding dairy products, also led to a decrease in PRAL. Conversely, in malnourished individuals, substituting dairy protein with red and processed meat was associated with a reduction in NEAP.

In patients with disturbances in acid-base metabolism, a high DAL, particularly from animal-based proteins, can lead to low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, contributing to metabolic acidosis. Metabolic acidosis, in turn, enhances protein catabolism and promotes muscle wasting [27, 28]. On the other hand, disturbances in phosphate metabolism in HD patients exacerbate imbalances in acid-base homeostasis [29]. Malnutrition in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a multifactorial condition resulting from a combination of factors, including systemic inflammation, hormonal imbalances, increased protein catabolism, and reduced dietary intake. These factors collectively contribute to significant disturbances in metabolic homeostasis [30, 31]. Dietary interventions aimed at reducing acid load may be ineffective if malnutrition is not addressed first. Current evidence suggests that the beneficial effects of DAL reduction are not observed in the presence of untreated malnutrition, highlighting the need to prioritize nutritional rehabilitation before implementing such dietary modifications [20].

Our results showed a more significant effect of food substitution on PRAL compared to NEAP. This may be related to the components used in the calculation of PRAL. While PRAL includes protein, potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium, NEAP primarily considers only protein (acid) and potassium (base) as the main factors influencing acid and base production. Therefore, NEAP does not account for other important minerals that affect acid-base balance and kidney function, which may explain the observed differences [32, 33].

These findings highlight the importance of protein source in modulating dietary acid–base balance and suggest that greater incorporation of plant-based proteins and dairy, as opposed to meats, may be beneficial in reducing DAL. As demonstrated in previous studies, various dietary components significantly influence the acid-base homeostasis. Sulfur, derived from the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acid, such as methionine, homocysteine, and cysteine, plays a key role in dietary acid load. These amino acids, predominantly found in animal proteins, cereals, and nuts, are metabolized to produce sulfate, a non-metabolizable acidic anion. This process contributes to the systemic acid load by releasing protons and acidic anions during metabolism [34, 35]. Notably, sulfate excretion has been shown to be inversely correlated with urinary pH, reflecting its acidogenic potential [31, 36]. Similarly, phosphorus, primarily sourced from meat and dairy products, exerts acid-forming effects. In contrast, dietary magnesium and potassium, which are abundant in plant-based foods such as fruits and vegetables, have an alkalizing effect. These minerals-potassium, magnesium, and calcium- contribute to the generation of base equivalents, including bicarbonate, thereby neutralizing excess acid [9, 37, 38]. Malate and citrate, produced during the metabolism of magnesium and potassium salts, require hydrogen ions for their own metabolic processing [39]. Furthermore, dairy products are a major source of dietary calcium, which may contribute to the alkali load through the generation of bicarbonate during metabolism. Increased dietary calcium intake is known to suppress parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion. Since PTH promotes the release of calcium and phosphate from bone into the circulation, its suppression leads to lower serum phosphate levels. As phosphate functions as a buffer in acid–base balance, elevated phosphate levels can contribute to an increased acid load. Therefore, higher calcium intake from dairy products may help reduce systemic acidity by both increasing bicarbonate production and modulating phosphate levels via PTH regulation [40–42]. Supporting these observations, Harter et al. reported that plant-based protein sources, such as legumes, are associated with a lower DAL compared to animal-based proteins. This is likely attributable to their higher content of potassium and magnesium and other base-forming nutrients [28, 43]. Phosphate derived from plant-based proteins is predominantly present in the form of phytate, which exhibits significantly lower bioavailability than inorganic phosphorus [39].

To our knowledge, this was the first study to evaluate the association between food group composition and PRAL and NEAP in HD patients. The questionnaires were administered by trained interviewers. We also performed substitution analysis to examine interactions with malnutrition. However, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality between dietary protein sources and dietary acid load indicators. Second, the reliance on self-reported dietary data, even when collected by trained interviewers, is subject to potential recall bias and may not fully capture long-term dietary patterns. Third, the median serum albumin level in our study population was 3.8 g/dL, which exceeds the conventional clinical threshold for hypoalbuminemia; this may have led to an underestimation of the prevalence of malnutrition and influenced the stratification of nutritional status in our analyses. Fourth, nutritional status stratification was based solely on serum albumin, which in hemodialysis patients is influenced by inflammation, hydration status, and liver function. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive nutritional assessment tools are needed to validate our findings. Fifth, despite adjustments for potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, some stratified estimates showed wide confidence intervals (e.g., nuts), reflecting low consumption frequencies and sparse data within subgroups. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power, although the main analyses based on more commonly consumed protein sources remain robust. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to hemodialysis patients with similar clinical characteristics, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other populations, such as those with earlier stages of chronic kidney disease or without acid-base disturbances.

In conclusion, dietary protein source significantly influences acid-base balance in hemodialysis patients. Replacing red and processed meats with dairy, legumes, or nuts is associated with reduced dietary acid load, primarily through plant-based proteins’ alkalizing effects. However, these benefits depend on adequate nutritional status; malnutrition must be addressed first for dietary interventions to be effective. These findings support prioritizing plant-based and dairy proteins in clinical dietary guidance for this population.
