Fundamental Anatomy and Its Impact on Clinical Practice
Authors: Adolfo Fontenla, Carsten Israel, Damián Sánchez-Quintana, José-Ángel Cabrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rightward extension of Bachmann's bundle (BB) represents a subepicardial myocardial continuum integrating the interatrial bridge with the sinus node region, terminal crest, and right atrial appendage. Rather than a discrete insulated tract, it consists of aligned cardiomyocytes forming a bifurcated arrangement that supports preferential longitudinal and circumferential conduction. This convergence zone near the superior cavoatrial junction enables coordinated biatrial activation but also introduces anisotropic properties that may facilitate conduction delay or arrhythmogenesis. These anatomical insights provide the basis for BB-region pacing, which aims to reproduce near-physiological atrial activation, improving interatrial synchrony and potentially reducing atrial arrhythmia burden compared with conventional right atrial appendage pacing.Take-Home MessagesThe rightward extension of BB constitutes a subepicardial, anatomically integrated myocardial system closely related to the sinus node region and the crista terminalis, characterized by alignment parallel aggregation of its cardiomyocytes rather than structural insulation. This organized architecture provides the anatomical substrate that underlies physiological activation of the bundle and supports the rationale for targeted stimulation strategies aiming to reproduce or improve native interatrial conduction and prevent atrial desynchronization during pacing.

Rightward Extension of Bachmann's Bundle: Anatomical Organization

The rightward extension of Bachmann's bundle (BB) represents the anatomical continuation of the interatrial muscular bridge into the right atrium (RA) and establishes a close spatial relationship with the sinus node region and the terminal crest.1, 2, 3, 4 When traced superiorly, the crest, retaining the parallel aggregation of its cardiomyocytes, passes in front of the orifice of the superior caval vein (SCV) as the precaval band. This part of the crest then merges with the anterior part of the superior interatrial groove, still with the cardiomyocytes aggregated in parallel fashion, to produce the preferential pathway for conduction from the sinus node to the left atrium, known as BB. Two main components of the rightward extension can be identified. The anterior-superior arm courses toward the SCV and right atrial appendage (RAA), cardiomyocytes that contribute to encircle its base and integrate with the trabeculated musculature. The inferior arm descends within the subepicardium of the right atrial vestibule, blending with circumferentially oriented myocardial strands that surround the tricuspid valve orifice.3 This dual anatomical arrangement underpins a functional longitudinal-circumferential activation pattern of the RA, as shown in Figure 2, with conduction spreading from the superior interatrial groove toward both the superior caval–appendage complex and the vestibular myocardium encircling the tricuspid annulus.

The terminal crest provides the structural framework that defines the anatomical context of the rightward extension.5 As a prominent muscular ridge separating the smooth venous component from the trabeculated appendage, it serves as a scaffold along which myocardial cells of BB converge. Importantly, the rightward extension is not encapsulated by fibrous tissue and does not fulfill the criteria of a discrete conduction bundle. Instead, it represents a region of preferential myocardial alignment within the atrial wall. The distinct orientation of its myocytes relative to the surrounding atrial myocardium confers anisotropic conduction properties without anatomical insulation. Variations in fiber orientation and thickness at the interfaces of these bundles may facilitate conduction block during sinus rhythm propagation or contribute to the substrate for atrial arrhythmias

Understanding the rightward extension as a 3-dimensional anatomical entity requires appreciation of its integration with the sinus node region, terminal crest, and RAA. This structural organization provides the substrate upon which functional conduction patterns are superimposed and forms the basis for subsequent discussion of targeted stimulation strategies.

Guest Insight on Clinical Application of Bachmann's Bundle Pacing

Targeted pacing at the level of BB has emerged as a physiologically attractive alternative to conventional RAA pacing, particularly in patients with interatrial conduction delay and/or atrial tachyarrhythmia vulnerability.8,9 The concept is grounded in the anatomical role of the interatrial muscular bridge and its rightward extensions at the atrial roof, which constitute a dominant pathway for rapid left atrial recruitment during sinus rhythm and right atrial pacing.

Anatomical Rationale: Why BB-Region Pacing “Makes Sense”

High paraseptal/atrial roof pacing close to BB aims to capture preferentially aligned myocardial cells in a region characterized by parallel aggregation of its cardiomyocytes, favoring fast longitudinal conduction toward the left atrium while maintaining coordinated right atrial activation through contiguous right atrial roof musculature and adjacent pathways. Importantly, this is not an insulated “tract” like the His bundle, but rather a convergence zone of aligned cardiomyocytes and interatrial connections, so success depends on anatomical targeting plus electrical confirmation, not on a single fixed landmark.10

Practical Fluoroscopic Lead Positioning: How to Implant It Reproducibly

A commonly used fluoroscopic approach for BB-region pacing (active-fixation atrial lead) targets the high superior paraseptal/RA roof region rather than the appendage. A simple, reproducible method describes the following criteria (Figure 4, Video 1)11:•Posteroanterior view: Lead tip positioned to the middle (as in RAA pacing, as opposed to a lead position at the free lateral right atrial wall) or a little superior to the usual RAA location (ie, “higher than appendage”). Of note, there is no “windscreen-wiper” movement of the lead that only moves minimally up and down during atrial contraction.•Left anterior oblique 40° view: Lead tip points posteriorly toward the spine (12-1 o’clock rather than to the roof, 2-3 o’clock rather than to the high septum) rather than RAA orientation that will point toward the sternum in a 10 to 11 o’clock position anteriorly.•Right anterior oblique 30° view: Lead tip points toward the right (approximately 1 o’clock) if positioned at the anterior roof, toward the left (approximately 10-11 o’clock) if positioned rather at the high posterior septum.

More contemporary “hands-on” descriptions align the target with the septal aspect of the inferior SCV near the RA roof/SCV-RA junction, using a combined strategy:•Fluoroscopy to reach the high paraseptal/roof region,•Electrogram guidance to identify BB area potentials when present,•Pace mapping: Pacing that yields a narrower P wave that is otherwise very similar to the patient's sinus P-wave morphology is considered supportive of true BB-region capture.

Implant “success” is better framed as a combined anatomical-electrical endpoint, because fluoroscopic landmarks alone can overlap with other high RA septal sites that do not consistently recruit interatrial conduction in the same way.

Traditionally, conventional stylet-driven leads have been used for BB pacing, however, with an implantation success of only 75%.12 More recently, a system with a non–stylet-driven lead and a 3-dimensional sheath has been introduced, which significantly facilitates implantation at BB.

Electrophysiological Implications: What Changes on the ECG and Why It Matters

From an electrophysiological perspective, BB pacing is designed to re-create a near-native sequence of biatrial depolarization, and has been associated with (Figure 5):•P-wave duration on average 50 ms shorter than in pacing from the RAA and also on average 20 ms shorter than the intrinsic P wave.12 This short P wave signifies a faster biatrial activation time, more homogeneous depolarization, and reduced dispersion of atrial refractoriness.•PR interval on average 20 ms shorter than intrinsic conduction from the sinus node,8 potentially reducing unnecessary ventricular pacing in some programming scenarios (RAA pacing is associated with a prolongation of the PR interval of 40 ms on average).12•Reduced atrial vulnerability and susceptibility to induction of atrial fibrillation in pacing from BB compared to pacing from the RAA.•Reduced interatrial conduction delay with improved interatrial synchrony compared with RAA pacing, where left atrial contraction may be delayed and dyssynchronous.Figure 5P-Wave Duration and PR Time in Pacing From the Right Atrial Appendage and Bachmann's Bundle(A to D) Patient with RAA pacing. Clinically, outcome data are mixed depending on definitions and populations, but several studies support a potential antiarrhythmic signal when BB-region pacing is accurately achieved and defined13,14:•A classic multicenter randomized experience compared BB-region pacing versus RAA pacing in patients with recurrent paroxysmal AF.15•More recent work emphasizes definition by P-wave criteria and/or local BB potentials, and reports associations with lower atrial arrhythmia burden/recurrence/de novo incidence compared with other atrial sites.16•Conversely, not all studies show superiority across endpoints (highlighting the importance of patient selection and how BB pacing is defined).
