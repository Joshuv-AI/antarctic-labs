A New Electrode Paradigm Enabled by Co‐Axial Electrospinning
Authors: Kaustubh Khedekar, Yu Pei, Paul Abi, Carlos Baez‐Cotto, Timothy Van Cleve, David A. Cullen, Kenneth C. Neyerlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA conventional electrode in a polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) often imposes competing requirements on the ionomer, which must enable efficient proton conduction while allowing sufficient oxygen transport. To address these material‐level requirements, a co‐axial electrospun electrode comprising of a pristine ionomer core and a multifunctional platinum‐carbon‐ionomer shell is introduced. By decoupling proton and oxygen transport pathways, this architecture enables independent tailoring of core and shell chemistries and their interfaces. The core–shell structure of the electrospun fibers is confirmed by microscopy, while electrochemical and transport properties are quantified using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and limiting‐current diagnostics. The co‐axial fibers in their current version meet or exceed the performance of conventional ultrasonically sprayed electrode at relative humidities above 75%, enabled by improvements in non‐Fickian diffusion processes. This work establishes co‐axial electrospun electrodes as a versatile platform for advanced PEMFC electrodes and a wide range of electrochemical applications, including CO2 electrolysis, water electrolysis, and critical mineral separations.

Introduction

Polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) represent a critical component of domestic energy reliability, providing high efficiency and fast load‐following capability, for both heavy‐duty transportation and stationary power generation applications. In heavy‐duty transportation, PEMFCs provide more than 300 miles of vehicle range without reducing freight capacity [1, 2], while in stationary power generation they provide reliable, rapid load‐following power for distributed, backup, and grid‐support applications, including conventional data centers and artificial intelligence (AI) computing infrastructure. The current state‐of‐the‐art PEMFC modules rely heavily on auxiliary systems to achieve efficient operation, constraining net power density and contributing to a higher cost. For instance, the commercially available heavy‑duty transportation PEMFC module FCmove‐XD (Ballard Power Systems) delivers ∼120 kW (∼0.33 kW L−1) at ∼60% peak efficiency [3], and the stationary FCwave module (200 kW) achieves ∼53.5% electrical efficiency at rated power with a lower power density of ∼0.10 kW L−1 due to the system‐level balance of plant (BoP) components [4]. In enabling wide‐scale adoption of PEMFCs, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)’s Multi‐Modal Fuel Cell Technologies (M2FCT) consortium has identified target metrics of 68% peak efficiency and 0.65 kW L−1 volumetric power density [2]. To meet these targets, PEMFCs must operate efficiently under more demanding operating conditions, i.e., higher temperatures, lower relative humidity (RH), and reduced pressures, thereby minimizing or eliminating auxiliary components and reducing overall stack size.

Membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) with conventional ultrasonically sprayed or Mayer rod coated catalyst layers exhibit a random electrode structure with tortuous ionomer and pore networks (Figure 1a) which impedes mass transport and reduces catalyst utilization. This increases the dependence of PEMFC performance on operating conditions, requiring bulky and costly auxiliary components. To overcome these intrinsic limitations, several advanced materials and electrode architectures have been developed, including multi‐layer electrodes [5, 6], zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF)‐templated micro/mesoporous catalysts [7], and patterned catalyst arrays [8, 9]. Although these designs have demonstrated improved activity or durability, their widespread application is hindered by complex synthesis procedures, limited scalability, and restricted control over ionomer and pore networks within the catalyst layer.

Electrospinning, a highly versatile and scalable nanofabrication technique, has emerged as a promising route to directly produce electrodes with tunable morphology and composition. In PEMFCs, electrospinning has been explored primarily through two approaches [10]: (i) to synthesize catalyst supports, where the as‐spun fibers are carbonized or calcined before integration into MEAs using conventional coating methods; and (ii) to fabricate free‐standing, electrospun fiber mat electrodes, in which the as‐spun polymer fibers themselves are proton‐ and/or electron‐conductive and can directly function as catalyst layers, either by incorporating catalyst nanoparticles in the spinning solution or by depositing them post‐spinning [11, 12]. The second approach eliminates high‐temperature treatments and multiple coating steps, thus offering a scalable pathway for manufacturing porous, high‐performance electrospun electrodes. Compared with ultrasonically sprayed electrodes, electrospinning induces high shear and rapid solvent evaporation, which can enhance catalyst dispersion and suppress ionomer agglomeration in the spun fibers [10, 13, 14]. The resulting 3D fiber network can exhibit a highly interconnected pore structure, facilitating efficient through‐plane gas diffusion and water management (Figure 1a) [10, 15]. Such characteristics are particularly advantageous under high current density operation. Moreover, the compatibility of electrospinning with roll‐to‐roll and multi‐nozzle configurations [16, 17, 18, 19], positions it as a viable technology for scalable, high‐throughput electrode fabrication.

The concept of an electrospun PEMFC electrode was first reported in 2009, featuring electrospun fibers consisting of polyethylene oxide (PEO) surrounding platinum (Pt) alloy/carbon (C)‐ionomer [20]. This electrospun electrode achieved substantially reduced mass transport losses, attributed to over tenfold higher in‐plane gas permeability, compared to a conventional electrode with identical Pt loading of 0.2 mgPt cm−2. Subsequent studies predominantly investigated uniaxial electrospun fibers. Pintauro et al. reported direct electrospinning of catalyst‐ionomer nanofibers using a single channel nozzle and mixed dispersion of Pt/C catalyst, water‐isopropanol solvent, Nafion ionomer (or its salt form), and carrier polymers such as poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) [11, 12, 21]. The electrospun Pt/C–Nafion–PAA electrode (0.1 mgPt cm−2) exhibited an electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of 114 m2 gPt −1 and a mass activity of 0.23 A mgPt −1 at 0.9 V and 80°C, approximately twice that of a comparable conventional electrode [12]. In later studies, removable carrier polymers such as poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) were used to create porous nanofiber electrodes through hot‐water extraction, yielding nanoscale porosity that was proposed to promote water retention by capillary condensation under low‐RH operation [22]. Recently, a CeO2‐modified Fe–N–C hollow fiber electrospun cathode was developed and found improved mass transport compared to uniaxial counterpart [23]. Despite these advancements, the performance remains below the DOE target of 0.44 A mgPt −1 (at 0.9 V, 150 kPa, 80°C, 100% RH, H2/O2) for transportation applications [24, 25]. Previously, our group demonstrated that, in uniaxial Pt/Vulcan–Nafion–PAA fibers, excess PAA caused ionomer migration toward the fiber exterior, rendering interior Pt sites inaccessible under low‐humidity conditions [26]. Overall, these findings emphasize the need to spatially decouple proton conduction, gas transport, and catalytic functionality within the electrospun fiber architecture.

Co‐axial electrospinning, utilizing a dual‐channel (concentric) nozzle, provides a powerful strategy to incorporate distinct materials with tailored functionalities into the core and shell of the electrospun fibers. Each fiber can consist of a catalyst–ionomer shell surrounding an ionomer‐only core. The core can be fully filled with ionomer to provide increased ionic conductivity, while the ionomer fraction within the shell can be adjusted to optimize gas and mass transport. Moreover, the core ionomer may be a distinct material or polymer chemistry specifically selected for high ionic conductivity, whereas the ionomer used in the shell can be independently tuned for high gas permeability to enable excellent mass transport.

In this work, we develop co‐axial electrospun fibers comprising a Pt/C‐Nafion containing shell and a hydrocarbon (Pemion) ionomer core as a novel PEMFC catalyst layer design. Na+‐form Nafion was used in the shell ink to enable stable electrospinning with PEO as the carrier polymer and to facilitate subsequent PEO removal during the hot‐water/protonation treatment, after which the ionomer was converted back to its protonated form [22]. The shell architecture of this novel design facilitates efficient oxygen transport and maintains electronic connectivity, while the core ensures continuous proton conduction. (Figure 1b). The core functions as a proton‐conducting backbone, while the Pt/C‐containing shell preserves electronic connectivity and oxygen accessibility. This spatial separation is central to the co‐axial design, as incorporating catalyst into the core would likely place Pt in regions with poor oxygen and electronic access, reducing rather than improving catalyst utilization. This approach decouples proton and mass transport pathways, enabling independent optimization of ionomer distribution and gas diffusion, and allowing the use of ionomers tailored for specific properties (e.g., high proton conductivity or high oxygen permeability) without requiring a single material to possess both. Although lower loadings can promote catalyst utilization, they reduce through‐plane proton‐ and oxygen‐transport limitations and can therefore mask the role of electrode architecture in governing mass transport and water management. Therefore, cathode loading of 0.25 mgPt cm−2 is used in this work to reveal architecture‐dependent transport under a practical catalyst‐layer thickness. Compared to a conventional ultrasonically sprayed electrode, the beginning‐of‐life (BoL) performance of the co‐axial electrospun electrode demonstrates advancements under high current density operation. Comprehensive electrochemical diagnostics elucidate how the co‐axial architecture influences mass transport, catalyst–ionomer interactions, and water management. These measurements provide key insights into differences in agglomerate structure, ionomer coverage, and electrode macroporosity between ultrasonically sprayed and co‐axial electrospun electrodes, and how these factors contribute to overall fuel cell performance. The limitations of the co‐axial design are also addressed, along with the broader implications of applying co‐axial electrospun electrodes to other electrochemical energy conversion and storage technologies.

Results and Discussion

Electrode Microstructure

The co‐axial electrode architecture is hypothesized to enhance overall transport efficiency and improve electrochemical catalyst utilization through two key advantages inherent to its hierarchical, multilayer design. The first advantage originates from the macroporous network formed during electrospinning (Figure 1a), while the second arises from the core–shell (co‐axial) fiber structure, which creates preferential proton‐transport pathways through a continuous, ionomer‐rich core with intrinsically higher proton conductivity, while preserving high oxygen permeability in the shell (Figure 1b). This architecture enables independent tuning of material properties in each domain, eliminating the need for any single material (e.g., ionomer) to simultaneously provide both optimal oxygen permeability and proton conduction.

To realize the co‐axial architecture, a shell solution containing catalyst, Nafion, and polyethylene oxide (PEO), and a core solution containing Pemion hydrocarbon ionomer were electrospun using a dual‐channel nozzle. The resulting fibrous electrode was subsequently hot‐pressed onto a NC700 membrane to fabricate the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) for single‐cell proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel‐cell testing. Cross‐sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to verify the formation of the hypothesized macroporous network. Figure 2a (left) shows the dense morphology characteristic of a conventional ultrasonically sprayed electrode, whereas Figure 2a (right) reveals the co‐axial electrospun structure, in which the introduced macroporosity is clearly visible (highlighted by the white dashed outline). Average catalyst‐layer thicknesses for the sprayed and co‐axial electrodes were 4.9 ± 1.4 µm and 6.2 ± 2.5 µm, respectively. The increased thickness in the co‐axial electrospun electrode is consistent with the presence of larger pore domains generated during electrospinning. All the cross‐sectional images were obtained after hot‐pressing and fuel‐cell testing of both the electrodes (Figure S5). Next, to verify formation of the intended core–shell architecture, TEM was performed on individual fiber cross‐sections collected from the as‐prepared (i.e., before hot‐pressing and fuel‐cell testing) co‐axial electrospun mat shown in Figure 2b; Figure S6. The bright‐field TEM image (left) clearly reveals a distinct shell surrounding a well‐defined core, consistent with successful co‐axial fiber formation. The dark‐field TEM image (right) confirms the spatial distribution of materials: Pt/C catalyst aggregates are detected exclusively in the shell region, while the core remains free of catalyst, validating the designed separation of oxygen‐permeable and proton‐conducting domains. Figure 2c; Figure S7 presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) plane‐view images of the as prepared (Figure 2c left) and after fuel‐cell testing (Figure 2c right) co‐axial electrospun electrode. The as‐prepared electrode exhibits extremely high porosity and an initial thickness of approximately 55 µm (measured using a micrometer gauge). While such a large thickness can impede through‐plane proton transport, even when ionomer‐rich domains are present, the subsequent hot‐pressing step reduces the thickness by nearly 90%. Despite this substantial compaction, the desired macroporosity is retained, as the interconnected fiber network preserves tortuous pore domains that do not collapse entirely under pressure. The after testing image confirms that the fibrous morphology remains intact following hot‐pressing, PEO removal, and full device‐level electrochemical operation, demonstrating the structural robustness of the co‐axial architecture and its ability to maintain the engineered macroporosity during use.

Catalyst Layer Architecture Effects on MEA Performance

Figure 3a–d (top) compares the electrochemical performance of the conventional ultrasonically sprayed electrode and the co‐axial electrode under various operating conditions, with the corresponding high‐frequency resistance (HFR) shown below each polarization curve. As current density increases, transport limitations in oxygen delivery and proton conduction typically become more pronounced. Under fully humidified conditions (100% RH, Figure 3a), the co‐axial electrode exhibits a substantial performance enhancement—approximately a 25% increase in current density at the practically relevant voltage of 0.7 V. This improvement continues to widen at higher current densities, indicating more efficient gas‐phase oxygen transport and sustained proton conduction enabled by the combined effects of macroporous pathways and ionomer‐rich proton‐conductive domains. The minimal difference in HFR between the two electrodes further suggests that the observed gains arise primarily from improved mass‐transport characteristics rather than bulk ionic resistance.

Electrochemical characterization of ultrasonic sprayed and co‐axial electrospun electrodes. Air polarization curves under different conditions. (a) At 80°C in 100% RH under 150 kPaabs. (b) At 80°C in 75% RH under 150 kPaabs. (c) At 80°C in 30% RH under 150 kPaabs.(d) At 90°C in 85% RH under 200 kPaabs (i.e., 15% O2).

To assess the robustness and humidity dependence of these improvements, the relative humidity was systematically reduced. At 75% RH (Figure 3b), the performance advantage at 0.7 V largely diminishes, with only modest gains appearing at low‐utility voltage regions. At 30% RH (Figure 3c), the co‐axial electrode begins to exhibit noticeable kinetic limitations, likely arising from reduced catalyst utilization; however, its performance partially recovers at higher current densities. This recovery can be attributed to two factors: (i) a lower HFR at low RH, suggesting the presence of a better membrane‐electrode interface from hot‐pressing, and (ii) enhanced oxygen accessibility enabled by the macroporous shell structure, which compensates for a potentially suboptimal ionomer network in the shell exposed under dry conditions.

Figure 3d presents performance at heavy‐duty automotive operating conditions (90°C in 85% RH and 15% O2 under 250 kPa pressure as specified by the M2FCT consortium). Even under such harsh conditions (characterized by elevated temperature and reduced oxygen partial pressure), the co‐axial electrode maintains the ∼25% current‐density improvement at 0.7 V, demonstrating its applicability to realistic operating environments. However, the performance decline at low RH indicates a strong dependence on water for effective catalyst utilization. Diagnostics probing local transport (e.g., ECSA, CO stripping, RH + ,eff, efficiency, and RO2) support the hypothesis that this sensitivity originates from suboptimal ionomer distribution within the shell rather than the macro‐scale co‐axial fiber architecture. As the core provides effective bulk proton conduction, these RH‐dependent losses are more likely attributable to changes in the local environment immediately surrounding the Pt sites within the shell.

Thus, to deconvolute these effects and quantify contributions from kinetic, proton‐transport, ohmic, and mass‐transport overpotentials, a series of electrochemical diagnostic measurements were performed, beginning with distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed at a current density of 1.5 A cm−2 for both ultrasonically sprayed and co‐axial electrospun electrodes to investigate the individual contributions of kinetic, ohmic, and mass transport limitations under identical operating conditions to polarization curves. The data, summarized in Table S1, provides high‐level insights into the influence of co‐axial electrospun electrode architecture on parameters such as catalyst utilization, bulk and local proton conductivity, and mass transport characteristics.

The ohmic overpotential was further deconvoluted into two distinct contributions: the high‐frequency resistance (HFR), which reflects the bulk proton conductivity of the electrolyte membrane, and the cathode catalyst layer (cCL) proton conductivity. A negative difference between overpotentials indicates inferior performance for the co‐axial electrospun electrode compared to the ultrasonically sprayed electrode, and vice versa.

Under fully humidified conditions (100% RH), the kinetic overpotentials of the two electrode types exhibited minimal differences, with a Δηkinetic of −3 mV. This suggests comparable catalyst utilization when sufficient water is present, likely due to effective proton transport facilitated by a fully saturated ionomer network. However, as humidity decreases, significant disparities emerge. At 75% RH, Δηkinetic increases to −10 mV, and under low humidity conditions (30% RH), this difference further escalates to −30 mV, indicating a disproportionately higher kinetic limitation in the co‐axial electrospun electrode. These observations strongly point to an inherently inhomogeneous ionomer distribution within the shell of the co‐axial electrospun fibers. Such inhomogeneity can severely restrict proton transport under dry conditions, limiting catalyst utilization.

The role of water in overcoming ionomer limitations is evident under fully humidified conditions (i.e., 100% RH), as water enhances proton transport and mitigates the deficiencies of the ionomer network in the shell of the co‐axial fibers. However, in dry conditions (i.e., 30% RH), the inhomogeneous ionomer distribution becomes more pronounced, leading to poor proton availability at reaction sites and significantly reduced catalyst utilization. Moreover, the overpotential difference also increases under dry conditions, with Δηi_RcCL reaching −41 mV, indicating limited proton conduction through the cathode catalyst layer. This trend exacerbates with higher cell temperatures and lower humidity (i.e., 90°C and 85% RH), further confirming the insufficient ionomer coverage within the shell of the co‐axial electrospun fibers.

The HFR overpotential (ηi_RHFR) contribution, however, consistently remains lower for the co‐axial electrospun electrode compared to the ultrasonically sprayed electrode across all operating conditions. This improvement is attributed to the hot‐pressing step during fabrication, which results in a superior membrane‐to‐electrode interface. The HFR advantage becomes particularly significant under dry and high cell temperature conditions, where Δ ηi_RHFR is observed to be +14 mV at high temperature and +44 mV under dry condition, suggesting better bulk proton transport efficiency.

Most importantly, the macroporous structure of the co‐axial electrospun electrode provides considerable benefits in reducing mass transport overpotentials (ηmass transport) across all operating conditions. The largest advantage in ηmass transport is observed under fully humidified conditions, yielding a Δηmass transport of +32 mV. The improved mass transport efficiency of the coaxial electrospun electrode compensates for the deficiencies seen in proton conduction and catalyst utilization, often resulting in overall performance surpassing that of the ultrasonically sprayed electrode.

To further examine the limitations stemming from proton conduction and the associated catalyst utilization issues, advanced electrochemical techniques such as CO coverage/stripping and oxygen‐limiting current measurements were employed. These measurements, discussed in subsequent sections, aim to refine the understanding of ionomer distribution within the shell and its impact on electrode performance under varying operating conditions. They also work to deconvolute and confirm that the limitations stem from the shell, which can be addressed through optimization, rather than being intrinsic to the co‐axial electrospun electrode structure.

Electrode Activity and Ionomer Coverage

Figure 4a presents the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) determined by CO‐stripping alongside normalized ECSA (NECSA) at various RH levels. At 100% RH, the ultrasonic sprayed electrode exhibits an ECSA of 59.7 m2 gPt −1, while the co‐axial electrospun electrode reaches 45.8 m2 gPt −1. This discrepancy is rooted in their contrasting morphologies. As shown by the cross‐sectional TEM images in Figure 2a, the electrospun electrode possesses high macro‐porosity, consistent with an open fiber network. However, increasing magnification through SEM (Figure 2c) reveals bead‐like features along the fibers that act as physical spacers between them. Such features may correspond to regions rich in PEO carrier polymer. Following reprotonation and PEO removal, these regions evolve into intra‐fiber voids that leave portions of the catalyst surface insufficiently contacted by ionomer, aligning with the lower ECSA relative to the disordered but dispersed network of the ultrasonically sprayed control electrode. Consequently, changes in ECSA and associated low‐RH limitations are primarily influenced by the shell ink formulation rather than being intrinsic to the coaxial architecture. In this system, high‐molecular‐weight PEO provides the viscoelasticity needed for fiber formation. Because Nafion is present at a lower volume fraction, rapid solvent evaporation is likely to promote micro‐phase segregation between PEO‐rich and ionomer‐rich domains. Taken together, these results show the coaxial architecture is robust, while further performance gains at low humidity may be achieved primarily through optimization of the shell ink formulation.

(b) Mass‐normalized Tafel plot in H2/O2 150 kPa, 80°C, and 100% RH. (c) Double layer capacitance (Cdl) contribution from Pt|ionomer, Pt|water, C|ionomer, C|water interfaces that determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). (d) Ionomer coverages on Pt and C.

The electrochemical performance and active surface area of both electrodes exhibit a strong dependence on relative humidity, driven by changes in ionic connectivity. At high RH, absorbed water may bridge discontinuities in the ionomer network, enabling proton transport to catalysts sites that are not in direct contact with the ionomer film (e.g., carbon support mesopores in Figure 5). As humidity decreases, the collapse of these water‐mediated pathways disrupts ionic connectivity and isolates portions of the catalyst surface, leading to the observed decline in ECSA (Figure 4a). This effect is more pronounced in the electrospun electrode, which shows a steeper loss in NECSA with decreasing RH, suggesting a heightened reliance on water‐mediated proton connectivity within and across fibers, whereas the sprayed electrode maintains more continuous ionomer and water pathways. The differences in ionomer distribution and water film connectivity are schematically illustrated in Figure 5.

To evaluate the reaction kinetics and mass activity (defined as the catalyst mass normalized current density at 0.9 V), both the ultrasonically sprayed and coaxial electrospun electrodes were tested in H2/O2 under 150 kPaabs in 100% RH at 80°C. The mass‐normalized polarization curves, measured in the anodic direction, are presented in Figure 4b. In these curves, the cell voltages have been corrected for high‐frequency resistance (HFR), and the current densities have been adjusted to account for H2 crossover. The two electrodes exhibit nearly identical mass activities and Tafel slopes: the electrospun electrode achieves 0.57 A mgPt −1 (corresponding to a specific activity of 1.25 mA cm−2 Pt) with a slope of 79.0 mV dec−1, while the sprayed electrode reaches 0.58 A mgPt −1 (0.97 mA cm−2 Pt) and 76.8 mV dec−1. The obtained specific activities fall close to the expected ±10% standard deviation range reported in the literature, indicating that the observed differences are consistent with experimental variability. The slightly higher specific activity of the co‐axial electrospun electrode, despite its lower ECSA, can be attributed to improved accessibility of Pt sites for oxygen, with its comparable mass activity and Tafel slope suggesting that the intrinsic ORR activity of the accessible Pt sites is similar to that of the ultrasonically sprayed electrode.

While the ECSA reflects the combined influence of water and ionomer on Pt accessibility, it cannot distinguish the individual contributions from specific Pt‐ionomer, Pt‐water, carbon‐ionomer, and carbon‐water interfaces. Thus, to better understand the differences in interfaces between the two electrode structures, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted under CO‐free and CO‐poisoned conditions to analyze the interfacial interactions at the Pt|ionomer, Pt|water, carbon|ionomer, and carbon|water interfaces [27]. The resulting double‐layer capacitance (Cdl) contributions from each interface are presented in Figure 4c. Compared with the ultrasonically sprayed electrode, the co‐axial electrospun electrode exhibits significantly reduced ionomer interaction with both platinum (Pt) and carbon materials. Specifically, the double‐layer capacitance associated with ionomer interaction on Pt (Cdl, Pt|ionomer) decreased by 23%, while the interaction on carbon (Cdl, carbon|ionomer) was reduced by 48%.

The corresponding ionomer coverage (θPt and θC) derived from these capacitance ratios and are summarized in Figure 4d. The lowered ionomer‐catalyst interaction in the co‐axial electrospun electrode results in significantly reduced ionomer coverage, with 9.4% on Pt and 14.0% on carbon surfaces, compared to 23.5% on Pt and 21.1% on carbon for the ultrasonically sprayed electrode. This reduction in Pt (θPt) and carbon (θC) coverage can be attributed to differences in—(i) at the agglomerate level, where single fiber cross‐sectional TEM (Figure 2b) reveals high porosity in the fiber shell, which likely contains intra‐fiber void spaces created by the removal of the PEO carrier polymer during reprotonation, leaving parts of the catalyst surface uncontacted by ionomer, (ii) at the electrode level, where electrode cross‐sectional TEM (Figure 2a) shows that the ultrasonically sprayed electrode forms a uniformly distributed and continuously connected catalyst‐ionomer network, whereas the electrospun electrode exhibits large inter‐fiber voids and a more tortuous 3D structure that restricts ionomer penetration. The hierarchical features of the co‐axial electrospun electrode result in lower overall ionomer coverage and less continuous ionomer films within the fiber network, thereby increasing its dependence on water‐mediated proton conduction. The dependence aligns with the rapid decline in NECSA (Figure 4a) and the reduced H2‐air performance (Figure 3c) observed under conditions of lower humidity. This observation is further supported by the overpotential contributions identified through DRT analysis. It is important to note that the limitations observed are not due to the intrinsic structure of the co‐axial electrospun electrode but rather pertain to the optimization of the fiber shell composition (e.g., increase in shell I/C). Further tuning of the ionic domain within the shell offers a clear pathway for improvement, which will be addressed in our future work.

Proton Mobility and O2 Transport

The ionomer distribution plays a crucial role in regulating ionic mobility within the catalyst layer. As illustrated in Figure 6a, the effective proton conduction resistance (RH+, eff) of both electrodes is highly sensitive to relative humidity. The stronger RH dependence observed for the co‐axial electrospun electrode aligns with the ECSA and NECSA findings, indicating that Pt site utilization and overall ionic conductivity are increasingly limited under dry conditions due to the reliance of proton transport on water‐mediated conduction in this structure. At low RH, the electrospun electrode exhibits substantially higher RH+, eff compared to the ultrasonic‐sprayed electrode, attributed to its lower ionomer coverage and less continuous ionomer and water network within the co‐axial fiber structure. However, with increasing humidity, the difference decreases significantly, and at 100% RH, the co‐axial electrospun electrode demonstrates slightly lower resistance (0.039 Ω cm2) compared to the sprayed electrode (0.052 Ω cm2), suggesting that sufficient hydration can establish continuous proton‐conducting pathways throughout the electrospun catalyst layer.

Proton and oxygen transport in ultrasonic sprayed and electrospun electrodes. (a) RH‐dependent cathode effective proton conduction resistance (RH+, eff) with an inserted plot for the resistances at 75% and 100% RHs. (b) Total oxygen transport resistance (RO2, total) at 75% RH.

Figure 6b shows the comparison of total oxygen transport resistance (RO2, total) at 75% RH for the two electrodes as a function of pressure. Both electrodes display a linear gas‐phase diffusion‐limited behavior, indicated by similar slopes of the two lines. This suggests that the bulk gas‐phase diffusion performance of the co‐axial electrospun electrode is comparable to that of the optimized ultrasonically sprayed benchmark. The key difference between the electrodes lies in the pressure‐independent non‐Fickian resistance (RO2, NF), which is associated with the ionomer thin‐film and mesopore‐scale (< 10 nm) gas transport, as evidenced by the intercept at zero pressure [28, 29, 30]. The co‐axial electrospun electrode demonstrates a significantly lower y‐axis intercept (0.122 s cm−1) compared to the ultrasonically sprayed electrode (0.338 s cm−1), highlighting a reduction in local oxygen transport resistance. Its lower RO2, total relative to the ultrasonically sprayed benchmark can be attributed to the combined effect of its highly macroporous 3‐D fiber mat architecture (Figure 2c), void space in the fiber shell (Figure 2b), and reduced ionomer coverage. The abundant macroporosity effectively reduces oxygen diffusion barriers, enhancing its accessibility to the Pt sites. From this perspective, the same formulation‐induced voids that hinder proton transport at low RH may simultaneously enhance oxygen accessibility by lowering local transport resistance through the ionomer film. This tradeoff highlights the architectural advantages of the coaxial fibers while distinguishing them from composition‐dependent limitations, suggesting that further optimization of the shell formulation could decouple these transport pathways and maximize performance.

Conclusions

This study pioneers co‐axial electrospun electrodes for PEMFCs, incorporating a Pt/C catalyst with Nafion ionomer into the fiber shell and a pristine ionomer core. The core–shell fiber structure offers distinct and complementary functionality by decoupling the requirements for proton conduction and oxygen transport, eliminating material co‐design requirements and providing tunability of ionomer/carbon ratios beyond those achievable with conventional fabrication methods.

Electrochemical diagnostics show that the co‐axial electrospun architecture constitutes a significant advancement toward next‐generation electrodes but will require fine‐tuning of the core and shell ink formulations to achieve performance gains under the desired operating conditions. By establishing a core–shell fiber structure, it provides independently addressable pathways for proton and oxygen transport, thereby fundamentally modifying the electrode's structural and interfacial characteristics. Despite a lower ECSA, the co‐axial electrospun electrode exhibits ORR mass activity comparable to the conventional ultrasonically sprayed benchmark, with enhanced beginning‐of‐life performance at high current densities. These results indicate that catalyst‐layer functionality can be effectively engineered through architectural design, rather than solely through incremental adjustments of ionomer chemistry or loading. Beyond this initial demonstration, further optimization of ink formulations, materials processing, deposition parameters, new material integration strategies, and ionomer content holds substantial potential to exceed the performance limitations of conventional “one‐pot” electrodes, thereby enabling the development of highly efficient PEMFC stacks suitable for heavy‐duty transportation and stationary applications.

The co‐axial electrospun electrode can also be extended to other electrochemical devices, such as CO2 electrolysis, water electrolysis, and for electrochemical separations of critical minerals. The fundamental advantages of the co‐axial electrospun architecture, including independently tunable material properties, enhanced mass transport, and improved reaction kinetics, can be leveraged in these applications to address challenges such as optimizing ion transport, gas permeability, or catalyst selectivity. By engineering core–shell chemistries to meet specific process requirements, this approach offers a versatile platform for advancing a wide range of energy and resource conversion technologies.
