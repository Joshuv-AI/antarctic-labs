Perioperative continuous vs. interrupted oral anticoagulation in TAVR: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoqin Gan, Zekun Lang, Xinyu Meng, Yurun Su, Youqi Zhu, Jing Zhao, Ming Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical outcomes associated with continuous versus interrupted oral anticoagulation strategies during the perioperative period of transcatheter aortic valve replacement, with a focus on balancing thromboembolic and bleeding risks.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Ovid and Embase up to February 2026. All studies compared continuous oral anticoagulation versus interruption of oral anticoagulation for TAVI. The primary outcomes were major bleeding, major vascular complications, and stroke. Data synthesis was performed by calculating risk ratios (RRs) or standardized mean differences (SMDs), each accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The study was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251070176).ResultsA total of five studies comprising 3,316 patients were included. Compared with interrupted anticoagulation, continued OAC was not associated with a statistically significant increase in major bleeding or major vascular complications. Continued OAC showed a borderline association with a lower risk of stroke, together with lower red blood cell transfusion and higher device success rates. However, given the limited number of studies, the predominance of retrospective evidence, and the trial sequential analysis findings, these results should be interpreted cautiously.ConclusionContinuation of OAC during TAVR appears feasible and was not associated with an increased risk of major bleeding or major vascular complications in the currently available evidence. Although continued OAC showed a possible association with lower stroke and transfusion risks, the evidence remains limited and statistically fragile. Larger randomized trials are needed before definitive clinical recommendations can be made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06116-w.Keywords: Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Oral anticoagulation, Perioperative period

Introduction

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR) has become a widely adopted treatment option for patients with severe aortic stenosis, particularly those at intermediate to high surgical risk [1]. Given the advanced age and comorbidities of this patient population, many individuals undergoing TAVR are also prescribed long-term oral anticoagulation (OAC) for complications such as atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism [2]. The perioperative management of OAC in TAVR presents a clinical dilemma, as both continuation and interruption of anticoagulation carry significant risks. Continuation of OAC during the perioperative period may reduce thromboembolic complications but is associated with an increased bleeding risk, particularly in procedures involving vascular access and potential perioperative trauma. Conversely, interruption of OAC may lower bleeding risk but elevates the likelihood of thromboembolic events, such as stroke or valve thrombosis [3, 4]. Although numerous studies have explored these strategies, the optimal perioperative anticoagulation regimen for TAVR remains uncertain. Existing meta-analyses are limited by a small number of included studies, some of which are based on abstract publications, introducing methodological opacity and substantial bias [5, 6].

Although previous meta-analyses have addressed perioperative OAC management in TAVR, the available evidence has evolved rapidly and remains methodologically heterogeneous. Earlier syntheses were limited by small study numbers, inclusion of preliminary or non–peer-reviewed data, and the lack of formal evaluation of random error. Importantly, the recently published randomized trial by van Ginkel et al. [7]. provides new high-quality evidence directly comparing continuation versus interruption of OAC during TAVI, thereby allowing a more updated and clinically relevant reassessment of this question. Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed not to claim definitive superiority over previous reviews, but to incorporate the latest peer-reviewed randomized evidence, exclude non–full-text or non–peer-reviewed reports, and apply trial sequential analysis to clarify whether the currently observed effects are sufficiently robust or remain hypothesis-generating. By doing so, we sought to provide a more cautious and evidence-based interpretation of the balance between thromboembolic protection and bleeding risk in patients undergoing TAVR.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [8]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251070176). A completed PRISMA checklist was provided as Supplementary Material.

Data source and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Ovid, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to February 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text terms related to transcatheter aortic valve replacement and oral anticoagulation. The core search concepts included “transcatheter aortic valve replacement,” “transcatheter aortic valve implantation,” “TAVR,” “TAVI,” “oral anticoagulation,” “oral anticoagulant,” “vitamin K antagonist,” “warfarin,” “direct oral anticoagulant,” “DOAC,” “continuation,” “interruption,” and “perioperative management.” The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material 1. No language restriction was applied during the initial search. Reference lists of relevant reviews and eligible studies were also manually screened to identify additional studies. Grey literature sources, including conference abstracts, trial registry records, and non–peer-reviewed reports, were screened for potential relevance; however, only full-text peer-reviewed comparative studies with extractable outcome data were included in the quantitative synthesis. Conference abstracts or non–peer-reviewed reports without sufficient methodological details or extractable data were excluded to reduce reporting bias and improve the reliability of pooled estimates. Records retrieved from all databases were imported into EndNote for duplicate removal, followed by manual verification. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, assessed full texts, and extracted data according to predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies (prospective or retrospective cohort studies) involving patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR); (2) Adult patients (≥ 18 years) who received OAC therapy, including either vitamin K antagonists or direct oral anticoagulants; (3) Studies reporting at least one relevant clinical outcome.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Non-comparative studies, case reports, case series, editorials, letters, conference abstracts without full text, or review articles; (2) Studies that did not report relevant clinical outcomes or lacked sufficient data to extract or calculate effect estimates; (3) Studies focusing solely on surgical aortic valve replacement or other cardiac procedures.

Data collection and quality assessment

Data extraction was conducted independently by two investigators and subsequently cross-checked for accuracy. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The extracted variables included the first author’s name, year of publication, total sample size, demographic characteristics of participants (including age, BMI, and gender), comorbid conditions, surgical approach, as well as primary and secondary outcome measures. For the purpose of this meta-analysis, “continued OAC” was defined as maintenance of oral anticoagulation during the perioperative period of TAVR/TAVI without planned preprocedural discontinuation, as defined in each original study. “Interrupted OAC” was defined as planned temporary discontinuation of oral anticoagulation before the procedure, with or without subsequent resumption after TAVR/TAVI. Because perioperative anticoagulation protocols varied across studies, we additionally extracted available protocol-level information, including interruption duration, INR targets for VKA-treated patients, bridging anticoagulation, intraprocedural heparin use, ACT targets, and timing of OAC resumption. These details are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

The quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed using the Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for randomized trials, as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook. This evaluation encompassed multiple domains, including methods of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and identification of other potential sources of bias. Based on the assessment of these domains, each study was classified as having a low, unclear, or high risk of bias [9]. For retrospective studies, quality assessment was performed by two independent reviewers using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). This tool evaluates methodological quality across three core domains: selection of study groups, comparability of cohorts, and ascertainment of exposure. Each domain includes specific criteria, and studies were awarded stars based on their performance, with up to two stars allocated for the comparability domain to reflect the adequacy of controlling for confounding factors.

Outcomes

Bleeding events and all other clinical endpoints were defined according to the updated criteria established by the Valve Academic Research Consortium-2 (VARC-2) [10]. The primary outcomes of this study were major bleeding, major vascular complications, and stroke. Secondary outcomes included any bleeding, all-cause mortality, length of hospital stay, and other relevant clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) and Stata SE 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Q test and the I² statistic. Considering the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in study design, anticoagulant type, perioperative interruption protocols, and vascular access routes, random-effects models were used as the primary analytical approach. Fixed-effect models were applied as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates rather than being selected solely according to the I² statistic. Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed using TSA software version 0.9.5.10 beta to evaluate the robustness of statistically significant outcomes and to adjust for potential random errors caused by sparse data and repeated significance testing. For dichotomous outcomes, the required information size was calculated using a two-sided type I error of 5% and a statistical power of 80%. The anticipated relative risk reduction was prespecified as 20%, unless otherwise indicated by the observed pooled effect and clinical plausibility. The control event rate was derived from the cumulative event rate in the interrupted OAC group for each outcome. Between-study heterogeneity was adjusted using the diversity-adjusted required information size approach. Trial sequential monitoring boundaries were constructed using the O’Brien–Fleming type alpha-spending function. Continuity correction was applied when zero-event cells were present; otherwise, no continuity correction was used. The required information size was diversity-adjusted for all TSA analyses.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,315 records were identified through database searches, including PubMed (n = 174), Web of Science (WOS, n = 279), Scopus (n = 106), The Cochrane Library (n = 294), Embase (n = 360), and Ovid (n = 102). After removing 926 duplicate records, 489 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 467 were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria. The full texts of 22 articles were assessed for eligibility. Subsequently, 8 reports were excluded for being non-peer-reviewed trials, 4 for not involving a comparison between continuous and interrupted OAC, 4 for not reporting relevant outcomes, and 1 for duplication of study cohorts. Finally, 5 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review [7, 11–14]. The literature screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

A total of five studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this analysis, comprising four retrospective cohort studies and one RCT. All included articles were peer-reviewed and focused on comparing continued OAC with anticoagulation interruption in patients undergoing TAVR. The pooled study population consisted of 3,316 patients, with a mean age of 81.2 years. Detailed baseline characteristics and general clinical information are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Definitions of continued and interrupted OAC varied across the included studies. Results of meta‑analysis

Because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across the included studies, random-effects models were used for the primary analyses. Fixed-effect models were additionally examined as sensitivity analyses, and the direction of effect was compared to assess the robustness of the findings.

As shown in Fig. 2A, the results showed that continued OAC may be beneficial in reducing the risk of major bleeding in patients (RR, 0.90), although the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, Fig. 2B indicates that the rate of major vascular complications did not differ significantly between the two groups, which suggest that continuation of OAC during the perioperative period of TAVR does not increase the risk of major vascular complications. Figure 2C shows that continued OAC was associated with a lower observed risk of stroke; however, the statistical significance was borderline and should be interpreted cautiously given the limited evidence base (RR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.41–0.97; p = 0.04; I² = 0%). In addition, continued OAC may lead to a higher risk of device success (Fig. 3A; RR, 1.08; 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.13; p = 0.001; I2 = 56%) and a lower risk of red blood cell transfusion (Fig. 3B; RR, 0.72; 95% CI: 0.60 to 0.87; p = 0.0008; I2 = 0).

Although the differences in secondary outcomes did not reach statistical significance, certain trends were observed. Continuation of OAC appeared to be associated with a potential reduction in postoperative mortality, myocardial infarction, and cerebrovascular events. Substantial heterogeneity was observed for length of hospital stay (I² = 85%) and any bleeding (I² = 72%). Because fewer than ten studies were available for these outcomes, formal meta-regression was not performed. Therefore, these findings were interpreted cautiously, and potential sources of heterogeneity were explored narratively in the Discussion. These findings suggest a possible benefit of uninterrupted OAC in select clinical endpoints, though further high-quality evidence is warranted to confirm these trends. Summary of other results of meta-analysis

Subgroup analyses according to anticoagulant type and vascular access route could not be reliably performed. Most included studies enrolled mixed populations receiving either VKAs or DOACs and did not consistently report outcome data separately by anticoagulant class. Similarly, most procedures were performed through transfemoral access, whereas some studies included mixed transfemoral and transsubclavian approaches without providing access-specific outcome estimates. Therefore, formal pooling by DOAC versus VKA or by transfemoral versus alternative access was not statistically appropriate. Similarly, assessment of publication bias using funnel plots or statistical tests (e.g., Egger’s or Begg’s test) was not conducted, in line with established methodological guidelines that discourage such analyses when the number of studies is insufficient. Sensitivity analysis is shown in Figure S7 in Supplementary Material 2, indicating that the result of stroke is robust and reliable. Under the fixed effects model, the magnitude or statistical significance of some estimates of device success changed, indicating that the research results are quite sensitive to model assumptions (Figure S8). Therefore, the interpretation of the results should be cautious.

Results of TSA

TSA for stroke demonstrated that the cumulative Z-curve crossed the conventional significance boundary but did not cross the trial sequential monitoring boundary or reach the RIS of 5,732 participants (Fig. 4A). This indicates that the current evidence may be subject to random errors, and further studies are required to confirm the observed association. For both device success (Fig. 4B) and red blood cell transfusion (Fig. 4C), TSA demonstrated that the cumulative Z-curves crossed the conventional significance boundaries and reached the required information sizes (RIS = 1,642 and 1,630, respectively). However, in both cases, the Z-curves did not cross the trial sequential monitoring boundaries. These findings indicate that although the current cumulative sample size may be adequate, the observed effects should be interpreted with caution, as the possibility of random errors and type I error cannot be fully excluded. Further high-quality randomized trials are needed to strengthen the reliability of these outcomes.

Discussion

This updated meta-analysis suggests that continuation of OAC during the perioperative period of TAVR does not appear to increase major bleeding or major vascular complications compared with OAC interruption. Signals favoring continued OAC were observed for stroke and red blood cell transfusion; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the stroke result was borderline significant and the overall evidence base remains limited. Overall, continued OAC was not associated with a statistically significant increase in major bleeding or major vascular complications. Although the pooled analysis suggested a lower risk of stroke in the continued OAC group, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the confidence interval was close to the null value and the statistical significance was borderline. In addition, most included studies were retrospective, and only one randomized trial was available. Therefore, the observed stroke reduction should be considered a potential signal rather than definitive evidence of clinical benefit. The pooling of one randomized trial with four retrospective cohort studies represents an important methodological consideration. Although combining different study designs can increase the amount of available evidence, it may also introduce clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Retrospective studies are more susceptible to selection bias, confounding by indication, and center-specific procedural protocols, whereas randomized evidence provides stronger internal validity. Therefore, the pooled estimates in the present analysis should be interpreted as an overall synthesis of currently available evidence rather than definitive causal estimates. The consistency of direction across several outcomes may suggest a potential signal, but the level of evidence remains limited. A key strength of the present analysis is the incorporation of the recent randomized trial by van Ginkel et al. [7]., which provides the highest-quality evidence currently available on this topic. Unlike earlier observational studies, this trial directly randomized patients undergoing TAVI to continuation or interruption of OAC, thereby reducing confounding by indication and center-specific treatment selection. Importantly, the trial helps contextualize previous retrospective findings by suggesting that routine continuation of OAC may be feasible in selected patients, but it does not by itself establish a clear net clinical benefit across all TAVR populations. Therefore, our pooled findings should be viewed as an updated synthesis that integrates this randomized evidence with earlier real-world data, rather than as definitive proof that continuation is superior to interruption.

Although the meta-analysis showed a statistically significant reduction in stroke risk with continued OAC, TSA revealed that the cumulative evidence remains insufficient. The Z-curve crossed the conventional boundary but did not surpass the trial sequential monitoring boundary or the calculated RIS (n = 5,732), suggesting that the result may be prone to type I error and should be interpreted with caution. TSA showed that the cumulative Z-curves for device success (RIS = 1,642) and red blood cell transfusion (RIS = 1,630) crossed both the conventional boundaries and required information sizes, but not the monitoring boundaries, suggesting that current evidence may still be subject to potential type I error. Although the conventional meta-analysis showed statistical significance, the cumulative Z-curve did not cross the trial sequential monitoring boundary and did not reach the required information size. This suggests that the apparent stroke reduction may be vulnerable to random error and should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence. Accordingly, our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

From a clinical standpoint, the management of OAC during the perioperative period of TAVR remains a delicate balance between minimizing thromboembolic risk and avoiding bleeding complications [15]. Our findings suggest that uninterrupted OAC may not significantly increase the risk of major bleeding or vascular complications, while potentially reducing the risk of stroke and red blood cell transfusion. These observations have important implications for clinical decision-making, particularly in patients with a strong indication for long-term anticoagulation, such as those with atrial fibrillation or mechanical heart valves [16]. Interrupting anticoagulation may lead to a transient hypercoagulable state, especially in elderly and high-risk TAVR populations, thereby increasing the risk of periprocedural thromboembolic events. On the other hand, maintaining anticoagulation could reduce these risks without substantially increasing bleeding, provided that appropriate procedural techniques and vascular access strategies are employed. Moreover, the observed trend toward higher device success rates in the uninterrupted group may reflect improved hemodynamic stability and procedural safety [17, 18]. These findings underscore the need for individualized anticoagulation strategies in TAVR patients, taking into account patient-specific thrombotic and bleeding risks, type of anticoagulant (VKA vs. DOAC), renal function, and procedural complexity. From a guideline-level perspective, the current evidence does not yet support a universal recommendation to continue or interrupt OAC in all patients undergoing TAVR. Contemporary antithrombotic guidance for TAVR generally emphasizes individualized assessment of thromboembolic and bleeding risks, particularly in patients with established indications for long-term anticoagulation such as atrial fibrillation. However, specific recommendations regarding perioperative continuation versus interruption of OAC remain insufficiently defined, largely because randomized evidence has been scarce. Our findings are therefore best interpreted as supporting the feasibility of uninterrupted OAC in carefully selected patients, while reinforcing the need for individualized decision-making rather than routine adoption of a single strategy [19, 20].

The higher observed rate of device success in the continued OAC group should also be interpreted with caution. Device success is a multifactorial VARC-2 endpoint involving procedural mortality, valve positioning, prosthesis performance, residual gradients, and regurgitation. Therefore, it is difficult to attribute this finding directly to uninterrupted anticoagulation. Residual confounding, operator selection, center-level expertise, procedural complexity, and temporal trends may have contributed to this association. Thus, this result should be considered exploratory rather than evidence that continued OAC improves procedural success. Another issue that should be considered is the temporal evolution of TAVR practice during the study period. The included studies spanned several years, during which valve technology, delivery systems, vascular closure devices, cerebral embolic protection strategies, perioperative imaging, and operator experience continued to improve. These temporal changes may independently influence procedural outcomes, including stroke, bleeding, device success, and red blood cell transfusion rates. Therefore, some observed differences between continued and interrupted OAC groups may partly reflect evolving procedural practice, center-level expertise, or study-period effects rather than the anticoagulation strategy itself. This possibility is particularly relevant for procedural endpoints such as device success and transfusion, and it further supports a cautious interpretation of the pooled results. The substantial heterogeneity observed for length of hospital stay and any bleeding also warrants consideration. These outcomes are particularly susceptible to clinical and procedural variability across studies. Potential contributors include differences in vascular access route, transfemoral versus alternative access, evolving-generation valve systems, vascular closure techniques, intraprocedural heparin dosing and reversal strategies, anticoagulant class, timing of OAC interruption or resumption, post-procedural antithrombotic regimens, center experience, and baseline patient frailty. Length of hospital stay may also be influenced by local discharge policies, rehabilitation requirements, and healthcare system factors, rather than anticoagulation strategy alone. Therefore, the pooled estimates for LOS and any bleeding should be regarded as less robust and more context-dependent than outcomes with low heterogeneity. Another important statistical consideration is the use of unadjusted event data for pooling. Because the included studies differed in design and patient characteristics, adjusted estimates would theoretically provide better control for confounding, particularly in retrospective studies. However, adjusted effect estimates were not consistently reported across all studies or outcomes, and the covariates included in multivariable models varied substantially. Pooling adjusted and unadjusted estimates together could therefore introduce additional methodological inconsistency. For this reason, we used unadjusted event data for the primary pooled analysis and interpreted the results cautiously, especially for outcomes mainly derived from observational studies.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of prior meta-analyses on this topic [5, 6, 21–23]. Rather than simply increasing the number of included studies, the main contribution of the present analysis lies in incorporating newly available peer-reviewed evidence, particularly the randomized trial by van Ginkel et al., and in applying stricter eligibility criteria by excluding non–peer-reviewed or abstract-only reports. This updated evidence synthesis suggests that continued OAC is not associated with an increased risk of major bleeding or major vascular complications and may be associated with a lower risk of stroke and red blood cell transfusion. However, compared with earlier evidence, our conclusions are deliberately more cautious [24]. Although the pooled estimate for stroke favored continued OAC, TSA showed that the cumulative evidence had not reached the required information size or crossed the trial sequential monitoring boundary, indicating that this apparent benefit may still be vulnerable to random error. Therefore, the present study refines rather than overturns prior conclusions: uninterrupted OAC appears feasible and potentially beneficial in selected TAVR patients, but the current evidence remains insufficient to support a definitive practice-changing recommendation.

Limitation

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the small number of included studies limited the statistical power of the present analysis. Although the total sample size exceeded 3,000 patients, only five eligible peer-reviewed comparative studies were available, and only one was a randomized trial. Therefore, the pooled results should be regarded as an updated synthesis of the best currently available evidence rather than definitive proof of clinical superiority. The limited number of studies also raises the possibility of small-study effects and precluded formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots or statistical tests. Nevertheless, potential publication bias cannot be excluded, as negative or neutral studies, particularly small observational cohorts, may remain unpublished or selectively reported. Second, most included studies were retrospective cohort studies, which may introduce selection bias, confounding by indication, and residual confounding. Patients selected for continued OAC may have differed systematically from those in whom OAC was interrupted in terms of baseline thromboembolic risk, bleeding risk, renal function, frailty, comorbidities, vascular anatomy, procedural complexity, and physician or institutional preference. Although some original studies may have performed statistical adjustment, adjusted estimates were not consistently available across all outcomes, and the covariates included in the adjusted models varied among studies. Therefore, the primary pooled estimates were mainly based on unadjusted event data and should not be interpreted as definitive causal evidence. Third, perioperative management strategies varied across studies, which may have influenced the observed outcomes. These differences may include the timing of OAC interruption and resumption, anticoagulation intensity, INR variability in VKA-treated patients, bridging anticoagulation, intraprocedural heparin dosing, reversal strategies, perioperative antiplatelet therapy, vascular closure techniques, transfusion thresholds, and post-procedural antithrombotic regimens. Operator experience and center volume may also have affected procedural outcomes, particularly bleeding, vascular complications, device success, and transfusion requirements. Such variations limit the interpretability of the pooled estimates. Fourth, subgroup analyses according to anticoagulant type, interruption duration, vascular access route, or baseline thromboembolic and bleeding risk could not be reliably performed. Most included studies enrolled mixed VKA and DOAC populations and did not provide outcome data separately for each anticoagulant class. Similarly, although most included patients underwent transfemoral TAVR, some studies included mixed access routes without reporting access-specific outcomes. In addition, endpoint definitions and adjudication processes may have differed across studies, further limiting comparability. Because anticoagulant class, vascular access route, and endpoint assessment are clinically relevant determinants of bleeding and thromboembolic risk, the absence of subgroup-specific and consistently adjudicated data limits the generalizability of our findings. Finally, although continued OAC was associated with lower observed risks of stroke and red blood cell transfusion in the conventional meta-analysis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The stroke result was borderline significant, and trial sequential analysis indicated that the cumulative evidence did not reach the required information size or cross the trial sequential monitoring boundary. Therefore, the current findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Conclusion

In this updated meta-analysis of five peer-reviewed comparative studies, including the recent randomized trial by van Ginkel et al., continuation of OAC during TAVR was not associated with an increased risk of major bleeding or major vascular complications and was associated with lower risks of stroke and red blood cell transfusion. Nevertheless, TSA indicated that the evidence for stroke reduction remains statistically fragile. These findings support the feasibility of uninterrupted OAC in selected patients but should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating until confirmed by larger, adequately powered randomized trials. The generalizability of our findings is also limited because the pooled population mainly consisted of elderly patients undergoing predominantly transfemoral TAVR, with OAC indications largely related to atrial fibrillation. Therefore, these results may not be directly applicable to younger valve patients, valve-in-valve TAVR, bicuspid anatomy, severe chronic kidney disease, non-transfemoral access, or mechanical valve populations.
