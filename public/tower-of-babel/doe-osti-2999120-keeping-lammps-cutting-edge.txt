ABSTRACT
Since its inception 30 years ago, LAMMPS has grown to be a world-class molecular dynamics code
and a cornerstone of computational materials science research. This project aimed to keep LAMMPS
at the forefront of molecular dynamics simulations by adapting LAMMPS to the latest developments
in machine learning technology and hardware. Initially, the project set out to provide a unified
implementation of active learning for efficient training data generation in LAMMPS, but the research
trajectory pivoted to address more immediate and impactful opportunities. On the hardware side,
recent record-breaking molecular dynamics simulations were developed on the Cerebras wafer-scale
AI chip, and this project has developed an interface between LAMMPS and the hardware-specific
molecular dynamics code to accelerate and simplify development and user adoption. On the software
side, PyTorch’s Ahead-of-Time (AOT) compilation features promised increased performance for
state-of-the-art equivariant neural network potentials, and this project laid the groundwork for their
adoption in LAMMPS, resulting in a nearly 20x acceleration in extreme cases. Combined with a
comprehensive benchmark study of LAMMPS across all current exascale systems, this project has
reinforced LAMMPS’s role as a versatile, high-performance tool for current and future materials
science applications. For a better and more detailed description of the different subprojects, the
reader is directed to references [2], [1] and [4].

Molecular dynamics simulations are a powerful, but conceptually simple, method for modeling
materials. In its simplest implementation and interpretation, it is the direct simulation of the
Newtonian motion of atoms by calculating the force on each atom at each timestep and performing
time integration. These forces can be computed with a variety of methods, as shown schematically
in figure 1-1, ranging from accurate-but-slow quantum mechanical calculations to inaccurate-but-
fast classical (or empirical) models, often referred to as “potentials”. Over the past two decades,
machine learning methods have caused a revolution in molecular dynamics, where machine learned
potentials now offer an appealing combination of the accuracy of quantum mechanics and the speed
of empirical models.

For the past 30 years, developers at Sandia and around the world have kept the LAMMPS code
at the forefront of computational materials science by adapting to changes in molecular dynam-
ics methodology and computing hardware. LAMMPS is an extremely flexible code, capable of
simulating matter from the atomistic to the continuum scale. This project has contributed to the
continued use and evolution of LAMMPS by adapting to new software (chapter 3) and hardware
(chapter 2) opportunities. Additionally, a new benchmark study (chapter 4) across all current exascale
computing systems demonstrates the performance, portability and scalability of LAMMPS.

2. SUPPORTING NEW HARDWARE

From its inception in the 1960s to the mid-2000s, molecular dynamics made strides in available
time and length scales due to computers becoming bigger and faster. Since the mid-2000s, however,
computers stopped getting faster; they only grew bigger. As a result, the time scales available in
molecular dynamics simulations stagnated, with the only exception being the special-purpose Anton
machine [3], which was limited to empirical bio-oriented potentials. Then came the Cerebras wafer,
the world’s largest AI chip, which, in 2024, was repurposed by a team of Sandians and Cerebras
developers to run molecular dynamics simulations at a record speed [2] of over one million timesteps
per second, representing a major leap in the timescales available on general-purpose hardware.

The molecular dynamics code for the Cerebras chip was, however, implemented in proprietary
languages due to the drastically different hardware capabilities that require targeted constructs to
extract optimal performance. As a result, the code was entirely decoupled from LAMMPS, and
required a separate and new workflow for setup, execution and analysis of molecular dynamics
simulations.

In this project, I have implemented an interface between LAMMPS and the Cerebras code based on
the LAMMPS-Python interface. The LAMMPS-Cerebras interface offloads the force calculation
and time integration for a large number of steps to the Cerebras code, but the setup and analysis is
done entirely within the familiar environment of LAMMPS. Surrounding each simulation segment
on the wafer, the LAMMPS-Cerebras interface translates the datastructures within LAMMPS to the
format required by the wafer, performs the necessary steps to launch the calculation, and reads the
results for analysis in LAMMPS.

This has several advantages, notably

 • Accelerated user adoption: Materials scientists already familiar with setup and analysis
 in LAMMPS can take their workflow to the wafer with only minor modifications to re-
 place LAMMPS’s force calculations and time integrations with those available through the
 LAMMPS-Cerebras interface.

 • Unified workflow: Pre- and post-processing can be performed within one single LAMMPS
 script, using LAMMPS’s full programmable flexibility.

 • Accelerated and enhanced development: By placing the Cerebras molecular dynamics code
 within the LAMMPS universe, developers of the Cerebras molecular dynamics code can
 focus entirely on the force calculations and time integration without having to reinvent and
 inevitably debug wheels for setup and analysis.

With the molecular dynamics revolution through machine learned potentials, LAMMPS has managed
to stay at the forefront of materials science by being sufficiently flexible and modular to incorporate
these drastically different methods. This challenge has grown over the past few years, with equivariant
neural networks providing a significant leap in accuracy for machine learned potentials. These
models are more complex than previous machine learned potentials such as SNAP, ACE and FLARE,
which were incorporated into LAMMPS through a C++ implementation. New models, however, are
typically implemented in deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch, which allow for more rapid
exploration and implementation of new architectures. For a traditional high-performance computing
code such as LAMMPS, this provides a challenge.

Figure 3-1 demonstrates a current solution for supporting Python-based models (Allegro being the
example in the figure) in LAMMPS, where the Python model is converted into some sort of black
box that can be called from C++ and gives forces and energy in return for atomic positions and
a neighbor list. Previously, the only such type of black box was a TorchScript model. Recently,
however, PyTorch has released functionality for ahead-of-time compiled models that can be called
from C++. By doing a holistic optimization of the model and fusing kernels where possible, such
models can provide significant acceleration of equivariant neural networks, with the acceleration
factor being larger for larger models such as the state-of-the-art “foundation” models. This project
laid the groundwork for the adaptation of LAMMPS to such compiled models, with an example
performance result from [4] shown in figure figure 3-2.

4. DEMONSTRATING PERFORMANCE PORTABILITY

While Cerebras wafers provide an exciting opportunity for future molecular dynamics simulations,
current exascale machines are based on GPUs from different vendors, which themselves have
provided a significant leap in performance over the past 15 years. With their massive parallelism,
GPUs have proven ideal for molecular dynamics with its large number of mostly independent force
calculations. Through the KOKKOS package, using the Kokkos library, LAMMPS is ideally suited
to face the heterogeneous exascale computing landscape, which was demonstrated in an extreme
scaling study partially supported by this project.

5. CONCLUSION

In summary, this project has cemented LAMMPS’s role as the leading molecular dynamics code
for materials science through the adaptation to modern hardware and machine learning technology.
Supported by this project, LAMMPS is now at the centerpoint of the molecular dynamics workflow
for the novel Cerebras wafer hardware, despite the non-portable Cerebras molecular dynamics code,
accelerating adoption and simplifying development of molecular dynamics workflows for the new
hardware. Furthermore, LAMMPS continues to be at the cutting edge of machine learned potential
integration, now supporting the latest and greatest of PyTorch compilation technology that provides
an acceleration of up to 18x for state-of-the-art equivariant neural network potentials. Finally, the
excellent performance portability and scalability of LAMMPS has been demonstrated on all current
exascale machines, providing hard evidence that LAMMPS is an excellent choice no matter the
computing cluster.
