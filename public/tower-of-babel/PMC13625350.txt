Handgrip strength and physical activity intensity levels in Emirati female college students
Authors: Arto Gråstén, Balazs Gabor, Gehan Handouk, Jamal Alnuaimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite widespread physical inactivity and rising health risks in young Emirati women, handgrip strength (HGS), a critical indicator of health, remains understudied. Barriers such as extreme heat, cultural norms, gender roles, and family expectations may limit physical activity (PA) in this population. This study investigated the associations between HGS and accelerometer- and self-report-based PA intensities among Emirati female college students, and how their HGS and PA levels compare with international standards.MethodsThis mixed-methods study involved 291 Emirati female college students aged 18 to 26 years (M = 20.49 ± 1.83). Assessments included cross-sectional baseline (T0) and follow-up measures (T1). HGS was assessed using a test-rest protocol, and accelerometer data were used to measure PA intensity at T0. Device wear time, muscle mass, and hand size were included as covariates in the models. Self-reported PA scores were collected at T0 and T1, eight weeks apart.ResultsStandardized results indicated that device-based sedentary time and light PA were significantly associated with HGS. However, the effect sizes were relatively small (β = −0.15 and β = 0.15, respectively; p < .05). Neither device-based moderate- nor vigorous-intensity PA, nor self-reported activity at any intensity, was significantly associated with HGS. Most students did not meet PA guidelines (~ 80%), with discrepancies between device- and self-reported vigorous PA. HGS fell around the 5th–10th percentile for women aged 20–24, indicating substantially lower strength than age-matched peers worldwide.ConclusionBased on these findings, reducing sedentary time and increasing everyday light PA may potentially support muscular strength. This finding should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to other studies beyond this sample, as the adjusted accelerometer wear time was implemented to accommodate cultural modesty norms regarding traditional attire. The absence of associations between moderate- and vigorous-intensity PA and HGS likely reflects the inability of accelerometers to capture strength-related activities or low participation at these intensity levels. Moderate- and vigorous-intensity PA, as well as muscular strength, need to be improved in this population to align with international guidelines.

Introduction

The United Arab Emirates (UAE), like other Gulf states, faces a growing burden of non-communicable diseases, with overweight and obesity [1, 2], hypertension [3], and type 2 diabetes [4] increasingly prevalent among young adults. A recent study in the Northern Emirates found weak but noteworthy correlations among physical activity (PA), body mass index, and blood pressure in overweight and obese young adults, highlighting the complex and sometimes inconsistent interactions among these health-related variables [5]. Although PA is widely assumed to mitigate the risks associated with excess adiposity [1] and hypertension [6], mixed findings in this context underscore the need for more detailed investigations that incorporate multiple measures of health behaviour.

Physical inactivity is a significant public health concern globally [7], including the Middle East [8, 9]. Systematic reviews have shown that approximately 25% of young people in the Middle East meet current moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA) recommendations, with participation particularly low among females [7, 9, 10]. Sedentary behaviour has been poorly characterized due to a lack of standardized measurement approaches [9]. Cultural and environmental factors strongly shape PA behaviours in this region, including limited access to women-oriented sports facilities, a hot climate, entrenched gender norms, and competing priorities such as academic performance [10, 11]. In Qatar, for instance, university students reported that family health values and cultural expectations shaped their MVPA, with male relatives often serving as role models. In contrast, female students reported limited opportunities to be physically active [12]. These findings indicate that similar trends are likely present in the UAE, where societal and cultural norms contribute to some of the lowest female PA participation rates globally [7]. Young females tend to be less physically active than males [5, 6], limiting their opportunities to develop optimal muscle strength [13, 14].

Handgrip strength (HGS) has been recognised as a reliable indicator of overall health and functional capacity [15, 16]. Low HGS is associated with chronic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and osteoporosis, as well as reduced quality of life [16]. In older adults, low HGS has been shown to predict poor overall physical fitness [17], whereas muscle-strengthening activities may reduce the risk of all-cause mortality and chronic illnesses [18]. HGS is a simple and reliable measure of maximal voluntary muscle strength that has shown complex associations with PA influenced by factors such as sex and age [19]. Some studies indicated that stronger HGS was associated with fewer years of sport practice and lower weekly activity, suggesting that the specificity of activity may be more important than overall activity levels [20, 21]. For instance, upper-body weight training is likely more effective than long-distance running for developing grip strength, regardless of years of training. In a study of young, healthy adults [22], women with higher HGS reported greater participation in sports. The finding indicates that increased activity may enhance young women’s relative strength, particularly given their lower muscle mass compared to men. In contrast, light-intensity activity was associated with higher HGS in men, but not in women. Sedentary behaviour, which independently predicts adverse health outcomes, has also been linked to reduced HGS, though this relationship has not been extensively studied in younger [16] nor Emirati [23] populations. For example, in a study by Qadah et al. [23] on muscle strength and PA among a multiethnic sample of young adults in the UAE, Emirati females were underrepresented (3.8%), limiting the findings’ relevance to this group. Given the high prevalence of physical inactivity, especially among Emirati women [7, 8, 24] and the importance of early adulthood in establishing lifelong health behaviours, targeted research is needed.

The current literature review revealed that the association between HGS and PA is complex and varies by factors such as sex, age, and activity type [20, 21, 22, 25]. Although HGS generally responds more to MVPA [1, 2] and to training type [20, 21], it is important to consider all intensity levels. Lower-intensity activities account for a significant share of daily energy expenditure, especially in young Emirati females [8, 12], and may be associated with muscle strength and overall PA. In addition, existing evidence is largely derived from older adults [16] and Western samples [16], with minimal focus on young women in the Middle East [23], underscoring the need to clarify the relationships between HGS and PA in this context. Therefore, young Emirati women are a particularly important population in this regard. Emirati women differ from expatriate populations, including other Arab women, in sociocultural norms, lifestyle patterns, and gender-specific barriers that influence PA and health [26]. Additionally, late adolescence and early adulthood are transitional periods when PA levels commonly decline [7, 8], especially among women in conservative cultural settings where opportunities for participation may be limited and sedentary lifestyles are often normalized [10, 12]. The declines in PA are concerning because of their potential long-term consequences for musculoskeletal health, cardiometabolic risk, and overall well-being. Investigating the relationship between HGS and PA in this specific group offers a valuable opportunity to understand the health behaviours and risks of young Emirati women and to inform prevention and intervention strategies in conservative, rapidly modernizing societies. From a methodological perspective, device-based measures (e.g. accelerometers) provide direct estimates of activity intensity and may help clarify associations between HGS and PA. However, they may underestimate strength-related activities, particularly upper-body movements relevant to HGS [10]. Given the inherent limitations of every PA assessment method under free-living conditions [27], combining device-based and self-reported measures may yield more interpretable estimates of HGS and PA associations than either method alone [10, 28, 29].

Drawing on the above, this study aimed to examine the associations between HGS, sedentary time, and light-, moderate-, and vigorous-intensity PA in Emirati female college students using both accelerometer-based and self-reported measures (Fig. 1). Specifically, the study examined the HGS–PA relationship after adjusting for device wear time, hand size, and muscle mass. It also examined how their HGS and MVPA levels compare to established international standards. Guided by existing evidence, it was hypothesized that PA at any intensity, not just MVPA, would be positively associated with HGS [22, 25], whereas sedentary time would be inversely associated with HGS [16]. It was further hypothesized that associations between PA intensity and HGS would vary by assessment method, reflecting known discrepancies between accelerometer-based and self-reported measures [28, 29].

Methods

Participants

The participants were 291 female Emirati college students. They were contacted directly by email to participate in the study. They were informed about the study protocols in the classroom and given the opportunity to ask questions before participating. The students who accepted the invitation received an email containing detailed instructions. The participating students were Emirati nationals. Inclusion criteria were: (1) female students, (2) Emirati nationality, and (3) current enrolment at the college. No explicit exclusion criteria were applied during the recruitment stage. All eligible students were invited to participate. However, no students with documented disabilities participated in the study. This indicates that the respondents were relatively homogeneous rather than the result of intentional exclusion, since participation was open to all eligible students. Procedure

The lab data were collected using identical measurement protocols in the laboratory setting during February-March 2023 and 2024. The samples were comparable, and any potential cohort or temporal effects are likely minimal. The students were randomly divided into six groups, and lab measurements were taken for one group at a time. One researcher and one to two female research assistants completed the measurements for each group. The students were asked to sign a written consent form for their participation. Background information, including sex, date of birth, year of study, study program, and self-reported PA, was collected via an online form. The students were emailed a link to the background information sheet. Device-based PA data were collected over four consecutive days, including Friday, a holy day of worship, in both on-campus and off-campus settings. Participants were informed that devices could be worn beneath the traditional abaya. However, many students found continuous use incompatible with traditional attire and national modesty norms because visible or invisible belts are not worn with traditional Emirati clothing. Following student feedback during the instruction session, continuous wear of waist-worn accelerometers was not enforced due to cultural considerations. As a result, participants primarily wore the devices during activity periods, typically when dressed in sports clothing rather than traditional attire. This approach was necessary to ensure participant compliance. Furthermore, it reflected a culturally responsive adaptation rather than participant noncompliance, underscoring the importance of prioritizing acceptability in study protocols. The ethics committee of the home university of the research team approved the study protocol.

Measures

Handgrip strength

HGS was assessed using a Lafayette 78010 hand dynamometer (Lafayette, IN, USA). The testing range for a dual-scale is 0 to 100 kg. The students were measured in the exercise physiology laboratory. Before measurements, the dynamometer accuracy was pre-tested using a 4kg calibration weight. Participants were asked to sit in a chair with both feet firmly on the ground and to place the hand to be tested at their side, with the elbow bent at 90 degrees. Then, the participant was asked to squeeze the dynamometer as hard as possible for three seconds without moving the arm. The dynamometer could not touch any body part or object during the test. The right hand was measured first, and then the left (test). After 5 minutes, the same protocol was repeated for both the right and left hands (retest). In the data, the average of four attempts (right/left test and retest) scores (kg) was included in the analysis. Averaged scores were used because they provide a more representative estimate of overall HGS. This protocol captured performance across both dominant and non-dominant hands, rather than relying on a single-hand measurement, which may overestimate or underestimate true strength. The grip strength norms (kg) for combined (right and left) scores were applied [30].

Device-based PA

Device-based PA intensity levels, including sedentary, light, moderate, and vigorous, were monitored using ActiGraph GT3X+ accelerometers worn on the waist and secured with a belt (Pensacola, FL, USA). Device-based measurement was taken at a single time point, as the UAE’s consistently hot climate offers similar opportunities for exercise year-round. In addition, the data were collected over eight weeks, making substantial changes in PA behaviour unlikely. Students were advised to wear the devices over four consecutive days, two weekdays and two weekends. They were instructed to wear the monitors during their waking hours (6 am to 10 pm), excluding swimming and other water activities. In addition, they were advised to wear the devices above or below their clothing, as they did not require skin contact. As detailed in the previous section, most students wore the devices primarily during active periods for cultural reasons. Participants with valid data (an average of 360 min per day) for at least two weekdays and one weekend day were included. Accelerometer wear time (%) accounted for an average of 37% of their waking hours. Reported active wear time, ranging from 3% to 82%, should not be interpreted as compliance with continuous monitoring protocols. Instead, the values reflect the proportion of time participants spent in light to vigorous PA. Periods outside the defined wear time were classified as sedentary behaviour, consistent with activity patterns in prior studies of young Middle Eastern samples [8, 12]. While not optimal, episodic wear represented a culturally appropriate method for device-based data collection [24]. Comparisons with other samples using different methods should be made with caution. Weekly intensity estimates were derived from valid three- to four-day records. The devices were set to store data in raw mode, with a sampling frequency of 30 Hz and a 60-second epoch, i.e., 1800 accumulated samples per enabled axis per 60-second epoch. The cut points applied to adults 18 and older by Troiano et al. [31] were used to calculate the sedentary time (0–99 cpm), light (100–2019 cpm), moderate (2020–5998 cpm), and vigorous (5999- cpm). Active time and sedentary time observed in this study were consistent with patterns reported in previous PA research among Emirati college students [8, 12], supporting the validity and comparability of the present scores.

Self-reported PA

Self-reported PA intensities were assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (IPAQ-SF), the last 7-day self-administered version. The IPAQ-SF is a standardized instrument designed to assess health-related PA in adults aged 15–69 years. It consists of seven items that measure the frequency and duration of vigorous-intensity activity, moderate-intensity activity, and walking, as well as time spent sitting on a typical weekday. Participants reported activities performed for at least 10 min at a time during the previous seven days. Because self-reports based on recall may be less reliable than objective data, baseline and follow-up measurements were implemented eight weeks apart. The IPAQ-SF has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in Middle Eastern populations [32].

Body composition

Body composition, including estimated percent body fat, fat mass, muscle mass, bone mass, and body mass index (BMI), was measured using a Tanita BC-420 MA body composition analyser (Arlington Heights, IL, USA). The equipment estimates scores using bioelectrical impedance analysis. The standard body type was selected for measurement. During the measurement, the students wore traditional abayas and were barefoot, and a 1 kg weight of cloth was used. Before stepping onto the equipment, their height (cm) was measured using the Seca 713 height-measuring scale. The students were asked to avoid excessive exercise for 12 h before measurement, excessive eating and drinking on the day before measurement, and any eating or drinking within three hours before measurement.

Hand size

Hand size was measured as the length and width of the palm of the right hand [33]. A hand-shaped figure with a scale was placed on the table. The students were instructed to put their hands on the figure, and the trained research assistant verified the measurements. Hand length (cm) was the distance from the distal wrist crease’s midpoint to the middle finger’s most distal point. Hand width (cm) was measured from the metacarpal’s lateral surface to the metacarpal’s medial surface. In the data, hand size scores were calculated as length x width (cm2).

Data analysis

First, the normal distribution, outliers, missing values, means, and standard deviations were examined. To answer research questions, path models were tested to examine associations between latent HGS (i.e., test and retest) and weekly sedentary, light, moderate, and vigorous-intensity PA. To avoid confounding effects across activity intensities, separate models were used. Hand size and muscle mass were included as covariates of HGS in the models. To account for variability in monitoring duration, device wear time was included as a covariate of device-based PA intensity.

The model fit was analysed using the chi-square test (χ2), which assesses the model’s overall goodness-of-fit to the data. A non-significant p-value between the observed and theoretical distribution was considered acceptable. In addition, the model’s appropriateness was assessed using the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), the comparative fit index (CFI), and the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). Cutoff values of approximately 0.08 for SRMR and 0.06 for RMSEA, and 0.95 for CFI and TLI, indicated an acceptable fit between the hypothesized model and the observed data [34]. The preliminary data analysis was performed using SPSS 31.0, and the path models using Mplus 8.11.

Results

Preliminary analysis

A graphical display indicated that the data were normally distributed, and no significant outliers attributable to incorrect coding were observed. The data matrix included 6.7% missing values (313 out of 4656) due to incomplete data from some students. The Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test (χ2(392) = 630.30, p < .001) showed that the data matrices with and without missing scores were unequal. A closer examination showed that matrices were equal (p > .05) when device-based PA scores were excluded, suggesting a MCAR condition. Accelerometer score missingness was tested using a logistic regression, with missing values coded as one and non-missing values as zero. A statistically significant difference (p < .001) between missing and non-missing values indicated missing at random (MAR) conditions for the device-based PA data. In the data matrix, the missing PA scores were not imputed or manipulated. The full-information maximum likelihood estimation method has been shown to produce unbiased parameter estimates and standard errors under two conditions: MCAR and MAR. Accordingly, the missing values were retained, yielding unbiased estimates.

Descriptive statistics

Sample sizes, minimum and maximum scores, means, standard deviations (Table 1), and Pearson’s correlation coefficients (Table 2) for the study variables were examined. The correlations between study variables ranged from weak to strong. The strongest positive correlation was observed between the HGS test and retest, while the strongest negative correlation was observed between device-based sedentary time and light-intensity PA. Self-reported sedentary time declined significantly from T0 to T1 (p < .001), while other PA intensities remained stable.

Meeting international MVPA and HGS standards

The proportion of students not meeting the current global PA guidelines (moderate ≥ 150 min/week; vigorous ≥ 75 min/week), based on device-measured minutes, was high: 87.7% accumulated < 150 min of moderate-intensity PA per week, and no students met the vigorous activity recommendation of a least 75 min. Self-reported PA aligned with device-based estimates in classifying students not meeting moderate PA guidelines: 81.1% of participants reported < 150 min of moderate-intensity PA per week at T0 and 80.0% at T1. In turn, the proportion of students meeting the vigorous activity recommendation of at least 75 min was 70.4% at T0 and 66.7% at T1. Compared with international standards [19], the sample’s HGS was approximately in the 5th–10th percentile for women aged 20–24, indicating markedly lower strength than age-matched peers worldwide.

Associations between HGS and PA intensity levels

The appropriateness of the theorized models (Table 3) was tested, including HGS and PA intensity levels, and the covariates of wear time, muscle mass, and hand size. When modelled simultaneously, the model demonstrated poor fit, likely due to substantial shared variance among the PA intensity variables. As a result, the strong intercorrelations among intensity variables, especially between sedentary and light PA (r = − .99, p < .001), made it difficult to disentangle their independent effects. When the model was tested without sedentary time, light to vigorous PA produced similar estimates whether included together or separately. The variables were estimated separately to allow sedentary time to be included in the analysis. Including wear time in the models was necessary to ensure measurement validity by controlling for variability in device adherence and reducing potential bias from differential data completeness. This protocol was recently reported [24], supporting its methodological robustness. The associations between HGS and PA intensities were tested in all directions. Models with bivariate HGS↔PA and opposite-direction HGS→PA paths demonstrated weaker model fit indices than the retained PA→HGS model. Standardized results showed that device-based sedentary time and light PA were both significantly associated with HGS, with small effect sizes. Neither moderate nor vigorous device-based PA was significantly related to HGS. Wear time was a significant covariate, with stronger effects on sedentary and light PA, thus, results should be interpreted cautiously beyond this sample. Muscle mass and hand size were significantly correlated with HGS. Self-reported associations with HGS were not found at either time point. Squared multiple correlations indicated that the device-based PA intensity models (1–4) accounted for 21–24% of the variation in HGS, and the self-reported models (5–8) accounted for 17–19%.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between HGS, sedentary time, and light-to-vigorous intensity PA in Emirati female students and compared their HGS and PA levels with international standards. The findings showed that greater sedentary time was associated with lower HGS, whereas more minutes of light PA were related to higher HGS scores. However, the effect sizes were small, indicating only a modest association beyond other contributing factors. Neither device-based moderate- nor vigorous-intensity PA, nor self-reported activity at any intensity, was significantly associated with HGS. Most students (~ 80%) did not meet PA guidelines, while discrepancies were observed between device- and self-reported vigorous PA. HGS was in the 5th–10th percentile for women aged 20–24, indicating markedly lower strength than age-matched peers worldwide.

First, when interpreting these findings, it must be recognized that accepting less than 60% accelerometer wear time in this study was a necessary, culturally responsive adaptation rather than a methodological limitation. Although Mattews et al. [35] recommend at least 10 h of daily wear, this recommendation assumes continuous wear is feasible. In this sample, traditional Emirati clothing and modesty norms made waist-worn devices impractical, so participants wore them primarily during activity periods. Enforcing standard protocols would likely have reduced compliance and increased bias due to data loss. Thus, lower wear time reflects contextual constraints rather than participant noncompliance. However, this approach affects interpretation. Because devices were worn primarily during active periods, estimates of MVPA are likely to be reasonably accurate, whereas estimates of sedentary time and light physical activity may be less precise. Non-wear time was classified as sedentary, potentially inflating sedentary estimates. Because this measurement approach was consistent across all participants, the internal validity of the associations is preserved, and the findings can be considered valid for this sample. Nevertheless, comparisons with studies using standardised wear-time criteria or alternative measurement approaches should be made cautiously.

The findings showed that greater HGS was associated with lower sedentary time and higher levels of light-intensity PA, underscoring the importance of habitual PA among Emirati female college students. While these associations reached statistical significance, effect sizes were small, indicating limited practical impact and suggesting that sedentary and light PA explain only a small proportion of the variance in HGS. This may reflect that accelerometers do not directly capture similar muscle-strengthening activities, as assessed by handgrip dynamometry. However, there is some prior evidence that, regardless of intensity level, muscular strength may be associated with total daily activity among women [22, 25]. Despite methodological constraints, reducing sedentary time and increasing light-intensity activity remain beneficial strategies for this population, particularly when sociocultural and environmental factors may limit engagement in higher-intensity exercise [10, 12].

Unlike MVPA, light-intensity activities are typically performed throughout the day and may help break up prolonged periods of sedentary time, supporting at least some level of muscle engagement [17, 25]. Increasing light PA may also help reduce overall sedentary time. These light-intensity, muscle strength-related activities may include daily routine tasks such as carrying a school bag or laptop, walking across campus, and using stairs within university buildings. Activities that involve carrying or gripping objects may be particularly relevant because they engage the hand and forearm muscles assessed by handgrip dynamometry. This finding is especially important for populations with low levels of MVPA, as light activity may be the primary form of muscle engagement. Experimental evidence indicates that even modest movement can mitigate strength decline by preserving motor unit recruitment and preventing disuse-related adaptations [16, 22]. In practical terms, encouraging increases in light-intensity activities that involve active muscle loading, such as stair climbing, carrying loads, household tasks, or brief body-weight or resistance exercises, may support muscular strength among Emirati female university students without requiring substantial behavioural or environmental change.

In this cohort of Emirati female college students, the lack of an association between HGS and moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity was likely due to the PA measurements used in the current study. Accelerometers may not fully capture muscle-strength-related activities, particularly those involving upper-body loading that generate limited acceleration signals. The activities performed during measurements were likely predominantly gait-based, with minimal upper-body involvement. In addition, the very low levels of device-measured higher-intensity activity may be associated with the absence of correlations. Most participants accumulated less than 150 min of moderate activity, and none met device-measured vigorous activity guidelines, limiting the physiological stimulus required for grip strength, which typically responds to moderate to vigorous and muscle-loading activity [1, 2]. This is consistent with the sample’s low HGS scores, suggesting insufficient exposure to strength-relevant activity rather than a lack of relationship.

The absence of an association between HGS and self-reported PA likely reflects recall bias, misclassification of intensity, and poor capture of muscle-strengthening activities [28, 29], which are most relevant to grip strength, particularly in young women. Similarly, device-measured higher PA intensities were not associated with HGS, likely because accelerometers may not capture the movements required to assess HGS. The absence of a significant association between device-based MVPA and HGS may also reflect the limited time students spent at these higher intensity levels, resulting in insufficient stimulus to influence muscular strength. This is consistent with evidence from Middle Eastern female populations showing low adherence to MVPA guidelines and highly limited participation in structured or vigorous activities [7, 9, 10, 24]. When exposure to MVPA is minimal, its contribution to strength outcomes may be difficult to detect, especially in cross-sectional analyses. Moreover, MVPA accumulated through aerobic activities may not provide the specific overload needed to elicit gains in grip strength, which is more sensitive to resistance-based or repetitive upper-limb loading [20, 21].

Approximately 80% of students did not meet current physical activity guidelines (moderate ≥ 150 min/week; vigorous ≥ 75 min/week), with some discrepancies observed between device-based and self-reported measures, particularly for vigorous activity. This suggests that many students may overestimate their levels of vigorous activity, underscoring the need for greater awareness and targeted strategies to promote regular, achievable physical activity in daily routines. Practical strategies to increase MVPA among Emirati female students may include (a) culturally appropriate, low-cost, women-only exercise opportunities, (b) structured on-campus activity sessions integrated into academic schedules, (c) group-based walking or fitness programs with support from friends and family, and (d) home-based or social media-guided exercise options. These actions may also help address commonly reported barriers, including a lack of time, social support, and cultural constraints [36].

The sample’s HGS is in the 5th–10th percentile, meaning it is much lower than that of most women their age worldwide. Practically, this indicates reduced muscle strength and a need to improve it through targeted physical activity, such as resistance training (e.g., handgrip exercises, weight training 2–3 times per week), daily functional tasks (e.g., carrying groceries), and increased overall physical activity [37].

This study included accelerometer-derived and self-report measures of PA intensity, a standardized laboratory-based assessment of HGS with a test-retest protocol, and relevant covariates of device-wear time, muscle mass, and hand size. However, limitations should be acknowledged. The study design limits causal inference because accelerometer data were collected at a single time point, and self-reported PA follow-ups were collected in free-living settings rather than under intervention or experimental conditions. Another limitation is that cultural modesty norms reduced the acceptability of continuous monitoring using belt-worn devices. The adjusted accelerometer wear time was implemented to accommodate norms for traditional attire. Although this approach was not optimal, it was necessary to ensure cultural appropriateness, which cannot be compromised in the study design. Based on this, the findings should be interpreted cautiously when generalizing to other studies beyond this sample. In addition, accelerometers may underestimate activities such as resistance training or upper-limb loading. This was addressed by incorporating additional self-reports. Regardless of the measurement method used, assessments of PA in free-living conditions are inherently estimates rather than absolute measures of behaviour. Other potential limitations included culturally required adaptations (e.g. wearing an abaya during body composition assessment) and the use of right-hand size measurements as a covariate in two-hand HGS, which may introduce minor measurement inaccuracy. Finally, the sample was limited to Emirati female university students, limiting generalizability to non-students or male samples. Despite these limitations, the study provides context-specific evidence on the relationships between PA intensity and muscular strength in a population that remains underrepresented in the literature.

Practical implications

These findings indicate that reducing sedentary time and promoting regular daily activity may be the most practical and culturally appropriate strategies to support more active lifestyles in this group, while underscoring the importance of incorporating accessible forms of strength training (e.g., bodyweight or home-based exercises) to directly target muscular development. At the same time, it is important to gradually encourage greater participation in moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities. However, implementing and evaluating these strategies requires careful consideration of local contexts, as cultural norms can hinder standard data-collection methods, such as continuous accelerometer use. This underscores the need for adaptable, culturally aware measurement tools and cautious analysis of activity data, while emphasizing the importance of designing activity and strength-focused interventions that align with, rather than conflict with, participants’ social and cultural environments.

Future studies should consider culturally adapted interventions to reduce sedentary time and promote daily movement, such as step-count goals, short walking breaks, or home-based activity routines. They should also test simple strength-training programs, such as bodyweight exercises, resistance-band exercises, or handgrip exercises, to improve muscular fitness. Researchers may also use flexible measurement methods, such as activity diaries, smartphone tracking, or shorter periods of accelerometer wear, to improve participant comfort and data quality in culturally sensitive settings.

Conclusion

This study indicated that, among this sample of Emirati female university students, muscular strength is more closely linked to everyday movement patterns than to higher-intensity exercise. Higher HGS was associated with lower sedentary time and higher light-intensity PA. Because device wear time was compromised by cultural considerations, findings should be generalized cautiously beyond this sample. The absence of associations with moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity likely reflects measurement limitations, restricted variability, or a mismatch between accelerometer-derived activity and grip-strength-related loading. By focusing on an understudied population, young Emirati women, these findings underscore the importance of reducing sedentary behaviour and promoting PA through culturally appropriate strategies to support overall activity and muscular health in a conservative, rapidly developing society.
