Distinct effects of AM fungi and root pathogens drive differential plant-microbial interactions in steppe graminoids and forbs
Authors: Jiahuan Li, Zijian Ding, Jing Man, Xiaori Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant-microbial interactions (PMI), governed by both mutualistic and antagonistic soil microorganisms, are crucial for plant performance. However, empirical evidence directly linking PMI strength to the extent of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungal colonization or pathogen infection remains limited, particularly across varying biotic and abiotic contexts. Here, we quantified AM fungal colonization and root pathogen incidence across two plant functional groups (13 grassland species) under monoculture and interspecific competition in overgrazed and restored soils, and investigated their relationships with PMI. Results showed that the strength of plant-microbial interaction varied significantly among different plant species. Notably, graminoid species, such as Leymus chinensis, Agropyron cristatum, Stipa capillata and Elymus dahuricus exhibited negative PMI, whereas forbs exhibited positive PMI. Forbs had higher AM fungal colonization in overgrazed soil, and their PMI was positively correlated with colonization across all treatments. Conversely, graminoids had higher pathogen incidence, and their PMI was negatively correlated with pathogen incidence under overgrazed, monoculture conditions. Our findings demonstrate that PMI in steppe ecosystems is functionally dichotomous, being primarily mediated by AM fungi for forbs and by pathogens for graminoids, with direct implications for grassland management and restoration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02531-x.Keywords: Steppe, Ggraminoids, Forbs, Plant-microbial interaction, AM fungi, Pathogen

Introduction

In grassland ecosystems, plant community assembly is fundamentally shaped by species coexistence dynamics. The steppe of northern China is characterized by a functional dichotomy between graminoids and forbs. Graminoids (e.g., Leymus chinensis) typically dominate in biomass via rapid resource acquisition and clonal propagation, whereas perennial forbs often employ more conservative growth strategies [1]. This functional divergence facilitates niche partitioning; however, the mechanisms governing their coexistence, particularly those mediated by soil microorganisms, remain unresolved. Understanding these dynamics is critical for effective grassland restoration and management.

Soil microbial communities play crucial roles in host nutrient absorption, growth, and physiological processes [2], ultimately influencing plant coexistence and shaping plant community structure [3]. Plant-soil interaction studies have established that soil microbial communities regulate plant coexistence through a continuum of antagonistic and mutualistic relationships [4, 5]. Positive effects primarily arise from arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi symbionts, which play a key role in plant nutrition and contribute to plant drought tolerance and pathogen resistance [6–8]. This mutualistic interaction enhance the competitive ability of plant hosts [9], potentially influencing species dominance within the community. The strength of this symbiosis, measured in terms of mycorrhizal dependency and sensitivity, varies with context, including plant functional group, genotype, and soil fertility [10]. For example, forbs generally exhibit higher mycorrhizal dependency than graminoids. Han et al. (2025) also demonstrated that species within the Asteraceae family showed greater mycorrhizal colonization and hyphal length than those in Poaceae, correlating with enhanced positive plant-microbial interactions in the Asteraceae and more negative interactions in the Poaceae [11].

In addition, negative plant-microbial interactions are frequently mediated by specialized pathogens that reduce plant performance at multiple life history stages [12]. These antagonists are recognized as key drivers of species abundance [13–15] plant species coexistence [16], community diversity [17], and community succession [18]. In grassland systems, evidence suggests different species may also differ in their susceptibility. For example, Klironomos indicated that the accumulation of specific pathogens is an important driver of the rarity (low relative abundance) of plant species in the community [13]. Research by Huang et al. in alpine meadow illustrated that forbs suffered more negative plant-microbial interaction than graminoids due to their greater sensitivity to soil pathogens, while graminoids can suppress the negative effects of pathogens by thin but well-defended roots [19]. However, other research in Chinese steppe found that dominant graminoids like Leymus chinensis and Stipa capillata experience stronger negative plant-microbial interactions, whereas characteristic forbs such as Sanguisorba officinalis and Allium mongolicum experience positive plant-microbial interaction [20]. These varying findings across systems highlight the complexity and context-dependency of plant-pathogen interactions within functional groups. They also reinforce the need to move beyond describing patterns of susceptibility to identifying the concrete microbial mechanisms. However, a direct, quantitative test linking PMI strength to simultaneous measurements of both AM fungal colonization and pathogen incidence in different functional groups remains a critical gap.

Furthermore, the interactions between plants and microorganisms are significantly influenced by biotic or abiotic context [21–23]. For example, increased soil nutrient availability can shift plant-mycorrhizal interaction from mutualistic to parasitic [24] and may favor pathogens [25], thereby reducing positive microbial effects on plant growth [26]. Conversely, some evidence suggests that elevated soil fertility may mitigate negative soil microbial effects through enhanced plant defense mechanisms [22]. In natural grasslands, overgrazing often depletes soil nutrients, which may shift the cost-benefit balance of mycorrhizal symbioses and influence pathogen virulence. Simultaneously, interspecific competition can alter plant resource allocation, thereby modifying interactions with both mutualists and antagonists [27]. Dostál (2021) demonstrated that plants grown with interspecific competitors developed the most negative plant-microbial interaction effects [28]. Nevertheless, few studies have simultaneously examined how natural soil fertility gradients and interspecific competition interact to influence microbial colonization and plant performance, particularly in species-rich grassland systems. Moreover, the role of AM fungi and pathogen in this process is not fully understood.

Here, we conducted a reciprocal experiment with 4 graminoids and 9 forbs grown under contrasting conditions: live versus sterilized soils; no-competition (monoculture) versus interspecific competition (mixed culture); and soils from overgrazed versus restored sites. We quantify the plant-microbial interaction (PMI) alongside natural AM fungal colonization and pathogen damage to address the following specific questions: (1) Do graminoids and forbs in the steppe differ in their associations with AM fungi and root pathogens? (2) How do these microbial associations correlate with and potentially drive the strength of their respective PMI? (3) How are the relationships between PMI and AM fungi (or pathogens) modulated by contrasting soil substrates (overgrazed vs. restored) and interspecific competition?

Methods

Study system

We selected 13 grassland species (4 graminoids and 9 forbs) commonly found in the steppe of China, with Leymus chinensis as the dominant species. The graminoids included Leymus chinensis, Agropyron cristatum, Stipa capillata and Elymus dahuricus, while the forbs included Plantago asiatica, Sanguisorba officinalis, Saussurea japonica, Heteropappus hispidus, Erodium stephanianum, Allium mongolicum, Thermopsis lanceolata, Taraxacum mongolicum and Lepidium apetalum. Soil used in the experiment was collected from the overgrazed grassland (> 30 years) and restored grassland (enclosure > 10 years) at National Field Station of Grassland Ecosystem (Guyuan, Hebei province, China, 41°46′ N, 115°40′E) in July 2017. The site is characterized by a temperate continental steppe climate. The mean annual temperature is 1.6 °C, and the mean annual precipitation ranges from 350 to 450 mm, with approximately 80% concentrated from June to September. The native vegetation is typical steppe.

Plant-microbial interaction

All seeds were collected in the National Field Station of Grassland Ecosystem during autumn and stored under dry, room-temperature condition. Prior to germination, seeds were surface-sterilized by sequential treatment with 75% ethanol and 10% ‘84 disinfector’ (dominant sector: NaClO, available chlorine 5.5-7%). Germination was carried out on moist filter paper or in sterilized soil in lighted growth chambers (16/8 light/dark photo regime, 3000 lux‌, 26/20°C). To synchronize seedling development, seeds that germinated earlier were temporarily transferred to a 4 °C lighted chamber to slow their growth. Seedlings were transplanted into pots once their roots reached approximately 2 cm in length. In the individual condition, one seedling from each species was transplanted into a single pot containing 500 ml mixed soil (approximately 0.75 kg; Pot: 6.5 cm bottom diameter; 10 cm top diameter; 12 cm deep). In the community condition, all 13 species were planted together in a box containing 6500 ml of soil mixture (approximately 9.75 kg; Rectangular boxes: 34 cm length; 23 cm width; 12 cm deep). Given that the distribution of plants in the field is random, the arrangement of plants in the community planting treatment was not systematic. All pots and boxes were cultivated in a greenhouse. The photoperiod was 16/8 light/dark, provided by natural light supplemented with artificial lighting (3000 lx) when natural light duration fell below the target level. The temperature was maintained at approximately 20–26 °C. Each pot was watered with 100 mL (box 1300 mL) of demineralized water every five days, and the pots were randomly rearranged weekly. Any dead seedlings were replaced during the first week after transplantation. The mixed soil consisted of substrate soil and inoculation soil (6:1 V: V). The inoculation soil was collected from the restored grassland and treated in either sterilized or non-sterilized forms, allowing us to measure the plant-microbial interaction (PMI). The sterilized substrate was composed of sterilized field soil and sterilized fine vermiculite (2:1 V: V). The soil used as substrate was collected from overgrazed grassland and restored grassland, both of which were sterilized. All soils were collected from the top 20 cm, sieved through a 2 mm mesh. The substrate soil and the inoculum soil used as controls were sterilized in an autoclave (121 °C, 103 kPa, 60 min, twice). In terms of nutrient content, the total soil C, N and P concentrations in sterilized restored grassland soil were 26.7 g/kg, 2.8 g/kg and 0.46 g/kg, respectively. The sterilized overgrazed soil showed significantly lower nutrient levels, with C, N and P concentrations were 17.4 g/kg, 2.0 g/kg and 0.34 g/kg respectively (t-test, p < 0.05). In total, we established two types of substrate soils (restored soil and overgrazed soil) and two soil microbial conditions (living and sterilized), with three replicates per treatment, resulting in 156 individual pots (2 substrate soils × 2 soil microbial conditions × 13 plant species × 3 replicates), plus 12 community pots (2 substrate soils × 2 soil microbial conditions × 3 replicates).

Plant measurements

Plants were grown for 4 months after transplantation. At harvest, the aboveground plant biomass was clipped flush with the soil, and the dry weight of the shoots for each species was measured separately after being dried for 48 h at 65 °C. The entire root system was carefully excavated from the soil and rinsed with water to remove adhering soil. For each plant, fifty 1-cm root segments were then excised using sterile surgical scissors and a scalpel. These segments were fixed in FAA (Formalin-Acetic Acid-Alcohol) fixative for subsequent assessment of AM fungal colonization and root disease incidence. In the community treatment, the plant roots were often intertwined. To ensure accurate determination of AM fungal root colonization and root disease incidence, specific procedures were carried out before selecting root segments. First, we rinsed the soil with running water to expose as many plant roots as possible. Second, for each plant, we selected root segments that are clearly connected to the above-ground portions of the plant. Root disease was quantified visually, with brown areas identified as diseased [29]. Root segments were visually inspected for disease symptoms. A segment was classified as infected if it exhibited at least 1 mm brown lesion. Pathogen incidence was then calculated for each plant as the number of infected segments divided by the total number of segments assessed (i.e. fifty 1-cm root segments per plant). To distinguish “brown area” from naturally lignified tissue, only brown root segments with white roots on both sides were identified as being infested by the pathogen. In fact, none of the root fragments we examined were brown throughout. For the analysis of AM fungal colonization (percentage of the root length colonized by AM fungi), root segments were washed in 10% KOH at 90 ℃ until pellucid (about 1–2 h), followed by a rinse in deionized water, soaking in 2% HCl for 5 min, and staining with 0.05% Trypan blue for 90 min at 90 ℃. Prior to microscopy, the root segments were soaked in a Glycerol-Lactic Acid solution for over 60 min [30, 31]. Quantification of colonization intensity was performed at 100–400 × magnification [32]. For each root segment, the proportion of root length colonized by AM fungi was visually estimated and assigned to one of six levels: 0, 0–1%, 1–10%, 10–50%, 50–90%, 90–100%. The number of root segments falling into each level was recorded as n0, n1, n2, n3, n4, and n5, respectively, with a total of 50 root segments examined per plant. The mycorrhizal colonization (M%) was calculated according to the formula: M%= (95×n5 + 70×n4 + 30×n3 + 5×n2 + 0.5×n1)/ (n0 + n1 + n2 + n3 + n4 + n5), where the coefficients (95, 70, 30, 5, 0.5) represent the mid-points of the respective colonization ranges (e.g., 95% for the 90–100% level).

Data analysis

Soil microbial effects were quantitatively represented as plant-microbial interaction (PMI) (PMI = lg plant biomass in living soil - lg plant biomass in sterilized soil). Each treatment was replicated three times. To calculate PMI, the three replicates in the living-soil treatment were paired with the three corresponding replicates in the sterilized-soil treatment (e.g., replicate 1 in living soil paired with replicate 1 in sterilized soil), yielding three PMI values per species. Given the homogeneity of the three replicates within each treatment, this arbitrary pairing does not influence the statistical conclusions. Higher positive values mean that plants grew more when they were within live soil than sterile soil, whereas negative values indicate better growth in sterilized soil [33]. The effects of plant functional group, interspecific competition, soil substrates, and their interactions on PMI, AM fungal colonization, and pathogen incidence were analyzed using a full-factorial three-way ANOVA, with plant species included as a random factor. The model examined all main effects and possible interactions. To further compare the differences between graminoids and forbs under specific treatment conditions, we used the Mann‑Whitney U test due to unequal sample sizes between the two functional groups. The relationship between PMI and AM fungal colonization or pathogen incidence was assessed using Pearson correlation. Because the Lepidium apetalum is a no mycorrhizal plant, when conducting analyses related to AMF, this species was removed from the data. Statistical analyses and plot were performed using R ver. 4.3.1, utilizing the packages ggplot2, ggpmisc, grid, scales, dplyr, agricolae, broom, gghalves, patchwork, writexl, and tidyr.

Results

Plant-microbial interaction

The three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of plant functional group on PMI (p < 0.05), as well as a significant interaction between functional group and interspecific competition (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Neither competition nor soil substrates had a significant main effect, and no other interactions were significant (Table 1). Graminoids, including Leymus chinensis, Agropyron cristatum, Stipa capillata and Elymus dahuricus generally exhibited negative PMI, whereas forb species tended to benefit from soil microorganisms, exhibiting positive PMI (Fig. 1). In both monoculture and mixed culture experiments, the PMI of forbs was significantly greater than zero in both restored and overgrazed soils, indicating a net benefit from soil microorganisms. In the mixed culture experiment, graminoids in overgrazed soil exhibited a PMI significantly lower than zero, indicating a net negative effect (one‑sample t‑tests, p < 0.05). Overall, PMI was significantly more negative for graminoids than for forbs across all conditions (Fig. 1). Furthermore, interspecific competition amplified these differences. It increased the negativity of PMI in graminoids and enhanced the positivity of PMI in forbs, a pattern observed in both soil substrates (Fig. S1).

AM fungal colonization and pathogen incidence

The three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of functional groups (p < 0.05) and interspecific competition (p < 0.05) on AM fungal colonization, as well as a significant interaction between functional group and soil substrate (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Mann-Whitney U test showed that under overgrazed soil, forbs exhibited significantly higher AM fungal colonization than graminoids, whereas no significant difference was detected under restored soil conditions (Fig. 2). On average, graminoids displayed 35.07% ± 5.03% (SD) AM colonization in monoculture and 34.05% ± 3.41% (SD) in mixed culture under overgrazed soil, while forbs reached 48.84% ± 3.48% (SD) and 59.06% ± 2.52% (SD) under the same conditions, respectively. Interspecific competition tended to increase AM fungal colonization for forbs in overgrazed soil and for both functional groups in restored soil (Fig. S2). Forbs in overgrazed soil showed stronger positive AM fungal colonization than in restored soil (Fig. S2).

We investigated whether AM fungal colonization and the pathogen incidence was associated with the strength of PMI and whether these relationships are influenced by interspecific competition across soils with varying nutrient conditions. For graminoids, the AM fungal colonization showed no significant relationship with PMI across all treatments (Figs. 4a and c and 5a and c). Conversely, for forbs, it exhibited a significant positive association with PMI strength under all interspecific competition and soil conditions (Figs. 4b and d and 5b and d). Discussion

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi and soil pathogens represent fundamental forces driving plant-microbial interactions (PMI), with the former typically enhancing plant performance through improved resource acquisition and pathogen defense, while the latter adversely affects plant health and nutrient absorption [34]. Our study provided a critical empirical link between these fundamental microbial drivers and the net outcome of PMI across plant functional groups in a northern Chinese steppe. By quantifying natural levels of AM fungal colonization and pathogen incidence alongside PMI under varying biotic and abiotic conditions, we demonstrated that the negative PMI for graminoids was primarily driven by susceptibility to root pathogens, particularly under nutrient stress, whereas the positive PMI observed in forbs was strongly correlated with AM fungal symbiosis. These differential associations underscored the functional dichotomy between these dominant plant groups in responding to soil microbial communities, with significant implications for understanding plant community assembly and guiding grassland restoration strategies.

Differential plant-microbial interactions in graminoids versus forbs

Variation in PMI among co-occurring species is a key mechanism promoting plant community diversity and species coexistence [35]. Our findings revealed that graminoids, which form the overwhelmingly dominant functional group in northern China’s steppe, consistently exhibited negative PMI. This pattern is a classic example of negative plant-soil feedback, a process in which plants accumulate soil microbes that subsequently inhibit conspecific growth and which is frequently observed in herbaceous communities [36]. This suggested that the long-term dominance of graminoids has enriched functional-group specific soil pathogens, which in turn suppress their growth. Such suppression might facilitate community richness by creating low-competitive conditions for other plant species, aligning with the concept of negative plant-microbial interaction, which frequently occurs when plant seedlings grow in conspecific soil [14]. At the species level, given that our soil samples were collected from a grassland perennially dominant by Leymus chinensis (biomass accounting for > 40%), it is not surprising that strong negative PMI was observed for Leymus chinensis. Similarly, other graminoids such as Agropyron cristatum, Stipa capillata and Elymus dahuricus also exhibited negative PMI, even though their dominance in the community was much lower than that of the Leymus chinensis. This shared pattern among graminoids could be explained by several factors. According to previous studies, closely related plant species are more likely to have shared natural enemies [37] and show more similar feedback effects due to their longer shared evolutionary history [38]. Moreover, graminoids generally release similar semi-polar metabolites to each other [39, 40], which are related to shaping soil microbial communities [41]. In contrast, most forb species, with the exception of Lepidium apetalum, exhibited positive PMI, indicating a net benefit from the soil microorganisms. As a member of the Brassicaceae family, which lacks mycorrhizal associations, Lepidium apetalum’s non-mycorrhizal trait likely explained this divergence. Overall, forbs experienced a more pronounced positive PMI than graminoids. This phenomenon might be partly attributed to the absence of host-specific pathogens for forbs in this graminoid-dominated soil and their relatively weaker nutrient competitive ability [20], resulting in a greater reliance on mutualistic microorganisms for nutrient acquisition. Furthermore, we observed that interspecific competition amplified the negative PMI in graminoids and the positive PMI in forbs. This could be explained by differences in nutrient acquisition strategies. Plant-microbial interaction (PMI) represents the net outcome of simultaneous positive and negative effects of different soil microorganisms, and this balance is strongly influenced by plant nutrient acquisition [26]. Graminoids, with their competitive advantage for soil nutrients under interspecific competition, likely reduced their reliance on beneficial soil microorganisms, such as AM fungi, leading to a more negative net PMI. In contrast, forbs, with their limited nutrient uptake capacity, became even more dependent on mutualistic microorganisms such as AM fungi under competition, thereby experiencing a more positive PMI.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi drive positive plant-microbial interactions in forbs

To identify the specific microbial drivers underlying these contrasting PMI outcomes, we analyzed AM fungal colonization and pathogen incidence. The results showed that forbs supported significantly higher AM fungi colonization than graminoids in overgrazed soil, and their PMI strength was positively correlated with colonization levels across all treatments. Conversely, PMI in graminoids showed no relation with AM fungal colonization. These suggested a limited effect of AM fungi on graminoids growth, consistent with previous research indicating that C3 grasses generally exhibit smaller responses to mycorrhizal inoculation compared to C4 grasses and non-N-fixing plants [42], as the graminoids selected in this experiment followed the C3 photosynthetic pathway. Moreover, the weak relationship between AM fungi colonization and PMI of graminoids could have been attributed to differences in root structures and physiology [43], and their consequent nutrient uptake strategies. Specifically, AM fungi benefit plants by enhancing nutrient access through hyphae exploration in plant-fungal symbiosis [44, 45]. The extensive fibrous root systems of graminoids enable them to rapidly occupy soil space and efficiently absorb nutrients, rendering them less reliant on AM fungi for nutrient uptake. In contrast, forbs in our research typically have smaller root systems, which may limit their nutrient absorption. Consequently, they exhibited a more pronounced increase in nutrient uptake facilitated by the hyphal networks formed by AM fungi. Notably, the difference in AM fungal colonization between forbs and graminoids was absent in restored soil. This context-dependency was likely attributed to increased mycorrhizal colonization of forbs under the nutrient stress of overgrazed conditions. It is well-established that mycorrhizal symbiosis becomes more beneficial and plants become more dependent on it under abiotic stresses such as low nutrient availability and competition [46]. For instance, a study in fragmented forests of Argentina documented increased AM fungal colonization in response to lower nutrient availability [47]. Schultz et al. (2001) similarly reported that plants’ dependence on mycorrhizal fungi increases in low-nutrient environments [48].

Pathogen drive negative plant-microbial interactions in graminoids

Graminoids exhibited a higher pathogen incidence compared to forbs. This phenomenon could have been attributed to the legacy of a grassland plant community that was dominated by Leymus chinensis, making the graminoids more susceptible to the diseases. Under the specific conditions of overgrazed soil and monoculture, we found a significant negative linear relationship between graminoid PMI and the incidence of pathogen-induced root disease. In contrast, the PMI of forbs showed no significant changes concerning the percentage of diseases. This finding suggested that the PMI of graminoids was more reliant on their resistance to pathogens, especially when these plants are unable to obtain sufficient nutrients. This aligns with the growth-defense trade-off theory [49], where a “fast” resource-acquisition strategy, often associated with traits like high root length in graminoids, is theoretically linked to a greater susceptibility to biotic stressors such as pathogens [50]. Under nutrient stress, their growth might have been directly limited, which could further compromise physiological defenses, amplifying disease susceptibility. In contrast, forb species typically adopt a “slower” strategy, prioritizing defense over rapid growth, which explained the lack of correlation between their PMI and pathogen incidence. Furthermore, the absence of a significant relationship between PMI and pathogen incidence in graminoids under interspecific competition or in restored soil suggested that their superior competitive ability in these contexts may facilitate greater resource acquisition. This could have alleviated nutrient stress and potentially enhance plant resistance, implying that the pathogen-driven negative PMI might have been mitigated through nutrient addition [51]. Moreover, we proposed that the diverse plant communities in our experiment might have reduced pathogen pressure through a dilution effect [52], thereby weakened the linkage between negative PMI and pathogen incidence. Supporting this, a study on Kalidium foliatum and Reaumuria songarica demonstrated that intraspecific interactions can dilute pathogens and weaken negative biotic soil feedbacks [53]. Although we did not observe a significant alleviation of negative PMI for graminoids, our results confirmed that the relationship between negative PMI and pathogen incidence was indeed weakened under these conditions.

Conclusion

This study provided an empirical framework for predicting PMI outcomes by establishing relationships between PMI strength and AM fungal colonization or pathogen incidence. We found that graminoids consistently exhibited negative PMI, driven primarily by pathogen susceptibility under nutrient stress, while forbs exhibited positive PMI, strongly correlated with AM fungal symbiosis. These relationships were context-dependent, being most pronounced under overgrazed, nutrient-poor conditions. Our findings highlighted the need to consider the differential microbial drivers of plant growth when designing restoration strategies. For instance, management practices that rely on or alter soil microbial communities, such as AM fungal inoculation, should account for the possibility that graminoids and forbs may respond dissimilarly due to their distinct microbial associations. Furthermore, our results showed that the relationship between pathogen incidence and negative PMI in graminoids was strongly context‑dependent. It was significant only in nutrient‑poor overgrazed soil under monoculture and disappears in restored soil. Although we could not disentangle the effects of nutrients from other soil properties, this pattern suggested that restoring soil conditions could alter the role of pathogens in driving negative PMI. Future studies with direct nutrient are needed to identify the causal factors. This approach underscores the potential of targeted soil management practices to support grassland restoration efforts.
