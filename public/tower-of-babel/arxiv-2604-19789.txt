From Data to Theory: Autonomous Large Language Model Agents for Materials Science
Samuel Onimpa Alfred, Veera Sundararaghavan

Abstract




Abstract

We present an autonomous large language model (LLM) agent for end-to-end, data-driven materials theory development. The model can choose an equation form, generate and run its own code, and test how well the theory matches the data without human intervention. The framework combines step-by-step reasoning with expert-supplied tools, allowing the agent to adjust its approach as needed while keeping a clear record of its decisions. For well-established materials relationships such as the Hall–Petch equation and Paris law, the agent correctly identifies the governing equation and makes reliable predictions on new datasets. For more specialized relationships, such as Kuhn’s equation for the HOMO-LUMO gap of conjugated molecules as a function of length, performance depends more strongly on the underlying model, with GPT-5 showing better recovery of the correct equation. Beyond known theories, the agent can also suggest new predictive relationships, illustrated here by a strain-dependent law for changes in the HOMO-LUMO gap. At the same time, the results show that careful validation remains essential, because the agent can still return incorrect, incomplete, or inconsistent equations even when the numerical fit appears strong. Overall, these results highlight both the promise and the current limitations of autonomous LLM agents for AI-assisted scientific modeling and discovery.

Introduction

Scientific discovery has long relied on human expertise, with foundational equations such as the Hall–Petch equation
hall1951deformation
in metallurgy and the Arrhenius equation
arrhenius1967reaction
in chemical kinetics emerging from close attention to experimental data. In 1889, Arrhenius examined published temperature-dependent reaction-rate data and showed that several datasets could be described by a common relation. Van ’t Hoff had already recognized in 1884 that the temperature dependence of equilibrium constants suggested a related mathematical form for the temperature dependence of forward and reverse reaction rates. Arrhenius then took the next step by showing that experimental rate data could be organized by a similar expression, turning an earlier theoretical idea into a broadly useful empirical law bearing his name
arrhenius1967reaction
. In a similar spirit, the present work treats LLMs as knowledge-based assistants that draw on prior scientific literature to connect observed data to plausible equations before those equations are tested quantitatively.

For decades, researchers have hoped that artificial intelligence (AI) could help speed up the full scientific workflow, from data generation and hypothesis building to equation discovery and experimental validation. Early practical progress came through machine learning, which benefited from the rapid growth of materials data through high-throughput experiments
hattrick2016perspective
, databases such as the Materials Project
jain2013commentary
, and broader efforts such as the Materials Genome Initiative
de2019new
. These tools have been applied to many problems, including prediction of metal fatigue properties
alfred2025data
and thermodynamic stability of inorganic compounds
zou2025predicting
. However, most machine learning models function as black boxes: they can make accurate predictions, but they do not naturally provide equations or explanations that can be turned into theory, checked against physical principles, or used to generate new hypotheses. Symbolic regression (SR), originally developed in the context of genetic programming, offers a more interpretable alternative by searching for mathematical expressions that fit data directly
koza1994genetic
. Yet this search quickly becomes difficult as equation complexity grows. More recent approaches combine deep learning with symbolic search to steer the search toward physically reasonable expressions
d2022deep
;
mundhenk2021symbolic
, but they still explore large mathematical spaces without the benefit of broad scientific knowledge, which can limit the physical meaning of the final result
udrescu2020ai
.

Large language models (LLMs) introduce a different kind of capability for materials science. Because they are trained on large collections of web text, scientific papers, textbooks, and technical documents, they can draw from a wide range of scientific knowledge rather than from a single task-specific dataset. In materials science, LLM-based agents have so far been used mainly for extracting information from the literature
gupta2022matscibert
;
kononova2021opportunities
;
dagdelen2024structured
;
polak2024extracting
;
gupta2024data
;
ansari2024agent
;
ghosh2026llm
. Several recent studies have shown that this can work well at scale. Dagdelen et al. used fine-tuned LLMs to extract structured information across multiple materials chemistry tasks
dagdelen2024structured
. Polak et al. reported precision and recall approaching 90% for materials data extraction using prompt-based conversational LLMs
polak2024extracting
. Gupta et al. extracted more than one million polymer-property records from about 680,000 full-text articles
gupta2024data
. Ansari et al. introduced Eunomia, a zero-shot autonomous agent with performance comparable to fine-tuned models across several extraction benchmarks
ansari2024agent
. Ghosh et al. developed a closed-loop multi-agent system with internal consistency checks to extract thermoelectric and structural data from roughly 10,000 articles
ghosh2026llm
. Together, these studies show that LLM-based agents can be useful in materials science, but they focus on extracting known information from the literature. They do not address the broader challenge of carrying out an end-to-end scientific fitting workflow without human intervention.

In practice, materials scientists often combine many individual experimental and computational tools, interpret intermediate results, and revise their approach when an initial plan does not work. This process is powerful, but it can also be slow, labor-intensive, and difficult to scale. The key question is therefore not only whether LLMs can help identify equations, but whether they can take part in the larger workflow used in day-to-day scientific analysis. In this work, we develop autonomous agents that decide what step to take next, use specialized tools, interpret intermediate results, and change course when needed, all while keeping a transparent record of their decisions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first LLM-driven autonomous agent designed specifically for end-to-end data fitting in materials science. Existing fitting workflows still require human input at one or more important stages, such as choosing the equation form, selecting initial fitting parameters, judging fit quality, or deciding whether another fitting attempt is needed. We are not aware of prior work that automates this entire process through LLM-driven autonomy.

This work makes four main contributions. First, the agent generates equations from scientific reasoning rather than choosing from a fixed library of templates, making the task a direct test of scientific knowledge. Second, the framework is designed to handle unclear or imperfect LLM outputs, which are common in scientific computing tasks. Third, the agent judges its own fitting results and decides whether re-fitting or a different strategy is needed. Fourth, the framework records the full chain of decisions, making it possible to inspect every step of the fitting process. A second major contribution is the systematic evaluation of LLM scientific knowledge in the setting of autonomous data fitting. Because we intentionally remove fallback options, successful completion depends on whether the LLM can supply the governing equation from its own prior knowledge. Each case study therefore becomes a direct test of scientific reasoning: does the agent know the right equation, can it turn that equation into working code, and can it recognize when the result is physically unreasonable? By evaluating OpenAI’s Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) models on materials science datasets of different complexity, this work provides a quantitative picture of the current strengths and limits of LLM-based scientific knowledge as a basis for autonomous scientific workflows.

Using this framework, we study several materials science datasets governed by foundational physical relationships. These datasets serve as case studies for testing LLM-based scientific reasoning across different levels of difficulty. The main contribution of the paper is the workflow itself, which provides a general template for any field in which governing equations can be written in closed form. Performance is evaluated in terms of success rate, types of failure, and quality of reasoning, providing a clearer view of the current capabilities and limitations of LLM-driven agents for autonomous data fitting.

2
The Autonomous Scientific Agent

2.1
Framework Design

A Reasoning and Acting (ReAct) loop
yao2022react
is combined with a structured tool registry to form an autonomous scientific agent capable of end-to-end empirical model fitting without human intervention. The framework is domain-agnostic: while specific materials science examples are used to illustrate its operation in subsequent sections, the architecture itself is the core contribution and is readily transferable to any empirical fitting task.

2.2
Framework Architecture

The framework consists of three primary components that interact iteratively to complete a fitting task, as illustrated in Fig.
1
:

1.

Reasoning Engine:
A general-purpose LLM (e.g., GPT-4 or GPT-5) that understands task context, reasons about the current state, and selects the appropriate action to take.

2.

Tool Registry:
A curated set of computational tools that include domain-specific operations, ranging from data loading to visualization.

3.

Agent State:
A persistent data structure that tracks progress, stores intermediate results, and maintains a complete history of the reasoning trace.

Figure 1:
Schematic overview of the three-component autonomous fitting framework, illustrating the closed-loop iterative workflow.
The Reasoning Engine, Tool Registry, and Agent State interact through a closed-loop iterative workflow comprising Thought, Action, and Observation steps.

The agent operates within a closed-loop, iterative workflow. At each iteration, the agent executes three sequential steps: (1)
Thought
, in which the agent observes the current state and formulates a plan in natural language, resulting in a tool call; (2)
Action
, in which the agent executes the selected tool; and (3)
Observation
, in which the agent processes the tool output, which updates the state. This cycle repeats until the task is complete or a maximum iteration limit is reached.

2.3
Large Language Models

Two OpenAI GPT models are employed in this work: GPT-4 and GPT-5. GPT-4, a large multimodal model, demonstrated significant improvements over its predecessors in reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, and scientific knowledge retrieval
achiam2023gpt
. For GPT-4, the temperature parameter is set to 0.1 to promote reproducible reasoning while allowing limited variability. GPT-5 further extends these capabilities, exhibiting enhanced reasoning, improved instruction-following, and a broader knowledge base
singh2025openai
; this model is used with its default temperature settings as temperature control is not exposed in the interface. The selection of these two models enables a comparative assessment of how advances in LLM architecture and training translate to performance in scientific workflow automation, particularly in the context of symbolic function generation and empirical model fitting.

2.4
ReAct Reasoning Loop

The ReAct paradigm, originally proposed by Yao et al.
yao2022react
, integrates reasoning traces with task-specific actions and is adapted here for scientific workflow automation. At each iteration, the LLM processes a structured prompt comprising four key elements: (1) the overall task description; (2) the complete tool registry with input schemas; (3) the current agent’s state, including loaded data, generated functions, fitted parameters, and validation metrics; and (4) a recent history of thoughts, actions, and observations. The LLM responds in a structured format, as shown in Fig.
2
:

Structured Format

THOUGHT:

<reasoning about current state and next steps>

ACTION:

{"tool": "<tool_name>", "input": <JSON object>}

OBSERVATION:

<output returned by the selected tool, used to update the current agent state and inform the next iteration>

Figure 2:
Structured response format
. At each iteration, the LLM responds with a
Thought
, an
Action
specifying a tool and its input, and an
Observation
that captures the tool’s output.

This structured output is parsed, validated against the tool registry, and executed. If the LLM returns malformed JSON, the agent logs the error, records the failure, and continues to the next iteration. This prevents temporary formatting mistakes from stopping the workflow. The tool’s result is then added to the agent’s history to inform future decisions.

2.6
Symbolic Function Generation

The symbolic function generation pipeline is critical to the success of the framework, as it directly tests the LLM’s scientific reasoning capabilities. The pipeline consist of four sequential steps:

1.

Equation Recall:
The LLM is prompted to recall the governing equation exclusively from its parametric knowledge, without access to external sources, search tools, or retrieval-augmented generation. The agent is initialized with a task-specific system prompt that defines the agent’s role, reasoning structure, and target physical context, as illustrated in Fig.
4
.

2.

Code Generation:
A subsequent LLM call converts the recalled equation into executable code. The prompt specifies the required function signature, input parameters, and syntax requirements, ensuring compatibility with the computational environment.

3.

Function Testing:
The generated function is evaluated with sample inputs to verify syntactic correctness and basic physical plausibility, confirming that the output values are within the expected range for the given physical model.

4.

Halt on Failure:
If any step fails, whether it be the LLM producing an incorrect equation, generating invalid code, or producing a function that fails testing, the agent halts execution immediately. No hard-coded fallback equation is used. This strict design ensures that completed tasks reflect genuine LLM understanding, rather than a reliance on hidden fallbacks or predefined templates. The agent’s reasoning trace (see Appendix
A
for sample reasoning traces) provides a complete record of how the equation was generated, enabling qualitative assessment of the LLM’s scientific knowledge.

2.8
Implementation Details

The framework is implemented in MATLAB
®
R2025b
MATLAB:2025b
, chosen for its robust numerical computing capabilities and native support for nonlinear optimization. GPT-4 and GPT-5 are accessed through the OpenAIChat interface within MATLAB’s LLM toolbox
mathworks2026llm
. Structured data exchange between tools is handled via JSON, and error handling is incorporated for malformed responses; failures are logged while allowing the agent to continue.

Tools are assigned different criticality levels. Failure of essential tools, such as
generate_function
, prompts the agent to retry before halting execution, whereas failure of auxiliary tools, such as
create_plots
, generates a warning but allows the agent to proceed. Intermediate functions generated during execution are evaluated in the base workspace, enabling seamless access by subsequent tools without re-evaluation. The complete reasoning trace is persistently stored and made available for post-hoc analysis and verification.

This domain-agnostic design fulfills the framework’s primary objective: to establish a general methodology for evaluating LLM scientific reasoning across multiple domains, rather than to address any specific materials science problem.

3
Case Studies

3.1
Evaluation Framework

The autonomous scientific agent is evaluated on four materials science relationships: the Hall-Petch equation for grain boundary strengthening, the Paris law for fatigue crack growth, the Kuhn equation for conjugated organic chain energy gaps, and a strain-modified extension of the Kuhn equation. These case studies represent increasing levels of domain specificity and complexity, progressing from the universally known Hall-Petch relationship found in introductory textbooks to the highly specialized Kuhn equation, which appears primarily in the polymer chemistry and organic electronics literature, and finally to the strain-modified task, which lacks a canonical governing equation. This progression enables systematic assessment of LLM’s scientific reasoning across varied materials science contexts.
Experimental datasets are extracted from peer-reviewed literature
barnett2004influence
;
paul2025fatigue
;
sundararaghavan2023computational
for each case study, ensuring the evaluation reflects authentic materials science challenges involving real experimental scatter and non-ideal physical behavior.

Agents powered by GPT-4 and GPT-5 are evaluated across all four case studies. Performance is assessed against three criteria: whether the model correctly recalls the governing equation from parametric knowledge , without reliance on externally provided equations or hard-coded fallbacks; whether the agent successfully completes the entire fitting pipeline, from data loading through parameter estimation to statistical validation; and the nature and frequency of any failures.

The incomplete equations generated by the agents can be attributed to several factors. The GPT-4 powered agent’s equation

(1-1/N)

The progression from GPT-4 to GPT-5 shows improved recall and extraction of complete scientific equations, suggesting that advances in model architecture and training data lead to better encoding of specialized domain knowledge.

3.4.3
Insights

The Kuhn equation case study reveals that agents can achieve excellent statistical fits even with incomplete physical models. Although theoretically important for capturing asymptotic behavior, the missing
(1-1/N)
correction term contributes minimally over the experimental
N
range. Its omission is compensated by slight adjustments in the fitted
V_{0}
parameter, producing nearly identical
R^{2}
and RMSE values.

This finding has important implications for autonomous scientific discovery: statistical validation metrics alone cannot distinguish between complete and incomplete physical models when missing terms are numerically small. Indeed, the GPT-4-powered agent’s incomplete equation, whether from knowledge recall or literature extraction, produced fit statistics indistinguishable from those of the GPT-5-powered agent’s complete equation. A human scientist relying solely on goodness-of-fit measures would accept both results as equally valid.

Thus, the primary difference between the agents lies not in fit quality but in the scientific completeness of the extracted knowledge. The reliability of knowledge retrieval is fundamentally constrained by the composition and coverage of the training data: relationships that are underrepresented or require multi-step inference may be less reliably recalled
miret2024llms
. Recent studies
udrescu2020ai
;
shojaee2024llm
;
guo2025sr
;
song2025llm
have sought to address this by integrating LLMs within SR pipelines, leveraging domain knowledge through dimensional analysis

and self-evaluation
ren2023self
to guide candidate equation generation. However, the assumption that LLMs possess sufficient knowledge to reliably guide equation discovery has been challenged in the literature
miret2024llms
and empirical benchmarks
shojaee2024llm
, consistent with our findings on Kuhn’s equation. Shojaee et al.
shojaee2024llm
introduced LLM-SRBench, a comprehensive benchmark of 239 problems across four scientific domains, specifically designed to evaluate LLM-based equation discovery while preventing trivial memorization. Performance was assessed using symbolic accuracy, which measures recovery of the exact correct mathematical expression rather than a numerically close approximation. The best-performing system achieved only 31.5% symbolic accuracy, revealing that LLMs struggle to generalize beyond memorized forms when confronted with genuine discovery tasks. As we show here with GPT-4 and GPT-5, as LLMs improve, one would expect corresponding improvements in knowledge retrieval. The GPT-5-powered agent’s successful extraction of the full equation, including the small correction term, demonstrates a superior ability to capture subtle but physically meaningful components of scientific models. As shown in the reasoning traces (Fig.

), the GPT-5-powered agent adaptively switched from failed PDF extraction to successful HTML extraction, while the GPT-4-powered agent continued confidently with incomplete information.

Both agents exhibited similar behavior: inconsistency across runs, occasional failures, and exploration of different functional spaces. 4
Discussion

The case studies reveal both the promise and the limitations of LLM-based autonomous agents for materials science: while they excel at executing structured workflows when scientific knowledge is reliably encoded, distinct failure modes emerge when operating at the boundaries of their parametric knowledge. This duality defines the current landscape for deploying autonomous agents in scientific workflows.

Across canonical relationships such as grain boundary strengthening and fatigue crack growth, GPT-4 and GPT-5 powered agents demonstrated reliable end-to-end fitting capabilities, correctly recalling governing equations, implementing domain-appropriate data selection strategies, and producing physically plausible parameters. This performance represents a significant advance over static pipeline approaches, suggesting that for well-established science, autonomous agents can function as capable research assistants. The Kuhn’s equation case studies, however, exposed sharp reliability boundaries. When relying on knowledge recall, GPT-4 produced a scientifically incorrect yet statistically indistinguishable approximation, a plausible hallucination that passed all quantitative validation tests. When tasked with literature extraction, GPT-5 successfully adapted to retrieval failure while GPT-4 proceeded confidently with an error message. The strain modification task further revealed functional instability, with both models generating widely varying functional forms across runs, each reported with high confidence.

4.1
Hallucination Failure Modes

1. Plausible hallucination.
The agent generates scientifically incorrect equations that pass standard quantitative metrics, as seen in the Kuhn’s equation approximation where
R^{2}
values differed by only 0.0006 from the true model.

2. Extraction failure with confident continuation.
The agent proceeds as if successful after retrieval failure, converting error messages into executable code without recognizing the underlying problem.

3. Functional instability.
The agent generates widely varying functional forms across runs for open-ended modeling tasks, yet each run reports success.

4. Pure fabrication.
The agent invents functional forms with no scientific basis, such as the piecewise form for the strain-modified Kuhn’s equation.

A critical insight from this study is that these failure modes share a common characteristic: they produce outputs that pass conventional validation metrics while being scientifically unreliable. The near-identical fit statistics between the correct and approximate Kuhn equation formulations demonstrate that quantitative goodness-of-fit alone is insufficient to detect scientific inaccuracies. This finding raises an important question for autonomous scientific discovery: if an agent can produce scientifically incorrect results that pass standard validation metrics, how can researchers confidently trust its outputs without additional verification mechanisms?

4.2
The Role of Self-Assessment

The literature extraction task reveals a capability that may be as important as tool execution: recognizing failure. GPT-5 identified when PDF extraction did not extract correctly and pivoted to an alternative HTML-based strategy, successfully retrieving the correct equation. GPT-4, by contrast, treated the error message itself as valid output and proceeded to the next reasoning step. This contrast suggests that epistemic awareness, specifically the capacity to assess uncertainty, recognize knowledge boundaries, and communicate confidence levels, represents a critical direction for developing trustworthy autonomous scientific agents.

4.3
Limitations and Future Directions

Several opportunities for future development emerge from the findings presented in the previous sections. The current framework operates with predefined tool registries; future iterations could explore enabling agents to identify and utilize tools directly within environments such as MATLAB without explicit pre-specification. Reasoning traces, while human-readable, are not yet machine-readable, pointing to opportunities for structured output formats that support automated verification. The framework currently relies on standard fit metrics for validation; incorporating uncertainty quantification mechanisms could provide additional confidence in agent outputs. Finally, the observed variability across runs for open-ended tasks highlights the need for improved consistency to support fully autonomous operation.

These observations point toward promising research directions. Validation frameworks could extend beyond quantitative metrics to incorporate source verification, physical consistency checks, and cross-validation against independent knowledge. Hallucination detection mechanisms could target each failure mode specifically, such as testing predictions outside fitted ranges or validating retrieved content for domain-specific terminology. Epistemic awareness could be cultivated through model architecture and training, enabling agents to recognize and communicate uncertainty. Multi-agent verification offers a promising approach, where multiple agents with different underlying models cross-validate results, with discrepancies signaling targeted human review.

5
Conclusion

This work presents an autonomous LLM-driven agent for end-to-end, data-driven materials theory development and fitting. Rather than serving only as a predictor, the agent can choose equation forms, generate and execute code, use specialized tools, and evaluate how well theory matches data without human intervention. The case studies show that this approach already works well for established materials relationships. For canonical laws such as the Hall–Petch equation and Paris law, both GPT-4- and GPT-5-powered agents correctly identified the governing equations, carried out the full fitting workflow, and produced reliable predictions on new datasets. For more specialized relationships, such as Kuhn’s equation for the HOMO-LUMO gap of conjugated molecules, performance depended more strongly on the underlying model, with GPT-5 showing better recovery and extraction of the complete physical form. At the same time, the results make clear that strong numerical agreement does not always imply scientific correctness. In the Kuhn equation case, incomplete or incorrect equations could still produce fit statistics nearly indistinguishable from those of the correct model, showing that goodness-of-fit metrics alone are not sufficient for validation. The open-ended strain-modified Kuhn case further showed that, when no canonical equation is available, current agents can generate useful candidate relationships but still exhibit substantial variability across runs. Taken together, these findings suggest that autonomous LLM agents are already capable of supporting parts of the scientific modeling workflow, especially when the target relationship is well represented in prior knowledge. More importantly, they point toward a broader role for such systems not only in recovering known laws from data, but also in helping researchers explore and test new candidate theories. Realizing that vision, however, will require stronger verification frameworks, better handling of uncertainty, and improved consistency in open-ended discovery tasks. Although demonstrated here in materials science, the overall framework is general and can be extended to other domains in which governing relationships can be expressed in closed form. In this sense, autonomous LLM agents should be viewed not as replacements for scientific judgment, but as emerging computational partners that may help accelerate theory building, model testing, and, ultimately, the discovery of new physical laws.
