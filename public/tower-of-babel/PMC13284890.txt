Hepatitis B Vaccination Coverage and Associated Factors Among Healthcare Workers: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Melaku Zewdu Yehualaw, Habtie Bantider Wubet, Negesse Zurbachew Gobezie, Nigist Ali Tegegn, Kaletsidk Desalegn Mossie, Shimelis Seid Tegegne, Alemie Fentie Mebratie, Begizew Yimenu Mekuriaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major occupational hazard among healthcare workers (HCWs), particularly in resource‐limited settings where vaccination coverage remains low. This study aimed to assess hepatitis B vaccination coverage and associated factors among healthcare workers.MethodsAn institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from September 20 to November 3, 2025, among 448 healthcare workers. Participants were selected using a census sampling method. Data were collected using a structured, pretested questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with vaccination status. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.ResultsOnly 170 (37.9%) participants were fully vaccinated against HBV. Good knowledge and favorable attitude were observed in 68.4% participants who responded appropriately for at least 70% of knowledge related questions and 79.9% of participants who responded appropriately for at least 70% of attitude related questions. Factors significantly associated with vaccination included infection prevention training (AOR = 8.44; 95% CI: 2.00–12.65), good knowledge of HBV infection (AOR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.60–3.11), knowledge of HBV vaccination (AOR = 4.29; 95% CI: 1.40–13.14), and favorable attitude (AOR = 3.62; 95% CI: 1.89–4.14).ConclusionHepatitis B vaccination coverage among HCWs was low. Strengthening institutional vaccination programs, improving access to free vaccines, and enhancing infection prevention training are essential to improve coverage.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a highly contagious, partially double‐stranded DNA virus that primarily affects the liver [1]. It is transmitted through exposure to infected blood and body fluids, including sexual contact, needle‐stick injuries, and vertical transmission from mother to child [2, 3, 4].

Globally, HBV remains a major public health problem, with approximately 296 million people living with chronic infection and over 800,000 deaths annually [5, 6]. Sub‐Saharan Africa is a high‐endemic region, with hepatitis B surface antigen prevalence exceeding 8% [6, 7]. Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at particularly high risk due to frequent occupational exposure to blood and body fluids [8, 9]. The risk of infection following a needle‐stick injury can be as high as 30%, significantly higher than HIV and hepatitis C virus [10].

Vaccination is the most effective preventive measure against HBV [11]. The standard three‐dose vaccine provides more than 90% protection [12]. Despite clear recommendations from the World Health Organization and national guidelines [12, 13], vaccination coverage among HCWs in many African countries, including Ethiopia, remains low [8, 14].

Previous studies in Ethiopia have reported low vaccination coverage; however, institutional‐level data remain limited. Moreover, factors influencing vaccination uptake, such as knowledge, training, and attitudes, are not consistently explored across settings. Therefore, this study aimed to assess hepatitis B vaccination coverage and associated factors among HCWs at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Methods

Study Design, Period and Setting

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from September 20 to November 3, 2025. All HCWs employed at the hospital during the study period were included using a census sampling method to maximize representativeness and reduce sampling error. Since the study included all available HCWs, no separate sample size calculation was performed. A total of 448 participants were included.

Operational Definition

Health care worker: is defined as one who provides care and service to a patient either directly or indirectly and have contact with patient's specimen, sharp materials, by the virtue of their duties.

Vaccination status: based on the number of doses received classified as fully vaccinated and not fully vaccinated.

Fully vaccinated: Are those HCWs who have received three or more doses of hepatitis B vaccine [14].

Not fully vaccinated: Are those who are partially vaccinated (took one or two doses of hepatitis B vaccine) or have not received any dose of vaccine [14].

Knowledge of HCWs About HBV Infection and Its Vaccination

The health care workers were asked to respond with “yes” or “no” to each question. Each correct response was given a score of ‘1’ while an incorrect answer was given a score of “0” both for HBV infection and its vaccine. Participants who responded appropriately for at least 70% of knowledge related questions were categorized as knowledgeable.

Participants who responded less than 70% of the knowledge‐related questions were categorized as poorly knowledgeable [15].

Attitude of HCWs

Participants who replied positively (agree and strongly agree) for greater than or equal to 70% of attitude‐related questions were categorized as having a “favorable” attitude and less than 70% were categorized as having “unfavorable” attitude [15].

Data Collection Procedures

A structured, self‐administered English questionnaire with four sections was adapted from previous studies [14, 16, 17]. Since participants were professionals trained in English‐based medical education, the questionnaire was administered in English. It collected data on HCWs' sociodemographic characteristics, HBV vaccination status, knowledge about HBV (21 items: five on infection, seven on vaccination, and nine on transmission), and attitudes toward the HBV vaccine. Data collectors received training. Before questionnaire administration, written consent was obtained from HCWs after explaining the study's purpose, potential benefits, the right to participate or refuse, and confidentiality.

Data Quality Control

A pretest was conducted on 5% of participants at another health facility. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha to ensure validity and reliability. The primary investigator collected completed questionnaires daily and checked them for completeness, accuracy, and clarity. Supportive supervision and immediate feedback were provided during data collection. Proper categorization and coding were applied to maintain data quality. Feedback from participants and experts was used to refine and improve the tool before full implementation.

Data Analysis and Interpretation

Data completeness was verified prior to analysis. Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population, and results were presented using tables and figures. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess associations between variables. To control for confounding, multivariable modeling was employed. Variables with a p‐value < 0.25 in the Bivariable logistic regression analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to avoid excluding potentially important predictors and to adequately control for confounding variables. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were used to examine associations between predictors and the outcome variable.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the College of Health Sciences (Ref No: CHS/358/2025) prior to data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The information collected was used solely for study purposes, and the privacy of each participant was kept confidential.

Knowledge Towards Vaccination, HBV Infection and Transmission

Nearly all participants (440, 98.2%) recognized that the HBV vaccine is recommended for HCWs, 422 (94.2%) were aware of the existence of a preventive vaccine and 384 (85.7%) knew that three doses are required for full adult protection. Overall, 307 (68.4%) responded correctly to > 70% of vaccination‐related questions.

Most respondents (218, 48.7%) correctly identified Hepatitis B as more transmissible than HIV, and 204 (45.5%) than Hepatitis C. Over half (248, 55.4%) knew that infected individuals can remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period. Overall, 287 (64.1%) answered correctly on > 70% of infection‐related questions.

Regarding transmission, 444 (99.1%) correctly identified needle sharing and 438 (97.8%) identified unprotected sex as routes of transmission. Overall, 346 (77.2%) answered correctly on > 70% of transmission‐related questions. The overall proportion of healthcare workers with good knowledge of HBV vaccination, infection and transmission was 69.9% (Table 2).

Attitude Towards Hepatitis B Infection and Vaccination

Nearly all 436 (97.3%) of our participants agreed that healthy people need vaccination against hepatitis B out of which 302 (67.4%) strongly agreed. Most of our respondents 318 (71.0%) and 96 (21.4%) strongly agreed and agreed respectively that their jobs put them at high risk of acquiring hepatitis B. Majority of our respondents 352 (78.6%) believe that hepatitis vaccine is safe (Strongly agree, Agree).

Vaccination Status of Health Care Workers

In this study, the participants were grouped into two categories: “fully vaccinated” and “not fully vaccinated.” Out of 448 respondents, 170 (37.9%) completed the recommended three doses (fully vaccinated), while 318 (62.1%) of participants had never been fully vaccinated, as shown in Figure 2.

The most common reasons for not immunized for HBV were found to be due to cost, 94 (21.0%), and lack of time, 42 (9.4%) (Figure 3).

Majority of our participants 234 (96.9%) reported that hepatitis B vaccination is not available in the institution for free, while 14(3.1%) reported that the vaccine is available in the institution. Regarding the affordability of the vaccine 180 (40.2%) of the participants reported the vaccine is affordable, while the rest 268 (59.8%) reported that hepatitis B vaccine is not affordable.

Those study participants who were vaccinated at least once but less than the maximum protective dose (three dose) reported the reason for not taking the full three dose being unavailability through governmental channels 28 (6.3%) and due to high cost 42 (9.4%).

Among the study participants who took 3 doses of hepatitis B vaccine only 34 (7.6%) has checked anti HBs level while 182 (40.6%) have not checked their anti‐HBs level.

Multi‐Variable Analysis

In the multi‐variable logistic regression analysis, the independent variables associated with vaccination status of health care workers included training on infection prevention, knowledge on HBV infection, knowledge on HBV vaccination and attitude towards HBV vaccine. Those who have taken training on infection prevention are 8.44 times more likely to be vaccinated compared to those who didn't take training (AOR = 8.44; 95% CI 2, 12.653). Additionally, health care workers who have good knowledge of HBV infection are 2.2 times more likely to be vaccinated against HBV compared to those who have poor knowledge (AOR = 2.29; 95% CI 1.60, 3.11). Furthermore, those HCWs who have good knowledge on HBV vaccine are 4.29 times more likely to be vaccinated than those who have poor knowledge (AOR = 4.29; 95% CI 1.40, 13.14). HCWs who have favorable attitude towards HBV vaccine are 3.6 times more likely to be vaccinated compared to those who have unfavorable attitude (AOR = 3.62; 95% CI 1.89, 4.14).

Discussion

This study found that only 37.9% of healthcare workers were fully vaccinated against HBV, indicating low coverage compared to global recommendations. Although low, this finding is higher than reports from Hawassa, where 21% received three doses of the hepatitis B vaccine [16], and from Shashemene Zonal Town, where only 12.9% were fully vaccinated [18]. However, it is lower than studies conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), Addis Ababa, which reported 78.2% [19]; Akaki Kality Health Center 48% [20]; and Mekelle, with 45% [21]. These differences may be explained by variations in vaccine availability, institutional support, and awareness programs.

The finding of this study is also lower than a recent study done in Dongola, Sudan in 2024 which found that 65.45% received all three doses [22], and is very low compared to developed countries like China which was 89.1% [23]. This much difference might be due to differences in sample size, availability of the vaccine in different clinical settings.

According to this study regarding knowledge about HBV infection, vaccination and transmission, it was found that 64.1% of the participants, health workers have good knowledge about HBV infection. This is higher than a study done in TASH which found that 53.1 has good knowledge [19]. This finding is in line with a study done in JUMC which found that 70.1% of HCW participants have good knowledge about the infection [17]. It is also in line with a study done in Saudi Arabia which found 66.3% good knowledge [24]. This might be due to increased availability of infection prevention training. Hence, infection prevention training was strongly associated with vaccination uptake. This may be because training improves awareness of occupational risk and reinforces preventive practices.

This study found that 68.4% of the participant HCWs have good knowledge about HBV vaccination, which aligns with the study done in Cambodia which was 65.6% [25].

According to this study, 69.9% has good knowledge of HBV transmission. This is much higher with the study done at TASH (49.7%) [19]. But it is in line with a study done in JUMC (64.9%) [26]. This alignment might be due to the same questions and closeness of the sample sizes.

This study found that 79.9% of the participant HCWs has favorable attitude towards HBV infection and vaccination. This finding aligns with a study done in Saudi Arabia which found that 84.62% has a favorable attitude towards hepatitis B infection and vaccination [24]. This might be due to the universal recognition of HBV as a critical occupational hazard.

Regarding the factors associated with the vaccination status in this study, training on infection prevention was identified as one of the factors; HCWs who took training on infection prevention were 8.44 times more likely to be vaccinated compared to those who had not received training on infection prevention (AOR = 8.44, 95% CI 2, 12.65). A systematic review done in Ethiopia showed that health care workers who received infection prevention training are three times more likely to take the full dose vaccine than those who didn't take the training [14]. In another study done at Addis Ababa health centers HCWs who took training on infection prevention were 1.73 times more likely to be vaccinated [27]. The reason could be that training improves awareness of occupational risk and reinforces preventive practices. Having good knowledge was found to be positively associated with vaccination status of HCWs. Good knowledge on HBV infection and vaccination is 2.2 times and 4.29 times more likely to be vaccinated against HBV compared to those who have poor knowledge (AOR = 2.29, 95% CI 1.60, 3.11) and (AOR = 4.29, 95% CI 1.40, 13.14) respectively. A study done at Addis Ababa health centers found that having good knowledge was 1.98 times more likely to be vaccinated [27]. A recent study done in Sudan, found that those with greater knowledge are two times more likely to be vaccinated [22]. The likely explanation is that healthcare workers who understand HBV severity and vaccine benefits are more likely to complete vaccination. This aligns with behavioral health models, where knowledge influences risk perception and health behavior.

In this study health care workers who have favorable attitude towards HBV vaccination were found to be 3.62 times more likely to be vaccinated compared to those who have unfavorable attitude (AOR = 3.62, 95% CI 1.89, 4.14). In a study done in Saudi Arabia, those who have favorable attitude were supporting the necessity of the vaccine and affirming its effectiveness [24]. Attitude played an important role as positive beliefs about vaccine safety and effectiveness increase acceptance and uptake.

Conclusion and Recommendation

Hepatitis B vaccination coverage among healthcare workers remains low. Key factors influencing vaccination include infection prevention training, knowledge, and attitude. Based on our findings, we recommend implementing institutional vaccination programs, providing free and accessible vaccines, introducing mandatory vaccination policies for HCWs, strengthening infection prevention training and establishing monitoring systems for vaccination status.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study identified specific factors influencing HBV vaccination status among healthcare workers, providing actionable insights for improving vaccine uptake. However, the use of self‐reported vaccination status may have introduced recall bias, and the single‐center study design may limit the generalizability of the findings.
