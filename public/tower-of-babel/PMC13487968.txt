Voxel-scale diffusion tensor phenomapping of the healthy and pressure-overloaded human heart
Authors: Christopher A. Rock, Y. Iris Chen, Ruopeng Wang, Anne L. Philip, Boris Keil, Rory B. Weiner, Sammy Elmariah, Choukri Mekkaoui, Christopher T. Nguyen, David E. Sosnovik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffusion tensor magnetic resonance imaging of the heart is typically performed at millimetre-scale resolution, yielding only four to five voxels across the ventricular wall, limiting the measurement of local spatial variation in cardiomyocyte organization. Here we present a submillimetre in vivo cardiac diffusion tensor imaging method achieved during free breathing that facilitates voxel-level characterization of myocardial microstructure. We introduce a phenomapping framework that combines voxelwise diffusion magnitude and anisotropy with radial and circumferential gradients of cardiomyocyte helix angle to identify distinct microstructural environments. The approach was developed in healthy volunteers and applied to patients with severe aortic valve stenosis, who had preserved cardiac function and marked myocardial thickening. Comparisons to conventional resolution imaging, together with downsampling analyses and ex vivo and histological validation, show that these voxel-scale features are less optimally detected using standard techniques. Four data-driven microstructural classes, defined by combined diffusion properties and orientation gradients, were observed in both healthy and pressure-overloaded hearts. Despite substantial hypertrophy, pressure overload was associated with preserved cardiomyocyte spatial organization. This framework supports studies of myocardial microstructural remodelling in vivo.

Diffusion tensor MRI (DTI) of the heart allows the microstructure of the myocardium, which varies markedly from endocardium to epicardium, to be measured non-invasively1-5. However, the resolution of most translational DTI techniques provides only 4 to 5 voxels across the myocardium without interpolation6-10, which probably under-resolves important features. In addition, DTI-derived metrics are usually reported on a per-patient basis, which can mask local microstructural features and variations. Here we present an approach to improve the spatial resolution of cardiac DTI by almost tenfold and analyse the microstructural properties of the heart at the level of each voxel. The key elements of the approach include a cardiac-tailored 64-channel radiofrequency coil11, a spatially tailored two-dimensional (2D) excitation pulse12, a free-breathing sequence in which the diffusion-encoding gradients are designed to null the first two moments of motion13-15, respiratory gating using a low-rank tensor multitasking approach12,16 and analysis of a voxel-based phenomapping matrix.

The phenomapping matrix includes scalar metrics of diffusivity and local gradients in cardiomyocyte orientation or helix angle (HA). At sub-mm resolution, HA in the left ventricle (LV) varies not only by radial depth across the myocardium but also by radial angle. Local gradients in HA were measured and used to compare the microstructure of the LV in patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) and age-matched controls (Ctl). Phenomapping matrices were created in the Ctl and AS participants, a cohort of young healthy volunteers and in human hearts imaged ex vivo. The approach allowed local microstructural associations and clusters in each cohort to be identified and compared.

Results

Cardiac DTI with sub-mm resolution

The development of sub-mm cardiac DTI was performed in a cohort of healthy volunteers (n = 8). DTI scans were performed with a true (non-interpolated) in-plane resolution of 0.85 mm × 0.85 mm (Fig. 1a) followed immediately by an acquisition at the conventional (2.5 mm × 2.5 mm) resolution (Fig. 1b). The left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was routinely visible in single-average b50 and b500 images at 0.85 mm resolution but became blurred at standard (2.5 mm) resolution (Fig. 1a,b). To determine whether the improved resolution produced the expected gains in image sharpness, the LAD was used as a point source (Extended Data Fig. 1) for quantitative evaluation (n = 7). Mean LAD diameter was significantly smaller at 0.85 mm resolution for both the b50 (2.6 ± 0.3 mm vs 5.0 ± 1.5 mm, P = 0.003, paired t-test; Fig. 1c) and b500 (3.0 ± 1.0 mm vs 5.6 ± 1.4 mm, P = 0.003, paired t-test; Fig. 1d) images. Similarly, sharpness was significantly higher at 0.85 mm resolution for both the b50 (0.55 ± 0.08 mm−1 vs 0.29 ± 0.08 mm−1, P = 0.003; Fig. 1e) and b500 (0.51 ± 0.08 mm−1 vs 0.30 ± 0.07 mm−1, P < 0.001; Fig. 1f) images.

Free-breathing DTI of the entire left ventricle (LV) was performed using 12 short axis slices. A stack of single-average b50 (Fig. 1g) and b500 (Fig. 1h) images is shown in Fig. 1 and demonstrates the high quality of the images at 0.85 mm resolution. Helix angle maps were generated using 12 averages (Fig. 1i) and consistently showed the expected evolution in HA from the endo to epicardium1-5. To evaluate the impact of the improved resolution, 3–5 HA maps at the midventricular level were selected in each participant (n = 8) and downsampled to the standard (2.5 mm) resolution. In each participant, the values in the selected slices were combined to yield a single value for that participant. While the HA maps at 0.85 mm resolution appeared sharp, those at 2.5 mm resolution were noisy and highly pixelated (Fig. 1j,k). Plots of HA vs % transmural depth were highly linear at 0.85 mm resolution but not at 2.5 mm resolution (Fig. 1l). The number of sampling points (voxels) from endo to epicardium in the HA plots averaged 12.3 ± 1.6 at 0.85 mm but only 4.5 ± 0.6 at 2.5 mm (Fig. 1m, P < 0.001). The transmural range in HA was significantly higher at 0.85 mm vs 2.5 mm resolution (106 ± 6° vs 94 ± 4°, P = 0.002, Fig. 1n), and the transmural HA slope was also significantly higher (10.3 ± 1.2° mm−1 vs 9.4 ± 1.0° mm−1, P < 0.001, Fig. 1o). The 0.85-mm-resolution DTI datasets also successfully supported analysis with DTI tractography (Fig. 1p).

Derivation of local HA gradients

We next imaged a group of participants with severe aortic stenosis (AS, n = 14) and a group of age-matched controls (Ctl, n = 15). We again acquired 12 short axis slices from base to apex but limited the analysis to 3–5 slices at the midventricular level (Fig. 2a). The rationale for this is demonstrated in the plot of single-average signal-to-noise (SNR) values from base to apex, shown in Fig. 2b. As proximity to the 64-channel surface coil increases, SNR grows, producing the highest signal in the mid-LV and apex. Analysis of the most apical slices, however, can be complicated by through-plane motion during free breathing12. High-quality HA maps could be consistently acquired at the midventricular level, hence the analysis was performed in these 3–5 slices. HA maps in a Ctl and an AS participant are shown in Fig. 2c,d. The increased thickness of the myocardium in AS can be easily appreciated but the transmural pattern of HA appears qualitatively very similar to that of Ctl.

DTI phenomapping provides an extremely deep analysis of myocardial microstructure but is vulnerable to local artefacts (Extended Data Fig. 2). Analysis of the HA maps was therefore performed in the septum, where image quality was highest and artefacts rare. The analysis region was demarcated by radial lines extending from the centre of the LV to the anterior RV insertion point (AIP) and the inferior insertion point (IIP) across a radial angle θ, which in most cases approached π/2 (Fig. 2e). The voxels in the septum were mapped using polar coordinates, where the position of each voxel is a function of its radial angle (θ) from the anterior insertion point and its radial distance (r) from the endocardial boundary, expressed either as an absolute distance or as percent transmurality. A conventional HA map and polar contour HA maps in a Ctl participant are shown in Fig. 2e-g. The magnified polar maps underscore that HA varies not only as a function of radial depth but also as a function of radial angle along circumferential contour lines. In any given voxel, radial and circumferential vectors can be defined (Fig. 2h) and local gradients in HA can be calculated along these unit vectors. Additional gradients can be calculated along oblique vectors to other neighbouring voxels. In each voxel, the radial (Fig. 2i), circumferential (Fig. 2j), minimum (Fig. 2k) and maximum (Fig. 2l) HA gradients could therefore be measured. Representative maps of these gradients, derived from the Ctl HA map shown in Fig. 2e, are shown in Fig. 2i-l.

Validation of HA gradients

The presence of local HA gradients in both the radial and circumferential directions was confirmed by ex vivo DTI of five normal human hearts and by histology. DTI of the ex vivo hearts was performed, as previously described4, with an acquired resolution of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 and 12 diffusion-encoding directions. Radial (Fig. 3c,d) and circumferential HA gradient maps (Fig. 3e,f) appeared extremely similar in vivo and ex vivo. No significant differences were present in the radial gradient ex vivo vs in vivo (1.06 ± 0.06°/% vs 1.07 ± 0.08°/%, P = 0.85; Fig. 3g) or at different levels of the heart ex vivo (base 1.09 ± 0.08, mid 1.06 ± 0.06, apex 1.01 ± 0.08°/%; P ≥ 0.14; Fig. 3h). Circumferential gradient was lower ex vivo vs in vivo (2.40 ± 0.41° mm−1 vs 3.40 ± 0.54° mm−1, P = 0.0016; Fig. 3i), reflecting the higher SNR obtained ex vivo. However, no differences in circumferential HA gradient were seen at different levels of the heart ex vivo (base 2.50 ± 0.56, mid 2.40 ± 0.41, apex 2.48 ± 0.60° mm−1; P ≥ 0.75; Fig. 3j).

HA range did not differ significantly ex vivo and in vivo (103.5 ± 4.7° vs 104.0 ± 7.7°, P = 0.87; Fig. 3k). Variations in HA in the circumferential direction result in a distribution of HA values at each transmural depth (Fig. 3l). HA variance was calculated by dividing each HA curve into 10 bins (Fig. 3l) and averaging the values in all bins and slices to produce a single HA variance value per participant. A comparison of HA slope and variance between the AS and Ctl participants is shown in Extended Data Fig. 3.

Characterization of the circumferential gradient in cardiomyocyte orientation was also performed in mouse hearts since the arrangement of cardiomyocytes in the human and murine hearts is highly conserved1, and the murine heart can be imaged histologically on a standard slide. In addition, the response of the murine heart to aortic banding is extremely similar to aortic stenosis in humans17. Masson’s trichrome sections of a healthy and an aortic-banded mouse heart are shown in Fig. 3m,n and Fig. 3q,r, respectively. Structure tensor imaging (STI) was used to quantify cardiomyocyte alignment in these sections, as previously described2,3,18. With STI of a 2D image, the secondary eigenvector identifies the direction of cardiomyocyte alignment (CA)19 and can be used to create maps of cardiomyocyte orientation (Fig. 3o,p,s,t), radial gradient and circumferential gradient (Fig. 3u,v). The circumferential gradient maps obtained with STI appeared extremely similar to those obtained with DTI. No significant differences in circumferential CA gradient were seen in the control vs aortic-banded mice across the entire myocardium (0.049 ± 0.009 rad pxl−1 vs 0.043 ± 0.005 rad pxl−1, P = 0.18; Fig. 3w) or when the analysis was limited to midmyocardium alone (0.048 ± 0.009 rad pxl−1 vs 0.043 ± 0.006 rad pxl−1, P = 0.30; Fig. 3x). Radial CA gradient was reduced in the aortic-banded mice (0.111 ± 0.013 rad pxl−1 vs 0.130 ± 0.016 rad pxl−1, P = 0.043), driven largely by a significant increase in LV thickness (11.7 ± 1.0 pxl vs 8.8 ± 1.6 pxl, P = 0.0027). Collectively, the ex vivo human and histological data confirm the presence of local HA gradients and show that they are similar, at the midventricular level, in anisotropic and isotropic voxels. The histological data further suggest that pressure overload and LV hypertrophy, when compensated, produce a reduction in radial HA gradient but does not cause a loss of microstructural coherence in the heart. Further exploration of the impact of acquired resolution and voxel anisotropy on HA gradients is provided in Extended Data Fig. 4.

Evaluation of LV microstructure in AS

Local HA gradients.

We next compared local HA gradients (in ° mm−1) between the AS (n = 14) vs Ctl (n = 15) participants. The echo time used to acquire the DTI data was 76 ms, hence the b50 images provided a T2-weighted signal map. Maps of b50, mean diffusivity (MD), fractional anisotropy (FA), HA and radial HA gradient are shown in a Ctl (Fig. 4a-e) and an AS participant (Fig. 4f-j). The myocardium in the b50 images, which were acquired in midsystole, was significantly thicker in AS (15.7 ± 1.7 mm vs 11.0 ± 1.5 mm, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4k). No differences in MD (1.39 ± 0.10 × 10−3 mm2 s−1 vs 1.43 ± 0.07 × 10−3 mm2 s−1, P = 0.31; Fig. 4l) or FA (0.338 ± 0.024 vs 0.326 ± 0.019, P = 0.16; Fig. 4m) were seen. HA range trended up but was not significantly increased in AS (109 ± 6° vs 104 ± 8, P = 0.072; Fig. 4n); however, radial HA gradient was significantly lower in AS vs Ctl (7.8 ± 0.8° mm−1 vs 9.9 ± 1.6° mm−1, P = 0.0002; Fig. 4o). No significant difference was seen in the circumferential gradient (3.60 ± 0.98° mm−1 vs 3.40 ± 0.54° mm−1, P = 0.98; Fig. 4z) between AS and Ctl. The impact of in-plane resolution on scalar metrics of diffusivity and local HA gradients is shown in Extended Data Fig. 5.

The orientation of the minimum (min) and maximum (max) HA gradients relative to the radial vector was also recorded. If the min./max. gradient subtended an angle of 0–30° this was regarded as radial, 30–60° as oblique, and 60–90° as circumferential. The min HA gradient was reduced in AS (1.25 ± 0.18° mm−1 vs 1.47 ± 0.23° mm−1, P = 0.0077; Fig. 4aa), and there was also a significant difference in max HA gradient (11.7 ± 1.9° mm−1 vs 13.1 ± 1.9° mm−1, P = 0.046; Fig. 4cc). The min gradient was less circumferential in AS (64 ± 5° vs 72 ± 4°, P = 0.0001; Fig. 4bb) and the max gradient was less radial (24.4 ± 3.8° vs 18.6 ± 3.4°, P = 0.0002; Fig. 4dd). These differences were largely a reflection of the lower radial HA gradient in AS (Extended Data Fig. 6). Collectively, the HA gradient maps confirmed that the hypertrophied myocardium in well-compensated AS maintains its microstructural coherence.

Microstructural and pathophysiological correlations in AS

We next sought to determine the correlations, on a per-participant basis, between metrics of LV microstructure and commonly used clinical variables (Fig. 5a). Metrics with strong correlations included diastolic LV thickness, HA variance, min gradient and circumferential gradient. A strong correlation was seen between the min and circumferential (circ) HA gradients (Fig. 5b), particularly in AS. Diastolic LV thickness and HA variance were not correlated (Fig. 5c), hence each was used for further comparisons. A negative correlation was seen between diastolic thickness and HA gradients, reaching significance in the AS participants (Fig. 5d-g). In both AS and Ctl, HA variance correlated or trended towards correlation with the circ and min HA gradients as well as the min and max angles (Fig. 5h-k). DTI tractography was performed in participants with AS. A loss of tract coherence was seen in participants with high HA variance and HA gradients (Fig. 5l,m). The clinical characteristics of the AS and Ctl participants are provided in Table 1 of the supplement.

Diffusion tensor phenomapping

We next sought to evaluate the associations between metrics of LV microstructure on a per-voxel basis and determine whether distinct microstructural microenvironments were present. This approach is conceptually analogous to single-cell RNA sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 2)20,21 and has also been used to analyse complex clinical populations22. In each participant, the voxels from all slices were collected and arranged into a participant data matrix (Fig. 6a-c). Each voxel (row) in the matrix contained 10 values (columns) derived predominantly from the HA gradient maps (Fig. 6b). The values for each parameter (column) were normalized using a z-score to create a normalized participant data matrix. Before normalization, the HA gradient values were square-root transformed to create highly Gaussian distributions. The normalized matrices of each Ctl (n = 15) and AS (n = 14) participant were then combined to form a population data matrix (Fig. 6d), containing 50,091 rows (voxels). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the matrix showed that the first three principal components (PCs) accounted for >75% of the observed variation. The first axis (44.5% of variance) was defined primarily by high radial and max HA gradients, the second axis (18.4% of variance) by high MD and low FA, and the third axis (12.5% of variance) by high circumferential and min HA gradients (Fig. 6e).

k-means clustering was performed on the population matrix as a whole, and separately on the voxels from the Ctl or AS participants only. A minimum of 4 voxel clusters were consistently identified, and the properties of corresponding clusters were extremely similar across all cases (Fig. 6f-h). The first cluster consisted of ordered voxels with average HA gradient values and FA values slightly above average. The second cluster consisted of highly ordered voxels with above-average FA values and below-average HA gradients. The third cluster contained voxels with average HA gradients but above-average MD and below-average FA. The fourth cluster contained the least-ordered voxels with the highest HA gradients and below-average FA. A phenomapping matrix of the ex vivo human hearts (n = 5) was constructed as above and contained 40,146 voxels. PCA of the ex vivo matrix produced principal components that were extremely similar to the in vivo PCA (Fig. 6i). Likewise, clustering the ex vivo matrix produced clusters that matched the in vivo clusters extremely well (Fig. 6j). PCA plots showed no difference in the distribution of voxels from the AS and Ctl populations (Fig. 6k) but could resolve voxels in clusters 2 from those in cluster 4 (Fig. 6l). Cluster maps were created by classifying each voxel in the septum by the cluster it belonged to. Cluster maps in Ctl and AS participants are shown with their corresponding HA maps in Fig. 6m-r. Small but significant differences in the proportion of voxels in each cluster were seen between AS and Ctl (Fig. 6s). The proportions were 30.2 ± 4.6% vs 37.2 ± 4.6% for cluster 1 (P = 0.0003), 27.2 ± 2.3% vs 25.2 ± 2.1% for cluster 2 (P = 0.073), 26.4 ± 4.1% vs 19.2 ± 3.1% for cluster 3 (P < 0.0001), and 16.2 ± 1.4% vs 17.6 ± 1.5% for cluster 4 (P = 0.0094).

We next assessed the spatial distribution of the voxels in the four clusters, with particular focus on %-transmural depth. The total number of voxels (all clusters combined) increased linearly with %-transmural depth due to an increase in circumference towards the epicardium (Fig. 7a-c). However, distinct transmural distributions were seen for all four clusters. The voxels in cluster 1 mirrored the transmural increase in voxel number but also showed a distinct prominence in the subepicardium (Fig. 7d-f). Those in cluster 2 had a bell-shaped distribution and were most concentrated in the midmyocardium (Fig. 7g-i). The voxels in cluster 3 showed a preference for the subendocardium and subepicardium (Fig. 7j-l) and those in cluster 4 for the subepicardium (Fig. 7m-o).

The relative contribution of voxels from each cluster across the layers of the myocardium is shown in Fig. 7p-r. In the subendocardium, voxels in clusters 3 and 2 are most prominent (33% and 31%), followed by cluster 1 (23%) and cluster 4 (13%) voxels. In the midmyocardium, cluster 1 (40%) was the most dominant followed by cluster 2 (33%). In the subepicardium, the proportion of voxels from clusters 2 (18%) and 3 (19%) decreased, while that from cluster 1 remained high (36%) and that from cluster 4 increased (27%). A small increase in cluster 3 voxels was seen in all layers of the myocardium in the AS participants. However, the transmural distributions of the clusters were extremely similar in AS and Ctl. Collectively, PCA and cluster mapping revealed subtle changes in AS but no clear loss of microstructural coherence in the AS participants.

Additional details on the phenomapping approach are provided in the Extended Data figures and Sections 9-12 of the supplement. The reproducibility of the technique is shown in Extended Data Fig. 7, and the rationale for square-root transformation during normalization in Extended Data Fig. 8. The impact of participant vs population-based normalization and k = 4 vs k = 5 clustering is shown in Extended Data Fig. 9. The impact of spatial resolution on voxel-based phenomapping is also further discussed in Section 13 of the supplement.

Discussion

An approach to characterize the microstructure in the heart, DTI phenomapping, is introduced in this study. The approach was validated in human hearts ex vivo, and yielded extremely consistent and reproducible results in healthy and control participants in vivo. DTI phenomapping in participants with severe AS revealed that despite a marked increase in load/hypertrophy, the microstructure of the myocardium in AS remained highly coherent and organized.

DTI phenomapping involves a voxel-based analysis approach, analogous to single-cell RNA sequencing of tissue20,21, and allows clusters with distinct structural microenvironments to be detected. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering of the matrices produced near-identical patterns in healthy volunteers, aged-matched Ctl participants and ex vivo human hearts. The cluster pattern in AS was also extremely similar. The voxels in the most-ordered cluster (cluster 2) had similar prevalence in AS and Ctl, and fell predominantly in the midmyocardium, where FA may be highest8. The proportion of mixed-order voxels (cluster 3) was increased in AS and could represent microenvironments in transition from higher to lower order. However, preclinical studies will be needed to confirm this and correlate cluster characteristics with histological changes in the myocardium.

The LV myocardium in the AS participants underwent significant hypertrophy but maintained its microstructural integrity. Similar findings were seen in the aortic-banded mice, suggesting that this response is conserved. A previous report of DTI in participants with AS (at conventional spatial resolution) showed that the LV adapted to the increased load by increasing the transmural slope of HA23. A similar increase in HA slope was observed in this study but neither PCA nor hierarchical clustering showed any major differences compared to the Ctl participants. It should be noted that the participants in our study had severe but well-compensated AS with no indication for aortic valve replacement at the time of imaging24,25.

The approach we describe provides a paradigm shift in both DTI acquisition and analysis, but several limitations of the approach merit discussion. The focused field of view (FOV) approach we use may not be suited to conditions that selectively affect the lateral wall. In addition, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 2, even highly localized artefacts can produce false clusters with DTI phenomapping. At present, we therefore limit our analysis to the LV septum where artefacts are rare. This approach is very consistent with other quantitative techniques including MR spectroscopy26 and T1/T2* mapping27 of the heart. The reproducibility and generalizability of the technique were tested by imaging the same participants with our 64-channel cardiac coil and a commercial 32-element array. The data on a per-participant basis were highly reproducible; however, the full complement of mid-LV slices could only be analysed in 5/8 participants with the 32-channel coil. A slight reduction in spatial resolution may be needed for the technique to become routinely feasible with commercial 32-element arrays.

The acquisition time of the high-resolution datasets remains long. However, we have shown that an artificial intelligence (AI)-based approach can be used to denoise DTI datasets28, and this could potentially reduce the time required for the acquisition of high-resolution data. We limited our analysis in this study to the primary eigenvector of the diffusion tensor, which reflects cardiomyocyte orientation29. Future studies will be required to determine whether the secondary eigenvector, which reflects the sheetlet angle in the heart6,30,31, can be accurately measured at sub-mm resolution and incorporated into the described phenomapping approach.

In summary, we present a technique to perform DTI of the human heart with sub-mm resolution and leverage this to measure local gradients in cardiomyocyte helix angle. In addition, we describe a phenomapping approach to identify voxel clusters with distinct microstructural properties. We show, using these techniques, that the myocardium in well-compensated AS maintains its microstructural integrity. The phenomapping approach we describe characterizes the microstructure of the myocardium with an unheralded level of depth and provides important insights into the plasticity of the heart.

Methods

Three cohorts of participants were studied: (1) A group of healthy volunteers (n = 8) who were used for technical development of the high-resolution DTI approach, (2) a group of participants with severe aortic stenosis (n = 14) and (3) a group of age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 15) (Supplementary Table 1). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Massachusetts General Hospital (IRB2016P001898) and written consent was obtained from all participants in the study who were reimbursed per protocol (IRB2016P001898).

Image acquisition

All participants were imaged on a commercial 3 T MRI system, equipped with 80mT m−1 whole-body gradients (Prisma, Siemens Medical). Imaging was performed using a cardiac-tailored 64-element array11 and/or commercial 32-element array. A free-breathing diffusion-encoded spin echo echoplanar imaging (EPI) sequence with diffusion-encoding gradients designed to null the first and second moments of motion was used (Extended Data Fig. 1). We have previously used this sequence to image the entire LV with an in-plane resolution of 2.5 × 2.5 mm12. Here the spatially tailored 2D excitation pulse in the sequence was used to selectively excite an even smaller FOV centred on the interventricular septum. Specific parameters included: FOV 23.7 cm, phase FOV 24.5%, acquisition matrix 286 × 70, slice thickness 8 mm, resolution 0.85 × 0.85 mm, chemical fatsat, spin echo EPI readout, echo-train length 46, Fourier 5/8, 12 slices interleaved, repetition time (TR) = 12RR, echo time (TE) = 76 ms, bandwidth 1,165 Hz pxl−1, b = 50 s mm−2 (2 averages), b = 500 s mm−2 (12 averages), 12 diffusion encoding directions. Imaging was performed in midsystole with a trigger delay of 80 ms, and scan duration was 30–35 min with a heart rate of ~60–70 beats min−1. Retrospective respiratory gating of the diffusion-encoded images was performed using a low-rank multitasking approach, as previously described12.

Additional images acquired in the cohort of healthy volunteers included:

Noise images of the heart at 0.85 × 0.85 mm in-plane resolution using the 64-element cardiac array. This allowed SNR and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) maps of the DTI sequence at 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution to be created.

Noise images of the heart at 0.85 × 0.85 mm in-plane resolution using the 64-element cardiac array. This allowed SNR and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) maps of the DTI sequence at 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution to be created.

DTI of the heart and noise images at 0.85 × 0.85 mm in-plane resolution using a commercial 32-channel receive coil. These were used to compare the SNR and CNR of the 64 and 32-element arrays.

DTI of the heart and noise images at 0.85 × 0.85 mm in-plane resolution using a commercial 32-channel receive coil. These were used to compare the SNR and CNR of the 64 and 32-element arrays.

DTI of the heart at 2.5 × 2.5 mm in-plane resolution using the 64-element cardiac array. This was used to compare the point spread of the coronary artery at 2.5 × 2.5 mm vs 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution.

DTI of the heart at 2.5 × 2.5 mm in-plane resolution using the 64-element cardiac array. This was used to compare the point spread of the coronary artery at 2.5 × 2.5 mm vs 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution.

Parameters for these sequences were as described above, but in the noise images the flip angle was set to zero and the TR to 2.6 s. DTI at 2.5 × 2.5 mm resolution was performed with 6 averages since the SNR in these larger voxels was expected to be higher. The FOV used in the standard resolution sequence varied slightly between participants, with a TE ranging from 72–76 ms (most commonly 76 ms). Typical parameters included: FOV 32.5 cm, phase FOV 25%, acquisition matrix 128 × 32, slice thickness 8 mm, resolution 2.5 × 2.5 mm, chemical fatsat, spin echo EPI readout, echo-train length 21, Fourier 6/8, 12 slices interleaved, TR = 12RR, TE = 76 ms, bandwidth 2,440 Hz pxl−1, b = 50 s mm−2 and 500 s mm−2, 12 diffusion encoding directions. In selected healthy volunteers, breath-hold balanced steady state free precession (bSSFP) cine images of the left anterior descending coronary artery were acquired with an in-plane resolution chosen to match that of the 0.85 × 0.85 mm DTI images. These cines were acquired with the 64-channel coil and the following parameters: FOV 320 × 268 mm, slice thickness 8mm, flip angle 44°, matrix 384 × 322, resolution 0.83 × 0.83 mm, TR = 40 ms, TE = 1.44 ms, 25 phases per RR, averages 1.

T1 maps and bSSFP cines of the heart were acquired in the AS and age-matched Ctl participants (see Supplementary Section 8).

Coronary point-source evaluation

The use of larger k-space matrices in SNR-constrained acquisitions can theoretically result in little improvement in image quality if a large number of k-space coefficients are sampled when the signal is extremely low32. To verify that the 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution images would result in meaningful increases in image quality, the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery in single-average b50 and b500 images in healthy participants (n = 7) was used as a point source. The location of the LAD was confirmed using bSSFP cine images (Extended Data Fig. 1). Diffusion-encoded images using the 64-channel coil were acquired at 0.85 × 0.85 mm resolution and then immediately repeated at 2.5 × 2.5 mm resolution. The sharpness (mm−1) of the coronary wall was calculated as previously described33 (see Supplementary Section 1).

SNR and diffusion CNR calculations

SNR calculations with the 64- and 32-channel coils were performed using single-average b50 and b500 images in 5 of the healthy volunteers. Noise images were acquired with the same sequence but with the transmit voltage set to zero, a repetition time of 2.6 s and an average of 240 repetitions. The standard deviation of the signal in each voxel in the noise images was calculated to create a noise map. SNR values were calculated by dividing the signal in each voxel by the standard deviation of the noise in that voxel and then averaged over all voxels in the septum. This was performed for each diffusion-encoding direction and on the full stack of short axis slices to derive a single average b50 and b500 SNR value for each individual. Diffusion contrast was defined as the difference in signal intensity in each voxel between the b50 and b500 images. The diffusion contrast-to-noise ratio (dCNR) was calculated in each voxel by dividing this difference by the standard deviation of the noise. An average dCNR value for each participant was calculated, as described above, across all diffusion encoding directions and short axis slices. SNR and dCNR maps with the 64- and 32-channel arrays are shown in Extended Data Fig. 1. SNR and dCNR in the healthy volunteers were significantly higher with the 64-channel coil (Extended Data Fig. 1) and imaging of the AS and Ctl participants was thus performed exclusively with the 64-channel array.

Image processing and masking

Retrospective gating of the free-breathing diffusion-encoded images was performed using a low-rank multitasking approach, as previously described12. Derivation of the MD, FA and HA maps was performed with a cardiac-tailored in-house version of Trackvis (Martinos Center, Boston, MA) and further processed using Matlab. The endocardial and epicardial borders were determined using the b50, MD and HA maps, and the generated masks were then applied to all maps derived from these images. Careful attention was paid to ensure that all endocardial and epicardial trabeculations were excluded from the boundaries. The centre of the LV and the RV insertion points were manually identified, creating a polar coordinate system going radially from endocardium to epicardium and angularly (Θ) from the anterior to the inferior RV insertion point. Voxels on a Cartesian axis were rebinned to a radial axis using bicubic interpolation to maintain the correct number of voxels per unit distance along each radial line. In many participants, the anterior and inferior walls were suitable for analysis, and in some cases most of the LV could be analysed. However, data analysis in this study was limited to the voxels in the interventricular septum, where image quality was consistently high in all participants. Diastolic LV thickness was measured in bSSFP cine images at the midventricular level that matched the central slice used in the DTI analysis.

HA variance and gradient calculations

HA variance was determined by separating the voxels within the septum of a single HA map into 10 bins on the basis of transmural depth. The bins were determined both by absolute depth in mm (var_mm) and by %-transmurality (var_%). The innermost and outermost bins were consistently noisy and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The variance per bin was calculated for the remaining 8 bins and averaged into a single per-map variance value. The variance values in all HA maps analysed in an individual participant were then combined to derive a single per-participant variance value.

HA gradients were calculated for each voxel using its 8 neighbouring voxels. The adjacent voxels on the radial line intersecting the target voxel were deemed radial, those perpendicular to the radial line circumferential, and the remaining voxels as oblique. For each neighbouring voxel, the gradient was measured as the absolute change in HA from the target voxel divided by the distance between the voxels. Radial and circumferential HA gradients can be calculated bidirectionally (using the mean of the two radial/circumferential voxels) or unidirectionally (radially from endocardium to epicardium only, and circumferentially in a counterclockwise direction only). The two approaches yielded very similar results, but to avoid duplicate use of the same gradient in two adjacent target voxels, we chose to use the unidirectional approach. One limitation of this approach, however, is that no radial gradient can be calculated for the outermost (most epicardial) voxels in the heart.

It should be noted that the HA slope is calculated by fitting a line to the full transmural HA profile and is influenced by all points/layers from endocardium to epicardium. HA range, in contrast, reflects only the voxels in the most endocardial and epicardial layers. The radial HA gradient is calculated at the individual voxel scale (Fig. 2h) and is a reflection only of the gradient between 2 adjacent voxels. The circumferential vector, and hence circumferential gradient, can be calculated in two ways: (1) by physical position in mm from the endocardium and (2) by %-transmural position from the endocardium. At 0.85 mm resolution, these two approaches yielded similar results. The calculation of circumferential HA gradient based on physical voxel position is probably a more physiologically meaningful measure (if two voxels are in physical contact, they will interact electrically and mechanically regardless of their %-transmural positions) and was therefore the approach used in this paper.

Minimum and maximum HA gradients were calculated using the smallest and greatest gradients between all 8 neighbouring voxels, respectively. Mean gradient was defined as the average of all 8 HA gradients, and gradient range was determined by the difference between the maximum and minimum HA gradients. In addition, the relationship (radial, circumferential or oblique) of the voxel producing the minimum and maximum HA gradients to the target voxel was recorded and then discretized into 1 of 3 possible orientation angles relative to the radial vector (radial, 0°; circumferential, 90°; oblique, 45°). The radial HA gradient was calculated as both an absolute gradient (° mm−1) and a relative gradient (°/%-transmurality). All other gradients were calculated only as absolute gradients. The HA gradients were measured in all voxels within the septum in all the slices analysed, and then averaged to yield a single per-participant value.

DTI tractography was performed in selected participants using an in-house cardiac-tailored version of Trackvis (Martinos Center). Cubic or spherical regions of interest (ROIs) were placed in the septum and cardiomyocyte orientation streamlines (tracts) intersecting the ROI were derived using a second-order Runge–Kutta algorithm. A tract termination angle of 35° was used and each segment (voxel) of the tract was colour coded by the HA in that segment.

Principal component and cluster analysis

DTI phenomapping was performed using hierarchical clustering techniques that have been used to analyse genetic20,21 and clinical datasets22 in participants with cardiovascular disease. Ten metrics with voxel-specific values were used: b50, MD, FA, radial gradient per mm, radial gradient per %-transmurality, circumferential gradient, mean gradient, minimum gradient, maximum gradient and gradient range. Since the b50 images are highly T2-weighted, this was used as a surrogate for T2 within the voxel. The HA gradient values followed Gaussian distributions except for the circumferential and minimum HA gradients. The absence of negative values and the low mean values of these parameters resulted in skewed distributions. To address this, all HA gradient values underwent square-root transformation, which resulted in highly Gaussian distributions suited to clustering (Extended Data Fig. 8). Each metric (column) in the voxel matrix then underwent z-score normalization. This could be done at the participant or population level, and the relative differences between these two approaches are extensively discussed in Supplementary Section 11. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 9, the differences between participant and population-based normalization were small. We chose to perform participant-based normalization, where a z-score is ascribed to each voxel only on the basis of the voxel values in that participant, for the following reasons: The aim of participant-based normalization is to detect distinct microstructural microenvironments within individuals and to determine how reproducibly these microenvironments are found across the population. In addition, participant-based normalization resulted in more weighting of scalar-based metrics, such as MD and FA, in the PCA and cluster analysis. While the results of PCA and cluster analysis on the participant and population-normalized data were highly similar (Extended Data Fig. 9), the properties of participant-based normalization were better aligned with the aims of the phenomapping analysis in this study.

The participant-normalized voxels in the phenomapping matrix were analysed using PCA and cluster analysis in Matlab. Ten principal components were identified, with the coefficients and contribution to total variance of each recorded. Cluster analysis was performed with k-means clustering, positioning centroids in a 10-dimensional space and minimizing total point-to-centroid distance. Clustering was performed using 1,000 iterations and 20 replications to derive global minima. The number of clusters (k-value) chosen was based on the Davies–Bouldin index, which revealed similar minima at k = 4 and k = 5 in all matrices (clusters produced with k = 5 are shown in Extended Data Fig. 9). However, for simplicity and initial demonstration of the approach, we focused principally on k = 4. The properties of the voxels in each of the 4 clusters were assessed to determine which DTI-derived metrics most strongly characterized that cluster. Cluster analysis was performed on the entire population (AS + Ctl) and in the AS and Ctl participants separately. To determine the impact of spatial resolution on the phenomapping approach, the HA gradient maps were downsampled to a resolution of 2.5 × 2.5 mm (Supplementary Section 13).

Ex vivo and structure tensor imaging

Diffusion-weighted images from a previously acquired dataset of ex vivo human hearts (italicn = 5) were reanalysed. Acquisition of the data was as previously described4; however, here we processed a dataset of the hearts obtained with 12 diffusion-encoding directions and 12 averages (as opposed to 6 directions and 24 averages) to match the in vivo data. The acquired resolution was 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 and quantification of the HA gradients and DTI phenomapping was performed at this resolution. The diffusion-weighted images were analysed and processed in the identical fashion to the in vivo images and the HA gradient maps were created as above. Analysis of the data in each ex vivo heart was performed at the base, mid-LV and apex, but only the mid-LV slices were used for comparison with the in vivo images, which were acquired at the mid-LV. Since the presence of artefacts was minimal, the ex vivo analysis included all segments of the heart and was not limited to the septum. A phenomapping matrix, consisting of >40,000 voxels, was created, and PCA and hierarchical clustering were performed in an identical fashion to the in vivo datasets.

To further validate our observations regarding the circumferential variation (gradient) in cardiomyocyte orientation, we also examined cardiomyocyte orientation in histological sections of the murine heart. All studies in mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Massachusetts General Hospital and mice were housed under standard conditions. We have previously shown that the cardiomyocyte architecture (helix angle) in the murine heart and human hearts is highly conserved1. In addition, the size of the murine heart allows it to be mounted intact on a standard slide. Histological sections from control and aortic-banded c57/BLK6 (Charles River Laboratories) mice (n = 6 for both groups) were stained with Mason’s trichrome and imaged on a white-light microscope (Leica) at ×10 magnification. Multiple fields were stitched together using the microscope settings producing a composite image of ~12,000 × 12,000 pixels. The tissue sections of the mice were acquired 4 weeks after aortic banding. We have previously shown that the structure and function of the myocardium in banded mice at this time point closely resembles well-compensated aortic stenosis of the human heart17.

Structure tensor imaging (STI) was used to measure cardiomyocyte orientation. In previous applications of STI in 3D tissues, the tertiary eigenvector provided an index of cardiomyocyte orientation2,3. In 2D histology images, however, cardiomyocyte orientation is reflected by the secondary eigenvector of the tensor19. The structure tensor was calculated using a 1st-order Gaussian kernel as previously described18,19. A coordinate system based on the radial axis normal to the epicardial tangent was used. This yielded an array of vectors at the same microscopic resolution as the original image. For comparison to human DTI, the secondary eigenvector maps were downsampled to produce a similar number of transmural pixels (~12) to the healthy control DTI images at 0.85 mm in-plane resolution. The radial cardiomyocyte alignment (CA) range was scaled to ±π/2. The circumferential gradient of the downsampled secondary eigenvector maps was calculated with the same scheme as for the human HA maps. Circumferential gradient was calculated across the full myocardium as well as focused on the midmyocardium, where the cardiomyocytes are in plane.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was done in Graphpad Prism v.10, except for the correlation calculations, which were performed in Matlab. The normality of all datasets was determined using the Shapiro–Wilk test in Prism. The relationships between all metrics available on a per-participant level were quantified using Pearson correlation or Spearman’s correlation if one or more variables failed normality. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-test. If the data in the groups were related (for example, high resolution vs lower resolution versions of the same image/map), paired two-tailed t-test was used. For non-Gaussian distributions that failed normality, two-tailed Mann–Whitney test was used. Comparisons between three or more groups were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey post-test comparison, or a Kruskal–Wallis test if one or more of the datasets failed normality. No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications6,7,28. No randomization or data exclusions were performed; data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. All data are reported and plotted as mean ± s.d. A P value < 0.05 was required to meet significance.

Conceptual basis of DTI-Phenomapping.

(a-d) DTI-derived metrics can be evaluated at several scales ranging from (a) per slice, (b) per segment, (c) per radial line and, (d) per individual voxel.
