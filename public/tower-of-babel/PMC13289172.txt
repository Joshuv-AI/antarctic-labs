Home-based exoskeleton use to improve quality of life in patients with multiple sclerosis: study protocol of a multicentre, randomised, cross-over trial
Authors: Emilie Leblong, Maxime Billot, Donze Cecile, Anthony Gelis, Dinomais Mickaël, Sophie Jacquin-Courtois, Raphael Gross, Claire Le Meur, Bodet Linda, Anne Carole, Nicolas Benoit, Fraudet Bastien, Gallien Philippe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) frequently leads to mobility impairment, fatigue and a significant decline in health-related quality of life (QoL). Home-based assistive technology, such as robotic exoskeletons, offers a promising solution to enhance independent mobility and increase the intensity of motor training. Long-term functional and quality of life benefits of light lower-limb exoskeleton home use have yet to be determined.ObjectiveThe primary objective of this study is to determine the efficacy of an 8-week period, home-based use of a robotic exoskeleton in improving QoL in individuals with MS, compared with a no-device control period.MethodsThis is a multicentre, randomised, controlled and single-blinded cross-over trial. A total of 28 patients with confirmed MS (Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score 5.0–7.0) will be recruited across three rehabilitation centres. Participants will be randomly assigned to two 8-week phases: intervention (daily home-based exoskeleton use) or control (physical activity advice), separated by an 8-week wash-out period. The primary outcome is the change in the MS Quality of Life assessed by the Functional Assessment of Multiple Sclerosis (FAMS) physical composite score from baseline to the end of each phase. Secondary outcomes include changes in walking performance (2-minute Walk, 10 M Walk, Timed Up-and-Go, Four Square Step Test and Sit-to-Stand Test), fatigue severity (Fatigue Severity Scale and Fatigue Impact Measurement Scale (EMIF)-MS), and strength capacity (Manual Muscle Testing, Tardieu scale), self-confidence (Rosenberg), anxiety and depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale), satisfaction to use (Technical Aid Satisfaction Scale) and intention to use (Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology).Ethics and disseminationThis study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 1 February 2024 (Trial registration number: NCT05835622 https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05835622). Patient recruitment is currently underway and is anticipated to be completed by January 2026. Primary endpoint data collection is expected to be completed in June 2026.This study protocol describes a rigorous trial designed to provide high-level evidence on the impact of a home-based robotic exoskeleton on QoL in individuals with MS. By determining intervention effectiveness, the results will provide clinical guidelines, potentially facilitating the widespread adoption of home-based assistive robotics to substantially improve the independence and overall QoL for patients with MS.Trial registration numberNCT05835622.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS), a chronic neurodegenerative inflammatory disease of the central nervous system, primarily affecting the brain white matter, represents the leading cause of non-traumatic disability among young adults.1 While clinical manifestations are considered heterogeneous and depend on both lesion location and cumulative lesion burden over time,2 more than 75% of patients with MS experience mobility impairments leading to loss of independence3 and daily function in a worst-case scenario. Preserving walking ability is thereby a major therapeutic goal in MS management.4 In addition to rehabilitation programmes, assistive devices would help to support preserving mobility in patients with MS at home and daily life.

While ambulatory assistive devices (ie, crutches, walkers or ankle-foot orthoses) are commonly employed to support MS mobility by providing joint stability, they failed to address underlying muscle weakness. Furthermore, these assistive devices can increase energy expenditure, leading to aggravated fatigue and functional limitations.5 Over the past two decades, robot-assisted gait training has emerged as an innovative rehabilitation approach, enabling intensive and repetitive gait training under standardised conditions and demonstrated significant benefits in improving gait parameters when combined with conventional therapy.68 More recently, technological advances have led to the development of lighter, mobile robotic exoskeletons, which offer greater mobility,9 a more natural gait pattern and increased portability.6 Lighter exoskeletons, providing assistance at one or two joints, have been used for both rehabilitation and personal mobility in neurological patients.10 11 Their relatively lower cost makes them promising for intensive, repetitive gait training and potentially for daily walking assistance.6

In a randomised cross-over trial, McGibbon et al12 showed significant improvement of unassisted walking performance using a 2-week lower-extremity exoskeleton at home in 29 patients with MS. In addition, the authors reported that functional capability benefits were associated with quality of life.13 In a recent systematic review with meta-analysis, including seven controlled randomised trials and 240 patients with MS, Christodoulou et al14 demonstrated that robotic and exoskeleton-assisted gait training provide quality of life improvement. The authors suggested to assess both quality of life and functional capacity so as to determine the full impact of exoskeleton in global health in patients with MS. Furthermore, long-term functional and quality of life benefits of light lower-limb exoskeleton home use have yet to be determined.

The ExHomeSEP study is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of 8-week home use of a lightweight robotic lower-limb exoskeleton as a walking-assistance device on quality of life in individuals with MS-related moderate gait impairment through an 8-week randomised cross-over study with 3-month follow-up. The secondary objectives will be to evaluate gait and mobility performance with and without wearing the exoskeleton, lower limb spasticity (quadriceps, hamstrings, adductors and triceps surae), perceived self-efficacy, confidence in walking, walking fatigability and endurance, affective state. In addition, daily walking distance in home environment, user satisfaction, acceptability and acceptance of the device are to be collected.

Methods/design

Design and setting

This protocol complies with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 guidelines for protocols of clinical trials.

The ExHomeSEP study is a prospective multicentre, cross-over, open study comparing gait with lower-limb exoskeleton use at home (interventional group) and without any device (control group). The study will be conducted at six French investigational sites, in Saint-Hélier Foundation in Rennes, Lille Catholic University Hospital, Montpellier Propara Rehabilitation center, Angers University Hospital – Les Capucins, Lyon Civil Hospital and Nantes University Hospital. The study was approved by the French Agency for the Safety of Health Products (ANSM, Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament) as well as by the Ethics Committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord-Ouest II) n°ID-RCB : 2021-A03094-37, and was registered on 9 December 2021, at Clinicaltrials.gov (number: NCT05835622; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05835622). Recruitment started on 2 February 2024 and is expected to end on January 2026.

Eligibility criteria

For eligibility, a patient must be diagnosed with MS according to the revised 2017 McDonald diagnostic criteria, in the relapsing form at least 6 months after a relapse period, or in the progressive form. Patient must meet all of the inclusion criteria to be eligible to participate in this clinical investigation. Any subject meeting a non-inclusion criterion will not be included. Subjects will be assessed for eligibility for the clinical investigation specific inclusion/exclusion criteria through the baseline visit.

Inclusion criteria

To be selected, a patient must be 18 years or older, with Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score between 5 and 6.5 related to MS,15 living at home, height taller than 1.52 m (as recommended by exoskeleton providers), hip muscle strength testing score: Medical Research Council (MRC) scale ranging from 0 (no muscle contraction) to 5 (normal strength) (MRC 1976) ≥3+ (flexors and extensors), knee muscle strength testing score: MRC≥2 (flexors and extensors), Berg Balance Scale score >20 affiliated with a social security scheme or benefiting from such a scheme, and given his/her written consent to participate in this study.

Non-inclusion criteria

Patient has comprehension disorders preventing completion of the protocol, inability to walk unaided for 10 min, scheduled rehabilitation stay during the clinical investigation follow-up period, diagnosed psychiatric disorders that may impact the experience of discontinuing technical assistance at the end of the protocol, severe cognitive impairment: Mini-Mental State Examination score<24, if there is any doubt regarding a participant’s cognitive abilities beyond these test results, the individual will not be enrolled, person deprived of liberty by judicial or administrative decision, person receiving psychiatric care or admitted to a health or social care facility for reasons other than research, spasticity>3 on the Held and Tardieu scale affecting hamstrings, quadriceps, adductors or triceps surae, complete paralysis of the lower limbs, severe osteoporosis, hip and/or knee arthroplasties sufficiently limiting range of motion for walking, sitting or squatting, severe vascular disorders of the lower limbs (of such severity that they would prevent safe tightening and wearing of the Keeogo system cuffs around the legs and exercising or would limit mobility or balance to the point of making safe use of Keeogo for walking impossible), existing wounds or skin lesions at the location where the device is worn, pregnant women or postpartum women who have not completed lumbopelvic recovery, orthopaedic disorders of the lower limb (knee flexion contracture, hip flexion contracture or ankle equinus) that could prevent satisfactory fitting of Keeogo, uncontrolled epilepsy or tremors, uncontrolled autonomic dysreflexia, severe balance disorders, neurological conditions (any condition preventing safe control of balance and/or limb movement at any time while wearing the Keeogo system), conditions preventing safe performance of moderate-intensity exercise will not be included.

Group allocation, randomisation

Patients eligible for inclusion will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio, using a minimisation algorithm stratified by study centre, to receive either Sequence 1 (experimental, washout, then control phase) or Sequence 2 (control, washout, then experimental phase), each period lasting 8 weeks. The experimental phase will consist of lower-limb exoskeleton use at home and the control phase will consist of individualised physical exercise recommendations. Trial duration per participant will be 9 months with a 6-month cross-over period and a 3-month follow-up. The study design is presented in figure 1.

Procedures

Patients will be recruited during follow-up visits with their physician-investigator or physiotherapist-investigator and informed about the objective and general nature of the study. They will then be provided with a patient information sheet and an informed consent form (online supplemental materials). The trial duration for each participant will be 9 months.

Experimental phase/exoskeleton training

Exoskeleton device description

The lower-limb exoskeleton phase will be performed at home with the Keeogo walking-assist exoskeleton developed and commercialised by the Quebec-based company B-Temia (St Augustin-de-Desmaures, Québec, Canada). Using sensors located at the hips and knees, powered knee joint actuators and built-in artificial intelligence software, the robotic system detects the required movement and provides the level of assistance necessary to perform the action. The Keeogo walking-assist exoskeleton was designed to improve walking, strength, endurance and stability.

Users wear the exoskeleton using a pelvic belt and a thigh and leg strapping system (figure 2). With proper training, users can independently put on the Keeogo device. The controller recognises standing, gait (walking and running), stair climbing and descent, sitting and rising from a chair, transitioning from sitting to standing, squatting, lunging and other locomotor tasks. The knee motor provides assistance to the user during specific phases of the task but neither initiates nor terminates the movements, while hip motion in the device is passive. The shin guards can accommodate most ankle-foot orthoses, and the device can be used with other assistive devices (canes, crutches, walkers, rollators, wheelchairs, etc). Including the hip-mounted battery, the total weight of the Keeogo device is 6.8 kg.

Photograph of the exoskeleton Keeogo (member of lab photograph).

The Keeogo device can be customised for each participant by adjusting the thigh length, pelvic belt and leg straps to achieve optimal suspension and alignment. The device is then fine-tuned by adjusting assistance parameters via a tablet connected to the device. Once activated, the participant practices walking and rising from a squat position, with further adjustments made as necessary. Five 2 hour fitting and training sessions will be scheduled for all participants. Participants will then complete the Keeogo Skills Test to assess their ability to use the exoskeleton independently. All settings and parameters are documented for subsequent sessions (stored locally on a tablet). Certain Keeogo parameters, reflecting the level of technical assistance provided by the device, will be recorded in the Case Report Form (CRF).

Training (5 days)

This phase begins at T0 or T2, depending on the patient’s randomisation arm. During the first week, patients will receive specific training on the use and wearing of the Keeogo robotic walking exoskeleton for 5 days. The goal of this training is to ensure familiarity with the device. This 1-week learning phase will consist of two daily sessions of 1 hour each with a professional experienced in Keeogo use. This programme was developed in collaboration with B-Temia to fully exploit the exoskeleton’s assistance potential.

Each session, lasting 1 hour, will focus on standing balance exercises (static and dynamic), lower-limb strengthening exercises (weight-bearing), aerobic walking sessions, transfer sequences (sit-to-stand, kneel-to-stand, etc), stair and slope navigation, passing and avoiding obstacles, walking on various outdoor surfaces, rehabilitation activities such as preparing meals, using the toilet, participating in leisure activities, and entering/exiting a vehicle, learning to manage the assistance level (1–5) according to activity type, current fatigue, learning to put on, take off, and store the device, learning battery management, including monitoring charge and replacing/recharging batteries.

Except for the two daily training sessions, and based on the referring therapist’s clinical judgement, patients will be encouraged to progressively use the exoskeleton independently within the facility. At the end of training, an evaluation will be performed to validate the patient’s ability to safely use Keeogo independently at home. This evaluation will verify the patient’s ability to (i) put on and take off the device independently, (ii) store the device independently or with minimal assistance, (iii) contact technical support if needed, (iv) select the appropriate assistance level for each situation, (v) walk 10 m, (vi) sit on a chair and (vii) stand up from a chair. Successful completion of all these tests is required to validate training and allow home use of the device.

Exoskeleton home use

At the end of the training week, the Keeogo device home will be provided to the patients and they will be asked to wear it daily from waking until bedtime over the next 7 weeks for any mobility-related activity, both indoors and outdoors, with a minimum of 3 hours of use per 24-hour period. Participants. During this phase, patients will be advised to follow standard health recommendations for physical activity, similarly to the control phase. Patients will maintain a daily log recording hour of exoskeleton use to monitor adherence. An ActiGraph activity monitor will also be provided to measure physical activity during this phase.

End of experimental phase

The experimental phase ends at T1 or T3, depending on the randomisation arm. During this end of experimental phase visit walking tests and spatiotemporal gait analysis will be performed with and without the exoskeleton. Questionnaires Technical Aid Satisfaction Scale (ESAT) and Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) will be completed. In addition, patients will return the ActiGraph, daily log, and Keeogo device.

Control Phase

Start of control phase

This phase will start at T0 or T2, depending on the patient’s randomisation arm. During the first control phase visit, patients will return home for 8 weeks with advice regarding regular physical activity based on WHO recommendations for adults with disabilities and adapted to the patient’s capacity.

Recommendations provided to the patients will be to perform at least 150 min per week of moderate-intensity endurance activity and at least 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity endurance activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous activities; at least two weekly sessions of moderate or higher intensity muscle-strengthening activities; varied multi-component activities emphasising functional balance and moderate or higher intensity strength exercises at least three times per week to improve functional capacity and prevent falls. In addition, we will advise patients to reduce sedentary time by replacing it with physical activity of any intensity (including low-intensity) and to exceed recommended levels of moderate-to-vigorous activity to mitigate the negative effects of high sedentary behaviour.

An ActiGraph will also be provided to measure physical activity during this phase, and patients will be asked to wear it daily from waking until bedtime.

End of the control phase

The control phase ends at T1 or T3, depending on the patient’s randomisation arm. During this visit, assessments and questionnaires will be completed according to the visit schedule. Patients will return the ActiGraph to the study centre.

Risks

As with any walking orthosis, there is a minimal risk of skin injury (abrasion from thigh straps). The risk of skin injury is mitigated by padding the straps. However, particular attention will be paid to areas under pressure: visual skin checks by therapists and training on proper device placement will be conducted. The risk of falling may be increased due to the addition of an exoskeleton, which adds 6.8 kg of weight to the body. Safety was carefully considered in this study. Participants were selected based on a pre-study assessment of fall risk and functional capacity, and detailed protocols for fall prevention, emergency procedures, and remote monitoring were implemented. The device’s technical specifications contributed to minimising the risk of falls during home use. No interactions are expected with concomitant medical treatments. The increased risk of falling is managed through training in the use of the exoskeleton, the presence of therapists during learning sessions, and the use of any necessary walking aids (canes, etc.). Given the expected benefits and the very low risk to patients, the benefit-risk ratio of this study is considered favourable.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary objective is the mean absolute change in the quality of life assessed by the Functional Assessment of Multiple Sclerosis (FAMS) between experimental and control phase from baseline to 8 weeks.16 FAMS consists of 44 items scored across six quality-of-life domains17: mobility (seven items), symptoms (seven items), emotional well-being (seven items), general contentment (seven items), fatigue (nine items) and family/social well-being (seven items). Patients have to indicate the response that best reflects their experience over the past 7 days. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). The total FAMS score (ranging from 0 to 176) is obtained by summing the scores of the six domains: mobility (0–28), symptoms (0–28), emotional well-being (0–28), general contentment (0–28), fatigue (0–36) and family/social well-being (0–28).17 18

Process measurement

The screening visit is a routine care visit scheduled before the baseline visit (T0). The information visit will be conducted by a physician or physiotherapist-investigator during a consultation. The study will be proposed to all individuals with MS who have an EDSS score between 5 and 6.5 and meet all eligibility criteria. All study procedures will be explained to the patients, and a written information sheet describing the study will be provided. A minimum reflection period of 3 days will be granted before obtaining written informed consent. Pre-selected patients who wish to participate in the study will be included during this visit. During the visit, the physician or physiotherapist-investigator will verify inclusion and exclusion criteria before obtaining written informed consent. The consent form will be signed in two copies, with one copy provided to the participant. As part of this verification, a urine pregnancy test will be performed for all women of childbearing age who are not post-menopausal to ensure the absence of pregnancy. If the pregnancy test is not performed, the reason must be documented in the patient’s file. The randomisation occurs.

Each patient will then be randomly assigned to a study group (1:1 ratio) via the eCRF:

Group 1: Experimental phase with exoskeleton (8 weeks) followed by the washout (8 weeks) and the control phase (8 weeks).

Group 2: Control phase (8 weeks) followed by the washout (8 weeks) and the experimental phase with exoskeleton (8 weeks).

Group 2: Control phase (8 weeks) followed by the washout (8 weeks) and the experimental phase with exoskeleton (8 weeks).

For organisational reasons, it will be acceptable for this inclusion/randomisation visit to be conducted up to 1 week before the start of the first study phase, if necessary. It should be noted that all assessments (other than eligibility) will be performed on the day of the start of the first clinical investigation phase, including patient-reported questionnaires.

At T0, the following questionnaires and assessments will be performed: EDSS, Mini Mental Scale Test (to assess the cognitive impairment), MMT on knee and hip (to quantify the muscle weakness), Berg Balance Scale (to evaluate balance), FAMS (to assess quality of life), EMIF-MS (to evaluate fatigue), HADS (to measure anxiety and depression), Rosenberg scale (which is a self-esteem scale), Held and Tardieu scale (to quantify spasticity by assessing the muscle’s response to stretch applied at given velocities), walking tests such as two min-walk, 10M-walk, TUG, FST, SST, spatio-temporal analysis and Actigraph data. For the experimental group, we assess the exoskeleton’s capacity for home use.

Participants in the experimental group will undergo 1-week exoskeleton training before returning home for 7 weeks. Participants in the control group will return home with an exercise booklet for 8 weeks.

At T1, participant questionnaires and assessments will be performed in the same way as during the baseline visit (T0); additionally, with ESAT (satisfaction device), UTAUT (Technology intention to use) and average daily device logbook. The washout period will begin for 8 weeks without any device or exercises.

For the second period, at T2, questionnaires and assessments will be performed: FAMS, EMIF-MS, HADS, Rosenberg scale, Held and Tardieu scale, walking tests (2 min-walk, 10M-walk, TUG, FST, SST, spatio-temporal analysis and Actigraph data). For the experimental group, we assess the exoskeleton’s capacity for home use. At T3 after the second period, the evaluation will be the same as at T1.

The follow-up will begin after the 8-week second period. The T4 visit will take place 12 weeks after the end of the second home-based period and will follow the same procedures as the T2 visit.

Unscheduled patient visits could occur between scheduled study follow-up visits due to patient discontinuation from the study or management of any complication.

During the 9-month follow-up, patients will have five study visits: T0, T1 (T0+8 weeks), T2 (T0+16 weeks), T3 (T2+8 weeks) and T4 (T0+9 months) to assess the various study endpoints.

So as to reduce measurement and observer bias, outcome assessors were fully blinded to participants’ treatment phase and intervention status. Participant blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, but efforts were made to maintain objective assessment.

Sample size and power calculations

The ExHomSEP is designed with the primary objective to improve patients’ quality of life with at least a moderate effect size is expected. Considering recruitment feasibility and the required sample size, Cohen’s d is set at d=0.6. Effect size was defined as small, medium and large effect sizes for d=0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, respectively. Based on current research in the applied literature, Sawilowsky suggests revising these empirical rules: d=0.1 (very small), d=0.2 (small), d=0.5 (medium), d=0.8 (large), d=1.2 (very large) and d=2.0 (huge).

Based on a cross-over design, with statistical considerations including Cohen’s d=0.6, significance level=0.05, power=0.8, paired test, two-sided alternative, the expected study population is 24 individuals. Including 15% for potential dropouts or missing data, the total sample size to recruit is 28 participants. Both the global score (primary outcome) and each sub-score will be analysed. Each of the six study centres is expected to recruit 4–5 patients over a 24-month recruitment period.

Procedures to minimise bias

Randomisation using simple random (and therefore unpredictable) sequence would prevent clinicians from identifying the upcoming allocations. Although the randomisation process is supposed to reduce the differences between baseline characteristics of the compared groups, the FAMS will be also adjusted at baseline to account for potential selection bias. To reduce performance bias, aside from the treatment itself, all patients will receive the same care and attention.

Statistical analysis

Data will be analysed according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for non-drug trials. A flow chart will be used to report patient flow, recruitment and dropouts. Statistical analysis will include all evaluated patients (intention-to-treat analysis). Analyses will be performed using R software, V.4.0.1, by the biostatistician of the Pôle Saint-Hélier Laboratory.

Final analysis

The study includes a single final analysis.

Initially, a complete group-wise analysis will be performed. This will include point estimates, counts and percentages for qualitative variables, and means, SD and IQRs for quantitative variables. The normality of quantitative variables will also be assessed.

For baseline data, a paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test (if assumptions are not met) will be used to compare quantitative variables. A paired χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test (if assumptions are not met) will be used to compare qualitative variables between the two groups at baseline.

Quality of life, measured by the FAMS score, is considered a quantitative variable. Data will be compared using a paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test, depending on the distribution normality. The same analytical strategy will be applied to secondary quantitative endpoints.

For secondary qualitative endpoints, a paired χ2 test will be used, supplemented by a logistic regression model if necessary for adjustment purposes.

Adverse events will be coded according to the MedDRA classification and analysed descriptively.

All tests will be conducted with a 5% significance threshold (p<0.05). This is an intention-to-treat study, meaning that all evaluated patients will be included in the analysis.

To verify the absence of a treatment period effect, a Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test will be performed comparing the two treatment sequences, AB and BA. If a period effect is present, only the results from the first phase will be retained for the analysis.

Discussion

The primary objective of the ExHomSEP multicentre, randomised, cross-over trial is to evaluate the effectiveness of an 8 week, home-based intervention using a lightweight robotic lower-limb exoskeleton (Keego) as a walking-assistance device compared with a no-device control period. The core endpoint is the impact of exoskeleton use on the quality of life of patients with MS. This approach represents a crucial potential achievement for patients experiencing gait impairment, a common symptom of MS that severely limits social participation.27 28 We expected that participants’ quality of life, mobility and fatigue will improve following the period of exoskeleton home use. Walking performance will be comprehensively assessed using a battery of complementary tests, including the Two-min Walk, 10 M Walk, TUG, FST and SST, alongside spatiotemporal gait analysis and daily walking distance monitoring. In addition, we will evaluate self-reported fatigue (EMIF-MS) and muscle strength (Aschworth scale) to determine the potential added value of daily lower-limb exoskeleton use at home.

While MS is a chronic neurodegenerative disease, lightweight robotic lower-limb exoskeleton offers a pathway to maintain autonomy and enhance quality of life. Our study will help to define the role of mobile exoskeletons among technological solutions for walking impairment, with potential clinical implications for other neurological disabilities, such as incomplete spinal cord injury, neuromuscular diseases, peripheral deficits such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease or residual deficits following radiculopathic injury.

Limitations of this study should be considered. First, it should be noted that participants’ awareness of using a high-tech device may have generated expectations or a placebo effect, potentially affecting patient-reported outcomes such as FAMS scores. Objective secondary outcomes were included to complement these measures, and the possible influence of expectation effects will be discussed in light of the study results. In addition, we cannot rule out the potential carryover effects in the group allocated to the exoskeleton first. However, based on available literature presenting wash-out periods from one to 8 weeks,12 clinical reasoning, and the progressive nature of the disease, we may safely assume that the selection of an 8-week wash-out period is conservative and robust. Moreover, concerning the selection of population, baseline depression was considered in participant selection, with exclusion of patients with psychiatric disorders that could affect the experience of discontinuing technical assistance. Depression and anxiety were assessed using the HADS. Stratified analyses will be conducted if differences in baseline depression are observed between groups to reduce potential confounding effects. Finally, the control group received only standard care, consisting of physical activity recommendations typically provided in this chronic neurological condition, rather than an active intervention. This approach reflects the primary aim of the study, which was to evaluate the exoskeleton’s use at home as an assistive device, rather than the effectiveness of a rehabilitation programme. Including an active control group in future studies to compare home-based rehabilitation programme with and without exoskeleton would help to better distinguish the device-specific effects from the general effects of increased activity and clinical attention.

Anticipated benefits, in terms of both public health and medico-economics, involve enhancing care for patients with MS within the financial and organisational limits of the healthcare system. Although daily use of a lightweight, robotic lower-limb exoskeleton demonstrates efficacy compared with a control period, its integration as a standard recommendation for patients with MS with walking impairments requires confirmation of minimal complication risk and optimal user-friendliness. Moreover, a robust evaluation of its potential benefits is essential to justify insurance reimbursement and effectively decrease the long-term cost of care. Cost-effectiveness is an important consideration, particularly for potential insurance coverage; consequently, a comprehensive medico-economic study should be conducted after providing evidence demonstrating the efficacy and impact of exoskeleton use within the patient with MS population.

Future technological advances must minimise the weight and enhance the usability of such lightweight robotic lower- or upper-limb exoskeletons. These improvements are critical to maximising the impact on the quality of life and functional capacity of individuals with neurodegenerative diseases. Achieving this goal requires concerted engineering efforts, particularly in minimising the volume and maximising the efficiency of the battery and torque controller units, while improving the ergonomics for autonomous use.

Overall, our study will provide novel insights supporting the adoption of lightweight robotic lower-limb exoskeletons for enhancing the quality of life of patients with MS in a home-based setting.

Trial status

The ExHomeSEP clinical investigation began patient recruitment in February 2024, and recruitment will be completed in January 2026. Follow-up measurements will be closed in August 2026.
