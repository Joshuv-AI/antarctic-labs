Compositions of n n -homomorphisms
Darij Grinberg

Abstract
Abstract. We study n n -homomorphisms in the sense of Khudaverdian–Voronov, but generalized to maps from arbitrary rings to arbitrary commutative rings. We show that the sum of an n n -homomorphism and an m m -homomorphism is an ( n + m ) \left(n+m\right) -homomorphism, and that the composition of an n n -homomorphism and an m m -homomorphism is an n ​ m nm -homomorphism. The proofs are entirely combinatorial.

0.1
n
-homomorphisms

The notion of an
n
-homomorphism is a generalization of the notion of a
pseudorepresentation, which was introduced by Taylor in
[
Taylor91
]
(based
on an idea of Wiles
[
Wiles88
, proof of Lemma 2.2.3]
).

We first introduce some notations.
Rings
are always understood to be
associative and unital (but not necessarily commutative); ring morphisms
preserve the unity (unless we speak of “nonunital ring
morphisms”). For any nonnegative integer
n
, we let
\left[n\right]
denote the set
\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, and we let

S_{n}
denote the
n
-th symmetric group (i.e., the group of permutations of
this set
\left[n\right]
). If
\sigma
is a permutation of a finite set
(e.g., of
\left[n\right]
), then
\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}
shall
denote the sign of
\sigma
. (If
\sigma\in S_{n}
, then
\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}=\left(-1\right)^{\ell\left(\sigma\right)}
, where

\ell\left(\sigma\right)
is the number of inversions of
\sigma
.) The

cycles
of a permutation
\sigma
shall be understood in the usual
combinatorial sense; for example, the permutation

\displaystyle\left[7\right]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left[7\right],

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\mapsto 8-i

of
\left[7\right]
has the four cycles
\left(1,7\right)
,
\left(2,6\right)
,
\left(3,5\right)
and
\left(4\right)
. (Fixed points
are
1
-cycles.) Note that cycles are only well-defined up to cyclic rotation;
for example,
\left(2,6\right)
and
\left(6,2\right)
are the same cycle.

In
[
BucRee97
]
, Buchstaber and Rees defined the notion of an

n
-homomorphism
between commutative rings; it was studied further in

[
BucRee01
]
,
[
BucRee04
]
and
[
KhuVor20
]
(among other works),
although mostly only for commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras. We shall here
generalize it to a notion of
n
-homomorphisms from an arbitrary (not
necessarily commutative) ring
A
to a commutative ring
B
. We define it as follows:

Definition 0.1
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
B
be a commutative ring. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
be any
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map.

(a)

We say that
f
is
central
if we have

f\left(aa^{\prime}\right)=f\left(a^{\prime}a\right)\qquad\text{for all
}a,a^{\prime}\in A.

(b)

If
f
is central, and if
n
is a nonnegative integer,
then we define the
\mathbb{Z}
-multilinear map
f_{n}:A^{n}\rightarrow B
by

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

(1)

(we will see in a moment why this is well-defined). For example, for
n=3
,
this is saying that

\displaystyle f_{3}\left(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{1}\right)f\left(a_{2}\right)f\left(a_{3}\right)-f\left(a_{1}\right)f\left(a_{2}a_{3}\right)-f\left(a_{2}\right)f\left(a_{1}a_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-f\left(a_{3}\right)f\left(a_{1}a_{2}\right)+f\left(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\right)+f\left(a_{1}a_{3}a_{2}\right)

(here, the first addend corresponds to the permutation
\sigma=\operatorname*{id}\in S_{3}
with its three cycles
\left(1\right),\left(2\right),\left(3\right)
; the second addend corresponds to the
transposition
t_{2,3}\in S_{3}
that swaps
2
and
3
and has two cycles

\left(1\right)
and
\left(2,3\right)
; the following two addends
similarly correspond to the transpositions
t_{1,3}
and
t_{1,2}
; and the
last two addends correspond to the two
3
-cycles in
S_{3}
).

Note that the right hand side of (
1
) is well-defined, since
the centrality of
f
ensures that the value
f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)
does not depend on where we start indexing the
cycle
c
(indeed, if we replace the cycle
\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
by its cyclic rotation
\left(i_{p},i_{p+1},\ldots,i_{k},i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{p-1}\right)
, then the centrality of
f
shows
that
f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)=f\left(a_{i_{p}}a_{i_{p+1}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{p-1}}\right)
).

The map
f_{n}
is called the
n
-Frobenius map
of
f
. For instance,
for all
a,b,c\in A
, we have

\displaystyle f_{0}\left({}\right)

\displaystyle=1;

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(a\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a\right);

\displaystyle f_{2}\left(a,b\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a\right)f\left(b\right)-f\left(ab\right);

\displaystyle f_{3}\left(a,b,c\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a\right)f\left(b\right)f\left(c\right)-f\left(a\right)f\left(bc\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-f\left(b\right)f\left(ac\right)-f\left(c\right)f\left(ab\right)+f\left(abc\right)+f\left(acb\right).

As we will soon see (Proposition
0.6
), the map
f_{n}
can also be
defined recursively by

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{n}\right)f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i}a_{n},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right).\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

(2)

(c)

Let
n
be a nonnegative integer. We say that
f
is an

n
-homomorphism
(or
Frobenius
n
-homomorphism
) if
f

is central and satisfies
f_{n+1}=0
(identically).

Example 0.2
.

The only
0
-homomorphism is the zero map. The
1
-homomorphisms are just the
nonunital ring homomorphisms. The
2
-homomorphisms are the central maps

f:A\rightarrow B
that satisfy

\displaystyle f\left(a\right)f\left(b\right)f\left(c\right)-f\left(a\right)f\left(bc\right)-f\left(b\right)f\left(ac\right)-f\left(c\right)f\left(ab\right)+f\left(abc\right)+f\left(acb\right)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c\in A.

An example of an
n
-homomorphism is the trace map
\operatorname*{Tr}:B^{n\times n}\rightarrow B
from the matrix ring
B^{n\times n}
(sending
each
n\times n
-matrix to its trace). Indeed, the fact that

\operatorname*{Tr}\nolimits_{n+1}=0
on
B^{n\times n}
is known as the

fundamental trace identity
for
n\times n
-matrices, and goes back to
Frobenius; proofs can be found (e.g.) in
[
Laue87
, Corollary]
,

[
Dotsen11
, Theorem 1]
,
[
Morel19
, §VII.7.3.2]
or
[
Proces76
, Theorem 4.3
(b)]
.

This general concept of an
n
-homomorphism also covers another classical
notion: that of a pseudocharacter, as considered (e.g.) in
[
Dotsen11
]
,

[
Morel19
, §VII.7.3]
,
[
Bellai10
, Definition 2.2]
,
[
Rouqui96
]
,

[
Chenev08
]
, generalizing the pseudorepresentations introduced by Taylor
in
[
Taylor91
]
(which, in turn, were based on an idea of Wiles
[
Wiles88
, proof
of Lemma 2.2.3]
). Again, much of the literature requires that
A
is
a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra or at least
n!
is invertible. If we turn a blind eye
to this requirement, then
n
-homomorphisms are more or less the same as
pseudocharacters: A
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map
f:A\rightarrow B
from a ring
A

to a commutative ring
B
is an
n
-homomorphism if and only if the
corresponding
B
-linear map

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}:B\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}A

\displaystyle\rightarrow B,

\displaystyle b\otimes a

\displaystyle\mapsto bf\left(a\right)

is a pseudocharacter of degree
n
. Conversely, a
B
-linear map

f:A\rightarrow B
from a
B
-algebra
A
to its (commutative) base ring
B

is a pseudocharacter of degree
n
if and only if it is an
n
-homomorphism.
Thus, the concept of an
n
-homomorphism and that of a pseudocharacter of
degree
n
subsume each other (again ignoring the invertibility requirement).
Apparently the two respective communities (pseudocharacters and
n
-homomorphisms) are mostly unaware of one another, even though both credit
Frobenius for the original idea.

In this note, I will prove certain basic (but nontrivial) properties of

n
-homomorphisms in full generality, most importantly
[
KhuVor20
, Theorem
3.2]
, without assuming
A
commutative or
n!
invertible. The
method used in
[
KhuVor20
]
becomes unusable in this generality, so one is
forced to do combinatorics.

The first main result of this note is a generalization of the first part of

[
KhuVor20
, Theorem 3.2]
(also part of
[
Rouqui96
, Lemme 2.8]
):
1
1

1

The notation
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all nonnegative
integers (including
0
).

Theorem 0.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be two rings, with
B
commutative. Let

n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f:A\rightarrow B
be an
n
-homomorphism, and let

g:A\rightarrow B
be an
m
-homomorphism. Then,
f+g
is an
\left(n+m\right)
-homomorphism.

In short: the sum of an
n
-homomorphism with an
m
-homomorphism is an

\left(n+m\right)
-homomorphism.

The second main result generalizes the second part of
[
KhuVor20
, Theorem
3.2]
:

Theorem 0.4
.

Let
A
,
B
and
C
be three rings, with
B
and
C

commutative. Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f:B\rightarrow C
be an

n
-homomorphism, and let
g:A\rightarrow B
be an
m
-homomorphism. Then,

f\circ g
is an
nm
-homomorphism.

In short: the composition of an
n
-homomorphism with an
m
-homomorphism is
an
nm
-homomorphism.

The proofs will use some auxiliary results that may well be useful on their
own. In particular, Theorem
0.17
is a formula for the
n
-Frobenius
maps of a composition of two central maps; this formula was found by the
GPT-5.4 LLM when I asked it for a proof of Theorem
0.4
.

Remark 0.5
.

It is worth mentioning the “aspirational” properties of
n
-homomorphisms and pseudocharacters, as they can be
helpful. A pseudocharacter of degree
n
wishes to be the character of an

n
-dimensional representation of the algebra (in the sense that the latter
are always instances of the former, and in some sense “generic” ones; see, e.g.,
[
Dotsen11
, Proposition 2]
).
An
n
-homomorphism wishes to be a sum of
n
ring homomorphisms (see

[
KhuVor20
, §1.2]
). The former wish can be fulfilled under certain
restrictive conditions (see, e.g.,
[
Morel19
, §VII.7.3.4]
). I don’t know
when and to what extent the latter can be fulfilled. However, when it can be
fulfilled, Theorem
0.3
becomes obvious, and Theorem
0.4
also
becomes easy (if
f:B\rightarrow C
is a sum of
n
(nonunital) ring
homomorphisms, and if
g
is an
m
-homomorphism, then
f\circ g
is an

nm
-homomorphism, as can be easily proved using Theorem
0.3
). Sadly,
this approach seems to be unsuited for the generality in which we have stated
the above theorems.

0.2
Recursion and explicit formula

First, I will pay a debt from Definition
0.1

(b)
, by proving
the equivalence of the two definitions of
n
-Frobenius maps:

Proposition 0.6
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
B
be a commutative ring. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
be a central
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map.

The recursive definition (
2
) of
f_{n}
(with the base case

f_{0}\left({}\right):=1
) is equivalent to the explicit definition (
1
).

Proof 0.7

(Proof sketch.)
.

We use (
1
) as the definition of
f_{n}
. Thus,
we must prove that
f_{n}
satisfies the recursion (
2
).

Let
n\geq 1
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
. Then, (
1
)
becomes

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\sigma\left(i\right)=n\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

(3)

(since each
\sigma\in S_{n}
satisfies
\sigma\left(i\right)=n
for a
unique element
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
). We shall now rewrite
the addends of the outer sum here, showing that they correspond (up to sign)
to the addends on the right hand side of (

).

We start with the addend for
i=n
.

Each permutation
\tau\in S_{n-1}
can be extended to a permutation

\widehat{\tau}\in S_{n}
by setting
\widehat{\tau}\left(n\right):=n
and

\widehat{\tau}\left(i\right):=\tau\left(i\right)
for all
i<n
. The
assignment
\tau\mapsto\widehat{\tau}
defines a bijection from
S_{n-1}
to

\left\{\sigma\in S_{n}\ \mid\ \sigma\left(n\right)=n\right\}
. Let us
use this bijection to reindex a sum:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\sigma\left(n\right)=n\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\tau\in S_{n-1}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\widehat{\tau}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\\
\text{(since }\widehat{\tau}\text{

\text{inversions as }\tau\text{)}\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\widehat{\tau}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=f\left(a_{n}\right)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)\\
\text{(since the cycles of }\widehat{\tau}\text{ are the cycles of }\tau\\
\text{plus the additional
}1\text{-cycle }\left(n\right)\text{)}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\tau\in S_{n-1}}\left(-1\right)^{\tau}f\left(a_{n}\right)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle
of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{n}\right)\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\tau\in S_{n-1}}\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)\\

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{n}\right)f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right).

(4)

Now, let

. Let us define
n-1
elements

a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n-1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\left(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n-1}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle:=\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i}a_{n},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right).

(5)

That is, we set
a_{i}^{\prime}:=a_{i}a_{n}
and

a_{r}^{\prime}:=a_{r}\qquad\text{for all }r\neq i.

(6)

Let
t_{i,n}\in S_{n}
be the transposition that swaps
i
with
n
.

Let
\tau\in S_{n-1}
be any permutation. Then, the permutation
\widehat{\tau}\in S_{n}
(defined above) sends
n
to
n
. Hence, the permutation

\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}\in S_{n}
sends

. Moreover, this permutation

\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}
has “almost” the same cycles as
\tau
: Namely, let

d=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{\ell}\right)

be the cycle of
\tau
that contains
i
, indexed in such a way that

. (If
\tau
fixes

, then this is a

.) Then, the cycles of
\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}
are exactly the

\tau
, except that the cycle

\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}

distinct from

\text{is a cycle
of }\tau\\
\text{distinct from }d\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right).

However, for each cycle

a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}=a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i_{k}}^{\prime}

(because

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{j_{1}}^{\prime}a_{j_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots a_{j_{\ell}}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i_{k}}^{\prime}\right).

(10)

Finally,

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\widehat{\tau}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\\
\text{(since }\widehat{\tau}\text{ has the same}\\
\text{inversions as }\tau\text{)}\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{t_{i,n}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=-1\\
\text{(since transpositions}\\
\text{have sign }-1\text{)}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=-\left(-1\right)^{\tau}.

(11)

Forget that we fixed
\tau
. Thus, for each permutation
\tau\in S_{n-1}
, we
have constructed a permutation
\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}\in S_{n}
that
sends

to
n
and satisfies (
10
) and
(

). Moreover, the assignment
\tau\mapsto\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}
defines a bijection from
S_{n-1}
to
\left\{\sigma\in S_{n}\ \mid\ \sigma\left(i\right)=n\right\}
(because if

\sigma\in S_{n}
sends
i
to
n
, then
\sigma\circ t_{i,n}^{-1}
sends
n

to
n
and thus has the form
\widehat{\tau}
for a unique
\tau\in S_{n-1}
).
Let us use this bijection to reindex a sum:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\sigma\left(i\right)=n\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\tau\in S_{n-1}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=-\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\\
\text{(by
\eqref{eq.darij2.p2.pf.10})}\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\widehat{\tau}\circ t_{i,n}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i_{k}}^{\prime}\right)\\
\text{(by \eqref{eq.darij2.p2.pf.9})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\tau\in S_{n-1}}\left(-1\right)^{\tau}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle
of }\tau\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i_{k}}^{\prime}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=f_{n-1}\left(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n-1}^{\prime}\right)\\
\text{(by the definition of }f_{n-1}\text{)}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=-f_{n-1}\left(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n-1}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=-f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i}a_{n},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)

(12)

(by (
5
)).

Forget that we fixed
i
. So we have proved (
12
) for
each
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\right\}
. Now, splitting off the
i=n

addend from the outer sum on the right hand side of (
3
),
we obtain

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\sigma\left(n\right)=n\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=f\left(a_{n}\right)f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)\\
\text{(by \eqref{eq.darij2.p2.pf.2})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\ \ \underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{n};\\
\sigma\left(i\right)=n\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle
of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=-f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i}a_{n},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)\\
\text{(by
\eqref{eq.darij2.p2.pf.15})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=f\left(a_{n}\right)f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{n-1}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i}a_{n},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n-1}\right).

This proves (

). Thus, Proposition
0.6
is proved.

0.3
Symmetry of
n
-Frobenius maps

Next, we note something simple:

Proposition 0.8
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
B
be a commutative ring. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
be a central
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, the map
f_{n}:A^{n}\rightarrow B
is symmetric in its
n
inputs. That
is, if
\tau\in S_{n}
is any permutation and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
,
then

f_{n}\left(a_{\tau\left(1\right)},a_{\tau\left(2\right)},\ldots,a_{\tau\left(n\right)}\right)=f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right).

Proof 0.9
.

Let
\tau\in S_{n}
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
. Then,
(
1
) yields

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

(13)

and

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{\tau\left(1\right)},a_{\tau\left(2\right)},\ldots,a_{\tau\left(n\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{\tau\left(i_{1}\right)}a_{\tau\left(i_{2}\right)}\cdots a_{\tau\left(i_{k}\right)}\right).

(14)

However, recall that conjugate permutations have the same cycle type. More
concretely: If
\sigma\in S_{n}
is any permutation, then the cycles of the
permutation
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
are in bijection with the cycles
of
\sigma
: Namely, if
\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
is a cycle
of
\sigma
, then
\left(\tau\left(i_{1}\right),\tau\left(i_{2}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(i_{k}\right)\right)
is a cycle of

\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
, and this gives a 1-to-1 correspondence
between the cycles of
\sigma
and the cycles of
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
. Thus, if
\sigma\in S_{n}
is any permutation, then

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{\tau\left(i_{1}\right)}a_{\tau\left(i_{2}\right)}\cdots a_{\tau\left(i_{k}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right).

Substituting this into (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{\tau\left(1\right)},a_{\tau\left(2\right)},\ldots,a_{\tau\left(n\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}}\\
\text{(since conjugate permutations}\\
\text{have the same sign)}\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

(here, we have substituted
\sigma
for
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
in the
sum, because conjugation by
\tau
is a bijection from
S_{n}
onto itself).
Comparing this with (
13
), we obtain
f_{n}\left(a_{\tau\left(1\right)},a_{\tau\left(2\right)},\ldots,a_{\tau\left(n\right)}\right)=f_{n}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\right)
. This
proves Proposition
0.8
.

The proof of Proposition
0.8
can be somewhat generalized, essentially
replacing the permutation
\tau
by a bijection between two finite sets of
integers. We record the result, since it will prove useful later on:

Proposition 0.10
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
B
be a commutative ring. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
be a central
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map.

Let
U=\left\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\right\}
be a finite set of
integers (with
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}
distinct). Let
S_{U}
denote the
group of all permutations of
U
. Let
a_{u}
be an element of
A
for each

u\in U
. Then,

f_{n}\left(a_{u_{1}},a_{u_{2}},\ldots,a_{u_{n}}\right)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in S_{U}}\left(-1\right)^{\alpha}\prod\limits\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\alpha\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right).

Proof 0.11
.

Let
\tau:\left[n\right]\rightarrow U
be the map that sends each

i\in\left[n\right]
to
u_{i}
. This map
\tau
is a bijection (indeed,
it is surjective because
U=\left\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\right\}
, and
it is injective since
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}
are distinct). Hence, for
each permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, the composition
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
is a permutation of
U
. Thus, we obtain a map

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow S_{U},

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\mapsto\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1},

(15)

which is easily seen to be a group isomorphism. Intuitively speaking,

\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
is what you obtain if you take the permutation

\sigma
of
\left[n\right]
and rename each element
i\in\left[n\right]
(say, in the two-line notation of
\sigma
, or in the cycle
decomposition of
\sigma
) as
\tau\left(i\right)
. In particular, the
cycles of the permutation
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
(for a given

\sigma\in S_{n}
) are in bijection with the cycles of
\sigma
: Namely, if

\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
is a cycle of
\sigma
, then

\left(\tau\left(i_{1}\right),\tau\left(i_{2}\right),\ldots,\tau\left(i_{k}\right)\right)
is a cycle of
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
, and this gives a 1-to-1 correspondence between the cycles of

\sigma
and the cycles of
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
. Thus, if

\sigma\in S_{n}
is any permutation, then

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{\tau\left(i_{1}\right)}a_{\tau\left(i_{2}\right)}\cdots a_{\tau\left(i_{k}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right).

(16)

Moreover, if
\sigma\in S_{n}
is any permutation, then

\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}=\left(-1\right)^{\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}}.

(17)

(This is easiest to see by factoring
\sigma
into a product of
transpositions; see
[
Grinbe15
, Exercise 5.12]
.)

But each
i\in\left[n\right]
satisfies
a_{u_{i}}=a_{\tau\left(i\right)}
(since the definition of
\tau
yields
\tau\left(i\right)=u_{i}
, thus
a_{\tau\left(i\right)}=a_{u_{i}}
). Therefore,

\displaystyle f_{n}\left(a_{u_{1}},a_{u_{2}},\ldots,a_{u_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{n}\left(a_{\tau\left(1\right)},a_{\tau\left(2\right)},\ldots,a_{\tau\left(n\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}}\\
\text{(by
\eqref{pf.prop.3g.4})}\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{\tau\left(i_{1}\right)}a_{\tau\left(i_{2}\right)}\cdots a_{\tau\left(i_{k}\right)}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)\\
\text{(by \eqref{pf.prop.3g.3})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by \eqref{eq.darij1.1}, applied to }a_{\tau\left(i\right)}\text{ instead of }a_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(-1\right)^{\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in S_{U}}\left(-1\right)^{\alpha}\prod\limits\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\alpha\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{k}}\right)

(here, we have substituted
\alpha
for
\tau\circ\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}
in the
sum, since the map (
15
) is a bijection). Renaming the index

\left(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\right)
as
\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
on the right hand side, we can rewrite this as

f_{n}\left(a_{u_{1}},a_{u_{2}},\ldots,a_{u_{n}}\right)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in S_{U}}\left(-1\right)^{\alpha}\prod\limits\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\alpha\end{subarray}}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right).

Thus, Proposition
0.10
is proved.

0.4
The sum of two
n
-homomorphisms

The proof of Theorem
0.3
will require two lemmas:

Lemma 0.12
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
B
be a commutative ring. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
be any central
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
f_{m}=0
. Then,
f_{n}=0
for all
n\geq m
.

Proof 0.13

(Proof sketch.)
.

If some positive integer
n
satisfies
f_{n-1}=0
, then

f_{n}=0
as well (by the recursion (
2
)). Hence, Lemma

0.12
follows easily by induction on
n
.

Lemma 0.14
.

Let
A
and
B
be two rings, with
B
commutative. Let

f:A\rightarrow B
and
g:A\rightarrow B
be two central
\mathbb{Z}
-linear
maps. Let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p}\in A
be some elements.

For any subset
I=\left\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right\}
of
\left[p\right]
(with
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}
distinct) and any central map

h:A\rightarrow B
, let us define

h_{I}\left(a\right):=h_{k}\left(a_{i_{1}},a_{i_{2}},\ldots,a_{i_{k}}\right)\in B.

(This is well-defined, i.e., does not depend on the order in which we label
the elements of
I
as
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}
, because Proposition

0.8
shows that the map
h_{k}
is symmetric.)

Then,

\left(f+g\right)_{p}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p}\right)=\sum\limits\limits_{U\sqcup V=\left[p\right]}f_{U}\left(a\right)\cdot g_{V}\left(a\right).

Here, the notation “
U\sqcup V=\left[p\right]
” means that
U
and
V
are two disjoint sets whose union
is
\left[p\right]
(that is,
U
is a subset of
\left[p\right]
, and

V
is its complement in
\left[p\right]
).

Proof 0.15

(Proof of Lemma
0.14
(sketched).)
.

From (
1
) (applied to

f+g
and
p
instead of
f
and
n
), we have

\displaystyle\left(f+g\right)_{p}\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{p}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\left(f+g\right)\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}_{=f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{p}}\left(-1\right)^{\sigma}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)\right).\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

(18)

Now, fix a permutation
\sigma\in S_{p}
. If we expand the product

\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle
of }\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)\right),

(19)

then we obtain a sum over all ways to choose, for each cycle
c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
of
\sigma
, one of the two addends of the
sum
f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)
. Such “ways to
choose” can be viewed as colorings of the cycles of
\sigma
, in which each cycle is either colored red (meaning that the
f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)
addend is chosen) or colored blue
(meaning that the
g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)

addend is chosen). Thus, the expanded form of (
19
) can be
written as follows:

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{coloring of all cycles of }\sigma\\
\text{in red and
blue}\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}\begin{cases}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right),&\text{if }c\text{ is
red};\\
g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right),&\text{if }c\text{ is
blue.}\end{cases}

Equivalently, instead of coloring cycles, we can just as well color the

elements
of these cycles red and blue (viz., all elements of all red
cycles are colored red, while all elements of all blue cycles are colored
blue). These colorings are not arbitrary, but must have the property that each
cycle is either completely red (i.e., all its elements are red) or completely
blue (i.e., all its elements are blue); in other words, they must have the
property that
\sigma
sends red elements to red elements and blue elements to
blue elements. Thus, the above expanded form of (
19
) can be
rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a
cycle of }\sigma\end{subarray}}\left(f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)+g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{coloring of all elements of }\left[p\right]\\
\text{in red and blue;}\\
\sigma\text{ sends red elements to red
elements}\\
\text{and blue elements to blue elements}\end{subarray}}\ \ \prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\\
\text{is a cycle of
}\sigma\end{subarray}}\begin{cases}f\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right),&\text{if }c\text{ is
red};\\
g\left(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}\right),&\text{if }c\text{ is
blue}\end{cases}

(where “
c
is red” means that all elements
of
c
are red, and likewise for “blue”).
