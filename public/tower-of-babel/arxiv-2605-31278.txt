Industrializing Prediction-Powered Inference: The GLIDE Library for Reliable GenAI and Agentic Systems Evaluation
Grégoire Martinon, Ibrahim Merad, Mohamed Raki

Abstract
Reliable evaluation of agentic systems requires unbiased estimates with valid uncertainty, but standard practice navigates between costly human annotation and biased LLM-as-judge proxies. Prediction-powered inference (PPI) combines both into debiased estimates with valid confidence intervals, yet its various methods remain scattered across papers under partial implementations. We introduce GLIDE, an open-source Python library that unifies state-of-the-art PPI estimators (PPI++, Stratified PPI, Predict-Then-Debias and its stratified variants, Active Statistical Inference) and samplers (uniform, stratified, active, cost-optimal) under a scipy-style API specialized to mean estimation. GLIDE ships with a reproducible Monte Carlo validation suite, an empirically grounded decision tree for method selection, and an agentic evaluation case study showing substantial annotation savings at equivalent precision. The GLIDE package is available at this URL: https://github.com/EmertonData/glide

Introduction

Consider a clinical decision-support agent that retrieves guidelines, reasons over a patient record, and proposes a treatment. Measuring whether such an agent behaves correctly requires expert clinician review of multi-step trajectories, a process where today’s two dominant practices both fail at scale. Full human annotation is reliable but slow and costly; LLM-as-judge is fast and cheap but biased, especially for knowledge-intensive, domain-specific tasks.

Rather than choosing between human reliability and proxy scale,
prediction-powered inference
(PPI)
(
1
;
2
)
combines them to obtain the strengths of both: a small set of human annotations with a large set of cheap proxy predictions to produce unbiased estimates with valid confidence intervals. Coverage holds unconditionally on the proxy: a worse proxy yields wider intervals, not invalid ones.

The PPI literature has matured rapidly, with extensions for power tuning
(
2

, stratified populations
(
10

11
)
, small-sample bootstrap procedures
(
14
)
, active sampling
(
18
;
12
)
, cost-optimal budgets
(
3
)
, and recalibration
(
13
)
. Yet, as already noted in
(
16
)
, this progress is scattered across papers with heterogeneous notation and partial reference implementations. The
ppi_py
library
(
1

established the foundation but predates several of these methods and emphasizes generality across estimands (GLM, M-estimators) over depth in mean estimation, where most use-cases live.

Agentic evaluation is a natural fit for PPI on several counts. The cost asymmetry between proxy and ground truth is extreme. Agentic pipelines also exhibit natural heterogeneity: performance and proxy quality vary substantially across the tools invoked, the types of queries handled, and the sub-agents involved, motivating stratified estimation. Modern judges increasingly produce calibrated per-sample uncertainty, unlocking active sampling. Existing libraries cover parts of this landscape but no single interface unifies the matching family of estimators and samplers, leaving practitioners to stitch methods together across academic repositories.

We close this gap with
GLIDE

Background: Prediction-Powered Inference

Setup.

Let
(X,Y)\sim P
where
X
is the input or behavior to be evaluated (in our case, a conversation, an LLM output, or an agentic trajectory) and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is the corresponding human-assigned label (e.g., a binary correctness indicator, a toxicity indicator, or a tool-use rating). The target estimand is the population mean
\theta^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[Y]
, which encodes a performance or risk metric of interest. We have access to two datasets drawn i.i.d. from
P
: a small
labeled
set
\mathcal{L}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i},f(X_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a large
unlabeled
set
\mathcal{U}=\{(X_{j},f(X_{j}))\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
N\gg n
, where
f
is a fixed proxy (typically an LLM-as-judge) producing predictions of
Y
. We assume
f
is trained on data disjoint from
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{U}
, so its predictions are i.i.d. across both sets.

The PPI estimator.

The classical PPI mean estimator
(
1
)
debiases the proxy mean using the labeled set:

\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{PPI}}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(X_{j})}_{\text{proxy mean on }\mathcal{U}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-f(X_{i})\right)}_{\text{rectifier on }\mathcal{L}}.

(1)

The first term is the cheap proxy mean; the second is a correction for the proxy’s bias. PPI++
(
2
)
introduces a tuning parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
:

\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{PPI}\text{++}}_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(X_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})\right),

(2)

which recovers (
1
) for
\lambda=1
and the classical labeled mean for
\lambda=0
. The optimal
\lambda^{\star}
is closed-form and minimizes the asymptotic variance, guaranteeing that PPI++ is never worse than the classical estimator in the limit, even when the proxy is uninformative or adversarial.

Confidence intervals.

Thanks to the Central Limit Theorem,
\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{PPI}\text{++}}_{\lambda}
is asymptotically normal, and a
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval is obtained from the plug-in variance estimator and the normal quantile
z_{1-\alpha/2}
. For small
n
, the central-limit approximation degrades; bootstrap-based constructions provide a finite-sample alternative
(
14
)
.

Extensions.

The same template (combine a labeled correction with a proxy summary) generalizes to several settings relevant to evaluation: stratified populations, where power tuning is performed per stratum
(
10
)
; non-uniform sampling driven by per-sample proxy uncertainty, known as Active Statistical Inference (ASI)
(
18
;
12
)
; small-sample regimes addressed by Predict-Then-Debias (PTD), which provides bootstrap-based confidence intervals
(
14
)
; and cost-aware sampling probabilities derived from a known proxy/annotation cost ratio
(
3
)
. See to
(
16
;
13
)
for a recent review. GLIDE implements all of these extensions; we develop the corresponding estimators and samplers in Section
4
.

Effective sample size.

A natural figure of merit for any PPI estimator is its
effective sample size
, defined as the number of fully labeled samples a classical estimator would require to match the same confidence-interval width:

n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n\cdot\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\bar{Y}_{n})}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{PPI}\text{++}}_{\lambda})}.

(3)

The ratio
n_{\mathrm{eff}}/n\geq 1
quantifies the practical return on investment of the proxy: it translates directly into human-review-hour or budget savings, and is the headline metric we report throughout Sections
5
and
6
.

3
Why Agentic Evaluation Needs PPI

Section
2
fixed the abstract setting; we now argue that agentic systems exhibit four properties, simultaneously and to an unusual degree, under which PPI is most valuable. Each property maps directly to a method in the recent literature, and to a corresponding GLIDE component.

1. Extreme cost asymmetry.

Evaluating an agentic trajectory is qualitatively more expensive than evaluating a single LLM output. A trajectory may involve retrieval, tool calls, intermediate reasoning steps, and a final response, all of which a domain expert must inspect to certify correctness. Annotation costs of several dollars per trajectory are common in healthcare, legal, and financial settings, while an LLM-as-judge call costs cents. This two-to-three-orders-of-magnitude gap is precisely the regime where cost-aware sampling
(
3
)
is relevant, setting the labeling rate as a function of the proxy/annotation cost ratio, and the available signal.

2. Natural stratification.

Agentic pipelines partition observations along several axes that are typically known at evaluation time: the tools invoked, the type of query handled, the sub-agents involved, and the retrieval source consulted. Proxy quality varies across these strata in ways that matter: an LLM-as-judge that reliably evaluates arithmetic reasoning may struggle with code-execution traces or with domain-specific clinical guidelines. Stratified PPI
(
10
;
11
)
runs PPI within each stratum and combines the results with per-stratum weights, isolating proxy-quality differences and yielding tighter intervals than the unstratified estimator.

3. Available proxy uncertainty.

Modern judges can produce per-sample uncertainty signals: verbalized confidence, log-probabilities, multiple generation consistency, or ensembles of judges. These signals identify the trajectories on which the proxy is least reliable, and therefore the trajectories where human annotation is most informative. Active Statistical Inference
(
18
;
12
)
exploits this by sampling labeling probabilities proportional to proxy uncertainty, concentrating expensive expert effort where it carries the most statistical value.

4. Consequential deployment regimes.

Criticial systems (e.g., clinical decision support, financial advisory, regulated automation, safety-critical autonomy) are also those where biased point estimates without valid uncertainty have direct consequences. Distribution-free coverage guarantees are a deployment requirement in these settings. PPI’s unconditional-on-the-proxy validity makes it well suited to agentic pipelines that must be auditable.

4
The GLIDE Framework

4.1
Three-step decomposition

GLIDE organizes evaluation around three sequential steps inherited from classical survey theory:
sampling
,
annotation
, and
estimation
. A small budget of observations (or trajectories) is selected from a large pool of proxy-labeled ones (sampling); domain experts produce ground-truth labels on the selected subset (annotation); the labeled subset and the proxy predictions are combined into a debiased point estimate with a confidence interval (estimation). Sampling and estimation are statistical and algorithmic; while annotation is irreducibly task-specific and out of scope for GLIDE. The library requires only that experts return labels in a machine-readable form (categories or numbers), whose business meaning is context-dependent but whose statistical treatment is not.

This factorization is more than a presentation choice. It allows samplers and estimators to be developed, tested, and contributed independently: a contributor proposing a new sampler need not touch any estimator, and conversely. It also lets practitioners mix and match across the families of methods recalled in Section
2
, choosing the sampler that matches their evaluation setup and the estimator that matches their statistical regime.

4.2
Samplers

GLIDE implements four samplers covering the regimes identified in Section
3
.

UniformSampler
draws
n
observations uniformly without replacement from the pool. It is the appropriate baseline when no auxiliary signal is available.

StratifiedSampler
allocates
n
observations across user-defined strata. Two allocation rules are supported:
proportional
(
n_{h}\propto N_{h}
), and
Neyman
(
n_{h}\propto N_{h}\sigma_{h}
), where
\sigma_{h}
is the stratum standard deviation of the proxy labels
(
11
)
. Both allocations produce non-integer values that GLIDE rounds via Hamilton’s largest-remainder method to ensure
\sum_{h}n_{h}=n
exactly.

ActiveSampler
draws each observation independently with a Bernoulli probability proportional to a user-supplied uncertainty score on the proxy . The proportionality constant is calibrated so that the expected sample size equals the budget
(
18
;
12
)
. Because the draws are independent Bernoullis, the realized sample size matches the budget only in expectation; this is the standard cost of admitting per-sample probabilities.

CostOptimalSampler
comes in two variants. The constant-rate variant (
CostOptimalRandomSampler
) computes a single sampling probability from a small burn-in dataset and known proxy/annotation costs, minimizing total cost
(
3
)
. The feature-dependent variant (
CostOptimalSampler
) additionally consumes a per-sample uncertainty score, computing observation-level optimal probabilities
\pi(x)
. The feature-dependent variant strictly dominates the constant-rate one when the uncertainty score is informative, but requires an extra signal that is not always available.

4.4
A decision framework

The decision tree of Figure
1
routes the user through three stages. The sampling stage selects among samplers based on three signals:
are cost estimates of human and proxy annotations available?
(cost-optimal samplers),
are per-sample proxy uncertainties available?
(active sampling), and
is the dataset structured into strata with heterogeneous proxy quality?
(stratified sampling). The annotation stage is the one revealing ground truth labels by human expert review, and is done outside GLIDE. The estimation stage selects among the estimators using a single threshold on the number of human labels:
are at least 50 human labels available?
(CLT-based intervals if yes, bootstrap-based PTD variants otherwise). When stratified estimators are used, this threshold applies per stratum. The decision tree is empirically supported by the validation suite in Section
5
.

4.5
API

GLIDE follows the conventions of the scientific Python ecosystem (scipy, scikit-learn). Estimators are stateful objects with an
estimate
method. Samplers expose a
sample
method returning a pair
(\pi,\xi)
:
\pi\in[0,1]^{N}
holds the per-observation sampling probabilities, and
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{N}
holds the binary inclusion indicators drawn as Bernoulli realizations of
\pi
. Results are dataclasses carrying the point estimate, the confidence interval at user-specified level
1-\alpha
, the effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, and the metric label. A complete end-to-end workflow (sampling, annotation, estimation) fits in fewer than six lines of Python; we provide a minimal example in Appendix
A
. New samplers and estimators can be contributed as single-file modules implementing the
sample
or
estimate
interface, lowering the barrier for the research community to deposit reference implementations.

5
Empirical Validation

Each estimator implemented in GLIDE is accompanied by a Monte Carlo validation notebook that establishes three properties: (i) the empirical coverage of its confidence intervals matches the nominal level across a range of proxy quality and confidence levels, (ii) the interval is no wider than the classical labeled-only baseline at any correlation, and (iii) the effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
grows with the proxy/ground-truth correlation. We describe the protocol and report representative results for the PTD estimator; other notebooks follow similar protocols and yielding consistent conclusions.

The full per-estimator notebooks are released alongside the library and documentation in the “deep dive” section.

5.1
Protocol

We instantiate a binary mean-estimation task with true mean
\theta^{\star}=0.55
and biased proxy mean
0.50

\rho
between the per-observation true and proxy labels. Each replication generates
N_{\text{true}}=500
labeled and
N_{\text{proxy}}=1000
unlabeled observations, computes a
90\%
confidence interval under each method, and records whether the interval covers
\theta^{\star}
, the interval width, and the effective sample size. We run
1000
replications per cell, sweeping correlations
\rho\in\{0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\}
.

We compare two estimators:
ClassicalMeanEstimator
on
y_{\text{true}}
(the labeled-only baseline, valid by construction and used as the reference for

), and
PTDMeanEstimator
(Predict-Then-Debias estimator). We additionally verify in the notebook that the proxy-only baseline (
ClassicalMeanEstimator
on
y_{\text{proxy}}
) collapses to near-zero coverage as expected, since its intervals are tight but centered on the biased proxy mean rather than
\theta^{\star}

6
Case Study: Agentic Evaluation in Practice

The validation suite of Section
5
establishes that GLIDE’s estimators are statistically valid on synthetic data with controlled proxy quality. We complement it with an end-to-end case study on a public agentic evaluation benchmark, where the proxy is a real LLM-as-judge and the ground truth is taken from the benchmark’s existing expert annotations.

We release the case-study notebook alongside the library as a reusable template.

6.1
Setup

We evaluate the safety of agentic trajectories on R-Judge
(
17
)
, a public benchmark of 568 user/AI-agent conversations spanning five application domains: general application, programming, finance, web, and IoT. Each trajectory carries a binary expert annotation indicating whether the agent’s behavior poses a security risk. We treat these expert labels as ground truth; the population safety-risk rate computed over the full benchmark is
\theta^{\star}\approx 0.525
. The five application domains form natural strata exploited by the stratified workflow.

The proxy is
claude-sonnet-4-6
run as a zero-shot LLM-as-judge on every trajectory. For each conversation, the model returns a binary verdict (safe/risky) and a self-reported confidence score on a
1
–
10
scale, which we invert into a per-trajectory uncertainty
u_{i}
used to drive active sampling. The proxy is substantially biased: its empirical mean (
\approx 0.655
) overshoots the true rate by about
13
percentage points, while its Pearson correlation with the expert labels is
\rho\approx 0.59
. This regime, a non-trivially biased but informative proxy, is precisely the one GLIDE is designed for.

We simulate a realistic annotation budget by masking all but
n=100
of the expert labels at each Monte Carlo replication; the remaining
N=468
trajectories carry only the proxy verdict. We compare five protocols, all targeting the same population mean: (i) the labeled-only baseline (

6.3
Discussion

Three properties of the case study are worth highlighting. First, the
\approx 13
-point LLM-as-Judge bias on R-Judge is large in absolute terms, yet the four labeled-data protocols absorb it without loss of coverage. Second, the per-sample uncertainty signal supplied by modern judges (here, verbalized confidence) is informative enough to make active sampling competitive but, on this benchmark, not strictly dominant: stratification on a low-cardinality, semantically meaningful axis (application domain) yields a comparable or slightly larger gain at zero engineering cost. Overall, we view the R-Judge case study as a single instance of a generic evaluation protocol that GLIDE makes routine.

The GLIDE layer is orthogonal to LLM evaluation orchestration frameworks (HELM, lm-eval-harness, DeepEval, RAGAS), which solve the upstream problem of running evaluations: GLIDE consumes their outputs (proxy labels and selected human annotations) and provides the downstream statistical layer that converts them into debiased estimates with valid confidence intervals.

8
Limitations and Roadmap

Limitations.

GLIDE specializes in mean estimation. This is a deliberate scope choice: the vast majority of performance and risk-control metrics in deployed settings (accuracy, relevance rate, hallucination rate, toxicity rate, precision, recall) are means or proportions, and restricting to this regime simplifies the codebase, since several methods that diverge in the general M-estimation setting collapse to the same estimator when the target is a mean. Other estimands of practical interest, in particular quantiles, and GLM coefficients, are out of scope;
ppi_py

(
1
)
remains the appropriate reference for the broader family. The CLT-based estimators in GLIDE (PPI++, Stratified PPI++, ASI) require, as a rule of thumb, at least fifty labeled samples per stratum for the asymptotic intervals to be reliable; below that threshold, the bootstrap-based PTD variants are the documented fallback. The library currently assumes a single proxy per observation and i.i.d. labeled and unlabeled sets drawn from the same population: it does not provide anytime-valid constructions, does not aggregate predictions from multiple proxies, and does not yet handle covariate or label shift between the labeled and unlabeled sets. Finally, the annotation step is intentionally unmanaged; practitioners must integrate their own labeling pipeline, which in domain-specific deployments is often the most labor-intensive component of the workflow.

Four directions are immediate.
Multi-proxy aggregation

15

Anytime-valid PPI

would enable continuous monitoring and principled stopping for evaluation pipelines that report metrics over time.
Non-deterministic evaluation
extends the framework to the one-input-many-outputs regime characteristic of stochastic agents, where the open question is how to allocate budget between input coverage and output replication. Last but not least,
Multi-annotator ground truth
addresses the common situation where several human experts disagree on the same trajectory, with inter-annotator disagreement (different experts give different labels) and intra-annotator disagreement (the same expert gives different labels at different times) propagating into the estimand itself: the resulting target is no longer a population mean but a population mean of a latent label whose own uncertainty must be modeled.

9
Conclusion

We presented GLIDE, an open-source Python library for prediction-powered evaluation of GenAI and agentic systems. GLIDE specializes in mean estimation, and integrates modern estimators (PPI++, Stratified PPI++, PTD, Stratified PTD, ASI) and samplers (uniform, stratified, active, cost-optimal) under a single scipy-style API. A reproducible Monte Carlo validation suite establishes nominal coverage across estimators and characterizes their efficiency gains as a function of proxy quality.

The library makes a practical point alongside its statistical one: investing in a higher-quality LLM-as-judge translates directly into a larger effective annotation budget, with the gain growing monotonically in the proxy/true correlation. Better proxies are not a substitute for human annotation; they are a multiplier on it.

Reproducibility.

The codebase is released under a permissive open-source license, with continuous integration on test coverage, dependency policy following SPEC-0, and versioning following SemVer. The validation notebooks and case-study scripts reproduce all figures in this paper. The GLIDE package is available at this URL:
https://github.com/EmertonData/glide
.

Impact Statement

GLIDE provides statistical tools that can inform consequential decisions about AI deployment, suspension, or auditing. Used responsibly, it contributes to more transparent and accountable AI evaluation. However, valid confidence intervals on a poorly chosen or biased metric remain uninformative about underlying harms: when applied to strongly biased evaluation data, GLIDE’s debiasing machinery addresses proxy bias, not the structural or societal biases the AI system itself may exhibit. Users should bear this distinction in mind, particularly in high-stakes settings where evaluation outputs carry direct operational consequences.
