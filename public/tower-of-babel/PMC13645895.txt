Spontaneous regression of colorectal liver metastasis with coexisting IgG4-rich inflammatory pseudotumor-like changes: a case report
Authors: Shoichiro Mizukami, Tatsuya Shonaka, Tomoki Takizawa, Katsuro Enomoto, Hiroyuki Takahashi, Koji Imai, Yuki Kamikokura, Sayaka Yuzawa, Mishie Tanino, Hideki Yokoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous regression of colorectal liver metastasis (CRLM) is an extremely rare phenomenon with only a few reported cases. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Infection-triggered immune reactivation and inflammation have also been proposed to be potential contributors. IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) is a systemic fibroinflammatory condition characterized by IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration and fibrosis, and rarely manifests in the liver as an inflammatory pseudotumor (IPT). We herein report a rare case of a 71-year-old man with possible spontaneous regression of CRLM accompanied by coexisting IgG4-rich IPT-like changes. Eight months after the laparoscopic Hartmann’s operation for rectal cancer, imaging revealed hepatic lesions in segments S5 and S7, which were diagnosed as CRLM. Partial hepatectomy revealed necrotic adenocarcinoma in the S7 lesion, with IgG4-rich IPT-like inflammatory changes. Postoperative evaluation revealed markedly elevated serum IgG4 levels and IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration in the mediastinal lymph nodes, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for systemic IgG4-RD. Twenty months postoperatively, the patient remained recurrence-free without adjuvant chemotherapy or steroid therapy. This case report describes the coexistence of spontaneous tumor regression and IgG4-related hepatic IPT within the same lesion and provides insight into the clinicopathological spectrum linking tumor regression and IgG4-RD.

Introduction

Spontaneous regression of cancer is defined as the complete disappearance of a malignant tumor in the absence of any therapy capable of inducing tumor regression [1, 2]. It is an extremely rare phenomenon, with only a few reported cases in colorectal cancer, particularly in colorectal liver metastasis (CRLM). The underlying mechanisms remain unclear; however, infection- or necrosis-triggered immune reactivation, alterations in the tumor microenvironment, and inflammatory responses have been suggested as contributing factors [3].

In the present case, spontaneous regression of the CRLM was confirmed pathologically. The regressed lesion showed dense fibrosis and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, with numerous IgG4-positive plasma cells, consistent with an inflammatory and fibrotic background resembling IgG4-related hepatic inflammatory pseudotumor (IPT). IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) is a systemic fibroinflammatory disorder that may occasionally involve the liver as an IPT and has been reported to coexist with various malignancies, possibly as a result of abnormal immune responses to tumor-associated antigens [4, 5]. Herein, we report a rare resected case of spontaneously regressed CRLM associated with IgG4-rich IPT-like changes and discuss its clinicopathological features.

Case report

A 71-year-old man with a medical history of hypertension was referred to our institution. His regular medications included lansoprazole and azilsartan. He consumed approximately 500 mL of beer daily and had smoked 20 cigarettes per day for 45 years. Eight months earlier, he had undergone a laparoscopic Hartmann operation for rectal cancer. Postoperative histopathological examination revealed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma, classified as pT3N0M0, Stage IIa (R0 resection, Cur A), with microsatellite stability, RAS and BRAF wild-type, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative status [6]. The patient did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy and was followed up without additional treatment.

Eight months postoperatively, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed two hypodense hepatic lesions in segments S5 and S7. The serum tumor marker levels were within normal limits (cancer antigen 19 − 9:5 U/mL; carcinoembryonic antigen test: 2.1 ng/mL). The patient did not have a fever before hepatectomy, and the C-reactive protein level was mildly elevated at 1.45 mg/dL. Serum IgG and IgG4 levels were not measured pre-hepatectomy. Dynamic CT revealed an 11-mm hypodense mass in segment S5 and a 6-mm lesion in segment S7. The lesions showed poorly defined internal enhancement, and in the arterial phase, faint perilesional enhancement was observed in the surrounding liver parenchyma, suggesting CRLM (Fig. 1a, b). No significant bile duct dilatation or strictures were observed. Mediastinal CT and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT revealed enlargement and fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) accumulation in mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes, with a maximum standardized uptake value of 7.0. These findings were interpreted as reactive lymphadenopathy associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Fig. 2). No abnormal FDG uptake was observed in the liver, pancreas, spleen, adrenal glands, or kidneys. Gadoxetate disodium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed mild T2 hyperintensity in lesions S5 and S7 and diffusion restriction. Ring-like peripheral enhancement was observed in the early phase with reduced uptake in the liver phase (Fig. 1c–f). Based on the clinical course and imaging findings, CRLM was diagnosed, and the patient underwent partial hepatectomy of segments S5 and S7. The patient was discharged without any complications on postoperative day 15.

Gross examination of the resected specimens revealed well-demarcated, solid, grayish-white lesions measuring 5 × 5 mm in segment S5 and 6 × 6 mm in segment S7 of the liver. Histologically, the S5 lesion showed moderate periductal lymphocytic infiltration within the fibrous stroma, along with venous fibrosis and prominent plasma cell infiltration, suggesting obliterative phlebitis. Numerous IgG- and IgG4-positive plasma cells (approximately 300 cells per high-power field [HPF]) were observed, with an IgG4/IgG ratio > 0.40. These findings were consistent with lymphoplasmacytic-type IgG4-related hepatic IPT (Fig. 3). No viable carcinoma was identified in the S5 lesion. In contrast, the S7 lesion showed necrotic glandular structures in the central area, suggestive of metastatic adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry revealed weak cytoplasmic positivity for CDX2 and AE1/AE3 without nuclear staining, and the morphological features were consistent with those of nonviable tumor cells. The surrounding tissue showed fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration, with approximately 50 IgG4-positive plasma cells/HPF, indicating IPT-like inflammatory changes associated with the tumor. Taken together, these findings suggest that the S5 lesion represents IgG4-related hepatic IPT, whereas the S7 lesion is consistent with metastatic adenocarcinoma showing extensive necrosis accompanied by IgG4-related IPT-like inflammatory changes, raising the possibility of spontaneous tumor regression in the S7 lesion (Fig. 4).

One month post-hepatectomy, serum immunoglobulins were measured for the first time, revealing markedly elevated levels: total IgG, 3284.8 mg/dL (reference range: 861.0–1747 mg/dL); and IgG4, 1530 mg/dL (reference range: 11–121 mg/dL). Postoperative imaging also revealed diffuse enlargement of the pancreas with mild dilatation of the main pancreatic duct and nodular change in the pancreatic body, as well as multiple focal areas of poor enhancement with mild cortical atrophy in both kidneys (Fig. 5). In addition, FDG-avid mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy, previously interpreted as reactive changes on PET–CT, was noted (Fig. 2). Taken together, these findings suggest possible systemic IgG4-related involvement.

Three months after hepatectomy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) was performed to evaluate mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy further. Histological examination of the sampled lymph nodes showed dense IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration, consistent with IgG4-related respiratory disease (Fig. 6). Based on these clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings, the diagnosis of IgG4-RD was established according to the 2020 revised comprehensive diagnostic criteria [7]. Twenty months post-hepatectomy, the patient remained free of colorectal cancer recurrence and showed no clinical or radiological evidence of IgG4-RD progression. After multidisciplinary discussion, neither adjuvant chemotherapy nor steroid therapy was initiated, and the patient continued under observation without treatment. Discussion

In the present case, the S7 lesion showed necrotic adenocarcinoma cells with ghost-like morphology, surrounded by fibrotic tissue and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, with prominent accumulation of IgG4-positive plasma cells. The necrotic tumor cells exhibited weak cytoplasmic positivity for AE1/AE3 and CDX2 without nuclear staining, confirming their nonviability. These findings suggest possible spontaneous regression of CRLM in the S7 lesion, accompanied by IgG4-related lymphoplasmacytic-type IPT-like inflammatory changes.

Spontaneous regression of cancer is extremely rare, and in CRLM, including the present case, only seven cases have been reported to date (Table 2) [8–13]. The mechanisms underlying tumor regression remain uncertain; however, a combination of factors, including alterations in the tumor microenvironment, host immune activation, ischemia, and tissue necrosis, has been proposed [3, 13]. Several reports have described the spontaneous regression of colorectal cancer or its metastases following immune status modulation. For instance, Pau et al. reported the regression of peritoneal metastases after methotrexate withdrawal in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis, attributing it to immune reactivation [14]. Zwart et al. observed regression of multiple CRLMs in a lung transplant recipient with mismatch repair–deficient colorectal cancer after cessation of immunosuppressive therapy, suggesting the reconstitution of immune surveillance [13]. Matsuki et al. described a case of postoperative regression of CRLM associated with chronic biliary infection, possibly attributable to local immune stimulation [11]. Collectively, these cases indicate that immunological factors may play a role in the spontaneous regression of CRLM.

In the present case, abundant IgG4-positive plasma cells were observed around the regressed S7 lesion, along with storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis, which are histological features compatible with the spectrum of IgG4-related hepatic IPT. Additionally, markedly elevated serum IgG4 levels, diffuse pancreatic enlargement with mild main pancreatic duct dilatation, multiple renal low-attenuation areas with mild cortical atrophy, and IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration in mediastinal lymph nodes obtained via TBNA fulfilled the comprehensive diagnostic criteria for IgG4-RD. Considering these systemic findings, the accumulation of IgG4-positive plasma cells surrounding the regressed S7 metastatic lesion may reflect an IgG4-RD–related immune response rather than a nonspecific inflammatory response. A direct causal relationship between IgG4-RD and tumor regression could not be established. Nonetheless, their coexistence in the present case suggested the presence of a unique inflammatory environment at the metastatic site.

Hepatic IPTs are rare, heterogeneous entities with diverse etiologies, including autoimmune conditions and infections [15]. The lymphoplasmacytic subtype, which belongs to the IgG4-RD spectrum, is histologically characterized by dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, storiform fibrosis, and obliterative phlebitis [4, 16]. Most IgG4-related hepatic IPTs occur in the perihilar region and are often associated with IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis [17]. Conversely, the lesion in this case was located in the peripheral parenchyma, a relatively uncommon site accounting for < 3% of the reported cases [18]. The presence of necrotic adenocarcinoma remnants within an IgG4-hepatic IPT–like lesion is unusual and expands the histological spectrum of hepatic IPTs associated with malignancy.

Recent studies have reported cases of “tumor-associated IgG4-RD,” in which IgG4-RD develops shortly after cancer diagnosis, raising the possibility of an immunological association between malignancy and IgG4-RD [16, 19]. Large cohort analyses have shown an increased prevalence of malignancy among patients with IgG4-RD compared with the general population, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [16]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain this association, including: (1) aberrant immune responses to tumor-associated self-antigens that trigger IgG4-RD; (2) shared inflammatory or genetic pathways predisposing to cancer and IgG4-RD; and (3) therapy-induced immune activation following tumor destruction, which may release tumor peptides and elicit secondary autoimmune responses in non-neoplastic tissues [5]. However, a definitive causal or histopathological link between malignancy and IgG4-RD has not been established.

Furthermore, recent immunopathological insights suggest that chronic inflammation, IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration, and the activity of T-follicular helper cells, M2 macrophages, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells contribute to tumor–immune modulation in IgG4-RD [19]. However, previous studies have demonstrated that IgG4 antibodies may impair effective antitumor immunity by inhibiting IgG1-mediated effector functions, such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, through interference with Fc receptor signaling [20]. Collectively, these findings support a model of complex bidirectional interactions between malignancy and IgG4-related immune dysregulation. In this context, the IgG4-rich inflammatory response observed in the present case may represent an immunomodulatory microenvironment with tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive potential rather than a direct driver of tumor regression, although a contributory role cannot be entirely excluded.

In the present case, the possibility of spontaneous tumor regression should be limited to the S7 lesion, in which necrotic tumor structures were identified histologically. This lesion also showed accompanying IgG4-rich IPT-like inflammatory changes, suggesting that both processes may have occurred concurrently within the same lesion. The diagnosis of hepatic IPT prompted further systemic evaluation, which revealed elevated serum IgG4 levels and IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration in distant lymph nodes, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for IgG4-RD. Pre-hepatectomy imaging findings were indistinguishable from those of typical CRLM; therefore, surgical resection was an appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Histopathologic examination of the resected specimens provided findings suggestive of tumor regression in the S7 lesion.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of spontaneous CRLM regression accompanied by IgG4-related hepatic IPT–like changes. Although the underlying mechanism remains uncertain, the coexistence of tumor regression and IgG4-related inflammation in this case suggests, but does not prove, that immune-mediated and microenvironmental factors may be relevant in CRLM biology. Because this report describes a single case and no detailed immunological analyses, such as lymphocyte subset profiling or cytokine evaluation, were performed, a causal relationship could not be established. Nevertheless, the accumulation of similar cases and further immunological investigations will be necessary to better understand the potential interplay between inflammatory responses, including IgG4-related reactions, and tumor regression.

In conclusion, this case represents a rare instance of possible pathologically spontaneous regression of CRLM with coexisting IgG4-rich IPT-like changes. The findings suggest coexistence of tumor regression and IgG4-related inflammatory changes within the same lesion, although a causal relationship between these processes remains uncertain. This case provides valuable insights into the potential interactions between tumor regression, hepatic IPTs, and host immune responses in CRLM.
