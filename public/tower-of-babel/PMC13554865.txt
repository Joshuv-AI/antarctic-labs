ECMO-Supported Pericardiectomy in Constrictive Tuberculous Pericarditis With Severe Ventricular Dysfunction
Authors: Mohamed Elmorshidy, Ayman Helal, Mohammed El-Din, Mohsin Farooq, Mohamed Alama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConstrictive pericarditis is a well-recognized complication of tuberculous pericarditis, whereas severe ventricular systolic dysfunction is rare.Case SummaryA 20-year-old man presented with progressive heart failure and was found to have severe biventricular systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction <15%) with marked pericardial thickening. Conventional investigations for tuberculosis were negative. Constrictive pericarditis is a chronic disorder characterized by a rigid, fibrotic pericardium that restricts diastolic filling and impairs cardiac output.1 Although the causes of constrictive pericarditis vary geographically, tuberculosis (TB) remains the leading etiology in many low- and middle-income countries and among migrants from endemic regions.2 Tuberculous pericarditis (TBP) is a severe extrapulmonary manifestation of TB that may progress from inflammation and effusion to irreversible constriction if not recognized and treated promptly.3 Marked biventricular systolic dysfunction in this setting is uncommon and raises concern for either advanced mechanical constraint or concomitant myocardial involvement.4 We report a patient with advanced constrictive TBP complicated by severe biventricular systolic dysfunction who recovered completely after veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO)–supported radical pericardiectomy.

History of Presentation

A previously well, 20-year-old man presented with a 6-month history of progressive dyspnea, abdominal distention, bilateral lower-limb edema, significant unintentional weight loss, and intermittent fever. On examination, he had elevated jugular venous pressure, tense ascites, and bilateral lower-limb edema extending to the sacrum. Initial blood tests showed mild anemia and raised inflammatory markers, with preserved renal and hepatic function.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical history. He had recently relocated to the United Kingdom, was of African origin, and reported prior exposure to a person with TB.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included viral myocarditis, idiopathic constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, and TBP.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was performed to clarify the etiology of heart failure and to assess for myocardial disease. This confirmed extensive pericardial thickening and severe biventricular systolic dysfunction but showed no significant myocardial edema, fibrosis, or enhancement to suggest myocarditis. Pericardial thickening measured 8 to 12 mm at the base of the heart and up to 16 to 17 mm around the mid-lateral wall. Ascitic fluid analysis showed a transudate with no evidence of malignancy, TB, or other infection. Viral serology, including HIV, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus, was negative. Repeated sputum acid-fast bacillus samples were also negative.

Preoperative coronary angiography demonstrated normal coronary arteries. Right heart catheterization was attempted to obtain invasive hemodynamic confirmation of constrictive physiology, but the procedure was abandoned because of hemodynamic instability.

Management

Despite the absence of microbiologic confirmation, the overall clinical picture raised strong suspicion for tuberculous pericarditis. Consequently, he was treated with empirical antituberculosis therapy (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol) with adjunctive corticosteroids, alongside guideline-directed heart failure therapy (bisoprolol, sacubitril/valsartan, dapagliflozin, eplerenone, and furosemide). However, his clinical status continued to deteriorate.

A multidisciplinary discussion involving cardiology, respiratory medicine, infectious diseases, and cardiothoracic surgery concluded that surgical intervention was indicated.

The patient underwent radical pericardiectomy through a median sternotomy. Operative findings revealed a thick, calcified pericardium that was densely adherent to the myocardium. The pericardium was resected anteriorly, laterally, and posteriorly from phrenic nerve to phrenic nerve, including the diaphragmatic pericardium. Following decompression, the central venous pressure fell abruptly from 30 mmHg to 10 mmHg, indicating success. Unfortunately, this was followed by severe hemodynamic instability and low cardiac output requiring support with inotropes and VA-ECMO.

The postoperative course was further complicated by mediastinal bleeding requiring repeat sternotomy, evacuation of mediastinal hematoma, and washout. After stabilization, he was successfully weaned from ECMO after 10 days and subsequently weaned from inotropic support.

Histopathology of the pericardium showed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation consistent with tuberculosis, and Xpert MTB/RIF polymerase chain reaction on pericardial tissue–confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient made a remarkable recovery, and repeat inpatient echocardiography demonstrated normalization of biventricular systolic function with improvement in left ventricular EF from <15% to 57% (Video 3). He was discharged on continued antituberculosis therapy with regular follow-up.

At 2 years, he remains asymptomatic with stable cardiac function.

Discussion

TBP remains the leading cause of constrictive pericarditis worldwide.2 The disease progresses through inflammatory and effusive phases to a chronic constrictive stage characterized by dense fibrotic thickening that limits ventricular filling and impairs diastolic function.1

Diagnosis of TBP is often difficult because conventional microbiological tests have limited sensitivity. Pericardial fluid smear and culture are frequently negative, and extrapulmonary disease may not be detected by sputum or ascitic fluid testing.5 Molecular methods such as Xpert MTB/RIF improve diagnostic accuracy, particularly when performed on tissue obtained at surgery.3

Echocardiography is the first-line investigation for suspected constrictive physiology and can demonstrate septal bounce, respiratory variation, and exaggerated ventricular interdependence.6 CMR is the gold standard noninvasive modality for characterizing pericardial thickening, inflammation, and constrictive physiology while simultaneously assessing myocardial involvement.7 In our patient, the CMR findings strongly supported constrictive pericarditis but did not demonstrate overt myocardial fibrosis or myocarditis.

Severe biventricular systolic dysfunction in constrictive pericarditis is uncommon and raises the key question of intrinsic myocardial disease vs functional impairment.4 In this case, the absence of myocardial fibrosis or inflammation on CMR suggests that the profoundly reduced EF was predominantly functional rather than due to primary myocardial damage. Chronic pericardial constriction leads to impaired diastolic filling, reduced preload, and marked ventricular interdependence, which can produce low stroke volume and an apparently reduced EF despite preserved intrinsic contractility.6 Prolonged underfilling may also lead to myocardial atrophy and reduced contractile efficiency, whereas tethering of the myocardium to a rigid, thickened pericardium may further impair systolic deformation.4

The rapid and complete recovery of ventricular function following pericardiectomy strongly supports a reversible, mechanically mediated process. Relief of pericardial constraint restores preload, improves ventricular filling, and normalizes myocardial mechanics. This case therefore suggests that severe ventricular dysfunction in advanced constrictive TBP should not automatically be interpreted as irreversible myocardial failure. Current guidelines recommend anti-TB therapy as first-line treatment for TBP; however, established constrictive physiology generally requires surgical pericardiectomy.

Medical therapy with anti-TB drugs, with or without corticosteroids, remains the foundation of treatment in early disease and may occasionally reverse constrictive physiology before dense fibrosis is established.8 Once advanced constriction is present, however, radical pericardiectomy is the definitive treatment and can provide major hemodynamic and symptomatic benefit.9

The perioperative course in this case also illustrates the potential instability that may follow sudden relief of long-standing constriction. Post-pericardiectomy low cardiac output syndrome can occur in severely compromised patients, and VA-ECMO may be lifesaving during this transition. The need for repeat sternotomy and mediastinal hematoma evacuation further highlights the complexity and high-risk nature of surgery in advanced disease.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates a rare but reversible cause of severe biventricular systolic dysfunction in advanced constrictive TBP. Timely radical pericardiectomy can be lifesaving, even when the perioperative course requires VA-ECMO support. The marked recovery of biventricular function in this patient supports the concept that myocardial dysfunction in constrictive TBP may be predominantly mechanical and therefore reversible after relief of constriction.
