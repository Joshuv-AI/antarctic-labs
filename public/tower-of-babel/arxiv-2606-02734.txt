Formation of multiple dust rings and gaps in protoplanetary discs by a single migrating planet
K. M. Weiskopf, S. C. Meiners, A. Ziampras and C. P. Dullemond

Abstract
Context. Dust structures in protoplanetary discs have been widely observed and their creation remains an active field of research. Several possible origins have already been explored, including magneto-hydrodynamics, shadows and planets–disc interactions. Aims. The goal of this paper is to investigate whether a single migrating planet in a low-viscosity disc, including radiative processes, is capable of generating observable dust structures. We aim to examine both the lifetime of such structures and potential asymmetries within them. Methods. We perform a set of high-resolution, two-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of migrating planets using three different equations of state: isothermal, constant β \beta -cooling and an adaptive β \beta model. Dust is included in all simulations and the resulting dust density profiles are then post-processed to create radiative transfer images. Results. For all equations of state considered, the planet undergoes one or several migration jumps, each producing dust rings and gaps. The lifetime of these structures depends on the phase of slow migration preceding and occurring between jumps, but in all cases they remain visible for at least 400 kyr. We find that cooling has a deciding effect on the migration behaviour and the number of jumps, but no measurable influence on the lifetime of the dust structures. The structures exhibit relatively few asymmetries, and large-scale vortices persist for an average of only 90 kyr. Conclusions. Our models highlight the capacity of planets to open multiple gaps while migrating, and stress the importance of a realistic cooling model. Care should be taken when interpreting and comparing such models directly to observations.



II: radiative discs and observational signatures

1
Introduction

The DSHARP survey (
1
) observed the
\lambda=1.25
mm continuum and
{}^{12}\mathrm{CO}\mathrm{\penalty\ J}=2-1
line emission of 20 nearby protoplanetary discs. They confirmed without a doubt, that
the brightest and largest
protoplanetary discs
can
contain visible dust rings and gaps as well as asymmetries in their dust profile. At
\lambda\approx 1.25
mm, the emission is dominated by thermal radiation from millimeter-sized dust grains in the disc mid-plane, which allows the identification of disc substructures. Similar observations have also been made by ODISEA (
42
), exoALMA (
66
),
MAPS (
40
) and the ALMA survey of the Lupus (
2
) and Taurus (
26
) star forming regions.
A better understanding of the formation of these dust structures would provide deeper insight into the dynamics of the disc.

In this active field of research, several different theoretical formation mechanisms for the development of substructure have been identified. Such mechanisms range from the formation of pressure bumps at the inner edge of the dead zone
(
4
;
68
;
16
;
15
;
53
)
, to spontaneous dusty rings due to the streaming instability
(
73
;
43
)
, differential radial drift
(
23
)
, magnetic effects
(
8
;
52
)
, or thermal instabilities
(
67
;
65
;
44
)
, to warps
(

;
63
)
and shadow-driven structures
(
64
;
49
;
80
;
76
)
.

Another commonly proposed explanation is the presence of embedded planets. They launch spiral waves, due to a resonance between the planet’s gravitational potential and the epicyclic motion of the disc’s material (
18
;
41
). For sufficiently high planetary masses or low disc viscosities, the excited spiral waves steepen into shocks, clearing material from the planet’s vicinity, thereby opening a gap (
19
;
50
).
77
;
36
;
78
and
81
showed that a single non-migrating planet has the ability to open multiple gaps and studied the effects of radiative processes on gap formation.

31
(hereafter
31
) and later
70
(hereafter
70
), who considered higher viscosities, further investigated the migration of a single planet in an isothermal disc and discovered migration jumps, referred to as ”intermittent migration”. Those jumps were able to create dust ring and gap structures which remained visible when plotting the continuum emission at (sub)millimetre wavelengths.
32
(hereafter
32
) continued an analysis of the migration, covering different aspect ratios, viscosities and disabling the gas self-gravity for all simulations. They also observed intermittent migration jumps, further confirming their existence.

In general, planetary migration is highly dependent on the level of turbulence within the disc, typically modelled as an effective ”viscosity” with an
\alpha
parameter
(
61
)
. For
\alpha\gtrsim 10^{-3}
, planets migrate in the classical type-I and type-II regimes (
46
;
24
), based on whether they can open a gap or not. Type-I migration is driven by an interplay of Lindblad and corotation torques, resulting in a rapid migration. For an Earth-sized planet this corresponds to a migration timescale of
\tau_{mig}\sim 10^{5}
yr (
24
).

If the planet opens a sufficiently deep gap, clearing its corotation region, then only very few Lindblad modes are acting, which produce a negligible net torque.
24
suggest, that the planet is now coupled to the viscous evolution of the disc, since the deep gap prevents any material from entering the inner disc. On the other hand,
14
found that the planet only prevents gas from crossing the gap in its immediate vicinity, challenging the idea that the planet is coupled to the viscous evolution. This results in migration rates which differ significantly from the viscous timescale. Furthermore,
60
showed that a planet in the type-II regime might switch from inwards to outwards migration, now decoupled from the viscous evolution but migrating with a similar rate. Additionally, a planet might undergo phases of very rapid migration, which are often labelled as type-III migration (
28
;
56
).

In recent years, a number of studies have suggested that the disc’s viscosity takes lower values, with a median around
\alpha\sim 10^{-4}
(
69
;
13
;
59
)
, but its value remains a topic of discussion. We further have to acknowledge, that inferring values for the viscosity from observations comes with limitations.

83
(hereafter
83
) introduced new migration regimes in these nearly inviscid discs, which emerge for planets in a certain mass range. Hence, migration is more diverse than thought of before.

As a continuation of
31
and
32
work, our goal of this paper is to investigate a single planet’s ability to open multiple gaps while including radiative processes in the disc. We tailor our setups towards modelling the outer disc, ensuring a comparability to the ALMA observations. We further analyse the lifetime and appearance of the created rings and gaps and make a statement about the structures observability.

In Sect.
2

3
and discuss them in Sect.
4

5

PHYSICS AND NUMERICS

In this section, we briefly outline the physical model and numerical setup. Our radiative cooling model is adopted from
84
, but we will provide a short summary.

2.1
Physical model

We model a vertically integrated disc around a central star. The gas is assumed to be ideal with a surface density
\Sigma
, internal energy
e
, and velocity
\mathbf{u}
. Its equations of motion are given by the Navier–Stokes equations in cylindrical coordinates
\{R,\phi\}
with the adiabatic index
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\,\nabla\,\Sigma=-\Sigma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u},

(1)

\displaystyle\Sigma\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\Sigma(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}=-\nabla P-\Sigma\mathbf{g}+\nabla\cdot\bar{\sigma},

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Sigma e}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\Sigma e\mathbf{u})=-P\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}-\Sigma c_{\mathrm{v}}\frac{T-T_{0}}{\beta}\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}.

(3)

Here,
\bar{\sigma}
denotes the viscous stress tensor and
P=(\gamma-1)\Sigma e
the vertically integrated pressure. The isothermal sound speed is given by
c_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{P/\Sigma}=\sqrt{\mathcal{R}T/\mu}
with the mean molecular mass
\mu=2.35
. We define a pressure scale height
H=c_{\mathrm{s}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}
with the Keplerian frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}=\sqrt{\text{G}M_{*}/R^{3}}
. The aspect ratio
h
is given by
h=H/R
, with
R
being the radial distance to the central star. The viscosity follows the model from
61
with
\nu=\alpha c_{\mathrm{s}}H
. We define the vorticity
\omega=\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{u}
and the inverse vortensity (IV) of the gas:

\text{IV}=\frac{\Sigma}{\omega}\,.

(4)

This quantity will be used to confirm the existence of small vortices in the disc, which present as local maximum.
We use the inverse vortensity, as it has been shown that the coorbital mass deficit, which is related to intermittent migration, scales with
IV

(
28
;
70
)
.

Viscous heating is not included since the paper focuses on planets predominately in the outer regions of the disc. Following
84
and
36
, we define cooling timescales for cooling by radiative loss from the surface (now surface cooling), cooling of the mid-plane through radial radiative diffusion (now mid-plane cooling) and flux-limited diffusion (FLD). Estimating a cooling timescale using
t_{\mathrm{cool}}\sim e/\dot{e}
(
81
) results in a dimensionless cooling timescale for surface cooling.

\beta_{\mathrm{surf}}=\frac{e}{|Q_{\mathrm{cool}}|}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{K}},\quad Q_{\mathrm{cool}}=-2\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}\frac{T^{4}}{\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}}\quad\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{3\tau}{8}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}+\frac{1}{4\,\tau},

(5)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}
denotes the Boltzmann constant and the effective optical depth
\tau_{\text{eff}}
follows
21
. Defining a timescale for mid-plane cooling is more difficult, since the diffusion length
l_{\mathrm{d}}
is not constant.
36
showed, that if we thermally perturb the disc, we can approximate the
m
-th azimuthal Fourier component of this perturbation. Combining this approximation with the assumption, that the dominant diffusion length
l_{\mathrm{d}}
is of order
H
(
84
) results in:

\beta_{\mathrm{mid}}=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}}{\eta}\left(H^{2}+\frac{1}{3}l_{\mathrm{rad}}^{2}\right).

(6)

We define
l_{\mathrm{rad}}=1/(\kappa\rho)
and with the opacity
\kappa
. Combining Eq.
5
and Eq.
6
produces a
\beta
-cooling description of FLD:

\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}=\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{surf}}}{1+f},\quad f=\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{surf}}}{\beta_{\mathrm{mid}}}=16\pi\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{R}}}\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{6\tau_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}+\pi}.

(7)

We assume that
\tau_{\mathrm{R}}=\tau_{\mathrm{P}}
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{R}}
is the Rosseland mean optical depth and
\tau_{\mathrm{P}}
the
Planck
mean optical depth. In contrast to a full radiative description,
\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}
cools the disc only locally.
84
showed that
\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}
can substitute full FLD with respect to the cooling time in shock-driven gap opening, but it cannot reproduce all migration-related effects of FLD (
82
). With this, we define three different equations of state, adjusting the complexity of them step-wise:

1.

locally isothermal
: There is no compressive heating and the disc is in thermal equilibrium. The temperature is given by a balance between irradiation and surface-cooling following
33
:

T(R)=18.07\,\text{K}\,\left(\frac{R}{\text{50\,au}}\right)^{-3/7}\,\Rightarrow\,\,h(R)=0.06\,\left(\frac{R}{\text{50\,au}}\right)^{2/7}.

(8)

With
c_{\mathrm{s}}\propto\sqrt{T}
, the pressure is linked directly to the surface density.

2.

constant
\beta=1
: The system exponentially cools back to the steady-state temperature profile given by Eq.
8
and is only heated by shocks, as in Eq. (
3
).

3.

adaptive
\beta
: The cooling timescale instead follows a density-, temperature-, and opacity-dependent formulation through Eqs. (
5
)–(
7
). This represents an accurate model of
\beta
following
36
;
84
;
79
. Figure
1
depicts
\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}
over the disc’s extent for our setup at
t=0
kyr.

We chose to set
\beta=1
for the constant
\beta
-cooling simulation, since it translates to strong radiative damping of planetary wakes and therefore a single gap configuration
(
37
;
84
;
75
)
. This minimizes the risk of secondary gaps produced by the planet
(
77
)
affecting its inward migration track, but does not necessarily eliminate it
(
25
;
71
, e.g.,)
.

As shown by
82
, radiative processes can modify the local vortensity
\varpi
via baroclinic forcing
\mathcal{S}
:

\frac{\partial\varpi}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\,\varpi=\frac{\nabla\Sigma\times\nabla P}{\Sigma^{3}}=\frac{P}{TR\Sigma^{3}}\left(\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial R}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial\phi}\frac{\partial T}{\partial R}\right)=\mathcal{S}.

(9)

Specifically, a parcel of gas approaching the planet within the horseshoe region, will gain (lose) vortensity as it U-turns behind (ahead of) the planet. Cooling results in an asymmetry between the two sides and typically leads to vortensity growth within the planet’s horseshoe region, with the effect maximized for
\beta\sim 1
. This can substantially influence the planet’s migration, as we will see below. We note that using
\beta_{\text{mid}}
as a substitute for in-plane diffusion can exaggerate this effect
(
82
)
, however we choose to use this approach due to the prohibitive computational costs of a fully radiative model.

2.2
Numerical setup

We use the hydrodynamic code
FARGOCPT
(
55
), a successor of
Fargo
(
29
), to create a vertically integrated, cylindrical symmetric (
R,\phi
) disc extending from 5–500 au. The data is evaluated using
python
. The grids resolution is set to 16 cells per scale height at 50 au in radial and azimuthal direction. This translates into
1230\times 1676
approximately square cells, logarithmically spaced in the radial direction. The inner and outer boundary is set to outflow, both having damping zones in front of them. These damping zones range from 5–6 au and
425
–
500
au respectively, damping the values exponentially on a timescale of
10^{-1}\,2\pi/\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}
,
evaluated at the boundary
. Different configurations of boundary conditions, damping zones and exponential cut-off where tested beforehand and the chosen ones prevent any pileups at the inner and reflections of density waves at the outer boundary. The initial surface density is set to:

\Sigma=10\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{cm}^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{R}{50\,\mathrm{au}}\right)^{-1}\,C_{\mathrm{o}}(r).

(10)

An outer surface density profile cut-off is given by
C_{\mathrm{o}}(r)
implemented starting at 350 au

C_{\mathrm{o}}=\left(1+\exp\left(\frac{\left(-\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)+r_{\mathrm{oC}}}{w_{\mathrm{oC}}}\right)\right)^{-1}.

(11)

Here
r_{\mathrm{oC}}=350
au is the cut-off point and
w_{\mathrm{oC}}=35
au the cut-off width. The total disk mass between 5–500 au is
\approx 0.12\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.

We have disabled the gas self-gravity, since the Toomre parameter
Q=\frac{c_{s}\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}}{\pi\mathrm{G}\Sigma}>1
over the entire disc. We use a turbulent
\alpha
-value of
\alpha=10^{-4}
for our viscosity. The disc’s aspect ratio follows Eq. (
8
). The central star has the parameters of our sun with the grid frame is centred on the star. All these disc parameters fall within the range characteristic of
DSHARP
discs, except for the radial extent (
13
;
20
;
74
). We set the planet mass
M_{\mathrm{p}}=100\,\text{M}_{\oplus}
. We introduce the thermal mass:

M_{\mathrm{th}}=2\frac{h_{\mathrm{p}}^{3}}{3}M_{*}

(12)

as well as feedback mass:

M_{\mathrm{f}}\approx 3.8\left(\frac{Q}{h}\right)^{-5/13}M_{\mathrm{th}}

(13)

for later analysis. If
M_{\mathrm{p}}\approx M_{\mathrm{th}}
, then the wakes created by the planet steepen to shock waves straight from launch, while a planet with
M_{\mathrm{p}}\approx M_{\mathrm{f}}

M_{\mathrm{p}}
to

M_{\mathrm{p}}

M_{\mathrm{f}}

and
\approx 3.4\,M_{\mathrm{f}}

R_{\mathrm{p}}=50
au and is allowed to migrate immediately. The planet’s potential is given by:

\Phi_{\mathrm{p,2D}}=-\frac{\text{G}M_{\mathrm{p}}}{\sqrt{d^{2}+s^{2}}},\quad\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{p}}\,

(14)

with the smoothing length
s=\epsilon H
and the smoothing parameter
\epsilon=0.6

RESULTS

3.1
Migration regimes

One aspect of the simulation analysis is the identification of the planet’s migration regimes and their link to the feedback and thermal masses
M_{\text{f}},\,M_{\text{th}}
(see Eqs. (
12
) & (
13
)).
31
and
83
demonstrated, that in nearly inviscid discs, planets with masses
M_{\text{f}}\lesssim M_{\text{p}}\lesssim M_{\text{th}}
migrate in the turbulent, vortex-assisted regime.

The planet opens a partial, asymmetric gap that is deeper behind the planet than ahead of it, resulting in a pronounced surface density gradient at the outer gap edge
(
83
, see also Fig. 5 in)
. This configuration is eventually unstable to the Rossby wave instability (RWI,
27
), and is further subject to baroclinic forcing via radiative processes that can significantly modify the vortensity around the planet’s orbit
(
82
;
83
)
. The change in vortensity is directly linked to a change in the surface density profile, which produces more RWI vortices. These RWI vortices (driven by baroclinic forcing or not) at the outer gap edge refill the gap and hence sustain the outer Lindblad torques (
3
), driving the migration inwards. With a width of
\sim 6
–
23
% of the half-width of the horseshoe region, these small vortices are often only visible when zooming closely into the inverse vortensity (IV) profile. They can also be detected by plotting the relative deviation from the azimuthally averaged inverse vortensity:
\text{IV}/\overline{\text{IV}}-1
. We will not distinguish between two vortex-assisted sub-regimes, one without cooling and one with cooling, but stress that if cooling is active, the baroclinic forcing significantly alters the regime.
As
M_{\text{th}}
decreases while the planet migrates inwards, the planet mass eventually becomes super-thermal and transitions into the type-II regime, ultimately stalling its migration.

We additionally observed the intermittent migration first introduced by
31

(
70
;
83
;
32
, see also)
. Intermittent migration can be described by brief episodes of type-III migration (
28
), where the dynamical corotation on the planet
(
45
)

\Gamma_{\text{h}}=2\pi\left(1-\frac{\text{IV}_{\text{h}}}{\text{IV}_{\text{p}}}\right)\Sigma_{\mathrm{p}}R_{\text{p}}^{2}x_{\text{h}}\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{\text{d}R_{\text{p}}}{\text{d}t}-u_{R}\right)

(15)

becomes strongly negative momentarily due to a sharp contrast between the IV enclosed within the horseshoe region (
\text{IV}_{\text{h}}
) and the local, approximately Keplerian, IV (
\approx 2\Omega_{\text{K}}^{-1}
). This process may repeat several times. In the above equation,
x_{\text{h}}
denotes the half-width of the horseshoe region
(
47
)
,
u_{R}
is the local radial velocity of the disc, and quantities with a subscript “p” are evaluated at the planet’s location.

Intermittent migration can be further
divided into the following phases, which we will explain using our fiducial model
iso
(Fig.
2
).

a)

First, due to the planet’s gap
opening, an IV maximum forms ahead of the planet (
R<R_{\mathrm{p}}
) and an IV minimum behind it (
R>R_{\mathrm{p}}
).

b)

The minimum and maximum
migrate inwards alongside the planet. In this paper, the planet migrates in the vortex-assisted regime.

c)

In this work, the planet’s migration is accelerated by the small RWI vortices at the outer gap edge. This acceleration enables the planet to catch up with the IV structures it has generated, which in turn triggers a short episode of runaway migration. All the material of the IV maximum ahead of the planet is now able to enter the horseshoe region, performing U-turns and accelerating the migration (see Eq.
15
), breaking into small vortices during this process.

d)

This runaway phase ends, if all the material once in the IV maximum ahead of the planet has entered the horseshoe region. After the jump, the planet’s semi-major axis fluctuates for a short duration, caused by the small vortices created during the jump.

4
DISCUSSION

In this section we evaluate our results and their implications. We further discuss their limitations of our method as well as possible ways to improve.

4.1
Planet migration

We observe, that regardless of the chosen EOS, our planet migrates in the vortex-assisted regime and may undergo one or several migration jumps. At its initial radial location, the planet has a mass of
M_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 2\,M_{\mathrm{th}}
in models
iso
,
beta
and
adapt
and
M_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 1.3\,M_{\mathrm{th}}
in model
adapt-low-mass
. Since model
adapt
did not experience a migration jump, but models
beta
and
adapt-low-mass
did, we can infer that the transition between the intermittent and type-II migration regimes is dependent on both planet mass and the local cooling timescale. This implies that modelling observed substructures in the context of migrating planets should be done carefully, including a prescription for cooling.

We further carried out models with with
\beta
values of
0.01,3
and
7.5
, the migration tracks for which are shown in Appendix
A
. The simulation with
\beta=0.01
and
3
also undergo intermittent migration when reaching the same radial location as
iso
and
beta
. This is most likely related to these models having comparable gap structures, due to the similar angular momentum flux profiles for
\beta=1
and
3
(
79
). The simulation with
\beta=7.5
does not experience any jumps and has a significantly different gap structure. This suggests that planets that open similar gap structures, regardless of cooling timescale, should perform a jump at similar radii.

Nevertheless, we have to keep in mind that
\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}
only cools locally. The full inclusion of FLD might be relevant, for example because
\beta_{\mathrm{fld}}
overestimates the vortensity growth rate (
82
). This could be studied further in future work.

4.2
Lifetime of radial structures

We have seen, that most of the created dust structures remain visible in the intensity image at
t=500
kyr. Our isothermal simulation was able to confirm the results from
70
,
31
and
32
. As
32
pointed out with their analysis for different levels of viscosity, long-lasting structures were only present for
\alpha\leq 10^{-4}
.

70
proposed, that the lifetime of those structures depends on the time spent in slow migration before or between the jumps. To validate this statement in radiative discs, we additionally evaluate the simulations mentioned in Appendix
A
. Comparing the pressure
maxima and minima
and surface density profiles, we can always find a clear correlation between the lifetimes of the structures and the respective duration of slow migration (vortex-assisted migration in this paper), in line with
70
. We find no direct correlation between the cooling timescale and the dissipation of pressure
maxima
(although an indirect connection can be identified, since the cooling timescale influences the planets’ migration and number of jumps). This is expected, as the lifetime of these structures is tied to the diffusion (i.e., viscous) timescale, which does not depend on the cooling timescale.

4.3
Vortex lifetimes

We observe large-scale vortices (
\sim
3–5 au in radial width) in models
iso
and
beta
, but not in model
adapt
or
adapt-low-mass
. They are decoupled from the planet, not influencing its migration, and persist for an average of
120
kyr in mode
iso
and
55
kyr in model
beta
. This is short compared to the disc’s lifetime and simulation runtime
(
57
;
51
, consistent with)
. Thus, one would expect observations of dust asymmetries caused by vortices to be less abundantly observed than ring-like structures. Additionally they are only created in the simulation with less realistic heating and cooling implementations.

An additional comparison of the vortex lifetime between the different simulation (including Appendix
A
) reveals, that vortices only survive marginally longer for
\beta=0.01
than for
\beta=1
, and no vortices are created if
\beta>1
. This partially agrees with
57
, which stated the shortest vortex lifetimes for
\beta=1
and longer lifetimes for
\beta<1
than for
\beta>1
. The planet mass used in both paper differs significantly, since
57
used a Jupiter mass planet. This may explain parts of the discrepancies.

We further observe, that the locations of the large-scale vortices coincide with the created pressure maxima in model
iso
and
beta
. In model
adapt-low-mass
, the pressure maxima still appear, but are considerably weaker without any pre-existing large-scale vortices. Furthermore, the lifetime of the vortices does not determine the visibility and dissipation of the dust structures. Therefore contrary to the results of
32
, for discs with more realistic cooling timescales, pressure maxima are nor strictly tied to large-scale vortices.

We find, that the inner disc of model

5
CONCLUSION

This paper demonstrates, that one single migrating planet could produce several visible dust rings and gaps for different EOS. These structures remain visible for at least 300–400 kyr after their creation and probably even several hundreds of kyr more, a sizeable fraction of the median disc lifetime (
5
). Therefore we could potentially explain the dynamical history of observed discs with similar structures. We could additionally confirm, that the lifetimes of the produced dust structures depend on the time spent in slow migration before or between migration jumps
(
70
, as in)
, even in radiative discs.

We also saw, that cooling substantially affects the migration behaviour of a planet. The now asymmetric baroclinic forcing is an additional source of vortensity in the horseshoe region, which may accelerate migration, if the planet has not yet opened a deep gap, as seen in models
beta
and
adapt
. How long a planet takes to clear its gap at its final location strongly depends on whether it has migrated far enough into the optically thick part of the disc, where cooling can assist with the gap opening process
(
83
, see)
.

Some simulations showed large-scale vortices, always tied to IV maxima. With an average vortex lifetime of only
\sim 90
kyr in our models, we should not observe protoplanetary discs with asymmetries caused by them quite as often as without, if they’re related to gap-opening planets. They do also not appear in the simulations with the most realistic cooling prescription, likely due to their faster dissipation due to cooling
(
17
;
57
, e.g.,)
. This of course does not include asymmetries created by other mechanisms, such as material left at the Lagrange point, observed in all simulations.

Our results indicate that migrating planets could be the origin of some of the substructure observed in millimetre emission with ALMA, and highlight the importance of including a realistic cooling prescription when modelling this behaviour. As such, care should be taken when both modelling and interpreting such structures.

Differentiating between observable signatures of migrating or non-migrating planets, or even of other origin, is beyond the scope of this paper, but could be the focus of further work.

does not undergo a migration jump, since the vortices did not accelerate the planet’s migration sufficiently for it to catch up with its own gap.

In Sect.

we discussed the mechanism behind the formation of a high-IV (i.e., low-vortensity) ribbon exterior to the planet’s orbit, notably outside of the planet’s horseshoe region. As mentioned in the main text, this ribbon is the product of significant baroclinic forcing as gas interacts with the shock front at
\phi\approx\phi_{\text{p}}
before shearing past the planet. This suggests that there should be a clear dependence of this forcing term on the temperature gradient across the shock
\partial T/\partial\phi
, and, therefore, on the cooling timescale.

To investigate this, we ran a set of five models using the
PLUTO
code
(
34

35
)
, mirroring the setup in Sect.
3.2.2
but using a lower resolution of
N_{R}\times N_{\phi}=256\times 768
and holding the planet fixed at 50 au for 10 orbits. Each of these five models uses a different cooling prescription: locally isothermal (
\beta=0
), cooling with
\beta\in[0.1,1,10]
, and adiabatic (
\beta\rightarrow\infty
). We then compute the individual components of the baroclinic forcing term at
t=10
planetary orbits as in Eq. (
9
):

\mathcal{S}_{1}=\frac{P}{TR\Sigma^{3}}\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial R}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\phi},\quad\mathcal{S}_{2}=-\frac{P}{TR\Sigma^{3}}\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial\phi}\frac{\partial T}{\partial R},

(17)

with
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2}
. The results are shown in Fig.
17
, which illustrates that this baroclinic forcing does indeed depend strongly on the prescribed cooling timescale. In particular, we find that the forcing responsible for the high-IV ribbons (identified by green patches close to the planet in the bottom row, as in Fig.
9

\beta\sim 1

3.2.2

By plotting the individual components of
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{S}_{1}
(top row) and is mostly sensitive to
\partial T/\partial\phi

7

\partial T/\partial\phi=0

3.2.1

\mathcal{S}_{2}
