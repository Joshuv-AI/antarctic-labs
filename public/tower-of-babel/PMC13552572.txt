Authors: Christina Lamb, Sophie Bertaud
Published: 2026

Introduction

This perspective examines how current approaches to ethical decision making for children who receive palliative care can be expanded through ethics of care. Ethics of care is a moral theory that emphasises the importance of interpersonal relationships, empathy, compassion and responsiveness [1].

Palliative care for children and young people with life‐limiting or life‐threatening conditions is an active and holistic approach to care. It encompasses physical, psycho‐social, spiritual and emotional care provision from the point of diagnosis up to, and including, death [2]. Professionals who provide paediatric palliative care (PPC) place emphasis on improving the child's quality of life and relieving suffering [2].

Clinical ethics has become an important part of delivering PPC. This is due to the ethically complex nature of paediatric care and the often‐uncertain trajectory of illnesses that require PPC. The context for making ethical decisions about children's care has become increasingly important through shared decision‐making models [3]. These approaches make children's voices, vulnerabilities and dependency much more transparent and their care is more attuned to their unique identities and contexts [3]. Attunement involves deeply sensing, understanding and responding to the needs of another in real time [2]. Attunement is clinically relevant because children who receive PPC are particularly vulnerable and susceptible to power imbalances in healthcare [2, 3]. Power imbalances may occur because adult‐centric perspectives often guide how care is provided in paediatrics. For example, most approaches to paediatric clinical ethics use bioethical approaches that originate in adult environments.

One such approach is principlism. Principlists use the ethical principles of autonomy, justice, beneficence and non‐maleficence to make ethical decisions (see Table 1) [4]. Although principlism has made helpful contributions to clinical ethics for adults, it is less relevant for PPC. This is because children's holistic needs increase as their health deteriorates. Children therefore require a different ethical approach: one that values the holistic and inter‐personal dimensions of care.

This Perspective seeks to highlight the benefit of adapting paediatric clinical ethics to the unique needs and experiences of children. This requires healthcare providers to move away from adult‐focused approaches, such as principlism, towards ethical theories that pay more attention to the relationships involved in paediatric care. We have drawn on our respective research to outline how ethics of care can add to clinical ethics in PPC [2, 3]. We provide a case example, analysis and recommendations for incorporating ethics of care into practice through training and education.

Principlism and Ethics of Care

Principlism was pioneered by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress [4]. Their approach has become internationally renowned for the role it plays in ethics education for medical and nursing students and training for clinical ethicists. However, the framework's simplicity has posed challenges for those dealing with ethical conflicts that are complex or population specific. Naturally, seriously ill children benefit from not being harmed and deserve to be treated justly in accordance with their clinical needs. However, paediatric care is unique, because children do not make decisions about their care in isolation. Rather, children exist on a spectrum of dependence [3]. Principlism maintains that autonomy is based on independence and self‐determination [4]. However, children require adults who are invested in ethical models that enable them to voice their concerns, honour their vulnerability and respect their dependency [3].

One approach to PPC ethics that triangulates these characteristics is ethics of care. This originated with Carol Gilligan's theory of care which acknowledges human vulnerability and interdependency [5]. The requisite theoretical values are respect, protection and understanding the power that lies within human systems and structures [5]. Ethics of care does not provide a fully developed bioethical framework for children. However, it does allow for an augmented approach to adult understandings of ethics that can be applied to children. We propose an amalgamated approach, which adapts ethics of care so that it can provide an ethics model that retains the traditional principled structure that clinicians are familiar with, whilst bringing in an understanding of how relationships are fundamental to paediatric care.

An Amalgamated Approach to Ethics in PPC

Ethics of care crucially recognises that human beings are vulnerable and that we depend on, and have responsibilities to, one another. The interdependence of all those involved in paediatric care, and particularly in PPC, is vital. One way to build a bridge between care ethics and principlism is through the concept of justice. Principlism is geared towards adult contexts and focuses on what patients are individually owed by others, such as healthcare staff or systems. This is known as distributive justice. In ethics of care, caring is a moral concept, and justice is shaped by society; a moral society is a caring one [1]. In paediatric healthcare, the child, family and care providers reflect a broader, inter‐personal picture of social justice. This is because they comprise a moral and, therefore, caring community. Following on from this, ethics of care considers what children are owed by others in moral communities because they exist in this inter‐personal context.

Similarly, adult versions of autonomy are rarely appropriate for paediatric patients. Our amalgamated model would allow traditional autonomy, which prioritises how an individual asserts their independence, to be replaced by relational autonomy. Relational autonomy recognises that our autonomy can fully flourish when it is developed and sustained as part of our caring and moral relationships with others [6]. Applied to children, relational autonomy respects a child's evolving ability to make their own decisions. This can allow space for decision‐making processes in PPC to be more responsive to children and for responsibility to feel shared. Crucially, it recognises how a child will make decisions within a family. This inter‐personal context is broader than the individual contexts used in principlism.

Potential power differentials in paediatric care and ethical decision‐making can also be balanced through accompaniment. In Ethics of care, accompaniment is defined as being present and journeying with vulnerable individuals [1]. This involves empowering others by listening and recognising our shared humanity. Accompaniment is especially important in paediatrics, where children depend on others for their care. Adults have an ethical responsibility to provide care in ways that children can understand and respond to while ensuring that their views and voices are heard.

Ethics of Care Case Study

This case study illustrates how ethics of care can inform PPC.

Rudy is a 9‐year‐old boy with relapsed neuroblastoma, who is currently receiving care on a paediatric ward. His parents, Jane and Alex, have been discussing whether to pursue further experimental treatments with his oncologist Dr. Smith. Jane wants to try anything to prolong Rudy's life, but Alex is worried that Rudy is getting tired and that his prolonged hospital stay is making him miserable. It is also very disruptive for his sister Lily. Dr. Smith is keen to try the experimental treatment and feels a huge weight of responsibility to help this family. Rudy has a close relationship with one of his nurses, Katie, and recently told her that he knows he is dying and feels too tired to undergo any more treatment. However, he is worried about how his mum will cope once he's gone.

This case shows how children are frequently part of an intricate network of relationships, which includes family members and healthcare professionals. Making the right decisions in this context can be complicated. Using an ethics of care approach in this situation could allow those involved to recognise and respect the child's connectedness to everyone around them. In this case, it could enable open conversations between Rudy, his parents and the healthcare team, which would allow Rudy's voice to be heard. It would also acknowledge the vulnerability of his parents, his wider family and the staff involved in his care, as they navigate decisions about his future treatment together.

Taking a decision too quickly, or relying on a purely principlist approach, could be detrimental in this case. This is because it may become impossible to resolve the conflict between the oncologist's commitment to beneficence, the mother's surrogate decision‐making authority and Rudy's emerging autonomy. The result could be painful disagreements and moral distress for all those involved and, ultimately, Rudy's care would suffer. A principlist resolution would focus on the narrow question of who has the authority to make a decision. However, our amalgamated approach would maintain the integrity of the network of relationships and allow the ethical concerns of all involved to be taken into account. Reconceptualising Rudy's autonomy as relational and extending care to his family and his medical team, may allow a space for his distress to be truly heard. In this way, a scenario involving seemingly conflicting rights can be teased apart to uncover more comprehensive and compassionate solutions.

Ethics of care requires those in caring roles to be fully present at times of deep human suffering, but this level of emotional investment can take its toll and make it difficult to maintain professional boundaries. It is important to ensure that children are prioritised when such approaches are amalgamated into institutional frameworks. However, it will also be essential to balance the well‐being of those being cared for and those caring for them.

Conclusions

Principlism is a practical and well‐established approach to clinical ethics, but to date has largely benefited adults. More attention needs to be paid to the unique contexts and experiences of children, and these considerations should inform clinical ethics frameworks for PPC. We suggest that existing principled approaches could be augmented by ethics of care theory to better guide the care of children receiving PPC.
