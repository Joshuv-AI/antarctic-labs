Yellow Palm Discoloration After Phenol Exposure
Authors: Makoto Kondo, Hanae Fujita, Koji Habe, Keiichi Yamanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhenol exposure typically causes irritant contact dermatitis with yellow‐brown discoloration. A worker developed characteristic discoloration followed by rapidly evolving distant eruptions consistent with an id reaction after phenol exposure. Symptoms resolved with conservative treatment and elimination of exposure. This rare presentation highlights the diagnostic value of yellow‐brown discoloration and the importance of prompt occupational history‐taking.

Case Image

A 46‐year‐old man, newly assigned to a resin adhesive manufacturing process, noticed painless yellow‐brown discoloration on the dorsal surfaces of both hands after his first workday (Figure 1A,B). He had no significant past medical history, including atopic dermatitis or contact allergies, and no previous history of similar skin eruptions. The following day, mild pain and swelling developed at the same sites (Figure 1C). Two days after exposure, erythematous plaques appeared on the right forearm, left thigh (Figure 1D), and right anterior chest wall (Figure 1E), prompting referral to our dermatology department.

Cutaneous manifestations after phenol exposure. (A) Yellow‐brown discoloration on the dorsal surfaces of both hands immediately after exposure. (B) Close‐up view showing discoloration predominantly affecting the dorsal aspects of the fingers. (C) Erythema and swelling of the right palm 1 day after exposure. (D) Erythematous plaque on the left thigh. (E) Erythematous plaque on the right anterior chest wall.

The patient was afebrile and systemically well, and routine laboratory investigations were normal. Occupational history revealed that, although protective cotton work gloves were worn, he had inadvertently reused gloves contaminated with phenol‐containing resin. Exposure lasted approximately 2 h, and he did not wash his hands until returning home. The immediate yellow‐brown discoloration after the first exposure and the clear history of phenol exposure supported a diagnosis of irritant contact dermatitis. The distant eruptions were considered consistent with secondary autoeczematization (id reaction). He also wore a protective gown and goggles, making direct chemical exposure at distant sites unlikely. None of his coworkers developed similar symptoms.

Treatment consisted of topical betamethasone valerate/gentamicin sulfate ointment twice daily and oral rupatadine 10 mg once daily. No clinical signs of active bacterial infection were present. Because phenol has corrosive properties that may disrupt the skin barrier and increase the risk of secondary bacterial infection, gentamicin was prescribed prophylactically rather than to treat an established infection. The distant eruptions had appeared before topical treatment, making gentamicin sensitization unlikely. The contaminated gloves were discarded, and proper glove replacement procedures were reinforced. All lesions resolved completely within 2 weeks without sequelae. After discussion with the patient, patch testing was not performed because of the irritant and potentially corrosive nature of phenol and because the diagnosis was considered clinically evident.

To our knowledge, reports describing phenol‐induced irritant contact dermatitis accompanied by rapidly developing distant eruptions consistent with an id reaction are extremely limited. Phenol is readily absorbed through the skin and causes protein coagulation, producing characteristic yellow‐to‐yellow‐brown skin discoloration [1]. In addition, phenol has a local anesthetic effect, which may explain the minimal pain observed in the early phase despite ongoing epidermal protein denaturation [2].

The differential diagnosis included allergic contact dermatitis, chemical burn, and secondary bacterial infection. Allergic contact dermatitis, including sensitization to p‐tert‐butylphenol formaldehyde resin, could not be completely excluded because patch testing was not performed [3]. However, the characteristic exposure history, immediate yellow‐brown discoloration after first exposure, absence of systemic inflammatory findings, and rapid resolution after eliminating exposure favored phenol‐induced irritant contact dermatitis. Although the timing was earlier than expected for a classic id reaction, acute phenol‐induced keratinocyte injury may have triggered rapid release of alarmins and damage‐associated molecular patterns, amplifying innate immune responses and producing an id‐like reaction.
