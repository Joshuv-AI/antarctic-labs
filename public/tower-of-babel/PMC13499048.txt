Genetic context shapes interpretation of AIRE and Notch4 in alopecia areata
Authors: Ryan P Hobbs
Published: 2026

Potter et al (2026) reported that Aire and Notch4 allelic variations do not increase alopecia areata (AA) severity in C3H/HeJ mice. In this paper, we place these findings in the context of prior mouse, rat, and human studies linking AIRE to AA and autoimmunity. We propose that AIRE deficiency supports a permissive autoimmune state, whereas genetic background and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) architecture shape disease manifestation and penetrance.

Potter et al (2026) examined the effects of Aire and Notch4 allelic variations in the C3H/HeJ mouse model of AA and reported that neither locus increased disease severity in this genetic background. These findings provide useful insight into gene function within the C3H/HeJ model and offer an opportunity to consider how study design and genetic context shape disease expression.

Our prior work demonstrated that Aire deficiency can give rise to a spontaneous AA-like phenotype in C57BL/6J mice, with incomplete penetrance and an onset that typically occurs at the age >8 months (Maglakelidze et al, 2023). Earlier studies of Aire-deficient mice on the C57Bl/6J background did not report alopecia, although these analyses were not designed to assess AA and did not include detailed or longitudinal evaluations of hair follicle pathology at later time points, when spontaneous AA becomes detectable (Anderson et al, 2002; Ramsey et al, 2002). These differences in experimental focus, timelines, and phenotyping likely contributed to the absence of a reported phenotype. The C57Bl/6J and C3H/HeJ strains differ in MHC haplotype (H2b vs H2k), which is a major determinant of autoimmune susceptibility, with H2b generally associated with resistance and H2k with permissiveness (Sundberg et al, 2003). Two aspects of the study design in Potter et al (2026) are particularly relevant to interpretation. The reported penetrance values (24% in the parental C3H/HeJ strain vs 13.6% in Aire congenic mice) would require substantially larger animal cohorts to detect moderate differences with high statistical confidence. In addition, the Notch4 locus could not be genetically separated from the linked H2b background, such that the absence of AA is more consistent with MHC-associated resistance than with an isolated effect of Notch4. Altogether, this supports a model in which Aire and, potentially, Notch4 function within a background-specific genetic architecture rather than as drivers of disease.

Data from various model systems of AIRE deficiency suggest that AIRE functions as a regulator of immune tolerance, whose phenotypic consequences depend on genetic context. In mice, Aire deficiency on the C57Bl/6J background can produce AA-like disease (Maglakelidze et al, 2023), whereas in the C3H/HeJ background, it does not enhance penetrance under the conditions tested (Potter et al, 2026). In FVB/NJ mice (H2q haplotype) aged to 12 months, Aire deficiency does not result in overt alopecia (unpublished observations, Hobbs Laboratory). In rats, Aire-deficient models generated on distinct strain backgrounds exhibit broadly similar autoimmune features and include alopecia, although whether these fully recapitulate AA remains to be defined (Ossart et al, 2018). In humans, genetic variation in AIRE has been associated with severe forms of AA (Tazi-Ahnini et al, 2002; Wengraf et al, 2008), which supports a role for AIRE in disease susceptibility across species. Consistent with this, AA develops in approximately 30–40% of patients with autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy, a monogenic autoimmune syndrome caused by AIRE deficiency (Sandru et al, 2024). Together, these observations support a framework in which AIRE deficiency establishes a permissive autoimmune state, whereas genetic background (and therefore MHC architecture in inbred mouse strains) shapes disease manifestation. These background-dependent effects likely extend beyond MHC alone and may also reflect broader differences in immune polarization and T-cell responsiveness among rodent strains.

From this perspective, the findings of Potter et al (2026) underscore the extent to which MHC-linked genetic architecture can influence AA penetrance in the C3H/HeJ model and, in doing so, can limit the ability to resolve the contributions of additional loci to AA pathogenesis. When considered in light of limitations in statistical power and genetic resolution, the Potter et al (2026) data are most consistent with MHC-driven effects in the C3H/HeJ model and do not exclude more modest or context-dependent contributions of Aire or Notch4 to AA. Indeed, these findings further emphasize the importance of studying AIRE and Notch4 within appropriate genetic contexts to fully define their roles in AA pathogenesis.
