Levothyroxine Discontinuation: The Potential Role of Excess Iodine Exposure
Authors: Sun Wook Cho
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the study by Ravensberg et al. [1] and the accompanying editorial by Papaleontiou and Cappola [2], which underscore the clinical importance of levothyroxine (LT4) deprescribing and offer practical guidance for its implementation. Building on these reports, we propose that the converging evidence on LT4 discontinuation may be interpreted within a complementary biological framework that accounts for population-level iodine exposure.

Across studies, approximately one-quarter of patients successfully discontinue LT4 therapy [1]. Similar findings were reported in a Korean cohort study, in which successful discontinuation was associated with lower LT4 requirements, lower baseline thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, and a shorter duration of therapy [3]. These shared clinical characteristics suggest that successful discontinuation may occur preferentially in patients with preserved intrinsic thyroid functional reserve, rather than in those with irreversible thyroid failure.

This interpretation is supported by population-based evidence showing that subclinical hypothyroidism frequently normalizes spontaneously in older adults [4]. In addition, the randomized trial cited by Ravensberg et al. [1] found that LT4 withdrawal in selected patients with subclinical hypothyroidism did not worsen thyroid-related symptoms or quality of life [5]. Together, these findings support the biological plausibility that, in a subset of patients, long-term LT4 therapy may reflect potentially reversible thyroid functional suppression rather than permanent gland failure.

In this context, population-level iodine exposure may be an important but under-recognized modifier of thyroid functional reserve. Excess iodine can induce reversible hypothyroidism in susceptible individuals through persistent inhibition of thyroid hormone synthesis and failure to escape from the Wolff–Chaikoff effect [6]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in iodine-replete populations such as Korea. Nationally representative data from Korean adults showed that iodine intake substantially exceeded recommended levels and was associated with an increased risk of thyroid disease [7].

These findings suggest that LT4 discontinuation depends on both patient-level thyroid functional reserve and contextual factors, including iodine-related thyroid suppression and the use of structured deprescribing strategies. In iodine-replete populations, some LT4-treated patients may have reversible thyroid dysfunction related to excess iodine exposure, whereas in iodine- sufficient populations, successful discontinuation may more directly reflect residual thyroid tissue and function. Incorporating thyroid functional reserve and iodine exposure may refine patient selection and support a more precise approach to thyroid hormone therapy.
