Diets fermented by crab-derived lactic acid bacteria enhance growth, modulate the gut microbiota, and improve hepato-intestinal health in Eriocheir sinensis
Authors: Jie Xu, Yuheng Wu, Jiasang Wu, Anran Wang, Xin Zhang, Xiumei Xing, Longlong Fu, Xuedi Du, Shuyan Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProper fermentation of aquafeed using functional probiotics can enhance crustacean health and feed utilization. In this study, two crab-derived lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum MW 2406C and Lactobacillus brevis Myh YC-01, were used for feed fermentation. Furthermore, their effects on the growth performance, intestinal microbiota, and hepato-intestinal health of Eriocheir sinensis were evaluated. A total of 300 crabs (initial body weight: 75.53 ± 0.10 g) were randomly assigned to five dietary groups, with six replicates of 10 crabs each: non-fermented diet (NFD); sterile fermented diet (SFD), a basal diet fermented without bacterial inoculation and only added with sterilized broth and water; L. plantarum-fermented diet (PFD), a basal diet augmented with L. plantarum at 1 × 106 CFU/g; L. brevis-fermented diet (BFD), a basal diet augmented with L. brevis at 1 × 106 CFU/g; and mixture of fermented diet (MFD), fermented with L. plantarum and L. brevis in a 1:1 ratio, with a total bacterial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/g. After feeding for 12 weeks, both the PFD and MFD groups had significantly higher digestive and antioxidant enzyme activities (P < 0.05). Moreover, the PFD and MFD groups showed upregulated expression of immune-related genes (hsp70, crustin, alf1, propo, toll1, toll2, and pelle) and intestinal barrier-related genes (pt, zo-1, and pm) (P < 0.05). Similarly, the absolute real-time quantitative PCR quantification and 16S rRNA sequencing analysis showed that fermented diets reshaped the gut microbiota by enriching beneficial taxa, suppressing potential pathogens, and increasing the detectable abundance of the supplemented LAB strains in the intestinal contents. This study also established a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced model of crab enteritis to investigate the mechanisms by which the strains alleviate intestinal inflammation. The results revealed that fermented diets attenuated LPS-induced inflammatory responses by reducing the expression of inflammation-related genes (ilf2, litaf, and p38mapk) and restoring the expression of genes associated with the gut barrier (pt, zo-1, and pm), potentially by modulating the NF-κB and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. In conclusion, functional compound diets fermented with crab-derived LAB strains represent a promising and effective strategy for enhancing growth performance, modulating gut microbiota composition, and improving hepato-intestinal health in crab aquaculture.Keywords: Crab-derived lactic acid bacteria, Fermented diet, Gut microbiota, Hepato-intestinal health, Eriocheir sinensis

Introduction

The Chinese mitten crab (Eriocheir sinensis) is a valuable freshwater aquaculture species due to its superior flavor, high market value, and strong consumer demand (Zhang et al., 2025b). In 2024, the annual production volume of E. sinensis was 894,395 tons (Bureau of Fisheries of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People's Republic of China, 2025). However, the rapid expansion of large-scale crab farming has created a series of challenges. Traditionally, E. sinensis farming primarily relied on feeding various wild fish species, resulting in increased environmental pollution from wastewater (Long et al., 2022). Recently, formulated feeds have been extensively used because they are easy to store and have stable nutrient composition and controllable quality (Aaqillah-Amr et al., 2022). Rising costs of traditional animal-derived protein ingredients, especially fishmeal, have imposed significant economic pressure on feed manufacturers (Xu et al., 2020). This has led to increased use of plant protein sources, such as soybean, peanut, and rapeseed meals, as practical alternatives to fishmeal in aquafeeds due to their relatively low cost (Mugwanya et al., 2022). However, excessive inclusion of plant protein ingredients is often associated with poor palatability, amino acid imbalances, high fiber content, and the presence of anti-nutritional factors (ANFs), all of which adversely affect the feed utilization, growth, and intestinal health of aquatic animals (Hussain et al., 2024).

Microbial fermentation has emerged as an effective approach to improve the nutritional value of plant-based feeds (Rice et al., 2025). For instance, solid-state fermentation reduces crude fiber and ANF contents, improves the amino acid profile, and produces functional bioactive compounds such as organic acids, small peptides, and antioxidants (Álvarez et al., 2025; De Villa et al., 2023; Phinyo et al., 2024). These changes not only increase feed palatability and digestibility but also support host health by regulating the immune system and shaping the gut microbiota. Various microbial strains, including Bacillus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Lactobacillus (Özyurt et al., 2019), have been used as fermenting agents, with each exhibiting strain-specific fermentation characteristics and functional effects. Among these microorganisms, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are particularly useful because they can produce lactic acid, lower pH, synthesize antimicrobial peptides, and enhance nutrient bioavailability. Previous studies have shown that diets fermented with LAB can improve aquatic animals' growth performance (Neves et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023), reduce feed costs (Flefil et al., 2022; Yamamoto et al., 2024), enhance immune function (Hai, 2015), and improve intestinal health (Dawood et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2023). Furthermore, incorporation of LAB-fermented diets can improve flesh quality by modulating amino acid composition and reducing lipid oxidation (Ahmed et al., 2017). However, despite the established use of fermented feed in livestock, poultry, and fish aquaculture (Xu et al., 2023), its application to crustaceans, especially in crab culture, remains underexplored. Notably, strains used for microbial fermentation in aquaculture are generally obtained from diverse sources, including environmental matrices, strains originally developed for other hosts (Jiang et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), or isolates from the target species’ intestine (Sutanti et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). Comparatively, host-derived strains may be more compatible with the physicochemical conditions of the host gut, which could facilitate their persistence and functional expression during long-term feeding (Jiang et al., 2025a, Jiang et al., 2025b). Based on these considerations, several bacterial strains were isolated from the intestine of wild E. sinensis and preliminarily characterized for probiotic properties and safety in the present study. Among them, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum MW 2406C and Lactobacillus brevis Myh YC-01 showed both effective fermentation and beneficial biological functions. Both strains were deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC No. 33339 and CGMCC No. 33441), which enhances traceability and reproducibility for subsequent applications.

Therefore, this study formulated solid-state fermented compound diets using the two crab-derived LAB strains and evaluated their effects on growth performance, hepato-intestinal health, and gut microbiota in E. sinensis. In addition, a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced enteritis model was established to further investigate the mechanisms by which the strains alleviate intestinal inflammation. The findings are expected to provide scientific evidence and practical guidance for the application of microbially fermented feeds in the crab aquaculture industry to promote the use of new feed resources, reduce production costs, and improve the sustainability of freshwater aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

This investigation was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Yangzhou University (approval No. YZUDWSY, 2017–09-06). All experiments were carried out per the recommendations of the State Council of the People's Republic of China on the management of laboratory animals.

Isolation and identification of bacterial strains

In Exp. 1, to investigate the probiotic potential of bacterial strains for functional feed fermentation, two strains were isolated from the intestines of wild E. sinensis and then identified and functionally characterized.

The strains were then propagated in the de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium. Representative colonies were purified and preserved in 20% glycerol at −80 °C. Furthermore, the morphology and growth curves of the strains were assessed. Carbohydrate utilization was assessed using an API 50 CHL kit (bioMérieux SA, Marcy-l’Étoile, Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, France). Genomic DNA was extracted from the isolated strains, and the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), and phylogenetic relationships were evaluated using MEGA version 7.0.

Functional characterization of isolated strains

The antioxidant activity of cell-free supernatants (CFS) was evaluated using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging assays. The antibiotic resistance of isolates was investigated following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (Humphries et al., 2021). An agar well diffusion assay was performed to assess the antimicrobial activity against various strains, including Staphylococcus aureus, Aeromonas hydrophila, and Vibrio anguillarum, and the antibacterial activity was expressed as the diameter of the inhibition zone. Acid tolerance was evaluated by incubating log-phase cultures in MRS broth (pH 5.78) adjusted to pH 4.00, 3.00, 2.00, and 1.00 at 37 °C for 3 h. The samples were then plated on MRS agar for CFU enumeration, and survival rates were expressed as percentages relative to the initial control, which was defined as 100%. Oxygen tolerance was evaluated by exposing log-phase bacterial suspensions to atmospheric oxygen for 0, 15, and 30 min. After each time point, samples were immediately plated on MRS agar and anaerobically incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The survival rates were determined relative to the 0 min control (100%).

Fermentation of strains and feed preparation

Five experimental diets were prepared in this study: non-fermented diet (NFD); sterile fermented diet (SFD), a basal diet fermented without bacterial inoculation and only added with sterilized broth and water; L. plantarum-fermented diet (PFD), a basal diet fermented with L. plantarum at 1 × 106 CFU/g; L. brevis-fermented diet (BFD), a basal diet fermented with L. brevis at 1 × 106 CFU/g; and mixture of fermented diet (MFD), fermented with a 1:1 mixture of L. plantarum and L. brevis at a total of 1 × 106 CFU/g.

The basal diet used in this experiment was based on a commercial crab feed, and the formulation is listed in Table 1, while the nutritional compositions of the fermented and unfermented diets are shown in Table 2. The proximate composition was analyzed following the standard procedures of the AOAC (2006). Moisture content was determined by oven-drying the samples in an oven (645, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) at 105 °C until a constant weight was reached (method 934.01). Crude protein was determined using the Kjeldahl method (method 988.05) with a Kjeldahl apparatus (Kjeltec 8200, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Zealand, Denmark), whereas the crude lipid content was analyzed by ether extraction using a Soxhlet apparatus (ANKOM TX15, ANKOM Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA) (method 2003.05). Moreover, ash content was measured by incineration in a muffle furnace (TM-2010, Beijing Innochem Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at 550 °C for 8 h (method 942.05). Organic matter content was calculated as dry matter minus crude ash. Crude fiber was analyzed via the filtration method (GB/T 6434-2022) (China National Standard, 2022) with a fiber analyzer (Model FT12, C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The gross energy content in the diets was determined using an oxygen bomb calorimeter (6200, Parr Instrument Company, Moline, IL, USA) in accordance with the standard procedure ISO 9831:1998 (ISO, 1998). The amino acid composition of the experimental diets was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to the method described by Heinrikson and Meredith (1984), using an Agilent 1220 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Microorganisms were cultured under specific conditions, including medium, temperature, and incubation duration. Briefly, L. plantarum and L. brevis were streaked on solid MRS agar plates. Single colonies were selected, inoculated into MRS broth, and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, the colonies were inoculated into fresh MRS broth containing peptone (10.0 g), beef extract (8.0 g), yeast extract (4.0 g), glucose (20.0 g), sodium acetate (5.0 g), ammonium citrate (2.0 g), K2HPO4 (2.0 g), MgSO4·7H2O (0.2 g), MnSO4·H2O (0.05 g), Tween 80 (1.0 g), and distilled water (1.0 L) at a 1% inoculum ratio, and incubated under static anaerobic conditions until the late logarithmic growth phase was reached. To prepare a fermented diet, the basal diet was mixed thoroughly with sterilized water at a diet-to-water ratio of 1:0.4. Then, the L. plantarum and L. brevis suspensions were premixed in sterile MRS broth to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/g. After thorough mixing, the mixture was promptly vacuum-sealed and allowed to undergo solid-state fermentation at 37 °C for 7 d. After fermentation, all diets were stored at 4 °C.

Animal rearing and sample collection

All crabs were obtained from a farm in Changzhou, Jiangsu, China, and were acclimated in an indoor recirculating cement pond (4 m × 4 m × 2 m) for two weeks. Crabs were fed a commercial diet three times daily to apparent satiation.

In Exp. 2, the effects of the experimental diets on growth performance, gut microbiota, and hepato-intestinal health of E. sinensis was evaluated. In total, 300 crabs (weighing 75.53 ± 0.10 g initially) were weighed and randomly allocated to 5 groups (n = 6 replicates per group, with 10 crabs per replicate; male-to-female ratio = 1:1). The crabs were reared individually in plastic cages (0.48 m × 0.38 m × 0.36 m) during the 12-week feeding trial.

In Exp. 3, a separate batch of healthy crabs with similar body size and physiological status to those used in Exp. 2 was used to investigate the mechanism by which the bacterial strains modulated intestinal inflammation in crabs. Briefly, 2 groups were fed the NFD diet, while the remaining 3 groups were fed the SFD, PFD, and BFD diets, respectively. Each group contained 6 tanks, each housing 10 individual crabs. After 30 d of feeding, LPS (derived from Escherichia coli 055: B5; purity ≥ 99.8%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to establish an enteritis model. Before injection, LPS was dissolved in sterile normal saline to prepare a working solution of 400 μg/mL. Then, crabs from each group were intraperitoneally injected with either normal saline (NS; received an equal volume of sterile normal saline at 400 μg/kg body weight) or LPS (400 μg/kg), resulting in five treatment groups, including NFD + NS, NFD + LPS, SFD + LPS, PFD + LPS, and BFD + LPS. After injection, crabs were returned to their original tanks, and hepatopancreas and intestinal tissues were collected 24 h post-injection for subsequent analyses.

Crabs were fed to apparent satiation twice per day (08:00 and 18:00) during the two feeding trials. Uneaten feed was collected after 1 h, and molting events were recorded. Furthermore, 50% of the water was replaced daily, and water quality was monitored weekly to ensure stable conditions, as follows: dissolved oxygen 6.7 to 7.2 mg/L, water temperature 20.5 to 25.5 °C, nitrite <0 .02 mg/L, pH 8.0 to 8.2, and total ammonia nitrogen < 0.1 mg/L.

After the feeding trial (Exp. 2), all crabs were fasted for 24 h and subsequently weighed and counted individually to assess feed utilization, growth, and morphological indices. The hemolymph was collected, after which the crabs were anesthetized on ice for 10 min before dissection. Hepatopancreas and intestinal tissues were collected from 5 crabs per replicate and pooled to form a single composite sample for enzyme activity analysis. Samples were fixed in Bouin's solution for intestinal histology analysis. Subsequently, five crabs were chosen from each tank, and their intestinal contents were combined to create a single sample per replication for intestinal microbiota analysis, resulting in three biological replicates after the exclusion of outliers. For gene expression analysis, hepatopancreas and intestinal tissues were randomly obtained from three crabs per replicate.

Following the completion of Exp. 3, including feeding and injection with sterile saline or LPS, hepatopancreas and intestinal tissues were collected as described above for subsequent gene expression analysis.

Antioxidant and digestive enzyme activity measurements

Hepatopancreas and intestinal tissues stored at −80 °C were homogenized and used for enzyme assays (Exp. 2). Digestive and antioxidant enzyme activities were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) with a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (UV1100, Shanghai MAPADA Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Spectrophotometric measurements were conducted at specific wavelengths for key enzyme markers, including α-amylase (α-AMS; C016-1-1) at 540 nm, lipase (A054-1-1) at 420 nm, trypsin (A080-2-2) at 253 nm, superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2) at 550 nm, catalase (CAT; A007-1-1) at 280 nm, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-1-2) at 520 nm, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2) at 412 nm, glutathione S-transferase (GST; A004-1-1) at 412 nm, and malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-1) at 532 nm.

Serum immune-related indices

From each replicate, 5 crabs were randomly selected and pooled to form one composite sample (Exp. 2). Hemolymph was then collected from the pereiopod base with a sterile 1-mL syringe, as described by Wang et al. (2021). The collected hemolymph was placed in 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes and allowed to clot for 4 h at 4 °C. Serum was obtained after centrifugation at 3500 × g for 10 min. The activities of alkaline phosphatase (AKP; A059-2-2), peroxidase (POD; A084-2-1), acid phosphatase (ACP; A060-2-1), and lysozyme (LZM; A050-1-1) were assessed using the relevant commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) with a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (UV1100), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Histological analysis

Intestinal tissue morphology was examined using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Exp. 2), as described by Huang et al. (2022). Briefly, intestinal samples were washed gently with sterile PBS, preserved (1 cm3 sample) in Bouin's solution for 8 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm) before staining with H&E. The stained sections were visualized and photographed using a Leica DM4000 B microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany).

Intestinal microbiota analysis

The intestinal contents of five crabs, randomly selected from each group under sterile conditions, were collected (Exp. 2) and then immediately stored at −80 °C. The microbial communities were analyzed by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Total microbial DNA was extracted using an E.Z.N.A. Bacterial DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). The barcoded universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were used to amplify the hypervariable V3–V4 regions of 16S rRNA genes. The amplicons were sequenced using a paired-end strategy on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Subsequently, raw sequencing data were subjected to quality filtering, then assembled and screened to remove chimeric sequences using QIIME 2 (version 1.17) (Bolyen et al., 2019). The operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold using UPARSE (version 7.1) (Edgar, 2010). Taxonomic annotation was performed using the RDP Classifier against the SILVA database (Quast et al., 2013). Alpha and beta diversity indices were also calculated using QIIME to assess community composition and structure.

RNA extraction and analysis of gene expression

Total RNA (Exp. 2 and 3) from the intestine and hepatopancreas was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). RNA concentration and purity were then evaluated using a NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using the All-In-One First-strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was carried out using a ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix on the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). The 20-μL reaction mixture comprised cDNA template (2 μL), forward primer (0.5 μL), Master Mix (10 μL), reverse primer (0.5 μL), and DEPC water (7 μL). The RT-qPCR cycling parameters were: 3 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 60 s at 60 °C (Xu et al., 2024). β-Actin served as the internal reference, and the 2-△△Ct method was used to calculate gene expression levels (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The primer sequences for the target genes are listed in Table S1. The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S2.

Absolute quantification of bacterial abundance

Total DNA from intestinal contents was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Species-specific primers targeting L. plantarum and L. brevis were used for absolute quantification. The primer sequences are listed in Table S3. The sensitivity and amplification performance of the species-specific primers were evaluated using 10-fold serial dilutions of recombinant plasmids to generate standard curves, which showed good linearity and reliable amplification efficiency. Amplification was conducted on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Bacterial abundance was calculated based on the standard curve.

Results

Isolation and molecular identification of bacterial strains

Two dominant bacterial strains from the intestine of wild E. sinensis were isolated and identified as L. plantarum MW 2406C and L. brevis Myh YC-01 based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogeny analysis (Fig. 1A and B). Morphological observations and growth-curve analyses showed typical colony morphology and bacterial growth patterns, with L. plantarum exhibiting a shorter lag phase and faster growth rate than L. brevis (Fig. 1C and D). Biochemical profiling revealed that both strains had broad carbohydrate fermentation capacities (Table S4).

Functional assessment of the two bacterial strains

The two isolated strains exhibited distinct probiotic-related characteristics in vitro. L. plantarum MW 2406C showed significantly higher DPPH radical-scavenging activity (68.77%) than L. brevis Myh YC-01 (61.05%) (P < 0.001), indicating stronger antioxidant potential (Fig. 2A). Antibiotic susceptibility analysis revealed broad sensitivity to β-lactams, macrolides, and tetracyclines in MW 2406C, while Myh YC-01 exhibited narrower sensitivity (Fig. 2B and C). Furthermore, both strains displayed antagonistic activity against A. veronii, A. hydrophila, and S. aureus, with MW 2406C producing greater inhibition zones (Fig. 2D and E). Oxygen tolerance assays showed time-dependent reductions in survival in both strains under aerobic conditions. These observations were consistent with the plate images, where visible reductions in colony density were observed under prolonged oxygen exposure (Fig. 2F, G, and H). Acid tolerance assays showed that L. plantarum MW 2406C retained higher viability at pH 2.0 to 5.78 compared to L. brevis Myh YC-01 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2I).

The feed utilization and growth performance of crabs

There were no significant differences in SR or FI among all the dietary treatments (P > 0.05; Table 3). Crabs fed the PFD diet had significantly higher FBW, WGR, and SGR than those fed the NFD or SFD diets (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were found across the SFD and NFD groups or among the BFD, PFD, and MFD groups (P > 0.05). The HSI values in the PFD group were significantly reduced relative to the BFD group (P = 0.025), but did not differ significantly from the MFD group (P > 0.05). Alpha and beta diversity

The Chao1 index, which reflects community richness, showed no significant differences across the dietary groups (P = 0.051; Fig. 4C), indicating similar levels of bacterial richness after treatments. However, the Shannon index, which reflects both richness and evenness, differed significantly among the dietary treatments. The Shannon index was significantly lower in the PFD group than in the SFD group (P = 0.044; Fig. 4D). However, no significant differences were observed among the PFD, NFD, BFD, and MFD groups (P > 0.05). To further evaluate the overall microbial community structure, β-diversity was analyzed using PCoA, which indicated distinct clustering patterns across different dietary groups (Fig. 4E). The two principal coordinates explained 58.7% of the overall variation, with PCo1 contributing 39.2% and PCo2 representing 19.5%. The findings demonstrate that the various dietary treatments affected the microbial populations within the intestines of healthy crabs.

Microbial community compositions

The classifiable sequences comprised 16 phyla. Analysis of relative bacterial abundance indicated that Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Fusobacteriota, and Cyanobacteriota were the dominant phyla in the intestines of E. sinensis (Fig. 4F). Crabs fed LAB-fermented diets showed an increased abundance of Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Cyanobacteriota, and a significantly decreased abundance of Pseudomonadota (P < 0.05).

At the order level, Eubacteriales was the dominant taxon in all groups, followed by Enterobacterales, Bacteroidales, and Aeromonadales (Fig. 4G). Crabs fed LAB-fermented diets exhibited increased abundance of Bacteroidales and Eubacteriales, while the abundance of Enterobacterales and Aeromonadales was reduced (P < 0.05). However, Alteromonadales showed no significant change (P > 0.05).

At the genus level, the intestinal microbiota was primarily composed of Bacteroides, taxa annotated as Lactobacillus, and Shewanella (Fig. 4H). The relative abundance of different genera varied in the intestinal microbiota of E. sinensis. Compared with the NFD and SFD groups, Bacteroides and taxa annotated as Lactobacillus were significantly enriched in the PFD, BFD, and MFD groups, whereas the relative abundance of Aeromonas and Citrobacter was significantly decreased (P < 0.05). In contrast, the abundance of Fusobacterium did not evidently differ in any group (P > 0.05).

The RT-qPCR analysis revealed significant differences in the absolute abundance of L. plantarum and L. brevis among the various dietary treatments (Fig. 4I and J). L. plantarum was almost undetectable in the NFD, SFD, and BFD groups, but its abundance was significantly higher in the PFD and MFD groups (P < 0.001). In contrast, L. brevis was almost undetectable in the NFD, SFD, and PFD groups, whereas its abundance was significantly higher in the BFD and MFD groups (P < 0.001). These findings confirmed that the supplemented bacterial strains were present in the intestinal contents, consistent with the genus-level enrichment observed in the 16S rRNA sequencing data.

Discussion

Fermented pellet feed is a novel type of biologically solid-state fermented feed. It is produced by inoculating pelleted feed with probiotics followed by short-term fermentation. Compared with the direct addition of fermentation broth or microbial powder to the diet, solid-state fermentation has the advantages of increasing both the abundance and activity of beneficial microorganisms. These advantages highlight the potential of solid-state fermented feed in aquafeed applications. The LAB strains, particularly L. plantarum and L. brevis, have received considerable attention because of their adaptability and metabolic versatility in both solid and liquid fermentation systems (Seddik et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2021). In this study, crabs fed with solid-state fermented pellet diets showed improved growth and feed utilization, especially in the PFD and MFD groups. Relative to the NFD and SFD groups, crabs in the PFD group exhibited higher final body weights and WGR, along with a significantly lower FCR. These findings are consistent with Jiang et al. (2018), who reported that the inclusion of 40 g/kg fermented soybean meal in the diet significantly improved growth performance in largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides). Similar results were also observed by Jiang et al. (2023) in E. sinensis, further supporting the positive influence of fermented feed on growth performance.

The mechanisms underlying the improved growth performance of E. sinensis observed in this study are likely multifactorial. A possible explanation is that feed fermentation can degrade ANFs and increase the levels of small peptides and organic acids in the diet, thereby enhancing palatability and nutrient absorption (Qu et al., 2025). Similarly, nutrient digestibility may also be improved. In this study, increased lipase activity was observed in the PFD group, while α-amylase activity was significantly elevated in the PFD, BFD, and MFD groups. The enhanced digestive enzyme activities potentially improved feed digestibility and nutrient utilization. These results suggest that solid-state fermented diets may optimize physiological conditions for nutrient digestion and absorption.

Certain LAB strains have been reported to interact with the host gastrointestinal tract and modulate physiological and immune functions in a strain- and dose-dependent manner (Lin et al., 2017). During solid-state fermentation, LAB proliferate rapidly and produce abundant metabolic by-products. In the present study, long-term exposure to LAB and its metabolites was found to significantly enhance the non-specific immune response and antioxidant capacity of E. sinensis. Non-specific immune-related enzymes, such as AKP, ACP, and LZM, have been widely used to evaluate the immune status of aquatic animals (Yamamoto et al., 2024). The current results demonstrated that the activities of serum immune-related enzymes, including POD, AKP, ACP, and LZM, were elevated in response to LAB-fermented diets, indicating that these fermented diets effectively enhanced non-specific immune defenses in crabs. This is consistent with findings reported in zebrafish (Danio rerio) (Esfahani et al., 2025), Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) (Nesara et al., 2024), and crucian carp (Carassius auratus) (Ma et al., 2024). The literature suggests that the antioxidant defense system of aquatic animals is often insufficient to counteract oxidative stress resulting from intensive farming, necessitating the enhancement of endogenous antioxidant capacity to improve stress resistance (Santacroce et al., 2012). Antioxidant enzymes, including CAT, T-AOC, SOD, GST, and GSH-Px, have been reported to play crucial roles in mitigating oxidative damage. Furthermore, it has been found that LAB-fermented diets can increase the activities of antioxidant enzymes in various tissues in various tissues of aquatic animals (Hoseinifar et al., 2017). Consistently, the present results showed that diets fermented with L. plantarum and L. brevis increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes in the hepatopancreas of E. sinensis. These results suggest that solid-state fermented diets may enhance crab health by improving both antioxidant and immune capacities.

The hepatopancreas is a crucial organ involved in metabolism and biosynthesis in various aquatic animals, and it serves as a key indicator of immune function in E. sinensis. Heat shock proteins are well-documented molecular chaperones that protect cells against various stresses, facilitate protein folding, and improve cellular immunity (Song et al., 2024). Moreover, crustin and alf1 are constituents of the innate immune system with strong antimicrobial properties. In the present study, the expression of immune-related genes was upregulated in the hepatopancreas of crabs fed diets supplemented with LAB-fermented diets, including increased levels of heat shock proteins (hsp70 and hsp90) and antimicrobial factors (alf1 and crustin), indicating enhanced immune responses. These observations align with the findings of Duan et al. (2018), who reported that in Litopenaeus vannamei, dietary supplementation with Clostridium butyricum significantly upregulated host immune-related genes. Furthermore, the Toll-like receptor axis is crucial for recognizing microbial pathogens and initiating innate immune responses, with the toll1, toll2, pelle, myd88, and tube genes representing essential components of the Toll immune pathway (Zhang et al., 2025a). In this study, dietary supplementation with LAB-fermented diets upregulated toll1, toll2, pelle, and tube expression, but downregulated myd88 expression. These findings suggest that fermented diets could effectively prime the Toll pathway, potentially enhancing microbial recognition and immune signaling.

Plant protein sources, such as soybean meal, peanut meal, and rapeseed meal, contain various ANFs (Jiang et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2023), which have been shown to elicit immune responses and induce intestinal inflammation in aquatic animals (Han et al., 2018). For intestinal health, ilf2 and litaf are recognized as pro-inflammatory factors, whose upregulation has been associated with the aggravation of enteritis (Miao et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025c). In this study, LAB-fermented diets significantly reduced the relative mRNA expression levels of ilf2 and litaf, and suppressed p38mapk expression in the intestine of E. sinensis. Furthermore, the results showed the upregulation of the tight junction protein gene zo-1 and peritrophic membrane protein genes (pm and pt) in the LAB-fermented diet groups, consistent with a study on L. plantarum-fermented diets in Haliotis discus hannai (Ke et al., 2025). These results suggest that solid-state fermented feed may alleviate inflammatory responses induced by plant-derived ANFs by inhibiting the p38MAPK pathway, reducing the release of inflammatory mediators, mitigating intestinal inflammation, and simultaneously improving the integrity of the intestinal barrier to effectively prevent pathogen invasion.

The intestinal microbiota has attracted considerable attention because it can influence probiotic colonization (Zhang et al., 2021). Probiotic strains may compete with resident microbes for adhesion sites and ecological niches in the intestinal mucosa, thereby suppressing the proliferation of competing or opportunistic microorganisms (Miao et al., 2018). In this study, the bacterial strains used for LAB fermentation were quantified to determine whether their abundance increased in the intestinal contents of crabs fed fermented diets. The 16S rRNA sequencing results showed enrichment of taxa annotated as Lactobacillus at the genus level in the PFD, BFD, and MFD groups. Absolute quantification further confirmed that the absolute abundance of the inoculated target strains, L. plantarum and L. brevis, were significantly more abundant in the corresponding LAB-fermented diet groups than in the non-inoculated groups. These findings indicate that diets fermented with L. plantarum and L. brevis increased the detectable abundance of the inoculated LAB strains in the intestinal contents of E. sinensis, suggesting the potential application of these two strains in healthy crab aquaculture.

Pseudomonadota is commonly associated with microbial dysbiosis and inflammatory responses in aquatic animals (Debnath et al., 2025). Fermented diets increased the relative abundance of Bacillota and Bacteroidota, while reducing that of Pseudomonadota. At the order and genus levels, beneficial taxa such as Eubacteriales, Bacteroides, and Lactobacillus were enriched in the PFD, BFD, and MFD groups, while the relative abundance of opportunistic or pathogenic genera such as Aeromonas and Citrobacter was significantly suppressed. These changes suggest that LAB-fermented diets increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria and reshaped the gut microbiota toward a more favorable and stable community structure, which may contribute to improved intestinal homeostasis and health.

Lipopolysaccharide, a component of Gram-negative bacterial cell walls, can stimulate leukocytes and promote the release of inflammatory mediators (Dong et al., 2016). To further verify the protective effects of LAB-fermented diets against intestinal inflammation, an LPS-induced enteritis model was established. It was observed that LPS impaired the intestinal function of E. sinensis, as evidenced by the onset of intestinal inflammation (Fig. 8A). Inflammatory cytokines are critical indicators of the extent of intestinal inflammation and the host immune response. Therefore, the genes ilf2 and litaf are recognized as pro-inflammatory factors that mediate leukocyte activation and cytokine release during pathogen-induced inflammation. In this study, LPS injection significantly elevated the expression of both ilf2 and litaf, indicating successful induction of intestinal inflammation. However, the ilf2 and litaf levels were significantly reduced after LPS stimulation in crabs fed with LAB-fermented diets (the PFD and BFD groups), consistent with the trend observed in p38mapk. After LPS injection, the expression of genes associated with intestinal permeability (pt, zo-1, and pm) was significantly reduced in the NFD and SFD groups, whereas it was significantly upregulated in the BFD and PFD groups, indicating that LAB-fermented diets can effectively alleviate LPS-induced inflammatory responses. This finding is consistent with the results of Babu and Mohanty (2025) in mice.

To further elucidate the potential mechanisms by which LAB-fermented diets mitigate LPS-induced intestinal inflammation, the expression of key genes associated with the NF-κB and PI3K/AKT/mTOR axes was analyzed. These pathways are well-documented for their central roles in regulating immune homeostasis and inflammatory responses (Jiang et al., 2025a, Jiang et al., 2025b; Mohseni et al., 2021; Roy et al., 2023). The findings revealed that pi3k, akt, iκb, and mtor levels were downregulated in the NFD and SFD groups following LPS injection, while they were elevated in the PFD and BFD groups, suggesting that fermented diets may activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis during inflammatory stress. Moreover, dorsal, a crustacean NF-κB homolog, was also upregulated in the PFD and BFD groups, suggesting the potential involvement of the NF-κB pathway in mediating the anti-inflammatory response. These transcript-level changes indicate potential modulatory effects of LAB-fermented diets on key inflammatory pathways. LAB-fermented diets may exert protective effects by promoting PI3K-mediated AKT activation, increasing IκB stability, and indirectly suppressing excessive NF-κB signaling, thereby attenuating inflammation. The application of mixed strains generally produces stronger effects compared to single-strain supplementation, due to potential functional complementarity and synergistic interactions (Dawood and Koshio, 2020; Puvanasundram et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021). However, in this study, the effects of the MFD were not superior to those of the PFD in terms of growth performance, intestinal microbiota composition, or hepato-intestinal health. This discrepancy could be related to a lack of synergy between the strains, which could have competed with each other for space and nutrients in the host organism, thereby preventing their probiotic effects (Dias et al., 2022). The underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that LAB-fermented diets significantly improved the growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and activities of immune-related enzymes in E. sinensis. The application of fermented feeds modulated the gut microbiota by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria, decreasing that of potential pathogens, and increasing detectable levels of inoculated LAB strains in the intestinal contents. Moreover, fermented diets effectively alleviated LPS-induced intestinal inflammation and altered transcriptional patterns, potentially by regulating the NF-κB and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways. These findings offer a theoretical basis for the application of fermented diets to improve intestinal health and disease resistance in crab aquaculture.
