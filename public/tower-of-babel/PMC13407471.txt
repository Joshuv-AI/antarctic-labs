Multidependency of Electroencephalographic and Bispectral Index Reactivity Rule Them Out as Outcome Predictors of Head Trauma
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Sakrikar et al. regarding an observational study investigating the validity of electroencephalographic reactivity (EEG-R) and bispectral index reactivity (BIS-R) as predictors of treatment success after traumatic brain injury (TBI).1 The study showed that EEG-R was detectable in 45% of the 40 patients included, while it was absent in the remainder. The absence of EEG-R correlated with low Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOSE) scores at 3 and 6 months. Furthermore, EEG-R showed a high predictive value for the failure to regain consciousness.1 The authors concluded that EEG-R in response to auditory and painful stimuli is a strong predictor of treatment success in patients with TBI.1 The study is promising, but some points warrant further discussion.

First, EEG-R and BIS-R are highly dependent on the patency of the pathways that transmit responses to sensory stimuli via peripheral or cranial nerves to the corresponding processing networks of the central nervous system (CNS). Reactivity to pain or sound stimuli for assessing EEG-R or BIS-R can only be reliably used as a prognostic parameter if the transmission of sensory information to the CNS is unimpaired. Since the spinal cord or peripheral nervous system may also be affected in patients with TBI, false-positive or false-negative results are possible if the integrity of these systems is not adequately ensured during the examination. Therefore, it is essential to confirm normal hearing and pain perception through appropriate functional tests. How many of the included patients had concomitant spinal cord injury or cranial nerve dysfunction?

The second point is that TBI is an umbrella term encompassing many types of damage. Traumatic damage can affect the skull, meninges, brain parenchyma, cerebral vessels, the local immune system, or the glymphatic system. In the case of parenchymal lesions, trauma can primarily affect subcortical or cortical structures, either predominantly supratentorial or infratentorial. Therefore, it is essential to include the nature of the structural or functional lesion caused by the trauma in the analysis. Due to the heterogeneity of TBI types, it is crucial to homogenize the study group with respect to the type, extent, and nature of the trauma-induced brain lesion. Heterogeneity in terms of lesion type and extent can lead to heterogeneous test results, thus calling general conclusions into question. It is also crucial to know whether the sympathetic system, the parasympathetic system, or both were affected by the trauma and whether the excitatory response was impaired by the trauma. If the spinal cord is involved, it is conceivable that patients with a spinal cord lesion would show different results in the EEG-R and BIS-R than patients without a spinal cord lesion. It is also crucial to include the type of intervention and the quality of its execution in the analysis.

The third point concerns the neurosurgical procedure in which the surgical wound was closed with sutures or staples. What measures were taken to avoid artifacts during the EEG recording?

The fourth point concerns the possible involvement of other organs in a TBI. In addition to the TBI, how many of the 40 patients also had polytrauma or injury to the heart or lungs that could have influenced the EEG-R and BIS-R results?

The fifth point concerns the high 6-month mortality rate in the study group (29/40).1 Why did three-quarters of the patients die within 6 months of the TBI?

The sixth point concerns the medication administered during the first 5 days of hospitalization. This should be included in the analysis, as should any complications arising from the TBI or surgery, such as infections, electrolyte imbalances, renal failure, sepsis, or endocrine/metabolic disorders.

Overall, the diverse dependencies of EEG-R and BIS-R may disqualify them as reliable predictors of treatment success in TBI.
