A pilot study investigating the feasibility of a positive body image yoga intervention for men and women across adulthood
Authors: Matthieu Dagenais, Shivani Mall, Sean Locke, Phillip Sullivan, Panagiota Klentrou, Kimberley L. Gammage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYoga interventions have been shown to promote positive body image, although these studies have focused predominantly on young women. The purpose of this pilot study was to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a positive body image yoga intervention in a sample of men and women across the adult lifespan and to examine the potential changes in body image following the intervention.MethodsSixty-two adults (≥18 years of age) who were not engaging in yoga and scored a 4 or lower on a measure of body appreciation (to avoid ceiling effects) were recruited from a small Canadian city. Participants were randomly assigned to an in-person 10-week yoga or waitlist control group. Participants self-reported demographic information; they completed body image measures online at baseline, 5 weeks, and program end. Study adherence, retention, and dropout rates were measured at program end to assess feasibility and acceptability.ResultsA total of 195 individuals contacted us with interest in the study, of which 82 (42%) were eligible. Fifty-seven participants were randomized (Mage of the sample was 52 years; 93% identified as White and 86% identified as a woman). Measures of feasibility (4.82/5 yoga, 3.42/5 control), acceptability (4.80/5 yoga, 3.33/5 control), and appropriateness (4.82/5 yoga, 3.35/5 control) supported the feasibility of the intervention condition. Secondary indicators of feasibility were also assessed. Twenty-one participants attended at least one yoga class, with mean attendance at the yoga classes 79%. All classes were delivered as intended. Sixteen (57%) and 18 (62%) participants in the yoga and control group, respectively, completed measures at all three time points. There was a total of 12 dropouts and 11 dropouts in the yoga and control group, respectively. There were improvements in functionality appreciation (η2p = .168) and body satisfaction with body function (η2p = .105) in favor of those in the yoga intervention group.ConclusionsThis pilot study provided insight into conducting a fully randomized controlled trial of a yoga program to improve positive body image in men and women across the adult lifespan. Although some changes, particularly to the recruitment of men and younger individuals, are necessary in subsequent studies, in general the yoga program was feasible to deliver and acceptable to participants. Changes to the control group to make it more acceptable would be beneficial. Exploratory analysis indicated the program could improve positive body image facets.Trial registrationNCT05899881. Key messages regarding feasibility

What uncertainties existed regarding the feasibility?While this yoga intervention to improve positive body image has been successfully implemented in young women, it was unclear whether it could be successfully conducted in a more diverse sample (across age and gender). Specifically, it was unclear whether recruitment of men and middle and older adults was feasible, and whether the program could be successfully delivered in this more diverse sample. It was also unclear whether there would be preliminary changes to an array of positive body image facets resulting from yoga in this sample.What are the key feasibility findings?A trial examining a yoga program to promote positive body image in men and women in those 18 years of age or older (including middle and older adults) appears feasible. All yoga classes were implemented as planned and adherence to the yoga program was adequate, with 75% of participants attending at least one class and a mean of seven (of 10) classes attended. Overall, the retention rate was 60%. Participants in the yoga intervention reported high levels of acceptability and satisfaction with the intervention. However, participants randomized to the control group reported poorer satisfaction. Further, recruitment strategies were not successful at recruiting men and young adults. There were increases across all positive body image measures from baseline to week 10 in the yoga group. By contrast, in the control group some measures increased slightly and some decreased. Results indicated large effect sizes for changes in functionality appreciation and satisfaction with body function favoring the yoga group.What are the implications of the feasibility findings for the design of the main study?Our findings suggest that the program can largely be implemented as designed for men and women across the adult lifespan. However, some refinement to the yoga program (e.g., pose selection that is more inclusive of older individuals) before progressing to the full randomized controlled trial would be beneficial. Further, more specific strategies to recruit men and younger adults are needed. Finally, additional strategies to engage the control group should be developed to help improve retention before conducting a larger-scale trial.

Background

The study of positive body image has been called ‘essential to the future of the field’ (p. 472) of body image [1] as an effort to counterbalance the almost exclusive focus on body image as a negative construct. As noted by Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi, examining the positive aspects of human functioning is critical for allowing ‘individuals and communities, not just to endure and survive, but also to flourish’ (p. 13) [2]. Tylka and Wood-Barcalow defined positive body image as a multidimensional construct that encompasses body acceptance and appreciation, broadly conceptualizing beauty, adaptive appearance investment, inner positivity, and filtering information in a body protective manner [3]. Positive body image likely promotes both physical and mental health and well-being, and is linked to engagement in health behaviors such as physical activity [3].

Menzel and Levine (2011) proposed a theoretical model for the development of positive body image through embodiment [4] that draws from Tylka [5] and Piran [6, 7]. Embodiment refers to a sense of ownership of the body and a feeling of being ‘in’ the body [4]. It can be conceptualized as a multifaceted construct, encompassing physical, mental and social domains where positive body image is a core facet of the broader construct of embodiment [8]. In this model, embodying physical activities contribute directly to the development of positive body image, as well as indirectly through increased embodiment and reduced self-objectification (taking an observer’s perspective of the body and focusing on appearance [4, 9]). Embodying physical activities are those that promote awareness of and responsiveness to the body, physical empowerment and competence, and a mind–body connection [4]. Numerous physical activities are typically considered embodying, including hiking, martial arts, and yoga [4]. Systematic review evidence found yoga may have positive effects on mental health outcomes such as well-being, life satisfaction, mindfulness, stress, anxiety and depression among health professionals and healthy adults 18 years of age or older [10, 11].

To date, few studies have empirically tested this model. Cross-sectional research conducted in Australia comparing yoga practitioners [12], belly dancers [13], and pole dancers [14] to female undergraduate students has generally supported this model. Participants in these embodying activities report higher embodiment, lower self-objectification, and greater body appreciation than university women. Further, the effects of embodying physical activities on body appreciation were mediated through higher embodiment and reduced self-objectification [12, 13, 15]. One major limitation is that these studies have all been cross-sectional and observational and are not able to show that embodying physical activities lead to increases in positive body image.

Three studies to date have investigated whether yoga programs can lead to higher positive body image. Halliwell et al. (2019) investigated the impact of a four-session yoga intervention on positive body image and mood in young women in the United Kingdom [16]. They found that, compared to a control group, yoga led to improvements in body appreciation, body connectedness, and body satisfaction, as well as positive mood [16]. More recently, Alleva et al. (2020) examined the effects of yoga on positive body image facets [17]. They randomized young adult women from the Netherlands (aged 18 to 30 years) to a 10-week yoga intervention designed to increase positive body image or a waitlist control group. The yoga group reported increased body appreciation, body compassion, appearance evaluation, and embodiment, and lower self-objectification, although no changes in functionality appreciation were found [17]. Embodiment mediated changes in body appreciation, body compassion, appearance evaluation; separately, self-objectification mediated changes in body compassion and appearance evaluation [17]. Webb et al. (2022) explored the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary efficacy of an online yoga program and body gratitude journaling intervention among young women (aged 18–30 years) in the United States [18]. Webb et al. (2022) found improvements in positive body image as measured by body appreciation, functional body appreciation, body satisfaction, and body awareness [18]. However, in all three studies, the samples were limited to young adult women recruited from college [17]. As noted, research in more diverse samples (e.g., age, gender) is needed [17], as positive body image may vary across a variety of these characteristics.

Findings regarding differences in a variety of positive body image measures across social identity, including gender and age, have been mixed. For example, in one meta-analysis of gender differences in body appreciation, men reported higher levels compared to women, although these gender differences decreased with age [19]. Alleva et al. (2017) reported that women scored higher on functionality appreciation compared to men [20]. However, some studies have shown no gender differences [21, 22] and still others have shown higher functionality appreciation in men [23–26].

In addition to body appreciation and functionality appreciation, other positive body image facets have shown some differences across social identity. Self-conscious emotions are complex emotions that require self-evaluation and self-reflection, and as such can motivate and regulate a variety of behaviours, including physical activity [27, 28]. Authentic pride is a positive body-related self-conscious emotion that occurs when people experience positive emotions resulting from achieving their appearance or fitness goals due to their own efforts [29]. Pila et al. (2016) found age and gender differences in body pride with young adults (18–24 years of age) reporting the highest authentic pride compared with adults (25–44 years of age) and middle adults (45–65 years of age) and women reported lower body pride than men [30] in Canada.

In addition, body image flexibility is a facet of positive body image defined as awareness and acceptance of all body image experiences, positive and negative, in a non-judgemental way; it also allows people to engage in behaviours consistent with their values even when body image experiences are negative [31, 32]. Linardon et al. [33] conducted a meta-analysis on body image flexibility in men and women (18–72 years of age), and found men reported higher body image flexibility than women [33].

Given these potential differences in positive body image based on age and gender, understanding how interventions (e.g., physical activity) to increase positive body image are effective across a range of social identities is critical. Yet, previous yoga trials are homogenous focusing predominantly on young adult women. Further, they have not addressed several important aspects of positive body image, such as self-conscious emotions (i.e., authentic body pride–achieving pride in appearance goals) or body image flexibility (ability to experience body-related thoughts or feelings, including negative ones,) without changing them or changing behaviors and values), which may be related to physical activity participation and provide a more holistic conceptualization of positive body image. Thus, it is important to investigate (1) whether yoga interventions can be implemented in diverse populations and (2) the potential changes in positive body image following yoga on positive body image outcomes.

Objectives

The primary purpose of this pilot study was twofold: (1) to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a randomized controlled trial in a sample of men and women across the adult lifespan by assessing participant retention, data collection procedures, and adherence to engaging in a supervised Hatha yoga intervention (or control group); and (2) to explore preliminary changes of the yoga intervention on positive body image (body appreciation, functionality appreciation, authentic body pride [related to both appearance and fitness], satisfaction with body function and body appearance, and body image flexibility) in this sample.

Methods

Trial design and study setting

Clearance from the Brock University Research Ethics Board was first obtained (file #22–205). Informed consent was obtained from participants prior to enrollment in the study. This study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05899881). This study is a pilot study for a two-arm, parallel group randomized controlled trial. The CONSORT Extension to Pilot and Feasibility Trials checklist was used for transparency (please see supplemental file). In one arm was the yoga intervention; the intervention was delivered for 60-min once per week for 10 weeks. Participants were asked to maintain their other physical activity during this time. The second arm was the waitlist control group where participants were asked to not engage in yoga but to continue their regular physical activity. Participants completed study measures online via Qualtrics at baseline, week 5, and week 10. All yoga classes were held in a yoga studio in a private gym with community-based exercise programs for special populations including seniors and those living with chronic disease and disability.

Participants

As a pilot study, traditional sample size calculations are not appropriate. Instead, we used recommendations for sample size for pilot studies from Ying et al. [34]. Our primary outcomes were the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of the yoga and control conditions. Given our goals to assess the feasibility of a future randomized control trial, we were also interested in determining participant recruitment and retention rates, implementation of data collection procedures, intervention fidelity, and yoga and control group adherence. We calculated required sample size based on estimated dropout rates of 30% (based on a similar intervention by Alleva et al. [17], for a 95% CI, and 10% precision [34]. Based on these parameters, we estimated we would need 55 participants (28 per group).

This study was not adequately powered to detect statistically significant changes in any of the secondary body image outcomes (nor was it intended to be adequately powered for this purpose) but rather evaluated the feasibility of the intervention.

Inclusion criteria were 18 years of age or older, community-dwelling, residing in Southern Ontario, Canada, and able to understand English. Participants were not currently practicing yoga, nor had they engaged in yoga within the past 12 months. In addition, participants must have reported a mean score of 4 or less on a measure of body appreciation (to avoid ceiling effects) at screening. Individuals living with any medical conditions preventing them from engaging in yoga, or having practiced yoga within the past 12 months, were excluded.

Participants were recruited through posters at the university community and community-based organizations (e.g., recreation facilities, allied health profession offices) in the area for a study examining the impact of yoga on well-being with permission. Posters were also uploaded to researcher social media pages as well as those of the facility hosting the yoga sessions. In addition, press-related recruitment (i.e., university news, local radio) was used. Snowball sampling was also used; individuals who saw the advertisement were encouraged to share study information with others.

Interested participants contacted the research team via email and were provided with an initial email confirming inclusion criteria and a copy of the consent form; they were provided an opportunity to ask questions at this time. If eligible and interested, participants were sent an email with a link to two screening measures to complete via Qualtrics. The Get Active Questionnaire (developed by the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology) is a self-administered pre-screening tool to ensure it is safe to engage in physical activity while reducing barriers to physical activity. Individuals are asked to indicate yes or no to 9 questions (e.g., related to heart disease, pain) to allow individuals to make an informed decision about whether they can start physical activity, or if they should consult with a health care provider or qualified exercise professional before beginning physical activity [35]. The Body Appreciation Scale-2 [BAS-2] was used to assess positive body image [36]. If they were still eligible (i.e., answered no to all questions on the Get Active Questionnaire and reported a mean score below 4 on the BAS-2), participants were sent a Qualtrics link to complete informed consent, demographic information, and baseline body image and physical activity measures. The first author randomized participants into the control or yoga intervention using a generated random number-producing algorithm on Microsoft Excel. Randomization occurred prior to baseline assessment, with only assessors blinded at baseline. Please refer to Fig. 1 CONSORT flow diagram and supplementary material 1 for the SPIRIT checklist. Intervention group

Participants randomized to the intervention group were asked to engage in one Hatha yoga session per week for 10 consecutive weeks, but were not expected to engage in any other yoga practice on their own. The Hatha yoga intervention adopted for these sessions was based on the procedures and manual from Alleva et al. [17]. The manual outlines how instructors create each class, but within these guidelines, instructors can design their own class each week. Each class was approximately 60 min in length and began with 10 min of relaxation asana (e.g., savasana), followed by 30 min of practice (6–8 seated or table poses focusing on alignment, 3–4 standing poses for strength and balance, 2–3 core strengthening poses, 2–3 back strengthening poses, and 2–3 hip opener poses). The class ended with an additional 5–10 min of relaxation (e.g., savasana) including some visualization. Each week there was a theme promoting positive body-self connection (but not referencing positive body image directly), with specific poses, mantras, mudras, and affirmations chosen to reinforce the theme of that week. Each class was designed by the instructor, within these parameters. Modifications to poses were provided by the instructor as needed. Yoga mats, blocks, and straps were provided to participants.

Three yoga classes were offered each week, at a variety of times of day (one morning, one afternoon, one evening) and days of the week. Participants indicated their availability and ranked their preference for the sessions via email; they were assigned to a class coinciding with their availability and preferences (where possible) with a maximum of 10 participants per class. If participants could not attend a class one week, they were able to attend another session that week (if available). Each yoga class was audio recorded to document the content to ensure guidelines for the classes were followed. Two instructors taught the classes (one instructing one class per week, one instructing two classes per week). Both instructors identified as White and as women; they were both over 50 years of age, RYT200 registered, and with a minimum of 5 years yoga teaching experience to diverse individuals. The instructors guided participants through their yoga practice consistent with the manual guidelines.

Instructors received the manual from Alleva et al. [17] outlining basic class structure and poses, themes, and language. The instructors were not told that the key study outcomes were related to positive body image. Following the manual guidelines, instructors were asked to create a sample class, which they then taught to the research team and the other instructor. Instructors were able to ask questions at this session and feedback was provided to them to ensure they could deliver the yoga intervention appropriately. At this practice class, both instructors were able to deliver classes consistent with the training manual.

Instructors were asked to refrain from language directly referencing the physical appearance of the body (e.g., this pose will give you toned muscles, yoga can help with weight loss). The instructors included two of the following themes in each class, such that all themes were addressed over the 10-week intervention: breathe into a specific area of the body, deepen the breath, take another deep breath, listen to your body, be gentle, do not overstretch, notice your thoughts, everybody’s body is different, there is no such thing as a perfect pose, practice non-violence towards the body, notice where you feel the stretch, exhale and soften into the pose, everybody’s pose will be different, and notice how you feel after doing the pose [17]. Although the language and themes broadly emphasize positive body image [17], instructors did not explicitly direct participants to focus more on body functionality, appreciation or to focus less on physical appearance. Instructors wore standardized clothing appropriate for Hatha yoga (e.g., yoga pants and a t-shirt) to minimize skin exposure, but to reflect appropriate yoga clothing.

Control group

Participants randomized to the control group were asked to engage in their usual lifestyle behaviors, including physical activity, for 10 consecutive weeks. They were asked to refrain from practicing yoga for the duration of the 10-week study. They were contacted at weeks 5 and 10 to complete study measures. Participants in the control group did not get any further contact throughout the study. At the end of the study, participants in the control group were offered one free yoga class in person and one free pre-recorded video of a yoga class, both delivered by one of the instructors.

Measures

Demographics

Participants self-reported demographic information including age, gender, ethnicity, height, weight, health status, and education. We also asked questions pertaining to their previous yoga experience.

Feasibility outcomes

The following three measures were adapted from Weiner et al. (2017) to assess the success of intervention implementation, by assessing acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of an intervention. They provided evidence of structural validity of the three factors, known groups validity, and test–retest reliability, as well as sensitivity to changes [37]. Each measure was adapted to fit the context of either the yoga or control group for this study.

Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM; [37])

This 5-item measure asked participants to rate each on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree, to assess participants’ agreeability, palatability, and satisfaction with the assigned condition (i.e., yoga or control). Yoga instructors also completed this measure. Higher scores indicate higher acceptability. Weiner et al. reported Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85 and test–retest reliability = 0.83 [37]. Cronbach’s alpha in this sample (week 10) was α = 0.95.

Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM; [37])

Each of the five items is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree, to assess the perceived fit, relevance, or compatibility of the yoga or control condition; yoga instructors also completed this measure. Higher scores represent higher perceptions of appropriateness of the condition. Yoga instructors also completed this measure. Weiner et al. reported Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91 and test–retest reliability = 0.87 [37]. Cronbach’s alpha (week 10) was α = 0.95.

Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM; [37])

This 5-item measure assessed the extent to which the yoga and control interventions could be successfully conducted from the participants’ a perspectives; yoga instructors also completed this measure. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree. Higher scores indicate greater feasibility. Weiner et al. reported Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89 and test–retest reliability = 0.88 [37]. Cronbach’s alpha (week 10) for the yoga group was α = 0.94.

The following measures were developed for the purposes of this study to further assess perceptions of the yoga intervention from both participants and instructors.

Perceptions of participation

Participants were asked a series of questions about their perceptions of their respective condition (e.g., length of sessions, length of intervention, enjoyment of condition), with each item rated on a 5-point scale where higher scores indicated more positive perceptions. We also asked open-ended questions regarding the yoga and control conditions, including what they enjoyed about each condition, and recommendations on how to improve each condition.

Instructor perceptions

We asked the two yoga instructors open-ended questions regarding the yoga intervention, including what they enjoyed about instructing the program, challenges they encountered, and recommendations for improving the yoga program.

Changes in positive body image outcomes

Body Appreciation Scale-2 (BAS-2; [36])

The BAS-2 was the primary outcome for this study as it has been used most often to assess positive body image [3]. The BAS-2 is comprised of 10 items, each rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always. Scores on the BAS-2 are averaged where higher scores represent higher levels of body appreciation. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.93, α = 0.94, and α = 0.95, respectively.

Functional Appreciation Scale (FAS; [20])

The FAS is comprised of seven items each one rated from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, where higher scores indicate higher levels of appreciating, respecting, and honouring the body for what it is capable of doing, beyond just awareness of body functionality [20]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.85, α = 0.88, and α = 0.92, respectively.

Body Responsiveness Scale (BRS; [38])

The body responsiveness scale is a 7-item scale assessing the extent to which people are attuned to their bodies and behave according to their body’s needs and has been used as an indicator of embodiment among adults living with chronic pain [39], and among adult women (18 years of age or older; [38]). Each item is rated on a 7-point scale where 1 = not at all true of me and 7 = always true of me [38]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.73, α = 0.60, and α = 0.77, respectively.

Body and Appearance Self-Conscious Emotions Scale – authentic pride subscale (BASES; [27]

This scale consists of 4 items assessing pride in achieving appearance goals [27] as a facet of positive body image. Participants rate the degree to which each item statement is true of themselves on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 = not at all true of me to 7 = very true of me. Higher scores indicate greater amounts of authentic body pride. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.94, α = 0.85, and α = 0.92, respectively.

Body Self-Conscious Emotions—Fitness related instrument – authentic rride subscale (BSCE-Fit [28])

This 4-item measure assesses a sense of achievement or accomplishment from achieving fitness-related goals. Participants rate the degree to which each item statement is true of themselves on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 = not at all true of me to 7 = very true of me. Higher scores indicate higher levels of fitness-related authentic body pride. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.94, α = 0.96, and α = 0.83, respectively.

Satisfaction with Body Function and Body Appearance (SBFA; [40])

This is a 9-item scale assessing satisfaction with body function (what the body can do; 6 items) and body appearance (what the body looks like; 3 items). Participants rate how satisfied they are on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 = very dissatisfied to 7 = very satisfied. Higher scores indicate higher levels of body satisfaction. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.94, α = 0.93, and α = 0.96, respectively.

Body Image-Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (BI-AAQ; [41])

This 12-item questionnaire assesses body image flexibility, which is the ability to experience body-related thoughts or feelings (including negative ones) without changing them or changing behaviors and values [41]. Participants rate how true each statement is on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 = never true to 7 = always true. Higher scores indicate higher body image flexibility. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha values for baseline, week 5, and week 10 were α = 0.95, α = 0.96, and α = 0.95, respectively.

Data analysis

All data were screened and assumptions were assessed prior to data analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 28. Descriptive statistics for demographic, body image, and feasibility measures were calculated as described above by group (intervention or control). A series of t-tests (for continuous variables) or chi-square (for categorical variables) was conducted to determine if there were differences between those who dropped out and those who completed the study on demographic, feasibility, or body image variables.

As the proposed study was a pilot study [42] and assessed the ability to collect data, no data imputation was performed to account for missing data. Listwise deletion was used for missing data. In addition to the three feasibility measures, interest in the study, recruitment information, adherence, retention, and dropout (for the total sample and by age and gender subgroups) for both the yoga and control groups were calculated, and fidelity was calculated to assess feasibility. Means and standard deviations of the three feasibility measures were calculated by group. The following assessments to evaluate feasibility are recommended by Eldridge et al. [42]. Interest was defined as the number of participants who contacted the research team to express interest in the study. Recruitment was defined as the number of participants who were randomized to either the yoga or control group. Adherence was defined as the proportion of total sessions attended by intervention participants (determined by the number of yoga sessions completed of a possible 10). Retention was defined as the proportion of participants from each group who completed questionnaires at each time point. Fidelity was defined as the proportion of yoga classes delivered as intended throughout the 10 weeks of the intervention, and the ability of the instructors to deliver the yoga class consistent with the manual. In each class a researcher was present with a checklist of poses and themes and to audio record the class; the checklist was used to ensure classes were delivered as intended.

To explore changes in facets of positive body image, we used a series of repeated measures analyses of variance to examine changes in primary and secondary body image measures by group (yoga vs control) across the three timepoints (pre-test, 5-week, and 10-week). Given the small sample size, statistical significance was not examined. Rather, exploratory analyses using effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were examined. Partial eta squared effect sizes of 0.01, 0.06 and 0.14 were defined as small, moderate and large effect sizes, respectively.

Responses from the instructors were summarized to determine changes to the exercise program to be appropriate to a more diverse sample of participants across age and gender. We used content thematic analytical techniques to identify themes that would impact the delivery of a future yoga trial [43]. The first author led the analysis and interpretation of the data. Given the small sample size, we coded by hand instead of using software. First, all responses were transferred into a single spreadsheet, organized by question. He then coded all data by question, where each code represented a single idea. Once all data was coded, codes were grouped into similar ideas/categories. The first and last authors met throughout the analytic process to discuss the coding and categories and where differences occurred, discussion continued until consensus was reached.

Feasibility progression criteria

Consistent with recommendations by Ying et al. [34] and CONSORT guidelines, we set minimum and goal criteria to determine whether we could progress from the feasibility stage to a fully randomized control trial (with or without modifications). For our measures of feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, we established a minimum of 80% (4/5) for both conditions, and a goal of 90% (4.5/5) for each. We set a maximum dropout rate of 30% in accordance to previous literature [17] with a goal of 10% dropout among participants in this study. We set a minimum criteria for intervention fidelity of 80% of yoga classes delivered as intended, with 90% or higher as our goal. For recruitment, we set a minimum threshold 30% of the sample recruited as men to participate in our study (based on 28% of men engaging in yoga [44] and similar findings from a systematic review exploring various demographic characteristics on who practices yoga [45], with a goal of 50%.

A total of 195 individuals contacted us with interest in the study, of which 82 (42%) were eligible.

Among our sample of 57 participants, 29 were randomized to the control (three men) condition and 28 were randomized to the intervention (five men); 49 (86%) identified as a woman. Adherence

Among those participants in the intervention group, 21 participants attended at least one yoga session. Participants attended a mean of 7 classes (SD = 3) with a range of 0–10 classes attended.

Retention

In the yoga group, 18 (64%) participants completed week 5 measures and 16 (57%) participants completed all three time points. Of the 12 dropouts, 7 dropped out at baseline, 3 dropped out at week 5 and 2 dropped out at week 10. In the control group, 18 (62%) participants completed measures at all three time points. Of the 11 dropouts, all dropped out at week 5 and 2 were men. A series of independent t-tests were conducted to examine differences between dropouts and those who completed the study on demographic and body image variables at time 1. There were no differences between dropouts and those who completed the study.

Fidelity

All 30 yoga sessions were delivered as planned. Adherence to weekly themes, addressing each theme at least once over the 10 weeks, and poses taught in each class was 100% for both instructors. No instructions related to physical appearance or to positive body image explicitly (e.g., functionality) were provided.

Perceptions of participation

In the yoga group, 15 participants (94%; M = 5.00, SD = 0) indicated they enjoyed taking part in the yoga program, five (31%; M = 2.07, SD = 0.88) thought the number of classes were too few, 14 (88%; M = 4.93, SD = 0.26) were very satisfied with the length of each class, 12 (75%; M = 4.80, SD = 0.41) felt very comfortable while practicing yoga, 15 (94%; M = 5.00, SD = 0) liked the instructor “very much”, 12 (75%) answered yes to continuing yoga practice, 13 (81%; M = 4.87, SD = 0.35) were very likely to recommend this yoga program to a friend, 14 (88%; M = 4.93, SD = 0.26) were very satisfied with the yoga program, and 12 (69%) viewed their body differently after participation. There were no reported injuries throughout the yoga intervention.

Open-ended questions showed the intervention group reported enjoying the yoga intervention, although some participants wished the yoga intervention was longer in duration and that they participated more frequently each week (i.e., twice a week yoga). Participants in the yoga intervention expressed their appreciation for the yoga instructors. One participant in the yoga intervention mentioned “Instructor was amazing, inviting, friendly, and helpful” and another participant said, “Instructor was perfect for this study’s group”. Some participants did express that some of the yoga poses were challenging and they had to be mindful of previous injuries or surgeries including knee replacement. Overall, they noted the importance of modifications of poses they found difficult.

In the control group, four participants (22%; M = 2.33, SD = 0.97) did not enjoy taking part in the control group, 14 (78%; M = 3.28; SD = 0.56) were neutral about the length of time involved in the study, 13 (72%) planned on practicing yoga, eight (44%; M = 2.61, SD = 0.98) were neither likely nor unlikely to recommend this study to a friend, and nine (50%; M = 2.89, SD = 0.83) were neither unsatisfied nor satisfied regarding their participation in this study.

In open-ended responses, participants from the control group expressed their displeasure about not being randomized to the yoga intervention. One participant from the control condition said “I felt stiffed… I was hoping to be in the other group” while another participant in the control said, “There was nothing to like or dislike, I didn’t do anything”. Some issues participants in the control group discussed included: lack of updates throughout the study, inability to engage in yoga, and wanting to discuss, as a group, topics related to yoga.

Perceptions from instructors

Please refer to Table 2 for feasibility outcome scores by instructors. Overall, instructors reported the yoga intervention was acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. Both yoga instructors enjoyed the yoga program; one instructor stated, “It is always so rewarding to instruct people who have never done any yoga…”. However, both instructors agreed there were challenges that arose while instructing, specifically the selection of yoga poses in the manual. One of the instructors noted, “The challenge was in the poses we had to choose from and that I thought many of those poses … just wasn’t suitable”. For example, the number of seated poses at the start of classes was noted to be difficult or uncomfortable for many participants particularly with respect to hip mobility. To improve the yoga intervention, the instructors suggested avoiding scheduling classes on Mondays and Fridays due to holidays (which sometimes meant participants went 2 weeks between sessions), incorporating more standing and prone poses to reduce the number of seated poses, a room with fewer (or no) mirrors and the ability to lower lights during savasana. The instructors were in favor of wanting to teach the yoga program again. Finally, both instructors believed the manual prepared them adequately to deliver the yoga program as intended.

Progression thresholds

Given our results, we met some of our minimum thresholds (e.g., feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness all greater than 80% in the yoga group). Intervention fidelity met our goal, with all yoga classes delivered as intended. However, we did not meet our minimum thresholds for dropout (greater than 30% of participants dropped out of both conditions). Further, the control group did not meet the minimum threshold value of 80% on feasibility, acceptability, or appropriateness. Finally, we did not meet the minimum threshold of at least 30% of those recruited men.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to determine the feasibility and acceptability of delivering a yoga intervention designed to improve positive body image to men and women 18 years of age or older. Based on our progression thresholds, we met some of our minimum thresholds (e.g., feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness for the yoga group, intervention fidelity). However, our other minimum thresholds were not met (dropout rate, feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness of control condition, 30% of those recruited men). Therefore, we will need modifications to our study design, including control group design and recruitment strategies, and strategies to reduce dropout. We also investigated the preliminary changes of the yoga program on facets of positive body image in this group. Changes in body image were generally in favour of the yoga intervention compared to the control condition.

Feasibility of the yoga intervention and control condition

This study aimed to recruit a diverse sample across specifically age and gender, as previous studies examining the impact of yoga on positive body image have only recruited young adult women (e.g., [17]). While we recruited a sample more diverse in age (age range 32–73) than previous studies, there were no young adults (18–30 years of age) and relatively few men. Finding ways to recruit the full range of adults, including young adults, is of importance. We started recruiting in the Spring of 2023 when many students attending the University were not on campus, which could have impacted recruitment of young adults. Although we recruited through social media (Facebook and Instagram), we also targeted health-care practitioners, community recreation facilities, and traditional news organizations (radio, newspapers) which likely targeted middle and older adults. Recruitment of younger adults may require the use of other social media platforms such as TikTok and Snapchat that are more popular with younger adults [46].

In addition, we did not have balance across gender, with a large majority of the sample identifying as a woman. In the U.S., women are twice as likely to report practicing yoga [47] with approximately 23% of women and 10% of men practicing in 2022 [47]. It is important to identify recruitment strategies for future yoga interventions to include both men and women. Cagas et al. [48] aimed to understand the barriers for yoga participation among men (age range 18–60 years). They found that yoga was perceived by men as a more feminine activity, preventing participation. They suggested information sessions for men, men’s only classes, and utilizing health care professionals to promote yoga could help increase men’s representation. Facilitators for the engagement in yoga among men include educating men on yoga practice [48], and reframing yoga as an activity for all and reducing stereotypes that yoga is a feminine-based activity [48, 49]. It may also help to have greater representation of men in yoga more generally [48]; therefore, having recruitment materials containing images of men may be one strategy to increase their recruitment. It may also be important to recruit from more men-dominated activities and settings. Finally, recruiting a man as a yoga instructor could also help. These suggestions may be taken into consideration when recruiting men for future yoga trials.

While we succeeded in reaching our goal of 50 participants, we had 195 participants initially express interest, and 139 completed screening. Thus, we lost almost 29% prior to screening. Further, over 40% of those who completed screening were excluded, with the primary reason for exclusion due to scoring above the cutoff score of 4 on the BAS-2, which we defined as high positive body image. This may not be surprising given that men and older adults score higher on body appreciation compared to women and younger age groups [3, 19], given the age of our sample. This may have contributed to the lack of men in the study. It is also possible that those drawn to yoga (or any physical activity intervention) may already report high positive body image. Potentially recruiting for those with negative body image (similar to Ariel-Donges et al. [50] would lead to fewer participants being excluded.

The number of participants who dropped out of the study was higher than expected, at almost 40% (n = 12 dropouts for the intervention; n = 11 dropouts for the control) compared to our 30% anticipated dropout. Alleva et al. [17] also reported that 40% of their sample did not complete post-test measures in their 10-week yoga intervention. They also conducted their study in the summer months. Given this is a popular time for holidays or participation in outdoor activities, it may be prudent to consider the time of year when conducting future studies. It is also possible that the length of the study (10 weeks) and relatively low contact (once per week) contributed to higher-than-expected dropout rates. By contrast, Halliwell et al. [16] reported that all participants in their yoga intervention attended all classes in a 4-week study (once per week yoga).

Overall, the participants randomized to the yoga intervention had positive experiences. Interestingly, even though we had a 40% dropout rate, one comment from participants in the yoga intervention was the desire to engage in yoga more than once per week. On one hand, this may allow participants to engage in yoga more frequently, which may have a more significant impact on positive body image [12]. It may also keep participants more engaged in the study, and many participants in our study indicated that they would have preferred a more frequent practice. On the other hand, participants would need to engage in yoga more often which may be a burden (e.g., time commitment, travel) and could reduce adherence rates.

In addition to frequency, length of program is also important to consider. Halliwell et al. [16] found a 4-week yoga intervention improved body appreciation, suggesting longer interventions may not be necessary to see improvements in positive body image. Given their adherence was much higher than in longer studies [17] including our own, it may be prudent to consider shorter length interventions with more frequent classes. Future studies should attempt to determine the appropriate dose–response required to elicit changes in positive body image, and the ideal combination of frequency of yoga and length of program to also optimize adherence.

By contrast, participants randomized to the control condition reported a generally less positive experience. They found there was a lack of contact and lack of engagement with the research team. One way to improve engagement may be to have them attend information sessions or provide information on the benefits of yoga (e.g., through a website or social media) throughout the intervention period. This may help to keep the control group more engaged, similar to Halliwell et al. [16] who provided the control group with leaflets outlining the benefits of yoga. Another option to potentially improve retention in the control group would be to use a waitlist control where participants allocated to this condition have the same opportunity at the end of the study to engage in the full yoga program. However, this will increase the resources (time, instructors, money) required for studies.

The yoga instructors provided feedback at the end of the study. Of the suggestions provided by the yoga instructors, adapting the yoga program to be more inclusive in terms of age, fitness, and health conditions was a key recommendation. They suggested specifically incorporating more standing and reclined yoga poses given the diversity in age, yoga experience, and health conditions in participants (e.g., arthritis) in the present study. The instructors reported some of the older participants were less comfortable in some of the seated yoga poses (e.g., seated cross-legged forward bend, seated side stretch) where the participants had to stay in these poses for significant amounts of time. With age, mobility and flexibility may decline which in turn may make some yoga poses more challenging [51]. Therefore, the yoga intervention should take physiological changes associated with aging into account when delivered to older adults and differing genders [52]. This suggestion was consistent with feedback from participants randomized to the yoga intervention who also provided feedback regarding previous injuries or conditions that made some of the yoga poses challenging. This key suggestion is of particular interest to create yoga programs that are adaptable, inclusive, and equitable for a diverse sample [53], especially older adults, and those who may have an injury or be living with a chronic illness.

This issue also emphasizes the importance of having qualified and experienced instructors, particularly those working with older individuals. The instructors in this study had previous experience instructing yoga to older adults. Several participants noted the need for modifications during class and it was critical for the health and safety of participants, as well as their comfort and confidence, that appropriate modifications could be made. Specifically, the yoga instructors provided modifications to the participants.

With respect to sustainability of the program, yoga for positive body image could be offered in community centres (e.g., YMCA) or other organizations catering to older adults or other diverse populations, such as seniors’ centres, to increase accessibility, visibility, and retention. These types of organizations may be more likely to recruit diverse participants, and offer yoga in no or low-cost formats. In addition, we recommend getting feedback on the yoga intervention from yoga instructors, teacher training programs, and continuing education trainings (e.g., non-profit organizations such as Accessible Yoga School) to address issues related to equity, diversity, and inclusion in yoga that were described by participants. Working with such organizations may provide educational opportunities for yoga instructors to deliver yoga to diverse populations. Finally, we recommend asking participants about their perceptions of the yoga instructors’ body size. Perhaps this could play a role for participants’ perceptions of facets of positive body image or social comparisons during physical activity.

Finally, the yoga instructors noted the yoga manual that was provided was a useful tool in allowing the yoga instructors who were unfamiliar with this particular research and positive body image to deliver the intervention as it was intended. Fidelity was extremely high in our study and instructors were able to create classes that adhered to the principles of the yoga program, but which also adhered to their personal styles. The manual therefore was a critical resource for the delivery of the intervention.

Changes in facets of positive body image

Our results indicate the potential positive impact of yoga on positive body image in men and women across the adult lifespan (or at least middle to older adults). Effect size ranged from small to large across our positive body image variables. This highlights the importance of assessing a variety of facets of positive body image. These findings are promising with regard to benefits of engaging in yoga and how yoga may be integral in developing positive body image in middle and older adults. An interesting finding in this study is with regard to increases in functionality appreciation and satisfaction with body function. By contrast, Alleva et al. [17] found no significant impact on functionality appreciation in their sample of young women. A recent systematic review of correlates of functionality appreciation reported no relationship between age and functionality appreciation in cross-sectional studies [26]. However, the majority of research on functionality appreciation includes adults 50 years of age and under [26]. Perhaps functionality appreciation might be more relevant to older adult populations [54]. Dagenais et al. [54] found that adults >65 years reported higher functionality appreciation compared to younger age groups. Further, Reboussin et al. reported age was positively associated with body satisfaction in sedentary adults between the ages of 35–75 years [40]. This may be related to older adults recognizing and being grateful for the function they have (see [54]), as older adults generally place greater importance on health and function relative to appearance [55].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include adapting an evidence-based yoga program and applying it to a more diverse adult population, adherence to CONSORT guidelines, and collecting data from instructors as well as participants. The diversity of the sample (in terms of age and gender) brings more equity to the understanding of interventions to improve positive body image, which has focused almost exclusively on young women. This study also provided specific suggestions to refine the study protocol and yoga intervention itself to be more inclusive. Specifically, a major recommendation was to incorporate poses that are more accessible to a diverse age range and to ensure instructors have the experience and training to modify poses for a variety of ages and medical conditions. Finally, our study has provided preliminary evidence that the yoga program may facilitate improvements in positive body image outcomes. Given the positive effects in a broad range of measures of positive body image, it is critical to continue to move beyond just body and functionality appreciation as facets of positive body image.

There are several limitations to this study that need to be acknowledged. First, this study included a sample from a small Southwest Ontario city, limiting generalizability. While this study was diverse regarding age, there was no representation from the young adult population (age 18–30 years of age). Further, the majority of participants were women, White, and well-educated. Recruiting from a more diverse geographic region may be more advantageous in terms of diversity of characteristics such as socioeconomic status and ethnicity that may be related to positive body image [3]. Thirdly, while we attempted to recruit diverse yoga instructors, both identified as women, White, and over 50 years of age. Recruiting diverse yoga instructors, varying in age, ethnicity and gender may assist in recruiting more diverse samples of participants. Finally, we used snowball sampling to recruit, where participants could share information with others about the study. It is possible that a participant may have signed up with a friend and thus known others in the class. It may have been possible that a participant may have known someone (although anecdotally this did not seem to be the case). We did randomize all participants individually to either yoga or control conditions to address the concern over independence; however, future researchers should ask participants if they knew anyone else in the classes.

With respect to the measures used, there was low internal consistency on the BRS scale at midpoint. This is more than likely due to a small sample size and restricted variance where we see a smaller standard deviation. Further, there have been some criticisms about the BI-AAQ as a measure of body image flexibility, as all items are phrased in the direction of inflexibility, and are reversed scored; this approach suggests body image flexibility and inflexibility are polar ends of a single continuum. However, recent evidence suggests body image inflexibility is distinct from flexibility, leading to questions about the construct validity of the BI-AAQ [56]. More recent approaches to measurement of body image flexibility as a multidimensional construct (with flexibility and inflexibility as separate factors) should be used in future research [56].

Recommendations

We suggest continuing to refine the procedures of the study assessing this yoga program for adults 18 years of age or older to promote positive body image. Specifically incorporating more inclusive poses for older individuals or those living with chronic illnesses may be beneficial. Conducting the study at a time of year other than summer may facilitate recruitment and adherence while reducing the dropout rate considering we exceeded this minimum threshold (>30% dropout). For the control group, providing regular (e.g., once a week) interaction, such as an information session, may increase engagement and potentially reduce dropout in this group. This could also increase scores on feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness as we did not meet our minimum threshold on those measures. There were some challenges to recruiting younger individuals and men in particular considering we did not meet our minimum threshold for recruitment rates among men (minimum threshold of 30% recruiting men). We suggest exploring recruitment strategies to include more men (e.g., information sessions for men) and younger individuals (e.g., Instagram, TikTok). Recruiting yoga instructors with extensive knowledge and experience teaching yoga to diverse individuals is critical to effectively deliver this particular yoga intervention.

We recommend conducting qualitative interviews with participants from both groups and instructors to provide further insight to testing the yoga program, for example to improve the retention rate in the control and intervention groups. These interviews could also assist in determining other body image benefits of the yoga intervention. Future studies should attempt to determine the appropriate dose (in terms of frequency of sessions per week and length of program in weeks) needed to optimize positive body image. Including a follow-up period post-intervention to see if changes in facets of positive body image are maintained would be an important next step. We also suggest future research explore additional facets of positive body image such as body compassion as prior research has shown yoga can increase body compassion through increases in embodiment in university women [17]. Finally, although we attempted to include men and women in the study, we recruited predominantly women. None of the men randomized to the yoga group dropped out indicating the program was acceptable to men and women (albeit with a very small sample of men). Future research should consider conducting focus groups with men to specifically explore ways to increase recruitment of men for yoga programs; one potential solution could be to recruit only for men, as some men may have been hesitant to participate in yoga in co-ed settings.

Conclusion

This pilot study indicated the potential for this yoga intervention to have positive effects on positive body image in men and women 18 years or older participating, but future large-scale implementation and dissemination in a larger sample size is warranted. This yoga program is a unique program to combine Hatha yoga (most commonly practiced in Western societies [57]), with themes promoting positive body-self connection. Our findings suggest the yoga program is feasible for use among adults 18 years of age or older, based on retention, adherence, and fidelity data. Yoga instructors, coaches, and personal trainers working in various fitness-related fields can incorporate positive body image aspects from this yoga program into their exercise classes, workouts, sports and at community-based facilities. Based on the results of this study, we would recommend some modifications to improve diversity of recruitment and to evaluate the changes in facets of positive body image of this program in a fully powered study.
