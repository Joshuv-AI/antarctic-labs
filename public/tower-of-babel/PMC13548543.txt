An exploration of sexual and relational impacts of transvaginal mesh injury: a systematic review using thematic synthesis
Authors: Natasha Beer, Danielle De Boos, Mark Gresswell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransvaginal mesh, used to treat Pelvic Organ Prolapse and Stress Urinary Incontinence, has caused substantial injury to a growing number of women. The impact of mesh injury is far-reaching, significantly disrupting women’s lives. Whilst there is a growing body of literature examining the impacts, there is little research that specifically aims to understand the sexual and relational implications of mesh injury. This study aimed to systematically review evidence regarding the impacts of mesh injury reported by women in relation to sexual and relational functioning.MethodsA systematic search of five databases (CINAHL, MEDLINE, PubMed, PsycINFO, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses) and a grey literature search was conducted in April 2026. Studies and inquiries reporting data about women’s experiences of mesh injury where sexual and relational impacts were reported were included. The quality of each study was assessed using the qualitative CASP checklist. The data was thematically synthesised.ResultsA total of nine reports were included, comprising of six published studies, one unpublished Doctoral thesis, and two government inquiries. Sample sizes were varied, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 7—84, and a secondary analysis of 399 accounts from one study. Participants included in government inquiries ranged between 517 and 555. A conservative total of 1483 participants is represented, reflecting the heterogeneity of the evidence base and limitations in the reporting of inquiry data. Of the nine included reports, eight were rated high quality with one rated as moderate. Seven overlapping themes were developed from the data: 1) Sexual functioning: changes due to pain, 2) Changes to sexuality, 3) Relational activity: changes due to pain, 4) Changes to relationship satisfaction, 5) Identity: changing sense of self navigating life with mesh injury, 6) Changes in relational roles and a seventh central theme of disrupted embodied sexual-relational self.ConclusionWomen with mesh injury experience significant sexual and relational disruption as a result of chronic pain, contributing to psychological distress. Psychological flexibility may be a protective mechanism for relational distress. Further research that seeks to understand these impacts is needed, which may inform the development of appropriate psychological interventions.Trial registrationPROSPERO ID: CRD420251077730.

Background

Transvaginal mesh injury is an example of iatrogenic harm experienced by women [1]. For the purpose of this review, the term ‘mesh injury’ is used to describe the range of harms, including physical and psychological harm, reported by women following transvaginal mesh procedures. For decades, transvaginal pelvic mesh procedures were commonly used in the treatment of Pelvic Organ Prolapse and Stress Urinary Incontinence [2]. Government commissioned national inquiries [2, 3] found widespread failings in relation to the identification and response to the harmful effects of mesh devices. Women’s experiences were dismissed at multiple levels across the system [2, 4], with radical changes required concerning the communication of patient-reported outcomes when evaluating treatment outcomes [1, 4].

A recent systematic literature review focusing on women’s accounts of the harm caused by transvaginal mesh indicates that the effects have caused significant, far-reaching and long-lasting disruption to women’s lives [5]. Mesh injury disrupts personal lives, relationships, careers, and social lives, resulting in psychological harm [4, 6]. Whilst there is a growing body of evidence aimed at understanding the physical, psychological and sociological impacts of mesh injury [6–12], there is little research focusing on the impact of mesh injury on sexual and relational functioning for mesh-injured women. The only existing qualitative paper that specifically aims to examine the sexual impact of mesh injury focuses on the male experience of sexual intercourse with a mesh-injured female partner [13]. This research was conducted whilst women were still campaigning for recognition of the serious complications associated with the use of transvaginal mesh. Understanding the differential challenges faced by both women and men is central to achieving transformative change [14]. Therefore, this review focuses on the sexual and relational impacts reported by women.

Sexual functioning is an important factor when considering overall quality of life [15]. Sexual behaviours are a broad and complex interplay of mechanisms [16–18], which is further complicated by the presence of chronic pain. Sexual relations not only support the development of new relationships but are an important factor in the maintenance of relationships [19]. Several studies have demonstrated that relational satisfaction is influenced by sexual satisfaction [20]. Women have been identified as engaging in sexual intercourse to achieve bonding and emotional closeness [21]; therefore, the implications of not having sex are broader than the achievement of physical intimacy and have relational implications.

Women experiencing chronic vaginal pain during intercourse are more likely to experience sexual self-discrepancies, which is associated with lower sexual satisfaction [22]. Several studies have identified a relationship between sexual dysfunction and decreased relationship satisfaction [23–25]. Chronic pain exists within a complex interplay of biopsychosocial processes [26], and has also been found to contribute to changes in relationship satisfaction [27]. Changes in relational satisfaction are influenced by chronic pain itself and contextual variables within the couple [28].

Whilst sexual pleasure for women is now recognised as an important consideration within their overall sexual health [29], the lack of recognition of the importance of sexual satisfaction for women as a surgical outcome may have contributed to the lack of sexual outcome measures following transvaginal mesh interventions [2]. Therefore, gaining an understanding of both women’s sexual and relational satisfaction in the context of mesh injury is important.

Due to the lack of research specifically exploring mesh-injured women’s sexual and relational experiences, much of the literature drawn upon relates to organic rather than iatrogenic pain processes [30]. This may limit the generalisability of the findings in the context of women who have suffered iatrogenic harm. Whilst not the primary aim of the study, the findings may highlight the differential experiences and needs reported by mesh-injured women.

Although the significant sexual, relational and psychological impacts of vaginal mesh injury are documented in the broader literature, there remains a lack of research examining effective psychological interventions for this population. Limitations have been identified in applying established chronic pain models to mesh-injured women’s experiences due to a lack of compatibility with the approach in the standard model of chronic pain disability [5]. Due to the differential needs of mesh-injured women, this approach increases the risk of the dismissal and invalidation of the physical suffering experienced, thereby perpetuating harm for this population [5].

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is recommended by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence as a psychological therapy for the management of chronic pain [32], with a growing evidence base for chronic pain management [33–36]. ACT interventions aim to increase psychological flexibility and utilise cognitive defusion techniques to support individuals to re-engage with valued activities and build a meaningful life even with the presence of pain [37, 38]. Psychological flexibility refers to the coping, acceptance and adjustment to stressors in a way which facilitates valued goal pursuit [39]. Psychological inflexibility in chronic pain cohorts has been identified as a predictor of pain-related dysfunction and distress [38]. In addition to the evidence base for chronic pain, ACT has an emerging evidence base for sexual and relational difficulties [40, 41].

ACT offers a theoretical framework for understanding mesh-injured women’s health-related psychological distress. Therefore, the discussion partly considers the findings of the review through the lens of psychological flexibility in line with the ACT approach [37].

Aims

The primary aim is the systematic identification, appraisal, and synthesis of the available evidence regarding the sexual and relational impacts that women experience as a result of vaginal mesh injury. The secondary aim is to identify gaps within the current literature to highlight areas for future research. Implications for clinical practice are considered in the discussion.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [42] guidelines were adopted to ensure consistency in reporting of the results. The review was registered on PROSPERO in June 2025 (ID: CRD420251077730).

Reflexive position

The primary author is a trainee clinical psychologist with no personal experience of harm caused by transvaginal mesh or chronic pain. The author identifies as a female and as an intersectional feminist, which has influenced the choice of topic. Whilst this shapes the lens in which the findings have been interpreted, this position offers valuable contributions to the understanding of gendered healthcare experiences. Research underpinned by feminist values creates space to critically examine the lived experiences of women, supporting exploration of marginalisation and power inequalities in a range of contexts [14].

A feminist phenomenological position has been adopted, which operates with the key assumption that embodied experiences are shaped by discursive and structural contexts [43]. This approach aims to uncover sedimented and hidden structures that influence those experiences, allowing for the interpretation of the subjective experiences of mesh-injured women whilst acknowledging structural explanations such as gender biases in medical research and institutions [43]. Feminist standpoint research places women’s lived experiences at the centre of research, establishing a position in which knowledge can be built from [44].

The reflexive position sensitised the author’s analysis towards themes of power, dismissal, embodiment, sexuality and relational identity during coding. Reflexive supervision discussions around coding decisions and the use of audit trail processes were used to challenge over-interpretation and consider alternative meanings within the data. Extracts from the data were used to anchor each theme and support the reader to consider the credibility of the interpretations.

Search strategy

Five databases were searched in April 2026: CINAHL, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, PubMed, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses. These databases were selected to allow for a broad search of publications relevant to the review. Additionally, the search strategy included a citation search and Google Advanced Search to capture relevant government inquiries. The inclusion of grey literature was important to capture global discourse and inquiries in relation to vaginal mesh injury. Date limits were applied, which restricted the search to published studies from January 2008 to April 2026.

The search window starting in 2008 corresponds with the first FDA notification indicating that there were rare, but serious complications associated with transvaginal placement of surgical mesh [2, 45]. Whilst mesh procedures and complications occurred earlier than 2008, prior to this date complications were both under-reported and women’s experiences were unexamined qualitatively. The post-2008 period reflects a shift in the visibility, legitimacy, and qualitative documentation of women’s experiences of mesh-related harm.

Search terms were combined using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ to include terms from each of the four domains. The truncation asterisk (*) was utilised to broaden search results by searching for variations of the search terms. Quotation marks (“ “) were used to find exact phrasing.

Screening and study selection

Once database searches were complete, the results were uploaded into Covidence. Initially, duplicate studies were removed, and the remaining reports were screened by the first author using the title and abstract to assess for suitability. The first author then manually retrieved final reports for full-text screening against the eligibility criteria. Citation searching was conducted, and a Google Advanced Search was used to identify relevant government inquiries. No disagreements arose during study selection.

Data extraction

All final reports included in the review were qualitative. Data pertinent to the research question was extracted from each report. Relevant data points from each government inquiry, such as inquiry submissions and key findings, were extracted. Data in relation to the characteristics of each report were extracted to include the author details, publication date, geographical location, study title, participant characteristics, surgical indication, data collection method, data analysis method, and research aim. Although ethical approval was not required for the review, consideration was given to the ethical implications of secondary analysis of publicly available accounts where participants did not provide explicit consent for research use at the time of submission. Where data were extracted for the purpose of the review, care was taken to respect the sensitivity of the material by avoiding misrepresentation and decontextualisation of the participants narratives. Carefully considered extracts were used to illustrate themes.

Data synthesis

A thematic approach was adopted to synthesise the data [46]. The results and discussion data from each study were extracted. The government inquiries were synthesised alongside the qualitative studies. Where available, first-hand accounts were extracted from the inquiries with discussion and reporting pertaining to the research question, which was then coded and synthesised using the same framework to integrate the diverse range of sources.

All data was extracted into a structed table in Microsoft Word, and coding was used to organise themes. The data was coded by the first author, generating an initial set of 55 codes. Codes were reviewed and iteratively grouped into descriptive themes, which were subsequently developed into analytical themes. Interpretations of the data, including coding decisions and theme development were reviewed and agreed with the supervisory team to ensure consistency. An audit trail of the coding decisions and development of themes was maintained throughout the process to enhance transparency and rigour. First-order data in the form of quotations is included in the result sections to illustrate the themes.

Results

The database search yielded 11,388 results in the search. Figure 1 illustrates the PRISMA [42] selection process. A total of 4211 reports were duplicates and therefore excluded before screening. The titles and abstracts of 7177 reports were screened, with a final 21 reports screened for eligibility using full-text review. In addition to the database search, three government inquiries were retrieved via Google Advanced Search, with one excluded due to not meeting the inclusion criteria. One paper identified through citation searching was not retrieved. A total of nine reports, made up of six studies, one Doctoral thesis and two government inquiries, were included in the final review.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram illustrating study selection process

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 3 with the corresponding identification number. Of the nine reports included, six were qualitative studies, one qualitative doctoral thesis, and two government inquiries.

The nine included studies were published between 2014 and 2024, indicating a period in which changes in understanding and the discourse around the use of transvaginal mesh occurred. Demographic characteristics were reported inconsistently across studies; however, age range is reported in Table 3 where available.

Sample sizes are reported for each report in Table 3. Sample sizes for studies A, B, C, D, E and I ranged from seven to 84. Study H used secondary data and reported a sample of 399 submissions, with saturation reached at 138. Both government reports (F, G) included individual submissions and engagement events but did not provide individual-level participant counts. Study F reported 517 participants attended patient engagement events as part of the pelvic mesh arm of the inquiry; however, the number of written and oral contributions was not reported. Study G reported 555 written submissions were received by the inquiry, with 399 of these being firsthand accounts. Data and discussion pertaining to firsthand accounts was included from studies F and G.

Consequently, the total number of participants across the nine reports is heterogeneous. Summing the reported individual-level accounts from the qualitative studies and inquiry reports yields approximately 1483 women; however, this figure reflects a combination of conventional study participants, secondary data reporting, and government inquiry. This both highlights the diverse nature of the review and presents a limitation in relation to identifying a definitive number of participants represented in the review.

The included studies primarily focus on the impacts of mesh injury rather than clinical classification. Mesh injury is not a standardised clinical category across the literature and is used broadly to refer to the multi-dimensional range of chronic and systemic physical, psychological and social adverse outcomes of transvaginal mesh. Additionally, the underlying mechanisms and clinical pathways leading to sexual pain, dyspareunia and relational disruption were not always reported.

The included studies predominantly represent mid-urethral sling procedures for SUI (A, D, E, I), with some reporting POP (B, I), mixed procedures (C, I) and a small sample of abdominal sacrocolpopexy represented in study I. The inquiry data (F, G, H) is large in volume however does not provide procedural specificity in the reported data. Surgical indication represented in each study is outlined in Table 3.

The studies each aimed to amplify the voice of mesh-injured women in research, and to gain an understanding of the impacts of vaginal mesh injury on women’s lives. Whilst the studies did not solely focus on the impacts of mesh injury in relation to women’s sex lives and relationships, this featured in each study to varying degrees. Sexual and relational impacts featured the most in study D. Studies F and A both reported sexual and relational impacts; however, the data was limited in these studies.

In study D, participants were categorised as experiencing mild, moderate or severe injury. Severity categorisation was limited to study D. The definition of the classifications was relative to the sample as opposed to the broader context of mesh-injury. Capturing nuances in the different outcomes women experienced based on the severity of their injury allowed for synthesis and analysis, which demonstrated the different outcomes for women who had received timely diagnosis and treatment options versus women who had experienced protracted complications.

The studies included were conducted in New Zealand (C), the United States of America (A, I), Australia (G, H), and the United Kingdom (UK; B, D, E, F). The pool of studies included in the review was conducted by a range of disciplines, including theological (C), sociological (D), political (F, G), (A, B, E), psychological (H) and medical (I) perspectives. Study C was conducted by a researcher with lived experience. Studies G and H both provided analyses based on one set of data collected as part of the Australian Parliament Senate Community Affairs References Committee [3].

Thematic synthesis of findings

Three overarching themes were identified: changes in sexual functioning, changes in relational activity due to pain and changes to identity. The three themes intersected with each other, creating three subthemes: changes in relationship satisfaction, changes in sexuality and changes in relational roles, with a central theme of disrupted embodied sexual-relational self. Sexual functioning: changes in sexual behaviour due to chronic pain

Pain disrupting sexual activity featured in each of the data sets (A – I). Women described the painful implications of having penetrative sex and experienced an array of physical symptoms during sex, including vaginal dryness, sharp burning pain and spasms, bleeding, infections and offensive odour, and prolonged pain after sex (C, F, G, H). The pain was felt locally and in other areas of the body, including the abdomen, back, and bladder (H). As a result of the painful and unpleasant symptoms, women reported changes in libido and felt unable to want or enjoy sex (C).

In study D participants were identified as being mildly, moderately, or severely injured by mesh. Women across each category experienced disruption to sexual intimacy, with pain interfering with the women’s ability to engage in sexual activity. Women who were identified as having mild injury appeared to identify other ways of bonding with their partner:

“Instead, they reduced the disruption caused by their lack of, or reduced, sex lives by reevaluating and shifting the meanings they attached to their romantic relationships.” (D).

“Instead, they reduced the disruption caused by their lack of, or reduced, sex lives by reevaluating and shifting the meanings they attached to their romantic relationships.” (D).

Some women reported feeling as though their sex life was ruined (I), and others reported refraining from sexual relations completely, with no plans to have sex again (D, F, H). The loss of sexual intercourse ranged from months (D) to upwards of 10 years (F).

“I have never had intercourse without pain since my first surgery in 2007.10 years and counting. I expect to never have intercourse again.” (H).

“I have never had intercourse without pain since my first surgery in 2007.10 years and counting. I expect to never have intercourse again.” (H).

The loss of sexual activity was associated with feelings of grief (G). However, some women reported feeling a sense of duty to continue to have sexual intercourse and regain their ability to function as a sexual partner despite the pain (A, B, D, E, H); this was sometimes related to factors such as maintaining financial security (D).

Additionally, mesh erosion was also associated with physical injury to partners (A, D, F, G, H) which resulted in women avoiding sexual intercourse, and contributed to psychological distress including anxiety, guilt, grief, and depression (H).

Changes in sexuality

The enduring pain meant women no longer felt able to physically want or enjoy sex, which negatively impacted their sexual identity (B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I). Women were left feeling as though they were not feminine (I), sexy (D) or attractive as a result of their mesh injury (B).

“I think, the inability to … to have a sexual relationship you know, sort of is another sort of very, difficult journey you know, to deal with I think, because you feel unattractive you know, you feel … that there is a strain on the relationship because of it” (B).

“I think, the inability to … to have a sexual relationship you know, sort of is another sort of very, difficult journey you know, to deal with I think, because you feel unattractive you know, you feel … that there is a strain on the relationship because of it” (B).

The physical harms caused by mesh implants and subsequent surgeries resulted in anatomical changes which felt incompatible with women perceiving themselves as a sexual being. One participant reported that they no longer had enough tissue to form a vagina (C) and another reported a comment made by their partner that they were a ‘cheese grater’ (G), leading to feelings of embarrassment. As a result of the ongoing complications and disruption to their sex lives, women reported reconciling with the idea that they would never function as a sexual partner again (D, G, H, I) and thus questioned if they would ever be able to maintain a relationship. This marked end to sexual intercourse represents the most profound and enduring sexual outcome for mesh-injured women.

In study D, women who were identified as mildly and moderately injured remained hopeful that they would be able to resume sexual intercourse and saw their injury as a temporary disruption to their sexual selves. Whilst the women in both these categories experienced severe symptoms in relation to sexual functioning, they either considered their symptoms to be manageable (mild) or expected that with treatment, they would be able to recover from the complications (moderate) (D).

Changes in relational roles

Changes in relational roles were reported across five studies (B, C, D, E, G, H). Women described feeling unable to be the partner they envisioned themselves being (H) and being reliant on partners who had transitioned into caring roles (C, D, H). Women also described their partners as friends, roommates, and platonic (B, D, E):

A key contributor to the change in roles was the loss of relational and sexual activity, resulting in relational roles shifting. Activities of shared interest, which may have previously facilitated bonding and maintenance of a romantic relationship, were lost due to the continued experience of pain (B, D, G, H).

“Me and my husband, we’re not a couple …It’s more platonic than anything else… He’s in his room, and I just lay in here and listen to music because I can’t even concentrate on a TV programme because of pain…” (D).

“Me and my husband, we’re not a couple …It’s more platonic than anything else… He’s in his room, and I just lay in here and listen to music because I can’t even concentrate on a TV programme because of pain…” (D).

Relational activity: changes in relational activity due to chronic pain

The pain experienced by women resulted in disruptions to other areas of their relationships, with women reporting they felt unable to participate in activities which they had previously enjoyed with their partners (D, G, H). There were, however, variations to this. Women who identified as having mild injury (D) felt that with adjustments, they were able to maintain some level of their pre-injury functioning, limiting disruption to their relationships.

However, women who were identified as moderately or severely injured in study D, were faced with a significant loss of relational activity, or felt forced to forfeit activities with their wider social network to focus their efforts on maintaining activity with their partners to protect the relationship.

“We used to go walking in the Lake District […] we used to go to football matches, gigs […] I just can't stand for any length of time I get so much pain… even sitting here now I’m in pain […] We still love each other but our relationship has changed. He’s one of those people who hates staying in, so we spend more time apart now.” (D).

He’s one of those people who hates staying in, so we spend more time apart now.” (D).

Changes in relationship satisfaction

Relationship satisfaction appeared to change for many couples due to the sexual and relational impacts caused by mesh injury (B, D, E, G, H, I). Women reported feeling heartbroken and isolated by the changes in their relationship (E). Women who had been experiencing mesh injury complications for protracted periods of time appeared to be more likely to be negatively impacted in this domain (D, G), signalling the long-term effects of the pressure felt within the couple.

In contrast, some relationships were able to withstand the effects of mesh injury. This appeared to occur when a timely diagnosis had been received (D). Timely diagnosis meant couples were able to attribute the difficulties to mesh injury and remained hopeful that treatment would mean they could resume their pre-injury relational activities, both sexually and leisurely. Thus, timely diagnosis acted as a protective mechanism.

“I’m blessed to be in an incredibly strong marriage…I've got a lovely husband who understands that there's no pressure. We just love each other… There’s other ways of showing your affection and showing your love and kindness…” (D).

“I’m blessed to be in an incredibly strong marriage…I've got a lovely husband who understands that there's no pressure. We just love each other… There’s other ways of showing your affection and showing your love and kindness…” (D).

In some couples, relationship satisfaction was closely related to sexual activity (A, D). Women felt that even though sexual activity was painful and presented physical harm to both them and their partners, they were obligated to engage in sexual activity to protect the satisfaction in the relationship (A, D). This carried an emotional and physical cost for the women.

“It’s keeping our relationship alive in a way so that after the surgery there’s still a relationship to return to.” (D).

“It’s keeping our relationship alive in a way so that after the surgery there’s still a relationship to return to.” (D).

For some the relational impacts of mesh injury were so profound that the relationship had not been able to withstand the stress. This resulted in a complete breakdown of the relationship leading to separation and divorce (A, C, D, G).

Women who were single and wishing to form new relationships also encountered difficulties (A, D, I). The challenges associated with living with mesh injury and being able to engage in sexual relations felt incompatible with a new relationship, with some women avoiding dating and entering into a new relationship altogether (D, I).

Identity: changing sense of self, navigating life with mesh , injury

Women have been faced with navigating a changing sense of identity as a result of mesh injury (B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I). Being unable to engage in activities that they were previously able to, such as social activities, household activities, and feeling unable to maintain the roles they had previously held in their relationship led to changes in their sense of self. Study C expanded on this, considering the challenges of reconnecting and reconstructing a sense of identity, within the shifting world mesh-injury that feels smaller.

“What once seemed like an expansive world of opportunity now feels contracted and is dependent on one’s body’s capacity to engage on any given day. This contracted life leads to fewer opportunities to re-construct a valued self.” (C).

“What once seemed like an expansive world of opportunity now feels contracted and is dependent on one’s body’s capacity to engage on any given day. This contracted life leads to fewer opportunities to re-construct a valued self.” (C).

Although only explicitly stated in one study, study G pointed to the impacts in communities of women where cultural practices and beliefs are impacted by mesh injury. This indicates that changing identity was not only felt by the participants but may be imposed on them by others in the community.

“The impact of isolation on Indigenous women, you will find that if they have this mesh, they will be totally ostracised. When our women talk about sex, sex is not just sex; it encompasses a whole community and involves love, intimacy, touch deprivation—everything.” (G).

“The impact of isolation on Indigenous women, you will find that if they have this mesh, they will be totally ostracised. When our women talk about sex, sex is not just sex; it encompasses a whole community and involves love, intimacy, touch deprivation—everything.” (G).

Disrupted embodied sexual-relational self

The final theme of disrupted embodied sexual-relational self brings together the complex and multifaceted nature of mesh injury. The women’s experience of changes to their sexual activity, sexual identity and relational identity created significant changes to their perception of their embodied selves.

‘‘I can’t function as a woman; I don’t feel like a woman. I don’t know how I will possibly find a relationship.’’ (I).

‘‘I can’t function as a woman; I don’t feel like a woman. I don’t know how I will possibly find a relationship.’’ (I).

Study C captured embodied impacts in the theme ‘lived body’. Women were described as no long feeling whole, and ‘inhabiting a body that could not be relied upon’. This was understood as causing disruption to the unity between their body and their sense of self, and their sense of being in the world (C). This disruption to the embodied self was captured across studies (C, D, H, I) and was so significant that it was though part of themselves had died.

“The pain slowly kills your soul.” (G).

“The pain slowly kills your soul.” (G).

Women felt as though parts of their lives, and thus parts of themselves had been robbed (G), stolen (H), spoiled, degraded, diminished (I). The strength of language in the studies points to the gravity of changes experienced by women.

Finally, reflective of the embodied impacts of mesh-injury, women voiced a need for an embodied approach to their care (E, F).

“Good care was considered to look beyond compartments to see the whole”. (E).

“Good care was considered to look beyond compartments to see the whole”. (E).

Discussion

The purpose of this review was to locate and synthesise accounts of women injured by transvaginal mesh implants, specifically in relation to sexual and relational functioning. The literature drawn upon in the discussion reflects different aetiological causes of chronic pain, which, although a limitation, supports broader understanding of how chronic pain influences sexual and relational outcomes for women across conditions.

Painful intercourse as a result of mesh injury was a common theme across all the studies, irrespective of pre-operative condition, surgical intervention, and severity of their mesh injury where these were reported, evidencing the significant disruption to women’s sexual functioning. Whilst POP and SUI differ in their underlying indication, anatomical placement of mesh and complication profiles, the reported impacts (pain, dyspareunia, relational disruption) were broadly consistent across studies where procedure type was specified.

Findings from the Cumberlege [2] review indicate that reporting on adverse outcomes of mesh devices was not representative of the risks and complications women have faced. Painful intercourse as a complication of the medical device was not reported as a risk of the procedures. This reflects a discrepancy in the importance women place on their ability to maintain a healthy sex life, compared to the manufacturers who did not perceive this as an important outcome. Sexual pleasure is a key aspect of women’s overall sexual health [29] and the Women’s Health Strategy for England [48] acknowledges current gender biases in healthcare, calling for the discrepancies to be addressed by healthcare providers. The findings of the review reinforce that for women to make an informed decision about mesh-related procedures, they should be fully informed about the potential sexual and relational outcomes.

It is important to acknowledge that experiences of mesh injury and subsequent pain may arise from diverse and overlapping mechanisms, which are not consistently reported across studies. Pre-existing severity of POP and SUI, prior symptoms and the number of attempted revision surgical interventions may all contribute to women’s painful sexual experiences. However, it is important to note that many women represented in the review attributed their experience to mesh injury, with participants reporting complications as new symptoms following placement of transvaginal mesh [2, 3, 6, 7, 9].

The existing literature demonstrates that for women chronic pain and sexual dysfunction are often co-occurring difficulties [49]. Findings from this review suggest that chronic pain following mesh injury is associated with sexual dysfunction, consistent with research in other chronic pain conditions. Existing research demonstrates that sexual dysfunction as a result of chronic pain conditions results in lower levels of sexual satisfaction for women [22, 49], which was reflected in the accounts of women included in the review. Sexual dysfunction has been described as a multifactorial problem, with multiple detrimental impacts creating psychological, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and economic difficulties [50]. The findings of this review demonstrate the multifactorial nature and wider impacts of sexual dysfunction for mesh-injured women such as their perceived sexuality, relationship satisfaction and changing identity.

The impact of chronic pain extends beyond the sexual act, with pain interfering with women’s feelings of sexual desire [51]. Sexuality is posited to be central to the human psychological experience [16]. Sexuality encompasses perceptions of self as a sexual being, thereby influencing sexual activities and behaviours [52]. This accounts in the review demonstrate that women not only felt physically unable to engage in sexual activity due to pain, but also reported a loss of libido and no longer viewed themselves as a sexual being.

Motivators for sexual relations change throughout the course of relationships [17, 53], which changes the nature and frequency of sexual intimacy. For women with mesh injury, pain has profoundly disrupted their sexual activity in new [6, 9, 11] and established relationships [2, 6–9, 11]. In line with previous research [17, 21], the findings from this review indicate women’s motivators for intercourse varied, with women seeking emotional intimacy, role fulfilment and relational security.

The development of individual identity, bonds, and intimate relationships are closely related to sexual functioning [19, 51]. Thus, sexual dysfunction as a result of mesh injury cannot be understood solely as a physical complaint or in silo. This is reflected in the overlap of changes in sexual functioning and women’s reports of changing sexuality, identity and relationship satisfaction.

The findings of the review indicate that relationships were damaged, sometimes irreparably by chronic pain. Spousal responses to their partners experience of pain has been found to directly impact relationship satisfaction [27, 28, 54]. As patient pain adjustment may be complicated by spousal pain-reinforcing behaviours; pain management interventions that seek to understand relational factors may support both pain adjustment and relationship satisfaction [26]. Reese et al. [30] posit that the affective emotional experience of pain may be influenced by the quality of the marital relationship. Women in happy unions that identify their partner as highly responsive to their needs have been found to have capacity to adopt adaptive coping strategies which increase positive affect and functioning during episodes of pain [55].

In contrast, people in relationships with poor adjustment are at increased risk of higher levels of pain and psychological disability [30]. Partners caring for spouses with chronic pain have been found to experience feelings of burden and poor marital adjustment [56], which may further contribute to changes in relational roles and thus relationship satisfaction. Male partners of mesh-injured women have been found to express similar experiences to the women in this study in relation to navigating changing roles in the relationship, increased social isolation and changes in intimacy [57]. The paralleled experiences highlight a shared understanding and difficulties for couples when faced with the implications of mesh injury, highlighting the importance of the availability of couple interventions.

The women’s reports of significant loss of valued activities in the relationship including both sexual and recreational activities that served as bonding experiences appeared to be related to not only decreased relationship satisfaction, but higher levels of psychological distress. Pain as a barrier to quality time in couples has been found to have a detrimental impact on relationship satisfaction [58], with which the findings of this review are consistent. In contrast, women who spoke positively about their relationship satisfaction appeared to have identified new ways of bonding with their partner, which may have functioned as a protective mechanism for the relationship.

Interventions that promote relational flexibility and relationship rebuilding activities have been found to improve pain related distress within couples [58]. The differences in how women responded to sexual and relational disruption may reflect varying levels of psychological flexibility, as conceptualised within the ACT framework. The development of psychological flexibility may support the exploration of new approaches to intimacy and relational activity, with the potential to improve relationship satisfaction.

ACT is an empirically supported transdiagnostic psychological therapy, aimed at fostering psychological flexibility as opposed to symptom reduction [34]. Although ACT was not directly examined within the included studies, several findings of the present review, particularly activity avoidance, loss of valued roles and variations in psychological flexibility, may be broadly consistent with ACT principles.

ACT approaches to chronic pain acknowledge that the avoidance of activities has long-term detrimental impacts due to the loss of meaningful life activities [38]. Recent findings demonstrate that ACT interventions reduce how much pain affects daily life with medium effect [36, 59]. Additionally, ACT has been found to improve participants ability to function with moderate to large effect sizes [36]. Whilst there are limitations to the approach, ACT has been shown to increase feelings of compatibility in couples, with ACT interventions supporting couples to adopt a team mentality when considering experiences and actions against their joint values [39]. Therefore, ACT approaches may represent a potentially useful avenue for future research with mesh-injured women and their partners.

The implications of each overarching theme resulted in a central disruption to the embodied sexual-relational self. The concept of embodiment is rooted in philosophy but has been garnering increased attention within the realm of psychology [60]. The self is a sum of traits that collectively inform an individual’s sense of being [61], with embodiment considering the combined experience of having a body and being a body, the synthesis of which is central to the development of self-identity [60]. Disharmonious synthesis is associated with psychological distress [60]. Mesh injury can be conceptualised as a significant event which creates disharmonious synthesis, inhabiting a body which no longer aligns with the ideal sense of self. Furthermore, social conditions and relationships are features of the embodied self [61]. As demonstrated in the review women felt at odds with the social conditions and relationships, which had previously informed their sense of self.

Feminist theories of embodiment consider the embodied experience within the discursive structures which shape women’s experiences [43]. This was particularly demonstrated in one study where women were at higher risk of being ostracised within their community as a result of their mesh-injury, highlighting the impact of the discursive structures that influence the embodied experience.

Intrinsic to the embodied self is sexuality [16]. Women who experience chronic pain are at increased risk of experiencing a cognitive dissonance, where their reality does not align with their desired sexual self, thereby disrupting the embodied sense of self and contributing to overall suffering [22]. The inability to have sexual intercourse is a threat to a sense of belonging and value, as women face pressures to comply with the heterosexual norms of intercourse [31].

Taken together, the findings suggest that mesh-injury extends beyond disruption of bodily function, impacting the integrated self as embodied, relational and sexual. Understanding each of these interconnected impacts from the women’s perspectives is central to compassionate and embodied approaches to care that address the biopsychosocial consequences of transvaginal mesh injury.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first systematic review seeking to understand the sexual and relational impacts women experience as a result of transvaginal mesh injury. The inclusion of a reflexive statement allows the reader to position the research in the context of the author’s approach and positionality, increasing transparency. Whilst this does not eliminate any potential bias in the development and construction of the coding and themes, readers are able to assess the strengths and limitations of the author's interpretation of the findings.

Several limitations need to be acknowledged in this study, and thus, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Screening and data extraction were primarily conducted by a single reviewer, increasing the risk of selection bias or interpretive bias. Supervisory input included appraisal of a subset of papers and oversight of coding decisions and themes; however, some risk of bias remains.

The review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, providing consistency and confidence in the reporting of the findings. Searching five databases allowed for broad inclusion of literature across a range of disciplines. The inclusion of grey literature supported minimisation of publication bias. The limited number of studies which report of sexual and relational outcomes for mesh-injured women creates a limitation in the conclusions that can be drawn, notwithstanding that the reports included in the review were not inherently focusing on sexual and relational outcomes after mesh injury.

The methodological quality of the studies included in the review was generally high. However, the aim of the studies was to broadly captures women’s experiences of transvaginal mesh injury as opposed to understanding the sexual and relational impacts, meaning important nuances may not have been captured and therefore not included in the discussion. The study samples were largely homogenous (age, gender, ethnicity), which limits the applicability to women not represented in the current research.

The data reported in the review is weighted towards complications following SUI and mid-urethral sling (MUS) procedures, with a smaller proportion representing pelvic organ prolapse POP. Additionally, inquiry data did not specify procedure type by submission. Whilst this limits the ability to differentiate experiences by procedure type, among women who reported harm, similar impacts within sexual and relational domains were consistently described across studies.

Details regarding the timing, severity and type of mesh injury were inconsistently reported across studies and inquiries. Due to this, these characteristics could not be meaningfully synthesised within the scope of this review. Additionally, baseline sexual functioning, pain and relationship status were not reported. Many participants attributed these changes to post-operative mesh complications, however, due to lack of baseline data it is not possible to determine the extent to which these experiences reflect new symptoms or exacerbation of pre-existing symptoms related to POP or SUI. The findings should therefore be interpreted in light of this limitation.

Whilst the heterogeneity of the sample limits clinical specificity in terms of understanding the underlying mechanisms of pain, qualitative synthesis aimed at understanding women’s lived experiences provides valuable insight to patient reported outcomes. This is particularly important for a cohort of women who have described feeling dismissed by health professionals. Synthesising the accounts of women may help validate experiences that have historically been minimised or overlooked.

The samples were heterogeneous in terms of research field and discipline (theology, medical, psychology, sociology, political). Drawing from multiple types of knowledge production was considered a strength of the review and supports in depth exploration of the lived experience of women.

The government inquiries differed in recruitment and sampling strategies, and data collection methods. Submission type varied widely including written, oral, first-person accounts, third-party and advocate led. Whilst steps were taken to place weight on first person accounts, the data was less methodologically controlled than peer-reviewed studies included in the review, which may have affected comparability within the synthesis. Additionally, there may be self-selection and response bias when calls to submit evidence voluntarily, which may increase the risk of selection bias to those who have experienced significant complications. Due to variation in length and quality of submissions, there may be inconsistencies in the depth of experiential data extracted from the inquiries.

Whilst valuable, government reviews differ in purpose and framing. The reviews aimed to understand women’s experiences, they were also aiming to capture systematic failures and inform policy and practice as opposed to interpreting understanding of experiences in a theoretically informed framework.

The influence of Government Inquiry reports on thematic findings was examined to understand whether thematic structure was altered with their inclusion (Appendix 2; Table 8). When inquiry data was excluded, the theme Relational activity: changes in relational activity due to chronic pain was less supported, drawing upon only two qualitative studies. This indicates that inclusion of inquiry data strengthened this theme, capturing relational impacts that may have been underreported in the published qualitative literature. All other themes were supported after exclusion of inquiry data.

The adoption of thematic synthesis is a strength as the approach accommodates for the disciplinary variation and tension in the interpretation of the findings. Whilst there are limitations to the review, a number of strengths are also identified. The common themes identified across disciplines indicate the significance of the findings, thereby supporting the application of findings in a range of contexts, and enhancing the utility of the findings.

Implications for practice and future research

Outcomes that relate to women’s overall health are an important consideration and should be addressed by healthcare providers [48], and arguably medical device manufacturers. The findings of the review are important for clinicians providing care for mesh-injured women. For women presenting with mesh complications, timely care that places importance on sexual and relational outcomes may mitigate some of the sexual and relational harms experienced by women.

Future research specifically aimed at understanding transvaginal mesh injury on sexual functioning, sexuality and relational outcomes would be beneficial to further understand the needs of women in this group. Additionally, future research may seek to further understand how partner responses impact mesh-injured women’s experiences. This may provide opportunity to capture nuances that may have been missed in this review.

ACT has a developed evidence base for treatment of chronic pain. However, there is currently a general assumption that an ACT approach is applicable across pain types [62]. Psychological flexibility was identified as a potential protective mechanism for mesh-injured women in relation to continued engagement with values-based actions such as sexual and relational activities. Therefore, further research in relation to ACT as an approach for mesh-injured women requires interventional studies to understand the effectiveness of the approach for this client group. Furthermore, research that seeks to understand adjustment to pain and the adaptive and maladaptive coping mechanisms within couples may inform couples-based ACT approaches to pain management where mesh injury is present.
