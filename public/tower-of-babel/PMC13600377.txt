Assessing home environmental hazards for older adults receiving in-home supportive services: the healthy homes assessment tool
Authors: Steven M Albert, Jemima Ohwobete, Jenn McCartney, Meredith Hughes, Sarah Haig, James Fabisiak, Jonathan Pearlman, Dan Ding, A Everette James, Shannah Gilliam, Pamela Toto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesHome- and community-based services (HCBS) assessments often fail to include environmental health hazards that can be a barrier to aging in place. This study describes the development of a tool assessing two categories of environmental health hazards found in older homes—poor chemical and physical indoor air quality (IAQ) and moisture-related mold conditions—and the evaluation of the feasibility of integrating it into HCBS assessment workflows.Research Design and MethodsThe Healthy Home Assessment Tool (H-HAT) includes resident reports and HCBS care coordinator observations/reports of indoor air quality (IAQ) and moisture-related mold conditions, conducted in partnership with an area agency on aging. For preliminary pilot validation, H-HAT ratings were assessed relative to consumer-grade IAQ monitoring and mold analysis.ResultsThe sample included 41 low-income older adult households (median resident age: 76; 78% female; 31.7% African American). Care coordinators successfully fielded the tool, sharing results via standardized reports and proposing remediation strategies with residents. Mold odor was noted in 23% of residences, and water damage in 48.7%. Relative to current EPA standards, 61.0% had fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations above the recommended limit (9 µg/m³), and 21.9% had radon levels above the action level of 4 pCi/L. PM2.5 was significantly higher in residences with H-HAT reports of smoking and a higher fungal burden in households with visible mold/mildew.Discussion and ImplicationsH-HAT is feasible to deliver in partnership with care management agencies. Poor IAQ and moisture/mold conditions are common and may be relevant in designing care plans and improving health outcomes.

Assessments of the safety and accessibility of homes largely neglect environmental health hazards that can be a barrier to aging in place. Two such hazards are poor chemical and physical indoor air quality and moisture-related mold conditions. The Healthy Home Assessment Tool (H-HAT) addresses this gap by combining resident reports and assessor observations to identify these hazards, and by providing residence-specific results and low-cost remediation strategies. H-HAT shows promise as a practical tool for integrating hidden home hazard screening into preventive care planning for older adults.

Aging in place, the ability to remain safe and independent in one’s existing home and community, is a goal for most older adults (75% of people aged 50+ in a 2024 survey: AARP, 2024). The physical environment and structural characteristics of a home, such as steep stairs, can pose barriers to aging in place. But older adults may be unaware of less visible but common characteristics of their homes that may limit their ability to age in place. For example, in a 2023 representative survey of 800 older adults in Pennsylvania, where we conducted this research, 5.4% reported mold, 2.6% poor indoor air quality (IAQ), and 4.6% active water leaks (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 2024).

Poor IAQ, reflecting chemical or physical exposures, is associated with a number of negative health outcomes, including respiratory and cardiovascular disease (Abdul-Rahman et al., 2024; Behinaein et al., 2023; Raju et al., 2020; World Health Organization, 2024) and dementia (Wilker et al., 2023), and is more common among lower income populations with poor quality housing (DeCamp et al., 2024; Vesper et al., 2023). Yet IAQ is rarely assessed in programs designed to provide in-home support and promote aging in place. Indeed, poor IAQ, indicated by elevated levels of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon dioxode (CO2), radon, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), is common in older homes but largely absent from assessments used to determine eligibility and care plans for older people receiving home- and community-based services (HCBS) (Hughes & James, 2025).

Mold exposures, including species such as Aspergillus spp. and Penicillium spp., can exacerbate respiratory and other health conditions and carry a significant economic burden, with health costs related to mold exposure estimated at $3.5 billion annually (Fisk et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2017).

Despite these impacts, assessment of chemical and physical IAQ components and moisture-related mold conditions is not routinely included in HCBS eligibility determinations or care planning, even though both may be prevalent in older low-income housing and relevant to the health of the HCBS population. Because these hazards have distinct causal pathways, health implications, and remediation requirements, they are best understood as separate constructs that each warrant dedicated assessment within HCBS workflows.

Assessing environmental hazards may be relevant for effective care plans and aging in place if, for example, an older person has a respiratory condition. Would such an assessment lead an HCBS care coordinator to alter a care plan, for example? Would addressing these hazards through low-cost remediation promote aging in place?

As an initial step toward answering these questions, this research developed an indoor environmental hazards assessment protocol, the Healthy Home Assessment Tool (H-HAT), which includes a questionnaire and home observation completed by an HCBS care coordinator or community health worker, along with a report providing remediation strategies. We sought to determine if a brief, non-expert assessment of IAQ and mold/moisture conditions, added to HCBS assessments, may be useful in identifying remediable hazards.

Our major focus was the feasibility of incorporating this tool in the HCBS workflow, which included determining if care coordinators could administer the tool in routine assessments and their comfort in providing results from assessments to residents (Phase I). A subsidiary goal was a preliminary validation pilot of H-HAT ratings (Phase II). In the preliminary validation pilot, we assessed the concordance of H-HAT questionnaire items with environmental sensor measures. Figure 1 shows the two phases of the research.

Method

Phase I: Developing the H-HAT protocol

In preparing the H-HAT, we first conducted a review of existing home environmental assessment tools used in HCBS settings (Hughes & James, 2025). We then developed a tool focusing on remediable hazards likely to be consequential for the health of older adults that could be administered by a care coordinator or community health worker without training in environmental health. We narrowed exposures to PM2.5, CO2, radon, and mold. We reasoned that other hazards (e.g., lead) were less likely to be relevant for old age health or were not easily remediable in the HCBS population because of expense. (Some pollutants, such as volatile organic compounds, also lack established thresholds for clinical significance; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025a.) We included only limited information on the built environment. Distinguishing masonry vs timber frame construction, for example, or assessing insulation retrofits, requires expertise beyond what can be expected of a care assessor. In developing the H-HAT tool, we sought a middle ground between eliciting environmental information useful for care planning and information that a care coordinator can reasonably expect to record with limited time in a residence. We received feedback on the tool in a series of focus groups conducted with care coordinators and community health workers who provide HCBS, as well as a community stakeholder board.

Review of home environmental assessment tools used in HCBS

We examined 125 federal and state HCBS assessment instruments (Hughes & James, 2025). We focused on tools that utilized visual inspection, as the original intent of H-HAT was to supplement simple visual inspection and questioning with technology (portable monitors and environmental sampling). We coded questions into heating and cooling, electrical or gas hazards, unsanitary conditions and clutter, pest infestations, fire safety and smoke alarms, mold/moisture, structure and home furnishings, lighting, ingress and egress, appliances, utilities, plumbing and water, security and safety. Only 10% of the 125 tools included specific language related to environmental hazard assessment. Of these, nearly half included assessment questions limited to signs of a pest infestation, and slightly more than half asked questions related to fire safety or the presence of a functional smoke alarm. No assessment tools explicitly asked about the presence of mold.

Developing the H-HAT protocol to assess and remediate environmental hazards

We drew from these tools in developing the H-HAT questionnaire. Measures were adapted from the Minimum Data Set (MDS), InterRAI-Home Care questionnaires, and federal and state assessment tools (including CDC and HUD measures), as detailed in Supplementary Material Section 1. The final questionnaire includes 23 items: general information on the built environment (3), utilities (3), sources of poor IAQ (exhaust [3], pest infestation [3], pets [1]), radon (1), detection of safety hazards (2), behavioral factors (smoking, plants [4]), and mold moisture (3). Additional questions are triggered when mold is observed or a pet is reported (Supplementary Material Section 2).

Healthy homes assessment tool questionnaires were completed by a research assistant (development phase) or by HCBS providers (feasibility-validation phase) in a Qualtrics survey on a smartphone or laptop. The questionnaire can be administered by a care coordinator or community health care worker in approximately 15 minutes. For the preliminary validation effort, we combined H-HAT with a test kit for mold and environmental sensors placed in the home to assess indoor CO2, PM2.5, and radon.

IAQ monitoring

Indoor air quality was monitored continuously over a two-week period in each home using the Airthings View Plus (https://www.airthings.com/view-plus) (Airthings, n.d.a.). Parameters measured included temperature, relative humidity, fine PM2.5, and CO2 (Mendell et al., 2024). A mobile hotspot was used to enable data transmission. Data were stored locally on the device or transmitted to a manufacturer-developed server.

To ensure measurements reflected typical occupant exposure, monitors were placed in the primary living area used most frequently by residents, which was most often the living room. Device placement was standardized by positioning monitors near the center of the room. When placement against a wall was necessary, devices were located near the midpoint of the wall and away from corners to promote adequate airflow to the sensors.

Particulate matter (PM) is classified based on aerodynamic diameter, with particles ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) of particular concern due to their association with respiratory and neurological health. PM2.5 can penetrate deep into the lungs, cross the alveolar–capillary barrier, and initiate inflammatory and oxidative stress responses. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has established a health-based ambient (outdoor) PM2.5 threshold of 9 µg/m³ (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2026). In the absence of established indoor PM2.5 standards, this threshold was used as an indicator of poor IAQ in this study.

Evidence for indoor CO2 thresholds remains limited; however, 1,000 ppm is the most commonly cited guideline in the literature (National Research Council, 1999) and was used as a reference value.

Radon concentrations were measured using the Airthings Corentium Home Radon Detector (https://www.airthings.com/home) (Airthings, n.d.b). The EPA action level for radon is 4 pCi/L (50 Bq/m³), and values exceeding this threshold were considered elevated (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2021, 2025b).

Mold analysis

Dust samples were collected using electrostatic cloths (Swiffer method) following established guidance described in the literature for EPA’s Environmental Relative Moldiness Index (ERMI). A dry Swiffer cloth was used to collect settled dust from multiple horizontal surfaces in the living room and bedroom. Samples were then shipped to the accredited laboratory EnvironBiomics for ERMI analysis (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2021). The ERMI metric integrates concentrations of 26 “water-damage–associated molds” and 10 “common indoor molds” into a single index score. Scores greater than 5 correspond to the highest quartile of mold burden based on the U.S. American Healthy Homes Survey (https://www.envirobiomics.com/ermi-testing-a-comprehensive-guide-to-environmental-mold-testing).

Focus group and community stakeholder feedback on H-HAT

The H-HAT questionnaire was reviewed for development and delivery by (i) intended users, that is, care coordinators and community health workers who arrange in-home services for people with disabilities (n = 15), in a series of three in-person focus groups as well as in individual interviews after use of the tool; (ii) an expert panel of clinicians, environmental health scientists, health services researchers, and public health scientists (n = 12) who met biweekly over the course of the project; and (iii) an external stakeholder panel of community environmental advocates, consumers of in-home services, and human services providers (n = 5), who met at project kickoff, mid-term, and completion. Based on this review, the H-HAT questionnaire was iteratively refined, with no items dropped but new ones added. Items added to H-HAT from this review included the inclusion of a radon assessment (community stakeholder panel) and information on passive smoking and pets (care coordinators and community health workers).

Representative questions from focus groups and interviews with care coordinators included the perceived challenge of including H-HAT in the HCBS workflow (e.g., “How difficult would it be to incorporate H-HAT into HCBS assessments?,” “Do you feel comfortable making a visual inspection of homes?,” “Would you feel comfortable discussing results with residents?”), and the perceived value of the tool (e.g., “If H-HAT shows indoor air quality is bad, do you think this will be helpful in your assessments?,” “If we give you a result and it shows poor IAQ, would you do anything different in care planning for a resident?,” “Do you think the questions we posed are good ones for assessing indoor air quality?”). These questions raised valuable insights, such as the difficulty of rating mold odor. Sessions were audiotaped, transcribed, and coded for analysis and will be the subject of a separate analysis.

Phase II: H-HAT feasibility-validation pilot

After developing the H-HAT, a research assistant accompanied care coordinators conducting standard HCBS assessments and piloted the tool. We then trained care coordinators to administer the tool in a second set of resident assessments. We correlated H-HAT questionnaire responses with environmental sensor data to assess the validity of observer and self-reported ratings. Finally, we developed a standardized report form for care coordinators to share H-HAT results with residents and interviewed them about their experience discussing the results of the assessment.

H-HAT field assessment

We worked with the Area Agency on Aging, Allegheny County Department of Human Services in Pennsylvania, and its three subcontracted partners who deliver the OPTIONS program to identify potential participants. OPTIONS is a state HCBS program that provides services to older adults who are ineligible for Medicaid long-term care services because of incomes just above the Medicaid limit but who meet criteria for “nursing facility clinically eligible.”

The Allegheny County Area Agency on Aging circulated our study information sheet to staff from the three agencies. The agencies assigned staff to implement H-HAT in residents’ homes. Staff contacted residents scheduled for semiannual assessments, explained the study (using an approved script), and obtained consent for the research team to make contact. The research assistant then explained the study in more detail and obtained consent to accompany the care manager for the assessment. Signed consent was obtained during the visit. The University of Pittsburgh Human Research Protections Office reviewed and approved this research, including the protocol and format of reports to residents. Residents received an air purifier two weeks after the H-HAT assessment (when we retrieved the Airthings View Plus) as a token of thanks for their participation.

Validation of H-HAT questionnaire items

For validating H-HAT questionnaire items, we conducted IAQ monitoring and collected a dust sample for fungal analysis (Healthy Home Assessment Tool (H-HAT): Manual of Administration, 2025). Measures of PM2.5, CO2, radon, and fungal burden were correlated with relevant questionnaire items. Summary statistics for the H-HAT sample were calculated for PM2.5, CO2, radon, and ERMI values. Values were assessed relative to EPA guidelines when available. To validate the H-HAT items, we compared IAQ and mold values in groups high and low in risk factors identified in the H-HAT questionnaire. These involved simple t-tests or rank-sum tests for means and x2 tests for proportions.

We did not assess H-HAT questionnaire items relative to radon because radon is less likely to be associated with resident behavior in the home. Radon is a naturally occurring hazard found in the bedrock of some areas in Pennsylvania. Average indoor radon concentrations in PA are estimated to range from 7 to 8 pCi/L (Li et al., 2025).

Reporting H-HAT results to residents

A sample communication packet reporting results of the H-HAT assessment for residents is shown in Supplementary material Section 3. Care coordinators used these packets, which report results for each household along with remediation suggestions, in follow-up meetings with residents. We tested the packet for reading level (6th grade) and accessibility of graphics. Our community advisory board reviewed it and made suggestions on the display of numeric values, which we incorporated into the final version.

Supplementary material Section 3 provides detailed information on remediation strategies and when to escalate according to the severity of environmental hazards. Remediation guidance is available for PM2.5, radon, and indoor climate-mold. For example, six strategies are presented to residents for humidity and nine for mold. The remediation strategies are quite detailed and are presented in order of expected price burden. For example, strategies for addressing mold include “open windows and improve ventilation,” “check for hidden mold,” “clean with vinegar or baking soda,” “use fans to help dry areas,” “use moisture absorbers,” “clean AC units,” “use a dehumidifier,” and “fix leaky pipes or roof.” Residents are told to seek professional remediation services if problems persist.

Results

Participation of residents, care managers, and agencies

Between February 20, 2024 and December 4, 2024, 41 older adult households completed the H-HAT protocol. These were 80.4% (41 out of 51) of households approached for the research. Non-participants were either unreachable or canceled assessments after giving telephone consent. All three agencies contributed to the sample (n = 9, n = 16, n = 16), and participation rates were similar across agencies. A total of 16 agency care coordinators were involved in H-HAT assessments, though 2–3 in each agency were most involved. The majority of homes were sampled in the summer.

Demography of residents

In this sample of low-income older adult households, the median age of residents was seventy-six, 78% were female, and 31.7% were African American. Two-thirds of homes had a single resident; 31.7% reported a smoker or someone who vapes in the household, and another 24% reported regular visitors who smoke or vape.

Features of home environments

Residences were built at a median of 74 years ago, and 30% had at least one room without windows; 62.8% were single-family homes, and 72.1% had brick or masonry exteriors. We were able to examine basements in two-thirds of dwellings. Half of the residences were in areas outside Pittsburgh proper; 73.2% had forced air heating, often supplemented with space heaters; 46.3% had central air-conditioning, and the remainder used window AC units; 61.5% used gas stoves, and only 20.8% were vented with an exhaust fan.

A little over a third of households had pets, and 41.5% had indoor plants. About a third reported vermin in the residence in the past year, and a quarter used an exterminator for pest control.

Smoke detectors were common (70.7%), but radon monitors were less so (43.6%). Among homes with smoke detectors, a third of residents reported changing batteries annually and another third only when alerted.

On observation, 22% of residences had a detectable mold odor, 48.8% were observed to have damage from water or moisture, and 22% had visible mold or mildew. Of the 20 residences with visible mold or mildew, more than two-thirds involved areas bigger than a sheet of paper, and 15% involved areas larger than a door.

Acceptability and usability of H-HAT

Agency participants in the final focus group included care coordinators and their supervisors, nine in total. All supported adding H-HAT to the resident assessment protocol. If anything, care coordinators suggested additional exposures to be assessed, such as carbon monoxide (CO). They reported that residents welcomed this inquiry into the health of their homes. Even an initial discussion based on H-HAT was enough to motivate some residents. For example, as reported by care coordinators, one obtained an air conditioner, and one recognized the need to open windows more frequently. Some of the care coordinators were leery of questions they might receive from concerned residents and recommended a hotline for residents seeking more information. They were pleasantly surprised by how easy it was to identify and rate levels of mold and moisture. They brought to our attention challenges to our suggested remediation, such as the inability of some residents to scrub walls or empty a dehumidifier.

Comments from care coordinators after presenting H-HAT results to residents were mostly positive. They reported that the results package was accessible to residents and the remediation resources were helpful. The Allegheny County Area Agency on Aging was able to provide additional resources for remediation in some cases. The assessments themselves may have had some benefit in promoting healthier behaviors. For example, one care coordinator reported the following after review of H-HAT findings with a resident: “The biggest thing we talked about was her PM2.5 being in the high range, and I said that number is probably a lot lower now since she is not smoking in the home and using the air purifier. She also said she would be interested in measuring [IAQ] again.”

Discussion

This preliminary research suggests that a simple questionnaire can be incorporated into service assessments for people receiving home and community-based services and may be effective in identifying potentially remediable home environmental hazards. Ratings of mold/moisture were associated with higher fungal burden assessed in DNA analysis of dust, but caution is appropriate given the trend findings only, the small sample, and the limited observation period. Likewise, reports of smoking were associated with higher PM2.5 concentrations. With assessment of a larger sample, which is currently planned, we will determine if it is possible to establish thresholds for observed ratings and questionnaire responses that predict sensor IAQ ratings and fungal burden above recommended thresholds.

We developed the H-HAT protocol to be brief enough that it could be added to an already lengthy assessment and straightforward enough that an HCBS care coordinator or community health worker could incorporate it into their workflow. Our pilot showed that (i) chemical, physical, and microbial conditions with potential health significance are common in these homes, (ii) non-expert HCBS staff can incorporate survey questions and observations into their workflow to identify potentially important IAQ and mold/moisture conditions, and (iii) HCBS assessors are comfortable communicating this information to residents, who appear to welcome information on remediation.

Although professional expertise is needed for definitive identification and remediation of hazards, H-HAT is useful as a first step in identifying likely indicators of poor IAQ and mold/moisture conditions that are potentially remediable when identified and linked to appropriate resources. Professional expertise, provided by building pathologists, certified home inspectors, and environmental health officers, is required to verify findings and guide remediation outside the immediate context of HCBS assessment. Our focus was the policy (and data) gap: environmental health hazards in the HCBS setting are currently mostly unmeasured and not addressed. An important future validation effort for H-HAT would involve paired assessments from both a care coordinator and a trained building assessor to determine concordance in identifying hazards.

Through our partnership with Pennsylvania OPTIONS providers, we were able to train HCBS staff to administer the H-HAT questionnaire and explain results to residents. They found the assessment helpful for care planning, as it supported many of their recommendations (e.g., smoking cessation). They also endorsed the effort as a way to provide residents with concrete guidance on remediation.

Residents appear to welcome home environmental hazard assessment. They found our communication of results accessible and welcomed guidance on remediation. Our initial concerns about the potential intrusiveness of these assessments and the fear associated with anything “fungal” appear to have been misplaced. Residents felt empowered to learn about health hazards in homes and recognized the need to address them. Our low-cost remediation strategies will allow most residents to address these hazards, but our local Area Agency on Aging has also agreed to investigate larger remediation challenges.

The H-HAT identified high levels of observed mold/moisture, elevated PM2.5 concentrations, and radon levels above recommended action levels in this sample of older homes. ERMI values were also elevated, though these results warrant careful interpretation. Several factors likely contribute, including seasonal sampling bias: approximately 70% of samples were collected during spring and summer months, when indoor fungal burden is known to be higher due to peak outdoor spore release. Summer sampling conditions also affect IAQ sensor readings more broadly, as ventilation behavior during warm months affects all IAQ sensor readings (Ohwobete et al., 2026a).

The very high ERMI values we detected may also reflect regional ecology. ITS amplicon sequencing data show that the fungal communities in these Pittsburgh homes are dominated by Ascomycota, the same phylum that accounts for approximately 80%–85% of the ERMI panel species (Ohwobete et al., 2026b). Because ERMI is taxonomically weighted toward Ascomycota, which are ecologically prevalent in humid, older housing environments such as those in western Pennsylvania, ERMI values in this region are likely inflated relative to the national reference.

ERMI values also vary within the same home over time and across environmental conditions (Ohwobete et al., 2026a). This suggests that ERMI may capture more dynamic and context-dependent aspects of indoor fungal ecology than originally intended, extending beyond the simple distinction between water-damage-associated and common indoor molds. Together, these findings indicate that the interpretation of ERMI remains an evolving area.

We therefore frame the null findings regarding observed mold and ERMI values not as a failure of the observational tool, but rather as an important contribution to ongoing efforts to better understand what ERMI represents across different residential, seasonal, and regional contexts. Given that Pittsburgh’s aging housing stock and humid continental climate are representative of many regions in the northeastern United States and beyond, this study provides useful context for that broader calibration effort.

Linking H-HAT measures of the home environment to features of participants (health status) and residences (time spent in the dwelling and length of residence) would be extremely valuable and is the ultimate goal of our research. The focus of this initial research was to develop the H-HAT protocol and assess its feasibility in a real-world delivery setting to add to the environmental gerontology toolkit.

In this research, we posed a number of questions. Is home environmental hazard assessment acceptable to older residents receiving supportive care in their homes? We saw high participation in the study, comfort with the assessment process, and high interest in the results of assessments and remediation strategies. Can H-HAT be incorporated into the workflow of a busy care manager? Care coordinators were able to add the H-HAT questionnaire to their regular assessments and found it helpful for discussing health issues in residents.

Some questions remain open. Will knowledge of home environmental health hazards lead an HCBS care coordinator to alter a care plan, as might be necessary in the case of a respiratory condition? Will addressing these hazards through low-cost remediation promote aging in place? Answers to these questions will be useful in supporting efforts currently underway to promote aging in place.

Assessment of environmental hazards in HCBS programs must also include services to remediate hazards. Documentation of environmental hazards in the home is more likely to prompt changes to a care plan if a corresponding remediation service or benefit is available to add to that care plan. Private entities providing medicare advantage (MA) and medicaid managed long-term services and supports (MLTSS) have some flexibility to offer coverage for a wider range of health-related social needs, and some MA and MLTSS plans offer benefits such as asthma remediation and IAQ equipment (Hughes & James, 2025). States also have flexibility to offer environmental hazard remediation in Medicaid, and a small number of states have used that flexibility to provide pest eradication, mold remediation, and indoor air filters or air conditioners (Hughes & James, 2025). H-HAT may be a useful tool for guiding efforts to enhance environmental hazard assessment, which can inform the design and availability of environmental hazard remediation in HCBS programs.

Finally, this research suggests that home environmental hazards should be considered an additional environmental resource or risk relevant for person–environment transactions (Iwarsson et al., 2007; Wahl et al., 2012). This perspective places chemical and physical IAQ and mold/moisture conditions alongside more traditional factors, such as barrier-free housing or innovative housing solutions, as resources that may affect the person–environment fit. The H-HAT protocol expands the range of environmental resources typically considered in promoting health in old age.

The research should be interpreted in light of its small sample and exploratory nature. The small sample prevents multivariate analyses. However, we limited the sample to people eligible for a particular HCBS program, which may help strengthen reported findings. Also, only three agencies and a limited number of care coordinators participated in the study. It is unclear if this sample of residents is representative of the agency census overall. Finally, longitudinal follow-up is required to determine if H-HAT assessment leads to greater remediation efforts and improved health in this vulnerable population.
