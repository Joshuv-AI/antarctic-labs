Optimal Hardness of Online Algorithms for Large Common Induced Subgraphs
David Gamarnik, Miklós Z. Rácz, Gabe Schoenbach

Abstract
We study the problem of efficiently finding large common induced subgraphs of two independent Erdős–Rényi random graphs G 1 , G 2 ∼ 𝔾 ⁡ ( n , 1 / 2 ) G_{1},G_{2}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2) . Recently, Chatterjee and Diaconis [ 2 ] showed that the largest common induced subgraph of G 1 G_{1} and G 2 G_{2} has size ( 4 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ log 2 ​ n (4-o(1))\log_{2}n with high probability. We first show that a simple greedy online algorithm finds a common induced subgraph of G 1 G_{1} and G 2 G_{2} of size ( 2 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ log 2 ​ n (2-o(1))\log_{2}n with high probability. Our main result shows that no online algorithm can find a common induced subgraph of G 1 G_{1} and G 2 G_{2} of size at least ( 2 + ε ) ​ log 2 ​ n (2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n with probability bounded away from 0 0 as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Together, these results provide evidence that this problem exhibits a computation-to-optimization gap. To prove the impossibility result, we show that the solution space of the problem exhibits a version of the (multi) overlap gap property (OGP), and utilize an interpolation argument recently developed by Gamarnik, Kızıldağ, and Warnke [ 10 ] that connects OGP and online algorithms.

In the

\mathsf{LCIS}

is the number of vertices of the common subgraph and the goal is to maximize this quantity.

The
\mathsf{LCIS}

In this paper we study arguably the simplest and most natural average-case version of the
\mathsf{LCIS}

Recently, Chatterjee and Diaconis

In this work, we initiate the study of the algorithmic side of the

What are the limits of efficient algorithms that solve

In particular, we focus on a broad class of online algorithms, which arise naturally for this problem.
We first show that a simple greedy online algorithm finds a solution of size at least

Theorem 1

(Algorithms, informal)
.

There exists an efficient online algorithm that finds a solution to
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
that
has size at least
(2-o(1))\log_{2}n
with high probability as
n\to\infty
.

Our main result shows that no online algorithm can do better than half-optimal.
The class of online algorithms we consider is broad; roughly, it is defined as follows.
An online algorithm for
\mathsf{LCIS}
processes the two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
one vertex pair
(u_{t},v_{t})\in V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
at a time. When a vertex pair is processed, all connections of
u_{t}
and
v_{t}
to previously processed vertices are revealed (in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively). The algorithm then has to make a decision whether or not to add a pair of vertices to the partial solution it has built so far. The main constraints are that both of the added vertices must have been already processed and
at least
one of them must be
u_{t}
or
v_{t}
.

Notably, an online algorithm does
not
have to add both
u_{t}
and
v_{t}
at the same time, and as a result, it is possible for an individual vertex to be added to the solution well after it was originally processed.
This is a fundamental difference compared to online algorithms for problems involving a single graph, where usually an immediate decision must be made about a vertex as soon as it is processed.

Overall, these results combined provide evidence that the

1.1
A half-optimal greedy online algorithm

A naive brute-force approach to finding a size-
k
solution to
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
would require checking all pairs of size-
k
subgraphs of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. For
k=\Theta(\log n)
, such an exhaustive approach would require superpolynomial runtime, since

\binom{n}{k}^{2}\geq(n/k)^{2k}\geq n^{\Omega(\log n)}
.

To get a polynomial-time algorithm, a useful initial observation is that independent sets of equal size are isomorphic, so any algorithm that finds independent sets can also solve the
\mathsf{LCIS}
problem (and the same applies to cliques).
The largest independent set in
G\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)
has size
(2-o(1))\log_{2}n
with high probability
and a simple greedy online algorithm finds an independent set of size
(1+o(1))\log_{2}n

[
14
,
18
]
.
So this immediately gives an efficient quarter-optimal solution to
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
: use a greedy algorithm to find independent sets of size
(1+o(1))\log_{2}n
in both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
However, there is no known efficient algorithm to find an independent set of size
(1+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
in
G\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)
— indeed, it is conjectured that such an algorithm does not exist — so it is not immediately clear how to go beyond this.

The key idea is to tailor the greedy algorithm for finding an independent set to the
\mathsf{LCIS}
problem.
Recall first the algorithm for finding an independent set in a single graph
G

[
14
,
18
]
,
which processes the vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
of
G
in an arbitrary order. It initializes the solution set
S
to be empty and proceeds greedily, adding
u_{t}
to
S
if and only if
u_{t}
is not connected to any of the vertices in
S
.
After processing all vertices, it outputs the induced subgraph
G[S]
, which is an independent set.

Turning to the
\mathsf{LCIS}
problem, the input is now two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
and

v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be arbitrary vertex orderings of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively,
and initialize the pair of induced subgraphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
to be empty.
The greedy algorithm processes vertex pairs
(u_{t},v_{t})
one at a time and does the following at time
t
.
First, all connections of
u_{t}
and
v_{t}
to previously processed vertices are revealed (in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively).
Then, for all
j\leq t
, do the following:

•

if
u_{t}
and
v_{j}
can be added to the subgraphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, respectively, while preserving
H_{1}\cong H_{2}
, then do so;

•

otherwise, perform the same check for
u_{j}
and
v_{t}
.

Note that once a vertex pair is added to
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
at time

the algorithm moves on to time
t+1
.
Once all vertices are processed, the algorithm returns
(H_{1},H_{2})
and a bijection
\pi:V(H_{1})\to V(H_{2})

that verifies
H_{1}\cong H_{2}
.

4

1.2
The limits of online algorithms

Our impossibility result rules out a certain broad class of online algorithms, which includes the
Greedy
algorithm described in Algorithm
1
. Instead of receiving the input all at once, these algorithms receive bits of the input in serial and
make irrevocable decisions about the output at each step
. It is this irrevocability property that constrains online algorithms; otherwise, the serial processing of the input is moot. Unlike the closely related independent set problem on a single graph, the input to
\mathsf{LCIS}
consists of
two
graphs, so there are several distinct notions of how information about the pair
(G_{1},G_{2})
should be processed by an online algorithm. What ought to be the atomic object?

We propose the following natural definition. At a high level, at each step, an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
will process a
pair
of vertices
(v_{1},v_{2})\in V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
and decide whether or not to add a (potentially distinct) pair of vertices to the partial solution, under the constraint that both of the added vertices must have been already processed, and
at least
one of them must be
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
. As a result, it is possible for an individual vertex to be added to the solution after it was processed. As highlighted previously, this is a fundamental difference compared to the definition of online algorithms for independent set
[
10
]
, and is due to the two-dimensional nature of the
\mathsf{LCIS}
problem. Our formal definition is below.

Definition 1

(Online algorithms for
\mathsf{LCIS}
)
.

The class
\mathsf{ONLINE}(n)
consists of algorithms
\mathcal{A}
which take as input
(G_{1},G_{2})
and a random string
\omega\in\Omega
, where for each
j\in\{1,2\}
,
G_{j}=(V_{j},E_{j})
is a graph with
V_{j}=[n]
. Each
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{ONLINE}(n)
proceeds in rounds, and at the end of round
t\in[n]
,
\mathcal{A}
maintains the following state:

•

The sets of vertices
P^{(t)}_{1}\subseteq V_{1}
and
P^{(t)}_{2}\subseteq V_{2}
inspected thus far.

•

Vertex sets
S^{(t)}_{1}\subseteq P^{(t)}_{1}
and
S^{(t)}_{2}\subseteq P^{(t)}_{2}
that induce a pair of isomorphic subgraphs. We write

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(t)}(G_{1},G_{2},\omega):=\left(G_{1}\left[S^{(t)}_{1}\right],G_{2}\left[S^{(t)}_{2}\right]\right)

(1)

for the (partial) solution at the end of round
t\in[n]
.

\mathcal{A}
initializes
P^{(0)}_{1}=P^{(0)}_{2}=S^{(0)}_{1}=S^{(0)}_{2}=\emptyset
. During each round
t\in[n]
,
\mathcal{A}
proceeds as follows, where for all
\{u,v\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
,
e_{j}(\{u,v\})\in\{0,1\}
denotes the edge status of the vertex pair
\{u,v\}
in graph
G_{j}
.

1.

(Processing a new vertex pair.)

Based on
\omega
and all of the information revealed through the end of round
t-1

(namely,
P_{1}^{(t-1)}
,
P_{2}^{(t-1)}
,
G_{1}\left[P_{1}^{(t-1)}\right]
,
G_{2}\left[P_{2}^{(t-1)}\right]
,
S_{1}^{(t-1)}
, and
S_{2}^{(t-1)}
),
for
j\in\{1,2\}
,
the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
selects
v^{(t)}_{j}\in V_{j}\setminus P^{(t-1)}_{j}
and defines
P^{(t)}_{j}:=P^{(t-1)}_{j}\cup\{v^{(t)}_{j}\}
. It then reveals
e_{j}(\{u,v\})
for all not-yet-seen vertex pairs
\{u,v\}\in\binom{P^{(t)}_{j}}{2}
, for
j\in\{1,2\}
.

2.

(Updating the solution.)

Based on
\omega
and all of the information revealed thus far,
\mathcal{A}
chooses how to update the vertex sets as

\displaystyle\left(S^{(t)}_{1},S^{(t)}_{2}\right)\in\begin{cases}\left(S^{(t-1)}_{1},S^{(t-1)}_{2}\right)\\
\left(S^{(t-1)}_{1}\cup\{v_{1}\},S^{(t-1)}_{2}\cup\{v_{2}\}\right),\end{cases}

(2)

under the constraint that
v_{j}\in P^{(t)}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
, and
either
v_{1}=v_{1}^{(t)}

or
v_{2}=v_{2}^{(t)}

(or both).
Moreover, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
must maintain that
G_{1}\left[S^{(t)}_{1}\right]\cong G_{2}\left[S^{(t)}_{2}\right]
.

We write the output of
\mathcal{A}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(G_{1},G_{2},\omega):=\mathcal{A}^{(n)}(G_{1},G_{2},\omega)=\left(G_{1}\left[S^{(n)}_{1}\right],G_{2}\left[S^{(n)}_{2}\right]\right).

(3)

We write
|\mathcal{A}(G_{1},G_{2},\omega)|:=\left|S^{(n)}_{1}\right|=\left|S^{(n)}_{2}\right|
to denote the size of the output.

Aside from the two-dimensional nature of
\mathsf{LCIS}
, Definition
1
closely tracks
[
10
, Definition 2.1]
. One difference is that the latter definition includes algorithms that can choose a set of
exceptional edges
, which allows for better-informed decisions at each step. We believe that Definition
1
could be similarly strengthened to include exceptional information without affecting our results (assuming an appropriate bound on the amount of exceptional information), but we choose not to for simplicity.

Our main result is the following, which rules out online algorithms beyond
2\log_{2}n
.

Theorem 4

(Impossibility, formal)
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
, let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{ONLINE}(n)
, and fix any
\varepsilon>0
. For all
\omega\in\Omega
and all sufficiently large
n
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{A}(G_{1},G_{2},\omega)|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n\right)\leq n^{-\Omega(\log n)}.

1.3
Related work

Existential results for
\mathsf{LCIS}
.

The
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
problem was first studied by Chatterjee and Diaconis
[
2
]
, who showed a precise two-point concentration result:
with high probability the largest common induced subgraph has size

\left\lfloor x_{n}-\varepsilon\right\rfloor

or

\left\lfloor x_{n}+\varepsilon\right\rfloor
,
where

x_{n}
satisfies

x_{n}=(4-o(1))\log_{2}n
.
Subsequently, Surya, Warnke, and Zhu
[
21
]
proved a more general result
for arbitrary edge densities
p_{1},p_{2}\in(0,1)^{2}
in the two graphs,
with three qualitatively different regimes of
p_{1},p_{2}\in(0,1)^{2}
.
Diamantidis, Konstantopoulos, and Yuan
[
4
]
used a different proof technique to show the same result, albeit for a slightly restricted set of edge densities.

Independent set.

\mathsf{LCIS}
is closely related to the much-studied problem of finding large independent sets. In the past decade a line of work has focused on giving evidence of the computational hardness of finding large independent sets in sparse random graphs
G\sim\mathbb{G}(n,d/n)
. Gamarnik and Sudan
[
11
]
showed that no local algorithms can find independent sets with size above some multiplicative factor
\beta
of optimal, for
\beta=1/2+1/(2\sqrt{2})\approx 0.85
. Rahman and Virág
[
19
]
strengthened this result to show that local algorithms cannot produce independent sets larger than half-optimal size, which is tight. Soon after, Wein
[
22
]
(building off of
[
7
]
) strengthened this result by extending the class of implicated algorithms to stable ones, which includes low-degree polynomials. Observing that a natural half-optimal approach
[
14
,
18
]
is online (but not stable), Gamarnik, Kızıldağ, and Warnke
[
10
]
proved that a natural class of online algorithms cannot beat half-optimality.

Overlap gap properties.

All of the computational hardness results above rely on proving that the independent set problem exhibits a type of
overlap gap property
(OGP). At a high level, an OGP is a statement about the geometry of the solution space; it stipulates the non-existence of tuples of large solutions with a particular overlap pattern. Proving more sophisticated OGPs (for instance, allowing solutions to come from an “ensemble” of problem instances rather than a single one, or considering
m
-tuples of solutions rather than pairs) have allowed for tighter hardness results. For instance, Wein
[
22
]
constructs an “interpolation path” of correlated random graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{T}
. The author argues by contradiction that, given a stable algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that outputs large solutions, one could run
\mathcal{A}
on each
G_{i}
and construct an
m
-tuple of solutions that violates an ensemble
m
-OGP.

Recently, Gamarnik, Kızıldağ, and Warnke
[
10
]
introduced a novel interpolation argument, building an interpolation path of correlated inputs that are defined in reference to any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
in a class of online algorithms that they define. This differs from the above OGP hardness results in that the non-existent structure is not of a tuple of solutions to the problem, but rather of a tuple of solutions
created by a particular type of algorithm
. As a result, the hardness proof is coextensive with the proof of the OGP, rather than coming afterward in, say, a proof by contradiction.
This approach has several advantages, including that it establishes
strong hardness
(i.e., ruling out algorithms succeeding even with only
o(1)
probability), something that was elusive in OGP-style arguments prior to the work of Huang and Sellke
[
15
]
.
Our impossibility proof utilizes this novel interpolation argument by
[
10
]
; our main new ingredient is to prove an OGP for
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
.

Computational hardness of random problems.

Our work fits into a broader landscape of using the overlap gap property to prove hardness results for random optimization problems. See
[
5
,
8
,
1
]
for further impossibility results for online algorithms. Other hardness results include
[
9
,
16
,
15
]
.
We note that such computation-to-optimization gaps have fundamental connections with statistical-computation gaps in planted problems which are widespread in high-dimensional learning.
We do not attempt to survey the literature here — see
[
12
,
13
]
for recent surveys and more.

1.4
Open problems

We leave open several natural questions for future work.

General dense case.

In this work we assumed that both graphs have edge density
1/2
. For arbitrary edge densities
(p_{1},p_{2})\in(0,1)^{2}
, Surya, Warnke, and Zhu
[
21
]

determined that the size of the largest common induced subgraph is roughly
C_{p_{1},p_{2}}\log_{2}n
, where interestingly the constant
C_{p_{1},p_{2}}
takes on qualitatively different forms in three different regimes of
(p_{1},p_{2})\in(0,1)^{2}
.
What can we say about efficient algorithms in this more general setting? Is greedy still half-optimal, and does the impossibility proof extend naturally to this setting?

Sparse case.

Can we find evidence of computational hardness for
\mathsf{LCIS}
given
G_{1},G_{2}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,d/n)
? Do these mirror analogous results for large independent sets in sparse graphs?

Other classes of algorithms.

Can we rule out other classes of algorithms? For instance, it would be natural to consider online algorithms with exceptional edge-pairs, as well as stable algorithms.

Many graphs.

What can we say about
\mathsf{LCIS}
for many graphs? A quick calculation suggests that a greedy algorithm remains half-optimal; it would be interesting to explore this setting further.

Average-case reduction from finding large independent sets.

As discussed above, the results that we obtained for
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
have similarities to those for finding large independent sets in
G\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)
; in particular, a greedy algorithm is half-optimal in both settings, and conjecturally there is no efficient algorithm that does better than half-optimal.
Is there a deeper connection between these two problems? In particular, is there an average-case reduction from finding large independent sets to finding large common induced subgraphs (or vice versa)?

One attempt could be to consider independent sets in
G\sim\mathbb{G}(n^{2},1/2)
, noting that the largest independent set in
G
has size roughly
2\log_{2}(n^{2})=4\log_{2}n
,
which is the same as the largest common induced subgraph in
(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
. Moreover, the
n^{2}
vertices of
G
have a natural correspondence with ordered pairs of vertices
(u,v)\in V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
.
It is not clear how to use these ideas to build a formal average-case reduction; doing so would be very interesting.

Proof ideas

2.1
Greedy algorithm

In Section
3
we prove Theorem
3
, that the algorithm
Greedy
from Algorithm
1
produces half-optimal solutions to
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
. Recall that
Greedy
builds up the common induced subgraph iteratively: at step
t\in[n]
,
Greedy
processes the vertex pair
(u_{t},v_{t})\in V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
. If there exists some already-processed vertex
v_{j}
(i.e., with
j\leq t
) such that
(u_{t},v_{j})
can be added to the partial solution, it does so — and similarly for any
(u_{j},v_{t})
.

Our goal is to give an upper bound on the probability that
Greedy
outputs a common induced subgraph of size less than
(2-\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
. We observe that if this happens, then there must be a window of
\widetilde{n}
successive steps where
Greedy
does not add to the partial solution, for

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}:=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2\log_{2}n}\right\rfloor.

In other words, over the course of the window,
Greedy
considers and rejects at least
\widetilde{n}^{2}
vertex pairs
(u,v)\in V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
. Let
1\leq k<(2-\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
be the size of the partial solution at the start of the window — we will eventually take a union bound over all possible values of
k
. If each pair
(u,v)
were independent, the probability of rejecting all such pairs would be at most

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right)^{\widetilde{n}^{2}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\widetilde{n}^{2}}{2^{k}}\right)<\exp(-\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{\varepsilon})),

which is small as desired.
Of course, the vertex pairs are not all independent; we handle this via a careful conditioning argument.

2.2
The limits of online algorithms

In Section
4
we prove Theorem
4
, that there is a vanishingly small probability that any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{ONLINE}(n)
can produce a solution to
\mathsf{LCIS}
that is better than half-optimal. We show in Lemma

that it suffices to consider deterministic algorithms. At a high level, we relate the probability that
\mathcal{A}
finds a large solution to the probability that
\mathcal{A}
finds a large solution within every input from a carefully-constructed set of correlated inputs, which will ensure that the solutions have substantial mutual overlap. Finally, we will use a combinatorial argument and the first moment method to show that such a set of solutions exists with vanishingly small probability.

In more detail, we say that a large solution

\mathsf{LCIS}(n)

.

We call these

that they exist with vanishing probability as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}.

In essence, Lemma
4
says that the
\mathsf{LCIS}

4

. To enable our analysis, we say that each
\sigma_{i}
has size
\alpha_{i}L
, for some

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\geq 2+\varepsilon.

Throughout the proof we use the standard upper bound
\binom{n}{k}\leq n^{k}
. We can then upper bound the number of such forbidden structures by

\displaystyle\left[\binom{n}{\lceil\gamma L\rceil}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\binom{n}{(\alpha_{i}-\gamma)L}\right]^{2}\leq n^{2L\left(\gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha_{i}-\gamma)\right)},

(5)

since the first term counts the number of choices for the set of common vertices of size
\lceil\gamma L\rceil
in each subgraph, and the second term counts the number of choices for the leftover
(\alpha_{i}-\gamma)L
vertices in each subgraph of each solution
\sigma_{i}
.

To analyze the probability that a given forbidden structure is in fact a set of solutions to
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
, we consider each
\sigma_{i}=(H_{i,1},H_{i,2})
, fix a bijection
\pi_{i}:V(H_{i,1})\to V(H_{i,2})
, and calculate the probability that
\pi_{i}
is a graph isomorphism, conditioned on the subgraph already seen by
\mathcal{A}
through step
t
. Ultimately, we take a union bound over all such permutations
\pi_{i}
, but since the subgraphs are of size
O(\log n)
, this contributes a multiplicative factor of only
(\log n)^{\log n}\approx n^{\log\log n}<n^{o(L)}
, which is negligible.
Since each
Y_{i}^{(t)}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
,
\pi_{i}
preserves adjacencies independently with probability
1/2
for every pair of vertex pairs
(u,u^{\prime})\in\binom{V(H_{i,1})}{2}
and
(\pi_{i}(u),\pi_{i}(u^{\prime}))\in\binom{V(H_{i,2})}{2}
, so long as the connectivity of at least one of the pairs has not been determined by our conditioning. There are at least
\binom{\alpha_{i}L}{2}-\binom{\lceil\gamma L\rceil}{2}
such pairs (this uses that
t\leq n-\Omega(\log n)
; the case when
t
is very close to
n
is simple to handle separately), so since each
Y_{i}^{(t)}
is conditionally independent, we can upper bound the probability that all
\sigma_{i}
are solutions by

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m}2^{-\left(\binom{\alpha_{i}L}{2}-\binom{\gamma L}{2}\right)}\approx 2^{-\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\right)}=n^{-\frac{L}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\right)},

(6)

where the
\approx
symbol means we have dropped lower-order terms in the exponent. Leveraging the linearity of expectation together with estimates (
5
) and (
6
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{m,t}]\leq n^{-\frac{L}{2}\left(-4\gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[(\alpha_{i}^{2}-4\alpha_{i})-(\gamma^{2}-4\gamma)\right]\right)}.

Moreover, since
\alpha_{i}^{2}-4\alpha_{i}\geq\varepsilon^{2}-4
for all
\alpha_{i}\geq 2+\varepsilon
and also
\gamma^{2}-4\gamma=\varepsilon^{2}/4-4
, we have that

\displaystyle-4\gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[(\alpha_{i}^{2}-4\alpha_{i})-(\gamma^{2}-4\gamma)\right]\geq-8+m\left(\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right).

So by choosing
m:=\left\lceil 40/(3\varepsilon^{2})\right\rceil
we obtain
\mathbb{E}[X_{m,t}]\leq n^{-L}
, which proves Lemma
4
. Chaining together Lemmas
2
,
3
, and
4

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})^{m}\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}

(7)

which immediately proves Theorem
4
.

,
let
(H_{1},H_{2},\pi)
denote the output of

Then

is a solution to

Furthermore, the size of

For completeness we include a brief analysis of the runtime of the

Claim 1

(Runtime)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two graphs with
n

The
Greedy
algorithm (Algorithm
1
) with input
(G_{1},G_{2})
runs in
O(n^{3})
time.
In addition, if
(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}

\textsc{Greedy}(G_{1},G_{2})

O(n^{2}\log n)

Proof.

The

n
. For any

∎

4
Proof of impossibility for online algorithms

In this section we prove Theorem
4
.
To abbreviate notation, we write

Y:=(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}

for the input pair of graphs.
Note that Definition
1
—and hence also Theorem
4
—allows randomized algorithms. However, a simple averaging-based argument shows that it suffices to consider deterministic algorithms.
We state this in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For any randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,
there exists a random seed
\omega^{*}\in\Omega
such that the deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{*}(Y):=\mathcal{A}(Y,\omega^{*})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y,\omega}(|\mathcal{A}(Y,\omega)|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n)\leq\mathbb{P}_{Y}(|\mathcal{A}^{*}(Y)|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n).

(12)

Proof.

Using the law of total expectation, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y,\omega}(|\mathcal{A}(Y,\omega))|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\mathbb{P}_{Y}(|\mathcal{A}(Y,\omega)|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n)].

By an averaging argument, there exists some
\omega^{*}\in\Omega
such that
\mathcal{A}^{*}(Y):=\mathcal{A}(Y,\omega^{*})
satisfies (
12
).
∎

Due to Lemma
1
,
in order to prove Theorem
4
, it suffices to prove it for deterministic online algorithms.
In the following we thus only consider deterministic online algorithms.
In particular, this means that the only source of randomness in what follows is the input
Y
.

We now formally define the interpolation paths and forbidden structures that will be key to our analysis. These definitions closely follow the analogous structures in
[
10
]
,
though we take care to
adapt them to the “two-dimensional” nature of the
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
problem.

Definition 2

(Interpolation paths)
.

Fix a deterministic
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{ONLINE}(n)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and sample
Y=(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
. For
t\in[n]
, the
t^{\text{th}}

interpolation path
is denoted
Y_{1}^{(t)},\dots,Y_{m}^{(t)}
, where
Y_{i}^{(t)}=\left(G_{i,1}^{(t)},G_{i,2}^{(t)}\right)
and each
G_{i,j}^{(t)}
is a graph on vertex set
[n]
. First, set

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{(t)}:=Y\qquad\forall t\in[n].

(13)

The remaining inputs are constructed based on the behavior of
\mathcal{A}
on input
Y
.
Let
P_{1}^{(t)}
and
P_{2}^{(t)}
denote the vertices in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
(respectively) processed by
\mathcal{A}
on input
Y
after the first
t
steps.
For every
t\in[n]
,
i\in[m]
, and
j\in\{1,2\}
, let
e_{i,j}^{(t)}(\{u,v\})
denote the edge status of the pair
\{u,v\}
in
G_{i,j}^{(t)}
.
Then, for each
t\in[n]
,
i\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
, and
j\in\{1,2\}
, we set

\displaystyle e_{i,j}^{(t)}(\{u,v\})

\displaystyle:=e_{1,j}^{(t)}(\{u,v\})\qquad\forall\{u,v\}\in\binom{P_{j}^{(t)}}{2},

(14)

\displaystyle e_{i,j}^{(t)}(\{u,v\})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)\qquad\forall\{u,v\}\notin\binom{P_{j}^{(t)}}{2},

(15)

where all of the random bits in (
15
) are independent of everything else.

See Figure
1
for an illustration of these interpolation paths.

Remark 1
.

Define the family of interpolation paths
\{Y_{1}^{(t)},\dots,Y_{m}^{(t)}\}_{t\in[n]}
as above. Let
\Gamma_{i}^{(t)}\in\{0,1\}^{2\binom{t}{2}}
encode the edge status of every pair of vertices that
\mathcal{A}
has inspected after
t
steps on input
Y_{i}^{(t)}
. Then (
13
) and (
14
) ensure that

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(t)}:=\Gamma_{1}^{(t)}=\cdots=\Gamma_{m}^{(t)}.

Since
\mathcal{A}
makes deterministic decisions based only on the information it has received by that point, we have that
\mathcal{A}^{(t)}(Y_{i}^{(t)})=\mathcal{A}^{(t)}(Y_{i^{\prime}}^{(t)})
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in[m]
. Moreover, (
15
) ensures that the inputs
\{Y_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i\in[m]}
are mutually independent (and identically distributed), conditioned on
\Gamma^{(t)}
. Combining this with (
13
), we have that marginally

\displaystyle Y_{i}^{(t)}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}\qquad\forall i\in[m],t\in[n].

Definition 3

(Forbidden structures)
.

Fix a deterministic
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{ONLINE}(n),m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\varepsilon>0
,
let
Y=(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
,
and consider a family of interpolation paths
\{Y_{1}^{(t)},\dots,Y_{m}^{(t)}\}_{t\in[n]}
.
For any
t\in[n]
, let
X_{m,t}
count the number of
m
-tuples
\left\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\right\}
such that for every
i\in[m]
the following two properties hold.

1.

(Large solutions.)
We have that
\sigma_{i}=\left(H_{i,1},H_{i,2}\right)
is a solution to the
\mathsf{LCIS}(n)
problem on instance
Y_{i}^{(t)}
, with

\displaystyle|\sigma_{i}|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n.

2.

(Overlap structure.)
For
j\in\{1,2\}
and
i\in[m]
we have that

\displaystyle V(H_{1,j})\cap P_{j}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=V(H_{i,j})\cap P_{j}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\left|V(H_{1,j})\cap P_{j}^{(t)}\right|

\displaystyle=\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil.

See Figure
2
for an illustration of these forbidden structures.
With these definitions we are now ready to prove Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Recall from Lemma
1
that we may and hence will assume that the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, we set the constant
m
to be

\displaystyle m:=\left\lceil\frac{40}{3\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil.

(16)

For notational convenience, in all of the arguments that follow, let

\gamma:=2-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad L:=\log_{2}n.

Recall that the input to the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
Y=(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)^{\otimes 2}
. Sample a family of interpolation paths
\{Y_{1}^{(t)},\dots,Y_{m}^{(t)}\}_{t\in[n]}
based on
\mathcal{A},m
, and
Y
according to Definition
2
.
Define the stopping time
\tau
to be

\displaystyle\tau:=\min\left\{\min\left\{1\leq t\leq n:\left|\mathcal{A}^{(t)}(Y)\right|=\lceil\gamma L\rceil\right\},n\right\}.

(17)

In words,
\tau
is the first time when the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on input
Y
reaches a (partial) solution of size
close
to half-optimal (specifically, of size at least
(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n
).
Next, define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\{|\mathcal{A}(Y)|\geq(2+\varepsilon)L\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{S}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\left\{\left|\mathcal{A}\left(Y_{i}^{(\tau)}\right)\right|\geq(2+\varepsilon)L\right\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{E}
is the event that we care about: that algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on input
Y
finds a solution
beyond
half-optimal (specifically, of size at least
(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
). Furthermore,
\mathcal{S}
is the event that this is true for all of the inputs along the
\tau
-th interpolation path, namely for
Y_{1}^{(\tau)},\ldots,Y_{m}^{(\tau)}
.

Recall the definition of
X_{m,t}
from Definition
3
.
The core of the proof lies in the following chain of inequalities:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})^{m}\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}.

(18)

We defer the proofs of these inequalities to Lemmas
2
,
3
, and
4
, respectively, below.
Theorem
4
then follows by taking an
m
-th root in (
18
), noting that
m
is constant (depending only on
\varepsilon
).
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to proving the inequalities in (
18
).
Lemma
2
is an analogue of
[
10
, Lemma 4.6]
; we include its proof here for completeness.

Lemma 2
.

Defining
m
,
\mathcal{E}
, and
\mathcal{S}
as in the proof of Theorem
4
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})^{m}\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

As in Remark
1
, let
\Gamma^{(t)}
be the random variable that encodes all the information processed by
\mathcal{A}
through step
t
on input
Y
; that is,
\Gamma^{(t)}
encodes the edge status of every pair
\{u_{1},v_{1}\}\in\binom{P_{1}^{(t)}}{2}
in
G_{1}
and
\{u_{2},v_{2}\}\in\binom{P_{2}^{(t)}}{2}
in
G_{2}
. Since
\tau\in[n]
deterministically, by the law of total probability we can write
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}\cap\{\tau=t\})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\cap\{\tau=t\}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right],

where the third equality follows because the event
\{\tau=t\}
is determined by
\Gamma^{(t)}
. Considering now the right-hand side of the desired inequality,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})
, the same reasoning gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,t}\cap\cdots\cap\mathcal{E}_{m,t}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right],

where
\mathcal{E}_{i,t}:=\{|\mathcal{A}(Y_{i}^{(t)})|\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n\}
.

Now we use an important property of the construction of interpolation paths in Definition
2
: all edges in
Y_{1}^{(t)},\ldots,Y_{m}^{(t)}
that are revealed after step
t
are i.i.d.
In particular, this implies that conditioned on
\Gamma^{(t)}
, the events
\mathcal{E}_{1,t},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m,t}
are mutually independent (see Remark
1
).
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,t}\cap\cdots\cap\mathcal{E}_{m,t}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i,t}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1,t}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)^{m}=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)^{m}.

Note that the random variable
\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}
is nonzero for exactly one term in the sum over
t\in[n]
. As such we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)^{m}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right)^{m}.

Finally, since
f(x)=x^{m}
is convex for
m\geq 1
and
x\geq 0
, we can apply Jensen’s inequality to get

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})^{m}=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right]\right)^{m}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}{\{\tau=t\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,\Gamma^{(t)}\right)\right)^{m}\right]=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}).\qed

We now turn to the second inequality in (

).
Lemma
3
is an analogue of

Lemma 3
.

Define
\mathcal{S}
as in the proof of Theorem
4
and

as in Definition

. We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma

, the law of total probability gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}\cap\{\tau=t\}\right),

so it suffices to show that for every
t\in[n]
the event
\mathcal{S}\cap\{\tau=t\}
implies
X_{m,t}\geq 1
. Fix any
t\in[n]
and suppose that
\mathcal{S}\cap\{\tau=t\}
occurs. For each
i\in[m]
, define

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}:=\mathcal{A}(Y_{i}^{(\tau)}).

To see that
\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\}
is a forbidden structure and hence

, note that Property
1
of Definition

is directly implied by

\{\tau=t\}

∎

Finally, we turn to proving the third inequality in (

\mathsf{LCIS}(n)

Lemma 4
.

Define
X_{m,t}
as in Definition
3
. We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}.

We first deal with some simple edge cases in
t
.
Note that if
t<\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil
, then
X_{m,t}=0
deterministically. This is because then

|V(H_{1,1})\cap P_{1}^{(t)}|\leq|P_{1}^{(t)}|=t<\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil
,
and so Property
2
of Definition
3
cannot hold.
Similarly, we claim that if

t>n-\left(\lceil(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n\rceil-\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil\right),

then
X_{m,t}=0
deterministically.
To see this, observe that if Property
2
of Definition
3
holds,
then the solution
\sigma_{i}
at time
n
can have size at most

\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil+(n-t)
.
Thus, in order for Property
1
to hold,
we must have that

\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil+(n-t)\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n
.

Thus in the following we focus on times
t
satisfying

\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil\leq t\leq n-\left(\lceil(2+\varepsilon)\log_{2}n\rceil-\lceil(2-\varepsilon/2)\log_{2}n\rceil\right).

(19)

For
t\in[n]
that does not satisfy (

), the previous paragraph shows that

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{m,t}\geq 1\right)=0
.

To analyze
X_{m,t}
, it is useful to use a truncation argument;
to this end, we introduce the following quantities.

Definition 4
.

Let

count the number of

that satisfy Definition

and have
\max_{i\in[m]}|\sigma_{i}|>6\log_{2}n
.
Similarly, let

count the number of
m

that satisfy Definition
3
and have
\max_{i\in[m]}|\sigma_{i}|\leq 6\log_{2}n
.

By definition, we have that

X_{m,t}=W_{m,t}+Z_{m,t}
.
Therefore, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{m,t}\geq 1\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(W_{m,t}\geq 1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{m,t}\geq 1\right),

(20)

and the following two claims bound these latter two quantities.

Claim 2
.

Define
W_{m,t}
as in Definition
4
. For every
t\in[n]
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq mn^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}.

Claim 3
.

Define
Z_{m,t}
as in Definition
4
. Suppose that
t
satisfies (
19

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{m,t}\geq 1)\leq n^{m}n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

By (

) and Claims
2
and
3
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{m,t}\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Bigl(\mathbb{P}(W_{m,t}\geq 1)+\mathbb{P}(Z_{m,t}\geq 1)\Bigr)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Bigl(mn^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}+n^{m}n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 2mn^{m+1}n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)}\leq n^{-\log_{2}n+o(\log n)},

where we use that
a+b\leq 2ab
for
a,b\geq 1
, and that

is a constant, so
2mn^{m+1}\leq n^{o(\log n)}
.
∎

It remains to prove the claims. Claim
2
follows from a straightforward union bound.

Proof of Claim
2
.

Note that
W_{m,t}\geq 1
implies the existence of some
Y_{i}^{(t)}=\left(G_{i,1}^{(t)},G_{i,2}^{(t)}\right)
that contains a pair of subgraphs
H_{1}\subseteq G_{i,1}^{(t)},H_{2}\subseteq G_{i,2}^{(t)}
such that
H_{1}\cong H_{2}
and

\displaystyle k:=|H_{1}|=|H_{2}|=\lceil 6\log_{2}n\rceil.

For any two subgraphs

, the probability that

is at most
(k!)2^{-\binom{k}{2}}
. Taking a union bound over all possible choices of

and subgraphs

, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{m,t}\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V(H_{1})\subseteq[n],\,V(H_{2})\subseteq[n]\\
|V(H_{1})|=|V(H_{2})|=k\end{subarray}}(k!)2^{-\binom{k}{2}}\leq m\binom{n}{k}^{2}(k!)2^{-\binom{k}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq m\left(n^{2}2^{-(k-1)/2}\right)^{k}\leq m\left(\sqrt{2}n^{-1}\right)^{k}\leq mn^{-6\log_{2}n+o(\log n)},

where the third inequality uses that

\binom{n}{k}^{2}k!\leq n^{2k}
,
the fourth inequality uses that

and hence
2^{-(k-1)/2}\leq\sqrt{2}n^{-3}
,
and the final inequality uses that

as well.
∎

.

Lemma

Lemma 5
.

Recall that
\gamma=2-\varepsilon/2

. For any

,
\vec{\alpha}\in[2+\varepsilon,6]^{m}
, and
\left(P_{1}^{(t)},P_{2}^{(t)}\right)\subseteq[n]^{2}

that satisfy Property
