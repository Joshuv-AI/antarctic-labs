Exploring Human Quadruped Locomotion for Exergames Conference: Computer-Human Interaction in Play; November 2-5 2026; York, UK CCS: Human-centered computing Mixed / augmented reality
Shamit Ahmed, Perttu Hämäläinen

Abstract
. Embodying non-human characters and exercising abdominal muscles are both underexplored in exergames. We address this by describing the design and evaluation of a novel human quadruped locomotion exergame. In the game, the player lies supine on the ground and moves their arms and legs to control a quadrupedal character (a tiger), similar to common bodyweight abdominal muscle exercises such as the Bicycle Crunch. The motion tracking is computer vision-based, utilizing a Kinect sensor placed above the player, which makes our approach suitable for commercial premises such as indoor activity parks where a system needs to run unattended and without any wearable components. Our system extends embodied interaction beyond traditional bipedal or controller-based systems, demonstrating how natural limb movements can generate responsive and immersive quadrupedal motion within virtual environments. We conducted a user study (N=15) to evaluate the system’s intuitiveness, control, and overall player experience. The findings demonstrate the usability and potential of our system, highlighting its intense physical nature. Participants reported that gameplay immersion masked physical exertion, allowing them to perceive rigorous core training primarily as play.

2.
Related Work

Below, we review related user-avatar motion mapping and locomotion techniques across fields such as exergames, VR and CV.

2.1.
Human-Quadruped Motion Mapping

Motion mapping/retargeting aims to transfer motion patterns from one skeletal structure to another while maintaining physical plausibility
(
24
)
. In the context of bipedal movement targeted to quadrupedal characters, this task becomes particularly challenging due to substantial anatomical and biomechanical differences between bipeds and quadrupeds.

Recent work by
21
presents Dog Code, a deep learning–based framework that maps human motion to quadrupedal movement in VR. Their model demonstrates improved performance for symmetric quadrupedal gaits (walk, trot, pace) compared to traditional inverse kinematics (IK)-based retargeting solutions, but falls short for faster, asymmetric gaits (gallop, canter), likely due to the lack of temporal alignment with fast, symmetric human gaits (jog, run). Moreover, the source motion in Dog Code is bipedal, whereas we study a full-body mapping system in which the human is actually mimicking quadrupedal movement in a supine position.

Virtual animal embodiment, i.e., a user inhabiting a non-humanoid body in a virtual environment, has been explored in multiple studies, especially in VR
(
39
;
34
)
.
39
investigated various non-human character body ownership techniques, including a full-body movement technique where users had to kneel and crouch on a yoga mat. using handheld controllers.
34
contributes a mapping algorithm to map human hand inputs to diverse non-humanoid avatars in VR.

Furthermore, the sense of embodiment in non-human avatars is closely linked to visual perspective.
31
demonstrated that the choice between first-person and third-person viewpoints directly affects a user’s sense of body ownership in VR. This is particularly relevant when mapping human movements to a quadrupedal form, as the perspective can either bridge or widen the perceived gap between the player’s physical body and their virtual animal counterpart.

2.2.
Exergames

Exergames, or "exercise video games," are games that require players to use their bodies to play, turning physical movement into engaging workouts. Exergaming has become an increasingly popular field that involves using immersive technologies to motivate physical activity, such as strength training, balance, aerobic exercise, and flexibility. Multiple studies
(
4
;
5
)
have also tried to understand the relationship between body movement and player engagement.

Based on results of 29 studies,
18
found that exergames can elicit moderate to vigorous exercise intensity comparable to traditional workouts while significantly lowering the user’s perceived exertion and enhancing enjoyment. Consequently, they conclude that exergaming is a viable, engaging substitute for traditional exercise that may improve adherence among sedentary individuals. While recent work by
13
highlights the physiological importance of lower-body engagement, current locomotion paradigms, such as Walking-in-Place (WIP)
(
70
)
, remain predominantly bipedal. Consequently, non-upright modalities like supine movement are underexplored, representing a significant gap in gamifying core intensive exercise.

Nintendo’s Wii Sports
(
57
)
and Wii Fit
(
58
)
are widely recognized as the pioneering works that popularized the exergame genre. By introducing intuitive motion controls and the Wii Balance Board, they democratized "exertion interfaces"
(
52
)
, proving that gamified physical activity could be accessible, commercially viable, and effective at shifting gaming away from purely sedentary paradigms.

Research into non-standing exergames highlights how physical posture significantly influences player experience and exertion. For instance,
23
explored the differences between sitting and standing play, providing a foundation for floor-based exergames and supine interaction models that cater to diverse physical abilities.

In the following subsections, we explore motion-driven locomotion techniques across multiple domains (MR, VR, non-VR, etc.) of HCI that are related to our approach. We focus on relevant techniques that employ less conventional body postures and input devices.

2.3.
VR Locomotion Techniques

Although our design centers on a vision-based, non-VR locomotion system, our approach has been significantly inspired and informed by innovative locomotion techniques across different mediums. Techniques in VR motivated us to study the strengths and drawbacks of physical movement as an input modality. Existing literature reviews frequently compile and compare a range of VR locomotion approaches
(
55
;
6
;
7
)
. VR has been well explored due to its novelty and ability to create immersive, embodied human-computer interactions.

Our approach can be classified as a Walk-in-Place (WiP) virtual locomotion technique, where the user performs physical motions to move their avatar through the virtual environment while remaining stationary in their physical space
(
67
)
. WiP eliminates the need for a large physical space matching the virtual space. Various WiP variants have been proposed over the years. Movement in WiP methods is commonly tracked using wearable motion trackers or omnidirectional treadmill-like devices
(
17

Although some VR WiP research claims reduced motion sickness when compared to joystick locomotion, WiP in VR is fundamentally problematic as it cannot avoid vection or visual-vestibular conflict which is a major cause of motion sickness
(
12
)
.
38
also noticed some of their users experiencing motion sickness in their supine locomotion setup. Moreover, using a VR headset while lying down can cause an upturned redirection of the user’s cognitive space, resulting in increased visual-vestibular sensory conflict, as investigated by
46
. Motivated by these problems, we implement our game using a screen instead of VR headset, which avoids the visual-vestibular conflict.

3.
Design

This section describes our system and game design. We start with an overview of the design process, followed by the design goals, challenges, technical setup, and motivation for using a vision-based approach. We conclude by detailing the design of various movement mechanics in the game.

3.1.
Overview

Our design stages can be explained using the Double Diamond design framework
(
15
)
:

(1)

Discovery
: We began by investigating the limitations of traditional locomotion interfaces, observing human supine motion patterns, and analyzing quadrupedal gait dynamics. This step helped to identify the specific biomechanical challenges involved in effectively mapping human motion to quadrupedal movement.

(2)

Definition
: We then established the primary goal of enabling a supine user to control a quadruped avatar across states such as walking, galloping, and jumping. We defined critical constraints, such as ensuring limb freedom and safety, and selected key metrics, including responsiveness and immersion, to guide the system design.

(3)

Development
: We first developed a motion capture system that tracked human motion input. We then implemented human to quadruped motion translation algorithms for movement inference and procedural limb mapping. Multiple prototypes based on various movement inference algorithms were explored in both 2D and 3D virtual environments, iteratively refining the logic to enhance intuitiveness and accuracy.

(4)

Delivery
: During the development and exploration, we noticed that the more versatile movement one wants to implement (e.g., full 3d movement with strafing and rotation around the vertical axis), the harder it is to avoid gameplay problems caused by movement tracking imperfections. In order to deliver a playable and fun game, we finally converged on a 2d game design where the player only walks or runs forward and jumps over gaps and obstacles, which allowed for a simpler movement mapping logic that was less prone to glitches. We also conducted a user study to evaluate the strengths, limitations, and future development potential of the final prototype.

3.2.
Design Goals

Our work is motivated by two primary goals. First, we aim to develop a novel human-quadruped locomotion system that bridges the morphological gap between bipedal players and four-legged avatars. By utilizing a supine, CV-based configuration, we seek to provide a high-fidelity interface that avoids the constraints of traditional methods, allowing full 4-limb control. Second, we aim to develop an exergame that uses this system to explore new ways to exercise one’s core muscles, which has received relatively little attention in exergames.

Our goal is not to compare against a baseline or to prove that our approach is superior in terms of any metric. Rather, in the Research through Design (RtD) tradition
(
40
)
, our goal is to probe and better understand the unexplored design space through the prototype that we developed.

3.3.
Design Challenges

Mapping non-standing humanoid motion onto quadrupedal characters presents two core challenges. Firstly, accurately inferring a target quadruped’s velocity, posture, and gait from raw humanoid motion data is non-trivial due to the fundamental differences in limb configuration, center of mass, and movement dynamics between bipeds and quadrupeds
(
24
;
2
;
75
)
.

Secondly, ensuring that the approach generalizes across diverse users with varying body proportions and movement patterns while maintaining real-time responsiveness and stability, often requires advanced calibration, adaptive retargeting, or possibly learning-based models that can adapt to individual behavioral nuances
(
21
;
47
;
29
)
.

3.6.
Utilizing Computer Vision and Depth Estimation

We adopted a Computer Vision (CV) approach to prioritize accessibility and natural movement, avoiding the restrictive hardware and complex setup of marker-based systems. To overcome the limitations of standard RGB cameras in full-body pose estimation, we utilized the depth sensing capabilities of the RGB-D cameras
(
28
)
. Motion data capture and body tracking have been implemented using the Azure Kinect DK by Microsoft
(
50
)
and their proprietary software development kit, the Azure Kinect Body Tracking SDK
(
51
)
. The Azure Kinect was selected for its superior skeletal tracking performance
(
1
)
, particularly for users in the supine position
(
64
)
.

While Microsoft has announced the discontinuation of the Azure Kinect Developer Kit in late 2023, the formal transfer of its depth-sensing technology to a partner ecosystem ensures continued hardware availability. A primary successor is the Orbbec Femto Bolt
(
59
)
, which was developed in collaboration with Microsoft and utilizes the same 1-megapixel Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensor and operating modes as the original device.

The Azure Kinect’s depth sensor, through its skeletal tracking, can measure joint input data in 6 degrees of freedom (6-DOF). It can provide position and orientation information for 32 joints. However, for velocity calculations, we primarily require tracking the joint data of the four limb end effectors (left hand, right hand, left foot, right foot). We additionally track eleven more joints (head, pelvis, left elbow, right elbow, left knee, right knee, chest, left shoulder, right shoulder, left hip, right hip) to construct a simplified skeletal visualization of the tracked humanoid for debugging purposes.

Depth information is required when the camera is in front or on top of the user. The camera placement is to 1) ensure that limbs are not occluding each other in the camera view, 2) minimize the footprint of the setup, which is a key variable if aiming to operate a game in commercial premises like indoor activity parks. A camera on the side would need to be far enough which requires a larger footprint. Moreover, the top mounted camera setup easily supports a multiplayer future extension with two or more players side-by-side, which is impossible with a side camera due to players occluding each other.

3.7.
Movement Mechanics

Our primary technical challenge is tracking and translating the user’s limb movements into corresponding quadruped avatar motion. Our movement mapping approach can be summarized as:

(1)

Movement emerges from physics simulation that also conveniently handles gravity and collisions. The quadruped has physics simulation skeleton composed of capped cylinders (capsules) and joints.

(2)

We use Inverse Kinematics (IK) to map the tracked user feet and hand positions to a target pose for the quadrupedal character. This pose is used as joint angle targets for the physics simulated skeleton’s joint actuators.

(3)

To optimize responsiveness and game feel, we adjust the physics simulation in two ways:

•

Instead of relying on simulated friction between the quadruped’s feet and the ground, which we have found to cause constant slipping of the feet, we implement our own "pseudo friction" where foot movement pushes the character forward if it is close enough to the ground.

•

When the movement code determines that the user wants to and should be able to move forward or jump, then we override the physics simulation’s velocity to apply an instant change, as opposed to accelerating towards the desired velocity by applying control forces.

Below, we provide additional details about how forward locomotion and jumping are implemented.

3.7.1.
Forward/backward locomotion

The user drives forward motion naturally, with the speed of limb movement directly controlling the avatar’s velocity. The algorithm calculates a weight
w
for each limb to determine how much influence it should have on overall movement as

(1)

w=\max\left(0,1-\frac{d}{c}\right),

where
d
is the limb’s distance from the ground and
c
is the ground contact zone thickness (0.25m in our case). If a limb is in touch with the ground (
d=0
), w is 1.0 (full influence). If a limb is too far from the ground (
d>=c
), w is 0.0 (no influence). The limbs closest to the ground have the most influence.

The target velocity to apply to the avatar equals a weighted average of all limb velocities:

(2)

v_{target}=b_{xz}\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}v_{i}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}},

where
v
denotes the tracked limb velocity,
i
is the limb index and
b_{xz}
is a velocity boost multiplier. Additionally, to allow the physics-simulated friction to slow the avatar down instead of it stopping immediately when the user stops, we set a limb’s weight to zero if it is not moving faster than a threshold value. If the sum of all weights is zero, the physics simulation velocity is not overridden.

3.7.2.
Jumping

Our jump logic can be summarized as: If the user’s limbs are moving fast enough upwards (towards the Azure Kinect) and if the avatar is grounded, we override the avatar’s velocity with a jump velocity. Otherwise, the velocity is not touched by the avatar movement code, letting the physics simulation control it according to gravity and collisions. To avoid false negatives, the grounded check includes "coyote time", i.e., it returns true if the avatar is touching the ground or has touched the ground during the last 200ms.

The jump velocity’s horizontal and vertical components,
v_{y\_jump}
and
v_{z\_jump}
, are determined as:

(3)

\displaystyle v_{y\_jump}

\displaystyle=clip(b_{y}v_{y},v_{y\_avatar},v_{y\_max})

\displaystyle v_{z\_jump}

\displaystyle=clip(v_{z}+b_{z}v_{y},v_{z\_avatar},v_{z\_max})

where the
clip(x,x_{min},x_{max})
function clips
x
in the range
[x_{min},x_{max}]
,
v_{y}
denotes the vertical tracked velocity of the user’s limb (the jump velocity update is performed for each limb, letting players decide to jump using only their hands, legs, or by using all four limbs),
v_{y\_avatar},v_{z\_avatar}
are the avatar’s current vertical and horizontal velocity,
b_{y},b_{z}
are movement boost multipliers, and
v_{y\_max},v_{z\_max}
are the maximum allowed avatar vertical and horizontal velocities.

The lower clipping limit ensures that the jump code never slows down the avatar’s current velocity, and higher limit prevents excessive velocities that can be caused by both tracking glitches and individual differences of players.

The forward jump velocity boost controlled by
b_{z}
ensures that the avatar does not just go straight up but lunges forward like a pouncing tiger. This makes it easier for the players to leap over gaps in the game levels.

4.
System Evolution

Our locomotion system underwent multiple design iterations through internal tests and pilot studies with the authors and five colleagues from the same research group. The primary challenge lay in accurately inferring the user’s intended movement from the tracked limb motion and translating this into meaningful quadrupedal avatar movement. This can be broken down to the following design decisions and problem areas:

(1)

Discrete vs. Continuous Movement Mapping:
Right from the beginning, our design prioritized a continuous mapping from player movement to avatar movement, as opposed to discrete gesture recognition. This allows for responsive and precise control over avatar velocity and behavior, without the extra lag that gesture recognition inevitably causes.

(2)

Motion Input Sampling:
By default, the Azure Kinect depth sensor captures depth input at maximum 30 frames per second (FPS), which is less frequent than the game physics and movement updates. Therefore, if the movement code tests for input pose updates since the last movement code update, the result is intermittently zero, resulting in jerky, inconsistent movement. We addressed this by syncing velocity updates with the sensor input, meaning the tracked velocity is only updated in a physics frame if there is new input available from the sensor in that frame.

(3)

Gait Cycle Inference:
In locomotion, a limb cycles through states of pushing towards the ground to change one’s movement state and repositioning the limb in air for the next ground contact. Inferring the current state from the noisy tracked movement turned out to be surprisingly tricky. We first implemented the simple solution by
38
where the front or back limb pushed furthest away from the user’s body is the one classified to be touching the ground. However, this is not robust if the user is not perfectly facing upwards. Otherwise, one gets occasional classification errors resulting in the inferred velocity being opposite to the user’s desired velocity, causing erratic avatar movement. To remedy this, we tested a velocity direction filtering approach where a tracked limb velocity was only considered valid if it aligned with the current avatar velocity; hence, if the avatar had already gained momentum, a misclassification could not instantaneously revert the avatar movement direction. However, this felt too unresponsive in the end and was scrapped. Furthermore, only a single limb driving the movement was not an optimal match for diverse quadrupedal gaits including both walking and galloping, motivating us to adopt the weighted average mapping described above. Finally, to further reduce glitches due to incorrectly inferred limb states, we removed backwards locomotion altogether, as it turned out to be unnecessary when the game levels have sufficiently frequent checkpoints so that falling off a platform due to not being able to backstep was not penalized too heavily.

(4)

Transitioning from 3D to 2D:
As mentioned earlier in
Section

3.1
, during our development, we observed a trade-off between locomotive versatility and tracking reliability. Implementing full 3D movement negatively impacted the user experience, as it exacerbated problems caused by incorrect gait state inference and Azure Kinect skeletal tracking noise and glitches, as errors affected not only forward/backward movement but also strafing and turning around. Hence, a more accurate tracking solution is necessary for 3D movement. Our final solution of only allowing forward movement and jumps in a 2D plane hides the gait inference glitches while still allowing a meaningful obstacle course game experience.

User Study

We conducted a user study to measure the usability of our proposed locomotion system and to better understand the overall user experience.

5.1.
Study Design

After signing the informed consent form, each participant encountered a progression of three game levels. It should be noted that the levels did not correspond to different experimental conditions of a within-subjects study; therefore, the order of the levels was not counterbalanced. Data analysis centered on the qualitative thematic analysis of think-aloud data captured during gameplay and responses from a semi-structured interview conducted post-gameplay based on the questions stated in Table
2

5.1.1.
Locomotion Task Design

The core task involved navigating three game levels consisting of multiple checkpoints, and participants had to use the two primary movements: forward locomotion and jumping. The desired locomotion movements were instructed as illustrated in Figure

Users were instructed to complete all three linear levels without any time limit.For safety and comfort, participants were prompted to take breaks during and in-between the levels and were instructed that it was not necessary to play through all the levels if they felt exhausted or uncomfortable.

The levels (see Figure

Although the initial focus was on tighter, high-penalty platforming, pilot testing resulted in pivoting to more open, race-track-like environments with lower penalties, encouraging continuous movement. The final levels tested traversal abilities such as walking, jumping gaps, climbing slopes, and timing movements.

5.2.
Participants

Fifteen participants (four female, eleven male) were recruited by reaching out to different communication channels and social circles throughout the university. We advertised the user study as a "core workout exergame". For safety reasons, the recruitment was targeted towards physically active participants who would be comfortable with exercising. Each participant was offered a 20 euro gift card to the on-campus university merchandise shop for their contribution.

Thirteen participants were in the age group of 25-34 years, while two belonged to the age group of 18-24 years. The majority of the participants identified themselves as university students, while others identified as university researchers, game designers, or engineers.

Three participants reported exercising daily; seven exercise two or three times a week, and five reported that they had not exercised in the past month or so. We did not specify what we exactly meant by "physical exercises"; hence, users were free to interpret it as going to the gym, doing core workouts at home or outdoors, walking, or running, etc.

Data Collection

Our user study utilized a mixed-methods approach

5.3.1.
During Gameplay:

We used the Borg CR10 perceived exertion scale
(
8

The study instructor explained the meanings of the minimum and maximum values on the scale and what exactly they were assessing.

Participants were also encouraged to think aloud during the gameplay and to comment on moment-to-moment interactions and events.

Additionally, we captured gameplay video of the virtual world alongside the user’s voice recordings during gameplay. The voice recordings were transcribed, and the video was analyzed to determine whether the transcripts needed further context. Furthermore, the study instructor took notes about observations, such as different movement styles, how the user approached a particular obstacle, and what they seemed to struggle with. We also collected a range of task performance data during gameplay. This included timestamps for each checkpoint reached, the total number of respawns per level, and the completion time for each level.

5.3.2.
Post-Session Assessment (After all three levels completed):

Upon completing the entire gameplay session, the following data collection sequence was administered:

(1)

A semi-structured interview was conducted to gather in-depth feedback on the overall experience of the locomotion system. We designed and used the questionnaire in
Table

2
for the interview.

(2)

A brief final survey was administered immediately following the interview, which included the System Usability Scale (SUS) scores and general demographic information. We also requested the users to describe the game movement using three adjectives.

All data were stored anonymously. The voice recordings were deleted after transcription. Participants were identified only by numeric IDs, with no record kept that allows mapping an ID to a name. Only the gameplay video was recorded, and no real world video was captured.

5.4.
Methods

5.4.1.
Qualitative Analysis:

We analyzed the recorded and transcribed audio data from the discussions during gameplay and the semi-structured interviews using qualitative thematic analysis
(
68
)
, following the standard progression of data familiarization, data coding, theme generation, reviewing and refining the themes, and reporting with illustrative quotes
(
9
)
. The transcripts were analyzed using inductive coding, without a predefined codebook.
All the data were collected, transcribed, coded, grouped, and refined into themes by the first author, informed by the author’s first-hand interaction with the participants in the role of the user study facilitator.

5.4.2.
Quantitative Analysis:

Quantitative data gathered from the survey responses, Borg CR10 scores, SUS scores, and performance metrics were analyzed using descriptive statistics, specifically averages, means, medians, and standard deviations. Due to our limited sample size (N=15), we do not perform any statistical significance tests. We also do not perform any comparisons with a baseline or existing locomotion systems since our goal is not to claim that our approach is superior to previous work, but rather to explore and understand the novel design space of quadrupedal supine exergames. Therefore, this approach allows us to clearly describe the observed patterns in player experience, usability, and control, prompting hypotheses and research questions for future work rather than drawing conclusions.

All fifteen participants successfully passed all the checkpoints across three levels, completing the full game. 7.
Discussion

The discussion below is structured based on the six overarching themes derived from the qualitative data, augmented with quantitative results when applicable.

7.1.
Physical Exertion and Perceived Health Benefits

Participants frequently reported that the game provided a good core workout, felt like real exercise, and was beneficial for muscles such as the abs, thighs, and pectorals (
"I feel good now that I have done some exercise" [P8]
). Almost all of the participants reported that our study was the most physically demanding research experiment in which they had participated (
"when I was doing the movement on the ground, it feels like I was doing real exercise" [P6]
). Participants generally appreciated that it was a way to exercise that does not feel like exercise (
"I think it was quite unique and fun because, um, I was also engaging my core, but I had not realized this because I was too focused on the game" [P15]
). Some users also noted benefits, such as weight management (
"And I feel like to reduce belly fat this is a very good one" [P11]
).

Despite the high intensity, participants generally appreciated the "stealth exercise" nature of the game, where the focus on gameplay masked the effort (
"it gets you exercising in a way that doesn’t feel like exercise. You were asking me if I wanted to take breaks but I just said, ’No, no, let’s keep going’" [P14]
). The gaming element served as a primary motivator (
"I would find very hard to motivate myself doing that kind of movement without that gaming element" [P8]
).

However, physical condition was cited as a main barrier for some participants. Feedback included comments about the movement being exhausting (
"Uh, after the tutorial level, uh, I was a bit exhausted. And after the game ends, I was really exhausted" [P1]
) and causing discomfort due to their long-term physical conditions (
"Um, for me, it got like painful pretty quickly. I was primarily stopped by like having lower back pain" [P9]
). Some participants even reported acute physical sensations like unexpected leg fatigue (
"Naturally, legs were getting tired. I didn’t expect legs to get that tired" [P15]
).

7.2.
Overall Control and Mapping Fidelity

Movement was often described as smooth, responsive, intuitive, and providing a feeling of control. Participants enjoyed the feeling of a direct, precise mapping where the character’s actions mirrored their physical intent (
"It feels quite good. When I want to jump, it jumps. When I want to run forward, it runs forward" [P14]
); this synchronization often felt natural and met user expectations (
"I mean, it imitates really good, like the smooth animation, I think. Um, that is something I liked. Like, I felt like I was in control when I played this" [P4]
). Some participants specifically highlighted the lack of perceived latency, which helped maintain a deep connection to the avatar (
"I felt it’s, uh, kinda very well connected between the movement and the translation on the tiger" [P6]
).

However, several participants indicated that the tiger movement felt unnatural or biologically inconsistent (
"I think, uh, my improvement would be a slight tweak to the tiger, uh, anatomical movements. Because sometimes it feels a little bit off. It doesn’t really follow like the tiger anatomical… So, it doesn’t look natural." [P6]
), and one participant felt the tiger was trying to emulate their movement instead of them controlling the tiger (
"It feels like he’s trying to emulate me, but it’s not that I’m controlling the tiger" [P9]
).

Tracking issues also impacted the experience, with several participants reporting a sense of input lag, particularly during jumps (
"Uh, there’s, uh, input lag when I jump. I feel like there’s an input lag" [P1]
). These delays could lead to a momentary loss of control and break the user’s immersion (
"Whenever there is like, input delay then I can snap out of it" [P1]
). This resulted in some participants feeling they were only in control intermittently (
"But it often also felt like I wasn’t fully in control though" [P12]
), while some others felt they needed to focus more on precision due to a perceived slipperiness and inability to stop the avatar.

7.3.
Navigating Jump Difficulties

The jump mechanic was identified as the system’s most crucial and polarizing element, driving both frustration and satisfaction. For some participants, jumping was described as either initially difficult, feeling delayed, or hard to estimate jump direction. There were some issues with false positives and false negatives related to jumps. We identified three possible reasons for the jump difficulties: (1) the jump was not properly tracked, (2) participant did not execute the intended jump motion, and (3) participant jumped too late and missed the ground contact required to initiate the jump. (
"Yeah, like I, I feel maybe the angle was wrong. But it felt a bit unsatisfying to like kind of not have the input register" [P3]
).

Notably, during pilot testing, some participants returned their limbs to the "ready" position for a next jump too late, when the avatar was already back on the ground, and the returning movement pushed the avatar backwards, causing frustration and failures. The disabling of backward movement mitigates the issue in our final game prototype, but solving the issue properly is a potential topic for future work.

On the other hand, jumping was responsive and easy to control for many of the participants. For others, after overcoming initial jumping difficulties and learning to jump properly, it was considered fun and satisfying (
"Um, it did feel very satisfying to jump. Especially if I was, like, able to get the motion right and I jumped really far, that was really satisfying and, like, it felt really good" [P10]
). Some participants noted that jumping instead of walking felt more efficient, indicating a possible need to reduce the jump velocity boosting or a need to revise the level design to encourage walking movement.

7.4.
Movement Exaggeration and Empowerment

Participants derived a strong sense of empowerment from the system’s exaggerated mechanics, particularly the jump (
"And then when I jumped, it clicked. Due to excitement, I would be doing more and then jumping super high. That was my liking moment" [P4]
). This sense of agency was further heightened by the satisfaction of mastering the movement to achieve finer control. For example, navigating through tougher obstacles, nearly escaping a fall, or "cheating" the game by skipping sections transformed physical effort into rewarding in-game moments. Multiple participants mentioned the movement as easy to start but hard to master, emphasizing good progression and buildup.

Finally, the experience of sustained immersion and physical effort was often described as a state of flow (
"…because it felt like I was in the flow which also helped me keep going" [P15]
) throughout the rigorous core workout.

7.5.
Ergonomics and Usability

For most participants, the supine position felt natural and was not a burden , with some finding the setup more comfortable than VR, based on their prior experience (
"I think this is definitely easier and more comfortable than a VR setup" [P7]
). However, users noted issues with physically slipping from their default position and the need for repositioning, which resulted in unintentional inputs.

Suggestions were made regarding placing the screen either directly above the user or at a tilted angle to reduce neck strain and increase overall comfort. One observation noted that the possibility of screen placement could affect players’ limb positioning. Since our hands tend to follow our eyes, having the screen at a more comfortable position can naturally improve proprioception
(
27
)
and the overall user experience.

One participant out of fifteen reported feeling dizzy after getting up from the supine position. They also mentioned that it was a bit difficult to get out of the position due to the specific angle of the mattress.

Taken together, the feedback above indicates that future work should pay more attention to the design of the physical setup.

We propose a recliner that is higher than our current mattress and, therefore, easier to get into and out of. The recliner angle should also be adjustable so that players can adjust the exertion level (moving one’s legs in the air is less effortful in a more upright position). 7.6.
System Expansion and Refinement

Participant feedback highlighted a clear demand for expanded locomotion capabilities, specifically requests for backward, lateral, and climbing mechanics to enhance navigational freedom. Users suggested that lateral movement could improve the experience (
"I think I would like to have more lateral movement…to be able to go left and right more freely" [P13]
), while others envisioned vertical actions such as climbing (
"maybe climbing up a tree or a cliff… something that a tiger would naturally do" [P6]
). One participant mentioned that adding in-air control could lead to an improved platforming experience.

Participants also suggested "tiger-specific" gameplay activities to better leverage the animal embodiment, such as chasing or interacting with the environment (
"add more tiger like activities… like hunting some deer… drinking water to regain stamina" [P2]
), more levels for longer playtime, adding a multiplayer game mode for social engagement, and additional features such as enhanced audio or haptic feedback. They also suggested mid-game recalibration to address tracking issues caused by user movement (
"if I’m slipping from the neutral position, then maybe it could recalibrate mid-game" [P10]
). The desire for "high scores" also points to a need for competitive structures to drive engagement (
"I wanted to see how I did compared to what other players have done. So I would want to see all time high scores" [P15]
).

8.
Limitations and Future Work

Despite the promising outcomes of our proposed locomotion system, several limitations must be acknowledged. We recognize the small sample size of 15 participants as a limitation; yet, such groups are standard for in-person qualitative HCI research
(
11
)
. Furthermore, such sample sizes are considered sufficient for effectively evaluating system usability
(
33
)
. Apart from the usability evaluation, our experiment aims to identify the key benefits and challenges of the presented system. The quantitative data we have gathered are used for descriptive purposes, as a larger sample is required for conducting any comparative analyses or statistical evaluations.

Player adaptation remains a critical factor influencing system performance. Individual differences such as age, body size, and motor behavior can result in unconventional or unpredictable movement patterns that challenge the system’s movement detection and mapping accuracy.

Moreover, the approach is inherently constrained by the technical limitations of the Azure Kinect depth sensor. Compared to more precise tracking devices such as VR controllers or dedicated motion trackers, the Azure Kinect offers lower spatial resolution, higher latency, and increased susceptibility to occlusion, which can reduce tracking fidelity and responsiveness during rapid or complex movements. Therefore, it should be addressed that the Azure Kinect tracking is not mature enough as it stands, and a commercial solution would require a custom tracking system optimized for the playing posture and movements in order to maximize accuracy and minimize latency and glitches.

The current system iteration is restricted to 2D navigation (linear movement and jumping), limiting its immediate application in fully explorative 3D virtual environments. A more accurate tracking solution would be particularly crucial for 3D interaction.

Finally, while the Borg CR10 scale
(
8
)
is a widely accepted and validated metric for assessing perceived exertion in HCI research, future work could incorporate physiological measures, such as heart rate monitoring, to provide a more robust and precise quantification of exercise intensity.

Overall, future developments of the locomotion system could aim to address current limitations and enhance adaptability, precision, and accessibility. As part of the motion mapping glitches originate from the Azure Kinect’s skeletal tracking latency, noise, and an arm or leg sometimes simply failing to track a fast movement; implementing a custom computer vision solution might be helpful. Here, the problem is simplified by our locomotion system, which only needs hand and foot positions without rotation information or full-body skeletal tracking.

For more robust inference of user intent and gait state based on the tracked movement, one might also collect a large movement dataset from multiple users, label the data frame-by-frame according to user intent (e.g., jump, forward, backward, etc.), and then train a machine learning system to infer the intent during gameplay. Such dataset labeling is labour-intensive, which might be mitigated by having the users themselves do the labeling in real-time. One could visualize a sequence of intended movements for users; similar to dancing games where the player attempts to follow a choreography; and let the users practice the sequence until the movements can be recorded in sync with the intent.

Lastly, there is significant potential in expanding the system into a multiplayer experience. Participants expressed a strong desire for social interaction, suggesting that one could enhance motivation and enjoyment by introducing competitive or collaborative gameplay. Physically, this could be realized by having two or more players lie side by side, tracked by the same depth sensor.

9.
Conclusion

We have presented a novel human quadruped locomotion system and an associated novel exergame that provides an intensive core workout. Our computer vision–based approach avoids the need for wearable tracking devices and allows an accessible and low-maintenance setup that could run unattended in commercial premises such as indoor activity parks. Our user study indicates that despite challenges regarding tracking fidelity and individual differences in the way players move, our game provides intensive exercise that is at the same time engaging and fun.

Regarding future development, we envision three main areas: First, a custom computer vision solution could provide better data for the human-to-quadruped movement mapping logic. Second, in order to ensure and validate that the movement mapping works across diverse ways and styles of movement, the currently handcrafted mapping approach could be replaced by a machine learning one, although this would require a substantial data collection and labeling effort. Third, many of our participants were asking for a social multiplayer version, and there is no reason why multiple players could not be placed side-by-side, tracked using the same depth camera.
