Sex-specific differences and diagnostic tool performance in the neuropsychiatric profile of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Yun-Sheng Chen, Chiung-Mei Chen, Chi-Hung Juan, Mei-Ling Cheng, Hsiu-Chuan Wu, Chih-Hong Lee, Yen-Shi Lo, Yi-Ru Wang, Kuo-Hsuan Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropsychiatric symptoms are prevalent in Parkinson’s disease (PD) but remain incompletely characterized, particularly concerning sex-specific differences and the comparative performance of diagnostic tools. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive neuropsychiatric profile of a Taiwanese PD cohort, evaluate the diagnostic sensitivity of various assessment tools, and explore correlations with clinical features.Materials and methodsWe enrolled 92 PD patients and 57 healthy controls. Participants underwent a comprehensive assessment battery, including motor scales, cognitive measures, and mood inventories. Diagnoses of dementia and depression were established using standard criteria. Diagnostic tool performance was evaluated by calculating the sensitivity and specificity, and relationships between variables were assessed using Spearman correlation.ResultsDementia was identified in 34.8% and depression in 28.3% of PD patients. Female patients had lower scores on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (25.18 vs. 28.08, p = 0.003) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (21.18 vs. 25.79, p = 0.001), and higher scores on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) (6.68 vs. 5.02, p = 0.046) and Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) (3.95 vs. 1.79, p = 0.018) compared with male patients. For dementia diagnosis, the clinician-rated MMSE and MoCA demonstrated sensitivities exceeding 90%, whereas the caregiver-rated Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) had a sensitivity of only 25%. For depression, the clinician-rated HAM-D (88.5% sensitivity) outperformed the self-reported Beck’s Depression Inventory II (BDI-II) (65.4% sensitivity). Most neuropsychiatric assessments were significantly correlated with age, disease duration, and motor severity.ConclusionsThese findings highlight a considerable neuropsychiatric burden in PD, with a distinct female-predominant vulnerability in cognitive and affective domains. The superior performance of clinician-administered assessments emphasizes their value in improving diagnostic certainty. These results support the routine, comprehensive, and sex-specific neuropsychiatric evaluations in the clinical management of PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05056-4.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease that primarily affects patients over 65, with a prevalence of about 1% in this population [1]. The current diagnosis of PD relies primarily on motor manifestations. However, PD is now recognized as a complex systemic disorder in which non-motor symptoms constitute core features and frequently precede the onset of motor signs by several years [2]. Among these, neuropsychiatric symptoms such as cognitive decline and depression pose significant challenges, but they remain insufficiently identified in routine clinical practice [3, 4].

Growing recognition of neuropsychiatric involvement in PD has prompted research using a variety of screening tests and assessments. However, findings across studies remain inconsistent. While previous research has established a high prevalence of cognitive decline and depression in PD, reported rates vary widely due to methodological inconsistencies and differing cutoff points across studies [5–9]. Furthermore, investigations into how these neuropsychiatric symptoms correlate with PD progression have yielded conflicting results regarding age, disease duration, and motor severity [10, 11]. Despite these efforts, comprehensive within-cohort assessments of multiple non-motor symptoms remain scarce, hindering direct comparisons of different screening instruments. Furthermore, most of the existing literature relies on Western populations. Cultural, genetic, or regional factors significantly influence symptom perception and reporting in Asian cohorts [12], highlighting the critical need for region-specific characterization to ensure accurate diagnosis and optimize clinical management.

To address this gap, the primary objective of this study was to provide a comprehensive neuropsychiatric profile of a Taiwanese PD cohort, focusing specifically on sex-based differences. The secondary objective was to compare the diagnostic sensitivity of multiple assessment tools for dementia and depression, and explore the correlations with clinical features.

Materials and methods

Subjects

We recruited 92 patients with PD at the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Linkou Medical Center in Taiwan, between July 1, 2018, to March 31, 2024. Fifty-seven age and sex-matched healthy controls (HC) were recruited from the neurology outpatient clinic. The diagnosis of PD was based on the UK PD Society Brain Bank clinical diagnostic criteria and was performed by two movement disorders neurologists (KH Chang and CM Chen) [13]. For the PD patients, levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was recorded [14]. All participants were Taiwanese. Subjects were excluded if they had systemic infection, chronic renal failure, cardiac or liver dysfunction, malignancies, autoimmune diseases, stroke, or neurodegenerative diseases other than PD.

Assessment

All participants received comprehensive parkinsonism neuropsychiatry batteries, including UPDRS for motor severity [15], Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging [16], Parkinson’s disease questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) summary index for quality of life [17], activities of daily living (ADL) for functional independence [18], mini-mental state examination (MMSE) [19] and Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) for global cognition [20], clinical dementia rating (CDR) for dementia severity [21], neuropsychiatric inventory (NPI) for behavioral disturbances [22], Hamilton depression rating scale (HAM-D) [23] and Beck’s depression inventory II (BDI-II) for affective symptoms [24].

In this study, dementia was diagnosed when either the MMSE score was ≤ 26 or the CDR global score (CDR-GS) was ≥ 1 [25, 26]. We also evaluated and compared the performance of using a MoCA score ≤ 24 as a diagnostic cutoff for dementia [27]. Depression was diagnosed according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [28]. In addition, we assessed the performance of the HAM-D (cutoff ≥ 8) and BDI-II (cutoff ≥ 14) in detecting depression [24, 29].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 (IBM). To compare examination scores between different groups, means and standard deviations were calculated and analyzed using independent Student’s t test, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to observe the relationship between the neuropsychiatric symptoms and motor symptoms, age, and disease progression. All statistical analyses were conducted using a two-tailed approach, with significance levels established at p < 0.05. The Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ) were interpreted as follows: |ρ| ≥ 0.8, very strong correlation; 0.6 ≤ |ρ| < 0.8, moderate correlation; 0.3 ≤ |ρ| < 0.6, fair correlation; |ρ| < 0.3, poor correlation [30]. The diagnostic performance of the screening tools was determined by calculating their sensitivity and specificity against the established reference standards for dementia and depression.

Results

Demographic and clinical presentations

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 92 PD patients and the 57 HC are summarized in (Table 1). No significant differences were found between the PD and HC groups in terms of age, sex distribution (proportion of males), and ADL, although the HC group showed a trend toward greater independence. PD group showed a clinically significant increase in symptom burden across all other measures. Specifically, UPDRS scores were markedly higher in PD patients (36.25 ± 23.98) compared with HC (1.58 ± 2.23, p < 0.001), as was H&Y stage (1.82 ± 0.69 vs. 0.00 ± 0.00, p < 0.001). Cognitive assessments also revealed greater impairment in PD patients, with lower MMSE (26.70 ± 4.68 vs. 29.51 ± 0.89, p < 0.001) and MoCA scores (23.59 ± 6.68 vs. 27.84 ± 2.43, p < 0.001) and higher CDR scores (0.37 ± 0.30 vs. 0.22 ± 0.25, p = 0.002). Neuropsychiatric evaluations indicated elevated symptom burden in PD, with higher NPI (2.83 ± 4.29 vs. 0.47 ± 1.64, p < 0.001), BDI-II (7.78 ± 6.21 vs. 1.40 ± 1.78, p < 0.001), and HAM-D scores (5.82 ± 3.95 vs. 1.35 ± 2.17, p < 0.001). Finally, quality of life, as measured by PDQ-39, was significantly lower in the PD group (31.52 ± 27.72) compared with HC (6.16 ± 8.33, p < 0.001). In summary, PD patients exhibited markedly greater motor, cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and quality-of-life impairments than HCs.

Sex-specific differences

Sex-stratified analysis of PD patients (48 males, 44 females) revealed a significantly greater neuropsychiatric burden in females (Table 2). Compared with males, female patients exhibited more pronounced cognitive impairment, as reflected by lower MMSE (25.18 ± 5.56 vs. 28.08 ± 3.15, p = 0.003) and MoCA scores (21.18 ± 7.94 vs. 25.79 ± 4.30, p = 0.001). In addition to cognitive deficits, females demonstrated higher levels of affective and behavioral symptoms, with elevated HAM-D scores (6.68 ± 4.43 vs. 5.02 ± 3.30, p = 0.046) and NPI scores (3.95 ± 5.31 vs. 1.79 ± 2.74, p = 0.018), suggesting greater burden of depression, anxiety, and other neuropsychiatric disturbances. Importantly, these sex-specific differences were not driven by pharmacological differences, as prescription rates for relevant medications such as rivastigmine and SNRIs were comparable between males and females (Table 2). These findings indicate that female PD patients may experience a more complex non-motor symptom profile.

Prevalence of dementia and performance of diagnostic tools

According to the defined diagnostic criteria, 32 of the 92 PD patients (34.8%) were diagnosed with dementia. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the demented and non-demented groups are summarized in (Table 3). Significant differences were observed across all measured variables. Compared to the non-demented group, PD patients with dementia were older, included a higher proportion of females, had longer disease duration, more advanced motor symptoms, poorer quality of life, and greater severity across all neuropsychiatric assessments.

Prevalence of depression and performance of diagnostic tools

According to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, 26 of the 92 PD patients (28.3%) were diagnosed with depression. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the depressed and non-depressed groups are summarized in (Table 5). Significant differences were observed across most measured variables, except for age, sex, the MMSE, and MoCA scores. Compared with the non-depressed group, patients with depression had longer disease duration, more advanced motor symptoms, poorer quality of life, and greater severity across most neuropsychiatric assessments. For cognitive function, a statistically significant difference was observed only in the CDR (0.48 ± 0.36 vs. 0.33 ± 0.27, p = 0.027), but not in the MMSE or MoCA.

The correlations between neuropsychiatric and clinical variables are summarized in (Fig. 1) and (Supplementary Fig. 1). The cognitive performance measures demonstrated fair to moderate correlations with motor symptoms, age, and H&Y stage (MMSE vs. UPDRS part III: ρ = -0.476; MMSE vs. age: ρ = -0.660; MMSE vs. H&Y: ρ = -0.441; MoCA vs. UPDRS part III: ρ = -0.437; MoCA vs. age: ρ = -0.706; MoCA vs. H&Y: ρ = -0.412; CDR vs. UPDRS part III: ρ = 0.476; CDR vs. age: ρ = 0.537; CDR vs. H&Y: ρ = 0.471; all p < 0.001), while showing poor or nonsignificant correlations with disease duration (MMSE vs. duration: ρ = -0.215, p = 0.039; MoCA vs. duration: ρ = -0.205, p = 0.050; CDR vs. duration: ρ = 0.123, p = 0.244). Both the BDI-II and HAM-D scores demonstrated fair to moderate correlations with motor symptoms, disease duration, and H&Y stage (BDI-II vs. UPDRS part III: ρ = 0.501; BDI-II vs. duration: ρ = 0.378; BDI-II vs. H&Y: ρ = 0.591; HAM-D vs. UPDRS part III: ρ = 0.416; HAM-D vs. duration: ρ = 0.375; HAM-D vs. H&Y: ρ = 0.464; all p < 0.001), while showing poor, albeit statistically significant, correlations with age (BDI-II vs. age: ρ = 0.289, p = 0.005; HAM-D vs. age: ρ = 0.223, p = 0.032). Discussion

Our study demonstrates a substantial neuropsychiatric burden in PD that is significantly correlated with motor symptom severity. Notably, this burden exhibited distinct sex-specific differences, with female patients experiencing significantly greater cognitive and affective impairments. These findings highlight the importance of sex-specific approaches in clinical evaluation.

Female PD patients in our cohort exhibited more severe impairments across cognitive and affective domains, as indicated by lower MMSE and MoCA scores, higher HAM-D scores, and greater neuropsychiatric burden on the NPI. These findings align with a Chinese study showing lower MoCA scores in females with PD [31]. However, higher MMSE scores were discovered in female PD patients in North American cohorts [32]. Such inconsistencies likely reflect complex cultural, regional, and biological influences. For example, male patients may perform worse on verbal memory but better on visuospatial and abstraction skills [31, 33], whereas females are more frequently affected by depression [33–35]. These sex-related differences may arise from a complex interplay of biological factors, including neuroprotective effects of estrogen [36, 37], and psychosocial and sociocultural influences on symptom perception, reporting, and lived experience [38, 39]. Given the mean age of our female cohort, the vast majority were post-menopausal. The loss of estrogen’s neuroprotective effects post-menopause may exacerbate cognitive and affective vulnerabilities [40], contributing to the heightened symptom severity observed in this group. Furthermore, while neuropsychiatric medications probably impact symptom severity, the comparable prescription rates of agents like rivastigmine and SNRIs between sexes in our cohort suggest that these pharmacological factors are unlikely to be the primary confounder driving the observed female-predominant vulnerabilities. These findings emphasize the importance of considering sex as a critical factor in both clinical care and research.

In our cohort, 34.8% of PD patients were diagnosed with dementia and 28.3% with depression. These prevalence rates are consistent with prior reports, with two systematic reviews estimating dementia prevalence at 31.3% and 26.3%, respectively [41, 42]. Reported prevalence of depression in PD ranges from 30% to 38% in most studies [9, 35]. Among Asian populations, a Chinese cross-sectional study reported a lower prevalence of 11.17% [43], whereas both Taiwanese and Sri Lankan cohorts reported rates of 36.6% and 37.5%, respectively [44, 45]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the burden of dementia and depression in Asian PD patients is broadly comparable to that of Western populations.

A key finding was the superior performance of clinician-rated tools over caregiver- or self-report instruments. For dementia detection, the clinician-rated MMSE and MoCA were highly sensitive (> 90%), in contrast to the caregiver-rated CDR (25%). For depression, the clinician-rated HAM-D also demonstrated higher sensitivity (88.5%) than the self-report BDI-II (65.4%). This discrepancy strongly indicates that relying solely on caregiver or patient reports may significantly underestimate the true prevalence of dementia and depression within this population. Several factors may contribute to this underestimation. Caregivers might lack awareness of the patient’s subtle cognitive impairments, or patients themselves may not recognize or disclose their difficulties. Moreover, the broad functional focus of CDR may be less sensitive to the executive and visuospatial deficits typical of early PD dementia. Cultural factors could also influence reporting, as caregivers may hesitate to describe behavioral or cognitive changes as signs of dementia. Overall, these findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on patient- or caregiver-administered measures and highlight the added value of clinician-rated assessments. Missed or underestimated symptoms may result in delayed treatment, greater caregiver burden, and suboptimal clinical management. Integrating clinician-rated tools into routine practice may therefore improve diagnostic accuracy and support early interventions to improve patient outcomes.

Our data revealed that motor symptoms are correlated with neuropsychiatric symptoms. It is noteworthy that motor symptoms are not uniformly associated with non-motor symptoms. Previous research has shown that axial bradykinesia, gait disturbance, and postural impairment are associated with more severe depression, whereas other motor subdomains such as tremor and rigidity do not correlate with depressive symptoms [46]. Similarly, bradykinesia has been linked to poorer visuospatial reasoning and psychomotor speed, while tremor, in contrast, has been associated with better performance on spatial orientation memory tests [47]. These observations suggest that distinct brain lesions correspond to specific motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Further investigations, including neuroimaging and longitudinal studies, are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these correlations.

Non-motor symptoms, including autonomic dysfunction, anosmia, constipation, and depression, can precede the emergence of motor manifestations in PD by several years [48, 49]. These prodromal features reflect early neuropathological alterations in specific neural structures, as delineated by Braak staging [50]. PD itself increases the risk of depression, suggesting a bidirectional risk relationship [3]. Neurobiological evidence also suggests shared mechanisms between depression and PD. Patients with late-onset depression often show dopaminergic deficits similar to those in PD [51]; alterations in noradrenaline, serotonin, and acetylcholine systems have also been implicated in PD and depression [9]. Recent evidence further suggests that mood disorders are closely correlated to glutamatergic deficiency [52], pointing to overlapping neurochemical disruptions in both the dopaminergic and glutamatergic systems underlying PD and depression. Moreover, cortical cholinergic denervation has been associated with both depression and dementia in PD [53], indicating overlapping pathophysiological pathways underlying cognitive decline, mood disorder, and PD.

In summary, our study highlights the pronounced impact of neuropsychiatric symptoms in PD and emphasizes the need for comprehensive, sex-specific assessments. The significant associations between non-motor and motor symptoms reinforce the importance of a holistic care approach aimed at improving quality of life. Nonetheless, our findings should be interpreted with caution. The cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding the progression of neuropsychiatric symptoms. The relatively small, single-center sample may limit generalizability. Cognitive scores such as the MMSE are inherently influenced by education levels, which were not rigorously adjusted for via stratified cutoffs in this analysis. Moreover, our quality-of-life analysis utilized the PDQ-39 summary index, and future studies should investigate domain-specific differences [54]. Although our assessments covered multiple neuropsychiatric features, other important non-motor manifestations, including anxiety, psychosis, and pain syndromes associated with depression, were not systematically evaluated. Future longitudinal studies with larger cohorts should incorporate these assessments and examine medication effects to better clarify the trajectory of cognitive and affective changes in PD. Future research should further investigate the mechanisms underlying sex-related differences and develop tailored interventions for high-risk subgroups.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the considerable impact of dementia and depression in PD and emphasizes the necessity of comprehensive, sex-specific assessments. Non-motor symptoms were closely associated with motor dysfunction and contributed substantially to the overall disease burden. Clinician-administered tools demonstrated greater reliability in detecting cognitive and affective impairments compared with self- or caregiver-report instruments. These findings underscore the need for holistic and tailored clinical approaches to improve quality of life in PD patients.
