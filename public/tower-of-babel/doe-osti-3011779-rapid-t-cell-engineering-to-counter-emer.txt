Rapid T cell engineering to counter emerging
 threats
 Claire Robertson

 22-DR-001

Abstract
Instead of genetic engineering, we sought to determine if a rapid method of membrane protein
delivery could generate functional CAR-T cells in a rapid, safer, cost-effective manner. Using cell free
protein synthesis, we generated CAR proteins embedded in a nanodisc, and thus effectively
solubilized the protein. We demonstrated the first functional CAR proteins outside of a cellular
context. We next tested uptake into immune cells and found extremely high uptake into T cells and
multiple other populations of immune cells. We found some evidence of cytotoxicity in vitro
conferred by the nanodisc delivered protein.

Background and Research Objectives
Recovery from an infection typically requires education of the adaptive immune system, comprised
of B cells, which secrete antibodies and T cells, which directly recognize and kill infected cells and
activate or regulate other immune cells. B and T cells contain an antigen specific receptor, i.e. each
cell recognizes a unique macromolecule or antigen: transferring the receptor to new cells also
transfers the reactivity to a given antigen. The relative role of antibodies and T cells depends on the
pathogen. For many pathogens, e.g. viruses and bacteria on the WHO global priority pathogen list and
select agent lists, an effective T cell response is the primary determinant of survival 1-7.
There is a robust pipeline to rapidly develop antibody therapeutics for emerging infectious diseases
(e.g. monoclonal antibodies for SARS-CoV2 were clinically available ~4 months after the start of the
pandemic8). Yet, despite the essential role of T cells in clearance of many infectious diseases, there
is currently no clinical use of T cell-based therapeutics in infectious disease. Unlike antibodies, which
can only bind the surface of pathogens exposed in bodily fluids, T cells can detect cells which have
been infected by pathogens and target them for cell killing, even in immune protected niches such as
the brain, kidney, etc., where antibodies have minimal usefulness. T cells also provide critical “helper
functions” to coordinate immune responses, optimizing B cell and antibody responses. The ability to
rapidly engineer antigen specific T cells for emerging pathogens would enable a paradigm shifting
approach to pathogen response for currently incurable diseases9. “
The barrier to T cell-based therapeutics is manufacturing. T cells with engineered antigen specificity
have proven curative a handful of highly lethal, otherwise untreatable cancers 10,11, but the intensive
extant manufacturing process for these cells is a non-starter for their use in infectious disease
treatment. Each patient’s dose of engineered T cells requires genetic modification with viral vectors
at a specialized GMP facility followed by cell expansion, a process that requires approximately 3
weeks of time at a cost of ~$500k12. Without an improved manufacturing process, engineered T cells
will only be useful in a narrow range of slow-moving infections (e.g. HIV, Epstein Barr Virus) 13,14.

Goals and Objectives:
Our goal in this research proposal is to disrupt the way that autologous transgenic T cells are
manufactured, enabling use of T cell engineering to respond to emerging pathogens with
unprecedented speed. Key to this disruption, our interdisciplinary team recently demonstrated the
unique capability to efficiently deliver proteins to cells resulting in altered cell behavior with minimal
toxicity from the delivery system 15-17. Instead of modifying cells with transgenes encoding receptors,
we plan to engineer T cells by directly delivering the receptor itself using the nanolipoprotein (NLP)
carrier system15-17 (Table 1). Due to the faster uptake, higher efficiency, lower toxicity, and biological
inertness of our system, we can transform the 3-week T cell engineering cycle into a one-day bedside
process (Fig 2), enabling the overnight deployment of engineered T cells for emerging pathogens.

1. Synthesis and characterization of CAR-NLPs
 1.1. Coexpression of CAR-NLPs

Our first goal in this work was to demonstrate that chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) could be
synthesized in nanodiscs. CARs are large transmembrane proteins and are not expected to be soluble
in aqueous solutions. Thus, we adopted methods developed for transmembrane synthesis using cell
free protein synthesis of both CAR and apolipoprotein scaffold with integrated lipids18,19.

Using an available CD19 targeting CAR construct (pSLCAR-CD19-28ζ, kind gift of Scott McComb,
Addgene plasmid #135991), we found that detectable CAR protein could be synthesized using these
methods but yield and especially solubility was poor relative to other proteins synthesized using the
same methods. We undertook a significant effort to improve yield using this strategy, including
codon optimization for expression in E. Coli lysates, CAR: Apo plasmid ratio, expression of CAR in the
presence of preformed NLPs, multiple lysate choices, scaffold proteins, lipid choice, protein folding
chaperones, redox reagents, telodendromer addition, synthesis temperature and expression time.
We found that lipid choice (Fig 1B) and synthesis temperature (Fig 1C), along with expression time
(not shown) had major effects on yield and solubility. Notably, increased synthesis temperatures
increased total yield but at the cost of protein solubility. We found that this optimization increased
protein yield from <10% to >95% and forms the basis of our first publication from this work.

 As we had observed significant variability between uptake runs, we next asked if cargo protein

loading for CAR was inferior to B2AR, similar uptake rates were observed between these two
conditions. Thus, the major obstacle to efficient delivery of CAR is CAR production and concentration.

4. Mission Impact
Our approach to T cell engineering offers a quantum leap in personalized medicine: rapid cellular
engineering (1day vs. 3weeks) at a sustainable cost (50k vs. 500k), deployable anywhere by medical
professionals (vs. requiring a manufacturing facility and expert cell technicians). The ability to
engineer T cells for infectious disease therapeutics would enable treatments or even cures for the
most dangerous pathogens currently facing humanity, including pathogens which have not yet
emerged. Pathogens on the WHO global priority pathogen list and select agent lists are pathogens
where antibiotics, protease inhibitors, vaccines and antibodies have not been proven effective 26: an
effective T cell response is the key determinant of survival in many of these diseases 1-7.
T cell-based countermeasures could secure humanity against antibiotic resistant microbes, cancer,
and any number of other cellular-level illnesses, and would greatly enhance national readiness for
biological threats, protecting the public health and directly strengthening both our national security
and bioresilience. We demonstrate a broadly applicable pipeline (analogous to the rapid
development pipeline for monoclonal antibodies), where T cell receptor complements from
recovered patients can be screened for ability to control pathogens, rapidly tested in isolation and in

Conclusion
In this work we have demonstrated the potential of CAR-NLPs for rapid engineering of immune cells.
We are actively seeking follow on funding from multiple sources to continue this work towards
preclinical and clinical development.
