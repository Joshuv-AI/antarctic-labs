 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 NLR and EDEN will partner together to develop renewable, biodegradable alternatives to
 polyfoam (polyurethane) polymer foams. The initial focus will be on developing cellulose-based
 foam with tunable mechanical properties. NLR will produce and test the cellulose-based foam
 materials and characterize its microstructure and mechanical properties. Relationships between
 mechanical performance formulation and fabrication methods will be developed.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Cellulose foam preparation and characterization

 Discussion: This task will fabricate and characterize cellulose foam samples. We will vary the
 formulation by modifying the composition of cellulose, surfactant, and crosslinking agent. The
 compression modulus of each sample will be measured and the microstructure will be
 characterized by SEM imaging.

 Contractor’s Deliverables: Samples of cellulose foams exhibiting mechanical properties that
 span the range of values specified by EDEN; Measurements of compression modulus and SEM
 images of each sample. Formula and fabrication instructions will be provided such that the
 material synthesis can be replicated by EDEN.

 At least 30 variations of cellulose foam composites were investigated. Initial investigations
 focused on cellulose nanofibers (CNF) with different surface modifications and the incorporation
 of surfactants and cross-linking agents. The results showed that nanocellulose foams produced
 by freeze-drying exhibited good overall mechanical strength, with clear performance differences
 depending on fiber type and chemical modification. TEMPO-oxidized CNF foams consistently
 demonstrated higher compressive modulus and faster recovery compared to unmodified CNF
 foams, indicating stiffer and more elastic behavior. Chemical crosslinking using citric acid
 improved foam integrity through covalent bonding with cellulose hydroxyl groups, while
 hydrophobic coatings such as trimer acid, B-Tough epoxy, and carbonated linseed oil further
 increased stiffness and compressive modulus. Overall compressive modulus calculations
 confirmed that chemically modified and TEMPO-based foams were stiffer than baseline foams.
 While all foam samples showed some degree of recovery after compression, recovery
 performance varied, with TEMPO foams recovering more rapidly than other formulations,
 indicating stronger resilience but still leaving room for improvement in elastic recovery.

 2

 Next, the use of ionic liquids was investigated to improve on the shape-recovery capabilities of
 the nanocellulose based foams. Cellulose fibers are dissolved in selected ionic liquids—including
 imidazolium-based ionic liquids such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate and related
 carboxylates, as well as cholinium-based ionic liquids such as cholinium acetate and cholinium-
 L-lysinate—followed by chemical cross-linking, primarily through ester bond formation with
 multifunctional cross-linkers. The resulting network retains the ionic liquid through non-covalent
 interactions and is foamed via agitation or gas introduction, precipitated into a solid porous
 structure, and dried by freeze-drying to produce a low-density foam. Mechanical performance
 assessed by cyclic compression testing shows tunable compressive modulus, high specific
 strength, and the ability to retain at least 90% of the original shape and volume after repeated
 compression to 50% strain. Among the ionic liquids evaluated, cholinium-based ionic liquids,
 particularly cholinium acetate, provided the most favorable balance of foam integrity and
 mechanical resilience, yielding superior compressive performance compared to other tested ionic
 liquid systems.

 The results reported in Table 1 demonstrate that the compressive strength of cellulose foams can
 be tuned over a very broad range by varying (1) the selection of ionic liquid used and (2) the
 amount of residual ionic liquid left in the sample during the washing step. Additionally,
 comparison of the specific strength of the foams, calculated as the ratio of the compression
 modulus to the density, reveals that the foam composed of 1% w/w residual 1E3MA has an
 exceptionally high specific strength. This could be particularly beneficial for applications where
 stiff, lightweight materials are desirable, such as in the aviation industry.

 Task 2: Cellulose/support composite preparation and characterization

 Discussion: This task will fabricate cellulose/support composites using the cellulose foams
 produced in Phase 1 and supports (3D-printed or other) provided by EDEN. This task will also
 investigate further material modifications to target industry specifications and performance
 metrics (e.g., flammability, moisture retention etc.) and compare properties of the resultant
 materials to industry standards.

 Contractor’s Deliverables: Samples of cellulose/composite materials exhibiting mechanical
 properties that span the range of values specified by EDEN; Measurements of compression
 modulus and SEM images of each sample. Measurements of additional material properties (e.g.,
 flammability, moisture retention etc.) Formula and fabrication instructions will be provided such
 that the material synthesis can be replicated by EDEN.

 This task was not completed, since only the first half of the CRADA was funded.
