Enhancing myrcene biosynthesis in yeast through nuclear compartmentalization
Authors: Zihan Wang, Jing Wang, Yanhui Liu, Mingxin Chen, Xiaocong Lu, Yihan Xia, Shi Xiao, Hongting Tang, Xiaoyu Yang, Yan He
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Myrcene is a high-value monoterpene extensively applied in the fragrance, flavor, and agricultural industries, yet its efficient microbial production remains challenging due to pathway competition and limited metabolic flux. Compartmentalization offers a unique strategy to spatially organize heterologous metabolic pathways in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, enabling improved pathway efficiency through physical separation from competing cytosolic metabolism. In this study, we engineered the S. cerevisiae nucleus as a synthetic metabolic compartment for myrcene biosynthesis. Screening of myrcene synthases identified two highly active enzymes from Snapdragon Oc15 and Picea abies that function efficiently in S. cerevisiae. Myrcene production was detected only when myrcene synthase and the engineered GPP synthase mERG20p were co-localized to the nucleus, whereas cytosolic expression failed to yield detectable myrcene under their co-expression. Reconstruction of the complete mevalonate (MVA) pathway in the nucleus further increased myrcene titers. By identifying and optimizing rate-limiting steps, we substantially enhanced metabolic flux toward myrcene, achieving a final titer of 23.4 mg L−1 in flask-shaking fermentation. This work demonstrates the feasibility of repurposing the yeast nucleus for myrcene efficient biosynthesis and provide a new strategy for further improving microbial production of myrcene.

Introduction

Monoterpenes constitute a large and structurally diverse class of natural products. Among them, myrcene is a high-value monoterpene widely used in the flavor and fragrance industries [1] and as a precursor for biofuels and fine chemicals [2]. Conventional myrcene production, relying on plant extraction or chemical synthesis, is increasingly constrained by low efficiency, high cost, and sustainability concerns. Currently, microbial cell factories have emerged as a promising alternative for sustainable and efficient biosynthesis of valuable products [3,4]. While the myrcene biosynthetic pathway has been successfully established in microorganisms such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae [5] and Escherichia coli [6], the production has not yet met the requirements for industrial applications.

Many efforts have been focused on microbial cell factory optimization for enhancing myrcene production. In E. coli, myrcene production has been improved to 510.38 mg L−1 [6]. However, E. coli poses biosafety concerns and lacks eukaryotic compartments. In contrast, S. cerevisiae is a generally recognized as safe (GRAS) organism with a well-characterized genetic background, mature metabolic engineering toolbox, and organized compartments [7], which is more suitable for plant-derived myrcene production. In S. cerevisiae, myrcene is synthesized by myrcene synthase using geranyl diphosphate (GPP) as the immediate precursor, which is derived from acetyl-CoA through the mevalonate (MVA) pathway. However, in S. cerevisiae, GPP is rapidly converted into farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) to support sterol biosynthesis, which is necessary for cell growth. This metabolic competition could severely limit GPP availability and result in low myrcene production.

To overcome this limitations, various metabolic engineering strategies have been used for improving myrcene production [5,8]. Zeng et al. established heterologous myrcene biosynthesis in S. cerevisiae through myrcene synthase screening, fusion expression of expression of the FPP synthase variant Erg20p(F96W, N127W) and myrcene synthase, and optimization of a two-phase fermentation system, achieving titers of 3.51 mg L−1 in shake flasks and 8.12 mg L−1 in a 5-L bioreactor [5]. Recently, Shu et al. systematically engineered S. cerevisiae for myrcene biosynthesis by integrating enzyme source screening, subcellular localization, enzyme fusion, and precursor pathway optimization. This combinatorial strategy elevated myrcene titers to 63.59–66.82 mg L−1 in shake-flask cultures and 142.64 mg L−1 in a 5L fed batch fermentation [8]. Despite the implementation of several optimization strategies, including enzyme-level engineering, myrcene production remain insufficient for industrial application, necessitating further metabolic optimization.

Subcellular compartmentalization offers several advantages, mainly relieving competition for precursors from side pathways, improving biosynthesis efficiency in confined environment, and alleviating the cytotoxicity of certain hydrophobic products and has been widely used for enhancing targeted compound biosynthesis. Yeast subcellular organelles, such as mitochondria [9,10], peroxisomes [11,12] and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [13], have been exploited for terpenoid biosynthesis. Moreover, synergistic engineering across multiple compartments can further enhance production. For instance, peroxisomal targeting increased α-humulene titers, while combined peroxisomal–cytosolic engineering achieved a 2.5-fold improvement over a purely cytosolic pathway [14]. In peroxisomes, the titer of myrcene was efficiently increased by 218-fold compared to the initial strain [8]. Therefore, subcellular engineering is also an effective strategy for enhancing myrcene production. The yeast nucleus preserves nuclear envelope integrity throughout mitosis, unlike many other eukaryotes, thereby maintaining functional compartmentalization [15]. Additionally, acetyl-CoA is actively supplied and maintained within the nucleus to support histone acetylation [16,17]. However, the yeast nucleus has not been explored as a platform for myrcene production.

In this study, we utilized the yeast nucleus as a synthetic compartment for myrcene production (Fig. 1). We firstly screened myrcene synthases from diverse sources to identify enzymes with high catalytic activity in S. cerevisiae. Myrcene production was detected when both the myrcene synthase and the FPP synthase variant Erg20p(F96W, N127W), named mERG20p, were located in the nucleus, but not when they were expressed in the cytosol. Subsequently, a complete mevalonate (MVA) pathway was introduced into the nucleus for efficient nuclear acetyl-CoA conversion, resulting in a substantial increase in myrcene titers. Finally, identification of pathway bottlenecks and optimization of rate-limiting enzymes further elevated myrcene production to 23.4 mg L−1 in shake-flask cultures with 2% galactose. Our findings demonstrate that nuclear engineering is a viable and effective strategy for myrcene biosynthesis, which offering a potential strategy to systemically optimize microbial cell factory for efficient myrcene production.Fig. 1Nuclear Engineering as a Platform for Myrcene Production. Results

Screening of myrcene synthase and its heterologous expression in yeast

Monoterpenes are generally toxic to S. cerevisiae, and two-phase extractive fermentation is commonly employed to mitigate their toxicity. Accordingly, we first evaluated myrcene toxicity in the presence of dodecane, which is routinely used for in situ extraction during monoterpene production. No significant growth inhibition of the wild-type strain CEN.PK2–1C was observed at the tested myrcene concentrations (from 0 to 10 g L−1) when dodecane was added (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Reconstructing biosynthetic pathways for high-value compounds in S. cerevisiae amenable to large-scale fermentation requires the identification of enzymes with optimal catalytic performance. To construct an efficient myrcene biosynthetic pathway, several myrcene synthases (MyrSs) from different sources were analyzed by phylogenetic tree analysis and classified into five groups (Fig. 2A and B). Based on successful heterologous expression in E. coli, product specificity (Supplementary Fig. 3) and myrcene abundance in native host organisms, QiMyrS from Quercus ilex [18], HlMyrS from Humulus lupulus [19], SdMyrS from Snapdragon [20], PaMyrS from Picea abies [21] were selected for expression in S. cerevisiae. Each MyrS was expressed in the wild-type strain CEN.PK2–1C after removal of the predicted N-terminal plastid-targeting peptide (SignalP 4.1), using a high-copy plasmid under the control of the strong galactose-inducible promoter SkGAL2p from Saccharomyces kudriavzevii [22]. To address this limitation, MyrSs were subsequently expressed in the yCAN10 strain, which accumulates elevated levels of GPP through overexpression of mevalonate (MVA) pathway genes driven by the native bidirectional GAL1/10 promoter [23]. These included ERG19p, ERG8p, ERG12p, IDI1p, mERG20p, and EfMvaE and EfMvaS from Enterococcus faecalis, which were heterologously expressed to perform the same function as the corresponding enzymatic steps, catalyzing the conversion of acetyl-CoA to mevalonate with high efficiency and reduced feedback inhibition [24]. Except for HlMyrS, all tested MyrSs exhibited detectable activity, with PaMyrS showing the highest myrcene production (Fig. 2C and D), reaching 1.0 mg L−1 at 24 h. PaMyrS was therefore selected for subsequent studies. Prolonging the fermentation time further increased myrcene titers to 3.8 mg L−1 at 96 h (Supplementary Fig. 4A), comparable to previously reported levels [5], and cell growth was stable at 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Nevertheless, myrcene production remained relatively low despite cytosolic overexpression of the MVA pathway in yCAN10, indicating additional pathway bottlenecks.

Developing yeast nucleus as platform for myrcene biosynthesis

Nuclear compartment offers distinct advantages for metabolic engineering, including increasing pathway flux via local enrichment of enzymes and intermediates, as well as reduced competition for intermediates from endogenous pathways. To produce myrcene efficiently, we pursued subcellular engineering to harness the nucleus as a platform, and the peroxisome was used as the positive control (Fig. 3A). To localize myrcene synthase in the nucleus, the SV40 sequence, a nuclear localization sequence (NLS), was fused to the C-terminus of PaMyrS. In contrast, PaMyrS with a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting signal (SKL) was used for peroxisomal localization (Fig. 3A). For localization verification, red fluorescent protein (mCherry) was fused to N-terminus of PaMyrS. DAPI staining was used as the positive control of the nucleus. Cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) fused with the native peroxisomal protein (PEX8p) was used as a positive control for peroxisomal localization. Fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed that the red fluorescence from PaMyrS with mCherry overlayed the blue fluorescence from DAPI staing, demonstrating the efficient targeting of NLS-tagged enzyme to the nucleus. Similarly, the red fluorescence was also overlayed the blue fluorescence from CFP, showing the correct peroxisomal localization (Fig. 3B). Despite correct localization, no detectable myrcene production was observed in strains expressing PaMyrS alone by a high-copy plasmid, whether localized to the nucleus (WT09) or peroxisome (WT07), similar to the cytosol (WT03) (Fig. 3B). To enhance GPP supply, mERG20p was co-expressed under the GAL1 promoter and fluorescence microscopy analysis confirmed the correct nuclear and peroxisomal localization (Fig. 3C). Co-expression of nuclear-localized mERG20p and PaMyrS (WT10) enabled myrcene production at 0.9 mg L−1, comparable to that obtained with peroxisomal targeting (WT08, 1.0 mg L−1), whereas cytosolic co-expression (WT06) failed to produce detectable myrcene (Fig. 3D). These results suggest the active expression of PaMyrS and mERG20p in the nucleus and peroxisome with NLS or SKL fusion, and precursors IPP and DMAPP can be transported into the nucleus for GPP formation and myrcene biosynthesis. Additionally, nuclear myrcene production did not affect cell growth, as well as peroxisomal expression, compared to the control strain WT01 with empty plasmid expression (Supplementary Fig. 5), demonstrating the feasibility of exploiting the yeast nucleus as an effective compartment for myrcene biosynthesis.

Reconstructing the MVA pathway in the nuclues improved myrcene production

To conduct nucleus acetyl-CoA toward GPP synthesis, the complete mevalonate (MVA) pathway was reconstructed within the nucleus by genomic integration of all pathway enzymes under the control of GAL1/10 promoters. Herein, EfMvaE and EfMvaS were overexpressed to enhance the conversion of acetyl-CoA to mevalonate due to their high efficiency and reduced feedback inhibition compared with the native enzymes (ERG10p, ERG13p, and Hmg1/2p) [25]. The localization of all enzymes in the nucleus were confirmed by fluorescence microscopy analysis (Fig. 4A). The growth curves demonstrate nuclear engineering by introducing the whole monoterpene biosynthetic pathway was not affect strain fitness (Supplementary Fig. 6). Only strains harboring the fully reconstructed nuclear MVA pathway exhibited a marked increase in myrcene production, with strain NLS0301 producing 12.6 mg L−1 (Fig. 4B). Because PaMyrS and mERG20p were initially expressed from plasmids, pathway robustness may have been compromised by plasmid instability. To overcome this issue, both genes were subsequently integrated into the yeast genome. In strains harboring the complete nuclear or peroxisomal MVA pathway, expression of mERG20p alone did not produce detectable myrcene, whereas co-expression of PaMyrS and mERG20p enabled myrcene synthesis. The nuclear production of myrcene was higher than the peroxisomal production with improved 61% (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that the limiting step of myrcene biosynthesis dynamically shifts depending on pathway expression levels. Under low-level PaMyrS expression conditions, compartment-dependent enzymatic efficiency plays a dominant role, whereas under high-expression conditions, precursor availability, particularly GPP supply, becomes the primary bottleneck.

Engineering rate-limiting steps in monoterpene synthetic pathway in the nucleus

To identify rate-limiting steps in the nuclear myrcene synthetic pathway, we compensated for the expression of chromosomally encoded enzymes in NLS05 using a high-copy plasmid. Overexpression of PaMyrS resulted in a 7.8-fold increase in myrcene production compared to the control strain with an empty plasmid expression (Fig. 5A). Co-overexpression of PaMyrS with mERG20p resulted in an additional 3.1-fold increase in production (Fig. 5A). In contrast, overexpression of other enzymes, including ERG12p, IDI1p, ERG19p, ERG8p, EfMvaE, and EfMvaS, had no positive effect on myrcene production (Fig. 5A). To overcome these rate-limiting steps and enhance titers, we increased genomic expression of mERG20p and MyrS. We proposed to use both PaMyrS and SdMyrS which also exhibited high activity (Fig. 2C) to reduce homologous recombination resulting from repeated use of PaMyrS. Initially, we verified that NLS-tagged SdMyrS was efficiently expressed and correctly localized to the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 9). In addition, SdMyrS exhibited higher protein expression levels than PaMyrS in the nuclear compartment (Supplementary Fig. 10). We then assessed whether the addition of the NLS would compromise SdMyrS activity. Surprisingly, expression of NLS-tagged SdMyrS significantly enhanced myrcene production, a 10.7-fold improvement compared to NLS05 expressing NLS-tagged PaMyrS, with the resulting strain NLS06 producing 10.3 mg L−1 of myrcene (Fig. 5B). This demonstrated that SdMyrS fused with NLS exhibited considerably higher activity than PaMyrS (Supplementary Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 7B). Co-overexpression of SdMyrS and PaMyrS (NLS07) did not lead to a further increase in myrcene production compared to the individual expression of SdMyrS (NLS06) (Fig. 5B). We then increased a copy of SdMyrS in NLS07 and the resulting strain NLS08 yielded 21.6 mg L−1 of myrcene, a 2.1-fold improvement over the parent strain NLS07 (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the integration of an additional copy of mERG20p (NLS09) increased myrcene production by 8.6% compared to NLS08 (Fig. 5B). However, further increasing the expression of SdMyrS (NLS10) did not lead to a subsequent increase in myrcene production (Fig. 5B), which was accompanied by decreased intracellular GPP levels (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that precursor availability became limiting under high-expression conditions, indicating that additional metabolic engineering is required to further enhance precursor supply.

Discussion

The nucleus of S. cerevisiae, similar to other intracellular organelles, provides a spatially confined environment that is advantageous for enzyme colocalization, local substrate enrichment, and insulation from competing bypass pathways. In this study, we repurposed the yeast nucleus as a synthetic compartment for myrcene biosynthesis by reconstructing the complete mevalonate (MVA) pathway and introducing heterologous myrcene synthases. This strategy enabled a myrcene titer of 23.4 mg L−1, demonstrating the feasibility of nuclear metabolic engineering and establishing a potentially generalizable platform for the production of MVA-derived compounds. Of note, the nucleus is also known to harbor acetyl-CoA pools that support histone and protein acetylation [16,17], further highlighting its potential as a productive chassis for acetyl-CoA derived metabolite biosynthesis. We acknowledge that acetyl-CoA availability within the nucleus may become a limiting factor for further improving metabolic flux. Previous studies have demonstrated that increasing cytosolic acetyl-CoA supply through overexpressing native acetyl-CoA synthases [26] or introducing heterologous acetyl-CoA biosynthetic pathways [[27], [28], [29]] can significantly enhance improve target compound production. Therefore, we propose that similar strategies could be implemented in the nucleus, such as introducing acetyl-CoA synthetic pathways, to further strengthen precursor supply and support higher flux through the engineered pathway.

Monoterpene synthases are often characterized by low product specificity, which can lead to the formation of multiple by-products and reduced yields of the desired compound [[30], [31], [32]]. This intrinsic promiscuity underscores the importance of enzyme selection for efficient heterologous myrcene production. By screening of multiple myrcene synthases, we identified PaMyrS as the most active enzyme, followed by SdMyrS, to our knowledge, both enzymes were characterized for the first time in S. cerevisiae. Consistent with previous reports, the active enzymes did not generate detectable by-products, indicating high product fidelity [8]. Interestingly, when targeted to the nucleus, NLS-tagged SdMyrS outperformed NLS-tagged PaMyrS in myrcene biosynthesis, despite its lower activity in the non-targeted context. This observation suggests that identical localization signals can differentially influence enzyme activity depending on the protein-specific properties, such as folding, stability, or interactions within the nuclear milieu. These findings emphasize that subcellular targeting strategies must consider not only localization efficiency but also the compatibility between targeting sequences and enzymes.

Subcellular compartmentalization has emerged as a powerful strategy in metabolic engineering for enhancing the biosynthesis of high-value compounds. Previous efforts have localized monoterpene biosynthesis to mitochondria [13] or peroxisomes [8] to shield the GPP precursor from rapid cytosolic consumption and thereby increase product titers. Moreover, synergistic engineering across multiple compartments has been shown to further boost productivity. For instance, peroxisomal targeting increased α-humulene titers, while combined peroxisomal–cytosolic engineering achieved a 2.5-fold improvement over a purely cytosolic pathway [15]. In this study, our work introduces the yeast nucleus as an additional compartment for myrcene biosynthesis, offering new opportunities for multi-compartment and synergistic pathway engineering to further enhance myrcene production.

This study demonstrates that the yeast nucleus possesses substantial untapped potential for the biosynthesis of myrcene. By reconstructing the complete MVA pathway and optimizing nuclear-localized myrcene synthases, we achieved efficient myrcene production in S. cerevisiae, demonstrating that nuclear compartmentalization can overcome key limitations associated with cytosolic monoterpene biosynthesis, including precursor competition and suboptimal enzyme performance. Given the industrial relevance of myrcene as a versatile precursor for fragrances, flavors, and pharmaceutical intermediates, our nuclear engineering strategy provides a promising alternative route for its sustainable microbial production.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, and reagents

All codon-optimized genes, localization sequences, plasmids, and strains in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 3, and Supplementary Table 4.

The Phanta® Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, Rapid Taq Master Mix, and DNA gel purification was obtained from Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Kits for RNA isolation. plasmid extractions were supported by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Myrcene and dodecane were provided by Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd. (USA). The DAPI staining solution was purchased from MedChemExpress Co., Ltd. (USA). Gibson assembly reaction mix was prepared in-house as previously described [33].

Genetic manipulation

The S. cerevisiae strain CEN.PK2–1C was used as the start strain. All primers used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 5. The promoters, terminators, and homologous flanking regions were employed CEN.PK2–1C genomic DNA as template. The guide RNA (gRNA) plasmids were constructed as previously reported [34]. The 2 μm-derived plasmid pJFE3 was used for high-level gene expression. DNA fragments and linearized plasmid backbones were conducted by Gibson assembly method. Gene fragments assembly were carried out by using fusion PCR. The codon-optimized genes were provided by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd.

Yeast transformation

Yeast transformation was performed by using the high-efficiency Liac/SS carrier DNA/PEG method [35]. Briefly, 1-2 μg repair fragments and gRNA plasmids were co-transformed into yeast cells and subsequently plated on synthetic complete without uracil (SC-Ura) and/or synthetic complete without tryptophan (SC-Trp) media. After plate screening and verification by PCR, correctly positive clone was subsequently plated on 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) medium to remove gRNA plasmid. The expression plasmids carrying the target gene, or its compartmentalized localization variant were constructed and processed in the same manner.

Cultivation condition

DH5α was cultured at 37 °C using Luria–Bertani medium (5 g L−1 yeast extract, 10 g L−1 tryptone, and 10 g L−1 NaCl, 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin). Yeast was cultivated at 30 °C using YPD medium (10 g L−1 yeast extract, 20 g L−1 peptone, and 20 g L⧿1 glucose). The speed of shake cultivation was routinely set at 220 rpm.

For flask-shaking fermentation, yeast single colony was inoculated into 1 mL of YPD medium and cultured overnight. The precultures were then transferred into 100 mL shake flasks containing 20 mL of YPG medium to an initial OD600 of 0.2, overlaid with 20% (v/v) dodecane, and incubated for 96 h. For evaluation of myrcene toxicity, myrcene at concentrations of 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, or 10 g L−1 were added to the 20 mL YPD medium. Overnight cultures were inoculated into corresponding solution system to an initial OD600 of 0.2 for 72 h. Cell growth was monitored by measuring OD600 at defined time points over a 3-day period.

Fluorescence confocal and western blot analysis

Yeast cells cultured for 12–24 h were harvested by centrifugation, washed three times with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 min. After three additional washes with PBS, the cells were stained with 1 μg mL−1 DAPI for 3 min, centrifuged, and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then resuspended in PBS, and 4 μL of the suspension was mounted on a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (A1R, Nikon, Japan). Excitation was performed at 488 nm for eGFP, 561 nm for mCherry, and 405 nm for DAPI and CFP. Image acquisition and processing were performed using NIS-Elements software. To assess the expression level of the target protein, the plasmids with flag tag were introduced into the CEN.PK2–1C strain and cultured in SC-Ura for 24–36 h. Cell lysates were prepared, followed by separation on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to a nitrocellulose blotting membrane (cat. 10600002, Amersham). Flag-tag Monoclonal antibody (cat. A8592, dilution 1:5000, Sigma-Aldrich or cat. 66008-4-Ig, dilution 1:5000, Proteintech) and GAPDH Monoclonal antibody (cat. ab181602, dilution 1:5000, Abcam or cat. 60004-1-Ig, dilution 1:5000, Proteintech) were used for detection. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (cat. no. 34580; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and captured with a chemiluminescence detection system (Tanon 5200 Multi).

Analytical methods

Fermentation broths were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. An aliquot of 100 μL dodecane overlay was mixed with 900 μL ethyl acetate and further diluted 10-fold with ethyl acetate prior to analysis. Gas chromatography mass spectrometry detection was carried out on a HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, USA) using an Agilent GC–MS system (8890 GC coupled to a 7000D MS, USA). The oven temperature was initially held at 60 °C for 5 min, increased to 80 °C at 10 °C min−1, then to 90 °C at 1 °C min−1, and finally ramped to 240 °C at 30 °C min−1, followed by a final hold at 300 °C for 5 min. The inlet temperature was set to 250 °C with a split ratio of 2:1. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The mass spectrometer operated in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV, and data were acquired in single-ion monitoring (SIM) mode. Data analysis was processed using MassHunter Workstation software (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Intracellular GPP extraction was performed as previously described [36]. Quantification of GPP in yeast cells was carried out using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus system coupled with a Triple Quad 6500+ mass spectrometer (LC–MS/MS).

Chromatographic separation was achieved on an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent, USA) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1. The mobile phases consisted of 10 mM ammonium carbonate containing 0.1% ammonium hydroxide (v/v) as solvent A and acetonitrile/methanol (75:25, v/v) supplemented with 0.1% ammonium hydroxide as solvent B. Gradient elution conditions were applied as previously reported [37].

Mass spectrometric parameters were optimized as follows: curtain gas, 35 psi; collision gas, medium; ionspray voltage, −4500 V; source temperature, 550 °C; ion source gases 1 and 2, 55 psi. Quantification was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using the ion transition m/z 313 → 78.9, with a declustering potential of −40 V and collision energy of −40 V. Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using OS-Q software (AB Sciex, USA).
