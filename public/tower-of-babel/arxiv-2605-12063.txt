Memory Constrained Adversarial Hypothesis Testing
Malhar A. Managoli and Vinod M. Prabhakaran

Abstract
We study adversarial binary hypothesis testing under memory constraints. The test is a time-invariant randomized finite state machine (FSM) with S S states. Associated with each hypothesis is a set of distributions. Given the hypothesis, the distribution of each sample is chosen from the set associated with the hypothesis by an adversary who has access to past samples and the history of states of the FSM so far. We obtain upper and lower bounds on the minimax asymptotic probability of error as a function of S S . The bounds have the same exponential behaviour in S S and match for a class of problems.



I
Introduction

In binary hypothesis testing, one of the most basic statistical inference tasks, given two distributions
p_{0},p_{1}
, and independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples drawn according to one of them, the goal is to determine which of the two distributions the samples came from.
Often,

and

are not exactly known. Composite hypothesis testing considers subsets of distributions
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, and samples are drawn i.i.d. according to a fixed (but unknown) distribution from one of the two sets. In classical robust statistics, these subsets
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
are “neighbourhoods” of the ideal model distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, respectively,
[
10
,
9
,
13
]
. Fangwei and Shiyi
[
6
,
7
]
studied a variant in which the distribution may vary from sample to sample (but all distributions are from one of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
). More recently, Brandão et al.
[
3
]
considered adversarial hypothesis testing, in which the distribution of each sample may be adversarially chosen (from one of the sets) depending on previous samples. Other forms of robustness have also been studied in the context of hypothesis testing
[
11
,
16
,
15
,
14
]
and, more generally, statistical inference
[
5
]
.

Another practical consideration is that testing algorithms have finite memory. Cover
[
4
]
initiated the study of hypothesis testing with memory constraints by modelling the algorithm as a finite state machine (FSM). If the FSM is allowed to be time-variant, it is known that arbitrarily small errors can be achieved with only three states
[
4
,
12
]
. For the binary hypothesis testing problem, Hellman and Cover
[
8
]
obtained the optimal trade-off between error probability and the number of states for time-invariant randomized FSMs. More recently, Berg, Ordentlich, and Shayevitz
[
1
]
obtained upper and lower bounds on this trade-off for time-invariant deterministic FSMs. The problem of memory constrained algorithms for statistical inference has received significant recent attention; see
[
2
]
for an excellent survey.

In this work, we study adversarial hypothesis testing using memory constrained algorithms. Like Hellman and Cover
[
8
]
, we consider time-invariant randomized FSMs. The adversary chooses the distribution of each sample based on previous samples as in Brandão et al.
[
3
]
. Furthermore, we allow the adversary’s choice to also depend on the history of states of the FSM so far. The adversary’s choices of distributions are confined to one of two sets,
\mathcal{P}_{0}
or
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, depending on whether the hypothesis is
H=0
or
H=1
, respectively. We obtain upper and lower bounds on the optimal minimax asymptotic error probability for a given number of states
S
. These bounds exhibit the same exponential behaviour in
S
and match for a class of problems.

We show our achievability using an FSM inspired by that of Hellman and Cover. Their FSM, with states
\{1,\ldots,S\}
, for testing
p
versus
q
distributed on
\mathcal{X}
, is organized as a
birth-and-death chain
that makes transitions to the right (resp. left) only on observing the letter
x\in\mathcal{X}
that maximizes (resp. minimizes) the likelihood ratio
q(x)/p(x)
(see Section
II-A
for a brief description). Arguably, this letter provides the best evidence for
H=1
(resp.
H=0
). In the adversarial setting, no one letter may provide good evidence under all possible distributions. We assign weights to the letters and these weights determine the probability with which our FSM transitions to the right/left. While Hellman and Cover analyse the stationary distribution of their FSM under
p
and
q
, to handle the adversarial setting, which may preclude ergodicity, we employ a martingale-based analysis. To show our converse, we demonstrate a saddle-point property for the optimal error exponent (of S) that we achieve and relate it to the Hellman-Cover exponent minimized over a pair of distributions
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
,
q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.

II-B

Classical Robust Statistics/Composite Case

In composite hypothesis testing, samples are generated i.i.d. according to
p
or
q
, depending on the hypothesis, but
p
and
q
are unknown, except that
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
(where
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
need not be closed or convex). Classical robust statistics considers
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
which are “neighbourhoods” of a pair of known ideal model distributions
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, respectively
[
10
]
. An important concept in this context is that of the least favourable distribution (LFD) pair.

Definition 1
.

For
\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{X})
,
\left(p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{0},q^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\right)
is said to be an LFD pair, if for every
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim p}\left[\frac{q^{*}(X)}{p^{*}(X)}>\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim p^{*}}\left[\frac{q^{*}(X)}{p^{*}(X)}>\eta\right],\;\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim q}\left[\frac{q^{*}(X)}{p^{*}(X)}>\eta\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim q^{*}}\left[\frac{q^{*}(X)}{p^{*}(X)}>\eta\right],\;\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}

(7)

It is known that an LFD pair exists when
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
are neighbourhoods of fixed distributions with respect to additive corruption, total-variation distance, Prohorov distance, etc.
[
9
]
. Classically, when an LFD pair
(p^{*},q^{*})
exists, the likelihood ratio test for
p^{*}
vs
q^{*}
performs at least as well as for testing
p
versus
q
for any pair
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0},q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
. It turns out, this continues to hold for memory constrained composite hypothesis testing.
When an LFD pair
(p^{*},q^{*})
exists, Hellman and Cover’s solution designed for testing
p^{*}
versus
q^{*}
performs at least as well for testing
p
versus
q
for any pair
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0},q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.
In fact, we observe that this remains true for a weaker condition than the existence of an LFD pair.

Definition 2
.

For
\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, we call
\left(p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{0},q^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\right)
a weak LFD pair if the sets
\mathcal{X}_{+}:=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{q^{*}(x)}{p^{*}(x)}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{-}:=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{q^{*}(x)}{p^{*}(x)}
satisfy

\displaystyle p(\mathcal{X}_{+})\leq p^{*}(\mathcal{X}_{+}),

\displaystyle p(\mathcal{X}_{-})\geq p^{*}(\mathcal{X}_{-}),\;\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0},

(8)

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{X}_{+})\geq q^{*}(\mathcal{X}_{+}),

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{X}_{-})\leq q^{*}(\mathcal{X}_{-}),\;\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}.

(9)

VII
Discussion

Memory limitation is a realistic resource constraint, and it is important to understand its impact on adversarially robust statistical inference. In the context of the problem considered here, many aspects are worth studying, including other contamination models,
speed of convergence, and power of randomness.
It is known from the results of
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
that randomness is a useful resource for (non-adversarial) hypothesis testing using finite memory time-invariant algorithms.
For delineating the power of randomness, three variations of the problem we studied could be considered: (i) deterministic FSM, (ii) randomized FSM whose randomness (random coins) are known to the adversary in advance (note that the adversary we considered does not know the randomness that the FSM will use in the future), and (iii) a weaker adversary who does not see the FSM’s states.
Note that our converse is, in fact, based on this weaker adversary.

,

then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbf{X}}\min_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}f(x,y)=\min_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{X}}f(x,y)

(32)

The basic idea of the proof is that due to
\min_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\leq\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}}{\sum_{i}b_{i}}\leq\max_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
,
the function
\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex and quasi-concave in each of its four inputs when the other three are fixed.
However,
\gamma
is not jointly quasi-convex in
(f,g)
, or quasi-concave in
(p,q)
.
Thus, to prove the lemma, we will write our optimisation problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\\
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{+},f_{-}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]\end{subarray}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]}\sup_{g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]}\gamma(f,g;p,q),

and then repeatedly apply Sion’s theorem to swap each
\min
with each
\max
.

Before we start applying Sion’s theorem, we need to ensure its conditions are met.
We set
\mathbf{Y}
to either
\mathcal{P}_{0}
or
\mathcal{P}_{1}
as the case may be.
Both of these are convex and compact.
Note that
\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is invariant to rescaling
f
or
g
.
Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}g(x)=1
.
We set
\mathbf{X}
to be either
\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X}):=\{g\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}):\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[g]>0\}
or
\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X}):=\{f\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}):\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]>0\}
as the case may be.
With this choice of domain,
\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is upper semi-continuous in
f
and
g
.
(Recall that we have the convention
\frac{0}{0}=0
.
The same issue does not arise for
p,q
, since there we only need lower semi-continuity.)
It is easy to check that
\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})
and
\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})
are convex.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle\gamma={}

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q).

We need one more fact for this proof which is that the function
\gamma(f,g;p,q)
splits as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma(f,g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]\mathbb{E}_{p}[g]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p}[g]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]},

\displaystyle=:{}

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}(f;p,q)\gamma_{-}(g;p,q),

and also as

\displaystyle\gamma(f,g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]\mathbb{E}_{p}[g]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p}[g]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]},

\displaystyle=:{}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;p).

Note that the functions
\gamma,\gamma_{+},\gamma_{-},\gamma_{0},
and
\gamma_{1}
all have convex domain for
f
and/or
g
, and convex, compact domain for
p
and/or
q
.
Furthermore,
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
are quasi-concave in
f
and
g
respectively; and
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
are quasi-convex in
p
and
q
respectively.

Step 1

We first show that
\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
p
and quasi-concave in
g
:

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;\lambda p+(1-\lambda)p^{\prime},q)={}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;\lambda p+(1-\lambda)p^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\max\{\gamma_{0}(f,g;p)\,,\,\gamma_{0}(f,g;p^{\prime})\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;p)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;p^{\prime})\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p^{\prime}q)\right\}.

Hence,
\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
p
.
Now, since

\displaystyle\gamma(f,\lambda g+(1-\lambda)g^{\prime};p,q)\geq{}

\displaystyle\min\{\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\gamma(f,g^{\prime};p,q)\},

we have

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,\lambda g+(1-\lambda)g^{\prime};p,q)\geq{}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\min\{\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\gamma(f,g^{\prime};p,q)\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g^{\prime};p,q)\right\}.

Hence,
\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-concave in
g
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)=\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)

(33)

Step 2

We now want to show that
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
p
and quasi-concave in
f
.

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;\lambda p+(1-\lambda)p^{\prime},q)={}

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\left(\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\right)\gamma_{0}(f,g;\lambda p+(1-\lambda)p^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\left(\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\right)\max\{\gamma_{0}(f,g;p)\,,\,\gamma_{0}(f,g;p^{\prime})\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\max\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;p)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{1}(f,g;q)\gamma_{0}(f,g;p^{\prime})\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\max\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p^{\prime},q)\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p^{\prime},q)\right\}.

Hence,
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
p
.

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(\lambda f+(1-\lambda)f^{\prime},g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(\lambda f+(1-\lambda)f^{\prime},g;p,q),

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma_{+}(\lambda f+(1-\lambda)f^{\prime};p,q)\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{-}(g;p,q),

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\min\{\gamma_{+}(f;p,q)\,,\,\gamma_{+}(f^{\prime};p,q)\}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{-}(g;p,q),

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\min\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{-}(g;p,q)\gamma_{+}(f;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{-}(g;p,q)\gamma_{+}(f^{\prime};p,q)\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\min\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f^{\prime},g;p,q)\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\left\{\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f^{\prime},g;p,q)\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f^{\prime},g;p,q)\right\},

where we can swap the min and the max in the first and last lines since
\gamma(f,g;p,q)
satisfies the conditions for Sion’s theorem.
Hence
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-concave in
f
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\gamma(f,g;p,q),

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q).

(34)

Step 3

For the final step we show that
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
q
and quasi-concave in
f
:

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(\lambda f+(1-\lambda)f^{\prime},g;p,q)={}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\lambda f+(1-\lambda)f^{\prime};p,q)\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{0}(g;p,q),

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\min\{\gamma_{1}(f;p,q)\,,\,\gamma_{1}(f^{\prime};p,q)\}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{0}(g;p,q),

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\left\{\gamma_{1}(f;p,q)\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{0}(g;p,q)\,,\,\gamma_{1}(f^{\prime};p,q)\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma_{0}(g;p,q)\right\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f^{\prime},g;p,q)\right\}.

Hence,
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-concave in
f
.
Since

\displaystyle\gamma(f,g;p,\lambda q+(1-\lambda)q^{\prime})\leq\max\{\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\gamma(f,g;p,q^{\prime})\},

we have

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,\lambda q+(1-\lambda)q^{\prime})\leq{}

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\max\{\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\gamma(f,g;p,q^{\prime})\},

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max\left\{\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)\,,\,\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q^{\prime})\right\}.

Hence,
\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)
is quasi-convex in
q
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q)=\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\sup_{f\in\Delta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\sup_{g\in\Delta_{0}(\mathcal{X})}\gamma(f,g;p,q).

(35)

Combining (
33
), (
34
), and (
35
) gives the result.
∎
