“Once Shocked, Twice Shy”: A case of post-traumatic stress disorder after a single inappropriate ICD shock
Authors: Taylor L. Stallings, Caroline Martin, Rajasekhar Nekkanti, Samuel F. Sears
Published: 2026

Introduction

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are effective for primary and secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death, as established via multiple clinical trials.1,2 Despite these clinical benefits, the patient experience of living with an ICD can be challenging, often requiring emotional and behavioral adjustment. Psychological distress is common, with notable rates of anxiety (22.6%), depression (15.8%), and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (12.4%).3 Key challenges include lifestyle disruption and device-related concerns such as fear of shock recurrence, fear of physical activity, poor device acceptance, and body image concerns.4, 5, 6, 7 The presence of device-related concerns has been associated with increased health care utilization, hospital readmissions, and 1-year mortality, although it has not been shown to be significantly associated with shock occurrence.8,9

The patient experience of ICD shock remains among the most unique in all of medicine. ICD shocks, both adjudicated appropriate and inappropriate, have an estimated combined annual prevalence of approximately 4%–6%.10,11 Most commonly, inappropriate shocks originate from supraventricular tachycardia, lead or device malfunction, or sensing errors.12 Although inappropriate shocks may theoretically cause less distress owing to their association with non–life-threatening arrhythmias, the patient’s experience of shock regardless of etiology may foster psychological distress. This case highlights a crucial consideration for cardiologists: patients’ perceived threat often outweighs objective medical reassurance. We report a case of a single inappropriate ICD shock delivered by a dual-chamber device resulting in the development of PTSD.

Case report

The patient is a disabled black male in his 40s with a medical history of ischemic cardiomyopathy, non–ST-segment elevated myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; left ventricular ejection fraction <30%), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation status post pulmonary vein isolation ablation, and type II diabetes mellitus. He had a dual-chamber ICD placed approximately 2 years before his presentation to our clinic with no previous ICD shocks until receiving a single, adjudicated inappropriate ICD shock owing to atrial flutter with rapid ventricular rate. He was referred by his cardiologist to our specialty cardiac psychology service with concerns regarding PTSD secondary to inappropriate ICD shock.

Clinical report

The patient presented with distress status post ICD shock, which occurred approximately 2 months prior. During the interview, he reported symptoms consistent with PTSD including phantom ICD shocks at night. He reported the cardinal features of depressed mood and anhedonia, including some thoughts about death and escape from his worries about ICD shocks. Safety assessment was performed and the patient was able to confirm his self-safety. Finally, we assessed his cardiac-specific adjustment and identified the presence of the “big 3” of cardiac psychological adjustment including avoidance of physical activity or exertion, somatic hypervigilance, and catastrophizing. Psychological assessment confirmed that the shock episode was traumatic and was perceived as potentially fatal. This correlated with the development of persistent cardiac-related anxiety, behavioral avoidance, and trauma-related distress. He reported pervasive fear of shock recurrence and mortality concerns. He disclosed the use of clonazepam 0.5 mg as needed, up to 3 times daily, with minimal symptom reduction.

Psychological patient-reported outcomes

The patient completed 6 validated self-report measures assessing general anxiety,13 depression,14 PTSD symptoms,15 cardiac-specific anxiety,16 ICD shock-related anxiety,17 and device acceptance.18

Results indicated clinically significant distress across all domains. His scores were consistent with severe generalized anxiety and moderately severe depressive symptoms, although further clinical discussion revealed that most depressive and anxious symptoms were cardiac related. He met the clinical threshold for PTSD symptoms related to an inappropriate ICD shock. Cardiac-specific measures revealed elevated anxiety across all subdomains, particularly heart-focused attention and ICD trigger–related fears. The patient also endorsed low device acceptance, with elevated device-related distress, body image concerns, and reluctance to return to normal life.

The clinical interview and psychological assessment findings were consistent with a diagnosis of PTSD (F43.1) secondary to ICD shock. Treatment

The treatment goals for ICD patients with PTSD symptoms should be 2-fold: (1) increasing patients’ knowledge of both their heart and device functioning and (2) using cognitive-behavioral therapy to address maladaptive cognitions, reduce behavioral avoidance, and increase exposure to feared stimuli. Exposure-based exercises can be implemented within cardiac-pulmonary rehabilitation, including exercise treadmill testing and structured walking programs. These interventions intentionally elicit increases in heart rate and dyspnea, allowing patients to directly experience these physiological responses while recognizing their safety in the context of adaptive health behaviors. Moreover, the inclusion of mindfulness-based relaxation techniques such as diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, and guided imagery can foster a reduction in stress and physiological arousal. Consistent attention to treatment adherence is also essential for patients to manage PTSD-related symptoms.

Comprehensive management of ICD patients can address both pre- and postimplantation management: preimplantation strategies include patient education, psychological assessment, and early stress management interventions to reduce anxiety. Postimplantation care involves monitoring of physiological or psychological responses, while reinforcing adherence to rehabilitation, and providing appropriate referral to a clinical health psychologist or another licensed mental health provider for evaluation and treatment of emerging PTSD or anxiety-related symptoms. In contrast to behavioral approaches, pharmacologic interventions have limited support as a standalone treatment for PTSD. PTSD is fundamentally characterized by avoidance and hyperarousal, processes that are most effectively addressed through exposure-based interventions. Accordingly, treatments that do not engage in these mechanisms may be insufficient, and reliance on pharmacologic treatment alone may hinder the development of tolerance to anxiety or disrupt the therapeutic gains associated with cognitive-behavioral interventions. After the evaluation, the patient opted to initiate treatment beginning with the ICD shock and stress management protocol.19,20 Initial focus was placed on psychoeducation to address ICD shock-related stress, anxiety, and avoidance alongside diaphragmatic breathing and behavioral activation. The patient showed initial engagement by attending 3 consecutive treatment sessions with early signs of symptom relief; however, he subsequently engaged in further avoidance of trauma-related stimuli and treatment became intermittent. Furthermore, he engaged in crisis-driven use of services; as such, it was evident that the patient modulates psychotherapeutic engagement based on his threshold for distress, limiting his treatment progress.

Discussion

This case highlights that the underlying medical causation of an ICD shock is often not associated with psychological distress.21,22 Some providers may minimize the distress experienced by this patient because it was not associated with an actual threat to the patient’s life, such as an inappropriate ICD shock. However, the patient perceives and feels the shock similarly, often interpreting it as potentially lethal regardless of etiology. Previous research from the MADIT-RIT trial indicated that even antitachycardia pacing episodes, without patients experiencing actual shock, can increase shock-related anxiety, suggesting that the perception of a possible shock alone may cause psychological distress.1,2 Patient education is a reasonable first step, but may not be sufficient to ameliorate the traumatic experiences.23 Systematic assessment and targeted psychological interventions are more likely to efficiently address the cognitive-behavioral response to ICD shocks, including both the acuity of discomfort with shock and the patient’s understanding of why the shock occurred, and their perception of future implications.

This case is particularly illustrative, given that the patient’s anxiety was provoked not by a life-threatening event, but by the perception and somatic experiences of shock, highlighting the cognitive-behavioral mechanisms (eg, avoidance, hypervigilance, and catastrophic thinking) that underlie PTSD and shock-related anxiety. Clinically, this underscores the need for early multidisciplinary assessment and targeted psychological intervention alongside device management, rather than relying solely on patient education, adjustment of ICD settings, or medication management alone.24 Moreover, the case emphasizes the broader relevance to practice, revealing that even seemingly minor or inappropriate cardiac events, such as an ICD shock, can trigger significant psychological sequelae.25,26 By integrating these insights, this case contributes to a greater understanding of the interplay between physiological and psychological responses to ICD therapy and reinforces the importance of a multidisciplinary, patient-centered approach to care that spans nursing, psychology, exercise physiology, and cardiac electrophysiology.

Conclusion

This case underscores a distinct issue in ICD care—the psychological and functional consequences of shocks. These consequences should be carefully considered in every patient who experiences ICD shocks, particularly in the context of their physical health, psychological functioning, and overall well-being.
