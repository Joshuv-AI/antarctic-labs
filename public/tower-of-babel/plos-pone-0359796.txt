An adaptive current–voltage step size solver for accurate and efficient modeling of solar photovoltaic modules

Nsulwa John Mlazi, Baraka Kichonge

ABSTRACT
Accurate modeling of photovoltaic (PV) modules is critical for system design, energy yield prediction, and maximum power point tracking (MPPT). Conventional single-diode models (SDM) provide a balance between simplicity and physical realism but require iterative numerical methods to solve the implicit current–voltage (I–V) equation, often leading to slow convergence and reduced stability under dynamic conditions. This paper introduces a novel adaptive current–voltage step-size solver (AIVSS) that dynamically adjusts both voltage and current increments based on the local slope of the I–V curve, thereby improving numerical accuracy and convergence efficiency. The proposed method was validated through 500 simulations under standard test conditions (STC), partial shading, high temperature, and dynamic irradiance, and was compared with the conventional adaptive voltage-step solver (AVSS). Performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), iteration count, and computation time. Results show that AIVSS reduced the average iteration count by 35–40%, decreased the average MAE from 6.15 W to 2.18 W, and reduced the average RMSE from 7.68 W to 2.85 W across all operating conditions. In addition, the proposed method achieved faster convergence and smoother dynamic response under rapidly changing irradiance. These findings confirm the strong potential of AIVSS for real-time PV system simulation and MPPT applications, providing a robust and computationally efficient tool for accurate PV module characterization.

Introduction The growing global demand for renewable energy has accelerated the deployment of solar PV systems as a clean, sustainable, and reliable electricity source. Accurate modeling of PV modules is essential for system design optimization, energy yield prediction, and the development of efficient MPPT algorithms [ 1 – 3 ]. The nonlinear I–V characteristics of PV modules are strongly influenced by irradiance, temperature, and intrinsic device parameters, making precise representation vital for both simulation and real-time control. Accurate I–V modeling directly impacts MPPT efficiency and the performance of embedded PV controllers, highlighting the importance of robust numerical solvers in PV applications [ 4 – 6 ]. The single-diode model (SDM) has been widely adopted due to its balance of simplicity and physical accuracy [ 2 , 7 ]. In this model, the I–V relationship is implicit and nonlinear, necessitating iterative numerical techniques for solution. Traditional approaches, such as the Newton–Raphson (NR) method and Lambert W function-based formulations [ 5 , 8 ], provide high accuracy but often encounter convergence challenges or incur high computational cost across wide voltage ranges. To address these issues, several enhanced numerical schemes have been developed. Modified or damped NR methods improve convergence stability near the maximum power point [ 9 , 10 ], while metaheuristic optimization techniques including particle swarm optimization (PSO), genetic algorithms (GA), and artificial bee colony (ABC) methods enable precise parameter extraction and curve fitting [ 11 , 12 ]. Although effective, these approaches are typically computationally intensive, limiting their practical applicability for real-time or embedded PV systems. Recent research has focused on reducing iteration counts and simulation time. For instance, Azure et al. [ 13 ] proposed a Broyden-like iterative approach with faster convergence than standard Newton-Raphson method, while approximate SDM formulations can reduce simulation time by up to 30% without sacrificing I–V curve accuracy. Hybrid analytical–numerical approaches further improve agreement with manufacturer datasheet values and minimize MPP errors [ 6 ]. Nonetheless, comparative studies indicate that nonlinear regions near the MPP remain challenging for both analytical and numerical models [ 6 ], emphasizing the need for more efficient and accurate solvers. Parallel developments in MPPT research demonstrate that variable or adaptive step-size techniques improve computational efficiency and reduce oscillations under dynamic conditions [ 13 ]. Similarly, adaptive numerical solvers in differential equation and control applications dynamically vary step sizes according to local slope or error [ 14 ]. While adaptive voltage-step methods have been applied to PV modeling, they focus solely on voltage increments, leaving current adaptation unaddressed. Consequently, many existing approaches still rely on uniformly spaced voltage sampling, which is inefficient near highly nonlinear regions such as the MPP or open-circuit voltage ( V o c ). This limitation motivates the exploration of dual-step adaptation strategies that simultaneously adjust both voltage and current increments for improved performance. Despite the advancements in adaptive solvers across computational domains, simultaneous voltage-current step adaptive in static PV modeling has received limited attention. Existing adaptive voltage-step approaches [ 15 – 18 ] improve convergence but do not fully ensure numerical stability or accuracy under nonlinear and dynamic operating conditions. To address this gap, this study introduces a novel adaptive current–voltage step-size solver (AIVSS) that adjusts both voltage and current increments according to the local slope of the I–V curve. Unlike existing adaptive solvers that adjust only voltage increments or rely solely on reactive adaptation, the proposed AIVSS uniquely integrates bidirectional step adaptation with predictive slope-based correction, enabling simultaneous control of both electrical variables. The proposed solver enhances computational efficiency by minimizing redundant calculations in quasi-linear regions while improving numerical accuracy near the MPP and V o c . It provides a lightweight yet effective enhancement to conventional PV simulation techniques, supporting faster and more precise real-time PV and MPPT applications. The primary contribution of this work lies in improving numerical PV module modeling through an adaptive dual-step solver. While the method can support MPPT applications, it does not replace MPPT algorithms but rather enhances their accuracy through improved I–V curve estimation. The rest contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. This work introduces a novel AIVSS that dynamically and simultaneously regulates both voltage and current increments to improve tracking efficiency. It further enhances system performance by incorporating predictive correction terms, which significantly improve convergence stability and suppress oscillations, especially in the vicinity of the maximum power point (MPP). In addition, the proposed method is extensively validated under both steady-state and dynamic conditions, including challenging scenarios such as partial shading and rapidly varying irradiance. Finally, experimental results obtained from a real photovoltaic module confirm the practical effectiveness of the approach, demonstrating improved accuracy, robustness, and overall performance compared to conventional methods. Mathematical model of solar PV module Accurate modeling of solar PV modules is critical for predicting performance, designing efficient systems, and implementing MPPT algorithms. The mathematical representation of a PV module captures the nonlinear relationship between voltage, current, and environmental factors such as irradiance and temperature. Among the various modeling approaches, the SDM is widely favored due to its balance of physical realism and computational simplicity. This section presents the SDM, describes its equivalent circuit, defines the governing I–V equation, and explains the temperature and irradiance dependence of key parameters, which are essential for realistic simulation and evaluation of numerical solvers. Single-diode equivalent circuit The SDM is the most widely adopted representation of a PV cell or module, providing an optimal balance between modeling accuracy and computational simplicity [ 7 , 19 ]. As illustrated in Fig 1 , the model consists of a photocurrent source ( I p h ), a diode representing the p–n junction, a series resistance ( R s ), and a shunt resistance ( R s h ) that accounts for leakage currents. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359796.g001 Fig 1 Electrical equivalent circuit of the single-diode PV model. The I–V relationship of the SDM is expressed as [ 20 – 24 ]: I = I p h − I 0 [ exp ( V + I R s n V t ) − 1 ] − V + I R s R s h (1) where I 0 is the diode reverse saturation current, n is the diode ideality factor, and V t = k T / q is the thermal voltage. Due to the implicit nature of Eq. (1) , iterative numerical solvers are required to compute the I–V curve [ 25 ]. Parameter dependence The SDM parameters ( I p h , I 0 , R s , R s h , and n ) are functions of operating temperature and irradiance. The photocurrent I p h is approximately proportional to solar irradiance G , while the saturation current I 0 increases exponentially with temperature [ 26 , 27 ]. The temperature coefficient of voltage ( β ) was not explicitly incorporated in the present model because the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the numerical performance of the proposed solver rather than to refine temperature-dependent parameter extraction. Since both AVSS and AIVSS were evaluated under the same modeling assumptions, the comparative accuracy of the proposed method remains valid. (4) Convergence is achieved when the change between successive iterations satisfies | I ( j + 1 ) − I ( j ) | < δ , (5) where δ is a small tolerance, commonly 10 –6 A. While NR provides quadratic convergence, it uses fixed voltage increments along the I–V curve. This can lead to inefficiency in regions of strong nonlinearity, such as near the MPP or open-circuit voltage ( V o c ), because it may require many iterations or fail to resolve rapid changes accurately. These limitations motivate the use of adaptive step-size strategies, such as Adaptive Voltage Step Size, which adjust the voltage increments dynamically according to local curve characteristics. Adaptive Voltage Step Size Strategy (AVSS) The Adaptive Voltage Step Size Strategy improves upon the standard NR method by dynamically adjusting the Δ V according to the local slope of the I–V curve and the voltage error. The general update is expressed as: Δ V n + 1 = Δ V n × f ( d I d V , ε V ) , (6) where: Δ V n is the voltage step at iteration n , d I d V is the local slope of the I–V curve, ε V is the voltage error at the current iteration, f ( · ) is an adaptive control function that scales the voltage step. Reactive step adaptation. In practical AVSS implementations [ 16 , 33 ], the adaptive function f is defined as a multiplicative factor based on the slope and error: f ( d I d V , ε V ) = { η inc , if | ε V | < ε prev and | d I d V | is small η dec , otherwise (7) Here: η inc > 1 is an acceleration factor, increasing the step in quasi-linear regions, η dec < 1 is a deceleration factor, reducing the step in highly nonlinear regions, Both η inc and η dec are tunable design parameters, often chosen empirically based on the PV module characteristics and desired convergence behavior, ε prev is the voltage error from the previous iteration. The criterion for small | d I d V | is often a design choice, typically corresponding to quasi-linear regions of the I–V curve [ 16 ]. Proposed adaptive current–voltage step size solver (AIVSS) The AVSS adjusts only the Δ V according to the local slope of the I–V curve. While effective in many cases, it may not fully capture rapid changes in current or power under dynamic conditions such as partial shading or fast irradiance fluctuations. To address this limitation, the proposed AIVSS introduces bidirectional adaptation, adjusting both voltage ( Δ V ) and current ( Δ I ) increments. Unlike AVSS, which reacts only to the current slope, AIVSS combines reactive adaptation with predictive trend estimation ( ϕ V , ϕ I ) to prevent overshoot and oscillations near MPP. Adaptive control functions. The reactive multiplicative factors f 1 and f 2 adjust the step size according to the local slope and the instantaneous voltage error, using predefined thresholds to regulate their behavior. When the slope magnitude exceeds the nonlinear threshold ε s (i.e., | d P / d V | ≥ ε s ) or when the prediction error surpasses the allowable error bound e V ≥ ε e , the factors contract the voltage and current increments to preserve numerical stability and prevent overshooting. Conversely, in regions where | d P / d V | < ε s and e V < ε e , the PV curve is locally linear and well behaved, allowing f 1 and f 2 to expand the step size to accelerate convergence. The learning coefficients λ V and λ I govern the sensitivity of the multiplicative factors to local slope and error variations. Their values were determined through a sensitivity analysis in which coefficients ranging from 0.05 to 0.8 were tested under different irradiance and temperature conditions. It was empirically observed that values within the range 0.1 ≤ λ V , λ I ≤ 0.5 provided the best compromise between convergence speed and numerical stability, while larger values occasionally produced oscillatory behavior near the maximum power point. Therefore, to ensure bounded adaptive updates across both linear and nonlinear regions of the PV characteristic, the coefficients were restricted to 0 < λ V , λ I ≤ 0.5 f 1 ( d P d V , ε V ) = { η inc , if | ε V | < ε prev and | d P d V | is small η dec , otherwise (8) f 2 ( d P d I , ε I ) = { η inc , if | ε I | < ε prev and | d P d I | is small η dec , otherwise (9) Here, η inc > 1 is the acceleration factor, η dec < 1 is the deceleration factor, and ε prev is the error from the previous iteration. These functions ensure faster convergence in quasi-linear regions while reducing steps in highly nonlinear regions. Predictive correction terms. To anticipate rapid changes in slope and prevent overshoot near the MPP or highly nonlinear regions, predictive correction terms ϕ V and ϕ I are introduced. These terms are based on the rate of change of the local derivative, allowing the solver to adjust the step proactively rather than purely reactively [ 34 – 36 ]: ϕ V = λ V ( d P d V ) n − ( d P d V ) n − 1 ( d P d V ) n − 1 Δ V n , ϕ I = λ I ( d P d I ) n − ( d P d I ) n − 1 ( d P d I ) n − 1 Δ I n , (10) where λ V , λ I ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) are learning coefficients controlling the influence of predictive adjustment. Typical values are empirically chosen in the range 0.1–0.5 to balance convergence speed and stability. This approach leverages the proposed approach to improve convergence speed and stability under dynamic operating conditions. Step size update equations. The preceding subsections introduced the reactive and predictive mechanisms governing voltage and current adjustments. Building on these formulations, the step size update equations integrate both components into a unified rule that governs the solver’s progression towards convergence. This subsection presents the mathematical expressions defining the joint Δ V and Δ I updates, which form operational core of the proposed AIVSS algorithm. Δ V n + 1 = Δ V n · f 1 ( d P d V , ε V ) + ϕ V , Δ I n + 1 = Δ I n · f 2 ( d P d I , ε I ) + ϕ I (11) These updates combine reactive and predictive adaptation to handle both quasi-linear and highly nonlinear regions efficiently. Stability is mathematically bounded for 0 < λ V , λ I ≤ 0.5 preventing divergent growth. Because Δ V and Δ I are updated independently according to local power derivatives, their ratio is not constant and varies with irradiance, temperature, and curve nonlinearity. Algorithmic procedure. The setup specifies test conditions, PV parameters, solver configuration, and implementation details to ensure consistent and reproducible comparisons with the conventional AVSS. To evaluate the performance and robustness of the proposed AIVSS solver, 500 numerical simulations were conducted under both standard and dynamic conditions. Irradiance profiles included constant ( G = 1000 W / m 2 ), step changes (200–1000 W/m 2 ), linear ramps (100–1000 W/m 2 ), and stochastic fluctuations to mimic real-world variability. Temperature conditions comprised fixed points at 25 ∘ C , 50 ∘ C , and 75 ∘ C , as well as dynamic variations from 25 − 60 ∘ C . These scenarios were designed to test convergence, accuracy, and stability of the solver under diverse nonlinear and time-varying operating conditions. Test conditions The simulations were carried out under standard test conditions (STC) with an irradiance of G = 1000 W / m 2 , a cell temperature of T = 25 ∘ C , and uniform illumination without partial shading. To evaluate the robustness of the proposed solver, additional dynamic test scenarios were introduced, including partial shading patterns, rapid irradiance variations between 400 and 1000 W / m 2 , and temperature fluctuations ranging from 25 to 50 ∘ C . For partial shading validation, one-third of the module was subjected to 400 W/m 2 while the remaining two-thirds received 1000 W/m 2 , producing a multi-peak P–V characteristic. These conditions emulate real-world PV operating environments characterized by transient variations. PV model parameters A single-diode PV model was adopted using the extracted electrical parameters: photocurrent I p h , S T C = 9.85 A , diode saturation current I 0 = 3.2 × 10 − 10 A , series resistance R s = 0.25 Ω , shunt resistance R s h = 800 Ω , and diode ideality factor n = 1.25. Unlike the manufacturer specifications listed in Table 1 , these parameters represent the internal SDM coefficients used as numerical inputs for the simulation of the PV module under standard test conditions. Solver configuration The I–V characteristics were modeled according to the single-diode equation defined in Eq. 1 . The proposed AIVSS algorithm applied the adaptive voltage and current step updates detailed in Eq. 11 , enabling dynamic adjustment of voltage and current increments based on instantaneous power–slope information. Convergence was achieved when successive current and power changes satisfied | I n + 1 − I n | < 10 − 6 A and | P n + 1 − P n | < 10 − 5 W , respectively, with a maximum of 50 iterations per voltage step. Implementation details Simulations were implemented in MATLAB/Simulink R2023a with initial values V 0 = 0 V and I 0 = I p h , corresponding to the short-circuit operating point used as the starting point for numerical tracing of the I–V curve. The initial step sizes Δ V 0 = 0.01 V and Δ I 0 = 0.01 A were selected as a balance between numerical stability and computational speed based on preliminary convergence tests. Both the conventional AVSS and the proposed AIVSS solvers were executed under identical environmental and numerical conditions to ensure a fair performance comparison. Conclusion This study presented an adaptive current–voltage step-size solver for accurate and efficient modeling of solar PV modules based on the SDM. By simultaneously adjusting voltage and current increments according to the local slope of the I–V curve, AIVSS improves convergence speed, numerical stability, and MPP accuracy compared to conventional AVSS and standard Newton–Raphson methods. Simulation results under standard test conditions, partial shading, high temperature, and dynamic irradiance demonstrated a 35–40% reduction in iteration count, lower MAE, and smoother dynamic response, confirming the solver’s robustness and suitability for real-time PV system applications. The experimental validation further corroborated the effectiveness of the proposed method, showing consistent MAE improvements of approximately 38–42% across all test scenarios. These results highlight the practical applicability of AIVSS in embedded PV controllers, MPPT algorithms, and large-scale simulation environments. The proposed AIVSS is primarily a numerical solver for PV modeling; its application to MPPT is indirect, through improved accuracy and convergence of the PV characteristics. In conclusion, the proposed dual-step adaptive solver offers a reliable and computationally efficient approach for precise PV module characterization, providing a solid foundation for both simulation studies and practical real-time applications.
