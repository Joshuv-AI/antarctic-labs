Burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh: a narrative review
Authors: S M Yasir Arafat, Sadeed Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare professionals are particularly vulnerable due to heavy workloads, long duty hours, and emotionally demanding work environments. In Bangladesh, structural constraints within the healthcare system may further increase the risk of burnout. This article synthesizes existing evidence on burnout among healthcare professionals in the country.MethodsA structured narrative review was conducted using a comprehensive literature search without time restrictions in PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and BanglaJOL up to 02 February, 2026. Studies were screened using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and eligible studies were thematically synthesized.ResultsEight cross-sectional studies were included with sample sizes ranged from 50 to 2705 and encompassed various healthcare professional groups, including intern doctors, medical officers, postgraduate trainees, consultants, nurses, dentists, Intensive Care Unit (ICU) physicians, and hospital attendants. The Maslach Burnout Inventory was the most frequently used assessment tool. Moderate to high burnout prevalence ranged from approximately 20% to over 55%, with particularly elevated levels reported among female physicians, ICU physicians, and early-career professionals. Domain-specific findings showed extremely high levels of emotional exhaustion (up to 95%) and depersonalization in some cohorts. Consistently identified risk factors were long working hours, excessive workload, frequent night shifts, poor work–life balance, inadequate remuneration, limited organizational support, and pandemic-related stressors.ConclusionBurnout appears to be common among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh, particularly in high-risk subgroups, and is associated with occupational and organizational factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00514-4.Keywords: Burnout, Bangladesh, Health professionals, Physicians, Review

Introduction

Burnout can be defined as emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment, usually resulting from prolonged workplace stress [1]. The World Health Organization has officially recognized burnout as an occupational phenomenon in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), highlighting its importance for modern health systems [2]. Burnout affects not only the mental and physical well-being of healthcare workers but also reduces patient safety, reduces the quality of care, increases absenteeism of staff, increases turnover rates, and overall reduces health system performance [3].

Healthcare professionals have a high prevalence of burnout because of their heavy workloads, long duty hours, increasing night duties, emotional demands in patient care, ethical challenges, and increasing administrative responsibilities in modern healthcare [4]. Healthcare professionals from both high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) suffer from high levels of burnout [5]. Early-career or young health professionals and those working in high-pressure environments like emergency departments and intensive care units (ICUs) are especially at risk [6, 7]. The COVID-19 pandemic further influenced burnout by increasing clinical workload, psychological stress, and exposure to moral and occupational risks [8].

In LMICs like Bangladesh, the risk of burnout is further increased due to the limitation of structural and resource-related issues. The healthcare system in Bangladesh suffer from workforce shortages, high patient-to-provider ratios, limited mental health support services, and inadequate organizational resources [9, 10]. Healthcare professionals working in public hospitals in Bangladesh have to face crisis like overcrowding, extended working hours, and poor working conditions [11, 12]. As a result, healthcare professionals are often exposed to chronic occupational stress, while their mental well-being receives limited attention within institutional and policy frameworks.

During the past decade, several studies have investigated burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh, including intern doctors, nurses, medical officers, postgraduate trainees, consultants, and intensive care physicians [13–20]. These studies have reported varying levels of burnout and have identified multiple contributing factors, such as long working hours, workload, job dissatisfaction, poor work-life balance, and unfavorable work environments. However, various methodological problems make it difficult to use existing research for policy and practice. First, there is a lack of synthesis, as studies have been conducted separately without being brought together into a unified understanding. Second, there is poor comparability across studies, because different tools measure different aspects of burnout and use different scoring systems, making direct comparison difficult. Third, there is no pooled interpretation, meaning that without an overall summary of findings, clinicians and policymakers do not get a clear big-picture understanding needed to guide burnout prevention and management in Bangladesh.

In Bangladesh, research about burnout remains limited and unevenly distributed, so the compilation of published papers is particularly valuable for informing policy decisions and workplace interventions. Therefore, this narrative review aims to synthesize existing evidence on burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh. Specifically, it examines study characteristics, burnout assessment tools, prevalence and severity of burnout, associated risk factors, and reported interventions or recommendations. The conceptual basis for this review draws primarily on the Maslach Burnout Model and secondarily on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) framework, which provides a lens for interpreting the consistent pattern of high occupational demands alongside limited organizational resources identified across studies.

Methods

Study design and search strategy

This study was conducted as a structured narrative review to improve clarity and allow flexible synthesis of existing evidence on burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh. This review was not pre-registered in PROSPERO or any other registry due its nature.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted without any time restriction up to 02 February, 2026 to identify relevant studies. Electronic databases commonly used for health and medical research were searched, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and BanglaJOL. These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of biomedical, multidisciplinary, and region-specific literature, including locally published studies from Bangladesh. Studies conducted in Bangladesh that assessed the prevalence of burnout, domains, associated factors, or recommended interventions among healthcare professionals were included.

All searches were conducted in English, and the search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used:

(“physician” OR “doctor” OR “medical doctor” OR “clinician” OR “dentist” OR “nurses” OR “attendants” OR “healthcare professionals”)

AND

(“burnout” OR “occupational stress” OR “work-related stress” OR “professional burnout”)

AND

(“Bangladesh”)

This combination of search terms was used in PubMed and Scopus, while plain text terms were used for Google Scholar and BanglaJOL (Supplementary file 1).

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they were conducted in Bangladesh and focused on healthcare professionals, including doctors, nurses, dentists, intern doctors, postgraduate trainees, consultants, intensive care unit physicians, or other hospital-based healthcare staff. Eligible studies assessed burnout or burnout-related outcomes using clearly described measurement tools. Studies were required to report sufficient information on burnout prevalence, burnout domains, associated factors, or proposed interventions or recommendations. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were considered to ensure a basic methodological quality and consistency in reporting.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were conducted outside Bangladesh or focused on populations without active clinical roles, such as undergraduate students. Review articles, editorials, commentaries, case reports, conference abstracts, and letters without primary data were also excluded. Additionally, studies that did not assess burnout or burnout-related outcomes, or those with insufficient methodological detail or unclear measurement approaches were excluded from the review.

Study selection

All records identified through the database search were initially screened by title and abstract to assess relevance to the review objectives. Duplicate records (n = 3) were identified and removed prior to screening. Full texts of potentially eligible articles (n = 10) were subsequently retrieved and assessed in detail against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following full-text review, 2 studies were excluded. The reasons for exclusion included were not assessing burnout or burnout-related outcomes (n = 1) and insufficient methodological detail or unclear measurement approach (n = 1). So, eight studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final narrative synthesis. The screening process was conducted by two reviewers independently to reduce selection bias. Although a formal PRISMA flow diagram was not developed due to the narrative design, the study selection process was structured to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Data extraction

Relevant data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a structured and predefined data extraction format in Microsoft Excel 2019 to ensure consistency. Any discrepancies in extracted data were resolved through discussion and consensus. Extracted information included author name, year of publication, study design, study setting, sample size, type of healthcare professionals, sex distribution, age characteristics, burnout assessment tools, burnout domains assessed, prevalence or severity of burnout, factors associated with burnout, and reported interventions or recommendations. The extracted data were summarized and presented in tabular form to enhance clarity.

Quality consideration

As this review followed a narrative approach, a formal risk-of-bias assessment tool was not applied. However, methodological quality was considered during study selection. Studies with unclear measurement of burnout, insufficient methodological description, or inadequate reporting of outcomes were excluded. Preference was given to studies using validated instruments such as the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI). This approach ensured a minimum level of methodological rigor among included studies. The absence of a formal risk-of-bias or quality assessment tool limits the ability to systematically evaluate the internal validity of included studies and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Data synthesis

Findings were synthesized using a thematic narrative approach. Extracted data were grouped into predefined domains, including study characteristics, burnout assessment tools, prevalence and severity, associated factors, and interventions. Within each domain, patterns and consistencies across studies were identified, while variations were interpreted in relation to differences in study populations, healthcare settings, and measurement tools. Rather than simple aggregation, the synthesis involved comparing results across studies to identify recurring determinants, high-risk groups, and contextual influences shaping burnout in Bangladesh.

An important consideration governing interpretation throughout this synthesis is the heterogeneity in burnout assessment tools across included studies. The included studies used different instruments, specifically the MBI (6 studies), the Chalder Fatigue Scale (1 study), and a modified burnout inventory (1 study), which measure different constructs and apply different scoring thresholds. Findings from these tools are therefore not directly comparable, and no attempt has been made to pool or equate results across instruments. The assessment tool used is clearly identified alongside each finding in the Results section, and cross-study comparisons involving different tools are made with explicit interpretive caution. This heterogeneity is acknowledged as a limitation of the review.

Ethical considerations

As this review utilized publicly available data and did not involve human participants directly, ethical approval was not required.

Results

Study selection and general characteristics

A total of eight cross-sectional studies published between 2017 and 2025 were included [13–20]. All studies were hospital-based and collectively represented diverse healthcare settings across Bangladesh, including public hospitals, private hospitals and medical college hospitals. Sample sizes ranged from 50 to 2,705 participants [14, 16].

Characteristics of study populations

The included studies reported on a broad spectrum of healthcare professionals, including intern doctors, medical officers, postgraduate trainees, consultants, general physicians, nurses, dentists, ICU physicians, and hospital attendants. Sex distribution varied across studies, with female participants predominating in three studies [13–15] and male in also three studies [17–19]. Studies focusing on physicians and specialists more frequently reported male predominance, and studies focusing on nurses had female predominance. Age distributions differed substantially across studies, with mean or median ages generally spanning early adulthood to middle age. Intern doctors and early-career professionals were primarily clustered around the 20–29-year age range, and studies involving senior physicians and consultants reported higher mean ages. Age distributions in some studies were reported as ranges rather than exact means, with the majority of participants spanning early adulthood to middle age.

Key findings across studies

As shown in Table 1, across the included studies, burnout prevalence and severity varied substantially, with reported rates ranging from approximately 20% to over 55% [14, 17]. Domain-specific rates, particularly emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, were substantially higher in some studies, reaching up to 95.8% and 98.2% respectively [17]; however, these figures reflect individual burnout dimensions and are not equivalent to overall burnout prevalence. Higher levels of burnout were particularly evident among ICU physicians and frontline healthcare workers, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic [14–17, 20]. Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization emerged as the most commonly affected dimensions [14, 17, 20].

Work-related and organizational factors such as long working hours, high patient load, frequent night shifts, and poor work-life balance were consistently identified as major contributors to burnout [13, 15, 16, 19]. Certain subgroups, including early-career professionals [13], female healthcare workers [18], and those working in high-intensity settings [14–17, 20], demonstrated a higher vulnerability to burnout .

Burnout assessment tools and domains

Multiple instruments were used to assess burnout and related psychological outcomes. The MBI was the most frequently employed tool, used in six of the eight studies [14–18, 20]. The MBI is a widely validated and internationally accepted instrument specifically designed for professionals working in human service settings, including healthcare [21]. It assesses burnout across three core dimensions: emotional exhaustion, which reflects feelings of being emotionally overextended and depleted by work; depersonalization, which captures an impersonal, detached, or cynical attitude toward patients and colleagues; and personal accomplishment, which measures feelings of competence, effectiveness, and achievement at work. Higher scores in emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, along with lower scores in personal accomplishment, indicate greater levels of burnout.

One study assessed burnout-related fatigue using the Chalder Fatigue Scale [13], which evaluates both physical fatigue and mental fatigue [22]. Although not a traditional burnout instrument, fatigue is considered a key component and early manifestation of occupational burnout, particularly among early-career healthcare professionals exposed to long working hours and sleep deprivation. This tool provides insight into exhaustion-related aspects of burnout that may not be fully captured by multidimensional burnout scales.

Another study used a general burnout inventory [19], which was a modified version of the MBI [23]. This instrument assessed overall burnout severity rather than distinct subdomains and allowed comparison of burnout levels between different healthcare settings, such as public and private hospitals.

Prevalence and severity of burnout

As shown in Fig. 1. the prevalence of burnout and burnout-related conditions varied markedly across studies. It is important to note at the outset that overall burnout prevalence, domain-specific prevalence (emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, personal accomplishment), and burnout risk categories are distinct outcomes and should not be interpreted interchangeably. For example, a high prevalence of emotional exhaustion within a sample does not equate to an equivalent prevalence of overall burnout. Among intern doctors, fatigue prevalence reached 39.1%, with both physical and mental fatigue reported [13]. Studies using the MBI reported moderate to high burnout prevalence ranging from approximately 20% to over 55% across different professional groups and settings [14, 17]. ICU-based studies demonstrated particularly elevated burnout levels, with more than half of ICU physicians experiencing moderate to high burnout [16]. High levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were frequently observed, with some studies reporting emotional exhaustion in over 90% of participants and depersonalization affecting nearly all respondents (Table 2) [17]. These domain-specific rates reflect the severity of individual burnout dimensions and should not be interpreted as equivalent to overall burnout prevalence. In contrast, personal accomplishment scores were relatively preserved in some cohorts, particularly among private-sector healthcare workers and younger professionals [20].

Figure 1 presents a descriptive summary of reported burnout prevalence across included studies. The figure was constructed using extracted prevalence estimates reported in individual studies without statistical pooling, given the heterogeneity in measurement tools and definitions of burnout. Therefore, the figure should be interpreted as an illustrative comparison rather than a quantitative synthesis.

Factors associated with burnout

Across studies, burnout was consistently associated with occupational and organizational factors. Commonly reported determinants included long working hours, frequent night shifts, increased patient load, poor work–life balance, inadequate sleep, and job dissatisfaction [13–16, 18–20]. Early career stage, younger age, female sex, and prolonged working experience were repeatedly identified as individual-level correlates. Workplace-related factors such as unfavorable work environments, inadequate organizational & social support, conflicts with colleagues, ethical dilemmas, and insufficient remuneration in respect of workload were also reported [18–20]. In studies conducted during or after the COVID-19 pandemic, frontline clinical duties and pandemic-related stressors were associated with higher burnout levels [14].

Reported interventions and recommendations

Several studies proposed institutional and individual-level interventions aimed at mitigating burnout. Common recommendations included regulating duty hours, reducing workload, improving work–life balance, ensuring mandatory rest periods, and enhancing organizational support systems [13, 15, 18, 19]. Psychological interventions such as stress management programs, online cognitive behavioral therapy, and mental health promotion initiatives were suggested in multiple studies [16, 17]. Additional recommendations focused on improving workflow efficiency, strengthening peer and supervisory relationships, and providing non-physician administrative support to reduce professional burden [16, 17].

Discussion

Burnout affects a substantial proportion of healthcare professionals in Bangladesh. Across the included studies, moderate to high levels of burnout were consistently reported, with prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 20% to over 55% [14, 17]. These results are broadly consistent with global estimates; a systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall physician burnout prevalence of 67% worldwide [24], however there was a wide variation in prevalence across studies due to methodological heterogeneity and differences in measurement tools, this limitation is equally applicable to our study.

Particularly high levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were observed among physicians working in intensive care units [15, 16]. Similar findings were reported in 16 Asian countries and regions where the majority of ICU physicians and nurses experience high levels of professional burnout [25]. Not only in Asian countries; similar findings can be found in other countries too [26]. This reflected the psychological and emotional demands of managing critically ill patients, high mortality rates, and sustained work pressure. Since this pattern is seen in both high-income countries and resource-limited countries like Bangladesh. It suggests that burnout in ICU staff is not only due to lack of resources, but also because ICU work is emotionally very stressful and involves difficult life-and-death decisions [25, 26]. However, high levels of professional burnout are not limited to specific subgroups but present among diverse healthcare professionals, including intern doctors, medical officers, postgraduate trainees, consultants, nurses, dentists, and hospital attendants.

In Bangladesh, burnout affected early-career healthcare professionals, who were particularly vulnerable, with younger age and junior professional status repeatedly associated with higher burnout levels. Intern doctors and medical officers often face long duty hours, frequent night shifts, academic pressure, and limited decision-making autonomy, which collectively increase emotional exhaustion and fatigue [13, 17]. This pattern is consistent with findings from India, where early career stage and workload were the primary determinants of burnout among junior doctors [27]. However, in the USA, mid-career physicians experienced the highest rates of emotional exhaustion and burnout [28], this may be because mid-career doctors in high-income countries often face extra pressures such as paperwork, billing responsibilities, and slower career progress. These types of pressures are less common in Bangladesh, which may explain why burnout patterns differ between settings.

Occupational and organizational factors emerged as the most consistent determinants of burnout across included studies. Long working hours, excessive workload, frequent night shifts, inadequate sleep, and poor work-life balance were reported in most of the studies [13–16, 19, 20]. Inadequate remuneration relative to workload, limited job security, insufficient organizational and social support, conflicts with colleagues, and unfavorable physical working environments were also identified as significant contributors [15, 16, 18]. These findings strongly reflect structural deficiencies within the healthcare system of Bangladesh. However, these issues are not unique to Bangladesh; healthcare professionals from other LMICs are also suffering from similar crisis like high workload, inadequate staffing, poor working conditions and lack of organizational support [29]. This is similar to what was found in Bangladesh and shows that the problem comes from how the health system is organized. It also suggests that the solution should focus on improving the system, not just individual doctors.

Even during the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare professionals in high-income countries, as well as those in low- and middle-income countries, experienced similar levels of psychological burden and burnout [30]. Collectively, these findings suggest that burnout among healthcare professionals all over the world, including Bangladesh, is primarily driven by modifiable system-level factors rather than individual vulnerabilities.

Several individual-level factors were associated with burnout, including younger age, early career stage, female sex, and prolonged working experience. Female healthcare professionals demonstrated higher burnout levels in multiple studies [17, 18]. Multiple studies worldwide provided strong evidence that showed that female healthcare professionals suffer from higher burnout levels in most of the countries [31, 32]. Female healthcare professionals experience higher burnout primarily due to higher workloads, dual work-family responsibilities, gendered patient expectations, workplace inequities, receiving fewer resources, compensation disparities, harassment and disproportionate non-work caregiving burdens [33].

Middle-aged professionals with additional family and financial responsibilities also showed increased burnout risk in one study [18]. Similar findings were reported by the physician of the USA [28]. These findings highlight the importance of considering sociodemographic and life-course factors when designing burnout prevention strategies.

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly exacerbated burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh. Studies conducted during or after the pandemic reported higher burnout levels, particularly among frontline healthcare workers [14, 17]. These findings are consistent with global studies [34]. Increased workload, fear of infection, moral distress, prolonged exposure to critically ill patients and insufficient psychosocial support influence professional burnout [35, 36].

Studies of Bangladesh proposed several evidence-based recommendations. These included regulating duty hours, reducing workload, improving staffing and workflow efficiency, ensuring mandatory rest periods, and enhancing organizational support systems [13, 15, 19]. Individual-level interventions such as stress management programs, online cognitive behavioral therapy, and mental health promotion initiatives were also suggested [16–18]. It should be noted that the international interventions discussed below have not been evaluated in the Bangladesh context and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research should assess their feasibility and effectiveness within the specific structural and resource constraints of the Bangladesh healthcare system.

Evidence from international research demonstrates that both organizational and individual-level interventions can produce measurable improvements in burnout and well-being among healthcare workers. Workplace interventions that include mindfulness training, meditation, yoga, peer support networks, workload modifications, and resilience-building activities have been linked with significant reductions in burnout, perceived stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, and improvements in well-being, work engagement, and quality of life in healthcare professionals [37].

Mindfulness-based interventions (such as structured programs, meditation, and stress reduction training) have been shown in randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews to decrease burnout and psychological distress and to improve resilience and sleep quality among nurses and other healthcare workers. Evidence from systematic reviews also supports the effectiveness of mindfulness and cognitive–behavioral approaches in reducing emotional exhaustion and enhancing psychological well-being, even if the magnitude of effects varies across settings and studies [38].

The findings suggest that in order to reduce burnout among healthcare workers in Bangladesh, action at both the system and individual levels is required. At the system level, health policies should include occupational well-being, with stronger institutional support, better working conditions, and accessible mental health services for healthcare workers. At the individual level, providing stress management and mental health support can help workers cope with job-related pressures. Together, these approaches are essential not only for preventing burnout but also for sustaining healthcare worker performance and ensuring quality patient care.

Strengths and limitations

This review provides a structured synthesis of all currently available peer-reviewed English-language evidence on burnout among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh, which to the authors’ knowledge constitutes the first such synthesis of this literature. This review included diverse professional groups and healthcare settings to provide a broad overview of burnout patterns in the healthcare system of Bangladesh. The structured extraction and synthesis of study characteristics, assessment tools, prevalence estimates, associated factors, and recommendations increased the clarity of findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a narrative review, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed. As a result, it was not possible to calculate pooled prevalence estimates or systematically compare the methodological quality of the included studies. Additionally, the lack of formal quality appraisal may have allowed inclusion of studies with varying methodological rigor, which could influence the reliability and comparability of findings. Second, although multiple major databases were searched, additional databases such as Web of Science, EMBASE, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library were not included, which may have led to omission of some relevant studies. Third, all included studies used cross-sectional designs, which means that causal relationships between burnout and its associated factors cannot be established. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish the directionality and temporal dynamics of these associations in the Bangladesh context. Fourth, the number of eligible studies was relatively small, and most were conducted in hospital-based settings. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent burnout among healthcare professionals working in other settings, such as primary healthcare centers or community-based services. Fifth, this review was not pre-registered, which is a transparency limitation and only peer-reviewed articles published in English were included, and grey literature was not systematically searched, which may have resulted in the omission of relevant unpublished or non-indexed studies and led to publication and language bias. Finally, given the heterogeneity in assessment tools and outcome definitions across studies, prevalence estimates should not be directly compared or aggregated.

Conclusion

Burnout is common among healthcare professionals in Bangladesh and affects workers across different roles and healthcare settings. Most studies show that burnout is mainly caused by work-related and organizational problems, such as heavy workloads, long working hours, poor work–life balance, and weak institutional support. Although studies from Bangladesh mostly suggest solutions rather than testing them, evidence from other countries shows that both system-level changes and individual support programs can reduce burnout. Including healthcare worker well-being in health policies, improving institutional support, and ensuring access to mental health services are therefore important steps to protect healthcare professionals and to maintain safe and high-quality patient care in Bangladesh.
