Causal effects of cholelithiasis on hepatopancreatobiliary diseases: a multi-cohort Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jiawei An, Xue Li, Yangsuolang Fu, Huien Xie, Kai Gao, Fuhao Zeng, Yao Wang, Mingyan Zhu, Yicheng Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCholelithiasis is commonly associated with multiple hepatopancreatobiliary diseases, yet whether these relationships reflect causal mechanisms or shared risk factors remains unclear.MethodsWe performed a phenome-oriented two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to evaluate the causal impact of genetic liability to cholelithiasis across hepatopancreatobiliary outcomes. Independent genome-wide significant variants were selected as instrumental variables. Primary analyses used inverse variance weighting, complemented by sensitivity analyses, reverse MR, and multivariable MR adjusting for body mass index (BMI).ResultsGenetic predisposition to cholelithiasis was associated with increased risk of acute pancreatitis and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), with consistent directionality across datasets.The association with acute pancreatitis was interpreted as a positive control, whereas the null association with alcohol-induced acute pancreatitis served as a negative control. No causal association was observed for portal vein thrombosis. Sensitivity analyses, including MR-PRESSO and MR Steiger filtering, supported the robustness and directionality of the causal estimates. Reverse MR analyses showed no consistent evidence supporting reverse causality. Multivariable MR indicated that observed effects were not fully explained by BMI-related pathways.ConclusionThese findings suggest that cholelithiasis susceptibility may contribute to the broader hepatopancreatobiliary disease network, extending its clinical relevance beyond a localized biliary disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05033-8.Keywords: Cholelithiasis, Mendelian randomization, Acute pancreatitis, Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA)

Introduction

Cholelithiasis has traditionally been viewed as a localized biliary disorder driven by mechanical obstruction and inflammation. However, clinical observations increasingly indicate that gallstone disease frequently coexists with a wider spectrum of hepatopancreatobiliary conditions, including pancreatitis and biliary malignancies. Whether these associations represent causal relationships or arise from shared metabolic risk factors remains unresolved [1].

The term “hepatopancreatobiliary diseases” encompasses a wide spectrum of conditions, including primary liver malignancies (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma), extrahepatic biliary cancers (e.g., extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, gallbladder cancer), pancreatic diseases (acute/chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma), vascular disorders (portal vein thrombosis), and autoimmune cholangiopathies (primary sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis). Due to the availability of large-scale GWAS data and robust genetic instruments, the present study focuses specifically on acute pancreatitis (including gallstone-induced and alcohol-induced), extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), and portal vein thrombosis. Other important conditions (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, primary sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis, hereditary pancreatitis, intrahepatic lithiasis as a primary exposure) are not examined, which is acknowledged as a limitation [2–4].

Observational studies are limited by confounding and reverse causation. For example, an episode of acute pancreatitis may cause abdominal pain that leads to incidental detection of asymptomatic gallstones, creating a spurious association in the reverse direction. Such protopathic bias is difficult to avoid in observational designs. Metabolic traits such as obesity predispose individuals both to gallstone formation and downstream diseases, making causal inference challenging. Shared metabolic risk factors for both cholelithiasis and hepatopancreatobiliary diseases include obesity (high BMI), type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol), insulin resistance, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [5–8]. Consequently, the possibility that cholelithiasis may act as a causal contributor to broader hepatopancreatobiliary disease progression has not been rigorously evaluated.

Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages genetic variants as proxies for lifelong exposure and enables causal inference with reduced confounding [9]. Most prior MR investigations have examined isolated exposure–outcome relationships, overlooking the interconnected structure of organ-specific disease systems.

Here, we implemented a multi-outcome MR framework to systematically evaluate the causal effects of genetic liability to cholelithiasis across hepatopancreatobiliary diseases. Rather than focusing on isolated exposure–outcome relationships, this study aimed to provide a systematic and multi-cohort evaluation of the causal role of cholelithiasis using sensitivity analyses, reverse MR, multivariable MR accounting for BMI, MR-PRESSO, and MR Steiger filtering. Portal vein thrombosis (PVT) was included as a negative control outcome. Biologically, there is no established mechanism by which cholelithiasis directly promotes portal vein thrombosis; therefore, a null finding would support the specificity of the observed positive associations (e.g., for acute pancreatitis and eCCA) and argue against a non-specific pro-inflammatory effect of cholelithiasis on all hepatobiliary conditions. Methods

Study design

This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework to investigate the causal effects of genetic liability to cholelithiasis on hepatopancreatobiliary diseases. Publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were used for both exposure and outcome datasets. The MR design relies on three core assumptions: (1) genetic variants are strongly associated with the exposure, (2) variants are independent of confounders, and (3) variants influence outcomes exclusively through the exposure [10].

Data sources

Genetic instruments for cholelithiasis were obtained from a large-scale GWAS conducted in individuals of predominantly European ancestry (ebi-a-GCST90013939). In this study, cholelithiasis (gallstone disease) is defined based on the GWAS dataset ebi-a-GCST90013939, which includes clinically diagnosed gallstones located in the gallbladder or bile ducts, comprising both cholesterol and pigment stones. However, due to the nature of summary-level GWAS data, the analysis does not distinguish between gallbladder stones, common bile duct stones, or intrahepatic stones. Therefore, our causal estimates reflect the average effect of genetic liability to cholelithiasis as a whole and cannot be directly extrapolated to specific anatomical subtypes such as isolated intrahepatic lithiasis. Outcome datasets included acute pancreatitis, extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), portal vein thrombosis, and related hepatopancreatobiliary conditions derived from publicly available GWAS repositories, including FinnGen, UK Biobank, and IEU OpenGWAS (Fig. 2).

Causal comorbidity map of hepatopancreatobiliary diseases. Causal comorbidity map illustrating Mendelian randomization estimates between genetic liability to cholelithiasis and hepatopancreatobiliary outcomes. Tile colors represent log-transformed odds ratios derived from IVW analysis. Labels indicate statistical significance after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Significant causal associations were observed for acute pancreatitis and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), while other outcomes showed no evidence of causal effects

All datasets consisted of anonymized summary-level data with no individual-level identifiers. Because ancestry differences may affect linkage disequilibrium structure, allele frequencies, and effect size estimates, primary analyses were based mainly on GWAS datasets of European ancestry or predominantly European ancestry whenever available. Analyses using datasets from other ancestries were interpreted cautiously and considered supportive rather than primary evidence.

Sample size information

The GWAS dataset for cholelithiasis (ebi-a-GCST90013939) included approximately 404,405 individuals of European ancestry.

The outcome datasets were obtained from publicly available GWAS sources. Acute pancreatitis data were derived from the FinnGen consortium (n ≈ 198,000), extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) from FinnGen (n ≈ 476,000), portal vein thrombosis from FinnGen (n ≈ 218,000), and additional datasets from the UK Biobank and IEU OpenGWAS database with comparable large sample sizes. In the FinnGen study, acute pancreatitis was diagnosed using ICD codes (K85 for acute pancreatitis), without subclassification by severity (mild, moderate, severe) or etiology in the primary analysis. Therefore, our findings reflect the overall risk of acute pancreatitis and cannot be extrapolated to specific severity grades.

Detailed sample size information for each dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data access and permissions

All GWAS summary statistics used in this study were obtained from publicly available databases, including the IEU OpenGWAS platform, FinnGen consortium, and UK Biobank. These data are accessible to qualified researchers without the need for additional permissions. All original studies had obtained appropriate ethical approval and informed consent from participants.

Characteristics of GWAS datasets

All GWAS summary statistics used in this study were derived from population-based cohorts (e.g., FinnGen, UK Biobank) that originally adjusted for basic covariates including age and sex. However, as summary-level data were used, individual-level clinical characteristics such as family history of cholelithiasis, history of cholecystectomy, specific symptoms (biliary colic, jaundice, cholangitis), or biochemical parameters (GGT levels, cytolysis) are not available. This is a inherent limitation of two-sample MR using aggregated data, and is addressed in the Limitations section.

Selection of genetic instruments

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with cholelithiasis at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected as instrumental variables. Independent variants were obtained through linkage disequilibrium clumping (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb). Instrument strength was evaluated using F-statistics, with values greater than 10 indicating sufficient strength to minimize weak instrument bias [11].

Mendelian randomization analysis

In the primary analysis, cholelithiasis was treated as the exposure. In bidirectional Mendelian randomization analyses, cholelithiasis was alternatively treated as the outcome to evaluate potential reverse causality.

Primary causal estimates were derived using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method under a random-effects model. Sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger regression and weighted median approaches to assess robustness against potential pleiotropy.

Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic, and directional pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test. Leave-one-out analyses were conducted to determine whether individual SNPs disproportionately influenced causal estimates. MR-PRESSO was performed to identify potential horizontal pleiotropy using global, outlier, and distortion tests. MR Steiger filtering was applied to assess whether each SNP explained more variance in the exposure than in the outcome, thereby supporting the assumed causal direction.

Reverse Mendelian randomization

To evaluate potential reverse causality, bidirectional MR analyses were performed by reversing exposure and outcome datasets for outcomes demonstrating significant associations in primary analyses.

Multivariable Mendelian randomization

Multivariable MR (MVMR) analyses were conducted to account for potential confounding by body mass index (BMI) [12]. Genetic instruments associated with BMI were incorporated to estimate the direct effect of cholelithiasis independent of adiposity-related pathways.BMI was prioritized over other potential confounders (e.g., type 2 diabetes, lipid profiles, smoking, alcohol intake, primary sclerosing cholangitis, inflammatory bowel disease) because it is the most consistently documented shared risk factor for both cholelithiasis and multiple hepatopancreatobiliary outcomes, and its genetic instruments are robust and widely available. Adjustment for other factors was not feasible due to data limitations, as acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2) with the TwoSampleMR and ieugwasr packages interfacing with the IEU OpenGWAS database. The study followed STROBE-MR reporting guidelines [13]. Statistical significance was determined using two-sided tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. An FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because this study used publicly available GWAS summary statistics without individual-level data, no additional inclusion or exclusion criteria for study participants were applied beyond those already implemented in the original GWAS.

Results

Primary MR analyses revealed a consistent causal association between genetically predicted cholelithiasis and acute pancreatitis across independent datasets (Fig. 3A). In the IVW analysis, genetic liability to cholelithiasis was associated with an increased risk of acute pancreatitis (OR = 1.522, 95% CI 1.418–1.635, P = 6.49 × 10⁻31). This expected association was interpreted as a positive control supporting the validity of the MR framework. Causal estimates across all evaluated outcomes, including non-significant associations, are summarized in Fig. 3B.

Forest plot of causal effects. A Forest plot showing significant causal associations between genetic liability to cholelithiasis and clinical outcomes. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the IVW method. Associations remained significant after FDR correction. B Forest plot summarizing causal estimates across all evaluated outcomes. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals are displayed for IVW analyses. The dashed vertical line indicates the null effect (OR = 1)

A positive association was also observed between cholelithiasis liability and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) (Fig. 3A). In the IVW analysis, genetically predicted cholelithiasis was associated with an increased risk of extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) (OR = 1.282, 95% CI 1.090–1.509, P = 0.00278). Although effect sizes varied slightly across populations, the consistency in direction supported a potential link between biliary stone formation and carcinogenesis.

No evidence supported a causal relationship between cholelithiasis and portal vein thrombosis. In addition, no significant association was observed for alcohol-induced acute pancreatitis, which was considered a negative control outcome given its distinct clinical pathophysiology from gallstone-related pancreatitis.

Sensitivity analyses produced concordant estimates without evidence of substantial heterogeneity or directional pleiotropy, as assessed by Cochran’s Q statistics and MR-Egger intercept tests (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). MR-PRESSO analyses showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy for most outcomes (Supplementary Table S5). For extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), the MR-PRESSO global test suggested marginal evidence of pleiotropy (P = 0.048), with one outlier SNP (rs12633863) identified; however, the distortion test was not statistically significant (P = 0.381), indicating that removal of this variant did not materially alter the causal estimate. MR Steiger filtering supported the expected causal direction for the main significant outcomes, including acute pancreatitis and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) (Supplementary Table S6). Leave-one-out analyses demonstrated stability of causal estimates (Fig. 4A and B).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. A Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA). Each point represents the IVW estimate after excluding one SNP at a time. Results demonstrate that no single variant disproportionately influenced the causal estimate. B Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for acute pancreatitis showing robustness of causal estimates across individual SNP exclusions

Reverse MR analyses showed no consistent evidence supporting reverse causality (Supplementary Table S7). Although one reverse analysis suggested a potential association between genetic liability to acute pancreatitis and cholelithiasis, this finding was not consistently replicated across datasets or supported across complementary MR methods, and was therefore interpreted cautiously rather than as robust evidence of bidirectional causality.

Multivariable MR adjusting for BMI showed that the observed associations were not fully explained by adiposity-related pathways (Supplementary Table S8). However, additional metabolic and inflammatory traits, including lipid profiles, type 2 diabetes, primary sclerosing cholangitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, were not fully evaluated and are discussed as limitations.

Discussion

This study extends existing evidence by providing a systematic and genetically informed evaluation of the role of cholelithiasis across multiple hepatopancreatobiliary outcomes. The strong association with acute pancreatitis aligns with longstanding clinical observations and served as a positive control for the MR framework rather than as the main novel finding. One possible explanation involves chronic biliary micro-obstruction and inflammatory signaling associated with lithogenic pathways.

The association with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) represents the primary clinically relevant finding of this study. This result suggests that prolonged biliary irritation or altered bile composition may contribute to carcinogenic processes [14, 15]. Importantly, multivariable MR analyses indicate that these relationships cannot be fully explained by BMI, suggesting that genetic liability to cholelithiasis itself may carry biological relevance beyond shared adiposity-related risk factors.

Notably, the lack of a significant association with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) may be biologically plausible, as gallstone disease primarily affects the gallbladder and extrahepatic biliary tract. A recent clinical cohort study of 112 patients with primary intrahepatic lithiasis followed for up to 30 years reported only one case of cholangiocarcinoma (0.9%), further supporting the low carcinogenic risk of isolated intrahepatic stones.This anatomical specificity supports the hypothesis that local biliary inflammation and obstruction may contribute more directly to extrahepatic or overall bile duct carcinogenesis than to intrahepatic malignancy [16]. Furthermore, a histopathological study of patients with ABCB4 mutations (which cause low-phospholipid associated cholelithiasis) showed ductular reactions but no or mild fibrosis and no biliary dysplasia [17]. Another study first reported cholangiocarcinoma in two patients with heterozygous ABCB4 mutations, suggesting that MDR3 deficiency-related “toxic bile” may increase biliary carcinogenesis risk [18].

Overall, our findings suggest that genetic susceptibility to cholelithiasis may increase the risk of acute pancreatitis and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA). The consistency of results across multiple sensitivity analyses supports the causal relevance of cholelithiasis for these outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, most GWAS datasets were derived from individuals of European ancestry or predominantly European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, potential sample overlap between exposure and outcome GWAS datasets could not be completely excluded because only summary-level data were available. Third, although multivariable MR was performed to adjust for BMI, other potential confounders or mediators, such as lipid profiles, type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, were not fully evaluated.The choice to prioritize BMI was based on its strong and consistent association with both exposure and outcomes in observational studies, whereas other factors either lacked suitable genetic instruments or had potential for sample overlap. Fourth, some outcomes, particularly extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), included relatively small case numbers, which may reduce statistical power and widen confidence intervals. Finally, MR analyses rely on instrumental variable assumptions that cannot be fully verified, although multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness.
