RVCBench: Benchmarking the Robustness of Voice Cloning Across Modern Audio Generation Models
Xinting Liao, Ruinan Jin, Hanlin Yu, Deval Pandya, Xiaoxiao Li

Abstract
Modern voice cloning ( VC ) can synthesize speech that closely matches a target speaker from only seconds of reference audio, enabling applications such as personalized speech interfaces and dubbing. In practical deployments, modern audio generation models inevitably encounter noisy reference audios, imperfect text prompts, and diverse downstream processing, which can significantly hurt robustness. Despite rapid progress in VC driven by autoregressive codec-token language models and diffusion-based models, robustness under realistic deployment shifts remains underexplored. This paper introduces RVCBench , a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates R obustness in VC across the full generation pipeline, including input variation, generation challenges, output post-processing, and adversarial perturbations , covering 10 robustness tasks, 225 speakers, 14,370 utterances, and 11 representative modern VC models. Our evaluation uncovers substantial robustness gaps in VC : performance can deteriorate sharply under common input shifts and post-processing; long-context and cross-lingual scenarios further expose stability limitations; and both passive noise and proactive perturbation influence generation robustness. Collectively, these findings provide a unified picture of how current VC models fail in practice and introduce a standardized, open-source testbed to support the development of more robust and deployable VC models. Modern voice cloning (
VC

(
2
)
. Recent progress in audio generation has driven substantial improvements in
VC
, driven by a shift from classic acoustic-model pipelines toward
(1) autoregressive codec token language models (LMs)

(
22
;
10
;
3
)
,
(2) diffusion and flow-based generation

(

;

;

;

)
, and
(3) hybrid systems that combine token LMs with diffusion refinement

(
10
;
8
)
.

Although audio generation models demonstrate strong capabilities in
VC
, their performance depends strongly on the quality of reference audio, the textual context, linguistic coverage, model architecture, and any downstream processing applied to the synthesized output. For example, reference audio captured “in the wild” (e.g., with background noise) can distort speaker cues and degrade cloning quality
(
26
;
15
)
. Likewise, long and noisy text prompts (e.g., special tokens, formatting templates, and mixed-language fragments) can impair content accuracy and lead to instability in long-form generation
(
11
)
.
Moreover, cloned audio is forensically perceptible due to persistent artifacts that facilitate deepfake detection.
While several studies
(
34
;
6
;
23
)
evaluate robustness in audio generation or audio watermarking broadly, to the best of our knowledge,
there is no focused study on the robustness of
VC
itself
.
These observations raise the crucial question for
VC
.

How robust is
VC
in practice to realistic setup shifts across the entire generation pipeline?

Existing audio-related benchmarks fall into two categories, neither of which answers our question.
(1)
VC
benchmarks
(e.g., CloneEval
(
7
;
24
)
) standardize comparisons of
VC
models, but are largely quality-centric under clean settings, with limited model coverage and metrics that prioritize utility over robustness failure modes (e.g., audio/text shifts or long-form identity drift).
Consequently, they neither systematically stress-test realistic setup shifts nor evaluate robustness in a unified pipeline view.
(2) General audio robustness benchmarks
(e.g., Aha-Bench
(
6
)
, AudioWatermarkBench
(
23
)
, and related efforts
(
38
)
) study robustness for broader audio generation or specific aspects of audio-LMs (e.g., hallucinations, or watermark resilience), but are not tailored to
VC
.
As a result, they miss key
VC
-specific stressors such as reference-audio domain shifts, cross-lingual/long-context identity drift, post-processing, and proactive perturbation-based robustness.

To investigate this question, we propose a comprehensive
Bench
mark on the
R
bustness of
V
oice
C
loning, i.e.,
RVCBench
.
As shown in Fig.
1
,
RVCBench
covers a wide spectrum of realistic and adversarial conditions spanning input variation, generation challenges, output post-processing, and audio perturbations, reflecting how state-of-the-art
VC
models are deployed and underperformed in real-world scenarios.
For
input robustness
,
RVCBench
covers demographic shifts in reference audio and text prompt diversifications such as special tokens and scam-related content.
For
generation robustness
,
RVCBench
probes the limits of cross-lingual synthesis, long-context stability, and emotion adherence.
For
output robustness
,
RVCBench
evaluate the output resilience of
VC
, by analyzing performance degradation under realistic post-processing compression and deepfake detectability.
For
audio perturbation robustness
,
RVCBench
assesses the model performance against both passive environmental noise and proactive perturbations added by anti-clone defenses, as well as denoising.

RVCBench
involves 11 models with three language settings, and across 10 tasks consistently aligned with different robustness dimensions.
Based on the empirical analysis of
RVCBench
, we derive the following key insights:

For input robustness
, content consistency is fragile to input shifts: textual variations and reference-audio fluctuations both destabilize cloning and reduce fidelity.

For generation robustness
, domain shift, cross-lingual cloning, and long-form synthesis expose bottlenecks, including timbre, emotion, and content preservation degradation.

For output robustness
, post-processing is a weak point: compression degrades output audios, and state-of-the-art deepfake detectors still reliably flag the resulting outputs, undermining both stability and stealth.

For audio-perturbation robustness
, passive and proactive
perturbations remain highly effective, sharply suppressing speaker similarity and generation quality under attack/defense settings.

Our contributions are threefold: (1) we propose
RVCBench
, the first benchmark that systematically evaluates robustness of modern
VC
systems across the full generation pipeline, covering 10 representative tasks.
(2) we release a comprehensive evaluation dataset of 225 speakers, 14,370 utterances supporting 18 robustness evaluations; and (3) we provide an extensive empirical study of 11 modern representative
VC
models, identifying key failure modes and vulnerabilities that motivate more robust voice-cloning methods.

RVCBench

In this section, we introduce
RVCBench
, a comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate robustness throughout
VC
generation pipeline.
In a

f_{\theta}
generates cloned speech
\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}=f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{ref}}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{t})
by aligning text prompt
\boldsymbol{t}
with reference audio
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{ref}}
(potentially perturbed by
\boldsymbol{\delta}
).
As illustrated in Fig.
1
,
RVCBench
systematically evaluates
VC
robustness under four dimensions of realistic shifts, by quantifying how well models preserve semantic correctness and acoustic fidelity.

3.1
Benchmark Overview

Dataset.

RVCBench
is built on eight public sources (e.g., VCTK
(
43
)
, Libritts
(
44
)
, etc). Specifically, we
reprocess
these sources into 18 robustness evaluations to support a unified robustness evaluation across
four robustness dimensions
(input sensitivity, generation bottleneck, output perception, and perturbation influences) and
10 tasks
. In total,
RVCBench
covers
225 speakers
and
14,370 utterances
, spanning clean English baselines, multilingual as well as cross-lingual settings. We post-process all these subsets to cover a comprehensive evaluation pipeline, where we standardize formats and sampling, assign text-audio pairs by task, and construct perturbations, enabling consistent robustness comparisons across diverse scenarios. We summarize the dataset details in Tab.
5
and tasks in Tab.
6
in appendix.

Voice clone models.

Recent
VC
models can be categorized by the
generative architecture
used to produce the output waveform (typically through a neural codec). We group them into three families: (1)
autoregressive codec token models (LLM-based models)
predict sequences of discrete neural codec tokens in a left-to-right manner (e.g., FishSpeech
(
22
)
, SparkTTS
(
41
)
, MOSS-TTSD
(
25
)
, Higgs Audio v2
(
3
)
).
These LLM-based
VC
models naturally support flexible conditioning (text prompts, instruction prompts, and reference audio) and can perform zero-shot cloning from short reference audio.
(2)
diffusion and flow acoustic models (diffusion-based models)
produce codec latents by transforming noise into a target sample either through iterative denoising (diffusion) or a continuous-time flow trajectory (flow matching) (e.g., StyleTTS-2
(
21
)
, PlayDiffusion
1
1

1

https://github.com/playht/PlayDiffusion
, and OZSpeech
(
17
)
. They are usually adopted for high naturalness, long-form, and for editing operations such as inpainting.
(3)
hybrid models
use an LM-style module for linguistic or coarse acoustic planning, followed by a flow generator for high-fidelity acoustic synthesis (e.g., CosyVoice 2
(
10
)
, GLM-TTS
(
8
)
, and VibeVoice
(
28
)
).
In addition to these
VC
-specific, recent
multimodal LLMs with speech heads
(e.g., MGM-Omni
(
39
)
) can be viewed as an
extension of the hybrid family
: a multimodal reasoning core (text/vision) first produces intermediate representations, and a dedicated speech head, often implemented as a hybrid LM+flow acoustic generator, then renders them into spoken responses, enabling interactive and personalized dialogue.

Metrics.

Following established practice in
VC

(
3
;
8
;
45
)
, we report widely used metrics to facilitate standardized and consistent evaluation on generation quality.
We implement the
speaker identity
via Speaker Similarity (SIM), Mean Opinion Score (MOS)
(
30
)
, the
spectral consistency
via Mel-cepstral distortion (MCD), the
inference efficiency
via real-time factor (RTF), and the
content accuracy
via word error rate (WER). Unless otherwise specified, for generation quality we report SIM and MOS where higher is better (
\uparrow
), and WER, MCD, and RTF where lower is better (
\downarrow
). The details can be found in the appendix.

3.2
Input Robustness

Reference audio shifts.

In the real-world deployment,
reference audio shifts
across languages and demographics. We design
RVC-AudioShift
, which varies demographic characteristics of the reference audio to examine which speaker traits are best captured by existing
VC
models.
Specifically, we assess performance using prompt audios with diverse demographic attributes from VCTK
(
43
)
, spanning 12 accents (ranging from widely represented varieties, e.g., American, to underrepresented ones, e.g., Indian and South African), gender (male and female), and age (from under 20 to over 30).

Text prompt shifts.

In practical
VC
applications, user queries exhibit variations in both
format
and
content
.
Irregular formatting (e.g., typos or mixed-language fragments) can induce hallucination by disrupting text–audio alignment, while content variations may involve
scam-related
user intent.
Motivated by these observations, we propose
RVC-TextShift
, which comprises two subsets of prompts: (1)
hallucination
prompts generated by prompting an LLM to introduce realistic format irregularities, and (2)
scam-content
prompts constructed from robocall scripts from a public dataset
2
2

2

https://github.com/wspr-ncsu/robocall-audio-dataset
.
Using
RVC-TextShift
, we evaluate robustness to text prompt shifts by measuring the maintenance of semantic and acoustic consistency.

3.3
Generation Robustness

Multilingual generalizability.

Recent progress in audio generation models improves their generalizability across speech-related tasks
(
3
;
24
;
39
)
.
In
RVC-Multilingual
, we test whether
VC
models can reliably synthesize speech under two types of language shifts, i.e., (1)
single-language

VC
containing
English-VC
built from VCTK and LibriTTS, and
Chinese-VC
built from AISHELL-1
(
4
)
, and (2)
cross-lingual

VC
instantiated with the EMIME
(
42
)
bilingual English-Mandarin set, where the language of the reference audio may differ from that of the input text.
These settings probe whether models preserve speaker identity while maintaining target-language content consistency under language shifts.

Long-form generation.

It is crucial to know whether the model can sustain coherent and stable synthesis when either the input text or the reference audio (or both) is lengthy, without accumulating errors or drifting in speaker identity
(
25
)
.
We further evaluate
long-context robustness
(
RVC-LongContext
) in two settings:
(1) Long-text
, which uses extended text prompts and requires sustained generation over long textual contexts, and
(2) Long-audio
, which uses long reference audios that may provide richer speaker cues. We measure how performance changes with increasing text length and reference duration, and report both content accuracy and speaker similarity to capture performance change over long spans.

Expressive preservation.

RVC-Expression
evaluates a
VC
model’s ability to preserve the target speaker identity while faithfully rendering paralinguistic attributes, e.g., emotion, emphasis, and speaking style, inferred in the text prompt.
This setting reflects practical voice interfaces (e.g., movie dubbing) and also covers high-risk scenarios such as telephone fraud.
We construct
RVC-Expression
by pairing VCTK speakers with robocall-style prompts from an online telephone-fraud dataset and augmenting the prompts with explicit style cues.
In addition to general metrics of generation quality, we primarily assess
VC
models
on emotion adherence between output audio and text prompts to determine whether they can successfully infer prosody while maintaining speaker consistency.

3.4
Output Robustness

Post-processing resilience.

In practical deployment, cloned speech is often subject to ubiquitous post-processing (e.g., compression and re-encoding), which can degrade both semantic intelligibility and speaker-related acoustic information such as timbre and prosody.
To explore the post-processing resilience,
RVC-Compression
transforms cloned speeches with a realistic encode–decode pipeline with 6 compression conditions, i.e., MP3, AAC, and Opus at 64/32/24/16 kbps, and a telephone narrowband simulation
(
31
)
.
Then, we compute the generation quality of speeches after compression, which verifies whether the cloned speeches maintain acoustic information.

Deepfake detectability.

As
VC
fidelity improves and synthetic artifacts become less apparent, distinguishing cloned speech from real utterances becomes increasingly challenging.
To quantify detectability, we introduce
RVC-Detectability
, which covers 300 ground truth utterances of VCTK, 100 benign cloned speeches with text from VCTK, and 200 scam cloned speeches with text from robocall
(
29
)
.
We assess the separability between ground truth audios and cloned speeches with both benign and malicious content using zero-shot SpeechLLM-as-Judges models (SQ-LLM) from Microsoft
(
40
)
.

3.5
Audio Perturbation Robustness

Passive perturbation.

In real-world scenarios, reference audio inevitably contains ambient noise and multi-speaker interference, which hinders
VC
models from reliably extracting speaker-specific acoustic features.
To simulate the scenarios and measure robustness under passive perturbations,
RVC-PassiveNoise
firstly mixes VCTK utterances with 10 types of environmental noises (e.g., cafeteria, car, and kitchen) from VoiceBannk+DEMAND
(
37
)
.

RVC-PassiveNoise
also contains 600 multi-speaker interfered audios, where clean target utterances are mixed with interfering speech from additional speakers at three different decibels.
We then evaluate
VC
outputs generated from the perturbed references to quantify how well each method preserves speaker identity and content fidelity under passive noise and interference.

Proactive perturbation.

For the concerns of deepfake misuse,
speaker-side proactive defenses (e.g., Enkidu
(
12
)
, POP
(
46
)
, SafeSpeech and its variant SPEC
(
45
)
) that add human-imperceptible perturbations to the reference audio to prevent unauthorized cloning.

RVC-AdvNoise
evaluates whether the
VC
methods can attack these methods.
Firstly, we generate the adversarial perturbation on the clean ground truth data to protect the speakers from VCTK, and compute perceptual metrics between perturbed utterances and original utterances, to ensure the reproducibility of proactive defenses.
Next, similar to passive perturbation, we assess the generation quality of
VC
methods to reflect their attack effectiveness in
RVC-AdvNoise
.

Counteract perturbation.

Though the adversary perturbation can be used to defend against cloning attacks, several denoising methods are being developed for removing them.
In
RVC-AntiProtect
, we purify the protected ground truth utterances with adversarial perturbation by DEMUCS
(
9
)
.
DEMUCS is widely used for removing a variety of background noise, i.e., stationary and non-stationary noises, and room reverb.
By using denoised speech as the reference, we re-evaluate generation quality to measure how effectively the target speaker’s identity is protected against denoising-based countermeasures.

4.3
Instability in Diversified Generation Tasks

Insight 4.3.

VC
models are generally not robust to language shifts, long-form text prompts, and emotionally expressive prompts.

Language shifts beyond English cause a sharp robustness instability.

For
RVC-Multilingual
, we compare the English results in Tab.
1
against the
Chinese-VC
and
Cross-Lingual
settings in Tab.
2
, and observe a clear robustness drop when moving beyond English.
On English (
English-VC-LibriTTS
and
English-VC-VCTK
), many models achieve high naturalness (MOS around 4) with low WER (often below 0.1), consistent with their heavy exposure to English training data. In contrast,
Chinese-VC
markedly degrades perceptual quality across all evaluated
VC
models (MOS shifts to the mid-to-low range), while content accuracy deteriorates dramatically (WER rises for most models).
Cross-Lingual
cloning further compounds these issues: although some models maintain relatively strong speaker similarity, preserving linguistic content becomes the primary bottleneck, evidenced by higher WER and less consistent spectral quality. Overall, these results suggest that language shifts remain fragile and vary considerably across backends.

Audio length variations can impact the generation, and long texts break the robustness of content accuracy.

In the long-form context, we evaluate
LongAudio
and
LongText
separately.
First
, we study
LongAudio
and find that audio length variations proportionally impact the generation quality. As shown in Fig.
4
, both metrics change most rapidly in the short-prompt regime, e.g., MCD (Fig.
4(a)
) decreases sharply as prompt length increases from a few seconds to roughly 8–12 seconds, while SIM (Fig.
4(b)
) rises accordingly.
Beyond this range, the margin of performance gains converges, indicating diminishing returns from additional reference audio.
Model sensitivity, however, differs: stronger backends (e.g., CosyVoice, Higgs, and MGM-Omni) continue to benefit with higher SIM and lower MCD, whereas others show limited SIM improvements even with long prompts (e.g., StyleTTS and OZSpeech). We also observe mild non-monotonicity for some methods (notably SparkTTS), suggesting that longer prompts can introduce instability in certain pipelines.

Conclusion

In this work, we propose
RVCBench
, a benchmark designed to evaluate the robustness of
VC
against realistic shifts. By extensively analyzing 10
VC
tasks with 14,370 utterances across 11 representative models, we uncover critical limitations, including (1) content degradation caused by input shifts, (2) generation bottlenecks across diverse tasks, (3) failures in maintaining spectral consistency and evading deepfake detection, and (4) vulnerability to perturbations.
These findings demonstrate that a deepened focus on robustness is essential to bridge the gap toward the practical and benign applications of
VC
technology.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
Voice clone

Voice clone.

Voice cloning (
VC
) aims to synthesize speech that preserves a target speaker’s identity (e.g., timbre and speaker-specific characteristics) while accurately rendering new linguistic content.
Following the standardized terminology proposed by Azzuni and El Saddik
(
2
)
, we view
VC
as an extension of TTS
speaker adaptation
and distinguish:
(1)
speaker adaptation
(fine-tuning a TTS system to a target speaker with limited data),
(2)
few-shot
VC
(adaptation with very short reference audio, ranging from seconds to a few minutes),
and (3)
zero-shot
VC
(no fine-tuning; the model conditions on a short reference clip via an explicit speaker representation such as a speaker embedding/encoder).
With the maturation of neural TTS, modern
VC
increasingly targets
zero-shot
cloning from only a few seconds of reference audio while emphasizing high-fidelity identity preservation, natural prosody, and controllability
(
2
)
.
This paper focuses on benchmarking
zero-shot

VC
models under realistic deployment conditions.
Representative system families include (1) sequence-to-sequence and codec/token-based pipelines that map text (and optionally reference speech) to discrete acoustic units followed by neural decoding, and (2) diffusion/flow-based approaches that synthesize mel or latent representations with improved naturalness and stability.
Despite strong progress, most prior
VC
research and evaluation centers on standard benchmarks and clean reference conditions, leaving gaps in robustness to realistic shifts such as demographics, accents, background noise, multi-speaker interference, and downstream transformations.

Modeling paradigms.

A useful lens to organize recent
VC
systems is the
generation paradigm
and where speaker identity is injected into the pipeline (e.g., speaker embedding vs. in-context prompting)
(
2
)
. A notable recent trend is to integrate LLM capabilities directly into speech generation, where an
audio LLM
(or LLM-like backbone) models speech tokens and aligns text and speech in a unified latent space; compared with classic acoustic-model pipelines, these systems often provide stronger instruction following, longer-context generation, and improved cross-lingual behavior while still supporting zero-shot
VC
from short references. The models in our benchmark (Table
4
) span three representative families:
(1) Autoregressive codec-token LMs
generate discrete neural codec units conditioned on text and (optionally) reference audio, then decode tokens to waveforms, exemplified by FishSpeech
(
22
)
, SparkTTS
(
41
)
, MOSS-TTSD
(
25
)
, and Higgs Audio
(
3
)
;
(2) Diffusion / flow-matching models
synthesize mel/latent features via iterative refinement or learn transport dynamics for improved stability and perceptual quality
(
2
)
, including O2Speech
(
17
)
, StyleTTS
(
21
)
3
3

3

RVCBench
implements the original StyleTTS, which only supports English cloning
, and PlayDiffusion
4
4

4

https://github.com/playht/PlayDiffusion
, for long-form conversational speech
(
28
)
; and
(3) Hybrid LLM + diffusion/flow models
that combine token-level modeling with continuous refinement for scalability and production readiness, such as CosyVoice 2
(
10
)
, MGM-Omni
(
39
)
, VibeVoice
(
28
)
, and GLM-TTS
(
8
)
. At the same time,
in-context
cloning via prompting (conditioning on reference audio without fine-tuning) lowers the barrier for fast personalization but can amplify misuse risks, motivating robustness evaluation beyond “clean MOS/SIM”: audio-LLM-based
VC
should tolerate noisy/short references and multilingual or scam-like text, remain stable over long contexts (identity consistency and reduced drift), and be accountable under detection and defense mechanisms.

Evaluation gaps and robustness motivation.

While prior work reports strong quality and similarity under curated benchmarks (e.g., MOS and speaker-similarity metrics)
(
2
)
,
real deployments face systematic shifts that are rarely stress-tested: noisy or extremely short references, multi-speaker reference contamination, accent/dialect mismatch, multilingual prompts, long-form generation with identity drift, and post-processing transformations (codec compression, resampling) that affect both perceptual quality and detectability.
The diversity of paradigms above also implies different failure modes (e.g., tokenization artifacts for codec-LMs, oversmoothing or style leakage for diffusion, streaming boundary artifacts for chunk-based hybrids).
Our benchmark is designed to capture these practical robustness dimensions across modern zero-shot
VC
model families rather than optimizing only for clean-condition MOS/SIM.

Related benchmarks for
VC
and audio robustness

Existing benchmarks that relate to our study broadly fall into two threads, but neither answers our central question of
VC
robustness
as an end-to-end pipeline problem
.

Voice cloning and closely related speech synthesis benchmarks
largely standardize
clean or near-clean
comparison, but they do not provide a comprehensive view of
VC
robustness across realistic pipeline shifts.
CloneEval
(
7
)
, for example, reports results for a small set of open-weight VC systems (five models: OuteTTS, SpeechT5, VALL-E X, WhisperSpeech, and XTTS-v2) and ranks them primarily using cosine similarity between WavLM speaker embeddings, with auxiliary analysis based on cosine similarity of hand-crafted acoustic features and emotion-wise breakdowns; however, its metrics remain dominated by similarity proxies and its evaluation does not systematically stress-test
VC
under deployment-relevant shifts such as corrupted or bandwidth-limited references, prompt artifacts, multilingual transfer, long-form identity drift, downstream codec/re-encoding, deepfake detectability, or proactive defenses. EmergentTTS-Eval
(
24
)
is closer in spirit to robustness testing, but it targets
TTS
prompt-following rather than voice cloning: it constructs 1,645 challenging prompts across six categories and evaluates a broad set of systems (seven open-source models plus multiple proprietary systems), using a model-as-a-judge win-rate alongside WER (Whisper-v3-large) and predicted MOS; crucially, it does not test the defining
VC
constraint of preserving a target speaker identity conditioned on a short reference, and thus cannot diagnose VC-specific robustness failures driven by reference-audio shifts or identity stability.

General audio robustness benchmarks
evaluate reliability in broader audio generation and audio-language model settings but are not tailored to
VC
.
AHa-Bench
(
6
)
targets
auditory hallucinations
in audio-language models via a taxonomy of hallucination types and QA-style tests, emphasizing semantic/perceptual reliability rather than speaker-identity preservation under cloning.
AudioWatermarkBench
(
23
)
focuses on the robustness of
watermarking
pipelines (e.g., detectability, removal, and forgery) under perturbations, which is orthogonal to evaluating the robustness limits of
VC
models themselves.
In summary, prior benchmarks either (1) evaluate voice cloning/speech synthesis under mostly controlled settings without comprehensive robustness coverage, or (2) evaluate robustness in general audio tasks without the
VC
scenario and its identity- and reference-conditioned failure modes.
This gap motivates our benchmark, which unifies robustness evaluation across inputs, challenging generation regimes, output post-processing, and passive/proactive perturbations to quantify how modern
VC
systems degrade under realistic setup shifts throughout the generation pipeline.

A.2
Proactive protection

Defense against unauthorized voice cloning.

The rapid progress of high-fidelity speech synthesis has made unauthorized
VC
inexpensive and scalable, motivating
proactive
defenses that “vaccinate” a speaker’s released audio by injecting carefully designed, human-imperceptible perturbations so that downstream cloners fail to learn a usable voice. In this line, we mainly reproduce and study
POP

(
46
)
,
SPEC

(
45
)
,
SafeSpeech

(
45
)
, and
Enkidu

(
12
)
, while covering other defenses more briefly.
POP (
Pivotal Objective Perturbation
) targets the
training stage
: it adds imperceptible, utility-preserving noise to speech samples so that training or fine-tuning on the protected data yields degraded synthesis quality or incorrect speaker identity, while the original audio remains natural for benign listening
(
46
)
. Building on similar “unlearnable audio” intuition, SPEC is developed as a
robust and universal
perturbation strategy that uses a surrogate synthesis pipeline to craft transferable protection across architectures; SafeSpeech subsequently integrates and strengthens SPEC with additional optimization for perceptual acceptability and robustness under stronger attacker adaptations, forming a practical framework for protecting audio prior to upload
(
45
)
. Complementary to these offline, upload-time protections, Enkidu emphasizes
real-time
privacy: it constructs lightweight, universal perturbation patches that can be applied to streaming or variable-length speech to mitigate live deepfake/voice-cloning threats while preserving intelligibility and perceptual quality
(
12
)
.

Security in broader audio domains.

The imperative for audio security extends beyond voice cloning to a wider spectrum of speech processing tasks. In the realm of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), recent studies have addressed privacy leakage in commercial and LLM-powered systems
(
19
)
, as well as phoneme-based anti-eavesdropping mechanisms
(
16
)
. In the entertainment sector, proactive prevention has been applied to singing voice conversion to stop illegal song covers
(
5
)
. Additionally, multimodal defenses have emerged, such as leveraging adversarial examples to nullify audio control in talking-head generation to protect visual privacy
(
13
)
.
Despite the diverse applications of adversarial defense across the audio domain, this study strictly focuses on the protection of voice identity against cloning attacks. Unlike general ASR privacy or multimodal generation tasks, our work aligns with approaches like WaveFuzz
(
14
)
, which employs clean-label poisoning to prevent the unauthorized training of voice models, and SceneGuard
(
32
)
, which utilizes scene-consistent background noise to secure the speaker’s biometric timbre. We aim to safeguard the acoustic identity of the speaker from being exploited by malicious synthesis algorithms.

B.1
Datasets and Subset Construction

Unified speaker-level JSON manifests.

We standardize how all corpora are exposed to
VC
systems by materializing each subset as a collection of speaker-level JSON manifests.
For each selected speaker, we create one JSON file with a subset-dependent number of entries (typically 25–100; smaller for long-context and robocall stress tests).
Each entry stores a reference waveform (
ori_pth
) and a ground-truth target waveform (
gt_pth
), together with the associated text (and optional phonetic annotations).
During evaluation, the model receives the
ori_pth
waveform as the reference voice to be imitated and is asked to synthesize speech for
gt_text
; we then compare the generated audio against
gt_pth
using MCD, speaker-similarity, intelligibility, and related metrics.
To reduce train–test contamination, we preferentially draw examples from official development/test splits and avoid training splits whenever possible.

LibriTTS (clean English audiobooks).

We construct a small, speaker-balanced LibriTTS subset from dev-clean and test-clean with strict per-speaker coverage:
(1) start from the 20 female and 20 male speakers in test-clean, and (2) whenever a test-clean speaker has fewer than 100 usable utterances, supplement with additional material from dev-clean while preserving gender balance.
Each selected speaker contributes exactly 100 waveforms, stored as 100 paired entries in a single speaker JSON file. Audio is standardized to 24 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM.

VCTK (clean English multi-accent).

We use a curated subset of VCTK v0.92 designed to be compact but accent- and gender-balanced following common practice in evaluating demographic fairness
(
18
)
: 40 speakers (22F/18M) spanning 12 accent categories.
Major accents contribute 4–6 speakers, and minor accents contribute 1–3 speakers. Audio is standardized to 48 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM.

AISHELL-1 (clean Mandarin).

We build a Mandarin subset from AISHELL-1 with 40 speakers (IDs S0724–S0763; 28F/12M).
Each speaker contributes 50 paired utterances. Audio is standardized to 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM.
This subset probes multilingual generalization beyond English.

EMIME bilingual 96 kHz (English
\leftrightarrow
Mandarin).

We use the Mandarin-talker portion of the 96 kHz EMIME Bilingual English–Mandarin Database.
We select 13 speakers (6F/7M) and exclude MF2 due to abnormal recordings.
Per speaker, we include 25 English
\rightarrow
Mandarin and 25 Mandarin
\rightarrow
English sentence pairs (50 bilingual pairs total), with audio standardized to 96 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM.

CommonVoiceFR (French crowd-sourced speech).

We construct a French subset from Mozilla Common Voice v23.0 using the
validated.tsv
split.
We select 40 unique French speakers and create 50 paired utterances per speaker.
Audio is standardized to 48 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM (downsampled in code if needed). This subset introduces real-world microphone and accent variability beyond studio corpora.

LibriSpeech-Long (long-context stability).

We derive a long-form English subset from LibriSpeech-Long (dev/test).
We select 10 speakers and include 2 long-form pairs per speaker (20 pairs total), where each segment is typically 0.4–4 minutes.
Audio is standardized to 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM. This subset stresses long-context behaviors that may not appear on short utterances.

VoiceBank+DEMAND (natural noise robustness).

To study robustness under realistic noise, we derive a noisy VCTK subset from VoiceBank+DEMAND, which mixes clean VCTK utterances with environmental noises.
We use 20 VCTK speakers (p226–p273) and 10 noise environments at 10 dB SNR (babble, cafeteria, car, kitchen, meeting, metro, restaurant, ssn, station, traffic).
Per speaker, we include 20 noisy–clean paired items plus 10 additional clean-only utterances (30 items/speaker), for approximately 600 sentence items overall.
Audio is standardized to 48 kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM.

Multispeaker Libri (overlap/interference robustness).

We construct a synthetic multi-speaker interference set from our LibriTTS subset by mixing clean target utterances from 10 speakers with interfering speech from two additional speakers at controlled SNRs.
We use 10 target speakers (5M/5F) and 2 interferers (1M/1F). We select 100 clean ground-truth segments (10 per target speaker).
For each ground-truth segment, we generate 8 mixtures by combining 4 SNR levels (
-5,0,+5,+10
dB) with the 2 interferers, yielding 800 mixture entries.

Robocall (FTC PPoNE transcripts; VCTK speaker identities).

We prepare a robocall-style subset using scam transcripts from the Robocall Audio Dataset (FTC Project Point of No Entry, PPoNE), but we use it in a transcript-driven manner (we do not include the original robocall audio recordings).
We randomly select 10 VCTK speakers (p227, p232, p234, p237, p238, p251, p253, p262, p283, p288).
We define multiple scam categories (scam_type) using keyword rules; for each category we select a small number of complete-sentence scam prompts (e.g., two sentences per category) and reuse the same prompt set across all speakers to enable controlled cross-speaker comparison.

Audio Compression Dataset (codec + phone channel artifacts).

The split contains 440 original audio files organized into 11 folders: VCTK plus 10 TTS systems (cosyvoice, fishspeech, glm_tts, higgs_audio, mgm_omni, moss_ttsd, ozspeech, sparktts, styletts2, vibevoice).
Each folder contains 4 speakers with approximately 10 audio files per speaker; all clean files are mono WAV, PCM 16-bit.
We then generate 7 compressed variants (440 files each; 3,080 total):
(1) MP3 @ 64 kbps, 24 kHz; (2) AAC @ 64 kbps, 24 kHz; (3) Opus @ 24 kbps, 24 kHz;
(4) MP3 @ 32 kbps, 24 kHz; (5) AAC @ 32 kbps, 24 kHz; (6) Opus @ 16 kbps, 24 kHz;
(7) phone narrowband: telephone-quality simulation (8 kHz
\rightarrow
300–3400 Hz bandpass
\rightarrow
24 kHz).
All compressed variants are produced by a two-step pipeline (encode to the compressed format
\rightarrow
decode back to WAV) to ensure realistic compression artifacts are present; all decoded outputs are mono, 24 kHz, PCM
s16le
.

Hallucination
dataset.

We construct a hallucination-focused stress-test set to probe token- and prosody-level failures (e.g., misread symbols, dropped spans, malformed special tokens) under factually grounded but audio-unfriendly prompts. We first select four speakers from VCTK as voice references, and use each speaker’s reference audio to condition the downstream
VC
models during evaluation. To create the text prompts, we use Gemini-2.5 Flash as a controlled text generator and prompt it with the instruction in
Section

C.6
. We generate multiple batches and keep only samples that (1) contain at least one required special-token pattern, (2) fall within the target length range, and (3) include a high density of hard-to-speak items (e.g., ISO dates, timestamps, URLs, scientific notation). We then deduplicate near-identical samples and randomly assign the remaining prompts across the four VCTK speakers to form
RVC-Hallucination
.

Hallucination generation prompt

You are a data generator for audio-language-model stress testing, specifically for

voice cloning, TTS, ASR, and audio-LLM evaluation.

Please generate EXACTLY 10 independent text samples.

Each sample MUST satisfy ALL of the following constraints:

[1] Fact correctness (STRICT)

- ALL facts must be objectively correct and verifiable

- This includes:

- named entities

- dates

- numbers

- scientific facts

- historical facts

Appendix C
Metrics

This section defines all evaluation metrics used in
RVCBench
and specifies
when
each metric is computed.
We evaluate
VC
systems from four complementary perspectives:

(1) generation quality
(speaker identity, content alignment, perceptual quality),

(2) fidelity under protection
(e.g., less perceptible under adversarial perturbations),

(3) detectability
(deepfake detection),

(4) efficiency
and
(5) emotion
.

Unless stated otherwise, metrics are computed on synthesized waveform
\hat{a}
paired with a ground-truth/reference utterance
\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}}
(or
\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{ref}}
) and target transcript
y
.
In our implementation, we provide two evaluation modes:

(1) generation evaluation
, which compares
(\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}},\hat{a})
and reports generation quality metrics; and

(2) protection fidelity evaluation
, which compares original audio
\boldsymbol{a}
and protected audio
\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{prot}}
to quantify an invisibility–utility tradeoff. We report relative metrics that are related to our research question in the main paper, while providing all detailed metrics in Appendix
F
.

C.1
Generation Quality

Speaker similarity (SIM) and speaker verifiability (SVA).

To measure speaker identity preservation, we use SpeechBrain’s ECAPA-TDNN speaker verification model (
speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb
).
Given a ground-truth/reference utterance
\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}}
and generated audio
\hat{a}
, the verifier returns a similarity score and an accept/reject decision:

(\mathrm{SIM},\mathrm{SVA})=\mathrm{ASV}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}},\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}),

(1)

where SIM is the verifier score (higher indicates closer speaker identity) and SVA is the boolean verification decision (True if accepted).
In implementation, we coerce the decision into a boolean and report the acceptance rate across samples as
\mathrm{avg\_sva}
.

Word error rate (WER).

We quantify content correctness by transcribing
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}
with Whisper (
medium
) using strict transcription settings (no timestamps and no conditioning on previous text).
Let
\hat{y}
be the ASR transcript and
y
the target text. WER is

\mathrm{WER}(y,\hat{y})\;=\;\frac{S+D+I}{|y|}.

(2)

We apply language-aware normalization before scoring:
For non-CJK text, we lowercase and remove punctuation; for Chinese (or when Whisper detects
zh
/ CJK characters are present), we (1) convert traditional to simplified Chinese when possible, (2) normalize punctuation, (3) remove punctuation, and (4) segment with
jieba
before computing WER.

Mel-cepstral distortion (MCD).

When paired ground-truth
\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}}
is available, we compute DTW-aligned Mel-cepstral distortion using
pymcd
(
MCD_mode=dtw
).
Lower MCD indicates closer spectral characteristics to the ground-truth recording.

Perceptual naturalness (SpeechMOS / DNSMOS).

Human MOS is expensive at benchmark scale, so we report learned MOS proxies.
We compute
SpeechMOS
using UTMOS (
tarepan/SpeechMOS
, entry point
utmos22_strong
) and report the mean across utterances.
In addition, we compute
DNSMOS
via Microsoft’s released DNSMOS models and report the three standard dimensions:
overall quality (
OVRL
), signal quality (
SIG
), and background quality (
BAK
).
We download and cache the DNSMOS ONNX models when needed and evaluate audio after resampling to the DNSMOS sampling rate.

C.2
Efficiency

Real-time factor (RTF).

We evaluate generation efficiency using the real-time factor:

\mathrm{RTF}\;=\;\frac{T_{\mathrm{gen}}}{T_{\mathrm{audio}}},

(3)

where
T_{\mathrm{gen}}
is wall-clock synthesis time (from
synthesis_timings.csv
when available) and
T_{\mathrm{audio}}
is total generated audio duration.
RTF
<1
indicates faster-than-real-time synthesis.
Optionally, we cap generated audio to a maximum duration before metric computation to ensure consistent evaluation under long-form outputs.

C.3
Protection Fidelity

For proactive defense/protection methods, we compare protected audio
\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{prot}}
against original audio
\boldsymbol{a}
.

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

We measure distortion magnitude via

\mathrm{SNR}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{prot}})\;=\;10\log_{10}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{prot}}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(4)

Higher SNR implies smaller perturbations.

Short-time objective intelligibility (STOI).

We compute STOI using a differentiable STOI implementation (
torch_stoi
) on aligned/resampled waveforms:

\mathrm{STOI}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{prot}})\in[0,1],

(5)

where higher is better. If a waveform is shorter than 1 second, we pad with zeros to stabilize STOI.

C.4
Emotion Consistency

We evaluate emotion consistency by comparing emotion labels predicted from ground-truth and generated speech.
We use a SpeechBrain emotion recognizer (
speechbrain/emotion-recognition-wav2vec2-IEMOCAP
) and resample audio to 16 kHz before inference.
Let
\ell(\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathrm{gt}})
and
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}})
be the predicted labels; we report an
emotion consistency
:

\mathrm{EMC}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\!\left[\ell(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{gt}})=\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}^{(i)})\right].

(6)

This metric captures whether the generated sample preserves the perceived emotion category of the source utterance.

C.5
Deepfake Detectability

We follow standard detector-based evaluation.
Let a detector output score
s(x)
for utterance
x
, where larger scores indicate fake speech. Given threshold
\tau
:

\hat{y}(x)=\mathbb{I}[s(x)\geq\tau],

(7)

with
\hat{y}=1
for bonafide and
\hat{y}=0
for spoof.
We report EER, minDCF, and ACC as defined in §
C.5
(same as in the main text), and clarify that EER is threshold-independent, minDCF depends on
(C_{\mathrm{miss}},C_{\mathrm{fa}},\pi_{\mathrm{tar}})
, and ACC depends on a fixed operating threshold.

C.6
Emotion Alignment with Text

Motivated by EmergentTTS-Eval
(
24
)
, we utilize Gemini-3-Flash as an LALM-based judge, and evaluate the emotion consistency between generated audios and the ground truth text prompts.
We range the score of consistency from 0 to 3, via the prompt as follows:

Hallucination generation prompt

You will receive:

text (ground-truth)

3: Emotion clearly matches the text; natural and appropriate.

1: Weak/flat or noticeably mismatched emotion.

Ignore audio quality, accent, speaker identity, pronunciation

--unless they prevent judging emotion.

Output only the integer score (0, 1, 2, or 3).

Inputs

audio: {{{audio}}}

Output:

text: only the integer score (0, 1, 2, or 3)

Appendix D
Experimental Details

D.1
Voice Cloning Implementation

Across all
VC
backends, we largely follow each model’s recommended inference configuration, and only expose a small set of high-level decoding hyperparameters for reproducibility. Unless otherwise noted, reference prompts are assigned in a round-robin manner across utterances. For CosyVoice 2, we run non-streaming synthesis with a fixed speed factor of
1.0
, and resample prompt audio to
16 kHz
(
prompt_sample_rate=16000
).

For audio-LLM-based models, we use controlled stochastic decoding. FishSpeech uses temperature sampling with nucleus sampling (
temperature=0.8
,
top_p=0.8
), with a maximum generation budget (
max_new_tokens=1024
) and chunked decoding for long inputs (
chunk_length=200
). Higgs Audio v2 adopts low-temperature decoding with both top-
k
and top-
p
constraints (
temperature=0.1
,
top_k=50
,
top_p=0.95
). MGM-Omni is run with stochastic decoding at
temperature=0.3
and an instruction-based template, e.g.,
‘‘Please read the following text using the same voice as the provided audio sample: {text}.’’
For MOSS-TTSD, we keep default decoding settings but standardize instruction prompting with an explicit system message:
‘‘You are a speech synthesizer that generates natural, realistic, and human-like audio from text.’’
SparkTTS follows temperature-based sampling with top-
k
/top-
p
control (
temperature=0.3
,
top_k=50
,
top_p=0.95
). OZSpeech uses conservative decoding with a very low temperature (
temperature=0.01
).

For diffusion-based models, we specify step-based inference controls. PlayDiffusion uses
100
denoising steps, with initialization controls (
init_temp=1.0
,
init_diversity=1.0
) and a token filter (
top_k=25
). StyleTTS2 uses a small number of diffusion steps (
5
) together with guidance-based weights (
alpha=0.3
,
beta=0.7
) and
embedding_scale=1.0
. Finally, for VibeVoice and GLM-TTS we use the default inference setup; when applicable, we run in
bfloat16
and enable FlashAttention (
attn_implementation=flash_attention_2
), without additional model-specific tuning.

D.2
Proactive Perturbation Implementation

GRNoise (Gaussian Random Noise).

We apply independent and identically distributed Gaussian white noise to the waveform, i.e., sampling from
N(0,\epsilon^{2})
with noise radius
\epsilon=0.03137
being similar to other adversarial methods.
Each audio utterance is perturbed independently, and the resulting waveform is clipped to the valid audio range
[-1,1]
.
This baseline does not rely on any model or optimization and serves as a simple random-noise baseline.

SafeSpeech and SPEC.

We implement the SafeSpeech protection framework using the BERT-VITS2 model as a surrogate TTS model.
In SPEC mode, we optimize perturbations to degrade TTS training via mel reconstruction loss, noise reconstruction loss, and KL divergence (with coefficients
\eta_{\text{mel}}=45
,
\eta_{\text{kl}}=1
, and
\beta=10
).
In SafeSpeech mode, a perceptual loss term (e.g., STOI or STFT-based) is additionally added with weight
\eta_{\alpha}=1.0
to ensure imperceptibility.
Perturbations are computed via projected gradient ascent (step size
\alpha=\epsilon/10
, max norm
\epsilon=0.03137
), and clipped to waveform range
[-1,1]
. We use a batch size of 8, with 200 perturbation epochs and a sampling rate of 24 kHz.

POP.

We implement the Pivotal Objective Perturbation (POP) strategy by using BERT-VITS2 as a surrogate TTS model.
We jointly maximize the model’s training losses, i.e., mel reconstruction, generator-discriminator feature loss, duration prediction error, and latent KL divergence with coefficients
\eta_{\text{mel}}=45
and
\eta_{\text{kl}}=1
.
The perturbation is the same with Safespeech and SPEC.

Enkidu.

Enkidu applies a speaker-specific universal perturbation in the frequency domain (STFT).
We train a complex-valued noise patch using the SpeechBrain SpeakerRecognition model as a surrogate, maximizing speaker embedding divergence while preserving intelligibility.
During training, we randomly mask a ratio of 0.3 of STFT frames and apply a random offset to vary frame alignment.
We follow the perturbation approach outlined in the original work and select a learning rate of 0.1 over 10 perturbation epochs, with a decay factor of 0.2.
The perturbation intensity is set as 0.4, and optionally smoothed via Wiener filtering.
Audio is reconstructed via inverse STFT with sampling rate as 16 kHz, window size as 1024, and hop length as 512.

Washer (Purification-based bypass).

We model an attacker-side bypass that attempts to remove proactive perturbations by treating them as “noise” and applying a speech denoiser before cloning.
Specifically, we use the Facebook
denoiser
toolkit with the pretrained DEMUCS model. We run the denoiser at 16 kHz and resample the purified audio back to the target sampling rate required by each
VC
model.

D.3
Deepfake Detection Implementation

For
RVC-Detectability
with 600 audios from 10 speakers, which are sampled from VCTK.
For each speaker, we leverage 30 ground truth utterances, 10 generated
speeches with benign text from VCTK, and 20 generated speeches with scam text from Robocall
(
29
)
.
Then we randomly shuffle the utterances, and implement the state-of-the-art SpeechLLM-as-Judges as a deepfake detector, i.e., SQ-LLM
(
40
)
from Microsoft, to classify whether the speech is generated or not.
We follow the original implementation of SSQ-LLM, which is derived from Qwen2.5-Omini,
and inference with the detection prompt as below:

Deepfake detection prompt

[Audio: <AUDIO_PLACEHOLDER >]

Determine if this speech is real or a deepfake.
