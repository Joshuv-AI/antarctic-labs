Rare Survival of a 9-Year-Old Boy Managed for Post-Neonatal Tetanus Without Intensive Care at Soroti Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda: A Case Report
Authors: Amos Odiit, Fridah Akello, Tadeo Lujuna, Joseph Katende Mukiibi, Charles E Engoru, Moses Ochora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTetanus is a vaccine-preventable disease caused by neurotoxin from Clostridium tetani bacteria. Although neonatal tetanus has declined in many settings, post-neonatal tetanus continues to occur in low-resource countries where vaccination coverage and booster implementation remain suboptimal. Mortality in post-neonatal tetanus remains high, particularly where intensive care services are limited.Case PresentationWe report a 9-year-old African boy who presented three days after a minor toe injury with generalized painful muscular spasms, trismus, and hypertonia consistent with generalized tetanus (Ablett classification- Grade 3). He neither received the tetanus vaccine during infancy nor any boosters prior to this injury. He was managed on the general paediatric ward in a dark, quiet room without access to intensive care. He was hospitalized for 25 days and his treatment included wound care, antibiotics, nasogastric feeding, bladder catheterization, and alternating sedation with diazepam and chlorpromazine. Due to limited availability, human tetanus immunoglobulin was administered 10 days after hospital admission. The child recovered and was discharged alive.ConclusionPost-neonatal tetanus can be successfully managed in low-resource settings with strong supportive care even in the absence of intensive care services. Human tetanus immunoglobulin may still improve outcomes even when administered several days after exposure.Keywords: post-neonatal tetanus, injury, tetanus toxoid vaccine, tetanus human immunoglobulin, low resource setting

Introduction

Tetanus is a vaccine-preventable neurological disorder associated with high mortality.1 It is caused by tetanospasmin, a potent neurotoxin produced by Clostridium tetani, an obligate anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium that is ubiquitous in soil and animal feces. Clinical tetanus is classified into three main forms; generalized tetanus, localized tetanus, and cephalic tetanus. Generalized tetanus is the most common and severe form, characterized by trismus(lockjaw), painful spasms, and autonomic instability.2–4 A severity classification (Ablett Classification) has also been proposed that grades tetanus as; Grade 1 (mild)-mild/moderate trismus, spasticity and no respiratory distress; Grade 2 (moderate)-moderate trismus, rigid muscles with short spasms and moderate respiratory distress; Grade 3 (severe)-prolonged spasms, significant rigidity, breathing difficulties and tachycardia and Grade 4 (Very severe)-Grade 3 symptoms with severe autonomic dysfunction.5,6

Tetanus may also be categorized by age of onset into neonatal tetanus (occurring within the first 28 days of life) and post-neonatal tetanus (occurring after 28 days of life).2–4 Although post-neonatal tetanus has a better prognosis than neonatal tetanus, mortality can exceed 50%, particularly in low-resource settings where intensive care support and tetanus immunoglobulin are often unavailable.7,8

An effective tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine (TTCV) has been available since the early 20th century and has been part of Uganda’s national immunization program since 1983.9 The WHO recommends a primary series in infancy and three booster doses in later childhood and adolescence.10 Uganda adopted the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation for early childhood TTCV vaccination at age 6 weeks, 10 weeks, and 14 weeks of age. That schedule is believed to be 100% effective in preventing tetanus deaths in children up to about 4 years of age.10 To ensure continued tetanus immunity, WHO recommends 3 booster doses of TTCV at ages; 12–23 months, another at 4–7 years, and the last at 9–15 years.10 However, many countries, including Uganda, have not fully implemented booster doses, leaving a proportion of older children and adolescents susceptible to tetanus. Immunization coverage for the third TTCV for children in Uganda is about 80%3,11 but more than 90% for the first two doses.12 That level of coverage therefore leaves a significant number of children (20%) unprotected.

This case report highlights two unique points; Firstly, the successful management of severe tetanus, Ablett Classification grade 3, in a 9-year-old child in a setting without intensive care services. Secondly, the use of human tetanus immunoglobulin, even after 10 days may have contributed to survival, even when literature shows less benefit if given more than 24–48 hours after exposure.

Case Presentation

Presentation

A 9-year-old African boy was admitted to the Paediatric ward of Soroti Regional Referral Hospital on 17 December 2025. He presented with chest and neck pain for three days, followed by progressive painful spasms involving the whole body and inability to drink or eat for one day. One week prior to admission, he sustained a small wound on the right great toe after accidentally kicking a rock while playing football barefoot. The wound was cleaned at home and no medical care was sought as he only had mild pain then. He did not receive tetanus prophylaxis or antibiotics at the time. He was otherwise well prior to this freak toe accident.

Past Medical and Immunization History

The child had received Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG) and oral polio vaccine at birth. However, he missed all the 6, 10 and 14 weeks mandatory vaccine as recommended by the Uganda National Expanded Program on Immunization (UNEPI) due to severe maternal illness and family-social disruption at that time. These include polio vaccines, pentavalent vaccine against Diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, hepatitis B, and Hemophilus influenza type B (DPT-Hep B-Hib), Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine and Rota virus vaccine given at 6,10 and 14 weeks. He later received Measles-Rubella vaccine at 9 and 18 months but did not get any of the missed vaccines. His growth and development were reportedly normal, with no history of major illness and he had a satisfactory school performance.

Examination

On admission, the child weighed 23 kg. He had a normal axillary temperature of 36.9°C, pulse rate of 90 beats/min, but he was tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 42/min, and had a normal oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. He had mild pallor but not jaundice or lymphadenopathy. A 1 cm wound was present on the dorsal aspect of the right great toe, with soil and debris. It was not discharging any pus or serous fluid. The wound was not septic and the surrounding skin was normal.

He was fully conscious but anxious, sweating profusely, and had difficulty speaking. He had generalized hypertonia with severe spasms triggered by light touch or movement. Other systemic exams were unremarkable at admission. On the day following admission, he developed low-grade fever (37.6°C) and urinary retention with a distended bladder.

Diagnosis

A clinical diagnosis of generalized tetanus (Ablett classification- Grade 3) was made based on the classic features of severe trismus, generalized spasms triggered by touch, hypertonia, respiratory distress and a history of a contaminated penetrating wound. Other differentials considered included acute bacterial meningitis and hypocalcemic tetany; however, the presence of classic stimulus-induced spasms, trismus, and a contaminated wound supported the diagnosis of generalized tetanus. We however did not do any other calcium levels to rule out tetany due to resource constraints.

Nursing and Supportive Care

The child was managed in a quiet, darkened isolation room within the main paediatric ward due to unavailability of an intensive care unit bed. Nursing care focused on minimal stimulation and close monitoring. A nasogastric tube was inserted for oral feeding. Intravenous normal saline and 10% dextrose were administered to supplement enteral intake. Urinary retention was managed by urethral catheterization. Wound toilet and debridement were performed under sedation.

Spasm Control

Sedation was initiated using intravenous diazepam 5 mg alternating every three hours with intravenous chlorpromazine 50 mg. Doses were gradually escalated based on spasm severity, reaching diazepam 10 mg and chlorpromazine 125 mg per dose over the course of admission. The high chlorpromazine dose was due to failure to achieve spasm control with the lower recommended doses of 25 mg−50 mg per dose. He had no adverse effects of the medications. Despite newer guidelines suggesting use of magnesium sulphate, we used chlorpromazine because of its previous successful use among older children and also from a previous case study of an 11 year old child in Kampala, Uganda.13

Antibiotics

Antibiotic therapy included intravenous ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg once daily and intravenous metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg every 8 hours for 21 days.

Tetanus Specific Treatment

Due to limited availability, human tetanus immunoglobulin was obtained 10 days after hospital admission. A total of 2500 IU was administered, with 2250 IU given intramuscularly and 1250 IU infiltrated locally around the affected limb. Such high doses are recommended for active treatment of tetanus.14,15 At discharge, 0.5 mL intramuscular tetanus toxoid (TTCV) was administered in the deltoid region. During admission, he developed cellulitis at a previous intravenous cannula site above the left ankle, which was treated pharmacologically. The cellulitis did not warrant change of antibiotics. On day 21 of treatment, tetanus toxoid was administered intramuscularly to initiate active immunization. The child continued to improve, resumed oral intake, and was later discharged on day 25 in stable condition with follow-up scheduled for completion of tetanus toxoid doses.

Discussion

This case highlights several important aspects of post-neonatal tetanus in low-resource settings. First, it demonstrates the consequences of missed routine immunization in infancy. Although TTCV is available in Uganda, barriers such as maternal illness, social disruption, and health system gaps can prevent timely vaccination despite the TTCV rates have been reported to be more than 90% in Uganda.12,16 This case supports the need for community follow-up mechanisms and catch-up immunization for children who miss early vaccines.17

Even with mortality rates of tetanus as about 51% in Ugandan literature, severe forms of tetanus can be managed successfully without intensive care when key supportive measures are provided.13 These include environmental control (quiet and dark room), sedation to reduce spasms, nasogastric feeding to maintain nutrition, bladder catheterization for urinary retention, and wound care. Autonomic dysfunction is common in severe tetanus and may manifest as sweating, urinary retention, and cardiovascular instability.3,13,18 Although this child did not have documented cardiovascular instability, he demonstrated features suggestive of dysautonomia. This has been demonstrated as well in a case series in India where 42 patients were treated without ICU with about 57.1% recovery albeit 14.3% sequalae.18

Third, this case, also postulates on the possible value of human tetanus immunoglobulin, even if given several days after exposure. In this patient, symptoms improved after giving human tetanus immunoglobulin. Human tetanus immunoglobulin neutralizes unbound circulating tetanus toxin, preventing further neuronal binding.1,10 Although it does not reverse toxin already bound to the nervous system, it may still offer benefit even when administered days after exposure, as seen in this case. Whereas this improvement cannot be solely attributable to the immunoglobulin, there could be value in its administration even though delayed. Currently, there is barely any literature that supports benefit of late administration of human tetanus immunoglobulin. More research is required to back our claim. However, there is need to improve access to human tetanus immunoglobulin in district and regional referral hospitals.

Finally, the case demonstrates the importance of initiating active immunization with tetanus toxoid after recovery because tetanus infection does not confer long-term immunity.

Patient Perspective

The parents expressed gratitude following their child’s recovery. They reported that the spasms had been frightening and distressing for the family. After discharge, they were pleased with his return to normal feeding and daily activities and expressed willingness to continue coming for review.

Limitations

The unavailability of paediatric intensive care and mechanical ventilation limited advanced supportive options.

Conclusion

Post-neonatal tetanus remains a life-threatening but preventable disease. This case demonstrates that survival is possible in low-resource settings even without intensive care services, provided that high-quality supportive care is delivered. Human tetanus immunoglobulin and tetanus toxoid should be administered whenever possible, even if delayed, as they may significantly improve clinical outcomes. Whereas this improvement cannot be solely attributable to the immunoglobulin, there could be value in its administration even though delayed. More research is required to back this claim.
