Executive Summary
Spark-ignited heavy-duty (HD) natural gas engines typically experience efficiency losses at part-load
operation due to throttling requirements needed to maintain stoichiometric combustion. A substantial
portion of medium- and heavy-duty engine operation occurs in this low-load region, resulting in increased
brake-specific carbon dioxide emissions and reduced overall efficiency. This project designed, developed
and demonstrated cylinder deactivation (CDA) technology on an HD natural gas engine to mitigate these
losses. CDA enables selective deactivation of cylinders, reducing pumping losses while maintaining required
torque output and meeting vehicle noise, vibration, and harshness (NVH) targets. The project objectives
were successfully achieved, demonstrating up to 12% improvement in fuel consumption over the next
generation HD NG engine on key low loaded HD cycles and maintained compliance with 0.02 g/hp-hr NOₓ
emissions with comparable or better vibrations to current product. These results confirm that CDA
technology can substantially improve the fuel efficiency and environmental performance of heavy-duty
natural gas engines, reinforcing their potential as a low-carbon bridge solution in the transition toward
zero-emission transportation technologies.

Relevance and Background
The costs of diesel engines and aftertreatment systems have increased significantly over the past two
decades as both on-engine and aftertreatment emission controls have been implemented to meet
regulations. The costs for these systems are expected to increase further to support the capability needed
to meet ultra-low NOX emissions. In comparison, spark ignited natural gas engines meet emissions with a
simpler architecture consisting of stoichiometric combustion and a three-way catalyst (TWC) system.
Natural gas engines have another benefit of lower greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions due to favorable
hydrogen to carbon ratio of fuel composition as compared to other liquid petroleum fuels. This translates
directly into lower CO2 emissions with the potential for zero or even negative GHG emissions with the use
of renewable natural gas (RNG).
Current natural gas engines in the market are not as efficient as diesel engines used in commercial vehicles.
The impact of lower efficiency is somewhat offset by relative advantage in fuel cost per unit of natural gas
vs diesel. One additional customer metric of natural gas engine viability is payback period. It is a measure of
time in months, to recover increase in capital expense associated with new technology expense through
reduction in operating cost or increased revenue enabled by the technology. Currently, only very high
mileage customers are finding acceptable payback periods for natural gas over diesel technology.
Therefore, it is essential to have increased efficiency on current natural gas technology to reduce the
payback period for broader adoption of the technology.

Current State of the Art
Spark ignited engines inherently suffer efficiency losses at part load due to throttling to maintain
stoichiometric operation. A significant fraction of the drive cycle and real-world usage of medium/heavy
duty engines are in the low load region where the engine is relatively inefficient. The current state-of-the-
art HD NG engines have a sharp rise in Brake Specific CO2 (BSCO2) emissions as the average power of the
duty cycle decreases. CDA solutions can improve low load efficiencies by deactivating a fixed number of
cylinders by reducing the pumping losses.

Addressing Barriers to Market Adoption
This project aims to reduce barriers to adoption of HD NG engines by developing and demonstrating
significant improvements in the low load efficiencies of natural gas engines while maintaining the low
emissions advantages. This is accomplished by designing and developing the CDA technology on a HD NG
engine.
CDA enables the engine to deactivate the cylinders to minimize the amount of pumping losses while
producing the required torque without compromising on NVH targets. Deactivation occurs both on the
intake and exhaust valves of each cylinder, thus preventing any flow of air in the deactivated cylinders for
minimizing the pumping losses. All cylinders would be equipped and capable of being individually
deactivated or reactivated from one combustion cycle to the next. The innovativeness of this approach is
the reduction of pumping losses over a wide range of speeds and loads with the ability to smoothly
transition between various states of operation. While CDA technology has been proven and implemented in
production in light-duty pick-up trucks with gasoline engines since 2018, the proposed work will widen the
applicability to a HD NG SI engine with cooled EGR and will provide some unique opportunities for a novel
solution that optimizes pumping losses and combustion efficiency. CDA hardware for HD applications are
not as mature as they are for LD applications and hence, the proposed project offers an opportunity to
advance the development of CDA mechanisms for HD engines, both spark-ignited as well as diesel engines.
The proposed project also aims to develop a unique overhead layout and component design solution that
can include Dual Overhead Cams (DOHC), Variable Valve Timing (VVT), Hydraulic Lash Adjusters (HLA), 2-
stroke Compression Release Engine Brakes (CREB), and CDA. This project would also be dealing with some
unique challenges with EGR flow control as well as estimation due to the pressure pulsations from cylinder
deactivation as well as its impact on air-fuel ratio control in addition to the challenges that exist on engines
without cooled EGR.

Major goals and objectives of the project
The overarching goal of this project is to bridge the efficiency gap between low-load and moderate-to-
heavy-load drive cycles while maintaining the capability to meet ultra-low NOₓ emissions in a HD NG engine
building on the strong foundation of Cummins’ next-generation HD NG engine. Below are the specific
objectives :
 • CDA component design, development and validation on a HD NG engine. Technology Readiness
 Level (TRL) 5 delivery.
 • Control system development for CDA.
 • Demonstrate up to 12% improvement in fuel consumption over the Next Gen HD NG engine on key
 low loaded HD cycles :
 o Low load cycle (LLC) 10-12%
 o Urban Dynamometer Driving Schedule (UDDS) 6-8%
 o Heavy Heavy-Duty Diesel Truck driving cycle (HHDDT) 3-5%
 • Maintain near zero (0.02 g/hp-hr NOx) emissions and meet all other criteria pollutant limits for a
 2027 Environmental Protection Agency/ California Air Resources Board (EPA/CARB) certification
 with no additional cost for of the emission control system.
 • Acceptable NVH is comparable or better than current product.
 • Build an engine and conduct dynamometer testing to demonstrate performance against program
 goals.

 carried over from Cummins’ next-generation HD NG engine and include the pent-roof cylinder head with
 tumble combustion, high-compression ratio pistons, port fuel injection, optimized camshafts and intake

 Components highlighted in yellow represent new developments within the scope of this project. These

 The engine has a type III valvetrain system with dual overhead camshafts (DOHC) and independent cam
 phasing. The complete valvetrain system is shown in Figure 6 below.

 Figure 6. Valvetrain System

 The cylinder head and valvetrain layout have been designed to include CDA and engine braking
 capabilities. For the assembly, cam caps are doweled and bolted to the cylinder head. Cam bearing
 journals are machined and then cam journal bearings are installed. This is shown in Figure 7.

 Figure 7. Cylinder Head Assembly

 This valvetrain architecture utilizes collapsing rocker technology to achieve CDA and 1.5SB operation
 modes. The lost motion of the collapsing rocker is achieved through the “Wedgelock” capsule. This
 Wedgelock is a hydraulically and mechanically controlled system that was chosen as prime path for
 packaging capabilities and for its high TRL. 1.5SB is an engine brake technology which retains the normal
 intake valve lifts but deactivates the main exhaust valve lifts and adds multiple valve lift events on the
 exhaust side to increase braking power over traditional compression release brakes. Analysis results
 from a benchmarked product predict that 1.5SB technology can enable diesel-like power targets.

 Figure 8. Valvetrain architecture to achieve CDA and Braking

 Intake and exhaust rockers are sub-assembled to the rocker shafts and then the rocker shafts are bolted
 to the cam caps. Bolts were sized to pass bolted joint analysis using estimated brake loads. Bolting the
 rocker shafts directly to the cam caps have helped in keeping the overall height of the engine to a

 minimum. The spring bars and spring packs are then installed to apply a relatively constant force in
 order to maintain contact between the cam roller and cam lobe. Finally, the oil control valve(OCV)
 housing manifolds are installed to aid in the lube system communication from the head supply, through
 the cam towers, into the bi-state solenoid valves, which determines where the main pressure feeds into
 the rocker shafts and then ultimately up to the rockers. Pressurized oil is then used for lubrication and is
 also utilized to activate CDA or engine braking. To enable CDA and brake modes of operation, the design
 layout utilizes 9 bi-state OCVs – 1 OCV per cylinder to activate CDA and 1 OCV per cylinder pair to
 activate engine brake. There are 3 identical manifolds per engine that each house 3 OCVs as shown in
 Figure 9 below.

 Figure 9. Oil Control Valve Housing Manifold

CDA hardware design
  The essential part of the system’s CDA operation is the Wedgelock capsule. The rocker system has been
  designed to utilize an existing capsule assembly. This capsule contains the lost motion mechanism as well
  as a bias spring. The bias spring forces keep the cam follower roller in contact with the cam lobe during
  lost motion in CDA mode. The Wedgelock capsule on the main rocker arms is “normally engaged” – the
  input rocker arm is coupled to the output rocker arm by default. When oil pressure is applied, the
  activation piston moves to allow the wedges to retract into the inner cylinder. This interrupts the load
  path between the rocker arm and valves, thereby allowing lost motion and deactivating the valves. The
  Wedgelock is shown in Figure 10 below.

 Figure 10. Cross-section of Wedgelock Capsule

  The intake rocker assembly as shown in Figure 11 is a 2-piece rocker arm which contains a normally
  engaged Wedgelock capsule. A single input rocker transfers its cam motion to a single output rocker that
  is connected to both valves.

 Figure 11. Intake Rocker Arm Assembly

 The exhaust rocker assembly shown in Figure 12 contains 4 rocker arms – 2 input and 2 output rocker
 arms. The brake output rocker translates motion from the engine braking input rocker to a single valve.

 The main output rocker arm translates motion from the main lift input arm to both valves. This is
 accomplished by using a “transfer arm” – when the main output rocker is actuated, it actuates the brake
 output rocker.

 Figure 12. Exhaust Rocker Arm Assembly

  Figure 13 shows the operating modes of the exhaust rocker assembly during each mode of operation.
  Both input rocker arms are kept in constant contact with the cam lobe and therefore are either
  articulating in normal operation or in lost motion. When the Wedgelock capsule is unlocked, it
  experiences lost motion and therefore will not transfer any motion to the corresponding output rocker.

 Figure 13. Exhaust Rocker Modes of Operation

  Finite element analysis (FEA) was completed on the rockers; Figure 14 shows fatigue margins for most of
  the geometries are within acceptable limits. Some very small areas on the rockers remain to be refined,
  but these do not impact the integration of the CDA hardware.

 Figure 14. FEA model set-up and fatigue margins for castings

 Fatigue testing of the key load-bearing sub-components of the wedgelock was completed. Sufficient
 samples were tested to develop an S-N curve and determine loading limit for infinite life. These results
 are used to establish a design limit. The wedge lock design has also been tested for thousands of hours
 and hundreds of thousands of miles to demonstrate reliability and durability.
 A simple force/area calculation of contact pressure in the rocker bore shows that the contact pressure is
 within Cummins limits. However, the valves put an offset load into the exhaust rockers, so a deeper
 study into local contact pressures has been completed. As shown in figure 15, the contact pressures are
 slightly above benchmarked designs however these design changes do not impact the integration of
 CDA hardware.

 Figure 15. Contact pressure analysis of the exhaust main rocker.

 Both FEA and hand calculations were completed to analyze contact stresses at the Wedgelock’s
 spherical joints. At the Wedgelock’s maximum load capacity, the contact stresses are acceptable.
 Furthermore, our system’s design keeps Wedgelock loads at <75% of their max load capacity, so the
 contact stresses on this engine are acceptable.

 The adjusting screw used on this engine program has been used on other engine programs at Cummins.
 Analytical tools examine contact stress at various tolerance conditions. The analysis predicts there are
 no tolerance conditions under which the contact stress or contact angles will exceed limits. This is
 shown in figure 16 below.

 Figure 16. Contact stress analysis of exhaust-side adjusting screw ball and socket joint.

 To reduce the impact of tolerance stack-up on contact stress, the rocker shafts are doweled to the
 cylinder head’s cam caps. Those doweling features are machined once the cam caps are assembled,
 providing the best location control possible. Machining set-up, tolerances and datums for rockers, cams,
 and cam caps are similar to existing products; therefore, cam lobe to follower roller misalignment is
 expected to be within Cummins experience. This arrangement is shown in figure 17.

CDA Simulation with Base Engine Maps
 Initially a GT 1D engine cycle simulation model calibrated with baseline HD NG engine data was utilized
 for predicting the fuel benefit improvements with CDA. Design of experiments were run in GT to develop
 cylinder firing fractions maps for optimum fuel benefits. Figure 18 shows these firing fractions(FF) and
 corresponding fuel benefits maps. Results constraint with standard flywheel NVH limits scaled for Low
 Pressure Exhaust Spring(LPES) air trapping and Compression Ratio(CR) show CDA operation to about 5
 bar brake mean effect pressure (BMEP) with up to 40% fuel benefit at low loads idle. For comparison
 purpose, operation was up to 7 bar BMEP with unconstrained NVH limits.

 Figure 18. Firing fraction and Fuel Benefit maps

 Simulations were also run to predict the avg CO2 benefits on a range of chassis and dyno duty cycles.
 The estimates incorporate fixed cylinder CDA instead of the rolling patterns dynamic skip fire (DSF) and
 with the prime path Idle ½ FF, 3 & 4 cyl firing elsewhere for good balance between efficiency vs NVH. It
 also considers the negative impact of rebreathing to mitigate oil consumption resulting due to deep
 cylinder vacuum for the LPES air trapping strategy. Estimates predict over 10% BSCO2 improvement over
 the low load cycle(LLC), meeting targets on most of the cycles except high load concentrated cycles.
 Impact to benefits due to rebreathing is predicted close to a 3% BSCO2 increase and negligible with CDA
 compared to DSF. Results are shown in figure 19. The project started with dynamic skip fire (DSF)
 controls strategy for CDA then restricted to fixed CDA as the partners providing the DSF controls
 technology left the project , however firing fraction transitions are managed by other control methods
 within the Cummins CDA software.

 Figure 19. Cycle avg BSCO2 improvements with CDA (%)

Rig Testing
  After the engine assembly was completed, before the performance test cell, it was first installed in a
  non-firing mechanical efficiency lab for rig testing to verify engine hardware and controls by completing
  a first spin of the engine, determine timing windows required for successful valve deactivation, test
  simultaneous operation of VVT and CDA, complete the CDA system duty cycle robustness test and
  measuring the full long block friction mean effective pressure (FMEP). For a successful LPES CDA, it is
  critical to get the CDA timing commands right, every time. Failure to do so, could potentially harm the
  engine and/or negatively affect efficiency gains. Timing commands are a function of following factors :
  engine speeds, lube oil pressure and temperature, phaser positions, hardware and electrical delays. Data
  was collected to establish a command angle function and use it in controls as shown in figure 22 below.
  These controls system algorithms was then utilized to satisfactorily demonstrate robustness of the CDA
  hardware and software functionality on both steady state and transient engine speeds. The steady state
  tests included a 3 cycle LPES CDA – 3 cycle normal operation repeated 300 times. The transient duty
  cycle mimicked the LLC with varying engine speeds and firing fractions. The results in figures 23 and 24
  shows a successful demonstration of the functionalities with 99% reliability with 95% confidence internal
  (CI) over the steady state test and 100% reliability for the transient tests.

Simulation based Powertrain NVH assessment
 Simulation methodology
 NVH assessment due to CDA was conducted using a combination of simulation and limited testing.
 Transfer path analysis (TPA) was utilized to predict cab vibration, in lieu of vehicle testing. Figure 28
 shows the methodology. It leverages existing truck response model characterization to proceed with
 vibration data collection and prediction vibration response with simulation. The approach is primarily
 divided into three categories:
 1. Truck response model characterization (step 1 &2).
 2. Vibration data collection with CDA along with rigid body properties determination (step 3 &4).
 3. Virtual integration of powertrain using vehicle models for vibration response characterization (step
 5 & 6).

 Figure 28. Simulation based approach for vibration assessment

 Vehicle Mounting System Design
  Figure 29 below show the current (baseline) mounting arrangement on the truck which utilizes a 3-point
mounting system.

 Figure 29. (a)Mounting arrangement on chassis (b) Close up view of front mounts

 Using Cummins in-house 6DOF mounting analysis tool, the natural frequencies and corresponding
 modal kinetic energy fraction (KEF) were evaluated. For a standard 6-cylinder engine, the dominant
 engine roll order of excitation is 3rd, whereas for 3-cylinder deactivation, the dominant engine order is
 1½. The vibration transmissibility calculations in figure 30 showed that this mounting system is not
 suitable for 3-cylinder deactivation and 1.5 order resonance was seen in operating speed range at 876
 RPM. It is to be noted that the guideline for acceptable vibration transmissibility is to be < 1 with the
 entitlement to be <0.5 at low idle speed and above.

 Figure 30. (a)Natural frequencies and energy content (b) Vibration transmissibility

 It was also seen that the modal decoupling was not satisfactory as the elastic axis of the mounting system
 is not at all aligned with the torque roll axis of the powertrain as shown in figure 31.

 Figure 31. Elastic Axis and Torque Roll Axis

 schematic doesn’t show the transmission. From the vibration data collected during engine shutdown,
 the roll natural frequency was seen to be at 22.3 Hz shown in figure 33, which matches quite well with
 the predicted value of 21.9 Hz from the 6DOF model.

Conclusions
Cylinder deactivation technology has been designed, developed, integrated and validated for robustness
into a heavy-duty natural gas engine, delivering significant efficiency improvements across real-world
chassis and dyno duty cycles with low and high average loads, while maintaining ultra-low NOx emissions
and meeting all other EPA criteria pollutant limits. This was achieved through the design and analysis of
advanced valvetrain components capable of robust cylinder deactivation, as well as modifications to
baseline control systems and calibrations to optimize cylinder firing fractions. The CDA architecture utilizes
a low-pressure exhaust spring air-trapping strategy that maximizes pumping-loss reductions.
Powertrain mounting arrangements and isolators were designed and optimized for the targeted firing
fractions to minimize vibrations, which was demonstrated successfully during engine testing in the lab. In
addition, a new simulation-based methodology was developed for vehicle-level NVH assessment as an
alternative to resource-intensive physical vehicle testing. Using engine input data and existing vehicle
response models, driver-seat vibration levels were predicted through transfer-path analysis, with results
showing vibration performance that is better than or comparable to current industry standards.
The program successfully demonstrated up to a 12% reduction (improvement) in fuel consumption across
mixed low- and high-load duty cycles, achieved bsNOx levels of 0.02 g/hp-hr, and maintained vehicle
vibration performance on par with or superior to acceptable current-production benchmarks.
