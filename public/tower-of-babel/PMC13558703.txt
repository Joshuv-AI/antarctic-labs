FL-TWIN: a unified federated learning system for intrusion detection with digital twins modelling
Authors: Anji Ayman Hamwi, Meenakshi Mittal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growth of networked environments has intensified the challenge of detecting distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, as centralized intrusion detection systems face scalability, privacy, and data heterogeneity limitations. This paper proposes a federated learning framework for DDoS detection, Unified FL-TWIN, that pairs each participating client with an edge-resident Digital Twin (DT), applies a four-stage poisoning defence pipeline, and records all aggregations and security events on a permissioned blockchain ledger. Also, each DT maintains a versioned ring buffer of model snapshots, enabling per-client targeted rollback upon adversary detection. The defence pipeline comprises: Layered Update Purification (LUP), Differential Privacy via DP-SGD, Dual Dynamic Aggregation, TracIn and a blockchain. The novelty of this work lies in systematically integrating them into a Unified FL-TWIN approach that addresses several challenges simultaneously. This proposed approach simultaneously provides privacy protection, robustness, trustworthiness, accountability, and adaptive learning within a single architecture. In experiments on the CIC-DDoS 2019 dataset, we are covering clean baselines and three attack types with 30% malicious participation. The FL-TWIN achieves peak test accuracies of 99.97%, 99.98%, and 99.98% under label-flip, gradient-noise, and backdoor attacks, respectively, compared to a stagnant 99.72% for the undefended baseline. LUP achieves F1 scores of 0.57, 0.75, and 0.80 across three attack types, while the blockchain ledger maintains full save across all experiments. These results show that combining Digital Twin rollback with a layered detection pipeline improves recovery from federated poisoning attacks.Keywords: Digital twin, Federated learning, Intrusion detection, Class imbalance, Privacy-preserving machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, the growth of the Internet of Things (IoT), Industrial IoT (IIoT), and cyber-physical systems has transformed many critical infrastructure sectors, including smart manufacturing, healthcare, transportation, and energy networks1–3. These systems connect a large number of devices and rely on continuous data exchange and automated decision-making. This improves efficiency, but it also creates new security risks.

In practice, distributed systems are now more exposed to attacks: model manipulation, data poisoning, and other adversarial behaviors that target synergic learning systems4–6.

Traditional cloud-based intrusion detection systems (IDS) rely on centralized data collection and processing, and are also not well-suited for large distributed networks because they can cause delays, increase bandwidth use, and create single points of failure1. Also, sending raw data from devices to central servers raises privacy concerns and complicates regulatory compliance, especially in healthcare and industrial systems3,6. Therefore, there is a need for a mechanism that can preserve the privacy of raw data from various devices, with low delay and minimal bandwidth usage. Thus, we have used Federated learning (FL) in this study.

Federated Learning (FL) is a useful approach to addressing the aforementioned challenges. It allows distributed devices to train machine learning (ML) models together without sharing raw data, but by sharing the model updates or parameters2,3,7. In this setup, each client trains its model locally and sends only model updates to a central server. This helps reduce privacy risks while sharing model updates only. At the same time, FL remains vulnerable to several attacks such as Malicious clients can send poisoned updates, manipulate gradients, or perform label-flipping attacks, which can strongly affect the global model4,8,9.

Detecting the malicious client alone is not enough; once the global model has been trained on corrupted updates over several rounds, simply removing detected adversaries does not restore model quality, because the consequences of poisoned parameters persist9,10. In other words, a complete defense requires not just that there be an efficient way to detect the malicious clients but also an effective means of recovering from attacks on the model. The digital twins mechanism is one possible method for recovering the model. The digital twin mechanism allows safe testing, anomaly detection, and version tracking of the current state of the system as well as the past states1,2,5 . As such, it can create virtual models of all edge device models with versioned model snapshots in order to monitor client behavior, allow for targeted recovery after a successful attack, and provide a record of training history.

Despite the benefits of both technologies, most existing research has treated DTs and FL as separate solutions. Many FL-based intrusion detection frameworks assume that clients behave honestly and therefore do not include mechanisms to detect malicious updates in FL systems, and some who have used DT haven’t used FL systems for privacy preserving of the raw data11.

Similarly, Digital Twin-based security systems have focused primarily on simulation and monitoring rather than on actively securing the FL process itself5. Integrating Digital Twins with a full-stack secure FL pipeline—including multi-layer detection, differential privacy, robust aggregation, and post-detection model recovery—therefore remains an open research challenge.

Problem statement

Federated learning (FL) enables distributed learning and privacy preservation by allowing learning across different devices while keeping raw data on local devices. While ensuring privacy, the system remains vulnerable to malicious clients that can introduce label flipping, gradient manipulation, and backdoor attacks in client updates during training, particularly in non-IID and heterogeneous environments, thereby degrading the global model. The existing FL approaches lack inclusive mechanisms for privacy protection, secure update validation, attack detection, robust aggregation, adaptive training with early stopping (ATES), and model recovery. While techniques like digital twins (DT), Local Update Profiling (LUP), Differentially-Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD), dual dynamic aggregation (DDA) approach, adaptive training with early stopping, TracIn, and blockchain can provide privacy protection, security, robustness, trustworthiness, accountability, and adaptive learning. But the existing FL approaches provide all these techniques, benefits in isolation. Additionally, the challenges of non-IID data distributions and data imbalance require effective strategies to maintain the model performance and fairness. Therefore, there is a need for a unified federated learning approach that integrates DT, LUP, DP-SGD, DDA, ATS, TracIn, and blockchain to ensure privacy preservation, attack detection, robust aggregation, traceability, and resilient model recovery in non-IID data distributions.

Related work

The integration of DTs and FL has attracted growing interest because it can improve security, scalability, and privacy in distributed networks.

In federated intrusion detection, Shalaka S. Mahadik et al.11 proposed a lightweight 1D-CNN-based IDS for edge environments. In this study, the model was tested on the CICDDoS2019 dataset and achieved 99.98% accuracy on IID data and 99.07% on non-IID data, while the centralized baseline reached 99.97%. The proposed approach performed well, assuming that all clients are trustworthy, so malicious or poisoned updates are not detected or removed in this study.

The authors have not considered the class imbalance problem in this study, which can reduce detection performance for smaller attack classes. Mohamad Arafeh et al.12 have suggested using a genetic warm-up based approach of selecting client to be able to improve the convergence of non IID Federated Learning and therefore to decrease the divergence of weights. The authors tested their method on two datasets (CIFAR-10, and MNIST), which resulted in an accuracy of approximately 84 %, after 500 iterations, as opposed to 83 %, obtained with a traditional warm up method, and 76 %, obtained with the basic FL baselines. Although this approach has shown to achieve faster convergence when data are heterogeneous, it does not include any mechanism to identify either malicious clients or poisoning attacks.

Mikail Mohammed Salim et al.1 introduced a DT-based cyber-threat detection system that uses FL together with an Adaptive Thresholding with Early Stopping (ATES)mechanism. The model was evaluated on the CICDDoS2019 dataset, achieving the F1-score of 0.98 after round 48, and across different attacks, an average F1-score of 0.82. The early stopping mechanism of ATES used in this study helped reduce computational cost by stopping local training when performance reached the desired thresholds. However, the framework assumed that edge devices and communication channels are trusted, leaving the global model vulnerable to adversarial participants.

Zhuoqun Zhang et al.2 introduced an Adaptive Asynchronous FL framework designed for DT-based smart grids. Authors tested the proposed approach on MNIST, FMNIST, EMNIST, and UK-SMEC smart meter datasets, achieving 98.5% accuracy on MNIST after 250 rounds. The approach uses a dual dynamic aggregation strategy that integrates Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Newton’s Cooling-based temporal weighting method.Although the system had a relatively stable performance as long as half of the nodes acted maliciously, identifying malicious client behavior versus delayed node behavior will continue to be difficult with increasingly large amounts of non-IID data.

Rezaei et al.13 proposed Adapt-LFA (Adaptive Gradient-Guided Label Flipping Attack), a sophisticated data poisoning attack against Federated Learning (FL)-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) in IoT environments. The attack selects training samples to flip labels strategically, considering the flip’s effect on validation error and the sample’s distance to the decision boundary, thereby formulating the attack as a bi-level optimization. Unlike conventional random label-flipping attacks, Adapt-LFA employs an iterative refinement mechanism that dynamically updates the set of flipped labels during training, enabling sustained degradation of the global model while remaining difficult to detect. The attack was evaluated in a ten-client FL environment with 40% malicious participants using the FedAvg aggregation algorithm with RNN and CNN models as local learners. Experiments evaluated on the CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and CICIoV2024 datasets over 20 communication rounds which demonstrated that Adapt-LFA significantly outperformed conventional LFA and reduced classification accuracy by 10% in RNNs and 13% in CNNs on CSE-CIC-IDS2018, and by 10% in RNNs and 14% in CNNs on CICIoV2024, while maintaining consistently higher loss values. The findings shows that standard aggregation methods, such as FedAvg, are not sufficient to counter such adaptive attacks which defines the critical need for more robust defense mechanisms in FL-based IDS deployments.

Eslamnejad et al.14 proposed a dual-contribution framework that addresses both attack and defense aspects of label-flipping threats in Federated Learning (FL)-based Android malware detection. On the attack side, the authors proposed two strategies first is Stealthiness-based Label-Flipping (SLF), which uses cross-entropy loss to identify and strategically flip samples while remaining difficult to detect and second, Weight-based Adaptive Label-Flipping (WALF), which targets high-confidence samples using model logits to maximize classification disruption. To defend against these attacks, the authors proposed two client-side density-based defense mechanisms: a Local Outlier Factor (LOF)-based defense, which detects adversarial samples by analyzing local density deviations among neighboring data points, and a DBSCAN clustering-based defense, which identifies manipulated samples as outliers or members of small abnormal clusters in the feature space. The framework was evaluated in an 8-client FL environment with up to 40% compromised clients, using API-level binary sparse matrix representations on three Android malware datasets: Drebin (118,505 samples), Contagio (28,760 samples), and Genome (1,200 samples). Experimental results showed that both attacks increased the number of malicious samples misclassified as benign while causing only minor changes in the False Positive Rate (FPR), with WALF producing greater performance degradation than SLF. Among the proposed defenses, the LOF-based approach consistently outperformed DBSCAN and state-of-the-art FedDefender variants, achieving up to 98.11% accuracy with zero false positives under the WALF attack on the Genome dataset.

To handle malicious updates there are several robust aggregation methods that have been studied. Using local updates purify (LUP) to identify malicious updates for use in DT-BFL was the method presented in the research conducted by Wael Issa et al.4. LUP uses PCA, median absolute deviation (MAD) and hierarchical clustering to determine whether an update should be accepted into a global model. The results using MNIST, CIFAR-10 and ToN-IoT datasets resulted in a MNIST accuracy of 97.97 %, even though half of the clients were acting maliciously. However, hierarchical clustering adds additional computational expense.

Protecting users’ private information while engaging in distributed learning was addressed in the research conducted by Martin Abadi et al.15, who developed the Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) algorithm. DP-SGD provides -differential privacy through adding gaussian noise to gradients; however, such addition can decrease model performance, particularly when the data is heterogeneous. Therefore, this study did not consider this issue.

To counter Sybil and model poisoning attacks, Sahaya Beni Prathipa et al.6 have proposed a Secure Federated Learning (SFL) framework that uses blockchain-based Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs) for client authentication. On the MNIST dataset, the SFL framework achieved an accuracy of 97 %. Nonetheless, since this approach depends upon static DT benchmarks they could result in false positives if the clients’ data varies significantly or are non-IID. Additionally, another concern is class imbalances.

The study by Haibo He and E. A. Garcia16 found that standard loss functions usually favour the majority classes, making it harder to detect minority attack classes. The authors pointed out the value of cost-sensitive learning, but the problem of detecting minority classes remains unresolved. Secure authentication of participants is another important consideration. According to Hoang-Anh Pham et al.17, blockchain based authentication techniques increase the transparency, traceability and resilience of systems, thereby preventing any tampering with updates. Building upon their work, a blockchain-IPFS framework utilizing Attribute-Based Encryption (ABE) was presented by Yueyue Dai et al.18, for secure access control in Digital Twin (DT) environments.

For improved traceability, Nicolò Romandini et al.3 have introduced a TrustFlow model, that is a federated learning (FL) model that combines DIDs, VCs, and blockchain-based identity management. The study utilized the TracIn scores to assess contributions made by clients; however identified suspicious updates. There is still an open question regarding malicious clients who submit only partially accurate updates; those could be “normal” appearing and thus potentially difficult to detect.

Although many approaches have been proposed, some challenges remain unresolved. These include detecting malicious clients in highly heterogeneous data, building scalable defense methods, and recovering the global model after repeated poisoning attacks. Research methodology

In this work, we propose a secure and Unified federated learning (FL) system to detect Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks using the CICDDoS2019 dataset19. In this study, binary detection has been used to distinguish between benign and DDoS traffic. The 82 features are used from the dataset, and records are distributed across 10 clients, using a non-IID Dirichlet distribution with parameter α = 0.1. This reflects a realistic, heterogeneous setting, as shown by the distribution statistics in Table 2 and Fig. 1. In the class imbalance skew, the proportion of benign traffic varies from 0.00% (Client 4) to 7.71% (Client 7) across clients, confirming the non-IID nature of the setup.

In the dataset, the 82 features were standardised across all samples to ensure consistency across heterogeneous data sources. During preprocessing, invalid entries frequently observed in network flow data—specifically, Infinity values and NaN values—were carefully handled. All samples containing Infinity entries were removed, and for NaN values, zero imputation was applied. Following data cleaning, z-score normalization was applied to all features. The preprocessed dataset was partitioned using stratified random sampling with an 80:20 train-test ratio.

Unified FL-TWIN architecture.

Proposed work: The mentioned digital twin, LUP, DP-SGD, robust aggregation, TracIn, and blockchain are established techniques; the novelty of this work lies in systematically integrating them into a unified federated learning approach that simultaneously addresses multiple challenges. The key advantage of combining these techniques is the development of a Unified approach that simultaneously provides privacy protection, robustness, trustworthiness, accountability, and adaptive learning within a single architecture.

A digital twin mechanism enables continuous monitoring of per-client behavior, saves model versions, and allows safe rollback if something goes wrong, while keeping data private. Local Update Processing (LUP) in the Unified system filters out three types of malicious updates (Label-Flip Poisoning, Gradient-Noise Injection, Backdoor Amplification) during training, while DP-SGD ensures privacy preservation. The Dual Dynamic Aggregation approach has been implemented at the server side for robust aggregation under non-IID settings. Adaptive Training Early Stopping (ATES) has been included to reduce the system’s computational complexity. TracIn provides contribution-aware assessment of client updates, and a blockchain mechanism provides transparency and tamper-resistant storage of training records and contributions. This state-aware representation enables continuous monitoring of client behavior, supports anomaly detection, verifies integrity, and allows rollback to trusted states when malicious updates are identified. In addition, patience-based criterion is employed, where training stops if the global test loss fails to improve for P consecutive rounds, preventing unnecessary computation in the case of convergence stagnation. This dual-condition strategy ensures that training halts either upon achieving satisfactory performance or when no further meaningful improvements are observed. By doing so, ATES reduces computational overhead, shortens training time, and improves system efficiency without sacrificing model performance, making it particularly suitable for large-scale and resource-constrained federated learning environments.

Algorithms overview

Each client trains a local intrusion detection model on its private data using weighted cross-entropy and Adam optimizer, then stores a Digital Twin snapshot before sending and to the server.

The server filters client updates using LUP through four checks: gradient norm (IQR), EMA-based loss deviation, cosine similarity, and norm-per-loss ratio, and assigns a suspicion score to detect malicious updates.

Accepted updates are clipped and perturbed with Gaussian noise using DP-SGD, then DDA aggregated using dynamic weights based on data size, MMD proximity, and temporal decay.

Each round is recorded in a blockchain, client influence is evaluated using TracIn, and training stops early using ATES based on test loss threshold or stagnation.

The Unified FL-TWIN loop executes client training, LUP filtering, DP-SGD, DDA aggregation, and blockchain auditing in each round, and stops when ATES conditions are met.

Algorithm 1 describes how each client trains locally and saves a Digital Twin snapshot. Algorithm 2 filters malicious updates through four detection stages and triggers a rollback on the first ban. Algorithm 3 clips and perturbs updates for privacy, then aggregates them with a three-factor dynamic weight. Algorithm 4 records each round on the blockchain, updates TracIn influence scores, and applies adaptive early stopping. Algorithm 5 is the orchestration loop that calls all four in sequence each round.

Discussion

A core design principle of Unified FL-TWIN is that no single defense mechanism is sufficient on its own. Prior work by Shejwalkar and Houmansadr20 showed that robust aggregation can be bypassed by adaptive attackers, while Fang et al.8 demonstrated that norm-based filtering is ineffective without differential privacy.

Our results confirm this observation: When using differential privacy (DP) alone, the system stagnates at 99.72% accuracy and lacks any capability to detect malicious updates. However, when all components—LUP, DP-SGD, and DDAS—are jointly applied, the system achieves improved performance, supporting the layered defense strategy proposed by Lyu et al.21. In particular, DDA mitigates the influence of outliers by assigning them lower weights rather than removing them, without requiring a labeled server dataset22.

Furthermore, without the rollback mechanism, the global model remains corrupted even after removing malicious clients, with accuracy stuck at 99.72%. This is because the model converges to a compromised state that cannot be corrected through standard updates, consistent with findings in9,10. The observed 70.55% drop in accuracy during rollback confirms that the mechanism effectively restores the model from such states.

The reported F1 scores (0.57, 0.75, and 0.80) highlight the inherent trade-off between precision and recall in adversarial federated learning. Similar challenges have been reported in Krum23 and coordinate-wise median methods24. Inspired by FLAME25, our fast detection path is triggered by strong anomalies (), enabling rapid response to highly suspicious updates.

However, detecting subtle attacks remains difficult in highly non-IID settings, where distinguishing malicious clients from naturally heterogeneous ones is inherently challenging.

From a privacy perspective, applying DP noise to parameter updates (deltas) rather than raw model weights follows the recommendation of Wei et al.26 and avoids instability in BatchNorm-based models27. Although the achieved privacy budget (–71) is higher than what is typically obtained with Rényi DP28 or the moments accountant15, the impact on accuracy at σ = 0.05 is negligible. This demonstrates that the proposed framework effectively reduces the accuracy loss commonly associated with DP-SGD, achieving a practical balance between privacy and utility in federated learning. Conclusion and future work

This paper presented a Unified FL-TWIN, a federated learning framework for network intrusion detection integrating Layered Update Purification, DP-SGD, Dual Dynamic Aggregation, and TracIn influence auditing with a Digital Twin architecture and a permissioned blockchain ledger. On CIC-DDoS2019 dataset, Unified FL-TWIN achieves peak test accuracies of , , and under label-flip, gradient-noise, and backdoor attacks—compared to 99.72% for the undefended baseline—and 99.96% in clean conditions. LUP detection F1 scores are 0.57, 0.75, and 0.80 respectively. The global model rollback is shown to be essential: without it, accuracy stagnates at 99.72% even after banning all adversaries.

Importantly, the proposed framework addresses key research gaps in federated learning security, including robust malicious client detection under non-IID data, secure aggregation without assuming trusted participants, mitigation of DP-induced accuracy degradation, and most critically, explicit recovery of the global model after multi-round poisoning through Digital Twin-assisted rollback.

Limitations and future work: A false-positive ban occurring in the clean-scenario full Unified FL-TWIN run identifies a limitation in the current EMA parameter calibration; this is acknowledged alongside three further limitations: the one-shot rollback policy, the TracIn subsample variance, and the privacy accounting. Future work will focus on adaptive rollback strategies, more precise privacy accounting methods, and improved anomaly calibration for non-IID settings to further enhance robustness and efficiency in large-scale federated environments.

The proposed LUP pipeline can introduce cumulative false positives under highly heterogeneous, non-IID distributions, but a sensitivity analysis of thresholds has not been considered. Therefore, we identified threshold sensitivity analysis as an important direction for future work.

In the TracIn implementation, many factors are left unconsidered, including checkpoint frequency, sample selection strategy, computational cost, and stability under non-IID conditions. This will be considered in future work.

Also, the study considered binary-class classification. In the future, we will evaluate the proposed unified approach for multiclass classifications.
