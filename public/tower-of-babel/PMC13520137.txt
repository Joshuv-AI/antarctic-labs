The relationship between internet addiction and psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents: a meta-analysis
Authors: Şenay Kılınçel, Furkan Bulut, Oğuzhan Kılınçel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternet addiction (IA) has emerged as a growing concern among children and adolescents and has been increasingly linked to a variety of psychiatric disorders. However, the strength and consistency of these associations remained unclear due to methodological differences across studies. This meta-analysis aimed to systematically evaluate the relationship between internet addiction and common psychiatric disorders in youth populations.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and PsycINFO for studies published between January 2015 and June 2025. Studies were included if they assessed IA using validated tools and reported its association with psychiatric disorders among individuals aged ≤ 18 years. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Risk of publication bias was evaluated with funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultsA total of 22 studies with 36,432 participants were included. Internet addiction was significantly associated with depression (OR = 2.35; 95% CI: 1.95–2.82), anxiety (OR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.72–2.60), ADHD (OR = 2.78; 95% CI: 2.09–3.69), and OCD (OR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.31–2.81). Heterogeneity was moderate to high for most outcomes. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of results across geographic regions, study design, and assessment tools.ConclusionThe findings supported the clinical relevance of assessing problematic internet use in child and adolescent psychiatric practice. Integrating routine screening into mental health evaluations may facilitate identify at-risk individuals earlier.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-05010-6.

Background

The use of the internet has become widespread in nearly every aspect of daily life. The rapid growth of digital technology and the increasing accessibility of internet-enabled devices have fundamentally changed how individuals, especially young people, engage with information, entertainment, and social interaction. While internet use offers numerous advantages in terms of academic enrichment, communication, and recreational opportunities, excessive or uncontrolled internet use has increasingly emerged as a significant public health concern, particularly among children and adolescents [1, 2]. This population is considered especially vulnerable due to ongoing neurodevelopmental processes, evolving self-regulation capacities, and heightened sensitivity to peer influences, which may predispose them to problematic internet behaviors [1, 2].

Internet addiction has been recognized in classification systems such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) under the category of "Internet Gaming Disorder," which is included in Section III as a condition warranting further study. Similarly, the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) has classified excessive and compulsive internet-related behaviors under the broader term "Behavioral Addiction," acknowledging their growing clinical relevance and the need for appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [3–5]. These classifications reflect the increasing global recognition of internet addiction as a distinct behavioral disorder that shares neurobiological and psychosocial features with substance use disorders [6, 7].

Accumulating evidence suggests that internet addiction may be associated with various psychiatric disorders including depression, anxiety, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) [8–12]. Several epidemiological and clinical studies have identified higher prevalence rates of these comorbid psychiatric conditions among youth with problematic internet use compared to their peers [8–12]. Depression and anxiety are frequently reported in adolescents with internet addiction, potentially mediated by social isolation, disturbed sleep patterns, and poor emotion regulation [8, 9]. Moreover, the impulsivity and inattention characteristic of ADHD may not only predispose individuals to internet addiction but may also be exacerbated by excessive screen time and online activity [10, 11]. Obsessive–compulsive tendencies, characterized by repetitive behaviors and intrusive thoughts, have also been linked to compulsive internet use patterns [10]. Moreover, the impulsivity and inattention characteristic of ADHD may not only predispose individuals to internet addiction but may also be exacerbated by excessive screen time and online activity [8, 9]. Obsessive–compulsive tendencies, characterized by repetitive behaviors and intrusive thoughts, have also been linked to compulsive internet use patterns [12].

However, the nature and strength of these associations remain not fully established, with some studies reporting inconsistent or conflicting findings. Methodological differences across studies, including variability in diagnostic criteria, assessment tools, cultural contexts, and sample characteristics, may contribute to these discrepancies [13–17]. Therefore, a systematic synthesis of the current evidence is necessary to clarify the magnitude and direction of these associations, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the psychiatric correlates of internet addiction in children and adolescents.

The aim of this meta-analysis was to systematically review and quantitatively synthesize the existing literature to better elucidate the association between internet addiction and psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents.

Methodology

Literature search

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and PsycINFO for articles published between January 2014 and June 2025. We selected January 2015 as the starting point because the diagnostic criteria for Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) were first formalized in the DSM-5 (2013) and subsequently refined in the ICD-11 (2018). Restricting the search to studies published from 2015 onward ensured that the included research adopted standardized diagnostic frameworks and validated assessment tools, thereby minimizing heterogeneity due to earlier, non-standardized definitions of gaming-related problems. Only peer-reviewed, full-text articles published in English were considered.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms:

(“internet addiction” OR “problematic internet use” OR “compulsive internet use” OR “internet gaming disorder”) AND (“psychiatric disorder” OR “mental disorder” OR “depression” OR “anxiety” OR “ADHD” OR “obsessive–compulsive disorder” OR “OCD”) AND (“children” OR “adolescents” OR “youth”).

The reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X20 (Clarivate Analytics) software.

The psychiatric disorders examined in this meta-analysis—depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD—were selected based on epidemiological evidence identifying them as the most commonly reported psychiatric comorbidities associated with internet addiction in children and adolescents. Depression and anxiety represent the most prevalent internalizing disorders in this population, ADHD is a frequent neurodevelopmental comorbidity linked to impulsivity and reward dysregulation, and OCD was included due to its theoretical and behavioral overlap with compulsive internet use patterns.

Exclusion criteria were: studies with adult-only populations, qualitative studies, reviews, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts without full text, and studies lacking extractable quantitative data.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) 11-item checklist, which is specifically validated for cross-sectional studies. Each item was scored as “Yes” (1 point) if the criterion was clearly met, or “No/Unclear” (0 points) if the information was absent or ambiguous. Total scores ranged from 0 to 11, with higher scores indicating better methodological quality. Based on the overall score, studies were categorized as low quality (0–3), moderate quality (4–7), or high quality (8–11). Study selection

A total of 4,863 records were identified through electronic database searching, and 12 additional records were identified through manual screening of reference lists. After removal of duplicates, 3,925 unique articles remained. Following title and abstract screening, 85 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 22 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the quantitative meta-analysis. Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA, version 3) and Review Manager (RevMan 5.4) software. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) to account for potential heterogeneity across studies [42]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test (p < 0.10 considered significant) and quantified using the I2 statistic, with I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [43]. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and formally tested using Egger’s regression test [44]. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including geographic location, study design, and type of assessment tool. Tools were categorized as validated if they were standardized instruments with established reliability and validity (e.g., Internet Addiction Test [IAT], Chen Internet Addiction Scale [CIAS], Young’s Diagnostic Questionnaire [YDQ]). Instruments were classified as non-validated if they were author-developed, modified, or locally adapted measures without formal psychometric validation (e.g., Social Media Disorder [SMD] scale, Problematic Internet Use [PIU] questionnaires).

Results

Study characteristics

As shown in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1), 22 studies were finally included in the quantitative synthesis. A total of 22 studies published between 2014 and 2025 were included in this meta-analysis, comprising 36,432 participants aged 18 years or younger. Most of the included studies employed validated instruments to assess internet addiction, such as the IAT, YDQ, and the CIAS. Psychiatric outcomes were measured using well-established assessment tools, including the BDI, BAI, CDI, DSM-IV/DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, and various structured clinical interviews. Among the psychiatric disorders evaluated, depression was the most frequently assessed condition, reported in 12 studies. Anxiety disorders were examined in 11 studies, followed by ADHD in 8 studies, and OCD in 4 studies. Additionally, psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) were each reported in one study. Several studies also investigated other psychiatric dimensions such as impulsivity, childhood trauma, and emotional dysregulation. The majority of studies were cross-sectional in design, while a smaller subset employed cohort or case–control methodologies. Pooled effect sizes

Internet addiction and depression

A total of 17 studies assessed the association between internet addiction and depression. However, only 12 studies provided ORs with 95% confidence intervals, and were therefore eligible for inclusion in the quantitative meta-analysis. The remaining 5 studies reported alternative effect sizes (e.g., correlation coefficients or regression β values) without ORs or RRs, and were narratively summarized but excluded from the pooled meta-analysis. The pooled OR for depression was 2.35 (95% CI: 1.95–2.82, p < 0.001). Internet addiction and anxiety

A total of 11 studies with a combined sample size of 18,934 participants examined the association between internet addiction and anxiety symptoms. The pooled OR for anxiety among individuals with internet addiction was 2.11 (95% CI: 1.72–2.60, p < 0.001). Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed through both visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. The funnel plots for depression and ADHD appeared symmetrical, suggesting a low risk of publication bias. For anxiety-related studies, slight asymmetry was observed; however, Egger’s test results were not statistically significant (e.g., p = 0.12 for depression, p = 0.18 for ADHD, and p = 0.09 for anxiety). Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Funnel plot interpretation is inherently subjective, and the statistical power of Egger’s regression test is limited when the number of included studies is small. This limitation is particularly relevant for outcomes such as ADHD and OCD, where only a limited number of studies were available for quantitative synthesis. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant evidence of publication bias should not be interpreted as definitive proof that publication bias or small-study effects were absent.

Discussion

This meta-analysis provided comprehensive quantitative evidence that internet addiction in children and adolescents is significantly associated with a range of psychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD. By synthesizing data from 22 studies encompassing over 36,000 participants, the present findings underscored internet addiction as an important transdiagnostic correlate in child and adolescent mental health. Unlike narrative summaries, the use of pooled odds ratios allowed a clearer estimation of relative risk and facilitates comparison across different psychiatric conditions.

The strongest association was found between IA and depression, with a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 2.35. This result was consistent with findings reported by Ho et al., who observed similar patterns of psychiatric comorbidity in adolescents with problematic internet use [21]. Choi et al. and Tan et al. also reported that IA exacerbated depressive symptoms, possibly through mechanisms involving social withdrawal, disrupted circadian rhythms, and poor emotional regulation [30, 38]. Furthermore, longitudinal data suggested that depressive symptoms both predicted and were predicted by internet addiction, supporting a bidirectional relationship [41].

While significant associations were identified between internet addiction and psychiatric disorders, the cross-sectional nature of most included studies precludes causal inference. We cannot determine whether psychiatric disorders increase vulnerability to internet addiction, whether excessive internet use precipitates psychiatric symptoms, or whether bidirectional relationships exist. Although a few longitudinal studies suggest potential reciprocal effects, definitive conclusions about causality cannot be drawn. Longitudinal research with clear temporal sequencing is essential to establish directionality and clarify underlying mechanisms. Importantly, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence that internet addiction directly causes psychiatric disorders. Several alternative explanations remain plausible. Psychiatric symptoms such as depression, anxiety, or ADHD may increase susceptibility to excessive internet use as a coping strategy or source of reinforcement. Conversely, excessive engagement with online activities may contribute to worsening psychological symptoms through mechanisms such as social withdrawal, sleep disruption, and impaired emotional regulation. A third possibility is that both problematic internet use and psychiatric symptoms arise from shared underlying vulnerabilities, including impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, family dysfunction, or adverse life experiences. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than causation.

The association between IA and anxiety disorders was also significant, with a pooled OR of 2.11. This finding aligned with earlier research by Andrade et al. and Ahmed et al., who emphasized the impact of excessive internet use on social and emotional functioning [8, 12]. The high heterogeneity observed in this domain (I2 = 71%) might have been due to differences in diagnostic instruments (e.g., BAI, STAI, DASS-21) and sociocultural factors, as evidenced in studies from Vietnam, Japan, and Pakistan [32, 33, 35]. In addition to measurement tools and cultural context, several other factors may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Age-related differences in anxiety expression across early versus late adolescence, variability in gender distribution—given the higher prevalence of anxiety symptoms among females—and regional differences in psychosocial stressors and help-seeking behaviors may all influence effect size estimates. However, inconsistent reporting of age stratification and gender ratios across studies limited the feasibility of formal subgroup or meta-regression analyses for these variables. These factors should therefore be considered as potential, but unquantified, sources of heterogeneity.

Additional sources of heterogeneity may also have contributed to the variability observed across studies. Factors such as differences in gender distribution, socioeconomic background, severity thresholds used to define problematic internet use, and the specific type of internet-related behavior assessed (e.g., generalized internet addiction, gaming-related behaviors, or social media use) may influence the strength of psychiatric associations. However, these variables were inconsistently reported across the included studies, precluding formal subgroup analyses or meta-regression. Future research incorporating more standardized reporting of these characteristics may help clarify their contribution to between-study heterogeneity.

For ADHD, a robust association was found, with a pooled OR of 2.78 and low heterogeneity. This result corroborated findings from Ko et al. and Kuss et al., who argued that the impulsivity and inattention characteristic of ADHD may have predisposed individuals to excessive and compulsive internet use [6, 22]. Additionally, the fast-paced and instant-reward nature of digital platforms may have served as reinforcing stimuli for adolescents with attentional challenges [10, 11].

Although several individual studies have consistently reported an association between internet addiction and ADHD, the present meta-analysis adds value by quantitatively synthesizing these findings and providing a precise pooled risk estimate. Beyond confirming consistency, our analysis demonstrates the relative magnitude of this association compared with other psychiatric disorders, revealing ADHD as one of the strongest correlates of internet addiction in youth. This comparative, disorder-specific quantification—derived from a unified methodological approach—represents a key contribution of the present study and supports its clinical relevance for screening and early intervention.

Although fewer studies examined OCD, the association with IA was still statistically significant, with a pooled OR of 1.92. Ko et al. and Restrepo et al. reported overlapping symptoms such as cognitive rigidity and compulsive tendencies that might have contributed to problematic internet use in youth with obsessive traits [22, 25]. However, this finding should be interpreted with considerable caution because only two studies contributed to the pooled quantitative estimate. The limited evidence base restricts confidence in the precision and generalizability of the observed effect size. Therefore, the OCD results should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a robust association. Additional well-designed studies are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the relationship between internet addiction and obsessive–compulsive symptomatology in youth populations.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these associations. Studies conducted in Asia and Europe showed similar effect sizes, suggesting that the findings were generalizable across different geographic and cultural contexts. The use of validated diagnostic tools, such as IAT, CIAS, and YDQ, did not substantially alter the results compared to non-validated tools. Likewise, effect sizes were consistent across study designs (cross-sectional vs. cohort/case–control), indicating that methodological differences did not substantially influence the observed associations.

The methodological quality profile of the included studies should also be considered when interpreting the findings. According to the AHRQ assessment, most studies were classified as moderate quality, while only a smaller proportion achieved high-quality ratings. This distribution reflects the inherent methodological challenges of observational research in this field, including reliance on self-report measures, limited control of confounding variables, and predominantly cross-sectional designs. Consequently, although the consistency of the observed associations across studies supports their robustness, the magnitude of the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding lower-quality studies produced similar results, suggesting that the overall conclusions were not driven solely by methodological weaknesses.

An additional source of heterogeneity should be acknowledged. Although all included studies examined problematic forms of internet-related behaviors, the operational definitions varied considerably and encompassed generalized internet addiction, problematic internet use, internet gaming disorder, social media disorder, and other compulsive online activities. These constructs share common features such as excessive engagement, impaired control, and functional impairment, which justified their inclusion under a broader internet addiction framework. Nevertheless, they are not entirely interchangeable from a clinical or theoretical perspective and may involve partially distinct motivational, cognitive, and neurobehavioral mechanisms. Consequently, the pooled estimates reported in this meta-analysis should be interpreted as reflecting a broad spectrum of problematic internet-related behaviors rather than a single homogeneous disorder. Future meta-analyses focusing on specific behavioral subtypes may help clarify whether the magnitude of psychiatric associations differs across these related but distinct constructs.

A further methodological consideration relates to the heterogeneity of psychiatric outcome assessment across studies. The included investigations used a wide range of approaches, including self-report symptom scales, screening instruments, structured clinical interviews, and formal DSM-based diagnoses. Although these methods all aim to capture psychiatric phenomena, they differ substantially in their diagnostic precision, construct validity, and clinical interpretation. Self-report instruments primarily assess symptom severity and psychological distress, whereas structured interviews and DSM-based assessments are designed to establish clinical diagnoses. Consequently, the pooled estimates may reflect a combination of symptom burden and formally diagnosed psychiatric disorders rather than a single, uniform clinical construct. This measurement heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the observed associations.

The mechanisms underlying these associations appeared to be multifactorial. Neurobiological models proposed that excessive internet use may have altered dopaminergic reward circuits, which are known to play a role in mood regulation and impulse control [5]. From a psychosocial perspective, IA may have contributed to emotional dysregulation, social withdrawal, and academic difficulties, all of which are risk factors for psychopathology [2, 8]. Moreover, studies suggested that childhood trauma, emotion dysregulation, and impulsivity served as mediating variables in the relationship between IA and mental health outcomes [16, 29].

From a conceptual perspective, internet addiction may not represent a single, uniform phenomenon and can be interpreted through several complementary frameworks. For some individuals, problematic internet use may function primarily as a coping strategy aimed at reducing psychological distress, loneliness, anxiety, or depressive symptoms. In other cases, it may resemble a behavioral addiction characterized by impaired control, reward-seeking behavior, tolerance-like features, and continued engagement despite negative consequences. Alternatively, excessive internet use may be conceptualized as part of a broader symptom cluster associated with emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and difficulties in self-regulation. Finally, problematic internet use may also represent a secondary manifestation of underlying psychiatric vulnerability rather than an independent disorder. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive and may help explain why different psychiatric disorders show varying patterns and magnitudes of association with internet-related behaviors. For example, ADHD may be more strongly linked through impulsivity and reward sensitivity mechanisms, whereas depression and anxiety may be more closely related to maladaptive coping and emotional regulation processes. These mechanisms may be viewed as components of a broader vulnerability–reinforcement model. In this framework, underlying vulnerabilities such as emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and heightened reward sensitivity increase susceptibility to problematic internet use. Internet-based activities may then provide immediate psychological reinforcement through distraction, mood regulation, social validation, or reward-related stimulation. Although these short-term benefits may temporarily alleviate distress, they can also contribute to social withdrawal, sleep disruption, academic difficulties, and worsening psychiatric symptoms over time. This reciprocal process may help explain the persistent associations observed between problematic internet use and a wide range of psychiatric conditions.

An additional conceptual challenge concerns the ongoing debate regarding the diagnostic validity and boundaries of internet-related behavioral disorders. Terms such as generalized internet addiction, problematic internet use, internet gaming disorder, social media disorder, and compulsive digital behaviors are frequently used interchangeably in the literature, despite important differences in their proposed definitions and underlying mechanisms. While internet gaming disorder has been formally recognized as a condition warranting clinical attention in contemporary diagnostic frameworks, broader constructs such as problematic internet use and generalized internet addiction remain less clearly defined. Consequently, the present meta-analysis adopts a pragmatic approach by considering these conditions as related manifestations within a broader spectrum of problematic internet-related behaviors. However, this approach should not be interpreted as implying that these constructs are diagnostically equivalent. Future research should further clarify construct boundaries and determine whether specific forms of problematic digital engagement demonstrate distinct psychiatric correlates and developmental trajectories.

Recent meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated a significant association between internet addiction and various psychiatric symptoms in adolescent populations; however, important methodological and conceptual differences distinguish the present findings from prior work. Soriano-Molina et al. conducted a large meta-analytic review focusing on adolescents’ mental health outcomes related to internet addiction, reporting moderate correlations with depression (r ≈ 0.32) and anxiety (r ≈ 0.25) [45]. While their study provided a broad overview of internalizing and externalizing symptoms, it relied primarily on correlation-based effect sizes and included heterogeneous mental health constructs (e.g., well-being, aggressiveness, suicidal behavior) [45]. In contrast, our meta-analysis focused on clinically interpretable odds ratios, allowing a clearer estimation of risk magnitude for specific psychiatric disorders. Notably, our pooled odds ratio for depression (OR = 2.35) suggests a substantially increased risk, reinforcing and extending the correlational findings reported by Soriano-Molina et al.

With respect to depression specifically, Ye et al. performed a comprehensive meta-analysis examining the bidirectional relationship between internet addiction and depression among adolescents, reporting that internet addiction conferred a higher risk for depressive disorders and vice versa [46]. Their findings highlighted depression as the most consistently studied outcome and emphasized reciprocal associations. Our results are concordant with these observations; however, our study extends the literature by simultaneously quantifying associations with multiple psychiatric disorders (depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD) within a single analytic framework, thereby enabling cross-disorder comparison of effect sizes.

Regarding ADHD, prior meta-analyses have largely examined problematic internet use in relation to ADHD symptoms or diagnoses in isolation. For example, Werling et al. demonstrated that children and adolescents with ADHD exhibit significantly higher levels of problematic internet or digital media use compared to controls [47]. While their work focused on ADHD as a risk group for problematic internet use, our meta-analysis approached the association from a complementary perspective by estimating the likelihood of ADHD among individuals with internet addiction, yielding a pooled odds ratio of 2.78. This bidirectional framing underscores the clinical relevance of screening for ADHD symptoms in youth presenting with problematic internet behaviors.

In contrast to depression and ADHD, OCD has received comparatively limited attention in prior meta-analytic research. Existing reviews have either excluded OCD or subsumed obsessive–compulsive symptoms within broader impulsive–compulsive spectra. Our findings indicate a significant association between internet addiction and OCD (OR = 1.92); however, this estimate was based on a limited number of eligible studies. Consistent with best practice in meta-analytic interpretation, this result should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Importantly, the identification of such evidence gaps represents a meaningful contribution, as it delineates priorities for future longitudinal and mechanistic research. Taken together, while previous meta-analyses have robustly established links between internet addiction and selected psychiatric outcomes [45–47], the present study adds value by providing updated, age-restricted (≤ 18 years), disorder-specific risk estimates, integrating multiple psychiatric conditions within a unified quantitative framework, and explicitly highlighting underexplored domains such as OCD.

This meta-analysis had some limitations. Most included studies were cross-sectional, which limited causal interpretations. Variations in assessment tools, sample characteristics, and cultural factors introduced heterogeneity that could not be fully controlled. Some studies relied on self-report measures, which may have been prone to bias. Although funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test did not indicate substantial publication bias, these methods have limited sensitivity when only a small number of studies are available. Therefore, the possibility of publication bias or small-study effects cannot be completely excluded. Another limitation is the conceptual heterogeneity of the internet addiction construct itself. The included studies assessed a range of related but distinct behaviors, including generalized internet addiction, problematic internet use, internet gaming disorder, and social media disorder. Although these conditions share overlapping characteristics, they may not represent a single underlying psychopathological entity, which may have contributed to residual heterogeneity across analyses. In addition, psychiatric outcomes were not assessed uniformly across studies. Some investigations relied on symptom-based self-report questionnaires, whereas others used structured clinical interviews or formal diagnostic criteria. Combining symptom severity measures with diagnostic outcomes may have introduced additional measurement heterogeneity and may partially explain the variability observed across studies. Several potentially important moderators, including gender distribution, socioeconomic context, severity thresholds for problematic internet use, and specific internet-related behavioral subtypes, could not be examined because of inconsistent reporting across primary studies. Furthermore, the overall methodological quality of the available evidence was predominantly moderate rather than high. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted in the context of the limitations inherent to the underlying primary studies.

Despite these limitations, this study had several strengths. It synthesized data from over 36,000 participants, employed rigorous quality assessment via the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, and incorporated detailed subgroup and sensitivity analyses. To the best of our knowledge, it represented one of the most comprehensive and up-to-date quantitative syntheses examining the link between internet addiction and psychiatric disorders in youth populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlighted the clinical importance of systematically considering problematic internet use within child and adolescent mental health services. Incorporating routine screening for problematic internet use may facilitate earlier recognition of individuals who may present with co-occurring psychiatric symptoms and support more comprehensive mental health assessment and management. From a research perspective, future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and to guide the development of targeted preventive and therapeutic interventions addressing internet addiction in young populations.
