Data on marine protection levels in Norwegian MPAs: A national application of The MPA guide
Authors: Barbara Horta e Costa, Inês Sousa, Alf R. Kleiven, Johanna M. Aarflot, Even Moland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset provides the first harmonized and validated classification of Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) in Norwegian waters according to The MPA Guide framework. It represents the first national-scale, local expert-validated application of this framework in Northern Europe, producing a detailed and fine-tuned assessment of protection levels across all Norwegian MPAs.Spatial and regulatory information was compiled from the Norwegian Environment Agency (Miljødirektoratet), Fisheries Directorate (Fiskeridirektoratet), World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA), complemented by national legislation and additional spatial layers from EMODnet and other datasets. Each site was assessed across the seven activity categories defined by The MPA Guide Levels of Protection (mining, dredging and dumping, anchoring, infrastructure, fisheries, aquaculture, and non-extractive uses). Fisheries received the most detailed treatment, combining gear-specific regulations, a refined national bathymetric layer, depth-based operability constraints, expert validation and, where relevant, AIS vessel-tracking data (mainly vessels above 15 meters). Regulations on other activities were primarily gathered from sectoral national sources and complemented with EMODnet data. Activity impacts were scored as low, moderate, high, or Incompatible with nature conservation, and each MPA was assigned the lowest resulting Level of Protection (LOP).The final dataset includes 814 MPAs and 53 zones encompassing approximately 88 966 km², corresponding to 4.4% of Norway’s marine waters (Territorial Waters and Exclusive Economic Zone). When classified using The MPA Guide, around 91% of the protected area is either Minimally Protected or Incompatible with conservation (≈4% of Norway’s marine waters), 9.5% is Lightly Protected (0.4% of Norway’s marine waters), while Fully Protected areas represent only 0.1% of the MPA area (0.004% of Norway’s marine waters), and no Highly Protected areas were identified. On the mainland, about 99% of MPA area is either Minimally Protected or Incompatible with conservation, 1% is Lightly Protected, and only 0.03% is Fully Protected, whereas in the archipelagos of Svalbard and Jan Mayen, 89% of the MPA area is Minimally Protected or Incompatible with conservation, with most of the remainder classified as Lightly Protected (10.5%), and only 0.1% being Fully Protected.The dataset is released in open format (GeoPackage + Excel table + metadata) and can be directly integrated with EMODnet, WDPA, and MPAtlas repositories. It provides a transparent, reproducible, and scalable reference for assessing marine Levels of Protection, supporting the implementation of strict protection and the Nature Restoration Regulation, as well as regional initiatives under OSPAR and the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP), including the EU Marine Action Plan that aims to reconcile marine conservation with sustainable fisheries.

Background

Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) are key for conserving marine biodiversity and ecosystem services. In Europe, they underpin the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030, the Marine Strategy Framework Directive, the Common Fisheries Policy, and the Nature Restoration Regulation, alongside the Global Biodiversity Framework Target 3. Despite increasing MPA coverage across Europe, many sites provide limited ecological protection because extractive or destructive activities remain permitted [1,2,6,7].

Assessing effective MPA protection requires harmonized information on regulated and occurring human activities [3], yet such data remain fragmented and incomplete, particularly for fisheries, as highlighted by EU-scale applications of The MPA Guide [6,7].

Although not an EU Member State, Norway is closely integrated into regional marine governance through OSPAR Convention (the Convention for the Protection of the Marine Environment of the North-East Atlantic – the OSLO-PARIS Convention) and cooperation in European policies and has ratified the Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ) Agreement, also known as the High Seas Treaty. Nevertheless, Norway lacks a comprehensive overview of MPA regulations: only 18 MPAs are formally designated under the national Marine Protection Plan, while many other marine areas are not recorded as MPAs1.

This dataset provides the first harmonized national list and classification of Norwegian MPAs by Level of Protection using The MPA Guide [3], based on detailed national data and local expert validation, and supports marine governance, conservation planning, and consistent reporting across Norwegian waters, including Arctic regions.

Data Description

The dataset provides a harmonized and validated inventory of all marine protected areas (MPAs) in Norwegian waters and classifies each site according to its Level of Protection (LOP) under The MPA Guide framework [3]. It includes spatial and tabular files describing MPA features, regulatory information, and human activities assessed through international and national databases as well as expert validation. This dataset is available in the repository: https://figshare.com/s/3c830385728562b3317b [8].

Summary of dataset content

The dataset includes 814 marine protected areas (MPAs) and 53 zones, encompassing approximately 88 966 km², which corresponds to 4.4 % of Norway’s marine waters (Territorial Waters and Exclusive Economic Zone, EEZ), including 1% of mainland waters and 7.5% of the waters surrounding the Svalbard and Jan Mayen archipelagos. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Overview

The dataset compiles, cleans, and classifies all Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) in Norwegian waters according to the MPA Guide framework [3]. It integrates information from international and national databases, legal documents, and local expert validation to ensure consistency and accuracy across designations. The workflow followed the same general protocol as previous EU-scale datasets [1,2] but was refined to include all marine areas under national protection, detailed fishing regulations, and spatially explicit validation using fishing gear characteristics and depth information (Fig. 4).Fig. 4General workflow showing the main steps for gathering information on Norwegian Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) and classifying them according to The MPA Guide Levels of Protection (LOP).Fig 4 dummy alt text

Compilation of MPA polygons

Spatial data for all protected areas in Norway were first obtained from the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA) and cross-referenced with data from the Norwegian Environment Agency (Miljødirektoratet) (https://kartkatalog.miljodirektoratet.no/). Almost all officially designated MPAs were listed in both the Miljødirektoratet database (Marin verneplan) and the WDPA. However, one recently designated official MPA recorded by Miljødirektoratet had not yet been submitted to the WDPA (‘Stad’). Additional protected areas from the WDPA were also retained when they contained a marine component and were associated with conservation objectives and/or legally established marine conservation measures, following internationally recognized MPA definitions and guidance. These included OSPAR MPAs, National Parks, Nature Reserves, Wildlife Conservation Areas, Wildlife/Botanical Conservation Areas, Zoological Protection Areas, and Ramsar Sites. Designation types without significant marine component were excluded, including Botanical Conservation Areas, Habitat Management Areas, Natural Monuments, Protected Geotopes, and Landscape Protection Areas.

Terrestrial overlaps were removed using the European Environment Agency coastline layer (https://www.eea.europa.eu/data-and-maps/data/eea-coastline-for-analysis-1/gis-data/europe-coastline-shapefile). Remaining fragments of land or wetland polygons were deleted, yielding a national set of marine and coastal polygons standardized to WGS 84 UTM 33 N, EPSG 32633.

Resolving overlaps and zoning

Since multiple overlapping designations (national, regional – OSPAR, and international – RAMSAR) are mostly coincident, the national designation was prioritized.

In cases where multiple legal typologies exist (within nationally-designated or OSPAR MPAs) but a smaller polygon (e.g., Nature Reserve) is entirely contained within a larger one (e.g., National Park), both were retained and assessed independently, while the overlapping surface of the smaller polygon was subtracted from the larger. Fisheries restricted zones partially overlapping MPAs were considered distinct MPA zones because they are subject to different regulations, despite Norwegian MPAs not being officially zoned.

Each MPA received a unique identifier (MPA_id), and any internal zoning (e.g., fisheries restricted zones overlapping MPAs and subject to additional regulations) was retained and assessed independently when distinguishable in the original data (MPA_zone_id).

Activity and regulatory data

Regulations and spatial information for human activities were compiled from multiple authority sources:•WDPA (Protected Planet) dataset for submitted MPA polygons and their corresponding main features;•Norwegian Environment Agency - Miljødirektoratet geoportal for MPA polygon validation and MPA official listing2;•Fisheries Directorate - Fiskeridirektoratet geoportal for spatial fisheries regulations (bylaws and fisheries restricted zones within MPAs)3;•Norwegian Environment Agency - Miljødirektoratet website (Norwegian legal database website) for the Marine Protection Plan (Marin Verneplan) under the Environmental Agency4;•Lovdata (Norwegian legal database website) for the Marine Resources Act (LOV-2008-06-06-37) under the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Fisheries, and associated regulations on the harvesting of marine resources5;•EMODnet Human Activities, Barents Watch, and national sectoral datasets (i.e., SODIR - Norwegian Offshore Directorate, Fiskeridirektoratet - Fisheries Directorate, Kystverket - Norwegian Coastal Administration, NVE - Norwegian Water Resources and Energy) for mining, dredging, infrastructure, aquaculture, anchoring, and non-extractive uses;•OSPAR MPA database for MPA features and activity data, although the available information was insufficient [7] for use in this assessment (source: https://www.ospar.org/work-areas/bdc/marine-protected-areas/mpa-webtool);•EMERALD Network database to confirm Norwegian MPAs in this network (source: https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/datahub/datahubitem-view/4c4c8086-c940-400b-9064-29063143b2de).

Where regulations were not spatially explicit (e.g., general prohibitions by depth or region), local experts and authorities clarified the geographic applicability and interpreted bylaws.

Activity status was coded as allowed or prohibited.

Fisheries refinement

Fisheries data required the most detailed treatment due to Norway’s complex gear- and depth-specific regulations. National laws and bylaws often detail prohibitions or specific management rules, but not which gears are allowed. Hence, a list of the allowed fishing gears was developed with the support of local experts, who identified all fishing gears commonly used in Norway and operability constraints.

Since fisheries regulations are insufficient to spatially assign fishing gears occurrences/allowances, to resolve this, we integrated:1.A refined national high-resolution bathymetric layer (EMODnet), allowing estimation of where depth-restricted gears can physically operate.2.A comprehensive legislation review was conducted for the 40 MPAs listed in the Protected Seas database as having strong fishing restrictions (fishing protection: level 4 - fishing heavily restrictive; level 5 - fishing prohibited) (https://navigatormap.org/).3.Expert validation from the Institute of Marine Research (IMR) and other MARHAB partners to verify realistic gear distributions (i.e., gears were not attributed to ecologically or physically unsuitable habitats) and potential impact. In addition, local experts supplemented legislative information and performed an in-depth look into a subset of 44 MPAs (those included in the ‘Marin Verneplan’, OSPAR MPAs, National Parks, and MPAs larger than 25 km2), to confirm fisheries regulations and occurrences.4.AIS vessel-tracking data, used, in some cases, to confirm fishing activity in MPAs, including nearshore or remote areas where impactful fisheries were assumed absent.

As regulations generally specified prohibited fishing gears rather than those explicitly permitted, the potential occurrence of fishing gears in areas without specific regulations was inferred from environmental and operational characteristics. For example, bottom trawling was considered unlikely to occur within MPAs where at least 70% of the area was shallower than 30 m, as these depths are considered unsuitable for the operation of this fishing gear.

The remaining commercial fishing gears were not aggregated and were assigned an individual impact following The MPA guide criteria6. For recreational fishing, low- and moderate-impact fishing gear were aggregated within the respective categories. No recreational fishing gear was considered as high impact.

For large MPAs (≥ 25km2) where trawling or Danish seine can legally occur but, according to local experts, are not currently occurring, these fishing gears were assumed as absent (Svellingsflaket, Kaldvågfjorden og Innhavet, Karlsøyfjorden, Lurefjorden og Lindåsosane).

Expert validation involved marine conservation and fisheries specialists from the Institute of Marine Research (IMR, Norway), including experts in marine protected areas, fisheries management, fishing gear operability, and Norwegian marine regulations. The Norwegian Environment Agency and the Directorate of Fisheries were consulted for clarifications at different stages of the assessment, when needed. Discussions with experts from Sweden (SLU) and Denmark (DTU Aqua) additionally contributed to harmonizing assumptions regarding fishing gear impacts and operability across Scandinavian fisheries typologies.

Assignment of impact levels

Ecological impact levels for each activity were assigned following The MPA Guide criteria and based on the combined use of EMODnet datasets, national regulations, and expert input. Fishing impacts were assessed in detail (see Section 4.5), whereas the remaining activities followed previous MPA guide applications and were scored as follows:

Mining. Mining-related activities (e.g., oil and gas, minerals extraction) were obtained from Fisheries Directorate (layer ‘Oil and gas - SODIR’; SODIR: Norwegian Offshore Directorate’) and EMODnet websites. Some MPAs overlapped this activity in the layer of the North Sea/Skagerrak Oil and Gas opening area (in layer ‘Oil and Gas – SODIR’). This large polygon indicates that the activity may potentially be licenced (not specifying where it is actually occurring) and hence was considered as allowed. Two MPAs (Trænarevet and Sularevet; confirmed by local expert input and reporting7) overlapped with the EMODnet mining layer. In both cases, extractive activity was considered potentially licensable. MPAs were considered Fully Protected for mining when prohibited and Incompatible when allowed.

Dredging and/or dumping. EMODnet categories for this activity were aggregated for this assessment. Impact attribution followed The MPA Guide guidance considering the expected frequency, scale, and potential biodiversity impacts of dredging and dumping activities. Desalination and water-treatment waste were classified as Lightly Protected (moderate impact), whereas dredging spoil disposal and dredging sites were classified as Minimally Protected (high impact) due to the potentially large volume, potential contamination and spatial extent of disposed material, despite disposal events generally being infrequent. Dumped munitions areas were also considered Minimally Protected (high impact), as no specific information indicating chemical munitions or associated contamination risks was available for these sites. The MPA was considered Fully Protected for this activity when no dredging/dumping was reported.

Anchoring. Anchoring information was obtained from Kystverket (Norwegian Coastal Administration) web portal. Some designated anchoring sites for large vessels were reported overlapping Norwegian MPAs and were classified as high-impact activities, resulting in Minimally Protected classifications for the affected MPAs. Some MPAs may lie close to harbours or marinas although these were considered under the Infrastructure activity. Small boats are widely used in Norway for both recreational and practical purposes, making anchoring common in coastal areas. However, available information was insufficient to consistently assess the intensity and spatial distribution of small-scale anchoring activities across MPAs. Consequently, no additional MPAs were downgraded based on small-scale anchoring activity, and hence were classified as Fully Protected for this activity. This classification may nevertheless represent an optimistic assessment in densely populated coastal regions.

Infrastructure. Spatial data on infrastructure were obtained from three sources: Kystverket (Norwegian Coastal Administration) for harbours, marinas and quays; NVE (Norwegian Water Resources and Energy) for wind farms; and EMODnet for wind farms and ocean energy installations. Infrastructure features overlapping MPA polygons were handled as follows: if harbours, port facilities, wind farms, or ocean energy structures were present, MPAs were classified as Minimally Protected, reflecting the potential high impact associated with these developments. Where quays were the only mapped infrastructure present, MPAs were classified as Highly Protected, given the low impact attributed to these structures.

Aquaculture. Aquaculture facilities, obtained from the Fisheries Directorate (Fiskeridirektoratet) and EMODnet websites, overlapped with multiple MPA polygons. Impact levels were assigned by facility type: Fully Protected where aquaculture was absent, Highly Protected for low-impact algae or shellfish farming, and Minimally Protected where finfish aquaculture was allowed or present. Finfish production was treated as high-impact due to its typical large industrial-scale and locations in less exposed coastal areas and sensitive fjord systems.

Fishing. Fishing was the most detailed activity assessed in this dataset (see section 4.5). This was central to this evaluation, as fishing regulations are predominantly exempted from the MPA regulations. Gear information was compiled from national regulations (Ministry of Trade, Industry and Fisheries: Fisheries Directorate; Lovdata), expert input, and, where relevant, AIS data. Levels of protection followed The MPA Guide framework: Fully Protected where no fishing occurs; Highly Protected where only low-impact gears (e.g., traps, single lines, hand-harvesting) occur; Lightly Protected where moderate-impact gears are present (e.g., gillnets, trammel nets, small longlines, regulated hunting); Minimally Protected where high-impact gears occur (e.g., pelagic trawl, pelagic purse seine, kelp trawl); and Incompatible where bottom-contacting gears such as bottom trawl or Danish seine were allowed or confirmed. The number of different types of fishing gears was also taken in consideration. Although trap impacts may vary according to habitat and target species, traps were generally considered low-impact gears in the Norwegian context because they are predominantly deployed outside fragile biogenic habitats. Non-extractive recreational activities. Information on non-extractive uses (e.g., boating, kayaking, diving) was inconsistent and not spatially comprehensive (e.g., there are some no-go areas during the seabird breeding season). Yet, non-extractive recreation is widespread in Norwegian coastal areas. No MPA was downgraded based on this activity, and all MPAs were therefore classified as Fully Protected for non-extractive recreational uses. For some regions with high population density, this might be an overestimation of Level of Protection (optimistic).

Classification of protection levels

For each MPA, The MPA Guide decision tree for Levels of Protection [3] was applied across the seven activity categories (mining, dredging and dumping, anchoring, infrastructure, aquaculture, fisheries, and non-extractive recreational uses).

The overall Level of Protection (LOP) was defined by the activity with the lowest protection level.

Because activities were explicitly identified and fisheries were validated with the available information, the Norwegian dataset required no uncertainty scenarios.

All MPAs and zones within zoned MPAs were classified into one of the four MPA Guide Levels of Protection: Fully, Highly, Lightly, Minimally Protected, or into Incompatible with nature conservation.

Quality assurance

Spatial operations (merging, intersection, area calculation) were performed in R (≥ 4.3) using the dplyr, sf and terra packages, and cross-checked in QGIS (3.34).

Attribute completeness and topology were verified through automated scripts.

Preliminary maps and results were reviewed by project partners, who confirmed consistency with known management and enforcement contexts.

Feedback revealed discrepancies – such as the unexpectedly low protection levels in some remote areas – which were verified through AIS evidence and retained as correct.

Output and reproducibility

The final dataset and metadata files (Geopackage + Excel table + Metadata) are published in an open repository with persistent identifiers (https://figshare.com/s/3c830385728562b3317b [8]).

All variable names, units, and coordinate systems are described in the metadata and in the current paper to enable replication and cross-comparison with other national or EU-level datasets.

Limitations

Although this dataset provides the most comprehensive and validated classification of Norwegian MPAs to date, some limitations remain. Spatial information for small nearshore areas or temporary fisheries closures may not be fully updated in national databases and may therefore be under-represented; however, temporary closures do not affect the classification of fishing gears allowed or occurring within MPAs. Legal information for older designations is sometimes dispersed or only partially digitized, requiring manual verification.

AIS data were incorporated where available but not applied systematically across the >800 MPAs assessed, and some mismatches between fishing-effort records and MPA boundaries may persist. AIS data were used to validate likely fishing occurrence and gear operability, but not to systematically assess illegal, unreported, or unregulated (IUU) fishing activities. Therefore, occasional non-compliant fishing activity may not be fully captured in the present assessment. Expert review was essential for validating regulated and occurring activities, but comprehensive expert validation was not feasible for all sites, with greater effort focused on larger and more strongly protected MPAs. Consequently, some site-specific details may have been overlooked.

Finally, marine area estimates depend on the quality of the underlying coastline layer; missing small islets may lead to slight overestimation of marine area. These limitations do not affect national-scale patterns or protection-level classification but may influence local area estimates.
