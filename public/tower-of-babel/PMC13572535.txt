A qualitative study of young people’s experiences of counselling in headspace Denmark as a civic approach to youth mental health
Authors: Siv Therese Bogevik Bjørkedal, Mads Fabricius, Rie Mandrup Poulsen, Anna Paldam Folker, Lene Falgaard Eplov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost research on youth mental health services has focused on clinical treatments and therapeutic approaches. Given the increasing prevalence of poor mental health among children and adolescents, there is a growing need to broaden this focus and explore the role of the civic sector in supporting young people with mental health issues. This study investigated young people´s experiences with headspace Denmark, a national civil society initiative promoting youth mental health and well-being. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted with 13 young people aged 14–25 years, who sought support from volunteer counsellors at headspace Denmark and analysed following Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis. Participants described headspace as a safe and welcoming space where they could share thoughts and feelings that were difficult to express elsewhere. Counsellors were perceived as caring, attentive, and genuinely interested: Conversations were often compared to supportive interactions with friends or family members. The physical environment was described as cozy and home-like, providing a contrast to clinical settings. Findings suggest that volunteer-based mental health services offer distinct benefits beyond compensating for limited professional resources, providing relational, accessible, non-clinical support that may play a valuable role in the broader youth mental health landscape. Background

The increasing prevalence of mental health problems among adolescents and young adults—including psychological distress, anxiety, depression, self-harm, and suicidal ideation—represents a major public health challenge1–4. These difficulties affect both young people and their families, with consequences for life expectancy, physical health5,6, and educational and vocational attainment7. Despite access to publicly funded mental health care in many high-resource countries, a large proportion of young people do not receive adequate support4,8–10, with a recent meta-analysis estimating that 54% of adolescents have unmet mental health care needs10. Barriers include stigma, limited mental health literacy, difficulty navigating complex systems, long waiting times, and rejection from services11,12. In response, youth-specific mental health services for young people aged 12–25 have emerged in Australia13 and other Western countries14–18. Although these models vary, common features include accessible entry points, youth participation, community-based approaches, and holistic, prevention-oriented care4,15,19. Headspace Denmark differs by operating within civil society rather than the healthcare or social sectors14. In Denmark, civil society is characterised by strong membership-based organisations, high trust and participation, and close collaboration with public institutions, contributing to social cohesion and social capital20,21. Headspace Denmark does not provide clinical treatment but offers young people “someone to talk to” through trained volunteer counsellors, including lay and peer youth workers14,22. This model aligns with evidence showing that trained lay counsellors can reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, and trauma23, and with robust research demonstrating that peer support improves depression, self-efficacy, recovery, hope, empowerment, and social inclusion, across settings and regions, including low- and middle- income countries24,25. Peer support relationships are characterised by acceptance, person-centredness, empowerment, reciprocity, and trust, fostering psychological safety, agency, and coping skills26,27. Combining lay and peer support in community settings may therefore enhance accessibility, engagement, and meaningful support23,25. Most headspace Denmark counsellors are volunteers from diverse backgrounds, including students and former service users, with selection emphasising relational competencies. Training combines courses, online learning, supervision in conversation techniques and in counselling following the headspace principles, and preparation for sensitive issues such as suicidal thoughts and self-harm. Counsellors are supported by paid counsellors, through structured briefing and de-briefing in conjunction with conversations, and by planned and ad hoc supervision. Guided by the principle that “no problem is too big or too small,” counselling is youth-led, flexible, and grounded in respect and non-judgement14,22. From a socioeconomic perspective, the model offers a low-intensity, low-cost alternative to public services and may inform youth mental health support in lower-resource settings14. While research has focused primarily on integrated youth mental health services4,13,15,28, studies of voluntary sector services highlight the value of non-clinical, non-hierarchical environments for well-being and social connection29–31. headspace Denmark conducts ongoing self-evaluations and has been externally evaluated, indicating a distinctive person-centred approach14,32. This study forms part of a five-year national evaluation (2022–2027) guided by the Medical Research Council Framework22,33. While quantitative analyses assess effectiveness, qualitative approaches are essential for understanding how support is experienced and how change occurs34,35. Accordingly, this qualitative study explores young people’s experiences of counselling at headspace Denmark, focusing on interpersonal and intrapersonal processes as mechanisms that contribute to self-reported changes in mental health.

Methods

A qualitative research design was chosen as it is well suited to exploring and interpreting human experiences36–40. The evaluation was informed by a preliminary programme theory outlining the intervention’s activities, mechanisms, and expected outcomes (see headspace Denmark logic model, Figure S1). The theory posits that user-driven counselling with minimal predefined structure provides accessible support through a flexible and open counsellor approach, enabling young people to feel understood, reassured, and less burdened by their concerns. These experiences are expected to reduce loneliness and strengthen well-being, mental health, and self-efficacy22. The study was further grounded in a phenomenological hermeneutic approach36–38, focusing on how young people made sense of their interactions at headspace Denmark. Through interpretative analysis of interview data, the study explored how the proposed mechanisms contributed to perceived changes in mental health and well-being. The first author (STBB) conducted most interviews and led data analysis and interpretation in collaboration with the second author (MF) and co-authors (RMP, APF, and LFE). STBB is a female occupational therapist with experience in adult mental health services. MF is a male psychologist with lived experience of mental health services. RMP holds a PhD in public health. APF is a philosopher of public health with experience in digital mental health promotion. LFE is a psychiatrist with extensive experience in mental health services. The authors range in age from their late 30 s to early 60s.

The reporting of this study was informed by the Reflexive Thematic Analysis Reporting Guidelines (RTARG)41.

Headspace Denmark

headspace Denmark was launched in 2013, as a mental health promotive and preventive initiative, and comprises 38 local headspace centers, offering free counselling to young people aged 12–25. Each centre is staffed with a manager, two paid counsellors, volunteer counsellors, and part-time professionals (15 h/week) from local mental health or social services. Centres are located near public transport and designed to feel welcoming and youth-friendly14,15,19. The opening hours are 12–18 (noon–6 PM), three days a week. Counselling is anonymous; — no name or ID is required. The young person can have as many conversations with counsellors as they request. The counsellors work in pairs, meaning that the young person is offered to have two counsellors present at the conversations. If further help is needed (e.g., mental health care, social services), the counsellors assist the young person in accessing the right support. This can be done by counsellors or part-time professionals facilitating referrals to external services with the young person’s consent, ensuring they understand what to expect, and offering accompaniment if helpful. Young people may continue to attend headspace alongside specialist services14,22.

Recruitment

The sample size was guided by the concept of information power42. Given a study aim of moderate scope and a heterogeneous group in terms of age, background, and mental health concerns, a modest sample size was considered appropriate. In-depth, dialogue-oriented interviews were expected to yield rich and relevant data42,43; thus, approximately ten participants were deemed sufficient. Initially, recruitment targeted young people shortly after their first counselling session, with a follow-up interview planned four months later. Following consultation with counsellors, eligibility criteria were broadened to include participants who had completed at least one session, with no upper limit on attendance. Study information was developed with counsellor input to ensure age-appropriate communication44,45 and was shared by counsellors and displayed in waiting areas to allow self-referral. With consent, contact details were forwarded to the researcher. Written informed consent was obtained from participants aged 15 years and older; for those aged 12–15 years, parental or caregiver consent was additionally required.

Data generation

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore participants’ experiences of counselling at headspace Denmark40. This method was suitable for generating rich and nuanced data on mental health and service use while allowing flexibility and sensitivity to participants’ emotional needs40,44,45. An interview guide (see Supplementary Material) was developed by the first author based on the research questions and discussed within the research group. It explored experiences of counselling characteristics, including headspace principles, volunteer-based support, and perceived mechanisms of change. Information on participants’ age, sex, number of sessions, and duration of contact with headspace was collected during the interviews. Interviews were conducted either in person at headspace centres or online, according to participant preference. Consistent with Kvale and Brinkmann’s view of qualitative interviews as dialogical and co-constructed40, interviews were conducted as conversational encounters using probes and follow-up questions. Participants were offered the option of having a trusted person present; two chose this option. All participants were invited to a follow-up interview one month later, of which four participated. Interviews began with a check-in to ensure comfort and voluntary participation, and participant well-being was assessed afterward. In total, 17 interviews were conducted, lasting 10 min to 1.5 h (mean 45 min; median 50 min). All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed.

Analysis

Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to identify shared themes, variations, and mechanisms of change in young people’s experiences of counselling at headspace. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-step approach46,47 and was chosen for its flexibility and its acknowledgement of the researcher’s active role in interpretation46–48. Transcripts were read repeatedly by STBB and MF to ensure familiarisation, after which initial codes were generated and organised into meaningful patterns. Familiarisation and initial coding were conducted independently, followed by collaborative discussions across transcripts to explore patterns, differences, and interpretations, with analysis progressing from semantic to more latent levels. Some codes were informed by the study questions, while others were derived inductively from the data. Codes were collated into potential themes, which were iteratively reviewed against the full dataset and then refined, named, and reported46,47. Theme refinement was led by STBB in close collaboration with MF, with APF and RMP consulted in later stages to reflect on coherence, boundaries, and integration. This iterative and reflexive process supported transparency, reflexivity, and depth of interpretation, in line with principles of reflexive thematic analysis41,49.

Ethics

The study complied with the study protocol, the Declaration of Helsinki50, and national and EU-data protection legislation51. Written informed consent was obtained, and participant anonymity was ensured through de-identified transcripts and pseudonyms. As the evaluation was non-biomedical and conducted within routine headspace services, ethical committee approval was not required under national legislation.(Committee Act § 14, stk. 2)52. Potential power imbalances and participant distress were carefully considered. Participation was voluntary, with safeguards to prevent coercion, and the interviewer remained attentive to participants’ wellbeing, including debriefing. Counsellors were informed and available on site during interviews conducted at headspace centre44,45.

Contacting headspace after coping alone

Struggling in solitude

Young people often approached headspace with a prior sense of being alone with their problems (Q1). They sought someone to talk to who was neither a friend nor a family member, but also not a psychologist or psychiatrist. Six participants described feelings of loneliness, or family difficulties (Q2). Six participants struggled with sadness, anxiety, or depression, while two felt inferiority and were worried about others’ opinions. School-related problems—such as bullying and social exclusion —were common, as were relationship difficulties, and other stressful life events, including parental illness, bereavement, or divorce (Q3). Six participants reported having experienced their personal boundaries being violated and had suppressed their distress for a long period (Q4). Generally, it was not single events that brought young people to headspace, but rather the accumulation of stressors over time.

Yearning for someone who understands

Participants wished to talk with someone sufficiently distanced from their usual social circle, without necessarily engaging with a healthcare professional. Two participants did not want to worry their parents (Q5), while two others feared that confiding in friends might lead to rejection (Q6)—motivating them to seek support from the headspace centre. Several expressed apprehensions about having to explain themselves and had felt uncertain about whether their problems were “serious enough” to justify contacting headspace. Three participants had previously seen a psychologist or psychiatrist and described a strong contrast between that experience and the counselling they received at headspace. They were used to healthcare professionals steering the conversation and focusing primarily on diagnosis, symptoms, and problems (Q7). In contrast, they experienced the counselling as tailored to the individual and as a space where they could talk about what was most important to them. For two participants, the counselling was chosen as a free and easily accessible alternative to a psychologist, whereas two other participants preferred counselling specifically because it felt different from traditional therapy (Q8).

Headspace is a safe place to share what can be difficult to share with others

The headspace centres were seen as safe spaces and regarded by young people as refuges where they could truly be themselves and as having a consistent place where they could express their feelings and receive support (Q9). A sense of safety was a prerequisite for discussing difficult topics. Participants experienced safety when they could set the pace and agenda for the conversations, and the way counsellors interacted with the young person during the conversation. Counsellors were perceived as approachable and non-judgemental, which allowed for conversations about taboo topics such as suicidal thoughts and self-harm. Participants reported generally being met with openness and curiosity, and one woman valued that the counsellors remained composed when confronted with challenging disclosures (Q10). Another participant appreciated that the counsellor always asked for consent before posing difficult questions, fostering a sense of respect and safety in the relationship (Q11).

Confidentiality reduces fear of stigma

Participants placed high value on confidentiality. One young woman feared that friends might spread rumours and worried that a mental health diagnosis could restrict future opportunities, making her cautious about sharing her thoughts (Q12). For her and others, confidentiality was a prerequisite for feeling safe enough to open up, and it was essential that counsellors upheld professional secrecy and provided a space where anything could be shared. Participants were informed that confidentiality could be breached in cases of acute concern, such as suicidal thoughts or physical harm, and generally trusted counsellors’ judgement in these situations. However, one participant worried that mandatory reporting to social services might prevent some young people from disclosing serious issues (Q13). It was crucial that counsellors did not contact parents or others without permission. Two participants described previous experiences where healthcare professionals or family members contacted their counsellors without consent, which were experienced as serious breaches of trust (Q14).

Alliance built on strong interpersonal relationships

Counsellors were perceived as highly attuned to the young person, and the quality of the personal connection was regarded as crucial for sustained engagement (Q15). Two participants stated that they would be unlikely to continue at headspace without contact with a specific counsellor (Q16). Others had experienced changes in counsellors, which were described as challenging, though they were generally satisfied with the new counsellors. Participants valued counsellors’ ability to read cues, adapt conversations, remember previous details, and follow the young person’s development over time, providing stability and reassurance.

Views differed regarding the importance of professional knowledge. While three participants valued some level of expertise, for example related to anxiety (Q17), counselling was appreciated for not being overly clinical. No participant identified volunteer status or professional qualifications as most important; instead, interpersonal skills were emphasised. When describing a good counsellor, participants highlighted empathy, patience, communicative ability, intelligence, and the capacity to listen and be present. Five participants met with a single counsellor, while others met with two, which was viewed positively as it enabled different perspectives and continuity, provided communication was clear. Most participants were unaware of whether counsellors were volunteers or paid staff, and this was not significant to them. Overall, young people valued counselling that was egalitarian, non-clinical, and grounded in empathetic human connection, while allowing autonomy in shaping conversations (Q18).

A caring environment

Young people’s experience of headspace as a safe space was closely linked to the care and support, they perceived from counsellors. The centres were described as homely, with counselling likened to “talking with a friend” or, in some cases, to a parental role characterised by warmth and genuine interest (Q19). Elements such as cushions, blankets, warm colours, plants, and the offer of hot cocoa contributed to a welcoming atmosphere. Small gestures, including counsellors remembering names, were described as important for continued engagement. One participant highlighted receiving a hug as conveying a sense of familial warmth and feeling that she genuinely mattered to the counsellor (Q20).

Only limited critique was expressed. Two participants living in rural areas identified opening hours and transport as challenges, with family support playing a key role in overcoming these barriers. One participant suggested extending opening hours to weekends, and five participants proposed peer-to-peer activities to strengthen social connections.

Participants described the conversations as a source of hope during difficult periods and emphasised the importance of headspace as a safety net. One participant explained that counselling had been crucial during exams and parental illness and had helped her avoid self-harm (Q21).

Support that makes it easier to open up

The counselling enabled participants to release pent-up emotions, with one young woman describing headspace as a place where she could show her true self and cry if needed. Anonymity, flexible structures, and the absence of a fixed number of sessions allowed young people to engage at their own pace, and conversations conducted on the young person’s terms were particularly valued (Q22). Although participants initially found it difficult to express their feelings, the sessions helped them better understand themselves and consider new perspectives. Several described how gaining language for their emotions prevented them from struggling alone and stopped problems from growing.

Counsellors supported reflection on issues such as jealousy, relationships, guilt, self-blame, and boundary-setting, and all participants expressed a desire to gain new perspectives (Q23). This was facilitated through questions that challenged assumptions, and changes in counsellors sometimes contributed fresh viewpoints. While practical advice was secondary to reflection, participants valued having it available when needed, appreciating suggestions offered without being directive or intrusive (Q24).

Changes in well-being and everyday life

Connecting with others

Engaging in conversations within a safe and supportive environment was described as facilitating subsequent openness with close others, which participants identified as a key outcome of counselling. One participant explained that after discussing intimate partner violence with her counsellor, she felt able to share this with her friends (Q25). A young man used the conversations with the counsellors, as a starting point to tell his mother that he had been struggling with depression for an extended period, which he had not previously disclosed to her (Q26). These examples illustrate a shared experience among the participants, in that conversations in the headspace reduced their sense of loneliness. For a young girl, it was about gaining new friends and interests and finding a place to belong. For other participants, it was a matter of being able to share thoughts and feelings, alleviating the feeling of being alone with one’s problem.

Conversations in the headspace supported participants` social engagement outside the centers. One participant articulated that counselling at headspace encouraged her to push personal boundaries (Q27) A common theme reported by the participants was that they became more outgoing and better equipped to manage social situations in their daily lives, such as attending school or discussing personal problems with others. Improved coping and renewed motivation for openness made it easier for them to reach out to their social network. A young woman described feeling more confident and motivated to speak her mind and engage in social encounters at work, which had been a challenge to her, before the conversations in headspace (Q28).

Feeling grounded in daily life

Participants described carrying their problems alone using vivid metaphors, such as thoughts “boiling over” or feeling like a balloon about to burst, reflecting overwhelming psychological pressure. Talking with counsellors allowed young people to express their thoughts before they escalated into larger problems. In general participants reported feeling relieved after conversations, gaining energy and resilience in daily life even when issues were not fully resolved (Q29). For four participants, having someone to talk to was more important than solving the problem itself, likened to letting air out of a balloon rather than patching it up. A prominent theme was increased security and self-confidence (Q30). Participants worried less about others’ opinions, and some described participating in the interview itself as an example of their growing confidence. Feeling more secure enabled them to make difficult decisions, and small changes were described as leading to larger transformations over time. While challenges such as low self-esteem or past trauma often remained, they occupied less space, allowing room for positive experiences and new friendships (Q31). Overall, participants reported a healthier relationship with themselves, marked by greater acceptance of their feelings and increased self-compassion, describing themselves as “good enough” (Q32).

Discussion

This study explored young people’s experiences of accessing counselling at headspace Denmark. Overall, headspace reached young people experiencing mental health challenges who had often managed their difficulties in isolation. headspace was perceived as a safe and caring space where young people could share thoughts and feelings and become more grounded in daily life. The quality of the relationship with counsellors, the non-clinical environment, and feeling understood and welcome were central to engagement and perceived change, highlighting the importance of relational factors and a health-promoting approach. Relational support has been identified as a key mechanism for positive outcomes in mental health care generally53,54 and in youth mental health specifically55,56. Across studies in various contexts, young people emphasise the importance of counsellors and therapists being responsive, present, respectful, and genuinely caring30,31,56–58, which aligns closely with participants’ descriptions of headspace counsellors as attentive, curious, and supportive. These findings suggest a strong need among young people to be seen and understood, and that listening, validation, and relational presence may be as important as specific therapeutic techniques. Consistent with meta-syntheses on peer support24,26,27, participants described counselling relationships characterised by acceptance, non-judgement, trust, and shared control, creating psychological safety and fostering agency, self-confidence, and self-acceptance. Participants emphasised that conversations were less about solving problems and more about feeling supported, grounded, and empowered, enabling better coping with ongoing challenges. A prominent theme was the value of accessing mental health support in a non-clinical environment. Formal clinical settings may feel intimidating and reinforce vulnerability or stigma59–61 and re-activate traumas62–64, whereas homely, non-formal environments reduce perceived power distance and encourage openness15,19,30,58, contributing to psychological safety19,30,31,59,64. Many participants mainly described immediate benefits of emotional expression rather than distinct long-term changes. This aligns with theories of emotional expression and regulation65,66, which highlight relief and improved well-being through being able to articulate feelings in supportive relationships65,67–70. Participants described unexpressed thoughts as overwhelming and experienced relief when able to speak openly, often emphasising that being heard mattered more than problem-solving. These findings suggest that emotional expression, supported by trusting and egalitarian relationships, is a key mechanism through which counselling enhances everyday well-being.

Comparison with the preliminary programme theory showed good alignment with several proposed mechanisms, including flexible, user-driven counselling. However, participants’ strong emphasis on relational connection, genuine care, and a safe environment suggests that interactional and relational processes should be explicitly recognised as mechanisms of change. Reported outcomes, such as increased confidence, connection with others, and improved grounding, align with proposed outcomes in the programme theory, including self-efficacy and well-being, both of which are associated with positive mental health in adolescence21–24.

Strengths and limitations

The principal strength of this study lies in its novelty, as it is the first to examine young people’s experiences of counselling at headspace centres in Denmark. A further strength is the reflexive and transparent analytic approach, ensuring coherence between the study’s aims, theoretical positioning, and use of reflexive thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clarke46,47,49. Trustworthiness was supported through sustained data engagement, reflexive awareness of the researchers’ interpretive role, and the development of coherent themes grounded in rich, contextualised excerpts41,48. Dependability was addressed through transparent reporting of recruitment, data collection, and analytic procedures, while confirmability was supported by grounding interpretations clearly in the data. The sampling strategy also strengthened the study by facilitating trust and rapport, which may have been particularly important given the sensitive nature of the topic and likely enabled participation from young people who might otherwise have been reluctant to engage in research44,45. Credibility was supported through close data engagement in all steps of the analysis, close collaboration between the members of the research team during coding, and data-grounded interpretation71.

The main limitation concerns transferability72, as participants could not be included at the outset of their counselling trajectories. Notably, young people attending one to three sessions accounted for 46% of all contacts with headspace in 2023, and including this group may have yielded additional insights into counselling experiences and potential mechanisms of change. Another limitation of this study is the absence of data on participants’ ethnicity and sexuality, which limits the scope for analysing issues related to culturally safe care and inequity in mental healthcare. Furthermore, the sample size was relatively small, which may affect the representativeness of the findings in the context of Denmark more broadly.

Implications and directions for future research

The findings point to key mechanisms of impact related to the fact that headspace is a service rooted in civil society and may have unique conditions for reaching young people with mental health problems. The study’s implications are to continue the counselling in its current form. Young people expressed recommendations for making headspace more accessible in terms of opening hours - for example, by being open on weekends - and for offering opportunities for young people to meet each other. Future research should explore how this approach can support help-seeking behaviour among young people, especially those in known risk groups who are particularly vulnerable to mental health problems. As noted, young people with shorter counselling courses (1–3 sessions) did not participate in this study. Research should investigate this group’s experience of the counselling sessions in headspace, as there may be distinct mechanisms unique for this group.

Conclusion

This study explores how young people experience mental health support delivered mainly by volunteer counsellors at headspace centres in Denmark. Young people described headspace as a safe place to share difficult concerns, supported by a warm, welcoming environment and counsellors’ engaging approach. The combination of anonymity, confidentiality, and flexible, youth-centred counselling made the service especially appealing. Positioned within civil society and characterized by supportive relationships and informal settings, headspace has strong potential to promote youth mental health. These findings complement effectiveness evaluations by clarifying key mechanisms underlying outcomes. Future research should examine experiences among those attending only one to three sessions, as this group represents a large proportion of users but was not included here.
