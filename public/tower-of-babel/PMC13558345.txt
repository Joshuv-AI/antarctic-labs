Investigating the Impact of Calcium Channel Blockers on the Pharmacokinetics of Lapatinib: Possible Role of Cytochrome P450 Enzymes and P-glycoprotein Efflux Transporters
Authors: Mrunal Pradeep Desai, Prajakta Harish Patil, Anithakumari Uttam Singh Rajpurohit, Vullendula Sai Krishna Anand, Jagadish Puralae Channabasavaiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveLapatinib, an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor used to treat breast cancer, is associated with cardiotoxicity. Therefore, cardioprotective agents, including calcium channel blockers, are often co-prescribed, creating a potential for pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions that need to be evaluated.MethodsThe current study assessed the effects of calcium channel blockers on P-glycoprotein-mediated efflux, CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450-3A4)-regulated metabolism, and the overall pharmacokinetics of lapatinib using an ex vivo everted gut sac model, in vitro metabolic stability assays, and in vivo pharmacokinetic interaction studies in rats.ResultsCalcium channel blockers such as verapamil and nicardipine increased the apparent permeability of lapatinib 1.92- and 1.68-fold, respectively. Furthermore, lapatinib’s metabolic clearance in human liver microsomes was decreased by 15.78- and 9.47-fold in the presence of nicardipine and diltiazem, respectively, at a concentration of 100 µM. In vivo, the Cmax (maximum plasma concentration) of lapatinib was increased by 29.30% on pretreatment with diltiazem (25 mg/kg) and 36.76% on pretreatment with nicardipine (4 mg/kg). However, verapamil (20 mg/kg) reduced its Cmax by 30.44%.ConclusionPretreatment with diltiazem and nicardipine increased lapatinib exposure, whereas pretreatment with verapamil reduced it. These findings from preclinical models suggest the potential for drug-drug interactions between lapatinib and calcium channel blockers, warranting further clinical investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13318-026-01015-z.

Key Points

This study evaluates the pharmacokinetic interaction between lapatinib and commonly co-administered calcium channel blockers using preclinical modelsPretreatment with calcium channel blockers was associated with altered pharmacokinetic parameters of lapatinib, suggesting a potential drug-drug interactionThese findings highlight the need for further clinical studies to determine the relevance of these interactions and support the development of safe therapeutic strategies

Introduction

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the global burden of breast cancer is expected to increase substantially between 2020 and 2040, with incidence projected to rise by approximately 40% and annual mortality exceeding 1 million deaths by 2040 [1]. In this scenario, the development of targeted therapeutic agents has revolutionized treatment regimens by improving specificity and survival outcomes in breast malignancies. Lapatinib, a targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has marked a significant breakthrough in the treatment of human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER)-2-positive metastatic breast cancer. Ye et al. reported a study in which breast cancer patients treated with lapatinib had a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 8.1 months (p = 0.005) compared with patients treated with other therapeutic regimens, who had a median PFS of < 5 months [2].

Despite these therapeutic benefits, around 95% of targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including lapatinib, are known to exert off-target effects. The anticancer efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors is primarily attributed to their ability to target signalling pathways involved in tumour growth and survival, including the PI3K/AKT and mTOR pathways. However, unintended inhibition of pro-survival kinases in normal cardiomyocytes may disrupt cardiac function and contribute to the development of cardiomyopathy [3]. Oxidative stress induction, mitochondrial dysfunction, and induction of apoptosis are also contributing factors to the occurrence of cardiac events or cardiotoxicity during tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy [4]. A sub-analysis of the ALTTO clinical trial of lapatinib plus trastuzumab therapy, reported by Eiger et al., concluded that cardiac events were observed in approximately 10% of the recruited population (n = 4190). Patients' symptoms included congestive heart failure, a low left ventricular ejection fraction, and cases of arrhythmia [5]. Consequently, prolonged lapatinib therapy has been linked to cardiotoxicity, making it one of the most critical adverse effects of this targeted therapy.

Therefore, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) have provided recommendations for cardiac monitoring and intervention [6]. These guidelines recommend prescribing angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and calcium channel blockers as first- and second-line therapy to treat cardiac symptoms in patients receiving targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy. Lapatinib is primarily metabolized by the CYP3A4 enzyme and is reported to be the substrate of P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and breast cancer-resistant protein (BCRP) efflux transporters [7, 8]. Hence, concurrent use of CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers, or P-gp inhibitors or inducers, can alter the plasma concentrations of lapatinib [9–11]. Additionally, the co-prescription of cardioprotective agents leads to to polypharmacy, which can potentially result in pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions with ACE-II inhibitors and calcium channel blockers. Therefore, this is a crucial factor to consider in patients on lapatinib therapy, especially in patients with comorbid cardiac conditions.

A clinical study reported by Smith et al. in healthy human subjects reported that a single dose of ketoconazole (CYP3A4 inhibitor) resulted in a 3.6-fold increase in lapatinib exposure (AUC0–24), whereas administration of carbamazepine (CYP inducer) decreased lapatinib exposure (AUC0–24) by 72%, highlighting the effect of co-administration of CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers on its pharmacokinetics [12]. Among the co-prescribed medications for cardiotoxicity management, calcium channel blockers, especially diltiazem and verapamil, are known to inhibit CYP450 enzymes, particularly CYP3A4 [13–17]. A study by Methaneethorn et al. reported that concomitant administration of verapamil with simvastatin increased its AUC0–24 four-fold, along with a five-fold enhancement in Cmax in healthy human subjects [18]. Also, nicardipine and verapamil have been reported to interact with several P-gp substrates, including digoxin, simvastatin, and oral therapeutic agents [19].

This underscores the potential for pharmacokinetic interactions in a polypharmacy setting. Therefore, the study aimed to investigate the pharmacokinetic interactions between lapatinib and calcium channel blockers mediated by CYP450 enzymes and P-gp using in vitro and ex vivo studies, followed by an investigation of their impact on the oral pharmacokinetics of lapatinib in rats.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Lapatinib ditosylate monohydrate (98%) was kindly gifted by MSN Laboratories Private, Ltd., Hyderabad, India. Nicardipine (97%), nifedipine (98%), and diltiazem hydrochloride (98%) were obtained from Hetero, India, as gift samples. Amlodipine (95%) was obtained as a gift sample from a central drug testing laboratory in Mumbai. Alectinib hydrochloride (98%) was obtained as gift sample from MSN Laboratories Private, Ltd., Hyderabad, India. Verapamil hydrochloride (98%) was purchased from Sigma, Germany. β-Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate reduced form (NADPH) was purchased from SRL, India. Pooled rat liver microsomes (RLMs) were purchased from Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Inc. Pooled human liver microsomes (HLMs) of CYP450 from 50 donors were purchased from Corning® UltraPool™ HLM 150, from mixed Gender Lot No. 38297. All other reagents used were of chromatographic grade. Deionized water was purified using a Milli-Q system (Millipore Corp., Billerica, MA).

Quantitation of Lapatinib by RP-HPLC

Instrumentation and Chromatographic Conditions

The bioanalytical method was developed to estimate lapatinib in ex vivo, in vitro, and in vivo study samples using the Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system, equipped with LC-20 AT binary pumps, the SPD-20A UV/VIS dual wavelength detector, and the SIL-20AC HT autosampler. Phenomenex C18 EVO column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) was used as the stationary phase, while the mobile phase consisted of a mixture of 10 mM ammonium formate buffer (pH 6), acetonitrile, and methanol in a 60:30:10 ratio. The separation was achieved using isocratic elution with a 1 ml/min flow rate, at the wavelengths of 272 and 254 nm.

Sample Preparation and Analytical Method Validation

To prepare the calibration standards, 190 μl of rat plasma was added to previously labelled micro-centrifuge tubes; 10 μl of a corresponding aqueous working stock solution containing lapatinib was spiked into it and further vortexed for 10 s. Lapatinib was extracted from rat plasma by the protein precipitation method using chilled acetonitrile:methanol in a ratio of 50:50% v/v as a quenching solvent which contained an internal standard, alectinib (2 μg/ml) [20]. The samples were vortexed and then centrifuged for 10 min at 4500 rpm using a cooling centrifuge, and the supernatant was analysed at optimised chromatographic conditions. The developed analytical method was validated in accordance with the Bioanalytical Method Validation Guideline issued by the US Food and Drug Administration (2018) for system suitability, sensitivity, linearity, precision, accuracy, extraction recovery, and stability [21]. The detailed methodology for analytical method validation is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Quantitation of lapatinib by RP-HPLC

Under optimized chromatographic conditions, the retention time of lapatinib was 11.8 min, and the retention time of alectinib (internal standard) was 8.8 min. Both compounds exhibited acceptable separation with a resolution of 4 and had good peak shapes with tailing factors of ≤ 2. Figure 1 shows a representative chromatogram of lapatinib with the internal standard alectinib. The method was found to be sensitive with %CV (coefficient of variation) of 3.7% for six repeated injections of lapatinib MQC (middle quallity control) with an LLOQ (lower limit of quantification) of 71.66 ng/ml. The calibration curve was linear over the range of 183,45.73–71.66 ng/ml, with a r2 value of 0.999. The calculated standard deviation (SD), %CV, and % nominal concentrations for all QC levels were within acceptable limits, demonstrating that the developed method was precise and accurate (Table S1). The method demonstrated sensitivity with mean recovery percentages of 96.0, 99.2, and 96.8% for LQC (lower quality control), MQC, and HQC (high quality control) of lapatinib, respectively, in the extracted samples from the rat plasma. The mean percent change for the stability study samples of lapatinib was also within acceptable limits (Table S2).

Effect of Calcium Channel Blockers on Apparent Permeability of Lapatinib

Approval of the study was obtained from the institutional animal ethics committee (IAEC), Manipal Academy of Higher Education in Karnataka, India (reference no. IAEC/KMC/93/2021). The study was carried out as per the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA) and the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All methods followed to carry out the experiments on rodents in this research were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations (IAEC and CPCSEA). All the experiments performed on rodents in this study are also in accordance with the ARRIVE (animal research and reporting of in vivo experiments) guidelines. Based on their well-characterized physiology, stable enzyme expression, and consistent reproducibility across the reported studies, female Wistar rats were selected for the ex vivo and in vivo experiments.

To assess the impact of calcium channel blockers on the apparent permeability of lapatinib, an ex vivo study was conducted using everted rat ileum sacs. Female Wistar rats weighing 220 ± 30 g were procured from the central animal house facility and housed at a controlled room temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (65–70%), 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Following the method described by Patil et al., a similar procedure was applied in this experiment to assess the effect of calcium channel blockers on the apparent permeability of lapatinib [22]. Equilibrated test solutions of lapatinib (200 µM) in the presence and absence of calcium channel blockers (nicardipine, amlodipine, diltiazem, verapamil, nifedipine) at a concentration of 200 µM in pH 7.4 Ringer’s lactate buffer were introduced into the donor compartment. The mucosal side of the sac was filled with blank Ringer’s lactate buffer, and individual transport of lapatinib from the mucosal to the serosal side was evaluated by collecting samples from the serosal side (i.e., the acceptor compartment) under sink conditions (n = 2) at time points of 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, and 90 min. The collected samples were then centrifuged at 4500 rpm, and the supernatant was analysed using the developed and validated RP-HPLC method to determine the concentration of lapatinib transported. In Vitro Microsomal Stability of Lapatinib

The use of liver microsomal enzymes derived from various species has widespread application in in vitro preclinical drug-drug interaction studies. Hence, the inhibitory effect of calcium channel blockers, along with the positive control, ketoconazole (10 µM), on the phase I metabolic stability of lapatinib was assessed in RLMs and HLMs. The total of 100 µL incubate comprising phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4), RLMs (1.0 mg protein/ml), NADPH (1 mM), and lapatinib (5 µM) in the absence and presence of nicardipine, amlodipine, diltiazem, verapamil and nifedipine spiked at the concentrations of 100, 30, 10, 3, and 1 µM (final DMSO concentration < 0.2%). After pre-incubating RLMs for 10 min at 37 °C, the reaction was initiated by adding 30 µL of 3.3 mM NADPH regenerating system. At 0, 5, 15, 30, and 60 min, all reaction processes were terminated by adding 400 µL of cold acetonitrile: methanol (50:50% v/v) containing alectinib as internal standard. The samples were then centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min using a cold centrifuge, and the supernatant was analysed using a developed and validated RP-HPLC method [23].

To confirm the effect of calcium channel blockers on CYP3A4-mediated lapatinib metabolism in RLMs, a microsomal stability assay was further performed in HLMs under similar experimental conditions and sample-processing approaches using nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil. Lapatinib (5 µM) was co-incubated with the positive control ketoconazole (10 µM), and calcium channel blockers (nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil) at concentrations of 100, 30, 10, 3, and 1 µM (final DMSO concentration < 0.2%). The reaction was initiated by adding 30 µL of 3.33 mM NADPH and was terminated at time points of 0, 5, 15, 30, and 60 min. The resulting sample supernatants were analysed using a validated RP-HPLC method [24].

In Vivo Pharmacokinetic Drug-drug Interaction Studies for Lapatinib

Animals

The experimental protocol for animal studies was approved by the IAEC of Manipal Academy of Higher Education, Manipal, India (ref. no. IAEC/KMC/93/2021). To investigate the effects of selected calcium channel blockers (i.e., nicardipine, diltiazem and verapamil) on the pharmacokinetics of lapatinib, female Wistar rats weighing 200–250 g were obtained from the central animal research facility in Manipal and were housed for 1 week prior to the initiation of the experiment at controlled room temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (65–70%), 12 h light /12 h dark cycle, with free access to food and water.

Pharmacokinetic Experiment Design

The rodents were first randomly divided into seven groups (n = 3). The animals were fasted for 12 h prior to dosing, and food was withheld for an additional 2 h post-dosing. Control group 1 was pretreated with the vehicle (0.5% Tween 80 with 0.5% w/v methyl cellulose in Milli-Q water) for 7 days; on the 8th day, lapatinib (25 mg/kg) was dosed as a suspension. The other test groups were pretreated with calcium channel blockers, as specified in Table 1, for 7 days and 30 min before the administration of lapatinib on the 8th day, as oral suspensions, to examine the effect of their pretreatment on the pharmacokinetic profile of lapatinib. The blood samples were withdrawn through the retro-orbital plexus of animals at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 15 min in a cold centrifuge to collect the plasma samples, which were stored at − 80 °C until analysis using a validated RP-HPLC method [22].

Data Analysis

The pharmacokinetic parameters were assessed using the non-compartmental analysis tool of Phoenix® Win-Nonlin software (version 8.0). Statistical analysis was carried out using Graph-Pad Prism 10.0.1 (San Diego, CA). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for comparisons. All mean values are presented with the mean ± SD.

Results

Effect of Calcium Channel Blockers on Apparent Permeability of Lapatinib

Lapatinib, when incubated at a concentration of 200 µM in the donor compartment, exhibited an apparent permeability of 0.1239 × 10-6 cm/s from the mucosal to the serosal side, identical to its reported permeability in Caco-2 cell lines [25]. When co-incubated with verapamil and nicardipine at a concentration of 200 µM, the concentration of lapatinib transferred from the mucosal to the serosal side after 60 min of co-incubation had increased 4.87- and 3.23-fold, respectively (Fig. 2). Amongst the calcium channel blockers co-incubated, verapamil exhibited the most pronounced effect on lapatinib transport by increasing its apparent permeability 1.92-fold. Co-incubation with nicardipine also increased lapatinib permeability 1.68-fold, thereby reducing its P-gp-mediated efflux and enhancing its concentration on the serosal side.

In Vitro Microsomal Stability of Lapatinib

A metabolic stability assay was conducted in RLMs and HLMs to assess the microsomal stability of lapatinib when co-incubated with various concentrations of calcium channel blockers and a positive control, ketoconazole. After 60 min of co-incubation in RLMs, lapatinib was metabolized up to 35% with an intrinsic clearance of 8.9 ml/min/mg of protein. When co-incubated in the presence of ketoconazole at a concentration of 10 µM, the intrinsic clearance decreased 4.05-fold. In the co-incubates of inhibitors (i.e., calcium channel blockers with lapatinib), the in vitro intrinsic clearance decreased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3). Nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil were observed to be the most potent inhibitors, which reduced the intrinsic clearance of lapatinib by 22.25-, 8.09-, and 6.85-fold, respectively, at a concentration 100 µM (Table 2). Inhibition of the CYP3A4-mediated metabolism of lapatinib by nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil resulted in decreased intrinsic clearance even at their lowest concentrations in RLM co-incubates. Hence, they were further chosen for the study of metabolic stability in HLMs.

In Vivo Pharmacokinetic Drug-drug Interaction Studies for Lapatinib

Based on the observed effects of nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil on CYP450-mediated metabolism and P-gp-mediated efflux of lapatinib, these calcium channel blockers were further selected for in vivo drug-drug interaction studies to evaluate their impact on the pharmacokinetic profile of lapatinib. The mean plasma concentration vs time profiles obtained for lapatinib in the control and groups pretreated with different doses of diltiazem, nicardipine, and verapamil are shown in Fig. 5. Based on the results, pretreatment with verapamil reduced the systemic exposure of lapatinib (Cmax ratio ≈ 0.85 and 0.70; AUC ratio ≈ 0.96 and 0.68 for 4 and 20 mg/kg, respectively). In contrast, diltiazem pretreatment showed a slight to moderate increase in Cmax (ratios ≈ 1.12 and 1.29) with minimal change in AUC (≈ 1.02 and 1.00). Nicardipine pretreatment produced a modest increase in lapatinib exposure, with Cmax ratios of approximately 1.01 and 1.37 and AUC ratios of approximately 1.05 and 1.15 for 2 and 4 mg/kg, respectively. The Cmax and Tmax of lapatinib were observed to be 779.3 ± 296.79 ng/ml and 2.0 ± 1.73 h when administered at a dose of 25 mg/kg, with in vivo clearance of 760.53 ± 114.1 ml/h/kg. In the groups pretreated with higher doses of nicardipine (4 mg/kg), the Cmax of lapatinib increased by 36.76% with a 15.29% increase in AUC0–24 and a decrease in in vivo clearance. However, when the animals were pretreated with diltiazem (25 mg/kg), the Cmax of lapatinib increased by 29.30%. In contrast to diltiazem and nicardipine, in groups pretreated with verapamil at the higher dose of 20 mg/kg, the Cmax decreased by 30.44% with a 46.91% increase in in vivo clearance. The AUC0–24 for lapatinib also decreased by 31.73% in the groups pretreated with higher doses of verapamil. In the groups pretreated with 20 mg/kg of verapamil, the Tmax of lapatinib was increased 2.66-fold. However, the lower doses of calcium channel blockers did not significantly affect the pharmacokinetic parameters of lapatinib, whereas the higher doses affected its AUC0–24, Tmax, and Cmax (Table 3).

Discussion

The increasing prevalence of tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy-related drug-drug interactions is primarily linked to polypharmacy in cancer patients. However, the research on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic drug-drug interactions between TKIs and co-prescribed cardiovascular drugs remains limited [19, 26]. Waters et al. identified CYP450-mediated interactions involving TKIs and co-administered drugs, in which 47% were substrates, 22% inhibitors, and 11% inducers [27]. Lapatinib, a CYP3A4 and P-gp substrate, has been studied regarding its interaction profile with probe inhibitors, such as ketoconazole for CYP3A4 and elacridar for P-gp, according to the USFDA guidelines. However, given lapatinib’s cardiotoxicity and the prescription of drugs such as calcium channel blockers for its management, it is essential to investigate the effects of these agents on its pharmacokinetic profile.

Calcium channel blockers are not only potent inhibitors of P-gp but are also reported to inhibit various isoforms of CYP450 enzymes, including CYP3A4 [28, 29]. In the current study, nicardipine demonstrated its inhibitory effect on P-gp by enhancing the mucosal-to-serosal transfer of lapatinib 2.88-fold at the end of 90-min co-incubation in the ex vivo everted gut sac compartment. Furthermore, in the metabolic stability assay, at a concentration of 100 µM, nicardipine decreased the in vitro clearance of lapatinib severalfold in RLM co-incubates, resulting in a 20.36-fold increase in lapatinib's metabolic half-life. With reported IC50 (half maximal inhibitory concentration) values of 1.3 µM for CYP3A4 and 17.5 µM for P-gp, nicardipine is widely reported to interact with orally administered targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy, such as almonertinib, to increase its AUC0–24 [30]. In line with these observations, in the in vivo studies, in the groups pretreated with higher doses of nicardipine (4 mg/kg), the Cmax of lapatinib increased by 0.73-fold with a 16.0% increase in AUC0–24. This highlighted the effect of nicardipine on lapatinib pharmacokinetics [15, 31].

While nicardipine was observed to affect the pharmacokinetic profile of lapatinib, co-incubation with diltiazem also elevated the concentrations of lapatinib transported from the mucosal to serosal side by 1.22-fold in ex vivo studies. In vitro, the intrinsic clearance of lapatinib decreased 8.09-fold with an 8.10-fold increase in its half-life in RLM co-incubates at 100 µM diltiazem concentration. Pretreatment with diltiazem at a dose of 25 mg/kg in animals enhanced the Cmax of lapatinib by 29.30%, possibly because of reduced metabolism and prolonged systemic exposure. These findings align with the known inhibitory properties of diltiazem toward CYP3A4 and P-gp, with IC50 of 15 µM and 7.0 µM, respectively [15, 30, 31]. Along with orally administered TKIs, diltiazem has also been reported to interact with other therapeutic agents, including cardiovascular drugs such as statins, in an oncocardiology setting [8, 10, 32–36].

Furthermore, when verapamil was co-incubated with lapatinib in an ex vivo experiment, its transport from the mucosal to the serosal side was increased by 3.23-fold. In the in vitro experiments, verapamil inhibited the CYP-mediated metabolism of lapatinib and decreased its intrinsic clearance by 6.85-fold in RLM co-incubates at 100 µM concentration, with a 7.53-fold increase in its metabolic half-life. Conversely, the in vivo study observations revealed a reduction in Cmax with Tmax of 5.33 ± 2.3 h, indicating a delayed absorption profile. Pretreatment with verapamil also increased in vivo clearance of lapatinib by 1.47-fold with a 1.46-fold decrease in AUC0–24. Although verapamil is reported to have an IC50 of 8.07 µM for P-gp and 4 µM for CYP3A4, another study by Lemma et al. additionally reports that it has a dual effect on P-gp. The study reports that, while it is administered in the short term, it acts as an inhibitor, whereas prolonged administration induces P-gp, leading to decreased bioavailability of co-administered substrates such as lapatinib [37]. Pedersen et al. also reported that, initially, co-administration of verapamil increased the concentration of P-gp substrate digoxin by 60%, which was further decreased to 30% post-prolonged administration [38]. This characteristic of verapamil could explain the discrepancies observed between in vitro and in vivo results. In this study, the pronounced inhibition of P-gp is observed in the presence of nicardipine, diltiazem, and verapamil, which highlights their significant role in modulating the P-gp-mediated efflux of lapatinib [7]. The dual inhibitory property of calcium channel blockers was further highlighted in a metabolic stability assay in RLMs and HLMs, which demonstrated their potential for concentration-dependent inhibition of CYP3A4. The metabolic stability assays conducted in HLMs yielded results for verapamil, nicardipine, and diltiazem consistent with those observed in RLMs, further validating the influence of calcium channel blockers on lapatinib metabolism (Table 4).

The findings of the present study indicate a potential risk of drug-drug interaction between lapatinib and calcium channel blockers. The concentrations selected for the study were based on reported human steady-state Cmax values for nicardipine, verapamil, and diltiazem (280–550 ng/ml), corresponding to approximately 1.21–10.05 µM. The lower concentration range (1–10 µM) aligned with clinically relevant systemic exposures, while higher concentrations were included to account for potential elevated exposure scenarios. However, for the in vivo pharmacokinetic studies, doses were derived from marketed human dose strengths and adjusted for purity and salt factors, thereby maintaining relevance to therapeutic human exposure. This study design enabled the rational evaluation of the pharmacokinetic interaction between lapatinib and calcium channel blockers. While these in vitro results offer valuable mechanistic insight, caution is warranted when translating them to clinical relevance.

Lapatinib is now being prescribed as an established medication in an outpatient setting for the treatment of HER-2-positive breast cancer. Due to the large inter-patient variability, any potential drug interaction could have serious consequences for a patient’s therapy and PFS. The findings from this study underscore the pharmacokinetic interactions between lapatinib and calcium channel blockers, primarily mediated by the inhibition/induction of P-gp and CYP3A4. A key limitation of the present study is that, although in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo preclinical models provide valuable mechanistic insights, they may not fully capture the complexity and variability of drug-drug interactions in humans due to physiological heterogeneity. Therefore, the translational relevance of these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed through well-designed clinical investigations. Given the widespread use of calcium channel blockers to manage cardiovascular conditions induced by TKIs such as lapatinib in clinical practice, these findings are of critical significance. However, further studies with physiologically relevant drug concentrations are essential to bridge the in vitro-in vivo gap and evaluate the translational potential of these interactions.

Conclusion

The findings of the current preclinical study demonstrated a potential risk of drug-drug interaction of lapatinib with calcium channel blockers, highlighting the underlying mechanisms, particularly the role of CYP450 enzymes and P-gp inhibition in the interaction. It was observed that while pretreatment with nicardipine and diltiazem increased the Cmax of lapatinib in rats, verapamil presented a distinct interaction pattern, with increased clearance and reduced Cmax. The dual inhibitory/inducing effects of calcium channel blockers on P-gp and CYP enzymes underscore the need for a more detailed clinical study to inform personalized therapy and close monitoring in polypharmacy settings, particularly in cancer patients, who often require complex medication regimens. The study also underscores the importance of thoroughly assessing drug-drug interactions to minimize the risks of compromised therapeutic efficacy or potential toxicity in tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy.
