Possible explanation of Höhler’s clustering: effective partial-wave mixing induced by truncation Journal: Physics Letters B
Alfred Švarc

Abstract
Höhler noted that resonance poles obtained from different partial waves in π ​ N \pi N scattering appear to bunch together near a small set of common complex energies, and suggested that this could indicate mixing between angular momenta. Here, we examine whether at least part of this pattern could arise effectively from the extraction procedure itself. Exact partial-wave unitarity preserves the separation of angular momenta in the infinite problem, whereas practical pole extraction from bilinear observables requires truncation of the partial-wave series. Combined with the truncation-induced mixing mechanism established in Ref. [ 5 ] , this provides a natural source by which fitted partial-wave coefficients can inherit overlapping pole-bearing content, thereby offering a plausible contribution to Höhler-type clustering.



1
Introduction

The extraction of baryon resonances from hadronic and electromagnetic reactions is usually formulated in terms of partial-wave amplitudes and their analytic continuation into the complex energy plane. In this framework, resonances are identified with poles of partial-wave scattering amplitudes, and the assignment of angular momentum quantum numbers is tied directly to the partial-wave decomposition. It is therefore striking that, in his classic analysis of
\pi N
scattering, Höhler pointed out a pronounced near-degeneracy of complex pole positions belonging to different partial waves and raised the possibility that the observed spectrum might be describable in terms of only a few complex poles of the invariant amplitudes rather than many independent poles in separate partial waves
[
1
]
. This observation, which we shall refer to as Höhler’s clustering, remains conceptually intriguing.

The problem is nontrivial because partial waves are normally expected to carry definite angular momentum, so the appearance of closely grouped poles across different waves raises the question of whether the clustering is a genuine dynamical property of the exact amplitude or whether it is generated by the way poles are extracted in practice. The phenomenon was explicitly noted by Höhler himself
[
1
]
. To our knowledge, the clearest subsequent explicit discussion was given by Kirchbach, who interpreted the so-called Höhler clusters in terms of Lorentz multiplets
[
2
]
, with a related continuation by Losanow and Kirchbach
[
3
]
. In the modern Particle Data Group compilations, however, the situation is less transparent than in the older Karlsruhe–Helsinki era. Present resonance listings combine information from several different analyses and channels, and only the better-established states are promoted to the summary tables. Moreover, the PDG explicitly emphasizes that resonance claims must be tested against the completeness of the fitted partial-wave set and against the effect of higher partial waves
[
4
]
. Thus the clustering problem remains conceptually relevant, but its visual identification in current listings is considerably less direct than in the older compilations. What appears to be missing in the literature is a systematic analysis of whether at least part of this clustering may arise effectively from truncation in the extraction of partial-wave coefficients from bilinear observables.

One possible interpretation is that the clustering reflects a genuine dynamical property of the exact amplitude. In the present paper we argue that this possibility is strongly constrained. As will be shown below, exact unitarity, together with the partial-wave decomposition, preserves the separation of angular momenta, so that true partial waves do not mix dynamically except in trivial cases. If so, then Höhler-type clustering should not be attributed to a genuine loss of angular-momentum identity in the exact amplitude.

This opens the door to an alternative explanation based on the discussion given in Ref.
[
5
]
. The key point is that the quantities fitted to data are not the amplitudes themselves, but observables that are bilinear in them. Once such bilinear observables are treated in a truncated form, the extraction of lower-order partial-wave coefficients becomes a coupled nonlinear problem. As a result, the fitted truncated coefficients need not correspond to pure exact partial waves; rather, they can inherit contributions from several underlying angular-momentum sectors. In this sense, the mixing is not a property of the exact theory, but an effective feature induced by truncation in practical calculations.

The purpose of this paper is to examine whether the truncation-induced mixing mechanism identified in Ref.
[
5
]
can contribute to a possible explanation of Höhler’s clustering. We first recall the clustering problem and the way it has been viewed in the literature. We then argue that genuine dynamical mixing of exact partial waves is strongly constrained by exact partial-wave unitarity. Finally, using the result of Ref.
[
5
]
that truncation in bilinear fits generates effective mixing in the extracted partial-wave coefficients, we discuss why this can naturally induce cross-wave correlations in the pole content obtained from truncated analyses, and hence provide a plausible origin of clustered effective pole positions across different fitted partial waves.

2
The problem of Höhler’s clustering

The phenomenon addressed in this paper was stated very explicitly by Höhler in his analysis of
\pi N
partial waves
[
1
]
. Ordering the resonances according to the real part of their pole positions, he observed that several resonances belonging to different partial waves appear to cluster within the errors around a few common complex energies. As a concrete example, he noted that among the nucleon resonances there are sizable groups with pole positions near
(1670-50i)\,\mathrm{MeV}
and
(2120-170i)\,\mathrm{MeV}

[
1
]
. This is surprising because the poles in question belong to partial waves with different angular momenta and parities.

Höhler then raised the question whether the notion of resonances with well-defined quantum numbers is really the appropriate description of the phenomenon at intermediate energies. In particular, he suggested that
\pi N
scattering might be describable by assuming a few complex poles of the invariant amplitudes instead of poles in many partial waves at slightly different positions
[
1
]
. In modern language, this amounts to asking whether the observed clustering reflects a genuine dynamical degeneracy of the exact amplitude, or whether it is produced effectively by the procedure used to extract poles from partial-wave analyses.

The problem was later discussed explicitly by Kirchbach, who interpreted the so-called Höhler clusters in terms of Lorentz multiplets
[
2
]
, with a related continuation by Losanow and Kirchbach
[
3
]
. These works treated the clustering as a genuine structural feature of the baryon spectrum. To our knowledge, however, the literature does not contain a systematic analysis of whether at least part of the observed clustering may arise from the practical extraction procedure itself.

The historical observation must also be viewed in the light of present compilations. In the current PDG review, resonance properties are drawn from a broader set of hadronic and photoproduction analyses, and only the more firmly established states are retained in the summary tables
[
4
]
. As a result, the clustering pattern emphasized by Höhler is not displayed today in as direct a form as in the older KH-based compilations. At the same time, the PDG stresses that reliable resonance claims require a sufficiently complete partial-wave basis and explicit tests of the effect of higher partial waves
[
4
]
, which points directly to the relevance of truncation issues.

This distinction is the starting point of the present paper. There are, in principle, two logically different possibilities. Either the clustering reflects a genuine dynamical feature of exact partial waves, or it is an effective phenomenon generated by the extraction of truncated partial-wave coefficients from bilinear observables. In the next section we argue that the first possibility is strongly constrained by exact partial-wave unitarity. We then turn to the second possibility and show how truncation can generate effective mixing in the extracted coefficients.

3
Sources of apparent partial-wave mixing

There are two conceptually different origins of apparent mixing. The first is a truly dynamical effect at the level of the exact amplitude; for example, when one tries to represent several partial waves using a single pole structure. The second is an effective effect introduced by the practical procedure used to extract the amplitudes.

3.1
Mixing at the level of invariant amplitudes

In this section, we contend that, in the exact formulation, different angular momenta do not mix dynamically; nonetheless, mixing arises once realistic calculations are performed.

3.1.1
Exact partial waves do not mix

We now show that the mechanism in which a single pole is introduced at the level of the invariant amplitudes and then redistributed over several angular momenta is strongly constrained by exact partial-wave unitarity.

To make the discussion explicit, consider elastic
2\to 2
scattering of spinless particles at fixed total energy
W
. At that energy, the scattering operator
\hat{S}(W)
acts on the Hilbert space of two-body angular states. In this representation, unitarity is not a local condition at a single scattering angle, but an exact identity for the kernel in angular space:

\int d\Omega_{q}\,S(W;\Omega^{\prime},\Omega_{q})\,S^{*}(W;\Omega,\Omega_{q})=\delta^{(2)}(\Omega^{\prime}-\Omega),

(1)

with

S(W;\Omega^{\prime},\Omega)=\langle\Omega^{\prime}|\hat{S}(W)|\Omega\rangle,

(2)

up to normalization conventions for angular states.

For rotationally invariant scalar scattering the kernel depends only on the relative angle between the incoming and outgoing directions,

S(W;\Omega^{\prime},\Omega)=S(W,\hat{p}^{\prime}\!\cdot\!\hat{p})\equiv S(W,x),\qquad x=\cos\theta.

(3)

Choosing the incoming momentum
\hat{p}
along the
z
-axis and parametrizing the intermediate direction
\hat{q}
by

y=\hat{q}\!\cdot\!\hat{p}=\cos\alpha,\qquad\phi=\text{azimuth of }\hat{q},

(4)

one has

\hat{p}^{\prime}\!\cdot\!\hat{q}=xy+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}\cos\phi.

(5)

The exact unitarity relation therefore takes the reduced form

U(W,x)\equiv\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dy}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\,S\!\left(W,xy+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}\cos\phi\right)S^{*}(W,y)=I(x),

(6)

where
I(x)
denotes the identity kernel in the rotationally invariant angular subspace. The important point is that this equality must hold as a function of
x
, so after expansion in a complete angular basis it must hold coefficient by coefficient. This leads directly to partial-wave unitarity.

This has a direct consequence for pole structure. A generic pole ansatz at the level of the full invariant amplitude may be written schematically as

T_{\rm pole}(W,x)=\frac{g(W)}{D(W)}\,h(W,x)\,P_{L}(x),

(7)

where
D(W)=M-W-ig(W)
vanishes at the pole position. However, if this term is to represent a single exact partial wave with definite angular momentum
L
, the factor
h(W,x)
cannot contain additional free angular dependence. Otherwise
h(W,x)
itself admits a Legendre expansion and the pole term already represents a superposition of several angular momenta. In that restricted case one has

h(W,x)\equiv h(W),

(8)

so that the pole contribution projects onto one isolated partial wave only.

The conclusion is therefore not that invariant amplitudes must be angle-independent. Rather, it is that an isolated, exact pole associated with a state of definite angular momentum cannot be distributed among several partial waves without breaking exact partial-wave unitarity.

3.1.2
Realistic cases

The situation becomes more complicated in realistic studies. Exact unitarity can only be enforced approximately, because practical models never include all open channels explicitly. This limitation is particularly problematic for single-channel treatments above inelastic thresholds, where exact unitarity is inevitably violated. Under these conditions, a pole inserted at the level of an invariant amplitude can, in effect, spread across several extracted partial waves. Moreover, even if the fundamental dynamics is roughly unitary, determining partial-wave content from experimental observables still requires a truncation of the expansion. This truncation introduces an additional source of mixing, arising from the nonlinear character of the bilinear fitting procedure
[
5
]
.

The conclusion is therefore clear. In the exact infinite partial-wave problem, poles with different angular momenta do not mix dynamically. Effective mixing can nevertheless appear in realistic pole extractions for two distinct reasons: incomplete implementation of exact unitarity and truncation in the bilinear observables used in practice. In the remainder of this paper we focus on the second mechanism.

3.2
Effective mixing generated by truncation

Besides the incomplete realization of exact unitarity discussed above, a supplementary mechanism is provided by the truncation of bilinear observables. The essential point, developed in detail in the companion paper
[
5
]
, is that the quantities fitted to data are not the amplitudes themselves but observables that are bilinear in them. As a result, truncation at the amplitude level does not carry over in a straightforward coefficient-by-coefficient way to the observables.

To make the structure explicit, let the exact amplitude be written as

f(W,x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}a_{\ell}(W)P_{\ell}(x),

(9)

and let the practical truncated ansatz be

g(W,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}b_{m}(W)P_{m}(x),\qquad M<N.

(10)

The corresponding Hermitian bilinears are

B_{f}(W,x)=f(W,x)\overline{f(W,x)},\qquad B_{g}(W,x)=g(W,x)\overline{g(W,x)}.

(11)

Expanding these quantities in Legendre polynomials gives

B_{f}(W,x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2N}F_{\ell}(W)P_{\ell}(x),\qquad B_{g}(W,x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2M}G_{\ell}(W)P_{\ell}(x),

(12)

where the coefficients
F_{\ell}
are bilinear in the exact amplitudes
a_{\ell}
, while the coefficients
G_{\ell}
are bilinear in the unknown truncated amplitudes
b_{m}

[
5
]
. The least-squares fit, therefore, does not determine each
b_{m}
by an independent projection of the corresponding exact coefficient. Instead, the truncated coefficients are obtained from a coupled nonlinear problem in which the whole low-order vector

(F_{0},F_{1},\ldots,F_{2M})

(13)

enters simultaneously.

The key consequence is that the fitted coefficients
b_{m}(W)
need not correspond to pure exact partial waves.
They depend on bilinear combinations of several exact amplitudes, including both nominally retained lower partial waves and omitted higher ones, and therefore carry mixed angular-momentum content. This is the central result of Ref.
[
5
]
: bilinear fitting in a truncated Legendre framework induces angular-momentum mixing in the extracted lower-order coefficients, even when the exact infinite partial-wave problem does not mix angular momenta. As emphasized in Ref.
[
5
]
, this statement is established directly for the fitted bilinear quantities and for the reconstructed truncated coefficients, while the further interpretation in terms of analytically continued poles of reconstructed truncated amplitudes is a physically motivated additional step.

4
Possible connection to Höhler’s clustering

We can now return to the question posed in Sec. 2. If Höhler-type clustering is not attributed to a genuine dynamical mixing of exact partial waves, then it is natural to ask whether it can arise effectively from the extraction procedure itself. The preceding discussion, together with the truncation result of Ref.
[
5
]
, shows that this is a plausible possibility: once bilinear observables are treated with a truncated ansatz, the extracted lower-order coefficients need not correspond to pure exact partial waves, but can inherit overlapping pole-bearing contributions from several angular-momentum sectors.

In the exact infinite partial-wave problem, angular-momentum sectors remain separated by exact partial-wave unitarity. In practical calculations, however, one does not determine the amplitudes directly, but fits observables that are bilinear in them. Once such observables are treated with a truncated ansatz, the extracted lower-order coefficients are obtained from a coupled nonlinear problem and need not correspond to pure exact partial waves. Instead, they carry mixed pole content.

This mixing is not limited to omitted higher partial waves. The nonlinear bilinear fit also reshuffles the pole content already present in the nominally retained lower sector. Consequently, the partial-wave coefficients obtained at a given truncation order should not, in general, be interpreted as the exact lower partial waves with small corrections from missing higher ones. Rather, they are effective coefficients in which lower and higher exact contributions are recombined.

From this point of view, Höhler’s clustering acquires a natural interpretation. If several fitted partial-wave coefficients inherit contributions from overlapping sets of exact amplitudes, then the pole content extracted from them can exhibit cross-wave correlations and may, in realistic truncated analyses, appear in groups with similar complex energies. In this sense, clustering may arise as an effective property of the extraction procedure, even though the exact theory itself preserves angular-momentum separation.

This may also help explain why Höhler-type clustering is much less visually obvious in present-day PDG summaries than in the older phenomenological compilations: modern resonance listings are filtered through broader data bases, mixed extraction strategies, and stricter criteria on partial-wave completeness and robustness
[
4
]
.

5
Discussion and conclusions

The central issue of this paper has been whether Höhler-type clustering reflects a genuine dynamical feature of the exact amplitude or an effective feature of realistic pole extraction. The analysis presented here, when combined with the truncation result established in Ref.
[
5
]
, supports the second possibility as a plausible and physically natural one.

In the exact infinite partial-wave problem, different angular momenta do not mix dynamically: exact unitarity, together with the partial-wave decomposition, preserves the separation of angular-momentum sectors. In realistic analyses, by contrast, two effects can generate apparent mixing: incomplete implementation of exact unitarity and truncation of bilinear observables. The second mechanism is the focus of the present work.

The main conclusion is therefore that the extracted lower-order partial-wave coefficients should not be identified directly with the exact lower partial waves. They are effective quantities produced by a nonlinear bilinear fit, in which omitted higher contributions enter, but the nominally retained lower sector is also reshuffled. Together with Ref.
[
5
]
, this provides a concrete mechanism for cross-wave correlations in the extracted pole-bearing content and hence a plausible route by which clustered pole positions may appear across different fitted partial waves.

We do not claim that truncation is the unique origin of Höhler’s clustering. The claim is instead that it provides a natural and unavoidable mechanism that can contribute to the type of cross-wave correlations emphasized by Höhler. Exact partial waves do not mix in the infinite problem, but realistic truncated bilinear extractions can generate effective mixtures of pole-bearing contributions, and this offers a plausible explanation for at least part of the observed clustering pattern.
