The effects of castration on the quality of black Tibetan sheep meat and muscle metabolites
Authors: Xianli Xu, Lijuan Han, Qingming Yue, Shengzhen Hou, Zenghai Luo, Linsheng Gui, Baochun Yang, Zhiyou Wang, Zhenzhen Yuan, Shengnan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCastration is a vital technical practice in Tibetan sheep farming on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. It reduces fighting among rams, making it easier to manage the herd while they are grazing. However, the impact of castration on the quality of Tibetan sheep meat and muscle metabolites is unclear at present. On this basis, this study examined differences in meat quality and metabolites between castrated (BTC) and non-castrated (BTN) Black Tibetan sheep using targeted and non-targeted metabolomics. Compared with the BTN group, the meat from the BTC group showed reduced drip loss and increased cooking loss, as well as higher levels of elasticity, chewiness and cohesiveness (P < 0.05). The levels of 11 amino acids, total amino acids, total umami amino acids, 23 fatty acids, SFA, PUFA and MUFA were significantly higher in the BTC group than in the BTN group (P < 0.05). A total of 34 differentially abundant metabolites were identified between the two groups, with uric acid, vitamin C, malate, glutamine and oleic acid being all upregulated in BTC. Purine metabolism, metabolic pathways, bile secretion, the phosphotransferase system, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and ABC transporters were the key metabolic pathways associated with meat quality in the castrated group. In conclusion, there are certain differences in meat quality and muscle metabolites between BTC and BTN. Castration can affect meat quality characteristics to a certain extent.Keywords: Castrated, Black Tibetan sheep, Meat eat properties, Correlation, Metabolomics

Introduction

Meat is an important source of animal protein in the human diet. As consumers become more health-conscious, they demand meat that is healthy, tasty, high-quality and nutrient-rich. The quality and nutritional characteristics of meat directly influence consumer choice. The Black Tibetan sheep, also known as the black-furred sheep, is native to the Tibetan Plateau and is considered one of the region’s three main coarse-wool sheep breeds (Zhang et al., 2023). This species inhabits alpine regions at altitudes of 3000 m, primarily in Guinan County in the Chinese province of Qinghai (Zhang et al., 2022). Tibetan black mutton is highly favoured by consumers for its delicious flavour and rich nutritional profile, being abundant in amino acids, fatty acids and other beneficial components (Ma et al., 2023).

The quality and nutrient content of mutton are influenced by feeding methods, the species and breed of the animal, its sex, age and live weight, and these factors interact with each other (Khan et al., 2015). Castration is a common practice in animal husbandry, particularly in sheep farming, to reduce aggressive behaviour and improve meat quality (Ahmed et al., 2022). Baik et al. (2014) demonstrated that castration led to a significant increase in the deposition of intramuscular fat in the loin muscle of Korean bulls. Bañón et al. (2004) demonstrated that castration increases the content of intramuscular fat and reduces off-flavours in pigs. The castrated group exhibited significantly lower drip loss than the uncastrated boars. Rehman et al. (2024) found that castrated rams had a higher average daily weight gain and superior tenderness. They also had thinner backfat and a tendency towards an increased loin eye area compared to intact rams. Castration improves meat quality by preventing puberty-related hormones, such as testosterone, from causing undesirable odours and flavours (Junior et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2013; Seiquer et al., 2019).

Metabolomics is one of the ‘omics’ disciplines used in life science research, alongside genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics. It focuses on global fluctuations in small-molecule metabolites (less than 1500 Da) in biofluids, tissues and organs (Deng et al.,, 2020). As an ‘omics’ technique closest to the phenotype, metabolomics offers a more accurate reflection of the organism’s physiological state (Yang et al., 2020). Targeted, non-targeted and combined metabolomics approaches have been used to investigate the effect of differentially abundant or specific metabolites on meat quality (Windarsih et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). For instance, Windarsih et al. (2024) developed a certified analytical method for distinguishing dog meat meatballs from halal beef meatballs using a non-targeted metabolomics approach, combining liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) with chemometric techniques. Liu’ study revealed associations between the gastrointestinal microbiota, muscle metabolites, and meat quality by combining 16S rDNA sequencing with UHPLC-Q-TOF mass spectrometry analysis (Liu et al., 2024).

Although extensive research indicates that castration improves the meat quality of domesticated animals such as pigs, cattle and sheep, the impact of castration on the meat quality and muscle metabolites of the indigenous Tibetan black sheep breed in the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau region is unclear. In this study, Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole-time of flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF/MS) was employed to identify differential non-targeted metabolites in the longissimus dorsi muscle of Black Tibetan sheep. Targeted metabolomics analysis was performed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-ion mobility separation (UPLC-IMS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to identify amino acid and fatty acid metabolites and corroborate alterations in metabolic pathways. This study aims to clarify the differences in meat quality and metabolism between castrated and non-castrated Tibetan sheep. This will provide a basis for standardised Tibetan sheep husbandry and the development of distinctive meat products.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted at the Black Tibetan Sheep Breeding Centre in Guinan County, Qinghai Province, China. It received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Qinghai University (approval number QUA-2020-0709).

A total of 60 male Black Tibetan sheep, aged two months and with a body weight range of 13 to 14 kg, were randomly assigned to two groups. Of these, the castrated sheep were castrated after weaning (Steiner and Janett et al., 2013), while the sheep that had been castrated (the BTC group) and those that were not castrated (the BTN group) were kept in two sheepfolds and managed and fed in the same way. Following a one-week acclimatisation period, during which the lambs were permitted unrestricted access to feed and water, a formal 120-day experimental phase was initiated (Table 1). After the feeding trial had finished, six sheep were randomly selected from the castrated and non-castrated groups. They were then transported to a nearby commercial slaughterhouse. After a 12-hour fasting period that adhered to animal welfare protocols by including both solid and liquid fasting, the animals were humanely slaughtered. The carcass was then left to stand in the slaughterhouse for a period of 45 min, after which the muscle samples were collected bilaterally between the 9th and 11th ribs. The slaughtering and sampling procedures were conducted concurrently by trained professionals in accordance with standardised procedures. All samples were preserved on dry ice and transported to the laboratory where they were stored at -80 °C. An analysis of meat quality and metabolomics were conducted on each group, with six replicates performed in order to ensure the robustness of the findings.

Analysis of meat quality

Eating quality

The Longissimus dorsi muscle (LD) of Black Tibetan sheep in both the castrated group and intact rams (non-castrated group) were dissected by removal of the upper fascia of the muscle, after which the muscles were cut into blocks for analysis. The palatability of the meat was evaluated in terms of conventional parameters (Zhang et al., 2022), such as the pH level, percentage of cooked meat, cooking loss, Warner-Bratzler shear force (WBSF), and drip loss, as well as textural properties, including firmness, elasticity, resilience, chewiness, and stickiness, among others.

Nutritional quality

The contents of crude protein, moisture, and crude fat were measured in the LDs using the accepted AOAC method (Seiquer et al., 2019).

Detection of untargeted metabolomics

The samples were thawed at 4 °C to ensure the precise weighing of 50 mg of each sample. The samples were then placed in a pre-cooled solution of methanol, acetonitrile, and water (2:2:1, v/v), followed by thorough vortexing and subsequent sonication for 30 min in an ice-water bath. The collection of the supernatants was conducted using a syringe, followed by vacuum-drying after a standing period at -20 °C for 10 min and a centrifugation at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. In preparation for mass spectrometry analysis, the dried samples were reconstituted by the addition of 100 µL of an aqueous acetonitrile solution, consisting of acetonitrile and water in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. Thereafter, the mixtures were vortexed and subjected to a centrifugation process at 14,000 g for a duration of 15 min at a temperature of 4 °C.

The chromatograms were acquired using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The separation of samples was accomplished through the utilisation of a HILIC column. Throughout the analysis process, the primary and secondary spectra of the samples were collected using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) with electrospray ionisation (ESI) detection in both positive and negative ion modes following ultrahigh-pressure liquid chromatography (UHPLC) separation of the samples.

Detecting of targeted metabolomics

Detection of amino acids

15 mg of sample were combined with 0.5 mL of a 2:2:1 methanol: acetonitrile: water mixture. The material was ground in liquid nitrogen, vortexed for 1 min, and sonicated for 30 min, with the process repeated twice. The mixture was then precipitated, centrifuged and stored at -80 °C.

The samples were separated using UPLC (1290 Infinity, Agilent). As outlined by Zhang et al. (2022), a linear gradient elution programme was implemented. The MS data were quantitatively acquired using the Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) method. The analysis was conducted in the positive ion mode with a 5500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB Sciex).

Detection of fatty acids

The meat samples (50 mg) were transferred to 2 mL glass centrifuge tubes, sonicated for 30 min, and then methyl-esterified for a further 30 min at 80 °C. For the gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis, 250 millilitres of methyl ester standard, in conjunction with 500 millilitres of the extract’s extractant, were amalgamated. The separation process was conducted on an Agilent gas chromatograph, equipped with a DB-WAX (Agilent) capillary column measuring 30 m in length, 0.25 mm in internal diameter, and 0.25 μm film thickness. The MS analysis was conducted using an Agilent 7890/5975 C system.

Data analysis

All quantitative data collected in this study were subjected to analysis using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) via SPSS software (version 22.0), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. For untargeted metabolomics data, the raw mass spectrometry (MS) data (wiff.scan files) were converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert. Subsequently, these files were processed using the XCMS software for alignment, peak detection, and retention-time correction. The data extracted by XCMS is then subjected to a series of processes, commencing with metabolite structure identification and data preprocessing. The preprocessing stage involves the implementation of null filtering, a process that eliminates ion peaks characterised by more than 50% missing values. This is followed by the application of null filling through the utilisation of K-nearest neighbours (KNN) imputation. Furthermore, data filtration is conducted to eliminate features exhibiting a relative standard deviation (RSD) that exceeds 50%. Subsequent to this, the experimental data quality is evaluated using six quality controls. The process is concluded with a comprehensive data analysis. The identification of metabolites was achieved through the utilisation of MultiQuant and MSD Chem Station software for the analysis of targeted metabolomics data. The untargeted metabolomics data were analysed using SIMCA software (version 14.0), and heatmaps were generated using the R programming language. Furthermore, the metabolic pathways associated with the metabolites were investigated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (www.genome.jp/kegg) for functional annotation of the metabolites.

A total of 586 metabolites were identified in the negative ion mode. These metabolites were categorized and quantified based on their chemical classifications, including lipids and lipid-like molecules (31.215%), organic acids and their derivatives (23.925%), undefined (13.084%), organic oxygen compounds (8.598%), and organic heterocyclic compounds (7.103%) (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, the OPLS-DA model was used to identify metabolites that differed significantly between the groups using the criteria of VIP > 1 and P < 0.05, as well as annotations using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and the KEGG database. The expression patterns of differential metabolites in the negative ion mode were effectively visualized using a volcano plot. Figure 2 demonstrates the distribution of metabolites across various classes. A total of 34 differential metabolites were identified in the corresponding database, as detailed in Table 4 (VIP > 1.0, P < 0.05). A comparison of the metabolic pathways between the BTC and BTN groups showed that 15 metabolites were up-regulated and 13 were down-regulated (Fig. 2b). The upregulated metabolites included lipids, lipid-like molecules, and organic acids, while the downregulated metabolites included organic oxygen compounds and lipids.

Analysis of targeted metabolomics

Using the MRM method for targeted metabolomics, amino acids and their derivatives were analyzed in the LD of sheep in the BTC and BTN groups. Table 5 shows significant differences in these compounds between the two groups. A total of 31 amino acids and derivatives were detected in the two groups of Tibetan sheep. As shown in the table, the levels of 11 amino acids and their derivatives were significantly higher in the BTC group than in the BTN group (P < 0.05). The contents of four amino acids, aspartate, glutamine, alanine/sarcosine, and taurine were significantly higher in the BTC group compared with the BTN group (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences in the levels of other essential amino acids between the two groups (P > 0.05). Both the total non-essential and umami amino acids contents were significantly higher in the BTC group relative to the BTN group (P < 0.05). Therefore, the total amino acid content of the BTC group was higher than that of the BTN group.

Table 6 shows the concentration of fatty acids in muscle of two groups (BTC and BTN). Overall, 40 fatty acids were identified in the two groups, of which 23 fatty acids showed significant or highly significant differences between the groups (P < 0.05). The LD muscle of the Tibetan sheep in the BTC group showed highly significant (P < 0.01) differences in the contents of saturated fatty acids (SFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) when compared to Tibetan sheep in the BTN group. The ratio of n-6 to n-3 fatty acids exhibited a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). However, the ratio of PUFA/SFA in the BTC group was less than that in the BTN group with no significant difference (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Castration, also referred to as orchidectomy or gonadectomy, involves the surgical, chemical, or immunological removal of an animal’s reproductive organs or the suppression of their reproductive function (Fernández-Fígares et al., 2023). The effects of castration on the meat quality, flavour and metabolites of Black Tibetan sheep are not well understood. This study aimed to investigate these effects, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the sustainable and healthy development of Tibetan sheep husbandry.

The pH value, tenderness, drip loss, and cooking loss, among others, are all key indicators in the evaluation of the eating quality of mutton. If this value is too high, the color of the meat will become darker, the texture will become hard, and it is easy to produce dark firm dry (DFD) meat. If the pH drops too rapidly after slaughter, the meat will be grayish-white, soft in texture, and prone to pale soft exudative (PSE) meat formation (Soji & Muchenje, 2016). The muscle pH changes with time and temperature after animals are slaughtered. It is well-known that the pH of animal muscles is strongly influenced by glycolysis. In this study, the pH values of the LD of castrated and non-castrated Tibetan black sheep were found to be significantly different at pH 5.51 and 5.78, respectively, 24 h after slaughter (P < 0.05). DL-2-Phosphatolycerate, which can be converted into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) by enolase during glycolysis. PEP is then converted into pyruvate by pyruvate kinase, which directly participates in the production of lactate. In our study, DL-2-Phosphatolycerate was significantly upregulated in the BTC group (Fig. 3b). Therefore, we speculate that DL-2-Phosphatolycerate is involved in the formation of lactic acid, leading to an decrease in its pH. From the results of off-target metabolic assays, it was found that the glyoxylic acid metabolism was up-regulated in the BTC group, resulting in the enrichment of lactate in the muscle and the decrease in pH both castrated and non-castrated black Tibetan mutton had pH values within the normal range for fresh meat, confirming their freshness. This result is consistent with the present study, indicating that castration has an effect on pH.

In addition, indicators such as the cooked meat rate and drip loss reflect the effects of storage and water retention in muscles. It is well-known that good water retention in muscles is associated with better taste, ensuring that the meat products are tender and juicy, thus enhancing consumer acceptance (Skele et al., 2024). A further study on boar meat found that castration increased muscle fat and reduced boar taint, resulting in significantly lower indicators of drip loss than meat from non-castrated boars (Bañón et al., 2004). This observation aligns with our findings, indicating that the drip loss in Tibetan sheep muscle was significantly reduced in the castrated group compared to the non-castrated group. In addition to the above indicators, it was found that the elasticity and chewiness of the meat from the castrated group were also significantly better than those of meat from the non-castrated group. The analysis of the above indicators indicates that castration affects the quality of muscle meat, with the indices showing that the quality of meat from the castrated group was superior to that from the non-castrated group. Previous research indicates that muscles with a higher proportion of type IIB fibers generally exhibit reduced tenderness and increased tenderness rates (Lang et al., 2020). Additionally, the diameter of muscle fibers and the characteristics of muscle bundles significantly influence the surface texture and sensory quality of meat (Lee et al., 2018). Furthermore, the cooking loss of meat is associated with the properties of muscle fibers, with studies suggesting that cooking loss primarily results from protein denaturation and juice expulsion during contraction (Kondjoyan et al., 2013). We propose that elasticity, chewiness and cooking loss are critical indicators of meat quality that are closely linked to muscle fibre type and structure. Future studies will involve a more detailed analysis of muscle fibre types and structures in castrated and non-castrated Tibetan sheep. The aim is to elucidate the reasons and mechanisms underlying these experimental outcomes.

Previous studies have shown that castrating animals can improve the nutritional quality of muscle tissue to a certain extent. Baik and colleagues observed that castrating Korean bulls led to a significant increase in intramuscular fat deposition in the LD (Baik et al., 2014). Fat deposition is associated with increases in both adipocyte number and size, with increased adipocyte numbers representing the first step in fat accumulation in animal muscles. Gravador et al. (2018) reported that the total weight and carcass fat of castrated rams increased with age compared to intact rams. In this study, it was found that castration did not affect the fat content of the muscles of black Tibetan sheep, which differs from the above results. In addition, indicators such as protein and moisture content also reflect the nutritional quality of meat products. The present study found that castration did not affect either muscle protein or water content in black Tibetan sheep, with no significant differences in the muscle indicators between the two groups. This result indicates that castration does not affect the crude nutritional content of Black Tibetan sheep.

Meat quality is significantly influenced by its amino acid composition, affecting both nutritional value and sensory attributes like flavor and texture (Tomaszewska et al., 2025). Studies indicate that Dongxiang Gongyang lamb possesses a richer content of essential and non-essential amino acids compared to other breeds, enhancing its meat quality (Lu et al., 2022). Additionally, variations in heat treatment methods alter the amino acid profile and flavor compounds in black Tibetan lamb, impacting its overall quality (Liu et al., 2022). Castration also affects amino acid levels, with castrated boars exhibiting higher umami amino acid content than sows (Xia et al., 2023), aligning with our findings. Umami intensity, primarily derived from amino acids such as Asp and Glu and 5’-nucleotides, is vital for meat taste and palatability (Wu et al., 2023). Research indicates that umami amino acids, including glutamic acid and aspartic acid, are present in significant quantities in lamb meat (You et al., 2024). These amino acids serve as fundamental components for protein synthesis and play a crucial role in the development of lamb meat flavor (Lu et al., 2022). Consequently, we hypothesize that castration may enhance both the diversity and abundance of umami amino acids in black Tibetan lamb. This enhancement could facilitate interactions between these amino acids and other flavour compounds, potentially improving the flavour profile of black Tibetan lamb. Nevertheless, further investigation into its flavour attributes is warranted.

The composition and concentrations of fatty acids have a significant influence on the nutritional value of mutton (Meale et al., 2021). Improving the quality of ruminant meat requires decreasing the levels of SFAs and increasing those of monounsaturated and polyunsaturated cis fatty acids, such as vaccenic acid and conjugated linoleic acids such as rumenic acid. Lee’s study showed that the fatty acid composition of meat from black goats had high levels of both MUFAs and PUFAs (Lee et al., 2023), with the concentrations of C18:1n9, C16:1N7, C22:1N9, MUFAs, and the MUFA: SFA ratio were influenced by castration, confirming earlier findings. Here, it was found that the proportion of oleic acid (C18:1 n-9 cis), the predominant MUFA, was affected by castration; consistent with our findings, Valenzuela-Grijalva et al. (2012) observed a notable impact of zeranol implantation on the proportion of C18:1 n-9 cis and MUFA content. The levels of linoleic acid and DHA in the LD of castrated small-tailed Han lambs were found to be markedly increased compared to untreated lambs and positively correlated with the duration of fattening, thereby enhancing the quality of lamb meat (Hur et al., 2007). Gao et al.‘s research indicates that, following castration, broiler chickens fed linseed oil exhibited increased levels of PUFAs, total n-3 and n-6 fatty acids in abdominal fat, alongside reduced levels of SFAs and MUFAs. The researchers also found that feeding linseed oil significantly increased the levels of 18:3 n-3 and n-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LC-PUFA), as well as total n-3 content. At the same time, total n-6 content and the n-6/n-3 ratio were reduced (Mašek et al., 2014). In our study, the SFA, MUFA, PUFA, and n6-PUFA contents in Tibetan sheep were affected by castration, with castrated animals having higher contents of these fatty acids than the non-castrated group. This study found that castration increased the total fatty acid content of Tibetan sheep, though it did not reduce the level of SFAs. Therefore, future research could involve feeding flaxseed to castrated sheep to further regulate the levels of unsaturated fatty acids, total n-3 and n-6, and the n-6/n-3 ratio in their muscle tissue.

In addition, the results of this study indicated a negative correlation between drip loss and uric acid levels. Furthermore, castrated Tibetan sheep exhibited increased uric acid expression, with its main enriched pathways being purine metabolism, bile secretion and metabolic processes. Research indicates that purine metabolism plays a role in enhancing the umami flavour (Bai et al., 2025). Huang et al. found a significant positive correlation between guanine content and umami flavour in pork (Huang et al., 2021). Therefore, this study concludes that castration induces the upregulation of uric acid expression in the muscle tissue of Tibetan sheep. Uric acid primarily functions through metabolic pathways, such as purine metabolism, to reduce drip loss in castrated Tibetan sheep and preserve the flavour of the meat. NEAAS and TAAS were positively related to vitamin C, malate, glutamine and oleic acid, as well as ammelide. Meanwhile, vitamin C, malate, glutamine and oleic acid were all found to be upregulated in castrated Tibetan sheep. Osowe et al. (2023) showed that total amino acid and isoleucine levels were related to vitamin C, and our findings are consistent with theirs. Furthermore, our study found that vitamin C was mainly enriched in metabolic pathways and the phosphotransferase system. Malate, meanwhile, was found to be enriched in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, as well as metabolic pathways. Glutamine was found to be present in the following metabolic pathways: purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and ABC transporters. Fu et al. (2022) investigated changes in metabolic products during the production of dry-cured beef, discovering that Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism is one of the primary pathways influencing its texture. ABC transporters are present in all organisms, including humans and microorganisms, and are one of the most extensively studied protein families to date (Liu et al., 2013). These transporters play a crucial role in the transmembrane transport of various substances, including toxins, xenobiotics, ions and metabolites. In their study of the dynamic changes in metabolic products and metabolic pathways during the dry-ageing process of Yanbian cattle, Sun et al. demonstrated that ABC transporters participate in the signalling of metabolic products (Sun et al., 2024). Therefore, this study proposes that the metabolites vitamin C, malate, glutamine and oleic acid are upregulated in castrated Tibetan sheep. These metabolites then regulate the amino acid content of Tibetan sheep muscle via mechanisms involving ABC transporters, the phosphotransferase system and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism.

Studies of the serum metabolome of quails of different ages, breeds and sexes indicate that identifying ABC transporters is key to distinguishing between quails of different ages. The critical metabolic pathways for differentiating quails of distinct breeds are purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and ABC transporters (He et al., 2025).This study identified purine metabolism and ABC transporters as key metabolic pathways that distinguish castrated Tibetan sheep from their non-castrated counterparts.

Furthermore, given the costs of experimentation and the ethical considerations surrounding animal studies, this study focused on six castrated and six intact Tibetan sheep. The correlation analysis results may have been influenced by the limitations of the sample size, and it is possible that additional metabolites relevant to meat quality remain unexplored. This warrants further systematic investigation in future research.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that castration affects the quality characteristics of Tibetan sheep meat, including drip loss, cooking loss, elasticity, chewiness, cohesiveness and amino acid and fatty acid levels. Metabolites, including uric acid, vitamin C, malate, glutamine and oleic acid, regulate the quality of Tibetan sheep meat through purine metabolism, metabolic pathways, bile secretion, the phosphotransferase system, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and ABC transporters. However, castration increases the levels of amino acids and fatty acids in muscle tissue, while reducing drip loss. It also increases cooking loss and the level of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) in BTC. Future research could focus on optimising this group’s feed formulation to reduce cooking loss and SFA levels in meat.
