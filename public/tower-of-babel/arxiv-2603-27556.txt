Towards Domain-Generalized Open-Vocabulary Object Detection: A Progressive Domain-invariant Cross-modal Alignment Method
Xiaoran Xu, Xiaoshan Yang, Jiangang Yang ⋆, Yifan Xu, Jian Liu, Changsheng Xu

Abstract
Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (OVOD) has achieved remarkable success in generalizing to novel categories. However, this success often rests on the implicit assumption of domain stationarity. In this work, we provide a principled revisit of the OVOD paradigm, uncovering a fundamental vulnerability: the fragile coupling between visual manifolds and textual embeddings when distribution shifts occur. We first systematically formalize Domain-Generalized Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (DG-OVOD). Through empirical analysis, we demonstrate that visual shifts do not merely add noise; they cause a collapse of the latent cross-modal space where novel category visual signals detach from their semantic anchors. Motivated by these insights, we propose Progressive Domain-invariant Cross-modal Alignment (PICA). PICA departs from uniform training by introducing a multi-level ambiguity and signal strength curriculum. It builds adaptive pseudo-word prototypes, refined via sample confidence and visual consistency, to enforce invariant cross-domain modality alignment. Our findings suggest that OVOD’s robustness to domain shifts is intrinsically linked to the stability of the latent cross-modal alignment space. Our work provides both a challenging benchmark and a new perspective on building truly generalizable open-vocabulary systems that extend beyond static laboratory conditions.

To address the alignment degradation, we propose Progressive Domain-invariant Cross-modal Alignment (PICA).

These two proxies jointly determine each region’s training priority: during training, we follow a curriculum-guided adaptation strategy that prioritizes samples with low ambiguity and high alignment confidence in the early stages, and progressively incorporates harder or less reliable instances as the projection layer becomes better calibrated.
This progressive stratification mitigates misleading image-text associations and the resulting gradient conflicts caused by uncertain cross-modal signals in early training, while preserving informative yet challenging samples for later refinement.
In summary, our work makes the following contributions:

•

We formalize Domain-Generalized Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (DG-OVOD), a systematic unified evaluation protocol designed to quantify the impact of distribution shifts on open-vocabulary generalization.

•

Motivated by our findings, we propose PICA, which enforces domain-invariant cross-modal alignment through a progressive curriculum, effectively rectifying the observed semantic-visual detachment.

•

We establish the first systematic benchmark for DG-OVOD and reveal that existing OVOD methods universally fail to maintain cross-modal alignment stability under distribution shifts, exposing a paradigm-level vulnerability.

Open-Vocabulary Object Detection

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVOD) enables detectors trained on a closed set of base categories to recognize novel classes at inference. Since annotations for novel categories are unavailable during training, OVOD is typically formulated as a vision-language task, allowing detectors to leverage pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) for knowledge transfer from seen to unseen categories.
Existing methods mainly fall into two categories. The first aligns detection heads with pretrained VLMs, most notably CLIP

The second line of work constructs object-aware vision-language spaces through large-scale heterogeneous supervision across classification

In this work, we focus on the first paradigm, which has been widely adopted in recent OVOD approaches, and systematically investigate its robustness under distribution shifts and corrupted inputs.

Domain generalization (DG) aims to enable models to perform reliably across unseen or distribution-shifted domains without access to target-domain data during training

In computer vision, models often face diverse domain shifts caused by adverse weather, motion blur, sensor noise

Efforts to improve domain generalization can be broadly categorized into three directions.
Data-centric approaches enhance model robustness by synthesizing diverse domains or applying corruption- and style-based augmentations

Model-centric approaches focus on architectural or adaptation strategies, such as domain adversarial training, normalization modulation, and meta-learning

Representation-centric approaches leverage self-supervised or contrastive pretraining to learn domain-invariant features, often preserving semantic consistency through vision-language alignment

Most studies focus on closed-vocabulary detection.
In contrast, open-vocabulary detection relies on fine-grained image-text alignment to generalize beyond seen categories.
Existing domain generalization and robustness approaches mainly enhance visual representations, yet they often overlook the cross-modal semantic consistency crucial for OVOD under domain shifts.

2.3
Curriculum Learning

Curriculum Learning (CL)
[
2
]
is a training paradigm that mimics the human learning process by organizing samples from easy to hard, thereby improving convergence and generalization. Extensive research has explored diverse formulations and scheduling strategies, including manually defined curricula and automatic mechanisms such as self-paced learning
[
24
]
, teacher-student frameworks
[
12
]
, and reinforcement-based sample selection
[
33

. These methods generally consist of a difficulty estimator and a scheduler that jointly determine the learning order of training instances.
In computer vision, CL has been applied to domain adaptation, semi-supervised learning, and robustness enhancement under distribution shifts. Recent extensions target multi-modal and cross-domain settings, including vision-language pretraining
[
59
,
44
]
and robustness
[
3
]

Most existing approaches overlook the progressive alignment between modalities and domains, focusing only on single-modality or data-level difficulty.
We introduce a curriculum-guided modality learning framework for domain-generalized open-vocabulary object detection, achieving a gradual refinement from coarse to fine-grained cross-modal alignment.

Methodology

3.1
Problem Statement

Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (OVOD) aims to detect objects from both base categories
\mathcal{C}_{\text{base}}
and novel categories
\mathcal{C}_{\text{novel}}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{\text{base}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{\text{novel}}=\emptyset
. The model leverages a pretrained vision-language model (VLM)
[
36
]
, with text and visual encoders denoted as
\mathscr{F}_{\text{text}}
and
\mathscr{F}_{\text{vision}}
, respectively. For a class
c
, its semantic embedding is
t_{c}=\mathscr{F}_{\text{text}}(\mathbf{prompt}(c))
; for an image region
r
, its visual embedding is
f_{r}=\mathscr{F}_{\text{vision}}(r)
. Detection scores are computed as
s_{r,c}=\mathbf{sim}(f_{r},t_{c})
, with higher values indicating stronger cross-modal alignment.

For regions without associated class labels (i.e., from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{novel}}
), we employ a learnable pseudo-word prototype
w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a textual proxy that approximates the semantic content of region
r
:

w_{r}=\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}(f_{r})=\mathbf{W}f_{r}+\mathbf{b},

(1)

where
\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}
is a trainable projection head, and
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b}
are learnable parameters, following common practice in vision-language alignment
[
32
,
36
,
52
,
20
]
.
Crucially,
\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}
is jointly optimized by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ovod}}
, which grounds
w_{r}
toward text embeddings
t_{c}
, ensuring that
w_{r}
approximates a semantic position in the language embedding space rather than a self-referential projection artifact.

The standard OVOD assumes that
\mathcal{D}_{\text{src}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{tgt}}
share the same distribution. We extend this to Domain-Generalized Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (DG-OVOD), where the model is trained on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{src}}=\{(I^{s},\mathcal{B}^{s})\}
with annotations only from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{base}}
, and evaluated on unseen
\mathcal{D}_{\text{tgt}}=\{(I^{t},\mathcal{B}^{t})\}
covering
\mathcal{C}_{\text{novel}}
, where
I^{t}
exhibits joint distribution shift encompassing both semantic shift and visual divergence from
I^{s}
.

Under the DG-OVOD setting, the core challenge lies in the domain-induced variance of visual embeddings
f_{r}
, which triggers an alignment-drift, causing a huge performance drop on novel categories, which lack explicit supervision, necessitating a mechanism to enforce domain-invariant cross-modal alignment.

To empirically validate this alignment-drift hypothesis, we evaluate a broad set of representative OVOD methods on our DG-OVOD benchmark, detailed in Table
1
and Table
2
.
Since novel-category generalization in these methods is entirely contingent on cross-modal alignment
[
52
,
58
,
1
,
57
,
53
,
51
,
22
,
48
]
, their catastrophic performance drop, averaging over 50%, is a direct proxy for cross-modal alignment collapse.
Critically, this failure is not merely a loss of precision but a structural breakdown of the semantic manifold: rankings under clean conditions fail to predict robustness under shift. This systematic evidence confirms that current alignment mechanisms, while effective in stationary environments, lack the domain-invariant correspondence to anchor novel-class signals against visual perturbations. This fundamental blind spot in the existing paradigm directly motivates the invariant alignment approach developed in the following section.

3.2
Progressive Domain-invariant Cross-modal Alignment

Sample-Adaptive Stratification under Domain Shift.

Domain shifts cause two distinct failure modes at the sample level, as illustrated in Figure
1
. The first is signal collapse: the positive alignment score degrades, meaning the region feature no longer activates its correct semantic prototype. The second is boundary confusion: the margin between positive and hardest negative shrinks, meaning the feature lies ambiguously close to incorrect categories. These two failure modes are orthogonal, yet existing OVOD methods treat all regions uniformly. We therefore propose two complementary proxies that directly characterize each failure mode:
signal strength proxy
and
ambiguity proxy
.

To operationalize these two proxies, we first establish cross-modal correspondences at the region level.
Specifically, for each region
r_{i}
in a training image
I\in D_{src}
, we extract its visual feature
f_{r_{i}}=\mathscr{F}_{\text{vision}}(r_{i})
. To enhance robustness, we also generate an augmented view
\tilde{I}=\mathcal{A}(I)
and obtain the corresponding feature
\tilde{f}_{r_{i}}=\mathscr{F}_{\text{vision}}(r_{i};\tilde{I})
. Each visual feature is mapped to the textual embedding space via the cross-modal projection
\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}
, yielding pseudo-word prototypes:

w_{r_{i}}=\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}(f_{r_{i}}),\quad\tilde{w}_{r_{i}}=\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}(\tilde{f}_{r_{i}}).

(2)

Let
s^{+}_{r_{i}}
denote the positive alignment strength between the region feature and its matched semantic prototype, and let
s^{-}_{r_{i}}
represent the strongest confounding negative similarity from a candidate set
\mathcal{Z}^{-}
, comprising in-batch pseudo-word prototypes and cross-batch memory samples. Since all pseudo-word prototypes are grounded in text embeddings
t_{c}
via
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ovod}}
,
\mathcal{Z}^{-}
implicitly encodes semantic language supervision, ensuring that
h_{r_{i}}
and
q_{r_{i}}
reflect genuine cross-modal confusion:

\displaystyle s^{+}_{r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{sim}(f_{r_{i}},w_{r_{i}}),

(3)

\displaystyle s^{-}_{r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}^{-}}\mathbf{sim}(f_{r_{i}},z),

where
\mathbf{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity. And we define the
signal strength proxy

q_{r_{i}}
as

q_{r_{i}}\triangleq s^{+}_{r_{i}}.

(4)

A larger
q_{r_{i}}
indicates stronger activation of the matched semantic prototype, suggesting that the visual feature carries a clear and reliable semantic signal. Samples with low
q_{r_{i}}
typically correspond to noisy, occluded, or background-dominated regions, where effective information transfer is limited regardless of their relative discriminative difficulty.

We define the
ambiguity proxy

h_{r_{i}}
as a margin-based proxy:

h_{r_{i}}\triangleq s^{-}_{r_{i}}-s^{+}_{r_{i}}.

(5)

Larger values of
h_{r_{i}}
indicate that the strongest negative is close to the positive similarity, implying higher posterior ambiguity. Such samples typically lie near the decision boundary. Although they are informative for refining discriminative structure, introducing too many highly ambiguous samples in the early stages may destabilize training under domain shift.
Guided by this perspective, we decouple curriculum scheduling and quality control:

1.

Curriculum via Ambiguity.
We partition the visual feature and the semantic prototype into
difficulty tiers (
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{Hard}},\mathcal{Q}_{\text{Medium}},\mathcal{Q}_{\text{Easy}}
) according to
h_{r_{i}}
, gradually expanding
training from low-ambiguity to high-ambiguity regimes as the model
becomes better calibrated.

2.

Soft Gate via Signal.
Within each tier, regions in the
bottom
p_{q}
percentile of
q_{r_{i}}
receive a penalty

\delta{=}2.0
on their normalized score
\hat{h}_{i}
, lowering their
sampling priority and preventing the model from overfitting to
signal-collapsed regions.

Curriculum-Guided Cross-Modal Alignment.

The core philosophy of our curriculum is to build a geometric anchor in the latent space before exposing the model to the high-variance regions characteristic of domain shifts.
As detailed in Algorithm
1
, rather than optimizing all regions simultaneously, we prioritize the stochastic selection of low-ambiguity, high-signal regions in the early training stages. This ensures that the projection layer
\mathscr{P}_{\text{img}\rightarrow\text{text}}
first learns to map clean, representative visual features to their textual prototypes without being distracted by domain-induced noise. As training progresses, we adaptively increase the sampling ratio of higher-ambiguity tiers.

The curriculum loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{curr}}
is computed on the sampled set

\mathcal{R}
, which is obtained by selecting regions according to their ambiguity tiers and signal strength scores as described above.
For each batch, regions from each tier are sampled based on the progressive ratios
(\tilde{r}_{Easy},\tilde{r}_{Medium},\tilde{r}_{Hard})
and ranked by signal strength proxy
\hat{q}
, ensuring that low

\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{curr}}=\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathcal{N}(\tilde{f}_{r},\{w_{r^{\prime}}\}_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}})+\mathcal{N}(\tilde{w}_{r},\{\tilde{f}_{r^{\prime}}\}_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}})\big],

This objective enforces visual consistency as a regularizer, ensuring that the learned pseudo-word prototypes remain robust to local perturbations and latent space collapse.

Experiment

We compare PICA with state-of-the-art Open-Vocabulary Object Detection (OVOD) methods and conduct detailed ablation studies to analyze the effect of each component.

We train a two-stage OVOD model: Faster-RCNN

]

Mixed precision is enabled via AmpOptimWrapper, and gradients are accumulated every two iterations, resulting in an effective batch size of 64.

Both image and text features are divided into three tiers, with

\lambda_{curr}=1.0

Following Baron
[

]

For fair comparison, Baron

Conclusion

We present PICA for Domain-Generalized Open-Vocabulary Object Detection, establishing the first systematic evidence that
cross-modal alignment instability is the binding constraint on open-vocabulary generalization under distribution shifts. Our benchmark and findings reveal a blind spot shared across existing OVOD methods, and demonstrate that progressive domain-invariant cross-modal alignment provides a principled path toward truly domain-generalized open-vocabulary systems beyond static laboratory conditions.
