Fast Computation of Conditional Probabilities in MDPs and Markov Chain Families
Milan Češka, Sebastian Junges, Luko van der Maas, Filip Macák, Tim Quatmann

Abstract
Computing optimal conditional reachability probabilities in Markov decision processes (MDPs) is tractable by a reduction to reachability probabilities. Yet, this reduction yields cyclic, challenging MDPs that are often notoriously hard to solve. We present an alternative, practically efficient method to compute optimal conditional reachabilities. This new method is numerically stable, can decide the threshold problem in linear time on acyclic MDPs, and yields performance comparable to standard reachability queries. We also integrate the method in an abstraction-refinement framework to analyse millions of Markov chains at once. We demonstrate the efficacy of the new methods on benchmarks from Bayesian network analysis, probabilistic programs, and runtime monitoring and show speed-ups up to multiple orders of magnitude.

Introduction

Markov chains (MCs) are a standard model for stochastic processes. The standard reachability query on Markov chains is, e.g., ‘What is the probability that one reaches an airport before the plane departs?’ As a variation, we study ‘What is the probability that one reaches the airport, if you already know that the buses will have at least a ten-minute delay?’ Such a query is a typical example for
conditional reachability probabilities

[
2
]
.
Intuitively, conditional reachability asks for reachability probabilities not over all paths from the initial state, but over paths that match the given evidence. It can be used to implicitly define an alternative initial state, that is obtained from observing a running system.
Conditional reachability probabilities have prominent applications:
In runtime monitoring, one aims to compute the probability of an imminent threat conditioned on the previous observations by the monitor
[
34
]
.
In Bayesian networks and MCs derived from them
[
31
]
, one computes the probability of an event like a break-in conditioned on evidence like a burglary alarm.
In probabilistic programs and their MC semantics, one aims to compute the probability of a return value conditioned on known inputs, e.g.,
[
23
]
.
These examples of conditional reachability probabilities can be efficiently computed
[
7
]
on reasonably-sized MCs using modern probabilistic model checking tools
[
22
,
15
]
. In this paper, we bring this performance to Markov decision processes and to Markov chain families by a fresh perspective.

Towards MDPs.

Many systems cannot be modelled concisely as a Markov chain:
Not all behavior can be captured probabilistically; probabilities may not be known, or the stochastic process must be abstracted by a smaller state space.
These observations are standard motivations to add nondeterminism to the model formalism and to thus consider
Markov Decision Processes
(MDPs). The ability to compute optimal (i.e., maximal/minimal) conditional reachability probabilities in MDPs then allows us to bound the conditional reachability probability in models for runtime monitors with nondeterministic agents
[
24
]
or with learned probabilities
[
33
]
, Bayesian networks with interval-valued probabilities
[
32
]
, or probabilistic programs under abstractions
[
13
]
or with explicit nondeterminism
[
18
]
.

A Bayesian perspective.

Computing conditional reachability probabilities in MCs is straightforward by applying Bayes’ theorem: The probability to reach the goal states
G
conditioned on also reaching evidence states
E
can be expressed as the fraction of two reachability probabilities: The probability to reach
G
and
E
divided by the probability to reach
E
, i.e.,

\Pr(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)~=~\frac{\Pr(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)}{\Pr(\lozenge E)}.

The upper probability can be computed on the product of a small DFA and the original Markov chain.
In MDPs, however, we optimize over the policies that resolve the nondeterminism, which prevents calculating the numerator and denominator independently:

\max_{\sigma}\frac{\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)}{\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge E)}~\neq~\frac{\max_{\sigma}\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)}{\max_{\sigma}\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge E)}.

The restart method.

Baier
et al.

[
7
]
provide a mathematically elegant solution to this challenge, basically mimicking the key idea of rejection sampling. Any probability mass that never satisfies the condition
E
cannot be reached must be redistributed (i.e., restarted). In terms of the MDP, this means that for all states from which
E
cannot be reached, the transitions are rewired to the initial state. In these new (restart) MDPs, one simply computes the probability to eventually reach the goal! The downside is that this method introduces a lot of loops, in particular even when the original MDP was acyclic
[
26
]
. MDPs with cycles back to the initial states are among the most challenging for methods based on value iteration
[
19
]
. Indeed, some(!) restart MDPs are known as very challenging benchmarks of standard reachability queries
[
26
,
20
]
.

A multiobjective perspective.

Intuitively, we consider conditional reachability queries as a variation of a bi-objective model checking problem
[
14
]
: Maximize the probability to reach
G
and
E
, while minimizing the probability to reach
E
. Consider the decision problem whether
\max_{\sigma}\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)\geq\lambda
.
Using Bayes’ law, some reordering, and some side-conditions detailed below, this can be seen as

\textstyle\max_{\sigma}\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)-\lambda\Pr_{\sigma}(\lozenge E)\geq 0.

This query can be efficiently computed (in practice and theory) using one so-called
total reward query

[
15
,
30
]
and is a simple instance of a
relational reachability property

[
16
]
. If the original MDP is acyclic this is possible in linear time with a numerically stable method.

Optimal conditional probabilities.

The decision method above gives rise to a method to approximate the optimal conditional reachability probability, using a standard bisection method. We accelerate this method using a smarter heuristic. Further, note that we also support computing the optimal conditional reachability probability. The main argument for the bisection to terminate is that the solution can be represented as a reachability probability
[
7
]
and that these must be a rational number representable by a bounded number of bits
[
12
]
. We therefore adapt a routine that bisects this set of bounded rationals
[
27
]
and extend it with termination criteria based on the computed optimal policies.

Towards Markov chain families.

Beyond MDPs, we are interested in analysing Markov chain families, i.e., large sets of Markov chains. Our interest is twofold:
(1) Markov chain families can capture uncertainty about the topology of a system and can capture systems that can be configured in various ways. Such families occur, e.g., when analysing (purely) probabilistic programs with non-deterministic inputs
[
10
,
13
]
.
(2) Given sets of states
E_{1},\dots E_{n}
, we are interested whether there is some evidence
E_{i}
such that
\Pr(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E_{i})\geq\lambda
. This question is natural in runtime monitoring
[
5
,
25
]
, where evidence itself may be uncertain and programs that encode a secret input (e.g., ciphers, see Section
6
), where we want to ensure a secret cannot be reasonably guessed from any possible evidence.

Embedding into abstraction-refinement.

We use a standard representation of Markov chain families using
colored MDPs
, where every color-consistent policy of the MDP represents a Markov chain
[
3
]
.
To solve colored MDPs, we use abstraction-refinement
[
3
]
, the state-of-the-art method for solving standard reachability queries in colored MDPs. However, the shift to conditional probabilities brings various challenges, that prevent simply applying previous methods: The optimal policies in (standard) MDPs are not memoryless and we only get a value for the initial state. Our experiments show that, nevertheless, abstraction-refinement can bound conditional probabilities on millions of MCs at once.

Related work.

The previous state-of-the-art is the restart method, outlined above, which was experimented with in
[
26
]
. The approach in this paper is completely different and rooted in multi-objective MDP model checking. To the best of our knowledge, the first MDP model checking algorithm for conditional reachability probabilities is presented in
[
2
]
: For acyclic MDPs, it suggests a method that computes numerator and denominator under each policy, along with some pruning that is broadly related to multiobjective model checking. However, the approach remains exponential and highly inefficient.

Our approach of tackling the threshold problem and using that as a subroutine also appears in work on conditional expected rewards
[
8
]
. Deciding the threshold problem for conditional expected rewards is already PSPACE-complete for acyclic MDPs, in stark contrast to our linear time algorithm. Whereas
[
8
]
focusses on establishing this and other complexity bounds, we present a concise algorithm with a focus on (practical) performance: We use value iteration over LPs, we efficiently extract policies, and use efficient bisection methods.

The recent work
[
13
]
develops novel probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) to solve various optimization problems over probabilistic inference. It explicitly remarks that computing conditional probabilities on MDPs directly is ill-supported. With this paper, we make reasoning about the operational semantics of PPLs with conditioning more feasible.

Contributions.

In summary, this paper yields a significant step forward in computing conditional probabilities on MDPs, building upon progress in (multiobjective) model checking from the last decade. In particular, we provide an empirically superior method for computing optimal conditional reachability probabilities in MDPs rooted in a reduction to a total reward query paired with an optimised bisection method.
The method is further equipped with a policy extraction technique that enables an effective integration into the abstraction refinement scheme to solve conditional reachability queries on colored MDPs. Our experiments on benchmarks from several application domains demonstrate speed-ups up to multiple orders of magnitude compared to the restart method
[
7
]
.

Preliminaries

The set of probability distributions over a finite set
S
is
\mathit{Dist}(S)=\{\mu\colon S\to[0,1]\mid\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)=1\}
,

\mathit{Supp}(\mu)=\{s\in S\mid\mu(s)>0\}
is the support of
\mu\in\mathit{Dist}(S)
.

Definition 1

A
Markov decision process (MDP)
is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},\mathbf{P},{s}^{I})
, where
S
and
\mathit{Act}
are finite sets of states and actions,
{s}^{I}
is an initial state, and
\mathbf{P}\colon S\times\mathit{Act}\rightharpoonup\mathit{Dist}(S)
is a (partial) transition probability function such that

\mathit{Act}(s)=\{\alpha\in\mathit{Act}\mid\mathbf{P}(s,\alpha)\text{ is defined }\}\neq\emptyset
for all
s\in S
.

We fix an MDP
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},\mathbf{P},{s}^{I})
.

\lVert\mathcal{M}\rVert=|\{(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})\mid\mathbf{P}(s,\alpha)(s^{\prime})>0\}|
denotes the size of
\mathcal{M}
. An MDP is
acyclic
if all cycles in its transition graph are self-loops.
A
path
of MDP
\mathcal{M}
is an infinite sequence
\pi=s_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}s_{1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}\dots
with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathit{Act}(s_{i})
and
s_{i+1}\in\mathit{Supp}(\mathbf{P}(s_{i},\alpha_{i}))
for all
i\geq 0
.

\mathit{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes the set of paths of
\mathcal{M}
.
A finite path
\hat{\pi}=s_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}\dots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n-1}}s_{n}
is a finite prefix of a path ending in state
\mathit{last}(\hat{\pi})=s_{n}
.
For a set of states
G\subseteq S
we define the set of paths that eventually reach a state in
G
as

\lozenge G~=~\big\{s_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}s_{1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}\dots\in\mathit{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrel{\bigm|}s_{i}\in G\text{ for some }i\geq 0\big\}.

A
policy

\sigma
for
\mathcal{M}
maps finite paths
\hat{\pi}
of
\mathcal{M}
to enabled actions
\sigma(\hat{\pi})\in\mathit{Act}(\mathit{last}(\hat{\pi}))
.
The set of all policies for
\mathcal{M}
is denoted as
\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}}
, when
\mathcal{M}
is clear from context, we also write
\Sigma
.
A memoryless policy satisfies
\sigma(\hat{\pi})=\sigma(\hat{\pi}^{\prime})
for all finite paths
\hat{\pi}
and
\hat{\pi}^{\prime}
with
\mathit{last}(\hat{\pi})=\mathit{last}(\hat{\pi}^{\prime})
.
As usual, we also write
\sigma\colon S\rightarrow\mathit{Act}
for memoryless policies, mapping last states to actions.
MDP
\mathcal{M}
and policy
\sigma
for
\mathcal{M}
induce a probability measure
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
on measurable sets of paths defined via a standard cylinder set construction
[
6
]
.
For two measurable sets of paths
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\subseteq\mathit{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}
, the conditional probability of
\Pi_{1}
given
\Pi_{2}
is defined as

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{1}\mid\Pi_{2})~=~\nicefrac{{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{1}\cap\Pi_{2})}}{{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{2})}}.

The probability is undefined iff
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{2})=0
.
We define

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\Pi_{1}\mid\Pi_{2})~=~~\sup\big\{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{1}\mid\Pi_{2})\mathrel{\bigm|}\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}}\text{ with }\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Pi_{2})\neq 0\big\}.

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\min}(\Pi_{1}\mid\Pi_{2})
is defined similarly.
When
\mathcal{M}
is clear from context and
s\in S
is a state of
\mathcal{M}
, we also write
\mathrm{Pr}_{s}^{\sigma}
,
\mathrm{Pr}_{s}^{\max}
, and
\mathrm{Pr}_{s}^{\min}
for the corresponding probability measures that arise from replacing the initial state of
\mathcal{M}
by
s
.

A
reward function

\mathcal{R}\colon S\times\mathit{Act}\times S\to\mathbb{R}
for MDP
\mathcal{M}
assigns a reward to transitions. The reward accumulated on a path

\pi=s_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}s_{1}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}\dots
until reaching a set
T\subseteq S
is given by

\mathcal{R}_{\lozenge T}(\pi)=\sum_{0\leq i<\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid s_{n}\in T\}}\mathcal{R}(s_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i+1})
.
1
1

1

In our setting, this sum always converges to a finite value. Generally, it might be infinite or undefined—due to possible mixtures of positive and negative rewards.

The
expected total reward
of
\mathcal{M}
for
\mathcal{R}_{\lozenge T}
under a policy
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}}
is the expected value of the accumulated reward:
\mathrm{Ex}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\mathcal{R}_{\lozenge T})~=~\int_{\pi}\mathcal{R}_{\lozenge T}(\pi)\,d\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\pi)
.
As for
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
, we write
\mathrm{Ex}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}
and
\mathrm{Ex}_{s}^{\max}
for the
maximal
expected values (assuming initial state
s\in S
).
The values
\mathrm{Ex}_{s}^{\max}(\mathcal{R}_{\lozenge T})
for each
s\in S
coincide with the least fixed point
\vec{x}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
of the
Bellman operator

\Phi\colon(S\to\mathbb{R})\to(S\to\mathbb{R})
given by

\Phi(\vec{x})(s)~=~\max_{\alpha\in\mathit{Act}(s)}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\mathbf{P}(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})\cdot\big(\vec{x}(s^{\prime})+\mathcal{R}(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})\big)
for
s\notin T
and
\Phi(\vec{x})(s)=0
for
s\in T
.
Common algorithms for computing
\mathrm{Ex}_{s}^{\max}(\mathcal{R})
for each
s\in S
are based on linear programming, policy iteration, and value iteration.
When mixtures of positive and negative rewards are present (as in our case), these algorithms commonly require collapsing end components as a preprocessing to ensure a
unique
fixed point of the Bellman operator
[
1
]
.

Deciding Conditional Reachability Properties

This section presents our new approach for answering the following problem.

{problemstatement}

[Conditional Reachability Threshold Problem]

Given an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with sets of states
G,E\subseteq S
and a threshold
\lambda\in[0,1]
, decide whether
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)\leq\lambda
.

For the rest of the section, we fix an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with state sets
G,E\subseteq S
.
We assume that the conditional probability
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)
is defined, i.e.,
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\lozenge E)>0
for some
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}}
.
This assumption can be checked using standard qualitative MDP model checking algorithms
[
6
]
.
The restart method outlined in
[
7
]
provides a polynomial time algorithm for computing
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)
and thus for solving
Section

3
.
Roughly, the approach constructs a new MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{M}
, where transitions leading to states that do not reach the condition

are rewired to the initial state.
The conditional reachability probability
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)\leq\lambda
in
\mathcal{M}
coincides with the (unconditional) reachability probability
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}^{\max}(\lozenge G)
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
The latter can be computed using standard MDP model checking methods.
Since
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
has approximately the same size as
\mathcal{M}
, the linear programming approach yields a polynomial time algorithm for
Section

3

Theorem 3.1

For
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
{\sim}\in\{<,\leq,=,\geq,>\}
:

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)~\sim~\lambda\quad\textrm{iff}\quad\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M},E}}\Big(\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)-\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\lozenge E)\Big)~\sim~0.

Using
Theorem

3.1
, our approach is to answer

3

V(\lambda)~\coloneq~\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M},E}}V^{\sigma}(\lambda)\quad\text{with}\quad V^{\sigma}(\lambda)~\coloneq~\Big(\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)-\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\lozenge E)\Big)

and comparing it against 0. The computation of
V(\lambda)
is done in two steps. (1) Restate
V^{\sigma}(\lambda)
as an expected total reward. (2) Construct an equivalent MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for which the set
E
is reached with positive probability under all policies (i.e.,
\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime},E}=\Sigma_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
).
After step (2), standard MDP algorithms for expected total rewards can be employed to decide
V(\lambda)\leq 0
.

Step 1: Reward Characterization.

The value
V(\lambda)
above is characterized using an expected total reward for the reward function
\mathcal{R}^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
, given as:

\mathcal{R}^{\lambda}(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})~=~\begin{cases}\mathrm{Pr}_{s^{\prime}}^{\max}(\lozenge G)-\lambda&\text{if }s^{\prime}\in E\\
\mathrm{Pr}_{s^{\prime}}^{\max}(\lozenge E)\cdot(1-\lambda)&\text{if }s^{\prime}\in G\setminus E\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Intuitively, once a state in
E
is reached, a policy
\sigma
that maximizes
V^{\sigma}(\lambda)
as above must maximize the probability to also reach
G
.
Similarly, once a state in
G
is reached, such a policy maximizes the probability to also reach
E
.

Lemma 1

\displaystyle V(\lambda)=\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M},E}}\mathrm{Ex}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}\big(\mathcal{R}^{\lambda}_{\lozenge G\cup E}\big)

(sketch)

The (maximal) expected values of

Step 2a: Preprocessing
\mathcal{M}
.

The example above does not yet discuss a method to find a policy
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{M},E}
. We first simplify the expected total reward query by considering the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{\circlearrowright}
obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by making the states in
G\cup E
absorbing, i.e., all outgoing transitions of each state
s\in G\cup E
are replaced by a self-loop
s\xrightarrow{\bot}s
.
For ease of notation, reward functions
\mathcal{R}
for
\mathcal{M}
also apply to
\mathcal{M}_{\circlearrowright}
, where
\mathcal{R}(s,\bot,s)=0
for all
s\in G\cup E
.
On
\mathcal{M}_{\circlearrowright}
, we consider a new set of terminal states

T~\coloneq~E\cup\big\{s\in G\mathrel{\bigm|}\mathrm{Pr}_{s}^{\max}(\lozenge E)>0\big\}.

Intuitively, paths of

Conditional Reachability in Colored MDPs

Colored MDPs
[
3
,
21
]
extend MDPs by grouping states. Intuitively, policies must take the same action across a group. This model captures various synthesis problems where additional constraints on policies are imposed.
Each policy
\sigma
that satisfies these constraints induces an MC
\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}
. Colored MDPs thus compactly represent families of MCs, each induced by such a policy.

This section shows how the methods above can be effectively combined with an abstraction-refinement (AR) method
[
3
]
to solve conditional reachability problem for families of MCs, represented by colored MDPs. In the experiments, we use this for probabilistic program verification and monitoring benchmarks.

For conciseness, we use a simplified definition of colored MDPs from
[
25
]
:

Definition 3
(Colored MDPs)

A colored
MDP is a tuple
\mathcal{M}^{C}=(\mathcal{M},C,c)
, where
\mathcal{M}
is an MDP with states
S
,
C
is a set of colors, and
c\colon S\rightarrow C
.

Definition 4
(Color-consistent policy)

A memoryless policy
\sigma
for a colored MDP
is
color consistent
, if for all states
s
,
s^{\prime}
:
c(s)=c(s^{\prime})
implies
\sigma(s)=\sigma(s^{\prime})
.

Policy synthesis for colored MDPs asks for a color-consistent policy such that the reachability probability exceeds a given threshold. This problem is NP-hard, but efficient heuristics based on abstraction-refinement, counterexample generalization, and SMT exist
[
3
,
21
]
. We lift the problem to conditional reachability.

{problemstatement}

and

Abstraction Refinement for Colored MDPs.

Consider a colored MDP
\mathcal{M}^{C}=(\mathcal{M},C,c)
and a constraint
\geq\lambda
.
Since the consistent policies in
\mathcal{M}^{C}
are a subset of all policies,
\mathcal{M}
provides a sound abstraction of
\mathcal{M}^{C}
. Therefore the policy
\sigma_{\max}
maximising the conditional probability
Pr_{\mathcal{M}}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)
in the MDP
\mathcal{M}
provides the upper bound
ub
on maximal conditional probability achieved by any consistent policy in
\mathcal{M}^{C}
.
If
ub<\lambda
then all policies, including the consistent policies violate
\geq\lambda
and thus there is no consistent policy satisfying the constraint. If
ub\geq\lambda
and
\sigma_{\max}
is consistent, we have the solution of
Section

5

The goal of the refinement is to remove the inconsistent policy
\sigma_{\max}
from the policies in the abstraction. This is achieved by splitting the set of policies in
\mathcal{M}
into two (disjoint) subsets such that
\sigma_{\max}
is not included in either subset. Let
\sigma_{\max}
be inconsistent in the states
s
and
s^{\prime}
, i.e.
c(s)=c(s^{\prime})
while
\sigma(s)=\alpha
and
\sigma(s^{\prime})=\beta
(where
\alpha
and
\beta
are two different actions). We can efficiently represent the subsets by two (colored) sub-MDPs
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. In
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, we disable
\alpha
in states
s
,
s^{\prime}
while in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, we disable
\beta
. Any consistent policy from
\mathcal{M}
exists in either
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Hence, the AR leads to an iterative algorithm that analyses a sequence of colored MDPs
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{C}=(\mathcal{M}_{i},C,c)

\mathcal{M}_{i}^{C}
is either discarded (if

), or refined. The finite number of policies implies termination of the algorithm.

Conclusion

Our novel approach to compute conditional reachability probabilities in MDPs clearly outperforms the previous state of the art. We show the benefits for various benchmarks from the literature, for Markov chain families, and in runtime monitoring applications. We would like to lift these approaches to symbolic state spaces and programs, as well as to interval MDPs or stochastic games. The extension towards Markov chain families raises additional questions how to generally handle specifications that require history-dependent policies. We furthermore hope to integrate support for conditional reachability into
[
21
]
, which allows reasoning about families while directly supporting assumptions, e.g., on the reachability of
E
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge G\mid\lozenge E)~

\displaystyle=~\lambda+\frac{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{*}}(\lozenge G\cap\lozenge E)-\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{*}}(\lozenge E)}{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{*}}(\lozenge E)}~\leq~\lambda+\frac{V(\lambda)}{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{*}}(\lozenge E)}.

Using these inequalities, the lemma follow immediately as

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\min}(\lozenge E)~\leq~\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{\lambda}}(\lozenge E)\,,~\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma^{*}}(\lozenge E)~\leq~\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\lozenge E).
