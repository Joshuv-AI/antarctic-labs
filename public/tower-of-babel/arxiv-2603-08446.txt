Abstract
. We present a general sparse domination principle which respects the cancellative structure of the functions under study. We obtain sparse domination results in general measure spaces, including general martingale settings in one and two parameters, and in the Euclidean setting. In the one-parameter martingale setting, we obtain a sparse characterization of the H 1 H^{1} norm. The proofs make critical use of precise level-set estimates for generalized versions of medians. Our results imply new, quantitatively sharp, weighted results for martingales and Calderón-Zygmund operators acting on H p H^{p} spaces.

Introduction

Many results in analysis involve the precise description of certain features of functions: size, speed of growth, mass, oscillation… A modern technique to study the size of functions which has been used very frequently in harmonic analysis is that of sparse domination. The technique was initiated by Lerner
[
Ler13b
,
Ler13a
]
as an alternative approach to the proof of the
A_{2}
theorem based on domination of functions, instead of their representation in terms of appropriate dyadic objects
[
PV02
,
Hyt12
]
. The subsequent developments over the past fifteen years have resulted in precise weighted bounds in
L^{p}
for numerous operators, including martingale transforms
[
Lac19
]
and Calderón-Zygmund operators
[
CR16
,
LN19
]
, and even some new characterizations of endpoint estimates
[
CCDO17
]
, to cite a few applications.

Sparse domination for an operator
T
is an estimate of the form

(SizeSparse)

|Tf|\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|,

where the inequality may hold pointwise or in some weaker form. The operator
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}
is an averaging operator over a family of sets
\mathcal{S}
:

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f\rangle_{Q}\ind_{Q}(x),

where
\langle f\rangle:=\frac{1}{\lvert Q\rvert}\int_{Q}f
.
The family
\mathcal{S}
depends on
f
but is always
\eta
-sparse for some
0<\eta<1
that does not depend on
f
. One of the equivalent ways to define an
\eta
-sparse collection
\mathcal{S}
is to say that for each
Q\in\mathcal{S}
there exists
E_{Q}\subset Q
such that
|E_{Q}|\geq\eta|Q|
and the sets
E_{Q}
are pairwise disjoint.

Sparse domination has been a very successful tool to analyze the
L^{p}
-norms of operators and functions, and especially so to tackle delicate quantitative questions pertaining to the
L^{1}
(or weak-
L^{1}
) norm. All available sparse domination techniques are critically based on an estimate for an operator that does not see any cancellation present in the function
f
. Indeed, sparse families are generally constructed using the level sets of some operator for which a local weak-type estimate is available, which is the key to averaging over a family that ends up being sparse. The process eliminates any information about the cancellation –or the smoothness– of the functions involved, and while that is harmless for weak-type estimates (or strong-type ones), one cannot hope to get any results applicable to spaces in which cancellation is essential, like the Hardy space
H^{1}
. Indeed,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}|f|
is not an
L^{1}
function, so (
SizeSparse
) does not recover the
H^{1}\to L^{1}
boundedness of
T
when it holds. This is easiest to illustrate in the dyadic setting: if we let
\mathscr{D}
be the standard dyadic grid and define

(MaxD)

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}f(x)=\sup_{x\in Q\in\mathscr{D}}|\langle f\rangle_{Q}|,

then
\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\mathscr{D}}}=\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}f\|_{1}
, and one is immediately tempted to search for sparse domination results of the form

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}f\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathscr{D}}|\langle f\rangle_{Q}|\ind_{Q}.

We shall see in the body of the paper that such an estimate cannot hold in general. Nevertheless, our goal is to give a form of sparse domination that preserves the cancellation properties of the function. What we shall do is to reinforce the right hand side of the desired inequality by precomposing with an operator which is bounded on
L^{p}
for
0<p<\infty
. Said operator is the percentile maximal function. Given
0<r<1
, a set of positive and finite measure
\Omega
and a locally integrable function
f
, we set

\mathsf{P}_{\Omega}^{r}(f):=\inf\Big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\,|\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)>\lambda\}|\leq r|\Omega|\Big\}.

We have

\displaystyle|\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)>\mathsf{P}_{\Omega}^{r}(f)\}|

\displaystyle\leq r|\Omega|

\displaystyle|\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)<\mathsf{P}_{\Omega}^{r}(f)\}|

\displaystyle\leq(1-r)|\Omega|,

so
\mathsf{P}_{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f)
is nothing but a choice of a median of
f
over
\Omega
. Given any value of
r
,
f
lies below
\mathsf{P}^{r}_{\Omega}(f)
on a portion of
\Omega
of measure
(1-r)|\Omega|
. Therefore, we interpret
\mathsf{P}^{1-r}_{\Omega}(f)
as the
r
-th percentile of
f
over
\Omega
. We form the maximal function

\mathcal{P}^{r}_{\mathscr{D}}f(x)=\sup_{x\in Q\in\mathscr{D}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(|f|).

When no confusion may arise, the precise value of
r
is both fixed and unimportant, so we omit it from the notation. Our first sparse domination result can be applied to many natural operators in dyadic or, more generally, martingale settings. For the latter context, we postpone introducing the terminology and associated technicalities to the body of the paper. Likewise, we postpone the definition of the operators under consideration until we specifically deal with them.

Theorem A
.

Let
T
be a martingale transform, a Haar shift operator, or
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
. There exists
0<r<1
such that for each
f\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there is a sparse
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathscr{D}
so that

Tf(x)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f)(x)\ind_{Q}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Theorem
A
is based on the local inequality

\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{Cr}(Tf)\lesssim_{r}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}(f)),\quad\mbox{for some }C>1.

In the proof, we use it to bypass the weak-type
(1,1)
inequality for the non-cancellative version of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
, which is used in one way or another in all of the existent sparse domination principles and which makes cancellative estimates hopeless. We shall prove Theorem
A
via the corresponding estimates for maximal truncations of the operators under study. We shall show in the body of the paper that the composite sparse operator

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f)\ind_{Q}(x)

maps
H_{\mathscr{D}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is because
\mathcal{P}^{r}_{\mathscr{D}}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
0<p\leq\infty
, and therefore

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x):=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(f)\ind_{Q}(x)

is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
0<p<\infty
. Hence, Theorem
A
allows one to recover the known Hardy space endpoint boundedness results for
T
. As we mentioned before, our arguments can be extended to general probability spaces, assuming the underlying filtration is regular. We show that the assumption of regularity is necessary in general.

Theorem
A
is an
\ell^{1}
cancellative estimate. In sparse domination, the easiest operators to study are often single-scale ones, of which maximal functions like the one above are the archetypal example. For the maximal operator in the non-cancellative setting one can easily prove the stronger
\ell^{\infty}
-estimate

\sup_{x\in Q\in\mathscr{D}}\langle|f|\rangle_{Q}\lesssim\sup_{x\in Q\in\mathcal{S}}\langle|f|\rangle_{Q},

with
\mathcal{S}
being sparse and dependent on
f
. In the body of the paper, we will give a cancellative analogue of this result in terms of the sparse maximal function
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}
–defined similarly to (
MaxD
) but with the sup taken over a sparse subfamily of
\mathscr{D}
–, which in turn yields a characterization of the dyadic
H^{1}
norm that we consider interesting in its own right.

Theorem B
.

Let
f\in H_{\mathscr{D}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\|f\|_{H_{\mathscr{D}}^{1}}\eqsim\sup_{\mathcal{S}\;\mbox{sparse}}\left\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f\right\|_{L^{1}}\eqsim\sup_{\mathcal{S}\;\mbox{sparse}}\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\right\|_{L^{1}}.

Theorem
B
is a sparse characterization of the
H_{\mathscr{D}}^{1}
norm. As we said above, its proof uses the cancellative sparse
\ell^{\infty}
-domination result for
\mathcal{M}
. We emphasize that the corresponding non-cancellative estimates do not yield a useful characterization in this setting, and we shall provide evidence of this in the body of the paper. Although above we formulated Theorem
B
for the (regular) dyadic martingale, the statement holds in any
\sigma
-finite filtered space. There are a few technicalities involved in the formulation of the corresponding results and the objects involved, which we postpone to Section
1
. Our sparse domination principle for single-scale operators is very flexible: neither the regularity of the filtration is needed, nor is it important that we have a family of nested partitions. Leveraging the fact that we do not need our operators to satisfy a weak-
L^{1}
estimate, we show that a cancellative sparse domination result holds for the (cancellative) strong maximal function. This can be defined as follows:

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q_{1}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\\
Q_{2}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})\end{subarray}}\sup_{x\in Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\left|\langle f\rangle_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\right|.

The corresponding maximal function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}}^{r}
is defined in the expected way, and we call any set
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
as above a dyadic rectangle. Our result reads as follows:

Theorem C
.

For each
f\in L^{\infty}(d_{1}+d_{2})
, there exists a sparse family
\mathcal{S}
of dyadic rectangles such that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}}f(x)\lesssim\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}.

The statement will be deduced from a general procedure, reminiscent of
[
CF75
]
, which allows one to always extract a sparse subfamily
\mathcal{S}
from any finite family of sets
\mathcal{E}
. Theorem
C
is the first biparametric sparse domination result, and is in sharp contrast with the negative result from
[
BCOR19
]
, which proved that the
non-cancellative sparse bound fails for the strong maximal function.

In the second part of the paper, we turn our attention to cancellative estimates in the Euclidean setting. We restrict our attention to Calderón-Zygmund theory, although we expect that our ideas can be pushed to many other operators of interest which exhibit similar behavior near the
H^{1}\to L^{1}
endpoint, or to the setting of
[
BFP16
]
. A Calderón-Zygmund operator is an
L^{2}
-bounded operator
T
with an associated kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{x=y\}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the representation

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)f(y)dy\quad\mbox{holds for}\;f\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;x\not\in\supp(f).

We assume that
K
satisfies the size condition
|K(x,y)|\lesssim|x-y|^{-d}
and, more importantly, a smoothness estimate of order
s
for some
s>0
. When
0<s<1
, it reduces to the classical Hölder regularity of the kernel, while higher values of
s
involve higher order differentiability properties for
K
. The details are postponed to Section
3
. This is by no means the weakest possible pointwise smoothness condition one can impose on
K
so that one gets meaningful
L^{p}
-boundedness results in the Banach range, but it is important in obtaining boundedness results in
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
0<p<1
. Calderón-Zygmund operators
T
with kernels satisfying the smoothness assumptions that we use in this paper are known to be representable as averages of Haar shift averages. However, we will justify in the body of the paper that said representation cannot be combined with Theorem
A
to get a sparse domination result for
T
. Instead, the role of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
in the continuous theory is played by a different, cancellative grand maximal function. Given a cube
Q
, denote by
\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)
the family of
L^{\infty}
-normalized
s
-smooth bump functions supported on
Q
. Then we define

\mathcal{M}_{Q}^{s}f(x)=\sup_{x\in R\subset Q}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)}\frac{1}{|R|}\left|\int_{R}f(y)\varphi(y)dy\right|.

The role of the above maximal function in the continuous setting is the same as that of the localized dyadic maximal function in the dyadic context. Our sparse domination for Calderón-Zygmund operators reads as follows:

Theorem D
.

Let
s>0
and
T
be an
s
-smooth Calderón-Zygmund operator. Let
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be supported on a cube
Q_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then there exists
r\in(0,1)
, only depending on
d
, and a
\tfrac{1}{2}
-sparse family
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathscr{D}(Q_{0})
such that

|Tf(x)|\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}(f))\ind_{Q}(x),\qquad x\in Q_{0}.

Theorem
D
’s statement is almost the same as that of Theorem
A
. As in that case, it recovers the
H^{1}-L^{1}
endpoint estimate for Calderón-Zygmund operators with Hölder smooth kernel. Moreover, one can also recover the boundedness from
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
when
p\in(d/(d+s),1]
from the statement, which coincides exactly with the optimal range of the classical theory, using the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
. This complements the known size results from sparse domination theory and can also be compared to the recent wavelet representation results that preserve smoothness, like
[
HL22
,
DWW23
]
. The latter are useful to study, for example, Sobolev norms, while our result is better suited for cancellative estimates of Hardy-space type.

The key technical novelty in our proof is the replacement of the standard determination of the sparse family as the level set of a maximal function –which must be non-cancellative for the method to work– by a good-
\lambda
type argument. After that step is performed, a tight application of classical Calderón-Zygmund methods allows us to extract the pointwise estimate in the statement. Theorem
D
can be proved by combining these ideas with the general sparse domination principle of
[
LLO22
]
.

Sparse domination was originally developed as a tool to prove sharp weighted norm inequalities, most notably in connection with the so-called
A_{2}
-theorem
[
Hyt12
,
Ler13b
]
. Our cancellative results, Theorems
A
and
D
, recover the familiar weighted estimates on
L^{p}(w)
for
p\in(1,\infty)
and
w\in A_{p}
, the class of Muckenhoupt weights. More interestingly, preserving the cancellation of the input functions allows one to consider estimates beyond the reach of previous sparse domination results, namely in the cases
p\leq 1
and/or
w\notin A_{p}
. In these regimes, the operators we study are not bounded on
L^{p}(w)
, but Hardy space estimates are known qualitatively. In the body of the paper, we obtain sharp quantitative
L^{p}(w)\to L^{p}(w)
results for variants of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
and deduce new weighted inequalities for our operators from them.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
1
contains our estimates for martingale maximal functions and the proof of Theorem
B
. The estimates for multiscale dyadic operators, like martingale transforms and Haar shifts, are in Section
2
. Section
3
is devoted to Calderón-Zygmund theory and other Euclidean results. Finally, in Section
4
we collect the norm estimates that one can deduce as a consequence of our sparse domination result.

1.
General martingales - single-scale operators

We start by presenting our first approach to cancellative domination, which works for general martingales –and of course includes the usual dyadic setting in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as we will detail below–. We work in the generality of filtered probability spaces, which includes the dyadic Euclidean setting of the Introduction. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be a probability space, equipped with a filtration of
\sigma
-algebras
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that
\cup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{k}
generates
\mathcal{F}
. We denote the associated conditional expectations by

f_{k}:=\mathsf{E}_{k}[f]:=\mathsf{E}[f|\mathcal{F}_{k}],\qquad f\in L^{1}(\Omega).

As is standard, we identify an integrable function
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
with the martingale
\{f_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
that converges to it. We also use standard notation for martingale differences:

\displaystyle df_{k}:=\mathsf{D}_{k}(f):=\begin{cases}\mathsf{E}_{k}[f]-\mathsf{E}_{k-1}[f]&\text{if }k\geq 1,\\
\mathsf{E}_{0}[f]&\text{if }k=0.\end{cases}

Fix
0<r<1
and a measurable function
f
. In the martingale setting, the natural analogue of the set-based percentile from the introduction is the following conditional version. Following
[
Tom75
]
, we define a
k
-th conditional percentile of
f
at ratio
r
as any
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable function
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f
with the following property: for each
A\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, the following two inequalities hold:

(1)

\mu(\{x\in A:\,f(x)>\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f(x)\})\leq r\mu(A)

(2)

\mu(\{x\in A:\,f(x)<\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f(x)\})\leq(1-r)\mu(A).

In general, there may be many possible choices for
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}
. For concreteness, we will choose the following:

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f(x):=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{Q}:\,\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{f>t}](x)\leq r\}.

In
[
Tom75
]
, only the case
r=\frac{1}{2}
is considered, and
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}f
is called the
k
-th conditional median of
f
. By Fujii’s theorem
[
Fuj91
]
, which holds in the conditional case as well, we have the pointwise differentiation theorem

\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f\to f,\;k\to\infty,

for all
0<r<1
. Also, by measurability, we have
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{k}=\mathsf{E}_{k}
. The key to our approach is the maximal function associated to the family
\{\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}\}_{k\geq 0}
:

\mathcal{P}_{r}f(x):=\sup_{k\geq 0}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}|f|(x),\qquad x\in\Omega.

The dyadic version of
\mathcal{P}_{r}
was studied in
[
MO11
]
when
r=1/2
. In most instances, the parameter
r
will be fixed from the start and we shall omit it from the notation, writing
\mathsf{P}_{k}f
and
\mathcal{P}f
instead of
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}f
and
\mathcal{P}_{r}f
, respectively. We will also be using truncated versions of
\mathcal{P}
, namely for
\ell\geq 0

\mathcal{P}^{\ell}f(x):=\sup_{k\leq\ell}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}|f|(x),\qquad x\in\Omega.

Lemma 1.1
.

For measurable
f
we have

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{\mathcal{P}f>\lambda\}\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{r}\ \mu\bigl(\{|f|>\lambda\}\bigr),\qquad\lambda\geq 0.

In particular, for
p\in(0,\infty)
we have

\|\mathcal{P}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq r^{-\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
f\geq 0
. Define

\Omega_{\ell}:=\{\mathcal{P}^{\ell}f>\lambda\}\cap\{\mathcal{P}^{\ell-1}f\leq\lambda\},\qquad\ell\geq 0,

using the convention
\mathcal{P}^{-1}f=-1
.
Since
\Omega_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, we have

\mu\bigl(\{x\in\Omega_{\ell}:\,f(x)\leq\lambda\}\bigr)\leq\mu\bigl(\{x\in\Omega_{\ell}:\,f(x)<\mathsf{P}_{\ell}f(x)\}\bigr)\leq(1-r)\mu(\Omega_{\ell}),

and thus

\mu\bigl(\{x\in\Omega_{\ell}:\,f(x)>\lambda\}\bigr)\geq r\mu(\Omega_{\ell}).

Therefore, we can compute

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{\mathcal{P}f>\lambda\}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mu(\Omega_{\ell})\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{1}{r}\ \mu\bigl(\{x\in\Omega_{\ell}:f(x)>\lambda\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r}\ \mu\bigl(\{f>\lambda\}\bigr),

as we wanted. The second claim follows via the layer cake formula.
∎

We now turn to the main goal of this section. The percentile maximal function
\mathcal{P}
is the enlargement that enables cancellative sparse domination of Doob’s maximal function, which for integrable
f
is defined as

\mathcal{M}f(x):=\sup_{k\geq 0}\left|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)\right|,\qquad x\in\Omega.

As with
\mathcal{P}
, we also define the truncated version by

\mathcal{M}^{\ell}f(x)=\sup_{0\leq k\leq\ell}\left|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)\right|,\qquad x\in\Omega.

A sequence of sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is said to be adapted to the filtration if
A_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
for each
k
. For our definition os sparsity, we take inspiration from
[
DPS25
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

For
0<\eta\leq 1
, an adapted sequence
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is said to be
\eta
-sparse if for every
k\geq 0
and every
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable set
A\subseteq S_{k}
we have

\displaystyle\mu\Big(A\setminus\bigcup_{m\geq k+1}S_{m}\Big)\geq\eta\mu(A).

For brevity, we refer to
\frac{1}{2}
-sparse sequences as sparse.

We can give an alternative definition of
\eta
-sparsity using conditional expectations. Indeed, define
S_{\geq k}:=\bigcup_{m\geq k}S_{m}
. Then,
\mathcal{S}
is
\eta
-sparse if and only if for every
k\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}]

\displaystyle\geq\eta

\displaystyle\mu\text{-a.e. on }S_{k},

or equivalently if

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{S_{\geq k+1}}]

\displaystyle\leq 1-\eta

\displaystyle\mu\text{-a.e. on }S_{k}.

Our notion of sparsity here is strictly stronger than the one stated in the Introduction. However, in practice, one usually proves the stronger notion in Definition
1.2
, so we do not lose much generality by using it. We next show that our definition is equivalent to that of Definition 2 in Section 3 of
[
DPS25
]
. There, an increasing sequence
\{\nu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of stopping times is called sparse if the sets
E_{k}=\{\nu_{k}<\infty\}
satisfy the following: for every
A\subseteq E_{k}
such that
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{k}}
,

\mu(A\cap E_{k+1})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\mu(A).

This is equivalent to saying that for each
k

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[\ind_{E_{k+1}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{k}}]\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{$\mu$-a.e. on $E_{k}$}.

From this, it is clear that if for a sparse sequence
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
we define

\displaystyle\nu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{m\geq 0:\,x\in S_{m}\},

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\nu_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{m>\nu_{k-1}(x):\,x\in S_{m}\}

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,\,k\geq 1,

then
\{\nu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an increasing, sparse sequence of stopping times. The other implication in the equivalence between the two definitions is contained in the following lemma.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\{\nu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be a sparse sequence of stopping times. Define

\displaystyle S_{m}:=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\{\nu_{k}=m\}.

Then,
\{S_{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
is sparse.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
and partition
S_{m}
into the disjoint subsets

B_{k}:=\{\nu_{k}=m\}\cap\{\nu_{k-1}<m\},\qquad k\geq 0

using the convention
\nu_{-1}=-1
. We note that

(1.2)

\displaystyle B_{k}\cap S_{\geq m+1}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{\nu_{k+1}<\infty\}=E_{k+1},

since for
x\in B_{k}
we have
\nu_{j}(x)\leq m
for all
j\leq k
, so for
x\in B_{k}\cap S_{\geq m+1}
there must be a
j>k
such that
\nu_{j}(x)<\infty
and hence
\nu_{k+1}(x)<\infty
.

We need to show

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{S_{\geq m+1}}]\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\qquad\text{$\mu$-a.e. on $S_{m}$},

for which it suffices to show that
\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{S_{\geq m+1}}]\leq\frac{1}{2}

\mu
-a.e. on
B_{k}
for every
k\geq 0
. This follows from

\displaystyle\ind_{B_{k}}\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{S_{\geq m+1}}]

\displaystyle\leq\ind_{B_{k}}\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{E_{k+1}}]

\displaystyle=\ind_{B_{k}}\mathsf{E}[\ind_{E_{k+1}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{k}}]\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ind_{B_{k}},

where we have used that
B_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
and (
1.2
) in the first step,
B_{k}\subseteq\{\nu_{k}=m\}
in the second step and sparsity of
\{\nu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
in the third step.
∎

Given an
\eta
-sparse sequence
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, we set the corresponding sparse maximal operator and sparse operator by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{k\geq 0}\ind_{S_{k}}(x)|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\ind_{S_{k}}(x)|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega.

Sparse operators are defined in terms of
\ell^{1}
-sums, while sparse maximal operators are
\ell^{\infty}
-type quantities. It is immediate that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)\leq\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x),\qquad x\in\Omega.

The following result is the key to our approach.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. There exists a sparse sequence
\mathcal{S}
such that

\mathcal{M}f\lesssim\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f\right).

Proof.

The strategy is as follows: we construct the sparse family by adding layers which dominate the different dyadic scales of the range of the maximal function, starting from the largest and decrasing from there. At each scale, we retain only those sets whose conditional overlap with previous scales is small; points we discard are automatically controlled by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)
.

For simplicity, we assume that
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=1
. For each
k\geq 0
and
\ell\geq 1
define

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}^{\ell}=\{x\in\Omega:\,\mathcal{M}^{k}f(x)>2^{-\ell}\text{ and }\mathcal{M}^{k-1}f(x)\leq 2^{-\ell}\},

where, as usual, we define
\mathcal{M}^{-1}f
as
0
.

For each
\ell
,
\{\Omega_{k}^{\ell}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a partition of
\{\mathcal{M}f>2^{-\ell}\}
. Next, for
each
k\geq 0
, we initialize by setting
S_{k}^{1}:=\Omega_{k}^{1}
. We will define
S_{k}^{\ell+1}
inductively for
\ell\geq 1
. Define

\displaystyle S^{\ell}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 0,m\leq\ell\end{subarray}}S_{k}^{m}

and set

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\Omega_{k}^{\ell+1}:\,\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{S^{\ell}}](x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

By construction, we have

S_{k}^{\ell+1}\cap\Big[\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq k,\\
m\leq\ell\end{subarray}}S_{n}^{m}\Big]=\varnothing,

because the second set on the left hand side of the above equation is
k
-measurable. This means that

S_{k}^{\ell}\cap S_{k^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}=\varnothing\quad\mbox{whenever }k\leq k^{\prime}\mbox{ and }\ell<\ell^{\prime}.

Finally, we put
S_{k}=\cup_{\ell\geq 1}S_{k}^{\ell}
and we claim that
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k}
is sparse. Indeed, the above equality yields

\displaystyle\ind_{S_{k}^{\ell}}\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{\cup_{j>k}S_{j}}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{S_{k}^{\ell}}\ind_{\cup_{j>k}S_{j}}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{k}\Big[\ind_{S_{k}^{\ell}}\ind_{\bigcup_{j>k,m<\ell}S_{j}^{m}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{E}_{k}\Big[\ind_{S_{k}^{\ell}}\ind_{S^{\ell-1}}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{2}\ind_{S_{k}^{\ell}},

where we can define
\mathcal{S}^{0}
as the empty set.

Summing in
\ell
yields the sparsity of
\mathcal{S}
. We now prove the domination. Fix
k\geq 0
and
x\in\Omega
, and define
\ell
by

2^{-\ell}<|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|\leq 2^{-\ell+1}.

Then, there exists
m\leq k
such that
x\in\Omega_{m}^{\ell}
. Now we have two possibilities.

If
x\in S_{m}^{\ell}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}
includes a dominating average. Indeed,

\displaystyle|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|\leq 2^{-\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq 2|\mathsf{E}_{m}[f](x)|=2|\ind_{S_{m}}(x)\mathsf{E}_{m}[f](x)|\leq 2\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x).

By Fujii’s pointwise convergence
\mathsf{P}_{j}^{r}g\to g
as
j\to\infty
, we have
g(x)\leq\mathcal{P}(g)(x)
for a.e.
x
, and therefore
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x)
. We conclude that
|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|\leq 2\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x)
.

Otherwise, we must have
x\in\Omega_{m}^{\ell}\setminus S_{m}^{\ell}
and so

\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{S^{\ell-1}}](x)>\frac{1}{2}.

On
S^{\ell-1}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f\geq 2^{-\ell+1}
. Since
r\leq\frac{1}{2}
, this gives
\mathsf{P}_{m}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x)\geq 2^{-\ell+1}
, and so

|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|\leq 2^{-\ell+1}\leq\mathsf{P}_{m}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x)\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x).

Taking the supremum over
k
in both cases yields
\mathcal{M}f(x)\lesssim\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
B
, which characterizes the
H^{1}
norm via sparse operators. The key point is that Proposition
1.4
makes the forward direction short.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

We first show that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

for some sparse sequence
\mathcal{S}
. By Proposition
1.4
, there exists a sparse
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that
\mathcal{M}f\lesssim\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)
. Therefore, we have by Lemma
1.1

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{M}f\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu.

Conversely, for each sparse sequence
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
we compute

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}}|\mathsf{E}_{k}f|\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k}\int_{S_{k}}|\mathsf{E}_{k}f|\cdot\mathsf{E}_{k}\ind_{S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{M}f\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\ind_{S_{k}\cap S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 2\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

where we used the sparsity condition
\mathsf{E}_{k}[\ind_{S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}]\geq 1/2
on
S_{k}
in the first inequality, and the fact that the sets
S_{k}\cap S_{\geq k+1}^{c}
are pairwise disjoint in the last step.
This finishes the proof.
∎

We remark that the percentile enlargement
\mathcal{P}
in Proposition
1.4
is necessary: in Section
2
we show that the naive estimate
\mathcal{M}f\lesssim|\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f|
cannot hold for any sparse
\mathcal{S}
with a universal constant.

We next turn to the proof of Theorem
C
. To that end, we generalize Proposition
1.4
to general maximal functions. The setting is the following: given a finite collection of sets
\mathcal{E}
on any measure space
(X,\mu)
such that
0<\mu(R)<\infty
for all
R\in\mathcal{E}
, we define

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}f=\sup_{R\in\mathcal{E}}|\langle f\rangle_{R}|\ind_{R},\quad\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}^{r}f:=\sup_{R\in\mathcal{E}}\mathsf{P}_{R}^{r}(f)\ind_{R}.

Theorem 1.5
.

For each
f\in L^{1}(X)
, there exists a sparse subfamily
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}f\leq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f).

Proof.

Assume
\#(\mathcal{E})=N
. We construct the family
\mathcal{S}
inductively. We start initializing
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\varnothing
,
\sh(\mathcal{S}_{0})=\varnothing
, and we order the sets in
\mathcal{E}
as
R_{1},R_{2},\dots
, imposing that

|\langle f\rangle_{R_{i}}|\geq|\langle f\rangle_{R_{j}}|\quad\mbox{if}\quad i<j.

The inductive step is applied to
\mathcal{E}
as follows: having processed all
R_{m}
for
1\leq m\leq n
, compute

\displaystyle\gamma:=\frac{\mu(R_{n+1}\cap\sh_{n}(\mathcal{S}))}{\mu(R_{n+1})}.

If
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
then we set

\mathcal{S}_{n+1}=\mathcal{S}_{n},\quad\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n+1})=\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n}).

Otherwise, we add
R_{n+1}
to the collection setting

\mathcal{S}_{n+1}=\mathcal{S}_{n}\cup\{R_{n+1}\},\quad\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n+1})=\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n})\cup R_{n+1}.

We claim that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{N}
is sparse. Indeed, for each
R=R_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
, define

E_{R}:=R\setminus\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n-1}).

By our selection procedure,
\mu(E_{R})\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu(R)
, and the sets
\{E_{R}\}_{R\in\mathcal{S}}
are pairwise disjoint: whenever
R_{n},R_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
for some
k>n
, we have

E_{R_{k}}=R_{k}\setminus\sh(\mathcal{S}_{k-1})\subseteq R_{k}\setminus\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n})=R_{k}\setminus\left[\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n-1})\cup R_{n}\right]\subseteq R_{n}^{c}\subseteq E_{R_{n}}^{c}.

Having chosen
\mathcal{S}
, it is enough to check that
|\langle f\rangle_{R}|\leq\mathsf{P}_{R}^{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)
for every
R\in\mathcal{E}
. If
R\in\mathcal{S}
, there is nothing to check, so suppose that
R=R_{n}\notin\mathcal{S}
. Since
R
was discarded during the construction of
\mathcal{S}
, there holds

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(R\cap\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n}))}{\mu(R)}>\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover, for every
S\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
we have
|\langle f\rangle_{S}|\geq|\langle f\rangle_{R}|
. Since
R
intersects
\sh(\mathcal{S}_{n})
on more than half of its measure, we must have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)\geq\mathsf{P}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}f)\geq|\langle f\rangle_{R}|,

which proves the assertion.
∎

By a standard approxmation procedure, we can assume that the family
\mathcal{E}
in the statement of Theorem
1.5
is countable. Then, if we take

\mathcal{E}=\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}),

we obtain the statement of Theorem
C
: for each
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}})
, there exists a sparse family
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
such that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}f(x)\lesssim\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}f)(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}.

As we mentioned in the Introduction, this domination result throws a new light to the negative result in
[
BCOR19
]
. We expect that it will open the door to considering plausible sparse estimates for multiparameter multi-scale operators like the double Hilbert transform.

2.
Regular martingales - multiscale operators and dyadic harmonic analysis

2.1.
Martingale transforms and square functions

In this section, we make the standing assumption that the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k}
is regular. This means that there exists a constant
R>0
such that for each non-negative, integrable function
f
, we have the estimate

\mathsf{E}_{k}f\leq R\;\mathsf{E}_{k-1}f\quad a.e.

Since we are now dealing with multiscale operators, we need to introduce stopped versions of the objects used in section
1
. Let
\nu
be a stopping time.
Given a martingale
f
we can define the
stopped
martingale
f^{\nu}
as

f^{\nu}_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}df_{m}\ind_{m\leq\nu}.

Likewise, the conditional median
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}^{r}
is given by

\mathsf{P}_{\nu}^{r}(f)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\ind_{\{\nu=k\}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f),

and the localized maximal function is

\mathcal{M}_{(\nu)}f(x)=\sup_{k\geq\nu(x)}|\mathsf{E}_{k}f(x)|.

Let
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of functions in
\Omega
such that
\sigma_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable for all
k
(where we can define
\mathcal{F}_{-1}:=\mathcal{F}_{0}
) and such that

\sup_{k}\|\sigma_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

The martingale transform
T=T(\sigma)
associated with such a sequence and its associated maximal truncation
T^{*}
are the operators respectively given by

\displaystyle Tf=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sigma_{k}df_{k},\quad T^{*}f=\sup_{\ell\geq 0}\,\Bigl|\sum_{0\leq k\leq\ell}\sigma_{k}df_{k}\Bigr|.

The corresponding objects localized at
\nu
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{(\nu)}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\nu(x)}\mathsf{E}_{\nu(x)}f(x)+\sum_{k>\nu(x)}\sigma_{k}df_{k}(x),

\displaystyle\quad T_{(\nu)}^{*}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\ell\geq 0}\,\Bigl|\sigma_{\nu(x)}\mathsf{E}_{\nu(x)}f(x)\delta_{\ell\geq\nu(x)}+\sum_{\nu(x)<k\leq\ell}\sigma_{k}df_{k}(x)\Bigr|.

Note that, with this notation,
T_{(\nu)}f
is slightly different from the martingale
Tf

started
at
\nu
.
The first one, which we call the localized operator, is natural when the operator only sees the future of
\nu
.
The second one, is the complement of the operator stopped at
\nu
.
Since we do not need to
start
martingales, no notation will be introduced for it.

We will study the median of
T
at the same time as that of the square function. We define it –and its localized version by a stopping time
\nu
– next:

\displaystyle Sf=\Bigl(\sum_{m\geq 0}(df_{m})^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad S_{(\nu)}f(x)=\bigl(|\mathsf{E}_{\nu(x)}f|^{2}+\sum_{m>\nu(x)}(df_{m})^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T
be either the square function
S
or the maximal truncations of a martingale transform
T(\sigma)
, and let
\nu
be a stopping time. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\nu}^{(R+2)r}(T_{(\nu)}f)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{r}}\,\mathsf{P}_{\nu}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(\nu)}f),

for any
r\leq\frac{1}{2(R+2)}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove, for each
k
-measurable set
A
,

\mathsf{P}_{k}^{(R+2)r}(T_{(k)}(f\ind_{A}))\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{r}}\,\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(\ind_{A}f)),

and we may further assume that
A\cap\{x:\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(\ind_{A}f))=0\}=\varnothing
. Consider the set

\displaystyle B=\{x\in A:\,\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f(x)>\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)(x)\}.

By definition,
\mu(B)\leq r\mu(A)
because
A
is
k
-measurable. Consider the enlargement

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}=\Big\{x\in\Omega:\,\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(\ind_{B})>\frac{1}{R+1}\Big\}.

By Doob’s maximal theorem, we have
\mu(\widetilde{B})\leq(R+1)r\mu(A)
. Define the following stopping time:

\displaystyle\tau(x)=\inf\Big\{m\geq k:\,\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{B}](x)>\frac{1}{R+1}\Big\}.

We claim that
f^{\tau}\leq\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)
. Indeed, fix
x\in B
and let
m\leq\tau(x)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{m-1}[\ind_{B}](x)\leq\frac{1}{R+1}\implies\mathsf{E}_{m}[\ind_{B}](x)\leq\frac{R}{R+1}<1,

by the regularity of the filtration. On the other hand, if
|f_{m}(x)|>\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)(x)
, there exists
A_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
such that
x\in A_{m}\subset B
, and therefore

\mathsf{E}_{m}(\ind_{B})(x)=1\quad\mbox{or}\quad\mu(A_{m})=0.

But
\mathsf{E}_{m-1}[\ind_{B}](x)<1
, so we must have
f_{m}(x)\leq\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)(x)
.
Next, note that on the complement of
\widetilde{B}
we have

\displaystyle T_{(k)}(f\ind_{A})_{n}=T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A})_{n},

thus

\displaystyle\mu(|T_{(k)}(f\ind_{A})|>(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f))

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\widetilde{B})+\mu(|T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A})|>(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)).

If
T
is the maximal truncation of a martingale transform, the above implies

\mu(|T_{(k)}(f\ind_{A})|>(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}})\leq(R+1)r\mu(A)+\mu(|\mathcal{M}_{k}(T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A}))|>(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)).

To estimate the second term on the right hand side above, we use Chebychev’s conditional inequality, the measurability of
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)
, and the
L^{2}
-boundedness of
T_{(k)}
and Doob’s maximal function:

\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A}))>

\displaystyle(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f))

\displaystyle=\int\mu\Big(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A}))>(2/r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k}\Big)\;d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{2}\int\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)^{-2}\mathcal{M}_{(k)}(T_{(k)}(f^{\tau}\ind_{A}))^{2}\;d\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2}\int\mathcal{M}_{(k)}\left(T_{(k)}\left(f^{\tau}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)^{-1}\ind_{A}\right)\right)^{2}\;d\mu

\displaystyle\leq r\left\|f^{\tau}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(k)}f)^{-1}\ind_{A}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq r\mu(A),

which is enough to conclude. If instead
T
is the square function, a similar computation holds using the equality

\int g(S_{(k)}f)^{2}=\int g|f|^{2},

if
g
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, we obtain Theorem
A
for martingale transforms. We include the precise statement below.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
r=1/(2(R+3))
and let
T=T(\sigma)
be a martingale transform.
For every bounded
f
there exists a sparse sequence of stopping times
\{\nu_{j}\}
such that

\displaystyle T^{*}f\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{P}_{\nu_{j}}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{(\nu_{j})}f)\ind_{\{\nu_{j}<\infty\}}.

Proof of Theorem
A
for martingale transforms.

Set
\nu_{0}=0
and consider the stopping time

\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\inf\{m\geq 0:\,\max((T^{*}f)_{m},|f_{m}|)>r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f)\}

and set
B=\{\nu_{1}<\infty\}
. By Proposition
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mu(B)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\mathcal{M}f>\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f))+\mu(T^{*}f>r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f))

\displaystyle\leq r+(R+2)r=(R+3)r\leq\frac{1}{2}.

If
\mu(B)=0
we are done. Otherwise, we can write

\displaystyle|(Tf)_{n}^{*}|\leq\underbrace{\big|(T^{*}f)_{\nu_{1}-1\land n}\big|+|f_{\nu_{1}-1}|}_{\mathrm{I}}+\Bigg|\underbrace{\sigma_{\nu_{1}}f_{\nu_{1}}+\sum_{\nu_{1}+1\leq m\leq n}\sigma_{m}df_{m}}_{\mathrm{II}}\Bigg|,

almost everywhere in
B
. By definition,
\mathrm{I}\leq 2r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f)
, and so we can just focus on
\mathrm{II}
, for which we adapt the idea from
[
Lac17
]
in the dyadic setting: term
\mathrm{II}
is a martingale transform with respect to the probability space started at
\nu
. To be precise, let
\Omega_{k}=\{\nu_{1}=k\}
. Then, we can form the probability space restricted to
\Omega_{k}
in the usual way. Let
\widetilde{\mu}
be the conditioned probability measure to
\Omega_{k}
and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{m}=\mathcal{F}_{m+k}.

Then
(\Omega_{k},(\mathcal{G}_{m})_{m\geq 0},\widetilde{\mu})
is a filtered probability space and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{m+k}

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{m}

\displaystyle:=f_{m+k}

are, respectively, a predictable sequence and a martingale. Also,

\displaystyle d\widetilde{f}_{m}=\begin{cases}f_{k}&\text{if }m=0\\
df_{m+k}&\text{if }m\geq 1,\end{cases}

which explains the first term in
\mathrm{II}
. Therefore, almost everywhere on
\Omega_{k}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}=\widetilde{T}\widetilde{f}_{n},

where
\widetilde{T}
is the martingale transform associated to
\widetilde{\sigma}
.

Hence, we can iterate this term. At the end of the process, we obtain a sparse sequence of stopping times which yields the result.
∎

The corresponding result for
S
is proved in a similar manner.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
r=1/(2(R+3))
. For every bounded
f
there exists a sparse sequence of stopping times
\{\nu_{j}\}
such that

\displaystyle Sf\lesssim\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}[\mathsf{P}_{\nu_{j}}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{\nu_{j}}f)]^{2}\ind_{\{\nu_{j}<\infty\}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof of Theorem
A
for square functions.

We define
\nu_{1}
in a similar way as before:

\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\inf\{m\geq 0:\,\max((Sf)_{m},|f_{m}|)>r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f)\}

Setting
B=\{\nu_{1}<\infty\}
, we have again
\mu(B)\leq 1/2
. This time we split

(Sf)^{2}\leq\sum_{m<\nu_{1}}|df_{m}|^{2}+|df_{\nu_{1}}|^{2}+\sum_{m>\nu_{1}}|df_{m}|^{2}\lesssim_{R}[\mathsf{P}_{0}^{r}(\mathcal{M}f)]^{2}+(S_{\nu_{1}}f)^{2},

and the rightmost term above can be iterated as in the proof of Corollary
2.2
.
∎

Clearly, Corollary
2.3
recovers the classical sparse domination result for the dyadic square function

\displaystyle(Sf)^{2}\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\ind_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}\mathsf{E}_{m}[|f|]^{2}.

H^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\Omega)
results for martingale transforms,
0<p<\infty
, and the comparability between
\|Sf\|_{p}
and
\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{p}
follow from the fact that the cancellative sparse operator

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}f:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\ind_{S_{k}}\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f).

is always
L^{p}
-bounded.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
0<p<\infty
and
\mathcal{S}
be a sparse family of sets. Then

\|\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

for all
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

For all
0<q<\infty
and each
A\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)^{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(A)\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)^{q}}{\mu(A)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)^{q}}{\mu(A)}\left[\mu(\{x\in A:|f(x)|\geq\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)\})+\mu(\{x\in A:|f(x)|<\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)\})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\left[\int_{A}|f|^{q}d\mu+(1-r)\mu(A)\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)^{q}\right],

from which we obtain

\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)\leq\left(\frac{1}{r\mu(A)}\int_{A}|f|^{q}d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

for all
A\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, which implies
\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)\leq r^{-1/q}(\mathsf{E}_{k}|f|^{q})^{1/q}
. Taking
q=1
this yields
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}f(x)
, and so by the argument in
[
DPS25
]
, the assertion follows for
p>1
. Otherwise, choosing
q=p/2
and using the boundedness of
\mathcal{M}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}}\left(\mathsf{P}_{k}^{r}(f)\right)^{p}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq r^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}}\left(\mathsf{E}_{k}\left(|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right)^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2r^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}}\left(\mathsf{E}_{k}\left(|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right)^{2}\mathsf{E}_{k}(\ind_{S_{\geq k+1}^{c}})d\mu

\displaystyle=2r^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}\cap S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}\left(\mathsf{E}_{k}\left(|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right)^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2r^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{S_{k}\cap S_{\geq k+1}^{c}}\left(\mathcal{M}\left(|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right)^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq 2r^{-2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{M}\left(|f|^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right)^{2}d\mu\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p},

as desired.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

The proofs of Corollaries
2.2
and
2.3
use the regularity of the filtration in an explicit way. One may wonder if regularity is necessary, since boundedness results near the

-endpoint for martingale transforms hold for general filtered spaces, without the regularity condition. The same is true for the positive sparse domination result in

shows that regularity is indeed necessary for our sparse domination results to hold. Otherwise, Theorem

for

2.2.
Dyadic harmonic analysis: Haar shifts

In this subsection we restrict ourselves to the filtration generated by the usual dyadic system
\mathscr{D}=\{\mathscr{D}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This means that we have the two-sided filtration that induces the family of conditional expectations

\mathsf{E}_{k}f=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}}\langle f\rangle_{Q}\ind_{Q},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

We assume that the underlying measure is the Lebesgue measure, although any dyadically doubling one would yield exactly the same results. A measure
\mu
is dyadically doubling if
\mu(\widehat{Q})\leq C_{\mathrm{doub}}(\mu)\mu(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathscr{D}
, which implies that the filtration is regular with regularity constant
R=C_{\mathrm{doub}}(\mu)
. Of course, the Lebesgue measure
m
is dyadically doubling with
C_{\mathrm{doub}}(m)=2^{d}
. The fact that the filtration is two-sided changes nothing in practice, since the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathscr{D}_{-\infty}
is almost trivial: indeed,

\cap_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sigma(\mathscr{D}_{k})
has finitely many elements, all of which have infinite measure. For a careful analysis of what happens in the general two-sided context, one can see
[
Tre10
]
. We will avoid the related technicalities here. In the dyadic setting, Doob’s maximal function can be written as

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}f(x)=\sup_{x\in Q\in\mathscr{D}}\left|\langle f\rangle_{Q}\right|.

We denote by
\mathscr{D}(Q)
the family of dyadic cubes contained in
Q\in\mathscr{D}
, and for each
\ell>0
we denote by
\mathscr{D}_{\ell}(Q)
the family of dyadic subcubes of
Q
with side length equal to
2^{-\ell}\ell(Q)
. Let
\{h_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathscr{D}}
be a fixed Haar basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. A Haar shift is an operator of the form

\Sh f(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{D}}\sum_{T\in\mathscr{D}_{t}(Q)}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{D}_{s}(Q)}\alpha_{TS}^{Q}\langle f,h_{T}\rangle h_{S}(x),

where
\|\alpha\|_{\ell^{\infty}}=\sup_{Q,T,S}|\alpha_{TS}^{Q}|<\infty
so that
\Sh
is bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and indeed
\|\Sh\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\sim\|\alpha\|_{\ell^{\infty}}
. The pair of parameters
(t,s)
is usually called the complexity of
\Sh
, and we omit it from the notation. Truncations of
\Sh
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
are defined in a similar way as above, localizing to some
Q\in\mathscr{D}
. This is the analogous procedure as the generation-based truncations of Section
1
:

\mathcal{M}_{Q}f(x)=\sup_{x\in R\in\mathscr{D}(Q)}\left|\langle f\rangle_{R}\right|,\quad\Sh_{Q}f(x)=\sum_{R\in\mathscr{D}(Q)}\sum_{T\in\mathscr{D}_{t}(R)}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{D}_{s}(R)}\alpha_{TS}^{R}\langle f,h_{T}\rangle h_{S}(x).

Given
Q\in\mathscr{D}
and an open set
A\subset Q
, define
A^{(0)}:=A
, and inductively,

A^{(n)}:=\left\{x\in Q:\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}(\ind_{A^{(n-1)}})>\frac{1}{2^{d}+1}\right\},\quad n\geq 1.

By the weak
(1,1)
boundedness of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
, we have

|A^{(n)}|\leq(2^{d}+1)^{n}|A|,

while by construction if
R\subset A
is a cube in
\mathscr{D}
, then
R^{(n)}\subset A^{(n)}
. Moreover, if
R\subset A^{(n)}
is a cube in
\mathscr{D}
which is maximal with respect to inclusion, then any
T\in\mathscr{D}_{n-1}(R)
satisfies
T\cap A^{c}\not=\varnothing
. As in the previous subsection, we will study the maximal truncations

\Sh^{*}f(x)=\sup_{\ell<m}\,\Bigl|\sum_{k=\ell}^{m}\sum_{x\in Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}}\sum_{T\in\mathscr{D}_{t}(Q)}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{D}_{s}(Q)}\alpha_{TS}^{Q}\langle f,h_{T}\rangle h_{S}(x)\Bigr|.

Maximal truncations also satisfy
\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\sim\|\alpha\|_{\ell^{\infty}}
uniformly on
Q\in\mathscr{D}
. The analogue of Proposition
2.1
for
\Sh^{*}
is the following local estimate.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
C_{0}=(2^{d}+1)^{s+t+1}+1
. If
r<\frac{1}{C_{0}}
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{C_{0}r}(\Sh_{Q}^{*}f)\leq\frac{\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{2}}{r^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f).

Proof.

We may assume, without loss of generality, that
\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f)=1
. Set
N=t+s+1
and define

B=\{x\in Q:\,\mathcal{M}_{Q}f(x)>1\},

so
|B|\leq r|Q|
. Fix a constant
A
whose value we will choose momentarily. By the definition of
\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}
we have

\displaystyle|\{x\in Q:\,|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|>A\}|

\displaystyle\leq|B^{(N)}|+|\{x\in Q\setminus B^{(N)}:\,|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|>A\}|

\displaystyle\leq(2^{d}+1)^{N}r|Q|+|\{x\in Q\setminus B^{(N)}:\,|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|>A\}|.

To estimate the second term on the last display above, we write

B^{(1)}=\bigsqcup_{j}Q_{j},

where the
Q_{j}
are maximal dyadic cubes inside
B^{(1)}
. We next split
f
into its bad and good parts

f=\Bigl[f\ind_{B^{(1)}}-\sum_{j}\langle f\rangle_{Q_{j}}\ind_{Q_{j}}\Bigr]+\Bigl[f\ind_{Q\setminus B^{(1)}}+\sum_{j}\langle f\rangle_{Q_{j}}\ind_{Q_{j}}\Bigr]=:f_{1}+f_{2}.

By construction,
\supp(\Sh_{Q}f_{1})\subset B^{(N)}
, while for each
j
,
|\langle f\rangle_{Q_{j}}|\leq 1
and so
|f_{2}|\leq 1
. Therefore, using the
L^{2}
-boundedness of
\Sh_{Q}^{*}
and sublinearity we get

\displaystyle|\{x\in Q\setminus B^{(N)}:\,|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|>A\}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}\int|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f_{2}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{2}}{A^{2}}\int_{Q}|f_{2}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq|Q|\frac{\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{2}}{A^{2}}.

We now choose
A=\frac{\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{2}}{r^{\frac{1}{2}}}
and combine the above computations to get

\displaystyle|\{x\in Q:\,\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)>A\}|

\displaystyle\leq|Q|\big((2^{d}+1)^{N}r+r\big)=[(2^{d}+1)^{N}+1]r|Q|,

which implies the assertion.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
A
for Haar shift operators. We provide the precise statement below.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there exists
r>0
and a sparse family
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathscr{D}
such that

(2.1)

|\Sh^{*}f(x)|\lesssim_{s,t}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f)\ind_{Q}(x).

Proof (proof of Theorem
A
for Haar shifts).

By standard reductions, we may assume that
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a mean-zero function and that there exists
\tilde{Q}_{0}\in\mathscr{D}
such that
\supp(f)\subset\tilde{Q}_{0}
. Therefore, there exists
\tilde{Q}_{0}\subset Q_{0}\in\mathscr{D}
such that

\Sh^{*}f(x)=\Sh_{Q_{0}}^{*}f(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Fix
r=\frac{1}{2(C_{0}+1)^{2}}
, where
C_{0}
is the constant on the statement of Proposition
2.6
. We are going to prove the following claim: for each
Q\in\mathscr{D}
, there exists a pairwise disjoint collection
\mathcal{F}(Q)=\{Q_{j}\}_{j}\subseteq\mathscr{D}(Q)
such that
\sum_{j}|Q_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|Q|
and

\displaystyle|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|\leq C\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f)+\sum_{j}|\Sh_{Q_{j}}^{*}f(x)|,\quad x\in Q.

Indeed, set

A=r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{Q\in\mathscr{D}}\left\{\|\Sh_{Q}^{*}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{2}\right\}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f),

and consider the set

By Proposition
2.6
and the weak
(1,1)
-boundedness of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
, we have

, and so

We next choose

such that

, getting

We can now proceed as in

]

\displaystyle\leq A\ind_{Q\setminus\Omega^{(N)}}(x)+\sum_{j}\ind_{Q_{j}}(x)|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq A+\sum_{j}\ind_{Q_{j}}(x)\Big|\Sh_{Q}^{*}f(x)-\Sh_{Q_{j}}^{*}f(x)\Big|+\sum_{j}\ind_{Q_{j}}(x)|\Sh_{Q_{j}}^{*}f(x)|.

and

, and assume

,
Q_{j}\in\mathscr{D}_{m_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{K}\lvert h\rvert^{\delta}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}},

\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m,

\displaystyle\lvert\partial^{\alpha}_{y}K(x,y)-\partial^{\alpha}_{y}K(x,y+h)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{K}\lvert h\rvert^{\delta}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}},

\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert=m,

for
x\neq y
and
\lvert h\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\lvert x-y\rvert
.

Note that Definition
3.2
is not symmetric in the
x
- and
y
-variables. Indeed, when
s>1
we assume more smoothness in the
y
-variable. Furthermore, as will be clear from the proofs below, the
\delta
-Hölder smoothness in the
x
-variable can be relaxed to
\varepsilon
-Hölder smoothness for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Theorem
D
involves a
Q
-localized, smooth maximal operator on the right-hand side, which we define next.
For
s\geq 0
and a cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)
the collection of
s
-smooth,
L^{\infty}
-normalized bump functions localized in
Q
, that is, writing
s=m+\delta
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
(or
\delta=0
when
s=0
), the class of functions
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\supp(\varphi)\subseteq{Q}
and such that for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\varphi(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ell(Q)^{\lvert\alpha\rvert}},

\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m,

\displaystyle\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\varphi(y)-\partial^{\alpha}\varphi(y^{\prime})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert y-y^{\prime}\rvert^{\delta}}{\ell(Q)^{s}},

\displaystyle\lvert\alpha\rvert=m.

Note that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)
and
\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)
with
R\subseteq Q
, we have that
c_{s}\cdot\varphi\cdot\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)
for some constant
c_{s}\geq 1
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
s\geq 0
and for
f\in L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
define the smooth maximal operator
\mathcal{M}^{s}f
by

\mathcal{M}^{s}f(x):=\sup_{Q}\,\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)}\,\frac{1}{\lvert Q\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{{Q}}f(y)\varphi(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|\ind_{Q}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with sides parallel to the coordinate axes. For a cube
Q_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
define the local version
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{0}}^{s}f
by

\mathcal{M}_{Q_{0}}^{s}f(x):=\sup_{Q\subseteq Q_{0}}\,\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)}\,\frac{1}{\lvert Q\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{{Q}}f(y)\varphi(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|\ind_{Q}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let us make a few remarks on the definition of this smooth maximal operator.

Remark 3.4
.

(1)

For
0\leq s_{0}<s_{1}
we have

\mathcal{M}^{s_{1}}f\lesssim_{s_{0},s_{1}}\mathcal{M}^{s_{0}}f.

(2)

When
s=0
, the extremizer in the definition of
\mathcal{M}^{0}f
is
\varphi=\mathrm{sgn}(f)\ind_{Q}
, recovering the classical (non-cancellative) Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator, i.e.

\mathcal{M}^{0}f(x)=\sup_{Q}\,\langle|f|\rangle_{Q}\ind_{Q},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3)

Since we can approximate an
s_{1}
-smooth function by a
C^{\infty}_{c}
-function with convergence in
C^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
0<s_{0}<s_{1}
, we have for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{M}^{s_{1}}f(x)\lesssim_{s_{0},s_{1}}\sup_{Q}\,\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}}(Q)\cap C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\frac{1}{\lvert Q\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{{Q}}f(y)\varphi(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|\ind_{Q}(x)\leq\mathcal{M}^{s_{0}}f(x).

The middle expression can be extended from
f\in L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to (tempered) distributions.

To prove Theorem
D
using
f_{Q}
as in (
3.1
), we need to estimate both

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}\bigl(\lvert T(f\psi_{2Q})\rvert\bigr)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{Q}f)

in terms of
\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f)
. For the former term, this is contained in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
,
s>0
and let
T
be an
s
-smooth Calderón–Zygmund operator. For any
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported on
3Q
we have

\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{2r}(\lvert Tf\rvert)\lesssim_{T,s}\tfrac{1}{r}\cdot\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f)

Proof.

Fix
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
0\leq\psi\leq 1
,
\psi\equiv 1
on
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
and
\psi\equiv 0
outside
(1+\frac{1}{8\sqrt{d}})[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
. For each cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
define

\psi_{Q}(x):=\psi\Bigl(\frac{x-x_{Q}}{\ell(Q)}\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Note that
\psi_{Q}\equiv 1
on
Q
.

Fix a cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported on
3Q
. Abbreviate
m_{Q}:=\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f)
, and for
\lambda>0
define

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{x\in Q:\lvert Tf(x)\rvert>\lambda m_{Q}\text{ and }{\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f(x)}\leq m_{Q}\bigr\}.

If there exists a
\lambda>0
, depending only on
T,r,s,d
, such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lvert E_{\lambda}\rvert\leq r\lvert Q\rvert,

we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\bigl\{x\in Q:\lvert Tf(x)\rvert>\lambda m_{Q}\bigr\}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\lvert E_{\lambda}\rvert+\bigl|\bigl\{x\in Q:\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f(x)>m_{Q}\bigr\}\bigr|\leq 2r\lvert Q\rvert.

and therefore
\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{2r}(\lvert Tf\rvert)\leq\lambda m_{Q}
, finishing the proof.

It remains to show (
3.2
). Define

\displaystyle\Omega:=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}(f)(x)>m_{Q}\bigr\}

and note that
\Omega\subsetneq 3Q
. Let
\{R_{j}\}_{j}
be a Whitney decomposition of
\Omega
(cf.
[
Gra14
, Section J.1]
), that is,

\displaystyle\textstyle\bigcup_{j}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\Omega,

\displaystyle\sqrt{d}\cdot\ell(R_{j})\leq\dist(R_{j},3Q\setminus\Omega)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{d}\cdot\ell(R_{j}),

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j}\ind_{\frac{9}{8}R_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq 12^{d}.

In particular, we have
\supp(\psi_{R_{j}})\subseteq\Omega
. Define

\displaystyle\eta_{j}(x):=\frac{\psi_{R_{j}}(x)}{\sum_{k}\psi_{R_{k}}(x)}\ind_{\Omega}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

so that we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j}\eta_{j}

\displaystyle=\ind_{\Omega},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\eta_{j}(x)}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\eqsim\lvert R_{j}\rvert,

\displaystyle\lvert\partial^{\beta}\eta_{j}\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}\ell(R_{j})^{-\lvert\beta\rvert},\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

Now define the smooth Calderón–Zygmund decomposition of
f
as

\displaystyle c_{j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)\eta_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle b_{j}(x)

\displaystyle:=f(x)\eta_{j}(x)-c_{j}\eta_{j}(x),

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle:=f(x)-\sum_{j}b_{j}(x),

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

and note that the support of
g
is contained in
3Q
.
Then we have, using any
z\in{3Q}\setminus\Omega
such that
\dist(z,R_{j})\lesssim\ell(R_{j})
, that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lvert c_{j}\rvert\lesssim\frac{1}{\lvert R_{j}\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)\eta_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|\lesssim_{d}\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f(z)\leq m_{Q}.

Moreover, by a smooth variant of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, for a.e.
x\in{3Q}\setminus\Omega
we have

\lvert f(x)\rvert\lesssim\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f(x)\leq m_{Q}

and therefore we have for all
x\in 3Q
that

\lvert g(x)\rvert\leq\lvert f(x)\rvert\ind_{{3Q}\setminus\Omega}(x)+\sum_{j}\lvert c_{j}\rvert\eta_{j}(x)\lesssim m_{Q}.

Combined with the
L^{2}
-boundedness of
T
, this yields

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lvert E_{\lambda}\rvert&\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}m_{Q}^{2}}\int_{Q}\lvert Tg(x)\rvert^{2}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x&&+\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\lambda m_{Q}}\int_{E_{\lambda}}\lvert Tb_{j}(x)\rvert\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\frac{\lVert T\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}m_{Q}^{2}}\int_{{3Q}}\lvert g(x)\rvert^{2}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x&&+\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\lambda m_{Q}}\int_{E_{\lambda}}\lvert Tb_{j}(x)\rvert\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\\
&\lesssim\frac{\lVert T\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\cdot\lvert Q\rvert&&+\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\lambda m_{Q}}\int_{E_{\lambda}}\lvert Tb_{j}(x)\rvert\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

It remains to estimate the sum in
j
. Fix a
j
and denote the center of
R_{j}
by
x_{j}
. Furthermore, fix an
x\in E_{\lambda}\subseteq\Omega^{c}
and note that
\dist(x,R_{j})\geq\sqrt{d}\ell(R_{j})
. Let
\varepsilon:=\min\{s,1\}
and define

\zeta_{j}(y):=\frac{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}\bigl(K(x,y)-K({x,x_{j}})\bigr)\psi_{(1+\frac{1}{8\sqrt{d}})R_{j}}(y),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
y\in\supp(\zeta_{j})
we have

\displaystyle\lvert y-x_{j}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{8\sqrt{d}}\bigr)^{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{d}}{2}\cdot\ell(R_{j})\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\bigr)\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{d}}{2}\cdot\ell(R_{j})

\displaystyle\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert

\displaystyle\geq(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sqrt{d})\cdot\ell(R_{j})=\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\bigr)\cdot{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\ell(R_{j}),

i.e.
\lvert h\rvert:=\lvert y-x_{j}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert

and therefore the assumptions on
K
yield that
\zeta_{j}
is, up to a multiplicative constant depending on
K
and
d
, an element of
\mathcal{F}_{s}(2R_{j})
.
Thus, since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b_{j}(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=0
, we can estimate

\displaystyle\lvert Tb_{j}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)b_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(K(x,y)-K(x,x_{j})\bigr)b_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}\cdot\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b_{j}(y)\zeta_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}\cdot\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)\eta_{j}(y)\zeta_{j}(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|+\frac{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}\lvert R_{j}\rvert\lvert c_{j}\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim_{K}\lvert R_{j}\rvert\cdot\frac{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}\cdot m_{Q}

where, in the final estimate, we used (
3.3
) and a similar inequality for the first term.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\lambda m_{Q}}\int_{E_{\lambda}}\lvert Tb_{j}(x)\rvert\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{K}\sum_{j}\frac{\lvert R_{j}\rvert}{\lambda}\int_{R_{j}^{c}}\frac{\ell(R_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\lvert x-x_{j}\rvert^{d+\varepsilon}}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{s}\sum_{j}\frac{\lvert R_{j}\rvert}{\lambda}\lesssim\frac{\lvert Q\rvert}{\lambda}.

Combined with (
3.4
), this yields
\lvert E_{\lambda}\rvert\lesssim_{T,s}(\lambda^{-2}+\lambda^{-1})\lvert Q\rvert.

Taking
\lambda>0
large enough we obtain (
3.2
), which finishes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
D
.

Proof of Theorem
D
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be supported on a cube
Q_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
r:=2^{-d-4}
. For each cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
\psi_{Q}
be as in Proposition
3.5
and set

f_{Q}:=T(f\psi_{2Q}).

Noting that
f_{Q_{0}}=Tf
on
Q_{0}
and combining Theorem
3.1
and Proposition
3.5
we find a sparse family
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathscr{D}(Q_{0})
such that

|Tf(x)|\lesssim_{T,s}\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f)+\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{2r}(\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{Q}f)\right]\ind_{Q}(x),\qquad x\in Q_{0}.

It remains to show for all cubes
Q\in\mathscr{D}(Q_{0})
that

\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{2r}(\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{Q}f)\lesssim_{T,s,d}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f),

for which it suffices to show that

(3.5)

\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{Q}f(x)\lesssim_{K,s}\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}(f)(x),\qquad x\in Q.

Fix a cube
Q\in\mathscr{D}(Q_{0})
and
x\in Q
. Take
R\in\mathscr{D}(Q)
such that
x\in R
and let
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in R
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be the largest integer such that
2^{n}R\subseteq 2Q
and note that
2^{n}\ell(R)\eqsim\ell(Q)
. Write
s=m+\delta
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
and for
k=1,\ldots,{n-1}
define

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(y)

\displaystyle:=2^{k(d+\delta)}\lvert R\rvert\cdot\bigl(K(x^{\prime},y)-K(x^{\prime\prime},y)\bigr)\bigl(\psi_{2^{k+1}R}(y)-\psi_{2^{k}R}(y)\bigr),\qquad

\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}(y)

\displaystyle:=2^{n(d+\delta)}\lvert R\rvert\cdot\bigl(K(x^{\prime},y)-K(x^{\prime\prime},y)\bigr)\bigl(\psi_{2Q}(y)-\psi_{2^{n}R}(y)\bigr),\qquad

\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
y\in\supp(\zeta_{k})
we note that
\lvert x^{\prime}-y\rvert\gtrsim 2^{k}\ell(R),
so the assumptions on
K
yield that
\zeta_{k}
is, up to a multiplicative constant depending on
K
and
d
, an element of

\mathcal{F}_{s}\bigl((1+\tfrac{1}{8\sqrt{d}})2^{k+1}R\bigr)

for
k=1,\ldots,n-1
. Furthermore,
\zeta_{n}
is, up to a multiplicative constant depending on
K
and
d
, an element of
\mathcal{F}_{s}(3Q)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\lvert f_{Q,R}(x^{\prime})-f_{Q,R}(x^{\prime\prime})\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(K(x^{\prime},y)-K(x^{\prime\prime},y)\bigr)(\psi_{2Q}(y)-\psi_{2R}(y))f(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{-k\delta}\frac{1}{\lvert 2^{k}R\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{k}(y)f(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{K,s}\mathcal{M}_{3Q}^{s}f(x).

Taking the supremum over all
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in R
and
R\in\mathscr{D}(Q)
, we obtain (
3.5
). This finishes the proof.
∎

4.
Weighted norm estimates

In this section we show how our cancellative sparse bounds lead to quantitative, and in some cases sharp, weighted Hardy space estimates. We begin with the
s
-smooth Calderón–Zygmund operators studied in Section
3
, as this case contains the main ideas and technical difficulties. In Remark
4.7
we indicate how the arguments can be adapted to the martingale setting of Sections
1
-
2
.

4.1.
Muckenhoupt weighted Hardy spaces

By a weight
w
we mean a locally integrable
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
. For a measurable set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we write
w(E):=\int_{E}w(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x
. For
p\in(0,\infty)
we define
L^{p}(w)
as the space of all measurable
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}:=\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert f(x)\rvert^{p}w(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/p}<\infty.

For
q\in(1,\infty)
we say that
w
belongs to the Muckenhoupt
A_{q}
-class and write
w\in A_{q}
if

[w]_{A_{q}}:=\sup_{Q}\,\langle w\rangle_{Q}\langle w^{-{\frac{1}{q-1}}}\rangle_{Q}^{q-1}<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Furthermore, we say that
w
belongs to the Muckenhoupt
A_{\infty}
-class and write
w\in A_{\infty}
if

[w]_{A_{\infty}}:=\sup_{Q}\frac{1}{w(Q)}\int_{Q}\sup_{R\subseteq Q}\langle w\rangle_{R}\ind_{R}(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x.

By
[
HP13
, Proposition 2.2]
we know

[w]_{A_{\infty}}\lesssim[w]_{A_{q}},

and thus
A_{q}\subseteq A_{\infty}
. In fact, we have
\bigcup_{q>1}A_{q}=A_{\infty}
, i.e. for all
w\in A_{\infty}
there exists a
q\in(1,\infty)
such that
w\in A_{q}
.
However, both
q
and
[w]_{A_{q}}
can depend at least exponentially on
[w]_{A_{\infty}}
, see
[
HP16
]
and the references therein. Therefore, to obtain quantitative weighted norm inequalities, we will assume
w\in A_{q}
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
. The key property of
w\in A_{q}
we will use is that, for a cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
E\subseteq Q
, we have

(4.1)

\Bigl(\frac{\lvert E\rvert}{\lvert Q\rvert}\Bigr)^{q}\leq[w]_{A_{q}}\cdot\frac{w(E)}{w(Q)}.

Next, we introduce the weighted Hardy spaces
H^{p}(w)
following
[
ST89a
]
, see in particular Chapters VI and VIII therein for further details. Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the spaces of Schwartz functions and tempered distributions, respectively. Fix a
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=1
. For
t>0
set
\psi_{t}(x):=t^{-d}\psi(x/t)
and for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
define

\mathcal{M}^{\psi}f(x):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t>0:\\
\lvert x-y\rvert\leq t\end{subarray}}\lvert\psi_{t}*f(y)\rvert,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
p\in(0,\infty)
and
w\in A_{\infty}
, we define

H^{p}(w):=\bigl\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\mathcal{M}^{\psi}f\in L^{p}(w)\bigr\}.

with quasi-norm

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}(w)}:=\lVert\mathcal{M}^{\psi}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}.

Then
H^{p}(w)
is a quasi-Banach space and different choices of
\psi
yield equivalent quasi-norms. We refer to
[
ST89a
, Chapters VI and VIII]
for numerous other equivalent quasi-norms on
H^{p}(w)
. Since the constants in these equivalences depend on the weight characteristic of

w
, one must fix a specific quasi-norm in order to obtain
quantitative
weighted Hardy space estimates. We shall therefore work with the quasi-norm defined via the smooth maximal operator
\mathcal{M}^{s}
introduced in Section
3
. As we show in the next proposition, this quasi-norm is equivalent to the one defined above.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
p\in(0,\infty)
,
s>0
and assume
q:=p(1+\frac{s}{d})>1
. For
w\in A_{q}
and
f\in H^{p}(w)\cap L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{s,\psi}\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p,s,w,\psi}\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}(w)}

Before turning to the proof, we record a few remarks concerning Proposition
4.1
.

Remark 4.2
.

(1)

We restrict to the dense subspace
H^{p}(w)\cap L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Proposition
4.1
, since
\mathcal{M}^{s}f
is not necessarily well-defined for arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Alternatively, one can modify the definition of
\mathcal{M}^{s}
by restricting the supremum to test functions in
\mathcal{F}_{s}(Q)\cap C^{\infty}(Q)
, see also Remark
3.4
3
.

(2)

We do not make the dependence of the implicit constant on
w
explicit in the second estimate of Proposition
4.1
, although the proof shows that it depends only on
[w]_{A_{q}}
. More precisely, inspection of the argument yields

\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p,s,\psi}[w]_{A_{q}}^{\max\{1,\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\}}\cdot\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}_{\mathrm{at}}(w)},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{p}_{\mathrm{at}}(w)}
denotes the equivalent quasi-norm on
H^{p}(w)
induced by the atomic decomposition. As far as the authors are aware, the quantitative dependence on
[w]_{A_{q}}
in the estimate

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{p}_{\mathrm{at}}(w)}\lesssim_{p,w,\psi}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{H^{p}}(w)}

has not been tracked in the literature.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Take
f\in H^{p}(w)\cap L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We first note that

\mathcal{M}^{\psi}f(x)\lesssim_{s,\psi}\mathcal{M}^{s}f(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

which is trivial if
\psi
has compact support and otherwise follows by decomposing into annuli and using that
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This proves the first claimed norm estimate.

For the second estimate,
define
N:=\lceil s\rceil
and note that by density (see
[
ST89a
, Chapter VII]
) we may assume without loss of generality that
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{\alpha}f(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=0,\qquad\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq N.

Now, by the finite atomic decomposition of
H^{p}(w)
(see, e.g.,
[
CMN19
, Theorem 2.6]
), we can write
f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}a_{k}
for a sequence of non-negative numbers
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
and a sequence of
(N,\infty)
-atoms
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
supported on cubes
\{Q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
with

(4.2)

\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\ind_{Q_{k}}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p,w,\psi}\lVert f\rVert_{H^{p}(w)}.

Here an
(N,\infty)
-atom (normalized as in
[
CMN19
]
) is a function
a
supported on a cube
Q
such that
\lVert a\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq 1
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{\alpha}a(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=0,\qquad\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq N.

Fix
1\leq k\leq n
and denote the center of
Q_{k}
by
x_{k}
. We trivially have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{s}a_{k}(x)\cdot\ind_{3Q_{k}}(x)\leq\lVert a_{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{k})}\cdot\ind_{3Q_{k}}(x)\lesssim_{s}\lVert a_{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{k})}\cdot M(\ind_{Q_{k}})(x)^{\frac{d+s}{d}},

where
M
denotes the Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator.
For
x\notin 3Q_{k}
, let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a cube such that
x\in R
and
R\cap Q_{k}\neq\varnothing
. Then
\ell(Q_{k})\leq\ell(R)
and

\lvert x-x_{k}\rvert\leq\sqrt{d}\bigl(\ell(Q_{k})+\ell(R)\bigr)\lesssim\ell(R).

Write
s=m+\delta
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
. Take
z\in R\cap Q_{k}
and for
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)
let
P_{z}^{m}(\varphi)
denote the Taylor polynomial of
\varphi
at
z
of degree
m
. Since
\varphi
is
s
-smooth and supported on
R
, we have

\lvert\varphi(y)-P_{z}^{m}(\varphi)(y)\rvert\lesssim_{s}\frac{\lvert y-z\rvert^{s}}{\ell(R)^{s}},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and therefore, using the cancellative properties of
a_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)}\,\frac{1}{\lvert R\rvert}\Bigl|\int a_{k}(y)\varphi(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(R)}\,\frac{1}{\lvert R\rvert}\Bigl|\int_{Q_{k}}a_{k}(y)\bigl(\varphi(y)-P_{z}^{m}(\varphi)(y)\bigr)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s}\frac{\lVert a_{k}\rVert_{L^{1}(Q_{k})}}{\lvert R\rvert}\frac{{\ell(Q_{k})}^{s}}{\ell(R)^{s}}\lesssim\lVert a_{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{k})}\cdot\frac{\ell(Q_{k})^{d+s}}{\lvert x-x_{k}\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle\eqsim\lVert a_{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{k})}\cdot M(\ind_{Q_{k}})(x)^{\frac{d+s}{d}}.

Hence, denoting
t:=\frac{d}{d+s}
, we have

\mathcal{M}^{s}f(x)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\cdot\mathcal{M}^{s}a_{k}(x)\lesssim_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\cdot M(\ind_{Q_{k}})^{1/t}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}M(\lambda_{k}^{t}\ind_{Q_{k}})^{1/t}.

By assumption, we have
p/t=q>1
and
1/t>1
, so by the weighted Fefferman-Stein inequality
[
CMP12
, Theorem 1.12]
we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s}\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}M(\lambda_{k}^{t}\ind_{Q_{k}})^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}M(\lambda_{k}^{t}\ind_{Q_{k}})^{1/t}\Bigr)^{t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p/t}(w)}^{1/t}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,s}[w]_{A_{p/t}}^{\frac{1}{t}\cdot\max\{t,\frac{t}{p-t}\}}\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\ind_{Q_{k}}\Bigr)^{t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p/t}(w)}^{1/t}

\displaystyle=[w]_{A_{q}}^{\max\{1,\frac{q^{\prime}}{p}\}}\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\ind_{Q_{k}}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}.

Combined with (
4.2
), this finishes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Weighted norm estimates for Calderón–Zygmund operators

We now turn to weighted norm estimates for operators from
H^{p}(w)
to
L^{p}(w)
for
0<p<\infty
and
w\in A_{\infty}
. Combining Theorem
D
and Proposition
4.1
, this has been reduced to the
L^{p}(w)
-boundedness of the cancellative sparse operator given by

f\mapsto\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)\ind_{Q}.

The following proposition contains the key estimate.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an
\eta
-sparse collection of cubes. Let
p,t\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in(1,\infty)
,
r\in(0,1)
and
w\in A_{q}
. Then for all
f\in L^{p}(w)

\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{t}\ind_{Q}\Bigr)^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p,q,r,t,\eta}[w]_{A_{q}}^{\frac{1}{p}}[w]_{A_{\infty}}^{(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{p})_{+}}\cdot\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}.

Proof.

We first note that it suffices to prove the statement for
p=t
. Indeed, the case
p<t
follows from the embedding
\ell^{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{t}
and the case
p>t
from
[
NSS24
, Theorem 4.2]
. Furthermore, using
[
LN19
, Lemma 6.6]
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{S}
is
(1-\frac{r}{2})
-sparse. Finally, we may assume that
\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)>0
for all
Q\in\mathcal{S}
.

Take
p=t
and for every
Q\in\mathcal{S}
let
E_{Q}\subseteq Q
be such that
\lvert E_{Q}\rvert\geq(1-\frac{r}{2})\lvert Q\rvert
and such that the
E_{Q}
’s are disjoint. Furthermore, let

F_{Q}:=\bigl\{x\in Q:|f(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)\bigr\},

for which we note that
\lvert F_{Q}\rvert\geq r\lvert Q\rvert
by definition. Hence, for
G_{Q}:=E_{Q}\cap F_{Q}
we have

\lvert G_{Q}\rvert=\lvert F_{Q}\setminus E_{Q}^{\mathrm{c}}\rvert\geq r\lvert Q\rvert-\tfrac{r}{2}\lvert Q\rvert=\tfrac{r}{2}\lvert Q\rvert.

Since
w\in A_{q}
, we deduce from (
4.1
) that

w(G_{Q})\geq[w]_{A_{q}}^{-1}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{2}\bigr)^{q}\cdot w(Q).

We can now calculate

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{p}\ind_{Q}\Bigr)^{1/p}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{p}w(Q)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{r,q}[w]_{A_{q}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{p}w(G_{Q})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}[w]_{A_{q}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{G_{Q}}|f(x)|^{p}w(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq[w]_{A_{q}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}w(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=[w]_{A_{q}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}^{p},

finishing the proof.
∎

Before using Proposition
4.3
to deduce
H^{p}(w)
to
L^{p}(w)
estimates for Calderón–Zygmund operators, we note that the weight dependence in Proposition
4.3
is sharp. Recall that
[w]_{A_{\infty}}\lesssim[w]_{A_{q}}
for all
q\in[1,\infty)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
p,t\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in(1,\infty)
,
r\in(0,1)
and
w\in A_{q}
. If
\alpha\geq 0
is such that for all
\eta
-sparse collections of cubes
\mathcal{S}
and all
f\in L^{p}(w)
we have

\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{t}\ind_{Q}\Bigr)^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim[w]_{A_{q}}^{\alpha}\cdot\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)},

with implicit constant independent of
w
, then
\alpha\geq\max\{\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{t}\}
.

Proof.

By

[
LN19
, Lemma 6.6]
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
and by monotonicity we may assume
r<\frac{1}{2}
.
For all
k\geq 0
define
Q_{k}:=[0,2^{-k})^{d}
and set
\mathcal{S}:=\{Q_{k}:k\geq 0\}
, which is
\frac{1}{2}
-sparse.

First let
R\subseteq Q_{0}
such that
\lvert R\rvert=2r\lvert Q_{0}\rvert
. For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
define

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\ind_{R}+\ind_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus R},

for which we have
[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}\eqsim\varepsilon^{-1}
. Take
f_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1/p}\ind_{R}
, for which we note that

\lVert f_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{p}(w_{\varepsilon})}=\lvert R\rvert^{\frac{1}{p}}\eqsim_{p,r}1.

Finally, we calculate

\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f_{\varepsilon}|)^{t}\ind_{Q}\Bigr)^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w_{\varepsilon})}=\bigl\|\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q_{0}}(|f_{\varepsilon}|)\ind_{Q_{0}}\bigr\|_{L^{p}(w_{\varepsilon})}=\varepsilon^{-1/p}\cdot w_{\varepsilon}(Q_{0})^{1/p}\eqsim_{p}[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}^{1/p},

so
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{p}
.

Next, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
define

w_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon\lvert x\rvert^{-d+\varepsilon}\ind_{Q_{0}}(x)+\ind_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus Q_{0}}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for which we have

[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}\eqsim\varepsilon^{-1}.
Furthermore, define
f:=\ind_{Q_{0}}
, which satisfies
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\eqsim 1
. We again calculate

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(|f|)^{t}\ind_{Q}\Bigr)^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl\|\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\ind_{Q_{k}}\Bigr)^{1/t}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{p/t}w(Q_{k-1}\setminus Q_{k})\Bigr)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\eqsim_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{p/t}\varepsilon 2^{-k\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/p}\eqsim_{p,t}\varepsilon^{-1/t}\eqsim[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}^{1/t},

so
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{t}
.
∎

Combining Theorem
D
with Propositions
4.1
and
4.3
, we get quantitative weighted estimates for
s
-smooth Calderón–Zygmund operators from
H^{p}(w)
to
L^{p}(w)
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
s>0
and let
T
be an
s
-smooth Calderón-Zygmund operator. Let
p\in(0,\infty)
and
w\in A_{\infty}
. For all
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T,p,s}[w]_{A_{\infty}}\cdot\bigl\|\sup_{Q}\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f)\ind_{Q}\bigr\|_{L^{p}(w)}

with
r\in(0,1)
only depending on
d
. For any
q\in(1,\infty)
such that
w\in A_{q}
, we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T,p,q,s}[w]_{A_{q}}^{\frac{1}{p}}[w]_{A_{\infty}}^{(1-\frac{1}{p})_{+}}\cdot\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}

In particular, if
s\geq d(\frac{q}{p}-1)
, then
T
is bounded from
H^{p}(w)
to
L^{p}(w)
.

Proof.

(
4.3
) follows by first combining Theorem
D
with
[
NSS24
, Theorem 4.2]
and then expanding the supremum to all cubes
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Similarly, (
4.4
) is a corollary of Theorem
D
combined with Proposition
4.3
using
t=1
. The last statement follows from (
4.4
) by Proposition
4.1
and the density of
H^{p}(w)\cap L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in
H^{p}(w)
.
∎

Qualitatively, the boundedness of
T
from
H^{p}(w)
to
L^{p}(w)
in Theorem
4.5
is already known; see, for example,
[
HO17
]
and the references therein.
The dependence on the weight characteristic in (
4.4
) is sharp for
p\geq 1
, as we will show in Lemma
4.6
below. Hence, Theorem
4.5
may be regarded as an
H^{1}(w)\to L^{1}(w)
analogue of the
A_{2}
theorem
[
Hyt12
]
. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, Theorem
4.5
is the first quantitatively sharp weighted estimate for
s
-smooth Calderón–Zygmund operators in the Hardy space setting.
Moreover, it recovers several results from the literature within a cancellative framework.
For example:

•

In
[
CF74
]
it was shown for
p\in(0,\infty)
and
w\in A_{\infty}
that

\displaystyle\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{T,p,w}\lVert Mf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)},\qquad f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(
4.4
) is an improvement of this result, replacing the Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator
M
on the right-hand side by the cancellative maximal operator
\mathcal{M}^{s}
, which is smaller. Furthermore, (
4.4
) provides (sharp) quantitative dependence on
[w]_{A_{q}}
.

•

By the weak
L^{1}
-boundedness of the Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator
M
, we can estimate

\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(\mathcal{M}^{s}_{3Q}f)\ind_{Q}\leq\mathsf{P}^{r}_{Q}(M(f\ind_{3Q}))\ind_{3Q}\lesssim\tfrac{1}{r}\,\bigl\langle\lvert f\rvert\bigr\rangle_{3Q}\ind_{3Q},

(
4.3
) implies

\lVert Tf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{T,p}[w]_{A_{\infty}}\cdot\lVert Mf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)},\qquad f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

which for
p=1
was previously obtained in
[
LOP09
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
s>0
,
p\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in(1,\infty)
and suppose
w\in A_{q}
. Suppose
s>\frac{1}{p}-1
and let
H
denote the Hilbert transform. If
\alpha\geq 0
is such that for all
f\in H^{p}(w)\cap L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\lVert Hf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim[w]_{A_{q}}^{\alpha}\cdot\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)},

with implicit constant independent of
w
, then
\alpha\geq 1
.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
define

w_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon x^{-1+\varepsilon}\ind_{(0,1)}(x)+\ind_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,1)}(x)\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

for which we have

[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}\eqsim\varepsilon^{-1}.
Write
s=m+\delta
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
and define

f(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{k}{{m+1}\choose{k}}\ind_{(k,k+1)}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

for which we note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}f(x)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq m.

Since
f
has a jump discontinuity in
x=0
, we know that
\lvert Hf(x)\rvert\eqsim\log(\frac{1}{x})
for
x\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\lVert Hf\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log(\tfrac{1}{x})^{p}\varepsilon x^{-1+\varepsilon}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/p}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-1}\eqsim[w_{\varepsilon}]_{A_{q}}.

Now to estimate
\mathcal{M}^{s}f
, fix
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,m+3)
and take an interval
I
containing
x
such
I\cap(0,m+2)\neq\varnothing
. Furthermore, fix a
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(I)
and let
P^{m}_{0}(\varphi)
denote the Taylor polynomial of
\varphi
at
0
of degree
m
. Since
\varphi
is
s
-smooth and supported on
I
, we have

\lvert\varphi(y)-P_{0}^{m}(\varphi)(y)\rvert\lesssim_{s}\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{s}}{|I|^{s}},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R},

and therefore, using the cancellative properties of
f
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|I|}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\varphi(y)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|=\frac{1}{|I|}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\bigl(\varphi(y)-P_{0}^{m}(\varphi)(y)\bigr)\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}y\Bigr|\lesssim_{s}\frac{1}{|I|^{1+s}}.

Hence, taking the supremum over all
\varphi
and
I
and noting that
|I|\gtrsim_{m}\lvert x\rvert
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{s}f(x)\lesssim_{s}\lvert x\rvert^{-1-s},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,m+3).

Therefore, since
s>\frac{1}{p}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{M}^{s}f\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{s}\lVert\ind_{(-1,m+2)}\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}+\lVert x\mapsto\lvert x\rvert^{-1-s}\ind_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,m+3)}(x)\rVert_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{p,s}1

proving that
\alpha\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Theorem
A
and Proposition
4.3
can be combined to give a version of Theorem
4.5
for Haar shifts. Indeed, with the same proof (only specializing definitions to the dyadic setting) one can prove that for all
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\|\Sh f\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{s,t}[w]_{A_{\infty}}\|\sup_{Q}\mathsf{P}_{Q}^{r}(\mathcal{M}_{Q}f)\|_{L^{p}(w)}

and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|\Sh f\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim_{s,t}[w]_{A_{q}}^{\frac{1}{p}}[w]_{A_{\infty}}^{(1-\frac{1}{p})_{+}}\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{L^{p}(w)},

where, as in Theorem
4.5
, the implicit constant depends on the exponent
q
such that
w\in A_{q}
. The right hand side of (
4.5
) is one of the possible definitions of dyadic weighted
H^{1}
from
[
GC79
]
and
[
ST89b
]
.
