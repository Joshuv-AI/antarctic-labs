DAPR-AM-Net: an end-to-end smart farming system powered by dual-attention progressive refinement and adaptive MixUp for explainable tomato leaf disease classification and forecasting
Authors: Ran Wang, Xiao Yu, Lina Lu, Cong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVision-based crop disease diagnosis plays a pivotal role in smart agriculture, yet challenges such as complex field backgrounds, high intra-class similarity of lesion morphology, and severe data imbalance continue to impede model stability and interpretability. To address these issues, this study proposes DAPR-AM-Net, an intelligent diagnostic framework for tomato leaf diseases that integrates dual-attention progressive refinement with adaptive MixUp. The method introduces four key innovations: (1) a Dual Attention Fusion Mechanism (DAFM) that jointly leverages channel-wise and spatial attention to enhance lesion-related texture, color, and structural cues while suppressing background noise via the CBAM module, thereby directing the network’s focus toward pathogenic regions; (2) an Adaptive MixUp with Attention-Aware Sampling (AMAAS) module that dynamically adjusts sample mixing ratios according to attention maps, effectively improving discrimination in complex boundary areas; (3) a Progressive Feature Refinement with Dual Attention (PFR-DA) module that incrementally optimizes deep feature representations through cross-hierarchical information flows; and (4) an Imbalance-Aware Multi-Objective Optimization (IAMOO) strategy that adaptively modulates loss weights based on category distribution to strengthen recognition of minority disease classes. On our self-constructed Tomato-DD dataset, DAPR-AM-Net achieves superior performance across all major metrics—including an accuracy of 99.73%, precision of 99.73%, recall of 99.74%, and an F1-score of 99.73%—outperforming current state-of-the-art approaches. On the full Plant-Village dataset, the model achieves 99.85% accuracy, 99.78% precision, 99.84% recall, and a 99.81% F1-score, while maintaining a compact model size of only 4.72 M parameters. Multi-level interpretability analyses corroborate the transparency and reliability of the model’s inference process. Additionally, we developed an end-to-end smart agriculture platform powered by DAPR-AM-Net. Overall, DAPR-AM-Net provides a forward-looking yet practical solution for high-accuracy and strongly interpretable disease diagnosis in smart agriculture scenarios, demonstrating both methodological innovation and real-world applicability.Keywords: Tomato disease detection, Convolutional neural networks, Agricultural visual diagnosis, Dual attention mechanism, Progressive feature refinement, Imbalance-aware learning, Smart agriculture platform

Introduction

Agriculture, as the foundation of human civilization, is currently confronted with multiple challenges, including climate change, population growth, and increasing resource scarcity [1]. Among these challenges, crop diseases have emerged as one of the major bottlenecks restricting the sustainable development of global agriculture [2]. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates that plant diseases cause annual global yield losses of 20%–40%, with tomatoes being particularly vulnerable due to their high economic importance. As a key vegetable crop, tomato yield and quality are closely associated with food security and farmers’ livelihoods. Tomato leaf diseases—including early and late blight, bacterial spot, Verticillium wilt, and pest-induced disorders—exhibit high incidence, rapid spread, and visually similar symptoms, making early and accurate diagnosis challenging under field conditions [3]. Traditional manual scouting is labor-intensive and unsuitable for large-scale monitoring, thereby motivating the development of computer vision–based automated disease recognition and intelligent decision-support systems in smart agriculture [4]. Despite the success of deep learning approaches, challenges such as data imbalance, complex backgrounds, limited interpretability, and the gap between laboratory studies and real-world deployment remain unresolved [5].

At the methodological level, attention mechanisms have become a central component in plant disease recognition by enabling models to focus on discriminative lesion regions and improve fine-grained classification under complex backgrounds. Channel and spatial attention modules, when integrated with lightweight backbone networks, have been shown to substantially enhance the extraction of disease-related texture and boundary features [6]. Meanwhile, hybrid data augmentation strategies such as MixUp, CutMix, and RICAP effectively improve model generalization and alleviate overfitting. Recent studies further refine these mixed augmentation schemes to better address the limited sample size and high variability in agricultural imagery [7].

Performance gains alone are insufficient for real-world agricultural deployment. Growers and agronomists require interpretable diagnoses and actionable guidance, encompassing both the rationale behind model predictions and corresponding management recommendations. Accordingly, the integration of interpretability techniques—such as Grad-CAM and activation-based visualization—into automated diagnostic systems to establish a closed loop of “visual evidence, interpretable explanation, and decision support” has emerged as a critical direction in both high-impact research and practical applications [8].

Despite notable advances, tomato leaf disease diagnosis still faces several critical limitations. Existing models struggle to capture subtle inter-class visual differences, while severe class imbalance in training datasets often degrades performance for minority diseases. Moreover, most interpretability approaches remain post-hoc and insufficiently integrated with attention mechanisms, limiting the reliability of model explanations. Finally, current studies largely emphasize algorithmic benchmarks, with limited validation through end-to-end agricultural platforms capable of delivering actionable management recommendations, thereby constraining real-world adoption and scalability [5].

Against this backdrop, we propose DAPR-AM-Net (Dual-Attention Progressive Refinement with Adaptive MixUp), a unified framework for tomato leaf disease recognition and prediction. Unlike conventional approaches that treat attention mechanisms, data augmentation, and optimization strategies as independent enhancements, the proposed method is built upon a coupled learning paradigm, where feature representation, data augmentation, and decision optimization are jointly optimized within a closed-loop system. Specifically, DAPR-AM-Net introduces four tightly integrated components. First, a Dual-Attention Fusion Mechanism (DAFM) embeds channel and spatial attention into the EfficientNet-B0 backbone, enabling hierarchical feature recalibration. Second, Adaptive MixUp with Attention-Aware Sampling (AMAAS) establishes a novel interaction between data augmentation and feature learning, where mixing strategies are guided by attention-derived saliency, rather than random sampling. Third, a Progressive Feature Refinement strategy (PFR-DA) reformulates feature extraction as a multi-stage refinement process, allowing iterative interaction between low-level textures and high-level semantics. Finally, an Imbalance-Aware Multi-Objective Optimization (IAMOO) scheme integrates class-sensitive loss design and adaptive sampling into a unified optimization objective. Importantly, these components are not independent modules but are coordinated through a progressive learning–augmentation–optimization loop, transforming the conventional static pipeline into a dynamic, self-reinforcing learning framework.

To enhance interpretability and real-world applicability, Grad-CAM visualizations are integrated with attention maps to form a dual-path, evidence-based explanation module. Furthermore, the proposed model is deployed within a smart-agriculture platform that provides disease-driven management services, including spraying and irrigation recommendations, meteorological monitoring, and agricultural question–answering. In contrast to prior studies focusing solely on model-level improvements, this work advances toward a methodological and system-level paradigm, delivering an interpretable, deployable, and operationally validated intelligent agriculture solution.

Related work

Methods for plant disease detection

Deep learning–based approaches for plant disease detection have rapidly evolved from early transfer learning–driven classification models to more integrated frameworks incorporating detection, segmentation, and interpretability. Early studies demonstrated the effectiveness of pre-trained CNNs under limited data conditions, achieving high accuracy in benchmark datasets such as Plant-Village [9]. As application scenarios expanded from controlled laboratory environments to open-field and greenhouse conditions, lightweight networks and mobile deployment became prominent research directions. Several studies have proposed compact architectures suitable for edge devices, enabling real-time detection and on-site deployment, with an average accuracy of 98.32% [10]. Furthermore, classification using MobileNetV3-small yielded a test accuracy of approximately 99.50% [11]. TrioConvTomatoNet: novel CNN with triple conv layers per stage and SGD optimizer, achieves 99.39% accuracy in tomato leaf disease classification [12].

From an architectural perspective, attention mechanisms—such as channel, spatial, and hierarchical fusion designs—are widely adopted to enhance lesion-focused representation and robustness under complex backgrounds [13–15]. More recently, Transformer-based models have been explored for end-to-end plant disease detection, leveraging their ability to capture long-range dependencies and global feature representations with competitive detection and classification performance. For instance, the Plant Multi-Modal Vision Transformer (PMVT) attained accuracies of 93.6%, 85.4%, and 93.1% on wheat, coffee, and rice datasets, respectively [16]. On a large-scale plant disease dataset, the PD-TR model achieved the highest reported mAP of 56.3 [17]. In crop segmentation and lesion localization tasks, channel and spatial attention mechanisms have been proven to substantially enhance model sensitivity to tiny lesions and low-contrast targets, thereby improving segmentation accuracy and localization reliability [18–20]. In crop classification and crop mapping, attention-based feature weighting enables models to better focus on discriminative spectral bands or local morphological patterns, resulting in improved classification performance on multi-temporal remote sensing and hyperspectral data, with reported accuracies reaching 97.82% [21]. The combination of attention mechanisms with lightweight backbones enables accurate and efficient models for edge deployment, supporting real-time inference on resource-constrained platforms such as drones, mobile devices, and IoT nodes [22, 23]. To address complex backgrounds and long-tail distributions, attention mechanisms have been combined with data augmentation and domain adaptation to emphasize rare lesions and domain-specific features, thereby improving cross-domain generalization [24, 25]. One such method outperformed the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) and baseline approaches by a remarkable 13.67% in classification performance [26]. In crop health monitoring, attention-guided multi-scale fusion is widely used to integrate global context with local details, supporting accurate field-level mapping and precision spraying decisions. The proposed SSMR-Net, incorporating an Adaptive Feature Mapping Attention (AFMA) module, achieved superior weed and wheat segmentation performance on a custom dataset, with weed recognition precision of 0.774, IoU of 0.696, and mIoU of 0.865 [27]. On a constructed strawberry disease dataset, the proposed approach attained a recognition accuracy of 99.10% [28]. In temporal and spatio-temporal modeling, attention-equipped sequence models can automatically identify critical time steps and spectral bands, leading to improved short-term accuracy in yield forecasting and disease spread prediction [29–31]. Recent studies have also explored the application of artificial intelligence in challenging agricultural monitoring scenarios. For instance, Abdusalomov et al. (2025) [32]demonstrated that integrating advanced deep learning techniques can significantly improve detection accuracy in agricultural fire monitoring under complex environmental conditions. This highlights the importance of robustness and adaptability in real-world agricultural AI systems. Overall, attention mechanisms provide both consistent performance gains and practical pathways for model lightweighting, generalization, and interpretability. Future studies should focus on improving the efficiency and robustness of attention modules under challenging conditions and exploring their integration with plant physiological knowledge to develop more reliable and actionable agricultural vision systems.

Data scarcity and class imbalance remain persistent challenges in crop disease detection. To mitigate these issues, data augmentation and hybrid sampling strategies, such as Balanced-MixUp, CutMix, and RICAP variants, have been shown to improve generalization for long-tail and few-sample classes across multimodal and multispectral datasets [33, 34]. For instance, the proposed PWDNet achieved 100% recall and 90% precision on a pine wilt disease test set [35], while another method attained classification accuracies of 99.79%, 92.59%, and 97.12% on two apple leaf datasets and one coffee leaf dataset, respectively [36]. In parallel, GAN-based augmentation and domain adaptation have been explored to alleviate domain shifts and improve model robustness across varying acquisition conditions. One representative study applied GAN-based image synthesis and augmentation to a small, severely imbalanced tomato leaf disease dataset, resulting in a 5.2% improvement in classification accuracy [37]. Recent reviews identify diffusion models as a more stable and high-quality alternative to GANs for agricultural image augmentation and multimodal analysis, particularly under resource constraints. In parallel, few-shot and low-shot learning frameworks have demonstrated strong recognition performance for rare diseases using only limited training samples; for example, a Vision Transformer equipped with PMF + FA modules delivered an average disease recognition accuracy of 90.12% [38]. Combining semi-supervised and few-shot learning has further alleviated the burden of labeled data scarcity, substantially boosting performance under low-sample regimes [39]. Despite recent advances, achieving robust cross-domain generalization with reduced annotation costs while maintaining accuracy and interpretability remains an open challenge. Motivated by this gap, DAPR-AM-Net integrates dual-attention–based progressive refinement with adaptive MixUp to improve long-tail and few-sample recognition, supporting a shift from passive diagnosis toward proactive disease prevention in intelligent agricultural systems.

Explainable AI in agriculture

As deep learning performance in agricultural applications continues to improve, explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as a key criterion for model trustworthiness. Recent research in agricultural vision emphasizes visual explanations, model validation, causal or symbolic interpretation, and user acceptance [40]. Visualization-based explainability methods, such as Grad-CAM and Integrated Gradients, are widely used in crop disease analysis to interpret model decisions, while comparisons with expert annotations help identify spurious features and inform data refinement and model retraining [41]. To enable quantitative evaluation of explainability, multiple works have proposed dedicated metrics and conducted user studies, aiming to transform “interpretability” from a subjective presentation into a measurable attribute, thereby assessing the trustworthiness and adoptability of model recommendations in agricultural contexts (e.g., by agronomists and farmers). Such research emphasizes that explanations should not only be “understandable” but also “actionable” [42, 43]. Causal inference [44] and symbolic reasoning have begun to be incorporated into agricultural decision-support systems to reveal mechanistic links between input features (e.g., meteorological, soil, or spectral indicators) and target outputs (e.g., disease risk, water demand), enabling models to support strategic interventions rather than merely providing “black-box” predictions. Causal modeling that integrates graph neural networks with attention mechanisms has shown promising results in crop growth and yield forecasting studies [45]. Despite rapid progress, agricultural XAI still faces challenges in explanation stability, integration with domain knowledge, and the conversion of insights into actionable management decisions. Future research should embed explainability into training objectives, design user-oriented interfaces, and establish standardized benchmarks to advance XAI from illustrative explanations toward decision-support systems [46].

Dataset description and preprocessing

Dataset description

In this study, two high-quality datasets were employed: the self-constructed Tomato-DD (Tomato Disease & Damage) dataset and the widely used publicly available Plant-Village dataset [47]. These datasets span real-world production and controlled experimental settings, enabling cross-domain training and generalization assessment under both high-interference and ideal conditions. This dual-domain design robustly evaluates the resilience and practical applicability of DAPR-AM-Net in complex tomato leaf disease scenarios.

It encompasses 11 major categories of tomato leaf diseases, including Bacterial Spot, Early Blight, Late Blight, Leaf Mold, Septoria Leaf Spot, Spider Mites, Target Spot, Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus, Tomato Mosaic Virus, Healthy, and Powdery Mildew. First, all images are resized to 224 × 224 and RGB channels are normalized, reducing inter-dataset statistical bias and enabling effective feature learning in DAPR-AM-Net.

Second, to improve robustness in complex field conditions, a multi-level augmentation strategy is employed. Strong augmentation simulates illumination variation, noise, blur, occlusion, and structural loss, while a moderate scheme is used during training. Third, building on the augmentation strategy, this study further incorporates an Adaptive MixUp with Attention-Aware Sampling (AMAAS). Unlike the conventional MixUp approach [48], AMAAS performs importance-adaptive pairing by leveraging the attention responses extracted from the model’s feature maps in the preceding stage. Samples with high-attention regions are preferentially mixed, directing learning toward true lesion areas. Together, these measures improve the accuracy and stability of long-tailed categories under realistic field conditions.

Architecture of DAPR-AM-Net

DAPR-AM-Net is an end-to-end visual diagnosis framework for tomato leaf disease identification, designed as a closed-loop learning system that tightly couples feature extraction, attention-driven refinement, data augmentation, and decision optimization. Rather than a conventional sequential pipeline, the framework operates as a mutually reinforced architecture, where each component dynamically influences the others. The backbone network is responsible for hierarchical feature extraction, while the proposed Dual Attention Fusion Mechanism (DAFM) serves as the core refinement unit. DAFM sequentially integrates SE channel attention and CBAM spatial–channel attention to progressively enhance disease-relevant representations. Building upon this, the Progressive Feature Refinement (PFR-DA) strategy introduces cross-layer interactions, enabling iterative refinement of features across different semantic levels. This transforms feature learning from a one-pass extraction process into a multi-stage adaptive refinement paradigm. During training, the framework further incorporates two tightly coupled mechanisms. The Adaptive MixUp with Attention-Aware Sampling (AMAAS) leverages attention feedback to guide semantically meaningful sample mixing, thereby forming a data–model co-adaptation process. Meanwhile, the Imbalance-Aware Multi-Objective Optimization (IAMOO) integrates weighted sampling and class-sensitive loss design into a unified objective, improving robustness under long-tailed distributions. Importantly, these components are not independently optimized; instead, they form a progressive learning loop, in which attention guides augmentation, augmentation influences feature learning, and optimization feedback further refines attention responses. During inference, the model outputs both disease probabilities and class activation heatmaps, enabling accurate diagnosis with intrinsic interpretability. Robustness analysis under environmental perturbations

To further validate the practical deployability of DAPR-AM-Net in real-world greenhouse and field scenarios, we conducted a series of robustness tests by intentionally degrading the test images with Gaussian noise, motion blur, and varying brightness conditions. These perturbations simulate common challenges in agricultural imaging, such as sensor noise, camera shake, and non-uniform illumination.

The detailed experimental results are summarized in Fig. 13. As shown, DAPR-AM-Net achieves a baseline accuracy of 99.67% on the original clean test set. When subjected to Gaussian noise (σ = 0.05), the accuracy slightly decreases to 99.27%, which is expected given the random corruption of fine-grained texture features. Remarkably, the model exhibits negligible performance degradation under motion blur (99.61%) and brightness changes (99.62%), with accuracy drops of less than 0.1% compared to the clean baseline. This exceptional stability can be attributed to two key design choices in our architecture: (1) The Dual-Attention Progressive Refinement (DAFM) mechanism, which emphasizes discriminative lesion areas even when edges are blurred; and (2) The Adaptive MixUp (AMAAS) strategy, which regularizes the model against synthetic distribution shifts during training. The robustness experiments under Gaussian noise, motion blur, and brightness variation demonstrate that the model can effectively handle common real-world perturbations. This robustness can be attributed to the attention-guided feature refinement and adaptive data augmentation strategies, which enhance the model’s ability to focus on disease-relevant regions under degraded conditions. Nevertheless, performance degradation under extreme perturbations highlights the inherent limitation of RGB-based approaches, particularly when dealing with subtle spectral differences.

Performance evaluation of the intelligent agriculture system

System performance was rigorously evaluated on 40 CPU-only devices (Intel i5/i7 laptops without discrete GPUs). The proposed framework achieved an average per-image latency of 0.87 s, encompassing preprocessing, inference, and Grad-CAM visualization. On mobile platforms (iPhone and mainstream Android devices), mean latency was 1.12 s, with 90.9% of requests completed within 2 s, satisfying real-time field deployment requirements. Grad-CAM introduced only 0.21 s of overhead while enhancing model interpretability. Under 4G networks, average page load time was 3.46 s, with first-screen rendering under 2 s; system stability was maintained even below 1 Mbps via WebP compression and lazy loading.

Consistency validation confirmed that system-level diagnostics fully preserved the accuracy of DAPR-AM-Net, with no performance degradation from deployment-side quantization or optimization. The pesticide spraying alert module effectively provided early warnings under high-risk conditions, reducing disease incidence and chemical input. The irrigation forecasting module, integrating Open-Meteo data with cumulative precipitation modeling, delivered accurate irrigation guidance and lowered water consumption, while the meteorological disaster module enabled proactive farmer responses through advanced extreme-event alerts.

Compared with existing platforms, the proposed system achieves superior diagnostic accuracy, interpretability, and early-warning performance, enabling a tightly integrated decision-making pipeline that advances a single-image–driven, full-season management paradigm. The current implementation focuses on system design and functional validation, and does not yet include quantitative field trials to assess agronomic outcomes.

Discussion

This study proposes DAPR-AM-Net as an end-to-end framework linking interpretable feature learning, inference, and decision support. Beyond optimizing performance, the framework unifies attention modeling, attention-guided augmentation, progressive refinement, and imbalance-aware optimization, while embedding interpretability directly into the deployed platform. This design enables an auditable “evidence–recommendation–assistance” workflow, reducing adoption barriers and facilitating evidence-based decision-making in real-world agricultural practice.

Scientifically, the integration of DAFM and PFR-DA establishes a channel–spatial–cross-layer attention loop that robustly localizes lesion regions under complex backgrounds, while AMAAS exploits attention feedback to form a self-reinforcing model–data–model augmentation process, improving recall for minority classes. From an engineering perspective, the study delivers a fully reproducible training-to-deployment pipeline with automated logging, class-wise evaluation, and checkpoint management, and seamlessly integrates inference, interpretability, and actionable recommendations into a practical decision-support system.

Nevertheless, several practical limitations remain. Reliance on RGB imagery may constrain performance under adverse illumination or for spectrally subtle pathologies. Although IAMOO and AMAAS alleviate long-tail effects, overconfident errors may still arise for extremely rare or previously unseen diseases, which introduces potential risks in fully automated decision-making scenarios. Therefore, the proposed system is more appropriately positioned as a decision-support tool rather than a fully autonomous system. In real-world deployment, confidence-aware triggers, uncertainty estimation, or threshold-based alerting mechanisms should be incorporated to flag ambiguous or out-of-distribution samples for manual review. Moreover, while attention visualizations enhance interpretability, they should be jointly considered with confidence scores, historical observations, and meteorological context to avoid potential visual misinterpretation. This highlights the importance of maintaining a human-in-the-loop paradigm to ensure safe and reliable use.

Based on these findings, we outline several directions for improvement. At the data level, multi-view and multi-spectral acquisition, together with guided multi-angle image upload, could enhance robustness. At the model level, extending PFR-DA to pixel- or instance-level segmentation may enable more precise lesion delineation under co-occurring diseases. At the system level, integrating active learning and online fine-tuning with expert feedback can support regional and temporal adaptation. To bridge this gap, future work should focus on conducting controlled field experiments and pilot studies, where the proposed system can be integrated into actual farming practices. Such studies may include A/B testing of management strategies, longitudinal monitoring of crop health, and quantitative evaluation using metrics such as pesticide usage reduction rate, disease incidence, and resource efficiency. In addition, recent advances in other complex classification domains have highlighted the growing importance of optimization-driven hybrid architectures, where heterogeneous model components are jointly optimized to enhance representation capacity and generalization. For example, hybrid frameworks that combine deep sequential modeling, convolutional feature extraction, and metaheuristic optimization—such as the BiLSTM–metaheuristic optimization with hybrid U-Net–MobileNetV2 encoder architecture proposed for sEMG-based gesture classification—have demonstrated strong performance in handling high-dimensional and nonlinear data distributions [62]. From an ethical and deployment perspective, staged pilot studies with agricultural stakeholders are recommended to incorporate human validation, risk assessment, and economic evaluation prior to large-scale adoption. Overall, DAPR-AM-Net moves beyond competitive metrics to establish an interpretable and practical paradigm for intelligent agriculture through human-centered, assistive, and risk-aware decision support. Future work will focus on multimodal integration, pixel-level refinement, and large-scale field validation to further enhance robustness and long-term deployability. In addition, domain adaptation and domain generalization techniques may be explored to further improve robustness when transferring from controlled datasets to real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

This study presents DAPR-AM-Net, an end-to-end visual diagnosis and decision-support framework for tomato leaf diseases, with integrated contributions at both algorithmic and engineering levels. Algorithmically, four complementary components—DAFM, AMAAS, PFR-DA, and IAMOO—are unified into a synergistic architecture spanning feature learning and training optimization, markedly improving discrimination under complex field conditions and recall for minority disease classes. From an engineering perspective, we further deploy the model within a smart agriculture platform that seamlessly integrates inference, interpretability, agronomic recommendations, and user interaction, forming a closed-loop workflow from model output to evidence-driven agricultural decision-making.

Experimental results show that DAPR-AM-Net achieves near-optimal classification performance on the Tomato-DD test set, with overall metrics approaching 99.8%. Ablation and robustness analyses verify both the independent effectiveness and synergistic interactions of the proposed modules: DAFM enhances lesion-aware semantic representation, AMAAS preserves critical features under few-shot and noisy conditions, PFR-DA improves cross-depth feature consistency, and IAMOO alleviates long-tail–induced bias. Comprehensive visualization, logging, and checkpointing strategies further ensure training transparency, reproducibility, and the reliability of the reported conclusions.

From an engineering standpoint, this work achieves a full translation from algorithmic design to field-ready deployment. Beyond image-level diagnosis, the system delivers intuitive interpretability, actionable recommendations, and expert feedback pathways, enabling human–machine collaboration and an active learning loop in real-world settings. This integrated implementation markedly improves model adoptability and lowers the institutional and cognitive barriers between laboratory research and agricultural practice.

Despite these advances, several practical limitations remain. Reliance on RGB imagery constrains robustness under extreme illumination and spectral variability, motivating future integration of multispectral or near-infrared data. Large-scale deployment will further require continuous drift monitoring and online fine-tuning to address regional and temporal disease shifts. To avoid misinterpretation of attention maps, confidence-aware decision thresholds and human-in-the-loop validation should be incorporated to progressively and safely extend automated decision boundaries.

In conclusion, DAPR-AM-Net advances both methodological design and engineering deployment by unifying interpretability, robustness, and imbalance-aware learning within an operational smart agriculture platform. Future work will extend toward multimodal integration, pixel-level lesion analysis, active learning, and large-scale field validation to establish a reliable and sustainable tool for precision agricultural disease management.
