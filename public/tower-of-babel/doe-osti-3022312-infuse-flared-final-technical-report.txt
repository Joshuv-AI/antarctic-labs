Executive Summary
This report summarizes the final outcomes of the INFUSE:FLARED collaboration between
Tokamak Energy (TE), the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC), and the Department
of Energy (DOE).

Context

The project aimed to experimentally determine and model the pumping, absorption, and separation
behavior of deuterium in flowing liquid lithium under gas and plasma exposure conditions. The
work supports the development of lithium-based plasma-facing components (PFCs) and tritium
management strategies for next-generation fusion plants.

Tokamak Energy Sponsorship

In sponsorship of the work, Tokamak Energy (TE) provided the University of Illinois at Urbana-
Champaign (UIUC) with $50,000 to purchase components for the ECR plasma source. These
components included: the ECR electromagnet, microwave waveguide vacuum feedthrough, and
bi-directional power meter. Alongside this, several TE employees attended bi-weekly meetings
with the technical team at UIUC to provide input and guidance throughout the project duration
(3.5 years). This is estimated at a cost of $65,000 (3 employees, 0.75hrs per week at $160 per hour)
and does not account for other TE project contributions such as foreign travel (which included a
TE team visiting UIUC for a week and hosting the UIUC technical team at the TE site for a week)
and conference presentations (given by both UIUC technical team members and TE employees).

These commitments, combined with the project cost and time overrun, led to TE expending
resources that exceeded the cost share agreed between the two parties at the project onset.
Consequently, an agreement was reached between the two parties that UIUC would assume sole
responsibility for the technical delivery of any milestones associated with the project.

Key Achievements
 • Commissioning of an ECR Plasma Source: An Electron Cyclotron Resonance (ECR)
 plasma source was installed onto APOLLO. This source was characterized by a suite of
 plasma diagnostics (Langmuir probe array, RFEA, and spectroscopic measurements) and
 shown to be able to generate a hydrogen ion flux of ~5x1017 s-1 and a hydrogen radical
       flux of ~1.5x1019 s-1.
 • s to the open surface of the lithium PFC.
 • Demonstration of hydrogen pumping by liquid lithium: Multiple hydrogen gas
 pressurizations to ~45 Torr led to a maximum molecular flux of 2.3x1021 s-1 being
 absorbed into the lithium within the HyDE chamber.

 • Exposure of liquid lithium PFC to hydrogen plasma: Over 26 hours of cumulative
 plasma exposure with fluxes ~5x1017 H+s-1 on the open surface tantalum 3D ordered
 mesh PFC.
 • Distillation at 650 C showed decrease in hydrogen concentration: Initial distillation
 tests after exposure to hydrogen plasma show a 140 μΩ drop in the resistivity probe
 reading corresponding to a 0.44% decrease in hydrogen molar concentration.
Future Work

 • Quantification of deuterium gas and plasma pumping by liquid lithium: APOLLO is
 currently being used to conduct additional hydrogen gas and plasma pumping experiments
 with an emphasis on quantification of the pumping rates for different parameters. Once a
 successful operating procedure is determined, the experiments will switch the isotope to
 deuterium and allow for even better quantification of release rates.
 • Validation and Verification of other Tritium Extraction Techniques: The modular
 nature of APOLLO enables different tritium management technologies, such as
 electrolysis cells, cold traps or permeation windows, to be installed. This opens many
 further research opportunities to compare performance of these technologies on a
 standardized system.
 • Continued refinement of tritium inventory modelling: As better quantification of
 pumping and release rates are determined for hydrogen and deuterium, these values can
 be used to inform the modelling of a future full-scale fusion plant tritium inventory.

Actively Pumped Open Surface Lithium Loop (APOLLO)
The Actively Pumped Open Surface Lithium Loop (APOLLO) is a test stand at UIUC for the de-
risking of various liquid lithium technologies including Electromagnetic (EM) pumps and
flowmeters, lithium distributors, PFC concepts, impurity concentration measurement, and
hydrogen isotope distillation. APOLLO consists of three main chambers and is shown in Fig. 1.

 Figure 1. A photo of part of APOLLO including the Reservoir, SLiDE, HyDE, resistivity probe,
 and various EM pumps and flowmeters.

The Reservoir (located in the center of the schematic) is the return point for the loop and is the
chamber used for loading lithium into the system utilizing a high vacuum load lock system. The
high vacuum load lock allows for up to three 100g Li ingots to be loaded into a vacuum suitcase
while under argon atmosphere in a glovebox. The vacuum suitcase can then be transferred to the
Reservoir and pumped down independently from the main Reservoir chamber. Once the pressure
in the vacuum suitcase has reached high vacuum, the separation gate valve can be opened, and the
Li ingots can be dropped individually into the Reservoir by three independently actuating
solenoids. Up to 1500 g of Li can be loaded into APOLLO with this method. Resistive band heaters
wrapped around the outside of the Reservoir vacuum chamber are then used to melt the lithium
and heat to temperatures up to 400 C.
The next chamber is the Solid Liquid Divertor Experiment (SLiDE) which is located in the left
side of Fig. 1. SLiDE houses the open surface plasma facing component (PFC) module and the
electron cyclotron resonance (ECR) plasma source. The PFC module, shown in Fig. 2, consists of
a tantalum 3D ordered mesh mounted on a helium cooled 316 stainless steel body with a
computationally designed lithium flow distributor to ensure even lithium distribution across the
ordered mesh. The PFC module itself was designed to be modular to allow for the replacement of
the distributor and/or the 3D ordered mesh. The ECR plasma source consists of a magnetron
microwave source, reflected power dump, three-stub tuner, waveguide, and an electromagnetic
coil required for producing the resonant magnetic field.

 Figure 2. A photo of the PFC installed into SLiDE. Lithium flows first through a distributor
 which spreads onto the open surface channel. A 3D tantalum structured mesh flow plate is
 installed in the channel.

The third main vacuum chamber in APOLLO is the Hydrogen Distillation Experiment (HyDE)
which is located on the left side of Fig. 1. HyDE consists of a distillation section that can be
inductively heated to 700 C, and a differentially pumped analysis chamber that houses a magnetic
sector RGA for measuring hydrogen isotope release. This RGA allows for in-situ Thermal
Desorption Spectroscopy (TDS) to be conducted on lithium samples. The distillation section has
a conically shaped air-cooled condensation stage that condenses evaporated lithium and returns it
to the loop.
The three major vacuum chambers are connected by ½” 316 stainless steel tubing with VCR metal
gasket face seal fittings. Four EM conduction pumps drive the lithium flow up to 12 g/s by applying
an electric current perpendicular to a permanent magnetic field to drive a jxB force. Five EM
conduction flowmeters measure the flowrate by relying on the inverse of the EM conduction pump
principle. As the conductive fluid (lithium) passes through the magnetic field, an electric potential

is created which can be measured and calibrated to give the lithium mass flow rate. The calibration
was done in-house utilizing a time domain reflectometry-based level probe [1].
Another important component on APOLLO is the resistivity probe. The resistance of liquid lithium
increases linearly as the concentration of hydrogenic isotopes is increased [2]. The resistivity probe
in APOLLO measures the resistance of the lithium across a 12” section of ¼” outer diameter (OD)
tubing. A ~40 A current is driven by a current-limited power supply, and the voltage drop across
the 12” section is read with a microvoltmeter. This allows for quantitative measurement of the
impurity concentration in the lithium in real time.

Work Package 1 (WP1): Plasma source
WP1.1: Specification and design
Significant upgrades to the existing SLiDE chamber were made to add the ECR plasma source. A
picture of the upgraded SLiDE with the ECR plasma source installed is shown in Fig. 3. The
plasma source consists of a magnetron head that produces microwaves which propagate through a
directional coupler, 3-stub tuner, waveguide transition, and finally through a quartz microwave
window into the SLiDE vacuum chamber. A new ECR electromagnet was installed onto the SLiDE
chamber which generates fields up to 1400 Gauss for high field side launching of the microwave.
The magnetic field topology in the chamber was measured using a hall probe and is shown in Fig.
4.

 Figure 3. A picture of the ECR plasma source in SLiDE including the waveguide components,
 magnetron microwave source, ECR magnet coil, 3-stub tuner, and directional coupler.

 Figure 4. Experimentally measured magnetic field topology within SLiDE with the new magnet
 setup. The blue dotted line is the PFC location and the red dotted line is the microwave launch
 point into SLiDE.

An ECR plasma source was chosen due to being able to achieve high ionization fractions at lower
neutral pressures (~1 mTorr) than other low temperature plasma sources. This low neutral pressure
minimizes the contamination of the lithium in the SLiDE chamber by lowering the total gas flow
required into the SLiDE chamber. It also allows for the differentiation of plasma-related ion and
radical effects from neutral gas effects on the lithium surface. The temperatures produced by the
ECR plasma source plasma are in the 5-10 eV range which matches the energy of ions in a detached
scrape off layer.

Components were procured, and the plasma source was assembled as designed.

WP1.4: Construction and testing
Following construction of the plasma source, diagnosis is necessary to find the operating
parameters that maximize the hydrogen flux onto the diagnostic plate. The operating parameters
with the greatest impact on hydrogen flux were determined to be the applied microwave power
from the magnetron, the configuration of the magnetic field resulting from the ECR magnet and
PFC magnets, and the density of the hydrogen background gas. Tests measuring the ion flux as a
function of varying these parameters were conducted.

Figure 7. Ion flux as a function of ECR power. For these tests, the microwave power was initially
2000W, then ramped down to 300W (blue dots) and back to 2000W (red dots).

Figure 8. Spectroscopy data from the power sweep tests showing both the electron and radical
density as a function of the microwave power. Similarly to the LP plots blue marks are for
measurements taken as the power is increased and the red marks indicate values taken when the
power was being decreased. The trends in spectroscopy align well with the Langmuir Probe data
with a peak in the density profile seen around 900W. The spectroscopy data also shows the
hysteresis in electron density observed previously. The radical density, however, shows a steady
increase with increasing power with near identical values measured during power increases and
decreases.

The first parameter that was tested was the applied microwave power. Tests were done by sweeping
the microwave power in a range from 300W to 2000W while keeping the power constant for 30s-
1min intervals and making diagnostic measurements. Fig. 7 shows a particular test where power
is swept from 2000W to 300W, and then back to 2000W, using diagnostic measurements from both
the Langmuir Probes and Retarding Field Energy Analyzer. A decrease in the ion flux is shown at
higher microwave power during the decreasing measurements, with a stable peak being shown to
be at about 1000W. Fig. 8 shows data from a similar experiment with electron density
measurements taken using spectroscopy diagnostics. This shows a similar trend to Fig. 7,
indicating a stable peak at 900-1000W. From this and additional data taken from spectroscopy, it
is determined that the optimum microwave power for maximizing flux to the PFC is 1000W.

 Figure 9. Plots comparing the RFEA and LP measurements, the plotted parameter is the
 integrated flux over the entire plate. The RGEA data and LP show very similar trends with each
 magnet sweep, and the values of the grounded LP align well with the RFEA data.

The second parameter that was tested was the magnetic topology within SLiDE. The dependence
of the ion flux on each magnet is tested individually. When not being swept, the magnet coils were
at constant currents of ECR: 130A, Upper PFC: 30A, Lower PFC: 125A. Fig. 9 displays the results
of sweeping the magnet currents from their minimum to maximum values in a similar experiment
to the microwave power. LP and RFEA data show similar trends for these experiments.

The ECR magnet is observed to only cause minor changes in the ion flux at currents above 110A.
At currents below this, the field is too low to drive resonances and large reflected microwave
powers are observed. The upper PFC magnet has a stronger effect on the ion flux with up to a
factor of 4 variation. An optimum upper PFC magnet current was found at 40A. The lower PFC
magnet current shows a continually increasing ion flux with field strength, therefore the highest
value available on the power supply (125 A) is the optimum. The upper PFC can act as a barrier
for ions traveling along magnetic field lines from the ECR magnet to the PFC surface which is
why a lower current value is optimal. The lower PFC magnet acts to focus the magnetic field lines
onto a smaller surface area, therefore increasing the flux per unit area.
The third parameter investigated is the effect of hydrogen neutral pressure in the chamber as it
affects the ion flux. Pressure was swept from 0.5mTorr to 2.25mTorr. Higher pressures up to 50
mTorr were investigated, but were found to be unsuitable for sustaining a stable plasma. The sweep
of pressures investigated was limited by the Brooks mass flow controller that was used to meter
the gas flow into SLiDE. Fig. 10 shows the trends of hydrogen flux to the diagnostics plate as a
function of the hydrogen pressure at two different microwave powers. Minimal change in flux is
observed from varying the pressure at either power. It was found that hydrogen pressure has little
effect on ion flux. As such, a 1mTorr hydrogen neutral pressure was found to be optimum.

 Figure 10. Number of hydrogen ions hitting the plate per second as a function of neutral gas
 pressure within the chamber. At both microwave powers minimal change is seen in the flux to
 the plate with both plots showing a small peak at 1 mTorr.

Work Package 2 (WP2): Pumping deuterium gas with
flowing liquid lithium

WP2.1: Assembly and testing
The initial assembly of APOLLO, which did not include HyDE, was completed in December 2024.
Initial tests ensured the proper operation of the vacuum and heating systems.

To successfully measure extended periods of hydrogen pumping, it was first important to ensure
that lithium flow could be maintained in APOLLO for several hours. In January 2025, a 24-hour
stress test was conducted with continuous lithium flow throughout APOLLO. Fig. 11 shows the
temperature and flowrate data from the 24-hour test. After a variety of tests over the first 5 hours,
the APOLLO systems were held steady for over 19 hours without fault. The 600g of lithium loaded
into the loop in January 2025 remained in the system until July 2025 during this time it was molten
for over 35 hours and was flowing for more than 30 hours. Throughout this period no significant
decrease in performance was observed and visual inspection of the lithium in the reservoir and on
the open surface PFC showed a shiny metallic surface during flow indicating high lithium purity
throughout the loop.

The second operational goal that had to be satisfied was to achieve full coverage of the open
surface flow channel with lithium. A constant surface area of lithium must be presented to the
hydrogenic plasma so that uptake rate can be accurately calculated. The inclusion of the 3D
tantalum mesh flow plate in the open flow region is required to achieve this. The structures in the
flow plate promote flow spreading through capillary action and with the mesh installed, complete
lithium coverage was achieved across the full range of APOLLO mass flow rates. Images of the
lithium coverage on the open surface is shown in Fig. 12.

Figure 11. Plots showing the temperature and flowrate data during initial stress tests conducted
on APOLLO in January 2026. Lithium flow was maintained for over 24 hours. The first 5 hours
consisted of a variety of different tests that affected the flowrate, but the final 19 hours of the
test showed the consistency of the steady state lithium flow in APOLLO.

 Figure 12. Images showing a snapshot of the lithium flow on the PFC within SLiDE. With the
 3D mesh flow plate installed, total coverage of the plate is observed across the full range of flow
 rates achievable in APOLLO.

WP2.2: Diagnostics
APOLLO contains diagnostics for deuterium accounting in multiple areas throughout the loop.
Deuterium is added to APOLLO in SLiDE through a Brooks Mass flow controller (GF080) with
an accuracy of ±1% of the setpoint. This mass flow controller has been factory calibrated for
deuterium gas. The SLiDE chamber also contains an SRS RGA100 to measure background
deuterium partial pressures.
Between the SLiDE vacuum chamber and HyDE vacuum chamber is a resistive impurity probe.
The resistive impurity probe consists of a 25 cm segment of ¼" OD 316 stainless steel tubing that
~40 A current is passed through. The voltage drop across this section is measured by a
microvoltmeter to determine the resistivity of the lithium flowing through the tubing segment. The
resistivity of the lithium is linearly related to the concentration of deuterium in the lithium up
sssthe saturation point [2]. Initial resistivity probe results showed a sensitivity to both temperature
and the applied currents for the EM pumps in APOLLO. Fig. 13 shows a calibration test conducted
on the resistivity probe where the inlet pump current was changed from 0 to 100 A and the
temperature of the resistivity probe was varied at both conditions. A linear relationship between
the measured resistance and the temperature can be seen for both conditions, however there is a
large offset in the data that occurs between the 0 A inlet pump current (purple dots) and the 100 A
inlet pump current (yellow dots). The temperature dependence was determined by fitting a line to
the data for the 100 A pump current and all further tests were conducted with the Inlet pump current

at 100 A. The effects of the other APOLLO pumps seem to be minimal; however, consistent pump
settings have been used to gain a self-consistent resistivity probe reading. To maintain a constant
temperature in the resistivity probe, the lithium flow must be turned on, which requires the use of
at least one of the EM pumps.

 Figure 13. The resistance measured by the resistivity probe as a function of temperature for two
 different inlet pump currents (pump current indicated by the color of the dots).

The HyDE vacuum chamber contains a differentially pumped analysis section with a magnetic
sector RGA. The magnetic sector RGA has particularly good resolution at low masses which are
less accurately measured by a quadrupole mass spectrometer. A series of calibration tests were
performed to determine a relationship between hydrogen release in the main HyDE chamber and
the current signal on the magnetic sector RGA. A precision leak valve was used to introduce a
constant source of hydrogen gas into the HyDE chamber while RGA measurements were taken.
The pumping to the chamber was then shut off and the pressure rise over time was measured to
determine the hydrogen flux into the chamber. Fig. 14 shows the magnetic sector RGA integrated
2 amu peak current as a function of hydrogen gas flowrate into the main HyDE chamber. From Fig
11., a power law relationship can be determined that can be used to determine the hydrogenic flux
evolved from the lithium during release experiments in HyDE. Expected removal rates from alpha
phase, beta phase, and a combined alpha+beta phase for the HyDE geometry are shown as vertical
dashed lines. The evolution flux for these phases come from previous work conducted at UIUC
[4]. Alpha phase refers to hydrogen in solution in the lithium. Beta phase refers to LiH precipitates
suspended in the lithium. Alpha+beta phase refers to a mixture that contains hydrogen in solution
with some LiH precipitates.

 Figure 14. The results of the HyDE magnetic sector RGA calibration showing the integrated
 peak mass 2 current as a function of hydrogen gas flowrate into the HyDE chamber. The data
 closely follows a power law fit and is insensitive to temperature changes.

The final diagnostic for deuterium accounting is the SRS RGA100 installed in the Reservoir
vacuum chamber. Hydrogenic isotopes evolve from the surface of liquid lithium at temperatures
as low as 300 C, which is the operating temperature of the APOLLO loop. Therefore, any lithium
surfaces exposed to vacuum must be monitored for hydrogenic species evolution, including the
Reservoir.

WP2.3: Experiments with deuterium gas
Before beginning deuterium gas experiments, it was decided that hydrogen gas would act as a
surrogate for deuterium. This was done to keep the APOLLO system deuterium free until the
experimental procedure had been finalized, and reasonable results were obtained with hydrogen.
Hydrogen and deuterium exhibit very similar chemical characteristics, and the adsorption and
release characteristics are likely very similar, however, deuterium will show up as mass 4 on an
RGA. This is valuable because it will allow for differentiation between background and
experimental concentrations given that there is a background level of hydrogen in the chamber due
to water adsorbed on the walls.
Fig. 15 shows the experimental data from an experiment where hydrogen gas was added to the
chamber at a constant rate with no lithium flow, and then the lithium flow was started. The top plot
shows the pressure, the middle plot shows the lithium mass flow rate, and the bottom plot shows
the magnetron power, all as a function of time. The mass flow rate data is noisy, but by 12980 s,
there is a constant flow rate above 3 g/s and the pressure data appears unaffected. During the entire
test, the hydrogen mass flow rate into the chamber was held at a constant 10 sccm. At ~13010 s,
there is a transient increase in pressure and then it settles at a lower value due to the powering of
the microwave source and the striking of a plasma. This experiment showed minimal hydrogen
pumping by the flowing liquid lithium. One potential reason for the lack of hydrogen pumping is

the low pressure of hydrogen in the chamber. At only ~1.5 mTorr, the hydrogen pressure gradient
was very low and therefore does not drive large diffusion into the lithium layer.

 Figure 15. SLiDE pressure, lithium flow, and magnetron power for a test with 10 sccm of
 hydrogen flowing into the SLiDE chamber. Note the pressure plot is in a log scale and that the
 shown pressure changes are extremely small.

To increase the concentration gradient to drive additional hydrogen into lithium, a new experiment
was designed. This experiment consisted of using the HyDE chamber for hydrogen pressurizations
up to 45 Torr while flowing liquid lithium through HyDE while disconnected from the rest of the
loop including SLiDE and the Reservoir. This experiment could not have been conducted in SLiDE
due to the high pressures that would have driven lithium flows into the reservoir. During these
tests, the vacuum pumping on the HyDE chamber was shut off and therefore all pressure changes
were attributed to the lithium pumping the hydrogen. Additionally, using the HyDE chamber
allowed for the use of the resistivity probe without exposing the lithium to additional free surfaces

where hydrogen could be lost. Fig. 16 shows the HyDE pressure as a function of time (red) during
5 consecutive pressurizations to ~45 mTorr. After pressurization, the pressure exponentially decays
to a value of ~5 Torr in ~30 minutes. This is an indication of hydrogen gas being pumped by liquid
lithium. The blue dots in Fig. 12 show the resistivity probe resistance reading as a function of time.
When the hydrogen is added, the resistance initially increases, then begins decreasing as the
hydrogen pressure continues to fall. This trend is seen for all five hydrogen pressurizations;
however, later pressurizations see less of a decrease in resistance as the hydrogen pressure
decreases and a slower rate of hydrogen pumping. This phenomenon can be explained by the
pressure composition phase diagram of the Li-H system [3]. As hydrogen is added to the chamber,
there is an initial flux of hydrogen atoms into the lithium that increases the hydrogen concentration
of the lithium. As the partial pressures begin to equalize between the hydrogen in the lithium and
hydrogen in the chamber, the pumping rate decreases. The resistivity probe measurement in this
region shows a rollover that could be indicative of hydrogen efflux from the lithium due to the
chamber pressure dropping below the partial pressure of hydrogen in the lithium.

 Figure 16. A plot of the HyDE hydrogen pressure as a function of time (red) overlayed on the
 resistivity probe resistance as a function of time (blue) during five consecutive hydrogen
 pressurizations of the HyDE chamber with lithium flow.

Fig. 17 shows the resistivity probe resistance reading as a function of time similarly to Fig. 16;
however, the red line shows the hydrogen concentration in the lithium in the HyDE section of the
loop assuming that the hydrogen is evenly distributed through the ~180 g of liquid lithium. The
pressure loss in the chamber is assumed to be hydrogen atoms entering the liquid lithium in
solution. The lower plot in Fig. 13 shows the lithium mass flowrate in HyDE as a function of time
and shares the x-axis with the concentration plot above. The lithium mass flowrate decreases
gradually as more hydrogen is added to the system with inflection points during the highest-
pressure periods in the HyDE chamber. Eventually, the mass flowrate falls to zero, and additional
current is applied to the EM conduction pump to restart lithium flow. The change in mass flow rate

is an indication of a constriction in the stainless steel tubing could be caused by the buildup of LiH
deposits. Eventually, the flow was constricted so much that the EM pump could not overcome the
pressure drop and flow halted in the loop. The saturation concentration for hydrogen in lithium at
300 C is 0.3%, which was exceeded during this test as an average concentration in the lithium and
likely exceeded by a much higher margin locally [2]. Additionally, it is likely that some regions in
the lithium flow path are slightly cooler with a lower solubility limit leading to preferential LiH
growth that could lead to localized pipe constrictions.

 Figure 17. The top plot shows the resistivity probe resistance and hydrogen concentration in the
 lithium as a function of time. The bottom plot shows the lithium flowrate through HyDE as a
 function of time. At 7.7 hours, the flow is finally halted due to LiH deposit buildup, and the EM
 pump currents are increased to restart flow. The increase in resistivity at this point can partially
 be attributed to the uneven temperature distribution in the resistivity probe due to the stalled
 lithium flow.

Work Package 3 (WP3): Pumping deuterium plasma with
flowing liquid lithium
WP3.1: Assembly/testing
The plasma source described in WP1 was originally installed on SLiDE as a part of APOLLO and
no further testing was required. The diagnostic plate was removed, and the lithium PFC was
installed in its place in the SLiDE chamber.

WP3.2: Experiments
Similarly to WP2, it was decided to use hydrogen as a surrogate for deuterium until a standardized
testing procedure could be determined. Fig. 19 shows the resistivity probe data from plasma
exposure testing. First, an argon plasma exposure was conducted for 1 hour to provide a baseline
that could be compared to the hydrogen plasma. The data from the argon plasma exposure is shown
in the bottom plot of the figure. There is an initial increase in resistance as the argon plasma is
turned on that is likely due to impurities in the gas lines used to feed the SLiDE chamber. During
plasma exposure, the resistivity remains constant. After the TDS test, the resistivity decreased to a
value below the starting value. The hydrogen plasma exposure data is in the top plot in the figure.

During the hydrogen plasma exposure, the resistivity increases 5 times the corresponding increase
for the argon plasma. Again, there is a decrease in resistivity after the TDS test to a level that is
lower than the starting value.

 Figure 19. Resistivity probe resistance as a function of time during a single day of testing. The
 bottom plot shows the results from an argon plasma exposure which occurred before the
 hydrogen plasma exposure shown in the top plot. The plasma exposures occurred between the
 green dashed line and the first red dashed line. Between the red dashed lines, a temperature ramp
 to 660 C was done in HyDE to attempt TDS. The TDS results for this test are shown in the WP4
 results.

The much greater increase in resistance for the hydrogen plasma does indicate that some hydrogen
was entering the lithium during this test. Modeling the lithium and surrounding stainless steel in
the resistivity probe as a circuit with parallel resistances, the resistivity of the lithium itself can be
calculated. The change in resistivity over the course of the test can then be used to determine the
hydrogen concentration change [2]. During this test, it was found that the hydrogen concentration
increased 0.33% during the plasma exposure and was decreased 0.44% by the subsequent TDS
test. The plasma exposure lasted 2000 s, which corresponds to a combined ion and radical flux of
1.98x1022 hydrogen atoms to the lithium surface in SLiDE. With a lithium inventory of 800 g in
APOLLO, this corresponds to only a 0.03% increase in hydrogen molar concentration. This
indicates the possible presence of another impurity source that is affecting the lithium resistance.
Alternatively, it could suggest a greater hydrogen flux to the PFC than originally measured.

Further testing is currently underway to determine hydrogen plasma absorption in flowing liquid
lithium. Additional lithium mass flow rates, temperatures, gas pressures, microwave powers, and
magnetic field configurations will be explored. Eventually, deuterium will be used to provide better
measurements for TDS results.

WP3.3: Simulation and interpretation of results
A simulation in CRANE was conducted to determine the relative concentrations of species in the
plasma. CRANE is open-source software that is part of the MOOSE framework and specializes in
time-dependent coupled rate equations. The CRANE simulations conducted were zero-
dimensional with a prescribed electron density and temperature. Fig. 20 shows results from a
CRANE simulation that varied electron temperature while maintaining electron density. As the
electron temperature increases, the concentration of all excited species increase. However, the
concentrations of ionized species proportionally increase more than the radical concentration. The
model was compared to the plamsa parameters measured as part of WP1 to validate. Good general
agreement is found between the model and the experimental data with ion and radical densities on
the order of ~x1016 m-3 and ~x1017 m-3 found respectively in both. Future simulation work should
focus on the boundary conditions of plasma and the effects of a low-recycling lithium boundary
as opposed to the typical stainless steel chamber walls.

 hydrogen plasma as a function of electron temperature.

Work Package 4 (WP4): Separation of deuterium from
lithium
WP4.1: Assembly/testing of distillation column/diagnostics
HyDE construction, vacuum tests, and heater tests were completed in April 2024. No distillation
column tests were performed before integration into APOLLO. A cross section of the HyDE
distillation section is shown in Fig. 21. The intended operation is for lithium to enter the chamber
through the VCR connection on the left side and fall into the annular region below. This annular
region is heated to ~700 C, causing the lithium to evaporate. The evaporated lithium condenses on
the actively cooled conically shaped condensation stage where it then falls under the influence of
gravity to the bottom of the cone. When the size of the drop is large enough that the surface tension
of the lithium holding the drop onto the condensation stage is overcome by gravity, the drop falls
to the outlet of the distillation section at the bottom of the figure.

 Figure 21. (left) A cross section of the distillation section of the HyDE chamber. The yellow
 arrows indicate the intended path for lithium to travel after being evaporated from the annular
 heated region and condensing on the condensation stage. The hydrogen gas escapes through the
 gaps between the condensation stage and the chamber wall. (right) A picture of the HyDE
 distillation section installed on APOLLO. The inlet faces directly towards the camera and
 insulation covers all heated regions.

WP4.2: Integration of distillation with liquid lithium loop and plasma
source
HyDE was integrated into APOLLO in May 2024. Initial tests consisted of confirming the intended
distillation operation and determining the time to clear the HyDE bucket of a single batch of
lithium through evaporation. Fig. 22 shows the mass flowrate and cumulative lithium removal
during a HyDE distillation at 630 C. The spikes on the mass flowrate suggest the formation of
lithium drops on the tip of the HyDE condensation stage that then fall into the outlet where they
induce lithium flow that is read by the HyDE outlet flowmeter. This test shows successful
distillation operations.

 Figure 22. The left plot shows the mass flowrate on the HyDE outlet flowmeter as a function of
 time during a distillation test. The right plot shows the cumulative lithium that was recovered
 from the HyDE bucket as a function of time during a distillation test.

Although the distillation tests initially succeeded, subsequent tests showed zero mass flowrate on
the HyDE outlet. While attempting to diagnose the issue, it was discovered that the condensation
stage mounting had been warped while moving the condensation stage. Fig. 23 shows images of
the retracted condensation stage before a successful distillation test and after an unsuccessful test.
The tip of the stage is clearly off-center in the right image. The off-center tip is hypothesized to be
the reason that the lithium was not exiting HyDE as it should in a successful distillation test, and
the flowrate being seen was zero. It is hypothesized that this warping is the reason that further
distillation tests did not show any mass flowrate in the HyDE outlet. Additional mounting support
was added to keep the condensation stage properly aligned; however, subsequent distillation tests
were still unsuccessful. Additional work will have to go into re-designing the distillation section
geometry to allow for lithium to successfully flow to the outlet of HyDE.

 before (left) and after (right) issues occurred with the distillation operation. The offset in the
 right image indicates that the stage mounting was warped by moving the stage during operation.

WP4.3: Experiments
Given the limited success with the lithium flow in HyDE, it was difficult to collect thermal
desorption spectroscopy data for lithium samples in HyDE, especially at the highest temperature
range between 650 C and 700 C. Fig. 24 shows a compilation of preliminary TDS tests conducted
in the HyDE chamber for a variety of conditions. TDS tests were conducted with a temperature
ramp rate of 3 degrees Celsius per minute. The following testing conditions were tested in order:
 1. Empty HyDE chamber with no lithium
 2. Clean lithium in HyDE chamber
 3. Clean lithium exposed to argon gas
 4. Clean lithium exposed to argon plasma
 5. Clean lithium exposed to hydrogen plasma
 During all the tests with lithium in the chamber, the RGA current increases rapidly at
 temperatures above 500 C with a similar trend no matter the experimental condition. There is
 a peak in the desorption at ~590 C during two of the tests. This peak is likely due to the
 dissociation of LiOH and indicates that the lithium in HyDE was exposed to some amount of
 water vapor. Previous results indicate that a lower temperature peak near 350 C was seen for
 the reaction between LiOH and LiH; however, this peak is not seen in the data for hydrogen
 plasma, likely indicating that no LiH was present. A unique peak is seen at ~550 C in the
 hydrogen plasma data. This peak is likely due to hydrogen desorption from the liquid lithium.
 Previous results do not comment on a peak near 550 C for lithium desorption but do show an
 increase in the desorption near that temperature in their data [4]. The drop in resistivity for the
 hydrogen plasma exposure shown in Fig. 19 is larger than argon plasma exposure; however,

 the mass 2 release shown in Fig. 24 for the hydrogen is lower than the argon. This can be
 explained by the mass 18 plot in Fig. 24. The background water content for the argon plasma
 exposure was higher than the hydrogen plasma exposure, leading to a higher mass 2 signal
 even if the hydrogen evolution from the lithium was less. Other effects on the system that
 could account for this measurement difference are slag layers forming on the lithium free
 surface and additional lithium evaporated on the walls in the HyDE chamber.

Work Package 5 (WP5): Reactor model
WP5.1: Incorporating experimental results in reactor model
Using data gathered from experiments on APOLLO, rate coefficients for lithium pumping,
hydrogen desorption in distillation, and hydrogen adsorption via plasma exposure are determined.
These coefficients are applied to an updated version of the tritium cycle model and used to
determine the lithium loop operation time until hydrogen saturation, and the molar content and
concentration of hydrogen within each loop component over operation. Fig. 25 represents how the

APOLLO loop is modeled for the purpose of simulation, with each component and path modeled
as a node. Fig. 26 shows the GUI of the reactor mode. This was developed for ease of adjustment
of each node in the model, and to change general simulation parameters such as the timescale the
simulation is run over. A sample of output of the model is shown in Figure 27, where the
experimentally determined ion flux is introduced into the SLiDE node and propagates over time.
This model has the capability to scale to larger devices and loop systems to determine tritium
inventory requirements for future fusion systems and plants. As more rate coefficients are
experimentally determined, this model will become increasingly valuable and more simulations
will be run.

Work Package 6 (WP6): [Stretch Goal] does liquid lithium
pump helium ash?
Prior to the onset of hydrogen retention experiments in APOLLO, experiments were conducted to
determine if flowing liquid lithium over the divertor plate would pump the helium gas from the
SLiDE chamber. These experiments pressurized SLiDE with ~1 mTorr of He with lithium flowing
through APOLLO. Fig. 28 displays Reservoir RGA and pressure data taken from one such
experiment, where spikes in the helium signal are observed in the Reservoir. These are thought to
be indicative of helium pumping from SLiDE to Reservoir via lithium flow. Figure 24 shows
images taken from Reservoir showing the formation of large lithium bubbles at the inlet. These
events are time-correlated with the helium spikes on the RGA data.

The spikes in the helium signal were seen particularly with a high SLiDE outlet pump current.
This high current lowers the level of lithium in the stainless-steel tube exiting SLiDE and allows
for helium to enter the tube. It is possible that lithium flowing from the collector into the tube is
then able to create a seal that traps a helium gas bubble. This helium bubble is then carried by the
flow in the lithium to the Reservoir where it is released and shows up as a spike in the helium
pressure on the RGA and visually as a bubble escaping the Reservoir inlet. With lower SLiDE
outlet pump currents, the lithium level in the outlet tube is near the surface of the PFC and any
helium bubbles formed would have a much longer vertical transit before reaching the horizontal
section of tubing where bubbles can be pushed into the Reservoir by the lithium.

 periodically in the reservoir, indicating pumping behavior of the system from SLiDE to the
 reservoir. The dotted line on the plot display the time periods when lithium flow was active (light
 blue), helium gas flow was active into SLiDE (green) and the plasma source was active (pink).

Conclusions and Future Work
This project laid the ground work for future experimental study of hydrogen isotope adsorption
and desorption in flowing liquid lithium using the APOLLO facility at UIUC. Major milestones
include the commissioning and characterization of an ECR plasma source providing a hydrogen
ion flux of ~5x1017 s-1 and a hydrogen radical flux of ~1.5x1019 s-1, the demonstration of
hydrogen uptake by liquid lithium under both gas and plasma exposure, the development of
diagnostics capable of tracking hydrogen concentration in real time, and the integration of a
lithium distillation system for hydrogen isotope extraction studies. Experimental results
confirmed that liquid lithium effectively absorbs hydrogen under sufficiently high-pressure
gradients and plasma exposure conditions. Initial distillation results showed a 0.44%
concentration decrease in hydrogen concentration after heating to 650 C. Preliminary modeling
efforts incorporating these experimental results demonstrate the potential to predict hydrogen
accumulation and transport in lithium loop systems relevant to fusion reactor environments.
While several components—particularly the distillation system—require further refinement to
achieve fully reliable operation, the work completed in this project establishes a strong
experimental platform for continued investigation of lithium-based plasma-facing components

and tritium management strategies. Ongoing and future experiments on APOLLO will focus on
further quantitative measurements of hydrogen and deuterium pumping rates, improved
hydrogen extraction methods, and integration of experimental results into reactor-scale tritium
inventory models. Collectively, these efforts contribute important data and tools for the
development of liquid lithium technologies for next-generation fusion reactors.
