Droplet digital PCR assays for Pvmdr1 and Pvcrt-o gene copy number variation determination in Plasmodium vivax
Authors: Suttipat Srisutham, Kanyarat Boonpeng, Phiraya Pitchayatanakorn, Kaewkanha Kijprasong, Francois H Nosten, Nicholas P J Day, Arjen M Dondorp, Mallika Imwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundmdr1 and crt-o amplification in Plasmodium vivax has been associated with antimalarial drug resistance and could thus be a molecular marker. Accurate copy number variation (CNV) quantification could provide valuable insights for monitoring resistance trends.ObjectivesTo develop a duplex digital PCR (ddPCR) assay for the simultaneous absolute pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o quantification using pvtubulin as a single-copy internal reference.MethodsDuplex ddPCR assays were optimized to target pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin. CNV was calculated as the ratio of the absolute concentration of the target gene to the reference.ResultsddPCR assays were successfully developed and validated. The quantification limits for assays targeting pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin, based on pvtubulin concentration, were 22.4 ± 3.5 and 21.5 ± 2.6 copies/µL, respectively. Analysis of field samples from Tak (N = 25) and Kanchanaburi Provinces (N = 97), Thailand, revealed that in 2008, 16% of isolates from Tak carried multiple copies of pvmdr1 and all samples possessed a single copy of pvcrt-o. In contrast, samples collected from the Kanchanaburi Province during 2023–2025 showed no evidence of multiple copies of either gene.ConclusionThis newly developed ddPCR assay provides a robust and highly reproducible tool for monitoring P. vivax drug resistance markers in endemic regions and a potential approach in surveillance programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05971-4.Keywords: Plasmodium vivax, pvmdr1, pvcrt-o, Droplet digital PCR, Copy number variation

Introduction

Malaria remains a major public health issue. According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 263 million malaria cases occurred globally in 2023 [1]. In countries of the Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS), including Thailand, Plasmodium vivax is now the predominant cause of malaria infections, since P. falciparum presence has declined under intensified control and elimination efforts. In Thailand, chloroquine remains the first-line treatment for P. vivax malaria [2], so its continued effectiveness is critically important for achieving national malaria elimination targets.

In 1989, a chloroquine-resistant P. vivax was first reported in Papua New Guinea [3]. Subsequent reports came from Indonesia in 1991 [4, 5] and Myanmar in 1993 and 1995 [6]. In Thailand, most P. vivax isolates remain susceptible and respond reliably to the standard chloroquine regimen [7], despite a documented case of chloroquine-resistant P. vivax infection in a pregnant woman [8]. To effectively monitor chloroquine resistance, sensitive and accurate molecular detection of relevant genetic changes is essential. Recent studies have reported molecular markers associated with chloroquine resistance in P. vivax such as the Y976F mutation in pvmdr1 [9–11]. Copy number variation (CNV) in P. vivax, particularly pvmdr1 amplification, has been implicated in antimalarial drug resistance. pvmdr1 encodes an ATP-binding cassette transporter on the parasite’s digestive vacuole membrane, involved in the efflux of diverse xenobiotic compounds, including antimalarial drugs [12, 13]. Increased pvmdr1 expression or amplification may enhance drug efflux and reduce intracellular drug accumulation, thereby contributing to decreased parasite susceptibility [14]. pvmdr1 is orthologous to P. falciparum mdr1, a known determinant of mefloquine resistance [15, 16]. Consistently, increased pvmdr1 copy number in P. vivax has been associated with lower susceptibility to several antimalarial drugs [17, 18]. Previous studies in Thailand have reported that ~ 9%–39% of P. vivax isolates carry multiple pvmdr1 copies [11, 17], with frequencies of 4–33% reported in Cambodia [11, 19], 4% in Laos, and 2% in Myanmar [17]. In India, 31.6% of isolates had multiple pvmdr1 copies [20]. In French Guiana, a high prevalence of pvmdr1 amplification, with estimates of ~ 71.3% in 1997–2004 declining to 12.8% in 2009–2013, was documented [21]; this decline occurred during a period when mefloquine was extensively used, either alone or in combination with artesunate, for the treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria, thereby exposing P. vivax to indirect drug selection pressure. In contrast, pvmdr1 amplification is relatively uncommon in some regions, such as Honduras in Central America, where only 2.7% of isolates were reported to carry multiple copies [22]. In addition to increased copy number, a recently described deletion that alters the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of pvmdr1 could modify mdr1 transcript levels, being associated with reduced ex vivo susceptibility to mefloquine in Cambodian isolates [23]. Moreover, increased pvcrt-o copy number has been linked to chloroquine-resistant P. vivax infections and more severe clinical manifestations [14, 24]. Functionally, an increase in pvcrt-o copy number may alter chloroquine transport at the digestive vacuole and lower the drug concentration inside this compartment required to kill the parasite [24, 25]. These findings suggest that pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o CNV could be a promising molecular marker system for monitoring chloroquine resistance in P. vivax.

Droplet digital PCR assays were developed as a molecular tool to support various aspects of malaria research. So far, this technique has been applied for the detection of Plasmodium species [26–29], CNV quantification [30], identification of mutations in kelch13 gene [31], and detection of hrp2/hrp3 deletions [32]. Unlike conventional real-time PCR, which determines gene copy number based on the ΔΔCt method relative to a reference gene, ddPCR provides an absolute quantification of target DNA copies not requiring standard curves. This approach enhances detection precision and improves accuracy in quantifying gene copy number using the ratio of target gene to reference gene. Nevertheless, quantitative methods for accurately determining pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o copy numbers in P. vivax are still scarce, especially when applied to field samples from Thailand. Accurate quantification is crucial for understanding the role of these markers in chloroquine resistance and for surveillance of emerging resistant strains. This study aimed to develop, validate, and apply ddPCR assays for quantification of pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o copy numbers in P. vivax isolates to support improved molecular surveillance of chloroquine resistance in endemic regions.

Methods

DNA samples

To develop the ddPCR assays, archived P. vivax samples collected from Kanchanaburi, Thailand, carrying single-copy pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o were used. The copy number status (pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o) had been previously determined using a validated real-time PCR assay [17]. The linearity and quantification limit of the assays were determined using three representative DNA samples from P. vivax parasites, each analyzed in triplicate, including one sample carrying single-copy pvmdr1, one carrying multiple-copy pvmdr1, and one carrying single-copy pvcrt-o alleles.

ddPCR assays

Duplex ddPCR assays for pvmdr1/β-tubulin and pvcrt-o/β-tubulin were developed. In each duplex reaction, one primer–probe set targeted the gene of interest (pvmdr1 or pvcrt-o), and the other the P. vivax β-tubulin (pvtubulin) reference gene. Probes for target genes were labeled with FAM, whereas probes for the pvtubulin reference gene were labeled with HEX. Primers and probes used here are provided in Supplementary File 1 and previously described [17]. Each 20 µL ddPCR reaction contained 10 µL of ddPCR™ Supermix for Probes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), 900 nM primers for the target and reference genes, 250 nM probes for target and reference genes, and 2 µL of template DNA. Then, the reaction was partitioned into ~ 12,000–20,000 droplets in an oil emulsion using a QX200™ Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad Laboratories). PCR amplification was performed on a T100™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories). During assay development, to identify optimal conditions for duplex amplification, a series of annealing temperatures was tested, and an annealing temperature of 57 °C selected as optimal for both duplex ddPCR assays. After 40 amplification cycles, droplets were read using a QX200 Droplet Reader and analyzed with QX Manager Standard Edition software version 2.1.0. For the pvmdr1/β-tubulin duplex ddPCR, the threshold was set at 2000 for pvmdr1 and 1200 for β-tubulin, while for other duplex ddPCR, threshold values were set at 2500 for pvcrt-o and 1200 for β-tubulin. Gene copy numbers were determined based on the ratio of the target gene concentration to that of the reference gene, according to the formula genecopynumber=CtargetCreference, where Ctarget and Creference represent the concentrations (copies/µL) of the target and reference genes, respectively.

ddPCR assay validation

The limit of quantification (LoQ) was determined based on the lowest concentration at which both target and reference genes were consistently detected with a coefficient of variation (CV) < 10%. The same limit was used for the calculated copy number. The lambda (λ), which represents the proportion of negative and total droplets, was calculated for each reaction using the Poisson equation λ=-ln(P(0)), where P(0) represents the proportion of negative droplets (negative droplets/total droplets), to determine the lowest acceptable concentration yielding a (CV) < 10%. Linearity was evaluated using data obtained from a fivefold serial dilution series.

Field sample analysis

A total of 122 P. vivax DNA field samples collected from Kanchanaburi (n = 97), Thailand, during 2023–2025, including 31 samples from 2023, 37 from 2024, and 29 from 2025, and from Tak Province (n = 25) collected in 2008, were analyzed. CNVs in pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o were determined using duplex ddPCR assays targeting pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin.

Assay validation

For analytical validation of the assays, we assessed dilutional linearity, copy number accuracy, and analytical sensitivity across a series of fivefold DNA dilutions. Fivefold serial dilutions of parasite DNA from three independent clinical isolates (one single-copy pvmdr1, one multiple-copy pvmdr1, and one single-copy pvcrt-o) were analyzed in triplicate. The duplex ddPCR assay targeting pvmdr1/pvtubulin demonstrated excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99) for both pvmdr1 and pvtubulin in isolates carrying either single or multiple pvmdr1 copies, allowing accurate discrimination between single- and multiplecopy genotypes at all tested concentrations (Fig. 2a and c)The calculated copy number ratios were consistently 1.0 in single-copy isolates (Fig. 2b) and ~ 2.0 in multiplecopy isolates (Fig. 2d), although higher dilution levels with very low DNA concentrations were associated with increased variability in copy number estimates. Similarly, the duplex ddPCR assay targeting pvcrt-o/pvtubulin exhibited excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99) for both pvcrt-o and pvtubulin (Fig. 2e), with measured copy numbers remaining consistently close to 1.0 across the dilution series (Fig. 2f).

The LoQ of the developed ddPCR assays was evaluated using genomic DNA extracted from P. vivax isolates harboring single- and multiplecopy pvmdr1 and single-copy pvcrt-o alleles. For the pvmdr1 single‑copy assay, the lowest dilution range that yielded a CV ≤ 10% was between 1:5 and 1:25, corresponding to an average λ of 0.018 for both pvmdr1 and pvtubulin and an average copy number of 0.99, consistent with a single copy (Supplementary file 2). At the LoQ, the mean pvmdr1 and pvtubulin concentrations were 22.09 ± 4.9 and 22.4 ± 3.5 copies/µL, respectively. At λ values < 0.018, the calculated copy number became less stable; for example, at the 1:1,250 dilution, the copy number ranged from 0.98 to 1.40. For the pvmdr1 multiple‑copy assay, the acceptable dilution range was 1:1–1:5, corresponding to average λ values of 0.077 for pvmdr1 and 0.040 for pvtubulin. At the LoQ, the mean pvmdr1 and pvtubulin concentrations were 96.5 ± 5.1 and 51.3 ± 8.7 copies/µL, respectively. In this range, the mean copy number was 1.9, which is in good agreement with the expected two copies. For the pvcrto single-copy assay, serial dilutions showed that a CV ≤ 10% between 1:1 and 1:25, corresponding to an average λ of 0.017 for both pvcrt‑o and pvtubulin. At this LoQ, the mean pvcrt‑o and pvtubulin concentrations were 19.2 ± 1.8 and 21.5 ± 2.6 copies/µL, respectively. Within this range, the mean copy number was 0.95, consistent with the expected single-copy state.

Copy number analysis of pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o in P. vivax field samples

A total of 122 P. vivax DNA field samples were analyzed to determine pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o copy number using duplex ddPCR assays. All isolates from Kanchanaburi (n = 97) carried a single-copy pvmdr1, and no evidence of pvmdr1 amplification was detected (Supplementary file 3). In contrast, multiple pvmdr1 copies were observed in 4 of 25 isolates (16.0%) from Tak, Thailand. For pvcrt-o, all samples carried a single copy.

The relationship between pvmdr1 and pvtubulin (copies/µL) obtained from the duplex ddPCR assay targeting pvmdr1/pvtubulin was predominantly linear, except for isolates carrying multiple pvmdr1 copies, which deviated from this linear trend (Fig. 3a). A strong linear association was observed between pvcrt-o and pvtubulin (copies/µL) in the duplex ddPCR assay targeting pvcrt-o/pvtubulin (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the comparison of pvtubulin concentrations measured by the two duplex assays (pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin) demonstrated a linear inter-assay correlation (Fig. 3c), indicating consistent pvtubulin quantification across the same set of samples.

Discussion

P. vivax malaria remains a major public health problem in Thailand and throughout the Greater Mekong Subregion, even as intensive control efforts have led to a marked decline in P. falciparum transmission in recent years. This epidemiological shift has increased the relative importance of P. vivax and underscores the need for strengthened surveillance systems for this species. Although first-line antimalarial regimens for P. vivax currently retain good clinical efficacy, the historical experience with P. falciparum demonstrates that drug resistance can emerge and spread rapidly, particularly under the selective pressure of widespread drug deployment during elimination campaigns. Therefore, proactive monitoring of potential resistance markers in P. vivax is critical to preserving treatment efficacy, guiding policy decisions, and preventing setbacks to national and regional malaria elimination goals.

Molecular surveillance targeting drug resistance–associated genes is a powerful complementary approach to clinical and in vitro efficacy studies. For P. vivax, polymorphisms and pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o CNV have been proposed as markers of altered susceptibility to chloroquine [14, 17, 18, 24]. Standardized and highly sensitive methods to quantify gene copy number are essential for detecting early changes in parasite populations and enabling comparisons across studies and settings. Conventional quantitative PCR (qPCR) methods can be influenced by amplification efficiency and inter-run variability, potentially limiting their precision at low parasite densities or small fold changes in copy number [17]. In contrast, droplet digital PCR, provides absolute quantification without the need for external standard curves and maintains good precision even when DNA levels are very low. By partitioning reactions into thousands of droplets, ddPCR is also more tolerant to PCR inhibitors and yields more stable copy‑number estimates at low template concentrations, where qPCR results can become more variable. At the same time, ddPCR is more expensive than standard qPCR and usually has a lower maximum throughput because of the extra droplet-generation step. For large-scale screening, qPCR may therefore remain more practical, whereas ddPCR is particularly useful in reference or surveillance laboratories that need very accurate copy-number data in selected samples. In this study, we developed and optimized duplex ddPCR assays to quantify pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o copy number using pvtubulin as an internal reference gene. The duplex format was selected to reduce interwell and inter-run variation by allowing simultaneous amplification of the target and reference within the same reaction, thereby improving quantitative precision. In addition, running duplex reactions decreases reagent consumption and sample volume requirements and can shorten turnaround time, something particularly important when working with limited field samples or integrating molecular surveillance into routine processes. After optimizing annealing temperatures, primer and probe concentrations, as well as droplet generation conditions, the assays produced well-separated positive and negative droplet clusters and demonstrated robust performance across a wide dynamic range of template concentrations.

Assay validation demonstrated that the accuracy of copy number estimation is critically dependent on DNA concentration. Insufficient DNA, resulting from low parasitemia or suboptimal extraction efficiency, leads to reduced template concentrations (copies/µL) for both target and reference genes, thereby increasing variability and compromising ratio stability. To determine the analytical sensitivity, the LoQ required a pvtubulin concentration of ~ 22 copies/µL, corresponding to a lambda (λ) value ≥ 0.040. The LoQs for the pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin duplex ddPCR assays were 22.4 ± 3.5 and 21.5 ± 2.6 copies/µL, respectively. These results are consistent with a previous report demonstrating acceptable precision within a λ range of 0.003–0.8 [30].

The copy number profiles observed here provide additional insight into the epidemiology of pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o CNVs in Thailand. The detection of multiple pvmdr1 copies in 16% of isolates from Tak in 2008, but not in samples from Kanchanaburi collected during 2023–2025, suggests possible regional and temporal variation, potentially reflecting changes in antimalarial drug pressure or parasite susceptibility in the area. Historically, intense use of mefloquine-based regimens for P. falciparum along the Thai–Myanmar border, including Tak Province, has been associated with selection of mdr1 amplification in both P. falciparum and P. vivax populations, likely through inadvertent exposure of P. vivax infections to mefloquine [17, 19]. Previous work in Thailand reported a higher prevalence of pvmdr1 amplification, with 39% of P. vivax isolates from Mae Sot district, Tak Province, between February and June 2009, carrying multiple pvmdr1 copies [11]. Moreover, a longitudinal study of isolates collected from clinics in Mae Sot, western Thailand, between June 2003 and August 2015 documented a declining trend in pvmdr1 multiplecopy clones over time, with a significant decrease in multiple copies prevalence from 52% in 2003–2011 to 17% in 2012–2015, coinciding with the discontinuation of mefloquine-containing regimens in the region [33]. Accordingly, no multiplecopy pvmdr1 genotypes were detected in the most recent samples from Kanchanaburi (2023–2025). In addition, the uniform single-copy pvcrt-o status across all samples aligns with earlier studies indicating seldom pvcrt-o amplification, being observed only sporadically, such as in the Western Brazilian Amazon [24], supporting the CNV at this locus is unlikely a major contributor to chloroquine responses in the study areas.

A limitation of this study is the absence of field isolates with confirmed multiple pvcrt-o copies in our sample set, which prevented full evaluation of assay performance at higher pvcrt-o copy numbers. This may reflect true absence or very low prevalence of pvcrt-o amplification in the areas and time periods sampled or that our sampling frame did not capture such variants, underscoring the need to investigate additional populations and time points. As a result, although the assay demonstrated high precision and stability for single-copy detection, its sensitivity and dynamic range for detecting modest or high-level pvcrt-o amplifications remain to be validated in appropriate reference strains or samples from settings where such variants are known or suspected to occur. A limitation of this study is that serial dilutions were performed using nuclease-free water without the inclusion of human DNA. This may not fully represent low-parasitemia field samples, where parasite DNA is mixed with host DNA. Future studies that include human DNA in dilution experiments may help to better assess assay performance under more realistic conditions. In addition, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, our study was limited to two provinces and a finite number of isolates, which may not capture the full spectrum of P. vivax genetic diversity in Thailand and neighboring regions. Future studies incorporating larger, longitudinal sample collections, as well as correlation of copy number data with clinical and ex vivo drug response phenotypes, is needed to clarify the epidemiological and functional significance of pvmdr1 and pvcrt-o CNV.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that duplex ddPCR assays targeting pvmdr1/pvtubulin and pvcrt-o/pvtubulin provide a precise, scalable, and operationally feasible platform for quantifying gene copy number in P. vivax field isolates. These assays can be integrated into molecular surveillance frameworks to support early detection of emerging resistance-related CNVs and to complement ongoing monitoring of point mutations in drug resistance genes. As Thailand and other GMS countries progress toward malaria elimination, such high-resolution molecular tools will become increasingly valuable for tracking P. vivax evolution under drug pressure, informing treatment policy and evaluating the long-term effectiveness of current and future antimalarial regimens.
