Factors that influence access to primary healthcare services for migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis: a scoping review
Authors: Norma Houlihan, Annmarie Grealish, Luca Greenan, Kathleen Markey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigrants frequently encounter significant stressors during their migration journey, which can adversely affect their mental health. The rising prevalence of mental ill-health among migrant populations is an increasing public health concern. Primary healthcare services are crucial in addressing both the physical and mental health needs of migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses. However, it is essential to understand the factors that facilitate and hinder access to primary healthcare services for this population.AimThis scoping review sought to identify factors that enable and hinder migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses when accessing primary healthcare services.MethodsThis scoping review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology as it facilitates the inclusion of all relevant information that has not previously been combined and helps to identify gaps in existing literature as a means of informing future research. A systematic search of five electronic databases, Medline, CINAHL, PsycINFO, ASSIA, and Web of Science, was conducted between 2014–2024.ResultsThirteen studies met the eligibility criteria and were synthesised using narrative synthesis. The findings reveal that migrants with pre-existing mental ill health diagnoses face significantly more barriers than enablers when accessing primary healthcare services. Notably, the interrelated barriers illuminate a lack of culturally appropriate alignment of primary healthcare services to address the diverse range of physical and mental healthcare needs of migrants living with pre-existing mental ill health. ConclusionThis scoping review offers an overview of the existing literature on the enablers and barriers faced by migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health when accessing primary healthcare services. The findings illuminate the complexities that migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis experience when accessing primary healthcare services. There is a need to tailor primary healthcare services to adequately meet the unique needs of migrant populations living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03375-6.

Introduction

In 2020, there were approximately 281 million people residing outside their country of birth, representing about 3.6% of the global population [1]. Consequently, adapting healthcare services to be responsive to the individual needs of migrant populations has become a key focus for healthcare policy [2]. Despite these policies, persistent evidence indicates that healthcare services continue to fall short in addressing the specific healthcare needs of migrants [3–5]. Unaddressed language barriers [6], cultural insensitivity [7] and difficulties navigating healthcare services [8] are commonly reported systemic difficulties migrants experience when accessing healthcare services. Growing evidence shows that healthcare practitioners experience significant difficulties in adapting their practices in culturally responsive ways to meet the needs of migrant populations [9–11]. Consequently, migrants can experience poorer health outcomes when compared to the general population [12, 13].

Many migrants encounter various traumatic experiences throughout their migration journey and during their resettlement period, which can increase their risk of developing or exacerbating mental health issues [14]. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that mental ill-health is more prevalent among migrant populations compared to the general population [15–17]. The concept of “mental ill-health” lacks a universally agreed-upon definition and is variably applied across clinical and public health contexts [18, 19]. The World Health Organisation [18] conceptualises mental health as a state of well-being and functioning, whereas mental disorders are defined in DSM-5 [19] as clinically significant disturbances in cognition, emotion, or behaviour associated with dysfunction. The boundaries between well-being, distress, and disorder remain conceptually complex and socially influenced [20, 21]. In this review, “mental ill-health” is used as an umbrella term encompassing both diagnosable disorders and subclinical psychological distress. Recent estimates by the World Health Organisation (WHO) highlight that the prevalence of mental ill-health in populations affected by conflict is 22.1% (or 1 in 5 people), which is significantly higher than previously thought [22]. However, growing evidence indicates that mental ill-health among migrant populations can be exacerbated by conditions encountered after their initial resettlement period, significantly contributing to the high prevalence of mental ill-health among this population [15, 23, 24].

Mental ill-health is strongly associated with reduced access to and engagement with general healthcare services. Individuals experiencing mental ill-health are more likely to encounter barriers such as reduced help-seeking, stigma, fragmented care, and difficulties navigating healthcare systems, which can negatively impact both mental and physical health outcomes [25, 26]. This contributes to well-documented disparities in physical health, including higher rates of chronic illness and premature mortality among those living with mental ill-health. For migrant populations, these challenges may be compounded by structural barriers such as language barriers, legal status, and cultural differences, further limiting access to appropriate care.

Primary care represents the first point of contact within healthcare systems and plays a critical role in the early identification, management, and coordination of both physical and mental health needs [25]. It is particularly important for individuals living with mental ill-health, who often require integrated, continuous, and accessible care across multiple domains. However, evidence indicates that individuals with mental ill-health experience poorer access to primary care and lower quality interactions when compared to those without such conditions [27, 28]. Given its gatekeeping and coordinating role, primary care is a critical setting for addressing inequities in access to healthcare among migrant populations living with mental ill-health. Research from the United Kingdom [29], Australia [30], and Canada [27] highlights ongoing limitations in the capacity of primary care services to provide holistic mental healthcare. These challenges may be further exacerbated for migrant populations living with mental ill-health.

The importance of ensuring optimal access to primary healthcare for migrant populations is widely acknowledged as a means of reducing health inequities [31, 32]. However, migrant populations have lower rates of primary healthcare utilisation when compared to the general population [33]. Therefore, it is crucial to deepen the understanding of the factors that facilitate and impede access to primary healthcare services for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis. There is increasing emphasis on the necessity of tailoring mental healthcare interventions in primary care to address the needs of migrants with pre-existing mental health diagnoses [34–36]. However, Lu et al. [36], in their scoping review of mental health care interventions for migrants in primary healthcare settings in high-income countries, found a dearth of evidence in this area, highlighting the need for further research. While there are literature reviews exploring the challenges with integrating mental healthcare into primary care services for the general population [37, 38], there is a notable lack of reviews examining these issues among migrants living with mental ill-health. This scoping review fills this gap as it reports on the enablers and barriers for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services.

Methods

This scoping review was carried out in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology [39]. The review protocol was registered with Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/efwru), and the PRISMA [40] was utilised in the reporting of this review (see Additional File 1 for reporting checklist). The Population Concept Context (PCC) framework was used to help formulate the research question. Our population of interest is migrants adopting the International Organization for Migration definition of a migrant, which is defined as ‘any person who is moving or has moved across an international border or within a state away from his/her habitual place of residence, regardless of the person's legal status, whether the movement is voluntary or involuntary, what the causes for the movement are and what the length of the stay is’ [41]. Our main concept is access to healthcare when living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis within the context of primary healthcare. Given the variability in how primary care is organised across international contexts, this review adopted a broad, inclusive definition of primary healthcare informed by the WHO [18]. Primary care was defined as first-contact, accessible, community-based healthcare services providing comprehensive and coordinated care, including general practice, family medicine, and community health services [25]. No restrictions were placed on specific models of primary care delivery, and studies were included if the service was described by the authors as primary care or clearly aligned with core primary care functions (e.g. first point of contact, continuity, coordination of care) within the relevant health system. This led to the formulation of the following review question: What are the enablers and barriers for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services?

Search strategy

Five databases were systematically searched: Medline, CINAHL, PsycINFO, ASSIA, and Web of Science between January 2014 and December 2024, with no restrictions on geographical location. Additionally, grey literature searches were conducted in Google Scholar, Open Grey database, Cochrane Library, and Prospero. The PCC framework informed the choice of keywords and subject headings (e.g. MeSH) associated with ‘migrant’, ‘mental ill-health’ and ‘primary healthcare’. A search strategy combining these search terms was developed and tested in MEDLINE, which guided the development of a search strategy template for searching all databases (see full search strategy in Additional file 2). Reference lists of the included full texts were searched to identify any additional relevant studies that the database searches may have missed.

Eligibility criteria

The PCC framework was used to determine the following eligibility: (1) Studies from qualitative, quantitative, and mixed method research designs that focused on the migrant adult population aged 18–65 who have a pre-existing mental ill-health; (2) enablers and barriers to accessing primary healthcare services. Studies on non-migrant populations were excluded. Where terminology varied across countries, inclusion was determined based on whether services fulfilled recognised primary care functions rather than specific service labels.

Data extraction and data synthesis

All retrieved studies from the five databases were imported into Covidence (www.covidence.org) for de-duplication, and all title/abstract screening and full–text screening were independently conducted by two authors (*Blinded*) against the pre-defined eligibility criteria. Any eligibility uncertainty or discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the review team. A Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was developed and piloted by the review team to extract data from each study relevant to the research question: title, author(s), year, country; study aims/objectives, research design, participants’ characteristics/setting, data collection/analysis and variables of interest (migrant adult population with pre-existing mental ill-health, enablers and barriers accessing primary healthcare services). Narrative synthesis using the four-stage technique outlined in Popay et al.’s [42] was used for data synthesis, which involved preliminary analysis (i.e. extracting, tabulating, and quantitatively summarising the descriptive characteristics of included evidence and producing a textual summary of results) and then exploring relationships through thematic analysis.

Results

Search outcomes

The search strategy yielded a total of 14,036 studies from the five databases, 5266 duplicates were removed, and 8770 were then independently screened for title/abstract. A total of 70 full-text papers were independently screened, and 13 studies met the eligibility criteria. Models of primary care delivery across included studies

Across the included studies, primary care was delivered through a range of organisational models reflecting differences in healthcare systems across countries. These included GP-led models (e.g. UK, Australia), community health centre-based models (e.g. Canada, United States), and more integrated, multidisciplinary primary care services incorporating mental health professionals such as psychologists, social workers, and nurses within primary care settings. Studies from countries with more established multidisciplinary primary care systems more frequently described integrated and collaborative approaches to mental healthcare delivery. In contrast, studies from settings with less integrated models tended to report greater fragmentation between primary care and specialist mental health services with limited coordination of care. These structural differences influenced both access to services and the extent to which migrants’ mental health needs could be addressed within primary care.

Language barriers and communication difficulties

Ten studies [44–46, 48–53, 55] reported that language barriers and communication difficulties presented as a key barrier for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services. Unaddressed language barriers negatively impacted migrants' abilities to make appointments with primary care professionals [46] and navigate primary healthcare services [48, 49]. Unaddressed language barriers also adversely affected the quality of care during consultations with primary healthcare professionals, leading to issues such as misdiagnoses due to limited capacity for comprehensive patient assessments [55] and communication breakdowns during consultations, resulting in misunderstandings [44, 52]. Cross-cultural communication difficulties were further exacerbated by the limited use of professionally trained interpreters during consultations [51, 55]. While the use of interpreters is recognised as an important intervention in enhancing cross-cultural communication in primary healthcare services, it is acknowledged that this alone does not fully eliminate healthcare disparities experienced by migrants [53]. For example, feelings of shame associated with relying on interpreter services have been reported [44], and the use of interpreters has been shown to significantly reduce engagement with collaborative mental health care models [53].

Structural barriers to accessing primary care services

Nine studies [43, 46, 47, 49–53, 55] reported on the structural barriers to accessing primary healthcare services for migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis. Unaffordable healthcare costs [43, 50, 53] and limited access to transportation [50, 52, 53] are significant structural barriers. Extended waiting periods to consult with primary healthcare professionals [43], along with limited consultation time [45, 50], further hinder access to effective primary healthcare. A lack of understanding regarding how primary care services operate, services available within primary healthcare settings, and how to access them constitutes another significant structural barrier [46, 47, 49, 50, 52, 53, 55].

Fears and concerns about trust

Six studies [44–47, 49, 53] reported how fears and concerns about trust presented as a barrier for migrants living with pre-existing mental ill-health when accessing primary healthcare services. A lack of trust in primary healthcare professionals and their ability to manage mental health issues [46, 47, 49, 53], along with fears of confidentiality breaches within both the wider cultural community and the authorities involved in the asylum application process [44, 46, 49] were significant concerns. Concerns were also raised about being negatively judged by primary healthcare providers [44, 49], as well as apprehensions regarding potential drug interactions and side effects associated with prescribed medications [45, 49].

Lack of cultural competence

Six studies [43, 44, 46, 51, 52, 55] reported how a lack of culturally competent care presented as a barrier for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services. Primary healthcare services lack the capacity to deliver culturally appropriate services for migrants experiencing mental ill health [43, 52]. It was also noted how healthcare professionals working within these services are often ill-prepared in terms of cultural awareness and cultural knowledge to provide culturally competent healthcare [44, 46, 52]. The growing societal resistance and negative attitudes towards migrants can negatively influence primary healthcare professionals' attitudes towards migrants and the delivery of culturally competent care [51, 55].

Insufficient consideration of culturally informed perceptions of mental health

Four studies [45–48] reported on the insufficient consideration of culturally informed understandings of mental health, health-seeking behaviour, and mental healthcare treatment in primary care settings. Some cultural beliefs impede health-seeking behaviour among migrants living with mental ill health. For example, some cultures discourage emotional disclosure for fear that sharing mental health concerns may worsen their situation [46], fail to acknowledge mental ill-health symptoms [45], or view mental illness as beyond personal control and resolvable only through religious means [46]. Consequently, primary healthcare professionals can sometimes fail to recognise and diagnose mental ill-health among migrant populations [47, 48]. In some cultural groups, there is scepticism toward the Western focus on pharmacological treatments for mental illness, with a preference instead for traditional remedies such as vitamins or herbal medicines from their countries of origin [45]. The absence of psychologists or psychiatrists in countries of origin for some migrants [46] and the lack of healthcare professional training in trauma-informed care [47] also impede access to appropriate healthcare interventions in primary care settings.

Mental health stigma

Four studies [45, 46, 48, 49] reported how the stigma associated with mental ill-health presented as a barrier for migrants living with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services. Key contributors to mental health stigma included fears of being stigmatised within one's own cultural community [49], concerns about being labelled and socially excluded [46], and the stigma associated with using medication to treat mental illness [45]. Consequently, migrant patients with pre-existing mental ill-health are reluctant to share their concerns about their mental health with primary healthcare professionals [46, 48] and are reluctant to engage with mental health interventions [45].

Integrated approaches to care

The extent to which integrated approaches were reported varied across countries and appeared to reflect differences in underlying primary care system organisation. Six studies [44, 46, 51–54] reported on the value of integrated primary healthcare and collaborative approaches to care delivery for migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health. Access to coordinated approaches to mental health care in primary care settings was highly valued and contributed to increased engagement with available services and continuity of care [44, 52]. The ease and promptness with which access to a wide range of multi-disciplinary primary healthcare professionals [51, 52, 54] and community groups [46] was valued by migrants living with mental ill-health. Opportunities to collaborate with a diverse range of primary healthcare professionals also served as a valuable source of support for the professionals themselves when providing care for migrants [52]. Such integrated approaches to care result in better health education opportunities and enhanced health outcomes for migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health [46, 52, 53]. However, investment in resources, such as providing free access to primary care professionals and prescribed medications [51], along with extended consultation times in primary care settings [52], has been shown to enhance both access to and engagement with primary healthcare services. Overall, integrated and multidisciplinary models of primary care were associated with improved access, continuity, and responsiveness of care, whereas less integrated systems were more likely to be associated with fragmented care and unmet needs.

Culturally responsive mental healthcare

Four studies [44, 52–54] reported how culturally responsive mental healthcare in terms of addressing language barriers and cultural differences enhanced the standards of care for migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis in primary care services. Having access to healthcare professionals who understand their cultural backgrounds [44, 52] and having access to interpreters [44, 52, 53] or bilingual healthcare professionals [54] enhanced communication between patients and primary healthcare providers. This led to an enhanced understanding of mental ill-health management and the appropriate use of prescribed medications, which in turn contributed to improved patient health outcomes [52–54].

Differences in primary care system organisation across countries shaped how barriers and enablers were experienced, particularly in relation to care coordination, access to multidisciplinary teams, and integration of mental health services within primary care.

Discussion

This review highlights the complex interplay of factors that both facilitate and hinder access to primary healthcare services for migrants living with a pre-existing mental health diagnosis. Overall, the findings indicate that migrants experience substantially more barriers than enablers when accessing primary care. These barriers reflect a misalignment between healthcare service provision and the linguistic, cultural, and psychosocial needs of migrant populations. Key barriers included language and communication difficulties, structural barriers, limited cultural competence among providers, and concerns related to trust and confidentiality. Additionally, inadequate recognition of culturally informed understandings of mental health and the impact of mental health stigma further hindered access to primary healthcare. Although reported less frequently, factors that enable primary healthcare access for this marginalised population include integrated models of care and the provision of culturally responsive mental healthcare.

The findings of this review highlight the challenges faced by migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses when accessing primary healthcare services. While difficulties in accessing specialist and acute mental health services are well documented [15, 24, 36, 56, 57], there has been less focus on primary care, despite its central role in healthcare systems. Primary care services are typically the first point of contact for migrants and often act as gatekeepers to specialist services, providers of ongoing management for common mental health conditions, and coordinators of both physical and mental healthcare needs [29, 54]. Migrants are particularly likely to rely on primary care due to its accessibility, lower thresholds for entry, and role in addressing a broad range of health and social care needs within community settings. As such, barriers encountered at this level can delay diagnosis, limit access to specialist services, and contribute to poorer overall health outcomes. This review therefore provides an important contribution by focusing specifically on primary care as a critical site for addressing health inequities among migrants living with mental ill-health.

The most frequently reported barriers to accessing primary healthcare services for migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses included unaddressed language barriers, insufficient cultural competence, stigma and a lack of attention to culturally informed expectations of mental healthcare and treatment. These findings resonate with other literature reviews, highlighting the critical role language and culture play in shaping healthcare access for migrant populations, recognising their intertwined nature [3, 4, 7, 58]. They underscore the need for primary healthcare services to better align with the diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds of migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses. This necessitates providing readily available resources, such as professionally trained interpreters and cultural competence education and training for healthcare professionals. The findings offer new insights into how primary care can better support migrants living with pre‑existing mental ill‑health by underscoring the need to respond sensitively to migrants’ culturally informed understandings of mental health, mental ill‑health, and preferred treatment approaches. While recent migrant health literature acknowledges the influence of culturally shaped perceptions of health and healthcare treatment [5, 7], it has not specifically examined the experiences of migrants living with pre‑existing mental ill‑health accessing primary healthcare services. Neglecting these culturally informed perceptions can damage the therapeutic relationship between healthcare professionals and patients and lead to cultural misunderstandings [59]. Given that primary care consultations often represent the only point of sustained contact with healthcare services for many migrants, these barriers have significant implications for early identification, treatment, and continuity of care.

This review also highlights the significant impact of stigma associated with mental ill-health, which continues to act as a barrier to help-seeking among migrant populations. These findings are consistent with existing research [7, 35] and emphasise the need for multi-sectoral approaches to reduce stigma and promote mental health awareness. Cultural context plays a critical role in shaping experiences of stigma, and therefore, interventions must be sensitive to the diversity of beliefs and attitudes across different migrant communities [60]. The findings of our review illuminate the impact and diversity of culturally informed perceptions of mental health and healthcare treatments, which require targeted and culturally sensitive interventions.

Cognisant of the high prevalence of mental ill-health among migrant populations, and the central role of primary care as a first-contact, continuous and coordinating service, it is paramount that primary healthcare services are tailored to meet the needs of migrants with pre-existing mental health diagnoses. However, systematic reviews by Wakida et al. [61] and Tunks et al. [28] highlighted a lack of knowledge, skills and confidence among primary healthcare professionals in assessing and treating mental ill-health and noted that mental health needs are often not appropriately prioritised in primary healthcare settings. Our review extends this evidence by showing that primary healthcare professionals need not only strong skills in managing mental ill‑health, but also the ability to do so in culturally responsive ways. There is also substantial evidence in the broader healthcare literature highlighting the challenges healthcare professionals face in adapting their practices to meet the needs of migrant patients from diverse cultural, ethnic and linguistic backgrounds [9–11]. Therefore, focused attention must be given to developing primary care services and training professionals within these services to be responsive to the needs of migrants with pre-existing mental health diagnoses. Involving migrant service users with lived experience, along with community and voluntary groups that support migrant communities with mental ill-health, in the design of effective primary healthcare services is critical [62]. By prioritising the integration of culturally informed practices and engaging with migrant communities, we can improve healthcare access and outcomes for this vulnerable population.

This scoping review synthesises evidence on the enablers and barriers experienced by migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis when accessing primary healthcare services. While this represents an important contribution to the literature, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, variability in how mental ill-health was defined across studies limited the ability to distinguish between specific conditions. Second, the interchangeable use of diverse terminologies in the literature to describe migrants may have led to the omission of some evidence. To address this, various test searches were conducted in consultation with the review team, including field experts. Initial evidence sources identified during these pilot searches were examined for keywords in their titles and abstracts to ensure the use of appropriate terminology. Third, the review focuses specifically on migrants with a pre-existing mental ill-health diagnosis. Often, migrants develop mental ill-health because of trauma experienced before their resettlement period, as well as socioeconomic obstacles they face once they have arrived in their host country. Fourth, due to reporting issues in the included papers, data on country of origin, length of stay in the host country, and residency type were not extracted. These factors should be closely examined in future studies, as they significantly affect migrants’ access to healthcare services. Finally, since the narrative summary of the results is descriptive, it is important to exercise caution when interpreting these findings, particularly given the variability of the included evidence.

Conclusion

This review reaffirms the complexities that migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses experience when accessing primary healthcare services. The findings indicate that migrants face significantly more barriers than enablers when accessing primary care services. There is a need for primary healthcare services to better align with the diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds of migrants with pre-existing mental ill-health diagnoses.
