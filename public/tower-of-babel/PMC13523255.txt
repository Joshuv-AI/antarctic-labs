Inhaled nitric oxide after lung transplantation: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies
Authors: Amer Alzahrani, Mohamed Mohamed Belal, Mashael Alfaifi, Ahmed Sedeek, Maha Z Albalawi, Faris Alamoudi, Atheer Suleiman Alkeraidees, Mashael Alaboud, Abdulmajeed Alshehri, Renad A AlShuraim, Khalid Al Sulaiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung transplantation (LT) improves survival and quality of life for patients with end-stage lung disease, but ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) poses significant challenges. IRI leads to prolonged ICU stays and increased mortality, primarily due to primary graft dysfunction (PGD), especially grade 3 PGD. Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), a known vasodilator and antioxidant, has been explored for the management of IRI and for mitigating PGD3 severity. Some studies recommend its preventive use; however, insufficient evidence has delayed regulatory approval.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted across four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) from inception through September 2024. Prospective studies evaluating adult lung transplant recipients who received inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) compared to a placebo or control group were included. The primary outcome was the risk of grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD3). Secondary outcomes included intensive care unit length of stay (ICU-LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation (MV), and 30-day mortality. Pooled estimates for the primary analysis were calculated using a random-effects model.ResultsOut of 2,503 studies screened, six met the eligibility criteria, encompassing 420 patients. iNO was administered 12 to 48 hours post-transplant at doses of 10 to 20 ppm. There was a trend toward a lower risk of PGD3 in patients who received iNO; however, this trend failed to reach statistical significance (RR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.45–1.19, I² = 30%). In addition, no significant differences in ICU LOS (MD = −0.21 days, 95%CI = −3.78 to 3.36, I² = 65%), mechanical ventilation duration (MD = −0.20 days, 95% CI = −0.69 to 0.28, I² = 9%), or 30-day mortality (RR =0.94, 95% CI = 0.35–2.55, I² = 0%) were observed in the random-effects analysis.ConclusionInhaled nitric oxide is well tolerated, and there is a non-significant trend toward reducing the risk of PGD3 and shortening mechanical ventilation duration. Larger, high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm these therapeutic benefits and establish definitive clinical usage guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04336-x.Keywords: Inhaled Nitric Oxide, Lung Transplant, Critical Care, INO, Ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), Primary graft dysfunction (PGD3)

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LT) has the potential to improve both life expectancy and quality of life for patients with a variety of end-stage lung diseases [1]. Because of the relative shortage of suitable donor organs and the mortality on waiting lists, the use of suboptimal donor organs, in particular those with prolonged ischemic periods, has become increasingly necessary, but this approach may carry a greater risk and degree of lung injury [2]. Throughout the procurement of donor lungs and their reperfusion following implantation, the lungs experience ischemia and profound hypoxia, which trigger a set of pathological changes, leading to ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) by stimulation of inflammatory mediators and formation of oxygen radicals.

[3]. Despite improvements in surgical methods and lung preservation, IRI continues to be a prevalent and concerning complication [26]. The IRI was pronounced to correlate with increased PGD3 rates, increased duration of mechanical ventilation with higher ventilatory pressures, and increased length of stay [12, 16]. Moreover, IRI has been linked to subsequent development of obliterative bronchiolitis, a frequent and severe late source of lung dysfunction and mortality [4–6].

PGD has been the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among lung transplant patients [2]. Targeted pulmonary vasodilators, such as inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), offer a promising method for enhancing graft protection in LT. This approach involves administering it to recipients during or after transplantation. The rationale for this stemmed from the observed decline in endogenous pulmonary NO production during the ischemia and reperfusion phases [3, 7, 8]. Moreover, iNO exhibits antioxidative and vasodilatory effects, supporting its use in post-transplant care. Therefore, it may help to reduce the development of IRI and severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD-3), which typically manifests within 72 h post-lung transplantation [9–11]. The potential advantages of using iNO as a preventive measure against PGD-3 have been explored in various RCTs and prospective studies, yielding mixed outcomes. Although numerous clinical trials and international guidelines advocate prophylactic iNO use following LT, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has yet to endorse this use. Importantly, there is a noticeable absence of systematic reviews that consolidate findings from RCTs and prospective studies on the effectiveness of inhaled NO in preventing or alleviating IRI. To address this knowledge gap, we undertook a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate and synthesize evidence from prospective studies on the role of iNO in preventing IR injury and Grade 3 PGD in lung transplant recipients.

Method

In this meta-analysis, we followed PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines (Fig. 1) [13]. Additionally, this study was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), with the record number CRD42024608017.

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted across four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) from inception through September 2024. The search strategy is set up to identify studies on lung transplants and inhaled vasodilators. Full details of the comprehensive search strategy may be referred to in Table S1. Two investigators (AA, AS) independently assessed each eligible study using predetermined terminology in diverse databases. Titles and abstracts were screened to ascertain studies that met the selection criteria. Full texts of the studies identified as eligible after the preliminary screening were reviewed independently by the two investigators. If any disagreements could not be resolved, a third investigator (MA) was consulted to ensure a rational and unbiased selection process.

Due to the limited number of available RCTs in this clinical setting, we included both randomized and prospective non-randomized studies to maximize the available evidence base. This approach has been used in prior meta-analyses in emerging or specialized fields where high-quality randomized data are scarce. However, we accounted for potential differences in study design through careful risk of bias assessment and cautious interpretation of pooled estimates.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the risk of grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD3). Secondary outcomes included intensive care unit length of stay (ICU-LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation (MV), and 30-day mortality.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Related data were extracted individually by two investigators using a common data extraction table. The information taken from each trial fell into eight categories: [1] study PubMed ID [2], study year [3], country [4], study design [5], sample size [6], age [7], intervention and control groups [8], characteristics of inclusion/exclusion, and [9] the main effect. We assessed the quality of evidence using RoB2, a tool developed by the Cochrane Collaboration to assess risk in RCTs.[25].

Data synthesis

Risk ratios (RRs) were used for combining categorical variables after recording events and totals; and for pooling continuous outcomes, the mean difference (MD) with each of their 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method. We statistically analyzed clinical outcome measures, i.e., PGD3, ICU days (days), MV duration, hospital stay (days), and 30-day mortality. Review Manager (RevMan) 5.3 for Windows was used to perform the statistical analysis.

Given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, the primary analyses were conducted using a random-effects model. Fixed-effects models were performed as supplementary analyses to assess the consistency of findings.

Our primary goal is to assess the study heterogeneity, and this was done using the Cochrane Q test (X² test) and an I-squared (I²) array. A significant difference in p-value (χ² <0.1) indicates heterogeneity. I-square values ≥ 50% mean extremely heterogeneous. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Planned subgroup analyses based on study design and comparator type were considered; however, these were not performed due to the limited number of included studies.

Results

1. Literature Search Results

A total of 2503 studies were identified through our literature search, and after removing 1211 duplicates, 1292 articles were retained for title and abstract screening. After extracting and unifying the outcomes, we identified 20 studies eligible for full-text review. Of these, 6 studies were included in the analysis, as shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) [13]. Three studies were double-blind RCTs [14,15,16], two non-blinded RCTs [9,17], and one prospective non-randomized trial [18].

2. Characteristics of the Included Studies

The meta-analysis comprised 6 studies involving 420 patients. Of the included studies in the analysis, 212 patients used iNO, and 208 patients served as controls. Included studies were conducted in the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK), Canada, Spain, and France. Of 420 patients in the included studies, 203 patients (54.7%) were male. iNO doses in the included trials were 10–20 parts per million (ppm), mostly given after transplantation or start of reperfusion, and each with varying durations, e.g., the durations range from 12 to 48 h post-transplant. The placebo control varied across trials, including standard anesthetic gas mixture [14], inhaled epoprostenol (iEPO) [15], unspecified placebos [9], or patients who did not receive iNO [17].

Quality assessment results

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool, evaluating five key domains: (1) randomization process (2), deviations from intended interventions (3), missing outcome data (4), measurement of the outcome, and (5) selection of the reported result. The overall risk of bias was categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk.

The quality assessment of the included studies revealed varying degrees of methodological rigor. Four studies [9, 14, 16, 18] exhibited a high risk of bias, primarily due to concerns related to the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and the measurement and reporting of outcomes. One study [15] demonstrated a low risk of bias across all domains except for the selection of the reported result, indicating strong methodological quality. Perrin et al. study [17] raised concerns, particularly regarding the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and selective reporting. These findings suggest that, while some studies maintained methodological robustness, the presence of high-risk studies could affect the overall reliability and interpretation of the meta-analysis.

GRADE assessment

The quality assessment tool used was RoB2 [25]. Three studies demonstrated a high risk of bias in the certainty assessment with the PGD-3, hospital length of stay, and 30-day mortality. Moreover, mechanical ventilation duration and ICU length of stay have been shown in two studies to be at high risk of bias, and in one with an unclear risk. On the other hand, the ICU length-of-stay events assessed across three studies showed inconsistency due to high heterogeneity (50–70%). Also, it is important to note that the results for ICU length of stay in three studies and hospital length of stay in two studies may be imprecise due to wide confidence intervals (Table S2).

Study outcomes

Primary Graft Dysfunction Grade 3 (PGD3)

Three of the included studies reported PGD3, with a total of 47 patients [9,14,15]. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the iNO and control groups (RR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.45–1.19, I² = 30%). The findings were consistent when applying a fixed-effects model (RR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.59–1.06, I² = 30%), as shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

ICU Length of Stay (LOS)

ICU length of stay (LOS), measured in days, was reported as an outcome in three of the included studies [15−17]. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed no significant difference between the iNO and control groups (MD = −0.21 days, 95% CI = −3.78 to 3.36, I² = 65%). The fixed-effects model yielded similar non-significant findings (Figs. 4 and 5).

Mechanical Ventilation (MV) Duration

Regarding the duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) in days, three studies included MV duration as an outcome of interest [15−17]. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the iNO and control groups (MD = −0.20 days, 95% CI = −0.69 to 0.28, I² = 9%). In contrast, the fixed-effects model showed a statistically significant reduction in mechanical ventilation duration (MD = −0.23 days, 95% CI = −0.36 to −0.10, I² = 9%, p = 0.0005), as illustrated in Figs. 6 and 7.

Hospital Stay Duration

Two of the included studies [Ghadimi 2021 et al. and Meade 2003 et al.] reported hospital stay in days as an outcome of interest. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed no significant difference between the iNO and control groups (MD = −0.72 days, 95% CI = −4.65 to 3.22, I² = 0%). The fixed-effects model yielded identical results, as shown in Figs. 8 and 9.

30-Day Mortality

The 30-day mortality was reported as an outcome in three studies [15, 16, 18]. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the iNO and control groups (RR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.35–2.55, I² = 0%). Similar findings were observed with the fixed-effects model (RR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.36–2.57, I² = 0%), as shown in Figs. 10 and 11.

Discussion

This meta-analysis aims to assess the clinical impact of iNO delivery in lung transplant recipients, including primary graft dysfunction, length of stay (LOS), 30-day mortality, and mechanical ventilation duration. The analysis revealed that, despite the different biochemical mechanisms underlying the effects of iNO, there was no consistent statistical benefit in mitigating the incidence of the primary outcome of PGD3 or the duration of mechanical ventilation. Furthermore, the pooled data did not indicate any significant differences in LOS or in the incidence of 30-day mortality between the various groups.

Despite experimental animal models and non-RCTs supporting the use of prophylactic iNO to reduce the risk of PGD, many RCTs have shown inconsistent results [3, 9, 19, 20]. Meade et al. and Ghadimi et al. conducted RCTs that reported transient improvements in oxygenation yet failed to demonstrate statistically significant reductions in PGD incidence [15, 16, 18]. Similarly, Perrin et al. found that iNO failed to prevent post-transplant pulmonary edema [17]. Those differences encouraged more scientific investigations to assess the clinical applications of iNO among transplant recipients.

This meta-analysis showed that iNO had no significant effect on PGD3. The pooled data were consistent with low heterogeneity. It showed a 19% reduction in PGD3 in the iNO group, with no statistically significant difference. In addition, Yao et al. screened 10,042 transplant recipients for risk factors for developing PGD in a meta-analysis that included non-RCTs. Their meta-analysis found that intraoperative iNO did not significantly affect the incidence of PGD [22]. Moreover, Botha et al., whose study was included in our meta-analysis, also demonstrated no significant reduction in PGD3 with the prophylactic iNO administration. In this study by Botha et al., 20 recipients of lung transplants were given either iNO or a standard anesthetic gas mixture during transplantation. They found no statistically significant effect of iNO or the conventional anesthetic gas mixture on the development of PGD3 [21]. Conversely, Moreno et al. conducted an RCT involving 32 lung transplant recipients who received either iNO or placebo [9]. Their findings revealed a statistically significant difference in lowering the PGD incidence (29%) among the iNO group compared to (40%) among the control group during lung transplantation [9].

In this meta-analysis, three trials examined the length of hospital stay after prophylactic iNo. The pooled data analysis showed no statistically significant difference in hospital length of stay. Both Ghamidi (2021) and Meada (2003) evaluated hospital LOS, including 140 participants receiving iNO and 145 controls. The fixed effect analysis showed a mean difference of −0.72 days in hospital LOS and no heterogeneity among the trials [15, 16]. Additionally, three of the included studies showed a trend toward reduced LOS in the iNO group compared with the control group, with a mean difference of −0.21 days but no statistically significant difference, and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 65%).

The impact of iNO administration on mechanical ventilation duration was evaluated in three studies included in our meta-analysis. Although the fixed-effects model demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in mechanical ventilation duration, this finding was not maintained in the random-effects model. Therefore, the overall evidence does not support a statistically significant effect of iNO on mechanical ventilation duration.

The majority of RCTs comparing iNO to placebo in lung transplant patients have failed to demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in 30-day mortality. Moreover, a meta-analysis of pooled results from three distinct studies revealed no statistically significant improvement in survival among patients who received iNO. These findings suggest that the routine use of prophylactic iNO in lung transplant recipients may not confer the anticipated survival benefit, warranting further investigation into its clinical efficacy in this patient population [15, 16, 18].

A key strength of this meta-analysis is its exclusive inclusion of prospective designs and RCTs, providing a focused synthesis within a highly specialized clinical setting where evidence remains sparse. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the overall sample size is relatively small (420 patients), reflecting the inherent challenges of conducting clinical trials in lung transplantation. This restriction, paired with the inclusion of both randomized and non-randomized prospective designs, introduces methodological heterogeneity and limits the statistical precision of our pooled estimates. Second, substantial clinical heterogeneity exists regarding iNO dosing and timing. Dosing protocols varied from fixed regimens of 10 ppm [9] or 20 ppm [15, 17, 21] to surgeon-titrated ranges of 5–20 ppm. Because iNO exhibits dose-dependent effects on pulmonary hypertension and hypoxemia secondary to allograft failure, standardizing doses is crucial for accurate outcome assessment. Similarly, the timing of initiation differed across trials. While some studies initiated iNO at the time of transplant, others started therapy at reperfusion [17] or 10 minutes post-reperfusion. Preclinical evidence indicates that early administration—particularly during the reperfusion phase—optimizes iNO’s protective effects by attenuating oxidative and inflammatory injury. Thus, these variations in timing and treatment duration likely influenced clinical outcomes and contributed to inter-study heterogeneity. Third, variability among comparator groups complicates direct comparisons. While most trials utilized a placebo or standard care, Ghadimi et al. compared iNO to inhaled epoprostenol, demonstrating clinical equivalency within a 19% margin for reducing Grade 3 PGD. Furthermore, the therapeutic benefits of iNO may be highly individualized. Patients with elevated baseline risk factors—such as prolonged ischemic times or severe pulmonary hypertension—might derive greater benefit from iNO. Unfortunately, most published trials did not stratify clinical outcomes by recipient or donor risk factors. Finally, the limited number of studies contributing to each outcome prevented us from performing subgroup or sensitivity analyses based on study design, comparator type, dosing, or timing. Several endpoints were derived from only two or three trials, increasing the risk of random error and reducing the stability of our pooled estimates. This sparse data structure, combined with a predominance of moderate-to-high overall risk of bias among the included studies, necessitates caution when interpreting the robustness of these findings.Future large-scale, multi-center RCTs must utilize standardized protocols to validate these results. Investigators should specifically target the timing of initiation during the critical reperfusion phase and compare standardized doses (10 ppm vs. 20 ppm) against consistent placebo controls. Moreover, the field must transition toward individualized, risk-stratified models that explicitly assess iNO efficacy in high-risk patient subgroups, such as those with prolonged donor ischemic times or severe pulmonary hypertension.

Future research directions

While our meta-analysis highlights the potential of perioperative iNO to optimize postoperative outcomes, several knowledge gaps remain. Future research should prioritize the following areas to refine clinical guidelines and enhance patient care: Long-Term Outcomes and Allograft Survival: Although current literature focuses heavily on immediate perioperative benefits, the impact of iNO on long-term endpoints—such as the development of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) and overall patient survival—remains poorlyunderstood and requires longitudinal evaluation. Granular Hemodynamic and Oxygenation Monitoring: To provide a more precise assessmentof iNO's safety and efficacy, future trials should systematically monitor mean pulmonary artery pressure alongside specific oxygenation parameters. This will facilitate the design of individualized protocols tailored to patient and procedural factors, including intraoperative hemodynamic stability, lung ventilation strategies, and reperfusion stress severity. Head-to-Head Comparative Trials: Large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to compare iNO directly against alternative pulmonary vasodilators, such as inhaled epoprostenol. Investigating alternatives like prostacyclin analogs, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, and nitrite-based therapies is vital to determine if they offer advantages regarding cost-effectiveness, ease of administration, or minimized side-effect profiles. Advanced Mechanistic Insights: Incorporating advanced functional imaging and molecular biomarkers into future trial protocols will be essential to evaluate real-time graft perfusion, inflammatory cascades, and fibrotic pathways. These techniques will provide deeper insights into the exact pathophysiological mechanisms through which iNO influences long-term graft outcomes.

Conclusions

Inhaled nitric oxide administration in adult lung transplant recipients demonstrates a statistically non-significant, trend toward mitigating the risk of Grade 3 primary graft dysfunction (PGD3) and accelerating weaning from mechanical ventilation. These findings suggest potentialclinical utility, yet the current evidence remains limited by small sample sizes and clinical heterogeneity. Consequently, large-scale, multi-center randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are urgently needed to definitively confirm these therapeutic benefits, optimize dosing protocols, andestablish standardized clinical practice guidelines.
