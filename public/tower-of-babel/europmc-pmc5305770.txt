are of frequent occurrence, more especially at the upper and
lower limits. The lesions occurring in the region of the
stomach and duodenum are, for the most part, inflammatory in
nature, whereas those at the lower.end may be associated with a
variety of diseases, such as enteric, dysentery, tuberculosis or
carcinoma. The intervening portion of intestine, the jejeunum,
is comparatively free from ulcerative lesions if one excepts the
peptic ulcer which occurs in a varying proportion of cases
following gastro-jejeunostomy. These, however, are secondary,
and do not come within the scope of this paper, which is more

present certain definite characteristics. They occur in the
majority of cases opposite the attached border of the intestine,
in only 4 cases was the site stated to be close to the mesenteric
insertion. Leotta believes these ulcers to be of the acute
variety, but he admits that they may pass to the chronic stage,
and Richardson, Walton and Bryan each report the presence
of chronic ulcers in the jejeunum. Perforation occurs very
frequently, the opening varying from the size of a millet seed
(Janowski) to one with a diameter of two centimetres (Ceci).
The ulcers are for the most part rounded, clean cut, with
punched-out edges and tend to be terraced, the mucosa suffering
more extensively from the necrotic process than the serous coat.
There is, as a rule, very slight inflammatory reaction or indura-
tion in the vicinity of the ileal ulcers, but in the jejeunum the
ulcers are often of a chronic nature. They are frequently
described as being exactly similar to the perforating ulcer of the
stomach or duodenum.
Etiology.?Cruveilhier initiated the idea that chronic ulcers
originated in acute lesions, and this has been further supported
by Bolton.4 Bolton believes that most acute ulcers heal rapidly,
and that chronic ulcers result only from unhealed acute lesions.
His work is more concerned with ulcers which occur in the
stomach and duodenum, but probably the essential factor in
their production is much the same as that producing the very
similar lesions occurring in the jejeunum and ileum.
In looking for a cause of these ulcers we exclude at once
such conditions as tuberculosis, carcinoma, enteric, and dysentery,
because, where a definite diagnosis of simple ulcer has been
confirmed, these conditions were found to be absent. We
cannot, however, deal so summarily with syphilis. There may
be a tendency to label any condition of obscure origin occurring
in a patient with a positive YVassermann reaction "syphilitic,"
and it is often exceedingly difficult to refute the possibility.
Cases have certainly occurred in persons who have been
exposed to infection, but they have also been met with in
cases where the blood gave no reaction to the specific virus.
Syphilis of the small intestine occurs more often in the jejeunum
than elsewhere. The lesions are frequently multiple, tending
to form an ulcerated ring round the gut, and may go on to
form a stricture. They may also perforate, and Richardson5

So much work has been done on focal infection and the
elective localisation of bacteria by Rosenow6 and others, that
this will be considered first.
As is already well known Rosenow repeatedly showed that
the^ injection into animals of cultures taken from the depths of
various lesions in man resulted in the production of similar
esions in a large percentage of cases. Furthermore, he found
t at a thorough examination of the teeth and tonsils of patients
suffering from such conditions as cholecystitis, gastric ulcer,
0r appendicitis frequently revealed a focus of infection which,
|vhen cultured and injected into animals, produced corresponding
esions in them. Other workers, however, have not always
uiet with the same success, nor have they been able to
corroborate Rosenow's findings, nevertheless Rosenow's results
ave been so strikingly uniform, both in his early work and
Jn his more recently published experiments, that for the present
t ey seem to offer striking proof of the affinity certain strepto-
cocci may have for certain areas of the body. Rosenow7 has
suggested that the degree of virulence of the streptococcus
seems to be one of the factors that determine the place of its
survival after intravenous injection. In proof of this he has
s own that the organisms from a diseased appendix are less
virulent than those from a gastric ulcer, but that, if the strain
rom the appendix be passed through animals, it becomes more
virulent and its injection then results in an increased percentage
?f gastric lesions.
So far as we are aware, no work has been done on the
production of intestinal ulcers by the injection of organisms
taken from intestinal lesions in man, but Rosenow has produced
mtestinal ulceration by the injection of gastric-ulcer-producing
streptococci. From a study of the published tables of his
results, it would appear that intestinal lesions tend to increase
Jn number in direct proportion to the virulence of the organisms.

separate occasions developed perforating ulcers of the ileum,
the attack in each case being preceded by a furunculosis of

On the whole there seems reason to believe that these
simple ulcers of the intestine are associated with some
inflammatory process. It is not clear, however, whether the
ulcer is primarily due to the elective localisation of the bacteria
or whether there is some alteration in the intestinal wall which
offers a suitable nidus for the organisms.
Many experiments have been done which show that the
infection is blood-borne. Attempts to produce ulcer of the
stomach by feeding with bacteria have been unsuccessful except
in starving animals. Feeding with streptococci which have
been proved to produce ulcers on intravenous injection also
failed, and even when sharp particles were included in the diet
no lesion was produced ; but the value of that experiment has
been diminished by the work of Simmons and von Glahn,10 who
showed that the administration of comparatively large quantities
of ground glass produced no lesions whatever in the gastro-
intestinal tract of dogs.
In an endeavour to discover some cause for the primary
alteration in the wall of the intestine which would allow of a
secondary focal infection, we must consider a number of factors.
Trauma may devitalise the intestinal mucosa, and though in
the majority of the reported cases of jejeunal or ileal ulcers
there is no such history, there is, in at least three cases, a history
of injury to the abdominal wall immediately preceding the
onset of acute symptoms. It is possible that a sudden blow
might result in the perforation of an area already weakened by
ulceration, but it could not cause the sudden appearance of a
perforating .ulcer in a previously healthy bowel.

Muller's work on the subject is quoted. He reports a case of a
perforating ulcer of a Meckel's diverticulum where examination
of the diverticulum revealed gastric mucosa resembling closely

The authors above referred to support Muller s hypothesis,
that though the injection of toxic material produces ulceration,
the essential factor is the presence of these groups of intestinal
gland-rests, which are devitalised by various contributing causes.
More recently, Nicholson13 has contributed a paper in
which he quotes a series of cases where he found gastric mucosa
with typical gastric glands in abnormal situations. Gastric
mucosa was found in a persistent vitelline duct, in a Meckel s
diverticulum, in a cyst of the pancreas, in a series of cases
of cholecystitis, and even in a case of tuberculous ulcer of
the colon.
If the presence of these rests is the essential cause, the
argument can only be definitely applied to ulcers of the
jejeunum, because gastric mucosa has not yet been demon-
strated in the lower part of the small intestine. But ^ if, as
Nicholson has shown, gastric glands are found so low in the
intestinal tract as the colon, it appears reasonable to expect a
similar occurrence in the ileum.
The endocrine glands have, of course, been blamed for that
devitalisation of the intestinal wall which may be necessary
before an ulcer can occur. Friedman14 says that the excess or
deficiency of endocrine secretion acts directly on the walls or on
the vessels of the whole gastro-intestinal tract, but as far as the
intestine is concerned, ulceration does not occur on account o
the absence of hydrochloric acid, though devitalised areas may
become the site of bacterial invasion. .

Clinical History.?It is difficult to give a clinical picture
characteristic of this condition. In the first place, in many of
the reported cases the history is given very briefly and lacks
detail; in the second place, where the history is complete, the
symptoms in different cases vary widely. Based on our two
cases and on those fully reported in literature, we describe what
appear to be the most usual clinical features.
Simple ileo-jejeunal ulceration is met with much more
frequently in men than women. From a perusal of the table
it will be seen that of the 18 persons in whom the lesion
definitely occurred in the ileum, 12 were males and 6 females.
Similarly, in a total of 9 jejeunal ulcers, 7 occurred in males
and 2 in females. In 4 other cases, where the exact site
of the lesion was not specified, the patients were men. The
age incidence varies widely. Kirmisson16 reports a case where
the patient was a male child of 8, whilst Dodson17 and Parcelier
each report the lesion as occurring at the age of 57. The
majority of simple ileo-jejeunal ulcers occur in persons between
the ages of 30 and 50, and this includes both sexes.
The early history of these persons varies. Some patients
give a definite history of " stomach trouble" or " indigestion,"
lasting for months or for years. In a case of primary jejeunal
ulcer recently reported by Walton,18 the patient had gastric
symptoms for nineteen years, while in one of our cases gastric

Pain is the most constant feature of these cases, and is
frequently associated with the taking of food. The onset o
pain occurs somewhat later than in ulcers of the pylorus. ^ e

the pain, but this depends largely on the site of the ulcer,
whether high or low in the intestinal tract. The site of Pairj_
is important. It is, as a rule, in or near the middle line o
the abdomen, but distinctly lower down than the usual gastric
pain, and it may radiate towards the lumbar region.
In the majority of cases the symptoms are not sufficient y
definite to cause the patient to seeksurgical aid until perforation
occurs. In a smaller proportion of cases, gastro-jejeunostomy
has been performed in an unsuccessful endeavour to relieve
somewhat vague duodenal symptoms, and not till later has
the intestinal ulcer been discovered to be the real lesion.
Meriel19 describes such a case ; a man who suffered from gastro-
intestinal symptoms for four months. A gastro-jejeunostomy
was performed though no ulcer or tumour was found. Durin^,
convalescence obstruction developed, requiring further operation,
and exploration revealed a perforated ulcer of the ileum w ic
had been localised by adhesions.
It is as an acute abdominal crisis that this type of case
usually presents itself. The necessity for immediate operation
is then apparent, but to make a definite diagnosis is very
difficult. The patient, who as a rule has been going about
as usual, is suddenly seized with acute pain in the lower part
of the epigastrium, and frequently it is definitely localised to
the umbilical region. This occurs especially in the case o
the perforated jejeunal ulcer. Vomiting occurs and ^

Treatment.?The condition is essentially surgical. If per-
foration has occurred the sooner the peritoneal cavity is opened
and the ulcer dealt with the better. How this can best be done
depends on a number of factors; the general condition of the
patient, depending on the length of time which has elapsed since
perforation occurred, the site of the lesion whether high in the
jejeunum or in the lower and more infective parts of the small
intestine; the nature of the lesion whether a single perforation
or a more extensive involvement of bowel, or lastly, whether
there is a superadded obstruction from adhesions.
Enterectomy under these conditions is not desirable if a
more simple procedure is likely to give satisfactory results. If
simple closure of the perforation can be done without undue
narrowing of the lumen of the bowel, this is the safest line of treat-
ment, provided that the vicinity of the ulcer is fairly healthy.
In dealing with perforations of the stomach and duodenum,
there is a difference of opinion as to whether the abdomen
should be drained or not. Gibson 20 states that he has never
obtained a growth when the culture from such lesions was taken
within eighteen hours of the perforation, and that drainage in
such cases is neither necessary nor useful. In the case of
intestinal perforated ulcers, especially when situated low down,
there can be little doubt that the peritoneum is infected and
then drainage is required.
