Geometric aspects of the curve shortening flow in the hyperbolic plane
Ivan Krznarić, Rafael López

Abstract
. We define a new notion of translations in the hyperbolic plane and explicitly solve the equation of the curve shortening flow. Next, we consider the class of ancient convex solutions and solve the equation of the curve shortening flow when the curvature function is given by separation of variables. Lastly, we prove some area estimates for closed ancient solutions of the curve shortening flow.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
53E10, 53C42

1.
Introduction

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
be a curve parametrized by arc-length in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. A one-parameter family of parametrized curves
\{\gamma_{t}\colon I\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\}_{t\in[0,T\rangle}
is said to be the
curve shortening flow
of
\gamma
if

(1)

\left\{\begin{split}\left(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)^{\perp}&=\kappa(t,\cdot)N(t,\cdot)\\
\gamma_{0}&=\gamma\end{split}\right.

where
\kappa(t,\cdot)
and
N(t,\cdot)
are the geodesic curvature and the unit normal vector of
\gamma_{t}
and
\left(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}\right)^{\perp}
is the normal part of velocity
\gamma_{t}
. The theory of the CSF in the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
was first developed in the 1980s by Gage and Hamilton (
[
11
,
12
]
) revealing that its study is non-trivial. This is due to the difficulty of calculating explicit solutions to the flow and also due to the development of singularities during the evolution. Therefore, it is convenient to study the solutions of the CSF within some specific class of curves. Common examples of such classes are solitons, translating solutions and ancient convex solutions. Although the CSF can be studied on arbitrary surfaces, the most interesting situations, besides the Euclidean plane, arise when studying the evolution of curves in the models of the remaining two
2
-dimensional geometries: the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}.
The curve shortening flow on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
has been considered via various approaches (
[
3
,
4
]
).

To the best of our knowledge, the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the ambient in which the curve shortening flow is studied the least. In
[
8
,
17
]
, the authors have classified the solutions which evolve by isometries during the flow and have calculated some explicit examples of such curves using their characterization. The mean curvature flow in higher dimensions has been studied in
[
1
,
6
,
7
]
. Following ideas of
[
6
]
, in
[
16
]
a modified flow of (
1
) has been studied preserving the length of the curves.

The purpose of this paper is to give some progress of the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
emphasizing some of its geometric aspects. For example, in Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the study of the CSF by geodesic translations reduces to the study of solitons of the CSF because Eq. (
1
) is invariant by translation. However the situation in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is different. The class of solitons appears in a natural way when we study those solutions of the CSF invariant by a one-parameter groups of isometries. In this paper we are also interested in the solutions obtained by geodesic translations. We will see that such solutions are curves in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with constant curvature. Other interest in this paper is the role of the angle parameter. Unlike in the Euclidean plane, it is not possible to geometrically and globally define the angle formed by tangent vectors with a fixed direction. Grayson is able to define a global angle where a ‘density’ is added at each point. Motivated by the paper
[
9
]
, we obtain the solutions of the CSF when
\kappa^{2}
is given by separation of variables, proving that besides solitons, the solutions are the curves with constant curvature.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notation of the one-parameter groups of isometries in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and we give examples of CSF for curves with constant curvature. In Section
3
we turn our attention to the study of solutions of the CSF by geodesic translations, proving that the initial curve is a curve with constant curvature. We explicitly find the CSF. In Section
4
we discuss when the angle can be considered as a parameter in the flow. By taking the natural angle
\varphi
defined by Grayson
[
13
]
, we find the solutions of the CSF when the pressure function
\kappa^{2}
is given by separation of variables
\kappa^{2}(t,\varphi)=a(t)+b(\varphi)
. Besides the trivial solutions, which do not depend on
t
, we prove in Thm.
4.3
that the solution of the CF is a family of curves with constant curvature, that is, circle, horocycles or equidistant lines. We also find the evolution of the Euclidean angle
\theta
which can be defined in the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane. Finally in Sect.
5
, we prove that the limit of the area enclosed by ancient simply closed convex solution to the CSF is infinity (Thm.
5.1
). In particular, the time interval of the flow is
(-\infty,0]
.

2.
Examples of CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}

Consider the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, that is,
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon y>0\right\}
endowed with the Riemannian metric
\langle,\rangle=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}=\{(x,0)\colon x\in\mathbb{R}\}
the horizontal line of equation
y=0
. The ideal boundary
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
is the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
, that is,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
. Identifying
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with the upper complex plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z=a+ib\in\mathbb{C}\colon b>0\}
, the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
coincides with the Möbius transformations
T(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
with real coefficients such that
ad-bc=1
. There are three one-parameter subgroups of isometries in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
which are described as follows:

(1)

Hyperbolic translations. We have
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
H_{t}(z)=e^{t}z
.

(2)

Parabolic translations. We have
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
P_{t}(z)=z+t
.

(3)

Rotations. We have
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
R_{t}(z)=\frac{\cos(t)z-\sin(t)}{\sin(t)z+\cos(t)}
.

These three subgroups allow us to find a basis of Killing vector fields of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. The Killing vector fields are obtained by

X_{H}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}H_{t}(z),\quad X_{P}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}P_{t}(z),\quad X_{R}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}R_{t}(z).

A simple computation gives

(2)

\begin{split}X_{H}(x,y)&=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y},\\
X_{P}(x,y)&=\partial_{x},\\
X_{R}(x,y)&=-(1+x^{2}-y^{2})\partial_{x}+2xy\partial_{y}.\end{split}

Notice that the vector field
X_{R}
can be written as
X_{R}=-X_{P}-(x^{2}-y^{2})\partial_{x}+2xy\partial_{y}
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2},\gamma=\gamma(u),
be a parametrized curve where
u
is an arbitrary parameter. If
\gamma(u)=(x(u),y(u))
, its curvature
\kappa
is given by

(3)

\kappa=y\frac{x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}}{(x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2})^{3\over 2}}+\frac{x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}}.

There is a relationship between the curvature
\kappa
of
\gamma
and the Euclidean curvature
\kappa_{e}
of
\gamma
when
\gamma
is considered as a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
endowed with the Euclidean metric. This relation is

(4)

\kappa=y\kappa_{e}+\frac{x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{2}}}.

This identity is also a consequence of (
3
).

We know how to describe the curves in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with constant curvature. In the upper-half plane model, these curves are the intersections of circles and straight-lines of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. The geodesics of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are given by vertical half-lines from
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
and half-circles with center at
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
. All other curves in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with constant curvature are the following:

(1)

Circles. A circle is the locus of all points a fixed distance from a given point. The distance is called the radius of the circle and the given point is its center. From the Euclidean viewpoint, they are Euclidean circles contained in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. If the radius is
R
, the curvature is
\kappa=\coth(R)
. Notice that
\kappa>1
.

(2)

Horocycles. A horocycle is either a circle tangent to the line
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
or a line parallel to
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
. Its curvature is
\kappa=1
.

(3)

Equidistant lines. An equidistant line is the set of points in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
equidistant from a given geodesic. Equidistant lines are (non-geodesics) arcs of circles and (non-vertical) half-lines from points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}
. If the equidistant line is of equation
y=mx+n
, then its curvature is
\kappa=1/\sqrt{1+m^{2}}
. The curvature
\kappa
is a number between
0
and
1
.

We give examples of CSF for curves with constant curvature. See also
[
13
, p. 76–77]
.

Example 2.1

(Flow of geodesics)
.

The evolution under CSF of a geodesic
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}
is given by
\gamma(t,s)=\gamma(s)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This is because
\kappa=0
.

Example 2.2

(Flow of circles)
.

A circle of center
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and radius
R
coincides with the the Euclidean circle with center at
(a,\cosh(R))
and radius
b\sinh(R)
. A parametrization
\gamma
of
a circle of radius
R
and center
(0,1)
is

\gamma(s)=(0,\cosh(R))+\sinh(R)\left(\cos(s),\sin(s)\right).

Analogously to the Euclidean case, an initial guess for its flow is the family of all circles centered at
(0,1)
. Therefore, let

\gamma_{t}(s)=(0,\cosh(r(t)))+\sinh(r(t))(\cos(s),\sin(s)).

We compute all of the quantities of (
1
). We know
\kappa=\coth(r(t))
and

\begin{split}N&=-(\cosh(r(t))+\sinh(r(t))\sin(s))(\cos(s),\sin(s)),\\
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}&=r^{\prime}(\cosh(r(t))\cos(s),\sinh(r(t))+\cosh(r(t))\sin(s)).\end{split}

Then

\langle(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t})^{\perp},N\rangle=-r^{\prime}(t).

Thus (
1
) reduces into

\left\{\begin{split}r^{\prime}&=-\coth(r),\\
r(0)&=R\end{split}\right.

The solution is the function
r(t)=\cosh^{-1}(\cosh(R)e^{-t})
.
Since
\cosh^{-1}
is defined for numbers bigger than or equal to
1
, we get
t_{\text{max}}=\ln(\cosh(R))
. At the maximal time we have
r=0
so the circles contract to a point in finite time under the CSF.

Example 2.3

(Flow of horocycles)
.

Let
\gamma
be the horocycle

\gamma(s)=(0,R)+R\left(\cos(s),\sin(s)\right).

The ideal boundary of
\gamma
is
(0,0)
. Since we guess that the CSF is formed by all horocycles with the same ideal boundary point
(0,0)
, let

\gamma_{t}(s)=(0,r(t))+r(t)(\cos(s),\sin(s)).

Now we have
\kappa=1
and

\begin{split}N&=-(r(t)(1+\sin(s))(\cos(s),\sin(s)),\\
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}&=r^{\prime}(\cos(s),1+\sin(s)).\end{split}

Therefore

\langle(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t})^{\perp},N\rangle=-\frac{r^{\prime}(t)}{r(t)}.

Then (
1
) now is

\left\{\begin{split}r^{\prime}&=-r,\\
r(0)&=R\end{split}\right.

The solution is
r(t)=Re^{-t}
.
In this case, we have
t_{\text{max}}=\infty
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=0
. Thus the horocycles contract to the ideal boundary point
(0,0)
under the CSF.

Example 2.4

(Flow of equidistant lines)
.

Let
0<c<R
. Let
\gamma
be the equidistant line

\gamma(s)=(0,c)+R\left(\cos(s),\sin(s)\right).

From the Euclidean viewpoint,
\gamma
parametrizes the Euclidean circle centered at
(0,c)
with radius
\sqrt{R^{2}-c^{2}}
. The curvature is
\kappa=c/R
. Again, we guess that the CSF of such an equidistant line is formed out of all the equidistant lines in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with the same ideal boundary as the initial curve. Thus, let
0<c(t)<r(t)
with
r(t)^{2}-c(t)^{2}=R^{2}-c^{2}:=k^{2},
k>0
. Define

\gamma_{t}(s)=(0,\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-k^{2}})+r(t)(\cos(s),\sin(s)).

Now we have
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}(t)-k^{2}}}{r(t)}
and

\begin{split}N&=-(\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-k^{2}}+r(t)\sin(s))(\cos(s),\sin(s)),\\
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}&=r^{\prime}(\cos(s),\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^{2}-k^{2}}}+\sin(s)).\end{split}

Then

\langle(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t})^{\perp},N\rangle=-\frac{r^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-k^{2}}}.

Thus (
1
) is

\left\{\begin{split}r^{\prime}&=-\frac{r^{2}-k^{2}}{r},\\
r(0)&=R\end{split}\right.

The solution is the function
r(t)=\sqrt{k^{2}+(R^{2}-k^{2})e^{-2t}}
.
In this case, we have
t_{\text{max}}=\infty
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=k
. Thus the equidistant lines
\gamma_{t}
contract to the geodesic
s\mapsto k(\cos(s),\sin(s))
, which coincides with the geodesic to which the initial curve
\gamma
is equidistant to.

As a consequence of the examples above, we conclude the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

The limit of the curve shortening flow

(1)

of a circle is its center;

(2)

of a horocycle is its ideal boundary point;

(3)

of an equidistant line is the geodesic to which the initial curve is equidistant to.

We finish this section by finding all the solutions to the CSF that evolve by the three types of one-parameter group of isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, namely,
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
. These solutions will be called
solitons
. Here we will use the notation of the Killing vector fields (
2
).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\gamma
be a curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. If under CSF
\gamma
evolves by:

(1)

Hyperbolic translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, then
\gamma
satisfies

(5)

\kappa=\langle N,X_{H}\rangle,\quad\mbox{(hyperbolic soliton)}.

(2)

Parabolic translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, then
\gamma
satisfies

(6)

\kappa=\langle N,X_{P}\rangle,\quad\mbox{(parabolic soliton)}.

(3)

Rotations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, then
\gamma
satisfies

(7)

\kappa=\langle N,X_{R}\rangle,\quad\mbox{(rotational soliton)}.

Proof.

(1)

The flow by hyperbolic translations is given by
\gamma_{t}(s)=e^{t}\gamma(s)
. If
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
, then
N(t,s)=e^{t}N(s)
and
\kappa(t,s)=\kappa(s)
. Notice that
N=\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}}(-y^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. Since
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}(s)=e^{t}\gamma
, then (
1
) is simply
\kappa=\langle N,\gamma\rangle
.

(2)

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
and consider the flow by parabolic translations
\gamma_{t}(s)=\gamma(s)+t(1,0)
. Again
N(t,s)=N(s)
and
\kappa(t,s)=\kappa(s)
. Since
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}(s)=(1,0)
, then (
1
) is
\kappa=\langle N,(1,0)\rangle
.

(3)

If
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
, the flow by rotations is given in complex notation by

\gamma_{t}(s)=\frac{\cos(t)\gamma(s)-\sin(t)}{\sin(t)\gamma(s)+\cos(t)}.

Since
R_{t}
are isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, then
\kappa(t,s)=\kappa(s)
. If the coordinates of
\gamma_{t}(s)
area
((\gamma_{t}(s))_{x},(\gamma_{t}(s))_{y})
, the unit normal vector is

N(t,s)=\frac{(\gamma_{t}(s))_{y}}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}A&B\\
B&-A\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{l}-y^{\prime}\\
x^{\prime}\end{array}\right)

where

A=y^{2}\sin(t)^{2}-x(s)(x\sin(t)+\cos(t))^{2},\quad B=2\sin(t)y(x\sin(t)+\cos(t)).

Then a computation yields (
7
).

3.
Geodesic translating solutions

When we consider each one of the three types of one-parameter subgroups of isometries
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
defined in Sect.
2
and we apply them to a given point of the hyperbolic plane, the orbit of this point is not a geodesic in general. In fact, we obtain, respectively, equidistant lines or geodesics, horocycles and circles.

In this section we will employ a suitable notion of a translations in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
where points move along geodesics. Given a point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
and a tangent vector
V\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we define
the geodesic translation
of
p
in the direction
v
as a map
p\mapsto\eta(1;p,V)
, where
\eta(t)=\eta(t;p,V)
is the geodesic which at time
t=0
passes through
p
with speed
V
. We point out that geodesic translations are not isometries of the hyperbolic plane.

In order to compute explicit parametrizations of these geodesics, we relax the condition that
|V|=1
. Depending on
V
, the parametrizations of the geodesics
\eta
are the following. Suppose
p=(x,y)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
V=(v,w)
. Then we have the following parametrizations:

(1)

Case
V
is vertical. If
V=(0,w)
, then
\eta(t)=(x,ye^{wt})
.

(2)

Case
V
is horizontal. If
V=(v,0)
, then
\eta(t)=(x+y\tanh(vt),\frac{y}{\cosh(vt)})
.

(3)

General case of
V
. If
V=(v,w)
, then

\begin{split}\eta(t)&=(a+R\tanh(t+t_{0}),\frac{R}{\cosh(t+t_{0})}),\\
a&=x+\frac{w}{v}y,\,R=\frac{y\sqrt{v^{2}+w^{2}}}{v},\,t_{0}=\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{R}{y}\right).\end{split}

In what follows, we will compute all curves that evolve by geodesic translations in the hyperbolic plane. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
V=(0,1)
(vertical direction),
V=(1,0)
(horizontal direction),
V=(v,w)
,
v^{2}+w^{2}=1
(arbitrary direction).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a parametrized curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
that under CSF evolves by geodesic translations along
V=(0,1)
. Then
\gamma
is a horocycle, an equidistant line or a geodesic and the flow is given by
\gamma(t,s)=(as+c,(bs+d)e^{t})
,
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
and
a^{2}+b^{2}\not=0
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
evolves by geodesic translations along
V=(0,1),
its flow is given by

\gamma(t,s)=(x(s),y(s)e^{t})

.
By direct computation we get

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\gamma(t,s)&=(0,e^{t}y)\\
N(t,s)&=\frac{ye^{t}}{\sqrt{e^{2t}y^{\prime 2}+x^{\prime 2}}}\left(-e^{t}y^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\\
\kappa(t,s)&=\frac{e^{2t}\left(x^{\prime}\left(yy^{\prime\prime}+y^{\prime 2}\right)-yx^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}\right)+x^{\prime 3}}{\left(e^{2t}y^{\prime 2}+x^{\prime 2}\right)^{3/2}}\end{split}

After some computations, the CSF equation (
1
) is

x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}=0.

By (
3
), this implies that the Euclidean curvature
\kappa_{e}
is identically
0
, hence
\gamma
is a straight-line. Therefore
\gamma
is curve with constant curvature, that is, a horocycle, an equidistant line or a geodesic. In any case, the parametrization of
\gamma
is
\gamma(s)=s(a,b)+(c,d)
, where
a^{2}+b^{2}\not=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a parametrized curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
that under CSF evolves by geodesic translations along
V=(1,0)
. Then
\gamma
is an equidistant line of curvature
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and the flow is given by

(8)

\gamma(t,s)=\left(s+(m-s)\tanh(t),\frac{m-s}{\cosh(t)}\right),

where
m\in\mathbb{R}
is an integration constant. Furthermore,

(1)

the flow is defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where if
t\to\infty,
then
\gamma_{t}
approaches the geodesic
s\mapsto(m,s)

and if
t\to-\infty,
then
\gamma_{t}
approaches the ideal boundary
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
;

(2)

for fixed
t
, the curve
\gamma_{t}
is a equidistant line of curvature
\kappa=\tanh(t)
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
evolves by geodesic translations along
V=(1,0),
its flow is given by

\gamma(t,s)=(x(s)+y(s)\tanh(t),\frac{y(s)}{\cosh(t)}).

Then
\gamma_{t}
is the straight-line of equation
Y=m(X-x)
, where
m=1/\sinh(t)
. Thus its curvature is
1/\sqrt{1+m^{2}}=\tanh(t)
. By computing all the quantities in (
1
), we get

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\gamma(t,s)&=\text{sech}(t)\left(y\ \text{sech}(t),-y\tanh(t)\right)\\
N(t,s)&=\frac{y\text{sech}(t)}{\sqrt{2\tanh(t)x^{\prime}y^{\prime}+x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}}\left(-\text{sech}(t)y^{\prime},\tanh(t)y^{\prime}+x^{\prime}\right)\\
\kappa(t,s)&=\frac{1}{\left(2\tanh(t)x^{\prime}y^{\prime}+x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}\right)^{3/2}}\Big(-y\text{sech}^{2}(t)x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}+3\tanh(t)x^{\prime 2}y^{\prime}\\
&+x^{\prime}\left(y\text{sech}^{2}(t)y^{\prime\prime}+\left(3-2\text{sech}^{2}(t)\right)y^{\prime 2}\right)+\tanh(t)y^{\prime 3}+x^{\prime 3}\Big)\end{split}

Then (
1
) becomes

e^{2t}\left(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\right)^{3}+\left(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\right)^{2}\left(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\right)+2y\left(x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}\right)=0.

Looking this equation on
e^{2t}
, we deduce

\begin{split}0&=x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\\
0&=\left(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\right)^{2}\left(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\right)+2y\left(x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

From the first equation, we have
x(s)+y(s)=m,
for some constant
m
. Now, the second equation implies
x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}=0
, that is,
\gamma
is a straight-line, which we can parametrize by
y(s)=-s+m
. So, it follows that
\gamma
parametrizes an equidistant line of curvature
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and the flow is (
8
).
The properties (1) and (2) are immediate.

∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a parametrized curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
that under CSF evolves by geodesic translations along
V=(v,w)
,
v,w\not=0
. Then
\gamma
is an equidistant line of curvature
\kappa=\frac{v}{\sqrt{2(1-w)}}
and the flow is given by

(9)

\gamma(t,s)=\left(\frac{m\left(e^{2t}-1\right)v+2s}{-e^{2t}(w-1)+w+1},\frac{2e^{t}\left(m+\frac{s(w-1)}{v}\right)}{-e^{2t}(w-1)+w+1}\right),

where
m\in\mathbb{R}
is an integration constant. Furthermore,

(1)

the flow is defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where if
t\to\infty,
then
\gamma_{t}
approaches the geodesic
s\mapsto(m,s)

and if
t\to-\infty,
then
\gamma_{t}
approaches the ideal boundary
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
;

(2)

for fixed
t
, the solution
\gamma_{t}
parametrizes an equidistant line of curvature
\kappa=\frac{v}{\sqrt{v^{2}+e^{2t}(w-1)^{2}}}
.

Proof.

Geodesic translations in the direction
(v,w)
of
\gamma
have the form

\gamma(t,s)=(a+R\tanh(t-t_{0}),\frac{R}{\cosh(t-t_{0})}),

where

a=x(s)+\frac{w}{v}y(s),\quad R=y(s)\cosh(t_{0}),\quad t_{0}=\tanh^{-1}(w).

Then

\gamma(t,s)=\left(\frac{\left(e^{2t}-1\right)vy(s)}{-e^{2t}w+e^{2t}+w+1}+x(s),\frac{2e^{t}y(s)}{-e^{2t}w+e^{2t}+w+1}\right).

By computing all the quantities in (
1
), we get an expression of the type

A_{0}(s)+A_{1}(s)e^{2t}+A_{2}(s)e^{4t}=0,

where

\begin{split}A_{2}(s)&=\left((w-1)x^{\prime}-vy^{\prime}\right)^{3},\\
A_{1}(s)&=2\Big(x^{\prime}\left(\left(-3v^{2}w+v^{2}+2w-2\right)y^{\prime 2}+(w-1)yy^{\prime\prime}\right)+v\left(v^{2}-2\right)y^{\prime 3}\\
&+v\left(3w^{2}-2w-1\right)x^{\prime 2}y^{\prime}-(w-1)yx^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}-(w-1)^{2}(w+1)x^{\prime 3}\Big),\\
A_{0}(s)&=-v^{3}y^{\prime 3}+x^{\prime}\left(v^{2}(3w+1)y^{\prime 2}-2(w+1)yy^{\prime\prime}\right)\\
&+v\left(-3w^{2}-2w+1\right)x^{\prime 2}y^{\prime}+2(w+1)yx^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}+(w-1)(w+1)^{2}x^{\prime 3}.\end{split}

Therefore, all coefficients
A_{n}
must vanish identically. From
A_{2}=0
, we get

(10)

y^{\prime}(s)=\frac{w-1}{v}x^{\prime}(s).

If
w=1,
then
y
is a constant function and hence the initial curve
\gamma
parametrizes a horocycle, but this is impossible since horocycle evolve by geodesic translations along
(0,1).
Therefore,
w\neq 1.

By substituting the expression of
y
given in (
10
) in
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
, then
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
vanish trivially. Definitively, by solving (
10
), the curve
\gamma
is a straight-line. By putting
x(s)=s
, then

y(s)=\frac{w-1}{v}s+m,\quad m\in\mathbb{R}.

Since the slope of this Euclidean straight-line is
\frac{w-1}{v}
, then
\gamma
is an equidistant line of curvature
\frac{v}{\sqrt{2(1-w)}}
. By substituting
\gamma
in the expression of
\gamma(t,s)
, we obtain (
9
). Notice that the curve
\gamma_{t}
parametrizes a Euclidean straight-line of slope
\frac{e^{t}(w-1)}{v}
.

∎

4.
The angle as parameter in the flow

Besides solitons and translating solutions studied in the previous sections, another interesting class of solutions are convex ancient solutions. Those are the solutions of the CSF whose time interval is of the form
J=(-\infty,T_{0}).
In
[
9
]
, the authors have been able to classify compact convex ancient solutions in the Euclidean plane by introducing
the pressure function

p=\kappa^{2}
and then proving the following result:

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem
1.1
in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
p(t,\theta)=\kappa^{2}(t,\theta)
be the pressure function corresponding to a compact convex ancient solution of the curve shortening flow. Then
p
is of the form

p(t,\theta)=a(t)+b(\theta),

for some functions
a,b
.

It is of our interest to examine to what extent is this theorem true in the hyperbolic plane. In order to do so, first we need to see what parameter can fill the role of the turning angle
\theta
in the Euclidean plane and secondly, we will find all convex solutions whose pressure function can be written by separating the variables.

When considering convex curves in the Euclidean plane, one can use the turning angle
\theta
for the parametrization which it is highly advantageous because of the geometric importance of such parameter. Because
\theta
was considered as a parameter in
[
9
]
(see also
[
2
]
), and since the hyperolic metric in the upper-half plane model is conformal to the Euclidean one in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we will compute the evolution equations for the Euclidean angle
\theta
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}.
However, because the turning angle is closely related only to the Euclidean plane, we do not expect the situation to be the same in
\mathbb{H}^{2},
as was indicated already by Grayson in Sect. 2 of
[
13
]
. With this purpose, Grayson introduced a parameter
\varphi
which is defined purely analytically and which can be used instead of the turning angle for curves on arbitrary surfaces. Grayson defines globally an angle
\varphi=\varphi(t,s)
. First, the function
\varphi=\varphi(t,u)
is defined on the initial curve
\gamma(s)=\gamma(0,s)
by

\varphi(0,u)=\int_{0}^{s(u)}\kappa\,ds.

Along
\gamma(s)
, this function
\varphi(0,s)
coincides with the angle of
\gamma^{\prime}(s)
with a fixed direction of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Next, a correction factor
\rho
is defined by the equation
\rho(\partial\varphi/\partial s)=\kappa
. Finally, the angle function
\varphi
is defined by the equation

\rho\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial s}.

The next step consists of modifying the initial flow to preserve tangent directions. To this end, the parameter
t
is replaced by
\tau
, where

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}.

This allows us to obtain the next identity for the evolution of curvature when
\varphi
is fixed:

(11)

\kappa_{\tau}=\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\varphi\varphi}+\kappa^{3}-\kappa,

where we write the partial derivatives as subindices. See
[
13
, Lem. 2.7]
. We take into account that
\tau
and
\varphi
are independent variables because it was proved in
[
13
, Lem.2.1]
that

\partial\varphi/\partial\tau=0
. When using
\varphi
as a parameter, the evolution equation
11
is analogous to the one in the Euclidean plane (see
[
11
]
).

Define the following functions:

p=\kappa^{2},\quad q=p_{\varphi}.

Lemma 4.2
.

The evolution equations for
p
and
q
are

(12)

\left\{\begin{split}p_{\tau}&=pp_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{1}{2}p_{\varphi}^{2}+2p^{2}-2p\\
q_{\tau}&=p(q_{\varphi\varphi}+4q)-2q\end{split}\right.

Proof.

We have
p_{\varphi}=2\kappa\kappa_{\varphi}
, so
\kappa_{\varphi}=p_{\varphi}/(2\kappa)
. This implies

p_{\varphi\varphi}=\frac{p_{\varphi}^{2}}{2p}+2\kappa\kappa_{\varphi\varphi},

hence

\kappa_{\varphi\varphi}=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(p_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{p_{\varphi}^{2}}{2p}\right).

Then using (
11
), we have

p_{\tau}=2\kappa\kappa_{\tau}=2\kappa\left(\frac{p}{2\kappa}(p_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{p_{\varphi}^{2}}{2p})+\kappa^{3}-\kappa\right)=pp_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{1}{2}p_{\varphi}^{2}+2p^{2}-2p.

For the computation of
q_{\tau}
, we use that
\tau
and
\varphi
are independent variables. This implies

\begin{split}q_{\tau}&=(p_{\varphi})_{\tau}=(p_{\tau})_{\varphi}\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\left(pp_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{1}{2}p_{\varphi}^{2}+2p^{2}-2p\right)\\
&=pp_{\varphi\varphi\varphi}+4pp_{\varphi}-2p_{\varphi}=p(q_{\varphi\varphi}+4q)-2q.\end{split}

∎

From now on, we replace the notation
\tau
by
t
, such as Grayson suggests in
[
13
]
(see also
[
11
]
). However, we keep the variable
\varphi
because we will study later the angle
\theta
. Because the curvature determines the curve, it is sufficient to solve the evolution equation for
p
. In order to do so, we consider the solutions that satisfy
q_{t}=0
. Then
p_{\varphi t}=0
which implies that the function
p=p(t,\varphi)
is of the form
a(t)+b(\varphi)
for some functions
a=a(t)
and
b=b(\varphi)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in J\}
be a convex solution to the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
such that the corresponding pressure function
p
can be written as

p(t,\varphi)=a(t)+b(\varphi),

for some functions
a,b
. Then the solution is a family of shrinking circles, horocycles, equidistant lines or solitons.

Proof.

Equations (
12
) become

(13)

\left\{\begin{split}a^{\prime}&=(a+b)b^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}b^{\prime 2}+2(a+b)^{2}-2(a+b)\\
0&=(a+b)(b^{\prime\prime\prime}+4b^{\prime})-2b^{\prime},\end{split}\right.

where
′
denotes, in each case, the corresponding derivative with respect to the variable
t
or
\varphi
.
If the function
a(t)=a
is constant, then at any time
t
, the curves
\gamma(t,\cdot)
and
\gamma(0,\cdot)
have the same curvature function, namely
\kappa(t,\cdot)=\sqrt{a+b(\cdot)}=\kappa(0,\cdot)
.
Because two curves have the same curvature, there exists an isometry of the ambient space
\Phi(t)
such that
\gamma(t,\cdot)=\Phi(t)\gamma(0,\cdot)
. Since
t
is an arbitrary time, the solution evolves by isometries, that is,
\gamma
is a soliton.

Now assume that
a
is not a constant function. We distinguish two cases.

(1)

Assume
b(\varphi)
is a constant function. Without loss of generality, we can assume that this constant is
0
. Then
p(t,\varphi)=a(t)
. The second equation in (
13
) is trivial and the first one reduces to

a^{\prime}=2a^{2}-2a.

We now solve this equation. This equation can be written as

\frac{da}{a(a-1)}=2\,dt,

obtaining

a(t)=\frac{1}{1+Ce^{2t}},\quad C\in\mathbb{R}.

Since the function
a
is positive, we have three types of equations depending on the sign of the integration constant
C
:

(14)

a(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\frac{1}{1-Ce^{2t}},&t<-\frac{1}{2}\log(C),C>0\\
&1&t\in\mathbb{R}\\
&\frac{1}{1+Ce^{2t}},&t\in\mathbb{R},C>0.\end{array}\right.

(a)

Case
a(t)=\frac{1}{1-Ce^{2t}}
, where
t\in(-\infty,-\frac{1}{2}\log(C))
and
C>0
. Let
c=\frac{1}{2}\log(C)
. Then
C=e^{2c}
. Define

r(t)=\cosh^{-1}(e^{-(t+c)}).

Then we have

\kappa(t,\varphi)=\sqrt{p(t,\varphi)}=\sqrt{a(t)}=\frac{1}{\tanh(r(t))}.

This implies that the corresponding solution is a family of shrinking circles.

(b)

Case
a(t)=1
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\kappa=1
. Thus
\gamma(t,\cdot)
is a horocycle for all
t
.

(c)

Case
a(t)=\frac{1}{1+Ce^{2t}}
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
C>0
. Again, if
c=\frac{1}{2}\log(C)
, let

r(t)=\sinh^{-1}(e^{t+c}).

Then

\kappa(t,\varphi)=\sqrt{p(t,\varphi)}=\sqrt{a(t)}=\frac{1}{\cosh(r(t))}.

In consequence, the solution is a family of equidistant lines. Notice that
k<1
.

(2)

Assume
b
is not a constant function. The second equation of (
13
) is

(15)

(a+b)(b^{\prime\prime\prime}+4b^{\prime})-2b^{\prime}=0.

If
b^{\prime\prime\prime}+4b^{\prime}=0
identically, then (
15
) implies
b^{\prime}=0
. Thus
b
is a constant function, which it is a contradiction. Thus
b^{\prime\prime\prime}+4b^{\prime}\not=0
. From (
15
), we have

a(t)=\frac{2b^{\prime}(\varphi)}{b^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi)+4b^{\prime}(\varphi)}-b(\varphi).

The left-hand side of the equation above depends only on
t
while the right-hand side depends only on
\varphi
. In particular, the function
a
is constant. This contradiction proves that this case is not possible.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in J\}
be a closed convex solution to the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
such that the corresponding pressure function
p
can be written as

p(t,\varphi)=a(t)+b(\varphi),

for some functions
a,b
. Then the solution is a family of shrinking circles.

Proof.

According to the Thm.
4.3
, the cases of horocycles and equidistant lines cannot occur, as well as of solitons because there are no compact solitons.
∎

We finish this section with obtaining the evolution equation of the Euclidean angle
\theta
when
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is viewed in the upper half-plane model. If
\gamma=\gamma(t,s)
, let
\gamma(t,s)=(x(t,s),y(t,s))
. Define
\theta=\theta(t,s)
be the Euclidean angle given by

(16)

T(t,s)=y(t,s)(\cos\theta(t,s),\sin\theta(t,s)).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\gamma(t,s)
be a solution to the CSF (
1
). If
\theta
is the Euclidean angle defined in (
16
), then

(17)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}&=\kappa_{s}+\kappa\sin\theta,\\
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}&=\frac{\kappa-\cos\theta}{y}.\end{split}

Proof.

The Levi-Civitta connection of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
in the upper half-plane model is

\nabla_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{x}=\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{y}=-\frac{1}{y}\partial_{x},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{x}=-\frac{1}{y}\partial_{x},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{y}=-\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}.

We have

(18)

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\kappa N)+\kappa^{2}T=\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial s}N.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}&=\nabla_{\partial_{t}}(y\cos\theta\partial_{x}+y\sin\theta\partial_{y})\\
&=(y_{t}\cos\theta-y\theta_{t}\sin\theta)\partial_{x}+(y_{t}\sin\theta+y\theta_{t}\cos\theta)\partial_{y}\\
&+y\cos\theta\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{x}+y\sin\theta\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{y}.\end{split}

We know
\partial_{t}=\gamma^{\prime}(t)=x_{t}\partial_{x}+y_{t}\partial_{y}
. Thus

\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{x}=-\frac{y_{t}}{y}\partial_{x}+\frac{x_{t}}{y}\partial_{y}.

\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{y}=-\frac{x_{t}}{y}\partial_{x}-\frac{y_{t}}{y}\partial_{y}.

Therefore,

(19)

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=(y\theta_{t}+x_{t})\left(-\sin\theta\partial_{x}+\cos\theta\partial_{y}\right)=\frac{y\theta_{t}+x_{t}}{y}N=(\theta_{t}+\frac{x_{t}}{y})N.

On the other hand, from (
1
) we have

\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}=(x_{t},y_{t})=\kappa N=\kappa y(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta).

This gives

(20)

x_{t}=-\kappa y\sin\theta,\quad y_{t}=\kappa y\cos\theta.

Using the value of
x_{t}
and equating (
18
) and (
19
), we obtain the first equation of (
17
).

Finally, we know that
\theta_{s}=\kappa_{e}
is the Euclidean curvature of
\gamma
. Then the relation (
2
) becomes now

\kappa=y\kappa_{e}+\cos\theta=y\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}+\cos\theta,

hence we get the second equation of (
17
).
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\gamma(t,s)
be a solution to the CSF (
1
). If
\theta
is the Euclidean angle defined in (
16
), then

(21)

(y-1)\left(k_{ss}+k_{s}\sin\theta\right)=0.

In particular, if
\gamma
is not the horocycle
y=1
, we have

k_{ss}+k_{s}\sin\theta=0.

Proof.

We use the formula for the Lie bracket
[\partial_{t},\partial_{s}]=\kappa^{2}\partial_{s}
: see
[
13
]
. Thus
\theta_{ts}=\theta_{st}-\kappa^{2}\theta_{s}
. Using this in combination with (
17
) and (
20
), we have

\begin{split}\kappa_{ss}&=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}-\kappa_{s}\sin\theta-\cos\theta\kappa\theta_{s}\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}-\kappa^{2}\theta_{s}-\kappa_{s}\sin\theta-\cos\theta\kappa\theta_{s}\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\kappa-\cos\theta}{y}-\kappa^{2}\theta_{s}-\kappa_{s}\sin\theta-\cos\theta\kappa\theta_{s}\\
&=-\frac{y_{t}}{y^{2}}(\kappa-\cos\theta)-\frac{\kappa_{t}+\sin\theta\kappa_{t}}{y}-\kappa(\kappa+\cos\theta)\theta_{s}-\sin\theta\kappa_{s}\\
&=\frac{1}{y}\left(-\kappa^{3}+\kappa+\kappa_{t}+(1-y)\sin\theta\kappa_{s}\right).\end{split}

This implies

y\kappa_{ss}+\kappa^{3}-\kappa=\kappa_{t}+(1-y)\sinh\theta\kappa_{s}.

To finish the proof, we use in this identity the expression of
\kappa_{t}
given by

(22)

\kappa_{t}=\kappa_{ss}+\kappa^{3}-\kappa.

See
[
13
, Lem. 1.3]
. This yields (
21
).
∎

Thanks to this theorem, we obtain a similar result that Thm.
4.3
for the Euclidean angle
\theta
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in J\}
be a convex solution to the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
such that
\kappa(t,s)=a(t)+b(s)
for some functions
a
and
b
. Then the solution is a family of shrinking circles, horocycles, equidistant lines or solitons.

Proof.

Suppose that
\gamma
is not a horocycle. Then (
21
) implies

b^{\prime\prime}+b^{\prime}\sin\theta=0
. We separate two cases.

(1)

Case
b^{\prime}=0
. Let
b(s)=b_{0}
. This implies that
\kappa
depends only on
t
. Then (
22
) is

a^{\prime}=(a+b_{0})^{3}-(a+b_{0})
. Renaming the function
a(t)
as
a(t)+b_{0}
, the solution of this equation is

a(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Ce^{2t}}},\quad C\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
\kappa(t)
coincides as (
14
), obtaining that the solution is a family of shrinking circles, horocycles or equidistant lines.

(2)

Case
b^{\prime}\not=0
. From
b^{\prime\prime}+b^{\prime}\sin\theta=0
we deduce that
\theta
depends only on
s
. Then the first equation of (
17
) implies
b^{\prime}+(a+b)\sin\theta=0
. Since
b^{\prime}\not=0
, then
\sin\theta\not=0
, hence
a
is a constant function. Thus
\gamma
is a soliton of the CSF.

∎

5.
An estimate of the area for ancient solutions of CSF

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in\langle-\infty,T_{0}\rangle\}
be an ancient solution to the CSF (
1
). If
T_{0}<\infty
, then we can rescale the flow so that
T_{0}=0
. Therefore, without the loss of generality, we can assume that either
T_{0}=0
or
T_{0}=\infty
. Ancient convex solutions to the CSF have been classified both in the Euclidean plane and on the sphere
[
2
,
4
]
.So far, the only explicit ancient convex solutions in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are contracting circles, geodesics, equidistant lines and horocycles.

In the case of simply closed solutions, we obtain the following computation of the area
A(t)
along the evolution, which are motived by the results in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in J\}
be an ancient simply closed solution to the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Then
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to-\infty}A(t)=+\infty
, where
A(t)
denotes the area enclosed by
\gamma(t,\cdot)
.

Proof.

The Gauss-Bonnet theorem in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
says
\int_{\gamma_{t}}\kappa(t,\cdot)\,ds=2\pi+A(t)
. We differentiate with respect to
t
. Here we use

\frac{d}{dt}ds=-\kappa^{2}ds.

See
[
13
, p. 75]
. By using this identity together (
22
), we have

\begin{split}\frac{dA}{dt}&=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\gamma_{t}}\kappa(t,\cdot)\,ds=\int_{\gamma_{t}}(\kappa_{ss}+\kappa^{3}-\kappa)(t,\cdot)\,ds-\int_{\gamma_{t}}\kappa(\kappa^{2})(t,\cdot)\,ds\\
&=-\int_{\gamma_{t}}\kappa(t,\cdot)\,ds=-2\pi-A(t).\end{split}

The solution of this equation is
A(t)=ce^{-t}-2\pi
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
. At
t=0
, we have
A_{0}:=A(0)=c-2\pi
. Thus
c=A_{0}+2\pi
, obtaining

A(t)=(A_{0}+2\pi)e^{-t}-2\pi.

If we let
t\to-\infty
, we get the claim.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon t\in(-\infty,T_{0})\}
be an ancient simply closed solution to the CSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Then
T_{0}=0
.

Proof.

From the proof of the Thm.
5.1
we know that the area enclosed at the time
t
is given by
A(t)=(A_{0}+2\pi)e^{-t}-2\pi
. If
T_{0}=\infty
, letting
t\to\infty
we would get
A(t)\to-2\pi
but that is impossible since the area must be non-negative. Therefore, it must be that
T_{0}=0
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In
[
13
]
, it was proved that if the maximal time
T_{0}
is finite, then the curve collapses to a point. In consequence
A_{0}=0
and therefore the area enclosed at time
t
is equal to

(23)

A(t)=2\pi e^{-t}-2\pi=2\pi(e^{-t}-1).
