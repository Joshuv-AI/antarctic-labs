Simulated EFAST module enhances applied abdominal anatomy learning in first-year medical students
Authors: Aby S. Charles, John Kandam Kulathu Mathew, Sandeep Nathanael David, Deepak Vinod Francis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe integration of ultrasonography (USG) into undergraduate anatomy education is increasingly recognised for its ability to enhance clinical relevance and spatial understanding. Contemporary evidence supports the use of USG-based teaching approaches to strengthen anatomical comprehension and facilitate the early acquisition of clinical competencies. However, there remains limited evidence regarding its effectiveness in imparting both anatomical knowledge and technical proficiency among first-year medical students.MethodsIn this prospective educational study, two cohorts of first-year medical students (n = 91; n = 97) participated in an Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (EFAST) intervention simulating a road traffic accident under an emergency physician’s supervision. Pre- and post-test assessments measured knowledge in ultrasound application, clinical reasoning, and anatomy. Focus group discussions evaluated students’ perceptions and highlighted barriers. A paired-samples t test was performed to compare scores between the pre- and post-test assessments, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as percentages, as appropriate. Considering each cohort individually, the pre- and post-test scores were analysed using a two-way ANOVA test, normalised and presented as percentages.ResultsThe simulated EFAST module demonstrated a significant improvement in post-test scores following the intervention (31.14% increase, p < 0.001). Cohort-based analysis revealed over 41% (p < 0.001) improvement in cohort I and over 21% (p < 0.001) improvement in cohort II post-intervention. Subgroup analysis revealed specific gains in ultrasonography-related questions (44.06%, p < 0.001), clinical scenario questions (48.16%, p < 0.001), and anatomical questions (8.73%, p < 0.001). Qualitative feedback indicated positive acceptance and perceived educational value of ultrasound-integrated experiential learning.ConclusionEFAST-based teaching substantially enhances knowledge and engagement with clinically relevant anatomy and ultrasound in emergency scenarios. Incorporating point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) into preclinical curricula effectively bridges foundational science with clinical practice and fosters preparedness for acute care settings. Background

A sound understanding of human anatomy is foundational to the training and practice of medical professionals [1, 2]. In addition to facilitating the comprehension of normal structure, anatomical education enables students to appreciate how morphological variations underpin pathological processes and influence organ function. This knowledge is crucial for grasping and understanding the etiopathogenesis of diseases and forms an essential prerequisite for safe and effective clinical practice [3, 4].

Contemporary advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic modalities have necessitated the integration of novel pedagogical approaches within medical curricula [5, 6]. There is a growing imperative to align anatomical teaching with technologies prevalent in clinical care, such as medical imaging, to prepare graduates for the complexities of modern medicine [1]. Radiological modalities, particularly USG, generate distinct anatomical visuals that have been underutilised in traditional anatomy instruction [7–9]. The rising prominence of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in emergency and primary care settings underscores the need for proficiency in interpreting sonographic anatomy from the early stages in medical training [2, 5, 10, 11].

The extended focused assessment with sonography for trauma (EFAST) protocol represents a key application of ultrasound and is utilised for the rapid identification of intraperitoneal and intrathoracic pathology in acute care scenarios [12, 13]. Early and accurate detection of such findings is vital for timely medical intervention, especially in resource-limited settings where initial management and referral decisions are critical to patient outcomes [6, 12, 13]. Nevertheless, interpretation of sonoanatomy remains challenging because of its dynamic and operator-dependent nature, necessitating structured educational exposure during the preclinical years [8, 14–16].

Despite the potential benefits, the integration of ultrasound into anatomy education remains variable, and evidence regarding its effectiveness for imparting living anatomy and associated clinical skills warrants further investigation [2, 4]. This study aims to assess the impact of an anatomy-driven, EFAST-centred ultrasound-based teaching module on first-year medical students’ ability to learn and apply clinically pertinent anatomical knowledge.

Methods

This study employed a prospective, mixed-methods design involving two consecutive cohorts of first-year medical students (academic years 2020–2022). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to their participation in the study. The participants underwent a structured educational module centred on the EFAST protocol. The module began with the administration of a pretest consisting of 15 validated questions organised into three domains: basic anatomy (3 questions), fundamentals of the USG (6 questions), and clinical applications (6 questions). This established baseline knowledge for each participant. Content validity was assessed qualitatively by senior faculty serving as course administrators for abdominal and thoracic anatomy, who reviewed each item for relevance and alignment with the stated course objectives. A live EFAST demonstration was conducted on a simulated patient (actor) by an experienced emergency medicine practitioner. This session emphasised real-time procedural dynamics inherent in emergency care and was immediately followed by a stepwise instructional segment. The practitioner explained and demonstrated probe handling, anatomical landmarks, image interpretation, and recognition of sonographic features indicative of pathology. To enhance comprehension, normal and pathological ultrasound images were shown in parallel, highlighting diagnostic differences. An interactive question-and-answer session ended the instructional period, allowing students to clarify concepts and resolve doubts. Due to time constraints in the first-year curriculum, the module was delivered as a one-hour demonstration followed by a question-and-answer session. At the end of the module, students completed a post-test that matched the pretest in structure and content. Quantitative data were analysed using paired t-tests to assess changes in domain-specific and overall test scores from pre- to post-intervention.

To explore participants’ perceptions and experiences, all students were invited to complete an open-ended feedback questionnaire after the module (Supplementary file 1). This questionnaire was developed specifically for the study and has not been published elsewhere. Written responses underwent thematic analysis to identify prevailing themes, areas of strength, and suggestions for improvement. Additionally, focus group discussions (FGDs) were held for each cohort; student participants were selected via systematic sampling to minimise bias. All FGDs were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and coded for recurring and emergent themes to corroborate the quantitative findings and extract novel insights.

Statistical analysis comparing pre- and post-test scores was performed via paired t-tests. Considering each cohort separately, the pre- and post-test scores were analysed via two-way ANOVA. Domain-specific analysis for the pre- and post-tests was performed via a paired t test. All the data were normalised and are presented as percentages. Perplexity AI was employed to refine the manuscript’s language and ensure grammatical accuracy.

Results

Quantitative analysis of participant performance on the basis of outcomes

Of the 199 students who consented, 187 (94%) completed the simulated EFAST abdominal anatomy module. Completion rates were high across two consecutive cohorts: Cohort I, 91 of 99 (91.9%); Cohort II, 96 of 100 (96%). Analysis of the pre- and post-test scores revealed a statistically significant improvement in knowledge acquisition across each cohort (Fig. 2b), as did the combined group (Fig. 2a). Paired t test comparisons demonstrated highly significant gains in overall scores (Fig. 2a) (p < 0.001 for all comparisons), and cohort-specific gains in scores (Fig. 2b).

Qualitative evaluation of the simulated EFAST module demonstration session

Student feedback was predominantly positive, with most respondents reporting increased interest in the module and greater appreciation for emergency department workflows. Many students indicated that the module enhanced their understanding of ultrasound principles, improved their ability to interpret anatomical structures in a clinical context, and deepened their grasp of peritoneal anatomy. The live demonstration and practical orientation of the module were particularly valuable for elucidating the clinical relevance of anatomical knowledge. Negative feedback was limited, primarily concerning the challenges of conducting the module in a large group setting. Some students reported difficulty correlating probe orientation with the ultrasound images displayed, especially those seated further from the demonstration area. Suggestions for improvement included increasing the frequency of such sessions, enhancing student participation, and improving the visualisation of probe placement and orientation (Fig. 3).

Focus group discussions conducted with both cohorts reinforced the positive reception of the module. The students appreciated the innovative approach, noting that the role-play scenario facilitated engagement and contextualised the learning experience within real-life emergency department practice. Recommendations include the integration of anatomical diagrams alongside ultrasound images to aid spatial orientation and comprehension. The students also expressed that the module fostered a renewed interest in anatomy, recognising its relevance to future clinical practice.

Discussion

Anatomy constitutes a fundamental component of medical education, underpinning the understanding of structure‒function relationships essential for clinical practice [17]. The paradigm of anatomy teaching has shifted from conventional dissection and structural memorisation to methods emphasising practical and clinical relevance [1, 11, 18]. Ultrasound-based learning provides students with a unique perspective, facilitating the exploration of living anatomy and enhancing diagnostic reasoning, which is necessary with the increasing implementation of POCUS at the bedside [1, 11, 17, 19, 20]. This study provides early exposure to USG aimed at fostering the development of clinically relevant competencies.

Evidence has demonstrated that integrating ultrasound training into preclinical curricula improves anatomical knowledge and clinical skills [21, 22]. However, ultrasound is highly operator dependent, and inadequate supervision may result in misinterpretations or missed diagnoses [7, 16, 23]. Structured, guided instruction, including faculty development, is crucial for maximising educational value and patient safety, although personnel and resource constraints can hinder broad implementation [16, 23]. Additionally, regulatory considerations concerning ultrasound use persist in some regions, underscoring the importance of early sensitisation to the ethical and legal contexts of imaging [5, 16, 20–23]. Introducing USG at this stage (first-year medical student) enhances learners’ foundational understanding and promotes the integration of practical imaging skills with clinical decision-making early in their medical education. This postulate has been supported by the obtained results. The observed difference in pretest scores between cohorts (48% vs. 70%) likely reflects curricular changes in point‑of‑care ultrasound (POCUS) instruction. During Cohort I, ultrasound teaching was in an initial, formative phase, and learners received only an introductory exposure to basic USG concepts. By Cohort II, POCUS had been formally integrated into the first‑year curriculum, and learners had received a more comprehensive basic USG training. These curricular differences plausibly explain the greater baseline knowledge and the smaller domain‑specific score gains observed in Cohort II.

Student feedback in this and comparable studies highlights the positive impact of experiential and clinically anchored modules, which effectively increase engagement and contextualise anatomical knowledge for future clinical scenarios [24]. Suggestions such as small-group sessions and hands-on practice reflect international consensus on best practices for ultrasound education [19, 24]. The simulated EFAST module fostered active learning and interest, addressing limitations in cadaveric instruction and improving the understanding of clinically relevant spaces, notably using simulated patients. Thematic analysis of the feedback elucidated both the strengths and limitations of the simulated EFAST module, as well as opportunities for further refinement of this educational intervention early in the medical curriculum.

Globally, integrated ultrasound curricula are advancing, and the majority of literature and educational initiatives focus primarily on medical students in their second year or later [1, 3, 25]. This study adds to emerging evidence that early ultrasound exposure (simulated EFAST) is feasible and effective, even with limited faculty resources, and encouragingly fosters confidence and competence in POCUS during clinical practice.

The principal challenge was integrating the module into an already compact, competency‑driven curriculum that lacked ultrasound anatomy. To accommodate the innovation, we reallocated time originally allotted to a clinician’s lecture, enabling students to attend a clinically relevant simulated USG session.

Conclusion

This study establishes a novel, pragmatic approach for integrating the EFAST protocol into the preclinical curriculum, demonstrating that core ultrasound skills can be effectively developed alongside foundational anatomy in first-year medical students. By using a simulated emergency context and live demonstration, the module bridged textbook knowledge and practical sonographic competence, fostering significant gains in engagement and applied learning. These results highlight the feasibility and value of early POCUS exposure and support wider adoption of integrated ultrasound education to better prepare students for contemporary clinical practice.
