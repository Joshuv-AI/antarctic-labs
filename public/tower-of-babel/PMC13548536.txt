Prognostic value of the systemic immune-inflammation index in acute non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding: a retrospective single-center cohort study
Authors: Rumeysa İnci, Nurhayat Başkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding (NVUGIB) is a life-threatening emergency associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Current risk stratification tools have limitations in predicting short-term outcomes. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), a composite biomarker integrating platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, has shown prognostic value across various acute conditions; however, its utility in NVUGIB remains unexplored.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center cohort study, 812 adults presenting with NVUGIB to the emergency department of Kartal Dr. Lütfi Kırdar City Hospital (Istanbul, Turkey) were analyzed. SII was calculated as (platelet count × neutrophil count) / lymphocyte count. Primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality; secondary outcome was 72-hour rebleeding. Discriminative performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and compared with established risk scores (Glasgow-Blatchford Score, pre-endoscopic Rockall Score, complete Rockall Score, and AIMS65). Independent predictors were identified using multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards analyses.ResultsThe 28-day mortality rate was 6.5% (n = 53) and the 72-hour rebleeding rate was 11.1% (n = 90). Median SII was significantly higher in non-survivors compared to survivors [1,714.9 (IQR: 980.5–3,776.0) vs. 910.5 (IQR: 599.3–1,501.0); p < 0.001]. For 28-day mortality, SII achieved an AUC of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.622–0.785), comparable to established scoring systems (all p > 0.05 by DeLong test). Notably, SII demonstrated superior discriminative performance for 72-hour rebleeding (AUC = 0.767; 95% CI: 0.714–0.820), significantly outperforming GBS (AUC = 0.540), AIMS65 (AUC = 0.541), pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (AUC = 0.561), and complete Rockall Score (AUC = 0.554). In multivariable logistic regression, SII (per 100 units; OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03; p < 0.001) was an independent predictor of mortality. Decision curve analysis demonstrated that adding SII to existing scores improved net clinical benefit.ConclusionsSII is an independent prognostic marker in NVUGIB, offering discriminative performance for 28-day mortality comparable to established risk scores and superior performance for 72-hour rebleeding prediction. Introduction

Acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) is one of the most frequent gastroenterological emergencies worldwide, with an estimated incidence of 48–160 cases per 100,000 persons annually and a hospital mortality rate ranging from 2% to 15% [1, 2]. Non-variceal causes account for the vast majority of UGIB presentations, with peptic ulcer disease being the predominant etiology, followed by erosive gastritis, Mallory-Weiss tears, and malignancy-related bleeding [3]. Despite advances in endoscopic hemostasis, proton pump inhibitor therapy, and critical care management, mortality and rebleeding rates have remained relatively unchanged over the past decade, underscoring the persistent clinical challenge of NVUGIB in emergency settings [4, 5].

Current risk stratification in NVUGIB relies primarily on clinical scoring systems, including the Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), the Rockall Score, and the AIMS65 Score [6, 7]. While these tools have been validated across diverse populations, each carries inherent limitations: the GBS does not incorporate endoscopic findings and may overestimate the need for intervention, the Rockall Score requires post-endoscopic data for complete computation, and the AIMS65, though computationally simpler, demonstrates variable performance across different healthcare settings [8–10]. Crucially, none of these scores captures the dynamic inflammatory response that characterizes acute hemorrhage and modulates short-term outcomes. There remains an unmet clinical need for objective, laboratory-based biomarkers that can augment existing risk stratification tools at the point of first contact in the emergency department, before endoscopic evaluation is performed.

The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), first proposed by Hu and colleagues as a prognostic marker for hepatocellular carcinoma, is calculated as (platelet count × neutrophil count) / lymphocyte count and reflects the complex interplay among inflammatory activation, immune suppression, and platelet-mediated hemostasis [11]. Unlike single-component indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, SII integrates three distinct immune-cell populations, potentially capturing a more comprehensive picture of the host inflammatory response [11]. Several studies have recently demonstrated the prognostic utility of SII in critically ill patients and emergency conditions, including spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and appendicitis [12, 13]. While individual immune-inflammatory indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been explored in UGIB settings, existing studies are largely focused on a single outcome (typically in-hospital mortality) and do not include systematic head-to-head comparisons with all four established risk scores (GBS, pre-endoscopic Rockall, complete Rockall, and AIMS65) for both mortality and rebleeding endpoints. In the context of UGIB, systemic inflammation plays a pivotal role in determining bleeding severity, mucosal repair, and susceptibility to rebleeding; yet, the prognostic value of the composite SII — which integrates all three immune-cell axes simultaneously — in NVUGIB remains inadequately characterized. We therefore conducted this retrospective cohort study to assess the discriminative performance and independent prognostic value of SII for 28-day all-cause mortality and 72-hour rebleeding in patients presenting with acute NVUGIB to the emergency department, with systematic comparison against all four established clinical risk scores.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective single-center cohort study was conducted at the Emergency Department of Kartal Dr. Lütfi Kırdar City Hospital, a tertiary-care referral center in Istanbul, Turkey, serving a catchment population of approximately 1.5 million. The study was approved by the Institutional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2026/010.99/26136; Date: 01.04.2026) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design and de-identified nature of the data.

Participants

All consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting to the emergency department with a primary diagnosis of acute NVUGIB between January 2022 and December 2024 were screened for inclusion. NVUGIB was defined as clinically evident upper gastrointestinal bleeding (hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, or melena) confirmed by endoscopy, with the bleeding source localized proximal to the ligament of Treitz and attributable to a non-variceal etiology. Patients were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for gastrointestinal hemorrhage (K92.0, K92.1, K92.2, K25, K26, K27, K22.6, K31.81). Exclusion criteria were: (1) known or endoscopically confirmed variceal bleeding; (2) age < 18 years; (3) absence of a complete blood count at admission; (4) missing outcome data; (5) in-hospital onset of bleeding; and (6) transfer from another facility with prior resuscitation. Of 1,047 patients screened, 235 were excluded for the following reasons: confirmed variceal bleeding (n = 41) or missing admission complete blood count (n = 48); age younger than 18 years (n = 12) or in-hospital onset of bleeding (n = 42); transfer from another facility with prior resuscitation (n = 61); and missing 28-day outcome data (n = 31). The remaining 812 patients met all inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the final analysis.

Data collection

Demographic variables, medical history, medication use (NSAIDs, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, proton pump inhibitors), presenting symptoms (hematemesis, melena, hematochezia), and vital signs at triage (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, Glasgow Coma Scale) were extracted from electronic medical records. Hemorrhagic shock was defined as systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or heart rate > 100 bpm with evidence of end-organ hypoperfusion. Complete blood count, coagulation parameters (INR, prothrombin time, aPTT), serum biochemistry (BUN, creatinine, albumin, AST, ALT, total bilirubin, CRP, lactate), and glucose were recorded from the first blood draw obtained at emergency department presentation, before any resuscitative intervention.

SII calculation and risk scoring

SII was calculated using admission laboratory values with the formula: SII = (platelet count [×10³/µL] × neutrophil count [×10³/µL]) / lymphocyte count [×10³/µL] [11]. The Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), pre-endoscopic Rockall Score, complete Rockall Score (incorporating endoscopic findings), and AIMS65 Score were calculated for each patient by two independent investigators blinded to outcome status. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus review.

Endoscopic evaluation and management

Urgent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed within 24 h of presentation in all hemodynamically stable patients and as soon as hemodynamic stability was achieved in those with hemorrhagic shock, in accordance with institutional protocols and current European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines. Endoscopic findings were classified according to the Forrest system for peptic ulcer bleeding and by anatomical location for other etiologies. Endoscopic hemostatic intervention (injection therapy, thermal coagulation, mechanical clipping, or combination) was applied at the discretion of the attending endoscopist. Post-procedural management including proton pump inhibitor infusion, blood transfusion triggers (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL or < 9 g/dL in patients with cardiovascular comorbidity), and ICU admission criteria followed standardized institutional protocols.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, ascertained from electronic medical records and, where applicable, national death registry linkage. The secondary outcome was 72-hour rebleeding, defined as recurrence of overt bleeding signs (hematemesis, melena, or significant hemodynamic deterioration with a hemoglobin decrease ≥ 2 g/dL within 72 h of index endoscopy without an alternative explanation) with endoscopic confirmation of active hemorrhage or the need for repeat hemostatic intervention within 72 h of the index endoscopy. All patients with clinical suspicion of rebleeding underwent repeat upper gastrointestinal endoscopy as per institutional protocol, and rebleeding was endoscopically confirmed in all 90 cases classified as such. The decision to perform repeat endoscopy was guided by standardized institutional criteria applied uniformly across the study period. Patients who died within 72 h of the index endoscopy (n = 7) were classified as non-rebleeding events in the primary analysis, as endoscopic confirmation could not be obtained before death; this approach is noted as a limitation and addressed by a sensitivity analysis in the Results.

Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test in conjunction with visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD); non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range [IQR]); categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test (with Monte Carlo simulation where applicable) for categorical variables.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative performance of SII and established clinical risk scores for 28-day mortality and 72-hour rebleeding. Area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated by the DeLong method; pairwise AUC comparisons between SII and clinical scores were performed using the DeLong test. The optimal SII cut-off was determined using the Youden index (maximizing sensitivity + specificity − 1).

Univariable binary logistic regression was performed for each candidate predictor. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis, together with those with established clinical relevance, were entered into multivariable logistic regression models for 28-day mortality and 72-hour rebleeding separately. The mortality model included 10 candidate predictors with 53 events, yielding an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 5.3, which is below the conventionally recommended threshold of 10; accordingly, the multivariable mortality model should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests were performed to assess calibration of the final logistic regression models. Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were < 2.0. SII was expressed per 100 units to yield clinically interpretable odds ratios (ORs). Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for 28-day survival, adjusting for the same covariates; proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (global p = 0.053). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed and compared using the log-rank test.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the net clinical benefit of adding SII to existing risk scores (AIMS65 and GBS) across a range of threshold probabilities for 28-day mortality. The dose-response relationship between SII and mortality probability was explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with 4 knots, adjusting for age, hemoglobin, and AIMS65. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout. Analyses were performed using the tidyverse, pROC, survival, survminer, gtsummary, rms, dcurves, and broom packages in R.

Results

A total of 812 patients with confirmed acute NVUGIB were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 62.7 ± 16.5 years, and 65.4% were male. The most common etiology was peptic ulcer (53.0%), followed by erosive gastritis/esophagitis (22.9%), Mallory-Weiss tear (9.0%), malignancy-related bleeding (7.3%), angiodysplasia (3.7%), and other causes (4.2%). Hemorrhagic shock was present at triage in 19.5% of patients. Comorbidity burden was substantial: 53.0% had hypertension, 30.4% had diabetes mellitus, 15.4% had chronic kidney disease, and 35.8% had coronary artery disease. NSAID use was documented in 36.6% and anticoagulant use in 17.4% of patients. Detailed baseline characteristics, vital signs, and laboratory parameters are presented in Table 1.

The median SII for the entire cohort was 938.4 (IQR: 604.2–1,576.5). Non-survivors had significantly higher SII values compared to survivors [1,714.9 (IQR: 980.5–3,776.0) vs. 910.5 (IQR: 599.3–1,501.0); p < 0.001]. Similarly, patients experiencing 72-hour rebleeding had markedly higher SII compared to those without rebleeding [1,946 (IQR: 1,121–3,947) vs. 882 (IQR: 590–1,383); p < 0.001]. Based on the Youden index-derived exploratory cut-off of 1,233 (derived and evaluated within the same cohort; sensitivity and specificity values should therefore be interpreted as optimistic estimates), patients were stratified into SII Low (n = 533, 65.6%) and SII High (n = 279, 34.4%) groups. The SII High group demonstrated significantly higher rates of 28-day mortality (12.9% vs. 3.2%; p < 0.001), 72-hour rebleeding (23.7% vs. 4.5%; p < 0.001), and longer hospital length of stay [8.0 (IQR: 5.0–11.0) vs. 7.0 (IQR: 4.0–10.0) days; p < 0.001].

For 28-day mortality prediction, SII achieved an AUC of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.622–0.785), with an exploratory cut-off of 1,233 yielding sensitivity 67.9%, specificity 68.0%, PPV 12.9%, and NPV 96.8%. This performance was statistically comparable to the GBS (AUC = 0.705; p = 0.986), pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (AUC = 0.718; p = 0.746), and AIMS65 Score (AUC = 0.723; p = 0.746) by DeLong pairwise testing (all p > 0.05). The noted PPV values are modest, reflecting the low event rate (6.5% mortality); the high NPV (96.8%) may be more actionable clinically, as a low SII score identifies patients at very low short-term mortality risk. Adding SII to AIMS65 increased the AUC for 28-day mortality from 0.723 to 0.741 (DeLong p = 0.12). For 72-hour rebleeding prediction, SII demonstrated superior discriminative performance (AUC = 0.767; 95% CI: 0.714–0.820), significantly outperforming all established scoring systems: GBS (AUC = 0.540), AIMS65 (AUC = 0.541), pre-endoscopic Rockall Score (AUC = 0.561), and complete Rockall Score (AUC = 0.554); adding SII to AIMS65 increased the rebleeding AUC from 0.541 to 0.771 (DeLong p < 0.001). In the peptic ulcer subgroup (n = 430, 53.0% of cohort), SII AUC for 72-hour rebleeding was 0.751 (95% CI: 0.688–0.814) and for 28-day mortality was 0.718 (95% CI: 0.621–0.815), consistent with the overall cohort findings. A sensitivity analysis censoring the seven patients who died within 72 h of index endoscopy yielded a rebleeding AUC of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.711–0.817), essentially unchanged from the primary analysis. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit testing indicated adequate calibration for both the mortality model (p = 0.38) and the rebleeding model (p = 0.41). All pairwise DeLong test p-values are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The full ROC comparison is presented in Table 3, and ROC curves are illustrated in Fig. 1.

DCA demonstrated that the model incorporating SII in addition to AIMS65 and GBS yielded consistently higher net clinical benefit compared to AIMS65 + GBS alone across all clinically relevant threshold probabilities (0–35%), supporting the potential incremental utility of SII beyond established scoring systems in a modeling context. The DCA was designed to inform the decision of whether to initiate high-intensity monitoring (ICU-level observation, early repeat endoscopy, or intensified nursing surveillance) versus standard ward care. The threshold probability range of 0–35% was selected to encompass the full range of clinically plausible treatment thresholds, consistent with the observed event rates (6.5% mortality, 11.1% rebleeding) and DCA methodology recommendations. It is important to note that DCA demonstrates potential for improved net benefit in a modeling context and does not constitute prospective implementation evidence; whether this translates into clinically meaningful changes in patient management requires prospective evaluation. RCS regression revealed a progressive, monotonically increasing relationship between SII and predicted 28-day mortality, with acceleration beyond the exploratory cut-off of 1,233, suggesting a dose-response pattern that warrants confirmation in prospective cohorts (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that SII, a simple composite laboratory index derived from routine complete blood count parameters, serves as an independent prognostic marker in patients presenting with acute NVUGIB. Unlike single-component indices such as NLR or PLR, which have been examined in UGIB settings with variable results, SII integrates all three immune-cell axes simultaneously and was compared here against all four established clinical risk scores for both mortality and rebleeding — a methodological scope not consistently addressed in prior work. SII achieved discriminative performance for 28-day all-cause mortality statistically comparable to established risk stratification tools — the GBS, Rockall Score, and AIMS65 — while demonstrating markedly superior performance for 72-hour rebleeding prediction, outperforming all clinical scores in this clinically critical outcome domain. These findings suggest that SII may complement existing risk stratification frameworks, particularly for early identification of patients at heightened rebleeding risk, though prospective validation is required before clinical adoption. The biological rationale for the prognostic role of SII in UGIB is compelling. Acute hemorrhage triggers a systemic inflammatory cascade characterized by neutrophil activation and mobilization, platelet aggregation at sites of vascular injury, and compensatory lymphocyte depletion as a marker of immune exhaustion [14, 15]. SII captures all three of these interacting processes simultaneously: elevated neutrophil counts reflect the acute-phase inflammatory response to tissue hypoperfusion; platelet count incorporates the hemostatic burden and thrombocytopenic risk of massive hemorrhage; and lymphopenia — the denominator component of SII — signals immunosuppression and correlates with disease severity across a range of critical illness states [16]. Prior work by Xia et al. in a large population-based cohort demonstrated that elevated SII was independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, reinforcing the biological credibility of SII as a systemic risk indicator [12]. In the specific context of emergency medicine, SII has been shown to predict outcomes in critically ill patients and across diverse acute presentations [18–21]. The superior performance of SII specifically for rebleeding prediction may reflect that existing clinical scores capture hemodynamic and anatomical severity at a single time point, whereas SII incorporates the host’s systemic inflammatory-immune status, which may more directly reflect the biological conditions governing clot stability and mucosal repair. Specifically, the combination of thrombocytopenia and neutrophilia at admission may jointly indicate impaired clot durability and persistent mucosal inflammation — two biologically plausible drivers of early rebleeding that are not captured by GBS, Rockall, or AIMS65. It is important to acknowledge, however, that SII elevation is not specific to gastrointestinal bleeding; it may also reflect age-related immune senescence, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, infection, chronic inflammatory states, steroid or immunosuppressant use, and acute physiological stress independent of hemorrhage. While multivariable adjustment in the present study partially accounts for age, chronic kidney disease, and malignancy, other unmeasured confounders may have independently influenced SII values. The mechanistic link between SII and rebleeding therefore remains a hypothesis requiring prospective validation.

The comparative performance of SII against established scoring systems in our study merits contextual discussion. For 28-day mortality, SII demonstrated an AUC of 0.704, statistically indistinguishable from AIMS65 (0.723), pre-endoscopic Rockall (0.718), and GBS (0.705) by DeLong testing, consistent with the broader literature indicating AUC values of 0.70–0.85 for these scores across NVUGIB populations [9, 15, 17]. A retrospective study by Ak and Hökenek reported an AUC of 0.706 for AIMS65 and 0.542 for GBS for mortality — strikingly similar to our findings and supporting the local external validity of these benchmarks [10]. Importantly, while the AUC values show that SII discriminates between patients with and without adverse outcomes, AUC alone does not establish clinical utility; calibration is an equally important dimension. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit results (mortality model p = 0.38; rebleeding model p = 0.41) indicate adequate calibration, though formal calibration plots are recommended for future validation studies. It is also important to distinguish statistical significance from clinical significance: the modest PPV values observed (12.9% for mortality, 23.9% for rebleeding) reflect the low event rates in this cohort and do not invalidate the marker, but do indicate that SII-positive findings require clinical integration rather than standalone decision-making. The high NPV values (96.8% and 95.5%, respectively) may represent the more clinically actionable metric, as a low SII at admission suggests very low short-term risk and may support less intensive monitoring pathways. The strength of our findings lies in the rebleeding domain: an AUC of 0.767 for SII substantially exceeds the 0.54–0.56 range for all clinical scores, a finding that is clinically meaningful given that rebleeding is the primary driver of secondary mortality and need for salvage intervention in NVUGIB [3, 22].

The finding that SII independently predicted both mortality and rebleeding in multivariable models aligns with emerging evidence on the prognostic utility of immune-inflammation indices in gastrointestinal emergencies. Altinsoy et al. demonstrated in a single-center emergency department cohort that elevated SII in patients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding was independently associated with in-hospital mortality, corroborating our primary findings [23]. In the specific context of rebleeding risk prediction — our study’s most distinctive finding — Tatliparmak et al. previously identified cancer, platelet distribution width, and total protein levels as independent predictors of 7-day rebleeding in emergency UGIB patients, highlighting the importance of hematological and nutritional indices in this outcome domain [19]; our data extend this line of evidence by demonstrating that the composite SII, which integrates platelet and immune cell dynamics, provides superior rebleeding discrimination compared to all established clinical scores. Aydin and Tatliparmak demonstrated an AUC of 0.81 for SII in differentiating confirmed from non-confirmed appendicitis in geriatric emergency patients, further supporting the cross-disease prognostic versatility of SII in the emergency setting [14]. Additionally, Emektar et al. reported that admission-derived hemodynamic markers added prognostic value beyond endoscopic risk classification in upper GI hemorrhage [24].

The incremental clinical utility of SII beyond established scores was further supported by decision curve analysis, which demonstrated that incorporating SII alongside AIMS65 and GBS consistently improved net clinical benefit across all threshold probabilities from 0% to 35%. This finding is practically significant: it implies that clinical decisions informed by SII-inclusive models would result in fewer undertreated high-risk patients and fewer overtreated low-risk patients compared to clinical-score-only models. The RCS regression further revealed a dose-response relationship between SII and mortality probability, with notable acceleration beyond the Youden-derived threshold of 1,233, suggesting the existence of a clinically meaningful inflection point above which inflammatory burden substantially escalates mortality risk. This non-linear pattern underscores the importance of quantitative SII assessment rather than binary categorization alone.

From a practical standpoint, SII offers several potential implementation advantages over some existing scoring systems. It is derived from a single complete blood count, which is universally obtained at emergency department presentation, requires no additional testing, and can be calculated instantaneously. An important distinction should be noted regarding the clinical scores compared in this study: GBS, pre-endoscopic Rockall Score, AIMS65, and SII are all applicable at initial triage before endoscopy, whereas the complete Rockall Score requires post-endoscopic data and is therefore not directly comparable for pre-procedural risk stratification in the emergency department. The association of SII with both mortality and rebleeding, demonstrated under multivariable adjustment, supports its potential value as a complement to existing tools. However, the current observational retrospective design and the absence of prospective implementation analysis preclude definitive conclusions about whether SII-guided triage would improve patient outcomes. Future prospective studies should test whether three-stage model comparisons (SII alone; established score alone; established score plus SII) confirm incremental value and should formally evaluate SII in pre-specified decision protocols before clinical integration.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design precludes causal inference and may introduce selection and ascertainment bias. Patients excluded due to transfer from another facility may represent higher-acuity cases in whom SII behavior differs following pre-hospital resuscitation. Patients excluded for missing complete blood count data may also differ systematically from included patients (e.g., requiring immediate intervention before full laboratory assessment), potentially attenuating SII effect sizes. External validation in multicenter prospective cohorts is necessary before SII can be recommended for routine clinical use. Second, the Youden index-derived SII cut-off of 1,233 was both derived and evaluated within the same cohort, without internal validation (e.g., bootstrap resampling or cross-validation). The reported sensitivity and specificity values are therefore optimistic estimates subject to optimism bias, and the threshold should be regarded as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating boundary rather than a validated clinical decision point. Third, the mortality multivariable model contained 10 candidate predictors with 53 events (EPV ≈ 5.3), below the conventionally recommended threshold of 10, introducing a risk of overfitting; this model should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Fourth, SII was calculated from a single admission blood count and may not reflect dynamic changes during resuscitation or the clinical course; moreover, SII elevation may reflect non-bleeding causes including age-related immune senescence, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, infection, chronic inflammatory states, and immunosuppressant use — all of which represent potential unmeasured confounders. Fifth, patients who died within 72 h of index endoscopy (n = 7) were classified as non-rebleeding events, as endoscopic confirmation was unavailable; although a sensitivity analysis confirmed that excluding these patients did not materially alter results, early death may have prevented recognition of rebleeding in a small number of cases. Sixth, the study population includes heterogeneous etiologies of non-variceal bleeding with different rebleeding risk profiles; while a peptic ulcer subgroup analysis supported consistent SII performance, etiologic heterogeneity remains a potential source of confounding. Seventh, H. pylori status, nonstandardized elements of post-procedural management, and timing of biomarker measurement relative to resuscitation represent additional unmeasured variables that cannot be fully accounted for in retrospective analyses. Finally, formal calibration plots and Brier scores were not generated; Hosmer-Lemeshow results suggest adequate model fit, but comprehensive calibration assessment is recommended in future prospective validation studies.

Conclusions

Admission SII was independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality and 72-hour rebleeding in this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with acute non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Its discriminative performance was moderate for 28-day mortality, comparable to established clinical risk scores, and comparatively stronger for 72-hour rebleeding prediction. These findings suggest that SII may complement, but should not replace, established risk stratification tools in the emergency assessment of NVUGIB. The proposed SII threshold of 1,233 should be regarded as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating estimate derived from the current cohort. Prospective multicenter validation studies are warranted to establish optimal SII thresholds, evaluate SII-integrated prediction models in diverse clinical settings, and confirm clinical utility before implementation.
