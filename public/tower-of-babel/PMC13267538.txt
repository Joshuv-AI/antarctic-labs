Adult transverse colon intussusception: A case report and literature review
Authors: Rahi Gandhi, Rajan Gurung, Susana Fernandez-Diaz, Duncan Bew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intussusception of the transverse colon in adults is commonly associated with a malignant lead point. Timely diagnosis and surgery are particularly important in weak patients with many comorbidities. An 84-year-old man presented with a 5-day history of vomiting, abdominal distension, and generalized weakness. He had severe frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale [CFS] score 7) and multiple comorbidities including Parkinson’s disease, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and anemia. Laboratory investigations revealed metabolic acidosis, leukocytosis, and acute kidney injury. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated bowel obstruction with pneumoperitoneum; however, perforation and an underlying malignant lead point were not identified on imaging. Emergency laparotomy revealed a perforated transverse colon intussusception with a 5 × 5 cm mucinous adenocarcinoma as the lead point. Palliative transverse colectomy with end ileostomy was performed owing to hemodynamic instability. Postoperatively, the patient developed hypoactive delirium, Parkinson-related rigidity attributable to the inability to administer oral dopaminergic medication preoperatively and a delay in initiating the transdermal rotigotine patch postoperatively, hypoglycemia, and sepsis. Despite intensive care support, his condition deteriorated and he died on postoperative day 12. This case highlights the limitations of preoperative CT imaging in identifying perforation and malignancy in colonic intussusception, the complexity of perioperative management in frail patients with multiple comorbidities, and the importance of early multidisciplinary decision-making in geriatric surgical emergencies.

Introduction

Intussusception the telescoping of one segment of bowel into an adjacent segment—is a rare cause of bowel obstruction in adults, accounting for approximately 1% of all intestinal obstructions and 5% of all intussusception cases [1]. Unlike pediatric intussusception, which is typically idiopathic and managed nonoperatively, adult intussusception is most frequently secondary to a pathological lead point. Malignancy accounts for the lead point in up to 65% of colonic intussusception cases [2]. Clinical presentations range from intermittent abdominal pain and nausea to acute bowel obstruction or perforation [3].

The imaging modality is computed tomography (CT) and can reveal typical appearances like the target sign or a sausage-shaped soft tissue mass with concentric rings that represent the intussuscepted bowel wall layers. Nevertheless, CT is not always able to reliably detect the underlying lead point, differentiate benign and malignant pathology, or detect related complications including perforation a diagnostic limitation that is central to the case presented here. The typical treatment of adult colonic intussusception is surgical resection, as there are a high chance of malignancy and the possibility of complications such as bowel ischemia and perforation [3].

We describe the case of an 84-year-old weak man with transverse colon intussusception due to mucinous adenocarcinoma with specific reference to the diagnostic limitations of CT imaging, intraoperative observation, and the perioperative issues of a patient with multiple comorbidities.

Case presentation

A 5-day history of vomiting, abdominal distension, decreased level of consciousness, and generalized weakness presented an 84-year-old man to the emergency department. His functional status had deteriorated over the past few months. He needed care four times a day, mobilization with a Zimmer frame, and could not perform activities of daily living independently, such as shopping and personal hygiene.

His medical history included cardiovascular disease, Parkinson disease (poorly managed preoperative due to poor absorption of oral medications in the setting of bowel obstruction), hypertension, chronic kidney disease, anemia, unexplained weight loss (under investigation due to possible colonic malignancy), and a history of duodenal ulcer. His Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) was 7, which is severe frailty.

On examination, the patient was hypoglycemic with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 14 following glucose administration and demonstrated clinical signs of peritonitis including generalized abdominal distension and diffuse tenderness. Laboratory investigations revealed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.2, lactate 4.8 mmol/L), leukocytosis (white cell count 18.5 × 109/L), and acute kidney injury (creatinine 245 µmol/L, eGFR 18 mL/min/1.73 m²).

Abdominal and pelvic CT with contrast enhancement showed the characteristics of large bowel obstruction and pneumoperitoneum. A transverse colon mass of soft tissue was detected, but the imaging failed to definitively define the underlying pathology as malignant, or to clearly define a point of perforation. Free intraperitoneal gas was a cause of concern of visceral perforation, but the exact location could not be identified on imaging. Mucinous tumors can be especially hard to differentiate between bowel wall edema or intraluminal fluid on CT because the mucinous content can be isodense to the surrounding structures, thus obscuring the morphology of the lead point. Without a confidently identified malignant lead point or a known site of perforation, the radiological differential diagnoses of the era were large bowel volvulus, adhesive obstruction with secondary ischemia, and an obstructing colonic neoplasm with local perforation.

Since the patient was clinically deteriorating rapidly and was at high risk of surgery, a multidisciplinary team of surgical and geriatric medicine specialists was assembled to advise the family on prognosis and risks of emergency surgery. The National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) mortality risk of 19.9% and a P-POSSUM predicted mortality of 39.2% were predicted operative risk scores, which confirmed the patient had an extremely high perioperative risk. The choice of emergency laparotomy was determined by the fact of the continuous clinical worsening and the lack of a feasible non-operative option.

Intraoperative findings and surgical management

Exploratory laparotomy revealed a perforated transverse colon intussusception. The lead point comprised a 5 × 5 cm firm, irregular mass that was subsequently confirmed as mucinous adenocarcinoma on histopathological analysis. Given the patient’s severe frailty, multiple comorbidities, and hemodynamic instability, a palliative transverse colectomy with end ileostomy was performed. Primary anastomosis was deliberately avoided to minimize the risk of anastomotic leakage and postoperative complications, in accordance with evidence-based recommendations for high-risk patients presenting with colonic perforation or obstruction [4].

Postoperative course

The patient was managed postoperatively in a high-acuity surgical ward with geriatric medicine input. Vasopressor support with noradrenaline was commenced; intubation was not undertaken in accordance with the established ceiling of care. Bilateral rectus sheath catheters were inserted on postoperative day (POD) 2 for regional analgesia.

Perioperative Management of Parkinson’s Disease: Preoperative administration of the patient’s oral dopaminergic medications was not possible owing to bowel obstruction and the nil-by-mouth requirement, resulting in worsening rigidity and bradykinesia. Abrupt interruption of dopaminergic therapy in the perioperative period carries the risk of an akinetic crisis and neuropsychiatric complications including delirium [5,6]. The transdermal rotigotine patch, which represents a validated non-oral alternative for maintaining dopaminergic therapy in patients who are nil by mouth or who have impaired gastrointestinal absorption [5,6], was not applied promptly postoperatively owing to delayed availability, contributing to hypoactive delirium and prolonged immobility.

Enteral nutrition was commenced via nasogastric tube on POD 3, with close monitoring for hypoglycemia. The patient remained delirious and agitated despite a functioning ileostomy.

On POD 7, the patient developed sepsis manifesting as tachycardia, hypotension, increased breathing, and pulmonary edema. Clinical assessment suggested intravascular hypovolemia despite apparent fluid overload. Progressive systemic deterioration was evidenced by hypothermia (35.2°C) and worsening respiratory distress.

Following multidisciplinary consultation with the patient’s family, and considering his severe frailty, multi-organ dysfunction, and underlying malignancy, a decision was made to transition to end-of-life care. The patient died on POD 12.

Discussion

This case identified the following important considerations in the management of adult transverse colon intussusception in frail, elderly patients.

Diagnostic limitations of imaging

Although CT is the diagnostic investigation of choice for adult intussusception [3], it failed to characterize the underlying malignant lead point or definitively identify the site of perforation in this patient. Classic CT features of intussusception—including the “target sign” on axial imaging and a sausage-shaped soft tissue mass with concentric layers corresponding to the intussuscepted bowel wall—were present; however, these findings are not pathognomonic of malignancy and can be replicated by benign lead points. Mucinous adenocarcinomas may be particularly challenging to characterize radiologically owing to their mucin content, which may appear isodense to surrounding bowel wall or fluid on CT, thereby obscuring lead point morphology. Free intraperitoneal gas (pneumoperitoneum) was identified, raising concern for perforation; however, the exact site could not be delineated. In the absence of a confidently identified malignant lead point or confirmed perforation site, the differential radiological diagnoses considered included large bowel volvulus, adhesive obstruction with secondary ischemia, and an obstructing colonic neoplasm with local perforation. This case underscores the importance of clinical judgement and multidisciplinary decision-making when CT findings are equivocal in the setting of an acute abdomen.

Effects of frailty on outcomes

Severe frailty of the patient (CFS score 7) was one of the key factors of postoperative outcomes, which led to hypoactive delirium, immobility, and intolerance to enteral nutrition. Frailty is a sign of reduced physiological reserve, which puts elderly patients at risk of poor surgical outcomes such as multi-organ dysfunction and delayed recovery [1].

Perioperative issues in comorbid patients

Comorbidities (especially Parkinson disease) were difficult to manage perioperatively. The patient had bowel obstruction that prevented absorption of her usual oral dopaminergic medications preoperative and the late initiation of the transdermal rotigotine patch postoperative led to increasing rigidity, bradykinesia, hypoactive delirium and extended immobility. Sudden withdrawal of dopaminergic treatment may cause an akinetic crisis or neuropsychiatric decompensation [5,6]. highlighting the importance of timely non-oral dopaminergic replacement in the perioperative period when oral feeding is not possible. Repeat hypoglycemia was also an indication of the metabolic instability of critical illness in the weak patients.

Surgical decision-making

A palliative approach was required due to the presence of a perforated intussusception and a malignant lead point in combination with hemodynamic instability. Transverse colectomy and end ileostomy were done, and primary anastomosis was intentionally postponed due to the high operative risk and contaminated operative field of the patient. This practice is in line with the published guidelines that recommend resection and defunctioning stoma creation in high-risk patients who present with colonic perforation or obstruction, where primary anastomosis is associated with a significant risk of anastomotic failure [4]. The procedure balanced oncological adequacy with physiological tolerance, prioritizing patient safety.

Prognostic considerations

The advanced age, extreme frailty, the presence of numerous comorbidities, the late presentation, and intraoperative perforation had a very negative prognosis. These compounding factors were independent of postoperative morbidity and mortality and led to progressive systemic deterioration and death of the patient on POD 12 despite optimal surgical and multidisciplinary care (Frailty).

Comparison with literature

In adults, up to 65 percent of cases of intussusception of the colon have a lead point of malignancy [2], adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, and gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common pathologies with adenocarcinoma [3]. The mucinous adenocarcinoma diagnosed in the case is in line with the literature. The surgical management depends on the location of the intussusception, complications, and the general condition of the patient [2]. En-bloc resection without reduction is suggested to reduce the risk of tumour seeding and spread [[4], [9]] and outcomes in large case series confirm the surgical and prognostic challenges in this population [9]. The use of a diversion ileostomy, as performed in this case, has been shown to reduce postoperative morbidity in high-risk patients [10].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates three important lessons in the management of adult transverse colon intussusception. First, preoperative CT scanning cannot detect the malignant lead point as well as the location of perforation, especially in the case of mucinous adenocarcinoma; clinical deterioration should thus inform the decision to go to emergency surgery even in cases where the imaging results are inconclusive. Second, extreme weakness and comorbidities, especially Parkinson disease and the need of dopaminergic drugs, significantly complicate the perioperative process and aggravate the postoperative results; timely non-oral dopaminergic replacement and immediate multidisciplinary consultation are critical in this group of patients. Third, in cases of hemodynamic instability and contamination, which do not allow primary anastomosis, palliative resection with end ileostomy is an evidence-based and suitable intervention that does not sacrifice oncological safety but focuses on physiological tolerance. The multidisciplinary intervention, which includes surgical, geriatric medicine, an aesthetic, and nursing teams, is essential in the treatment of high-risk elderly patients with surgical emergencies.
