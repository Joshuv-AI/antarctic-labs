Unsupervised Causal Abstractions Discovery
Théo Saulus, Simon Lacoste-Julien, Dhanya Sridhar

Abstract
Causal abstractions formalize when a high-level structural causal model (SCM) captures the interventional behavior of a lower-level SCM. Existing applications of this notion largely follow a hypothesis-testing paradigm: an expert proposes a candidate high-level model and then evaluates if the low-level system implements it. We study the complementary problem of learning a high-level model directly from low-level measurements. Our contributions leverage hypotheses from low-rank causal discovery, and can be summarized as follows: (1) we show that observations generated by a low-rank graph induce latents that form a causal abstraction, (2) we provide identifiability results about these latents, and (3) we propose a practical objective to learn this high-level SCM.



1
Introduction

Complex systems can be described at different levels of abstraction: from low-level measurements about the system, one can form high-level summaries of its behavior. For example, we can record the neural activity of an individual performing a given task, and group the neurons by the stimuli they respond to, yielding a coarse description in terms of functional groups rather than individual neurons.

However, many scientific queries are about predicting the effects of interventions, such as "what happens if we activate this part of the brain?". Addressing such questions requires
causal
abstractions
[
5
,
38
]
:
put informally for now, a causal abstraction encodes at the high-level the effects of some interventions of interest on low-level variables, satisfying a form of consistency.

In mechanistic interpretability, this viewpoint motivates hypothesis testing methods where one proposes a candidate high-level model and checks whether it is implemented by a deep neural network (DNN)
[
19
,
20
,
40
]
. The underlying hope is that once we know the high-level computations the DNN implements, we can translate interventions on high-level variables into corresponding interventions on neurons to steer the model behavior.
However, enumerating over candidate abstractions to test is costly, and worse, not possible when we study systems that we do not fully understand (for example, a DNN trained to solve protein folding).
Motivated by these applications in interpretability and steering, this paper is about the unsupervised discovery of such causal abstractions.

Unsupervised objectives are typically under-specified and admit many equivalent solutions, thus requiring further constraints or assumptions.
In this work, we discover causal abstractions for a family of low-level systems whose graphs exhibit a low-rank structure.
Specifically, we consider factor directed acyclic graphs (factor DAGs, or f-DAGs), a family of models over low-level variables that are parameterized by a small number of latent factors, as introduced by
27
.
This low-rank assumption is well-suited to modeling cell or neural biology where many low-level variables like genes or neurons carry out similar functions. Likewise in DNNs, because they perform computations in a distributed way, neurons are also likely to cluster into groups that capture the same abstract “concept” in a higher-level computation graph.

The goal of
27
was to learn a causal graph over low-level variables.
They show that when observations come from a model that satisfies the f-DAG property, the DAG that best explains these low-level observations is likely to be unique and can be learned from data.
Our goal with this paper is not to propose a new algorithm, but to prove that this causal discovery algorithm identifies a causal model over latent factors that is a causal abstraction of the low-level system. Additionally, we show that some assumptions allow us to make these latent factors identifiable up to trivial equivalence.
We call this the
anchor assumption
.
Loosely speaking, it requires that for each latent factor, there exist low-level variables that uniquely influence and are influenced by this factor.
These anchor variables do not need to be known, but by ensuring that the solutions to Boolean matrix factorization satisfy the anchor assumption, we guarantee that the overall unsupervised objective only admits solutions that are simple reparameterizations of the true model over latent factors.
We validate the theory empirically by studying simulated settings where the target causal abstraction is known, and also study a fully unsupervised case where we qualitatively analyze abstractions learned from a DNN that solves an arithmetic task.

Related work

Causal abstractions formalize when a high-level SCM preserves the interventional behavior of a lower-level SCM through a state map and an intervention map
[
38
,
5
,
4
]
. This formalism has been used in mechanistic interpretability to test whether a proposed high-level causal model is implemented by a neural network
[
19
,
20
,
18
,
40
]
. Our work instead studies the complementary problem of discovering such a high-level model directly from low-level measurements. This follows the older motivation of causal feature learning, which seeks macro-causes and macro-effects from high-dimensional observations
[
7
,
8
]
, but uses the modern causal-abstraction formalism based on interventional consistency.

Several recent papers study learning causal abstractions rather than only testing expert-proposed ones.
44
,
16
,
31
,
12

both
levels. In contrast, we discover the high-level latent SCM from low-level observations only.
46

Their setting, however, relies on linear SCMs, shift interventions, linear state and intervention maps, and constructive abstractions, which implies that each low-level variable can only belong to one latent factor. In contrast, we identify high-level latent factors from low-level measurements through a low-rank hypothesis and weaker identifiability assumptions (the anchor condition). Targeted reduction of causal models
[
23
]
learns a reduced high-level causal model and a map from low-level shift interventions to high-level interventions from interventional data. However, it is target-centered: the high-level model is built around a known variable of interest, rather than discovering a general latent-factor SCM.

Our structural assumptions connect causal abstraction discovery to low-rank causal discovery and latent-variable learning. We build on factor DAGs
27
, where a low-rank adjacency structure is represented by latent factors mediating low-level causal relations. Low-rank causal discovery has also been studied as a structural restriction on causal graphs
[
11
,
15
]
. Structural Causal Bottleneck Models
[
6
]
, are closely related in that they posit that causal effects between high-dimensional variables can factor through low-dimensional bottlenecks, and they provide identifiability results for such bottlenecks. However, their framework treats bottlenecks primarily as task-specific sufficient summaries for estimating causal effects in a known graph, rather than as a learned high-level SCM equipped with explicit state and intervention maps satisfying a causal-abstraction criterion.

Our anchor assumption, inspired by topic modeling
[
3
]
, can be related to pure-child assumptions in causal representation learning. For example,
41

30

These resemble our out-anchor condition, but our setting differs in that our observed variables also mediate latent factors relations. In different setups, other works about the identifiability of deep generative models
[
33
,
26
]
or about the identifiability of Boolean matrix factorization
[
32
,
10
]
also resort to very similar assumptions.

4
Towards causal abstractions discovery

Most current applications of causal abstractions are related to neural network interpretability: an expert proposes a candidate high-level model
\mathcal{H}
about what logic the model might implement to solve a given task, and then tests whether the computational graph
\mathcal{L}
of a neural network implements it
[
19
,
20
,
40
]
.

Intuitively, we view
\mathcal{H}
as a model of latents that summarize the computations done by the low-level computational graph
\mathcal{L}
: the value of each latent
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
can be computed with a subset of variables
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and in turn there is another subset of variables
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
which can essentially be determined by the value of this
Z
. In other words,
Z
summarizes
all there is to know
about the causal relations
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
.

Proposing a candidate high-level model
\mathcal{H}
is difficult, because it requires intuition or expertise about the task at hand: for instance, we can propose a logic for two-digit addition because we master this task.
In the following, we develop assumptions under which we can
learn
a high-level model
\mathcal{H}
directly from low-level measurements by minimizing a differentiable objective.

In particular, we recall from
27
that for an SCM
\mathcal{M}
whose graph
G
is low-rank, one can naturally construct a latent SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.
We prove that this latent SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is a uniform transformation of
\mathcal{M}
.
Since causal discovery tools allow us to learn the graph and mechanisms of
\mathcal{M}
from data, we propose later a practical algorithm for unsupervised causal abstraction discovery inspired by it.

Factor directed acyclic graphs.
Consider the causal graph
G
between observed variables
\mathcal{X}
, encoded by an adjacency matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, where
n
is the number of variables.
In causal discovery, where we seek to learn the causal graph
G
from observations, a common structural assumption to restrict the space of candidate graphs is the low-rankness of the adjacency matrix
A

[
11
,
15
]
.
More specifically, assuming that
A
has Boolean rank
m<n
means that there exists
Q,R\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
such that

A\ =\ Q\diamond R^{\top}\ :=\ \bigvee_{k=1}^{m}\bigl(Q_{*k}\wedge R_{*k}\bigr),

where
\diamond
denotes the Boolean matrix product, illustrated in Figure
2
.
Intuitively, this low-rank decomposition captures the idea that many low-level variables, like genes in a regulatory network
[
1
]
or neurons in the brain
[
42
]
, share the same parents and children due to higher-order functions. Matrix factorization methods have previously been used to discover such higher-level functions
[
35
]
.

This factorization admits a graphical interpretation via
factor directed graphs
.

Definition 9

(Factor SCM)
.

An
f-SCM
is an SCM
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F})
such that
\mathcal{V}
is partitioned into two subsets: observed
variables

\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and unobserved latent
factors

\mathcal{Z}=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m}\}
. Additionally, we suppose that the causal graph
G
of an f-SCM is bipartite, directed, and strongly acyclic, and we denote it an
f-DAG
.

In particular, the edges in
G
may only link variables to factors or factors to variables:

G=(\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{V}}),\quad\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{V}}\subset(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z})\cup(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}).

Relating back to Boolean matrix factorization, the matrix
Q
captures the adjacency matrix of directed edges from low-level variables to factors, i.e.,
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
, while
R
captures the directed edges from factors
\mathbf{Z}
back to low-level variables
\mathbf{X}
. Thus,
Q
and
R
fully characterize the f-DAG.

Further, the graph
G
can be collapsed into two distinct (“mono-partite”) graphs: one over variables
G_{X}
with

X_{i}\to X_{j}\iff\exists Z\in\mathcal{Z},\ X_{i}\to Z\to X_{j}\text{ in $G$,}

and one over factors
G_{Z}
defined analogously.

From now on, we will distinguish 3 different notions of parents in an f-DAG: for any
Z\in\mathcal{Z},\ X\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle X^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}

\displaystyle\iff X^{\prime}\rightarrow Z\text{ in }G

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}_{X}

\displaystyle\iff Z^{\prime}\rightarrow X\text{ in }G

\displaystyle X^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}^{L}_{X}

\displaystyle\iff X^{\prime}\rightarrow X\text{ in }G_{L}

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}^{H}_{Z}

\displaystyle\iff Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z\text{ in }G_{H}

Key idea.
The notion of f-DAGs seems to meet our intuitive desiderata for causal abstractions discovery in that they summarize the way low-level variables interact with fewer factors, and they are learnable from data with low-rank causal discovery.

The following sections formalize these two points: we show how the factor-level description can indeed be cast as a uniform transformation of the variable-level description, and we identify conditions under which the factor representation is identifiable.

5
Theoretical results

This section formalizes the previous insights with three results that justify the use of low-rank causal discovery to discover causal abstractions: (i) first, we show that if we knew the underlying f-SCM that generates low-level observations, we could derive a causal abstraction of the low-level model; (ii) since we do not know the underlying f-SCM, we establish assumptions under which Boolean matrix factorization can recover the latent factor variables up to simple reparameterizations; (iii) completing the story, we derive assumptions under which the full high-level SCM can be recovered up to simple reparameterizations.
We state our main results, and sketch some of the proofs; the full derivations are available in Appendix
B

5.3
Identifiability of the mechanisms

Latent factors are only observed through their downstream effect on the variables, so the mechanisms of an f-SCM are identifiable only up to some reparameterization of the factor value spaces. This section makes this statement precise and gives simple conditions under which the admissible reparameterizations reduce to affine or monotone transformations.

Definition 12

(
\mathcal{X}
-equivalence)
.

Two f-SCMs
\mathcal{M},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
are
\mathcal{X}
-equivalent
if they induce the same low-level collapsed mechanisms on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\forall X\in\mathcal{X},\ F_{X}^{L}=\widetilde{F}_{X}^{L}
.

Lemma 4

(Reparameterization symmetry)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an f-SCM and let
(\rho_{Z})_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}
be any family of bijections
\rho_{Z}:\mathrm{Val}_{Z}\to\mathrm{Val}_{Z}
. Define
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
over the same f-DAG by
\widetilde{F}_{Z}:=\rho_{Z}^{-1}\circ F_{Z}
and
\widetilde{F}_{X}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u}\bigr):=F_{X}\bigl((\rho_{Z}(\mathbf{z}_{Z}))_{Z\in\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u}\bigr)
.
Then

is
\mathcal{X}
-equivalent to
\mathcal{M}
.

Factors can be reparameterized by arbitrary bijections without affecting observational and interventional behavior on
\mathcal{X}
. We propose assumptions to restrict this equivalence class.

Definition 13

(Linear parent mechanisms)
.

An f-SCM has

if for every

the mechanism

is affine: for all

where

, and with at least one nonzero coefficient per factor:
\forall Z\in\mathcal{Z},\ \exists X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z},\ a_{ZX}\neq 0
.

Theorem 3

(Linear parent identifiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be two
\mathcal{X}
-equivalent f-SCMs sharing the same f-DAG with out-anchors for all factors, linear parent mechanisms and real supports.
Then for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
there exists a unique bijection
\rho_{Z}
such that
\tilde{F}_{Z}=\rho_{Z}^{-1}\circ F_{Z}
, and it is affine:
\rho_{Z}:\mathbf{z}_{Z}\mapsto\alpha_{Z}\mathbf{z}_{Z}+\beta_{Z}
with
\alpha_{Z}\neq 0

We show in Appendix
B.5
similar results when child mechanisms are affine.

These results motivate the metrics that should be used to evaluate the method in practice. In particular, using metrics invariant to linear reparameterization to match factors
Z
against external hypotheses only makes sense if we are willing to assume linearity of the mechanisms.

6
Practical implementation

We now specify the learning objective for our
unsupervised causal abstraction discovery
method (UCAD), and show additional connections with causal abstractions. Our practical implementation draws inspiration from low-rank causal discovery, with specific additional constraints. On synthetic experiments, we show that our algorithm recovers ground-truth latent factors, up to the expected equivalence classes predicted by theory.

6.1
Learning objective

We extend the low-rank causal discovery objective of
27
with specific additional constraints. We parameterize separately the conditional distributions with
\Theta
and the causal graph with
\Phi
. The constrained optimization problem is the following:

\displaystyle\max_{\Phi,\Theta}S(\Phi,\Theta)\text{ such that }\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}^{\text{DAG}}(\Phi)=0\\
\mathcal{C}^{\text{anchors}}(\Phi)=0\\
\mathcal{C}^{\text{sparsity}}(\Phi)\leq\text{tol}_{s}\end{cases}

Objective function.

\displaystyle S(\Phi,\Theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}G^{\prime}\sim G(\Phi)\\
i\sim\mathbb{P}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p^{(i)}_{\text{data}}}\sum_{X\notin\mathbf{I}}\log p_{\Theta}^{X}(\mathbf{x}_{X}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}^{G^{\prime}}})

with
G(\Phi)
the causal graph model,
\mathbb{P}
is the empirical distribution over intervention regimes
\mathcal{I}_{L}
,
p^{(i)}_{\text{data}}
the empirical distribution over observed variables
\mathcal{X}
under intervention
i
, and
p_{\Theta}^{X}
is a density model for the conditional distribution of
X\in\mathcal{X}
conditioned on its parents
\mathrm{Pa}_{X}^{G^{\prime}}
in the sampled graph
G^{\prime}\sim G(\Phi)
.

Our graph model
G(\Phi)
is parametrized with a Gumbel-softmax on a matrix
W\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}
such that
Q_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{W_{ij}=1\}
and
R_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{W_{ij}=-1\}
. This way,
Q_{ij}
and
R_{ij}
cannot be simultaneously equal to
1
, and no self-loop is introduced.

Our conditional densities
p_{\Theta}^{X}
, which aim to recover the mechanisms
F_{X}^{L}
, are parameterized such that for each factor
Z
indexed by
k\in[m]
in
Q
and each variable
X
indexed by
j\in[n]
in
R
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{Z}=F_{Z}^{\Theta}(Q_{:k}\mathbf{x});\qquad\mathbf{x}_{X}\sim\text{Normal}(F_{X}^{\Theta}(R_{j:}\mathbf{z}),\ \sigma_{j}^{2})

where
\sigma_{j}^{2}>0
, and
F_{Z}^{\Theta},F_{X}^{\Theta}
are linear models or MLP in practice. In other words, we assume additive Gaussian noise on variables, while we do not introduce noise on factors.

Acyclicity constraint.

\mathcal{C}^{\text{DAG}}
enforces that the final graph is indeed a DAG. Several formulations have been proposed in the literature, which we describe in Appendix
C
. Additionally, we propose a constraint formulation for the case where variables
X\in\mathcal{X}
are “layered”, as in a neural network where a neuron
X_{1}
cannot causally influence another neuron
X_{2}
if both are in the same layer or if
X_{2}
precedes
X_{1}
in the computational graph.
In other words, we restrict the search space to block upper-triangular adjacency matrices.
For a low-rank matrix
A=Q\diamond R^{\top}
, this translates into the per-factor condition:

\forall k\in[m],\qquad\max_{i:Q_{ik}=1}\ell(i)<\min_{j:R_{jk}=1}\ell(j),

i.e., every parent selected by column
Q_{:k}
lies in a strictly earlier layer than every child selected by column
R_{:k}
. This translates into a differentiable constraint that can be efficiently computed in
O(nm+mL)
time, with
L
the number of layers, applied on
G(\Phi)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\text{DAG}}(\Phi):=\sum_{\underset{l_{p}\geq l_{c}}{k}}\left[\sum_{i:\ell(i)=l_{p}}Q(\Phi)_{ik}\right]\left[\sum_{j:\ell(j)=l_{c}}R(\Phi)_{jk}\right]

Anchor constraint.

As discussed previously, the Boolean factorization of the adjacency matrix is not unique unless additional assumptions are made, e.g. Assumption
4
. For our model to find such solutions, we consider
\mathcal{C}^{\text{anchor}}
, applied to
G(\Phi)
.

A factor
Z
indexed by
k\in[m]
has an in-anchor if and only if there exists a row
i\in[n]
such that
Q_{ik}=1
and
\forall\,\ell\neq k:Q_{i\ell}=0
.
Equivalently,

\forall k\in[m],\quad\max_{i}Q_{ik}\bigwedge_{\ell\neq k}(1-Q_{i\ell})=1

We have a similar formulation for out-anchors.
This formulation is made differentiable using classic optimization tricks, to finally obtain
\mathcal{C}^{\text{anchor}}
, as described in Appendix
C
.

Sparsity constraint

As in many causal discovery algorithms, we impose some sparsity constraint on the learned graph, in the form of an
L_{1}
constraint

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\text{sparsity}}(\Phi):=\frac{\sum_{ij}|Q(\Phi)_{ij}|+|R(\Phi)_{ij}|}{mn}

One can adjust the tolerance level
\text{tol}_{s}
depending on prior knowledge of the structure to be learned.

7
Discussion and future work

In this paper, we demonstrated that it is possible to discover causal abstractions directly from low-level measurements without relying on an expert to propose a candidate high-level model. By leveraging factor directed acyclic graphs and introducing the anchor assumption, we proved that low-rank causal discovery methods can identify high-level latent factors up to simple reparameterization. We translated these theoretical guarantees into a practical learning objective and empirically tested it on both synthetic datasets and a small neural network.

While our theoretical results provide a foundation for UCAD, they rely on knowing the true number of latent factors. A direction for future work is understanding the behavior of the learning objective when this number is misspecified.

Scaling our approach to larger and more complex systems is another direction. Our current experiments on the divisibility task highlight the difficulty of interpreting the heuristics learned by deep neural networks. Applying it to larger models in language, vision, or to noisy scientific data, will require finding scalable optimization procedures.

In particular, the hope of a causal abstraction in mechanistic interpretability lies in its ability to reliably control a system’s output. Future research should focus on steering experiments, to test whether intervening on the unsupervised discovered high-level factors can reliably alter the behavior of DNNs on complex tasks.

B.2
Discussion on the ordering of interventions

When we apply two interventions
i_{1},i_{2}
on
\mathcal{M}
, it might occur that some variables are affected by both
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
. To determine which assignment prevails,
18
introduce the notion of intervention algebras, to fix the order in which the interventions are applied:

Definition 15

(Intervention algebras)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a set of interventions allowed on
\mathcal{V}
. We equip it with an associative composition operator
\circ:\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{I}
and an identity element
\mathrm{id}
.
The pair
(\mathcal{I},\circ)
, together with
\mathrm{id}
, forms an
intervention algebra
if the following properties are verified:

•

the identity element
\mathrm{id}
is defined such that
\mathcal{M}^{id}=\mathcal{M}
, i.e., no intervention performed.

•

the composition operator
\circ
is such that
\forall i_{1},i_{2}\in\mathcal{I},\ \mathcal{M}^{\,i_{1}\circ i_{2}}=(\mathcal{M}^{\,i_{1}})^{\,i_{2}}
, and
\circ
is commutative if
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
target different variables, and left-annihilative if they target the same:

\displaystyle(i_{1}\circ i_{2})_{V}=\begin{cases}(i_{2})_{V}&\text{if }V\in\mathbf{I}_{2}\\
(i_{1})_{V}&\text{if }V\in\mathbf{I}_{1}\setminus\mathbf{I}_{2}\\
(\mathrm{id})_{V}&\text{if }V\notin\mathbf{I}_{1}\cup\mathbf{I}_{2}\end{cases}

An induced partial order
\preceq
is defined by
\circ
such that
\forall i_{1},i_{2}\in\mathcal{I},\ i_{1}\preceq i_{2}\iff i_{2}\circ i_{1}=i_{2}
, i.e., the model
\mathcal{M}^{i_{2}\circ i_{1}}
remains identical to
\mathcal{M}^{i_{2}}
if and only if
i_{1}
agrees with the replacements performed by
i_{2}
.

As shown by
18
, hard and soft interventions both can always be equipped with an intervention algebra. This is not the case of parametric interventions, which require more care.

Although the family of parametric intervention we consider might be relatable to intervention algebras, we stay closer to prior notions of exact transformation
[
5
,
38
]
which only require the existence of partial orderings on
\mathcal{I}_{L},\mathcal{I}_{H}
with respect to which
\omega
is order preserving.

We introduce a more flexible condition that does not require that each factor has a in- and out-anchor. We only show a property of this condition, not a full identifiability result.

(
r
-disjunctedness)
.

Columns of
Q
are
r
-disjunct if

\displaystyle\forall k\in[m],\forall S\subseteq[m]\backslash\{k\}\quad\text{with}\quad|S|\leq r,\ \exists j\in[n]\quad\text{such that}\quad Q_{jk}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\forall l\in S,\ Q_{jl}=0

In words, every factor can be distinguished from the union of any combination of at most
r
other factors by at least one parent variable.

Example: the following matrix is 1-disjunct but not 2-disjunct:

\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1&1\\
1&1&0&1\\
1&1&1&0\\
0&1&1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

Indeed, for any
k\neq l
, the column
j=l+1\mod 4
satisfies
Q_{12}=0
and
Q_{kj}=1
.

2
-disjunctness fails because every column of
Q
contains exactly one zero, so no column vanishes on two distinct rows simultaneously. Hence for any
k
and any pair
S=\{a,b\}\subseteq[m]\setminus\{k\}
, no column
j
satisfies
Q_{kj}=1
and
Q_{aj}=Q_{bj}=0
.

We can of course have a similar definition for the rows of
R^{\top}
.
Note that considering

defines exactly the in-/out-anchors.

Definition 17

(Row and column sparsity)
.

The row-sparsity of

, and similar definition for the column-sparsity of
R^{\top}
.

Lemma 9

(Reading

from

)
.

Let’s assume the following points:

•

•

the columns of

(resp.

Proof.

Recovering

.
For all factors
Z

X
with index

A_{i*}

For all variable index

Theorem
3

(Linear parent identifiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be two
\mathcal{X}
-equivalent f-SCMs sharing the same f-DAG with out-anchors for all factors, linear parent mechanisms and real supports.

Z\in\mathcal{Z}

\rho_{Z}
such that
\tilde{F}_{Z}=\rho_{Z}^{-1}\circ F_{Z}

\rho_{Z}:\mathbf{z}_{Z}\mapsto\alpha_{Z}\mathbf{z}_{Z}+\beta_{Z}

\alpha_{Z}\neq 0

Proof.

Fix
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
and let
X
be its out-anchor. Since
\mathrm{Pa}_{X}=\{Z\}

\mathrm{Pa}_{X}^{L}=\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}

\mathcal{X}
-equivalence at

\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle F_{X}\bigl(F_{Z}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\bigr)=\widetilde{F}_{X}\bigl(\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\bigr).

By linearity of the parent mechanisms, we can write

F_{Z}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b
and
\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{b}
,
with
\mathbf{a}\neq 0
and
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\neq 0

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\mathbf{a}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}
are linearly independent. Then the affine map
T:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\bigl(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b,\ \tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{b}\bigr)

\mathrm{Val}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}

\mathbb{R}^{2}

(F_{Z}(\mathbf{x}),\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x}))

\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}

F_{X}
and
\widetilde{F}_{X}

\mathbb{R}

Fixing

shows that
\widetilde{F}_{X}(\cdot,\mathbf{u})
is constant, and thus
F_{X}(\cdot,\mathbf{u})
as well, contradicting the faithfulness assumption for the out-anchor. Hence

\lambda\neq 0

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\neq 0

Now, define

\mathbb{R}

Since
\mathbf{a}\neq 0

\mathrm{Val}_{X}=\mathbb{R}

is surjective on
\mathbb{R}

\rho

\widetilde{F}_{Z}=\rho^{-1}\circ F_{Z}
satisfies
\rho^{-1}=\rho_{Z}^{-1}

\rho=\rho_{Z}

Theorem 5

(Linear child identifiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be two
\mathcal{X}
-equivalent f-SCMs sharing the same f-DAG with out-anchors for all factors, linear child mechanisms and real supports. Then for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
there exists an affine bijection
\rho_{Z}:\mathbf{z}_{Z}\mapsto\alpha_{Z}\mathbf{z}_{Z}+\beta_{Z}
with
\alpha_{Z}\neq 0
such that

\widetilde{F}_{Z}=\rho_{Z}^{-1}\circ F_{Z}
, and the coefficients
(\alpha_{Z},\beta_{Z})

are uniquely determined.

Proof.

Fix
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
and let
X
be its out-anchor. Since
\mathrm{Pa}_{X}=\{Z\}
, we have
\mathrm{Pa}_{X}^{L}=\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}
, and
\mathcal{X}
-equivalence at
X
gives for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathcal{U}}

F_{X}\bigl(F_{Z}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\bigr)=\widetilde{F}_{X}\bigl(\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\bigr).

As
\mathrm{Pa}_{X}=\{Z\}
, the linear child mechanisms read
F_{X}(z,\mathbf{u})=a(\mathbf{u})\,z+b(\mathbf{u})
and
\widetilde{F}_{X}(z,\mathbf{u})=\tilde{a}(\mathbf{u})\,z+\tilde{b}(\mathbf{u})

and
\tilde{a}(\mathbf{u}):=\tilde{a}_{XZ}(\mathbf{u})
.
Substituting, we get that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle a(\mathbf{u})F_{Z}(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{u})=\tilde{a}(\mathbf{u})\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x})+\tilde{b}(\mathbf{u}).

By faithfulness, the edges
\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}\to X
in
G^{L}
are not spurious, so
\mathbf{x}\mapsto F_{X}^{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})=a(\mathbf{u})\,F_{Z}(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{u})
is

\mathbf{u}^{\prime}

X
reaches
\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}
only through
Z
,
this forces both
a(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\neq 0

Fixing
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
in the previous equation and dividing by
a(\mathbf{u})\neq 0

\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}

F_{Z}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\tilde{a}(\mathbf{u})}{a(\mathbf{u})}\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\tilde{b}(\mathbf{u})-b(\mathbf{u})}{a(\mathbf{u})}=:\mu\widetilde{F}_{Z}(\mathbf{x})+\nu.

is non-constant, it ranges at least two distinct points, and an affine map is determined by its values at two points. Hence the relation
\widetilde{F}_{Z}=\rho_{Z}^{-1}\circ F_{Z}

\rho_{Z}^{-1}
, and therefore the coefficients

(\alpha_{Z},\beta_{Z})

Theorem
4

(Approximate transformation bound)
.

If all mechanisms
F_{Z}^{\Theta},F_{X}^{\Theta}
are Lipschitz, then
\hat{\Delta}
is linearly bounded by
S(\Phi,\Theta)
.

Proof.

We instantiate the learned state map componentwise by the learned parent mechanisms:

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathcal{X}},\ \forall Z\in\mathcal{Z},\quad(\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{x}))_{Z}:=F_{Z}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}})

Consider the high-level model to be the learned one
\mathcal{H}_{\Phi,\Theta}
, with collapsed mechanisms given by

F_{Z}^{H,\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}^{H}},\mathbf{u}):=F_{Z}^{\Theta}\Big(\{F_{X}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})\}_{X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}\Big),

and
\mathcal{L}_{\Phi,\Theta}
the corresponding low-level model on
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume
d_{H}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\mathbf{z}_{Z}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{Z}\|^{2}
, i.e., Euclidean MSE.

Fix an intervention
i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}
with support
\mathbf{I}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and a context
\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{Val}_{\mathcal{U}}
.

Set
\mathbf{x}:=\mathrm{Solve}(\mathcal{L}^{i};\mathbf{u})
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}^{(i)}
, and

\hat{\mathbf{z}}:=\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{z}:=\mathrm{Solve}(\mathcal{H}_{\Phi,\Theta}^{\omega(i)};\mathbf{u}).

Pointwise propagation inequality.

For each
X\in\mathcal{X}
, define the (regime-dependent) prediction

\hat{x}_{X}:=\begin{cases}F_{X}^{\Theta}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})&\text{if }X\notin\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{x}_{X}&\text{if }X\in\mathbf{I}\end{cases}\qquad\tilde{x}_{X}:=\begin{cases}F_{X}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})&\text{if }X\notin\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{x}_{X}&\text{if }X\in\mathbf{I}\end{cases}

so that
\tilde{x}_{X}
matches the value of
X
used inside
\mathrm{Solve}(\mathcal{H}^{\omega(i)};\mathbf{u})
, and for intervened variables
X\in\mathbf{I}
we have
\tilde{x}_{X}=\mathbf{x}_{X}
by construction.
Define per-variable residuals and per-factor errors

r_{X}:=\mathbf{1}\{X\notin\mathbf{I}\}\,\|\mathbf{x}_{X}-\hat{x}_{X}\|^{2},\qquad\delta_{Z}:=\|\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{Z}-\mathbf{z}_{Z}\|^{2}.

Fix
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Using
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{Z}=F_{Z}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}})
and
\mathbf{z}_{Z}=F_{Z}^{\Theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}})
, and that
F_{Z}
is
L_{Z}
-Lipschitz, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{Z}

\displaystyle=\|F_{Z}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}})-F_{Z}^{\Theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{Z}^{2}\sum_{X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}}\|\mathbf{x}_{X}-\tilde{x}_{X}\|^{2}.

For each
X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}
, apply
\|a-b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a-c\|^{2}+2\|c-b\|^{2}
with
a=\mathbf{x}_{X}
,
b=\tilde{x}_{X}
,
c=\hat{x}_{X}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{X}-\tilde{x}_{X}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}_{X}-\hat{x}_{X}\|^{2}+2\|\hat{x}_{X}-\tilde{x}_{X}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2r_{X}+2\|F_{X}^{\Theta}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})-F_{X}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}.

If
F_{X}^{\Theta}
is
\ell_{X}
-Lipschitz in its first argument (uniformly in
\mathbf{u}
), then

\displaystyle\|F_{X}^{\Theta}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})-F_{X}^{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}},\mathbf{u})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{X}^{2}\sum_{Z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}_{X}}\|\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{Z^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{Z^{\prime}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\ell_{X}^{2}\sum_{Z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}_{X}}\delta_{Z^{\prime}}.

Combining the last three results yields, for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\delta_{Z}\ \leq\ \sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\underbrace{\bigl(2L_{Z}^{2}\ \mathbf{1}\{X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}\}\bigr)}_{=:M_{Z,X}}\ r_{X}\ +\ \sum_{Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\underbrace{\bigl(2L_{Z}^{2}\ \mathbf{1}\{X\in\mathrm{Pa}_{Z}\}\bigr)}_{M_{Z,X}}\underbrace{\bigl(\ell_{X}^{2}\ \mathbf{1}\{Z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pa}_{X}\}\bigr)}_{=:N_{X,Z^{\prime}}}\ \delta_{Z^{\prime}}

In vector form:

\delta\leq Mr+(MN)\delta,

where
r=(r_{X})_{X\in\mathcal{X}}
and
\delta=(\delta_{Z})_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}
.

Expectation and nilpotency.

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\sim\mathbb{P}\\
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathop{\mathrm{P}r}\nolimits\end{subarray}}
preserves the componentwise inequality, giving

D\leq MR+(MN)D,

where
D_{Z}:=\mathbb{E}[\delta_{Z}]
and
R_{X}:=\mathbb{E}[r_{X}]
.

Because the underlying f-DAG is acyclic, the induced factor-level graph is acyclic, by Lemma
10
.

The matrix
MN
has support contained in this adjacency, hence is nilpotent:
(MN)^{\gamma}=0
for some
\gamma\leq|\mathcal{Z}|=m
. Therefore

(I-MN)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\gamma-1}(MN)^{k}

is well-defined and entrywise nonnegative.

Left-multiplying
(I-MN)D\leq MR
by
(I-MN)^{-1}\geq 0
preserves the order:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\leq(I-MN)^{-1}MR

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\gamma-1}(MN)^{k}MR

Summing coordinates and using any submultiplicative norm (e.g.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\delta_{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=\|D\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\|M\|_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\gamma-1}\|MN\|_{1}^{k}\Bigr)\cdot\|R\|_{1}

Relating
\|R\|_{1}
to the score
S(\Phi,\Theta)
.

Under the Gaussian conditional model used for
p_{\Theta}^{X}
(with variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}
),

\log p_{\Theta}^{X}(\mathbf{x}_{X}\mid\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{Pa}_{X}})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{X}^{2}}\|\mathbf{x}_{X}-\hat{x}_{X}\|^{2}-c_{X},\qquad c_{X}:=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{X}^{2}),

hence, taking
\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\sim\mathbb{P}\\
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathop{\mathrm{P}r}\nolimits\end{subarray}}
and summing over
X\notin\mathbf{I}
,

\displaystyle S(\Phi,\Theta)=-\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{X}^{2}}R_{X}-C_{0},\qquad C_{0}:=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathbb{P}}\Big[\sum_{X\notin\mathbf{I}}c_{X}\Big].

Let
\sigma_{\max}^{2}:=\max_{X}\sigma_{X}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\|R\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X}R_{X}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{\max}^{2}\sum_{X}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{X}^{2}}R_{X}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{\max}^{2}\bigl(-S(\Phi,\Theta)-C_{0}\bigr)

Combining with the previous bound on
\hat{\Delta}
gives

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}\leq 2\sigma_{\max}^{2}\ \|M\|_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\gamma-1}\|MN\|_{1}^{k}\ \bigl(-S(\Phi,\Theta)-C_{0}\bigr),

which is an affine bound in
S(\Phi,\Theta)
.
∎

Appendix C
Practical implementation details

C.1
Acyclicity constraint formulations

We implement the main differentiable formulations for acyclicity constraints, all of the form
h(A)=0\iff G
is a DAG. We consider
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{+}
, for which nilpotence is equivalent to DAGness of
G
.

Matrix exponential (tr-exp).
Introduced by
45
, this formulation defines:

h_{\text{exp}}(A)=\tr\bigl(e^{A}\bigr)-n

where
A
is the (possibly weighted)
n\times n
adjacency matrix. Computing
e^{A}
costs
O(n^{3})
.

Matrix power.
This formulation uses:

h_{\text{pow}}(A)=\tr\left[\left(I+\frac{A}{n}\right)^{n}\right]-n

which avoids the matrix exponential but retains the same
O(n^{3})
cost due to the matrix power computation.

Spectral radius.

Proposed by
[
25
]
, the spectral radius formulation enforces:

h_{\rho}(A)=\rho\bigl(A\bigr)

where
\rho(\cdot)
is the spectral radius (largest eigenvalue in modulus). The spectral radius is computed via power iteration with
T
steps, costing
O(Tn^{2})

Spectral radius adapted for the bipartite structure.

27

M=\begin{pmatrix}0&Q\\

2.

For

, then
x_{v}\leftarrow Q\,x_{f}/\|Q\,x_{f}\|
; symmetrically for

Compute the spectral radius estimate via the gradient formula:

\rho=\frac{y_{v}^{\top}Q\,x_{f}+y_{f}^{\top}R^{\top}x_{v}}{y_{f}^{\top}x_{f}+y_{v}^{\top}x_{v}}

with
\nabla_{Q}\rho=\frac{y_{v}x_{f}^{\top}}{y_{f}^{\top}x_{f}+y_{v}^{\top}x_{v}}

Layered DAG constraint.

When variables
X\in\mathcal{X}
are organized into
L
known layers (e.g., neurons in successive layers of a neural network), we can replace the general acyclicity constraint with a cheap structural constraint that also enforces this ordering constraint. Let
\ell(i)\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}
denote the layer of variable
i
. For the graph to be acyclic, it suffices that every factor has all its parents in strictly earlier layers than all its children:

\forall k\in[m],\quad\max_{i:Q_{ik}=1}\ell(i)<\min_{j:R_{jk}=1}\ell(j).

To make this differentiable, we aggregate the relaxed assignment matrices
Q,R\in(0,1)^{n\times m}
into per-layer masses for each factor
k
:

q_{kl}:=\sum_{i:\ell(i)=l}Q_{ik},\qquad r_{kl}:=\sum_{j:\ell(j)=l}R_{jk}.

This aggregation is computed in
O(nm)
time. We then compute for each factor
k
a violation score that measures the total mass of parent-child pairs violating the layer ordering:

b_{k}:=\sum_{l_{p}\geq l_{c}}q_{k,l_{p}}\cdot r_{k,l_{c}}.

This is evaluated efficiently using a cumulative sum: letting
\bar{r}_{k,l}:=\sum_{l^{\prime}\leq l}r_{k,l^{\prime}}
, we obtain
b_{k}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}q_{k,l}\cdot\bar{r}_{k,l}
in
O(mL)
time.
In the binary case,
b_{k}=0
is equivalent to the strict layer ordering; in the relaxed case,
b_{k}
continuously measures the degree of violation.

For scale stability, we optionally normalize by the total mass:

\bar{b}_{k}:=\frac{b_{k}}{\left(\sum_{l}q_{kl}\right)\left(\sum_{l}r_{kl}\right)+\epsilon}.

The constraint is formulated as the inequality
\bar{b}_{k}-\varepsilon\leq 0
for each factor
k
, with tolerance
\varepsilon>0
. The total cost is
O(nm+mL)
, i.e.
O(nm)
since
L\leq n
.

C.2
Causal graph sampling

During training, the graph
G(\Phi)
is sampled stochastically at each forward pass. We parameterize the bipartite structure through a single logit tensor
W(\Phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times 3}
, where for each variable-factor pair
(i,k)
the three categories encode: 0 for a parent edge (
Q_{ik}=1
, i.e., variable
i
is a parent of factor
k
), 1 for child edge (
R_{ik}=1
, i.e., variable
i
is a child of factor
k
), and 2 for no edge. Sampling is performed with the Gumbel-softmax trick
[
22
,
29
]
, using straight-through hard samples for the forward pass while allowing gradients to flow through the soft relaxation.

C.3
Anchor constraint

Recall that Assumption
4
requires each factor
Z
with index
k\in[m]
to have at least one in-anchor and one out-anchor. We formalize this as a differentiable constraint.

For in-anchors, the score measuring how exclusively variable
i
is assigned to factor
k
as a parent is:

s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k}=Q_{ik}\prod_{\ell\neq k}(1-Q_{i\ell}).

The in-anchor count for factor
k
is then
c^{\text{in}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k}
, and symmetrically
c^{\text{out}}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}s^{\text{out}}_{j\to k}
for out-anchors computed on
R
.

To avoid numerical underflow from the product of many terms close to 1, we compute the scores in log-space using the log-sum-exp decomposition:

S_{i}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\log(1-Q_{i\ell}+\epsilon),\qquad\log s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k}=\log(Q_{ik}+\epsilon)+S_{i}-\log(1-Q_{ik}+\epsilon),

where
\epsilon>0
is a small constant for numerical stability.
S_{i}
is the total log-complement sum across all factors, and subtracting the
k
-th term gives the product over
\ell\neq k
without an explicit loop. The anchor score is recovered as
s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k}=\exp(\log s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k})
, and the count is
c^{\text{in}}_{k}=\sum_{i}s^{\text{in}}_{i\to k}
.

This computation runs in
O(nm)
time for all factors simultaneously. The constraint is formulated as:

\mathcal{C}^{\text{anchor}}(\Phi):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left[\max\bigl(\tau-c^{\text{in}}_{k},0\bigr)+\max\bigl(\tau-c^{\text{out}}_{k},0\bigr)\right]

where
\tau\leq 1
is a threshold (we use
\tau=0.99
). In practice, we impose per-factor inequality constraints
\tau-c^{\text{in}}_{k}\leq 0
and
\tau-c^{\text{out}}_{k}\leq 0
within the Augmented Lagrangian framework.

Appendix D
Experiments

D.1
Implementation details

Our implementation heavily modifies the open source implementation of
27
, in particular in the way we solve the constrained optimization problem.
Our model is implemented in PyTorch
[
2
]
. Constrained optimization is handled by Cooper
[
17
]
, which provides an Augmented Lagrangian framework for differentiable constraints.

Training loop.

At each epoch, the model iterates over mini-batches of observational and interventional data. For each batch
(\mathbf{x},\text{mask},\text{regime})
:

1.

A graph
G^{\prime}
is sampled via Gumbel-softmax from the tensor
W(\Phi)
.

2.

The model computes predictions
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
by passing inputs through the sampled factor graph (variables
\to
factors via
F_{Z}^{\Theta}
, then factors
\to
variables via
F_{X}^{\Theta}
).

3.

The data-fitting loss (negative log-likelihood) is computed over non-intervened variables.

4.

Constraint violations (DAG or layer ordering, anchors,
L_{1}
sparsity) are evaluated.

5.

Cooper’s Augmented Lagrangian solver performs alternating primal-dual updates: the primal optimizer (Adam
[
24
]
) minimizes the Lagrangian, while the dual optimizer (SGD) updates the Lagrange multipliers.

Constrained optimization.

All constraints use an Augmented Lagrangian formulation with inequality constraints
\mathcal{C}(\Phi)\leq 0
.

Hyperparameters.

The primal learning rate is selected from
\{2\times 10^{-4},5\times 10^{-4},7\times 10^{-4},1\times 10^{-3},2\times 10^{-3}\}
depending on the setting.
The dual learning rate is set as
\frac{1}{5}
of the primal rate. The penalty coefficient
\rho_{c}
is grown at a multiplicative rate of
1.003
or
1.004
per epoch.
Batch size is
512
and training runs for up to
50\,000
epochs with early stopping: patience of
2000
epochs begins once all active constraints are satisfied. The
L_{1}
sparsity tolerance is set to
\text{tol}_{s}=0.2
.

Post-processing.

After training, the continuous logits
W(\Phi)
are discretized: for each variable-factor pair, we apply softmax over the three categories and assign the argmax direction (parent, child, or no edge), subject to a minimum confidence threshold on the total edge probability. Acyclicity of the recovered graph is then verified.

Model selection and evaluation.

For each experimental setting, we run a grid of hyperparameters (learning rate, penalty growth) over multiple random seeds and DAG instances. Model selection is performed on a validation set: among all runs that reach feasibility (all constraints satisfied), we select the one with the lowest validation negative log-likelihood. Test metrics are then computed on held-out observational data from the same DAG.

D.2
Metrics

We evaluate the quality of the learned causal abstraction by measuring factor recovery. Since factors are identifiable only up to reparameterization, we evaluate whether the learned factors
\hat{\mathbf{z}}=\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{x})
align with the ground-truth factors
\mathbf{z}
, using metrics invariant to the admissible symmetry class.

•

Mean correlation coefficient (MCC): finds the permutation alignment with highest absolute correlation between
\mathbf{z}
and
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
averaged over all factors. We report MCC with Pearson correlation (invariant to component-wise affine transformations), Spearman rank correlation (invariant to component-wise monotone transformations), and the Randomized Dependence Coefficient (RDC,
28
, invariant to component-wise nonlinear transformations).

•

Explicitness (
R^{2}
): for each ground-truth factor, we fit a linear regression (or a small MLP) from all learned factors and report the variance-explained
R^{2}
, averaged over factors.
R^{2}
-linear measures linear decodability;
R^{2}
-MLP measures nonlinear decodability. Contrary to MCC which is sensitive to linear mixing by a matrix,
R^{2}
is not.

•

Disentanglement, Completeness, Informativeness (DCI)
[
13
]
: fits gradient boosted trees from learned factors to each ground-truth factor, extracts feature importances, and computes entropy-based scores.
Disentanglement
measures whether each learned dimension captures at most one ground-truth factor;
Completeness
measures whether each ground-truth factor is captured by at most one learned dimension;
Informativeness
is the predictive
R^{2}
.

D.3
Synthetic experiments

Data generation.

We generate synthetic f-SCMs by first sampling a random bipartite DAG
G
over
n
variables and
m
factors. Edge probabilities are set so that the graph is moderately sparse (probability of an edge set to
0.1
). When anchors are desired, the graph generator enforces Assumption
4
by checking whether they are verified, otherwise the graph is resampled. When variables are organized into layers, factors are constrained to have parents in strictly earlier layers than children.

For each DAG, variable-to-factor mechanisms

[\pm 0.25,\pm 1]

activation and standard normal weights, i.e., each weight drawn i.i.d. from

0.4

Data are generated under hard stochastic interventions: a certain amount of interventional regimes target
1
to
3
variables each, plus one observational regime. Each dataset contains

D.4
Interpretability

We consider the problem of classifying whether an integer
n\in\{0,\dots,999,999\}
is divisible by
6
.
The input is the base-
10
representation
(d_{0},\dots,d_{K-1})\in\{0,\dots,9\}^{K}
with
K=6
digits (from ones to hundred-thousands), and the label is
y=\mathbf{1}[6\mid n]\in\{0,1\}
.
Each digit
d_{k}
is encoded as a
10
-dimensional one-hot vector, giving an input dimension of
K\times 10=60
.

We train a fully-connected MLP with architecture
[60,32,32,32,1]
: three hidden layers of
32
neurons with ReLU activations, followed by a single output logit trained with binary cross-entropy loss.
Training uses the Adam optimizer (learning rate
10^{-3}
, batch size
128
) with early stopping (patience
25
, up to
200
epochs) on an
80/20
train/test split of all
10^{6}
integers. The network reaches an almost perfect test accuracy.

We view the trained MLP as a low-level SCM
\mathcal{L}
whose endogenous variables
\mathcal{X}
are the post-ReLU activations of all hidden neurons, giving
n=|\mathcal{X}|=32+32+32=96
variables.
The causal graph
G_{L}
of
\mathcal{L}
is determined by the network connectivity: every neuron in hidden layer
\ell
is a parent of every neuron in hidden layer
\ell+1
(and the last hidden layer feeds into the logit), forming a strictly layered DAG with layer sizes
[32,32,32]
. The mechanisms
\mathcal{F}_{L}
are the trained affine maps composed with ReLU (or identity for the logit), and the context
\mathcal{U}_{L}
corresponds to the input
n
(encoded as one-hot).
Since the network is deterministic, the context (here, the input of the network) fully determines all activations.

To collect interventional data, we perform hard interventions on each of the
96
hidden neurons individually (excluding the output logit).
For each neuron
X_{j}
in hidden layer
\ell
, we clamp its post-ReLU activation to a value resampled from its empirical training distribution and record the resulting activations of all downstream neurons.
Concretely, for a subsample of
500
inputs per intervention, we perform a forward pass where
X_{j}
is replaced by a draw from its marginal:
X_{j}\leftarrow\tilde{x}_{j}
, with
\tilde{x}_{j}
sampled uniformly from the training-set values of
X_{j}
. This yields
96\times 500=48,000
interventional samples, combined with
200,000
observational samples (test-set forward passes) for a total of
248,000
samples.

Among the
248,000
samples, 80% are used to train UCAD, and 20% are used for validation and model selection.
We build a separate held-out observational set of
1,000
samples for test, on which we report the metrics below.

When applying UCAD we search over
m\in\{2,3\}
factors, and select the model with the lowest validation NLL among runs that achieved constraint feasibility (DAG, anchor, and sparsity constraints).
We evaluate the learned factors against
positive
concepts:
c_{2}=\mathbf{1}[2\mid n]
,
c_{3}=\mathbf{1}[3\mid n]
,
c_{6}=\mathbf{1}[6\mid n]
, and
\tilde{c}_{3}=\sum_{k}d_{k}
(the sum of all digits, an intermediate quantity involved in the intuitive computation of divisibility by
3
); and
negative
controls:
\mathbf{1}[5\mid n]
,
\mathbf{1}[7\mid n]
,
\mathbf{1}[11\mid n]
.
Results are reported in Table
14
below.

We measure a stronger reconstruction of hypothesized concepts than with negative controls (average MCC-RDC of

These results remain difficult to interpret, keeping in mind that our intuitive method to compute
c_{3}
is recursive.
Furthermore, a low agreement with some concept may not necessarily indicate a failure of UCAD, but could simply mean that the DNN implements a different strategy to solve the problem, for example a heuristic that works only up to 1 million that might sometimes rely on divisibility by
5
for some reason. This illustrates the difficulty of validating such a method: we look for tasks where we expect the DNN to implement a given logic, but our method might unveil that the network implements a different algorithm compared to what was expected. Thus, more work is required to interpret the factors that were learned.
