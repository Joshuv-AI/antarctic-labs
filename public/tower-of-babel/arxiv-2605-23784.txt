Reconstruction methods for inverse scattering problems with phaseless data
John C. Schotland, Shenwen Yu

Abstract
. We investigate phaseless inverse scattering problem for the Schrödinger equation and develop reconstruction methods based on the inverse Born series (IBS). We consider three types of phaseless data: the far-field total field, the total field and the far-field scattered field. For phaseless total-field data, we extend the IBS framework and analyze its radius of convergence. In the far-field setting, we propose a Fourier-based reconstruction method that exploits the scattering reciprocity between incident and observation directions to recover Fourier coefficients of the scattering potential. For phaseless scattered-field data, we employ a polarization-based approach to recover phase information and enable IBS reconstruction. Numerical experiments are conducted to validate the proposed methods.



1.
Introduction

We consider the Schrödinger equation

-\Delta u+V(\bm{x})u=k^{2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the field
u
is generally complex–valued and the potential
V\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
.
The scattered field
u_{s}
is taken to obey the radiation condition

\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left(\frac{\partial u_{s}}{\partial r}-\mathrm{i}k\,u_{s}\right)=0,

where
r=|\bm{x}|
. This paper is concerned with the inverse problem of recovering
V
from
phaseless measurements. We consider several variants of this problem, with data consisting measurements of
|u|
, as well as far-field measurements of
|u|
and
|u_{s}|
.

Inverse scattering problems play a central role in nearly every branch of
physics. A comprehensive theory is available for the conventional problem
in which both the modulus and phase of the field is known
[
5
,
12
]
. In many practical applications, however, phase information is difficult or even impossible to acquire, and one has access only to intensity measurements. This gives
rise to phaseless inverse scattering problems. We emphasize that the phaseless setting is significantly more challenging than conventional inverse scattering problems due to the sublinear nature of the absolute–value function and related matters of non-uniqueness.

Historically, the study of phaseless inverse problems can be traced to the
one-dimensional setting, where uniqueness and reconstruction results were
first established. See, for example, Refs.
[
22
,
23
,
20
,
16
,
13
,
2
]
.
In higher dimensions, several classes of phaseless problems can
be distinguished. For the case of the phaseless total field, uniqueness results and numerical methods have been established
[
19
,
18
,
15
]
.
In addition, high-frequency
approximations have been studied
in
[
14
]
.

A second approach focuses on the phaseless scattered
field. A common strategy is to make use of superpositions of incident waves, including plane waves and
point sources. Related uniqueness results for such problems have been reported
in
[
30
,
27
,
25
,
21
]
.
Related work on numerical methods includes boundary integral equation
approaches
[
29
]
, optimization
[
28
]
.
A third approach is to introduce a reference scatterer into the system.
Uniqueness results can be found
in
[
33
,
32
]
,
and numerical methods in
[
8
,
1
,
31
,
10
]
.
Finally, we note that the optical theorem provides an alternative approach to the phaseless inverse problem
[
9
,
4
]
. This approach has been applied to inverse problems with incident fields consisting of a superposition of plane waves and to problems in which a reference scatterer is present.

In this work, we investigate reconstruction methods for two phaseless inverse scattering
problems. The first problem assumes that phaseless measurements of the total
field are available, while the second is concerned with phaseless measurements of the scattered
field. Both problems are treated within the framework of the
inverse Born series (IBS), which expresses the solution to the inverse problem as an explicitly computable functional of the measured data. We note that this method has been extensively studied in the context of conventional inverse problems where the phase of the field is assumed to be known. See
[
17
]
for a review of the relevant literature.

The main contributions of this work are as follows.

(1)

We extend the IBS to treat phaseless
measurements.

(2)

For far-field phaseless total-field data, we propose a
Fourier-based reconstruction method that exploits the symmetry
between incident and observation directions.

(3)

For far-field phaseless scattered-field data, we employ a
polarization-based approach to recover phase information.

(4)

For each of the above problems, we analyze the convergence of the IBS.

(5)

The proposed reconstruction methods are tested with numerical experiments.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section
2
, we introduce the definition and analysis of the IBS. In
Section
3
, we derive the inversion formulas and analyze
the convergence of the Born series and the IBS for phaseless
total-field data. We also provide a justification of the Fourier
method. In Section
4
, we study the phaseless scattered-field
problem and analyze the polarization method. In
Sections
5
and
6
, we present the numerical
algorithms and demonstrate their performance in numerical experiments.
Finally, our conclusions are presented in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Far-field Approximation

The total field is written as

u(\bm{x})=u_{0}(\bm{x})+u_{s}(\bm{x}),

where
u_{0}
is the incident field and
u_{s}
is the scattered field.
The incident field obeys Eq. (
1
) in the absence of
the scatterer. The scattered field obeys

-\Delta u_{s}+V(\bm{x})u_{s}=k^{2}u.

It follows that
u
obeys the Lippmann–Schwinger equation

u(\bm{x})=u_{0}(\bm{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})V(\bm{y})u(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},

where
G(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is the free-space Green’s function
[
5
]
.

The Green’s function is given by

\displaystyle G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|),\quad d=2,

\displaystyle G(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|}}{4\pi|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|},\quad d=3.

When
|\bm{x}|\gg|y|
, the Green’s function admits the asymptotic expansion

G(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim C_{d}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}e^{-\mathrm{i}k\,\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{y}},

where
\hat{\bm{x}}=x/|\bm{x}|
and

C_{d}=2^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\pi^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\exp\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}(d-3)\right).

Consequently, the scattered field has the far-field asymptotic form

u_{s}(\bm{x})\sim C_{d}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}A(\hat{\bm{x}}),

where the scattering amplitude is given by

A(\hat{\bm{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathrm{i}k\,\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{y}}V(\bm{y})u(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}.

(1)

In the Born approximation, we replace the total field
u
in (
1
) by the incident field
u_{0}(\bm{y})=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{y}}
. This yields

A_{B}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathrm{i}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{y}}V(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},

where

\bm{k}=k\hat{\bm{x}},\quad|\bm{k}|=|\bm{k}^{\prime}|=k.

Therefore,

A_{B}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})=\widehat{V}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}),

where
\widehat{V}
denotes the Fourier transform of the potential
V
. In particular, we note that

\widehat{V}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}\geq 0.

2.2.
Inverse Born Series

We employ IBS in the form described in
[
11
]
.
Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces and let
K_{m}:X^{m}\rightarrow Y
be
m
-multilinear operators for
m\geq 1
. Consider the operator
\mathcal{F}:X\rightarrow Y
defined by the forward series

\mathcal{F}[\eta]=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}K_{m}(\eta,\ldots,\eta).

(2)

The
K_{m}
are referred to as forward operators. The forward problem consists of evaluating the map
\mathcal{F}:\eta\mapsto\phi
for
\eta\in X
and
\phi\in Y
.

The inverse problem is to recover
\eta
from
\phi
. To this end, we seek an operator
\mathcal{I}:Y\rightarrow X
which acts as an inverse of
\mathcal{F}
in an appropriate sense. Formally,
\mathcal{I}
is defined by the inverse series

\mathcal{I}[\phi]=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{m}(\phi).

(3)

By substituting (
3
) into (
2
),
it can be shown in
[
11
]
that the inverse operators
\{\mathcal{K}_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
are defined recursively by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}(\psi_{s})

\displaystyle=K_{1}^{+}(\psi_{s}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2}(\psi_{s})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{K}_{1}\!\left(K_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{1}(\psi_{s}),\mathcal{K}_{1}(\psi_{s})\right)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}(\psi_{s})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=2}^{m}\sum_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}=m}\mathcal{K}_{1}K_{n}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{i_{1}}(\psi_{s}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{i_{n}}(\psi_{s})\right).

Here
K_{1}^{+}
denotes the bounded inverse of
K_{1}
when it exists, and otherwise its pseudoinverse.

We next recall the convergence radius and an error estimate for the IBS as described in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be positive constants. Suppose that

\|K_{m}(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m})\|_{Y}\leq\nu\mu^{m-1}\|\eta_{1}\|_{X}\cdots\|\eta_{m}\|_{X},\quad m=1,2,\ldots.

Then the IBS converges if
\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\phi\|_{X}<r
, where the radius of convergence
r
is given by

r=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left[\sqrt{16C^{2}+1}-4C\right],

with

C=\max\{2,\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\|\nu\}.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{K}_{1}\phi\in B_{r}
, then the inverse operator
\mathcal{I}
maps
B_{r}
into
B_{r_{0}}
with

r_{0}=\frac{2\mu}{\sqrt{16C^{2}+1}}.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
hold and that both the forward Born series and the IBS converge. Let
\tilde{n}
denote the sum of the IBS and define
n_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\psi_{s}
. Set

\mathcal{M}:=\max\{\|n\|_{X},\|\tilde{n}\|_{X}\},

and assume that

\mathcal{M}<\frac{1}{\mu}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\nu\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\|}{1+\nu\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\|}}\right).

Then the approximation error satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|\eta-\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}_{m}(\psi_{s})\right\|_{X}\leq

\displaystyle M\left(\frac{\|n_{1}\|_{X}}{r}\right)^{N+1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\|n_{1}\|_{X}}{r}}

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{\nu\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\|}{(1-\mu\mathcal{M})^{2}}+\nu\|\mathcal{K}_{1}\|\right)^{-1}\|(I-\mathcal{K}_{1}K_{1})n\|_{X},

where

M=\frac{2\mu}{\sqrt{16C^{2}+1}}.

3.
Phaseless Total Field

In this section we consider the inverse problem when phaseless measurements of the total field are available. We first derive the Born series and the corresponding IBS in this setting.
This framework applies to both general and far-field measurements.
When only far-field data are available, we further introduce a Fourier-based method to efficiently solve the linearized problem. This step plays a crucial role in the practical implementation of the IBS.

3.1.
Born Series

Suppose that only the magnitude of the total field
|u|
is measured.
We introduce the Born series expansion

u=u_{0}+u_{1}+u_{2}+\cdots,

(4)

where

u_{m}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{1})V(\bm{y}_{1})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})V(\bm{y}_{2})\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{y}_{m-1},\bm{y}_{m})V(\bm{y}_{m})u_{0}(\bm{y}_{m})\,d\bm{y}_{1}\cdots d\bm{y}_{m}.

The Born series expansion is obtained by iterating the Lippmann–Schwinger equation. This yields:

u=K^{p}_{1}(V)+K^{p}_{2}(V,V)+\cdots,

(5)

where

K^{p}_{m}(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{1})V_{1}(\bm{y}_{1})\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\bm{y}_{m-1},\bm{y}_{m})V_{m}(\bm{y}_{m})u_{0}(\bm{y}_{m})\,d\bm{y}_{1}\cdots d\bm{y}_{m}.

(6)

To obtain the Born series for phaseless data, we expand the squared magnitude of the field, which yields

|u|^{2}-|u_{0}|^{2}=K_{1}(V)+K_{2}(V,V)+\cdots,

(7)

where

K_{m}(V,\dots,V)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}u_{j}\,u_{m-j}^{*}.

(8)

It will prove useful to introduce a sequence of potentials
(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})
.
For
i\geq 1
, define

u_{i}(V_{1},\dots,V_{i})(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{1})V_{1}(\bm{y}_{1})\cdots\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{y}_{i-1},\bm{y}_{i})V_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})u_{0}(\bm{y}_{i})\,d\bm{y}_{i}\cdots d\bm{y}_{1},

and set
u_{0}(V_{1},\dots,V_{0})\equiv u_{0}
. Here we assume that
V
is compactly supported in
\Omega
.
Then the
n
th-order multilinear term in the forward series is

K_{n}(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})(\bm{x}_{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}u_{i}(V_{1},\dots,V_{i})(\bm{x}_{d})\,u^{*}_{n-i}(V_{i+1},\dots,V_{n})(\bm{x}_{d}),

where by convention
u_{0}(V_{n+1},\dots,V_{n})\equiv u_{0}
.

Suppose that there are
l
incident waves.
For any multilinear operator
K
of order
n
on
\left(L^{\infty}(\Omega)\right)^{n}
, we define

|K|_{\infty}=\sup_{V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\neq 0}\frac{\left\|K\left(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\right)\right\|_{\left(C(\omega)\right)^{l}}}{\left\|V_{1}\right\|_{\infty}\cdots\left\|V_{n}\right\|_{\infty}}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Define

\mu_{0}=\sup_{x\in\Omega\cup\omega}\int_{\Omega}|G(\bm{x},\bm{y})|\,d\bm{y}.

Then

|K_{m}|_{\infty}\leq(m+1)\mu_{0}^{m}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}.

Furthermore, assume that
d=2
and the spatial domain
\Omega
is contained in a disk with radius
R>1
. Then we have the estimate:

\mu_{0}\leq\frac{k^{2}}{4}\left(I(k)+\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{k}}\big(R^{3/2}-1\big)\right)

where

I(k):=\begin{cases}\pi+2\left(\gamma+\log\frac{k}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+\dfrac{8}{k^{2}}e^{-2\gamma},&k\;\geq\;2e^{-\gamma},\\[11.99998pt]
(\pi+1)-2\gamma-2\log\frac{k}{2},&k\;<\;2e^{-\gamma},\end{cases}

and
\gamma
is the Euler constant.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu=2\mu_{0}
and
\nu=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}
.
Then,

|K_{m}|_{\infty}\leq\mu^{m}\nu,\quad m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

The proofs of the above results follow directly from the definitions of
u_{m}
and
K_{m}
, together with repeated applications of the triangle inequality, and are therefore omitted.

Remark 1
.

For inverse scattering with phase information, the corresponding bounds for the Born series are
\mu=\mu_{0}
and
\nu=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. Detailed calculations for
\mu_{0}
in 2 and 3 dimensional space can be found in
[
11
,
24
]
.

Since the operator
K_{1}
is explicitly defined in (
8
), its pseudoinverse
\mathcal{K}_{1}
can be implemented numerically, following the approach in
[
6
,
7
]
.

3.2.
Fourier Method

We now introduce a Fourier-based approach for constructing
\mathcal{K}_{1}
from far-field data. The key idea is to recover the Fourier coefficients of the potential
V
by exchanging the incident and observation directions. It follows from (
7
) that the linear term in the forward series is given by

u_{0}u_{1}^{*}+u_{1}u_{0}^{*}.

Define

D(\bm{x},\bm{k}^{\prime})=u_{0}(\bm{x})u_{1}(\bm{x})^{*}+u_{1}(\bm{x})u_{0}(\bm{x})^{*},\quad u_{0}(\bm{x})=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}}.

Using the far-field approximation (
1
) and substituting the expression for
u_{1}
, with
\bm{k}^{\prime}=k\hat{\bm{y}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle D(\bm{x},\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}}C^{*}_{d}\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}^{*}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})+e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}}C_{d}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}}C_{d}^{*}\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{k})+e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}}C_{d}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|\bm{x}|}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}).

Writing
\bm{x}=r_{x}\hat{\bm{x}}
gives

\displaystyle D(\bm{x},\bm{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C_{d}^{*}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{x}(\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}}-1)}}{r_{x}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{y}}-\hat{\bm{x}}))+C_{d}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{x}(1-\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})}}{r_{x}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{x}}-\hat{\bm{y}})).

Thus

D(\bm{x},k\hat{\bm{y}})\,r_{x}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}=C_{d}^{*}e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{x}(\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}}-1)}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{y}}-\hat{\bm{x}}))+C_{d}e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{x}(1-\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{x}}-\hat{\bm{y}})).

(9)

Now choose another measurement point
\bm{y}\in\partial\Omega
, with
\bm{y}=r_{y}\hat{\bm{y}}
, and take the incident wave to be

u_{0}(\bm{z})=e^{\mathrm{i}k\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{z}}.

That is, we interchange the direction of incidence and the observation direction, and obtain

D(\bm{y},k\hat{\bm{x}})\,r_{y}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}=C_{d}^{*}e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{y}(\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}}-1)}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{x}}-\hat{\bm{y}}))+C_{d}e^{\mathrm{i}kr_{y}(1-\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})}\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{y}}-\hat{\bm{x}})).

(10)

Combining (
9
) and (
10
) yields a linear system that allows us to recover

\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{x}}-\hat{\bm{y}}))
and
\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{y}}-\hat{\bm{x}}))

from the measurements
D(\bm{x},k\hat{\bm{y}})
and
D(\bm{y},k\hat{\bm{x}})
, provided that

(C^{*}_{d})^{2}e^{\mathrm{i}k(r_{x}+r_{y})(\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}}-1)}\neq C_{d}^{2}e^{\mathrm{i}k(r_{x}+r_{y})(1-\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{y}})}.

From the definition of
C_{d}
we obtain

\frac{C_{d}^{2}}{(C^{*}_{d})^{2}}=\begin{cases}-1,&2\mid d,\\
1,&2\nmid d.\end{cases}

Using this procedure, we can recover the Fourier coefficients of
V
at a collection of frequencies, after which standard reconstruction techniques can be applied to reconstruct
V
.

We remark that the linear system arising from (
9
) and (
10
) may become severely ill-conditioned. To ensure numerical stability, the corresponding frequencies are discarded in such cases. This step is incorporated into the numerical algorithm.

4.
Phaseless scattered field

In this section, we consider the case where phaseless measurements of the scattered field are available.
Since
|\widehat{V}|
alone does not uniquely determine
V
, additional data are required. To overcome this difficulty, we consider incident fields of the form

u_{0}(\bm{x})=e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{x}}+ae^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}_{2}\cdot\bm{x}},

where

|\bm{k}_{1}|=|\bm{k}_{2}|=k,\quad a\in\{\pm 1,\pm\mathrm{i}\}.

It follows that the scattering amplitude in the Born approximation is given by

A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})=\widehat{V}(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2})+a\widehat{V}(0).

Using the polarization identity

\displaystyle z_{1}z_{2}^{*}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(|z_{1}+z_{2}|^{2}-|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}+\mathrm{i}|z_{1}+\mathrm{i}z_{2}|^{2}-\mathrm{i}|z_{1}-\mathrm{i}z_{2}|^{2}\Big),\quad z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}

and setting

z_{1}=A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2}),\quad z_{2}=A_{B}(0,0)\in\mathbb{R},

we obtain

\displaystyle A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})\,\widehat{V}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\Big(

\displaystyle|A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})+A_{B}(0,0)|^{2}-|A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})-A_{B}(0,0)|^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}|A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})+\mathrm{i}A_{B}(0,0)|^{2}-\mathrm{i}|A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})-\mathrm{i}A_{B}(0,0)|^{2}\Big).

Therefore,
A_{B}(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2})
can be determined by performing four experiments corresponding to the choices
a\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
. Once
A_{B}
is obtained, the potential
V
can be reconstructed in the usual way via the inverse Fourier transform:

V(\bm{x})=\int_{|\bm{k}|\leq 2k}\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\widehat{V}_{B}(\bm{k}).

Remark 2
.

This method differs from conventional phase retrieval, whose goal is to recover the missing phase of the measured data, a problem that is severely ill-posed, as discussed in
[
3
]
. Rather than recovering the phase explicitly, we incorporate additional physical measurements, namely data generated by superpositions of incident waves. While these measurements may increase the practical difficulty of data acquisition, they markedly improve the stability of the reconstruction.

5.
Numerical Algorithms

In this section, we present the details of our numerical algorithms, including both the forward and inverse solvers.

5.1.
Forward Solver

Following standard procedures
[
26
]
,
we introduce the auxiliary field

\sigma(\bm{x})=k^{2}V(\bm{x})\,u(\bm{x}).

The scattered field can then be represented as the convolution

u_{s}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,\sigma(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}.

(11)

Since
V
is compactly supported in
\Omega
, the density
\sigma
has the same support.
Substituting (
11
) into the definition of
\sigma
yields the Lippmann–Schwinger equation

\sigma(\bm{x})-k^{2}V(\bm{x})\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,\sigma(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}=k^{2}V(\bm{x})\,u_{i}(\bm{x}).

(12)

Once
\sigma
is obtained, the scattered field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be evaluated via (
11
).

Since
V
is compactly supported, we restrict computations to a box

\Omega=[-L,L]^{d}

containing
\mathrm{supp}(V)
and discretize it by a uniform Cartesian grid.
Let
\{x_{j}\}
be the grid points and denote
\sigma_{j}=\sigma(\bm{x}_{j})
,
V_{j}=V(\bm{x}_{j})
.
Discretization of (
12
) leads to the linear system

\sigma_{j}-k^{2}V_{j}\sum_{k}G(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{k})\,\sigma_{k}=k^{2}V_{j}\,u_{i}(\bm{x}_{j}).

(13)

The discrete integral operator in (
13
) has a convolution structure, and the associated matrix is Toeplitz .
Therefore, matrix–vector products can be evaluated efficiently using FFT-based convolution.
The linear system (
13
) is then solved using GMRES.

5.2.
Inverse Solver

5.2.1.
Efficient computation of the operators
K_{n}

Recall that the operator
K_{n}
is defined by

K_{n}(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}u_{j}(V_{1},\dots,V_{j})\,u^{*}_{n-j}(V_{j+1},\dots,V_{n}),

where

u_{j}(V_{1},\dots,V_{j})(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{1})V_{1}(\bm{y}_{1})\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})V_{2}(\bm{y}_{2})\cdots\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{y}_{j-1},\bm{y}_{j})V_{j}(\bm{y}_{j})u_{0}(\bm{y}_{j})\,d\bm{y}_{1}\cdots d\bm{y}_{j}.

A direct evaluation of these nested integrals is computationally expensive.
In particular, computing
u_{j}(V_{1},\dots,V_{j})
independently for each
j
requires on the order of

1+2+\cdots+n=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})

convolution operations.
To reduce this cost, we exploit the recursive structure of the Born terms and reuse intermediate results.
Although
u_{j}(V_{1},\dots,V_{j})
is written with potentials ordered from
V_{1}
to
V_{j}
, the numerical evaluation proceeds naturally from the innermost layer outward.
We therefore introduce a prefix recursion:

P_{0}=u_{0},\quad P_{k}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,V_{k}(\bm{y})\,P_{k-1}(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},\quad k=1,\dots,n.

It follows that

P_{k}=u_{k}(V_{k},V_{k-1},\dots,V_{1}).

That is,
P_{k}
corresponds to the
k
th Born term with the reverse ordering of the potentials.
Similarly, we define a suffix recursion:

T_{n}=u_{0},\quad T_{k}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}G(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,V_{k+1}(\bm{y})\,T_{k+1}(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},\quad k=n-1,\dots,0,

which satisfies

T_{k}=u_{n-k}(V_{k+1},\dots,V_{n}).

With these intermediate fields, the operator
K_{n}
can be evaluated as

K_{n}(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}P_{j}\,T_{j}^{*}.

We note that the use of reversed prefix recursion does not affect the inverse Born series.
Indeed, the IBS involves a summation over all compositions
(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})
of
m
,

\mathcal{K}_{m}(\varphi)=-\sum_{\ell=2}^{m}\sum_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{\ell}=m}\mathcal{K}_{1}K_{\ell}\big(\mathcal{K}_{i_{1}}(\varphi),\dots,\mathcal{K}_{i_{\ell}}(\varphi)\big).

Whenever a composition
(i_{1},\dots,i_{j},i_{j+1},\dots,i_{\ell})
appears in this summation, the reversed ordering
(i_{j},\dots,i_{1},i_{j+1},\dots,i_{\ell})
also appears.
Thus, evaluating the Born terms in reversed order amounts to a reorganization of the same set of contributions, while enabling the reuse of intermediate fields.
Consequently, after computing
\{P_{k}\}
and
\{T_{k}\}
once, evaluating
K_{n}
requires only
2n
convolutions, rather than
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.
Therefore, the complexity of evaluating
K_{n}
is reduced from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(n)
convolution operations.

5.2.2.
Recovering
V
from nonuniform Fourier samples

In the weak scattering regime, the measured far-field data can be converted (via the corresponding linearized inverse map) into a collection of Fourier samples of the potential,

\widehat{V}(\bm{p}_{j})\approx\phi_{j},\quad\bm{p}_{j}\in P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \ j=1,\dots,|P|,

where
P=\{\bm{p}_{j}\}
is a nonuniform set of frequencies contained in a ball such as
|p|\leq 2k
.
To reconstruct
V
on a spatial grid, let

X=\{x_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{|X|}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

be the set of recovery locations, the uniform grid points in the computational domain.
We discretize
V
as

v=\bigl(V(\bm{x}_{\ell})\bigr)_{\ell=1}^{|X|}\in\mathbb{C}^{|X|},\quad\phi=\bigl(\phi_{j}\bigr)_{j=1}^{|P|}\in\mathbb{C}^{|P|}.

Define the nonuniform Fourier transform (NUFFT) operator
\mathcal{F}_{X,P}:\mathbb{C}^{|X|}\to\mathbb{C}^{|P|}
by

\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{X,P}(v)\bigr)_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{|X|}v_{\ell}\,e^{-\,\mathrm{i}\,\bm{x}_{\ell}\cdot\bm{p}_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,|P|.

(14)

Its adjoint
\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}:\mathbb{C}^{|P|}\to\mathbb{C}^{|X|}
is defined by

\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}(\phi)\bigr)_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{|P|}\phi_{j}\,e^{\,\mathrm{i}\,\bm{x}_{\ell}\cdot\bm{p}_{j}},\quad\ell=1,\dots,|X|.

(15)

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{F}_{X,P}
and is not, in general, its inverse, since the sampling set
P
is nonuniform and the system is typically overdetermined.

We recover
v
by solving the least-squares problem

\min_{v\in\mathbb{C}^{|\bm{x}|}}\ \|\mathcal{F}_{X,P}(v)-\phi\|_{2}^{2},

(16)

optionally with Tikhonov regularization,

\min_{v\in\mathbb{C}^{|X|}}\ \|\mathcal{F}_{X,P}(v)-\phi\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

(17)

The normal equations associated with (
17
) are

\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{X,P}+\lambda^{2}I\bigr)v=\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}\phi.

(18)

We solve (
18
) iteratively using GMRES.
Each application of the normal operator

v\ \mapsto\ \mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{X,P}(v)\bigr)

is evaluated efficiently by two NUFFT calls: one forward NUFFT for
\mathcal{F}_{X,P}
and one adjoint NUFFT for
\mathcal{F}_{P,X}^{*}
.
Thus, one GMRES iteration costs
\mathcal{O}((|X|+|P|)\log(|X|+|P|))
, rather than
\mathcal{O}(|X||P|)
for direct evaluation.

5.2.3.
Comparison of numerical algorithms

We summarize the reconstruction procedures for the three types of measurement data considered in this work. In all cases, the reconstruction of phaseless data is performed using the IBS, while the main differences for the problems considered lie in how the linear operator
\mathcal{K}_{1}
is constructed from the measurements.

When phase information is available, the standard complex-valued Born series defined in (
4
) is used.
In this situation, there is no essential difference between measuring the total field and the scattered field.
For general measurements,
\mathcal{K}_{1}^{p}
is implemented as a regularized pseudoinverse of the first-order Born operator
K_{1}^{p}
, defined in (
5
) and (
6
), similar to
[
6
]
.
For far-field measurements, the data are first converted into Fourier samples of the potential using the far-field approximation (Section
2.1
), and the potential is reconstructed through the NUFFT-based least-squares inversion described in Section
5.2.2
.

When only phaseless measurements of the total field are available, we employ the phaseless Born series (
7
). In this case the data are first converted into the quantity
\psi=|u|^{2}-|u_{0}|^{2}
, from which the linear operator
K_{1}
is constructed.
For general data, a numerical pseudoinverse of
K_{1}
is used to obtain
\mathcal{K}_{1}
.
For far-field data, we first discard Fourier samples

\widehat{V}(k(\hat{\bm{x}}-\hat{\bm{y}}))
, referred to as
“bad” samples, whenever the linear systems in
(
9
) and (
10
) are ill-conditioned.
The filtering criterion is based on the condition number of the associated

2\times 2
system: samples are discarded if this condition number exceeds
a prescribed threshold. Since the sum of the squared singular values of this
matrix is fixed, ill-conditioning occurs precisely when the smaller singular
value becomes close to zero. In practice, we therefore discard direction pairs
for which the smaller singular value is below
10^{-2}
.
Then, the Fourier method described in Section
3.2
is used to recover the Fourier coefficients of the potential, which are subsequently inverted via the NUFFT-based reconstruction in Section
5.2.2
.

Finally, when only phaseless measurements of the scattered field are available, we first apply the polarization method (Section
4
) to construct an approximate potential
V^{a}
.
This approximation is then used to infer an approximate phase of the scattered data.
The reconstructed complex-valued data are subsequently treated as phase data, and the standard IBS procedure described above is applied.

The complete reconstruction procedures are summarized below.

•

Algorithm 1: Data with phase

(1)

Collect the complex-valued measurements.

(2)

Construct the first-order Born operator
K_{1}^{p}
.

(3)

general data: compute a regularized pseudoinverse of
K_{1}^{p}
to obtain
\mathcal{K}_{1}^{p}
.

Far-field data: convert the measurements to Fourier samples of
V
using the far-field approximation and construct
\mathcal{K}_{1}
via the NUFFT-based least-squares inversion.

(4)

Apply the inverse Born series

V\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{K}_{m}(u_{s}).

•

Algorithm 2: Phaseless data of the total field

(1)

Measure the phaseless total-field data
|u|
.

(2)

Compute the data difference

\psi=|u|^{2}-|u_{0}|^{2}.

(3)

Construct the linear operator
K_{1}
associated with the Born expansion.

(4)

General data: compute a numerical pseudoinverse of
K_{1}
to obtain
\mathcal{K}_{1}
.

Far-field data: Discard “bad” Fourier samples; recover the Fourier coefficients of
V
via the Fourier method; and construct
\mathcal{K}_{1}
via the NUFFT-based least-squares inversion.

(5)

Apply the inverse Born series using
\{\mathcal{K}_{m}\}
to reconstruct
V
.

•

Algorithm 3: Phaseless data of the scattered field

(1)

Measure the phaseless scattered-field data
|u_{s}|
.

(2)

Apply the polarization identity to obtain an approximate potential
V^{a}
.

(3)

Use
V^{a}
to compute the scattered field
u_{s}^{a}
.
Construct an approximate complex-valued dataset by combining the phase of
u_{s}^{a}
with the measured magnitude
|u_{s}|
:

u_{s}^{\mathrm{rec}}=\frac{u_{s}^{a}}{|u_{s}^{a}|}\,|u_{s}|.

(4)

Treat
u_{s}^{\mathrm{rec}}
as complex-valued measurements and apply Algorithm 1 to perform the IBS reconstruction.

7.
Conclusion

We have investigated the inverse scattering problem with phaseless data using the IBS method.
We present three numerical methods for different types of data: the phaseless total field, the far-field phaseless total field, and the far-field phaseless scattered field.
We analyzed the convergence of the IBS in all cases. For the phaseless IBS with total-field intensity data, we also develop an efficient recursive implementation of the multilinear operators
K_{n}
, reducing the cost of evaluating each
K_{n}
from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(n)
convolution operations.
Numerical experiments were conducted to validate the methods.
We also compare these methods with each other, as well as with the corresponding cases when phase data are available.

We have found that all the methods perform well for low-contrast potentials and fail for high-contrast potentials, which is consistent with the analysis of the IBS.
We also find that, in general, reconstructions from phase data are more accurate than those from phaseless data and the corresponding IBS exhibit a larger radius of convergence.
Finally, the method for the far-field phaseless scattered field is superior to that for the far-field phaseless total field. This follows because the scattered-field setting contains more informative data, while the total-field approach requires discarding part of the Fourier information.

In future work, we plan to investigate other phaseless inverse scattering problems. These the wave equations of elasticity and the nonlocal equations that arise in optics.
