Substitution of Aromatic Oil with Palm Oil in Natural Rubber/Silica Composites via Latex Processing
Authors: Hassarutai Yangthong, Papawarin Udomsin, Phakamat Lim-arun, Supitta Suethao, Pornsiri Kaewpradit, Pairote Jittham, Sedthawatt Sucharitpwatskul, Karine Mougin, Arnaud Spangenberg, Antoine Le Duigou, Kheng Lim Goh, Wirasak Smitthipong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rubber materials incorporating vegetable oils are being developed as sustainable alternatives to conventional petroleum-derived plasticizers. In this study, palm oil (PO) was used as a green processing oil for natural rubber (NR) composites, and aromatic oil (AO) was included for comparative analysis. The preparation of rubber material via the latex method, with and without vulcanizing agents, was also compared to the conventional preparation method. The test results showed that replacing AO with PO in the latex method yielded a similar cure rate index. The Payne effect, indicating the extent of filler–rubber interaction via the latex method, was 182 kPa for AO and 171 kPa for PO. The mechanical properties in terms of the Young’s modulus of the PO-based composites were higher than those of the AO-based counterparts. Furthermore, the PO-based composites exhibited the smallest tan δ value at 60 °C (0.05), indicating the lowest rolling resistance. Life cycle assessment results showed that the PO-based natural rubber latex reduces fossil fuel depletion, human toxicity, and ozone layer depletion. Moreover, the latex method reduces human toxicity compared to the conventional method. Substituting PO in place of AO in rubber compounds serves as a suitable alternative plasticizer for rubber products.

Introduction

Natural rubber (NR) plays a crucial role in transportation, medicine, construction, and other sectors due to its excellent resilience, elasticity, and durability. Silica is commonly incorporated into NR compounds to improve tensile strength, abrasion resistance, skid resistance, rolling resistance, and wet traction in tire applications. − When silica is added in high amounts, plasticizers are needed to lower the viscosity of the compound to facilitate processing and more evenly disperse the filler. , A processing oil for NR compounds is important for enhancing the tensile strength, tear resistance, and aging characteristics of the final product for a variety of applications. Different types of oils, including aromatic and nonaromatic varieties, serve specific functions that influence the rubber properties. Environmental considerations are also important in the selection of processing oils, as industries are subject to regulations on volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions.

Mineral oil, particularly distillate aromatic extract (DAE), has been widely used as a rubber processing oil for decades due to its excellent compatibility with various types of rubber and its low cost. However, DAE contains high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which are carcinogenic and have recently been restricted in Europe and several non-European countries. In response, vegetable oils have gained growing attention as viable alternatives to petroleum-based oils for the production of environmentally friendly rubber products. , Vegetable oils are renewable, free of PAHs, and economically viable. Replacing petroleum-based plasticizers with vegetable oils can reduce environmental impact, particularly greenhouse gas emissions, and improve the properties of rubber compounds. ,

Recent studies have compared various natural oilssuch as palm oil (PO), coconut oil (CO), soybean oil (SO), rapeseed oil (RO), sunflower seed oil (SSO), and palm kernel oil (PKO)with mineral oils for use in rubber production. Their global large-scale output contributes to a stable supply and favorable cost structures, making them viable plasticizers for the rubber industry. , Geunho Kim et al. synthesized a variety of acid-esterified soybean oils (ASOs) from SO to enhance interactions with silica and promote integration of a substantial fraction of ASOs into the rubber matrix. Moreover, the fatty acids in PO reduced scorch time, cure time, minimum torque, and viscosity. The presence of PO in rubber compounds can enhance the mechanical properties, as evidenced by the low tan δ values at 60 °C. The tensile properties, tear strength, hardness, and abrasion resistance of the PO-based compounds were influenced by the plasticizing effect of the oil. However, these properties were generally lower compared to rubber compounds with no PO. PO contains both saturated and unsaturated fatty acids in similar proportions. Compared to other vegetable oils, it resists thermal oxidation more effectively, making it more stable at high temperatures. − Additionally, PO is a biomaterial and less toxic, which can reduce the environmental and health risks associated with rubber production. , These environmental advantages make PO a compelling alternative to petroleum-based oils.

NR mixed with a silica composite was prepared using the conventional dry method. However, in this method of producing silica-reinforced rubber, silica is highly aggregated and unevenly dispersed throughout the rubbery matrix due to filler-to-filler interactions. Additionally, the incompatibility between hydrophobic natural rubber (NR) and hydrophilic silica leads to rubber products with poor mechanical properties. Utilization of the latex approach for the preparation of NR/silica composites can address these problems. Life cycle assessment (LCA) offers a robust method for quantifying environmental impacts over a product’s life cyclefrom raw material extraction to disposal, enabling systematic evaluation of such benefits. LCA comparison of aromatic and vegetable oils reveals their respective environmental impacts. This comparison helps identify more sustainable material choices. By evaluating criteria such as oil type, processing techniques, and application specifications, rubber manufacturers can make informed decisions that align with sustainable development goals.

Thailand is one of the largest producers of palm oil. Thus, substituting conventional aromatic oils with vegetable oils, such as palm oil, in rubber formulations can contribute to reduced carbon emissions, aligning with global sustainability goals and Thailand’s commitment to carbon neutrality. There is no work in the literature that used palm oil in NR composites with improved silica dispersion.

In the current study, NR/silica composite materials made using palm oil were studied to evaluate their cure characteristics, morphological properties, Payne effect, ATR-FTIR characteristics, thermal properties, computer modeled static and dynamic mechanical properties, by comparing the rubber material processes with using commercial aromatic oil. The present work also focused on the effect of the preparation method (i.e., conventional dry and latex methods). The latex method was developed to address problems with incompatibility between the rubber and silica, and to improve the silica dispersion in the rubber matrix. Finally, all studied formulations were investigated for their environmental impact using a gate-to-gate LCA.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Natural rubber latex (NRL) with a high ammonia concentration (60% of the dry rubber content) was provided by Num Rubber and Latex Co., Ltd. (Trang, Thailand). The STR 20 grade of NR was manufactured by Thai Hua Rubber Co., Ltd. (Trang, Thailand). Silica (granulate) was supplied by Global Chemical Co., Ltd. (Samut Prakarn, Thailand). BET surface area and bulk density of silica were 135 m2/g and 0.23 g/cm3. Aromatic oil (AO) was supplied by H&R ChemPharm (Bangkok, Thailand). The PO used in this study was crude palm oil from the seed, supplied by S.C. Kernel Company Co., Ltd. (Chumphon, Thailand). The viscosities of AO and PO were 233 and 40 cP, respectively, measured using a Brookfield Viscometer. Zinc oxide (ZnO) and stearic acid (SA) were utilized as activators and were obtained from Global Chemical Co., Ltd. (Samut Prakarn, Thailand) and Imperial Industry Chemical Co., Ltd. (Pathum Thani, Thailand), respectively. Accelerators, N-cyclohexyl benzothiazole-2-sulfenamide (CBS) and diphenylguanidine (DPG) were made by Xiamen AmoyChem Co., Ltd. in Xiamen, PR of China. Sulfur (S) was supplied by Ajax Chemical Co., Ltd. (Samut Prakan, Thailand). Ammonium laurate, vultamol, and bentonite were purchased from the Rubber Authority of Thailand (RAOT) (Bangkok, Thailand).

Experimental Section

Preparation of a Silica Dispersion

A 15% silica dispersion was prepared by adding water (82.5 g), silica (15.0 g), ammonium laurate (0.5 g), vultamol (1.0 g), and bentonite (1.0 g). Subsequently, the mixture was ball-milled for 72 h.

Formulation Details for NR/Silica Composites Prepared by Conventional (Control) and Latex Methods

Figure a shows a flow diagram of the conventional method. In this method, an NR/silica composite containing 40 phr of silica was prepared using a 300 cm3 capacity internal mixer (Charon Tut Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) at 60 °C, a 0.8 fill factor, and a 60 rpm rotor speed. NR was initially premixed for 3 min. ZnO and SA, used as activators, were then added and mixed for 2 min. Silica was subsequently introduced into the mixing chamber and mixed for 3 min, followed by the addition of AO, which was further mixed for 3 min. After discharging the mixture from the internal mixer, DPG, CBS, and S were incorporated and blended in a two-roll mill (Charon Tut Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) at ambient temperature. The specimen prepared using this conventional method was referenced as the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control.

Flowchart of the process of (a) dried conventional method, and (b) latex method of producing NR/Si composites with and without vulcanizing agents. In the case of unvulcanized composites for the latex method (b), DPG, CBS, and S were not added to the composites.

Figure b shows a flow diagram of the latex method. In this method, NR/Si/AO and NR/Si/PO composites were prepared by first agitating NRL with a mechanical stirrer at 150 rpm. A 15% silica dispersion, detailed in the preceding section, was then added to the latex. After 5 min of continuous stirring, the mixturecorresponding to a final silica loading of 40 phrwas poured onto a glass plate and left at room temperature for 4 h and subsequently dried at 50 °C using a hot air oven for 48 h. These dried NR/Si sheets were used as masterbatches for the next stage. The NR/Si masterbatches were incorporated and mixed in a two-roll mill (30 °C), then passed through the rollers for 2 min to homogenize them. ZnO and SA were then added and mixed for another 2 min, followed by the addition of AO or PO, which was mixed for a further 3 min. The resulting samples were designated as Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex and Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex, respectively. Finally, DPG, CBS, and S were added and mixed, and the compound was then formed into sheets using a two-roll mill at room temperature. Figure b shows the latex method of producing the unvulcanised NR/Si composite with AO (Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex).

All rubber compounds were stored for at least 16 h at room temperature after mixing. After that, all the samples were vulcanized.

Preparation of Vulcanized Rubber Composites

The rubber compounds underwent vulcanization via electrically heated compression molding (PR1D-W400L450PM; Charon Tut Co., Ltd., Thailand) at 70 bar and 160 °C. Following vulcanization, the rubber sheets were cooled to room temperature and postcured for a minimum of 24 h before characterizations (2.3.4–2.3.8). Dumbbell-shaped specimens were then prepared for testing using a Type II die cutter.

Characterizations

Oil Characterization

Before use, the chemical composition of AO and POspecifically carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen contentswas analyzed using a CHN analyzer (LECO 628 Series, LECO Corporation, USA). The surface tension of the oils was measured using a surface tension meter (Krüss K12; Akribis Scientific Supplies Ltd., UK). The elemental content of the oils was also determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) (NexION 2000, PerkinElmer, USA).

Payne Effect

The interaction among silica particles in the uncured compound was analyzed using a strain sweep test. Measurements were performed at 80 °C and 1 Hz employing a rubber process analyzer (RPA2000, Alpha Technologies, USA). The applied strain range was 0.56–100%. The storage shear modulus (G′) was recorded at low strain (0.56%) and high strain (100%). The difference in G′ between low and high strain, denoted as ΔG′, commonly referred to as the Payne effect, was used to assess the extent of filler–rubber interaction.

Cure Characteristics

The rubber compound was compressed into sheets and subsequently conditioned at 25 ± 2 °C for approximately 24 h in a sealed container before curing analysis using a moving die rheometer (MDR-01; CG Engineering Co., Ltd., Thailand) at 160 °C. The scorch time (t s2), optimal cure time (t c90), maximum torque (M H), and minimum torque (M L) were determined from the resulting cure curves. The cure rate index (CRI, min–1) was calculated using eq 1 CRI=100tc90−ts2

Morphological Properties

The morphological characteristics of all formulations were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (FEI Quanta 400, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (SmartSPM, Horiba, France) to visualize the surface topology of the biobased composites. In SEM analysis, the specimens were cryogenically fractured to expose fresh cross-sectional regions and subsequently gold-coated under vacuum. For AFM analysis, the samples were carefully sliced using a razor blade to produce smooth surfaces. Imaging was performed in contact mode at ambient temperature and measured over a 40 × 40 μm2 area using a 5 nm cantilever tip.

Elemental mapping was conducted to evaluate the distribution of silica within the rubber matrix. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) (X-Max, Oxford Instruments, UK) was used to analyze the effect of renewable palm oil on silica dispersion at the surface and near-surface regions of the rubber.

ATR-FTIR Analysis

Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to qualitatively analyze the functional groups and chemical properties of NR/Si composites. The FTIR spectra were obtained in transmission mode using a Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (FTIR, Vertex70, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) fitted with a germanium ATR crystal probe. The spectra obtained at a resolution of 4 cm–1 comprised 32 scans across the 4000 to 400 cm–1 wavenumber range.

Thermal Properties

The thermal stability of all formulations was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland). A sample of approximately 20 mg was positioned in a ceramic pan and subsequently heated at a rate of 10 °C/min over the temperature range of 30–600 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere.

Static Mechanical Properties

Tensile strength, elongation at break, and Young’s modulus, were assessed using a tensile testing machine (Z010, Zwick Roell, Germany). Dumbbell-shaped specimens were tested to fracture at a displacement rate of 500 mm/min, in accordance with ISO 37 procedures. The reinforcement index (RI) is defined by eq . 2 RI=M300M100 where M100 and M300 refer to the stresses at 100% and 300% strain, respectively.

Computational modeling of the mechanical properties of the sample employed the Ogden model. Curve-fitting analysis of sample data was performed using the Marc program, which was applied to fit the static mechanical results.

Dynamic Mechanical Properties

The dynamic mechanical properties of NR/Si composites were assessed using dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) (DMA1, Mettler Toledo, USA) in tension mode, across a temperature range of −80–80 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The strain amplitude and testing frequency were set at 0.1% and 1 Hz, respectively.

Leaching Behavior of Rubber

The leaching behavior of vulcanized rubber was evaluated to assess the potential release of metals into an aqueous environment, with particular emphasis on zinc due to its use as a vulcanization activator and its environmental relevance. Each elastomeric vulcanizate specimen (1.2 g, cut into 2.0 cm2 fragments) was immersed in 100 mL of distilled water. After 3 months, water samples were collected, and the metals concentration was measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS, NexION 2000, PerkinElmer, USA).

Life Cycle Assessment

A cradle-to-gate (hybrid) life cycle assessment (LCA) (Figure S1) was conducted to quantify the environmental impacts associated with producing 1 kg of rubber material, which served as the functional unit for all calculations. In this study, the cradle-to-gate approach applies to inputs with background data, meaning that part of the LCA system boundary includes upstream life cycles from resource extraction to the factory gate. Gate-to-gate applies to the on-site manufacturing stages, meaning that the other part of the system boundary is modeled only from the factory in-gate to the out-gate. A representative model of a rubber processing factory in Thailand was developed to perform the assessment. Results and Discussion

Properties of Processing Oil

Table shows the element content and surface tension of AO and PO. AO had a significantly higher carbon content (84.7%) compared to PO (62.6%). Both oils had comparable levels of hydrogen (9.8% and 9.5%, respectively) and no detectable nitrogen. The high carbon content in AO is attributable to its aromatic structure, characterized by condensed ring systems with high carbon content and low polarity. The low polarity reduces compatibility with polar fillers such as silica, which decreases filler dispersion and weakens filler–rubber interactions. PO is a mixture of triacylglycerols (TAGs) with oxygen-bearing ester linkages. The oxygen atoms lower the carbon fraction relative to aromatic oils.

These characteristics are relevant for long-life rubber goods and applications targeting reduced environmental impact. Increasing regulatory pressure to replace petroleum-derived aromatic oils due to their PAH and toxicity concerns indicates that PO can be considered a potential alternative.

The surface tension values of the oils may be related to differences in their molecular composition. AO has a higher surface tension (35.90 mN/m) than PO (31.40 mN/m). Higher surface tension arises from stronger cohesive molecular forces at the liquid–air interface. The slightly higher surface tension of AO may result from π–π interactions among aromatic rings. These interactions increase molecular cohesion at the liquid–air interface. PO has a lower surface tension. Its saturated and unsaturated fatty acids reduce interfacial tension due to their amphiphilic nature.

Rheological Characteristics

Figure a shows a graph of the storage modulus versus strain for NR/Si composites with and without vulcanizing agents. The inset of Figure a is the corresponding graph of ΔG′ versus strain. The storage modulus of NR/Si compounds primarily depends on four factors: the hydrodynamic effect, the rubber network, filler–rubber interactions, and filler–filler interactions. The surface silanol groups (Si–OH) on silica can form hydrogen bonds, which facilitate filler–filler interactions and contribute to the Payne effect in rubber compounds. , The storage modulus of rubber composites decreases with increasing strain due to the progressive breakdown of the silica–silica network within the rubber matrix, which is characteristic of the Payne effect. The Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample exhibited a higher initial storage modulus at low strain, which can be attributed to strong filler–filler interactions in the un-cross-linked compound. Moreover, the extent of filler–rubber interactions in the rubber matrix was assessed by the difference in storage modulus at low (0%) and high (100%) strain, represented as ΔG′ or G′max – G′min (Figure a, inset). The results showed that the use of PO as a processing oil produced filler dispersion and interaction characteristics comparable to those of AO. The slightly lower Payne effect observed with PO (171 kPa) suggests that it may serve as a viable alternative to AO (182 kPa) in rubber compounding. Additionally, the preparation method (dry or latex) impacts silica dispersion in the NR matrix. The Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample (182 kPa) had lower ΔG′ than the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control sample (310 kPa) due to better silica dispersion in the latex phase compared to the dry phase.

Cure Characteristics

Figure b shows the cure curves of the biobased rubber composites with and without vulcanizing agents. Table summarizes the cure characteristics, M L, M H, M H-M L, t s2, t c90, and CRI of biobased rubber composites. The curing curves can be categorized into two groups: (i) those with changes observed in vulcanized samples (Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control, Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex, and Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex), and (ii) samples where no curing response was observed in the Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample, due to the absence of a vulcanization system. The test results indicated that the scorch time (t s2) and cure time (t c90) were shorter for the conventional method compared to the latex method. This may be attributed to poor silica dispersion in the conventional method, which can result from silica agglomeration. Such agglomeration could act as premature cure sites that can trigger earlier scorch and accelerated vulcanization. Conversely, the latex technique generally achieves better silica dispersion, and this could reduce premature curing.

During the vulcanization period, the increased torque was mostly attributed to the enhancement of cross-linking density within the rubber matrix. Conventional and latex methods of NR/Si/AO showed similar M H, M L, and M H-M L results. The latex method, which requires less mixing energy than the conventional method, did not change the M H-M L. Additionally, Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample showed lower M H and M H-M L values than the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample using the same methodology. This could be attributed to the molecular composition of PO, which is a triglyceride coupled to various unsaturated and saturated fatty acids. The double bonds of an unsaturated fatty acid can react with rubber molecules via sulfur bridges. Although the unsaturated fatty acids may partially react with the sulfur used in vulcanization, reducing its availability for cross-linking formation, , the cure rate index remained comparable to that of the AO-based compound. Hence, PO can be a useful processing aid as it did not significantly alter the cure kinetics. In addition, the Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample was excluded from Table due to the absence of vulcanization.

Morphological Properties

SEM was used to examine the cross sections of the composites and assess the distribution of silica within the rubber matrix. Figure a shows that the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control specimens prepared by the conventional method with aromatic oil exhibit slight silica aggregation. This indicates limited dispersion efficiency. The Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample (Figure b) shows silica aggregation that can be attributed to the absence of cross-linking and insufficient immobilization of silica particles. Vulcanized latex-based samples prepared with AO (Figure c) and PO (Figure d) both exhibit well-dispersed silica within the rubber matrix. The improved dispersion is primarily attributed to the latex method, which enable uniform distribution of silica prior to coagulation. Although PO has a more polar chemical structure, no significant difference in silica dispersion between AO and PO systems. It was observed from SEM and EDS analyses, indicating that processing method plays a more dominant role than oil polarity under the present conditions.

ATR-FTIR Results

Figure a shows the FTIR spectra of NR/Si composites with and without vulcanizing agents. All spectra exhibit similar profiles, with the major component contributed by the functional groups of NR. All formulations exhibited the characteristic absorption bands of NR, notably stretching vibrations of aliphatic −CH2 and −CH3 groups at 2960 and 2916 cm–1, , and bending vibrations at 1445 and 1375 cm–1. The peak at 1664 cm–1, corresponding to CC stretching of the polyisoprene backbone, is present in all samples but exhibited a relatively small shift and reduction in intensity. In the AO samples (i.e., Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control, Un-NR/Si/AO-Latex, and Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex), the FTIR spectra showed peaks related to aromatic rings, particularly in the 1500–1600 and 1719 cm–1 region. This suggests the presence of benzene ring vibrations from the aromatic oil. , These peaks were absent in the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex composite. Instead, a strong absorption band was observed at around 1740 cm–1, corresponding to the ester carbonyl (CO) stretching of triglycerides in PO. , This indicates the presence of natural esters and suggests that the PO has been incorporated into the NR. In contrast, the nonpolar nature of AO limits hydrogen bonding potential, which could result in reduced interfacial adhesion between silica and the NR matrix. The FTIR results suggest that PO offers a more polar and reactive environment.

Static Mechanical Properties

Thermal Properties

Figure c,d show the weight retention and derivative weight curves as a function of temperature for the biobased rubber composites. The main decomposition stage occurs between 300 and 450 °C. This stage corresponds to the degradation of the NR backbone. All rubber composites exhibit similar peak degradation temperatures (T d) in the range of 380.1–381.2 °C (Figure d). Aromatic oil provides thermal stability because it lacks ester linkages, which degrade more easily under heat. At 700 °C, all samples retain a small mass. This remaining residue corresponds to the inorganic silica filler, as shown in Figure c. The Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample retains more char residue than the AO-based composite. Biobased oils promote char formation through cross-linking and carbonization at high temperatures. Although the NR/Si/AO composite shows slightly greater thermal stability, the PO-based composite maintains sufficient thermal performance for standard rubber processing and applications. This result, along with the renewable origin of PO, indicates that PO can serve as a practical alternative to petroleum-derived plasticizers. Previous studies on bio-oil-plasticized elastomers reported similar thermal decomposition behavior.

Dynamic Mechanical Properties

This section discusses the DMA results of the filler–rubber interactions and the reinforcing effects in composites, relating the three physical states, glassy, glass transition, and rubbery regions. Figure shows dynamic mechanical properties in terms of the (a) storage modulus, (b) tan δ, (c) tan δ at 0 °C, and (d) tan δ at 60 °C as a function of temperature of the NR/Si composites with and without vulcanizing agents. The storage modulus (E′) is predominantly influenced by structural factors such as molecular weight and polymer–filler interfacial bonding. This modulus decreased appreciably at about −60 °C, which may indicate the transition from the glassy to the softer rubbery state (Figure a). In the glassy region, molecular chains are immobile, as previously reported. As the temperature increases, the chains gain mobility, and the tight molecular packing is disrupted.

Dynamic mechanical properties: (a) storage modulus, (b) tan δ, (c) tan δ at 0 °C, and (d) tan δ at 60 °C versus temperature of NR/Si composites with and without vulcanizing agents.

The Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control sample shows the highest storage modulus in the rubbery region (Figure a). This may arise from the agglomeration of silica, which is in good agreement with the high Payne effect (Figure a). However, the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample exhibits a higher storage modulus in the rubbery region than the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample. This enhancement is likely due to strong interfacial bonding between the silica filler and the natural rubber matrix, facilitated by ester groups in PO that interact with silanol groups on the silica surface. This interaction improves filler dispersion and restricts polymer chain mobility. The un-cross-linked sample represents flow properties at higher temperatures.

Figure b shows the variation in the loss tangent (tan δ) with temperature for NR/Si composites with and without a vulcanizing agent. This result enables evaluation of the damping characteristics of the rubber composites. These characteristics depend on the type of rubber matrix, the filler (in this case, silica), and the filler–matrix interface. The latter involves frictional slipping in unbound interfacial regions, interfacial delamination, and energy dissipation through crack propagation within the rubber matrix. A high maximum of the damping peak (tan δmax) indicates greater molecular mobility. The Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample exhibits the highest tan δmax in this region. This indicates increased molecular mobility and suggests enhanced damping capacity and elastomeric behavior at very low temperatures. The temperature at which tan δ peaks is commonly used to approximate the glass transition temperature (T g). All samples exhibited T g values within a narrow range (−52.0––53.4 °C), indicating comparable thermal transitions, consistent with values reported in earlier studies using silica as a filler in a dry NR matrix.

Tan δ values at 0 and 60 °C are critical indicators when rubber is used in tire tread applications. The tan δ at 0 °C reflects a tire’s wet traction; a higher value indicates better resistance to wet sliding. In contrast, tan δ at 60 °C corresponds to rolling resistance; a lower value implies reduced rolling resistance and improved fuel efficiency. , The Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control and Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex samples show comparable tan δ values at.

0 °C (Figure c). This result indicates that both formulations provide good wet traction. Additionally, the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample exhibited lower tan δ values at 60 °C than the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Control samples. This is due to the latex-based method increasing the dispersion of silica, which is consistent with the storage modulus with function of strain from RPA (Figure ). Furthermore, the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample shows the lowest tan δ at 60 °C (Figure d). This value reflects the lowest rolling resistance. Moreover, the tan δ at 60 °C of the NR/Si/PO-Latex sample without silane was similar to the conventional method of dried NR/Si with silane (at the same silica loading, 40 phr). This indicated that the latex preparation method can enhance the dispersion of silica particles in NR latex and reduce the silica aggregation with no silane in NR composites, leading to similar energy-saving properties compared to conventional NR/Si with silane. The reduction in tan δ arises from stronger interactions between the silica filler and rubber molecular chains. These interactions reduce chain mobility and limit heat accumulation caused by viscoelastic friction. PO significantly strengthens these filler–rubber interactions. Overall, the green tire tread developed using PO demonstrates comparable or superior performance to that using AO.

Environmental Impacts

Table shows the concentrations of elements in water leachates from NR/Si composites over time. The concentration of Mg in the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample is higher than for the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample. The presence of Mg is likely associated with residual inorganic species originating from silica filler production or other compounding additives rather than from the processing oils themselves. Additionally, aluminum (Al) levels are also higher in the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample than in the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample. Rather than being attributed to higher solvent power or chemical reactivity of AO, this difference is more reasonably related to compositional variations or trace inorganic impurities present in the formulation and their mobility within the rubber matrix. Similarly, the concentration of iron (Fe) in the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex sample exceeded that in the Vul-NR/Si/PO-Latex sample. This observation may partly arise from the higher Fe content associated with AO itself, as well as possible contributions from processing equipment, which together can increase Fe availability in the leachates. The ICP–MS results therefore indicate that differences in elemental release between AO- and PO-based formulations are more likely governed by impurity content, elemental composition, and filler–matrix interactions rather than differences in intrinsic oil reactivity. Consequently, statements relating metal leaching directly to oil reactivity have been removed. The observed variations in metal release remain relevant for assessing environmental impact and long-term material stability.

LCA Results

The cradle-to-gate (hybrid) LCA results indicate that the conventional method (AO-Control) exhibits lower fossil fuel depletion than the AO-based NRL formulation. This decrease can be attributed to the latex route requiring additional energy for silica dispersion in the aqueous phase and the drying step before compounding. That is, drying and water removal processes significantly contribute to fossil energy demand in rubber processing systems. Additionally, the PO-based NRL formulation yields lower fossil fuel depletion, 49.56 MJ, compared to 102.09 MJ for the Vul-NR/Si/AO-Latex formulation (Figure a). Thus, this reflects reduced use of fossil-based components within the PO-based formulation. The PO-based formulation has lower human toxicity, 1.31 kg 1,4-DB eq than AO-based NRL (1.38 kg 1,4-DB eq) (Figure b). A remarkable result when comparing the preparation methods was that the latex method of AO-based NRL resulted in lower human toxicity than the conventional method (3.02 kg 1,4-DB eq). The PO-based formulation also yields lower impact on ozone layer depletion at 6.56 × 10–8 kg CFC-11 eq, compared to the AO-based NRL formulation (Figure c). The impact on ozone layer depletion for the conventional method (AO-Control) is of a similar order of magnitude to the AO-based NRL formulation (5.90 × 10–7 kg CFC-11 equiv).

However, the PO formulation yields the highest eutrophication potential at 0.0144 kg PO4 3– eq, suggesting nutrient runoff from fertilizer application contributes to water body nutrient loading, compared to 0.00278 kg for the AO-based NRL formulation (Figure d). The control formulation yields a eutrophication potential comparable to the AO-based NRL formulation. The PO formulation also yields higher freshwater aquatic ecotoxicity at 10.30 kg 1,4-DB eq, versus 0.591 kg for the AO-based NRL formulation (Figure e). The control formulation yields a similar order of magnitude value compared to AO-based NRL formulation. These increases likely reflect upstream agricultural inputs, particularly fertilizer and pesticide/herbicide runoff into aquatic environments. Last, the PO-based formulation yields a higher global warming potential, 6.36 kg CO2 eq, than the AO-based NRL formulation (2.68 kg CO2 eq) (Figure f). The higher global warming potential could be attributed to upstream emissions associated with palm oil cultivation, including fertilizer production, land-use change, and transport. − However, the results of the latex and conventional method (AO-Control) showed similar global warming potential values (Figure f).

Overall, relative to AO-based NRL and control formulations, the PO-based formulation shows lower fossil resource depletion, human toxicity and ozone layer depletion, but a slightly higher global warming potential, eutrophication potential, and freshwater aquatic ecotoxicity. In this study, the term cradle-to-gate (hybrid) LCA refers to the use of cradle-to-gate background data for purchased raw materials in combination with gate-to-gate modeling for in-house production processes. Within this boundary, the higher impacts for the PO-based formulation are contributed by emissions and pollutants originating from agricultural production systems, including fertilizers use, land-use change, and agrochemical runoff.

Conclusions

The results demonstrate that the latex method provides more effective silica dispersion in natural rubber composites than the conventional dry mixing process. Morphological observations from SEM and AFM, together with rheological and mechanical analyses, indicated that latex processing promotes more uniform filler distribution prior to vulcanization, resulting in improved structural homogeneity of the composites. A comparison between latex-based formulations containing AO and PO showed that both oils enable good silica dispersion when prepared with latex method. Although SEM and EDS analyses reveal no significant morphological differences between AO- and PO-containing systems, slight improvements in dynamic mechanical behavior and filler–rubber interaction were observed for the PO-based composite, suggesting marginally enhanced compatibility within the rubber matrix. Moreover, the hysteresis of PO based compound was the lowest. Environmental assessment through leaching analysis indicates lower metal release from PO-containing composites compared with AO formulations, highlighting the potential advantage of PO as a more environmentally favorable processing oil. Moreover, the LCA results indicate that the PO-based NRL formulation reduces fossil fuel depletion, human toxicity, and ozone layer depletion compared to AO-based and control formulations. Particularly, the latex method (AO based NRL) exhibits a lower human toxicity than the conventional method. Overall, the study confirms that latex method is a key factor governing silica dispersion, while PO represents a promising sustainable alternative to conventional AO for preparing silica-filled natural rubber composites.
