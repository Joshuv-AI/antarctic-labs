Integration of artificial intelligence applications in clinical pharmacy services: A scoping review
Authors: Anmar Al-Taie, Ahmed Shaheen Abdullah, Oritsetimeyin Arueyingho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into healthcare systems, offering new opportunities to enhance the safety, efficiency and effectiveness of clinical pharmacy services. This scoping review aimed to systematically map the current applications of AI in clinical pharmacy practice and to identify the medication-management functions supported by these technologies.MethodsA literature search was conducted following PRISMA-ScR guidelines, covering studies published between 2010 and 2025 in databases such as PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus. Included studies focused on the application of AI in clinical pharmacy services within hospital and community settings. Studies were screened at the title, abstract and full-text levels, followed by data extraction and synthesis. Eligible studies were required to be peer-reviewed, written in English, and to include relevant keywords exploring the intersection of artificial intelligence and pharmacy practice.ResultsA total of 36 publications were included in the final assessment and analysis. Most studies focused on AI applications for the detection of drug interactions and adverse drug effects (n = 12, 33.3%), followed by studies for medication errors and potentially inappropriate medications (n = 11, 30.6%). Machine learning techniques, including natural language processing and deep learning, were the most commonly used AI technologies.ConclusionsThe development of AI-powered applications and tools for clinical pharmacy services is a promising approach. However, significant efforts, in collaboration with relevant stakeholders, are needed to explore how these AI technologies can add value to clinical pharmacy services in both community and hospital settings.

Introduction

Clinical pharmacy services are central to the delivery of safe, effective and patient-centred healthcare. They can be defined as pharmacist-led or pharmacist-supported activities focused on medication optimisation and safety, therapeutic monitoring, prescribing review, pharmacovigilance, and clinical decision support in patient-care settings such as community and hospital environments.1, 2, 3 Studies consistently demonstrate that these services improve medication adherence, reduce adverse drug events, decrease hospital admissions and readmissions and contribute to reductions in mortality, thereby reinforcing their central role in patient safety and quality of care. However, the increasing complexity of modern pharmacotherapy, driven by multimorbidity, population ageing and polypharmacy, has substantially expanded the cognitive and operational burden on pharmacists, creating growing demand for more scalable and efficient approaches to medication management.4, 5

Alongside these clinical pressures, healthcare systems have undergone rapid digital transformation. Over the past decade, the adoption of electronic health records, e-prescribing systems, hospital information systems and digital prescribing platforms has generated large-scale, high-dimensional clinical datasets.6 This digital infrastructure has created new opportunities for data-driven approaches to support clinical decision-making, particularly in medication management and safety-critical processes which are core aspects of clinical pharmacy practice.7 However, while digitalisation has improved access to clinical information, it has also intensified information overload and workflow complexity, particularly in pharmacy practice, where time-sensitive decisions must be made across large volumes of prescriptions and patient data.6, 7

Within this broader digital health landscape, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a key enabling technology. It encompasses computational techniques capable of simulating aspects of human cognition including learning, reasoning, pattern recognition and decision making. Within healthcare, AI systems can analyse large and complex datasets derived from electronic health records, laboratory findings, prescribing patterns and clinical documentation to generate actionable insights to support clinical care.8, 9

AI in healthcare has advanced rapidly, resulting in significant progress across various medical domains by assisting healthcare practitioners in lowering costs, improving patient outcomes and enhancing the overall quality of patient care. In pharmacy practice, AI has the potential to improve the accuracy, efficiency and personalisation of medication management processes through predictive analytics, automated risk detection, adaptive learning systems and data-driven clinical recommendations.9, 10 Practices such as diagnostic procedures, treatment protocol development, medication development, personalised medicine and patient monitoring have facilitated the development and application of AI programs.7, 11, 12, 13 This approach has allowed for the reallocation of human resources to more valuable, patient-centred responsibilities within clinical pharmacy services, which play a crucial role in ensuring safe and effective medication management and optimal patient care.14 The increasing number of prescriptions, complex drug treatments and administrative responsibilities, due to rapid advancements in the medical field, have created a growing need for advanced technology to assist healthcare providers in their everyday tasks and improve the overall provision of healthcare services.15 AI algorithms are also relevant in improving medication safety, determining possible adverse drug reactions (ADRs), drug–drug interactions (DDIs), saving time and costs, monitoring medication adherence, providing well-informed recommendations specific to each patient, and predicting health risks and outcomes across various populations.15, 16, 17, 18

Within hospital and ward pharmacy settings, AI-driven clinical decision support systems (CDSS) have shown considerable promise in reducing prescribing and dispensing errors and improving medication safety. These systems use machine learning (ML) algorithms to identify potentially inappropriate medication orders, dosing errors, contraindications and clinically significant interactions using patient-specific clinical data. Several studies indicate that AI-supported pharmacy systems may substantially reduce medication distribution and prescribing errors, while supporting pharmacists in identifying patients at increased risk of adverse drug events and enabling earlier clinical intervention.19, 20

Despite these advances, the current evidence base remains inconsistent, with limited studies showing how AI technologies are being applied across clinical pharmacy services and the specific functions that they support in practice. Although previous reviews have explored AI applications in healthcare and selected aspects of pharmacy practice, most have focused on specific settings, specific outcomes (eg medication safety or adverse drug event prediction) or individual AI techniques. Few have comprehensively mapped AI applications across both hospital and community pharmacy settings, particularly with an emphasis on pharmacist-facing clinical workflows and use cases spanning different aspects of medication management. Also, rapid advances in ML and natural language processing (NLP) over the past decade have substantially expanded the range of AI applications in clinical pharmacy, thereby highlighting the need for an updated synthesis of the evidence. To address these gaps, this scoping review systematically mapped and synthesised the available literature on the application of AI in clinical pharmacy services. The review was guided by the following research questions: ʻWhat are the current practical applications of AI in clinical pharmacy services?’ and ʻWhat medication-management functions and clinical pharmacy activities are supported by these AI applications?’. These questions are addressed through the following objectives:1.To map the current applications of AI technologies in clinical pharmacy services within hospital and community settings.2.To identify reported benefits, implementation challenges and future directions for AI integration in pharmacist-facing clinical workflows.

Methods

We followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines for conducting and reporting scoping reviews. Unlike meta-analyses and systematic reviews, scoping reviews do not require quantitative data synthesis, comparisons between groups, assessment of missing data or sensitivity analysis according to the PRISMA-ScR guidance. Ethical approval was not required for this study as the scoping review included data from previously published studies.

In alignment with the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework recommended for scoping reviews, the population of interest in our review included patients receiving pharmacist-supported medication management and clinical pharmacy services. The concept focused on the application of AI technologies, including ML, NLP and AI-enabled clinical decision support systems, to support clinical pharmacy practice, while the context included hospital and community pharmacy settings where pharmacists are directly involved in medication-related care, medication safety activities, prescribing review, pharmacovigilance, medication reconciliation or therapeutic decision support. Medication reviews, medication safety, pharmacovigilance and the understanding of adverse drug events are critical aspects of clinical pharmacy practice.

Search strategy

The database search for this scoping review focused on the applications of AI in clinical pharmacy services in hospital and community settings. Clinical pharmacy services include integrated clinical activities directly related to medication management, patient care, medication safety, therapeutic optimisation, clinical decision support, pharmacovigilance, medication reconciliation, prescribing review, and related healthcare interventions in hospital or community settings.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Web of Science, PubMed and Scopus databases from 2010 to 2025. The search strategy was developed using combinations of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to AI and clinical pharmacy practice. Search strings included Boolean combinations of keywords related to AI (eg artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing) and pharmacy practice (eg clinical pharmacy, pharmacovigilance, medication safety, clinical decision support systems, medication therapy management, medication reconciliation, community pharmacy, prescribing error, pharmaceutical care, hospital pharmacy, drug dispensing).

Selection criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they evaluated the application of AI technologies within clinical pharmacy services or pharmacist-supported clinical care in hospital or community healthcare settings. Data extraction

Study selection was conducted independently by two reviewers in accordance with predefined eligibility criteria aligned with the PCC framework. Prior to screening, all retrieved records were exported into a reference management system (EndNote) and duplicates were removed. The deduplicated records were then transferred to a structured screening database (Microsoft Excel), where decisions were systematically recorded at each stage of the selection process. Screening was performed in two sequential stages: title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review. At both stages, studies were assessed independently by the two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated the final decision.

Data extraction was subsequently conducted for all included studies using a standardised extraction framework in Microsoft Excel. Extracted data included study characteristics and key findings relevant to the review objectives. The included studies were then tabulated, and findings were mapped and synthesised descriptively in structured tables aligned with the review objectives. As this review adopted a scoping and narrative synthesis approach, no meta-analytic techniques, statistical modelling or machine learning tools were applied.

Results

The initial database search identified 2,603 records across PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. After duplicate removal, 894 unique records remained for screening. These records underwent title and abstract screening independently by two reviewers using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria aligned with the PCC framework. Following title and abstract screening, 629 records were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria, leaving 265 reports for full-text assessment. Full-text screening was subsequently conducted independently by two reviewers to determine methodological relevance and alignment with the scope of the review. Following full-text review, 229 reports were excluded for reasons including non-clinical pharmacy focus, non-AI applications, publication type exclusions (eg reviews, editorials, conference abstracts) or lack of relevance to pharmacist-facing clinical workflows.

A total of 36 studies were included in the final scoping review synthesis. Of the included studies, 12 (33.3%) focused primarily on AI applications for the detection of drug interactions and adverse drug effects, while 11 studies (30.6%) examined medication errors and potentially inappropriate medications. Machine learning approaches, including natural language processing and deep learning techniques, were the most commonly reported AI methodologies. Narrative synthesis of included literature

In this section, we present a narrative review of the included literature:a)Electronic health record and medication order review: Dalal et al21 developed a Patient Safety Learning Laboratory (PSLL) to provide a set of digital health technologies integrated with electronic health records (EHRs) to instantly detect, evaluate and reduce patient safety risks. An ML model was developed in a study by Balestra et al,22 using provider behaviour and other contextual factors to detect drug orders requiring pharmacist intervention. In this model, information about pharmacy orders and provider activities was gathered from the EHR system. Similarly, a prospective study by Hogue et al23 aimed to evaluate the clinical impact of an ML model designed to identify atypical medication orders, where clinical pharmacists labelled orders and pharmacological profiles (patients’ prescription lists) as either typical or atypical. When compared to pharmacist labels, the study found that prescription order predictions had a precision rate of 35%, a recall (sensitivity) of 26%, and a specificity rate of 97%. Additionally, nine out of 15 participants favoured medication-specific predictions over profile-based ones. This performance profile illustrates a key trade-off in AI-supported medication order review systems. High specificity reduces unnecessary alerts and may help limit alert fatigue, but low recall indicates that a substantial proportion of atypical or potentially inappropriate orders are not identified, which limits standalone clinical reliability and reinforces the need for pharmacist oversight in interpretation and decision-making.b)Health products dispensing: Alhorishi et al26 developed a healthcare framework that reduces patient wait times by using an ML technique to build an early prediction model for hospital pharmacy prescriptions. The study evaluated four ML algorithms and found that the random tree method provided the best accuracy, increased patient satisfaction, and overall improved the quality of care. Contrastingly, a baseline deep learning drug identification (DLDI) model was developed by Ting et al24 to assist hospital pharmacists in medication dispensing via blister-packaged drug identification with more than 90% accuracy. This model could help pharmacists identify drugs while preventing medication errors.c)Medication therapy management and medication adherence: Bu et al28 created an AI-based pharmacy service model for online hospitals during the COVID-19 pandemic, including online drug catalogue search, AI-powered prescription preview, drug distribution and dispensing, and AI-based medication consultation response. In this study, prescription guidelines and AI for reviewing prescriptions were developed and integrated into the online hospital system. The drugs were then dispensed following a secondary check and review by pharmacists. A total of 426 medication consultations were served. The AI preview's qualifying rate was 83.65%. Nearly 16.4% of prescriptions were deemed improper, 49% were proactively accepted and changed by physicians, and 51% were approved upon pharmacist intervention. The study concluded that AI-enhanced telephonic medication management services may have the potential to reduce healthcare expenditures and support service expansion, based on observed workflow efficiencies within their implementation context.Sumner et al,30 in a study to improve medication adherence, used a human-centred design method to construct a digital AI-driven nudge intervention involving patients, clinicians and technologists. The reminder nudges and contact with healthcare workers were highly rated by all. The study found that the four DLMs demonstrated equivalent diagnostic features and performance, supporting the potential application of AI in the binary classification of video frames to predict medication adherence. Synthesis of AI applications in clinical pharmacy practice across medication management domains

Across the included studies, AI applications in clinical pharmacy services cluster into five functional domains: electronic health record and medication order review, medication dispensing, medication therapy management and adherence support, medication error detection and prevention, and detection of drug interactions and adverse drug reactions. We recognise that most studies represent early-stage retrospective model development using electronic health record or historical datasets. For instance, Balestra et al,22 Hogue et al23 and Dalal et al21 in electronic health record-based applications, Ting et al24 and Alhorishi et al26 in medication dispensing, and Yalçin et al,44 Nguyen et al,34 Xingwei et al,43 Wongyikul et al42 and Zhuo et al40 in medication error detection. A smaller subset includes prospective observational validation, and only very few studies demonstrate integration into real-world clinical pharmacy workflows with active pharmacist deployment. Conversely, several studies, particularly in adverse drug reaction and drug interaction prediction, remain purely computational or network based in silico models without clinical validation.

In relation to the first objective of this review paper, mapping current applications of AI in clinical pharmacy services, findings show a clear dominance of machine learning and related data-driven approaches applied to electronic health record data. In medication order review, studies such as those by Balestra et al,22 Hogue et al23 and Dalal et al21 demonstrate that AI systems are primarily used to triage medication orders, flag atypical prescribing patterns, and prioritise pharmacist review. These systems rely on structured and semi structured electronic health record data, including prescribing history and provider behaviour, to generate risk signals that pharmacists then validate. Importantly, reported performance metrics such as low recall and moderate precision in some models indicate that these tools’ function more as screening mechanisms rather than definitive diagnostic systems, reinforcing the continued necessity of pharmacist oversight. However, most studies evaluated algorithmic performance in retrospective datasets, and clinical benefit is inferred from workflow simulation or expert validation rather than demonstrated changes in patient outcomes.

In medication dispensing workflows, AI applications are largely operational. Alhorishi et al26 demonstrate predictive modelling to reduce prescription wait times, while Ting et al24 apply deep learning-based image recognition to support blister pack and drug identification. These tools extend AI into the logistical and safety critical stages of the medication use process, where they primarily aim to reduce dispensing errors and improve throughput. Medication therapy management and adherence represent a more heterogeneous application area spanning system level digital pharmacy platforms, behavioural interventions, and patient monitoring tools. Bu et al28 illustrate integrated AI enabled online pharmacy services combining prescription screening, dispensing workflows, and pharmacist verification within a unified digital system. Conversely, Sumner et al30 and Sekandi et al31 focus on patient-facing adherence support, using behavioural nudging and deep learning-based video monitoring respectively. Worrall et al33 further demonstrate AI-supported adherence programmes embedded within pharmacist-led chronic disease management, showing improvements in adherence and cost outcomes. Across these studies, AI is used both as a behavioural intervention tool and as a monitoring mechanism, but implementation approaches vary substantially and are often context specific.

For medication error detection and potentially inappropriate medication identification, the literature shows consistent use of machine learning classification and association-based methods to support early identification of prescribing risks. Studies such as those by Yalçın et al44 and Nguyen et al34 demonstrate AI systems trained on clinical and prescribing data to predict or flag medication errors across paediatric intensive care and general prescribing environments. Additional work on potentially inappropriate medications in older populations highlights the use of predictive modelling to stratify risk and support clinical decision support systems. Collectively, these findings indicate that AI is most frequently applied in high-risk inpatient contexts where medication complexity and clinical vulnerability are high.

In reference to drug interactions and adverse drug reactions, AI applications extend into both structured data modelling and unstructured clinical text analysis. Van Laere et al54 and Yalçın et al55 demonstrate predictive modelling approaches for identifying drug–drug interaction risks and adverse drug reactions in neonatal populations, while Chapman et al52 extend AI use to natural language processing of clinical notes. Duan et al46 further illustrate probabilistic and network-based approaches for adverse drug effect discovery. These studies show that AI is increasingly used to augment pharmacovigilance processes by integrating heterogeneous data sources, although most models remain at the proof of concept or early validation stage.

The reviewed papers reveal benefits, implementation challenges, and future directions of AI in clinical workflows. Reported benefits include improved prioritisation of pharmacist workload, reduction in medication errors, improved detection of high-risk prescriptions, enhanced dispensing efficiency, and potential improvements in medication adherence and healthcare costs. However, these benefits are consistently accompanied by important limitations. For instance, across the studies there is limited evidence of external validation, inconsistent reporting of model performance, and minimal assessment of real-world clinical outcomes beyond metrics such as accuracy or predictive performance. Few studies also report implementation at scale, and even fewer assess long-term sustainability within routine pharmacy workflows. However, one key observation is that pharmacist involvement remains integral to all AI applications identified. Across studies, AI functions as a decision support layer generating recommendations, alerts or predictions, while pharmacists retain final clinical responsibility; however, the majority of evidence remains limited to retrospective evaluation, pilot implementation or computational validation rather than confirmed clinical effectiveness in routine practice.

Finally, across the included studies, explicit reporting of explainability, auditability and accountability mechanisms was limited. Most AI systems were described primarily in terms of predictive or classification performance, with minimal detail on whether outputs were interpretable to pharmacists, whether recommendations were accompanied by transparent rationale or confidence indicators, or whether systems included structured audit trails for reviewing AI driven decisions. Similarly, few studies explicitly described governance structures clarifying responsibility when AI-generated recommendations contributed to clinical decisions or potential medication-related harm. Where AI was embedded in clinical workflows, pharmacists were consistently described as the final decision makers, indicating that accountability remained human centred rather than algorithmically distributed. However, the absence of detailed reporting on override mechanisms, error tracking systems and post-implementation auditing represents a significant evidence gap in current literature on AI-enabled clinical pharmacy services.

Discussion

Given the growing use of robots, packagers and smart devices that generate vast amounts of data, it is likely that clinical pharmacy practice will incorporate more AI-powered apps and technologies in the coming years. Hospital-to-community pharmacotherapy efficacy and patient safety can be personalised more efficiently and accurately using ML models. This is achieved through applications of ML, NLP or natural language understanding (NLU) to simulate conversations between customers and customer care representatives. AI can also assist community pharmacists in stocking medications, anticipating future needs, and using customised software to notify patients via email about their medication needs. AI-powered data analytics can predict a patient’s future medicine purchases, allowing pharmacists to make better decisions about stock procurement.57, 58, 59, 60

Hospital and health system pharmacies may find that using NLP and ML to access and analyse unstructured, free-text data stored in millions of electronic health records (EHRs) is a crucial tool for enhancing patient care and conducting real-time assessments of medication efficacy. This includes medication safety, patient medication history, ADRs, DDIs, medication errors, therapeutic outcomes and pharmacokinetic consultations, as reported in the study by Dalal et al21 By incorporating specific clinical knowledge, patient data and other health information into medical decisions, a CDSS can enhance healthcare delivery. In a CDSS, a computerised clinical knowledge base is compared to the unique characteristics of each patient, and the clinician is supplied with recommendations or assessments tailored to the patient for consideration. Pharmacists can use this technology to sort through data and take action to avoid medication errors, minimise patient complications and reduce costs.61, 62 Similar models using ML have been reported in previous studies by Balestra et al22 and Hogue et al23 requiring hospital pharmacist interventions, whether for entire or targeted medication order reviews on specific medicine classes, such as anti-infective agents. In each of these studies, pharmacists reevaluated prescription errors detected by ML algorithms to determine whether an error had occurred. This could help enhance and prioritise the medication order review process.22, 23, 24, 25, 26

Despite the growing potential of AI-enabled CDSS in pharmacy practice, important implementation risks must be critically considered. A major concern is the generation of excessive false positive alerts, which may increase pharmacist workload, disrupt clinical workflow and contribute to alert fatigue. In medication management systems, false positives, false negatives, alert fatigue and workflow burden are among the most significant safety and usability concerns associated with AI-enabled CDSSs and pharmacist-facing medication management tools. Previous studies have shown that large volumes of low-specificity alerts often lead clinicians to override or ignore warnings, thereby reducing the effectiveness of decision support systems and potentially compromising patient safety.63, 64 In pharmacy settings, poor positive predictive value may paradoxically increase cognitive burden and decrease efficiency by requiring pharmacists to spend additional time evaluating clinically insignificant alerts. Conversely, low sensitivity and false negative outputs are equally problematic because they may create false reassurance and result in missed medication-related harm or delayed intervention.65 Importantly, the clinical utility of AI systems should not be assessed solely by overall accuracy metrics; evaluation should also include sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, alert appropriateness, usability and effects on workflow integration. Emerging evidence further suggests that AI-based optimisation strategies may help reduce unnecessary alerts and improve prioritisation, although concerns regarding external validation, transparency and implementation reliability remain unresolved. Therefore, future research should prospectively evaluate how AI tools influence pharmacist workload, alert acceptance rates, medication safety outcomes and clinician trust in real-world clinical environments.63, 66

Clinical pharmacy operational and therapeutic efficiency may be further enhanced by AI-enabled MTM. This would allow effective allocation by prioritising the assessment of medication orders for high-risk patients, providing clinical decision support software for pharmacists, or developing novel tools. Furthermore, AI offers clinical pharmacy the opportunity to cooperate more closely with various entities that serve the same patient. AI could be a helpful tool for advising on where and how to access the most affordable healthcare, interacting with healthcare providers, maximising the value of wearable data, offering advice on daily living, integrating exercise and diet, and promoting treatment compliance,15 as reported in earlier studies.27, 28

About half of patients with chronic medical conditions do not take their medicines as directed, raising the risk of morbidity and mortality.67 Given the complexity and diversity of medication adherence challenges, multifactorial approaches are needed to increase adherence, and AI technology may be a promising component of such interventions.68 AI-assisted interventions that monitor medication use, empower individuals, enhance communication between physicians and patients, and ultimately boost adherence levels could lead to better clinical outcomes and an improved quality of life for patients with non-communicable diseases. Depending on their technological designs and adherence monitoring features, a variety of AI technologies are employed to improve and monitor drug adherence. These include ingestible sensors, blister packs, electronic medication management systems, electronic pillboxes or bags, electronic pill bottles, motion sensor technology, video-based technology and patient self-report technology.68, 69

Several promising strategies for reducing the incidence of medication errors can be implemented, such as the introduction of barcode medication administration systems, effective medication error detection, and the implementation of electronic prescription systems.70 This was reported in several earlier studies by Nagata et al,41 Santos et al,36 Yalçin et al44 and Nguyen et al34 On the other hand, PIMs may result in prolonged hospitalisations and various complications such as DDIs and ADRs. The incidence of PIMs and ADRs could be significantly reduced by establishing a risk warning model for PIMs to screen high-risk patients and by introducing targeted interventions.71, 72 Predictive models for the prescription of PIMs are rapidly being developed using several types of AI and machine learning (ML) techniques. Such interventions were discussed in studies by Xingwei et al,43 Tai et al,38 Wongyikul et al,42 Patel et al39 and Zhuo et al40

The assessment of DDIs is critical to ensuring patients’ drug safety and avoiding undesirable complications as they can contribute to increased healthcare expenses. Predicting DDIs necessitates the use of multiple drug characteristics.73 However, AI can also help notify healthcare professionals to effectively detect drug–patient interactions, food–drug interactions and DDIs, thereby enhancing patient safety and adherence, and reducing expenses.74 One benefit of employing AI to monitor DDIs is the ability to create personalised medication regimens. AI can predict how to modify a patient’s prescription list in a way that would be most beneficial by gaining access to their medication history, current health status and objective data.75, 76 Concerns about AI utilisation in predicting DDI interactions were reported in several studies.45, 48, 49, 53, 54, 55, 77, 78, 79 Other studies revealed the wide spectrum of AI applications in ADR detection, including knowledge graph-based algorithms, clinical decision support tools and prediction models.50, 51, 80, 81

Therefore, the integration of AI and ML into ward pharmacy practice has significantly transformed traditional clinical pharmacy workflows. In conventional ward pharmacy practice without AI support, pharmacists rely heavily on manual review of medication orders, clinical guidelines and patient medical records to identify prescribing errors, drug–drug interactions and dosing issues, which can be time-consuming and may be influenced by human limitations such as workload pressure and variability in clinical judgement. In contrast, AI- and ML-enabled systems provide real-time clinical decision support by automatically analysing electronic health records to detect potential medication-related problems, prioritise high-risk patients, and predict adverse drug events before they occur. Studies have shown that AI-based clinical decision support systems can improve the detection of prescribing errors and enhance medication safety compared to standard practice alone, particularly in high-complexity inpatient settings. Furthermore, AI-assisted pharmacy systems streamline workflow processes such as medication reconciliation, alert generation and risk stratification, thereby reducing pharmacist workload and allowing more time for direct patient care activities during ward rounds. Hospital pharmacy automation and AI integration have also been associated with improved efficiency and reduction in medication-use process errors compared to traditional manual systems. However, while AI enhances speed and predictive capability, traditional clinical pharmacy practice remains essential for contextual interpretation, ethical decision-making and individualised patient care, as AI tools may be limited by data quality issues, algorithmic bias and alert fatigue. Therefore, the optimal model in modern ward pharmacy practice is a hybrid approach in which AI/ML systems augment, but do not replace, the clinical expertise of pharmacists.20, 57, 82, 83

Nevertheless, a major requirement for pharmacist-facing AI systems is interpretability and explainability, which is especially important in pharmacy because medication-related decisions are often nuanced and context dependent. Pharmacists must evaluate renal function, allergies, contraindications, therapeutic duplication, drug interactions and patient-specific variables before approving therapy. If an AI system flags a severe interaction or recommends a dosage adjustment without revealing the evidence basis, pharmacists may struggle to determine whether the recommendation is clinically justified.84 Explainable AI mechanisms – including evidence-linked recommendations, confidence scores, citation tracing and interpretable decision pathways – help pharmacists determine whether outputs align with accepted medical standards. Reviews of explainable AI in healthcare emphasise that explanations should not merely be technically accurate, but also clinically meaningful, understandable, stable and relevant to decision-making.85 Therefore, the increasing adoption of pharmacist-facing AI tools in medication management, clinical decision support, dispensing workflows and patient counselling has introduced major governance, accountability and safety considerations. Beyond technical performance alone, it is critical to evaluate whether these systems provide interpretable explanations for alerts, allow pharmacists to override recommendations, maintain robust audit mechanisms, and clearly define responsibility when AI-supported recommendations contribute to patient harm. These concerns are central to safe implementation because pharmacy practice involves high-risk clinical decisions where errors can directly result in adverse drug events, hospitalisation or death. The future AI international consensus guideline emphasises that healthcare AI tools should provide ʻclinically meaningful information about the logic behind AI decisions,’ allowing clinicians to interpret outputs, understand limitations, and intervene when necessary.82, 84

Furthermore, in clinical pharmacy applications, pharmacist intervention thresholds and medication-review practices are often shaped by local policy and staffing models, which may further limit model portability across institutions. An important limitation relates to external validity and the broader generalisability of AI models in clinical pharmacy practice. Models developed using data from a single institution may capture local prescribing cultures, formulary restrictions, pharmacist intervention behaviours, and institution-specific workflows that differ substantially across healthcare systems. Variability in EHR architecture, coding standards, documentation practices, language-specific clinical notes, and medication-use processes may introduce dataset and workflow shifts that reduce model performance when deployed in external settings. Previous studies have demonstrated that healthcare AI models frequently exhibit performance degradation across sites despite strong internal validation results, highlighting persistent challenges in model transportability and reproducibility.86, 87 Furthermore, recent methodological work has argued that conventional external validation alone may be insufficient to guarantee real-world robustness because healthcare systems continuously evolve and local clinical practices differ substantially between institutions.88 These concerns are particularly relevant in clinical pharmacy settings, where medication-use policies, pharmacist documentation practices, antimicrobial stewardship strategies and intervention thresholds are often highly localised. Consequently, models trained on institution-specific data may encode local clinical culture rather than universally transferable clinical reasoning. Future work should prioritise multicentre prospective validation, evaluation across heterogeneous EHR systems and patient populations, and implementation strategies such as recalibration, domain adaptation, federated learning and recurring local validation to improve generalisability and clinical applicability.

In the same context, AI, particularly large language models (LLMs) and generative AI systems, is increasingly being integrated into healthcare workflows for clinical documentation, medication management, patient counselling and decision support. Despite these potential benefits, generative AI introduces important safety limitations that are particularly critical in medication-related decision making. A key concern is the generation of hallucinated outputs, including incorrect or fabricated medication recommendations, dosing instructions or clinical rationales, which may appear plausible but lack grounding in validated clinical evidence. This is especially problematic in pharmacy practice, where even minor inaccuracies can lead to adverse drug events. In addition, performance may vary considerably across clinical scenarios, patient populations and prompt structures. Hallucinated outputs are particularly of concern in prescribing and therapeutic recommendation systems because clinicians and patients may over-trust AI-generated responses. These limitations are especially critical in healthcare because even a single incorrect recommendation may result in adverse drug events, delayed treatment or patient harm.89 Therefore, generative AI should currently be regarded as an adjunctive support tool requiring pharmacist oversight rather than an autonomous clinical decision-maker, particularly in high-risk medication management settings.

Limitations

This scoping review has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis was restricted to studies indexed in the selected databases (Web of Science, PubMed and Scopus), and grey literature was not included. As a result, relevant evidence from conference proceedings, institutional reports, regulatory documentation and other non-indexed sources may have been missed, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the review. Also, the review was limited to studies published in English. This language restriction may have introduced language bias and led to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages, particularly given the global nature of artificial intelligence research and its application in diverse healthcare systems.

Furthermore, although a structured search strategy was used, the selection of search terms may have limited sensitivity. Variability in terminology used to describe AL, ML and clinical pharmacy activities means that some relevant studies may not have been captured. This is particularly relevant in a rapidly evolving field where inconsistent indexing and emerging terminology are common. Also, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of study design, clinical setting, AI methodology, data sources and outcome measures. This heterogeneity limited the ability to directly compare findings across studies and precluded quantitative synthesis. It also reflects the early and disconnected nature of the evidence base in this field.

We also highlight that although inclusion criteria were designed to capture pharmacist relevant clinical pharmacy applications, some included studies may have only indirect or partial relevance to pharmacist-facing workflows. This reflects the challenge of defining clinical pharmacy boundaries in AI research, where systems may support medication-related processes without explicit pharmacist integration. Finally, this review did not include a formal quality appraisal or systematic validation assessment of the included studies. As a result, the methodological robustness, risk of bias and clinical validity of individual AI models could not be systematically evaluated. This limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness or readiness for clinical implementation.

Finally, evidence of real-world prospective implementation remains limited across the included literature. Most studies were retrospective in nature, focused on model development or internal validation, or assessed performance using historical datasets. There is therefore limited evidence demonstrating sustained integration of AI tools into routine clinical pharmacy practice or their impact on patient outcomes in real world settings.

Conclusion

This scoping review systematically mapped and synthesised the current applications of AI in clinical pharmacy services across hospital and community settings, with a focus on pharmacist-facing clinical workflows and medication management functions. The review demonstrates that AI applications are most commonly concentrated in five core domains: electronic health record and medication order review, medication dispensing, medication therapy management and adherence support, medication error detection and prevention, and detection of drug interactions and adverse drug reactions. This mapping shows that AI is primarily being applied to high-volume, high-risk medication processes where decision support and risk stratification can potentially enhance pharmacist efficiency and patient safety.

Furthermore, the review identifies that the principal functions supported by AI include prioritisation of medication order review, identification of potentially inappropriate prescriptions, prediction and prevention of medication errors, optimisation of dispensing workflows, and support for adherence monitoring and pharmacovigilance activities. Across these domains, AI systems predominantly operate as decision support tools that augment pharmacist judgement rather than replace clinical decision-making. A key finding is that pharmacist oversight remains central across all applications, reflecting the continued necessity of human clinical interpretation in medication management.

A further key takeaway from this review is that most AI applications in clinical pharmacy are still in early stages of development, with the majority of studies based on retrospective datasets or proof-of-concept models. While reported performance metrics and early implementation studies suggest improvements in workflow efficiency, error detection and risk prediction, evidence of sustained real-world clinical impact and patient outcome improvement remains limited. In addition, variability in model performance, inconsistent external validation, and limited evaluation of usability and workflow integration indicate that many tools are not yet ready for routine clinical deployment.

Conclusively, AI applications in clinical pharmacy services are expanding rapidly and show clear potential to enhance medication management processes. However, their current role is best understood as supportive and exploratory rather than fully operational within routine care. Future research should therefore prioritise external validation across diverse healthcare settings, prospective clinical trials or real-world implementation studies, rigorous evaluation of workflow impact, and continuous monitoring of patient safety outcomes. Strengthening these areas will be essential to ensure safe, effective and equitable integration of AI-enabled tools into clinical pharmacy practice.
