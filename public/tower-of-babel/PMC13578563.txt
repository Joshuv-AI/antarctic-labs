Artificial Intelligence‐Assisted Wireless Handheld Ultrasound for Screening Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip in Infants
Authors: Dandan Zhang, Simiao Tao, Ping Chen, Jiangtao Wang, Yu Yang, Xiuxiu Zhang, Tieqiang Wang, Hailin Pan, Jiong Chen, Baoying Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUltrasonography is increasingly the preferred method for infant hip screening to enable timely diagnosis and treatment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). However, its reliance on experienced specialists and bulky equipment limits its application in routine screening, particularly in resource‐limited and remote settings. We aimed to develop an artificial intelligence wireless handheld ultrasound hip diagnostic system (AI‐HUDs) for accurate and robust DDH diagnosis.MethodsAn AI‐HUDs was developed to automatically identify and analyze 6 key anatomical landmarks in static and dynamic hip ultrasound images, employing the Graf method to measure α and β angles. The dataset comprised 1192 ultrasound images and 498 dynamic videos. The YOLOv8 model was trained and tested to construct AI‐HUDs. Evaluation focused on the consistency between AI‐HUDs' automatic angle measurements and manual measurements by experts using traditional ultrasound equipment. Additionally, 104 hip ultrasound videos from 52 infants were acquired by a resident physician using AI‐HUDs, while corresponding static images were manually measured by ultrasound experts to assess agreement between novice‐operated AI‐HUDs and expert manual assessment.ResultsCompared with expert manual measurements, the AI‐HUDs demonstrated good performance in static mode: the differences in α and β angles exhibited standard deviations of 1.38° and 2.14°, and mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 1.05° and 1.65°, respectively. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were 0.82 (α) and 0.64 (β). In dynamic mode, the MAEs increased slightly to 1.18° (α) and 1.86° (β), while the ICCs decreased to 0.77 for α and 0.51 for β. When operated by the resident physician, AI‐HUDs maintained good performance with expert manual measurements: MAEs were 1.16° (α) and 1.91° (β); ICCs were 0.69 (α) and 0.61 (β). Bland–Altman analysis showed 96.15% (α) and 95.19% (β) of data points within the limits of agreement.ConclusionAI‐HUDs demonstrated high reliability and accuracy in automatically measuring α and β angles. Resident physicians using this system achieved diagnostic performance comparable to experts. AI‐HUDs show promise as a convenient DDH screening tool in resource‐scarce regions, potentially facilitating wider adoption of infant hip ultrasound screening.Keywords: artificial intelligence, developmental dysplasia of the hip, handheld ultrasound, infants

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) encompasses congenital or developmental abnormalities of the hip joint, including acetabular dysplasia, instability, subluxation, and dislocation, with an incidence of 0.15–2%. 1 , 2 Although DDH can present in toddlers and adolescents, most cases are diagnosed and treated within the first few months of life. Untreated DDH can lead to complications such as hip dysfunction, abnormal gait, early arthritis, and degenerative joint changes in childhood or adulthood. 3 , 4 Early diagnosis enables timely interventions to promote normal hip development. 5 Diagnosis typically combines clinical examinations (e.g., Ortolani and Barlow tests) and imaging (X‐ray, ultrasound, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)). 6 MRI's high cost limits its widespread use, while X‐ray cannot visualize cartilage effectively before 6 months of age. Consequently, ultrasound is the favored diagnostic technique due to its ability to depict non‐bony structures and lack of ionizing radiation. 7 , 8

Graf pioneered the use of static ultrasound and quantitative α and β angle assessments for DDH diagnosis in 1980, 7 while Harcke et al demonstrated ultrasound's suitability for visualizing neonatal hip anatomy. 8 , 9 Ultrasound remains the primary clinical tool for DDH screening and early detection, offering accuracy, real‐time feedback, and safety. 10 The Graf method is the most widely recognized ultrasound approach. However, manual identification of anatomical landmarks to calculate α and β angles introduces significant inter‐observer variability, 11 with reported standard deviations reaching 3° for α and 6° for β angles. 12 This variability, particularly pronounced in resource‐limited settings lacking expertise, can lead to diagnostic errors, potentially compromising prognosis and treatment. 3 Therefore, obtaining standardized ultrasound images with low inter‐ and intra‐observer variability for accurate measurements remains a challenge.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a powerful deep learning architecture, excel at autonomously detecting patterns in medical images after training. 13 , 14 , 15 Their efficiency, objectivity, and automation offer potential for improved DDH diagnosis. For instance, Liu et al developed a neonatal hip bone segmentation network reducing diagnostic error rates from 6–10% to 2%, 16 while Aysun et al enhanced CNN accuracy to 97.70% using innovative data augmentation. 17 Huang et al achieved 98.64% accuracy with DDHnet, an AI system for static ultrasound diagnosis. 18 Despite existing research efforts dedicated to convenience and accuracy, currently most methods still use large ultrasound equipment, suffering from limitations in terms of cost‐effectiveness, portability, adaptability for immediate use, wireless connectivity, and data sharing. Concurrently, handheld ultrasound devices offer portability, ease of use, and rapid imaging capabilities, finding increasing utility in clinical and emergency settings, 19 , 20 , 21 with demonstrated adequacy as point‐of‐care tools. 22 Several studies have explored embedding AI diagnostic algorithms into portable devices: Libon et al validated the feasibility of using an AI‐enhanced handheld ultrasound tool for DDH screening in a sample of 306 infants. 23 A Philips Lumify handheld ultrasound with a 12 MHz linear probe confirmed the feasibility of a portable ultrasound protocol in primary care. 24 Nevertheless, the aforementioned research still has room for improvement in terms of full‐process automation, enhancing real‐time measurement capabilities, reducing dependence on operator experience, and for deploying wireless AI‐integrated devices in resource‐scarce regions via edge computing.

To address these issues, we developed an artificial intelligence wireless handheld ultrasound diagnostic system (AI‐HUDs) utilizing a 6‐keypoint landmark‐based model for both static images and dynamic videos, which improved the measurement, automation, intelligence, and convenience of processes through wireless devices.

Applying Graf's method within the standard anatomical plane, the wireless device connects to a tablet within 10 meters, providing edge‐computed diagnostic results. This study evaluates the agreement between AI‐HUDs and human expert angle measurements. While in early development, AI‐HUDs demonstrate potential to improve diagnostic quality, enhance portability, and reduce clinician workload.

Adhering to standards from the American College of Radiology (ACR) 25 and the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM), 26 this research aims to develop an innovative, automated, portable, and accurate approach for ultrasonic DDH assessment.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Participants

A prospective, single‐center, paired‐design study was conducted at the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital (IPMCH) from June 1 to December 31, 2024, and was approved by the IPMCH Ethics Committee (ChiCTR2400092215). Two ultrasound systems were employed: (1) A GE Voluson E10 with a linear probe (8.0–10.0 MHz), used manually to capture images, with expert consensus Graf angle measurements serving as ground truth; (2) A registered wireless handheld linear probe ultrasound (GUSG75L, 7.5 MHz, Beijing, China, registration certificate number 20232060372), integrated into the AI‐HUDs framework. This device wirelessly connects to an Android tablet (Huawei A9000) running AI‐HUDs software, transmitting images and 6‐second videos. It's worth noting that this device is extremely lightweight at just 165 g, and offers long‐lasting battery life—capable of operating continuously for 3.5 hours on a single charge. Moreover, the device meets the IPX7 waterproof standard. All images were converted from the original format of DICOM to JPG. Examinations were performed by 2 accredited sonographers with 5 and 7 years of infant hip screening experience. An expert sonographer with 10 years of experience served as the blinded reviewer for all Graf angle measurements to establish the ground truth. Furthermore, this expert provided the blinded ground truth for Graf angles in the agreement test and conducted independent scans on the generalization test cohort to assess real‐world applicability. For comparison, a first‐year resident physician in the Department of Ultrasound Medicine—who had completed a standard 3‐month rotation in musculoskeletal ultrasound but had no prior experience in infant hip screening or DDH diagnosis—performed the same scans on the generalization test cohort, allowing direct evaluation of diagnostic performance between the expert and a novice operator.

The resident underwent a structured 14‐hour training program, comprising 6 hours of didactic learning (anatomy, pathology, software usage) and 8 hours of supervised hands‐on practical training involving infant positioning and probe manipulation. Each operator was required to work in compliance with the protocol. The protocol primarily covered standardized scanning procedures; image quality control; and data and privacy protection.

Study Population and Ultrasonography Dataset

Participants were recruited from infants attending routine well‐child check‐ups at the IPMCH outpatient clinic. These were asymptomatic infants without prior orthopedic referrals, representing a general screening population. The inclusion criteria were that: (i) infants aged 5–8 weeks; (ii) normal neurological examination; (iii) parental informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were that: (i) hip disorders (e.g., suppurative arthritis, multiple joint contractures); (ii) conditions impairing hip movement (e.g., rickets, decreased muscle strength); (iii) uncooperative infants or incomplete data.

Ultrasound examination followed Graf's method: infants were positioned laterally with the examined hip relaxed and the limb slightly flexed and internally rotated. The probe was placed laterally near the greater trochanter, parallel to the body's long axis, aligned with the pelvic coronal plane. Slight adjustments yielded a standard coronal image showing key structures: chondro‐osseous border, femoral head, synovial fold, joint capsule, labrum, cartilage, bony roof, bony rim, lower limb of os ilium. 27 , 28 The α and β angles were measured according to the Graf' method. The baseline (L1) was defined as the straight line along the iliac crest. The α angle was formed between the baseline (L1) and the line (L2) tangent to the bony acetabular roof, originating from the inferior edge of the iliac branch. The β angle was formed between the baseline (L1) and the line (L3) tangent to the cartilaginous roof, extending from the inflection point at the bony margin to the center of the labrum. Six keypoints were extracted from these 3 lines, 2 of which frequently coincided with the bony rim point (Figure 1).

A paired‐design was utilized wherein each subject was scanned sequentially with both the GE Voluson E10 and the wireless handheld device GUSG75L. The scanning order was randomized to mitigate potential order bias. To manage data storage and processing load, videos were not collected for every hip scan, but were acquired from a randomized subset of subjects. This approach ensured sufficient statistical power for model development while avoiding selection bias. The dataset was partitioned into training and agreement test subsets in a 3:1 ratio. Figure 2 outlines the data selection and allocation process. Initially, 1262 ultrasound images and 504 ultrasound videos were collected from 608 infants. After excluding inadequate data, 1192 images and 498 videos were retained for further analysis. Among these, 1088 images and 394 videos were used for model development. The training set comprised data from 400 infants. Due to motion artifacts or lack of cooperation, 100 infants underwent unilateral scanning (only 1 hip joint). To enhance the model's generalization, 80% of the data were acquired using the GUSG75L and 20% using the GE Voluson E10. The final dataset yielded 700 images (560 GUSG75L, 140 GE) and 200 videos (all GUSG75L). All images were annotated with key points using LabelMe based on Graf's method, covering the 6 anatomical landmarks required for angle measurement. All annotations were reviewed by an expert ultrasonographer to ensure consensus. The agreement test set comprised data from 120 infants. Specifically, 46 infants in this set were scanned unilaterally; consequently, the final agreement test set comprised 194 valid hip assessments. For each assessment, a pair of static images (1 GE, 1 GUSG75L) and 1 handheld video clip were analyzed, resulting in a total of 388 static images and 194 videos. Additionally, a separate generalization test set was formed with 104 images (from GE Voluson E10) and 104 videos (from GUSG75L), derived from 52 infants.

Development of the AI Model and the AI‐HUDs

Data Preprocessing

All static images and frames selected from dynamic videos at 6 seconds according to Graf standards for measurement purposes were annotated, reviewed, Graf‐classified by 2 sonographers, and assigned exclusively to 1 group.

Following the official recommended hyperparameter settings, all images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels. To ensure compatibility with general detection frameworks, single‐channel images were converted into a 3‐channel format (3 × H × W) without altering the original grayscale information. Additionally, data augmentation was performed through random horizontal flipping, contrast adjustments, and low‐level Gaussian noise.

Network Architecture

We selected YOLOv8 as the core algorithm for model construction. At the methodological level, we first developed a 3‐dimensional stratification scheme—“single‐center, case‐based, and scene‐based”—for data partitioning and augmentation. Specifically, static frames and dynamic videos from different infants were subjected to cross‐validation, ensuring that all frames from the same infant were always allocated to the same fold to avoid data leakage. On this basis, brightness perturbations were designed to mimic 3 common sources of noise in handheld operations: depth gain adjustment, artifact spots, and probe tilt. During training, sampling was performed without replacement so that the model could encounter difficult samples with a balanced distribution in each epoch. Within the overall framework, we adopted a dual‐network strategy based on YOLOv8: a standard‐plane screening network built with YOLOv8 was first applied to continuous frames for rapid candidate ROI detection, with a gated confidence mechanism used to filter out non‐standard frames. Subsequently, a keypoint detection network (KP‐YOLOv8) performed 6‐point localization and real‐time calculation of the α and β angles in the filtered frames. This unified dual‐network architecture based on YOLOv8 not only reduced invalid inference but also ensured real‐time performance and high accuracy in angle estimation. The adaptive switching between static and dynamic modes was achieved.

The YOLOv8 network architecture comprises a 20‐layer backbone network (encoder‐decoder) and a head structure. The VGG16‐inspired backbone connects to the decoder through dense skip paths, which is beneficial for the encoder to progressively extract features and enrich its semantic content, which has been gradually enhanced before being fused with the semantically rich feature maps in the decoder, as shown in Figure 3. Stage 0–1: Primary image features were captured through 2 convolutional layers. Stages 2–9: Using convolutional layers and C2f modules, enhanced by a fast spatial pyramid pooling (SPPF) layer in Stage 9 to achieve continuous feature fusion and enhancement, widen the receptive field and aggregate multi‐scale features. Hence, enhancing the model's ability for object detection.

Backbone network.

The head structure is responsible for converting the high‐level features from the backbone network into the final detection outputs. This includes: Stages 10–13: Features are continuously amplified and refined through upsampling layers and feature fusion layers, such as the concatenation operation, to better capture and locate targets. Stages 14–19: Features are further refined through consecutive convolutional layers, along with dimensionality reduction, in preparation for the final output. Stage 20: Finally, a series of convolution and fusion operations transform the refined feature maps into output results for object detection, as shown in Figure 4.

Model Training and System Development

For dynamic video images, key frames that display the dynamic changes in the hip joint are automatically identified and extracted using the previously trained model. Each key frame is then inputted into the trained model, which is further optimized to enhance its performance and accuracy under complex dynamic conditions. For completing the AI‐HUDs used in hip examinations, the steps involve embedding the model into the tablet and connecting it to the handheld ultrasound device via Wi‐Fi. Experimental Tests

Agreement Test

Performance was evaluated on the independent agreement test set (388 static images, 194 dynamic videos). Metrics focused on consistency between AI‐HUDs automatic angle measurements and expert manual measurements using traditional ultrasound.

Generalization Test

A resident physician (3 months of DDH experience) used AI‐HUDs in dynamic mode, while an expert sonographer (10 years' experience) performed traditional ultrasound (GE Voluson E10) on the same infants using the hold‐out dataset (104 videos/images). Both operators measured α and β angles bilaterally, with both blinded to each other's measurements. Agreement in Graf angles was assessed.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS (v23.0, IBM) analyzed all data. Measurement data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K‐S) test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The mean absolute error (MAE) was employed to evaluate measurement bias among different observers. The consistency of angle measurements (α and β) obtained by the AI‐HUDs, residents, and experts was analyzed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), along with its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Additionally, scatter plots and Bland–Altman plots were generated to visualize the agreement between measurements acquired via the AI‐HUDs and conventional ultrasound.

Results

AI‐HUDs was tested on 194 images (static mode) and 194 videos (dynamic mode) to verify consistency with 194 images from the GE Voluson E10. The hold‐out generalization set comprised 104 GE Voluson E10 images and 104 AI‐HUDs videos. Infant median age was 46.71 days (range 35–55); 42.9% male, 57.1% female.

Statistical Measurement Results: Static Mode

As shown in Table 1, the AI‐HUDs in static mode demonstrated good performance compared to the traditional ultrasound device for measuring α and β angles. Differences in α and β angles had standard deviations of 1.38° and 2.14°, and MAEs of 1.05° and 1.65°, respectively. ICCs were 0.82 (α) and 0.64 (β), indicating good and moderate agreement. Bland–Altman plots (Figure 6) showed 95.36 and 94.85% of α and β measurements within limits of agreement, symmetrically distributed.

Discussion

Ultrasound is crucial for early DDH detection and joint assessment. 29 This study developed a CNN‐based framework registering 6 key hip anatomical landmarks to automatically measure α and β angles according to Graf's method. Integrating this AI with a wireless handheld ultrasound creates a practical DDH diagnostic tool, particularly valuable for primary and remote healthcare settings. Our study exemplifies the potential of AI‐HUDs to effectively process both static and dynamic videos. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to combine the strength of artificial intelligence with a wireless, handheld, portable ultrasound to achieve an accurate, lightweight, low‐resource‐dependent, and robust AI screening system.

Gokharman et al 30 showed that ultrasound screening of the hip joint performed at 8 weeks after birth is preferable to the one performed at 4 weeks. Donnelly et al 31 believe that 6 weeks after birth is a significant time for hip ultrasound examination. The data for our study include infants aged 5–8 weeks, which ensures a shorter time span, better image quality, and more accurate construction and training of the AI‐HUDs.

In static mode, Lee showed the mean absolute deviation of the α angle was 3.4° and the β angle was 4.5°, which were higher than ours (1.05°, 1.65°). 32 Simon demonstrated that in static image measurements, the ICCs for α and β (0.72, 0.34) exceeded those observed among humans, which were lower than our ICCs (0.82, 0.64) partly. 33 Roovers noted lower observer variability with static images than among humans. 34 In our study, in the static image measurement mode, the MAEs of angle measurements (α: 1.05°, β: 1.65°) were similar to those of experienced sonographers. The performance was better than that of other computer‐aided methods. 35 , 36

In the dynamic measurement mode, the AI‐aided framework was designed to automatically select a standard coronal plane from the ultrasound video and subsequently execute the angle measurement as performed in the static image mode. The selection of the optimal standard plane is decisive for accurate angle measurement and DDH diagnosis. While we utilized a deep neural network (YOLOv8) to detect key anatomical structures (eg, acetabulum and femoral head) for plane selection, 37 we observed that the dynamic mode exhibited slightly greater deviation, higher MAEs, and lower ICCs compared to the static mode. We attribute this minor performance gap to error accumulation inherent in the dynamic workflow. Unlike static analysis, dynamic measurement involves a sequential 2‐step process: (1) frame selection and (2) angle measurement. Any suboptimal frame selected by the AI—despite our efforts to evaluate spatial interplay and ilium shape 38 —will inevitably propagate error into the subsequent measurement phase. This highlights a current limitation: while AI algorithms rely on quantifiable confidence scores and geometric rules, 38 they still exhibit a slight gap compared to the intuitive, experience‐based judgment of human experts in identifying the ‘perfect’ standard plane. Importantly, we acknowledge that experienced sonographers remain superior to AI in capturing and identifying standard sections. The purpose of this study is not to replace the expert, but to bridge the skill gap for novice operators. Although the MAEs in our dynamic mode (1.18° for α and 1.86° for β) were slightly higher than those reported in static settings, they remain within clinically acceptable limits. This validates the AI‐HUDs as a robust auxiliary tool to assist beginners in standardizing their scanning protocols. Various deep learning models have already been proposed and applied in clinical assisted diagnosis for automatic recognition and classification of hip joint ultrasound images. Darilmaz et al proposed a real‐time detection system (AI‐SPS) based on SSD‐MobileNetV2 and YOLOv11n, designed for automatic recognition of the Graf standard plane, which showed the YOLO series outperformed traditional lightweight CNNs in both accuracy and real‐time performance. 39 We selected YOLOv8 as the core algorithm for model construction due to its outstanding performance metrics and its advanced technical features, including an anchor‐free design, a decoupled detection head architecture, powerful data augmentation and label smoothing, automated diagnostic capabilities, and strong support for interpretability and visualization. The end‐to‐end frame rate of 30 fps was maintained under the 10 m wireless link, which fully reflects our system innovation in data planning, structure cutting, loss function design and dual network coordination. These characteristics enable YOLOv8 not only to deliver highly accurate detection results in medical image analysis tasks, but also to assist doctors in making faster and more precise diagnostic decisions.

The assistance of artificial intelligence and deep learning has enabled AI‐HUDs to have advantages in automatic learning, algorithm optimization, continuous learning, and so on, significantly improving the automation of detection. Although ensuring that the AI‐HUDs selects plane exactly matching those chosen by sonographers remains a challenge, it adheres to Graf's standards and yields dependable measurement outcomes. Although Chen et al fully automated Graf angle evaluation method supports plane recognition for both static and dynamic ultrasound images, this research does not address the integration of wireless devices and dynamic mode processing. 38 The handheld Philips Lumify probe (12 MHz linear probe) confirmed the feasibility of a portable ultrasound protocol in primary care, with a follow‐up rate of 14% and a detection rate of 2%, which are comparable to the results of traditional large‐scale screening studies conducted by experts in some developed countries. 24 In comparison, our study simplified the device to wireless mode, which helps babies in special environments free themselves from the constraints of wires. Additionally, we optimized the algorithm to automatically measure standardized sections in video images, enhancing accuracy. Therefore, this study showcases the potential application in resource‐constrained regions by combining hardware integration with dynamic video analysis, especially for remote diagnostics through edge computing. In the generalization test, the resident doctor showed good performance of junior staff using AI‐HUDs. They can report the same Graf type. This automated approach not only provides reliable diagnostic support in resource‐limited settings but also reduces reliance on specialized physicians. The AI‐HUDs reduce operator dependence mainly through automated landmark recognition, minimizing manual searching for key points; providing real‐time feedback during operation to guide probe movement toward the optimal imaging plane; minimizing errors by optimizing images through AI; and precisely calculating angular parameters for objective measurement. Our approach provides an automatic and effective way to utilize deep learning techniques in diagnosing DDH. Given its status as a completely automated diagnostic system with rapid processing and no need for additional manual input, employing a dynamic measurement approach could significantly enhance the efficiency and quality of sonographers' work in screening for infant DDH.

Handheld ultrasound is characterized by its small size, light weight, portability, good image quality, wireless transmission capability within 10 m, and relatively low cost, which can help to expand the reach of medical services, particularly in regions where traditional medical resources are difficult to access. Based on the above conditions, a tablet and a mobile phone‐sided wireless portable ultrasound device embedded with an AI‐based model can replace the need for large ultrasound equipment to accurately complete the hip examination of infants.

This study still has several limitations. First, there were no pathological cases in the study cohort (Graf type II–IV), all enrolled infants were classified as Graf type I (normal), limiting the evaluation in detecting severe dysplasia. However, this is not entirely unexpected given that the study cohort consisted of infants screened in a healthy outpatient clinic population, and considering the overall low incidence of DDH in the general population (approximately 1.5‰). Notably, accurate quantitative assessment of Graf angles remains theoretically essential for precise DDH diagnosis. The scope of our study was focused on establishing the baseline performance of the AI‐HUDs in a normal population. Second, the lack of long‐term follow‐up data is another limitation. Without follow‐up assessments, we could not validate the long‐term screening efficacy of the AI system or its diagnostic accuracy for severe developmental dysplasia (Graf II–IV) over time. To address this, we have initiated a prospective longitudinal study to track the hip development of these infants until skeletal maturity. This future work will validate the clinical utility of the AI‐HUDs in predicting long‐term prognosis. Third, although the dataset size was sufficient for model development/validation, a larger test set would better evaluate generalizability. In the future, we will collect more hip joint data, including a wider range of infant ages and different Graf types, to further improve and evaluate the proposed framework's performance. Finally, infants need to be placed in a lateral decubitus position. Due to varying degrees of cooperation among infants, positional variations may occur. Operators require basic medical training to obtain videos capturing the standard coronal hip section. Non‐specialized personnel cannot currently operate the system.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates the potential of AI‐HUDs to provide an effective, efficient, and feasible automated method of measuring hip angles and performing Graf classification. It demonstrates significant potential for DDH diagnosis in primary and remote healthcare settings lacking specialized personnel and equipment.
