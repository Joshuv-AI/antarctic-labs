 CIE Power Generation Guide
 Purpose: Investigate and develop CIE approaches and guidance
 that accelerate the rapid and secure deployment of power
 generation technologies

 • Technologies considered: Natural gas, hydropower, nuclear, wind, solar,
 bioenergy, geothermal, and hydrogen
 • Addresses “digital challenges,” which are examples of critical cybersecurity
 challenges for each technology group. The digital challenges are formulated to
 restrict the guide’s scope to critical issues, allowing targeted guidance without
 being exhaustive.
 • Technology overviews respond to questions from the CIE Implementation
 Guide (inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/Sort_67122.pdf).
 • Use cases highlight specific challenges and suggest CIE-based mitigations.

6

 Key Takeaways
 • Consider including cyber-induced impacts in safety and hazard
 analysis
 • Identify consequences with both engineers and security
 professionals
 • Cybersecurity practitioners:
 o Excel at identifying threats and potential consequences
 o Security knowledge does not equal the ability to design CIE mitigations.

 • Engineers:
 o Excel at designing mitigations
 o Engineering knowledge does not equal the ability to analyze cyber-based
 risks and consequences.

 Hydropower Consequence Focused Design
 1.C.4: What are the critical components and subcomponents
 in the system design? What are the consequences of failure
 or misuse of each critical component?
 • Misuse of the water flow sensors could result in damage to the turbine
 and associated components such as the generator.
 • Compromise of the water level sensor could increase the possibility of
 a flooding event or compromise the dam’s role in water management
 during droughts.
 • Compromised pressure sensors could result in damage to the water
 management mechanisms.
 • Compromise of the vibration sensor could result in increased risk of
 damage to the structural integrity of the dam.
 • Compromise of the speed sensor could result in problems in frequency
 regulation, which could lead to blackouts.

 Hydropower Engineered Control
 What consequences of failure or misoperation are the
 engineered controls designed to prevent?
 • Consequence: When a turbine operates at partial load or when
 the turbine is not operating at its optimal efficiency the flow of
 water through the turbine becomes uneven, creating turbulence,
 cavitation, and pressure fluctuations causing the turbine to vibrate
 excessively. This is called operating in the rough zone and is
 generally undesirable because it reduces the turbine’s lifespan,
 increases maintenance costs, and can lower the overall efficiency
 and reliability of the power plant.
 • Engineered Control: Turbine governors and speed protection
 devices automatically adjust water flow and shut down the turbine
 if necessary. In addition, operators have manual controls through
 which they try to avoid this zone by adjusting the turbine’s load,
 flow, or operational settings to bring it back into a more stable and

 Natural Gas Planned Resilience
 1.C.4—a: What are the consequences of failure or misuse of each
 critical component?
 • Loss of plant critical function (i.e., electricity generation) coincides with the
 following:
 o Loss of gas turbine subsystems (i.e., fuel, combustion, lubrication oil, overspeed, etc.)
 o Loss of gas turbine/steam turbine generator excitation control, rotor speed control,
 and stator monitoring (i.e., voltage, current, phase angle, and others)
 o Loss of protective relaying (i.e., generator and substation)
 o Loss of substation control/monitoring.

 10.A.6—b: Have conceptual alternatives been identified to
 maintain system operation when upstream interdependent
 systems are in diminished operating modes?
 • Consideration of alternative fuel gas supply focused on the natural gas
 delivery (e.g., transmission infrastructure, asset operator)
 • Rely on the manual operation of critical subsystems to the extent that baseline
 “unit” automation is still functioning properly.

 Nuclear Consequence-Focused Design
 How are identified high-consequence events documented, monitored
 for change, and reassessed?
 • Documented within the final safety analysis report and probabilistic risk assessment
 models in accordance with 10 CFR 52.47.12
 • Changes to the facility or procedures must be documented according to 10 CFR
 50.59.13, including a brief report of any changes, tests, and experiments.

 What are the critical processes, operations, and/or administrative
 actions required to protect against unacceptable impacts and ensure
 the execution of critical facility functions?
 • Passive safety design features: The main decay heat removal mechanism through
 some combination of natural convection, radiation, thermal expansion, or capillary
 effects
 • Cybersecurity procedures and equipment: Data diodes and firewalls are
 introduced to prevent connections to nonplant or external networks.

 Nuclear Design Simplification and Layered Design
 4.C.2: What simplification techniques and procedures can the
 owner/operator adopt to simplify design?
 • Natural circulation for core cooling instead of pumps (inherently simpler and more
 reliable)
 • Gravity-driven safety systems for reactor shutdown (avoids complex control systems
 and reduces reliance on power).
 5.C.3: Are layered defenses correlated to the reliability of the system
 and/or consequence magnitude?
 • NRC RG 5.7128 dictates that defensive levels correspond to more controls required
 in a layer, where the higher the level, the higher the consequence and required
 defensive elements. In small modular reactors, with the potential for remote
 operations and modularity, defensive levels might not correspond to physical security
 areas because the cables/cloud service running between facilities might not require
 immense physical security.

 Wind and Solar Interdependency Evaluation
 7.A.1: What dependencies does the conceptual system have on
 other regional systems, infrastructures, and services?
 • Electrical grid infrastructure: Wind and solar farms depend on the regional
 power transmission and distribution network to deliver generated electricity to
 consumers.
 • Transportation infrastructure: Roads and transportation systems are
 important for construction, maintenance, and equipment delivery to these
 remote locations.
 • Telecommunications networks: Depending on the location of the wind and
 solar plants, the choice of telecommunication network depends on specific
 operations and constraints. LTE/5G networks are ideal for their coverage,
 reliability, and scalability.
 • Weather monitoring systems: Accurate weather forecasting is critical to
 predict wind patterns/solar irradiance and optimize energy production.
 • Local utilities: Water and waste management services may be required for
 facility operations.
 • Emergency services: Fire, medical, and police services are necessary for
 safety and security.

 Wind and Solar Digital Asset Awareness
 8.E.3: How will the system tests ensure delivered digital assets contain
 the expected hardware, software, and firmware?
 • Detailed inventory checks, version audits, and automated scans are performed
 • Sometimes forbidden by contract language enacted by the vendor—explore
 contractual terms with the vendor and promote policy and regulatory solutions.

 8.E.3–a: How will a system test validate the configuration?
 • Configuration validation should involve both automated and manual checks, including
 review of configuration files, settings, and system parameters against predefined
 baselines.
 • Testing and certification frameworks allow asset owners/operators to verify the
 configuration is valid—an example for inverter-based resources is UL 2941.

 Case Study: Anemometer Compromise in a Wind Farm
 Problem: Consider that the anemometers are
 sourced from a third-party based out of a sensitive
 country.
 • Use of anemometer from other turbines on the farm
 (wake-based models): Leveraging data from multiple
 anemometers across a wind farm, operators can create wake-
 based models that cross-verify wind speed and direction
 measurements.
 • Use of generator torque/power output to cross-verify wind
 speeds: Cross-verify measurements by comparing the power
 output or torque of the wind turbine generator with the wind
 speeds reported by the anemometer.
 • Obtain wind data from mechanical anemometers on met
 masts: Using mechanical anemometers on met masts
 provides a nondigital source of wind data.

 Case Study: Retrofitting of Nonpowered Dams
 Problem: Retrofitting existing dams to include hydroelectric power
 generation.
 Pistol Grip Switch With Digital Control Command
 • Mitigation: Implement a pistol grip switch, which is a physical
 multifactor control mechanism that operates in conjunction with
 the digital control command system. This manual switch should be
 used along with the digital control command to control critical
 turbine functions, such as emergency shutdown or the adjustment
 of water flow through the wicket gates.

 Mechanical Systems for Autonomous Flood Control, Overspeed
 Protection, and Torque Limitation
 • Mitigation: This mechanical system ensures floodgates and other
 critical protection systems can still function and respond to
 hazardous water levels even if the digital control system is
 compromised.
