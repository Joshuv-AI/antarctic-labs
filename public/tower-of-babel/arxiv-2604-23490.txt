Efficient Quantum Fully Homomorphic Encryption
Fengxia Liu, Zixian Gong, Kun Tian, Yi Zhang, Zhiming Zheng, Maozhi Xu

Abstract
Quantum fully homomorphic encryption (QFHE) promises secure delegated quantum computation but has been impeded by the prohibitive quantum resource demands of existing constructions. This paper introduces a unified framework that achieves an exponential improvement in efficiency by synergistically integrating three theoretical tools: modular arithmetic programs (MAP) , the garden-hose model , and measurement-based quantum computation (MBQC) . Our central innovation is a novel MAP tailored to the algebraic structure of Learning-with-Errors (LWE) decryption. Unlike generic approaches that incur exponential overhead, our MAP computes the inner product ⟨ 𝒔 ​ 𝒌 , 𝒄 ⟩ mod q \displaystyle\langle\bm{sk},\bm{c}\rangle\bmod q by tracking a partial sum modulo q \displaystyle q , requiring only O ⁡ ( log ⁡ q ) \displaystyle O(\log q) bits of state width. This yields branching programs of width O ⁡ ( log ⁡ λ ) \displaystyle O(\log\lambda) and length O ⁡ ( λ ​ log ⁡ λ ) \displaystyle O(\lambda\log\lambda) , thereby reducing the size of the essential quantum gadget from O ⁡ ( λ 2.58 ) \displaystyle O(\lambda^{2.58}) to O ⁡ ( λ ​ log 2 ​ λ ) \displaystyle O(\lambda\log^{2}\lambda) EPR pairs—a concrete improvement factor of 2 15 \displaystyle 2^{15} to 2 18 \displaystyle 2^{18} for standard security parameters. Critically, we demonstrate that LWE decryption is not a symmetric function , necessitating our specialized MAP design beyond prior symmetric-function optimizations. The framework provides a direct mapping from the MAP to an efficient gadget via the garden-hose model, with MBQC furnishing the deterministic control flow for homomorphic evaluation. The resulting QFHE scheme supports fully classical clients , relies solely on the classical LWE assumption (avoiding circular security or quantum hardness assumptions), and maintains compactness. This work dramatically lowers the quantum resource barrier for practical QFHE, paving the way for realistic privacy-preserving quantum cloud computing.



1
Introduction

The emergence of quantum computing promises to revolutionize computational capabilities across numerous domains, from cryptography and optimization to quantum simulation and machine learning. However, this revolutionary potential brings with it significant security challenges, particularly when users wish to leverage quantum computing resources provided by untrusted third parties. This fundamental tension between computational utility and data privacy motivates one of the most profound questions in quantum cryptography: Can we compute on encrypted quantum data without ever decrypting it?

This question lies at the heart of quantum fully homomorphic encryption (QFHE), a cryptographic primitive that enables arbitrary quantum computations to be performed on encrypted quantum states while preserving both the privacy of the input data and the correctness of the computation. Unlike classical homomorphic encryption, which deals with classical bits and Boolean circuits, QFHE must contend with the unique properties of quantum information: superposition, entanglement, and the no-cloning theorem. These quantum mechanical features make the construction of efficient QFHE schemes substantially more challenging than their classical counterparts, yet also more essential for the secure deployment of quantum computing in cloud environments.

1.1
Historical Development and Prior Work

The theoretical foundations of fully homomorphic encryption were established by Gentry [Gen09], who constructed the first FHE scheme using ideal lattices. This breakthrough demonstrated that arbitrary computation on encrypted data was possible, though the initial construction was computationally expensive. Subsequent developments simplified the constructions [VDG+10] and established security on more standard assumptions [BV11], paving the way for practical implementations.

The quantum analogue—QFHE—extends homomorphic encryption to quantum data and quantum computations. This extension is significantly more challenging due to the unique properties of quantum information. While classical FHE deals with bits and Boolean circuits, QFHE must handle qubits, superposition states, and quantum gates that introduce complex phase relationships.

Early QFHE Constructions.

Liang [Lia13] demonstrated the first symmetric-key QFHE scheme, proving that information-theoretically secure evaluation of quantum circuits on encrypted data is theoretically possible. However, this scheme suffered from exponential overhead: the ciphertext size grew exponentially with the circuit depth, rendering it impractical for all but the simplest computations.

Broadbent and Jeffery [BJ15] constructed the first computationally secure QFHE schemes using a hybrid approach combining the quantum one-time pad (QOTP) with classical FHE. Their fundamental insight was to use the QOTP as the underlying encryption mechanism for quantum states. The QOTP encrypts a quantum state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}
as
\displaystyle\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\ket{\psi}
for random bits
\displaystyle a,b\in\{0,1\}
, providing information-theoretic security. The classical bits
\displaystyle(a,b)
—called the Pauli keys—are then encrypted using classical FHE.

This hybrid approach enables homomorphic evaluation of Clifford gates (Hadamard
\displaystyle H
, Phase
\displaystyle S
, and CNOT) by updating the encrypted Pauli keys. For example, applying a Hadamard gate to the encrypted state transforms the encryption as follows:

\mathsf{H}\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\ket{\psi}=\mathsf{X}^{b}\mathsf{Z}^{a}\mathsf{H}\ket{\psi}.

(1)

The server can update the encrypted keys
\displaystyle(a,b)\to(b,a)
using the homomorphic properties of the classical encryption, without learning the actual key values.

The
\displaystyle\mathsf{T}
-Gate Challenge.

The critical limitation of Broadbent-Jeffery schemes is the
\displaystyle T
gate, which is essential for universal quantum computation. The
\displaystyle T
gate does not commute with Pauli operators in the same simple way as Clifford gates:

\mathsf{T}\mathsf{X}^{a}=\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{P}^{a}\mathsf{T},

(2)

where
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{T}^{2}=\text{diag}(1,i)
is the phase gate. When a
\displaystyle T
gate is applied to a QOTP-encrypted state
\displaystyle\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{}Z^{b}\ket{\phi}
, the result is:

\mathsf{T}\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\ket{\phi}=\mathsf{P}^{a}\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\mathsf{T}\ket{\phi}.

(3)

The server now holds a state with an extra
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}^{a}
factor. To obtain a properly encrypted state, the server needs to apply
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}^{\dagger}
if and only if
\displaystyle a=1
. However,
\displaystyle a
is encrypted under the classical FHE scheme, so the server cannot directly determine its value.

Broadbent and Jeffery’s solution required interaction: the server would send the encrypted state back to the client, who would decrypt
\displaystyle a
, apply the appropriate correction, and return the state. This interaction requirement severely limits the practicality of the scheme for cloud computing scenarios where the client may be offline or bandwidth-constrained.

Non-Interactive QFHE.

The breakthrough for non-interactive QFHE came with Dulek, Schaffner, and Speelman (DSS) [DSS16], who constructed gadgets enabling the server to apply
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
conditionally on the encrypted bit
\displaystyle a
without client interaction.

The DSS construction relies on Barrington’s theorem [Bar89], which states that any function in
\displaystyle\text{NC}^{1}
(logarithmic-depth Boolean circuits) can be computed by a width-5, polynomial-length branching program. Specifically, for a circuit of depth
\displaystyle d
, Barrington’s theorem yields a branching program of length
\displaystyle O(4^{d})
.

The key insight is that the decryption circuit of a classical FHE scheme can be computed by a branching program. The server prepares a quantum gadget based on this branching program. When evaluating a
\displaystyle T
gate, the server uses the gadget to homomorphically decrypt the control bit
\displaystyle a
and conditionally apply
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
.

The Efficiency Bottleneck.

For LWE-based FHE schemes, the decryption circuit has depth
\displaystyle O(\log\lambda)
, where
\displaystyle\lambda
is the security parameter. Applying Barrington’s theorem yields a branching program of length
\displaystyle O(4^{\log\lambda})=O(\lambda^{\log_{2}4})=O(\lambda^{2.58})
.

The garden-hose model [Spe15] connects branching programs to quantum resources: a width-
\displaystyle w
, length-
\displaystyle L
branching program can be implemented using
\displaystyle m=w\cdot L
EPR pairs. For the DSS construction, this gives:

m=5\cdot O(\lambda^{2.58})=O(\lambda^{2.58})\text{ EPR pairs}.

(4)

This exponential dependence on circuit depth creates a fundamental barrier. For concrete security parameters, the DSS scheme requires billions of EPR pairs, far exceeding the capabilities of near-term quantum devices.

Subsequent Developments.

Several works have explored alternative approaches to QFHE. Mahadev [Mah18] achieved classical verification of quantum computations using trapdoor claw-free functions, enabling a classical client to verify that a quantum server performed the correct computation. However, this scheme requires circular security assumptions and quantum LWE, which are stronger than the classical LWE assumptions used in our work.

Gupte, Vaikuntanathan, and Wee [GVW22] developed techniques for achieving a fully classical client by outsourcing quantum state preparation to the server using dual-mode trapdoor functions. Their approach eliminates all quantum requirements from the client while maintaining security based on classical LWE. This represents a significant step toward practical QFHE deployment. Guangsheng and Hongbo [ML22] devised a novel QFHE scheme based on quaternion one-time pad encryption. Compared with conventional schemes relying on Pauli one-time pads, QOTP encryption features an expanded key space, extending the discrete finite Pauli group to the continuous
\displaystyle SU(2)
group.

Other notable works include research on quantum homomorphic signature schemes, quantum obfuscation, and quantum copy-protection. While these primitives are related to QFHE, they address different security requirements and computational models.

Despite these advances, the fundamental efficiency bottleneck in QFHE—the exponential growth of quantum gadgets with circuit depth—has remained unresolved until this work.

These results lead to the following main open problem:

Is it possible to construct a quantum homomorphic scheme that allows

evaluation of polynomial-sized quantum circuits with polynomial gadget?

1.2
Our Contributions

Having identified the critical efficiency bottleneck in existing QFHE schemes, we present our unified framework that addresses these limitations through a novel synthesis of three theoretical constructs.

Novel Modular Arithmetic Programs for LWE Decryption.
Existing QFHE schemes rely on Barrington’s theorem to convert the decryption circuit into a branching program, which incurs exponential growth in program width with respect to circuit depth. For LWE decryption circuits with depth
\displaystyle O(\log\lambda)
, this leads to prohibitively large gadget sizes of
\displaystyle O(\lambda^{2.58})
EPR pairs. We introduce a
modular arithmetic program
(
MA-Program
) construction specifically designed for the algebraic structure of LWE decryption. LWE decryption computes
\displaystyle\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{sk}_{i}\cdot c_{i}\bmod q
, which is
not
a symmetric function of the secret key bits. A symmetric function satisfies
\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})
for all permutations
\displaystyle\pi
. However, LWE decryption involves coefficients
\displaystyle c_{i}
that differ across positions. Consequently, techniques for symmetric function evaluation, including Sinha’s
\displaystyle O(\log n)
depth construction [Sin95], do not apply to LWE decryption. Our
MA-Program
construction addresses this non-symmetric structure through sequential modular accumulation—tracking partial sums with state space
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, requiring only
\displaystyle O(\log q)
bits of state width. This yields branching programs with width
\displaystyle w=O(\log\lambda)
bits and length
\displaystyle L=O(\lambda\cdot\log\lambda)
, reducing the gadget size from
\displaystyle O(\lambda^{2.58})
to
\displaystyle O(\lambda\log^{2}\lambda)
EPR pairs—an improvement of approximately
\displaystyle 2^{15}
to
\displaystyle 2^{18}
for concrete security parameters.

Garden-Hose Model Integration for Quantum Computation.
We develop a novel theoretical connection between modular arithmetic programs and the garden-hose model [Spel15]. Each layer of the modular arithmetic program corresponds to a set of “pipes” (quantum states), with transition matrices realized as connection configurations. Bell measurements implement the connections, enabling conditional quantum operations based on encrypted control bits. This mapping allows us to translate the logarithmic-width
MA-Program
directly into an efficient quantum gadget with
\displaystyle m=O(w\cdot L)=O(\lambda\log^{2}\lambda)
pipes, each corresponding to one EPR pair in the final gadget construction. Previous QFHE schemes [DSS16,DK06]]applied the garden-hose model to Barrington-based branching programs with constant width but exponential length. Our approach achieves both logarithmic width
and
polynomial length, fundamentally changing the asymptotic behavior.

MBQC Framework with Flow Functions.
We reconstruct homomorphic evaluation within the measurement-based quantum computation (MBQC) model [RB01,DK06]. Flow functions provide the global control logic, specifying measurement dependencies and correction operations. This framework naturally accommodates the conditional
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
gates required for
\displaystyle T
-gate evaluation, enabling deterministic homomorphic evaluation with adaptive measurement patterns that respond dynamically to encrypted control bits. Unlike the circuit-based approaches of Broadbent-Jeffery [BJ15] and the direct gadget constructions of DSS [DSS16], our MBQC-based approach provides a unified framework for both the control logic and the quantum operations, simplifying security proofs and enabling modular analysis.

Classical Client via Dual-Mode Trapdoors.
Following Gupte et al. [GVW22], we achieve a fully classical client by outsourcing quantum gadget preparation to the server using dual-mode trapdoor functions. The client generates only classical gadget keys; the server deterministically constructs the required quantum states. This eliminates all quantum requirements from the client side while maintaining security. While Mahadev [Mah18] also achieves a classical client, her scheme requires circular security assumptions and quantum LWE. Our scheme achieves a classical client based solely on classical LWE, without circular security, representing a significant conceptual simplification.

Technical Overview

To provide context for our technical contributions, we review the foundations upon which our scheme builds. The theoretical foundation for non-interactive QFHE was established through the combination of two powerful results:

Theorem 1.1

(Barrington [Bar89)
.

Any function
\displaystyle f\in\text{NC}^{1}
(computable by depth-
\displaystyle O(\log n)
circuits) can be computed by a width-5, polynomial-length branching program. Specifically, if
\displaystyle f
has circuit depth
\displaystyle d
, it has a width-5 BP of length
\displaystyle O(4^{d})
.

The significance of Barrington’s theorem for QFHE is that it provides a way to evaluate decryption circuits with bounded width. However, the exponential dependence on depth creates a fundamental limitation: for LWE decryption with circuit depth
\displaystyle O(\log\lambda)
, the branching program length becomes
\displaystyle O(\lambda^{2.58})
, leading to gadgets requiring
\displaystyle O(\lambda^{2.58})
EPR pairs.

The garden-hose model [Spel15,Chiu14] provides a complementary framework for instantaneous non-local quantum computation:

Definition 1.1

(Garden-Hose Complexity)
.

The garden-hose complexity
\displaystyle\text{GH}(f)
of a function
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is the minimum number of pipes
\displaystyle m
such that there exists a protocol where: Alice (holding
\displaystyle x
) and Bob (holding
\displaystyle y
) each connect pipes according to their inputs; Water enters at Alice’s pipe 1 iff
\displaystyle f(x,y)=0
; Water exits at Bob’s pipe 1 iff
\displaystyle f(x,y)=1
.

The connection between branching programs and the garden-hose model is given by:

Theorem 1.2

(Garden-Hose and Branching Programs)
.

For any function
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
computable by a width-
\displaystyle w
, length-
\displaystyle L
branching program:
\displaystyle\text{GH}(f)\leq w\cdot L
.

In contrast to the generic approach of applying Barrington’s theorem to the Boolean circuit representation of LWE decryption, our work exploits the specific algebraic structure of modular arithmetic. The key insight is that LWE decryption computes
\displaystyle\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q
—an operation that admits a natural sequential decomposition with state space
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

At a high level, our QFHE scheme operates as follows. A quantum state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}
is encrypted using the quantum one-time pad:
\displaystyle\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\ket{\psi}
for random bits
\displaystyle a,b\in\{0,1\}
. The pad keys
\displaystyle(a,b)
are then encrypted using a classical leveled homomorphic encryption (LHE) scheme. Clifford gates (Hadamard
\displaystyle H
, Phase
\displaystyle S
, CNOT) can be applied homomorphically by updating the encrypted pad keys. The
\displaystyle T
gate requires special handling because
\displaystyle\mathsf{T}\mathsf{X}^{a}=\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{P}^{a}\mathsf{T}
, where
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{T}^{2}
is the phase gate. When a
\displaystyle\mathsf{T}
gate is applied to a QOTP-encrypted state
\displaystyle\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\ket{\phi}
, we obtain
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}^{a}\mathsf{X}^{a}\mathsf{Z}^{b}\mathsf{T}\ket{\phi}
. The server must apply
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}^{\dagger}
conditionally on the encrypted bit
\displaystyle a
. Our innovation lies in constructing a quantum gadget that takes as input a quantum state and encrypted control bit
\displaystyle\tilde{a}
, uses
\displaystyle m=O(w\cdot L)
EPR pairs where
\displaystyle w=O(\log\lambda)
and
\displaystyle L=\operatorname{poly}(\lambda)
, applies
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
to the state if and only if
\displaystyle f_{\text{sk}}(\tilde{a})=\text{HE}.\text{Dec}_{\text{sk}}(\tilde{a})=a
, and outputs the state with updated encrypted pad keys. The gadget is constructed from a modular arithmetic program for the LHE decryption function. Each layer of the program corresponds to a set of quantum “pipes” (half-EPR pairs). For each layer, the server homomorphically selects one of two connection configurations based on the encrypted input bit. Bell measurements implement the connections, and the
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
gate is embedded at the position where an accepting state would flow through.

2.3
Branching Programs and Symmetric Functions

Branching programs provide a powerful model for computing Boolean functions with bounded space. This model is central to our construction, as it bridges classical computation and quantum homomorphic evaluation through the garden-hose model.

Definition 2.4
.

A branching program is a directed acyclic graph with a designated source node and some number of sink nodes. Each sink node is labeled with an integer. Each non-sink node has out-degree two and the two outgoing edges are labeled
\displaystyle x_{i}=0
and
\displaystyle x_{i}=1
for some input variable
\displaystyle x_{i}
. In this case we say that the node reads the variable
\displaystyle x_{i}
. The branching program computes a function in the following way: it is easy to see that any setting of input bits
\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
defines a unique path from the source node to one of the sink nodes in which all edge labelings are consistent with the assignment of
\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
; the label of this sink node is the value of the function.

Barrington’s seminal theorem shows that all functions in
\displaystyle\text{NC}^{1}
can be computed by constant-width polynomial-length branching programs. However, for specific function classes, more efficient constructions exist.

Symmetric Functions and Sinha’s Construction.

A Boolean function
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
symmetric
if its output depends only on the Hamming weight of the input, i.e.,
\displaystyle f(x)=f(x^{\prime})
whenever
\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}
. Equivalently,
\displaystyle f
is invariant under any permutation of its input bits:

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})\quad\text{for all permutations }\pi.

Important examples of symmetric functions include:

•

Majority:

\displaystyle\text{MAJ}(x)=1
iff
\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}\geq n/2
,

•

Parity:

\displaystyle\text{PARITY}(x)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
,

•

Threshold:

\displaystyle\text{TH}_{k}(x)=1
iff
\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}\geq k
.

Sinha [Sin95] established an optimal depth bound for computing symmetric functions with branching programs:

Theorem 2.1
.

Any symmetric function
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
can be computed by a branching program of width
\displaystyle O(\log n)
and length
\displaystyle O(n)
.

Proof Sketch.

The key insight is that a symmetric function only needs to track the Hamming weight of the bits seen so far. The BP maintains a counter
\displaystyle c\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
representing
\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}
for the processed prefix
\displaystyle S
. This counter requires
\displaystyle O(\log n)
bits of state. For each input bit
\displaystyle x_{i}
, the counter updates as
\displaystyle c\leftarrow c+x_{i}
. After processing all
\displaystyle n
bits, the BP accepts if
\displaystyle f
evaluates to 1 on the final count.
∎

The efficiency of Sinha’s branching program construction stems from its optimization tailored to the properties of symmetric functions. The output of a symmetric function depends only on the number of ’1’s in its input (the Hamming weight), rather than the specific positions of the ’1’s. This allows the program to maintain only a counter tracking the current Hamming weight during computation, without storing the exact bit sequence, thereby compressing the state space (program width) to
\displaystyle O(\log n)
. Below are detailed descriptions of three classic symmetric functions:

Majority Function (MAJORITY)
: This function outputs 1 if the number of ’1’s in the input bits reaches or exceeds half of the total bits, and 0 otherwise. For an
\displaystyle n
-bit input, its acceptance set
\displaystyle S_{acc}
is
\displaystyle{\lceil n/2\rceil,\lceil n/2\rceil+1,...,n}.
In Sinha’s branching program, the program starts with a count of 0 and increments the counter by 1 each time a ’1’ is encountered. After processing all inputs, it accepts if the final count falls within
\displaystyle S_{acc}
. The efficiency of Sinha’s branching program construction stems from its optimization tailored to the properties of symmetric functions. The output of a symmetric function depends only on the number of ’1’s in its input (the Hamming weight), rather than the specific positions of the ’1’s. This allows the program to maintain only a counter tracking the current Hamming weight during computation, without storing the exact bit sequence, thereby compressing the state space (program width) to
\displaystyle O(\log n)
. Below are detailed descriptions of three classic symmetric functions:

Parity Function (PARITY):
This function computes the modulo-2 sum (XOR) of the input bits, outputting 1 when the number of ’1’s is odd and 0 when even. Its acceptance set consists of all odd values:
\displaystyle S_{acc}=\{c:c\equiv 1(mod2)\}.
Notably, since only the parity of the current count needs to be tracked (two states) rather than an exact count, the branching program width implementing this function can be further optimized to
\displaystyle w=2
, which is superior to the general
\displaystyle O(\log n)
, demonstrating room for further optimization for specific symmetric functions.

Threshold Function (THRESHOLD):
A generalization of the majority function, it sets a threshold
\displaystyle k
and outputs 1 if the number of ’1’s in the input is at least
\displaystyle k
. Its acceptance set is
\displaystyle S_{acc}=\{k,k+1,...,n\}.
Its branching program construction is similar to that of the majority function, with a width of
\displaystyle O(\log n)
as well. This function family illustrates how symmetric functions can define a rich class of functions via a simple integer threshold parameter while remaining efficiently computable by logarithmic-width branching programs.

Why Sinha’s BP Only Works for Symmetric Functions.
The fundamental limitation of Sinha’s construction is its reliance on permutation invariance. The counter mechanism tracks only the count of 1s, discarding all positional information. Formally:

Proposition 2.1
.

(Symmetry Requirement).
Sinha’s
\displaystyle O(\log n)
-width branching program construction works if and only if the function
\displaystyle f
is symmetric.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle f(x)
depends only on the Hamming weight of

\displaystyle f
symmetric by definition.
If
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle f(x)=g\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}\right)
for some predicate

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}
and applies
\displaystyle g

∎

Connection to QFHE.

In the context of quantum homomorphic encryption, branching programs enable the construction of

3
Modular Arithmetic Programs for LWE Decryption

Following the roadmap presented in Section
2
, we now develop the core theoretical innovation of this work: modular arithmetic programs (
MA-Program
) tailored for LWE decryption. These programs exploit the specific algebraic structure of modular computation to achieve logarithmic state complexity, fundamentally improving upon the exponential growth inherent in generic branching program constructions.

We begin by formalizing the notion of a modular arithmetic program, which generalizes standard branching programs to operate natively over modular state spaces:

Definition 3.1

(Modular Arithmetic Program)
.

A modular arithmetic program (
MA-Program
) over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{m}
for
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
consists of: (1) a modulus
\displaystyle m
; (2) a sequence of instructions
\displaystyle\langle(i_{\ell},a_{\ell},b_{\ell})\rangle_{\ell=1}^{L}
where
\displaystyle i_{\ell}\in[n]
and
\displaystyle a_{\ell},b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
; (3) initial state
\displaystyle s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
; (4) accepting set
\displaystyle S_{\text{acc}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. On input
\displaystyle x
, the state updates as
\displaystyle s_{\ell}=s_{\ell-1}+(1-x_{i_{\ell}})a_{\ell}+x_{i_{\ell}}b_{\ell}\pmod{m}
. The output is
\displaystyle 1
iff
\displaystyle s_{L}\in S_{\text{acc}}
.

This definition captures the essence of modular computation: rather than manipulating bits directly, the program tracks a state in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{m}
that evolves through modular additions. The branching behavior is determined by which of two values (
\displaystyle a_{\ell}
or
\displaystyle b_{\ell}
) is added based on the input bit
\displaystyle x_{i_{\ell}}
.

The following lemma establishes our key technical result: LWE decryption admits an efficient MA-Program representation with logarithmic state space.

Lemma 3.1

(LWE Decryption via MA-Program)
.

For fixed ciphertext
\displaystyle c

\displaystyle f_{c}(\text{sk})=\text{HE}.\text{Dec}_{\text{sk}}(c)
can be computed by a modular arithmetic program over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{q}
with
state space

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{q}
(represented using
\displaystyle O(\log q)
bits) and
program length
\displaystyle O(n\cdot\log q)
.

Proof.

LWE decryption computes
\displaystyle\text{HE}.\text{Dec}_{\text{sk}}(c)=\left\lfloor\frac{p}{q}\cdot(\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q)\right\rfloor\bmod 2
where
\displaystyle\text{sk}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is the secret key and
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is the ciphertext.

We compute the inner product
\displaystyle\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q
sequentially using a modular arithmetic program. The state
\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
tracks the partial sum. Initially
\displaystyle s_{0}=0
, at step
\displaystyle i

\displaystyle s_{i}=s_{i-1}+\text{sk}_{i}\cdot c_{i}\pmod{q}
.
Each update depends only on the current state and the input bit
\displaystyle\text{sk}_{i}

\displaystyle\text{sk}_{i}=0

\displaystyle s_{i}=s_{i-1}

\displaystyle\text{sk}_{i}=1

\displaystyle c_{i}\pmod{q}

\displaystyle s_{i}=s_{i-1}+c_{i}\pmod{q}

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle s_{n}=\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle O(\log q)

\displaystyle O(n)
steps, each performing constant-time modular arithmetic.
∎

This result stands in sharp contrast to the generic Barrington-based approach: where Barrington’s theorem would yield width-5 but length
\displaystyle O(4^{\log\lambda})=O(\lambda^{2.58})

From Modular Arithmetic to Logarithmic Width.

The starting point for our efficiency improvements is the recognition that LWE decryption computes a modular arithmetic function. The connection between Garden-Hose and Branching Programs is as follows: each layer of the BP corresponds to a set of

6.2
Security Analysis

This section aims to provide a rigorous security justification for the concrete LWE parameters proposed in Section 6.1, ensuring that the efficiency improvements claimed in this work are built on a solid security foundation rather than at the cost of security. The core question to address is: why can the specific parameter combinations
\displaystyle(n,q,\sigma)
in Table 2 achieve 128-, 192-, or 256-bit security levels?

The security of our scheme fully reduces to the computational hardness of the classical LWE problem. To this end, we formally state the following core assumption:

Assumption 6.1

(LWE Hardness)
.

For parameters
\displaystyle(n,q,\sigma)
as in Table
3
, the decision-LWE
n,q,χ
problem provides the claimed security level against known classical and quantum attacks, including:
Primal lattice attacks, Dual lattice attacks and Hybrid attacks.

This assumption is grounded in the foundational work of Regev [Reg09], which reduces the average-case hardness of LWE to the worst-case hardness of the approximate Shortest Vector Problem (Approx-SVP) on lattices. This provides a solid theoretical basis for our parameter choices and emphasizes that security explicitly depends on the values of parameters
\displaystyle n
,
\displaystyle q
, and
\displaystyle\sigma
.

The conservative choice of
\displaystyle\sigma=3.2
provides a safety margin against Arora-Ge attacks while maintaining decryption correctness with overwhelming probability[GHP12]. We conservatively select the standard deviation of the error distribution as
\displaystyle\sigma=3.2
. This selection is not arbitrary; instead, it follows rigorous consideration of defense depth against all known attack models, achieving a delicate trade-off between security and decryption correctness:

1.

Against algebraic attacks (e.g., Arora-Ge attack): The Arora-Ge attack and its variants exploit the ultra-small noise property of LWE, transforming LWE instances into polynomial system solving via linearization techniques. The complexity of such attacks grows exponentially with the upper bound of noise, approximately
\displaystyle O(n^{d})
, where
\displaystyle d
is correlated with
\displaystyle\sigma
. If we choose an extremely small
\displaystyle\sigma
(e.g.,
\displaystyle\sigma<\sqrt{n}
),
\displaystyle d
becomes sufficiently small to make the attack feasible in polynomial time. Nevertheless, the value
\displaystyle\sigma=3.2
drastically increases the degree
\displaystyle d
required to construct effective linearized polynomials, triggering combinatorial explosion in system scale and solving complexity. Accordingly,
\displaystyle\sigma=3.2
provides substantial security margins against such algebraic attacks, rendering them completely infeasible under current and foreseeable computational capabilities.

2.

Against hybrid attacks: Hybrid attacks reduce lattice dimensions by guessing partial secret bits, then execute lattice reduction algorithms on the dimension-reduced lattice. The success rate of such attacks highly relies on extra noise introduced by bit guessing. A larger
\displaystyle\sigma
leads to rapid accumulation of error variance in incorrect guessing branches, resulting in excessive noise in dimension-reduced LWE instances. This prevents subsequent lattice reduction procedures (e.g., BKZ) from finding sufficiently short vectors.
\displaystyle\sigma=3.2
supplies ample
noise buffer
, making the profit of guessing steps in hybrid attacks far lower than the accompanying computational overhead and noise penalty, thus effectively neutralizing the advantages of such attacks.

The current construction and security proofs of this work (including Theorem 5.3 and the aforementioned LWE parameter analysis) are established under the
semi-honest
server assumption. That is, the server is required to faithfully execute gadget preparation and measurement operations in strict accordance with protocol specifications, and only attempts to extract extra information from interaction transcripts. Nevertheless, in real-world deployment scenarios, the server may behave maliciously, introducing two core categories of risks as follows:

1.

Privacy leakage risk
: During the gadget preparation phase, a malicious server may deviate from the standardized entangled state preparation and distribution procedures. For instance, the server may deliver non-maximally entangled states or deliberately engineered quantum states, attempting to extract additional information about the secret key
\displaystyle\mathbf{sk}
from subsequent client measurement responses. Although our scheme relies on QOTP, whose randomness conceals plaintexts to a certain extent, the absence of server quantum state verification mechanisms in the current protocol allows maliciously constructed quantum channels to break the perfect secrecy of QOTP, resulting in indirect leakage of secret key information.

2.

Function integrity & verification risk
: A malicious server may tamper with MBQC measurement bases or measurement outcomes of gadgets during the evaluation of conditional gates (e.g.,
\displaystyle P^{\dagger}
gates). Since our framework assumes a fully classical client, the client cannot directly monitor quantum state evolution, and only passively receives final classical ciphertexts. The server can inject errors to make the decrypted final quantum state deviate drastically from the expected computation result. Without quantum verification mechanisms, the client cannot distinguish whether such deviations stem from legitimate decryption failures or malicious tampering.

Mitigation Pathways
: Resolving the above risks is essential for constructing practical malicious-resistant QFHE. Potential solutions include:
Introducing quantum obfuscation or blind verification technologies, enabling classical clients to detect server deviations with overwhelming probability;
Adopting trapdoor-based authentication schemes in gadget generation.

However, achieving security against malicious servers while preserving the advantages of
fully classical clients
and logarithmic-scale gadget size remains an open problem, and will be the primary focus of our future research.

\displaystyle\langle\text{sk},c\rangle\bmod q

The computation tracks only a partial sum modulo
\displaystyle q

\displaystyle O(\log q)

•

Deterministic Routing:
In the garden-hose model, modular accumulation admits regular “flow” patterns where quantum states follow predictable paths, simplifying the Bell measurement structure.

•

Cyclic Structure:
The modular arithmetic underlying the computation (addition modulo
\displaystyle q
) aligns naturally with quantum phase estimation and related techniques.

These properties collectively explain why our
MA-Program
-based approach achieves significant efficiency gains over generic Boolean circuit simulation. The modular structure is not merely a convenience but a fundamental alignment between the algebraic properties of LWE and the requirements of quantum computation.

\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle sk

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle n
. Therefore, the number of EPR pairs required for the final Gadget becomes
\displaystyle O(n\cdot n\log q)=O(n^{2}\log q)
.

C.2
Application to NTRU

NTRU is a post-quantum cryptosystem based on the algebraic structure of polynomial rings. Its security relies on the hardness of finding short vectors in a specific polynomial ring (the NTRU assumption).

Detailed decryption process and MAP extension:

1.

Operation Environment: Computations are performed over the ring
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{Z}_{q}[x]/(\Phi(x))
, where typically
\displaystyle\Phi(x)=x^{n}+1
and
\displaystyle n
is a power of two.

2.

Keys and Ciphertext: The secret key consists of two short polynomials
\displaystyle f,g\in R
. The public key is
\displaystyle h=g/f\in R_{q}
. A ciphertext encrypting a message
\displaystyle m
has the form

c=h\cdot r+m\in R_{q},

where
\displaystyle r
is a short random polynomial.

3.

Core Decryption Operation: Decryption first computes
\displaystyle a=f\cdot c\bmod q
, yielding

a=g\cdot r+f\cdot m\bmod q.

Since the coefficients of
\displaystyle f,g,r,m
are all small, after computing
\displaystyle a
modulo
\displaystyle q
, its coefficients lie in a narrow interval. The value
\displaystyle f\cdot m
can be accurately recovered via rounding, and then
\displaystyle m
is retrieved using the inverse of
\displaystyle f
.

4.

Challenges and Extensions for MAP Construction: The key step in decryption is to compute the polynomial multiplication
\displaystyle f\cdot c
and then reduce modulo
\displaystyle q
. This can be viewed as computing a set of
\displaystyle n
coefficient-wise inner products:

(f\cdot c)_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j\equiv k\\
\bmod n\end{subarray}}f_{i}\cdot c_{j}\bmod q.

To represent this as an MA-Program, the program needs to track all
\displaystyle n
partial sums simultaneously. Therefore, the program state must represent a vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
of length
\displaystyle n
, where the
\displaystyle k
-th component tracks the accumulated value of
\displaystyle(f\cdot c)_{k}
.

5.

Efficiency Analysis:

•

State width
: The state
\displaystyle\mathbf{s}
requires
\displaystyle n\cdot\log_{2}q
bits to represent, i.e.,
\displaystyle O(n\log q)
. This is significantly larger than the
\displaystyle O(\log q)
state width for LWE.

•

Program length
: The convolution relation between the
\displaystyle O(n)
coefficients of
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle c
must be processed, resulting in a length of approximately
\displaystyle O(n)
.

•

Gadget size
: According to the garden-hose model, the number of EPR pairs required for the gadget is

m=\text{width}\times\text{length}\approx O(n\log q)\times O(n)=O(n^{2}\log q).

Extending the MAP framework to NTRU is theoretically feasible, but it comes with a significant increase in quantum resource overhead—from
\displaystyle O(n\log^{2}q)
to
\displaystyle O(n^{2}\log q)
. This reflects a classic trade-off in cryptology: NTRU offers excellent classical efficiency (e.g., small key size, fast computation) at the cost of more demanding quantum resource requirements. Within our framework, this implies that constructions based on LWE are superior in terms of minimizing quantum resource consumption and maximizing security assumptions. In contrast, the NTRU extension provides a viable candidate solution for application scenarios where classical efficiency is of paramount importance and the acceptance of structure-specific hardness assumptions is acceptable.

C.4
Why Symmetric Functions Fail for LWE

Consider the same LWE instance. If we permute the secret key bits:

•

Original:
\displaystyle\text{sk}=(1,0,1,1)
, Hamming weight = 3

•

Permuted:
\displaystyle\text{sk}^{\prime}=(1,1,0,1)
, Hamming weight = 3

For a symmetric function,
\displaystyle f(\text{sk})=f(\text{sk}^{\prime})
since both have Hamming weight 3. But:

•

\displaystyle\langle\text{sk},c\rangle=3+0+7+11=21\equiv 4\pmod{17}

•

\displaystyle\langle\text{sk}^{\prime},c\rangle=3+5+0+11=19\equiv 2\pmod{17}

The decryption output changes under permutation, proving that LWE decryption is
not
symmetric.

Appendix D
Future Directions

1.

Is Sequentiality a Fundamental Limitation for Depth-Optimized MBQC Flow Functions?

This work employs the flow function of MBQC to provide deterministic control logic for homomorphic evaluation. The framework requires that measurements be performed sequentially in layers: the measurement outcome of layer
\displaystyle\ell
dynamically determines the measurement basis of layer
\displaystyle\ell+1
via the flow function
\displaystyle f
. Such causal dependence introduces inherent sequential latency. Is this a fundamental theoretical limitation? Can the logical depth of evaluation be reduced by optimizing the flow function or compilation strategy?

2.

Malicious Security:
Our current scheme relies on the semi-honest server assumption. Achieving malicious security is a critical step toward practical deployment. We can draw technical insights from verifiable quantum computation (e.g., TrapTP protocols) and quantum authentication codes, while resolving compatibility issues with our high-efficiency framework. Construct QFHE schemes secure against malicious servers under the trustless setup, while preserving the core advantages of
fully classical clients
and compact gadget structures.

3.

Classical-Quantum Hybrid Schemes:
Our scheme supports fully classical clients and solely relies on classical LWE assumptions, laying a solid foundation for classical preprocessing. Existing results (e.g., remote state preparation techniques in [GVW22]) demonstrate the feasibility of outsourcing quantum resource generation, serving as a crucial starting point for this direction. Adopt classical computation for preprocessing, shifting partial quantum resource demands to the classical side, and further reducing the overhead of real-time quantum operations.

4.

What Is the Correct Noise Model for Near-Term QFHE?

The security analysis in this work relies on ideal noiseless quantum operations (perfect EPR pairs, error-free measurements). Realistic physical implementations inevitably suffer from decoherence, measurement errors, and photon loss. How can MA-Program gadgets be made compatible with quantum error correction (QEC)? What are their fault-tolerance thresholds?
