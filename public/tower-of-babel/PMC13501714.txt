Does true crime increase negative affect, or negative affect lead to true crime? A cross-lagged analysis on the dynamics of consuming frightening media
Authors: Corinna M. Perchtold-Stefan, Christian Rominger, Andreas Fink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEven in today’s high-choice media landscape, people are drawn to stories of crime and murder, best exemplified by the massive appeal of the true crime genre. Research on negative effects of violent media on consumers has yielded highly contested results though, with traditional models often predicting negative impacts that are absent in other empirical studies. Newer avenues in horror research even suggest that frightening media may act as training grounds for coping with anxiety.MethodsWe investigated both perspectives in a two-week daily diary study on positive and negative affect (anxiety, anger) and true crime consumption (data collected in 2024). Cross-lagged multilevel modelling (Bayesian estimation) was applied to data of n = 199 participants to examine if affect on day n predicted true crime consumption on the following day (n + 1) and vice versa.ResultsWe found that today’s anxiety positively predicted violent true crime consumption of tomorrow, and in turn, violent true crime consumption of today positively predicted positive affect of tomorrow. However, none of these effects exceeded our smallest effect size of interest (β = .10).ConclusionsIf our associations replicate, they could suggest that anxiety may motivate engagement with self-relevant negative media for emotion regulation purposes, potentially yielding affective benefits. Alternatively, true crime consumption and affect may be largely unrelated in everyday life. Consistent with the latter, we found no evidence that true crime consumption increased anxiety or anger, failing to support traditional theories of media-induced fear and aggression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04975-8.

Introduction

In the modern media landscape, nothing seems to be quite as entertaining as dark and gruesome stories about crime and murder. Both fictional crime and true crime media portray a wide variety of human violence and criminality, including assault, kidnappings, sex crimes, or serial killings and seem to be ever growing in popularity, as evident from podcast charts, book sales, or streaming statistics.1 This human fascination with crime is intriguing both from the perspective of what needs consumers seek to fulfill by engaging with crime media (uses and gratifications framework, [11], and from the perspective of cultivating fear and aggression through violent media worlds (media cultivation theory, [43], also see [89]. Clearly, the question how media portrayals of crime may resonate with individuals is not novel in literature [20, 22]. However, compared to fictional crime dramas or violent horror movies, true crime stories are real and narrate true events from recent and distant history (even if sensationalism is possible),2 and may thus have a greater personal impact due to stronger emotional engagement and perceived relevance. There is indeed some theoretical and empirical work contrasting fictional vs. true crime in terms of consumer motivations and effects on emotion and cognition around crime [21, 49, 86, 111]. Still, the true crime genre has been largely neglected when it comes to the question why people are drawn to media violence and how it may affect their feelings and perceptions of reality (see [82]. This is surprising, as the modern true crime industry is huge and allows personalized consumption in countless ways (e.g., type of crime, status of investigation, degree of violence, style of narration) due to the sheer availability of content in various formats, making it distinct from non-customizable crime TV programs of the past decades (see [21]. Additionally, while fictional violence and horror is preferred by men [23, 73], the true crime audience is predominantly female [13, 74], making it a unique phenomenon worthy of investigation. In this study, true crime is defined broadly as non-fictional media focusing on real crime. While scholars sometimes distinguish between traditional journalistic crime reporting and true crime entertainment, these boundaries are increasingly blurred in a high-choice, modern media environment. Many popular true crime productions employ rigorous investigative journalism, while some news outlets utilize narrative dramatization and sensationalism to engage audiences. As our research focuses on true crime consumption from a psychological consumer perspective, both journalistic crime reporting and true crime entertainment are grouped together, to understand engagement with real-world crime from an emotional perspective.

In this paper, we seek to provide insights into two pressing questions around true crime consumption that have emerged in literature: For one, is true crime consumption driven by certain (negative) affective states and by implication, need for emotion regulation as has been suggested for fictional horror consumption [94, 95, 97]? Second, is there a negative impact of true crime consumption on people’s affect, in terms of increased anxiety and anger, or lower positive affect after engaging with scary true crime worlds (e.g., [4, 20]? While our first question addresses motives for crime media engagement, our second question targets the idea of fear and violence cultivation by crime media. Here, while a significant body of research has speculated on negative outcomes of (crime) media consumption, it has also been criticized for its overreliance on cross-sectional designs, and for neglecting dynamic perspectives of media – emotion relationships (see [100]). Consequently, the field has struggled to move beyond correlation to establish a temporal order of effects. For this reason, in our investigation, we employ a daily diary approach over the duration of two weeks to examine if dynamic changes in positive affect, as well as anxiety and anger may impact true crime consumption and vice versa. In other words, we investigate if affect predicts true crime consumption, or if true crime consumption predicts affect in daily life. Overall, both questions integrate well into the only recently emerging psychological framework on causes and consequences of true crime consumption [74, 80, 82].

Perspective I: affective and affect-related motives for true crime consumption

Media frameworks like the mood management theory [59] or media uses and gratifications [11] suggest that individuals select media content based on their social and emotional needs, which on the one hand, may imply improving negative emotions by consuming positive, uplifting media content. Yet on the other hand, individuals may deliberately choose media content that matches and validates their negative feelings (see [46], and offers opportunities for emotion regulation that resonate with current affective states [9, 57, 97]. With regard to the often called “paradox” of horror consumption, researchers have presented persuasive arguments that frightening media worlds may offer critical opportunities for emotion regulation and personal growth, as controlled and safe exposure to horror may improve emotional clarity, tolerance of negative somatic experiences, and build up cognitive reappraisal skills [95, 97]. For these reasons, horror media may be particularly appealing for individuals higher in anxiety, as it allows engagement with fear in a controlled and predictable way (showing parallels to cognitive behavioral and exposure therapy, see [95]. Although this therapeutic idea of horror is still fairly theoretical, individuals high in trait anxiety do report a higher preference for horror fiction [56], also see [95], and state anxiety seems to be linked to choosing horror movies for entertainment [104]. So, what does this tell us for potential affective motives for true crime consumption?

Research on true crime motives is far from conclusive, with proposed motives ranging from boredom over escapism to voyeurism [13, 74, 112] which however, also partly overlap with fictional crime consumption [86]. Still, next to morbid curiosity as a general interest in negative things [77, 94], researchers speculate that true crime consumption in women specifically may be driven by defensive vigilance [74], which refers to knowledge acquisition about dangerous situations to better prepare for aversive events in real life, like becoming the victim of a violent crime. Given that fear of crime is higher in women than men [40, 53] and is associated with a reduced quality of life [1, 103], anxiety around crime and victimization may indeed motive women to engage with true crime content to reduce uncertainty, experience control over frightening narratives, and regulate negative emotions [95]. Tentatively supporting this idea with cross-sectional data, Perchtold-Stefan et al. [82] found that women’s true crime consumption was more strongly motivated by defensive vigilance and at a trend, emotion regulation than men’s, and that individuals higher in anxiety and fear of victimization consumed more true crime content. Similarly, McDonald et al. [74] had found that women with a higher fear of sexual assault reported a higher frequency of true crime consumption, specifically to learn defensive strategies for preventing attacks.

Taking this as support for the idea that anxiety and fear of crime may drive true crime consumption for threat coping and emotion regulation, we could expect that higher anxiety predicts more true crime consumption in our daily diary study. However, there are also opposing perspectives: First, anxious people may be primarily drawn to recreational horror because its scary and violent aspects are blatantly fictional, and, same as in fictional crime media, the killers, criminals, or monsters are usually caught at the end, tension is resolved, and justice is served. This is not the case for true crime, which often does not feature a satisfying resolution (e.g., cold cases, negligent police work, wrongful conviction) and lacks retributive justice for heinous crimes like serial murder. Thus, the question is whether anxious individuals attribute the same benefits proposed for playing with fear in fictional horror to more realistic true crime [3, 97]. Second, the cross-sectional link between anxiety, fear of crime, and true crime consumption can also be interpreted in the reverse direction – that engagement with violent true crime worlds may increase anxiety, fear of crime, and potentially, aggression, which leads us to perspective II.

Perspective II: negative effects of true crime consumption on affect and well-being

The influential cultivation theory by Gerbner et al. proposes broad effects of heavy television viewing of violence and crime on fear and mistrust, and thus, a heightened sense of danger, as people are believed to align their social reality with what is shown on TV (mean world syndrome, [43]. This idea of fear cultivation by negative media has been examined for crime news, but also fictional violent crime TV, which have been linked to increased fear of crime and risk perception in cross-sectional studies [22, 76, 101, 111], also see [89]. Within this cultivation framework, impacts of crime media are proposed to extend beyond individual emotional states to also include behavioral and social consequences. In this regard, Rader et al. [84] proposed that heightened threat perceptions induced by crime media can lead to avoidance behaviors, where individuals constrain their lives by changing their social activities and daily routines to minimize perceived risks. Furthermore, related research suggests that elicited fear is not an individual phenomenon, but also extends to fear for the safety of loved ones [109]. This so-called altruistic fear is often more intense than fear for oneself, and may thus serve as a primary driver for precautionary measures that people take in their daily lives. Moreover, consumption of fictional crime shows was found to influence juror decision-making on forensic evidence (CSI effect) and support for capital punishment in earlier studies [60, 93]. This research seems to suggest sizable effects of negative media exposure on negative affect. Yet, contemporary meta-analyses on the cultivation theory have only obtained small relationships between cumulative media exposure and threat perceptions (see Hermann et al., 2021, [51]). While some treat this as evidence that even small shifts in perception can lead to tremendous negative consequences [50], other studies have questioned the practical significance of small effects, especially given the range of moderators qualifying them [91]. In fact, most studies reporting fear or belief cultivation effects also stress the relevance of interindividual characteristics and news preferences (e.g., general vs. alternative news) for media effects on consumers [2, 20, 91]. The CSI effect has also proven less robust than initially claimed (see [49]). In even starker contrast, a range of studies investigating modern media consumption (online news, social media) do not find a link between media portrayals of crime and anxiety at all [21, 87, 100], with null effects of media consumption on crime perception in longitudinal approaches highlighting the danger of reverse causation [100]. In sum, modern evidence that crime media influence consumer cognition and affect, and thus, cultivate fear and avoidance is mixed. Yet, the idea that true crime could have negative effects is attractive. Perceived realism of fictional content, i.e., the extent to which stories seem to capture the real world, was found to moderate media effects on consumers [17, 70]. As true crime stories are based on real events and likely elicit a stronger feeling of personal relevance, this may make it more difficult to distance oneself from elicited emotions and thoughts (“It’s not real” or “This could never happen”), which may have a stronger impact on cognition and affect (see [80, 82]). Delivering some support for this idea, Kort-Butler and Hartshorn (2011) found that only non-fictional crime shows on TV (“true crime”) were linked to higher fear of crime and lower trust in the justice system, a link that was not found for fictional crime dramas. Accordingly, we could also expect that a higher degree of true crime consumption predicts higher anxiety in our daily diary study.

Another heavily debated question is whether consumption of violent media promotes anger and aggressive behavior. The General Aggression Model (GAM; [4, 7]) serves as a dominant framework in this regard, proposing both short- and long-term processes through which media violence may foster aggression. According to the GAM, short-term effects of violent media include activation of aggressive cognitions, imitation behavior (e.g., copycat aggression in children), increased anger and physiological arousal. Proposed long-term effects are consolidation of aggressive scripts in memory, disinhibition through beliefs that aggression is “normal”, and desensitization, which may also relate to reduced empathy and prosocial behavior ([5, 6, 14, 62, 63]). In the context of crime entertainment media, some studies found that higher viewer aggression was linked to more consumption of violent crime and action dramas [8, 44], although curiously, Gibson et al. [44] found stronger aggression links to consumption of reality TV than to crime TV (but see [39]), finding no link between reality TV and aggression). Cross-sectionally, true crime indeed shows small positive correlations with self-reported trait aggression [82], which however, may also indicate that aggressive individuals selectively prefer violent media (see [18]). Accordingly, for true crime, this could imply that habitual consumption of detailed and realistic acts of human violence may increase anger and aggression in consumers through similar mechanisms as proposed by the GAM, perhaps more strongly than for fictional crime media, if we assume more emotional engagement and physiological arousal by violence that is real [21, 82]. However, assumptions of the GAM have also been increasingly challenged [27, 35, 52], with studies contesting initial meta-analytic conclusions [5] that media violence induces aggression. For instance, [52] argued that methodological re-evaluations of media-induced aggression using bias-correction techniques significantly reduced proposed effect sizes, with corrected estimates pointing to negligible media on aggression effects. In this vein, critics have questioned the GAM’s heavy reliance on social learning mechanisms, while simultaneously under-emphasizing genetic, biological, and interindividual variables that may or may not predispose individuals to aggression, along with modulating their responses to aggression in the environment [38]. In support of this criticism, several studies, some of them pre-registered, have failed to replicate core assumptions of the GAM, often reporting null findings or practically negligible associations between violent media consumption and aggression, especially when other interindividual differences are taken into account [31, 33, 35, 36, 83]. (Near) null effects of violent media on aggression seem to be particularly prominent in longitudinal studies [19, 29, 66], with a recent meta-analysis highlighting that overall, reported violent media effect sizes are statistically indistinguishable from zero [35].

In our investigation, we thus contrast different, competing perspectives on media effects on aggression: While the GAM framework may expect true crime consumption to increase anger in our daily diary study, alternative frameworks would expect that this is not the case. Importantly, while this study does not test null hypotheses (and no such expectations were pre-registered), we may still consider the outcome that true crime consumption may have no notable effects on anger or anxiety.3

The present study

The purpose of the present study was to investigate the direction of associations between affect (positive affect, anxiety, anger) and true crime consumption based on the above-mentioned perspectives, to shed light on previous cross-sectional findings. By applying a two-week, smartphone-based daily diary method in combination with cross-lagged multilevel modelling, we tested if true crime consumption is more likely the result of certain affective states and potentially associated emotion regulation motivations, or if true crime consumption more likely influences affect in terms of increased anxiety or increased anger. To determine whether obtained effects are specific to true crime consumption, fictional crime consumption (fictional crime entertainment media) was controlled in all models. Overall, this study aims to contribute to literature by specifically examining the often-debated temporal relationships of anxiety, anger and crime media consumption.

Methods

Transparency and openness

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures relevant to the present study. All data, analysis code, and research materials are available at the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/hkd5v. Data were analyzed using R, version 4.4.0 [85] and the package R package brms (Version: 2.18.0; [15]). This study’s design and the bidirectional hypotheses were pre-registered prospectively, before data were collected (aspredicted.org, #180,137, https://aspredicted.org/sv72-5dqz.pdf). Specifically, we pre-registered our study design (how data will be collected), and bidirectional, competing hypotheses that were, however, of a more general nature, referring to well-being in a broader sense. First, we pre-registered our intention to test whether true crime consumption results in lower-wellbeing (i.e., whether true crime consumption day 1 decreases well-being on day 2). Second, we pre-registered our intention to test whether lower well-being results in higher true crime consumption (i.e., whether lower well-being on day 1 increases true crime consumption on day 2). After collecting the data, we then decided to focus our investigation on affect specifically (positive affect, anxiety, anger), to narrow the scope of the present paper. Thus, we did not have specific a-priori hypotheses for the true crime – affect links reported in this paper. In sum, this study was pre-registered to test bidirectional hypotheses regarding links of true crime with well-being, and thus, two competing pathways. We also pre-registered our statistical analyses with cross-lagged multilevel modelling. Importantly, we did not pre-register our Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE) analyses that allowed us to test whether our effects exceeded the smallest effect size of interest. ROPE analyses were added based on Reviewer comments during the revision of this manuscript. We also did not pre-register the idea that there may be no notable effects of true crime consumption on anger or anxiety. The review process led us to consider this alternative outcome of our analyses, which we then added to our manuscript.

Participants

Sample size was determined based on previous similar daily diary studies [26, 61, 90]. As another approximation, we also ran a frequentist power analysis for linear multiple regression (GPower 3.1.9.7, [32]) with a small effect size (f2 = 0.10), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and three predictors. This suggested a minimum of n ~ 114 participants. Yet, due to a much higher response rate than anticipated, 251 participants ultimately filled in our online surveys. The survey was open to any interested participant over the age of 18. Data was collected between July and November 2024. We only included participants who filled in the daily diary throughout two consecutive days at least.4 This resulted in a sample of n = 199 participants (160 women, 28 men, 1 diverse, 10 no data5) with a mean age of 23.33 years (SD = 4.58). All participants were German speaking adults currently residing in Austria or Germany. We did not record data on race or ethnicity. In terms of education, the majority of the sample had at least a high school degree (63.5%), with 24.9% holding a Bachelor’s degree, and 10.1% holding a Master’s degree. 87.3% of the sample self-identified as “true crime fans”, 12.7% considered themselves “not fans”. Self-reported income was positively skewed, with most participants (74.20%) in the lower income brackets. All participants gave informed consent before participating in the daily diary study and where financially compensated for their participation (40 € in total). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Daily diary method

After filling in an online survey on demographics and other traits,6 participants received an email with download instructions for ESMira [69], an open-source experience sampling app that can be accessed via PC, tablet, or smartphone. For this study, participants were encouraged to install the app on their smartphones in order to conveniently receive the daily prompts to fill in the diary. Each day, prompts to fill in the survey were sent out at 7 p.m., with two additional reminders at 8 p.m., and 9 p.m., though participants could fill in the survey until 11.30 p.m. on the same day. After 14 days, the prompts ended automatically. The survey included ratings on positive and negative affect (PANAS) followed by questions on true crime consumption, and questions on fictional crime consumption.7 Participants could start the daily diary on any day, with technical support available for the entire duration of the study, which lasted from July to November of 2024. Overall, participants were instructed to keep up their true crime and other media habits as usual, and that true crime consumption was not expected to participate in the study. The daily diary survey was developed for the purpose of this study and can be found in the supplementary material (see supplementary file).

Affect

Each day, participants rated 20 items of the German version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; [64]), according to positive affect (interested, excited, strong, enthusiastic, proud, alert, inspired, determined, attentive, active) and negative affect (distressed, upset, guilty, scared, hostile, irritable, ashamed, nervous, jittery, afraid). Items were rated on a scale from 1 = not at all to 5 = very much. Analyses for negative affect were run separately for anxiety (scared, nervous, afraid) and anger (upset, hostile, irritable). The reliability of positive affect, negative affect, as well as negative affect subscales were good for both between person (positive: RkR8 = 0.83, anxiety: RkR = 0.80, anger: RkR = 0.89) and within person (positive: Rc = 0.87, anxiety: Rc = 0.78, anger: Rc = 0.65). The ICC showed that 28.7%, 39.4%, and 25.4% of the sum variance were due to between-person differences for PA and anxiety and anger.

In total, 1013 days (46.83%) of the 2163 days were without true crime consumption. Nine participants (4.69% of the total sample) did not consume true crime throughout the duration of the study. Of all true crime formats, true crime podcasts (38.81%) and social media content (32.54%) were consumed the most, followed by TV (16.83%), news (7.62%), books (2.14%), and movies (2.06%). ICC analyses indicated that 22.5% of variance of overall true crime consumption, 23.8% of duration of true crime consumption, 24.8% of violence in true crime consumption, and 33.3% of intended true crime consumption before sleep were due to between-person differences.

Fictional crime consumption

The questions for fictional crime consumption were identical to the ones on true crime consumption. In the online survey prior to the daily diary, 57.7% of participants self-identified as “fans of fictional crime”, while 38.1% identified as “not fans, and 4.2% did not give an indication to either. In total, 460 days (21.27%) of the 2163 days were without fictional crime consumption. Of all fictional crime formats, TV (52.74%) and books (22.12%) were consumed the most, followed by movies (11.91%), social media (7.37%), podcasts (4.91%), and news (0.95%). All participants consumed fictional crime at least once throughout the duration of the study. ICC analyses indicated that 29.6% of variance of overall fictional crime consumption, 33.6% of duration of fictional crime consumption, 30.5% of violence in fictional crime consumption, and 33.2% of intended fictional crime consumption before sleep were due to between-person differences.

Statistical analyses

Missing data was confined to missed diary days rather than item-level nonresponse, as our survey did not permit partial responses. Inspection of completion rates across the 14 study days revealed no systematic pattern of attrition, suggesting that missing data was not concentrated at particular time points. Across 199 participants, a total of 2358 days were completed (M = 11.8 days per participant, range: 2–14). Of these, approximately 1961 were available for the cross-lagged analysis, as day-pairs where a participant had no corresponding next-day entry do not contribute to the lagged estimates. Missing diary days were not imputed. Instead, we used Bayesian multilevel models estimated on all available observations, which can accommodate unbalanced repeated-measures data without excluding participants who missed some entries. This approach does not require complete cases, although, like other longitudinal models, it assumes that missing diary days do not introduce substantial systematic bias into the estimated effects.

Multilevel cross-lagged panel models with random intercepts for participants were used to examine bidirectional pathways linking true crime consumption and affect (positive, anxiety, anger) across days [92]; for similar applications, see [90, 110]). For true crime consumption, overall consumption, duration of consumption, violence in consumption, and intended consumption before sleep (4) were used as different variables of interest.

Fictional crime consumption (overall, duration, violence, or intended consumption before sleep, respectively, depending on the tested true crime variable) was used as a covariate to test the robustness of effects. Our cross-lagged approach allows to investigate the directionality of associations next to estimating their relations. Each variable at the time point of measurement (day n) predicts variables at the next time point of measurement (day n + 1 = cross-lagged component). These associations are controlled for longitudinal stability (autoregressive component). The results indicate how the constructs at the initial time point influence changes in other constructs, relative to the baseline, while accounting for the effects of other pathways. The R package brms (Version: 2.18.0; [15]), non-informative priors, 4 chains, 20,000 iterations, and 50% warm-up) was used to fit Bayesian models for estimating the parameters in the multilevel cross-lagged models for different aspects of true crime consumption and specific affect by using the probabilistic programming language Stan. When the dichotomized variables of overall true crime consumption, consumed formats, and true crime consumption before sleep on the next day were used as outcome variables, Bernoulli distribution was applied. When outcome variables were duration of true crime consumption and violence of true crime consumption, or affect on the next day, Gaussian distribution was applied. Due to the high proportion of zeros in overall true crime consumption, we applied Zero-Inflated Poisson models to account for excess zeros in the data. To facilitate comparability of regression coefficients across models, continuous predictor variables and continuous outcomes in Gaussian models were z-standardized prior to model estimation (M = 0, SD = 1). Binary predictor variables were rescaled by dividing by two standard deviations following Gelman [42], rendering their coefficients approximately comparable to those of standardized continuous predictors. Count and binary outcome variables were not standardized, as doing so would violate the assumptions of the Bernoulli and Zero-Inflated Poisson distributions. Coefficients for these models are reported as log-odds estimates with 95% credible intervals. Effects were interpreted as meaningful when the credible intervals (CIs) did not include zero.

To evaluate the practical significance of our effects, we conducted Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE) analyses using the bayestestR package [72]. The ROPE procedure is a Bayesian approach to equivalence testing that defines a range of effect sizes considered practically equivalent to zero — the ROPE bounds — and evaluates what proportion of the posterior distribution falls within this range [65]. An effect is considered practically significant when the majority of its posterior distribution falls outside the ROPE, indicating that the effect is unlikely to be negligible. Specifically, effects with less than 2.5% of their posterior inside the ROPE are considered clearly outside it, while effects with more than 97.5% inside are considered practically equivalent to zero. Determining appropriate ROPE bounds requires specifying a smallest effect size of interest (SESOI, [67]), with in this study, we set at β = 0.10. This SESOI is based on recommendations for minimum thresholds regarding practical relevance of effects in media and aggression research (see [37], also see [34]).

Discussion

This study investigated if positive and negative affect bidirectionally correlate with true crime consumption via multilevel cross-lagged panel models. We tested assumptions around two research questions: why people consume true crime content and what consequences may result from true crime consumption. Per perspective I, people may consume true crime content to manage negative affect by engaging with mood-congruent stories that allow for a safe experience and regulation of anxiety [95, 97], or aggressive people may seek out violent media [18]. This would suggest a path from anxiety and/or anger on day n to true crime consumption on day n + 1. For perspective II, based on (violent) media cultivation effects on fear of crime, perception of danger, and aggression [4, 88], true crime consumption may increase negative affect. This would suggest a path from true crime consumption on day n to more anxiety and/or anger on day n + 1.

Before discussing our findings, we note an important limitation regarding effect magnitude. ROPE analyses following Ferguson and Heene's [37] recommended SESOI of β = 0.10 indicated that none of our observed cross-lagged effects exceeded this threshold. More recent cross-lagged multilevel studies on media – affect relationships suggest smaller SESOI’s of β = 0.05 ([10, 25]), arguing that within-person cross-lagged autoregressive models systematically yield smaller coefficient magnitudes than cross-sectional correlations. However, even with a SESOI of β = 0.05, our observed effects only approach, but do not exceed this threshold. We therefore treat the following discussion as preliminary pending replication in future, and we explicitly caution against overinterpreting the observed associations.

Overall, our results suggest distinct and directional associations between specific affective states and specific aspects of true crime consumption. These associations involved both anxiety, and positive affect, but manifested in different directions, and generally aligned with perspective I: Higher anxiety on day n predicted more violent true crime consumption on day n + 1 and notably, more violent true crime consumption on day n predicted more positive affect on day n + 1. As a secondary finding, intended true crime consumption before sleep on day n predicted lower anxiety on day n + 1. There were no effects for anger in either direction. All these effects remained stable after controlling for fictional crime consumption on day n and day n + 1. There are several aspects worthy of discussion with regard to these findings. Importantly however, effects with violent true crime consumption must be interpreted in light of its operationalization. Our violent true crime variable was coded as 0 = no true crime consumption on a specific day, 1 = consumption of nonviolent content to 5 = consumption of highly violent content. This variable therefore represents the full continuum of violent true crime engagement in daily life, ranging from non-consumption to highly violent content consumption. Accordingly, effects reflect violent true crime engagement more broadly — capturing both the contrast between consuming and not consuming true crime, and the gradient of violence among those who did consume. This operationalization was retained intentionally, as we consider non-consumption days a natural and theoretically meaningful part of the daily true crime consumption distribution relevant to our research questions.

Should our observed association of anxiety predicting violent true crime consumption replicate with stronger effects in other studies, this would support the idea that feeling stressed, anxious, and vulnerable may draw people to violent and scary media to cope with their negative feelings [95, 104]. But why pick true frightening stories over fictional or true positive ones? One vital mechanism suggested by Scrivner [95] is that engagement with frightening horror stories allows individuals to shift their anxiety to a more predictable and controllable source, making it substantially easier to engage various emotion regulation strategies and experience agency over one’s fears (also see [3, 23, 24]). On this point, research underlines stressor controllability as a critical factor for physiological and behavioral stress responses, as well as associated mental health outcomes [28, 71]. If we transfer this idea to true crime, it may allow people who feel vulnerable and anxious to engage with their fears and uncertainties around crime, victimization, and human violence in a safe and controllable way, as a true crime show can be paused or watched/listened to with the lights on or in the presence of others (see [95]). True crime stories in podcasts specifically are often interrupted by banter, opinions, or consolation by the hosts, and along with true crime content on social media, may give the strong impression that one is not left alone with these frightening true stories, but is part of a like-minded community (see [12]). With this in mind, certain true crime formats may even allow for and elicit interpersonal and collective emotion regulation (see [45, 113]) of danger and existential threat. In terms of relevance, Oosterwijk [77] and Vivanco Carlevari et al. [108] showed that people are exceptionally curious about dangers and suffering originating from other humans. Further, both morbid curiosity and interest in horror have been rooted in a fundamental human desire to make sense of and prepare for threats [94, 96], – which may explain the tremendous appeal of the true crime genre as a safe but realistic “playground” for coping with fear of crime and mortality. In nice agreement, Taylor [107] found that mortality salience (thoughts of death) was linked to a higher preference for TV programs with law and justice themes, and interpreted as a terror management response by invoking core values and reliable institutions of society.10 Overall, in light of higher true crime interest [74, 82] and fear of victimization in women [40], true crime consumption could thus be rather gender-specific example of “scary play” with fear of crime.

Importantly, our study delivers preliminary insights that this scary play may be beneficial from an emotion regulation perspective, as we found that certain aspects of true crime consumption predicted more positive affect and lower anxiety the next day. These our results are well in line with previous research on the benefits of scary play (see [3]). Both Kerr et al. [55] and Scrivner et al. [97] reported improved mood and lowered anxiety in visitors of haunted houses, in addition to participants’ feeling that they had developed better emotion regulation skills. Similarly, [98] found that fans of contagion and zombie movies experienced less stress during the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, noting that playfully simulating similar scenarios had made them more resilient when faced with a related real-life threat. Our findings may suggest that for certain individuals, true crime consumption may act as a similar safe and immersive training ground for emotion regulation (also see [82]). However, we need to acknowledge that our observed patterns are not fully circular. While anxiety on day n predicted more violent true crime consumption on day n + 1, violent true crime on day n did not predict lower anxiety, but instead, more positive affect on day n + 1. This may suggest that potential regulatory benefits of violent true crime engagement may manifest as positive affect generation, rather than anxiety reduction specifically. However, horror research still interprets this as broad regulatory benefits of controlled threat simulation, as studies find that certain types of horror fans both report pleasure-related benefits and having coped with existential fears after horror engagement (see [97]). Still, our SESOI analysis indicated that among our observed findings, the effect of violent true crime consumption on more positive affect was the one of least practical relevance. Thus, while media enjoyment has also been linked to need satisfaction [105, 106], which reverts back to established true crime motives of defensive vigilance and in part, emotion regulation [82], for now, this finding only serves as an interesting angle for future research. Still, our study may have captured some anxiety regulation mechanisms of true crime, as intended true crime consumption before sleep on day n did predict lower anxiety on day n + 1. Questioning the path from true crime consumption to positive affect also does not negate the observed path from anxiety to true crime consumption, which aligns with previous findings and ideas on anxiety and horror selection [56, 95, 104]. Moreover, in the cross-sectional study by [81, 82], individuals whose true crime consumption was motived by defensive vigilance reported more adaptive emotion regulation in daily life, and individuals whose true crime consumption was motivated by emotion regulation showed higher cognitive reappraisals skills for downregulating anxiety. In sum, while future studies will have to more directly test these mechanisms (from anxiety to horror or true crime consumption to emotion regulation to positive affect), our findings converge with theories on functions of recreational horror and previous empirical studies [58].

As an important note, our paths from anxiety and to positive affect were mostly obtained for violent true crime consumption. Overall true crime consumption (having consumed or not) and duration of true crime consumption (in minutes per day) did not show any associations with affect. Several explanations may account for this. If we assume that fear of victimization and defensive vigilance motivation drive true crime consumption (see [82]), it is reasonable that anxious individuals are drawn to more violent true crime stories that simulate their fears about serious physical harm (for women: murder and sexual assault, [74, 75]. This higher personal relevance may also translate into higher immersion into violent true crime, which may provide better opportunities for self-regulation (for the importance of self-relevance in cognitive reappraisal trainings, [81]. In addition, the most popular true crime shows focus on homicide (Serial podcast, Dateline NBC TV, Cold Case Files, etc.) suggesting that participants’ preferred shows are usually higher in violence. Together with the lack of associations for overall true crime consumption, these results further suggest that type of true crime content, immersion, familiarity, and enjoyment of content may be critical aspects to consider in a more thorough analysis of motives and outcomes around true crime consumption (see [82]). It is also plausible that links to duration of true crime consumption could have been suppressed by participants’ retrospective evening reports of consumption during the day. In this respect, a meta-analysis stressed insufficient correlations between subjective and objective digital media use, hinting at perceptual biases in self-report (see [79]). While in the future, studies could utilize smartphone meta data for tracking true crime consumption via podcast and streaming apps (see [102]), this may still only capture a portion of consumed true crime content, along with posing ethical and privacy challenges (see [48]).

On a final note, our study did not find evidence that true crime consumption increased anxiety (fear cultivation by crime media, see [88] or anger (violent media facilitating aggression, see [4]. Here, it needs to be noted that while cross-lagged multilevel modeling offers more insights into dynamic changes of affect and behavior than interpretations from cross-sectional studies, this approach still does not allow inference of strict causality, which only controlled experiments can achieve [47]. We also need to emphasize that while anger (as an emotion) is linked to aggression (as a behavior), anger does not directly or inevitably cause aggression [16, 68], which suggests a daily diary assessment of aggression as the next step in understanding true crime effects. Still, for now, our findings coincide with an array of both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies failing to obtain meaningful associations between violent media consumption and aggression [19, 29, 31, 33, 35, 36, 66, 83]. For true crime consumption specifically, our findings also match observations of a two-study cross-sectional investigation, which found no evidence that true crime consumers were more prone to harming others in creative ways and on the contrary, even suggested buffering effects of true crime interest on malevolent intentions (see [80]). Taken together, these results do not support the idea that true crime consumption induces negative emotional states that may motivate aggression toward others.

Limitations and outlook

As noted before, ROPE analyses using a SESOI of β = 0.10 following Ferguson and Heene [37] indicated that while credible intervals excluded zero, our observed associations are negligible by these practical significance standards. Our results can thus only be treated as a preliminary observation informing future investigations, and not as evidence for any beneficial effects of true crime consumption from an emotion regulation perspective. While we obtained bidirectional links of true crime consumption, anxiety, and positive affect, our time-lag of one day also only captures comparatively more long-lasting effects around true crime consumption, and not same day dynamics (but see [54, 90]), for similar approaches to dynamic affect changes). Thus, when it comes to future true crime daily diaries, multiple assessment per day may be required to better understand more fleeting motives and effects of true crime. Critically, our effects were mostly observed for our variable of violent true crime consumption, which captured violent true crime engagement across the full continuum from non-consumption (0) to consumption of nonviolent content (1) to highly violent content (5). Whether this effect is driven primarily by the decision to consume violent true crime at all, by the specific degree of violent content among consumers, or by a combination of both, cannot be determined from the present data. Future studies are warranted to disentangle these mechanisms in larger samples over a longer daily diary period. Next, our obtained association between intended true crime consumption before sleep and lower anxiety the next day can be questioned, as our study did not verify that participants followed through on their intentions. Still, our data showed that true crime consumption before sleep on day n predicted the same on day n + 1, suggesting a rather stable habit. This fits anecdotal evidence that people like to fall asleep to true crime stories [112], suggesting new avenues of investigating sleep quality and absorption/engagement in true crime stories. The large number of days on which participants intended to consume true crime before sleep (~ 72%) also indicates that this particular aspect of true crime consumption should be investigated in more detail. Additionally, while our recruitment was open to all interested individuals, the resulting sample was naturally comprised of more highly engaged consumers. However, given the study’s aim to investigate the reciprocal dynamics between affect and true crime consumption, focusing on a very engaged true crime audience allowed to test these effects in a sample for which they are most relevant. Moreover, while we collected data on specific true and fictional crime formats (e.g., TV, podcasts, social media), they only served descriptive purposes in our study. We deliberately refrained from conducting further exploratory analyses by media formats to maintain the statistical parsimony of our models, and not to inflate the risk of Type I errors. We believe that format-specific analyses are best left for future studies in larger, more diverse samples that are specifically designed for these more fine-grained comparisons. Our findings are broadly compatible with vulnerability-based accounts of crime media use as part of the differential reception thesis (see [87]). This framework suggests that people may either be more receptive to crime media if they have no prior crime experience (substitution hypothesis), or the opposite, if they have a lot of experience (resonance hypothesis), if they themselves resemble the portrayed crime victims (affinity hypothesis), or if they view themselves as vulnerable to crime (vulnerability hypothesis). We find higher anxiety predictive of violent true crime consumption in a sample largely composed of young women, who report higher fear of crime [40], and seemingly constitute the main true crime audience. This may suggest that feeling scared and vulnerable is linked to more true crime engagement for emotion regulation and defensive vigilance, and is thus, compatible with a vulnerability account. At the same time however, our study was not designed to test differential audience effects as outlined by Roche et al. [87]. Therefore, we cannot rule out that our results also represent other effects related to affinity, resonance, or substitution, which should be followed up in future investigations. As another limitation, affect items were consistently presented before media consumption items, as we aimed for capturing participants’ undiluted affective baseline before they reflected on their media consumption. This fixed order may have led to certain priming effects. Future studies could explore counterbalanced designs to empirically test whether our reported effects are sensitive to the order of item presentation. Altogether, while the interpretations of our directional effects should be treated as preliminary and with caution, our cross-lagged daily diary study may nonetheless allow for first insights into relevant mechanisms around true crime consumption [90, 92].

Constraints on generality

Next to the already noted limitations, given that the vast majority of our sample was composed of young female university students, our results might not generalize to other groups and require replication with other samples that should also scrutinize individual differences that may moderate or mediate true crime – affect links. Still, since studies have shown that the true crime audience is female and young (see [13, 82, 112]), this first daily diary study on true crime consumption directly examines the population most engaged with this media genre, which enhances the relevance of our findings. Nonetheless, the unbalanced gender composition in our study still needs to be treated as a limitation, and future studies are warranted to consider gender as a between-person effects in more advanced statistical models.

Conclusion

In today’s high-choice media environment, true crime continues to be a popular entertainment choice, which may be questioned with regard to motives for and consequences of engaging with tales of violent crimes. In this paper, we explored both the idea that certain affect may motivate people towards true crime consumption, and that true crime consumption may yield changes in people’s affect (positive, anxiety, anger). In our two-week, cross-lagged daily diary study, anxiety of today positively predicted violent true crime consumption of tomorrow, violent true crime consumption of today predict more positive affect of tomorrow, and intent to consume true crime before sleep predicted lower anxiety of tomorrow. A more generous interpretation of our small effect sizes may suggest that anxious individuals use true crime for threat coping and emotion regulation, matching ideas from horror research that engagement with frightening worlds could be beneficial. From a more conservative perspective however, our findings did not meet suggested thresholds for practical significance, and the observed associations may ultimately reflect noise rather than meaningful psychological dynamics. In sum, whether true crime consumption is a means for everyday emotion regulation or simply a popular cultural phenomenon with negligible psychological consequences, both conclusions converge on the same implication: Based on the findings of this study, the widespread consumption of true crime does not appear to carry the affective risks that are occasionally attributed to crime entertainment media.
