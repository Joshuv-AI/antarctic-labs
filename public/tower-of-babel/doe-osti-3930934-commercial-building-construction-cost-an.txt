Executive Summary
The buildings industry represents significant contributions to the U.S. economy, employment,
energy consumption, and material use. Building construction in the U.S. represented about $1.29
trillion (U.S. Census Bureau 2023) and 2.5 million jobs in 2022 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
2023). Commercial and industrial building construction costs saw steady price increases from
2000 through 2021 and then saw significant prices increases during the COVID 19 pandemic
from late 2021 through early 2023. Prices have stayed high since then and started an increasing
trend starting in mid-2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026). In addition, building
construction and operations account for 37% of the primary energy consumption, and the energy
consumption is projected to increase by approximately 0.2% per year for the next several
decades (EIA 2020).

High building construction and operating costs represent a burden on U.S. consumers and the
economy. The U.S. Department of Energy is very interested in understanding and reducing the
cost to construct, maintain, and operate buildings. In order to better understand construction
costs, this report examines some of the factors that lead to higher costs that provide little
additional value to the customer and sets out to identify potential strategies and targeted RD&D
to reduce construction and retrofit costs.

Commercial building construction projects involve several performers including designers,
engineers, general contractors (GC), subcontractors, consultants, suppliers, financers, inspectors,
and others. Each entity has their own cost structure making the overall costs quite complex and
defining categories and disaggregating costs extremely difficult. We roughly divide construction
costs into hard costs and soft costs. Hard costs are generally defined as the hardware or physical
items in a building, labor required for construction, and overhead and profit. Soft costs generally
refer to all other costs in a project. Soft costs are generally between 10% and 12% but can vary
between 3% to 40% of total project costs.

Gathering detailed data on construction costs and costing practices is challenging. In this project,
we gathered information and detailed cost data from a literature review, cost estimators, industry
data bases, industry cost estimation software, interviews, and a questionnaire. From this
information, we learned about many inefficiencies in the construction process and identified
opportunities to overcome these inefficiencies and reduce costs. We did not gather cost and
performance data for specific energy efficiency retrofit measures, and we did not explore cost
targets for these technologies.

Controlling construction costs is challenging due to the complexities and number of parties
involved in projects. There are several conditions that influence the overall cost of construction
projects. The complexities are reflected in the information and data collected, but we were able
to see recuring themes around a lack of transparency, nonstandard information and physical
systems, complex projects, and inconsistent and sometimes conflicting regulations. Across these
themes, we identified 23 factors that contribute to the costs and represent opportunities for
improvements and cost savings.

1 Introduction
The design, construction, maintenance, operation, and demolition of buildings represent a
significant portion of the U.S. economy, employment, energy consumption, material use, and
environmental impact. Building construction in the U.S. represented about $1.29 trillion (U.S.
Census Bureau 2023) and 2.5 million jobs in 2022 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2023).
Commercial and industrial building construction costs saw steady price increases from 2000
through 2021 and significant price increases during the COVID 19 pandemic. Prices have stayed
high since then and started an increasing trend starting in mid-2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor
Statistics 2026). In addition, building construction and operations account for 37% of the
primary energy consumption, and the impact of the growth of U.S. building stock is projected to
increase energy consumption by approximately 0.2% per year for the next several decades (EIA
2020). High building construction and operating costs and energy consumption represent a
burden on U.S. consumers and the economy.

There is a recognized need for more housing in the U.S. and there is a need to retrofit existing
buildings to replace aging systems and improve the operating performance. Because of the
tremendous size of the existing building stock, upgrades to existing buildings represent the
largest opportunity for economic impact and cost savings. However, the rising cost of
construction is seen as a significant barrier to completing retrofits on a national scale. A survey
of 236 facility management experts revealed their top three barriers to energy efficiency retrofits
are payback period, life-cycle cost, and the funding mechanism (Medal et al. 2021).

One major challenge are declining efficiencies in the construction process. Construction labor
productivity in the U.S. has decreased by 1% per year since 1995, and the decrease for specialty
trades necessary for building retrofits is even higher (MGI 2017). Labor productivity from the
MGI report includes total construction project costs and is a measure of the economic challenges
faced by the retrofit market.

This project aims to gain a deeper understanding of the construction process and market
dynamics and identify opportunities to reduce costs for commercial building construction and
retrofit projects.

The desired outcomes for this project:

 • Document factors in commercial building construction and retrofit projects that lead to
 higher costs with little or no additional value to the customer
 • Identify potential strategies and targeted RD&D to reduce construction and retrofit costs.

To achieve these outcomes, we established the following primary objectives:

 • Collect data on challenges and factors that contribute to process inefficiencies for
 commercial building construction and retrofit projects
 • Collect detailed construction cost data for at least two building types and three regions,
 preferably deep energy retrofit projects. Priority buildings include K-12 schools and
 multifamily housing
 • Document and quantify construction project cost categories

 1

 • Identify unique challenges and opportunities for major retrofit projects.

To approach these objectives, we took a close look at the construction industry and associated
costing models. Commercial building construction projects involve several performers including
designers, engineers, general contractors (GC), subcontractors, consultants, suppliers, and others.
Each entity has its own cost structure, making the overall costs quite complex and defining
categories and disaggregating costs extremely difficult. We roughly divide construction costs
into hard costs and soft costs. Hard costs are generally defined as the hardware or physical items
in a building, labor required for construction, and overhead and profit. Soft costs generally refer
to all other costs in a project; however, soft costs are not always delineated and are often lumped
into overhead costs especially for subtier performers. Smaller subtier performers often do not
complete detailed costing of their operations, making it difficult to identify cost reduction
opportunities. This report identifies general categories for cost reductions and provides specific
recommendations for cost reductions. We did not analyze specific technologies and cost targets
for those technologies.

2 Previous Cost Analysis Projects
The building construction industry has been documented to have very low and sometimes
declining productivity compared to the rest of the US economy (Figure 1; MGI 2017; Goldman
Sachs 2026). There are several factors that contribute to this decline, such as incremental
innovation, technological changes, and regulation impacts (Goldman Sachs 2026, Federal
Reserve Bank of Richmond 2025), which can add to the overall construction project cost. Many
of the factors that contribute to higher costs have been identified in various reports. The New
York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA) RetrofitNY program
completed a detailed cost analysis of business-as-usual and zero-energy retrofits of six
multifamily buildings (NYSERDA 2021), the National Association of Home Builders and
National Multifamily Housing Council published a 2018 study investigating the cost of
regulation on multifamily housing developments (Emrath and Walter 2018), the Terner Center
for Housing Innovation published a report analyzing recent trends in labor and material costs for
apartment buildings (Raetz et al. 2020), and the Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies
published insightful cost analysis reports on residential and multifamily housing (Hoyt 2020 and
Colton and Ahluwalia 2019).

2.1 SunShot PV
NLR completed a cost analysis of PV installations from 2010 through 2025 to understand the
factors contributing to the high cost of installed PV systems with results shown in Figure 2 (NLR
2026). This project collected costs through interviews and PV installation databases. Data
collection was repeated annually starting in 2010. These regular data collection and analysis
efforts documented the tremendous decline in the cost of installed PV systems. In addition, they
have been able to identify complex and varied permitting as a challenge and developed an
automated process to reduce permitting from five days to a few hours in some cases. 1

2.2 Energy Efficient Window Replacement
NLR completed a similar cost analysis project for energy efficient window replacements for
residential buildings. Window replacements are often prohibitively high cost and often cannot be
justified based on energy savings alone (McCall et al. 2019). The window cost analysis project
collected data through an industry average construction cost database, individual window
replacement estimates, and direct contact with contractors. There were wide variations in the
expected costs of window replacements, with the major cost drivers (beyond the size of the
project) being the state of the existing building and effort required to complete the job, quality of
the new windows, quality of the installation, location, and the installation company structure.
The report provides a breakdown of average cost categories based on the size of the project as
shown in Figure 3. Window material and manufacturing costs are the largest category and
provide the largest opportunity for overall cost reduction. Consumer education and customer
acquisition costs are an important cost driver from the contractor perspective and may be another
area for cost reduction opportunities.

LBNL identified some key takeaways from their data collection. First, they were able to develop
a database structure that can identify common energy savings measures and their associated
costs. They defined six cost compression pathways:

 • Rebates and incentives
 • Upgrade energy efficiency package optimization
 • Technology innovation
 • Soft cost reductions
 • New equipment performance metrics
 • No- and low-cost methods.

They recommended for future DOE efforts to increase the number and effectiveness of energy
efficient retrofits to include focused outreach and the need to engage industry through larger
stakeholder meetings.

In addition to this effort, LBNL used another industry survey to collect information from home
energy professionals to determine promising cost reduction strategies for different project types.
The survey used qualitative and quantitative questions to demonstrate evidence of estimated cost

 6

reduction potential in residential upgrades and the likelihood of their use in the construction
industry. Their findings show that significant cost reductions and savings are possible by
reducing and minimizing additional infrastructure costs, streamlining project planning or
management, and using innovations that make installation easier. More specifically, the top
strategies that respondents were likely to adopt include using a reliable workforce with reduced
turnover, rapid permitting and compliance tools, and bundling energy efficiency measures into
packages (such as combining hot water, HVAC and insulation together) (Satre-Meloy et al.
2024).

2.4 RetrofitNY Multifamily Housing
The NYSERDA RetrofitNY High-Performance Retrofit Solutions Design program was designed
to create solutions that improve the performance of multifamily residential buildings
(NYSERDA 2021). A special project within RetrofitNY explored the costs to achieve net-zero
energy retrofits of multifamily housing. This project included six buildings that represented low-
rise projects in upstate New York and mid-rise projects in the New York metropolitan area to
determine the logistical and economic challenges associated with building typology and
geographic differences. Each project included retrofit estimates for business-as-usual and zero-
energy retrofits. Cost-compression studies were completed to evaluate the main cost drivers
(Arena and Ceci 2020). The study determined that the biggest cost drivers were consistent across
all projects but varied in order depending on the building type. The four largest cost drivers
across the zero energy designs were:

 • Mechanical and electrical systems
 • Façade (windows, doors, walls, and roof)
 • General Contractor (GC) operations (overhead, profit, and contingency)
 • Soft costs.

Some of the cost compression strategies identified include:

 • Standard designs for façade panels and mechanical pods can be optimized for
 performance, manufacturing, installation, and economies of scale
 • Design and installation can be simplified with standard system designs for light weight
 façade panels that do not require cranes for installation and for mechanical pods with
 standard connections that can be quickly installed
 • Multiple supply chain markups can exceed 50% of the cost of products. Simplifying the
 supply chain and upstream incentive programs can reduce these markup costs.

2.5 Additional Industry Review
The Terner Center for Housing Innovation published a report analyzing trends in labor and
material costs for 240 multifamily projects built in California (Raetz et al. 2020). Their results
show that hard costs for multifamily housing construction increased 25% over ten years in
California, with the most significant costs coming from finishes (65% increase) and wood,
plastics, and composites (110% increase). Labor and material cost trends contributed to the hard
cost increase but do not entirely account for the rate of the increase. Labor and material costs
were tracked together, but to assess the relative role of labor, the authors compared changes in
line item costs to publicly available data on state wage rates (wages rose from 2006-2018 by

 7

29%). The authors also define soft costs as costs associated with legal and professional fees,
insurance, and development fees, and are separate from “costs of conversion once the project is
complete” and costs of acquisition. Soft costs were estimated to be 19% of the total development
costs for multifamily projects in California.

The Joint Center for Housing Studies at Harvard University analyzed the costs of construction
for multi-family buildings. They found that land costs represent 10%-20% of total cost, soft costs
(design, engineering, financing, permitting) account for 20%-30% of total costs, and hard costs
(labor and materials) represent the remaining 50%-70% of total costs (Hoyt 2020). Furthermore,
labor costs and labor availability are “the number one issue related to housing affordability”
(Colton 2019), where 82% of single-family home builders surveyed and 86% of multifamily
home builders surveyed placed the availability and cost of labor at the top levels of importance in
driving building price.

2.6 Material, Equipment, and Labor Cost Trends
S&P Global Market Intelligence and Procurement Executives Group publish an Engineering and
Construction Cost Indicator, which is a diffusion index that started in 2012 and is updated
monthly and shown in Figure 4 (S&P Global Market Intelligence 2026). The data is taken
monthly via a survey, where respondents are asked whether prices for 12 categories of
materials/equipment and subcontractor rates were higher, lower, or the same as the prior month.
A reading of 50 indicates that on average, industry saw prices remain the same across the set
basket of construction goods. Diffusion indices for material and labor track one another and were
relatively stable from 2012 to 2019. In early 2020, a brief period of downward pressure was
observed, immediately followed by a period of escalating expansionary pressure that lasted
through 2022, likely due to the pandemic’s disruption of supply chains. Post-2022, the diffusion
index generally remains oscillating around ~60 for both materials/equipment and subcontractor
labor, indicating a sustained period of inflationary pressure.

pandemic. Post-pandemic wood product cost indexes have receded back toward the trend seen

The National Association of Home Builders and National Multifamily Housing Council
published a study investigating the cost of regulations on multifamily housing developments
(Emrath and Walter 2018). They concluded that government regulations can impose costs in
several ways, and 90% of multifamily developers incur costs of delays by lengthy approval
processes. Multiple levels of regulation can have conflicting standards which can cause delays
and increase costs. The study concluded that regulations imposed by all levels of government
account for 32% of the cost of an average multifamily development.

These reports provide some recommendations for controlling and reducing the soft and hard
construction costs. Streamlining and bringing more certainty to permitting and the approval
processes can mitigate labor and material cost increases and could bring down pre-construction
and contingency costs (Raetz et al. 2020). While some soft cost reduction recommendations such
as reforming zoning and fee regulations fall within the purview of local governments, other
suggestions focus on reducing construction time and increasing predictability (Hoyt 2020).

Improved communication across the various parties of complex projects like including the GC in
the design process and sharing information with subcontractors for more accurate cost-estimation
can improve project outcomes and reduce costs. They also highlight that training programs are
necessary to create a pipeline of talent for the construction industry since contingency costs are
required for risk against a restricted workforce.

As for hard cost reductions, Hoyt recommends a focus on new techniques and materials,
including modular off-site construction and cross-laminated timber (CLT). However, for
modular construction to have significant cost savings, designs must be standardized and
streamlined, such that duplication of materials is reduced and time can be efficiently optimized
(Klammer et al. 2021).

3 Commercial Building Construction Cost Data
To gain a better understanding of the cost of projects, it is useful to disaggregate the costs into
categories. There are several approaches to defining and assigning construction costs within the
building construction industry. At the highest level, costs can be defined as hard costs and soft
costs. Hard costs include all the physical items, labor, contractor overhead and profit, and
contractor insurance and bonds. Overhead includes operating expenses and often includes a lump
sum of all associated costs. Labor costs include the worker’s pay and expenses associated with
the workers such as taxes and compensation packages. Soft costs include everything else
required for a project and are typically for non-tangible items. Soft costs can vary from 3% to
40% of an overall project cost depending on the project and how they are defined. There is not a
standard definition of soft costs, but examples of soft costs are listed in Table 2 (Rodriguez
2019). Land purchasing costs are usually separate and are not included in these discussions.

Looking further into the hardware costs between the two cases reveals that three of the projects
had the highest cost increase in the façade system and the other three had the highest increase in
the MEP systems. It is not known how much of the increases were due to higher labor and
installation costs compared to higher equipment costs. High efficiency systems can have higher
costs because of the premium cost of new technology and higher cost for specialized installation
requirements. Costs of new technologies are sometimes inflated by the seller because they can
demand a higher cost in the market, and installation costs can be inflated to cover uncertainty
and perceived risk by contractors. This is an area for potential cost reductions for deep energy
retrofits through market expansion of new technologies and specialized training.

The RetrofitNY project cost template included a detailed breakdown of soft costs with 47
categories. No project used all 47 categories, three projects had very few soft cost categories, and
three projects used most of the categories. There are some large costs and several small costs that
contribute to the soft cost total, which varied from 3.4% to 31% of the total project costs with an
average of 12%. Owner Engineering/Architectural fees were significant factors in all projects
(average of 4%). Two projects had significant loan interest and bank fees (4% to 6%). Title
insurance and owner insurance was a factor in two projects (2% to 4%). One project included a
high developer fee (10%). Inspection and permit fees ranged from 0% to 2.3% of total project
costs.

Two main conclusions can be made from these data. First, it is challenging to have a detailed
common cost structure with cost data for all projects. Every project has different costs, and cost
estimators have different ways of defining and aggregating costs. The second conclusion is that
every project is unique, and there are many approaches to construction project management, and
the combination of these two leads to a range in project activities and costs. Some projects may
have additional design requirements or require additional inspections that add to the project
costs. However, the approach to managing projects has the greatest impact on the final cost.
Some companies have worked hard to streamline operations and minimize overall project costs.

 23

These companies have low soft costs and lower overall project costs while still delivering high-
quality projects. The details that lead to cost effective construction project management are
discussed in the remaining sections of this paper. A final observation is that there are costs that
are mostly outside of the control of the designers and contractors such as material and system
costs, labor shortages, regulation costs, and other fees. Contractors and project managers can
work to control some of the effects of these costs.

This section provides a summary of results from the questionnaire and interviews. The
questionnaire was distributed through email listservs, organization listservs, and personal
contacts. BNi Publishing distributed the questionnaire to two listservs with 20,000 and 70,000
subscribers with an offer of a 10% discount on purchases in their store. This mass mailing was
not very effective with only 11 responses. We collected seven more through personal contacts
for a total of 18 responses. We also conducted interviews with nine industry experts.

Respondents from the questionnaire covered a broad range of the construction industry and
building type experience as shown in Figure 17 and Figure 18. We also asked if they worked on
new construction, retrofits, or both, and 83% of the respondents replied that they work on both
project types with 17% working on retrofits only.

We also asked what percent of projects include utility rebates to offset some of the cost of energy

6.2 Questionnaire Responses – Costing Practices
We asked a series of questions about costing practices to better understand how contractors and
cost estimators approach projects and where they get their cost information. The first two
questions asked about certification and standard cost estimating practices. Only 30% of the
respondents said they were certified cost estimators; however, 70% said they follow standard
practices, which is encouraging to see practitioners following industry standards. The source of
cost data is shown in Figure 20. Respondents were allowed to select all sources that apply. The
highest responses were historical data, industry data, and manufacturer data. These results are not
surprising as estimators are most likely to use information they are familiar with and that is
easily available. This approach is often sufficient but can lead to problems when existing cost
data do not align with the project in question or if there are sudden market changes, resulting in
an inaccurate cost estimation.

One challenge with costing building construction is that every project is unique and can require
significant time to evaluate, design, and estimate costs. Often, there is not enough time and
budget to conduct a thorough evaluation, and estimators are left to use their professional
judgment. If there is uncertainty, they will generally err on the side of higher costs to ensure they
do not lose money on a project. Professional practice and state of the market influence how much
contingency is added to estimates. Contingencies and cost estimates are typically low when
companies are eager for work and are higher when they have plenty of projects.

 27

 Figure 20. Source of cost data
We asked about soft costs and factors that increase or impact project costs. We asked what
percent of a project cost can be considered “soft cost,” defined in the questionnaire as all costs
that are not classified as materials, supplies, labor, overhead, and profit. The average response
was 13.4% of the total project cost is considered soft, with a range from 1% to 30%. One
response notably mentioned that while they are a subcontractor, the GC carries the majority of
soft costs. This range and average values roughly align with results from the DCD data,
NYSERDA, and MSBA data.

communication and contract documentation. Design and installation errors can also be factors

challenge from several different sources and questionnaire responses show that it can be a

8 Cost Reduction Solutions
Throughout this project, we collected and analyzed information about the challenges and
solutions for cost effective commercial building construction. Commercial building construction
projects can be small or very large and are usually unique projects. Energy retrofit projects
generally involve several interrelated systems and require custom solutions. Medium and large-
scale projects can be complex with multiple decision makers, numerous and sometimes
conflicting regulatory requirements, multiple contractors and suppliers, and long lead times for
materials and equipment. In addition, many of the processes and costing practices are not
transparent and sometimes intentionally obscured, making it challenging to understand the
processes and develop solutions for reducing costs.

When thinking about cost reduction measures, it is useful to define categories based on the
solution type and cost categories addressed by the measure. For this project we define four cost
reduction categories:

 • Regulatory solutions are policies and regulations that are clear and concise, promote
 efficient outcomes, avoid conflicting requirements, and avoid adding barriers
 • Process solutions streamline operations, improve communications and information
 sharing, standardize processes, and avoid conflicting and duplicating efforts
 • Labor solutions reduce inefficient practices, automate tasks, support labor retention, and
 offer flexible work force development approaches
 • Hardware solutions include advanced technologies with lower cost and higher
 performance, standard system and building designs based on design for assembly and
 disassembly principles, and industrialized offsite and onsite construction methods.

8.1 Potential Construction Cost Reduction Solutions
This section provides four examples of cost reduction solutions. These recommendations are not
comprehensive but provide starting points for further development.

8.1.1 Integrate Codes into Building Design Software
Building codes set the minimum performance requirements to maintain health, safety, and
performance of buildings and can become quite complicated as they touch all aspects of
buildings. In addition, they are updated regularly and may vary by jurisdiction, making it
challenging for designers to know all the codes. According to one building code expert, the
largest challenge in the code industry is that the construction industry does not fully understand
the code. Designers are challenged to learn and correctly design buildings to meet (or exceed the
code), and contractor’s attitude is often “just do what the inspector says,” which often results in
non-optimal, delayed, and more costly building projects. The complexity and variations make
designing to code compliance challenging and time consuming and often requires design
iterations, wasted time, and increased costs.

Building design and construction management software are essential tools for construction
project development and management tasks. Those software tools are the core engines of modern
trends like Building Information Modelling (BIM) and Integrated Project Delivery (IPD)
focusing on improvement in project efficiency. However, code compliance is one area that is not
fully integrated with the project design and delivery. Most code compliance reports are derived
from design documents for approval purposes rather than as an integrated part of project
development process. The addition of standardized code data to building software will not only
reduce resource demand on project development side but also improve the quality and speed up
the approval process of inspection agents and decrease projects costs.

There are several platforms that assist in reviewing designs for code compliance before
submitting their design packages for official code reviews. ResCheck and ComCheck 2 developed

applications to verify energy code compliance with IECC, ASHRAE Standard 90.1, and many

inspection and permitting officials. 3

8.1.2 Automated Permitting and Inspection Platform
From the NYSERDA projects, the permitting and inspection fees can be up to 2.3% of a project
costs; however, the full costs for permitting and inspections are difficult to quantify. Preparation
efforts, time for responding to comments and making changes, and wait times to get inspections
completed can be significant and are difficult to track. According to one multifamily retrofit
company, these delays can add weeks and even months to project completions, which impact
completion and occupancy schedules, ties up project financing, and adds to the overall project
costs. Many jurisdictions have adopted custom or commercially available applications that
support electronic filing, review, and finalization of permitting and inspection, which has
improved the quality and reduced time and costs of permitting and inspections. However, there is
no standard process and there are still many improvements that could be made to provide a
common approach and streamline the process. In addition, a standard and secure approach to
long-term project information storage would save significant time and effort for follow-on
projects.

DOE and NLR implemented a common permitting platform for residential solar systems that
reduced the average permitting process from five days to a single day. 4 While residential PV
systems are simple compared to building construction projects, the SolarAPP+ shows one
example of how effective a common permitting platform can be and how quickly it may be
adopted. Whole-building construction or retrofit projects may be challenging to overcome in one
step, and it may be more effective to start with common building components. HVAC and hot
water replacements are good examples of common retrofit technologies that are essential for
energy efficiency goals. In a survey of 250 cities around the world, it was found that 56% are
using AI powered solutions to streamline processes and improve services to citizens
(ServiceNow et.al., n.d.). Some cities are using AI powered platforms to help streamline permit
application process, read and review permit applications, and develop permitting reports. These
systems have been very successful in reducing time for these processes by 60% to 70% (National
League of Cities, 2025).

8.1.3 Remote and Automated Inspection Technologies
Remote virtual inspections offer many efficiency, safety, and time saving advantages; however,
there are several logistic and technology challenges to overcome to fully take advantage of the
benefits and to get wide adoption. The global COVID-19 pandemic forced many jurisdictions to
rapidly implement remote inspections with various levels of success. The pandemic forced rapid
growth in technology and learning and helped change attitudes toward remote inspections. The

International Code Council supports remote virtual inspections and developed guidance for the

Success of remote inspections is reliant upon advanced planning, good communications,
complete and reliable record keeping, and several technologies. Live video feeds from someone
on site or from drones is one of the most useful technologies. Virtual meeting platforms
expanded rapidly during the beginning of the pandemic and are very useful for remote
inspections. Drones for inspecting the outside of buildings or inside building systems such as
duct work are becoming viable and are gaining popularity. Another useful tool for inspectors is
real time and historical system performance data and other building measurements. These data
streams can come from onsite personnel, existing building automation systems, temporary data
acquisition systems, nearby weather stations, or sensors installed on drones. Drones equipped
with enhanced video cameras, infrared cameras, lidar, and photogrammetry capabilities are being
used to inspect buildings today in limited applications. New York City released a report claiming
that drones used for façade safety inspections can gather detailed information quickly with less
people and reduced safety risk for tall buildings. 6 However, the technology is still nascent and
there are safety concerns with flying drones in cities that need to be addressed. Drones hold great
promise to reduce costs of inspections, improve the quality and amount of information collected,
and greatly reduce the safety risk associated with inspecting buildings. The technical capabilities
of drones are advancing rapidly; however, there is a need to conduct research and development
on building specific applications for inspection and monitoring of building systems.

Remote and on-site inspections could benefit from AI integration as well. Machine learning
models (MLM) could be used for remote and on-site inspections by training the MLM on
pass/fail example media (videos, photos) datasets. Then, the trained models could be
implemented on a handheld smartphone or other device at the construction site and offer live
feedback via the device’s camera and simultaneously prepare documentation. As adoption
increases, ongoing anonymized data collection and user feedback could be utilized to improve on
the models, further increasing the model’s capabilities and decreasing required time and cost.

8.1.4 Standard Building Element and Equipment Configurations, Connections,
 and Controls
One of the most common challenges we heard for reducing the cost and increasing the reliability
of major building retrofits is the unique design of buildings and building systems. Other major
industries such aviation and automobiles have a large degree of standardization, which improves
reliability, improves efficiency, and reduces overall costs. Buildings have unique designs and
unique system architectures that make design, installation, commissioning, retrofits, and
maintenance take longer and increase costs. The uniqueness of buildings is especially
challenging for cost effective deep energy retrofits. Every building requires a detailed

8.1.5 Industrialization and Automation in Construction
The largest potential change and disruption to the construction industry is from the growing field
of industrialization and automation of building construction. Automation can take many forms in
construction projects including the use of AI tools for design, analysis, and costing; the use of 3-
D printers to form concrete walls and whole buildings; the use of robots for material handling
and brick laying; and factory-built housing and building sections. Automation in construction is
relatively new and growing because of the potential to increase labor productivity, accelerate
construction times, increase construction quality, reduce workplace injuries, and reduce material
waste. A study of 14 construction case studies found that firms utilizing automation reduced
project completion times by up to 30%, reduced material waste by up to 40%, and realized a
50% decrease in workplace accidents (Islam et al. 2025). Another broad review of 53 studies
identified seven benefits from the use of industrialized construction: interoperability, scheduling
optimization, production traceability, production safety, manufacturability, quality assurance,
and constructability (Xu et al. 2025). The use of industrialization and automation holds
enormous potential to reduce costs for construction projects; however, it is a nascent field and
there is a need for a strategic review and development process to optimize the outcomes.

8.2 Comprehensive Cost Reduction Approach from NYSERDA
As has been noted several times in this report, the buildings industry is complex, and making
significant impacts on overall costs and quality of retrofit projects is best approached with a
coordinated and wide-ranging set of solutions. The NYSERDA RetrofitNY program took a
comprehensive approach to controlling the quality, performance, and cost of multifamily deep
energy retrofits. Their overall approach is designed to systematize and standardize processes to
drive down costs through saved time, reduced middlemen, and increased product life and
reliability. The goals and methods used to achieve cost reductions are listed below.

Goals:
 • Reduce uncertainty in building financial performance
 • Reduce installation time and costs
 • Increase installation quality control
 • Reduce operational and maintenance costs
 • Improve risk profile and reduce insurance premiums

 40

Methods:
 • Fabricate components using offsite manufacturing in order to reduce unit production and
 installation time requirements and increase product quality
 • Encourage cooperation between component manufacturer and solution provider to
 improve product delivery and reduce product markups
 • Encourage service contracts and warranties that are coupled with extended product life
 will reduce uncertainty and subsequent financial risk/cost and reduce life cycle cost.

In order to access funding, designers, engineers, architects, construction firms, component
manufacturers, and building owners have to qualify through a Request for Qualifications (RFQ)
and are then available to further collaborate to develop and implement NZE retrofits in New
York. Applicants are evaluated on numerous criteria, including several that point to the goals
listed above through the lens of cost reduction.

Specific to component manufacturers, offsite manufactured retrofit solutions should provide
increased savings to the building owner in the short term through reduced construction times and
decreased debt service on construction loans and the long term through reduced utility,
operational, and maintenance costs. Specific criteria that focus on reduced costs include warranty
and service contract support, unit economics, life cycle costs, and installation costs.

Solution providers are evaluated on criteria specific to cost reduction. These criteria include
whole building retrofit solutions, solution provider business model, cooperation between
component manufacturer and solution provider, digital processes used, service contracts and
warranties, and interior scopes of work.

It is too early to measure the successes and failures of these efforts, but this comprehensive
approach is expected to yield results that could impact how future retrofits are executed.

9 Conclusions
We successfully gathered construction cost data from nearly 2,500 projects and obtained
insightful information from industry experienced practitioners. We learned about many
inefficiencies in the construction process and identified opportunities to overcome these
inefficiencies and reduce costs. Recommendations are provided for activities that will improve
construction processes and reduce costs. We did not gather cost and performance data for
specific energy efficiency retrofit measures, and we did not explore cost targets for these
technologies.

We heard multiple times during this project that consistency, transparency, and standardization
of information, contracting, permitting, and physical building systems will help avoid duplication
of efforts, avoid delays, improve outcomes, and reduce costs. This standardization includes
automating estimation processes, definitions and documentation, bidding, information sharing,
contracting, and permitting phases, such that the entirety of projects can run more smoothly,
quickly, and routinely. With respect to cost, our respondents advocate for making cost data, as
well as goals, scope and budget more accurate and available to all parties involved. This active
emphasis on communication helps ensure information is known equally across parties. Capital
markets only function properly with complete information sharing—having all parties privy to
the same information will help increase the efficiency of the buildings market, driving it to a
more optimized process.

Standardization of building and system designs will increase efficiency, quality, reliability, and
support industrialization of manufacturing and construction. Standardization of building systems
and components will have enormous impacts on deep energy retrofit projects, which have
historically been burdened by high cost due to their bespoke nature from project to project. Labor
will become more productive as projects have clear and consistent requirements. Time required
for estimation, permitting, documentation, and construction will all be shortened and errors and
change orders will be drastically reduced.

This information has provided new insights to the construction industry and dispelled and
strengthened preconceived ideas. We have only gained a small grasp on this complex industry
and there is still much to learn, but these early insights provide an interesting and logical lens
into the mechanisms that could help reduce costs in the energy retrofit marketplace.
Recommended follow-on activities include additional investigation to gain deeper insights to
some of the key cost challenges, re-evaluation of the solutions, and further development of the
high impact solutions including action plans for carrying out these solutions. Building
partnerships with key stakeholders and industry leaders will be necessary to be successful in any
activities.
