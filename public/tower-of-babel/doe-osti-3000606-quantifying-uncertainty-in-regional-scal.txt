ABSTRACT
We examine the ability of three different inversion methods: 1) first motion (FM) inversions, 2)
amplitude inversions, and 3) full waveform (FW) time variable moment tensor (TVMT) inversions,
to recover an accurate source mechanism for simulated data for an earthquake as well as an
explosion. The ability of inversion methods, such as the ones described above, to recover accurate
models representative of the data depends on both a priori information as well as the quality of data
being inverted. Therefore, we examine the effect that station geometry, geologic model, and noise
level has on the inversion and estimated source mechanism. We find that FM data can provide more
robust solutions than amplitude data and are not as sensitive to inaccurate earth models, especially
in low-noise cases, and overall FW inversions are the most accurate out of the methods examined,
but can still be biased by inaccurate earth models.

1. INTRODUCTION

Accurate and efficient characterization of seismic sources is an ongoing area of research within
source discriminant studies. There has often been ambiguity in classifying events and separating
out whether the recorded data at a station was due to an earthquake or an explosion sources without
a priori information (e.g. Taylor et al., 1989). Many different methods have been proposed to
discriminate between event types, notably calculating phase ratios such as between P, S, and Lg
phases (Kim et al., 1993; Walter et al., 1995; Fisk, 2006; Allmann et al., 2008; Baruah et al.,
2016), Ms/Mb (Taylor et al., 1989), inverting the first motions and/or amplitudes of a source for the
radiation pattern of a source (e.g. Knopoff & Gilbert, 1960) or inverting seismic data for a moment
tensor representation of the source (e.g., Stump and Johnson, 1977).

Calculating the ratio between phases can aid in discriminating between earthquakes and explo-
sions, but the overlap for these ratios is considerable (Walter et al., 1995), so earthquakes could
be mischaracterized as explosions and vice versa based on ratio alone. Determining source type
based on the radiation pattern or moment tensor can provide a better estimate of source mechanism.
A moment tensor representation of a source generally uses time-invariant moment tensors, which
represent the source as a single source mechanism for the entirety of the energy output into the
Earth. However, time-variable moment tensor (TVMT) inversions that allow independent parame-
ters through time can capture the time evolution of a seismic source (e.g. Poppeliers et al., 2018;
Poppeliers and Preston, 2020, 2021, 2022; Berg and Poppeliers, 2022) and provide an estimate
of the confidence of a single source interpretation depending on how the recovered moment rate
function changes through time.

All of these estimates and inversions often require some a priori information, such as an approxima-
tion of the geologic subsurface of the area, and can return inaccurate results if the assumptions made
in the inversion are inaccurate. Therefore, the uncertainty associated with each of these methods
needs to be understood and quantified to be able to use these methods with confidence. Within
this study, we look at the effect that station coverage, noise level, and knowledge of the geologic
model has on inversion results for three different inversions: 1) first motions (FM) inversions, 2)
amplitude inversions, and 3) full-waveform TVMT inversions. We apply these inversions to data
simulated using three different geologic models to quantify the uncertainty each of these has on the
final inversion result.

2.2. Uncertainty Quantification

To evaluate the uncertainty and sensitivity of moment tensors to various factors, we perform a
series of simulations and determine solutions over combinations of factors including the type of
data used to determine the focal mechanisms, the earth model used to create the “observed” data,
the earth model used to generate the Green’s functions, the source type (earthquake vs. isotropic),
the noise level, the azimuthal coverage of seismic stations, and the number of stations. The various
parameters used for each factor are provided in Table 2-1. We focus on the region shown in Figure
2-1, which is roughly centered on Utah and extends from 35.0° to 46.8° latitude and -116° to
-109° longitude. We generate synthetic “observed” data from three different Earth models: a 3-D
heterogenous model based on the Geologic Framework Model of the Western US (Boyd, 2019)
with stochastic perturbations added (“het”), a 1-D model based on that model (“layered”), and
1-D ak135 (Kennett et al., 1995) (Figures 2-2 and 2-3). In all cases, we use the “layered’ model
for Green’s functions as this model is the simplest out of all model choices and so describes our
‘baseline’ case from which we will estimate errors resulting from other models. From Table 2-1,
there are 72 cases per “observed” data Earth model for all three data types.
The first-motion (FM) and amplitude data (amps) use a grid search method to find solutions with
10° bins in strike, dip, and rake and isotropic/DC ratios of -1.0, -0.7, -0.3, 0, 0.3, 0.7, and 1.0 with
negative isotropic parts being implosive sources and positive being explosive types (Figure 2-5).
While all true sources used in this work are stationary and do not vary in time, we use the full
waveform (FW) data use the TVMT inversion methodology describe in Section 2.1 of this report
for all six unique components of the time-variable moment tensor (Figure 2-6) to test the ability of
the TVMT inversion to accurately and consistently resolve single source types. Cases describe the
combinations of properties (Table 2-1) that is used to generate a certain number of realizations or
inversion runs. For each of the 216 cases, which are permutations of varying factors for each earth
model, 100 realizations are performed for the amps and FM data types, while for the FW cases, only
10 realizations are completed per case. Each realization randomly selects one station within each
azimuth and distance bin (Figure 2-4). In all cases there are 5 azimuthal bins, each spanning 72° of

2.3. Parelasti Improvements

We have been making improvements to the core Parelasti code over the past several years to
improve execution speed, increase its flexibility, and ease its maintenance. We have already
documented some of these changes including upgrading to a parallel NetCDF file framework,
which greatly increases reading and writing speeds, which were a major bottleneck in execution
time (Preston, 2024). Recently, we have been focusing on internal changes that allow more code
reuse, modernizing the C++ base to take advantage of increased performance and stability in new
language features, and more easily allow new features and physics to be added in the future. Several
of these tasks are ongoing and will be continuing in future years.

As part of our efforts to improve flexibility and maintainability, we redesigned the receiver interpo-
lation so that it allows selection of the order-of-accuracy for the interpolation, is topography-aware,
and makes it more generalizable to different receiver types. First-order accurate interpolation gen-
erally only uses the eight nodes surrounding the receiver location to interpolate from, which makes
it localized, but low accuracy. Higher order interpolation is more accurate but requires a greater
reach of the interpolation operator, making it more non-local. If the receivers were all located far
from strong contrasts, this would not be an issue, but since most receivers are located near the
surface, where a strong contrast exists in material properties, an interpolation operator could reach
across the boundary and use values inappropriate for the receiver type, e.g., a seismometer using
nodes in the air to interpolate a value, which will degrade the accuracy of the interpolated value.

 19

To mitigate this, we design our interpolator operators to be topography aware. To accomplish this,
the ideal operator box is first determined based on the desired order of accuracy with a centered
interpolator operator. If this crosses a high contrast interface, it first tries to move the operator box
so that all nodes of the operator are within the same side of the interface, making the interpolation
non-centered. If it is unable to find such a box due to highs and lows in the topography, it reduces
the accuracy order, and attempts to find a box that has all nodes on the same interface. It keeps
moving the interpolator box first then reducing the order until such as box is found, providing the
user diagnostic output if the order had to be reduced. If it cannot find even a first-order accurate
box that fits requirements, it exits with an error.
Our changes to make the code more generalizable to new receiver types helped make the addition
of the gradiometer receiver type quite fast. In this case, we simply use a differentiator operator
for one of the dimensions instead of interpolators in all three dimensions. For example, for an x-
derivative, we use differential operators in the x-dimension and interpolator operators in the y- and
z-dimensions. Besides this change, we get the same topography aware operator box as traditional
seismic receivers.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Uncertainty Quantification

As described in Section 2.2, we performed multiple realizations in each of the 72 cases, for each
of 3 input data types, for each of 3 "observed" Earth models, for a total of 648 cases. For FM
and amplitude data types, we made 100 realizations in each case, while for full waveform data, 10

For FM and amps data types, since these are grid searches, the plots show the fractional number of
realizations that have a solution within the given grid search cell. Additionally, because we have
access to the data variance at every point in the grid, we compute F-test statistics at each point and
include all solutions that are equivalent to the minimum variance solution within 95% confidence.
Although we have a 4-D grid comprised of strike, dip, rake, and isotropic fraction, in this report we
are primarily concerned with the fractional isotropic component; thus, we only show the marginal
distribution in this latter variable on the lune plots (Figure 3-1). These figures show the fraction of
all viable solutions that fall within each isotropic fraction bin (-1, -0.7, -0.3, 0, 0.3, 0.7, 1) for the no
noise, az gap case described in Section 2.2 using the ak135, 3-D heterogenous, and layered Earth
models. The full waveform data type uses a deterministic inversion as described before, so we
simply plot the solution of each of the 10 realizations on the lune. Figure 3-2 shows two examples
of full waveform inversion summary results.

for FM and amps, we can evaluate what fraction of realizations in each case has the true solution
within its 95% confidence interval. We evaluate two end-member cases: one where the true source
is a pure double-couple (DC) and a second where the true source is an explosion. The pure DC

data; the respective plots for the pure explosion source are displayed in Figure 3-5 and 3-5. All
of the Earth models for both source types show the same basic pattern versus number of stations

contain the true model more often than the noisy cases and remain nearly constant or worsening
somewhat as the number of stations increases. Explosion sources for all three models contain the
true solution 100% of the time. Only the layered model, the only case where the Green’s functions
exactly match the Earth model, contains the true model 100% of the time for the pure DC source.

4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

In this work we examined three different ways to characterize source mechanisms and the effect of
Earth model inaccuracies, noise level, and station geometry on the recovered source mechanism.
Error statistics and patterns of statistics can behave quite differently depending on how accurate
the Earth model used is and whether noise is present in the data; thus, errors in both must be
investigated together to fully quantify the effect of both Earth model accuracy and noise level on
source mechanism estimation. Earth model errors can cause biases in moment tensor results that
basic statistical tests like the 95% confidence F-test used here will not identify, but a 1-D model
based on a 3-D can help mitigate biases. Overall, these results show that FM data can give more
stable solutions and are not as sensitive to earth model error as amplitude data, but only when the
first motions are not corrupted by noise. Generally, noise further complicates and increases scatter
in results, reducing the the likelihood that any methods can correctly characterize a given source
mechanism. Additionally, errors due to noise are non-Gaussian; thus, standard F-test confidence
intervals are inaccurate especially when realistic noise contaminates observations. When varying
station geometry, selecting only five stations results in much different statistics than when using
10 or higher, especially with earth model errors. Inverting data from ≥10 stations only subtly
improves accuracy, error variance, and confidence intervals and can actually worsen results in
some cases. Finally, we find that FW inversions provide more accurate, higher confidence results
than amplitudes or FMs, but still can be biased by inaccurate earth models.
