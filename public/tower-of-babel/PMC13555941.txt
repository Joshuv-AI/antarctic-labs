The impact of social-emotional learning interventions on teacher well-being: a meta-analysis
Authors: Diya Dou, Yahan Chen, Xiaoqin Zhu, Xue Wu, Louisa S M Lei, Jieping Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost previous meta-analytic reviews of social-emotional learning (SEL) have primarily focused on assessing the effects of SEL on students, often overlooking the pivotal role of teachers in these programs. Teachers are the driving force behind the implementation of SEL programs, and their well-being is significantly impacted by these programs, which in turn is essential for promoting student development.MethodsTo address this gap, the current study adopts a meta-analytical approach to examine the impact of universal school-based SEL programs on teacher well-being and identify factors that may influence the effectiveness of SEL. A dual-pathway model is employed to distinguish effects on positive versus negative well-being outcomes.ResultsThe final sample consisted of 18 studies involving 3,465 teachers and indicated a significant positive effect on overall well-being (g = 0.257). Specifically, SEL programs exhibited marked improvements in teachers' positive well-being (g = 0.313) and significant alleviation of negative well-being (g = 0.149). Three-level meta-regression analyses identified research design, additional coaching, and mindfulness as significant moderators influencing SEL effectiveness, whereas duration and dosage were not found to be significant factors.ConclusionThe integration of supplementary coaching for educators into SEL programs has been demonstrated to result in a substantial enhancement of teachers’ positive well-being. Concurrently, the incorporation of mindfulness practices has been shown to engender a significant reduction in teachers’ negative well-being. The distinction between positive and negative pathways offers insights into enhancing teacher well-being through the implementation of SEL programs, with a focus on the selection of appropriate strategies that are tailored to the needs of teachers. Limitations, practical implications, and future directions are finally discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05053-9.Keywords: Social-emotional learning, Teacher well-being, Meta-analysis, Mindfulness

Introduction

Teacher well-being plays a vital role in both school improvement and educational reforms. Research has consistently demonstrated a positive correlation between teachers’ work-related well-being and students’ well-being and development [19, 55]. Conversely, low teacher well-being can result in higher rates of teacher absenteeism [63, 93], which ultimately compromises the overall quality of schooling [60]. Therefore, educators must prioritize the effectiveness of interventions aimed at enhancing teacher well-being to enhance the appeal of the teaching profession and elevate the quality of teaching and learning [93]. In recent decades, a plethora of teacher interventions have been implemented with the objective of enhancing well-being, including but not limited to, knowledge-based programs, mindfulness-based programs, skill development programs, and cognitive-behavioral programs [23]. Among these various interventions that contribute to teacher well-being, teacher social emotional learning (SEL) has emerged as a promising approach [22, 53]. Recent research has also begun to underscore the importance of considering teachers within the broader SEL framework. While SEL interventions are primarily designed for students, there is increasing recognition that teachers’ engagement with SEL practices is crucial not only for their own well-being but also for the overall school climate and student psychological development [42, 103]. SEL equips teachers with essential personal competencies and situational supports that can empower them to thrive in their roles. Previous research shows that SEL practices are closely tied to teacher’s professional experiences and classroom quality, indicating that teacher’s role in promoting student outcomes is more than merely instrumental [83]. Furthermore, teachers’ personal emotional competencies play a pivotal role in fostering inclusive classroom environments and maintaining their own well-being [11]. Collectively, these studies underscore the necessity for a meta-analytic synthesis that directly examines the impact of SEL interventions on teacher well-being, an area that has been less extensively examined compared to the substantial body of literature focused on students.

Previous reviews on SEL programs have primarily focused their effects on students rather than on teachers. Over a dozen meta-analytic reviews have consistently found that school-based SEL programs significantly improve students’ social-emotional skills, self-perception, attitudes toward others, prosocial behaviors, and academic achievements. Concurrently, these programs effectively mitigated conduct disorders, emotional distress, and substance abuse among students [28, 58]. Despite the wealth of research on SEL’s effect on students, there is, to the best of our knowledge, only one existing study that has examined the impact of SEL on teachers. The study synthesized 13 SEL studies to investigate the impact of SEL on burnout in teachers. The findings revealed that SEL significantly improved teachers’ personal accomplishments and reduced their emotional exhaustion but had no significant effect on depersonalization [61]. However, the study was limited by the low quality of the included studies (e.g., sample sizes below 30) and insignificant moderators that failed to explain the heterogeneity of teacher outcomes. To address this research gap, the current study applies a set of stringent inclusion criteria (e.g., sufficient sample sizes, baseline equivalence) to examine the effects of SEL interventions on teacher well-being. Additionally, it investigates potential moderating factors (e.g., research design, duration, and dosage) that may influence the impact of SEL on teachers.

In-service teacher well-being: definitions and interventions

The definition of teacher well-being varies across research fields [35]. Previous reviews have identified multiple research areas that conceptualize teacher well-being in different ways, including but not limited to well-being psychology [25], positive psychology [72], organizational psychology [5], occupational well-being [92], and health [69]. The lack of systematic usage of the term “teacher well-being” across these fields may hinder the establishment of a robust theoretical foundation for research on teacher well-being, which can lead to the perception of teacher well-being as an arbitrary set of teacher-related variables [35]. However, it is worth acknowledging that previous research has demonstrated that positive and negative experiences do not exist within a single dimension but can co-exist separately [97]. Recent studies have used two-dimensional indicators to measure teacher well-being in schools by focusing on positive and negative states (e.g., [7, 16, 47, 63, 78, 82, 95, 101]). Positive psychological states include dedication, job satisfaction, and engagement, while negative psychological states encompass burnout, emotional exhaustion, depression, psychosomatic reactions, and turnover intentions. A recent meta-analysis of 393 studies on various psychological interventions has revealed that therapies aimed at enhancing positive psychological resources tended to have larger effect size estimates than those focused on reducing and controlling negative mental states [1*]. Therefore, it is necessary to differentiate between positive and negative states of well-being in this study.

In addition, in-service teacher well-being should be conceptualized as a complex construct that has both personal and professional dimensions. From a school psychology perspective, teacher well-being is not only an individual matter but also a systemic issue influenced by school climate, leadership, and peer collaboration. It is crucial for this construct to include not only transient emotional states but also the broader scope of teachers’ work-related well-being. In-service teachers engage in constant interactions with individuals, and those interactions give rise to frequent emotional experiences and cognitive assessments of their work. These interactions, particularly student–teacher relationships, are central to the well-being of educators, with research indicating that positive classroom relationships contribute to higher teacher emotions and reduced anxiety [33]. Thus, teacher well-being is influenced by events within the school environment and is contingent upon the external context [52]. These factors are closely intertwined with the daily work context and job performance.

Therefore, this study adopts a theoretical perspective that acknowledges the distinction between positive and negative states and encompasses both teachers’ personal emotional perceptions and the positive and negative distinctions of emotions and perceptions constructed within the school context. Positive well-being refers to the range of positive emotions that teachers experience, such as joy, happiness, and life satisfaction. It also includes the teachers’ ability to use sufficient SEL competencies or other supportive resources within a high-pressure professional environment to enable them to feel satisfied and fulfilled while effectively coping with the demands of daily schooling. In contrast, negative experiences of well-being among teachers pertain to feelings of sadness and anger as well as a lack of appropriate SEL competencies in high-pressure environments or access to adequate supportive resources, which results in experiences of workaholism and burnout in response to daily teaching demands. Given the increasing focus on school-wide social-emotional frameworks, interventions that foster SEL competencies in teachers have gained traction as a means to improve overall school well-being.

Interventions aimed at enhancing positive states of teacher well-being differ from those designed to reduce their negative states. Research has identified three primary categories of interventions that enhance teachers’ positive well-being [23]. The first category centers on positive psychology-related concepts, such as self-compassion and mindfulness. The second category focuses on skill and competency development programs for teachers, including classroom management, voice techniques, and emotional competence. The third category concentrates on providing knowledge and techniques for stress relief, such as inquiry-based stress reduction and psychological counseling. Interventions addressing negative well-being among teachers can be categorized as knowledge-based, behavioral, cognitive-behavioral, or mindfulness-based programs. Knowledge-based interventions provide information to teachers through, for example, lectures on teacher education and student problem behaviors. In behavioral interventions, teachers practice specific skills to reduce negative experiences. Such skills include meditation and relaxation techniques [3, 45], gratitude journaling exercises [15], and engagement in dyadic consultation on student behavioral problems [65]. Cognitive-behavioral interventions combine cognitive training with strategic behavioral practices to equip teachers with the necessary knowledge and skills to handle negative work-related experiences. These interventions often rely on particular treatment protocols outlined in manuals [36, 96]. Finally, mindfulness interventions build on cognitive-behavioral interventions by emphasizing the process of experiencing and thinking rather than focusing solely on the specific content of thoughts [66]. Based on existing studies, the effects of interventions for different states of well-being remain unclear for teachers. More research should be conducted to determine which interventions work most effectively [61].

The evolution of SEL interventions for teachers

SEL refers to the acquisition and use of essential abilities across five domains: self-awareness and self-management, also known as intrapersonal social emotional competence; social awareness and relationship skills, also known as interpersonal social-emotional competence; and responsible decision-making [27, 88]. SEL has been widely recognized as an essential component of effective schooling and student development [6, 28]. While most existing literature has examined SEL in relation to students [21] and its important contributions to student achievement and instructional quality, researchers are increasingly acknowledging the importance of SEL for teachers as well. The target for SEL was updated in 2013 to encompass the entire school community, including both students and teachers [12].

Within the field of education, there is a consensus that SEL serves as an effective intervention for well-being [28, 73, 74, 86]. There is an increasingly prevalent trend of schools and programs adopting “whole school” approaches to address social-emotional interventions in a systematic, collaborative, and coordinated manner [32, 43, 56, 59]. As a result, teachers have shifted from mere facilitators of SEL interventions to the focus of the interventions [70]. In recent years, interventions directly targeting teachers through SEL programs have emerged [51, 71]. However, since most previous interventions have primarily focused on student advancement, teachers evidently lack the necessary social and emotional competence to effectively enhance their subjective well-being and cope with the various well-being challenges they may face in their personal and professional lives [35, 61, 93]. This lack of competence poses a challenge in terms of enhancing teacher acceptance of interventions and increasing their effectiveness. Therefore, it is important to assess and understand the impact of teacher SEL interventions to improve SEL training for teachers. Accordingly, the first research question (RQ1) of this study is, “What are the overall effects of SEL programs on teachers’ well-being?”.

Previously studied covariates of teacher SEL

Despite the urge to integrate teacher well-being with SEL programs, these two focal areas do not perfectly overlap. The only systematic review to date that has examined the impacts of SEL on teachers revealed a positive effect on the alleviation of teacher burnout [61]. However, that review had several limitations. First, some studies included in the meta-analysis had a limited number of participants, for example, the sample size of Flook et al. [29] was only 18. This limitation may have introduced bias into their findings. Additionally, the moderators did not adequately explain the heterogeneity of the included studies, which limited the suggestive implications for educational practice. Nevertheless, some potential features may be relevant for the effectiveness of SEL interventions. Many previous reviews of SEL interventions and other educational interventions have examined the impacts of common methodological and implementation factors, such as research design, sample size, grade, country, duration, and dosage [61]. Based on their findings, a set of features can be identified to explain the variance in teacher well-being produced in different studies.

Additional coaching for teachers may be a factor that serves to moderate the effects of SEL programs. The implementation of coaching as a strategy to augment teacher social emotional training has the potential to enhance their pedagogical practices and facilitate professional growth [73, 74]. For instance, Roberts et al. [67*] examined the impact of a SEL program with different components and found that teachers in the groups that received additional coaching exhibited substantially higher levels of self-efficacy and experienced reduced levels of burnout and stress compared to teachers in the groups that received only the course. As for the inclusion of mindfulness behaviors or elements in interventions examined in this study, it is noteworthy that mindfulness has been mentioned in interventions addressing both positive and negative emotional states. Previous reviews have identified positive effects of mindfulness meditation in addressing threats to teacher well-being [38, 76, 78]. However, these reviews have not compared findings across positive and negative well-being states, which is an area of focus in the present study. Therefore, the covariates in this study encompass the aggregation of methodological characteristics, implementation factors, and intervention features. By drawing from existing studies examining methodological and implementation features (e.g., research design, duration, and dosage) as well as the intervention features (e.g., coaching and mindfulness), the present study addresses a second research question (RQ2): “Which features are related to the effectiveness of these SEL programs?” To answer this question, the study explores factors that may significantly influence the effectiveness of SEL among the aforementioned covariates.

In summary, there is a general consensus that SEL is significantly effective in enhancing teacher well-being. Nevertheless, the field of teacher SEL is still in its infancy. The present study aims to complement extant research in several respects. Firstly, the majority of extant meta-analytical reviews of SEL programs have focused on the impact of SEL on students, and only one meta-analytic review has examined the influence of SEL programs on teachers’ burnout. The present study is the first one to examine the impact of SEL interventions on teachers’ well-being through a meta-analysis. Secondly, extant reviews have failed to provide a comprehensive comparison of the specific moderating effects of different states of teacher well-being (i.e., positive versus negative well-being). Consequently, it is recommended to undertake a more comprehensive investigation into the intervention effects on positive well-being states in comparison to negative well-being states. Thirdly, the present study examines the moderating effect of specific methodological, implementation, and intervention features on the effectiveness of SEL interventions and their different moderating effects on teachers’ different well-being states. By addressing these gaps, this study endeavors to update and enrich the findings of previous reviews related to teacher SEL and teacher well-being.

Methods

This study strictly complied with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PRISMA; [62] throughout all stages of research design and execution. The meta-analytic process comprised four methodologically robust phases: a) executing comprehensive literature searches through multi-channel literature retrieval, b) dual-independent screening of the identified records based on stringent inclusion criteria, c) extracting and coding data on study characteristics and outcomes, and d) performing statistical analyses.

Search procedures

The last author independently conducted a systematic literature search to identify all empirical studies examining the impact of SEL interventions on teacher well-being. The search strategy incorporated three complementary approaches: a) Database searching: Web of Science All Collections, ProQuest All Databases, ERIC, and PsycINFO were systematically queried using the search string with Boolean operators and truncation applied to capture variant spellings and derivatives in the abstract. The search strings included the following terms: (“social emotional learning” OR “SEL” OR “social emotional skill” OR “social emotional competence” OR “social skill” OR “emotional skill” OR “emotional intelligence”) AND (“teacher*” OR “educator*”) AND (“experiment” OR “program” OR “intervention” OR “trial” OR “project” OR “treatment”); b) Backward citation tracking: manual screening of reference lists from eligible meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and seminal articles in SEL research; and c) Additional literature retrieval: A comprehensive search of the official websites of leading SEL programs (e.g., Second Step, Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies [PATHS], and RULER) and Google Scholar was conducted to ascertain if additional studies were available. All searches were conducted up to November 5, 2025. Database searching yielded 2,973 records, while manual reference tracking and grey literature retrieval identified additional 13 studies, resulting in a total of 2,986 records. Duplicate removal was performed using EndNote software, reducing the dataset to 2,693 records for eligibility assessment.

The systematic literature screening process was rigorously conducted in two phases. From an initial pool of 2,693 unique records identified through database searches, 2,612 studies were excluded during the title and abstract screening phase based on predetermined eligibility criteria. To maintain methodological consistency, the exclusion decisions at this stage mainly centered on non-experimental designs (e.g., qualitative studies, theoretical papers), studies involving non-teacher populations, and research lacking quantifiable outcome measures. The remaining 81 studies were then subjected to full-text evaluation. In the full-text screening phase, 63 studies were excluded for the following methodological reasons: lack of control groups, baseline differences exceeding 0.25 standard deviations, and insufficient sample sizes as defined by our analysis thresholds. This rigorous selection process ultimately identified 18 studies that fully met all inclusion criteria for meta-analytic synthesis.

Four authors (the first, second, third, and last authors) participated in the screening process independently. Prior to the screening, they had reached a consensus on the inclusion criteria through discussions to ensure consistency among them. To guarantee reliability, all raters screened each record independently based on the inclusion criteria, and the inter-rater agreement exceeded 95% with a Kappa value of 0.85. Any disagreements were resolved through iterative discussions between the first and last authors until complete consensus was attained. Coding

After the rigorous literature screening process, a total of 18 studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria were identified for coding. The last author developed a comprehensive coding sheet to systematically collect relevant information, including research design, sample size, characteristics of the sample (e.g., age, gender, and teaching experience), intervention duration, the country where the study was conducted, detailed experimental intervention content (e.g., the specific activities and techniques used in the SEL program), and outcomes assessing the effects on teacher well-being (e.g., the magnitude and significance of the effects).

Prior to coding, the first author and the last author conducted a thorough and in-depth discussion to establish a clear and consistent approach for coding each item on the coding sheet. After consensus was achieved, they independently coded the 18 studies to enhance the reliability of the coding process and minimize potential biases. Discrepancies in the coding results were resolved through further discussion by the two authors, ensuring alignment with the coding guidelines. The following research features were extracted and categorized as moderators: (1) research design (quasi-experimental or randomized controlled trial), (2) duration (less or more than half a year), (3) dosage (hours per week, continuous), (4) presence of additional coaching, and (5) presence of mindfulness practices. In addition, based on the findings of previous reviews on teacher well-being, outcomes were classified into positive (e.g., vigor, self-efficacy, personal accomplishment) and negative well-being states (e.g., stress, anxiety, emotional exhaustion). This coding and categorization process enhances the depth of data analysis and supports the rigor of subsequent meta-analytic research.

Statistical analysis

In this study, the effect sizes represent the impacts of SEL interventions on teacher well-being outcomes in the included studies. The effect size in each included study was calculated using the standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d), which quantifies the difference between the mean outcomes of the treatment and control groups, standardized by the pooled standard deviation. The statistical data (e.g., means, standard deviations, t-values, or F-values) from each eligible study were extracted to calculate Cohen’s d using the practical meta-analysis effect size calculator [100]. The final effect size value of Cohen’s d for each outcome of all included studies was equivalent to the posttest Cohen’s d minus the pretest Cohen’s d. For studies that employed clustered randomization (e.g., randomizing schools rather than individual teachers), adjustments for clustering were applied [37], following the methodology adapted from the What Works Clearinghouse [99]. The statistical analysis of the body of included studies utilized Hedge’s g to mitigate the influence of sample size and weighted the effect sizes for each study based on the inverse variance, as described in the methodology outlined by Borenstein et al. [8]. A random-effects model was used to estimate the overall effect size, rather than a fixed-effects model, based on the assumption that the included studies were heterogeneous and did not originate from a common population [8]. Three-level meta-regression models were conducted for positive well-being and negative-well-being to examine the potential moderating effects of methodological variables and implementation characteristics. In cases where studies reported multiple effect sizes for different outcomes, the robust variance estimation approach was utilized to account for correlations among effect sizes [87]. All analyses were conducted in R [64] using the metafor package [94]. To ensure consistent interpretation, all scores were harmonized so that positive scores indicate superior performance in the treatment group compared to the control group irrespective of the specific outcome domains.

Results

Descriptive information of included studies

This systematic review encompassed 18 studies rigorously selected based on inclusion criteria to evaluate the impact of SEL interventions on teacher well-being. Detailed information about each included study and descriptive statistics of categorical distributions are shown in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. These studies examined both positive well-being outcomes (e.g., emotional resilience, self-efficacy, job satisfaction; n = 16, 88.9%) and negative well-being outcomes (e.g., burnout, stress, anxiety; n = 15, 83.3%), with thirteen studies (72.2%) assessing both types of outcomes. Methodologically, the majority were randomized controlled trials (n = 11, 61.1%), including five cluster-randomized designs to accommodate school-level characteristics (e.g., [14, 50*]). The remaining seven studies (38.9%) employed quasi-experimental designs (e.g., [13*, 30*]), often utilizing waitlist controls to enhance feasibility in naturalistic educational settings. This methodological distribution reflects the fact that, although the evidence is stronger for randomized experimental studies, quasi-experimental studies are often employed in real-world educational research.

Total Studies

The participant pool included 3,465 teachers (1,803 in treatment groups and 1,662 in control groups) with a diverse range of career stages and educational contexts (from preschools to secondary schools). Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from small-scale pilots (n = 36; [30*]) to large-scale implementations (n = 708, [89*]). Among these studies, half (N = 9, 50%) were classified as small samples (< 100 participants), and the remaining half (n = 9) were classified as large samples. A notable gender skew towards female teachers was observed in most studies. Most studies (n = 13) had 70% or more female teachers (e.g., [13*, 24*]), only one study [89*] had 76% male teachers. The gender disparity potentially reflects the prevalence of female teachers in prekindergarten through high school level schools. Participant demographics demonstrated diverse teaching experience, capturing perspectives from early-career teachers, with an average of 7 years of experience [85*], to veteran educators with 29 years of experience [30*]. Additionally, the age of participating teachers also varied, with an average age ranging from 34 years [85*] to 47 years [68*]. There was limited reporting of educational attainment across the studies. In one instance, Conroy et al. [24*] noted that 65% of participants had attained a bachelor’s degree.

Geographically, 55.6% of studies (n = 10) originated in the United States, predominantly targeting teachers in K-12 systems (e.g., [2, 26*]). The remaining 44.4% (n = 8) of studies spanned 14 countries across six continents, including European (Spain, Italy, Latvia, Romania, Portugal, Croatia, Greece, Finland, UK), North American (Canada), South American (Ecuador), African (Democratic Republic of the Congo), Middle Eastern (Israel), and Oceanian (Australia) contexts. In particular, Cavioni et al. [14*] involved teachers from kindergarten to upper secondary school in six countries, including Italy, Latvia, Romania, Portugal, Croatia, and Greece. This distribution highlights both the global interest in teacher well-being research and the persistent underrepresentation of low-income regions (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa outside the Congo study, Southeast Asia).

As for the intervention design and implementation, these SEL interventions demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in terms of program goal, theoretical underpinnings, duration, and implementation approaches. The vast majority of programs (n = 13, 72.2%) were exclusively designed for teachers, of which 9 were mindfulness-based interventions. These included adapted mindfulness training programs, such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) programs with weekly 2-h sessions to address teacher stress and improve well-being [30*], as well as hybrid models, such as the Cultivating Awareness and Resilience in Education (CARE) program, which combined integrated mindfulness practices, classroom management training, and SEL practices to cultivate teachers’ social-emotional skills and reduce teachers’ emotional stress [41*]. Non-mindfulness teacher-focused interventions used skill-building approaches, such as the Effective Classroom Interactions program, which aimed to improve teachers’ emotional support and classroom organization [67*], and the 4-day intensive Teacher Effectiveness Training program, which aimed to support teachers’ autonomy and self-responsibility [84*]. In contrast, 27.8% of interventions (n = 5) employed dual-focus models that targeted both teachers and students, and these interventions did not incorporate any components related to mindfulness. Notable examples included leading comprehensive SEL programs such as the RULER program [13*] and the PATHS program [26*], which included professional learning for staff and classroom instruction for students to build their social-emotional skills and create a supportive school climate.

Mindfulness components were operationalized diversely across 50% of studies (n = 9), ranging from structured daily 20-min practices [34*] to embedded elements within broader SEL frameworks. Implementation fidelity and dosage showed marked variability, with initial training durations ranging from 4 days [84*] to 10 days [89*], supplemented by heterogeneous ongoing support mechanisms such as weekly coaching [26*] and quarterly cluster meetings [89*]. With regard to supplementary coaching, seven studies (38.9%) provided teachers with additional one-to-one coaching beyond the scope of routine training, for example via telephone, while the remaining 11 studies did not include such supplementary coaching. Dosage, defined as the average weekly intervention hours, ranged from 0.3 to 4.5 h, with an average of 1.76 h per week and a median of 1.5 h per week. Furthermore, 61.1% of studies (n = 11) used medium-term interventions (half a year or less), while 38.9% (n = 7) went beyond half a year. This diversity in methodological approaches, participant demographics, and intervention designs highlights both the complexity of applying SEL interventions in educational settings and the need for more standardized implementation measures.

Overall effect of SEL programs on teacher well-being

A set of three-level random-effects models with robust variance estimation was conducted, assuming the correlations among effect sizes in the same study to be 0.8, in order to examine the overall effect size of SEL interventions. The meta-analytic synthesis demonstrated robust evidence for the beneficial impact of school-based SEL interventions on teachers’ overall well-being, with a significant and positive effect size of 0.257 (95% CI: [0.123, 0.392], p < 0.01) under a three-level meta-analytic random-effect model (see the forest plot, Fig. 2). In particular, the overall effects of SEL interventions were found to be 0.313 (95% CI: [0.161, 0.464], p < 0.001) on positive well-being and 0.149 (95% CI: [0.070, 0.229], p < 0.01) on negative well-being, indicating that SEL interventions significantly enhanced teacher positive well-being while concurrently mitigating negative well-being. The estimated variance components at study-level and effect size-level were 0.027 and 0.077, respectively. With regard to I2, 20.55% of the total variation was attributed to between-study heterogeneity, and 59.08% to within-study heterogeneity. The Q test results demonstrated that Q = 1181.167, p < 0.001, thereby indicating a significant heterogeneity. Consequently, the utilisation of moderators to elucidate the observed heterogeneity within the included studies is both reasonable and feasible.

Forest plot

As a sensitivity analysis, the Cook’s Distance values were calculated in order to identify any potential outliers and influential cases at both the study level and the effect size level. The analysis revealed that all Cook’s Distance values were less than 0.45, indicating the absence of outliers (Appendix Figure S1 and Figure S2). Moreover, results showed a large difference (0.313 vs. 0.149) in the effect sizes between positive and negative well-being, in line with previous research on well-being. Therefore, separate meta-regressions were conducted for positive and negative well-being in the analysis of the effects of the moderating variables. Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 18), the moderating variables included in the analyses comprised research design, duration, dosage, mindfulness, and additional coaching.

Duration

Based on the duration of the intervention implemented, each study was categorized as having a duration of more than half a year or half a year or less. The impact of SEL interventions lasting less than half a year did not demonstrate significant variation when compared to the impact of interventions lasting more than half a year, irrespective of positive or negative well-being (p > 0.05). Consequently, duration was found not to be a statistically significant moderator of the effect of SEL programs on teacher well-being.

Dosage

Dosage was coded as a continuous variable based on the number of hours teachers participated in the SEL programs each week. Given that an increased intervention dosage generally has the capacity to markedly enhance program effectiveness [75], it was incorporated within the meta-analysis models. However, the findings of the study indicated that for both positive and negative well-being, although dosage coefficients were positive, neither was statistically significant (p > 0.05). As a sensitivity suggestion, dosage was coded as a dichotomous variable with two levels (high or low based on the median), yet its moderating effect remained insignificant in the meta-regression models. The findings indicated that dosage did not have a substantial impact on the effectiveness of SEL in enhancing teacher well-being.

Mindfulness elements

Included studies were further categorized based on the inclusion of mindfulness practices in the SEL interventions. The findings indicated that mindfulness functioned as a significant moderator of the impact of SEL on teachers’ negative well-being. The SEL interventions incorporating mindfulness elements demonstrated a significant reduction in teachers’ negative well-being by 0.203 standard deviation in comparison to interventions devoid of mindfulness practices (p = 0.017). Nevertheless, SEL programmes incorporating mindfulness components did not demonstrate a more efficacious impact on enhancing positive well-being. These findings suggest that mindfulness elements in SEL interventions may be effective in reducing teachers’ negative well-being, but not in increasing their positive well-being.

Additional coaching

The eligible studies were then categorized based on the provision of supplementary one-on-one coaching to teachers by SEL programs. The findings indicated that SEL programs augmented by supplementary coaching exhibited a substantial enhancement in teachers’ positive well-being, with an increase of 0.332 standard deviation in comparison to SEL programs that did not incorporate additional coaching. However, no significant difference was observed in negative well-being between the two groups. Consequently, supplementary coaching functioned as a substantial moderating variable in enhancing teachers’ positive well-being, but not for their negative well-being.

Publication bias

The current meta-analytic review employed the selection model approach to assess the presence of publication bias. This approach is widely regarded as the most effective method for investigating publication bias [90], consistent with the methodology employed in prior meta-analyses to investigate publication bias (e.g., [102]). In the adjusted model with cut-points set to 0.025, the estimate of the true average effect size was 0.315 (95% CI: [0.263, 0.366]), indicating that our meta-analysis was not substantially biased by a lower selection probability of non-significant results. This finding was in line with the non-significant result of the likelihood ratio test of selection model parameters (χ2 = 0.206, p = 0.650), suggesting that there was no substantial publication bias. Additionally, a funnel plot was employed to intuitively exhibit the distribution of the effect sizes, indicating that they were roughly symmetrical (see Fig. 3). Altogether, the techniques collectively indicated that no severe publication bias influenced the findings of the current meta-analytic review.

Discussion

A comprehensive set of meta-analytic reviews has investigated the effects of SEL programs on students from preschool to high school. However, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding the influence of SEL programs on teachers. To the best of our knowledge, only one meta-analytic review has synthesized findings on the effectiveness of teacher-targeted SEL interventions for addressing teacher burnout. To fill this research gap, the current meta-analytic review explored the impact of SEL interventions on teacher well-being, including positive and negative well-being, and identified five moderators—research design, duration, dosage, mindfulness, and additional coaching—that might influence the effectiveness of SEL interventions. To obtain the most reliable evidence, the current review applied stringent inclusion criteria (e.g., initial equivalence, minimum of 30 participants) to meticulously select and synthesize 18 studies involving 3,465 teachers (1,803 in treatment groups, 1,662 in control groups). The results of this meta-analysis revealed that SEL programs have a substantial effect on teacher well-being, exhibiting an effect size of 0.257. This impact manifested in two distinct aspects, including an improvement in positive well-being of 0.313 standard deviation and a reduction in negative well-being of 0.149 standard deviation. Following Kraft’s [49] recommendations for interpreting the effects of education interventions, the effect sizes pertaining to teacher well-being were determined to be small to moderate in magnitude. Nevertheless, the small-to-medium effect sizes should not be overinterpreted as evidence that SEL alone can substantially transform teachers well-being. Furthermore, the impact of SEL programs on teachers’ positive well-being was found to be approximately twice that of negative well-being, suggesting that SEL programs were more effective in enhancing teachers’ positive well-being and might be ill-suited to addressing structural or occupational causes of teacher stress and burnout. These findings imply that SEL programs could be innovative and valuable tools for enhancing teachers’ positive well-being but would not largely alleviate negative well-being.

Several explanations may account for these findings. One possible explanation is that most SEL programs are grounded in positive psychology with a focus on improving personal, intrapersonal, and interpersonal skills, and they may not incorporate strategies for coping with negative emotional experiences. Additionally, teacher burnout is often rooted in organizational and policy conditions, such as heavy workloads, resource scarcity, and accountability pressures, rather than solely in individual psychological or cognitive capacities [54]. Consequently, individual-level SEL interventions may exhibit a ceiling effect in addressing burnout when organizational and policy support is absent. These findings suggest that teacher well-being could be enhanced through multi-level approaches that integrate individual capacity-building with systemic reforms addressing the structural conditions of teacher distress. In this sense, SEL is not a substitute for but a complement to school policies regarding workload distribution and institutional support.

To further understand the heterogeneity of effect sizes among the included studies, five methodological, implementation, and intervention features were identified and analyzed as potential moderators: research design, duration, dosage, mindfulness, and additional coaching. Meta-regression analyses were performed to explore the moderating effects of these features on teachers’ positive well-being and negative well-being, respectively. The results showed that research design and coaching were significant moderators for positive well-being, whereas research design and mindfulness were significant moderators for negative well-being. Specifically, for both positive and negative well-being, the effect sizes of randomized controlled trials were significantly lower than those of quasi-experimental studies. This finding was consistent with those of previous meta-analytic reviews reporting higher effects for quasiexperimental studies. As demonstrated in the study by Shi & Cheung [73, 74], the results of randomized controlled universal school-based SEL interventions suggested a decline in effectiveness in terms of student outcomes, specifically in the domains of social, emotional, and behavioral development, when compared to the outcomes observed in quasi-experimental studies. Cheung and Slavin [20] conducted an exhaustive investigation into the impact of methodological characteristics on the effectiveness of educational programs, revealing that the impact of randomized controlled studies was significantly less pronounced than that of quasi-experimental studies across a range of educational programs. The present study aligns with this pattern and underscores the critical role of randomization in mitigating selection bias and confounding. The elevated effect sizes frequently observed in quasi-experimental studies may be partly attributable to residual bias or the influence of unmeasured variables. Nevertheless, this does not invalidate quasi-experimental evidence. Such studies remain imperative for investigating research questions where randomization is impractical or unethical. These studies frequently offer valuable real-world evidence and can provide strong causal inference when designs are rigorous (e.g., using difference-in-differences or regression discontinuity). Therefore, it is imperative to interpret findings within the methodological context, acknowledging the distinctive strengths and limitations of each approach.

Regarding the two implementation characteristics of duration and dosage, neither exhibited a significant effect on the effectiveness of SEL in enhancing teacher well-being. SEL experiments with a duration of over six months did not yield significantly larger effect sizes in comparison to those with a duration of six months or less. The dosage of the intervention, measured in weekly hours, was found to have a non-significant impact on the effectiveness of the SEL program in terms of both positive and negative well-being. These findings are consistent with the conclusions of existing research. For instance, Oliveira et al. [61] examined the relationship between duration, dosage, and SEL effectiveness in reducing teacher burnout, revealing neither duration nor dosage to be significant predictors. These findings indicate that extending the duration of the program or augmenting weekly intervention hours does not intrinsically guarantee enhanced outcomes for educator well-being. A well-structured, focused, high-quality intervention can effectively achieve its core objectives even within a shorter timeframe or at moderate weekly dosage (e.g., the CARE program). It is important to note that beyond a certain threshold, additional time or sessions may not yield extra benefits if they lead to participant burnout, program deviation from core objectives, or dilution of key content. Conversely, a SEL program lacking a coherent structure and demonstrating inefficacy will not yield substantial results merely through the extension of its duration and dosage.

The provision of additional coaching for educators exerted a substantial moderating influence on the effectiveness of SEL programs in enhancing teachers’ positive well-being, while leaving their negative well-being largely unimpacted. This finding indicated a crucial distinction in teacher well-being interventions, that is, the promotion of well-being and the mitigation of negative well-being are two distinct psychological processes influenced by different factors. The implementation of supplementary coaching emerged as a strategic initiative aimed at enhancing teachers’ professional resources, including but not limited to improving teachers’ skills (e.g., classroom management, SEL teaching techniques), boosting their self-efficacy, and strengthening their professional autonomy. These improvements, in turn, would lead to a marked enhancement in the positive well-being of teachers. For instance, the Incredible Years Teacher Classroom Management program offered individualized coaching via email and classroom visits to support teachers [31*], while the CARE program provided one-on-one coaching via phone calls [40*]. However, the impact of coaching on negative well-being did not reach statistical significance. One potential explanation for this phenomenon is that, while SEL interventions provide educators with the tools to cultivate skills and practices that promote positive well-being, they do not address the underlying systemic and contextual factors contributing to elevated stress and burnout in the teaching profession [17]. This finding suggests that the mitigation of negative well-being may necessitate more targeted approaches that address specific stressors or coping mechanisms directly, extending beyond the scope of general SEL intervention.

Mindfulness was also identified as a significant moderator. Specifically, mindfulness significantly moderated the effectiveness of SEL interventions in reducing teachers’ negative well-being but not in improving positive well-being. SEL interventions incorporating mindfulness elements or practices were found to effectively diminish teachers’ negative well-being compared to those without. Mindfulness can complement positive psychological interventions by specifically targeting and alleviating stress and negative well-being. For instance, Singh et al. [77] found that a mindfulness-based positive behavior support program had the most positive effects in reducing mothers’ perceived psychological stress and improving the behavioral outcomes of their children with autism spectrum disorder, compared to mindfulness training or skills-based training alone. Moreover, these improvements were observed to persist for three years following the intervention. These findings underscore the potential benefits of integrating mindfulness practices into SEL interventions as a more effective approach to alleviating teachers’ negative well-being without necessarily impacting their positive well-being. In sum, the findings of the present study present a compelling and nuanced picture, demonstrating that teacher coaching significantly enhances the effectiveness of SEL programs in promoting teachers’ positive well-being, while mindfulness training significantly enhances their effectiveness in reducing teachers’ negative well-being. This substantial discrepancy in moderating effects provides valuable theoretical and practical insights, which are essential for a comprehensive understanding of the impact of SEL programs on teachers.

The current study contributes significant insights into the impact of SEL programs on teachers’ well-being. First, while prior meta-analytic research has extensively established the impact of SEL interventions on students [28, 61], the present study supported the positive contribution of SEL programs to teachers’ well-being by shifting the focus from students to teachers. In line with existing literature, the present study differentiated well-being into positive and negative states to further analyze the specific impact of SEL on teachers [16, 79, 82, 95, 101]. The findings indicate that SEL interventions had statistically significant effects on teacher’s well-being across both positive and negative dimensions. This suggests that SEL can serve as one promising approach for supporting teacher well-being.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analytic review to systematically synthesize the impact of SEL interventions on teacher well-being across both its positive and negative dimensions. Additionally, the present study underscored the pivotal function of additional coaching and mindfulness in SEL interventions, acknowledging that their moderating effects may be different for different emotional states (i.e., positive versus negative well-being; [1, 91]). In this regard, the findings indicate that SEL interventions incorporating additional coaching yielded a significantly higher effect in improving teachers’ positive well-being (e.g., personal accomplishment) compared to those without coaching, but not in reducing teachers’ negative well-being. SEL interventions incorporating mindfulness effectively mitigated teachers’ negative well-being (e.g., emotional exhaustion) but did not significantly enhance their positive well-being. Therefore, given the potential for disparate approaches to enhancing positive and negative well-being, it is imperative to consider the implementation of two distinct strategies. The first strategy involves the provision of supplementary and customized coaching for educators aimed at fostering positive well-being. The second strategy entails the incorporation of mindfulness practices for educators, with the objective of alleviating negative well-being. In order to optimize the effectiveness of SEL interventions designed to enhance teacher well-being, it is imperative to consider and augment both dimensions of well-being, as opposed to targeting a single dimension in isolation [48]. This underscores the importance of considering teachers’ actual needs in SEL design and raises the question of how to effectively incorporate additional coaching and mindfulness exercises into SEL interventions to enhance both positive and negative well-being for teachers. School principals are advised to adopt a dual‑pathway strategy by allocating resources to coaching for enhancing positive teacher well‑being and to mindfulness training and broader organizational efforts for reducing negative well‑being, each tailored to the respective outcome target.

Despite these contributions, the current study has some limitations to acknowledge. First, the relatively small sample size of eligible studies constrains the generalizability of the findings. This limitation is partly due to SEL experimental studies focusing predominantly on students and rarely examining the effects of SEL on teachers. In a similar vein, a prior review that examined the impact of SEL on teacher burnout only identified a mere 13 eligible studies [61]. Nevertheless, this limitation may impose constraints on the precision of the overall effect size estimate and restrict the statistical power to detect significant moderators, potentially affecting the generalizability of our conclusions. Therefore, the results of the current study, particularly the statistical power of the publication bias tests, should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of studies (n = 18). It is imperative that future empirical research prioritize not only the examination of the impact of SEL on students, but also that on teachers. Second, the implementation of the rigorous baseline equivalence criterion (i.e., < 0.25 SD) may result in the exclusion of valuable studies with imbalanced baselines, thereby constraining the comprehensiveness of the evidence. The employment of this inclusion criterion was deliberate, with the objective of augmenting the internal validity of this study and the robustness of its conclusions (e.g., [18, 73, 74]). In the event that substantial disparities in outcome scores are observed between the experimental and control groups during the pre-test phase, it becomes challenging to ascertain whether the post-test variations are attributable to the intervention itself or to inherent group disparities. Consequently, we adhered to the recommendation of the What Works Clearinghouse regarding baseline differences, thereby preventing “garbage in, garbage out” and ensuring the robustness of our findings. Nonetheless, it is plausible to note that the present approach might have excluded methodologically sound studies with unreported or larger baseline imbalances, reflecting a trade-off between internal validity and the comprehensiveness of the evidence. Future research may entail a more exhaustive evaluation of the variations or sensitivity in meta-analysis results that stem from divergent methodologies employed for baseline difference. Third, the protocol was not pre-registered in a public registry, such as PROSPERO, which may limit the transparency of this study. Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 18), it was not feasible to incorporate all theoretically relevant moderators into the final multivariate meta-regression models. In order to ascertain model stability and avoid overfitting, we prioritized theoretically core moderators for the final multivariate model. We also relied on the adequacy of studies within each category (n > 5) and the multicollinearity among moderators to identify the key moderators and determine the final models. For the sake of readability, the main text focuses exclusively on the moderators in the final parsimonious multivariate meta-regression results. Subgroup comparison results for other excluded moderators (i.e., sample size, grade, country, and type) are reported in the supplementary materials to ensure transparency. Given the possibility that other potential moderators not included in this study may also influence the SEL effectiveness on teacher’s well-being, the findings must be interpreted with caution. Finally, our searching scope was extensively constrained to English-language documents and a specific set of databases (e.g., Web of Science, ProQuest, ERIC, and PsycINFO), excluding others such as Scopus or PubMed. While this approach aligned with many previous meta-analytic reviews in the field of SEL, it necessarily excluded evidence published in languages other than English and potentially overlooked relevant studies indexed only in non-selected databases. This may introduce language and database coverage biases, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the evidence base and the global generalizability of our findings. Future research endeavors would benefit from incorporating multi-language searches, expanding database coverage, and, when feasible, translation support to synthesize the global corpus of literature on this topic. In sum, it is imperative to exercise discretion when interpreting the findings of this study, particularly with regard to their generalizability. The current evidence base was predominantly rooted in high-income, Western cultural contexts and reflected both SEL and mindfulness-based intervention frameworks to some extent. Consequently, the applicability of these conclusions to diverse socioeconomic settings or cultural backgrounds remains uncertain and constitutes a critical avenue for future research.

Conclusions

This meta-analytic review provides valuable insights into the impact of SEL programs on teacher well-being, addressing a significant gap in the literature by shifting the focus from students to educators. The findings revealed that SEL interventions significantly enhanced teachers’ positive well-being and negative well-being, with effect sizes deemed small to moderate and practical meaningful. Furthermore, the effect of SEL programs on positive well-being was approximately twice that on negative well-being. This highlighted the need for SEL programs to incorporate more strategies that specifically address negative emotional experiences, in addition to fostering positive psychological skills. The present study also examined the potential moderators of SEL effectiveness, revealing that research design, additional coaching, and mindfulness significantly influenced the effectiveness of SEL interventions, whereas duration and dosage did not. The findings indicated that SEL interventions incorporating supplementary coaching were more effective in enhancing teachers’ positive well-being, suggesting that integrating coaching in SEL programs may yield substantial benefits. Furthermore, mindfulness emerged as a moderator in the reduction of negative well-being, thereby underscoring its significance as a complementary component in SEL interventions. These findings provide practical implications for designing and implementing SEL programs that maximize their impact on teacher well-being. In conclusion, this study underscores the potential of SEL programs as innovative tools for improving teacher well-being, particularly when designed as comprehensive interventions that incorporate coaching and mindfulness and address the unique needs of teachers. By advancing the understanding of SEL’s impact on teachers, this research contributes to the development of more effective strategies for supporting teachers’ well-being, ultimately benefiting both teachers and their students. Future efforts should focus on refining SEL interventions to address the dual dimensions of well-being and exploring the specific way of integrating coaching and mindfulness in SEL programs in educational settings.
