Autologous peritoneal patch for venous reconstruction: A systematic review of clinical outcomes
Authors: Abdallah Al Farai, Gabriela Del Angel-Millán, Shady Salama, ZakaUllah Jan, Mariantonietta Alagia, Mohammed Al Hosni, Fatma Al Zadjali, Gianluca Cassese, Fabrizio Panaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVenous reconstruction is increasingly required during complex abdominal and oncologic surgery. Autologous parietal peritoneum (PP) has emerged as a potential graft material due to its availability, biocompatibility, and low thrombogenic profile. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the safety and clinical outcomes of venous reconstruction using PP. A systematic search of MEDLINE (PubMed), ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Embase was conducted from database inception to December 2025, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Studies reporting outcomes of venous reconstruction using PP were included. Data were extracted on patient characteristics, reconstruction site, technique, anticoagulation, imaging follow-up, and outcomes. The primary outcome was vascular complications (thrombosis and stenosis). Secondary outcomes included reintervention and 30-day mortality. Risk of bias was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute tools. A narrative synthesis with pooled proportions was performed due to heterogeneity. Twenty studies, including 277 patients, were analyzed. The most common reconstruction sites were the inferior vena cava (45%), mesenterico-portal system (36%), and hepatic veins (19%). Patch repair was performed in 77% of cases. The pooled vascular complication rate was 15.1%, including thrombosis in 5.4% and stenosis in 8.3%. The pooled 30-day mortality rate was 1.4%. Follow-up ranged from 3 to 18 months, with variable anticoagulation and imaging protocols. Autologous PP is a feasible option for venous reconstruction with acceptable short-term outcomes. However, evidence is limited by heterogeneity and a lack of long-term data, warranting further prospective studies.Keywords: Peritoneum, Veins, General surgery

INTRODUCTION

Abdominal surgery has advanced significantly in recent decades. New technologies, improved operative techniques, and modern preoperative imaging now allow surgeons to perform increasingly complex procedures, particularly in trauma and surgical oncology [1,2]. For these complex cases, vascular resection is a crucial technique for achieving resectability and clear margins [2].

Restoring vascular continuity with sufficient blood flow is vital for preserving organ perfusion. Various options exist for vascular reconstruction, including synthetic and biological grafts. However, there is no consensus on the optimal graft type for venous reconstruction, primarily due to concerns about thrombosis and long-term patency [3]. Synthetic grafts are often less desirable due to their high cost and increased risk of thrombosis. In contrast, biological grafts have a lower thrombosis risk, reducing the need for anticoagulation. These grafts can be sourced from animal tissues (though availability is limited), deceased donors (a viable option in transplant centers), or autologous veins (such as the renal, iliac, jugular, or saphenous veins). Nevertheless, autologous grafts necessitate additional incisions, extend surgical time, and may increase patient morbidity [4].

Parietal peritoneum (PP) was first described as a graft for venous reconstruction in 1965 by Kurbangaleev et al. [5]. It can be used as a conduit or a patch and offers several advantages, including wide availability, suitability for harvesting from the same operative site, and a low thrombogenic risk [6]. A recent systematic review reported favorable outcomes, with a thrombosis rate of 8.9%, which is lower than rates observed with synthetic grafts and even some autologous veins [3].

Despite these advantages, the use of PP in abdominal surgery remains poorly documented, with existing evidence primarily consisting of case series. Therefore, the aim of this study is to systematically review the outcomes of venous reconstruction using PP in abdominal surgery. Unlike previous reviews that focused primarily on portal vein reconstruction during pancreatic surgery or included a limited number of studies, the present review evaluates the use of autologous PP across all abdominal venous reconstruction sites. This review provides a comprehensive assessment of technical approaches, anticoagulation strategies, imaging follow-up, reintervention rates, and vascular outcomes, thereby offering a broader perspective on the role of peritoneal grafts in abdominal venous reconstruction.

METHODS

Literature search strategy

We searched four electronic databases (MEDLINE via PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Embase) for studies published up to December 2025. The search terms “(Peritoneum OR peritoneal) AND (patch OR vein OR venous)” were applied to the title and abstract fields. We included studies published in English and involving human subjects, without restriction on publication year. An additional search was performed by reviewing the references of retrieved articles. Our search methods followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [7], and the study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (CRD420251002063).

Three authors (GDAM, ZUJ, SS) independently screened the search results by title and abstract to identify potentially relevant articles. The same authors then conducted full-text reviews and extracted data. An additional reviewer (AAF) made the final decision on article inclusion. A cross-reference search was performed on the selected articles, followed by another round of full-text review and data extraction. Fig. 1 presents the flow diagram of the study selection process.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the study selection.

In accordance with the registered PROSPERO protocol (CRD420251002063), the primary outcome of this systematic review was the rate of vascular complications, including thrombosis and stenosis, following venous reconstruction using peritoneal patches. Secondary outcomes included: patency rates (assessed by Doppler ultrasound or contrast-enhanced computed tomography [CT]), postoperative mortality (30-day and 90-day), need for vascular reintervention, use and type of anticoagulation therapy, imaging modality and timing, and follow-up duration and outcomes. Due to clinical heterogeneity across studies, these outcomes were extracted from each study and synthesized narratively.

Study selection

The inclusion criteria for the selected studies were: (i) Studies investigating postoperative outcomes related to the use of peritoneal grafts including falciform ligament in vein reconstruction, and (ii) availability of data regarding vascular and graft complications.

The exclusion criteria were: (i) Studies that did not clearly report postoperative outcomes, (ii) articles published in languages other than English, (iii) video or conference abstracts, (iv) studies with fewer than 3 patients, (v) meta-analyses, (vi) review articles, (vii) animal studies, or (viii) duplicate studies.

Data extraction

Three researchers (GDAM, ZUJ, SS) independently extracted data from the selected studies. In cases of discrepancies, a fourth researcher (AAF) was consulted. The extracted data included:

• Study details: First author’s name, publication year, title, journal, country, and article type.

• Patient demographics: Number of patients, age, sex, diagnosis, and type of surgery performed.

• Vein reconstruction data: Resected vein(s), type of reconstruction, side of the peritoneum used, duration of vascular reconstruction, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss.

• Postoperative outcomes: Vascular complications, Clavien–Dindo classification for complications, time of occurrence of complications (if any), postoperative mortality, need for reintervention (type and indication), need for anticoagulation therapy, postoperative imaging, and follow-up details.

Given the anticipated heterogeneity in patient populations, reconstruction sites, surgical techniques, anticoagulation protocols, and outcome reporting, a formal meta-analysis was not planned. Accordingly, results were synthesized narratively, and pooled proportions are presented solely as descriptive summary rates rather than as formal meta-analytic estimates.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for each included study was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for case series and cohort studies, which consists of 8 items. Each item was answered as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not applicable [8]. This tool provides a systematic framework for evaluating methodological quality based on key criteria, including sample selection, study design, data collection, and analysis methods. Each ‘Yes’ response received 1 point, while ‘No’ or ‘Unclear’ received 0. Items marked ‘Not applicable’ were excluded from the denominator. Studies scoring ≥ 70% were considered low risk of bias, 50%–69% moderate risk, and < 50% high risk.

RESULTS

Literature selection

A total of 1,914 records were identified from four electronic databases (MEDLINE, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and Embase). After removing 20 duplicate records, 1,894 studies were screened by title and abstract, leading to the exclusion of 1,865 records. Subsequently, 29 reports were sought for retrieval, but 4 could not be accessed due to study design limitations or being video abstracts. An additional cross-reference search identified more relevant studies, resulting in 39 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Of these, 3 studies were excluded due to insufficient outcome data and 16 were excluded due to a total patient number of less than 3. Ultimately, 20 studies were included in the systematic review [9-28]. Given the heterogeneity of the included studies, a narrative synthesis was conducted using simple pooled proportions rather than a meta-analysis. Technical aspect

Reconstruction type

All the papers included in this review focused on the use of peritoneal patches for venous reconstruction across three specific sites, with no reported cases of arterial application. The three venous sites reconstructed were the inferior vena cava (IVC), hepatic veins (HVs), and the mesenterico-portal system. The distribution of studies among these sites included 9 articles on the IVC, 4 on the HVs, and 14 on the mesenterico-portal system. The primary types of venous repairs described were patch repair following tangential resection, which constituted the majority of reported cases, and tubular graft repair for more extensive reconstruction.

Peritoneal graft harvesting site

Various locations within the abdominal cavity were utilized as sources for peritoneal patches. The most commonly harvested sites included the hypochondrium, encompassing both the diaphragmatic and subcostal peritoneum. Additional sources included the falciform ligament, anterior renal fascia, lumbar peritoneum, and the anterior abdominal wall peritoneum, with or without the rectus sheath fascia. Among these, the anterior abdominal wall peritoneum combined with the rectus sheath fascia was frequently selected for IVC tubular repair. This preference may be attributed to its enhanced structural integrity, potentially providing superior mechanical support and durability to the graft.

Anticoagulation strategies

A key consideration in these surgical reconstructions was anticoagulation therapy; however, no universally agreed-upon protocol for its management existed. Among the 20 papers reviewed, 9 explicitly mentioned using anticoagulation, either prophylactically or therapeutically. Specifically, 6 papers described prophylactic anticoagulation, while 3 reported therapeutic strategies. Conversely, four studies explicitly stated that no anticoagulation was administered, and 7 papers did not report their anticoagulation strategy, highlighting the variability in postoperative management approaches.

Imaging follow-up

Postoperative imaging assessment of reconstructed veins most commonly utilizes two primary modalities: Doppler ultrasound and contrast-enhanced CT scanning. These techniques are essential for evaluating graft patency, detecting potential complications like thrombosis or stenosis, and ensuring the overall success of vascular reconstruction. However, two of the reviewed studies did not disclose their specific imaging protocols. Imaging evaluations can be conducted as early as the first postoperative day. The choice between Doppler ultrasound and contrast-enhanced CT may depend on institutional protocols, patient-specific factors, and the treating physician's clinical judgment. Follow-up duration was inconsistently reported; among the 12 studies that provided this information, the median follow-up ranged from 3 to 18 months.

Complications overview

The various complications were defined among the included studies as follow:

• Thrombosis: Complete or partial occlusion of the reconstructed vein as reported on postoperative imaging.

• Stenosis: Radiologic evidence of luminal narrowing associated with altered flow dynamics.

• Patency: A reconstructed vein segment remaining open with demonstrable blood flow on Doppler or CT imaging.

• Reintervention: Any unplanned procedure related to the reconstructed vein, including reoperation or stenting.

The total number of patients included across all studies was 277. Among these, vascular complications were reported in 42 patients, accounting for approximately 15.1% of the total population. When examining thrombosis specifically, 15 patients (5.4% of the total cohort) were reported to have experienced either partial or complete thrombosis of the reconstructed vein.

In terms of stenosis, it was reported as a complication in 23 patients, representing about 8.3% of the total number of patients across the studies. However, one study did not mention or document any instances of stenosis complications. Moving on to aneurysms, it seems a relatively rare complication as only one patient was reported to have it among all the included papers [11]. However, it is important to acknowledge that 1 study did not report the presence or absence of this type of complication.

In terms of mortality, the 30-day and 90-day mortality rates were reported in 4 and 1 patient, respectively, representing about 1.4% and 0.3% of the total cohort. However, a significant limitation arises as 8 studies did not provide any documentation regarding 30-day mortality. Regarding reintervention, this complication was reported in 13 patients (4.6%). Nevertheless, 7 studies did not document whether reintervention was necessary, meaning that the actual rate of reintervention might be inaccurate in this analysis.

When narrowing the focus to cohort and case series studies that included 10 or more patients, as referenced in the study [10-12,14,16,19-21,23,24,26,28], the total number of patients in these studies was 235. Among these, vascular complications were reported in 34 patients, or 14.4% of the total cohort, which is slightly higher than the overall complication rate observed across all studies. Mortality within this specific group was reported in 3 individuals, accounting for 1.2% of this subset. However, it is important to note that 5 studies did not investigate or report on 30-day mortality. The reintervention rate in this particular cohort was reported in 10 patients, or 4.2% of the total. However, 4 studies did not document their reintervention rates.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for the included studies revealed a diverse range of methodological rigor. Among the 20 studies analyzed, 12 studies (60%) were classified as having a moderate risk of bias, while 5 studies (25%) demonstrated a low risk of bias, indicating a robust methodological approach. However, 3 studies (15%) were categorized as having a high risk of bias, which may impact the reliability of their findings. Table 2 shows the final risk of bias assessment results of each study.

DISCUSSION

Advances in surgical techniques, particularly in oncological surgery, have led to more extensive resections to achieve R0 status, as well as increasingly frequent vascular resections, especially in hepatobiliary, pancreatic, renal, and sarcoma surgeries [29-31]. Peritoneal patch reconstruction can be particularly useful for tangential defects of the portal vein, mesentericoportal system, or IVC that require patch angioplasty. It may also serve as an alternative conduit for selected segmental venous resections when primary repair is not feasible. The availability of autologous peritoneum without additional donor-site morbidity and its potential utility in contaminated fields make it an attractive option during complex hepatopancreatobiliary procedures.

This was the first systematic review to include comprehensive papers on the use of PP in all types of abdominal surgery. The included papers consistently affirm the feasibility, utility, and accessibility of peritoneal patches for vascular reconstruction.

The findings highlight technical feasibility with promising short-term outcomes and an increasing trend in the application of PP during abdominal vascular reconstruction. Notably, studies published before 2015 were predominantly case reports, while those published after 2015 were primarily case series or cohort studies, reflecting this growing trend. In their 2021 systematic review, Labori et al. [3] examined four studies utilizing peritoneal or falciform patches during pancreatic surgery, reporting satisfactory results. In contrast, due to different inclusion and exclusion criteria, our systematic review encompassed 14 papers related to pancreatic surgery.

In the current review, stenosis was the most frequently encountered complication, affecting 23 (8.3%) patients; however, no clear surgical techniques were reported to avoid or decrease the risk of this complication. Thrombosis was the second most common complication, often occurring concomitantly with stenosis. Anticoagulation practices varied considerably across the included studies, ranging from therapeutic anticoagulation to prophylactic regimens or no anticoagulation. Although thrombosis and stenosis remain significant concerns following venous reconstruction, the available data were insufficient to determine whether any specific anticoagulation strategy was associated with improved vascular outcomes.

Despite the advantages of the PP, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these results. A key limitation of this review is the restricted data availability, with most available studies focusing solely on the technical aspects of the procedures. The majority of included studies provided limited details regarding long-term outcomes, thrombosis rates, and graft patency. This lack of comprehensive data prevents a robust evaluation of the clinical impact of PP compared to alternative grafting materials. Additionally, the high degree of heterogeneity in the reports of postoperative outcomes limits the generalizability of these findings.

Another critical limitation is the absence of prospective and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) within the reviewed literature. The entire dataset was composed of case reports, case series, and only two cohort studies, which reduces the level of evidence supporting the use of PP. Without RCTs or at least case-control comparative studies, it is challenging to determine whether the observed outcomes are directly attributable to the use of PP or if they are influenced by other surgical and patient-specific factors.

The review identified a total of 2 cohort studies from the overall number of included publications [27,28]. These studies provide some level of comparative analysis, yet they remain susceptible to biases inherent in non-randomized designs. Furthermore, while cohort studies offer valuable insights into patient outcomes, the limited sample sizes of only 3 and 13 patients and variations in patient selection criteria across studies reduce their ability to establish definitive conclusions.

One of the most significant challenges encountered during this review was the enormous amount of data for extraction, much of which included insufficient or unclear information to draw significant conclusions. Many papers failed to provide details on operative and reconstruction times, estimated blood loss, and mortality rates. This incomplete data underscore the need for standardized reporting guidelines to improve the quality and utility of future research on this topic. The lack of consistent data reporting across studies also made it difficult to perform a meaningful meta-analysis or directly compare results between studies.

Additionally, while short-term outcomes appear favorable, long-term durability and graft patency remain areas of concern. Limited data are available on the longevity of PP as vascular grafts, and it is unclear whether these patches maintain structural integrity over time. Further research should focus on long-term follow-up and the long-term behavior of peritoneal patches.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review suggests that autologous PP is a technically feasible and promising option for venous reconstruction during abdominal surgeries.

However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to several limitations in the available evidence. The studies are predominantly retrospective, have small sample sizes, and exhibit variability in surgical indications, anticoagulation regimens, and imaging follow-up protocols. Furthermore, long-term patency outcomes were inconsistently reported and remain unclear.

Given these limitations, while early results appear encouraging, definitive conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness, long-term safety, and durability of PP grafts cannot yet be made. Future prospective studies with standardized definitions, consistent outcome reporting, and longer follow-up are needed to better define the role of PP in abdominal venous reconstruction.
