Compressible Navier–Stokes Flow in Schrödinger-Type Variables
James R. Beattie, Max Sokolova, Khush Negandhi, Bart Ripperda

Abstract
Fluid equations are nonlinear, dissipative, and non-Hamiltonian, which makes their relation to Schrödinger evolution and quantum algorithms nontrivial. We derive an exact Eulerian Cole–Hopf-type reformulation of isothermal compressible Navier–Stokes (NS) flow in Schrödinger-type amplitude variables. To our knowledge, this gives the first exact Cole–Hopf-type Schrödinger-variable reformulation of compressible NS flow. In two dimensions, a Helmholtz decomposition separates the velocity into compressive and vortical potentials, whose logarithmic transforms yield two scalar imaginary-time Schrödinger-type equations with nonlinear self-consistent potentials. We show that the mixed density-compressive amplitude Ψ α = ρ α ​ Θ 1 − 2 ​ α \Psi_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha} , where ρ \rho is the density, Θ \Theta is the compressive amplitude, and α ≠ 0 , 1 / 2 \alpha\neq 0,\,1/2 , satisfies a nonlinear Schrödinger-type equation with a vector-potential-coupled Laplacian. The transformed system is exactly equivalent to compressible NS and is nonlocal only through Helmholtz and Poisson projections. In three dimensions, the density-carrying equation retains the same vector-potential-coupled structure, while the solenoidal sector admits a compressible analogue of Ohkitani’s 22 incompressible NS Cole–Hopf formulation. Unlike unitary hydrodynamic Schrödinger-flow representations, the present equations are imaginary-time heat or drift-diffusion equations with self-consistent potentials, but they remain an exact change of variables for compressible NS. A two-dimensional Kelvin–Helmholtz shear-layer calculation verifies the transformed equations against a direct compressible NS simulation. The formulation exposes operator structures that may be useful for reduced flow descriptions, quantum algorithms for operator evolution, and quantum partial differential equation solvers.

A second motivation comes from quantum and quantum-inspired approaches to nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs). Because quantum operations are linear and quantum measurement returns limited classical information, nonlinear field equations are often recast before either evolution or readout. Closest in algebraic strategy, Uchida et al.

The gain is not that the compressible equations become linear. Rather, the transformation gives an exact operator decomposition of the flow. It separates compressive, vortical, and density-carrying degrees of freedom; identifies the scalar and vector self-consistent potentials through which nonlinear advection, pressure, and projection effects enter; and places the leading linear pieces in heat or drift-diffusion form. This structure makes the nonlinear couplings explicit enough to compare with incompressible Cole–Hopf analogues, to define possible reduced operators, and to isolate which terms would have to be retained or approximated in any quantum or quantum-inspired implementation.

II
Two-Dimensional Navier–Stokes

II.1
Governing equations

We consider the two-dimensional isothermal
(p=c_{s}^{2}\rho)
compressible NS equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\ln\rho+\nu\Delta\bm{u}+(\zeta+\nu)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}),

(2)

where
c_{s}
is the isothermal sound speed,
\nu
is the shear viscosity, and
\zeta
is the kinematic coefficient multiplying the additional compressive viscous term. The isothermal pressure term already appears as a gradient of
\ln\rho
, so it is useful to make the logarithmic density a primary scalar variable,
s\equiv\ln\rho
. With this substitution the continuity equation becomes a transport equation with source
-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}
. This source depends only on the compressive component of the velocity, which we represent by the scalar potential introduced below.

Throughout the two-dimensional derivation we assume either periodic boundary conditions, with zero-mean potentials used to fix the inverse Laplacian, or sufficiently decaying fields on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The Helmholtz potentials are therefore understood modulo the usual constant freedoms. Nonlocal scalar potentials are fixed by the corresponding Poisson equations together with these conventions. The resulting zero-mode choices do not affect the reconstructed velocity field, but they fix the amplitudes used below.

II.2
Helmholtz decomposition

In two dimensions we write the velocity as

\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\phi+\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\psi,\qquad\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\psi\equiv(\partial_{y}\psi,\,-\partial_{x}\psi).

(3)

Then

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=\Delta\phi,\qquad\omega\equiv\partial_{x}u_{y}-\partial_{y}u_{x}=-\Delta\psi.

(4)

In terms of
s
, the continuity equation becomes

\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=-\Delta\phi,

(5)

II.3
Potential formulation of the momentum equation

Using the two-dimensional identity

\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\!\left(\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}\right)+\bm{u}^{\perp}\,\omega,\qquad\bm{u}^{\perp}\equiv(-u_{y},u_{x}),

(6)

we rewrite (
2
) as

\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{\nabla}\!\left(\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}+c_{s}^{2}s\right)+\bm{u}^{\perp}\omega=\nu\Delta\bm{u}+(\zeta+\nu)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}).

(7)

We define

\bm{F}\equiv\bm{u}^{\perp}\omega,

(8)

and decompose
\bm{F}
into gradient and skew-gradient parts,

\bm{F}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi_{F}+\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\Psi_{F},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{F}

\displaystyle=\Delta\Phi_{F},

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{F}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\Psi_{F},

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{F}

\displaystyle\equiv\partial_{x}F_{2}-\partial_{y}F_{1}.

(12)

Projecting the momentum equation onto the gradient and skew-gradient sectors gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}+c_{s}^{2}s+\Phi_{F}

\displaystyle=(\zeta+2\nu)\Delta\phi,

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\Psi_{F}

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta\psi.

(14)

II.4
Logarithmic variables

We now introduce logarithmic amplitudes for the compressive and vortical potentials,

\phi=-2\mu_{c}\tau,\qquad\psi=2\mu_{s}\chi,\qquad\tau\equiv\ln\Theta,\qquad\chi\equiv\ln\Xi,

(15)

where

\mu_{c}\equiv\zeta+2\nu,\qquad\mu_{s}\quad\text{is the solenoidal transform scale.}

(16)

One may specialize to the canonical choice
\mu_{s}=\nu
, but we keep the symbols distinct to avoid conflating the transform normalization with the physical viscosity. Thus
\mu_{s}
normalizes
\chi
; it is not an additional physical transport coefficient. The corresponding amplitudes are

\Theta=e^{\tau},\qquad\Xi=e^{\chi}.

(17)

The velocity becomes

\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+2\mu_{s}\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\chi.

(18)

Its divergence is

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\Delta\tau.

(19)

Therefore (
5
) becomes

\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s=2\mu_{c}\Delta\tau.

(20)

II.5
Compressive Schrödinger-type equation

Substituting
\phi=-2\mu_{c}\tau
into (
13
) and using

\partial_{t}\Theta=\Theta\,\partial_{t}\tau,\qquad\Delta\Theta=\Theta\bigl(\Delta\tau+|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}\bigr),

(21)

we find

\partial_{t}\Theta=\mu_{c}\Delta\Theta+V_{\Theta}\Theta,

(22)

with scalar potential

V_{\Theta}=-2\mu_{s}\,\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\chi+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{\mu_{c}}|\bm{\nabla}\chi|^{2}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{\Phi_{F}}{2\mu_{c}}.

(23)

II.6
Vortical Schrödinger-type equation

Substituting
\psi=2\mu_{s}\chi
into (
14
), and then writing
\chi=\ln\Xi
, gives

\partial_{t}\Xi=\nu\Delta\Xi+V_{\Xi}\Xi,

(24)

where

V_{\Xi}=-\frac{\Psi_{F}}{2\mu_{s}}-\nu|\bm{\nabla}\chi|^{2}.

(25)

II.7
The nonlocal forcing field

Since

\omega=-\Delta\psi=-2\mu_{s}\Delta\chi,

(26)

and

\bm{u}^{\perp}=2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\tau+2\mu_{s}\bm{\nabla}\chi,

(27)

we have

\bm{F}=\bm{u}^{\perp}\omega=-4\mu_{s}(\Delta\chi)\left(\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\tau+\mu_{s}\bm{\nabla}\chi\right).

(28)

We determine the nonlocal potentials from

\Delta\Phi_{F}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{F},\qquad\Delta\Psi_{F}=-\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{F}.

(29)

II.8
Mixed density-compressive amplitude

We now seek an amplitude built from the density and compressive potential that has a second-order Schrödinger-type form. We choose the powers so that the explicit
\Delta\tau
and
|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}
terms cancel from the reaction coefficient. Consider

\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{\beta}=e^{\alpha s+\beta\tau}.

(30)

We write

q_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv\ln\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}=\alpha s+\beta\tau,

(31)

then

(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}=\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})q_{\alpha,\beta}.

(32)

Using (
20
) and

\partial_{t}\tau=\mu_{c}\Delta\tau+\mu_{c}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+V_{\Theta},

(33)

which follows from (
22
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})q_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})s+\beta(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\tau

\displaystyle=2\alpha\mu_{c}\Delta\tau+\beta\bigl(\partial_{t}\tau+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}(2\alpha+\beta)\Delta\tau+\beta\mu_{c}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\bigl(V_{\Theta}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr).

(34)

On the other hand,

\bm{\nabla}q_{\alpha,\beta}=\alpha\bm{\nabla}s+\beta\bm{\nabla}\tau,\qquad\Delta q_{\alpha,\beta}=\alpha\Delta s+\beta\Delta\tau,

(35)

so

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}\bigl(\Delta q_{\alpha,\beta}+|\bm{\nabla}q_{\alpha,\beta}|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}\Bigl(\alpha\Delta s+\beta\Delta\tau+\alpha^{2}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta^{2}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+2\alpha\beta\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\Bigr).

(36)

Substituting (
34
) and (
36
) into (
32
) gives

(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}=D\,\Delta\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}+U_{\alpha,\beta;D}\Psi_{\alpha,\beta},

(37)

with

\displaystyle U_{\alpha,\beta;D}

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}(2\alpha+\beta)\Delta\tau+\beta\mu_{c}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\bigl(V_{\Theta}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr)-D\Bigl(\alpha\Delta s+\beta\Delta\tau+\alpha^{2}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\beta^{2}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+2\alpha\beta\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\Bigr).

(38)

Choosing the coefficients so that the explicit
\Delta\tau
and
|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}
terms cancel yields

\beta=1-2\alpha,\qquad D_{\alpha}=\frac{\mu_{c}}{1-2\alpha},\qquad\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2}.

(39)

Thus we define the mixed density-compressive amplitude

\Psi_{\alpha}\equiv\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha}.

(40)

Equivalently,

\Psi_{\alpha}=e^{\alpha s+(1-2\alpha)\tau}.

(41)

For a fixed
\Theta
, any two choices
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\neq 0,1/2
are algebraically equivalent:

\Psi_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\Psi_{\alpha}^{\alpha^{\prime}/\alpha}\Theta^{1-\alpha^{\prime}/\alpha}.

(42)

Thus
\alpha
does not introduce a new physical field; it redistributes density and compressive-potential information between
\Theta
and
\Psi_{\alpha}
. The restrictions
\alpha\neq 1/2
and
\alpha\neq 0
have different roles:
\alpha\neq 1/2
keeps
D_{\alpha}
finite, while
\alpha\neq 0
is needed to reconstruct
s=\ln\rho
from
\Theta
and
\Psi_{\alpha}
.
Its advection-diffusion equation is

(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\Psi_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}\Delta\Psi_{\alpha}+U_{\alpha}\Psi_{\alpha},

(43)

with

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\Delta s-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha^{2}}{1-2\alpha}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mu_{c}\alpha\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau+(1-2\alpha)\bigl(V_{\Theta}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr).

(44)

To absorb the advective derivative into a shifted Laplacian, rewrite (
43
) as

\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}\Delta\Psi_{\alpha}-\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{\alpha}+U_{\alpha}\Psi_{\alpha}.

(45)

We define

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}\equiv-\frac{\bm{u}}{2D_{\alpha}}.

(46)

Then
2D_{\alpha}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}=-\bm{u}
, and for any scalar
f
,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha})^{2}f

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}\Delta f+2D_{\alpha}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\nabla}f

\displaystyle\quad+D_{\alpha}\bigl(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}+|\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}|^{2}\bigr)f

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}\Delta f-\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}f

\displaystyle\quad+D_{\alpha}\bigl(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}+|\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}|^{2}\bigr)f.

(47)

Applying (
47
) to (
45
) gives the vector-potential-coupled Schrödinger-type equation

\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha})^{2}\Psi_{\alpha}+V_{\alpha}^{(v)}\Psi_{\alpha},

(48)

where

V_{\alpha}^{(v)}=U_{\alpha}-D_{\alpha}\bigl(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}+|\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}|^{2}\bigr)=U_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}-\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{4D_{\alpha}}.

(49)

Using (
18
), the vector potential is

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}=(1-2\alpha)\bm{\nabla}\tau-\frac{\mu_{s}(1-2\alpha)}{\mu_{c}}\,\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\chi.

(50)

Equations (
48
)–(
50
) are the vector-potential-coupled Schrödinger-type equation for the transformed density variable
\Psi_{\alpha}
. The operator is analogous to the magnetic Schrödinger operator, where a vector potential enters through the shifted derivative in the Laplacian
1
. Here, however,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}
is a real self-consistent flow potential rather than an imposed electromagnetic vector potential. It contains both compressive and vortical contributions and is not divergence-free in general. Consequently,
D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha})^{2}
is a real drift-diffusion operator and is generally not self-adjoint under periodic or decaying boundary conditions; its formal adjoint contains
(\bm{\nabla}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha})^{2}
. This distinction is important when comparing the transformed density equation with Hermitian magnetic Schrödinger operators.

III
Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes

III.1
Helmholtz decomposition in three dimensions

We now turn to a three-dimensional extension. We replace the scalar streamfunction used in two dimensions by a divergence-free vector streamfunction, and the scalar vorticity by the vorticity vector. We write the velocity in Helmholtz form

\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\phi+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\psi}=0,

(60)

so that

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=\Delta\phi,\qquad\bm{\omega}\equiv\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}=-\Delta\bm{\psi},

(61)

where the second identity uses the Coulomb gauge. The continuity equation again becomes

\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s=-\Delta\phi.

(62)

III.2
Gradient projection and compressive amplitude

In three dimensions,

\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\!\left(\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}\right)-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}.

(63)

We define

\bm{G}\equiv-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}.

(64)

We decompose
\bm{G}
into gradient and solenoidal parts,

\bm{G}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi_{G}+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{G},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Y}_{G}=0.

(65)

Projecting the momentum equation onto the gradient sector gives

\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}+c_{s}^{2}s+\Phi_{G}=\mu_{c}\Delta\phi,\qquad\mu_{c}\equiv\zeta+2\nu.

(66)

We set

\phi=-2\mu_{c}\tau,\qquad\Theta=e^{\tau},

(67)

and denote the divergence-free part by

\bm{u}_{s}\equiv\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{s}=0.

(68)

Then

\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{u}_{s},

(69)

and (
62
) becomes

\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s=2\mu_{c}\Delta\tau.

(70)

The same logarithmic calculation used in the two-dimensional compressive sector yields

\partial_{t}\Theta=\mu_{c}\Delta\Theta+V_{\Theta}^{(3d)}\Theta,

(71)

with

V_{\Theta}^{(3d)}=-\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{u}_{s}+\frac{|\bm{u}_{s}|^{2}}{4\mu_{c}}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{\Phi_{G}}{2\mu_{c}}.

(72)

III.3
Transformed density amplitude in three dimensions

The transformed density variable has the same density-compressive form as in two dimensions:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}\equiv\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha s+(1-2\alpha)\tau},

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{c}}{1-2\alpha},\qquad\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2}.

(73)

The same cancellation mechanism as in two dimensions gives

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\Psi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}\Delta\Psi_{\alpha}+U_{\alpha}^{(3d)}\Psi_{\alpha},

(74)

where

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}^{(3d)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\Delta s-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha^{2}}{1-2\alpha}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mu_{c}\alpha\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau+(1-2\alpha)\bigl(V_{\Theta}^{(3d)}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr).

(75)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}\Delta\Psi_{\alpha}-\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{\alpha}+U_{\alpha}^{(3d)}\Psi_{\alpha}.

(76)

We introduce the effective vector potential

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(3d)}\equiv-\frac{\bm{u}}{2D_{\alpha}}=(1-2\alpha)\bm{\nabla}\tau-\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\mu_{c}}\,\bm{u}_{s}.

(77)

Then the transformed density variable satisfies the three-dimensional vector-potential-coupled Schrödinger-type equation

\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(3d)})^{2}\Psi_{\alpha}+V_{\alpha}^{(v,3d)}\Psi_{\alpha},

(78)

with

V_{\alpha}^{(v,3d)}=U_{\alpha}^{(3d)}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}-\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{4D_{\alpha}}.

(79)

Using (
69
), this becomes

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}^{(v,3d)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\Delta s-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha^{2}}{1-2\alpha}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mu_{c}\alpha\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau-\mu_{c}\Delta\tau

\displaystyle\quad-3\mu_{c}(1-2\alpha)|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+(1-2\alpha)\,\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{u}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(1-2\alpha)c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\mu_{c}}\Phi_{G}.

(80)

Thus the transformed density equation has the same vector-potential-coupled structure in three dimensions as in two dimensions.

III.4
Solenoidal sector and vector-streamfunction transform

For incompressible three-dimensional flow, Ohkitani showed that the solenoidal vector-potential equation can be rewritten component-wise in Cole–Hopf form
23
;
31
. We write the corresponding velocity potential as the vector streamfunction
\bm{\psi}
, reserving
\bm{A}
for the magnetic vector potential in Appendix
B
. This construction builds on the earlier vector-potential evolution equations for incompressible Euler and NS flow
22
. We treat the compressible case by the same sequence of steps after projecting the momentum equation onto its solenoidal sector. We start from the compressible momentum equation written in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}

\displaystyle+\bm{\nabla}\!\left(\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}\right)+c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}s+\bm{G}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{c}-\nu)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})+\nu\Delta\bm{u},

\displaystyle\bm{G}

\displaystyle=-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}.

(81)

Here
\mathbb{P}
is the Leray projector onto divergence-free vector fields
15
,

\mathbb{P}\bm{F}=\bm{F}-\bm{\nabla}\Delta^{-1}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{F}),

(82)

so
\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\mathbb{P}\bm{F})=0
and
\bm{F}
splits into a gradient part plus its solenoidal projection. In Fourier variables,

\widehat{\mathbb{P}\bm{F}}(\bm{k})=\left(I-\frac{\bm{k}\otimes\bm{k}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}\right)\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{k}).

(83)

Applying
\mathbb{P}
annihilates all gradient terms. Since
\mathbb{P}\bm{u}=\bm{u}_{s}
,
\mathbb{P}\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})=0
, and
\mathbb{P}\Delta\bm{u}=\Delta\bm{u}_{s}
, we obtain

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{s}+\mathbb{P}\bm{G}=\nu\Delta\bm{u}_{s}.

(84)

Using the Helmholtz decomposition
\bm{G}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi_{G}+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{G}
, the projected nonlinear term is
\mathbb{P}\bm{G}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{G}
. Therefore

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{s}+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{G}=\nu\Delta\bm{u}_{s}.

(85)

Since
\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}
, (
85
) determines the vector streamfunction only up to a gradient gauge. We now show that one can choose this gauge, compatibly with the periodic or decaying boundary conventions used to define the inverse Laplacian, so that

\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}+\bm{Y}_{G}=\nu\Delta\bm{\psi},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\psi}=0.

(86)

Substituting
\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}
into (
85
) gives

\bm{\nabla}\times\Bigl(\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}+\bm{Y}_{G}-\nu\Delta\bm{\psi}\Bigr)=0.

(87)

Thus the residual is a pure gradient: there exists a scalar field
\lambda
such that

\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}+\bm{Y}_{G}-\nu\Delta\bm{\psi}=\bm{\nabla}\lambda.

(88)

We use the gauge transform
\bm{\psi}\mapsto\bm{\psi}+\bm{\nabla}q
, with

\partial_{t}q-\nu\Delta q=-\lambda,

(89)

which preserves
\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}
and removes the pure-gradient residual. In this gauge
\lambda=0
, yielding (
86
). In the incompressible limit, this reduces to Ohkitani’s
vector-streamfunction equation. The forcing is determined by the full compressible field

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},

(90)

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}=-\Delta\bm{\psi},

(91)

\displaystyle\bm{G}

\displaystyle=-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}.

(92)

Substituting
\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{u}_{s}
gives the explicit split

\bm{G}=-\bm{u}_{s}\times\bm{\omega}+2\mu_{c}\,\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega}.

(93)

The first term is the incompressible Ohkitani contribution, while the second is the genuine compressible correction. Accordingly,

\mathbb{P}\bm{G}=\mathbb{P}(-\bm{u}_{s}\times\bm{\omega})+2\mu_{c}\,\mathbb{P}(\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega}).

(94)

Choosing
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Y}_{G}=0
, we write

\bm{Y}_{G}=\bm{Y}_{s}+\bm{Y}_{c},

(95)

where

\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{s}=\mathbb{P}(-\bm{u}_{s}\times\bm{\omega}),\quad\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{c}=2\mu_{c}\,\mathbb{P}(\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega}).

(96)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{s}

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times(-\bm{u}_{s}\times\bm{\omega}),

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{c}

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{c}\,\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega}),

(97)

since
\bm{\nabla}\times(\mathbb{P}\bm{F})=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{F}
and
\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y})=-\Delta\bm{Y}
under the Coulomb gauge. Thus the compressible effect on the solenoidal sector is encoded in the additional nonlocal term
\bm{Y}_{c}
. Applying the component-wise Cole–Hopf transform

\psi_{j}=k\ln\vartheta_{j},\qquad j=1,2,3,

(98)

where
k
is a transform scale, gives

\partial_{t}\vartheta_{j}=\nu\Delta\vartheta_{j}+V_{\vartheta,j}^{(3d)}\vartheta_{j},\qquad\text{(no summation)},

(99)

with

V_{\vartheta,j}^{(3d)}=-\frac{(Y_{G})_{j}}{k}-\nu\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{j}|^{2}}{\vartheta_{j}^{2}}.

(100)

This follows component-wise from

k\frac{\partial_{t}\vartheta_{j}}{\vartheta_{j}}+(Y_{G})_{j}=\nu k\left(\frac{\Delta\vartheta_{j}}{\vartheta_{j}}-\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{j}|^{2}}{\vartheta_{j}^{2}}\right),

(101)

after substituting
\psi_{j}=k\ln\vartheta_{j}
into (
86
) and using
\Delta\ln\vartheta_{j}=\Delta\vartheta_{j}/\vartheta_{j}-|\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{j}|^{2}/\vartheta_{j}^{2}
. The Coulomb-gauge constraint becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\vartheta_{j}}\partial_{j}\vartheta_{j}=0.

(102)

We summarize the coupled transformed system below.

III.5
Coupled transformed system in three dimensions

Collecting the preceding results, we write the compressible analogue of Ohkitani’s three-dimensional formulation as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Theta

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\Delta\Theta+V_{\Theta}^{(3d)}\Theta,

(103)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(3d)})^{2}\Psi_{\alpha}+V_{\alpha}^{(v,3d)}\Psi_{\alpha},

(104)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vartheta_{j}

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta\vartheta_{j}+V_{\vartheta,j}^{(3d)}\vartheta_{j},\qquad j=1,2,3.

(105)

There is no summation over
j
in (
105
). Here

V_{\Theta}^{(3d)}=-\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{u}_{s}+\frac{|\bm{u}_{s}|^{2}}{4\mu_{c}}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{\Phi_{G}}{2\mu_{c}},

(106)

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(3d)}=(1-2\alpha)\bm{\nabla}\tau-\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\mu_{c}}\,\bm{u}_{s},

(107)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}^{(v,3d)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\Delta s-\frac{\mu_{c}\alpha^{2}}{1-2\alpha}|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mu_{c}\alpha\,\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau-\mu_{c}\Delta\tau

\displaystyle\quad-3\mu_{c}(1-2\alpha)|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+(1-2\alpha)\,\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{u}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(1-2\alpha)c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\mu_{c}}\Phi_{G},

(108)

and

V_{\vartheta,j}^{(3d)}=-\frac{(Y_{G})_{j}}{k}-\nu\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{j}|^{2}}{\vartheta_{j}^{2}}.

(109)

We reconstruct the physical variables from

\tau=\ln\Theta,\qquad s=\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigl(\ln\Psi_{\alpha}-(1-2\alpha)\tau\Bigr),\qquad\rho=e^{s},

(110)

\psi_{j}=k\ln\vartheta_{j},\qquad\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},\qquad\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{u}_{s},

(111)

with the Coulomb-gauge constraint

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\vartheta_{j}}\partial_{j}\vartheta_{j}=0.

(112)

These reconstruction formulas require
\alpha\neq 0
and
\alpha\neq 1/2
. Together, they give the coupled compressive-solenoidal-density transformed system for the present Eulerian compressible generalization.

IV
Numerical validation

We validate the two-dimensional transformed system (
51
)–(
53
) by evolving it alongside a direct simulation of the compressible isothermal equations (
1
)–(
2
) on a doubly periodic square domain
(x,y)\in[0,2\pi]^{2}
. The calculations use
dedalus
, an open-source parallel framework for solving partial differential equations with spectral methods
8
. Here
dedalus
provides the Fourier discretization for the direct simulation and the periodic Poisson solves used in the transformed equations. The transformed state consists of
\Theta
,
\Xi
, and
\Psi_{\alpha}
, with
\Phi_{F}
and
\Psi_{F}
obtained at each time from the zero-mean Poisson solves in (
29
),

\Delta\Phi_{F}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{F},\qquad\Delta\Psi_{F}=-\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{F}.

(113)

The physical variables are reconstructed point-wise from

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\ln\Theta,

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=\ln\Xi,

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigl(\ln\Psi_{\alpha}-(1-2\alpha)\tau\Bigr),

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=e^{s},

(114)

and

\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+2\mu_{s}\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\chi.

(115)

The logarithmic reconstruction requires positivity of the transformed amplitudes; loss of positivity would signal either numerical failure or departure from the regime in which the amplitude variables are well conditioned.

The test problem is a smooth Kelvin–Helmholtz unstable shear layer, a standard instability (the KHI) problem that has also been used to test potential-based formulations of incompressible flow
22
. It is a good test because it has analytical growth rate and gets into nonlinear regime where the numerical methods are stringently tested. The initial density is uniform,
\rho(x,y,0)=1
, and the velocity is initialized as

\displaystyle u_{x}(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=U_{0}\bigl[P(y)-\langle P\rangle\bigr],

(116)

\displaystyle u_{y}(x,y,0)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sin(k_{x}x)E(y),

(117)

where

\displaystyle E(y)

\displaystyle=e^{-[(y-y_{1})/\sigma_{p}]^{2}}+e^{-[(y-y_{2})/\sigma_{p}]^{2}},

(118)

\displaystyle P(y)

\displaystyle=\tanh\frac{y-y_{1}}{\sigma_{s}}-\tanh\frac{y-y_{2}}{\sigma_{s}}-1.

The parameters are

,

,

(120)

which is the inverse peak shear of an isolated
\tanh
layer with the present normalization. The direct and transformed systems are initialized from the same transformed state, so the reconstructed fields (
114
)–(
115

\Phi_{F}
or
\Phi_{G}

V
Discussion and Conclusion

The main result of this paper is an exact Eulerian Cole–Hopf-type operator reformulation of the 2D and 3D isothermal compressible Navier–Stokes (NS) equations and its extension to MHD. To our knowledge, this is the first exact Cole–Hopf-type Schrödinger-variable reformulation of compressible NS flow. In Sec.
II
, the system (
51
)–(
53
) gives the closed transformed dynamics in terms of three amplitudes:

1.

a scalar compressive amplitude
\Theta
satisfying the heat or imaginary-time Schrödinger-type equation (
51
),

2.

a scalar vortical amplitude
\Xi
satisfying the corresponding equation (
52
),

3.

a scalar density-carrying amplitude
\Psi_{\alpha}
satisfying the vector-potential-coupled Schrödinger-type equation (
53
), with effective vector potential
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}
given by (
50
).

The density sector does not close as a pure density amplitude; instead, the mixed amplitude
\Psi_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha}
, with
\alpha\neq 0,\frac{1}{2}
, cancels the compressive Laplacian terms and closes in the vector-potential-coupled form. The resulting system is exact, but nonlocal: the Helmholtz decomposition and the projected nonlinear forcing require Poisson solves, with the forcing potentials determined by (
29
). Table
2
summarizes the bookkeeping used in this discussion. Its first two columns identify the physical content of each amplitude, its third column lists the corresponding transformed evolution equation, and its final column locates the self-consistent potentials that carry the nonlinear and nonlocal couplings. The
\Theta
,
\Xi
, and
\Psi_{\alpha}
rows give the closed 2D amplitude system, while the
\Theta
,
\Psi_{\alpha}
, and
\vartheta_{j}
rows show how the same operator structure extends to the 3D compressive, density-carrying, and solenoidal sectors.

The Kelvin–Helmholtz calculation in Sec.
IV
validates this transformed 2D system against a direct compressible NS simulation for the test considered here. At
t/t_{\rm sh}=10.00
, the reconstructed
\rho
and
\bm{u}
differ from the direct fields by relative
L^{2}
errors
1.05\times 10^{-5}
and
5.42\times 10^{-6}
, respectively, and the reconstructed vorticity field remains phase-aligned with the direct simulation (Fig.
1
). The transformed fields in Fig.
2
show how the logarithmic variables separate vortical, compressive, and density-coupled structure. The amplitudes satisfy
\Theta,\Xi,\Psi_{\alpha}>0
throughout the run, the direct and reconstructed momenta
P_{i}(t)
agree to small absolute drift, and the normalized isothermal free energies
E(t)/E(0)
differ by at most
2.9\times 10^{-10}
(Fig.

). These diagnostics check the implemented transformed right-hand side, nonlocal projections, and reconstruction formulas; they should not be read as a convergence study or as evidence that the transformed variables automatically improve numerical stability.

The construction has three immediate limitations. First, the logarithmic reconstruction requires
\Theta,\Xi,\Psi_{\alpha}>0
, so positivity must be monitored in any numerical use of the transformed variables. Second,
\rho
cannot be represented by a single pure density amplitude satisfying a closed second-order Schrödinger-type equation within the local point-transform classes considered here. We make this obstruction explicit in Appendix
C
and derive related transform classes in Appendix
D
. Third, we assume an isothermal equation of state,
p=c_{s}^{2}\rho
, which closes the mass–momentum system by prescribing the pressure directly from
\rho
rather than by evolving an independent energy or entropy equation; extending the formulation to fully adiabatic compressible flow would require transforming or evolving this additional thermodynamic degree of freedom. Evolving the energy or entropy equation would be necessary for any relativistic reformulation, where the equation of state must keep the sound speed causally bounded
c_{s}\leq c
by the speed of light,
c
.

In Sec.
III
, we show that the 3D transformed density equation keeps the same vector-potential-coupled structure, (
104
), once
\bm{u}
is split into compressive and solenoidal parts. As summarized in Table
2
, the 3D vector-streamfunction amplitudes
\vartheta_{j}
satisfy component-wise heat or imaginary-time Schrödinger-type equations with multiplicative potentials
V_{\vartheta,j}^{(3d)}
. The solenoidal sector follows the vector-streamfunction strategy used for incompressible NS flow by Ohkitani and by Vanon and Ohkitani

;
31
, with an additional nonlocal term
\bm{Y}_{c}
driven by
\bm{\nabla}\tau
in (
97
). In Appendix

These 3D and MHD results therefore give novel formal exact changes of variables for compressible systems, while leaving open whether the new variables yield bounds, regularity criteria, stable numerical schemes, or computational advantages.

The transformed operators may also suggest reduced descriptions of the flow. Each snapshot defines self-consistent operators
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}=\mu_{c}\Delta+V_{\Theta}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\Xi}=\nu\Delta+V_{\Xi}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi_{\alpha}}=D_{\alpha}(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha})^{2}+V_{\alpha}^{(v)}
, associated with (

V_{\Theta}
,
V_{\Xi}
,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}

5

\mathcal{L}_{\Psi_{\alpha}}
need not be self-adjoint, and it remains to determine whether such bases are more compact or more informative than Fourier, proper orthogonal decomposition, or other standard modal representations.

The same explicit potentials also suggest a way to build controlled truncated models. The final column of Table
2
separates the scalar multiplicative potentials, the vector-potential-coupled density terms, and the nonlocal forcing potentials. In 2D, the nonlinear couplings enter through
V_{\Theta}

in (

from (

\bm{F}=\bm{u}^{\perp}\omega

\Phi_{F}=\Psi_{F}=0
while retaining the local pressure, density-gradient, and logarithmic-gradient terms would define a nonlocal-forcing-free amplitude model that tests how this advective vorticity force feeds back on the transformed amplitudes. Retaining or suppressing selected pieces of these potentials would break exact equivalence to compressible NS, but it provides a systematic way to isolate the nonlinear compressive, vortical, density-coupled, and nonlocal projection effects.

For quantum-computing applications, this reformulation should not be read as a quantum speedup claim. Rather, it rewrites the compressible isothermal NS equations as a coupled set of amplitude equations whose heat- and Schrödinger-type linear parts may be able to benefit from quantum algorithms for operator evolution, in the same limited sense explored for transformed Burgers and heat-equation problems
30
;
16
. The closest quantum-fluid comparison is the HSE of Meng and Yang
19
: their two-component wave function gives a unitary Schrödinger-type evolution and motivates a prediction-correction quantum algorithm, whereas our logarithmic amplitudes give imaginary-time heat or drift-diffusion equations with self-consistent potentials. The tradeoff is also different. Their incompressible Schrödinger flow is designed to resemble important structures of viscous turbulence; our construction remains exactly equivalent to isothermal compressible NS, but only by retaining nonlocal Poisson projections, nonlinear potentials, and positivity constraints. More broadly, the present variables sit alongside quantum and quantum-inspired approaches based on lattice-Boltzmann or flow-specific algorithms
6
;
27
;
32
, hydrodynamic or Madelung-type Schrödinger reformulations
18
;
34
;
19
, and variational or tensor-network representations of nonlinear PDEs
20
;
33
;
29
. The remaining obstacles are explicit: nonlinear self-consistent potentials, elliptic projections, positivity of logarithmic amplitudes, state preparation, and observable extraction. Recent quantum-hardware studies suggest that statistical quantities may be more accessible readouts than full pointwise fields
12
;
30
, but determining whether these amplitude systems lead to useful analysis, reduced models, improved algorithms, or practical quantum implementations remains an open problem.

Appendix A
Equivalence of Leray projection and Green-function operators

We relate the Leray-projector formulation used in Sec.
III
to Ohkitani’s Green-function operator
T
. They are not different reductions; rather,
T
is the explicit integral realization of the projected nonlinear term after inverting curl and Laplacian.

Let
\bm{F}
be a sufficiently regular vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The Leray projection
15
is

\mathbb{P}\bm{F}=\bm{F}-\bm{\nabla}\Delta^{-1}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{F}),

(123)

so
\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\mathbb{P}\bm{F})=0
. Choose
\bm{Y}_{F}
in the Coulomb gauge,
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Y}_{F}=0
, and require

\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{F}=\mathbb{P}\bm{F},

(124)

Applying curl once more gives

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{F})

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Y}_{F})-\Delta\bm{Y}_{F}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\bm{Y}_{F}=\bm{\nabla}\times(\mathbb{P}\bm{F})=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{F},

(125)

because curl annihilates gradients. Therefore

\bm{Y}_{F}=-\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{F}.

(126)

Applying this identity to the vortical sector, the projected equation

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{s}+\mathbb{P}\bm{G}=\nu\Delta\bm{u}_{s},\qquad\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},

(127)

is equivalent, up to gauge, to

\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}+\bm{Y}_{G}=\nu\Delta\bm{\psi},\qquad\bm{Y}_{G}=-\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{G}.

(128)

Hence

\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}=\nu\Delta\bm{\psi}+\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{G}.

(129)

We identify the Green-function operator associated with
\bm{G}
as

\bm{T}_{G}[\bm{G}]\equiv\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{G}.

(130)

In the incompressible case,
\bm{u}=\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{G}=-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}
. Then (
129
) becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bm{T}_{s}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{\psi}]+\nu\Delta\bm{\psi},

\displaystyle\bm{T}_{s}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{\psi}]

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times(-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}),

(131)

which is Ohkitani’s vector-potential equation written in the present
\bm{\psi}
notation. Ohkitani’s operator
\bm{T}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{\psi}]
is the explicit principal-value kernel representation of
\bm{T}_{s}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{\psi}]
.

In the compressible case,

\bm{u}=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{u}_{s},\qquad\bm{G}=-\bm{u}_{s}\times\bm{\omega}+2\mu_{c}\,\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega},

(132)

so the Green-function operator splits as

\bm{T}_{G}=\bm{T}_{s}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{\psi}]+\bm{T}_{c}[\tau,\bm{\psi}],

(133)

with

\bm{T}_{c}[\tau,\bm{\psi}]\equiv 2\mu_{c}\,\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{\nabla}\tau\times\bm{\omega}).

(134)

Thus the Leray-projector formulation in Sec.
III
and Ohkitani’s
T
-operator formulation are equivalent descriptions of the same solenoidal dynamics. Our compressible generalization leaves the operator structure unchanged and adds the Green-function term driven by
\bm{\nabla}\tau
.

Appendix B
Compressible MHD extension

Vanon and Ohkitani give the incompressible MHD analogue of the vector-potential Cole–Hopf formulation in their appendix
31
. Here we derive the corresponding compressible isothermal system. We use units in which the magnetic permeability is one and write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(135)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}s+\nu\Delta\bm{u}

\displaystyle\quad+(\zeta+\nu)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})

(136)

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-s}\bm{J}\times\bm{B},

(137)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{u}\times\bm{B})+\eta\Delta\bm{B},

(138)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}

\displaystyle=0,

(139)

where
s=\ln\rho
,
\bm{B}
is the magnetic field,
\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B}
, and
\eta
is the magnetic diffusivity. The induction equation is equivalently

\partial_{t}\bm{B}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}-\bm{B}\,\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}+\eta\Delta\bm{B},

(140)

using
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
.

As in Sec.
III
, let

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{u}_{s},

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{s}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\psi}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mu_{c}

\displaystyle=\zeta+2\nu,

(141)

and introduce a magnetic vector potential

\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{A},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{A}=0.

(142)

Then
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}=-\Delta\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{J}=-\Delta\bm{A}
. We define the Lorentz acceleration and the total non-gradient forcing in the velocity equation by

\bm{L}\equiv e^{-s}\bm{J}\times\bm{B},\qquad\bm{K}\equiv-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}-\bm{L}.

(143)

Using
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}(|\bm{u}|^{2}/2)-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}
, the momentum equation becomes

\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{\nabla}\!\left(\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}+c_{s}^{2}s\right)+\bm{K}=(\mu_{c}-\nu)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})+\nu\Delta\bm{u}.

(144)

We decompose
\bm{K}
into gradient and solenoidal parts,

\bm{K}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi_{K}+\mathbb{P}\bm{K},\qquad\mathbb{P}\bm{K}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{Y}_{K},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Y}_{K}=0.

(145)

Equivalently,

\bm{Y}_{K}=-\Delta^{-1}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{K}.

(146)

The gradient projection gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{|\bm{u}|^{2}}{2}+c_{s}^{2}s+\Phi_{K}

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\Delta\phi,

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{c}\tau,

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=e^{\tau},

(147)

hence

\partial_{t}\Theta=\mu_{c}\Delta\Theta+V_{\Theta}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}\Theta,

(148)

with

V_{\Theta}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}=-\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{u}_{s}+\frac{|\bm{u}_{s}|^{2}}{4\mu_{c}}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{2\mu_{c}}s+\frac{\Phi_{K}}{2\mu_{c}}.

(149)

Thus the density-carrying amplitude

\Psi_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha},\qquad D_{\alpha}=\frac{\mu_{c}}{1-2\alpha},\qquad\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2},

(150)

obeys the same vector-potential-coupled form as in the hydrodynamic case,

\partial_{t}\Psi_{\alpha}=D_{\alpha}\bigl(\bm{\nabla}+\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}\bigr)^{2}\Psi_{\alpha}+V_{\alpha}^{(v,\mathrm{MHD})}\Psi_{\alpha},

(151)

where

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}=(1-2\alpha)\bm{\nabla}\tau-\frac{1-2\alpha}{2\mu_{c}}\bm{u}_{s},

(152)

and
V_{\alpha}^{(v,\mathrm{MHD})}
follows from (
80
) after replacing
\Phi_{G}
with
\Phi_{K}
.

The solenoidal velocity projection gives

\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{s}+\mathbb{P}\bm{K}=\nu\Delta\bm{u}_{s},

(153)

or, after the same gauge choice used in Sec.
III
,

\partial_{t}\bm{\psi}+\bm{Y}_{K}=\nu\Delta\bm{\psi}.

(154)

With the component-wise logarithmic transform

\psi_{j}=k\ln\vartheta_{j},\qquad j=1,2,3,

(155)

we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vartheta_{j}

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta\vartheta_{j}+V_{\vartheta,j}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}\vartheta_{j},

\displaystyle V_{\vartheta,j}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(Y_{K})_{j}}{k}-\nu\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{j}|^{2}}{\vartheta_{j}^{2}},

(156)

with no summation over
j
, and the Coulomb constraint becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\vartheta_{j}}\partial_{j}\vartheta_{j}=0.

(157)

We now transform the induction equation. We define

\bm{W}\equiv\bm{u}\times\bm{B}.

(158)

Since
\partial_{t}\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{W}+\eta\Delta\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{A}
, the magnetic vector potential satisfies

\partial_{t}\bm{A}=\mathbb{P}\bm{W}+\eta\Delta\bm{A}=\bm{W}+\bm{S}_{W}+\eta\Delta\bm{A},

(159)

where

\bm{S}_{W}\equiv-\bm{\nabla}\Delta^{-1}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{W})

(160)

is the gradient term that enforces the Coulomb gauge. We set

A_{j}=\ell\ln\mathcal{M}_{j},\qquad j=1,2,3.

(161)

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{j}

\displaystyle=\eta\Delta\mathcal{M}_{j}+H_{j}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}\mathcal{M}_{j},

\displaystyle H_{j}^{(\mathrm{MHD})}

\displaystyle=\frac{W_{j}+(S_{W})_{j}}{\ell}-\eta\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{M}_{j}|^{2}}{\mathcal{M}_{j}^{2}},

(162)

again with no summation over
j
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}_{j}}\partial_{j}\mathcal{M}_{j}=0.

(163)

We therefore obtain the transformed compressible MHD system as the coupled set (
148
), (
151
), (
156
), and (
162
), together with the nonlocal definitions of
\Phi_{K}
,
\bm{Y}_{K}
, and
\bm{S}_{W}
. We reconstruct the physical variables from

\tau=\ln\Theta,\qquad s=\frac{1}{\alpha}\bigl(\ln\Psi_{\alpha}-(1-2\alpha)\tau\bigr),\qquad\rho=e^{s},

(164)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}

\displaystyle=k\ln\vartheta_{j},

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=\ell\ln\mathcal{M}_{j},

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\psi},

\displaystyle\bm{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{A}.

(165)

The density reconstruction requires
\alpha\neq 0
.
The incompressible limit
s=0
,
\tau=0
, and
\rho=1
reduces
\bm{K}
to
-\bm{u}\times\bm{\omega}-\bm{J}\times\bm{B}
, so (
154
) and (
159
) recover the velocity- and magnetic-potential structure used by Vanon and Ohkitani.

Appendix C
Obstruction to a pure density Schrödinger reduction

It is natural to ask whether the pure density amplitude

R_{\alpha}\equiv\rho^{\alpha}

(166)

can itself satisfy a closed Schrödinger-type equation. From the continuity equation,

\partial_{t}R_{\alpha}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}R_{\alpha}=2\alpha\mu_{c}(\Delta\tau)R_{\alpha}.

(167)

Using (
18
), this becomes

\partial_{t}R_{\alpha}+2\mu_{s}\bm{\nabla}^{\perp}\chi\cdot\bm{\nabla}R_{\alpha}=2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\tau\cdot\bm{\nabla}R_{\alpha}+2\alpha\mu_{c}(\Delta\tau)R_{\alpha}.

(168)

Thus a first-order compressive transport term remains. More generally, for any smooth local point transform of the density alone,

R_{g}\equiv e^{g(s)}=F(\rho),

(169)

the continuity equation gives

\partial_{t}R_{g}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}R_{g}=2\mu_{c}g^{\prime}(s)(\Delta\tau)R_{g}.

(170)

This evolution generates no Laplacian of
R_{g}
: every density-only point transform remains a first-order transport-reaction equation. Consequently, a closed Schrödinger-type equation for a pure density variable cannot arise from a pointwise reparametrization of
\rho
alone.

We then enlarge the ansatz to a local mixed transform

\Psi_{g,\beta}\equiv e^{g(s)+\beta\tau}.

(171)

A direct calculation gives

(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\Psi_{g,\beta}=D\Delta\Psi_{g,\beta}+U_{g,\beta;D}\Psi_{g,\beta},

(172)

with

\displaystyle U_{g,\beta;D}

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\bigl(2g^{\prime}(s)+\beta\bigr)\Delta\tau+\mu_{c}\beta|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\bigl(V_{\Theta}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\bigr)-D\Bigl(g^{\prime}(s)\Delta s+\beta\Delta\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(g^{\prime\prime}(s)+g^{\prime}(s)^{2}\bigr)|\bm{\nabla}s|^{2}+\beta^{2}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\beta g^{\prime}(s)\bm{\nabla}s\cdot\bm{\nabla}\tau\Bigr).

(173)

Requiring a constant diffusion coefficient
D
and cancellation of the explicit
\Delta\tau
and
|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}
terms gives

D\beta=\mu_{c},\qquad 2g^{\prime}(s)+\beta=1.

(174)

Hence
g^{\prime}(s)
must be constant, so
g(s)=\alpha s+\mathrm{const}
, and therefore

\beta=1-2\alpha.

(175)

Thus the family
\Psi_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha}
already exhausts the local separable transforms of the form
e^{g(s)+\beta\tau}
with constant diffusion and exact elimination of the
\tau
-Laplacian terms.

Appendix D
Remarks on other transform classes

We also tested broader transform classes to check whether the density obstruction can be avoided.

1. Local transforms involving gradients

First consider a scalar transform that depends locally on the fields and their first spatial
derivatives,

Q=F\bigl(s,\tau,\chi,\bm{\nabla}s,\bm{\nabla}\tau,\bm{\nabla}\chi\bigr).

(176)

The key differentiated variables obey

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{\nabla}s

\displaystyle=2\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\Delta\tau-(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u})^{\!\top}\bm{\nabla}s,

(177)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\nabla}\tau

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}\bm{\nabla}\Delta\tau+\bm{\nabla}\!\bigl(\mu_{c}|\bm{\nabla}\tau|^{2}+V_{\Theta}\bigr),

(178)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\nabla}\chi

\displaystyle=\nu\bm{\nabla}\Delta\chi-\frac{1}{2\mu_{s}}\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{F}.

(179)

Hence any nontrivial dependence on
\bm{\nabla}s
,
\bm{\nabla}\tau
, or
\bm{\nabla}\chi
introduces third-order derivatives such as
\bm{\nabla}\Delta\tau
or
\bm{\nabla}\Delta\chi
. Therefore this local
scalar
gradient transform does not close to a second-order Schrödinger-type equation unless the gradient dependence is absent.

2. Fixed spatial nonlocal transforms

Let
K
be a time-independent spatial operator, for example
\Delta^{-1}
,
\bm{\nabla}\Delta^{-1}
, or
(-\Delta)^{-1/2}
, and define

Q_{K}\equiv KR_{g},\qquad R_{g}=F(\rho).

(180)

Using (
170
), we find

\partial_{t}Q_{K}+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}Q_{K}=[\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla},K]R_{g}+2\mu_{c}K\!\bigl(g^{\prime}(s)\Delta\tau\,R_{g}\bigr).

(181)

The transformed equation acquires the nonlocal commutator
[\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla},K]
, which vanishes only in very special flows. Thus a fixed nonlocal spatial transform does not naturally produce a local Schrödinger-type operator.

3. Auxiliary-field and doubled-amplitude formulations

Finally, we can write an exact doubled Schrödinger-type formulation. For any
\alpha\neq\frac{1}{2}
, we define the partner amplitudes

\Psi_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}\Theta^{1-2\alpha},\qquad\Psi_{1-\alpha}=\rho^{1-\alpha}\Theta^{2\alpha-1}.

(182)

Their product recovers the density exactly:

\rho=\Psi_{\alpha}\Psi_{1-\alpha}.

(183)

Moreover, each partner satisfies an equation of the form (
48
),
with parameters

D_{1-\alpha}=-D_{\alpha},\qquad\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{1-\alpha}=-\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}.

(184)

Thus the continuity sector admits an exact doubled vector-potential-coupled Schrödinger representation, although not a single pure density-carrying one.
