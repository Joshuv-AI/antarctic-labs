High resolution mapping of pleiotropic QTLs and candidate genes for seed quality using a whole genome resequencing approach in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.)
Authors: Runfeng Wang, Lu Huang, Muhammad Jawad Umer, Qianxia Yu, Qi Liu, Qing Lu, Hao Liu, Haifen Li, Shaoxiong Li, Yanbin Hong, Xiaoping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is a major oilseed crop, and seed quality particularly total oil content, the balance between oleic and linoleic acids, and protein level is a primary target for breeding and health oriented improvement. Recent advances in peanut genomics, including high quality reference genomes, have created new opportunities for precision breeding; however, comprehensive high density linkage maps coupled with functional validation of quality related loci remain limited.MethodsIn this study, we constructed a high density genetic linkage map comprising 4,746 bin markers spanning 3,328.40 cM, with an average marker interval of 0.70 cM. Using multi environment phenotypic data collected over four years, we identified 48 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) controlling eight seed quality traits, including oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, total oil content, and protein content.ResultsPleiotropy analysis revealed 20 QTL clusters, highlighting shared genetic control among multiple quality traits. Integrative genomic annotation further identified 20 candidate genes involved in fatty acid biosynthesis and lipid metabolism. Notably, haplotype analysis of Ahy_A09g042118 (AhFATB1) uncovered a functional non-synonymous SNP associated with significant variation in total oil content and fatty acid composition. The favorable haplotypes showed contrasting effects on oleic, linoleic, and palmitic acid accumulation without affecting protein content, indicating a key role of AhFATB1 in modulating carbon flux within seed lipid biosynthesis.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings elucidate the genetic architecture underlying peanut seed quality traits and provide valuable molecular targets for marker assisted selection, particularly for the development of high oil and high oleic peanut cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09138-w.Keywords: Peanut, Quality traits, Genetic mapping, Quantitative trait loci (QTLs)

Introduction

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is a vital oilseed and cash crop globally, serving as a significant source of oil and protein [1]. With ongoing economic development and the enhancement of living standards, there is an increasing demand for peanuts of superior quality. Consequently, identifying the genetic loci associated with quality traits in peanut is crucial for peanut breeding, which is essential to meet consumer demands for diverse peanut quality and to foster the growth of the global peanut industry.

Peanut comprises approximately 40–60% oil, 20–40% protein, and 10–20% carbohydrate, rendering it a valuable crop for both agricultural and nutritional applications [2]. The quality of peanut oil is affected by its fatty acid composition, including palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), oleic acid (C18:1), linoleic acid (C18:2), arachidonic acid (C20:0), behenic acid (C22:0), lignoceric acid (C24:0), among others [3]. Enhancing the content of oleic acid in peanut oil can improve its storage stability and prolong the shelf life, while excessive consumption of saturated fatty acids is associated with elevated levels of low density lipoprotein (LDL) in the blood, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4]. Consequently, breeding peanut varieties with high oil, high oleic acid, low saturated fatty acids, and high protein represents a crucial focus for the genetic enhancement of peanut quality. Deciphering the genetic architecture governing key quality traits in peanut provides a foundation for precision breeding strategies, through marker-assisted selection (MAS) and genomic prediction models.

Several studies have focused on identifying quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with quality traits in peanuts, such as oil content, protein content, and fatty acid composition [5–10]. Pandey et al. (2014) detected 78 main effect and 10 epistatic QTLs for oil content and oil quality traits using S-population (SunOleic 97R × NC94022) and T-population (Tifrunner × GT-C20) [5]. Similarly, Wang et al. (2015) identified 164 main effect and 27 epistatic QTLs for minor fatty acids with the same populations [3]. Moreover, Shasidhar et al. (2017) identified 8 QTLs for oil content and 21 QTLs for different fatty acids using two different F2 mapping populations [7]. Huang et al. (2015) identified 12 QTLs for 8 quality related traits using an F2:3 population (Zhonghua 10 × ICG12625) [10]. Liu et al. (2019) identified 7 QTLs for oil content and found a major and stable QTL, qOCA08.1, through a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population (Xuhua 13 × Zhonghua 6) [11]. Yang et al. (2023) detected two stable QTLs, qCOA08_1 and qCOA08_2, using an RIL population (JH5 × KX01-6) and identified that AhyHOF1 may play a critical role in peanut oil synthesis [12]. Although significant strides have been made in QTL mapping for peanut quality traits such as oil content and fatty acid composition, these traits remain inherently complex due to their polygenic architecture and intricate gene environment interactions. Therefore, further efforts are essential to identify more QTLs and genes related to peanut quality traits, as this will greatly aid in peanut variety improvement and molecular breeding initiatives.

With the advancement of high throughput sequencing technologies and the completion of the peanut genome sequencing, significant progress has been made in QTL mapping studies, enabling more precise identification of genetic determinants underlying agronomically important characteristics [13–15]. For instance, Wang et al. (2024) constructed a high density genetic map with 2,042 bins and 24,142 SNP markers, and identified two major QTLs for sucrose content in peanut [15]. By generating genome-wide, high density molecular markers (e.g., SNPs, indels) at scale, high throughput sequencing circumvents the limitations of traditional low-throughput methods, thereby enhancing both the statistical power and spatial accuracy of QTL detection [16–18]. Given this, high throughput sequencing technology and the development of diverse genetic mapping populations can provide novel insights into the genetic regulatory mechanisms governing peanut qualitative traits.

In this study, recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations derived from parental lines exhibiting significant divergence in peanut quality traits were used. Utilizing whole genome resequencing of the parental lines and F7 progenies, we constructed a high density genetic linkage map and systematically mapped QTLs associated with these traits, providing important references for molecular marker assisted breeding of peanut quality traits.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and phenotyping

A mapping population of 221 RILs (recombinant inbred lines, F7 generation) was developed by single seed descent from a cross between the parental lines Jihua 7 (JH7) and Kainong 1715 (KN1715), which differ in their fatty acid composition. Specifically, “KN1715” exhibits a higher oleic acid content (> 80%) and lower linoleic acid content (< 8%) as compared to “JH7”. The mapping population along with the parental lines were cultivated over four consecutive years (2016–2019) at the experimental site of the Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Guangzhou, China (23° 7′ 44.3784″ N and 113° 15′ 11.7000″ E). The plants were planted in the field with a spacing of 30 × 10 cm. All recommended agronomical practices were followed when conducting experimental procedures.

Phenotyping for oil content, fatty acids, and protein content

Key quality traits, including total oil content, fatty acid composition (oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid), protein content and amino acid content were measured in the RIL population using near infrared reflectance spectroscopy (NIRS) (Model XDS RCA, FOSS Analytical AB, Sweden, Denmark) and measured in percentages. Near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy (NIRS) was selected due to its high throughput, reproducibility, and established reliability for oil and fatty acid phenotyping in peanut breeding programs. The calibration models applied in this study were routinely used and previously validated, as reported in Liu et al. (2019) [19] and Lu et al. (2024) [20]. The successful identification of well-characterized loci such as FAD2A and FAD2B in the present study further supports the accuracy and robustness of the phenotypic dataset.

DNA isolation and whole genome resequencing of the mapping population

Genomic DNA was extracted from the first expanded leaves of seedlings derived from both the parental lines and the mapping population. DNA extraction was performed using a standardized protocol to ensure high-quality genomic DNA suitable for downstream applications. The sequencing libraries were prepared using these high purity DNA samples and whole genome sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform. For each sample at least 30 Gb of raw data were obtained, with 150 bp pair ended reads. Whole genome resequencing generated approximately 42.5 Gb of clean data per parental line and an average of 48.7 Gb per RIL, corresponding to an average sequencing depth of approximately 18 × per RIL based on the peanut reference genome size (~ 2.7 Gb). The raw sequencing data were processed to remove adapters and low quality sequences. Only, clean reads, with over 95% of bases exhibiting Phred quality scores ≥ 30 were retained for further analysis. The Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software [21] was employed to align the clean reads to the reference genome of Arachis hypogaea var. Fuhuasheng genome [22] (NCBI accession: PRJNA500997). The alignments were subsequently processed with Picard tools (http://sourceforge.net/projects/picard/) to sort the data and mark duplicated read. Sequencing depth across each base was estimated using BedTools [23]. Variant calling, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (InDels), was carried out using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) [24]. Variants were filtered based on predefined criteria using GATK VariantFiltration function with the following parameters: QD < 4.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, GQ < 20. The filtered variants were annotated using ANNOVAR [25].

Construction of a genetic linkage map

The identified variants (SNPs and InDels) were used as molecular markers for constructing a genetic map. The genotype of the parents was analyzed to categorize the markers into different segregation types. The markers showing distorted segregation (chi-square test P-value < 0.001), more than 10% missing data or greater than 20% heterozygous data were excluded from further analysis. Only markers exhibiting the aa x bb segregation pattern (where both parents were homozygous with different genotypes) were retained. The genotyping errors were corrected using the SMOOTH algorithm [26], missing data were imputed using the nearest neighbor method. Individuals with high heterozygosity rates (> 20%) or those exhibiting more than 30% missing genotypes were also excluded. The retained markers were then assigned to linkage groups (LGs) based on their positions on the reference genome. Markers within each LG were ordered according to their physical location on the genome. The genetic distances between markers, as well as the distances of each chromosome were calculated using the OrderMarkers2 module of Lep-MAP3 [27]. To access the quality of high, the following metrics were considered: the number of markers, the total genetic distances (cM), and the presence of gaps larger than 5 cM. To reduce potential noise and simplify the calculation, bin markers were selected from each genetic position within the high density genetic map. These bin markers were subsequently used to construct the bin map, offering a more efficient and less error prone representation of the genetic linkage.

QTL analysis and identification of the candidate genes

Quantitative trait loci (QTL) analysis was performed using the ‘scanone’ function of the R/qtl package, employing the interval mapping method [28, 29]. A significance threshold for QTL detection was established using 1000 permutations at a 5% confidence level, with a mapping step size of 1 cM. The position with the highest LOD (logarithm of odds) score within an interval was designated as the QTL peak. Adjacent significant peaks within a distance of 10 cM were merged into a single QTL using the ‘define.peak’ function of eqtl package. QTL confidence intervals were defined as the region extending from the peak until the LOD score dropped by 1.5 LOD support interval, with a maximum extension of 10 cM in either direction. If the LOD score did not decrease by 1.5 LOD support interval within 10 cM from the peak, the boundary was set at 10 cM. Given the very high marker density of the genetic map (average interval 0.70 cM), interval mapping (IM) provides sufficient resolution and statistical power to detect stable and biologically meaningful QTLs. Consistency of QTL detection across multiple environments and BLUE values was used as the primary criterion to prioritize robust loci and reduce false positives. The physical map of the identified QTLs was generated using MapChart v2.1 [30]. The genes located within the QTL confidence intervals were annotated and further analyzed based on the Arachis hypogaea var. Fuhuasheng genome [22], providing insights into potential candidate genes involved in the traits under investigation.

Haplotype analysis

Population resequencing data of peanuts [31] were utilized to deduce the haplotypes of the target gene through bioinformatic approaches. Subsequently, a systematic analysis was conducted to examine the significant variations in oil content and fatty acid composition, including levels of oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, and stearic acid, across different haplotypes. The haplotype analysis was performed using a previously published peanut diversity panel for which population structure and genetic relationships have been comprehensively characterized (Lu et al. 2024) [20].

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of AhFATB1(Ahy_A09g042118)

Four recombinant inbred lines (RILs) with different AhFATB1 haplotypes (AA haplotype: line 103 and 168; CC haplotype: line 202 and 238) were selected from the RIL population. Seeds from developing pods were sampled at three developmental stages (20, 40, and 60 days after flowering), with three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted from developing peanut seeds using a standard plant RNA extraction kit. First strand cDNA synthesis was performed using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using gene specific primers for AhFATB1 (AhFATB1-F: TGTCGCCTCAACCACTCTTG, AhFATB1-R: TGGGATATTCTGGCGCTGTC), with actin used as an internal reference gene (actin-F: GAGGAGAAGCAGAAGCAAGTTG, actin-R: AGACAGCATATCGGCACTCATC). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each sample.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2). The significance of trait differences between parental lines was assessed using an analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc tests (e.g., Tukey’s HSD) to compare means. The QTL analysis was performed using the R/qtl package [28, 29], employing the interval mapping method. To integrate phenotypic data across multiple environments, best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) were calculated and used for QTL mapping. BLUE values effectively reduce environmental noise and are widely applied in multi-environment genetic analyses for complex traits. QTLs detected consistently across individual years and BLUE values were considered environmentally stable and of high breeding relevance. Formal QTL × environment interaction modeling was not performed in the present study and is acknowledged as an important direction for future research.

Results

Phenotypic differences between parental lines

The parental lines “JH7” and “KN1715” displayed distinct phenotypic differences in their fatty acid profiles. “KN1715” consistently showed a significantly higher oleic acid content (> 80%) and lower linoleic acid content (< 8%) compared to “JH7,” across all four years of the study (2016–2019). These differences were evident in both the oil composition and total oil content, with “KN1715” also exhibiting superior oil quality in terms of oleic acid percentage.

Whole genome resequencing, SNPs detection and construction of high density genetic linkage map

A total of approximately 10.95 Tb of clean data (~ 73 billion clean reads) were generated for the parental lines and 221 recombinant inbred lines (RILs). From this data a total of 141,077 SNPs were identified across the RIL population. A density linkage map was constructed using, 4,746 bin markers, which were distributed across 20 linkage groups (LGs). The map spanned a total genetic length of 3328.402 cM with an average marker distance of 0.701 cM (Table 1, Fig. 1).

The genetic map of the RIL population

The genetic map consisted of two subgenomes: 2545 bins were assigned to the A sub-genome, which covered a map length of 1892.63 cM, while 2201 bins were assigned to the B sub-genome spanning 1435.78 cM. The number of SNPs identified in individual LGs varied widely, ranging from 907 SNPs on B07 to 14,596 SNPs on A03. The length of the LGs also showed considerable variation, with LG B07 being the shortest at 92.52 cM, and LGA09 being the longest at 275.62 cM. The number of bins per LG ranged from a minimum of 124 bins on B10 to a maximum of 332 bins in B04, indicating uneven distribution of markers across the genome. Current results provide a robust and high density genetic framework for the further study of traits in the mapping population. Segregation distortion analysis indicated that the majority of markers followed the expected Mendelian segregation ratio, with a small proportion exhibiting significant distortion (P < 0.05). Distorted markers were not excluded from map construction, as their inclusion did not affect overall marker order or map quality, consistent with previous high-density peanut linkage mapping studies. Comparison between the genetic linkage map and the reference genome demonstrated high collinearity between genetic and physical positions across all chromosomes, indicating accurate marker ordering and reliable map construction.

Phenotypic analysis of quality related traits

Phenotypic data for quality traits were collected from 221 RILs and their respective parental lines over four consecutive years (Table 2). The parental lines exhibited significant differences in several key quality traits. The female parent ‘KN1715’ showed significantly higher oleic acid (OA, C18:1) content, along with lower levels of linoleic acid (LA, C18:2) and palmitic acid (PA, C16:0), as compared to the male parent ‘JH7’ (Table 2). In the RIL population, substantial variation was observed in the content of these fatty acids, with coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 3.93% (for OA) to 61.48% (for LA). This variation is indicative of the genetic diversity within the mapping population and highlights the potential for identifying genetic loci associated with fatty acid composition. To assess the phenotypic distribution and correlation among quality traits, Best linear unbiased estimate (BLUE) values were calculated. The correlation analysis revealed strong positive correlation between linoleic acid and palmitic acid contents, as well as a significant negative correlation between linoleic acid and oleic acid contents (Fig. 2).

The genetic map of major QTLs for quality traits in the RIL population

QTLs for oleic acid (OA, C18:1) content

For oleic acid content, ten QTLs were detected and mapped on chromosomes A08 (1), A09 (8), and B09 (1), explaining the phenotypic variation ranging from 0.03% to 14.65%. Among these, qOA_A09_2 and qOA_B09_1 were identified as major effect QTLs, each explaining substantial phenotypic variation of 5.82% and 14.65%, respectively across multiple environments. Minor effect QTLs such as, qOA_A09_3, qOA_A09_4, qOA_A09_5, qOA_A09_7 and qOA_A09_8 exhibited smaller contributions, ranging from 0.09% to 3.44%. Some QTLs were only identified in specific years or through BLUE values, such as qOA_A08_1 (2019), with a phenotypic variation of 8.79% and qOA_A09_1 and qOA_A09_6 were identified in 2018 and with phenotypic variations of 0.29% and 0.53%, respectively.

QTLs for linoleic acid (LA, C18:2) content

Nine QTLs for linoleic acid were identified on chromosomes A08(1), A09(7), and B09(1), explaining phenotypic variation from 0.00% to 16.58%. Among these, qLA_A09_2 and qLA_B09_1 were the major effect QTLs, accounting for 7.00% and 16.58% of the phenotypic variation, respectively, and were detected in multiple environments. Minor QTLs (qLA_A09_3, qLA_A09_5, qLA_A09_6 and qLA_A09_7) were also detected across environments, with the phenotypic variations ranging from 0.05% to 2.15%. The QTL qLA_A08_1 (2019) explained 8.69% of the variation, while, qLA_A09_1 (BLUE), and qLA_A09_4 (2018), contributed to smaller phenotypic variations of 0.18% and 0.21%, respectively.

QTLs for palmitic acid (PA, C16:0) content

Eleven QTLs associated with palmitic acid content were detected on chromosomes A08(2), A09(7), B08(1) and B09(1), explaining the phenotypic variation ranging from 0.00% to 15.41%. The most prominent QTL, qPA_B09_1 accounted for 15.41% of the phenotypic variation across multiple environments. Other QTLs, including qPA_A09_2, qPA_A09_3, qPA_A09_5 and qPA_A09_6 were detected across environments, with phenotypic variation ranging from 0.22% to 3.78%. QTLs such as qPA_A08_1, qPA_A08_2 and qPA_A09_1 were identified in 2019 and through BLUE, explaining the phenotypic variation between 0.43% to 1.80%. QTLs such as qPA_A09_4 and qPA_B08_1 were only identified in 2019, with the phenotypic variation of 0.22% and 2.91%, respectively. Minor QTL, qPA_A09_7 was only identified through BLUE value, has the phenotypic variation of 0.42%.

QTLs for stearic acid (SA, C18:0) content

Three QTLs for stearic acid content were detected and mapped on chromosomes A08(1) and A09(2), explaining the phenotypic variation from 0.27% to 11.06%. The QTL qSA_A09_2 was identified in 2016 and through BLUE, with the maximum phenotypic variation of 11.06%. qSA_A08_1 identified in 2018, contributed the phenotypic variation of 10.07%, while qPA_A09_7 was only identified through the BLUE value, with a smaller phenotypic variation of 0.27%.

QTLs for arachidic acid (ARA, C20:0) content

Seven QTLs related to arachidic acid content were detected and mapped on chromosomes A04(2), A07(1), A09(3) and B03(1), explaining the phenotypic variation from 0.00% to 10.48%. The major QTL, qARA_A04_2 was identified in multiple environments, explaining the phenotypic variation upto 9.65%. Other QTLs such as qARA_A04_1, qARA_A07_1, qARA_A09_1, qARA_A09_2 and qARA_B03_2 were identified in 2018, with phenotypic variations ranging from 0.00% to 5.49%. qARA_A09_3 was identified in 2018, with the high phenotypic variation of 10.48%.

QTLs for oil content (Oil)

Two QTLs for oil content were detected and mapped on chromosomes A07(1) and A08(1), explaining the phenotypic variation from 0.13% to 8.01%. The QTL, qOil_A07_1 identified in 2019 and through BLUE, explained the phenotypic variation of 6.86%, and qOil_A08_1 was only identified in 2019, contributed 8.01% phenotypic variation.

QTLs for protein content (Prot)

Four QTLs related to protein content were detected and mapped on chromosomes A02(1), A04(1) and A07(2), explaining the phenotypic variation from 0.01% to 10.75%. The QTL, qProt_A07_1 was identified in multiple environments, contributed 1.22% to the phenotypic variation. Whereas qProt_A02_1 (2018), showed a phenotypic variation of 10.75% and qProt_A04_1 (2019), explained phenotypic variation of 4.53%. Minor QTLs such as, qProt_A07_2 was identified through the BLUE value, contributed a small phenotypic variation of 0.01%.

QTLs for amino acid content (AA)

Two QTLs for amino acid content were detected and mapped on chromosomes A02(1) and A07(1). The QTL, qAA_A02_1 identified in 2018, explained the phenotypic variation of 11.65%, while qAA_A07_1 identified through the BLUE value, accounted for phenotypic variation of 9.04%.

Haplotype variation in AhFATB1 is associated with seed oil content and fatty acid composition in peanut

Haplotype analysis of Ahy_A09g042118 (AhFATB1) revealed a functional single-nucleotide polymorphism (C to A) located near the 3′ end of the coding sequence, resulting in a non-synonymous amino acid substitution from proline to glutamine (Fig. 4A). This SNP defined two major haplotypes, CC and AA, which showed clear phenotypic differentiation in seed oil traits. Accessions carrying the CC haplotype exhibited significantly higher total oil content compared with the AA haplotype (Fig. 4F), whereas protein content did not differ significantly between the two haplotypes (Fig. 4G), indicating that AhFATB1 primarily affects lipid metabolism rather than protein accumulation. Analysis of fatty acid composition showed that the CC haplotype was associated with significantly higher palmitic acid and linoleic acid contents (Fig. 4C, D), while the AA haplotype displayed markedly increased oleic acid levels and a modest but significant increase in stearic acid (Fig. 4B, E). Given that AhFATB1 encodes a fatty acyl-ACP thioesterase involved in the release of saturated fatty acids during plastidial fatty acid biosynthesis, these results suggest that the Pro to Gln substitution alters enzyme activity or substrate preference, thereby redistributing carbon flux among fatty acid species. Consistent with the genetic and haplotype analyses, the expression data showed that AhFATB1 is actively expressed during seed development, supporting its involvement in lipid biosynthesis. Moreover, higher expression levels were observed in CC haplotype lines, further reinforcing the contribution of AhFATB1 to variation in seed fatty acid composition (Fig. 4H). Collectively, the haplotype analysis demonstrates that natural variation in AhFATB1 contributes substantially to seed oil content and fatty acid composition in peanut and provides a molecular basis for breeding strategies aimed at improving oil yield and quality.

Discussion

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) is an essential global oil and cash crop, widely cultivated and contributing substantially to food security and economic stability due to its dual role as a source of edible oil and protein. Consequently, elucidating the genetic architecture underlying peanut quality traits has become a major focus of agricultural research. Genetic linkage mapping forms the foundation for quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis, providing a framework to dissect complex traits. Advances in high throughput sequencing have revolutionized map construction, transitioning from initial reliance on SSR markers to the current use of whole genome resequencing, thereby dramatically increasing marker density, resolution, and genomic coverage [32–37]. Early work by Varshney et al., (2009) [32] produced the first time SSR-based genetic linkage map for cultivated peanut with 135 SSR loci. While Agarwal et al., (2018) [36] constructed the first sequence-based high-density map in peanut (Tifrunner × GT-C20) with a map length of 3120 cM and an average distance of 1.45 cM. More recently, Zhang et al., (2023) [14] constructed a high-density map with a length of 1216 cM and an average distance of 0.54 cM. In this study, we developed a high-density genetic map comprising 141,077 SNP markers, spanning 3328.402 cM with an average inter marker distance of 0.701 cM. Such high density genetic maps derived from whole-genome resequencing (WGR) offer superior advantages over traditional maps by offering abundance of molecular markers, like SNPs and Indels. This enhances the sensitivity of linkage disequilibrium analysis, enabling the detection of minor-effect QTLs that are often missed in low-density maps. Moreover, the strong collinearity between these genetic maps and the physical genome aids in the integration of genetic and genomic data, accelerating candidate genes identification via genome annotations.

A total of 48 QTLs associated with key quality traits were identified and mapped on 8 chromosomes, explaining the phenotypic variation explained (PVE) from 0.00% to 16.58%. Some QTLs spanned relatively large physical intervals, particularly on chromosomes such as A04. This is primarily attributable to local recombination suppression and uneven marker recombination rates, rather than low marker density, as the genetic map used in this study was highly saturated. Similar patterns have been reported in previous peanut QTL studies and are common in regions with complex genomic architecture. Several QTLs corresponds with previously reported loci. Notably the clusters, qOA_A09_2, qLA_A09_2, and qPA_A09_2 (A09, 12.50–18.50 cM) and qOA_B09_1, qLA_B09_1, and qPA_B09_1 (B09, 105.36–117.11 cM) co-localized with the FAD2A (Ahy_A09g041968) and FAD2B (Ahy_B09g099786) genes, respectively, which are well-documented regulators of oleic acid, linoleic acid content, and the O/L ratio [38–40]. The mutations we identified in FAD2A and FAD2B were consistent with earlier findings [41, 42]. Another important QTL cluster on chromosome A08 (95.00–214.00 cM) encompassing qOA_A08_1, qLA_A08_1, qPA_A08_1, and qOil_A08_1 (A08, 195.00–214.00 cM) aligned with prior studies [11, 12] and contained the candidate gene Ahy_A08g040760 (WRI1), a key transcription factor in the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway. Additionally, the interval for qOA_A09_5, qLA_A09_5, qPA_A09_5, qSA_A09_2 and qARA_A09_3 (A09, 30.75–58.00 cM) coincided with the previously reported qA09, reported by Xue et al., (2024) [43], harboring Ahy_A09g042422 (KCS) a gene critical for very long chain fatty acids (VLCFA) synthesis. Functional studies have confirmed that modulating AhKCS1/28 expression alters VLCFA content [44, 45]. The consistent identification of these QTLs across diverse populations and traits suggests either pleiotropic effects or tight genetic linkage, reinforcing the reliability of these genomic regions. The identification of numerous QTLs with relatively small individual effects is consistent with the polygenic nature of seed oil content and fatty acid composition in peanut. Such traits are influenced by the cumulative contribution of many loci, each with modest effects, rather than by a limited number of large-effect genes alone. Therefore, QTLs with low phenotypic variance explained (PVE) should be interpreted as components of a broader genetic network rather than as independent major determinants. n this study, particular emphasis was placed on QTLs consistently detected across multiple environments and BLUE values, as these loci are more likely to represent genetically stable effects with practical breeding relevance. Environmentally stable QTLs are especially valuable for crop improvement, as they are less sensitive to environmental fluctuations and more reliable for marker assisted selection.

Several genomic regions were identified as QTL clusters affecting multiple seed quality traits. Although overlapping confidence intervals suggest potential pleiotropy, we acknowledge that such regions may also represent tight linkage of multiple functionally related genes. Distinguishing true pleiotropy from linkage would require fine mapping or conditional QTL analyses, which were beyond the scope of the present study. Our research confirms established loci and provides novel insights. Through the analysis of overlapping QTL intervals, we identified 20 pleiotropic QTLs and 20 candidate genes associated with quality traits. Eleven QTL clusters were identified on chromosomes A02, A07, A08, A09, and B09, each influencing two or more traits. Such clustering may arise from closely linked distinct genes or from the pleiotropic action of a single locus. Reporting both major and minor QTLs provides a comprehensive view of the genetic architecture underlying seed quality traits. While only a subset of these loci may be immediately useful for breeding, the complete QTL landscape offers valuable insights for future fine mapping, genomic selection, and functional studies. A significant novel finding involves the cluster qOA_A09_3, qLA_A09_3, and qPA_A09_3 (physical position: 7.54–8.59 Mb on A09). Among the candidate genes identified, AhFATB1 emerges as a particularly compelling genetic candidate based on its co-localization with stable QTLs for oil content and saturated fatty acids, the presence of a nonsynonymous coding variant, and clear haplotype phenotype differentiation. FATB genes are well documented to regulate the release of saturated fatty acids from plastidial acyl-ACP pools, providing strong biological plausibility for their involvement in seed oil composition. Beyond AhFATB1, several additional candidate genes identified within stable QTL intervals are functionally associated with fatty acid biosynthesis, modification, and lipid assembly pathways. These include genes encoding fatty acid desaturases, acyltransferases, and enzymes involved in plastidial and endoplasmic reticulum lipid metabolism. Collectively, these genes participate in coordinated pathways regulating carbon flux from sucrose-derived precursors into fatty acids, triacylglycerol biosynthesis, and final oil accumulation in developing seeds. The convergence of multiple lipid-related genes within QTL regions underscores the complex and polygenic regulation of seed oil content and fatty acid composition in peanut. Such pathway-level associations are consistent with previous reports in oilseed crops, where variation in oil traits is controlled by interacting genes across fatty acid synthesis, elongation, desaturation, and storage lipid assembly pathways. Variant analysis revealed a nonsynonymous SNP mutation in exon 6 of AhFATB1 (A09_8003372, C:A), resulting in a proline-to-glutamine substitution within the conserved Acyl-ACP thioesterase (Acyl-ACP_TE) domain (Fig. 4A).

Further haplotype analysis demonstrated that the two haplotype groups defined by this SNP exhibited statistically significant differences across multiple key oil-related traits, including oil content and fatty acid composition (oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, and stearic acid levels) (Fig. 4B-G), supporting a potential functional consequence of this allelic variation. In addition, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that AhFATB1 is actively expressed during seed development, and differential expression trends were observed between haplotypes, further supporting its relevance to lipid biosynthesis.

Consistent with these genetic and expression based observations, Tang et al., (2022) demonstrated that CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of AhFatB reduces saturated fatty acid content, resulting in a low palmitic, high oleic acid phenotype [46]. Collectively, these findings support AhFATB1 as a genetically supported candidate gene contributing to natural variation in seed oil content and fatty acid composition, and suggest that it represents a promising breeding target complementary to the well-characterized FAD2 loci.

Peanut quality traits are classic polygenic quantitative traits. In this study, the PVE of individual QTLs was generally low (mostly < 10%), a phenomenon characteristic of complex traits where phenotypic variance is partitioned across numerous minor-effect loci, potentially masking the contribution of any single QTL [47–49]. These minor effect QTLs collectively shape the final phenotype through polygenic interactions. It should be noted that individual QTLs with very small phenotypic variance explained (PVE < 1%) were reported primarily to reflect the polygenic architecture of peanut seed quality traits and are not interpreted as independent major determinants. In this study, greater emphasis is placed on QTLs that were consistently detected across multiple environments and BLUE values, as well as on major and pleiotropic loci with clear biological relevance. Such an approach aligns with breeding-oriented genetic studies of complex quantitative traits. While the breeding value of a single minor QTL may be limited, strategies such as multi-gene pyramiding or genome editing (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9) to modulate several loci simultaneously hold significant promise for substantial trait improvement [50]. From a breeding perspective, the high density genetic map and stable QTLs identified in this study provide valuable resources for marker assisted selection (MAS) aimed at improving seed oil content and fatty acid composition in peanut. Major loci such as FAD2A/B and the genetically supported candidate AhFATB1 represent promising targets for the development of peanut varieties with enhanced oil quality, including high oleic acid and reduced saturated fatty acid content. Furthermore, the identification of stable QTLs across multiple environments enhances their reliability for use in breeding programs. The tightly linked SNP markers reported here can be readily incorporated into molecular breeding pipelines to accelerate the selection of superior genotypes, thereby contributing to the development of nutritionally improved peanut cultivars.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study constructed a high-density genetic linkage map, consisting of 4,746 bin markers, spanning a total genetic distance of 3,328.402 cM with an average inter-marker interval of 0.701 cM. Using multiyear phenotypic data, we identified 48 QTLs associated with eight major quality traits, including oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, oil content, and protein content. Pleiotropy analysis further revealed 20 QTL clusters, and subsequent genomic annotation identified 20 candidate genes involved in fatty acid metabolism. Together these results deepen our understanding of the genetic architecture governing key quality traits in peanuts and provide a valuable resource for marker assisted breeding. This foundation holds significant promise for accelerating the development of improved peanut varieties with enhanced oil quality and optimized nutritional profiles, particularly those targeting for high oleic acid content.
