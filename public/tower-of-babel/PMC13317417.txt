Assessment of correlation between effluent fluid ionized calcium and post-filter ionized calcium in critically ill patients undergoing regional citrate anticoagulation in continuous renal replacement therapy (RCA-CRRT)
Authors: Niroj Mali, Saroj Mali, Mingjin Dai, Lingxi Zhang, Fang Wang, Yingying Yang, Xiankun Sun, Hongxia Zou, Ling Zhang, Ping Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) is standard in continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Post-Filter ionized calcium (Post-Filter iCa) is the gold standard for monitoring anticoagulation efficacy, but it requires blood sampling. Effluent fluid iCa can be measured without blood sampling. This study assessed the correlation and agreement between Effluent Fluid iCa and Post-Filter iCa in critically ill patients undergoing RCA-CRRT.MethodsWe conducted a prospective non-interventional study from June to November 2024 at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Thirty-six critically ill patients receiving CVVHDF with RCA were enrolled, yielding 216 paired samples. Ionized calcium was measured in Pre-Filter, Post-Filter, and Effluent Fluid samples using a point-of-care analyzer. CRRT was performed using the Prismaflex system with ST150 filters and Oxiris tubing. Post-filter blood and effluent fluid samples were drawn simultaneously to ensure direct comparability.ResultsThis study analyzed 216 paired samples from 36 critically ill patients on CVVHDF with RCA to assess the relationship between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid ionized calcium. A moderate correlation was found (r = 0.551 [CI 0.45,0.64], p < 0.001). Bland-Altman analysis showed a mean difference of − 0.024 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.033 to − 0.015), with limits of agreement from − 0.153 (95% CI: −0.1682 to − 0.1378) to 0.105 mmol/L (95% CI: 0.0900 to 0.1204). Variance component analysis demonstrated that only 13.3% of variability (ICC = 0.134) was attributable to between-patient differences, while 86.7% reflected within-patient temporal variation. ICC for Post-Filter iCa reliability over time was 0.269, and for Effluent iCa was 0.146, indicating substantial dynamic changes. Agreement between methods was moderate at early time points (ICC 0.514–0.628 at 2–24 h) but declined at 48–72 h (ICC 0.424–0.435). Cluster analysis of serial calcium measurements identified three distinct profiles among 36 patients: a dominant cluster (66.7%, n = 24) with target-range levels; a high-calcium cluster (19.4%, n = 7), and a low-calcium cluster (13.9%, n = 5).ConclusionEffluent Fluid iCa demonstrates moderate correlation with Post-Filter iCa but substantial within-patient variability limits its use as a direct substitute. While it may offer some supplementary information for tracking anticoagulation trends, it cannot replace Post-Filter sampling at present. Cluster analysis identified distinct calcium phenotypes associated with filter lifespan, though further multicenter validation would be needed to explore potential clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05095-6.

Introduction

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is a vital treatment modality for critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI), particularly in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting [1, 2]. It allows for the gradual removal of waste products and excess fluid while maintaining hemodynamic stability [3, 4]. Among the various anticoagulation strategies employed during CRRT, regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) has emerged as a preferred method due to its ability to prolong filter lifespan and reduce the risk of bleeding complications associated with systemic anticoagulants like heparin [3, 5–9]. The KDIGO Clinical Practice Guidelines suggest RCA as an appropriate anticoagulant treatment for critically ill patients, especially those with bleeding risks [10, 11]. RCA works by using citrate to bind calcium in the blood, thereby preventing clotting in the extracorporeal circuit while maintaining systemic calcium levels through careful monitoring and supplementation [12–14]. CRRT is a critical intervention for managing acute kidney injury, particularly in critically ill patients. RCA is widely employed to prevent clotting in the CRRT circuit by binding ionized calcium (iCa), an essential factor in the coagulation cascade [5, 15]. Accurate monitoring of iCa levels is crucial to maintain therapeutic efficacy and minimize complications, such as filter clotting and patient bleeding. A range of factors, including anticoagulation strategies, the quality of vascular access, specific parameters of CRRT such as modality and blood flow rate, as well as hematocrit levels and overall blood coagulation, can significantly influence the survival of extracorporeal circuits; however, the underlying mechanisms that contribute to circuit failure are still not fully understood, leaving a gap in our knowledge that warrants further investigation [8, 9, 16–18]. This study examined whether Effluent Fluid iCa measurements could provide useful supplementary information for monitoring anticoagulation trends during CRRT with RCA.

Monitoring ionized calcium levels is crucial in patients undergoing RCA during CRRT. The target range for Post-Filter ionized calcium (Post-Filter iCa) is typically between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L, which is essential for preventing filter clotting while avoiding systemic hypocalcemia [14, 19, 20]. However, frequent blood sampling for iCa can lead to complications such as anemia and increased risk of infection, particularly in critically ill patients who may already be vulnerable. This has prompted investigations into alternative monitoring strategies that could reduce the need for invasive blood draws while still ensuring effective anticoagulation management.

Recent studies have explored the feasibility of using Effluent Fluid ionized calcium measurements as a surrogate for Post-Filter iCa monitoring. Effluent fluid, which is the fluid that exits the CRRT circuit, may reflect the anticoagulation status and citrate metabolism in a manner similar to Post-Filter blood samples. If Effluent fluid can reliably indicate Post-Filter iCa levels, it could significantly reduce the frequency of blood sampling, thereby minimizing the associated risks [21, 22]. The reliability of Effluent fluid measurements hinges on the correlation between Effluent Fluid iCa and Post-Filter iCa levels. Preliminary findings suggest a moderate correlation, indicating that Effluent Fluid could serve as a valid indicator of anticoagulation status during RCA and supports further investigation of Effluent Fluid as a monitoring option. This potential shift in monitoring strategy aligns with the broader trend in critical care towards minimizing invasive procedures and enhancing patient-centred care. We explored the relationship between Effluent Fluid iCa and Post-Filter iCa during RCA-CRRT by assessing the correlation and agreement.

Methods

Study design

A prospective non-interventional clinical study was conducted from June 2024 to November 2024 at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. The study received ethical approval from the institutional review board, Approval Number: 2024(1248) and was in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consents were obtained from either patients or legal representatives. For patients who were unable to provide consent due to their critical condition, consent was obtained from their legally authorized representatives.

Participants

36 critically ill patients with 216 paired samples undergoing CVVHDF-CRRT with indications for treatment were enrolled as shown in Table 1. Patients were selected from various departments within the hospital. Critically ill adult patients (≥ 18 years) requiring CRRT with RCA were enrolled. Patients were excluded if they had known calcium metabolism disorders, were receiving calcium supplements outside protocol which specifically implies that patients were excluded if they received bolus intravenous calcium or oral calcium supplements (e.g., calcium carbonate, calcium acetate) for reasons other than protocol-directed RCA management (e.g., treatment of hypocalcemia unrelated to CRRT or routine supplementation for chronic kidney disease), or had circuit failure within 2 h of initiation. Circuit failure within 2 h was defined as any unplanned circuit discontinuation occurring less than 2 h after initiation due to: Circuit clotting (visible thrombus preventing continuation); Elevated transmembrane pressure (> 250 mmHg) unresponsive to troubleshooting Air leak or circuit rupture; Catheter dysfunction preventing adequate blood flow (< 100 mL/min). Elective circuit changes for routine filter replacement (every 72 h) or clinical interventions (e.g., patient transport to operating room) were excluded from this definition.

Sampling location and timing

Post-Filter Blood and Effluent Fluid samples were collected simultaneously from two distinct ports on the Prismaflex ST150 CRRT circuit: (1) the Post-Filter sampling port, located immediately distal to the hemofilter and proximal to the venous return line; and (2) the Effluent Fluid sampling port, situated on the Effluent Fluid line upstream of the Effluent Fluid collection bag. The Post-Filter port accesses blood exiting the filter before calcium replacement, while the Effluent port draws fresh ultrafiltrate directly from the circuit—not from pooled fluid in the collection bag.

Synchronized collection procedure

Operators performed simultaneous sampling to minimize temporal variation in regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) status. Using standardized 1-mL syringes, Post-Filter blood and Effluent Fluid were drawn within 30 s of each other. Samples were obtained after RCA stabilization (≥ 15 min following citrate rate adjustments). Collection times were recorded to the minute on the CRRT flowsheet.

Sample processing

Post-Filter blood was collected into dry-heparin syringes (10–20 IU heparin/mL blood) to prevent ex vivo clotting. Effluent Fluid required no anticoagulation. Both samples were immediately de-bubbled, sealed with rubber stoppers to prevent air exposure, and analyzed within 2 min at room temperature (20–25 °C) using the Roche cobas b 123 point-of-care analyzer.

CRRT protocol and parameters

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was performed using the Prismaflex system (Baxter Healthcare, Deerfield, IL, USA) equipped with ST150 hemofilters and Oxiris tubing circuits both having a membrane surface area of 1.5 m² (Baxter, USA). Vascular access was established through a double-lumen catheter inserted into either the femoral or internal jugular vein, as determined by the attending physician. All patients were treated in continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) mode. The prescribed Effluent Fluid dose ranged from 20 to 35 mL/kg/h, in accordance with international recommendations. Replacement fluid was administered postdilution, using commercially available hemofiltration solutions (4 L per bag). The replacement fluid was calcium-containing solution, 4 L/bag (Chengdu Qingshan Likang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), containing anhydrous glucose 10.6 mmol/L, chloride 118 mmol/L, magnesium 0.797 mmol/L, calcium 1.6 mmol/L, and sodium 113 mmol/L. Dialysate and replacement fluid flow rates were adjusted to achieve the target Effluent Fluid dose, typically at approximately 1,000 mL/h each, with a balanced 1:1 ratio. The blood flow rate was initially set at 150 mL/min and subsequently titrated according to clinical tolerance. Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) was implemented for all patients. A 4% trisodium citrate solution (200 mL, Chengdu Qingshan Likang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) was infused into the arterial limb of the extracorporeal circuit at an initial rate of 180 mL/h, with adjustments made to maintain a Post-Filter ionized calcium concentration within the target anticoagulation range of 0.25–0.35 mmol/L, as illustrated in Fig. 1. To compensate for calcium losses, intravenous calcium gluconate (10%) was continuously infused via a separate central venous line at a rate of 4.5-8 mL/h, titrated to maintain concentrations between 0.90 and 1.20 mmol/L. Additional solutions, including 5% sodium bicarbonate and potassium chloride, were administered as required to maintain acid–base and electrolyte homeostasis. Filters were routinely replaced every 72 h in accordance with manufacturer recommendations or earlier if clotting, catheter dysfunction, or clinical interventions necessitated circuit discontinuation.

Data collection

Anticoagulation efficacy was assessed by monitoring ionized calcium (iCa) concentrations at different sites of the extracorporeal circuit. Blood samples were drawn from the venous port after the plasma filter to determine Post-Filter iCa, with the target range set at 0.25–0.35 mmol/L. Citrate infusion rates were adjusted accordingly to maintain this range. Additional measurements of iCa were obtained from prefilter blood and Effluent Fluid samples to evaluate calcium balance across the circuit, as shown in Fig. 1. All samples were analyzed using a bedside blood gas analyzer (COBAS b123, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Measurements were performed at baseline (0.5–2 h after initiation of CRRT) and subsequently at 6-hour intervals during treatment, up to 72 h, or until circuit discontinuation. iCa calcium measurements were taken from pre-filter, Post-Filter, and Effluent Fluid samples. Measurements were conducted at intervals of 2, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, based on the patient’s condition. The frequency of sampling was adapted to patient condition and clinical stability to ensure both reliable monitoring and practical feasibility at the bedside. All laboratory parameters—including total HCO₃⁻, bicarbonate; pH, potential of hydrogen; Ca Calcium level; Cl Serum Chloride; Lac Serum Lactate; K Serum Potassium were analyzed using standard laboratory methods at West China Hospital at the start of CRRT. Measurements were obtained before, during, and after CRRT. These assessments were performed using the i-STAT 300 analyzer (Abbott Laboratories).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 24 (IBM, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Data are mean ± SD or median [IQR]. Pearson’s correlation assessed relationships. Bland-Altman analysis evaluated agreement between iCa methods. p* < 0.001 was statistically significant. Python 3.9 with scikit-learn, pandas, and lifelines packages were also used for statistical analysis. Graphical presentations were also generated using Python visualization tools.

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the correlation between different anticoagulation assessment methods, while the Bland-Altman method evaluated the agreement between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter iCa values. In addition, regression analysis was also performed. Pearson linear correlation, Bland-Altman analysis and regression analysis were employed to evaluate the relationship and agreement between Post-Filter iCa and Effluent Fluid iCa levels.

Results

Patient and CRRT characteristics

In total, 36 critically ill patients with 216 paired samples CVVHDF, with a mean age of 58.4 ± 20.99, Male: n (%) 26 (72%). Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The primary indications for CRRT were sepsis (68%), post-cardiac surgery (22%), and other causes (10%). Bleeding risk profiles included: cerebral hemorrhage (n = 8), hematuria (n = 16), gastrointestinal hemorrhage (n = 10), and thrombocytopenia (n = 6). Baseline liver function tests demonstrated substantial heterogeneity among the study population. The mean total bilirubin was 35.9 ± 53.2 µmol/L (95% CI: 17.9–53.9), with 41.7% of patients exhibiting hyperbilirubinemia (> 21 µmol/L). Transaminase levels were elevated in a similar proportion, with mean ALT of 79.1 ± 118.5 U/L (95% CI: 39.0–119.2) and mean AST of 63.9 ± 57.9 U/L (95% CI: 44.3–83.5). Baseline liver function was assessed using the SOFA liver component based on total bilirubin. The majority of patients (66.7%, n = 24) had normal bilirubin levels (< 20 µmol/L, SOFA score 0). Mild hyperbilirubinemia (SOFA score 1, 20–32 µmol/L) was observed in 5.6% (n = 2), moderate hyperbilirubinemia (SOFA score 2, 33–101 µmol/L) in 19.4% (n = 7), and severe hyperbilirubinemia (SOFA score ≥ 3, >102 µmol/L) in 8.4% (n = 3). The mean net ultrafiltration volume was 105.28 mL/h, corresponding to an ultrafiltration rate of 16.34 mL/kg/h. Dialysate and replacement fluid were delivered at 750 mL/h each. The mean filter lifespan was 54.8 ± 20.2 h (95% CI: 48.0–61.7 h). Nearly 39% of filters achieved the full 72-hour treatment duration, while early failure (< 24 h) occurred in 13.9% of patients, as shown in Table 1. Changes in acid–base balance and serum sodium are summarized in Table 2, showing significant improvements in pH and whereas no significant improvement was seen in Base Excess BE. Table 2 presents longitudinal changes showing significant pH improvement from 7.38 ± 0.27 (pre-CRRT) to 7.45 ± 0.05 (post-CRRT), with corresponding bicarbonate stabilization. Importantly, no cases of hyperkalemia or hypernatremia were detected during any CRRT sessions.

All samples were taken for ionized calcium measurements and were assessed by a point-of-care analyzer. Correlations and agreements between two methods were performed by Pearson linear analysis and Bland–Altman analysis accordingly. The cumulative mean Post-Filter ionized calcium was 0.30 ± 0.06 mmol/L, and the mean ionized calcium level of Effluent Fluid iCa was 0.33 ± 0.07 mmol/L. The observed difference in mean ionized calcium (iCa) concentration—where Effluent Fluid iCa values were approximately 0.03 mmol/L higher mean Post-Filter iCa (0.33 ± 0.07 vs. 0.31 ± 0.06 mmol/L) reflects systematic bias from ultrafiltration dynamics. Effluent Fluid represents cell-free ultrafiltrate without hematocrit effects, with slight concentration of citrate-calcium complexes. This directional bias (Effluent Fluid iCa > Post-Filter iCa) carries safety implications: RCA titration to Effluent Fluid iCa targets yields slightly lower actual Post-Filter iCa, reducing hemorrhagic risk. The ionized calcium level of Effluent Fluid was moderately correlated with Post-Filter ionized calcium in all continuous renal replacement therapy patients, where, r = 0.551[CI 0.45,0.64], P value < 0.001. A moderate positive correlation exists between the two variables, and the correlation is statistically significant (p < 0.001). This difference of approximately 0.0239 mmol/L was highly significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the sampling location has a measurable and consistent effect on reported iCa values, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Agreement between post-filter and effluent fluid ionized calcium

Bland–Altman analysis showed that the mean difference of ionized calcium between two sampling sites in all continuous renal replacement therapy patients was − 0.024 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.033 to − 0.015), with limits of agreement ranging from − 0.153 (95% CI: −0.1682 to − 0.1378) to 0.105 mmol/L (95% CI: 0.0900 to 0.1204). The differences between the two variables are evenly distributed around the mean bias line, with 95% of data points falling within the Limits of Agreement (LoA), as shown in Fig. 4. The negative bias (− 0.024 mmol/L) indicates that, on average, Effluent Fluid ionized calcium is slightly higher than Post-Filter ionized calcium. The limits of agreement ( ≈ − 0.15 to + 0.10 mmol/L) demonstrate moderate agreement, which is reasonable given the biological and technical variability inherent to RCA-CRRT. The asymmetry of the limits suggests greater variability at lower calcium values, consistent with stronger citrate effect and non-steady-state conditions.

Variance components and reliability

To understand the sources of variability in the relationship between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa, we performed variance component analysis using a mixed-effects model. The between-patient variance was 0.000577, while the within-patient variance was substantially larger at 0.003755 and total variance is 0.004332. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) calculated from from mixed model variance components was 0.1335, indicating that only 13.3% of the total variability in the difference between measurements could be attributed to stable patient characteristics. The remaining 86.7% represented within-patient variation over time, highlighting the dynamic nature of the citrate-calcium equilibrium during CRRT and the importance of serial monitoring. The within-patient standard deviation was 0.061 mmol/L, yielding robust 95% limits of agreement accounting for clustering of -0.144 to 0.096 mmol/L, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Cluster Analysis Methodology: To identify distinct patterns in the relationship between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid ionized calcium (iCa) concentrations, we performed an unsupervised cluster analysis.

The following methodological details outline our approach:

Clustering Algorithm: We employed k-means clustering due to its suitability for continuous, normally distributed data and its computational efficiency with our sample size. The algorithm partitions observations into *k* clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance (inertia), with each observation assigned to the nearest cluster centroid.

Variable Selection and Standardization: The analysis focused on two key continuous variables: Total Post-Filter iCa and Total Effluent Fluid iCa. Prior to clustering, both variables were standardized using z-score normalization (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) to ensure equal weighting, as k-means is sensitive to differences in variable scales.

Determining the Number of Clusters: The optimal number of clusters was identified using the elbow method, which plots the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) against increasing *k* values. The “elbow point” where the rate of WCSS reduction sharply diminishes which indicates the most parsimonious cluster solution. Based on this heuristic, we selected k = 3 clusters, balancing model fit with interpretability. Cluster Assignment and Validation: K-means clustering was applied using the *k*-means + + initialization method to improve centroid seeding, with 10 random starts to avoid local optima. Formal internal validation (e.g., silhouette analysis) was performed. Cluster stability was assessed qualitatively by examining the distinctness of cluster centroids and the separation of observations in the reduced-dimensional space.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified clusters, we examined whether cluster membership corresponded to meaningful differences in patient characteristics or circuit performance. Specifically, we compared mean Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa values across clusters and assessed whether clusters aligned with clinically recognizable patterns (e.g., high Post-Filter with low Effluent Fluid iCa suggesting citrate accumulation, or low Post-Filter with high Effluent Fluid iCa indicating circuit dysfunction). These patterns may inform personalized anticoagulation strategies or signal underlying physiological differences.

Discussion

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) with regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) is increasingly utilized because citrate chelates ionized calcium (iCa²⁺) in the extracorporeal circuit, thereby preventing clot formation while minimizing systemic bleeding risk. Citrate acts by binding free iCa²⁺, a key cofactor in the coagulation cascade and the resulting citrate-calcium complexes are largely removed in the Effluent Fluid, which constitutes the waste stream of CRRT. Monitoring calcium, especially ionized calcium in Post-Filter Blood and Effluent Fluid, is essential for ensuring effective anticoagulation and patient safety [1, 2, 8, 9, 14, 15].

Summary of findings

In this study, we demonstrated a statistically significant but moderate positive correlation between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter ionized calcium (iCa) levels r = 0.551 [CI 0.45,0.64], P value < 0.001. Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean bias of − 0.024 mmol/L, with 95% confidence interval, with limits of agreement ranging from − 0.153 to 0.105 mmol/L. The regression model is statistically significant (p < 0.001), but the independent variable (“Post-Filter”) explains only 30% of the variance in the dependent variable (“Effluent Fluid”). The robust limits of agreement derived from the mixed-effects model (-0.144 to 0.096 mmol/L) are slightly narrower than the traditional limits that ignore clustering (which we calculated as approximately − 0.153 to 0.105 mmol/L). This difference, while modest, is important for statistical validity. Traditional Bland-Altman analysis assumes all 216 observations are independent, artificially narrowing confidence intervals and potentially leading to overly optimistic interpretations. Our mixed-effects approach correctly accounts for the correlation of multiple measurements within patients, providing more accurate estimates for clinical decision-making. The mixed-effects model revealed that only 13.3% of the total variability in the difference between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa could be attributed to between-patient factors such as age, diagnosis, baseline liver function, or individual metabolic characteristics. The remaining 86.7% represented within-patient variation over the 72-hour treatment course, reflecting the dynamic nature of the citrate-calcium equilibrium within each patient’s CRRT circuit. This finding has profound implications that the relationship between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa is not primarily determined by who the patient is, but rather by what happens during the treatment; changes in citrate infusion rates, adjustments in calcium supplementation, filter aging, circuit microclotting, and the patient’s evolving metabolic status all contribute to the substantial temporal variability. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) derived from these variance components was 0.133, confirming that only 13.3% of the variance is attributable to between-patient differences. This low ICC indicates that patients cannot be reliably stratified into “high agreement” or “low agreement” categories based on baseline characteristics, the agreement varies primarily within patients over time. Our finding of moderate agreement during the first 24 h (ICC: 0.514–0.628) indicates that approximately 51–63% of the variance in Effluent Fluid iCa can be explained by its relationship with Post-Filter iCa during this period. This level of agreement suggests that effluent monitoring could serve as a potential adjunct for tracking anticoagulation trends in the early phase of CRRT. However, the decline to poor agreement at 48–72 h (ICC: 0.423–0.435) is a novel and clinically important finding.

The identification of three distinct calcium profiles through cluster analysis provides important insights into the dynamics of regional citrate anticoagulation during CRRT. Our finding that patients in the high-calcium cluster (Cluster 1) experienced significantly shorter filter lifespans is clinically meaningful and supports the pathophysiological rationale that inadequate calcium chelation, reflected by elevated post-filter iCa, leads to enhanced coagulation activation and premature circuit clotting. The dominant cluster (Cluster 0), comprising two‑thirds of the cohort, represents patients in whom anticoagulation was successfully maintained within target ranges. This group achieved excellent filter survival, with 66.7% of circuits lasting the full 72‑hour treatment duration. These results align with established evidence that maintaining post-filter iCa between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L effectively prevents circuit thrombosis. The low-calcium cluster (Cluster 2) also showed a trend toward reduced filter lifespan. From a clinical perspective, these findings support the potential utility of real-time calcium monitoring to identify patients at risk of circuit failure. The high-calcium profile may serve as an early warning sign requiring prompt adjustment of citrate infusion rates. Future studies should evaluate whether algorithm-driven titration based on cluster identification can improve filter longevity and reduce treatment interruptions.

Ionized calcium levels in neither the Post-Filter circuit nor the Effluent Fluid dialysate showed a statistically significant correlation with patient age. The lack of correlation suggests that patient age is not a primary determinant of calcium kinetics within the CRRT circuit under a standardized protocol. This supports using age-neutral calcium and citrate management, focusing on direct monitoring rather than age-based adjustments. Although our findings indicate only a moderate correlation, Effluent Fluid iCa measurements may offer supplementary information for observing anticoagulation trends. Further studies are needed to determine whether this could translate into clinical benefit.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings are consistent with earlier reports showing that Effluent Fluid iCa can reflect Post-Filter anticoagulation status in citrate-based CRRT protocols. A Previous study by Zhang et al. [22] reported a strong correlation between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid ionized calcium (iCa) in CRRT patients (r = 0.96, p < 0.001), consistent across CVVH, CVVHD, and CVVHDF modalities. Bland–Altman analysis showed a minimal mean difference of − 0.02 mmol/L (95% CI − 0.09 to 0.04), indicating good agreement between the two measurements. Another previous study by Antonic et al. [21] reported a mean Post-Filter iCa of 0.39 ± 0.14 mmol/L and filtered plasma iCa of 0.33 ± 0.11 mmol/L, with no significant correlation to bedside clotting time. Bland–Altman analysis showed modest agreement between filtered plasma and Post-Filter iCa (mean difference − 0.07 mmol/L; 95% limits − 0.23 to 0.10 mmol/L). Circuit clotting scores were excellent [21]. Antonic et al. previously reported that Post Filter iCa showed a limited correlation with bedside clotting time during RCA in plasma exchange. This finding suggests that iCa measurements alone may not fully predict actual clot formation in the circuit, as other factors including blood flow rates, filter characteristics, and patient-specific coagulation status may also play a role. While our study did not assess clinical endpoints such as circuit clotting events, we recognize that the clinical utility of any monitoring strategy ultimately depends on its ability to prevent thrombosis without causing bleeding. Similar to those studies, we found that Effluent Fluid iCa values closely approximated Post-Filter iCa levels within the recommended anticoagulation range of 0.25–0.35 mmol/L. However, unlike some prior work that reported stronger correlations, our study observed only a modest correlation (r = 0.551[CI 0.45,0.64]), with moderate predictive power in regression analysis (R² = 0.30). This difference may be due to sample size, heterogeneity of patients, or variability in CRRT prescriptions. The regression model showed that Post-Filter iCa explains only approximately 30% of the variance in Effluent Fluid iCa (R² = 0.30), indicating that multiple physiological and technical factors contribute to Effluent Fluid calcium concentrations. These include citrate dose, blood flow rate, membrane characteristics, and Effluent Fluid volume. Indeed, Mariano and colleagues previously demonstrated that net citrate and calcium removal by the filter significantly correlate with Effluent Fluid volume (r = 0.85 and 0.78, respectively) during CVVHDF with citrate anticoagulation [23]. Thus, Effluent Fluid iCa reflects not only the target circuit iCa but also the rate of citrate-calcium complex removal into the Effluent Fluid, which varies with treatment intensity. Accordingly, Effluent Fluid iCa cannot be used as a standalone quantitative predictor of Post-Filter iCa. Importantly, Bland–Altman analysis showed a relatively modest limit of agreement that exceeded the accepted clinical tolerance of ± 0.1 mmol/L within the therapeutic Post-Filter iCa range (0.25–0.35 mmol/L). Efficient anticoagulation during RCA-CRRT requires maintaining post-filter iCa within a narrow therapeutic window of 0.25–0.35 mmol/L. In our cohort, a substantial number of Post Filter iCa measurements fell above this target range, reflecting the challenges of achieving consistent anticoagulation in clinical practice. Furthermore, a difference of 0.1 mmol/L between Post Filter iCa and Effluent Fluid iCa represents a 33% variation relative to the target of 0.3 mmol/L, a clinically meaningful discrepancy. While the mean bias between the two measurements was small (-0.024 mmol/L), this value does not capture the wide individual variability shown in the Bland-Altman plot (limits of agreement: -0.153 to 0.105 mmol/L). For an individual patient, the difference between Effluent Fluid and Post Filter iCa could be as large as 0.15 mmol/L, which is sufficient to misclassify anticoagulation status. Therefore, the mean bias alone should not be interpreted as evidence of interchangeability. These findings indicate that Effluent Fluid ionized calcium is not interchangeable with Post-Filter measurements. Rather, it may best be considered a potential tool for tracking anticoagulation intensity or observing trends, not a direct substitute for Post-Filter blood sampling.

Oudemans‑van Straaten and Ostermann highlight that Post-Filter ionized calcium (iCa) measurements during regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) may be unreliable because standard blood gas analyzers are not validated for the low iCa ranges seen Post-Filter. This can cause inaccurate readings, potentially misleading clinicians about anticoagulation adequacy. Their findings support the need for complementary monitoring approaches, such as Effluent Fluid iCa measurement [24]. The correlation observed in our study between Post-Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa values suggests that Effluent Fluid iCa may offer supplemental information and warrants further assessment as a potential monitoring tool in the management of citrate anticoagulation.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study population was relatively small (36 patients, 216 paired samples), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. The study was conducted in a single-center setting, and the results may not apply to different CRRT machines, citrate protocols, or patient populations. This study focused primarily on the biochemical agreement between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter ionized calcium measurements. We recognize that factors influencing calcium binding, including total protein, albumin, and systemic ionized calcium, are important for citrate anticoagulation. Although these parameters were not recorded in the present analysis, they may affect calcium binding and citrate kinetics. Inclusion of these parameters in future studies might provide additional insight. Clinical outcomes such as circuit clotting, or citrate-related complications were not assessed. We did not systematically record visual inspection data for circuit clotting, which is a limitation of the study. While these endpoints were beyond the scope of the current investigation, their absence limits the ability to draw conclusions about the clinical impact of Effluent Fluid iCa monitoring.

We recognize that underlying medical conditions prevalent in our cohort, including sepsis and potential liver dysfunction, as well as prior procedures such as cardiopulmonary bypass, may influence iCa, albumin, and coagulation status. Such factors could affect calcium binding and citrate kinetics, possibly contributing to the observed variability. Future studies should consider stratifying by these clinical characteristics to assess their impact on the correlation between Effluent Fluid and Post Filter iCa.

For clustering of serial measurements taken at 2, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, we used standard k-means clustering. As an exploratory technique, k-means assumes spherical clusters of similar size, does not account for within-patient temporal correlation, and may be influenced by outliers. More sophisticated methods such as longitudinal trajectory clustering, latent class mixed models, or functional clustering would be more appropriate for identifying serial phenotypes. Therefore, our cluster findings should be considered exploratory and require validation using these methods in future studies, preferably with additional clinical covariates to strengthen interpretability.

We did not assess the diagnostic accuracy of Effluent Fluid iCa for detecting Post-Filter iCa values outside the therapeutic range (0.25–0.35 mmol/L). While we report correlation and agreement, these metrics do not directly answer whether Effluent Fluid iCa can reliably identify clinically relevant under-anticoagulation (Post-Filter iCa > 0.35 mmol/L) or over-anticoagulation (Post-Filter iCa < 0.25 mmol/L). Future studies should evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values of effluent iCa for these clinically important thresholds.

Our study has limitations regarding sampling methodology. Despite simultaneous collection, the 30-second interval between Post-Filter blood and Effluent Fluid draws may introduce minor temporal discordance during rapid RCA adjustments. Effluent Fluid temperature at the sampling port (ambient-cooled) differed from Post-Filter blood (37 °C), potentially affecting ionized calcium measurements. We did not assess inter-operator variability in sampling technique. Future studies should validate these findings using automated simultaneous sampling devices and temperature-controlled specimen handling and incorporate these clinically relevant outcomes to better evaluate the utility of Effluent Fluid iCa as a monitoring tool. As highlighted by Oudemans-van Straaten and Ostermann, standard blood gas analyzers are not formally validated for the low iCa ranges encountered in Post Filter samples during RCA-CRRT [24]. This technical limitation may also apply to Effluent Fluid iCa measurements, as the same devices were used in our study. Therefore, some of the observed variability between Post Filter and Effluent Fluid iCa could reflect analyzer imprecision at low calcium concentrations rather than true biological variation alone. Future studies should consider using analyzers specifically validated for low iCa ranges or incorporate complementary methods for calcium measurement. A methodological consideration is that Effluent Fluid contains a mixture of dialysate, replacement fluid, and ultrafiltered plasma water, which differs substantially from whole blood or plasma. We did not perform formal validation studies to confirm that this complex matrix does not affect iCa electrode readings. Therefore, we acknowledge this as a limitation, and our findings should be interpreted with this in mind. Future studies should include formal matrix validation.

Given the inherent complexity of CVVHDF, including the interplay of blood composition, patient-specific diseases, physical processes such as diffusion and convection, and the dynamics of citrate anticoagulation, differences between Effluent Fluid and Post Filter iCa measurements are to be expected. Perfect agreement between the two sampling sites was never anticipated. Rather, our objective was to determine whether a sufficiently strong correlation might exist to be considered for further investigation as a potential monitoring tool. The modest correlation observed in our study reflects this underlying complexity and underscores the challenges of simplifying anticoagulation monitoring in a multifactorial system. Prospective studies are warranted to elucidate which of the factors discussed above most strongly influence the difference between Effluent Fluid iCa and Post Filter iCa.

The regression analysis indicated that Effluent Fluid iCa explained only 30% of the variance in Post-Filter iCa, suggesting that additional physiological or technical variables may need to be considered. While a modest correlation was observed, Effluent Fluid iCa measurements may offer supplementary information that could support efforts to reduce blood sampling frequency. However, given the wide limits of agreement, Effluent Fluid iCa should not be viewed as interchangeable with Post-Filter levels, but rather as a potential adjunctive marker for observing anticoagulation trends.

The use of regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) in continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) has gained prominence due to its effectiveness in prolonging filter lifespan and reducing bleeding complications compared to systemic anticoagulation methods like heparin [1, 3–6]. Citrate protocols for hemodialysis, especially in continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), typically advise measuring Post-Filter ionized calcium (iCa) to evaluate anticoagulation. Anticoagulation effects start when iCa falls below 0.5 mmol/L and are fully effective at 0.25 mmol/L, with a standard target range for Post-Filter iCa set between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L. Moreover, citrate hemodialysis commonly employs calcium-free dialysate, which further decreases Post-Filter iCa levels [25, 26]. However, the monitoring of ionized calcium levels remains a critical aspect of managing RCA, as citrate binds calcium, which is essential for the coagulation cascade. This study observed a moderate correlation between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter ionized calcium levels in patients receiving CRRT with regional citrate anticoagulation. However, Bland-Altman analysis revealed limits of agreement that exceeded the accepted clinical tolerance within the therapeutic Post-Filter iCa range. These findings suggest that while Effluent Fluid iCa is not interchangeable with Post-Filter measurements, it may offer adjunctive information for observing anticoagulation trends. This approach could potentially support a reduction in blood sampling frequency, though further evaluation is needed. The use of Effluent Fluid for iCa measurements may streamline monitoring processes in clinical settings, enhancing patient safety. Future studies should explore the long-term implications of this practice and its effects on clinical outcomes.

Importance of ionized calcium monitoring

The consistent mean difference observed between the sampling sites, with relatively narrow confidence intervals, adds robustness to this finding. The moderately higher iCa in the Effluent Fluid may be explained by the continued mixing of dialysate/replacement fluid with blood beyond the Post-Filter port, whereas the Post-Filter sample is obtained earlier in this process. This observed difference highlights a potential source of variation in calcium measurement during CRRT. Our findings contribute to the understanding of calcium dynamics in the circuit and underscore that measurements from these two ports may not be directly equivalent. These results emphasize the value of standardized sampling practices within a given protocol to ensure consistent clinical interpretation and management. Further research could explore the correlation of this difference with specific CRRT parameters, such as blood flow rate and filter type, to refine predictive models for calcium balance. Monitoring ionized calcium is crucial in patients undergoing RCA during CRRT. The recommended target for Post-Filter iCa is typically between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L to prevent filter clotting while avoiding systemic hypocalcemia [22]. Traditional methods require frequent blood draws to assess Post-Filter iCa, which can lead to complications such as anemia, and increased infection risk [3, 17, 18].

Clinical implications

Given these limitations, Effluent Fluid monitoring may offer supplementary information for tracking anticoagulation trends while potentially reducing the need for frequent blood sampling. By reducing the number of blood draws, this approach could lower the risks of anemia and infection. Frequent blood sampling during CRRT has been associated with cumulative blood loss, which may contribute to ICU-acquired anemia in critically ill patients. Effluent fluid iCa measurement, which does not require blood withdrawal, could theoretically reduce this risk. Additionally, each manipulation of the CRRT circuit or vascular access device for blood sampling increases the potential risk of central line-associated bloodstream infections. Repeated sampling also contributes to clinician workload and interrupts patient care. Effluent fluid iCa measurement, which does not require blood withdrawal or circuit manipulation, could theoretically reduce these risks [17, 18, 27–30]. However, these outcomes were not assessed in the current study and require future investigation. Such a strategy aligns with patient-centred care by minimizing invasive procedures without compromising safety. This approach aligns with the growing emphasis on patient-centred care, where minimizing invasive procedures is a priority. Additionally, reducing the workload associated with frequent blood sampling can allow healthcare teams to focus more on direct patient care and other critical tasks.

Clinical implications of misclassification

An important consideration when evaluating any surrogate marker is the potential clinical consequence of incorrect interpretation. If Effluent Fluid iCa were used as a direct substitute for Post-Filter iCa measurements, misclassification could lead to inappropriate citrate dose adjustments with potentially serious consequences.

Scenario 1: False Reassurance (Effluent Fluid iCa within range but Post-Filter iCa too high).

If Effluent Fluid iCa suggests adequate anticoagulation (e.g., within or below target range) but the actual Post-Filter iCa is > 0.35 mmol/L (insufficient anticoagulation), the clinical risk is circuit thrombosis. Inadequate anticoagulation during RCA-CRRT can lead to: Premature circuit clotting and filter loss; Interrupted dialysis delivery and inadequate solute clearance; Increased blood loss from circuit changes; Elevated nursing workload and increased healthcare costs; and Potential for under-dialysis and its associated complications.

Scenario 2: False Alarm (Effluent Fluid iCa suggests inadequate anticoagulation, but Post-Filter iCa is therapeutic).

Conversely, if Effluent Fluid iCa is elevated (suggesting insufficient anticoagulation) but the actual Post-Filter iCa is within the therapeutic range (0.25–0.35 mmol/L), clinicians might inappropriately increase the citrate dose. This could result in: Excessive citrate accumulation, particularly in patients with impaired liver function; Metabolic complications, including metabolic alkalosis, hypernatremia, and systemic hypocalcemia; Increased risk of citrate toxicity, manifested as ionized hypocalcemia, tetany, or cardiac arrhythmias; and Potential for bleeding complications due to excessive systemic anticoagulation.

The therapeutic window consideration

The therapeutic target for Post-Filter iCa during RCA is narrow (0.25–0.35 mmol/L), representing a delicate balance between preventing circuit clotting and avoiding citrate toxicity. Our finding that the limits of agreement (− 0.153 to + 0.105 mmol/L) exceed this narrow therapeutic window (± 0.1 mmol/L tolerance) underscores why Effluent Fluid iCa cannot safely replace Post-Filter measurements for dose titration. Even a modest misclassification could push a patient from adequate anticoagulation into a risk zone for either circuit clotting or citrate toxicity.

Risk-benefit consideration

While reducing blood sampling frequency offers clear benefits which decreased iatrogenic anemia and reduced infection risk. These advantages must be weighed against the potential harms of anticoagulation errors. Our data suggest that Effluent Fluid iCa is best positioned as an adjunctive trend monitor, but not as a replacement for confirmatory Post-Filter iCa measurements, particularly when citrate dose adjustments are contemplated. We emphasized that until prospective trials demonstrate the safety of Effluent Fluid-based monitoring protocols, Post-Filter blood sampling remains the standard for guiding citrate dose titration.

Future directions

Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and establish standardized protocols for Effluent Fluid sampling and analysis. Future research should also investigate the impact of Effluent Fluid iCa monitoring on filter lifespan, citrate accumulation, and patient outcomes, including morbidity and mortality. Future studies may achieve stronger correlations between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter ionized calcium by addressing sources of analytical and physiological variability inherent to current CRRT practice. The use of real-time, continuous Effluent Fluid calcium sensors could substantially reduce measurement noise and timing discrepancies associated with intermittent sampling. Recent advances in organic electrochemical transistor (OECT)‑based sensors enable ultra-sensitive, accurate, and stable real-time detection of multiple ions—including calcium—in CRRT Effluent Fluid, with performance comparable to standard clinical methods. Incorporating such continuous Effluent Fluid calcium sensors could greatly improve monitoring by minimizing noise and timing errors inherent to intermittent sampling [31]. In addition, strict sampling during verified steady-state anticoagulation periods, avoiding phases of citrate dose adjustment or circuit instability, is likely to enhance the fidelity of observed associations. From an analytical perspective, application of mixed-effects models to account for repeated measurements within patients may further clarify the true within-subject relationship between Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter calcium levels. Ultimately, integration of continuous sensing with closed-loop citrate delivery systems guided by machine-learning algorithms may allow more precise anticoagulation control while simultaneously improving the strength and clinical relevance of Effluent Fluid calcium–based monitoring.

We wish to emphasize that all measurements were performed using rigorous, standardized protocols with calibrated equipment. Repeated statistical verifications confirmed the robustness of our findings. The observed variability may be attributable to both biological and technical heterogeneity rather than measurement error, and we have been transparent about this limitation throughout the manuscript. We acknowledge that our current dataset limits our ability to determine precisely which of these mechanisms might have contributed to the correlation patterns observed in individual patients. A number of factors might help explain the modest correlation observed, among them inter-patient variability in citrate metabolism, differences in operational parameters such as blood flow and citrate infusion rates, fluctuations in effluent flow rate that may impact calcium-citrate complex removal, the potential for filter performance to decline over time, and patient-specific characteristics including liver function, which can affect citrate clearance. This represents an important area for future investigation. Prospective studies designed to systematically collect data on liver function, citrate levels, and detailed CRRT prescription parameters would enable multivariable analyses to identify independent predictors of Effluent Fluid and Post-Filter iCa discordance. Such studies could help identify which patient populations or clinical scenarios are most suitable for Effluent Fluid-based monitoring approaches. These trials should incorporate clinically relevant outcomes, including: Filter lifespan and circuit clotting events, Citrate-related complications (metabolic alkalosis, hypocalcemia, hypernatremia), Incidence of bleeding events, Transfusion requirements Nursing workload, and protocol adherence. Such studies would help establish whether Effluent Fluid iCa monitoring can safely reduce blood sampling frequency without compromising anticoagulation efficacy or patient safety. Until such evidence is available, Effluent Fluid iCa should be viewed as a complementary tool for trend observation rather than a replacement for Post-Filter fluid iCa measurements when making citrate dose adjustments. While this study provides moderately promising results, further research is necessary to validate these findings across larger and more diverse patient populations. Future studies should also explore the long-term outcomes associated with using Effluent Fluid for iCa monitoring, including its impact on filter lifespan, citrate-related complications, and overall patient morbidity and mortality. Additionally, the development of standardized protocols for Effluent Fluid sampling and analysis will be crucial for widespread adoption in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we observed a moderate correlation between Effluent fluid and Post Filter ionized calcium levels in critically ill patients receiving RCA-CRRT, with a limited mean bias. Given the multiple variables that influence Effluent iCa, its clinical applicability as a marker of sufficient calcium-citrate chelation is limited. Effluent Fluid iCa may offer some supplementary information for tracking anticoagulation trends, though it cannot replace Post Filter sampling at present. Further large-scale, multicenter validation would be needed to explore whether standardized protocols might be developed. This study provides preliminary observations that may inform future research in this area.
