Following in the footsteps of Virginia Lynch: the attitudes of Israeli emergency department nurses toward the role of forensic nurses: a descriptive cross-sectional study
Authors: Sivan Cukerman, Orli Grinstein-Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to examine ED nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and their perceived importance of these tasks.MethodA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between November 16, 2023, and April 5, 2024, among 438 nurses working in Israeli hospital EDs. Data were collected using an online questionnaire that included demographic and professional characteristics, self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks, and perceived importance of these tasks. Data were analyzed using Pearson correlations, independent-samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, and hierarchical linear regression.ResultsThe participants’ ages ranged between 23 and 76 years (M = 39.66, SD = 8.83), and their mean professional experience was 10.75 years (SD = 9.32, Range = 0.1–43). Only a minority of participants had received training in forensic nursing during their studies (7.5%), but most (88.4%) expressed a wish to receive training in this area. The self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks was relatively low (M = 2.26) on a scale from 1 to 5, indicating that ED nurses reported performing these selected tasks infrequently. The perceived importance of performing these tasks was relatively high (M = 4.01) on a scale from 1 to 5, indicating that ED nurses recognized their importance. Older nurses, nurses with a master’s degree, nurses with greater professional experience, and nurses wishing to receive training in forensic nursing were positively associated with perceived importance.ConclusionsThe low self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks, together with their high perceived importance, may reflect gaps in forensic nursing education and training, as well as a need to further clarify organizational support for managing forensic-related situations in ED practice. These findings suggest a need to integrate forensic nursing content into nursing education and to further examine institutional and regulatory frameworks relevant to forensic-related situations in ED practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04893-5.Keywords: Nursing, Forensic, Frequency, Importance, ED, Israel

Introduction

Violence is a public health issue that affects people of all ages worldwide. Each year, over a million people around the world die from self-inflicted, interpersonal, or collective violence [1]. According to data from Emergency Departments (ED) in Israel in 2022, over 630,000 patients were admitted due to deliberate injuries, traffic accidents, work accidents, and unintentional injuries [2]. The consequences of violence are commonly seen in the ED, which is often the first place where victims and/or perpetrators of violence are brought for medical care [3]. Thus, nursing staff may be among the first healthcare professionals to encounter these patients, communicate with their families, handle their personal possessions, and identify potential forensic evidence [4]. When nursing staff care for victims of violence, they are also providing care with forensic relevance [3].

However, in Israel, forensic nursing is not currently established as a formal nursing role or independent specialty. Structured forensic nursing education is limited, and little is known about ED nurses’ self-reported performance of forensic-related tasks, their perceived importance of these tasks, and their educational needs. This study therefore addresses an important national gap by examining these issues among ED nurses in Israeli hospitals.

Background

Violence is a major public health issue, and many victims of interpersonal, sexual, domestic, child, and elder abuse seek care in emergency and hospital settings [5, 6]. Forensic nursing is a nursing specialization that addresses the needs of victims of violence and trauma, including medical, psychosocial, and medico-legal aspects of care [7–10]. In the ED, nurses may be among the first professionals to encounter victims, identify forensic-related circumstances, document injuries, protect personal belongings, and recognize potential evidence relevant to later legal processes [3, 4, 8–10].

Previous studies have described several potential benefits of forensic nursing, including improved patient support, empowerment, access to services, professional competence, documentation, evidence recognition, and coordination between healthcare and legal systems [11, 12]. Forensic nursing has been described as an integral component of contemporary medico-legal systems. According to Lynch, trained forensic nurse examiners may improve the management of interpersonal and sexual violence cases, reduce unnecessary delays and workload for forensic physicians, support evidence recovery, and strengthen coordination between healthcare institutions and law-enforcement agencies. However, due to the limited forensic and medico-legal content in traditional nursing education, nurses may not always identify medico-legal circumstances, and valuable evidence may be lost or compromised. Therefore, the presence of a forensic nurse on each shift could provide a more immediate medico-legal response to trauma and emergency cases, reduce delays in care, and help protect patients’ ability to seek justice [12].

ED nurses care for patients with both unintentional and deliberate trauma, including injuries related to violence, sexual assault, domestic violence, child or elder abuse, and suicide attempts [13]. These patients may arrive with forensic evidence on their bodies, clothing, or personal belongings, but ED teams often prioritize lifesaving interventions, resuscitation, and acute clinical care. Without appropriate awareness, training, protocols, and resources, forensic evidence may be missed, lost, or compromised, potentially affecting patients’ access to justice [14].

Several studies among ED nurses have found that between 84% and 97% of participants considered forensic nursing to be very important, particularly regarding legal knowledge, the need for trained forensic nurses, the integration of forensic nursing into nursing curricula, forensic identification training, and the introduction of legal protocols [15–18]. Training is important for increasing the efficiency of nurses in collecting and preserving the chain of evidence, providing tools for behavior when encountering perpetrators or victims, and avoiding missing the identification of forensic cases due to a lack of knowledge and education related to the process of testimony and court procedures [8, 9, 19]. Training in forensic nursing will produce positive outcomes, including improved patient care, security care, reducing the load on the healthcare system, improving the confidence and skills of nurses providing care, and higher patient satisfaction [20].

When examining the perceived importance of representative forensic cases, several studies found that the cases ranked highest in importance were documenting child abuse versus accident injury, collecting evidence from forensic patients including sexual assault victims, skills in giving bad news, informing medical bodies about receiving a patient who was dead on arrival without receiving treatment, and providing evidence in court [15, 18, 21, 22].

Previous studies investigating the association between the self-reported frequency of performing forensic-related tasks and the perceived importance of such tasks found that the average grades of importance were significantly higher the more frequently such tasks were required [15, 21, 22]. Regarding the correlation between self-reported task-performance frequency and perceived importance, Emami et al. [21] found a positive correlation between the frequency grades and the importance grades only for twenty-four representative cases (p < .05) out of 39. Also, there was no significant correlation between the overall frequency of performance grades and the importance grades [21]. This is in contrast with Lynch [22], who found a positive Pearson correlation of 0.452 at a significance level of p = .022 regarding the association between frequency of performance and importance [22]. The nurses’ competence is based on their knowledge and skills [23], and insufficient knowledge and skills regarding forensic matters may impair their ability to provide quality care in such cases [15]. According to the theory of direct experience in attitudes, the frequency of performing tasks increases the accessibility and strength of the attitude towards the tasks, and is therefore expected to change the strength of the relationship between demographic or professional characteristics and the importance of performance [24, 25].

There is limited information describing the state of forensic training in healthcare and the extent to which ED staff receive such training [26]. Forensic nursing education may include topics such as legal photography, interpretation of injuries, elder abuse and neglect, sexual assault, child abuse and neglect, drug abuse, evidence recognition, documentation, and medico-legal communication [22, 26]. Without appropriate education and training, ED nurses may lack the knowledge and confidence needed to identify forensic-related situations, preserve relevant information or evidence, and support patients whose care may later involve legal proceedings [4, 10, 11, 27]. Therefore, expanding forensic nursing education in undergraduate and continuing education programs is important for improving ED nurses’ preparedness and for strengthening the interface between healthcare and legal systems [10, 11, 26].

The study

Aims

To examine ED nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and their perceived importance of these tasks.

Objectives

The results of the study will help understand ED nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and the importance they attribute to these tasks. The non-identification of these patients and nursing care lacking in forensic aspects may later harm the patients’ legal rights. Based on the findings of this study, when forensic nursing is introduced to Israel, follow-up studies can be conducted in the ED and other departments to evaluate the effectiveness of educational training and the perceptions of the topic’s importance.

Research questions

What is ED nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks? What is their perceived importance of performing these tasks? Is there an association between the self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and their perceived importance? Is there an association between various socio-demographic and professional variables and the perceived importance of selected forensic-related tasks? Does nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks moderate the association between socio-demographic and professional variables and perceived importance?

Methods

Research design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between November 16, 2023, and April 5, 2024. A convenience sample was recruited through posts published in general social media groups for nurses in Israel, mainly on Facebook and WhatsApp. These groups were not limited to ED nurses. Therefore, the invitation post specified that participation was open only to registered nurses currently working in hospital EDs. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and the questionnaire link was accessible only after participants read the study information and provided electronic informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

Registered nurses working in the ED in any Israeli hospital (adult ED, pediatric ED, obstetrics and gynecology ED, and mental health ED).

Research tools

The study’s data were collected using an anonymous online questionnaire through Google Forms, distributed over social networks. The original questionnaire, Emergency Department Forensic Nursing Survey Questionnaire, was developed by the researcher Virginia Lynch in 1990 in the USA [22]. The questionnaire used in the present study was therefore not developed specifically for this study. It has previously been used in published studies [15, 21, 22]. High internal consistency was reported for both frequency of performance (α = 0.90) and perceived importance (α = 0.92) [22]. The questionnaire was translated using standard forward–backward translation procedures from English to Hebrew and back from Hebrew to English, and was adapted for nursing practice in Israel. The research was conducted in Hebrew. In order to adapt the questionnaire for nursing in Israel, 2 statements were removed that describe practices of ED nurses that do not exist in Israel: “ED nurse determines the sex of skeletal remains brought to the ED”; and “ED nurse reads dental charts and is aware of forensic dentistry techniques”. In addition, 8 statements were removed that described the practice of specialist forensic nurses, a role that does not exist in Israel. The third section, with open questions, was also removed.

The questionnaire was first pilot-tested with five ED nurses to assess clarity and functionality of the online form. The pilot data were not included in the final analysis.

The final adapted quantitative research tool included a two-part questionnaire:

First part: A general questionnaire including socio-demographic and professional details of each participant, such as gender, year of birth, religion, education, professional experience, and workplace. In addition, the questionnaire included questions about background and training in forensic nursing.

Second part: The adapted questionnaire included 29 selected forensic-related items from the previously published Emergency Department Forensic Nursing Survey Questionnaire. These items were not intended to capture the full range of possible forensic nursing activities, but rather selected forensic-related tasks adapted to the Israeli context. The items described the practice of ED nurses (e.g., “ED nurses help the police investigators in performing their work”). It should be noted that the questionnaire includes a broad spectrum of forensic-related tasks. Some tasks may overlap with general ED nursing responsibilities, such as identifying signs of abuse, documenting injuries, observing relevant circumstances, and reporting suspicious cases according to institutional procedures. Other tasks, such as collecting and preserving bodily tissues or fluids as evidence, maintaining chain-of-custody procedures, preparing medico-legal reports, or serving as a mediator between the hospital and law-enforcement or forensic medicine authorities, may require specialist forensic training, formal protocols, institutional authorization, and medico-legal consultation. Therefore, in this study, the term “forensic-related tasks” refers broadly to tasks with forensic relevance in ED nursing practice and does not imply that all such tasks can be performed independently by any ED nurse without appropriate authorization. The participants were asked to grade for each statement the frequency of performing the task described on a 5-level Likert scale (1 = “never” up to 5 = “always”). In this study, “frequency” refers to nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing the selected forensic-related tasks included in the questionnaire, and not to the actual incidence of forensic cases or encounters with forensic patients in the ED.

Similarly, the participants were asked to grade the perceived importance of each task described in the statements on a 5-level Likert scale (1 = “not important at all” up to 5 = “very important”) (Appendix 1). The self-reported frequency and perceived importance scores were calculated by averaging all items, so that higher scores represented higher self-reported task-performance frequency and higher perceived importance, respectively. The internal consistency found in the current study was similar to that reported in the original study: α = 0.91 for frequency and α = 0.95 for perceived importance.

Statistical analysis

Before performing the statistical analyses, the study variables were examined for their normal distribution [28]. Additionally, the means, standard deviations, and ranges of the study variables were presented. Then, the associations between socio-demographic and professional variables and perceived importance were examined using Pearson correlations, independent-samples t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance for continuous, dichotomous, and categorical socio-demographic and professional variables, respectively. The association between self-reported task-performance frequency and perceived importance was tested using a Pearson correlation. A hierarchical linear regression model was conducted in three steps to predict the importance. In the first step, the socio-demographic and professional variables found to be significant in the preliminary tests. In the second step, the self-reported task-performance frequency variable was introduced. In the third, final step, all the two-way interaction terms between the socio-demographic and professional variables and self-reported task-performance frequency were added. The interaction was probed using simple slopes analysis. The enter method was employed for Steps 1 and 2, in which the main effects were entered based on the study hypotheses and preliminary analyses. In Step 3, a stepwise procedure was used only for the two-way interaction terms, as an exploratory approach to identify the most parsimonious moderation model among several possible interaction effects. Therefore, the moderation findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in future theory-driven studies. Continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis [29, 30], and categorical variables were coded using effect coding (i.e. -0.5 and 0.5) [30, 31]. The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 29, with the PROCESS macro (model 1) [30] employed for probing the interaction. The research hypotheses were examined at a significance level of 5%, two-tailed.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ben-Gurion University Institutional Ethics Committee, approval no. 43-2023, on 15 November 2023. The questionnaire was distributed via social media networks (Facebook, WhatsApp). Informed consent to participate was obtained electronically from all participants before completion of the questionnaire. At the start of the questionnaire, the participants received information about the study’s purpose, its risks, privacy, and confidentiality, and their right to participate voluntarily, to refuse to answer any question, and to withdraw at any time without possible consequences. This study might cause psychological discomfort for the participants due to personal memories of treating forensic patients. This issue was mentioned at the start of the questionnaire, stressing the availability of free mental health assistance, with a telephone number provided.

Results

After filtering, 438 registered nurses working in hospital EDs were included in the study. The participants’ ages ranged between 23 and 76 (M = 39.66, SD = 8.83). The participants’ mean professional experience was 10.75 years (SD = 9.32, Range = 0.1–43).

Correlation between self-reported frequency of task performance and perceived importance

A small-to-moderate positive correlation was found between nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and their perceived importance, r(436) = 0.27, 95% CI [0.18, 0.36], p < .001. Higher self-reported task-performance frequency was associated with higher perceived importance.

Additional findings

Differences between ED types: Differences were found between ED types in nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and in their perceived importance. Nurses working in mental health EDs reported the highest task-performance frequency, obstetrics and gynecology EDs showed the lowest reported task-performance frequency, while integrated EDs (adults and pediatric) showed the lowest perceived importance.

Importance of training: This was examined using a 5-level Likert scale (1 = “not important at all” up to 5 = “very important”). Very high importance (M = 4.38, SD = 0.87) was found for including forensic nursing content in the generic nursing curriculum.

Advanced forensic nursing education and specialty training exist in several countries; however, such formal training is currently not available in Israel. In Israel, undergraduate nursing programs include content on violence against women, children, and older adults, but forensic nursing is not taught as a complete dedicated course. In addition, no advanced forensic nursing training currently exists.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined ED nurses’ self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks, their perceived importance of these tasks, the association between them, and their associations with socio-demographic and professional characteristics. In examining the hypotheses, the first hypothesis that ED nurses would report a high frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks was refuted. The second hypothesis, that ED nurses would report high perceived importance of performing selected forensic-related tasks, was confirmed. The third hypothesis, which assumed associations between socio-demographic and professional characteristics and perceived importance, was partially confirmed. The fourth hypothesis, which assumed a positive association between self-reported task-performance frequency and perceived importance, was confirmed. The fifth hypothesis, that the associations between socio-demographic and professional variables and perceived importance would be moderated by self-reported task-performance frequency, was partially confirmed. Because the interaction term was selected using a stepwise exploratory procedure, this moderation finding should be interpreted cautiously and should be confirmed in future studies using theory-driven regression models. The current study indicates a significant gap between ED nurses’ low self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and the high perceived importance they attribute to these tasks. The study’s findings suggest that ED nurses are exposed to forensic-related situations in their clinical work and recognize the importance of forensic-related tasks.

Education and training gap

However, only a minority of participants reported receiving any formal forensic nursing training during their nursing studies or after entering clinical practice, while most expressed a wish to receive such training. This finding highlights an important educational gap. Previous studies have also reported low self-reported performance frequency of forensic-related tasks among ED nurses [15, 21, 22]. This may be explained, at least in part, by limited knowledge and insufficient training, which aligns with studies emphasizing the need for focused forensic nursing education for ED staff [8, 21, 32–34].

The findings also suggest that nurses may attribute lower importance to tasks that are less familiar or perceived as outside routine nursing practice. In the present study, the items with the lowest perceived importance were serving as a mediator in forensic matters between the hospital/victims and the police or National Institute of Forensic Medicine, and using scientific knowledge from legal medicine. These tasks may be perceived as exceeding the boundaries of the nursing role or as requiring additional professional preparation, formal protocols, and interprofessional collaboration [21, 35]. Therefore, expanding forensic nursing content in undergraduate nursing education and continuing professional development may improve nurses’ preparedness, confidence, and ability to identify and respond appropriately to forensic-related situations.

Clinical and organizational implications

The findings also have clinical and organizational implications for ED practice. The ED work setting, culture, and policies have been described in the literature as possible barriers to nurses’ awareness and management of patients exposed to violence and abuse. These barriers may include time constraints, staff turnover, limited opportunities for training and skill development, inefficient reporting and referral mechanisms, and insufficient resources to support both staff and patients [10].

In the present study, the tasks performed most frequently were those that appeared to be more clearly embedded in institutional procedures. For example, the most frequently reported task was reporting to the responsible person, according to institutional procedures, when patients arrive dead and their death was not observed by anyone. Other tasks with higher-than-average self-reported performance frequency included identifying signs of physical abuse of children and assisting medical staff in cases of suspicious deaths. In contrast, the tasks with the lowest self-reported performance frequency were those that may be less clearly embedded in routine ED nursing procedures or may require additional authorization, training, institutional protocols, and interprofessional coordination, such as serving as a mediator in forensic matters between the hospital/victims and the police or National Institute of Forensic Medicine, and collecting and preserving bodily tissues and fluids as evidence.

These findings suggest that ED nurses’ self-reported performance of selected forensic-related tasks may be shaped not only by individual knowledge and training, but also by organizational factors, such as the clarity of local procedures, role boundaries, referral pathways, access to relevant resources, and opportunities for interprofessional collaboration with medical, legal, law-enforcement, and forensic medicine professionals.

Policy implications for Israel

In Israel, formal national guidance regarding forensic evidence collection has been established for specific contexts, particularly the care of sexual assault victims in designated acute centers [36]. However, the findings of the present study suggest a need to further examine how broader forensic-related situations are addressed in routine ED nursing practice, particularly in relation to local procedures, role boundaries, referral pathways, training, and interprofessional collaboration. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that ED nurses are authorized to independently collect forensic evidence or maintain chain-of-custody procedures outside approved institutional and regulatory frameworks. Rather, they highlight the importance of clarifying institutional procedures and professional responsibilities in forensic-related situations encountered in ED settings.

These findings support the need to define forensic nursing competencies in Israel within undergraduate nursing education, continuing professional development, and ED standards. Such competencies should focus on identification of forensic-related situations, accurate documentation, awareness of evidence preservation, referral pathways, and interprofessional collaboration with medical, legal, law-enforcement, and forensic medicine professionals. In this context, forensic nursing education should be understood as a foundation for safe and appropriate clinical response, rather than as independent authorization for forensic evidence collection outside approved institutional and regulatory frameworks.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is the relatively large sample of ED nurses (n = 438), including nurses from different types of EDs, such as adult, pediatric, obstetrics and gynecology, and mental health EDs.

This study also has several limitations. First, the use of a convenience sample recruited through social media may have introduced selection bias, as nurses who were more active in professional online groups or more interested in forensic nursing, legal aspects of care, trauma, or violence-related cases may have been more likely to participate. Second, the sample included only ED nurses and therefore does not represent all Israeli nurses working in other hospital departments or community settings. Third, non-Jewish nurses were underrepresented in the sample, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader nursing workforce in Israel. Fourth, the questionnaire was adapted from an older instrument and modified for the Israeli context; although it demonstrated high internal consistency in the present study, further validation is needed. Fifth, the use of self-report data may be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions regarding the associations between self-reported task-performance frequency, perceived importance, training, and professional characteristics. In addition, the moderation analysis included a stepwise exploratory procedure for selecting interaction terms; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in future studies using theory-driven models.

Conclusions

This is a preliminary study in the field of forensic nursing in Israel. The findings indicate a significant gap between ED nurses’ low self-reported frequency of performing selected forensic-related tasks and the high importance they attribute to these tasks. These findings highlight the need to integrate forensic nursing content into nursing education and continuing professional development. Nurses should be provided with the knowledge and essential skills required to identify, respond to, and refer forensic-related situations appropriately within approved institutional and regulatory frameworks.
