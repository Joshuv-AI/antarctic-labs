Complementary views: addressing blind spots in the direct versus video laryngoscopy debate
Authors: Stephen Gerard Flynn, Mary Lyn Stein, Samuel Y Kim, Raymond S Park, Gabriel Soares de Sousa, Pete G Kovatsis, James M Peyton
Published: 2026

Paediatricians routinely balance individual patient care needs with training the next generation of clinicians. An important example is tracheal intubation. Should inexperienced trainees perform tracheal intubation, or is this critical intervention best left to more experienced clinicians? If the answer is more experienced clinicians, how do junior trainees become experienced? The tension between patient care and training has always existed, but it has become especially prominent in discussions of airway management due to clear evidence linking severe complications with the number of tracheal intubation attempts.1 A second question in airway management further complicates this debate: Is direct laryngoscopy or video laryngoscopy the best option, particularly when working with trainees? Robust evidence shows that video laryngoscopy has higher success rates, lower complication rates and fewer failed tracheal intubation attempts across various patient care settings, including operating rooms, intensive care units, delivery rooms and emergency departments.24 Given the strength of the high-quality evidence, some have asked if there is a place for traditional direct laryngoscopy in the care of paediatric patients in 2026 and onwards? While these questions are important, they highlight a blind spot in discussions of video and direct laryngoscopy—great for arguments and editorials, but less helpful for patient care and medical education. Both issues can be addressed with a simple approach: video-assisted direct laryngoscopy (VADL).

Direct laryngoscopy is an important skill that has been used safely for a long time. Direct vision provides a wide field of view, may decrease oropharyngeal trauma, and affords the laryngoscopist opportunities to manage secretions, blood and other airway soiling. Some argue that routine video laryngoscopy will result in the decay of skill in traditional direct laryngoscopy, viewing it as a choice between traditional direct and video laryngoscopy.5 This view presumes that direct and video laryngoscopy are mutually exclusive techniques, ignoring the potential to use them together. Many video laryngoscopy systems have traditional laryngoscopy blades designed to perform direct laryngoscopy, but enhanced with video capability to also enable indirect video laryngoscopy. When we use video technology to assist in performing and teaching direct laryngoscopy, we call the technique VADL. This allows trainees to perform direct laryngoscopy while their supervisor uses the video screen to provide real-time feedback to guide and improve their technique. Suppose the intubation is difficult for the trainee. In that case, the supervisor will have more information about why this occurred and, if indicated, direct the intubating clinician to immediately proceed to indirect video laryngoscopy, using the video screen to perform intubation during the same laryngoscopy attempt. Additionally, the intubation can be recorded and used for post-procedure debriefing to enhance the intubator’s knowledge and skill set.

Early videolaryngoscopy often used hyperangulated devices, which initially raised concerns about increased oropharyngeal soft-tissue injury, as evidenced by case reports of palate, pharyngeal wall and tonsillar pillar injuries.6 7 Standard blade video laryngoscopy now improves alignment of the pharynx, glottis and trachea, and supervisors can remind trainees to optimise patient positioning and the airway axes, such as aligning the tragus with the sternal notch. With growing familiarity with both systems, research in children shows soft tissue injuries are not more frequent than with direct laryngoscopy, as confirmed by large databases such as the PeDI Registry8 and NEAR4NEOS.9 In fact, complication rates are lower in these databases for standard blade and hyperangulated videolaryngoscopy when compared to direct laryngoscopy.

Despite this, some clinicians argue that video laryngoscopy should only be reserved for the most challenging patients or used as a backup, with traditional direct laryngoscopy as the first choice for low-risk patients. However, the evidence shows that even experienced clinicians are imperfect at predicting challenging intubations. In a large prospective multicentre study of airway management in neonates and infants, nearly 7 out of 10 difficult intubations were unanticipated.10 Patients with difficult tracheal intubations have high rates of complications, including hypoxaemia, bradycardia and cardiac arrest.1 11 We must use equipment and approaches on the first intubation attempt that give us the highest probability of success at securing the airway and preventing complications among our young patients. We strongly advocate adopting VADL for routine paediatric intubations. We think direct and indirect video laryngoscopy are not mutually exclusive; they are complementary techniques that can and should be taught together—ensuring both patients and learners’ benefit.

In addition, another simple intervention that has been shown to increase intubation success and reduce cognitive load for trainees performing intubations is just-in-time coaching. Our recent work demonstrates that a just-in-time manikin simulation session, coached by a supervisor within 1 hour of infant intubation, yields a 10% improvement in first-attempt success for trainee intubations.12 This study shines a light on another blind spot—warming up or rehearsing, routine for any athlete or musician, is also beneficial for clinicians.

Finally, apneic oxygenation should be strongly considered during intubation of neonates and young infants. Randomised clinical trials demonstrate improvement in successful intubation on the first attempt without physiological instability13 and a decrease in the incidence of hypoxaemia when supplemental oxygen is administered, leading recent guidelines to recommend routine apneic oxygenation for anaesthesiologists performing neonatal intubations.14 Ongoing studies are underway to identify barriers to implementing this technique.

The relevance of these strategies may be even greater in low- and middle-income countries and remote clinical environments, where clinicians often face a higher burden of emergency airway management but fewer opportunities for structured training and supervision. Workforce shortages, limited access to paediatric airway expertise and inconsistent availability of advanced airway devices further complicate safe airway management in children.15 In this context, approaches that simultaneously support patient safety and training are particularly valuable. VADL provides a pragmatic bridge when video-enabled devices are available, allowing clinicians to maintain direct laryngoscopy skills while providing shared visualisation and real-time coaching. Further, brief simulation (even low-fidelity)16 and cognitive rehearsal before airway management may represent scalable strategies to improve procedural performance in settings with limited training opportunities. Ensuring that advances in paediatric airway management remain adaptable to diverse clinical environments is essential if improvements in airway safety are to benefit children globally. Videolaryngoscopy can be a financial constraint in such settings; ongoing efforts to lower costs, including open-source three-dimensional printing of these systems, will increase availability in low- and middle-income countries.

Suggestions for the paediatric medical community

Use VADL to train the next generation whenever possible. Encourage trainees to view video and direct laryngoscopy as complementary techniques, rather than in opposition to each other. Given the educational benefits and clear patient safety advantages of VADL, we urge our colleagues studying paediatric airway management to move on from simple comparisons of video and direct laryngoscopy and begin to focus on other aspects of airway management, including optimal training regimens for inexperienced clinicians and training intervals needed to attain mastery.

VADL, preferably with a just-in-time training session, can improve intubation success, maintain crucial skills and reduce complications in vulnerable children. Focusing on a single aspect of the debate (video vs direct laryngoscopy), where robust evidence has already been gathered, distracts us from further research and implementing techniques to increase patient safety. We can address our blind spots, but only if we avoid repeatedly looking into the same ones that have already been illuminated.
