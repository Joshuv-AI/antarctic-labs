Trichosporon asahii subcutaneous soft tissue infection after trauma surgery in an immunocompetent patient: a case report
Authors: Hongzi Zhu, Wenxiu Ge, Longtao Zhou, Yuchi Wang, Xiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trichosporon asahii (T. asahii) is a rare non-Candida basidiomycetous yeast. It commonly causes invasive infections in immunocompromised individuals, while severe subcutaneous soft tissue infections in immunocompetent patients are extremely uncommon.Here, we report a 67-year-old immunocompetent female who developed secondary T. asahii infection after debridement and suturing for left lower leg trauma, presenting with local skin necrosis, purulent exudation and undermining abscesses, with normal systemic inflammatory markers. The pathogen was confirmed as T. asahii via Vitek Compact system, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Antifungal susceptibility testing verified susceptibility to voriconazole. The patient achieved marked improvement after 3 weeks of treatment with surgical debridement, vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) and voriconazole. Local infection was controlled and the wound began to heal, and antifungal drugs were then withdrawn. This case highlights that T. asahii infection should be suspected in refractory post-traumatic soft tissue infections in immunocompetent patients. The discordance between severe local lesions and minimal systemic inflammation serves as a key clinical clue. Early etiological diagnosis, surgical intervention combined with susceptible antifungal agents are critical for improving prognosis.

Introduction

T. asahii is a non-Candida yeast that is widely distributed in natural environments such as water, soil, and vegetation and can also colonize the skin, respiratory tract, genitourinary tract, and gastrointestinal mucosa of humans [1–3]. It is an opportunistic fungal pathogen, and the traditional high-risk groups for infections caused by this fungus are immunocompromised individuals [4], such as those with malignant tumors, leukopenia, hematological diseases, and organ transplant recipients, who often develop severe infections [2]. Reports in the literature indicate that this fungus can cause various types of infections, including bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, skin infections, and deep invasive infections, among which systemic infections are particularly common and have high mortality rates [2, 5]. In immunocompetent populations, T. asahii infections mostly present as superficial infections (e.g., white piedra) or local infections associated with medical procedures (e.g., surgery, catheterization) [2, 6]. In recent years, with the development of etiological detection technology, reports of skin and subcutaneous soft tissue infections caused by T. asahii in immunocompetent populations have gradually increased, but cases of deep soft tissue infections (e.g., undermining abscess cavities and fascial involvement) complicated by posttraumatic surgery remain rare [7, 8]. This paper reports an immunocompetent patient with subcutaneous soft tissue infection of the left lower leg caused by T. asahii after traumatic surgery and provides a reference for clinical diagnosis and treatment on the basis of its diagnosis and treatment process.

Case presentation

General information

The patient was a 67-year-old female who was admitted to the Department of Osteopathy ward of a Grade A tertiary hospital in Shandong Province on October 16, 2025, due to “ulceration and exudation of the left lower leg after trauma for more than 70 days”. The patient had no previous history of underlying diseases such as diabetes, autoimmune diseases, or malignant tumors; no history of long-term use of antibiotics, glucocorticoids, or immunosuppressants; and no history of HIV infection; her daily activity ability was normal. More than 70 days prior, the patient accidentally fell while riding an electric bike, resulting in a skin laceration on the medial side of the distal left lower leg. She underwent “debridement and suture” in a local hospital, was discharged after the incision scabbed, and received regular outpatient dressing changes. During the follow-up visits, local skin necrosis and purulent exudation gradually occurred. She came to the outpatient clinic of our hospital for further diagnosis and treatment and was admitted with a diagnosis of “left lower leg soft tissue infection”.

However, due to the retrospective nature of this study and the patient receiving fragmented care at multiple external outpatient facilities, the specific records regarding the exact antibacterial agents administered and their precise durations were unavailable. The patient was also unable to recall the detailed regimens. Despite the lack of specific documentation, based on the standard protocols for postoperative wound management in our region, it is likely that the patient received empirical broad-spectrum therapy, potentially including beta-lactams or cephalosporins. It is important to note that the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate despite these treatments.

Relevant examination data

Physical examination at admission

Her temperature was 36.5 °C, her pulse was 69 beats per minute, her respiration rate was 17 breaths per minute, and her blood pressure was 130/86 mmHg. The patient was conscious and in good spirits; no enlarged superficial lymph nodes were palpable throughout the body. A skin necrosis lesion was visible on the medial side of the distal left lower leg, with obvious surrounding redness and swelling, accompanied by extensive pigmentation and a large amount of purulent exudation (Fig. 1a); local sensation was decreased, and there was no significant limitation in the movement of the left lower extremity; the pulse of the dorsalis pedis artery was palpable, and no other abnormalities were found during the remaining physical examination.

Imaging examinations

Plain MRI scan of the left lower leg: (1) Local skin defect on the medial side of the middle segment of the left lower leg, extensive edema of the superficial fascia of the left lower leg, and effusion and abscess in the fascial space of the middle and distal segments; uneven edema of the dorsal muscle tissue and intermuscular space of the middle and distal segments of the left lower leg, please combine with clinical findings; (2) Local bone marrow edema under the medial articular surface of the talus of the left ankle joint is most likely caused by local cartilage denudation.

Treatment and follow-up

After admission, the patient was administered intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 h. Considering the poor curative effect after long-term external antibiotic therapy, debridement and dressing changes, pus culture was performed to identify the pathogenic strains, and preoperative preparation was completed simultaneously. On October 20, the patient underwent “left lower leg debridement + pedicled fasciocutaneous flap plasty + split-thickness skin grafting + vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) " under general anesthesia on October 20. Intraoperative exploration revealed skin necrosis on the medial side of the distal left lower leg, with significant surrounding redness and swelling; the blood supply at the bottom of the wound and the skin edge was poor; there were undermining spaces under the skin, containing a large amount of pus and inflammatory necrotic tissue; the infection involved the skin, subcutaneous fat, and fascia layers; the great saphenous vein was ruptured (the broken end was embolized); and the saphenous nerve was partially ruptured with severe contusion. After thorough debridement of the necrotic tissue, the wound was approximately 18 cm in length. Pus was collected for etiological culture, and infected fascial tissue was taken for pathological examination. Pathology results (Fig. 1f) revealed microscopic infiltration of lymphocytes, histiocytes, and multinucleated giant cells in the fibroadipose tissue, accompanied by small blood vessel proliferation, suggesting panniculitis, and no definite neoplastic lesions were found.

According to the results of pus culture and susceptibility test, T. asahii was highly sensitive to voriconazole, and its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was 0.125 µg/mL(Table 2).Therefore, voriconazole (200 mg q12h intravenous drip) was administered for antifungal treatment on October 21, and liver and kidney function was monitored regularly (no abnormalities were found).The patient recovered smoothly after surgery; the pain in the left lower extremity was tolerable, and there were no systemic symptoms, such as fever or chills. The VSD was removed 8 days after surgery; the surgical area was completely covered with dressings, the surface was dry, the incision had mild redness and swelling, the blood supply and superficial sensation of the left lower extremity were not significantly abnormal, and the dorsalis pedis artery pulse was good (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, oral voriconazole (200 mg every 12 h) was continued for a total course of 3 weeks. On the 19th day after surgery, most of the skin graft area survived, granulation began to grow in the surrounding necrotic area, exudation was minimal, and the peripheral edge of the wound had mild redness and swelling, so the sutures were removed (Fig. 1c). On the 25th day after surgery, most of the skin graft area survived, the granulation growth vitality at the skin edge was slightly poor, and exudation was minimal (Fig. 1d). Follow-up until the 39th day after surgery revealed that the blood supply to the skin graft area was good, the wound edge had healed well (Fig. 1e), and there was no recurrence of infection.

Culture and identification

Colony morphology and staining

Pus samples were inoculated on blood agar plates and chocolate agar plates. After 24 h of culture, the colonies were gray, rough and dry on the surface, thin at the edges with a light color, and white in the center with short fine villi (Fig. 2a and b). In contrast, after 72 h of culture, the colony edges on the blood agar plates and chocolate agar plates were petal shaped, with wide and deep root-like or gyral fissures on the surface (Fig. 2c and d). Smears were prepared from colonies cultured for 72 h and observed under a microscope at ×1000 magnification after Gram staining. A large number of septate hyphae and arthrospores were visible. The hyphae were branched with uneven thickness, and the arthrospores were rectangular and oval (Fig. 2e). Under the fluorescence microscope, septate hyphae predominated, which were branched and relatively uniform in thickness with distinct septa. Plenty of rod-shaped to oval arthrospores were also observed, arranged along the hyphae or scattered in the field (Fig. 2f).

Chromogenic medium characteristics

At the same time, pus samples were inoculated on chromogenic media (Bibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). After 24 h, the front side was milky white (Fig. 3a), and the back side was obviously blue (Fig. 3b), suggesting T. asahii, while all Candida spp., Cryptococcus spp., Rhodotorula spp., etc., did not show this color. In addition, when the colonies on the agar surface were scraped off with an inoculating loop or cotton swab, the medium was still blue (Fig. 3c), which was not observed in other colored Candida spp. This finding indicates that the pigment produced by T. asahii has the ability to penetrate into the medium or that T. asahii has the ability to grow into the medium and produce pigments, whereas Candida spp. and Cryptococcus spp. do not have this pigment penetration or invasive growth ability.

Identification

Vitek Compact and MALDI-TOF-MS results revealed that T.asahii had an identification rate of 98% and a mass spectrometry score of 2.25 (> 2 points). Fungal ITS amplicon NGS sequencing was performed on the isolated colonies (Jinqirui Biotechnology Co., Ltd.,China). After homogenization analysis, a total of 78,101 valid homogenized sequences were obtained. Species annotation confirmed the pathogen as T.asahii, with no other fungal or bacterial sequences detected. The molecular identification result was single with high specificity.

Discussion

T. asahii is the most common pathogen of invasive trichosporonosis. Invasion can occur through exogenous pathways, i.e., the microorganism enters the central line and catheters through colonized skin, or through endogenous invasion via intestinal translocation in immunocompromised patients [9]. Early cases were mostly immunocompromised patients, such as those with malignant tumors, leukemia, and organ transplant recipients, who usually presented with bacteremia and disseminated infections involving the skin, lungs, liver, spleen, and central nervous system, with mortality rates as high as 30% to 90% [5, 10–12]. However, in recent years, there have been increasing reports of infections in immunocompetent patients, suggesting that its pathogenic spectrum may be expanding.

This patient was an elderly female with normal immune function. The infection was clearly linked to a traumatic skin laceration: trauma disrupted the cutaneous barrier, enabling locally colonized T. asahii to invade the subcutaneous soft tissue. Beyond physical barrier disruption, trauma-induced local tissue hypoxia, inflammation, and dysregulated immune microenvironment further impaired the host’s ability to clear the pathogen, increasing infection susceptibility [13–14]. Therefore, even immunocompetent individuals may exhibit transient or localized defects in antifungal immunity under traumatic stress [13]. Nevertheless, immunocompetent hosts with intact innate and adaptive immunity can effectively confine fungal colonization and proliferation to the local site, preventing bloodstream invasion and systemic spread. This containment limits the pathological process to localized inflammation and tissue necrosis, without triggering a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [2, 3]. Furthermore, the patient’s prolonged prior antibacterial therapy may have modulated the inflammatory profile: broad-spectrum antibiotics altered the local microbiome, reducing bacterial competition and facilitating fungal overgrowth, while attenuating bacteria-mediated systemic inflammatory signals, thereby resulting in high local fungal burden but a blunted systemic response [4, 9].

At the molecular and cellular levels, host antifungal defense is mainly mediated by innate immunity. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), represented by Dectin-1, identify β-glucan on the cell wall of T.asahii and activate phagocytes to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-6, which jointly exert antifungal effects. G-CSF promotes neutrophil proliferation and migration to control local infection. Defects of the adaptor protein CARD9 in the Dectin-1 pathway will markedly weaken antifungal immunity [15, 16].In addition, microRNAs (miRNAs) participate in regulating the expression of various immune molecules. For example, dysregulated expression of miR-155, miR-21, or miR-146a can alter Dectin-1 signaling or cytokine secretion, creating a permissive microenvironment for T. asahii colonization and invasion [17, 18]. Although this patient had no known primary immunodeficiencies, trauma and surgical stress may induce transient dysregulation of these immune pathways and miRNA expression, leading to localized impairment of antifungal defense and facilitating T.asahii infection.

Collectively, the intact local immune barrier in immunocompetent hosts and post-traumatic microenvironmental changes fully explain the paradox of extensive subcutaneous involvement with minimal systemic abnormalities. In recent years, there have been increasing reports of postoperative T.asahii infection in immunocompetent patients. Cases following open lumbar discectomy, total knee arthroplasty and carpal tunnel release have been documented, confirming that T.asahii can cause localized postoperative infections in immunocompetent populations [7, 19–20].

Compared with previously reported cases, this patient suffered from severe deep soft tissue infection involving fascia, blood vessels and nerves. On the basis of thorough surgical debridement, we combined vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) to optimize the wound environment and improve antifungal efficacy. Although the SWHSI-2 trial demonstrated limited efficacy of negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) for conventional surgical wounds [21], we adopted VSD based on our institutional clinical experience, the unpublished status of this trial during treatment planning, and the unique features of fungal wounds. VSD optimizes the local microenvironment and inhibits fungal proliferation, acting synergistically with antifungal agents [22]. It serves as an effective individualized regimen for complex deep fungal soft tissue infections.

The key to the diagnosis and treatment of this case lies in two points: first, accurate etiological diagnosis. T. asahii is easily misjudged as Candida in conventional fungal culture, and species identification needs to be confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS or molecular biological methods (such as ITS sequencing) [9, 23]; second, reasonable selection of treatment options is needed. Recent studies have shown that the resistance rate of T.asahii to fluconazole is gradually increasing, while new triazole drugs such as voriconazole and itraconazole have better in vitro sensitivity [6, 24, 25], which can cause leakage of cell contents, cell lysis, and cell death by damaging the fungal cell membrane [26]. The ESCMID/ECMM guidelines recommend voriconazole as the first-line drug for T. asahii infections [26]. In addition, relevant molecular biology studies have shown that T. asahii is most sensitive to voriconazole [27]. In this case, after confirming T.asahii infection, voriconazole was selected on the basis of the drug sensitivity test results, combined with thorough debridement and VSD drainage, to achieve rapid control of the infection. In addition, adverse drug reactions (such as liver injury and visual abnormalities) should be monitored during voriconazole treatment [26]. The patient in this case received a 3-week course of treatment without relevant adverse reactions, indicating good safety.

Upon retrospective analysis, besides the failure of conventional antibacterial therapy, several specific clinical features in this case should have raised early suspicion for a fungal etiology. First, the patient presented with undermining abscess cavities and extensive tissue necrosis that were disproportionate to the mild systemic inflammatory response. This discordance between severe local infection and mild systemic symptoms is a characteristic often observed in chronic fungal infections but can be easily overlooked. Second, the presence of purulent exudate persisting despite surgical debridement and antibiotic use is another red flag for fungal involvement. Third, the involvement of deep fascial layers and neurovascular structures (saphenous vein and nerve) suggested an aggressive invasive nature, which is less common in typical bacterial cellulitis but reported in Trichosporon infections. Recognizing these signs earlier, combined with a high index of suspicion for opportunistic fungi in post-traumatic wounds, could have potentially shortened the diagnostic timeline. This delay highlights the limitation in our initial clinical assessment and underscores the need for routine fungal screening in refractory soft tissue infections.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that T. asahii can cause severe deep subcutaneous soft tissue infection in immunocompetent patients after trauma surgery. Trauma disrupts the cutaneous barrier, and local tissue hypoxia, inflammation, and transient impairment of antifungal immunity collectively create a microenvironment conducive to fungal invasion and persistent infection. The discordance between extensive local necrosis, undermining abscesses, and minimal systemic inflammation is a key clinical clue suggesting non-Candida yeast infection, such as T. asahii. Accurate species identification using MALDI-TOF-MS or molecular methods is essential to avoid misdiagnosis. Voriconazole combined with aggressive surgical debridement and vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) effectively controls infection and promotes wound healing. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for fungal etiology in refractory post-traumatic soft tissue infections in immunocompetent patients, enabling timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment to improve patient outcomes.
