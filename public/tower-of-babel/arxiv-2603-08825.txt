Abstract
. Discrete graph generation has emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling graph data, often relying on highly expressive neural backbones such as transformers or higher-order architectures. We revisit this design choice by introducing GenGNN, a modular message-passing framework for graph generation. Diffusion models with GenGNN achieve more than 90% validity on Tree and Planar datasets, within margins of graph transformers, at 2-5x faster inference speed. For molecule generation, DiGress with a GenGNN backbone achieves 99.49% Validity. A systematic ablation study shows the benefit provided by each GenGNN component, indicating the need for residual connections to mitigate oversmoothing on complicated graph-structure. Through scaling analyses, we apply a principled metric-space view to investigate learned diffusion representations and uncover whether GNNs can be expressive neural backbones for discrete diffusion.

Introduction

Graph generation exists as a burgeoning subfield of graph representation learning, with applications extending from drug discovery
(
57
)
to code modeling
(
6
)
. Subsequently, models which leverage discrete graph diffusion demonstrate impressive performance for molecule generation
(

)
. Recent work integrates continuous-time modeling into discrete diffusion via flow matching
(
45
;
58
)
, where further decoupling of training and sampling results in near-perfect performance on some benchmark datasets
(
39
)
.

In order for discrete diffusion models to achieve best-in-class results, the current landscape for graph generative models (GGMs) rely on specialized graph encoder backbones. This includes variants of either the transformer architecture
(
50
;
40
;
35
)
or maximally-expressive graph networks
(
32
;
34
)
. The need for integrating expressive neural backbones into GGMs relies on observations from prior work and is distilled into a simple two-part intuition.
One
, message-passing within less-expressive graph networks fail to distinguish simple isomorphic substructures
(
55
)
.
Two
, graph neural networks (GNNs) suffer from over-smoothing, which translates to failure when scaling on long-range tasks
(
2
)
. This lack of expressivity implies that simple GNNs fail to accurately denoise complicated graph-structure across the many diffusion timesteps required for successful sampling, as demonstrated in Figure
1(a)
with a simple GNN backbone on the Planar dataset versus a graph transformer backbone in Figure
1(b)
.

However, a conflicting story is apparent within the node- and graph-classification tasks, where recent work shows simpler GNNs (with modifications) achieving comparable performance to graph transformers (GTs)
(
25
;
27
;
28
)
. This is noteworthy, as simple GNNs are more efficient than expressive models reliant on tupled-based representations
(
32
;
34
;
40
;
35
)
, therefore translating to inference speed improvements
(
28
)
. Any kind of speed improvement is important for discrete graph diffusion, which currently struggles with scalability from the quadratic time-complexity required for graph reconstruction
(
51
;
39
)
. This leads us to the ask the question of,
are expressive encoders really necessary for discrete graph diffusion?

Despite any efficiency boost promised by a simpler baseline, repeated empirical observations indicate their failure to re-create their training data within generated graphs. Therefore, a balance must be achieved between expressivity and time efficiency.

To answer this, we first design GenGNN, a neural framework created to address the shortcomings of simpler GNNs as neural backbones for discrete graph diffusion. Unlike graph transformers, GenGNN relies solely on message-passing to denoise latent representations, which is further integrated into a modular framework for effective sampling of generated graphs. The message-passing restriction of GenGNN is intentional, enabling analysis to focus on the
threshold at which expressiveness matters for discrete graph diffusion
.

Initial benchmarking indicates GenGNN achieves performance competitive with SOTA models, 99.35% validity and over 2.5x inference speed-up relative to theoretically-expressive architectures on the QM9 dataset
(
54
)
. This inference speedup is comprehensively illustrated with 1.7-5x improvements
and

(

47

We further derive theoretical relationships to how these latent representations correlate to improved downstream generation within multivariate-systems, formalizing the intuition of how decoupled frameworks allow bypassing of standard expressivity hierarchies.

The contribution of our work is as follows:

•

We introduce GenGNN, a modular neural message-passing framework enabling less-expressive model backbones to achieve competitive graph generation performance with 1.7-5x inference speedup.

•

We detail a theoretical underpinning as to why GenGNN enhances standard message-passing for discrete graph diffusion.

•

We conduct detailed analysis ablating components of the GenGNN framework and further scaling framework layers to demonstrate the connection between downstream performance and latent representations

2.2.
Discrete Graph Diffusion

Discrete-state diffusion alters continuous denoising
(
46
)
by transitioning between noisy and denoised states as marginal probabilities across categorical distributions, adding noise to form
G_{t}=(X_{t},E_{t})
, where
t
is the
t
-th diffusion timestep. This amounts to sampling each node and edge type from a categorical distribution, as defined by
(
51
)
:

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}q(M_{t}\mid M_{t-1})&=\bigl(X_{t-1}M_{t}^{X},\;E_{t-1}M_{t}^{E}\bigr),\\
q(M_{t}\mid M)&=\bigl(X\bar{M}_{t}^{X},\;E\bar{M}_{t}^{E}\bigr),\end{split}

where
\bar{M}
is a transition matrix that defines the forward diffusion process.
In practice, Eq. (
1
) enables the denoising backbone to be trained via cross-entropy loss on node and edge features for strong graph generation performance. Recent work integrated continuous flow-modeling into the discrete-space, enabling multimodal learning on sequences and discrete data
(
9
)
. In order for flow-modeling to operate directly on graph structure, DeFoG
(
39
)
computes a transition dynamic via a rate matrix as follows:

(2)

R_{t}^{(n)}\!\left(x_{t}^{(n)},x_{t+\Delta t}^{(n)}\right)=\\
\mathbb{E}_{p_{1\mid t}^{(n)}\!\left(x_{1}^{(n)}\mid G_{t}\right)}\Bigl[R_{t}^{(n)}\!\left(x_{t}^{(n)},x_{t+\Delta t}^{(n)}\mid x_{1}^{(n)}\right)\Bigr],

and analogously for the edge transitions,
\tilde{p}_{t+\Delta t\mid t}^{(ij)}\!\left(e_{t+\Delta t}^{(ij)}\mid G_{t}\right)
where
\tilde{p}(\cdot)
represent the probability of an edge existing between nodes
i
and
j
at time
t+\Delta t
. The rate matrix computation allows a decoupling of discrete denoising, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance when applying graph transformers as a denoising backbone. However, specialized message-passing architectures have demonstrated impressive capability for generative modeling of protein sequences
(
11
)
, yet remains untested for structured learning within graph generation.

Oversmoothing in GNNs

A common concern with vanilla GNNs is that they over-smooth node signals when propagating distant information
(
1
)
. Whether this phenomenon translates to the denoising transitions of discrete-space diffusion remains crucially untested, making it infeasible to determine if GNNs are capable diffusion backbones. Indeed, foundational work
(
23
)
has indicated that the inclusion of residual connections allows the integration of more layers within graph convolutional networks, or GCNs
(
21
)
to target long-range information. Recent theoretical work demonstrates that the inclusion of normalization and residual connection ‘anchor’ the initial feature signal of weights within GNNs, enabling retention of relevant graph signals in deep layers
(
44
)
. We pull inspiration from these foundational works in the next section.

2.4.
Evaluation

Foundational work which tested evaluation metrics for GGMs
(
49
)
revealed that maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
(
14
)
is effective at determining whether GGMs successfully generate local structures (i.e. orbits, clustering)
(
19
)
, but requires tuning to fully-consider the coverage of potential modes visible within training graphs. To remedy this,
(
26
)
propose MagDiff applying the magnitude of metric space
(
22
)
and solving the integral between the difference in area between predicted and reference embeddings as a means gauge the diversity of downstream generated graphs. They represent this measure as:

(4)

\mathrm{MagDiff}\;:=\;\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathrm{cut}}}\big(\mathrm{Mag}_{X}(t)-\mathrm{Mag}_{Y}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

which solves the area between reference and predicting embeddings to determine if the two meet adequate coverage in metric space.

3.
GenGNN

Current encoder backbones for GGMs
(
51
;
39
)
tend to use powerful but expensive model designs which hamper efficiency. On the other hand, recent work has shown that simpler models can best more complex ones for more traditional graph tasks such as node classification
(
27
)
and graph tasks
(
28
;
27
)
. We are therefore interested in investigating whether
similar performance can be achieved via simpler GNN models
?

We therefore propose a simple GNN design that incorporates best practices
(
28
;
27
)
while still maintaining strong efficiency. Our framework, Generative Graph Neural Network framework (GenGNN), is modular neural framework designed to integrate message-passing neural networks or graph neural networks into diffusion models as effective generative backbones. This effectiveness is achieve through a stackable list of components which are integrated after initial message-passing layers.

3.1.
Model Design

Similar to prior work
(
39
)
, GenGNN concatenates RRWP encodings
(
29
)
to the initial node and edge features of a given graph, denoted as:

(5)

\mathbf{P}_{ij}:=\bigl[\mathbf{I},\,\mathbf{M},\,\mathbf{M}^{2},\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{M}^{K-1}\bigr]_{ij}

We retain this choice given recent work indicating the high-expressive power of RRWP without extensive computational overhead relative to other available encoding schemes
(
48
)
.

In addition to the previously-mentioned message-passing layers, GenGNN makes use of the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)
(
21
)

(6)

\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{W}.

and the Edge-Learnable variant of the Graph Isomorphism Network (GINe)
(
55
;
20
)
:

(7)

\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{ij})=\text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}+\phi(\mathbf{e}_{ij})\right),

(8)

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{(\ell)}=(1+\epsilon)\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)},

where we treat
\epsilon
as learnable.

Edge and Node Gating.

Within any message-passing layer, we leverage the gating mechanism proposed in
(
5
)
across edges and
(
31
)
across nodes to modulate aggregated signals.
For each node pair
(i,j)
, we define a conditional edge gate

(9)

\mathbf{h}_{ij}^{(\ell)}=\left[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\|\,\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(\ell)}\,\|\,\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\,\|\,\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\right],

(10)

\alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\!\left(f_{\text{edge}}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right)\right),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. When edge gating is disabled,

\alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{1}[i\neq j]
. This can be further be applied to modulate node messages by

(11)

\beta_{i}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\!\left(f_{\text{node}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\|\,\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\|\,\mathbf{y}^{(\ell)}\right)\right),

(12)

\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\beta_{i}^{(\ell)}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}.

Feed-forward networks (FFN).

All feed-forward networks in the model follow a two-layer MLP with nonlinearity and dropout:

(13)

\mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{W}_{2}\,\phi\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\right)+\mathbf{b}_{2},

where
\phi(\cdot)
denotes the GELU activation
(
17
)
. Given that GenGNN aggregates node, edge, and graph signals, we integrate FFNs to improve nonlinear expressivity between backbone channels
(
56
;
10
)
.

Residual connections

Residual connections
(
16
)
are applied whenever input and output dimensions match.

(14)

\mathrm{Res}(\mathbf{h},\Delta\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{h}+\Delta\mathbf{h}.

Layer normalization.

Layer normalization
(
3
)
is applied feature-wise:

(15)

\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{h})=\boldsymbol{\gamma}\odot\frac{\mathbf{h}-\mu(\mathbf{h})}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{h})+\epsilon}}+\boldsymbol{\beta},

where
\mu(\mathbf{h})
and
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{h})
denote the mean and variance computed over the feature dimension,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are learnable parameters, and
\epsilon
is a small constant for numerical stability.

Unified update form.

Combining feed-forward transformation, residual connection, and normalization, node, edge, and global updates in each layer take the form

(16)

\mathbf{h}^{(\ell+1)}=\mathrm{LN}\left(\mathrm{Res}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)},\mathrm{FFN}\!\left(\Delta\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}\right)\right)\right),

where
\Delta\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}
denotes the backbone-specific update (e.g., message aggregation for nodes, pairwise updates for edges, global readouts for graph timestep embeddings).

Pooling operators.

Mean pooling is used solely to aggregate node- and edge-level representations into a global summary and is not treated as a learnable or ablatable component.
Specifically, node and edge pooling are defined as

(17)

\mathrm{Pool}_{X}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{i\in V}\mathbf{x}_{i},\quad\mathrm{Pool}_{E}(\mathbf{E})=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\mathbf{e}_{ij}.

These pooled representations are used exclusively for global conditioning updates and do not affect local message passing. However, pooling does enable the utility of classifier-free guidance
(
18
)
on global readouts from the GenGNN framework.

In principle, the six ablateable components of GenGNN integrate intuition and results from previous work to provide the opportunity to test whether standard message-passing degrade is viable for denoising graph diffusion samples.

3.2.
Effective Message-Passing Denoisers: A Theoretical Perspective

Within this section, we formally define a process by which message-passing can learn from node signals in order to effectively denoise graph samples without oversmoothing. Given how denoising GGM backbones sample graph structure from noisy input across numerous timesteps, the current standard for SOTA GGMs often leverages 10
+
layers to track long-range dependencies
(
39
;
51
)
. Simple message-passing backbones suffer from over-smoothing at these layer depths
(
24
)
, translating to mode collapse and poor performance in downstream graph generation (as shown in Figure
1(a)
).

Setup.

To quantify the impact of over-smoothing for denoising GNNs, we consider
(
44
)
’s definition on oversmoothing, which they show is universal with respect to other measures of GNN over-smoothing.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the unit vector with
\|v\|_{2}=1
and define the projector

P:=I-vv^{\top}
. For any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, define the distance to

\mathrm{span}\{v\}
by

(18)

\mu_{v}(X):=\|PX\|_{F}^{2}=\|(I-vv^{\top})X\|_{F}^{2}.

Given that projecting one-hot encodings into latent space operates independently from residual signals, Eqn (
18
) enables treatment of node signals through discrete diffusion. How then could the model backbone ensure that it does not collapse to the most frequent modes within noisy systems?

Definition 3.1 (Node-wise Structural Dispersion).

In order to provide a lower-bound on the behavior for
\mu_{v}
, we define
\gamma
as the value when:

\gamma\;:=\;\inf_{\|v\|_{2}=1}\mu_{v}(X)

Assumption 1 (Positional-encoding non-degeneracy).

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for the dominant eigenvector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as determined within our embedded feature space,

\mu_{v}\big(S_{X}[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]\big)\;\geq\;\gamma,

where
[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]
denotes the concatenated positional-embedding coupled with the noisy channel zeroed-out and
S_{X}
represents a residual node-signal propagated through message-passing channels.

This assumption holds when residual-connections access unaltered embeddings, regardless of whether a full-forward message-pass occurs, meaning
\mu_{v}
can not collapse to
\gamma
.
This assumption is also reasonable given the empirical effectiveness of diffusion transformers
(
37
)
and deep neural networks
(
16
)
incorporating residual-connections into their design.

Assumption 2 (Backbone orthogonal bound).

Within the discrete diffusion task, a denoising GNN is dependent on visible nodes (not masked by forward noising steps) propagating signals to learn the denoising process. The propagated signals within the denoising GNN are bound by a constant
(C)

(
44
)
. Such that, there exists
C<\infty
for all diffusion steps
t
,

\Big\|P\,G_{\theta}\big(X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},y_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},\mathrm{mask}\big)\Big\|_{F}^{2}\;\leq\;C,

where
G_{\theta}
is the noisy input graph and
in
corresponds to denoising GNN input for a given (de)noising step. Effectively, this assumption means that the process of adding and removing noise across each timestep is constrained by the total possible operations applied to an input graph.

Assumption 1, 2 and Definition
3.1
extend reasoning from
(
44
)
to lay the foundation for how diffusion depends on incremental changes between (de)noising steps. This is possible given
C
is a generalized measure for GNN oversmoothing
(
8
;
53
;
43
;
36
)
. In practice, GenGNN ensures learnable denoising behavior bounded below
C
by integrating: Empirical analysis in Section
4.5
demonstrates the need residual-connections and linear-attention gating as mechanisms for modulating noisy node signals. We hypothesize that these modulated signals minimize the risk of saturated gradients
(
13
)
, which can exacerbate oversmoothing
(
60
)
.

How then does message-passing samples the training distribution from completely-noisy graphs? Noisy input graphs are unavoidable in discrete diffusion: where late denoising steps intentionally destroy feature information. Even if residual-connections preserve orthogonal signal components from positional encodings, early denoising steps still operate on near-random inputs.

Theorem 3.2 (Uniform non-collapse of residual-anchored graph denoisers).

Given Eqn. (
18
) and Assumptions
1
–
2
, for all diffusion steps
t
and
the dominant eigenvector
v
. Where
\mu_{v}
is the margin estimating node-signal collapse and
X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}
is the node-wise denoised output, then:

\boxed{\mu_{v}\big(X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}\big)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma\;-\;C.}

In particular, if
\gamma>2C
then the denoiser outputs cannot collapse to
\mathrm{span}\{v\}

at any reverse diffusion step.

In practice, this means that the denoising GNN backbone retains it’s original orthogonal bound (given an input node-signal with weight-initialization where
\gamma>0
). Theorem
3.2
also explains why denoising GNNs trained on just structural features trend quickly towards over-smoothing. For example, the synthetic Tree dataset maintains a fixed node-size of 64. This fixed node-size means all learned node-embedding are non-unique. If denoising backbones which is solely-reliant on message-passing receives a non-unique embedding as input, then the model is likely to converge on noisy diffusion signals instead of the single desired mode.

Given that Assumption
1
treats residual-connections in isolation, then Theorem
3.2
can be agnostic across graph-diffusion backbones. However, the span measure from Eqn. (
18
) constrains the scope of Theorem
3.2
to message-passing. We derive Corollary
3.3
to fully-relate this argument to discrete denoising through message-passing.

Corollary 3.3 (Anchored denoising robustness).

Suppose the forward marginal transitions drive
X_{t}
toward a stationary categorical marginal
as
t\to T
, potentially making
X_{t}
uninformative. Under the conditions of
Theorem
3.2
, the reverse denoiser retains a non-vanishing component
orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}\{v\}
at all steps due to the embedding’s residual channel.

Corollary
3.3
indicates that denoising GNNs maintain informative signals throughout their discrete-diffusion processes. We verify empirically that this must occur under two conditions: 1) proper-initialized model-weights are maintained throughout message-passing layers, 2) residual connection propagates the input node-signal. largely building off principles set in foundational work
(
13
)
. To our knowledge, this is the first formal treatment on how residual anchoring prevents representational collapse in graph diffusion denoisers, linking GNN over-smoothing theory to discrete diffusion models. Proof sketches are included in Appendix
C
.

4.2.
Ablation Study

In order to empirically test Theorem
3.2
, we ablate individual components of the GenGNN architecture. Complete results are shown in Table
7
. We include demonstration on the Tree dataset within Figure
4
, where alternating plots for MMD and V.U.N indicate the level severity for downstream generation induced by the removal of a single component. This allows us to conduct foundational analysis where two relationships become clear. 1) If we notice a decrease in validity but limited change in MMD, then downstream generation fails to capture distinct dataset attributes (i.e. leaf nodes and branches in the Tree dataset) even though the denoising backbone is still expressive to input structure. 2) If we notice a decrease in validity and an increase in MMD, then both downstream generation and the model’s internal representation are failing to capture dataset structural attributes (i.e. noisy graphs contribute to oversmoothing).

Figure 4
.

The change in MMD and V.U.N. across individual ablated components of the GenGNN framework (log-scaled), with a simple GNN backbone (on right).

Our analysis draws three separate conclusions.
(1)
Figure
4
indicates the importance of each GenGNN framework component in ensuring strong downstream graph generation performance. The most drastic change occurs with the validity metric, where removing any GenGNN component results in a drastic decrease in validity; often falling over 10% or dropping to 0% entirely.
(2)
There is a near-inverse correlation between MMD and V.U.N, where GenGNN drops to 0% Validity and 77 MMD after the removal of just the residual. This provides empirical support for Theorem
3.2
, detailing the necessity of residual-connections to prevent oversmoothing and capture long-range dependencies necessary for diffusion.
(3)
Ablating the RRWP positional encoding results in 72% drop in V.U.N, indicating expressivity boosts can influence message-passing performance without the need for multi-head attention. The full ablation study results, as detailed within Table
7
detail the impact of each GenGNN component for the remaining synthetic and molecular datasets. In particular, we note the need for gating and residuals to maintain high-downstream performance.

4.3.
Oversmoothing Analysis

We follow the methodology proposed in
(
59
)
to further validate the extent to which residuals prevent oversmoothing. The validity ratio is calculated between validity scores at layer depth 24 over layer depth 1. Note, raw ERank
(
41
)
and NumRank
(
42
)
calculations scale with layer depth and size. The Pearson correlation between Validity and ERank/NumRank enables a standardized measure of downstream performance to internal model oversmoothing. As shown in Table

, when residual connections are ablated, positive Pearson correlation (~
0.99
) for ERank
(
41
)
and NumRank
(

)
indicates clear oversmoothing. This further translates to a lower validity ratio, where downstream graph generation degrades with more layers. The correlation to rank and validity within Table

also indicates

0.97

1.0
demonstrate a measurable performance improvement across increasing layers; establishing the need for residual connections to mitigate oversmoothing within GenGNN.

4.5.
Metric-Space Scaling Investigation

In order to isolate whether GenGNN learns latent representations which effectively capture the necessary modes for proper graph generation, we integrate the GINe-variant of GenGNN into the randomly-initialized feature extractor framework introduced by
(
49
)
. The feature extractor is given reference and predicted graphs as input in order to produce embeddings useful for evaluating latent diversity of learned model representations. To do this, we select the MagDiff metric, as introduced in Equation
4
. MagDiff provides the practical benefit of near-perfect correlation to identifying structural diversity as well high-sensitivity to mode-collapse, even with highly-noisy input samples
(
26
)
.

In Figure
5
, both rows of left and center subplots gradually increase the number of layers for: GT, a fully-enabled GenGNN baseline, and GenGNN with RRWP and Residuals ablated. The top-right subplot indicates the tradeoff between MagDiff and Validity for each diffusion backbone. The bottom-right subplot is restricted to Average MMD Ratio and Accuracy due to the Tree dataset containing non-unique feature embeddings due to it’s fixed node size of 64.

The scaling analysis is conducted in this way to utilize MagDiff’s sensitivity to measuring latent diversity of generated samples and correlate it to Validity and Accuracy. Both left-most subplots indicate GenGNN is resilient to oversmoothing. This is demonstrated in two ways: 1) GenGNN’s gradual performance increase with the number of layers, 2) the stability of GenGNN’s performance at a higher number of layers relative to GT and when residual-connections are ablated. The bottom center and rightmost subplots demonstrates that GenGNN consistently performs on-par with GT on the Tree dataset, regardless of layer depth. The top center and rightmost subplots demonstrate GenGNN often clusters around MagDiff values lower than GT, indicating the message-passing can better capture latent diversity in graph substructure for the QM9 dataset. Given these clear trends,
GenGNN overcomes oversmoothing in a way where it’s learned representation translates directly to improved downstream performance; just as might be expected from a GT backbone
.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we take aim at graph transformer backbones typically used in discrete graph generation. In practice, existing models tend to use powerful encoders, which come with the downside of being far less efficient than vanilla GNNs. We propose GenGNN, a simple and modular GNN framework which incorporates best practices to demonstrate comparable performance to expressive systems with improved efficiency. Across a variety of real-world benchmarks GenGNN achieves similar if not better performance, at more than 2-5x the inference speed. Further analysis shows GenGNN is capable of capturing substructures. We believe this opens exciting future work with faster and more performant discrete graph generation models.

Appendix C
Residual non-degeneracy for diffusion backbones

Setup.

To quantify the potential for denoising MPNNs, we extend the generalized definition of oversmoothing from
(
44
)
to consider projection layers applied within diffusion backbones. Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
and define the projector

P:=I-vv^{\top}
. For any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, define the distance to

\mathrm{span}\{v\}
by

\mu_{v}(X):=\|PX\|_{F}^{2}=\|(I-vv^{\top})X\|_{F}^{2}.

At reverse diffusion step
t
, the denoiser input is the channel-wise concatenation

X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}=\big[\,X_{t}\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}\,\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d_{t}+d_{p})},

where
X_{t}
is the noisy node state and
X_{\mathrm{ENC}}
are positional encodings
(which are not corrupted by the diffusion process). The denoiser can be written abstractly as

(19)

X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}\;=\;G_{\theta}\!\big(X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},y_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},\mathrm{mask}\big)\;+\;S_{X}\,X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},

where
G_{\theta}
denotes the message-passing backbone with in/out projections and
S_{X}
is the linear residual-normalization (or identity slice) applied to module input.

In turn, this allows use of the constant
C
derived from
(
44
)
, to function along the noisy graph.

Lemma C.1 (Equivalence of oversmoothing measures
(
44
)
).

Let
G
be a fixed graph with message-passing operator
A
and let
\phi(\cdot)
be any standard
oversmoothing function used in prior work (e.g., the distance to the dominant eigenspace

d_{\mathcal{M}}

(
36
)
, the Dirichlet energy
\mathcal{E}

(
8
)
, or the distance to the constant-vector subspace
\mu(\cdot)

(
43
;
53
)
).
Then there exist constants
C_{3},C_{4}>0
and a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(depending on
G
and on
\phi
) such that for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,

C_{3}\,\mu_{v}(X)\;\leq\;\phi(X)\;\leq\;C_{4}\,\mu_{v}(X).

In particular,
\phi(X)=0\iff\mu_{v}(X)=0
, and any uniform lower bound

\mu_{v}(X)\geq c
implies
\phi(X)\geq c^{\prime}:=C_{3}c
. For simplicity, we treat the definition of this bound as it relates to diffusion as
C
.

Definition C.2 (Node-wise Structural Dispersion).

In order to provide a lower-bound on the behavior for
\mu_{v}
, we define
\gamma
as the value when:

\gamma\;:=\;\inf_{\|v\|_{2}=1}\mu_{v}(X)

Assumption 3 (Positional Encoding non-degeneracy).

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for all unit vectors
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of interest,

\mu_{v}\!\big(S_{X}[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]\big)\;\geq\;\gamma,

where
[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]
denotes the concatenated input with the noisy channel
zeroed-out.

Assumption 4 (Backbone orthogonal energy bound).

There exists
C<\infty
such that for all diffusion steps
t
,

\Big\|P\,G_{\theta}\!\big(X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},y_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},\mathrm{mask}\big)\Big\|_{F}^{2}\;\leq\;C.

Remark.

Assumption
3
holds whenever the positional encodings retain nontrivial
variation orthogonal to
v
after the (learned) residual-norm. Since graph positional-encoding often capture
p
-cycles where
p>4
, they are generically not rank-1 aligned; we
verify this effect empirically by showing the impact of increasing layers on downstream graph generation in Tables
10
and
4
. Assumption
4
is mild at initialization (finite
weights) and is further stabilized by per-layer GELU, FFN, and residual connections.

Lemma C.3 (Two-term lower bound in an orthogonal subspace).

For any matrices
A,B
of compatible shape and any projector
P
,

\|P(A+B)\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\|PB\|_{F}^{2}-\|PA\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
a:=PA
and
b:=PB
. Then

\|a+b\|_{F}^{2}=\|a\|_{F}^{2}+\|b\|_{F}^{2}+2\langle a,b\rangle
.
Using
2\langle a,b\rangle\geq-\|a\|_{F}^{2}-\|b\|_{F}^{2}
gives

\|a+b\|_{F}^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\|b\|_{F}^{2}-\|a\|_{F}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma C.4 (Positional Encoding anchor lower bounds residual).

Let
X_{\mathrm{in}}=[X_{t}\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]
and define
X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}:=[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]
.
Then for any projector
P
,

\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}\|_{F}^{2}\;-\;2\big|\langle PS_{X}X_{t}^{\oplus},\,PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}\rangle\big|,

where
X_{t}^{\oplus}:=[X_{t}\;\;0]
.
In particular, discarding the cross term yields the conservative bound

\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

Write
S_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}=S_{X}X_{t}^{\oplus}+S_{X}X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}
and apply

\|u+w\|_{F}^{2}=\|u\|_{F}^{2}+\|w\|_{F}^{2}+2\langle u,w\rangle
with
u=PS_{X}X_{t}^{\oplus}
and

w=PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{ENC}}^{\oplus}
. The second claim follows by dropping the nonnegative
term
\|u\|_{F}^{2}
and lower bounding
2\langle u,w\rangle
by
-2|\langle u,w\rangle|
,
then further discarding the cross term entirely for a conservative bound.
∎

Lemma C.5 (Outer Residual induces a deterministic non-collapse certificate).

Under Assumption
3
, for any unit
v
,

\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\gamma\qquad\text{for all }t.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.4
,
\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}\geq\|PS_{X}[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}]\|_{F}^{2}
.
The right-hand side is
\mu_{v}(S_{X}[0\;\;X_{\mathrm{ENC}}])
, which is at least
\gamma
by
Assumption
3
.
∎

Proposition C.6 (Per-step non-collapse bound for the denoiser).

Assume (
19
). Then for any
t
and any unit
v
,

\mu_{v}\!\big(X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}\big)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}\;-\;\Big\|P\,G_{\theta}\!\big(X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},E_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},y_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)},\mathrm{mask}\big)\Big\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
C.3
with
A=G_{\theta}(\cdot)
and
B=S_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}
:

\|P(G_{\theta}(\cdot)+S_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)})\|_{F}^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}-\|PG_{\theta}(\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}.

The left-hand side is
\mu_{v}(X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)})
by definition.
∎

Theorem C.7 (Uniform non-collapse of residual-anchored graph denoisers).

Under Assumptions
3
–
4
, for all diffusion steps
t
and
any unit vector
v
,

\boxed{\mu_{v}\!\big(X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}\big)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma\;-\;C.}

In particular, if
\gamma>2C
then the denoiser outputs cannot collapse to
\mathrm{span}\{v\}

at any reverse diffusion step.

Proof.

By Proposition
C.6
and Lemma
C.5
,

\mu_{v}(X_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|PS_{X}X_{\mathrm{in}}^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}-\|PG_{\theta}(\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\gamma-\|PG_{\theta}(\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}.

Assumption
4
upper bounds the final term by
C
, giving the claim.
∎

Corollary C.8 (Anchored Denoising Robustness).

Suppose the forward marginal transitions drive
X_{t}
toward a stationary categorical marginal
as
t\to T
, potentially making
X_{t}
uninformative. Under the conditions of
Theorem
C.7
, the reverse denoiser retains a non-vanishing component
orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}\{v\}
at all steps due to the residual channel.
