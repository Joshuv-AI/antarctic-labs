1.0 Introduction

The continuing move toward establishing fusion systems for power generation and the
associated research to that end is prompting examination of the potential health and safety
impacts of such plants to the environment and human health. As many fusion facilities will have
tritium inventories on site as part of the fusion fuel, evaluating the potential for downwind
impacts from fusion facilities or power plants resulting from accident or routine emissions is a
general requirement for assessing location and impacts to workers and the public. As part of
siting considerations and permitting, the fusion facilities would be evaluated for potential for
downwind concentration and dose impacts. For accident assessment scenarios, the downwind
impacts are usually modeled as an instantaneous (or near-instantaneous) release of material
transported following the wind. A range of meteorological conditions are usually assessed to
determine a bounding case which results in a dose exceeding a specified threshold (e.g., 95th or
99th percentile; DOE 2015).
This report provides initial estimates of the downwind dose impacts from a potential tritium
release at a fusion power plant-relevant facility and identifies potential distances required to limit
impacts to nearby population. This effort is meant to provide a bounding analysis and theoretical
understanding of impacts of tritium releases for facilities subject to various environmental and
atmospheric conditions. Using a Gaussian dispersion model to simulate a brief plume,
downwind concentration and dose is projected for tritium oxide. Releases are assumed to
consist entirely of tritium oxide due to the increased dose impacts from the oxide form relative to
the elemental form of tritium. We also briefly identify how climatological conditions could
potentially be used to support risk profile determination.

2.0 Methodology

2.1 Gaussian Plume Calculations

The Gaussian dispersion methodology assumes that the average, time-integrated conditions of
an airborne release can be simulated using statistical descriptors which describe the distribution
of airborne concentration of a plume using Gaussian distributions in the horizontal and vertical
plane. Gaussian models benefit from the ability to provide rapid calculations of predicted
concentration for a particular point in space and time using minimal inputs consisting of wind
speed, wind direction, and turbulent diffusion parameters based on estimates of stability. While
Gaussian models are not recommended for long-distance transport or complex terrain, they are
good for providing rapid estimates of downwind concentration within a plume within
approximately 40 km.
Following a release, the gaseous material will be advected downstream by the mean wind
direction and speed, with turbulent diffusion parameters describing the lateral and vertical
spread of the plume. The turbulent diffusion parameters are estimated based on measured or
estimated atmospheric stability in the atmospheric boundary layer.
For accident analysis, the center-line concentration as a function of distance is of the greatest
interest as this would represent the highest concentration and corresponding maximum dose as
a plume moves downwind. The center-line concentration, C, in the Gaussian dispersion model
can be written as:

2.2 Evaluation of Potential Dose

One year of meteorology during the year 2023 was evaluated at each location to provide an
example of potential downwind distances to selected dose limits for various exposure and
accident scenarios. The parameters used for accident scenarios are given in Table 3. For each
scenario, the simulation assumed that the release consisted entirely of HTO given that the
inhalation committed effective dose for HTO is approximately 10,000 times larger than for HT.
The effective dose conversion factor for tritium is approximately 64 mrem Ci-1; we use a dose
factor here that is increased by a factor of 1.5 to account for HTO absorption through the skin,
resulting in a dose conversion factor of 96 mrem Ci-1 (Karajgikar et al. 2023).

4.1 Consideration of Stack Height

To inform if releases from different stack heights should be considered in the site-specific
analysis, further inspection of predicted downstream concentration and it’s relation to stack
height was performed. Adjustments in stack height were found to have more localized impacts
(Figure 8). Closer to the source, differences spanned at least one order of magnitude regardless
of atmospheric stability class. Further from the source, the downwind concentration prediction
converged, though the distance to convergence differed based on stability. For more stable,
cases, it took approximately 2 km of transport to reach full convergence while under unstable
conditions, convergence occurred within a few 100s of meters.
Due to higher concentrations resulting from surface releases for dose considerations, and thus
more conservative conditions for the purpose of performing a safety basis analysis, only surface
releases will be considered for the following analyses.

4.2 Safety Basis Distances

For each scenario, the distance to the selected dose limit was calculated as the 95th percentile
case, suggesting that conditions would only extend beyond the selected distance approximately
5 percent of the time. The use of 95th percentile meteorology is a common practice in safety
basis analyses. Plume projections were grouped based on wind direction using each of 16 bins
representing winds from the N, NNE, NE, ENE, etc. This was done to examine potential
variation due to changes in wind direction distribution at each location.

5.0 Summary

This report has illustrated the use of a Gaussian plume model to evaluate the potential for
downwind concentration and dose for a range of hypothetical releases that could result from a
fusion reactor. Based on the calculations and measured or estimated factors such as breathing
rate, duration of exposure, and wind speed, evaluations can be made which predict potential

 13

consequences to human health and safety. For siting of new fusion plants, it is possible that
location-specific considerations could be made incorporating regional wind patterns relative to
urban or other public areas, but additional considerations and regulatory permissions would be
needed to ensure all safety regulations are being met.
