A Quantum Encoding of Traveling Salesperson Tours via Route Generation, Cost Phases, and a Valid-Permutation Oracle
Alexander Johannes Stasik, Franz Georg Fuchs

Abstract
We present a compact quantum encoding of the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) based on a time-register representation of tours. A candidate route is represented as a sequence of n n city labels over discrete time steps, with one fixed start city and the remaining cities encoded in binary registers. We describe three ingredients of the construction: uniform route generation over the route register, a reversible oracle for marking valid tours, and a phase oracle that encodes the total tour cost. The validity oracle distinguishes permutations of the non-start cities from invalid assignments, while the cost oracle accumulates the contribution of the start edge, intermediate transitions, and return edge into a tour-dependent phase. This yields a coherent superposition of candidate routes with feasibility and tour-length information embedded directly in the quantum state. The number of qubits required is 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ ( n ) ) \mathcal{O}\left(n\log_{2}(n)\right) and the circuit depth scales quadratically in n n . The encoding is compatible with amplitude amplification or spectral filtering techniques such as the quantum singular value transform (QSVT) or Grover’s algorithm. However, due to the exponentially small fraction of valid tours, the overall complexity remains exponential even when combined with amplitude amplification.



Keywords:
Traveling Salesperson Problem, Traveling Salesman Problem, quantum encoding, phase oracle, permutation oracle, quantum algorithms

Introduction

The Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) is one of the standard benchmark problems in combinatorial optimization
[
9
]
. Given a weighted graph on
n
cities, the goal is to find a minimum-cost Hamiltonian cycle that visits each city exactly once and returns to the start. The difficulty of the problem lies in the factorial growth of the number of valid tours with the number of cities.

Quantum algorithms provide a natural language for representing exponentially large combinatorial spaces in superposition. Canonical approaches to combinatorial search include Grover-style amplitude amplification methods
[
5
,
1

, quantum backtracking and constraint-satisfaction constructions
[

2

[
4
,
11
]
.

Edge-based encodings of the Traveling Salesperson Problem represent candidate solutions through binary variables associated with graph edges, indicating whether a given edge is included in the tour. Such formulations arise naturally in QUBO and Ising-type models used in quantum annealing and variational algorithms. In particular, unconstrained binary formulations of TSP variants explicitly compare edge-based and node-based encodings and analyze their resource requirements, e.g.,

In contrast, we adopt a route-based encoding in a quantum circuit model, where candidate tours are represented directly as time-ordered sequences of city labels. This makes the permutation structure explicit and allows feasibility and cost information to be encoded using reversible quantum oracles.

The contribution of this note is modest and concrete. We describe a compact circuit construction consisting of uniform route generation over a time register, a valid-permutation oracle that marks valid tours, and a phase oracle encoding the tour cost. The goal is not to claim a practical quantum speedup but to provide a clear baseline encoding suitable for small simulated instances and for later combination with amplitude amplification or spectral filtering methods.

2
Encoding

Let
V=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
denote the set of cities, and let city
n-1
be fixed as the start and end city. A TSP tour is determined by an ordering of the remaining
n-1
cities. We encode this ordering across
T=n-1
time steps. Each time step stores one city label from
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
. Since the start and end city is fixed, we do not need to encode it. Let

b=\lceil\log_{2}(n-1)\rceil

be the number of qubits required to encode one label. The route register is

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{route}}=\bigotimes_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{C}^{2^{b}},

and hence
Tb=n\log_{2}(n)
qubits are needed for the encoding.
A basis state

\ket{\x}=\ket{x_1}\cdots\ket{x_T}

represents a candidate route. Valid tours correspond precisely to permutations of
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
. We prepare a uniform superposition over the full route register:

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{unif}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{bT}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{bT}}\ket{\x},

implemented by Hadamard gates on all route qubits. This coherently enumerates all candidate routes, valid and invalid.

2.1
Validity oracle

The feasibility oracle marks all permutations of the non-initial nodes and thus identifies all valid tours, given by the following definition.

Definition 1

(Valid tour)
.

A basis state
\ket{x_1,\dots,x_T}
is valid if the multiset
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{T}\}
equals
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
.
Equivalently, each non-start city label appears exactly once.

The validity oracle computes a Boolean flag

v(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\{x_{1},\dots,x_{T}\}=\{0,\dots,n-2\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We implement this reversibly by introducing one ancilla qubit for each non-start city label,

\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)},

together with a final flag qubit
\ket{0}_{\mathrm{good}}
. For each label

i\in\{0,\dots,n-2\}
,
define the parity indicator

p_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}[x_{t}=i]\pmod{2},

where
[x_{t}=i]\in\{0,1\}
is the equality predicate.

Since
T=n-1
labels are drawn from the same set
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
, the condition that every label
appears exactly once is equivalent to requiring odd parity for every label.

In circuit form, the computation proceeds in two stages:

1.

For each time step
t
and each label
i
, compare the register
\ket{x_t}
with the binary encoding of
i
.
If they are equal, flip the ancilla qubit corresponding to label
i
.
This accumulates the parity of occurrences of label
i
across all time steps.

2.

Apply a multi-controlled
X
gate from all parity ancillas onto the final flag qubit

\ket{\mathrm{good}}
, so that

\mathrm{good}(\mathbf{x})=1\iff p_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1\text{ for all }i.

Hence the validity oracle is

O_{\mathrm{valid}}:\ket{\x}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\ket{0}\longmapsto\ket{\x}\ket{p_0(x),\dots,p_{n-2}(\x)}\ket{v(\x)},

where

v(\mathbf{x})=\bigwedge_{i=0}^{n-2}p_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

For the specific implementation used here, the equality tests are realized by temporarily
bit-flipping controls corresponding to
0
-bits in the binary representation of
i
, followed by a multi-controlled
X
onto the parity ancilla, and then uncomputing those temporary
X
gates.
The final validity bit is obtained by a multi-controlled
X
from all parity ancillas onto the

\mathrm{good}
qubit.

The validity oracle uses
(n-1)^{2}
MCX gates of type
C^{b}X
, arising from comparisons at each time step and label, together with a single
C^{\,n-1}X
gate to compute the final validity flag.
According to Table
1
the validity oracle can be implemented using

\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\log_{2}(n)\right)
CX- and
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\log_{2}(n)\right)
T-gates.

2.2
Cost oracle

The cost oracle assigns a phase to each candidate route according to its total length.

Definition 2

(Tour cost)
.

A basis state
\ket{x_1,\ldots,x_T}
has cost

L(\mathbf{x})=C_{0,x_{1}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}C_{x_{t},x_{t+1}}+C_{x_{T},0}.

The oracle computes a phase

O_{\mathrm{cost}}\ket{\x}=e^{iL(\mathbf{x})/\Lambda}\ket{\x},

encoding the tour length into the quantum state.
Here
\Lambda
is an upper bound on the longest tour length.
\Lambda
is bound by the longest edge in the graph times
n
, the number of cities, so
\Lambda\leq\max(e)\cdot n
. Any tighter limit will make subsequent amplifications more efficient due to a larger spectral gap.

In circuit form, the oracle may be implemented by applying controlled phase rotations for each edge contribution appearing in
L(\mathbf{x})
. This naturally decomposes into three parts:

1.

a phase conditioned on the first label
x_{1}
, contributing the start edge
C_{0,x_{1}}
,

2.

phases conditioned on each adjacent pair
(x_{t},x_{t+1})
for
t=1,\dots,T-1
, contributing the intermediate edges,

3.

a phase conditioned on the final label
x_{T}
, contributing the return edge
C_{x_{T},0}
.

For the naive multiplexed implementation used here, the start and return terms require
(n-1)

b
-controlled phase gates each, while the intermediate transitions require one
2b
-controlled phase gate for every ordered pair of non-start labels at every transition step. Since there are
T-1
transition positions and
(n-1)^{2}
ordered label pairs per position, the naive cost oracle scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\right)
in the number of multi-controlled phase operations.
According to Table
1
one needs therefore
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\log_{2}(n)\right)
CX gates and
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}(\log_{2}(n)+\log_{2}(1/\epsilon))\right)
T gates for this naive implementation.
Since gates acting on disjoint time steps can be executed in parallel, the circuit has depth
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\log_{2}(n)\right)
in CX gates and
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}(\log_{2}(n)+\log_{2}(1/\epsilon))\right)
in T gates.

The route register consists of
T=n-1
time registers, each of size
b=\lceil\log_{2}(n-1)\rceil
, encoding the sequence of non-start cities. Applying Hadamard gates prepares a uniform superposition over candidate label strings. The reversible oracle
O_{\mathrm{valid}}
uses auxiliary work qubits and the flag qubit
val
to mark whether the encoded sequence is a valid permutation of
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
. The oracle
O_{\mathrm{cost}}
then applies a phase
e^{i\phi(x)}
for each route
x
, with
\phi(x)\propto L(x)
, encoding the total tour length. Internal equality tests, counting logic, and controlled-phase sub-circuits are suppressed.

1
. Note that in this example, the graph in non-symmetric. This does not have any impact to our approach, making it agnostic to the graph structure.

4
How to use the prepared state

The prepared state naturally supports subsequent amplification procedures. A first step is to restrict the solution space to the valid subspace using phase-adapted amplitude amplification based on the phase version of the validity oracle
[
1
,
5
,
8
]
. Since the route register contains
(n-1)^{n-1}
possible label assignments, while only
(n-1)!
of these correspond to valid permutations, the fraction of valid states is known explicitly as

\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-1)^{n-1}}\sim\sqrt{2\pi(n-1)}\,e^{-(n-1)}

by Stirling’s approximation
[
6
]
. Consequently, the cost of amplitude amplification scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{p}}}\right)
, so approximately as
\exp((n-1)/2)/(2\pi(n-1))^{1/4}
, growing exponentially in
n
. Because this success amplitude is known in advance, one may use phase-adapted Grover
[
8
]
iterations to rotate exactly into the valid subspace, thereby preparing the normalized equal superposition over all valid tours. Although this procedure provides a quadratic speedup over classical rejection sampling, the exponentially small fraction of valid configurations implies that the overall cost remains exponential in
n
. Once restricted to the valid sector, one may then apply further transformations that favor shorter tours, for example threshold-based amplitude amplification, polynomial filtering, or QSVT-based spectral methods
[
1
,
4
]
.

In principle, one could instead attempt to bias the full superposition toward low-cost assignments before isolating valid tours. However, because invalid strings dominate the Hilbert space, cost amplification prior to feasibility filtering tends to waste amplitude on invalid candidate routes. For this reason, it is typically more effective to first amplify the valid subspace and then apply cost-selective amplification within it, although hybrid strategies are also possible depending on circuit depth constraints and oracle costs.

7
Conclusion

We presented a compact quantum encoding of TSP tours based on a time-register representation. The route register encodes candidate routes directly, the validity oracle isolates valid Hamiltonian cycles, and the cost oracle attaches the objective function as a coherent phase.
The implementation uses
\mathcal{O}\left(n\log_{2}(n)\right)
qubits and requires
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\log_{2}(n)\right)
CX gates and
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}(\log_{2}(n)+\log_{2}(1/\epsilon))\right)
T gates. Since gates acting on disjoint time steps can be executed in parallel, the circuit has depth
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\log_{2}(n)\right)
in CX gates and
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}(\log_{2}(n)+\log_{2}(1/\epsilon))\right)
in T gates.

The present construction is primarily representational and does not yield a polynomial-time quantum algorithm, as the fraction of valid tours is exponentially small, leading to exponential cost even with amplitude amplification. In addition, the naive cost oracle incurs
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\right)
controlled operations, resulting in high overall gate complexity despite partial parallelization. Future work should therefore focus on more efficient oracle constructions, for example using reversible arithmetic or block-encoding techniques, as well as alternative encodings that increase the fraction of valid states. Another promising direction is the use of spectral methods such as QSVT to bias the state toward low-cost tours without explicit feasibility projection.
