Limitations to air free cooling in data centers under rising heat and humidity
Authors: Christina Karamperidou, Jake W. Casselman, Sean B. Cleveland, Damena Agonafer, Vivek V. Manepalli, Roshith Mittakolu, Lingnan Lin, Gerardo A. Rivera Tello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable data center operation depends on effective thermal management, with air free cooling (direct air-side economization) serving as a widely deployed energy-saving and water-minimizing strategy, implemented either as a primary mode or within hybrid cooling architectures that incorporate periods of direct exposure to ambient air. This study provides a global assessment of historical and projected climate-driven constraints on air free cooling operability using high-resolution hourly observations and climate model simulations. Over the past 45 years, the number of hours characterized by co-occurring high temperature and humidity conditions that limit direct air cooling has increased significantly, particularly across the tropics and the southeastern United States. Even regions with modest long-term trends experience longer daily exceedance events, highlighting intensifying operational thermal stress. A site- and market-level analysis further shows that the share of data centers exposed to conditions that limit air free cooling availability for at least one quarter of the year is rising. Projections through mid-century indicate continued expansion of these constraints under sustained warming and increasing humidity. These results define evolving climatic boundary conditions for facilities incorporating direct air free cooling and provide a quantitative basis for evaluating adaptive cooling strategies and hybrid system designs under future climate conditions.

Introduction

The reliable operation of data centers has become essential to all sectors of modern society, including government services, power grid operation, banking, defense, disaster relief, space exploration, medical research, academia, and the private sector. Global data center electricity consumption is expected to grow at annual rates as high as 15–20%, driven in part by the rapid development of AI/ML capabilities1–3. A substantial share of this electricity is devoted to thermal management, ranging from approximately 7% for efficient hyperscale data centers to as much as 40% for less-efficient enterprise data centers3–5.

Economization strategies are widely deployed to reduce cooling energy demand by limiting reliance on mechanical refrigeration5–8. Air-side economizers operate either by directly introducing outdoor air into the data center (direct air-side economization or air free cooling) or by transferring heat between outdoor and indoor air streams via a heat exchanger (indirect air-side economization). Water-side economizers leverage naturally cold water sources or use outside air to cool circulating water that subsequently conditions indoor air5. In suitable climates, economization can reduce cooling energy demand by 20–50% and improve Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), defined as the ratio of total facility energy use to IT energy use5,7,9.

The feasibility of these strategies depends strongly on regional climate, air quality, and system design constraints. Water-side and indirect evaporative systems are primarily governed by wet-bulb temperature, whereas air free cooling is constrained by combined temperature and humidity requirements because outdoor air is supplied directly to the IT space and must meet recommended inlet conditions. Air free cooling offers substantial energy savings without direct water use–an increasingly salient advantage amid growing concern over data center water consumption and regional water stress.

In practice, air free cooling is implemented either as a primary mode in favorable climates or within hybrid configurations in which ambient conditions determine the fraction of the year during which compressor-free operation is feasible. Industry surveys indicate continued deployment of such air-based economization strategies: in the Uptime Institute Cooling Systems Survey 2025, 29% of respondents reported using fresh-air cooling and 26% reported using indirect air cooling, while supplier-reported trends indicate that in North America, 27% of respondents reported that most of their customers and 70% that at least some customers deploy direct air free cooling10,11. Characterizing the climatic boundary conditions that govern the feasibility of these cooling strategies is therefore essential for understanding how environmental change affects their availability and operational trade-offs.

Recent heat waves have underscored the vulnerability of even the most energy-efficient hyperscale data centers to increased temperatures, as exemplified by the major outages at centers operated by Oracle and Google in London and Twitter in Sacramento during the heatwaves of 2022. In these events, both dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures reached the tails of their historical distributions12. This raises concerns about heightened stress on data center operation, as humid heat exposure has increased over the past few decades13 and is projected to further increase in the future14. Superposed on such long-term trends, year-to-year climate variations, such as El Niño or La Niña conditions in the tropical Pacific, can further amplify year-to-year exposure to air free cooling–limiting conditions12.

Previous studies examining temperature and humidity constraints on data center cooling have largely focused on individual facilities or city- and climate zone-scale analyses based on typical meteorological years7,15–18. Available feasibility maps of air-side economization rely primarily on climatological dry-bulb temperature and dew point19, while ASHRAE guidance provides illustrative reliability estimates based on outdoor temperature alone20. As a result, the combined influence of co-occurring temperature and humidity trends, extremes, and spatial patterns–particularly under operating regimes that do not assume active moisture control–remains insufficiently characterized at global scale.

Here, we present a global, hourly-resolved assessment of historical and projected exceedances of temperature and humidity thresholds relevant to air free cooling. Using high-resolution, multi-decadal observational datasets and climate model simulations, we quantify trends, interannual variability, and extremes in joint temperature–humidity conditions and evaluate the resulting exposure across major data center markets. The analysis is explicitly scoped to air free cooling (i.e., direct air-side economization) in which outdoor air is introduced directly to the IT space without active humidity conditioning. Accordingly, the results characterize the evolving climatic feasibility window for water-free direct air cooling and should not be interpreted as the maximum achievable compressor-free cooling potential across all cooling technologies.

Results

Combined temperature-humidity constraints to air free cooling

These recommendations define environmental operating limits for the direct introduction of outdoor air into the data center without any active moisture control.

The inlet temperature threshold protects against overheating of equipment, which can lead to failure and outages. The dew point temperature threshold reflects the absolute moisture content of the air inside the data center and prevents condensation on cold surfaces inside servers and keeps latent load on cooling coils at manageable levels. The relative humidity threshold protects against surface wetness and reduces the risk of corrosion. Both the temperature and humidity recommendations should be simultaneously satisfied for air free cooling; considering dew point alone for the humidity thresholds does not suffice, as a printed circuit board would corrode at high relative humidity even when dew point is low21–23. Depending on equipment density and ambient air pollution levels17, allowable operating ranges can be wider19; for instance, certain equipment classes allow dew points up to 24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C. Such extensions are typically treated as short-duration operational excursions, as operation above the recommended thresholds is associated with higher failure rates due to thermal stress and corrosion24,25. In practice, prolonged exceedances generally necessitate a transition away from air free cooling toward increased mechanical cooling to re-establish recommended inlet conditions, or the adoption of alternative economization strategies such as indirect air-side or water-side methods.

Here, we calculate the number of hours per day during which air free cooling is prevented due to exceedance of the recommended upper limits for inlet temperature and humidity, hereafter referred to as “out-of-spec hours”. While the ASHRAE guidelines define an acceptable operating envelope based on simultaneous constraints on temperature, relative humidity, and dew point, our analysis focuses specifically on subset of exceedance conditions that limit air free cooling under elevated heat and moisture. Hours exceeding the defined thresholds represent periods during which air free cooling would be restricted or unavailable because the direct introduction of outdoor air into the IT space could lead to increased corrosion risk, condensation potential, and elevated latent loads, rather than periods when compressor-free cooling is universally infeasible.

Simultaneous exceedance of temperature and dew point typically occurs in warm, moisture-laden environments such as coastal or tropical regions, where both sensible and latent heat loads are high. Except for western North America, northern and western Europe, south Australia, and dry inland areas with low population and economic activity density (e.g., the Saharan desert, Siberia), most regions experience at least 2 hours per day above the recommended thresholds, averaged annually (Fig. 1a), and during their respective summer season (Supp. Fig. 1). Tropical regions average above 6 out-of-spec hours per day annually, and between 8 and 24 hours during the boreal summer months (Supp. Fig. 1e). This includes regions with significant population and economic activity density, such as South and Southeast Asia. In the summer months, the southeastern United States averages between 10 and 16 hours per day out-of-spec, and at least 2 to 7 hours further north (Supp. Fig. 1). Over the past 45 years, there has been a marked increase in the number of out-of-spec hours per day (Fig. 1b). The most significant increases have occurred in the tropics, amounting to up to 2 hours per day per decade. The trends exhibit distinct seasonal variations (Supp. Fig. 1); for example, the eastern United States has experienced increases of approximately 1.5 hours per day per decade during the summer months.

On average, the most extreme day in each year during 1980-2024 featured about 24 hours of exceedance in most regions in the subtropics and tropics, including the southeast United States, south and east Asia, and most of Australia (Fig. 1c). In Europe, the most extreme days feature about 12 hours out-of-spec, even in its northern regions. This value represents the long-term mean of the annual maximum number of out-of-spec hours within a single day and provides a benchmark for the intensity of full-day exceedance events.

In regions where annual maxima do not already approach the 24-hour upper bound, trends in the annual maximum daily exceedance (Fig. 1d) reveal a shift toward longer-duration exceedance events, suggesting a greater operational risk associated with single-day extremes. Such changes underscore the need to account not only for average exposure but also for the evolving severity of the worst days, especially in the summer season (Supp. Fig. 2). These worst-day trends are substantially stronger than trends in the annual mean daily exceedance (cf. Fig. 1b and 1d), with increases exceeding 4 hours per day per decade and a peak value of 5.85 hours per day per decade along the Black Sea coast. The amplification is especially evident in coastal regions, the Mediterranean, and the Middle East, indicating that climate-driven changes are disproportionately affecting the most extreme days and reflecting a widening of the upper tail of the joint temperature-humidity distribution.

In regions such as Southeast Asia, the southeastern United States, and much of Australia, where the most extreme days already approach continuous exceedance, further intensification cannot be expressed as increases in the annual maximum due to metric saturation. In these cases, muted trends in annual maxima should be interpreted as a transition toward persistently unfavorable daily conditions rather than as evidence of stabilization.

While the annual maximum highlights changes in the intensity of the most extreme days, the total number of out-of-spec hours per year (Fig. 1e) captures cumulative exposure across all days, providing a complementary perspective on evolving environmental constraints. Similar to the average and worst-day metrics, the western United States, western Asia, and the European region present the most conducive climate for meeting the recommended temperature and humidity thresholds for data centers, spending on average most of the year below them (Fig. 1e). On the contrary, the southeast United States averages about a quarter to a third of the year out-of-spec (≈3,000 hours), and most tropical regions are on average above the thresholds the majority of the year. In the tropical regions, the total number of hours per year has increased significantly over the last 45 years (Fig. 1f), by more than 300 hours per year. These positive trends of total annual out-of-spec hours in the subtropics, tropics, and coastal regions indicate a systematic lengthening of the total time spent above the recommended thresholds, reflecting a broadening of exceedance frequency in addition to the aforementioned intensification of individual extremes. The total hours and their trends are not only dominated by the summertime but also by the shoulder season values (Supp. Fig. 3), potentially reflecting an expansion of the summer season in the past 50 years26.

Trends in the 90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles of daily exceedance hours (Supp. Fig. 4) closely mirror trends in the total annual number of out-of-spec hours, indicating that percentile-based metrics primarily capture changes in the frequency and persistence of unfavorable days rather than further amplification of the single worst day (Fig. 1d). Percentile metrics are substantially lower than the annual maxima and may therefore understate the operational significance of the most extreme days. From an operational perspective, both metrics are relevant, but the worst day is particularly critical, as it often governs contingency planning, system overrides, and failure risk.

Exceedance tiers

Current thermal guidelines for data centers20 do not specify fixed annual exceedance hours for optimizing economization; depending on hardware robustness and each center’s operational needs and load, multiple allowable exceedance budgets can be defined. Here, we consider four exceedance tiers (Fig. 2) based on the fraction of the year during which air free cooling is constrained by inlet temperature and humidity limits: Locations in Tier 0 exceed the recommended upper limits for only 1% of the year (≤ 88 hr) and are therefore considered optimal for year-round air free cooling. Locations in Tier 1 exceed the limits for up to a quarter of the year, remaining within operable conditions for approximately three quarters of the year and thus generally conducive to sustained economizer use. Locations within Tier 2 exceed the upper limits for more than 1/4 year but less than 1/2 year, allowing for intermittent air free cooling and partial energy savings for most of the year. In Tier 3, the limits are exceeded for more than 1/2 year, rendering air free cooling largely impractical as a primary cooling strategy. Very few locations globally permit operation in air free cooling mode for approximately 99% of the year under the criteria outlined in this study (Fig. 2a). These locations include western North America and parts of Northern Europe. As a reminder, this analysis is intentionally scoped to limitations arising from elevated heat and humidity, and therefore does not account for lower-limit constraints associated with cold conditions, nor does it consider periods in which active humidification or dehumidification could extend the applicability of air free cooling19. As such, the Tier-0 classification should be interpreted as indicating regions with minimal heat-humidity limitations, rather than as universally optimal climates under all operating assumptions.

Many major data center markets, including the central and eastern United States, Europe, and eastern China, remain below the upper temperature and humidity thresholds for at least three quarters of the year. Other large markets, such as South Asia, remain below the thresholds for more than 50% of the year, enabling significant–though intermittent–energy savings via air free cooling. In contrast, certain tropical regions, including mainland Southeast Asia and the Maritime Continent (Indonesia and surrounding island nations), exceed the thresholds for more than half of the year, rendering air free cooling frequently infeasible as a primary cooling strategy. This pattern highlights the growing importance of alternative, energy-efficient cooling technologies in hot and humid climates, where reliance on direct exposure to ambient air is increasingly constrained.

Beyond the trends, there is apparent interannual and decadal variability in the number of data centers in each exceedance tier. Frequency analysis reveals significant spectral peaks at 3.8, 9–12, and 28 years (Supp. Fig.5). These results are consistent with the findings of Karamperidou (2026)12, who demonstrated the impact of these large-scale climate phenomena on the exposure of data centers to extreme humid heat.

The largest data center market, Northern Virginia, which in 2025 comprises 154 data centers of varying size and features the largest installed capacity3, exceeds the recommended upper thresholds of temperature and humidity for approximately 10% of the year. The second-largest market, Silicon Valley, is located in a region with generally favorable heat and humidity conditions and correspondingly limited restrictions on air free cooling. In contrast, the Dallas-Fort Worth market, comprising 75 data centers, exceeds the recommended thresholds for more than 20% of the year. Outside the United States, the Pearl River Delta market in China, encompassing Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Hong Kong, is exposed to limiting conditions to air free cooling for more than 40% of the year. Singapore represents the most extreme case among major markets, with exceedances occurring during more than 85% of the year. Operation in such environments typically requires equipment and cooling systems capable of tolerating sustained high temperature and humidity, or increased reliance on alternative cooling approaches when air free cooling is constrained.

Future projections

By 2050, the number of hours that exceed the upper thresholds of temperature and humidity for air free cooling, both at daily and annual scales, is projected to increase in climate model simulations under high greenhouse gas emissions compared to historical simulations (Fig. 4). In most regions globally, the average number of hours per day during which air free cooling is constrained increases by more than 2 hours/day, primarily driven by increases during each region’s summertime (Supp. Fig. 6). Especially in the Northern Hemisphere, the mean out-of-spec hours in the summertime are projected to increase by more than 3 hours over most of the middle latitudes, especially in North America, southern Europe, and across Asia (Supp. Fig.6g). The site-level analysis reveals that markets already exposed to elevated heat and humidity for at least 20% of the year in the past 45 years show large projected increases in air free cooling-limiting conditions under future climate scenarios; these increases mostly exceed interannual variability (Fig. 3b). The largest increases in the fraction of the year during which air free cooling is constrained, of more than 10% compared to the observed 1980-2024 values, are found for Singapore and the Pearl River Delta region. Singapore, which already experiences limiting heat and humidity conditions for more than 85% of the year, is projected to exceed the recommended upper thresholds year-round. In addition, several large markets that are currently less exposed, including Dublin, Chicago, and Northern Virginia, show substantial proportional increases in threshold exceedance hours, signaling emerging operational risk for facilities that rely on direct exposure to ambient air for cooling.

Summary and discussion

Successful deployment of air free cooling (direct air-side economization) has been demonstrated since the 2000s and remains a widely used strategy for hyperscale and enterprise data centers in suitable climates. It is implemented either as a primary mode or within hybrid architectures that alternate between economizer and mechanical cooling depending on ambient conditions. Because outdoor air is supplied directly to the IT space, ambient temperature and humidity jointly determine both the duration and effectiveness of air free cooling. Even in tropical regions, partial-year operation has been shown to yield meaningful energy savings.

Previous assessments of the applicability of air-side economization have generally relied on typical meteorological years or site-specific weather-station analyses18,19, without examining multi-decadal trends, extremes, or interannual variability. Here, we provide a global, hourly-resolved assessment of historical and projected climate-driven constraints on air free cooling based on multi-decadal observations and climate model simulations. By resolving trends, interannual variability, and changes in the joint temperature–humidity distribution, the analysis characterizes evolving climatic boundary conditions and associated operational exposure for facilities that incorporate direct air free cooling within their thermal management strategies.

The results should be interpreted as a climate-based characterization of the environmental feasibility window for air free cooling under water-free, direct-air operating assumptions. Specifically, the results quantify the occurrence of co-occurring high-temperature and high-moisture conditions that are most restrictive for air free cooling, rather than all exceedances of the ASHRAE recommended operating envelope. Facilities that permit active moisture control may extend economizer availability beyond the limits shown here; however, the global framework reveals spatial coherence, concurrent exposure, and emerging risk across major data center markets that are not apparent in isolated site-scale analyses. In this way, the study complements existing local assessments by placing them within a broader climate context.

Over the past 45 years, we identify significant increases in both daily and annual exceedance hours that constrain air free cooling due to co-occurring high temperature and humidity conditions exceeding recommended thresholds. The strongest trends occur in tropical regions and the southeastern United States. Even in regions with modest long-term mean trends, such as parts of Europe and the Middle East, worst-day statistics show increasing intensity of peak events, with exceedances increasingly spanning full days. These shifts indicate growing persistence and duration of environmental conditions that limit air free cooling within both primary and hybrid configurations.

At the site and market scale, we find increasing numbers of data centers exposed to combined heat and moisture conditions that limit air free cooling for at least one quarter of the year. Some markets, such as Singapore, already exceed recommended thresholds for most of the year. Climate model projections through mid-century indicate continued expansion of these constraints under sustained warming. Regions currently considered climatically optimal–remaining below thresholds for approximately 99% of the year–are projected to become less favorable. Even markets with historically low exposure show substantial relative increases in annual exceedance hours, signaling emerging operational risk.

The focus on upper-limit constraints reflects the increasing importance of humid heat stress under a changing climate. While cold conditions can impose operational requirements (e.g., humidification or preheating), these are generally more readily mitigated through system controls than sustained high temperature–humidity exposure. Including cold thresholds would reduce the apparent favorability of some high-latitude regions but would not alter the central finding that rising heat and humidity increasingly constrain air free cooling availability across many present-day markets.

This analysis draws on a database of 9,447 data centers globally as of July 2025. Because future growth is typically reported in terms of power demand, floor area, or compute capacity rather than facility counts, we estimate, based on projected global electricity demand and anticipated site-size mix, that roughly 20,000 additional facilities may be required by mid-century.

Within this broader expansion, our results provide insight into the relative climatic suitability of locations for facilities incorporating air free cooling, without prescribing siting or technology decisions. Climate is only one determinant of data center placement; power cost and availability, network infrastructure, security, regulation, and workforce considerations also shape deployment. The findings therefore contribute to strategic planning under evolving environmental conditions.

Conclusion

This study shifts the focus from isolated facilities to a global, hourly-resolved assessment of climate-driven constraints on air free cooling, defined here as direct air-side economization without active humidity conditioning. The global perspective reveals patterns in co-occurring high temperature–humidity exceedances that are relevant to portfolio-level planning, redundancy strategies, and long-term infrastructure investment.

Exceedance metrics derived from climatic thresholds correspond to hours during which air free cooling under direct-air, water-free operating assumptions must be curtailed, increasing reliance on mechanical or alternative cooling within hybrid systems. These metrics reflect conditions in which elevated temperature coincides with high atmospheric moisture, rather than all individual exceedances of recommended temperature or humidity limits. Although facility-level energy use or failure probabilities are not explicitly modeled, the metrics provide a first-order indicator of operational stress, reliability, and adaptive cooling requirements. Observed and projected trends indicate increasing exposure to both persistent exceedance conditions and extreme single-day events. In addition, localized urban heat island effects and waste heat feedbacks, which are not fully resolved in reanalysis products such as ERA531, can elevate near-surface temperature (and in some cases humidity) in dense urban environments32–36. As a result, the constraints identified here may be conservative at local scales.

These findings do not imply that compressor-free cooling is infeasible in warm or humid regions, but rather that the environmental window for direct air free cooling is narrowing under rising heat and humidity. Alternative strategies–including indirect evaporative cooling, liquid cooling, and hybrid architectures–can partially offset these constraints, albeit with distinct trade-offs in water use, system complexity, and operational design.

At the hardware level, liquid-based approaches reduce dependence on ambient air. Immersion cooling offers high heat-transfer performance37; single-phase systems circulate dielectric coolant without phase change, while two-phase systems leverage evaporation and condensation to achieve PUE values near 1.1538, particularly for high-density AI and HPC workloads. These systems, however, face material, environmental, and regulatory constraints. Direct-to-chip cold-plate systems provide a more immediately deployable alternative, as they leverage existing infrastructure and avoid the fluid compatibility challenges of full immersion39–41 by circulating coolant through microchannels in contact with processors. Emerging two-phase direct-to-chip designs operating at elevated coolant temperatures (50–70\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}C inlet) reduce chiller loads and enable more efficient use of dry coolers or ambient liquid loops, as well as heat recovery for secondary applications42–46. Together, these approaches complement air free cooling by mitigating exposure to warm, humid air, and improving performance where free cooling alone is insufficient.

Beyond hardware optimization, at the system-management level, predictive analytics and advanced control strategies further enhance cooling flexibility47,48. Model Predictive Control and AI-driven optimization improve responsiveness and energy efficiency49–54, while integrated airflow and building-energy simulations55,56 support coordinated system management.

In conclusion, this study defines the evolving climatic boundary conditions governing air free cooling availability under continued warming and increasing humidity. Because air free cooling requires no direct water use, its environmental feasibility has implications beyond energy efficiency, particularly amid growing scrutiny of water consumption by data centers. As climatic constraints intensify, integrated, technology-aware strategies that combine adaptive controls, hardware innovation, and climate-informed planning will be essential for sustaining reliable and resource-efficient digital infrastructure.

Data and methods

Data

To calculate exceedances of relevant thresholds, we use hourly temperature and humidity fields at 0.25 degree resolution from the Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts ERA5 reanalysis dataset57, spanning 1940-2025. Locations for 9,447 data centers are extracted from the Data Center Map Database58 and are used here with permission by the database administrators for academic research purposes. We use 3-hourly climate model output from the historical (highres-SSTpresent) and future (highres-SSTfuture) high-resolution simulation with the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFDL) model CM4-CM192 with nominal resolutions of 50 km in the atmosphere and 25 km in the ocean59. In the historical experiment, the model’s atmosphere component is forced with observed sea surface temperature, sea-ice, and historical forcings (greenhouse gas concentrations, aerosols, solar and volcanic forcing) in the period 1950-2014. In the future experiment, the model is forced with the multimodel ensemble mean sea surface temperature and sea ice from the CMIP5 RCP8.5 simulations over the period 2015-2050, while the climate forcings come from the SSP5-8.5 scenario.

While allowable ASHRAE operating classes permit higher temperature and humidity limits (e.g., A2/A3), the present analysis focuses on recommended conditions to provide a conservative and consistent baseline for assessing climate-driven constraints. Use of broader allowable thresholds would expand the feasible operating window and reduce the absolute number of exceedance hours, although the underlying tendency toward increasing exceedance conditions under warmer and more humid climates would remain.

To calculate the number of hours per day that exceed the ASHRAE limits, we re-index the hourly data so that each longitude is expressed in local solar time rather than UTC. To do this, we divide by 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} per hour and round to the nearest whole hour to obtain an integer offset from UTC. This enables us to compare hour-of-day exceedances in a physically consistent way across all longitudes. Note that this solar shift is not necessary to calculate annual hours out-of-spec, but we retain the data in local solar time for consistency. Local time also allows consideration of operational factors such as diurnal demand cycles and potential load-shifting strategies.

To characterize the most severe daily conditions, we computed the annual maximum number of hours per day exceeding the threshold (i.e., the single “worst day” per year). Averaging these annual maxima over 1980-2024 yields a climatological measure of the typical intensity of the worst day in a given year, distinct from metrics of mean exposure or exceedance frequency. Because this metric is bounded by 24 h, trend estimates may underestimate change in regions where the annual worst day already approaches continuous exceedance. In such cases, further intensification cannot be captured by this metric, and muted trends should be interpreted as indicating a transition to a regime of persistent daily exceedance rather than weak or absent change.

Daily exceedance-hour percentiles (90th, 95th, and 99th) were computed separately for each calendar year from the distribution of daily out-of-spec hours, and monotonic trends in these annual percentile time series were estimated at each grid point using the nonparametric Mann–Kendall test with Sen’s slope, expressed in hours per decade; no correction for serial autocorrelation is applied.

Averages and trends of the metrics are calculated over 1980-2024. This window balances length and relevance: large-scale data center deployment accelerated after the 1980 s, and modern global reanalyses are more reliable when satellite observations began to be assimilated after 1979. Uncertainty is higher prior to the satellite era (pre-1979), particularly for humidity fields; therefore, longer-term trends (Fig. 3a) should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Throughout the analysis, only significant trends (p<0.05) are shown or discussed.

For the climate model simulation output, which is available only as 3-hourly time-mean temperature and humidity, we computed daily out-of-spec hours by flagging each 3-h interval that exceeds the thresholds, summing flags within each local calendar day, and multiplying by three. This treats each 3-hour mean as representative of the full interval (i.e., sub-3-hour variability is unresolved).

The impact of this temporal aggregation is evaluated using ERA5 hourly data resampled to 3-hourly resolution (Supp. Fig.9). Differences in annual exceedance hours are negligible: for a representative year (2020), the area-weighted mean difference is 3.7 hours per year, the median difference is 0 hours per year, and the 5th–95th percentile range is −11 to +23 hours per year. The area-weighted mean relative difference is ≈2.4%, indicating a slight underestimation when using 3-hourly data. These differences are small relative to typical annual exceedance totals, confirming that the use of 3-hourly model output does not materially affect the results.

Site- and market-level analysis

Market-level metrics (% of hours/year out-of-spec) are computed from site samples, not from area averages. For each data center location in our inventory, we extract the annual hours above the ASHRAE recommended thresholds by sampling the nearest model/observational grid point at that site’s latitude–longitude. Those site-level hours are converted to percent of hours per year (using 8,760 h/yr) and then aggregated to markets by taking the simple arithmetic mean across all facilities assigned to that market (markets are defined by lists of cities and states/countries). Thus, every facility carries equal weight; results are not area-weighted, population-weighted, or capacity-weighted.

This provides a measure of proportional change that is not biased by differences in tier size.

Bias correction

Hours of exceedance are computed directly from the raw hourly temperature and dew point fields for ERA5 and the raw 3-hourly climate model output. No bias correction is applied to the underlying fields prior to threshold counting, so the joint temperature–humidity distribution and sub-daily co-occurrence structure are preserved. While advanced multivariate and distribution-preserving bias-correction methods exist, we instead prioritize preserving the model’s native joint temperature–humidity variability and temporal co-occurrence structure by computing exceedances directly from the raw model output.

Bias correction is applied only when presenting absolute future projections on the same scale as the observations. Specifically, we use an additive delta method applied to the aggregated annual exceedance hours, with bias term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = \overline{O}_{\textrm{hist}} - \overline{M}_{\textrm{hist}}$$\end{document} computed over 1980–2014. The bias-adjusted future is then given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{M}_{\textrm{fut}} + \delta$$\end{document}, ensuring that future values are anchored to the observed historical baseline.

For continuous quantities, this correction does not affect the displayed change, which is determined solely by the model-projected difference between future and historical periods (Fig. 4). For categorical metrics such as tier transitions (Fig. 2b), the bias correction ensures that changes are evaluated relative to the observed baseline rather than the model’s historical state.

Because the correction is applied to aggregated exceedance hours rather than to the underlying temperature or humidity fields, the model’s projected changes in joint temperature–humidity exceedances are preserved. The method assumes some degree of stationarity in the aggregated annual exceedance metric being corrected, consistent with additive delta approaches commonly used in climate impacts studies. Previous work has suggested that stationarity of climate model biases is generally more robust for large-scale climatological patterns than for local-scale impact metrics61.

Spectral analysis

Spectra are computed from annual time series of tier counts after removal of the linear trend and mean. A Hann window is applied prior to computing the one-sided discrete Fourier transform, and resulting spectra are lightly smoothed in frequency space using a 5-bin moving average. Statistical significance is assessed relative to a red-noise (AR(1)) null hypothesis, with the lag-1 autocorrelation estimated from the detrended series; no formal confidence intervals are constructed.
