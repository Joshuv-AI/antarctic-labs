Thoracic medial branch peripheral nerve stimulation for adjacent segment pain in a patient with a long construct fusion: a case report
Authors: Lindsay Kate Wanner, Michael A. Fishman, Jennifer Renninger, Tori Yohe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdjacent segment degeneration is a known late complication of long construct fusions. While several experimental treatment options exist, including medial branch blocks, no definitive treatment options have been identified. Previous studies have demonstrated the benefit of restorative stimulation of the erector spinae group on patients’ pain, physical function, and disability. This case hypothesizes that motor and sensory programming at the segment adjacent to the long-construct fusion has the potential to be both therapeutic for this patient’s adjacent segment thoracic pain and protective through improved muscular stability.Case DescriptionA 71-year-old woman with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis and prior T11 to S1 laminectomy with fusion and bilateral pedicle osteotomy presented with moderately severe 7/10 adjacent segment pain. She had previously completed conservative therapy, including physical therapy, epidural steroid injections, and medication management with NSAIDs, muscle relaxants, anticonvulsants, topical lidocaine, and chronic daily opioid use totally 30 morphine milliequivalents daily, without significant relief. Physical exam was significant for bilateral T8–T10 facet pain and flat back kyphosis, without focal or motor sensory deficits. She underwent a peripheral nerve stimulator device implantation with bilateral four electrode leads placed at the bilateral T10 medial branches using a minimally invasive retrograde approach to accommodate the fusion. Leads were advanced in a cephalad to caudad direction aimed at the transverse process of T9. Stimulation was programmed using a combined motor- and sensory-based approach to address both functional restoration and pain control. An external therapy disc was utilized to deliver stimulation, allowing the patient to independently apply and remove therapy, supporting day-to-day management of stimulation based on symptom severity, activity level, and personal preference. At her 3-month follow-up, the patient reports significant relief in her pain with an average pain score of 3/10, improved physical function, and a decrease in her pain medication usage.ConclusionsRestorative neuromodulation using motor and sensory programming is a potentially effective treatment adjunct for select patients with adjacent segment degeneration, with treatment sessions aligned towards pain relief and functional restoration. Further studies are needed to better define its role, protective effects and expected outcomes in this patient population.

Introduction

Background

Long-construct fusions have been known to have degeneration at the adjacent segments (1). In the lumbosacral region, this is usually protected using S2AI fixation (2). There is typically no specific treatment for adjacent segment thoracic pain from thoracolumbar fixation. Pain physicians do perform radiofrequency ablation of the medial branches at these levels, but this remains considered experimental (3). In the lumbar spine, it has been shown that restorative stimulation has a durable improvement in patient pain, function, and disability at 5-year follow-up (4). Restorative stimulation uses motor programming to activate the multifidus muscle chain and improve pain and function (4).

Rationale and knowledge gap

We hypothesized that motor and sensory programming at the segment adjacent to the long-construct fusion would both be therapeutic for this patient’s adjacent segment thoracic pain, as well as protective of her adjacent segment through improved muscular stability. The device and approach were particularly selected to suit the patient’s anatomy, and the retrograde approach was both simple and elegant, such that it can easily be reproduced by pain physicians and surgeons alike.

Objective

To demonstrate the result of restorative neurostimulation in the management of adjacent segment pain in a patient with a long construct fusion. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0063/rc).

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis presented for chronic back pain status-post extensive instrumented fusion. She had been treated for low back pain for over 25 years, initially undergoing an L4–L5 laminectomy in 2002 and an L3–S1 instrumented fusion in 2009 for extensive thoracolumbar scoliosis. Unfortunately, she developed flat back syndrome following the fusion as well as adjacent segment kyphosis, requiring revision. In 2014, the fusion was revised to a T11-S1 laminectomy with fusion and bilateral pedicle osteotomy. Additionally, she underwent surgical repair of a hardware fracture in 2015. She presented to the clinic with moderate to severe 7/10 adjacent segment pain in the thoracic spine. Flexion of the spine exacerbated the pain, and pain was slightly alleviated with positional changes. Medication management included celecoxib 200 mg daily, metaxalone 800 mg three times a day, pregabalin 100 mg two to three times a day, 4% lidocaine patches daily, and chronic daily opioid use totaling 30 morphine milliequivalents daily, without significant relief. She has completed physical therapy and continues to perform a home exercise program. She previously underwent multiple epidural steroid injections without significant pain relief.

On examination, she appeared well developed, standing 5’1 and weighing 146 lbs, with a body mass index (BMI) of 27.1 kg/m2. She had bilateral T8–T10 facet pain and flat back kyphosis. Axial load and slump tests were positive bilaterally, and there was pain on prone instability testing. Strength was 5/5 in bilateral upper and lower extremities, and she had no focal motor or sensory deficits. Deep tendon reflexes were diminished symmetrically in the upper and lower extremities.

Following a comprehensive physical exam and review of the patient’s prior X-rays and lumbar and thoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images, the working diagnosis was determined to be adjacent segment degeneration affecting the T10 level. MRI imaging of the thoracic spine demonstrated moderate degenerative disc disease with associated moderate central canal narrowing at the T6–T10 levels. Radiographic review did not demonstrate evidence of progressive deformity or instability requiring surgical intervention. Differential diagnoses considered included pseudoarthrosis, hardware related pain, and myofascial pain of the surrounding musculature. There was no radiographic evidence of motion across fused segments on dynamic imaging or radiographic signs of nonunion suggestive of pseudoarthrosis. Hardware failure was considered given her history of prior hardware fracture, however there was no radiographic evidence of failure, including screw loosening, rod fracture, or implant mispositioning on X-ray and MRI imaging. Additionally, central sensitization in the setting myofascial pain was also considered, however the patient lacked key symptoms of allodynia, widespread pain, global sensory hyperresponsiveness, and hyperalgesia, making this diagnosis less likely. Given these findings, the most likely diagnosis was determined to be adjacent segment degeneration.

A variety of treatment options were discussed with the patient for management of the adjacent segment degeneration, including epidural steroid injections, medial branch blocks, bracing and additional surgery. It was discussed with the patient that due to the lack of radicular pain and symptoms she would likely not receive significant benefit from epidural steroid injections. Medial branch blocks with progression to radiofrequency ablation and facet injections were considered, however, it was hypothesized that this would further weaken the surrounding musculature and exacerbate the adjacent segment degeneration. Bracing was discussed with the patient but due to the cumbersome and unwieldy nature of the braces she preferred other options. Further surgical revision of the fusion was not indicated, and the patient is not interested in additional revision surgeries of the fusion. Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) of the medial branch nerves using both a sensory programing for pain modulation, and motor programming for potential functional restoration was proposed as an alternative treatment option.

After appropriate workup and counseling, she underwent a PNS trial with electrodes placed at the bilateral T10 medial branches. She reported 60% pain relief following a 5-day trial and elected to move forward with permanent implantation. The permanent device was implanted using a retrograde approach, given the anatomy of the thoracic spine with fusion rods at T10 (Figure 1). The fluoroscope was positioned to firm up the endplates of T9. A skin entry point was marked to permit a trajectory from the cephalad to caudad direction at approximately a 45-degree angle aimed at contacting the transverse process of T9. The skin was anesthetized with 1% lidocaine, and the 4-contact electrode was then placed bilaterally. A 10 mm incision was made through the skin and subcutaneous tissues using fluoroscopic guidance and a 17-gauge Tuohy needle was placed in a cephalo-caudad trajectory until the needle was resting on the transverse process of T9. The same was repeated on the contralateral side in a similar fashion. Motor stimulation was tested at 20 Hz with a 212 µs pulse width until a contraction was perceived. The sensory stimulation was tested at 100 Hz and the patient reported pleasant sensation. Strain relief loops were created at each incision site, and the leads were tunneled into a micro-IPG (1.5 cc) pocket created over the left trapezius (Figure 2).

A combination of a four-electrode 25-cm tined lead and a four-electrode 40-cm tined lead were selected to optimize neural target coverage while maintaining a minimally invasive approach. The use of tined leads allowed fully percutaneous placement, allowing adequate reach to the intended stimulation targets while accounting for the necessary tunneling distance. This lead selection strategy provided procedural flexibility and ensured stable lead positioning without surgical dissection, contributing to procedural efficiency and patient comfort.

Stimulation parameters were programmed using a combined motor- and sensory-based approach to address both functional restoration and pain control. A low-frequency motor program was implemented using stimulation parameters of 12 Hz at 212 µs, with the goal of facilitating neuromuscular activation and supporting functional motor restoration in the thoracic region. Multiple sensory stimulation programs were utilized to address analgesia across a range of frequencies and pulse widths. Sensory parameters included 1,490 Hz at 30 µs, 1,250 Hz at 150 µs, 750 Hz at 250 µs, 250 Hz at 500 µs, and 40 Hz at 1,000 µs. The sensory programs were delivered using a scheduled programming approach, allowing cycling between stimulation settings over defined intervals. This layered programming strategy enabled individualized therapy delivery, combining motor activation with sensory modulation while allowing parameter adjustments.

An external therapy disc was utilized to deliver stimulation, offering a removable and non-implanted power source. This design allowed the patient to independently apply and remove therapy, supporting day-to-day management of stimulation based on symptom severity, activity level, and personal preference. The externalized configuration enabled real-world assessment of therapy effectiveness while preserving patient autonomy and minimizing long-term implant burden.

The patient’s treatment response was assessed using two validated outcome measures, a PROMIS-29 questionnaire and a Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC). The PROMIS-29 questionnaire was administered at baseline before the device trial and implantation; and at the three month follow up visit. All PROMIS-29 domains were given a T-score ranging from 20–80, except for Global Pain which was rated using a 10-point numeric rating scale. The Pain Impact Score is a combination of the raw Pain Intensity [0–10], Pain Interference [4–20], and the inverted Physical Function [4–20] scores. A T-score of 80 indicates maximum symptom severity for Anxiety, Depression, Fatigue, Pain Interference, and Sleep Disturbance domains. For the Physical Function and Social Participation domains, a T-score of 80 indicates the highest level of function. A minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for a PROMIS-29 domain is considered a 5-point change on the individual level.

Patient perspective

“Prior to having my PNS placed, I thought I was going to live with this back pain forever. I tried so many treatments, and nothing worked, and the pain made doing my everyday activities difficult. Now that I have the device, my quality of life has greatly improved, and I can get back to doing the things that I love. The trial and the implant were easy, and my recovery was quick. Sometimes I get a little sore around the generator, but this pain is nothing like what I had before. I am very happy with the procedure and the results.”

Discussion

Key findings

At the 3-month follow-up visit, the patient reported a global pain score of 3/10, from her baseline of 7/10 pain. The patient’s Pain Impact Score improved from 33/50 to 25/50 at the most recent follow up visit. She was given PGIC questionnaire, at the three month follow up, and scored a two for “much improved”. The patient reported significant benefit from thirty-minute sessions twice daily, with perceived contractions when using the motor programming settings and adherence to therapy was confirmed using stimulation session records from the device company. She notes that due to improvement in her pain with the stimulator, she has discontinued using her topical lidocaine patches but continues with the remainder of her prescription medication regimen. No adverse events have occurred to date.

Strengths and limitations

As a single case report, the observations should not be used to make causal inferences, as this data is not generalizable to a larger patient group. This report is limited by a relatively short follow-up period of only 3 months, which precludes the assessment of sustainability and long-term durability of treatment effects. While clinically meaningful improvements were observed across validated outcomes measures during this period, longer term data is needed to evaluate persistence of benefit, potential later complications and overall response trajectory. Ongoing follow-up will aim to address these questions in further analyses.

Comparison with similar research

There is a limited number of prior studies investigating the role of restorative neuromodulation on adjacent segment disease; however, neuromodulation has previously been used to treat failed back surgery syndrome and post-laminectomy syndrome. In patients with axial low back pain, lumbar spine medial branch PNS has been shown to reduce pain scores by fifty percent (5). While these studies examine effectiveness of targeting the lumbar medial branch nerves, and not thoracic, the results support the use of the medial branch target for both functional restoration and relief of axial back pain and informed the hypothesis underlying the present case report. Importantly, prior medial branch PNS studies largely excluded patients with previous surgery at the target level, limiting the generalizability of existing data to patients with adjacent segment disease and highlighting the relevance of the present case.

Previous neuromodulation techniques for the management of persistent spinal pain after fusion surgery include peripheral nerve field stimulation and spinal cord stimulation (SCS). Peripheral nerve field stimulation has been found to decrease visual-analog scores at 12-month follow-ups in patients with failed back surgery syndrome and post-laminectomy syndrome (6,7). Peripheral nerve field stimulation modulates pain via sensory stimulation of distal peripheral nerves, whereas restorative neuromodulation targeting medial branch nerves incorporates motor and sensory activation. While prior studies demonstrate benefit from sensory-targeted stimulation approaches, the efficacy of proximal motor-targeted stimulation in this population remains incompletely characterized. In contrast, SCS targets the central nervous system, with epidural lead placement along the dorsal columns to achieve segmental inhibition; as opposed to the peripheral targets seen in PNS, such as the medial branch of the dorsal ramus (8). SCS has traditionally demonstrated greater efficacy in radicular and neuropathic pain syndromes, whereas medial branch PNS may be better suited for mechanical, nociceptive, axial pain, as observed in the present case (9).

Recent studies exploring the mechanisms associated with neuromodulation suggest that the effects of neuromodulation may extend beyond localized muscle control. Neuromodulation has been shown to change somatosensory function following targeted stimulation, and influence central pain processing and autonomic regulation, supporting the potential for peripheral neuromodulation to induce central neuroplastic adaptations (10,11). This is particularly relevant in the present case, in which the observed clinical improvements may be reflective of global modulation of pain processing pathways in addition to local motor effects.

Explanations of findings

The observed improvements in patient reported outcomes require careful contextualization, as the extent to which these changes can be attributed to the intervention remain uncertain. The reduction of pain intensity from a 7 to a 3 meets thresholds for a minimal clinically important difference, with the patient now in the range of mild pain, however the duration and persistence of benefit remain unknown. Improvements in several PROMIS-29 domains including pain impact and pain interference, also exceed MCID thresholds, but should be considered in the context of ongoing limitations.

Additionally, the impact non-specific effects such as a placebo response, regression to the mean, and natural symptom fluctuation should be considered in regard to the observed improvements. Without a control condition, the contribution of these factors cannot be determined, and the above findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis generating.

Implications and actions needed

The benefits of restorative neuromodulation on adjacent segment pain suggest that this is a minimally invasive and potentially effective treatment for adjacent segment pain following long-construct fusions. This procedure may serve as an alternative treatment for patients who are not surgical candidates either due to risk profile or lack of indication for surgical intervention, such as instability, progressive deformity, or neurologic compromise. Further studies examining the effects of restorative neurostimulation on a larger cohort of patients with adjacent segment pain should be completed.

Conclusions

This case suggests that thoracic medial branch PNS may represent a feasible, minimally invasive option for select patient populations with refractory adjacent segment pain after long construct thoracolumbar fusion, particularly in the absence of a surgical indication. However, broader conclusions regarding efficacy, functional restoration, or protective effects should be interpreted with caution.
