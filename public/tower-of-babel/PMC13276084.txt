The Effect of Variable Resistance Training on Post-Activation Performance Enhancement: A Systematic Review and Three-Level Meta-analysis
Authors: Zhijie Yan, Shengfa Lin, Junchen Wu, Xiaolan Yi, Jianxiu Liu, Ruidong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVariable resistance training (VRT) has been proposed as a practical conditioning strategy to acutely enhance subsequent performance, but the evidence for its ability to induce post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) remains inconsistent. This study systematically quantified the acute effect of VRT on PAPE using a systematic review with a three-level meta-analysis and explored key moderating variables.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, systematic searches were conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and EBSCO databases. Given the nested effect sizes within studies, a three-level meta-analysis model was employed to calculate Hedges’ g, supplemented by subgroup analyses and meta-regression.ResultsThirteen studies (95 effect sizes) were ultimately included. Main effects analysis revealed a significant modest effect of VRT on PAPE (k = 95, g = 0.27, p = 0.0016), though with substantial heterogeneity (I²= 60.1%). Subgroup analysis indicated significant modest improvements in jump performance: Tier-3 participants (g = 0.22) exhibited significant PAPE effects, whereas Tier-2 showed no significant effect; furthermore, PAPE effects were significant for multi-set training (g = 0.43, p = 0.002) and for equal intensity VRT designs (g = 0.39, p = 0.003).ConclusionAcute VRT constitutes an effective strategy for inducing PAPE, yielding small to moderate positive effects. For the acute elicitation of PAPE, high-level athletes may benefit from using VRT during warm-ups or complex training, particularly when multiple sets, an equal-intensity design, and a relatively high proportion (≥ 30%) of variable resistance loading are applied.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01028-7.

Introduction

Post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) is typically defined as an acute improvement in subsequent voluntary performance following a conditioning activity (CA) and an appropriate recovery period [1]. While previous studies have largely focused on the aftermath of high-intensity or near-maximal conditioning activities, recent evidence suggests that moderate-intensity protocols—as well as low-intensity protocols combined with blood flow restriction—are also capable of inducing PAPE [59]. This effect typically manifests within a window of several minutes following conditioning and can yield substantial improvements in athletic performance [2]. Currently, PAPE-oriented conditioning strategies are frequently used in warm-up and complex-training contexts to acutely enhance subsequent explosive performance [55–57]. The induction of PAPE fundamentally depends on the dynamic equilibrium between potentiating physiological mechanisms (such as myosin light chain phosphorylation) and muscle fatigue [1]. Consequently, optimizing CA protocols hinges on maximizing the potentiation effect while minimizing fatigue accumulation [58, 59].

Variable Resistance Training (VRT) is a training method that employs equipment such as iron chains and elastic bands to achieve dynamic load variation throughout the entire range of motion (ROM) [3]. Previous research has confirmed that long-term VRT can effectively enhance athletes’ muscular strength and explosive power [4–6]. Furthermore, by aligning with the human body’s ascending force curve, VRT reduces load at mechanically disadvantageous ‘sticking point’ while delivering greater load at the mechanically advantageous concentric end [7–8]. Within the context of PAPE, this load-regulation characteristic may offer unique advantages, as effective PAPE induction requires providing sufficient neuromuscular stimulation while simultaneously minimizing unnecessary fatigue accumulation [1, 59]. Existing research indicates that VRT can deliver a more potent stimulus under relatively low average load conditions by progressively increasing the load during the concentric phase [9]; this characteristic of incremental loading may also enhance power output, concentric velocity, and the rate of force development during barbell exercises [54, 60]. Concurrently, the relatively lower load near the sticking point may help limit unnecessary fatigue and preserve a favorable neuromuscular condition [10, 34]. From a practical standpoint, these features suggest that VRT holds promise in assisting coaches to achieve high levels of activation while minimizing fatigue—thereby optimizing the balance between potentiation effects and fatigue—and increasing the likelihood of observing enhanced athletic performance following short recovery intervals between compound movements [14]. This advantage is particularly significant in the contexts of pre-competition warm-ups and contrast training.

In recent years, systematic reviews and meta-analyses concerning the effects of VRT on athletic performance have made considerable progress [11–13, 61]. However, existing research exhibits significant limitations: firstly, current meta-analyses have almost exclusively focused on long-term training adaptations to VRT, neglecting its acute PAPE-inducing effects as a CA; secondly, existing primary studies present inconsistent and contradictory conclusions regarding VRT’s ability to induce PAPE. Some research confirms that VRT can enhance subsequent neuromuscular activity [9, 14], while others report negative findings [15]. A systematic quantitative synthesis is urgently required to clarify the true effect. Meta-analyses evaluating the PAPE effect must appropriately address the nested data structure arising from the common practice of multiple time-point measurements and multiple outcome indicators in PAPE studies [16]. Consequently, this study employs systematic review and three-level meta-analysis methodologies to systematically evaluate the induction of PAPE by VRT. The aim is to provide evidence-based guidance and theoretical reference for developing efficient warm-up protocols for athletes.

Methods

This systematic review adhered to the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [17]. Additionally, this review protocol was preregistered with the Open Science Framework (OSF; registration ID : https://osf.io/nku8r/overview) on September 15, 2025. The protocol was registered after initial piloting of data extraction and assessment of the available literature to finalize methodological details. No studies were added or removed after registration to avoid any risk of selective reporting.

Eligibility Criteria

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, studies meeting the following inclusion criteria were included: (1) randomized controlled trials, randomized crossover trials, or pre-post studies incorporating a control condition, published in peer-reviewed journals as full-text articles; (2) participants were healthy adults (≥ 18 years); (3) CA format involved acute VRT intervention using elastic bands or chains as variable resistance equipment; (4) studies must have assessed the PAPE effect through one or more quantifiable motor performance indicators following the VRT intervention. Studies meeting any of the following criteria were excluded from this review: (1) non-acute VRT interventions; (2) studies involving children, adolescents, or individuals with acute/chronic injuries; (3) non-original research articles (clinical trial registrations, conference abstracts, review articles); (4) incomplete data, where studies failed to provide sufficient quantitative data.

Data Sources and Search

This study systematically searched three databases—PubMed, Web of Science, and EBSCO—to identify original research published prior to 1 October 2025. Search terms were constructed around three core concepts: (1) VRT interventions (‘Variable Resistance Training’,‘accommodating resistance’,‘elastic band’,‘chains’); (2) the PAPE phenomenon (‘Post-activation Potentiation’,‘PAPE’,‘PAP’); and (3) performance indicators (‘strength’,‘power’,“jump”,‘speed’). The search strategy was adapted according to each database’s syntax rules, with no publication year restrictions applied. Detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Data Extraction

The retrieved literature was first imported into EndNote (EndNote 20, Clarivate Analytics) for preliminary deduplication. Subsequently, two researchers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Any discrepancies during screening were resolved through discussion; where consensus could not be reached, a third researcher provided the final decision. Data extraction encompassed: study design, sample characteristics (sample size, sex, age, training experience), variable resistance equipment, VRT design format, number of intervention groups, VRT intensity (free weights + variable resistance load), and outcome measures. Where data reporting was incomplete, corresponding authors were contacted via email. For data presented graphically, extraction was performed using the WebPlotDigitizer program (https://apps.automeris.io/wpd/) [18].

Quality Assessment

The Physical Therapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale was employed to assess article quality. As the first item carried no score, the total score ranged from 0 to 10. A total score of ≥ 6 indicated high quality, 4–5 signified moderate quality, and < 4 denoted low quality [19]. One author conducted the quality assessments, with a second author independently cross-checking these evaluations. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Cochrane Rob2 (Risk of Bias tool 2) was employed to assess the risk of bias in randomized crossover trials. This tool evaluates domains including the randomization process, intervention bias, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Assessments were conducted independently by two researchers, with disagreements resolved through discussion and consensus. The two authors discussed and resolved any discrepancies.

Meta-Analysis and Heterogeneity

Given that PAPE studies frequently report multiple outcome measures or involve repeated measurements at various time points post-intervention—thereby creating dependencies among effect sizes—this study adopts a three-level meta-analysis approach [24] to address this nested data structure, drawing upon established meta-analytic methodologies within the field of exercise science [62–64]. In this model, variance is decomposed into sampling variance (Level 1), within-study variance (Level 2), and between-study variance (Level 3) [16]. Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), supplemented by maximum likelihood (ML) estimation as a method for sensitivity analysis. Tests of model coefficients and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated based on the t-distribution. The degree of heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, wherein I² values ​​of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively [21].

Moderators and Subgroup Analysis

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity in greater depth, this study employed subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses, conducting statistical assessments of both categorical and continuous variables. The Cochrane Handbook recommends a minimum of 10 studies for meta-regression [21], whereas study-level methodological guidance suggests that subgroup analyses are more appropriate when at least four studies are available within each subgroup [65]. Subgroup analyses encompassed the following variables: (a) outcome measures; (b) training level; (c) recovery time; (d) number of intervention groups; (e) variable resistance equipment; (f) variable resistance design format; (g) training movements; (h) control group type. Given the complexity of outcome measures in the included literature, these were categorized into maximum strength measures, counter-movement jump measures, vertical jump measures, sprint time measures, and peak power measures. Most participants demonstrated relative strength levels of ≥ 1.5 times body weight, they were classified according to previous research [25] by athletic experience into: Tier-1-recreational athletes (adults aged 18–64 completing at least 150–300 min of moderate-intensity activity or 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity activity weekly, plus muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days per week); Tier-2 - Trainees/Developing Level (representatives at local level, training regularly approximately 3 times per week, committed to a specific sport, training with competition objectives, limited skill advancement); Tier-3 - High-Level Trainees/National Level (competing at national level; team sport athletes participating in national and/or state leagues/championships; achieving approximately 20% of world record performances and/or world-leading standards; NCAA Division II and III athletes); Based on prior research [2], VRT intervention training sets are categorized as single-set or multi-set; For recovery time, following prior research [2], post-VRT recovery periods were categorized as: short (0–3 min), medium (4–7 min), long (8–11 min), and ultra-long (≥ 12 min); Variable resistance design formats were categorized into three subgroups: (1) Equal intensity: VRT exhibited low intensity at the bottom of the movement and high intensity at the top; (2) High VRT intensity (VRT↑): VRT and CRT shared identical intensity at the bottom of the movement; (3) Low VRT intensity (VRT↓): VRT and CRT share equivalent intensity at the top of the movement; Variable resistance equipment was categorized into two subgroups (chain weights and elastic bands); Subgroup analysis was conducted for CA movements to evaluate the efficacy of variable resistance combined with hex bar deadlifts and squats.

To determine whether continuous variables potentially influence the PAPE effect, this study conducted meta-regression analyses on recovery time and variable resistance load magnitude. When evaluating regression predictive relationships, linear and non-linear functions were separately fitted and compared, selecting the model with optimal goodness-of-fit [26, 27]. When handling load-related variables, a highly significant negative correlation was identified between variable resistance load and free weight load as defined by training mode, indicating severe multicollinearity. This collinearity precluded the accurate separation of their independent effects within a multiple regression model [28]. To address this issue and more directly examine core hypotheses related to training strategies, this study calculated the metric variable resistance load ratio. This metric was operationally defined as the proportion of variable resistance load within total load (i.e., VR% = VR / (VR + FW)). Subsequently, VR% was incorporated as the primary continuous moderator variable into multilevel linear regression models for testing. The statistical significance level for all moderation effects was set at p < 0.05. Given the number of moderators examined, these analyses were interpreted cautiously and were intended primarily to explore potential sources of heterogeneity rather than to establish definitive causal relationships.

Risk of Publication Bias and Sensitivity Analysis

To assess publication bias, this study employed statistical methods including visual inspection of funnel plots, Egger’s regression test [29], the fail-safe N method, and the trim and fill method [30]. To test the robustness of the results, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the impact of varying correlation coefficients on standard error calculations was assessed. Given the study’s assumed r = 0.5, sensitivity analyses were conducted for a smaller r = 0.2 and a larger r = 0.8. Second, leave-one-out analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to examine their influence on the overall pooled effect size. Finally, the model was re-estimated using ML estimation and compared with REML results.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and its metafor and meta packages, with data visualization conducted using the ggplot2 and orchard packages.

Results

Study Selection

Preliminary database searches (PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCO) identified 714 publications. Following deduplication and screening of titles and abstracts, full-text reviews were conducted on the remaining articles. Ultimately, 11 publications met al.l inclusion criteria. Forward and backward tracing of references from these 11 studies yielded an additional 2 eligible publications. Thus, this systematic review and meta-analysis ultimately included 13 original studies [14, 31–42]. The detailed inclusion process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Characterization of Participants

The total sample size across the 13 included studies was 190 participants (range: 9 to 20 participants per study). A marked sex imbalance was evident: 11 studies (n = 161) recruited only males, one study (n = 14) recruited only females, and one study (n = 15) included both sexes. All studies recruited participants with training experience (n = 190); none recruited untrained individuals. Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Outcome Measures

Based on the classification of neuromuscular capacity assessed, all 13 studies included at least one measure of strength or power (e.g., CMJ, SJ, PP, or 1RM maximum strength). Additionally, two studies measured anaerobic capacity (i.e., 10-metre or 9.1-metre sprint). None of the included studies reported outcomes related to aerobic endurance, muscular endurance, agility, or coordination (Table 1).

Primary Analysis

The main effect analysis of the three-level meta-analysis (Fig. 2) indicated that VRT as CA produced a statistically significant, small positive effect on PAPE(k = 95, g = 0.27, 95% CI [0.11, 0.44], 95% PI [-0.29, 0.83], p = 0.0016). The two-level model also showed a statistically significant effect size (g = 0.25, 95% CI [0.17, 0.31], 95% PI [-0.19, 0.68], p < 0.0001). Heterogeneity analysis revealed significant heterogeneity (I² = 60.1%, p < 0.001). This study decomposed the variance distribution across the three levels: 49.1% of the total variance was attributed to level-3, 10.9% to level-2, and the remaining 39.9% was attributable to sampling error. To further verify the statistical significance of the heterogeneity sources, the variance components of the model were tested using a likelihood-ratio test (LRT). The results showed that removing the level-2 variance component did not significantly decrease the model fit (LRT = 1.62, p = 0.203), indicating that the heterogeneity at this level was not significant. However, removing the level-3 variance component resulted in a significant decrease in model fit (LRT = 18.41, p < 0.0001), demonstrating that the between-study heterogeneity was highly significant. According to the recommendation of Hunter and Schmidt, moderator analysis should be conducted when the variance attributable to sampling error is less than 75%. Therefore, a subsequent moderator analysis is warranted [43].

Moderator Analysis

This study conducted subgroup analyses on eight pre-specified categorical moderator variables to investigate the moderating effects on the PAPE effect induced by VRT (the subgroup analysis table is presented in Table 2; the orchard plot visualization is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6.)

Preliminary analysis results indicated that test type and participant training level were significant moderating variables influencing the magnitude of the PAPE effect (p < 0.01). However, further examination revealed that the aforementioned analysis included subgroups with a limited number of effect sizes (1RMBS and Tier-1 groups) each comprising only two effect sizes. Given that the methodology section of this study pre-specified that confirmatory subgroup analyses would be included only when a category possessed a sufficient number of effect sizes to support meaningful estimation, categories with fewer than four effect sizes were excluded from the confirmatory subgroup comparisons presented in Table 2. Based on this pre-established criterion, Tier-1 participants were excluded from the subgroup analysis based on training level, and the 1RMBS group was similarly excluded from the subgroup analysis based on test type. To assess the robustness of the results, the relevant analyses were re-run after excluding subgroups with fewer than four effect sizes; the adjusted results are presented in Table 2. Following the exclusion of the 1RMBS subgroup, a re-analysis of the remaining four test-type subgroups indicated that the overall moderating effect of test type was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.198). Nevertheless, VRT continued to significantly enhance CMJ height (g = 0.22, p = 0.008) and SJ height (g = 0.25, p = 0.033); however, no significant improvements were observed in CMJPP or sprint performance (p = 0.379; p = 0.232). Similarly, after excluding the Tier-1 subgroup from the training-level subgroup analysis, a re-analysis of the remaining two subgroups indicated that VRT continued to induce a small, yet significant, PAPE effect among Tier-3 participants (g = 0.22, p = 0.002), whereas no significant effect was observed among Tier-2 participants (p = 0.285).

No statistically significant differences were observed between subgroups across the six variables: recovery time, VRT design format, control group type, number of training sets, variable resistance equipment, and CA movement type (p > 0.05). However, significant PAPE effects were observed within specific subgroups. Specifically, subgroup analyses by recovery duration revealed significant PAPE effects from VRT at 0–3 min (g = 0.21), 4–7 min (g = 0.31), and 8–11 min (g = 0.30); multiple-set VRT interventions produced a small yet significant PAPE effect (g = 0.43), whereas single-set VRT failed to effectively induce PAPE (p = 0.25); and regarding VRT design intensity, only designs matching CRT intensity demonstrated significant effects (g = 0.39), whereas relatively low-intensity designs failed to induce significant effects. Regarding training equipment, both iron chains (g = 0.54) and elastic bands (g = 0.25) produced significant induction effects.

To investigate the effect of variable resistance load magnitude on PAPE outcomes, this study conducted a series of meta-regression analyses (Supplementary Fig. 7). The results indicated that within the univariate linear model, variable resistance load magnitude exhibited a marginally significant positive association with g values (β = 1.60, 95% CI = [-0.10, 3.31], p = 0.06). The quadratic model constructed to examine its nonlinear effects was not significant overall (p = 0.18). The linear term coefficient was (β = 8.87 (95% CI = [-15.75, 33.49], p = 0.47), while the quadratic term coefficient was (β= -14.63 (95% CI = [-63.20, 33.95], p = 0.55). Comparing model fit via the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the linear model (AIC = 73.28) outperformed the nonlinear model (AIC = 73.81), indicating linear trends provide a superior interpretation. To assess the combined effect of variable resistance load magnitude and free weight size, a multiple regression model was constructed. The results indicated that the overall effect of the model was non-significant (p = 0.12). Specifically, the independent effect of free weight size was non-significant (p = 0.66), while the effect of variable resistance load size showed marginal significance (p = 0.09). Furthermore, linear regression analysis of the VR% revealed a marginally significant positive relationship with effect size (β = 1.48, 95% CI = [-0.22, 3.18], p = 0.08), whereas the non-linear regression analysis indicated that the overall effect of the model was not significant (p = 0.19). The linear term coefficient was not significant (β = 1.13, 95% CI = [-14.18, 16.44], p = 0.88), and the quadratic term coefficient (β = 1.00 (95% CI = [-31.28, 33.29], p = 0.95). Similarly, the linear model for this variable (AIC = 72.96) demonstrated superior goodness-of-fit compared to its nonlinear counterpart (AIC = 75.00).

The meta-regression analysis of recovery time revealed no significant moderating effect on the PAPE effect size. Neither the linear relationship (β = 0.008, 95% CI = [-0.01, 0.03], p = 0.42) nor the nonlinear quadratic relationship aimed at identifying an optimal recovery window (overall model F-test p = 0.58; where the linear term coefficient (β = 0.024 [95% CI = -0.03, 0.08, p = 0.37]) and quadratic term coefficient (β= -0.001 [95% CI = -0.005, 0.003, p = 0.52]) both failed to reach statistical significance).

Risk of Bias and Quality of Methods

A comprehensive assessment of publication bias was conducted. First, a fail-safe N analysis yielded a value of 3007, which far exceeds the critical threshold of 5k + 10 (5 × 95 + 10=485), indicating that the conclusions regarding the effect size are highly robust. Second, the results of the three-level Egger’s test showed no significant evidence of publication bias (p = 0.40). Interestingly, the two-level Egger’s test indicated significant publication bias (p < 0.001), a finding that is likely a statistical artifact or an inflated Type I error rate resulting from the neglect of data dependency. To further assess the robustness of the main effect, we applied the trim-and-fill method within a two-level framework. No studies were imputed, and the main effect remained essentially unchanged. In summary, the threat of publication bias to the robustness of our study’s conclusions is low. For further details, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 2.

In the moderator analysis, we further examined publication bias for subgroups containing at least 10 effect sizes (k ≥ 10). The results revealed no significant asymmetry in subgroups related to the testing metric (CMJPP subgroup, p = 0.27), participants’ training level (Tier-2 subgroup, p = 0.64), and the number of training sets (single-set intervention subgroup, p = 0.54). In contrast, several other subgroups exhibited statistically significant asymmetry (p < 0.05), suggesting a potential risk of publication bias. For more information, please see Supplementary Fig. 3.

The PEDro methodological quality assessment of the 13 included studies (Supplementary Table 3) yielded scores ranging from 4 to 6, with an average of 5.5 ± 0.8. Ten studies were classified as high quality (≥ 6 points), while three were of moderate quality (4–5 points). All studies met criteria including random allocation (D2), baseline comparability (D4), and between-group statistical comparison (D10). However, none reported allocation concealment (D3) or blinding of participants (D5), therapists (D6), or assessors (D7), reflecting common limitations in exercise science intervention studies. Some studies incurred deductions for dropout rates exceeding 15% (D8) [14, 31] or failure to conduct intention-to-treat analyses for missing data (D9) [14, 31, 40]. The Rob2 bias risk assessment (Supplementary Fig. 1) indicated generally sound methodological quality overall. Five studies were rated as low risk, eight as some concerns, and none as high risk. Primary sources of bias risk included insufficient reporting details regarding the randomization process and widespread concerns about selective reporting of outcomes.

Sensitivity Analysis

First, we performed leave-one-out analyses at both Level-2 and Level-3. The results demonstrated that the study’s conclusions regarding statistical significance were highly robust. Regardless of the removal of any single effect size (n = 95), the overall g remained within a range of 0.212 to 0.289 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Similarly, upon removing any single study (N = 13), the g remained within a range of 0.131 to 0.311 (Supplementary Fig. 5). In all scenarios, the p-value consistently remained statistically significant (p < 0.05). However, the analysis also revealed that the precise magnitude of the effect size was highly sensitive to a specific study. Specifically, when the high-influence study was removed, the g dropped from 0.27 to 0.13. This study was also identified as the primary source of model heterogeneity. Second, to assess the robustness of the assumed pre-post correlation coefficient (r) (main analysis r = 0.5), the model was re-estimated under two alternative scenarios: r = 0.2 and r = 0.8. The results indicated that while the magnitude of the pooled effect size showed sensitivity (g range: 0.20 to 0.53), the conclusion of statistical significance (p < 0.01) remained unchanged. Finally, the robustness of the estimation method was cross-validated by comparing the main REML model with a model estimated using ML. The two estimation methods yielded highly consistent results regarding the effect size (g = 0.26 vs. g = 0.27), statistical significance (p = 0.0007 vs. p = 0.0016), and 95% CI (Fig. 3). In summary, the sensitivity analyses confirmed that the study’s core conclusion—namely, that VRT has a positive effect on PAPE—is highly robust in terms of its direction and statistical significance. Although the precise magnitude of the effect was sensitive to a specific high-influence study and the statistical assumption (r), the core finding of a “significant positive effect” did not.

Discussion

This study is the first to quantitatively evaluate the acute inductive effect of VRT on PAPE using a systematic review and three-level meta-analysis. The main findings indicate that, compared to CRT or control groups, VRT as a form of CA produces a small, yet statistically significant, positive effect on subsequent athletic performance (g = 0.27, p < 0.01). However, an in-depth moderator analysis reveals that the magnitude and effectiveness of PAPE are moderated by participant training level and the refinement of the training design. These findings not only confirm the potential of VRT in inducing PAPE but also reveal the complexity of its effects, providing a deeper evidence-based foundation for designing precise warm-up protocols.

Test Indicators

Subgroup analysis results indicated that outcome measures did not have a significant moderating effect on the PAPE effect (p = 0.198). Specifically, VRT produced a small but significant improvement in CMJ height (g = 0.22, p = 0.001) and SJ height (g = 0.25, p = 0.03). However, there was no significant enhancement of sprint performance (p = 0.22) or CMJ peak power performance (p = 0.38).

Only two studies have used squat 1RM to assess the PAPE effect induced by VRT, one using resistance bands [35] and the other using chains [36]. In these studies, 81% and 62% of participants, respectively, improved their subsequent squat 1RM performance. Although the evidence base is limited, both studies suggest that VRT may acutely facilitate maximal strength performance. A plausible explanation is the high degree of task specificity between the conditioning activity and the subsequent test. Previous reviews have noted that PAPE is more likely to occur when the CA shares substantial biomechanical similarity with the performance task [58], whereas acute transfer is often limited when this similarity is low [66]. In the present case, the squat-based CA and the subsequent 1RM squat test were highly consistent in movement pattern, joint kinematics, and force-time characteristics, which may partly explain the observed acute enhancement in 1RM performance.

One possible mechanism is that the load-accommodating profile of VRT during the concentric phase may provide a favorable acute neuromuscular stimulus for subsequent maximal force output [3, 60], although direct mechanistic evidence remains limited [44]. Notably, Mina et al. employed a warm-up protocol consisting of 2 sets of 3 back squats at 85% 1RM, with elastic bands contributing approximately 35% of the total load, followed by a free-weight 1RM squat test after 5 min of recovery. Although significant acute improvements in 1RM performance were observed, no marked increase in quadriceps surface EMG was detected [35]. This suggests that the acute enhancement may not depend solely on greater activation of a single muscle group, but may instead reflect task-specific changes in overall movement coordination. However, because this category comprised only two effect sizes, it was not included in the confirmatory subgroup comparison presented in Table 2. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory rather than definitive.

The observed improvements in jump height, particularly CMJ and SJ height, may be partly attributed to the load-accommodating mechanical characteristics of VRT. By using elastic bands or chains, VRT reduces relative load at mechanically disadvantaged joint angles while progressively increasing resistance in stronger ranges of motion, which may better match the ascending strength characteristics of the movement [3, 10]. In the squat, this loading pattern may help reduce the limitations imposed by the sticking region during the early concentric phase. Studies comparing VRT with CRT have shown that, although loads are similar during the pre-sticking phase, VRT produces greater resistance during the sticking and post-sticking phases, indicating that elastic resistance can alter load distribution across the ROM [67]. Additional evidence suggests that applying elastic resistance around the sticking region allows subjects to complete more repetitions and lift heavier loads while reporting lower RPE and physiological stress [68]. Together, these findings suggest that VRT may provide a more favorable phase-specific mechanical stimulus for acute performance enhancement, although the exact neuromuscular mechanisms remain uncertain [10].

PAPE is generally considered to reflect the interaction between potentiation-related processes and fatigue, with possible contributors including acute neural and mechanical factors [1]. Compared with CRT, VRT may provide a more favorable phase-specific loading stimulus by increasing resistance after the sticking point and during the latter portion of the concentric phase [9, 53, 67]. Because jumping take-off and sprint acceleration both require effective force production during propulsion, this loading profile may be especially relevant to these tasks [76, 77]. Previous meta-analyses have also shown that, under certain conditions, VRT may be more effective than CRT for acutely enhancing strength-, velocity-, and power-related outcomes [47, 61]. Similarly, recent meta-analyses have reported greater concentric-phase muscle activation during VRT than during CRT under some loading conditions [45]. Shi et al. further observed increased activation of the vastus lateralis and vastus medialis under 30% and 40% VRT conditions, suggesting that the phase-specific mechanical and muscle-activation demands associated with VRT may partly contribute to subsequent jump-related performance, although direct mechanistic evidence remains limited [40].

VRT may also influence mechanical factors relevant to SSC function. By increasing resistance near the end of the concentric phase, it may alter the transition into the subsequent eccentric phase and increase eccentric velocity [46], thereby affecting braking demands and elastic energy utilization. However, these mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, the present study found that VRT did not significantly enhance CMJ peak power (p = 0.38), which is consistent with previous evidence showing that the overall effect of VRT on peak power is not superior to CRT [47]. Existing studies have shown that, although high-intensity CA can induce PAPE and significantly increase subsequent CMJ height, CMJ peak power does not always change to a statistically significant extent [72, 73]. This suggests that improvements in jump performance may be more closely related to movement strategy, net impulse, or take-off velocity than to changes in peak power alone [74, 75].

Subject Training Level

The subgroup analysis results showed that participant training level was not a significant moderator (p = 0.32), but the effect was mainly reflected in the difference between Tier-3 and Tier-2 participants. Tier-3 participants (g = 0.22, p = 0.002) produced a significant PAPE effect after the VRT intervention, whereas Tier-2 participants showed no significant enhancement (p = 0.28). Tier-1 participants were not included in this confirmatory subgroup analysis, as only two data points regarding effect size were available for this category. Notably, both of these Tier-1 effect sizes were derived from studies that utilized the 1RM as their outcome measure [35, 36]. Consequently, the maximal strength gains observed in these studies are more likely attributable to “task specificity”—the alignment between the conditioning activity and the performance test—rather than to the participants’ own training status.

The results did not indicate that training status exerted a significant moderating effect; however, subgroup analyses suggested that the VRT-induced PAPE effect appeared more likely to manifest in subjects with a higher training status rather than in those at a developmental stage. Previous studies have frequently stratified subjects’ strength levels based on relative 1RM, noting that individuals with higher relative strength are more likely to exhibit a significant PAPE effect [69, 70]. Nevertheless, the interpretation of the present study’s results requires caution, as the classification of training status employed herein was based on the framework proposed by McKay et al.—a system grounded in athletic experience and competitive level [25]—rather than on the direct metric of relative strength. Consequently, the findings of this study cannot be directly interpreted as definitive evidence that individuals with higher strength levels will invariably derive greater benefits from VRT.

A plausible explanation for this discrepancy lies in the fact that subjects with a higher training status are more adept at achieving a rapid dissipation of fatigue following high-intensity CA, thereby allowing the potentiation effect to manifest more fully within the subsequent recovery window. This may be attributed to a more favorable balance between fatigue and potentiation in these individuals. First, individuals with a higher training status typically possess superior neuromuscular characteristics, making them more likely to establish the physiological underpinnings associated with classic PAP—such as enhanced contractile sensitivity mediated by the phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chains [1, 2, 69]. Second, their superior capacity for high-energy phosphagen and metabolic recovery may facilitate an earlier abatement of residual fatigue [71], thereby rendering the performance enhancement that emerges several minutes later more readily apparent. In contrast, subjects at a developmental stage may experience a relatively unfavorable balance between fatigue and potentiation following the same stimulus, making it more difficult for them to exhibit a discernible PAPE effect. However, given that the overall test for the moderating variable did not reach statistical significance—and considering the limited availability of study data reflecting the subjects’ relative strength levels—the aforementioned explanations should, for the time being, be regarded as speculative rather than definitive conclusions.

Training Design

This study systematically examined key moderating variables influencing the PAPE effect induced by VRT through a series of subgroup analyses and meta-regression of training designs.

Firstly, the design format of VRT and the proportion of variable resistance load emerged as critical determinants of the PAPE effect. Subgroup analysis revealed that VRT protocols employing identical intensity significantly induced PAPE (g = 0.39, p = 0.003), whereas other design formats yielded suboptimal results. This finding aligns with a prior biomechanics-based meta-analysis confirming that VRT protocols of equal intensity more effectively enhance mean velocity and power [47]. The core principle of the equal-intensity protocol lies in creating a pronounced load difference between the top and bottom of the movement. This design, featuring high load at the top and low load at the bottom, better aligns with human muscle force production capabilities across different joint angles [8]. A smaller load difference has no significant impact on subsequent strength performance [15], whereas a larger load difference effectively enhances strength performance [9, 14]. Meta-regression analysis further revealed a marginally significant positive trend between VR% and PAPE effects (p = 0.06). Although not reaching statistical significance, this trend may suggest the existence of a dose-response relationship. This finding is corroborated by multiple primary studies: Shi’s research clearly demonstrated a performance hierarchy of 40% VRT > 30% VRT > 20% VRT [40], while Krčmár’s study also confirmed the superiority of 30% VRT over 20% VRT [31].

One possible explanation for the significance of the VR% is that a higher VR% may create a more favorable distribution of mechanical load throughout the concentric phase of a movement. Compared to CRT, VRT allows for a reduced relative load when the joint is in a mechanically disadvantageous position, while progressively increasing resistance as the athlete’s joint angle shifts toward a more mechanically advantageous position. Given that most concentric movements involve both acceleration and deceleration phases, this inherent ability to automatically adjust the load in tandem with the movement’s progression may enable athletes to prolong the acceleration phase and delay the onset of deceleration at the end of the movement. This extended acceleration phase not only increases the duration of effective force production but also contributes to heightened neuromuscular activation levels; this hypothesis aligns with existing evidence, as studies have demonstrated that muscle activation levels during VRT with a high VR% are significantly higher than those observed during CRT or VRT with a low VR% [45]. Specifically, the elevated load experienced during the latter stages of the concentric phase may provide a more potent phase-specific stimulus, which—under certain loading configurations—can facilitate improvements in power output, rate of force development, and selective muscle activation [48, 54, 60]. From a practical standpoint, this underlying mechanism may partially explain why incorporating a higher proportion of variable resistance components into training appears to be more conducive to achieving immediate improvements in athletic performance. This allows the enhancement effect induced at the top to improve force production in the bottom phase, ultimately manifesting as increased muscle concentric contraction velocity [49], peak force [3], and muscle activation levels [9, 50].

Secondly, subgroup analysis of training sets revealed that multi-set VRT intervention significantly induced the PAPE effect (g = 0.43, p = 0.002), whereas single-set VRT intervention showed no significant effect (p = 0.25). This aligns with previous research indicating that multiple sets of CA typically induce a stronger PAPE effect than a single set, particularly in individuals with higher strength levels [2]. For the relatively well-trained participants included in many of the studies, a single set of VRT stimulation may have been insufficient to achieve a net enhancement effect post-fatigue. Furthermore, VRT induces more pronounced acute physiological stress compared to CRT, such as elevated extracellular matrix metalloproteinase and serum hormone concentrations [51]. Although this heightened stress is accompanied by fatigue in the acute phase, it may provide a stronger acute stimulus. Consequently, for relatively well-trained participants, cumulative stimulation from multiple sets of VRT may be more likely to produce a net potentiation effect that exceeds fatigue, ultimately inducing a significant PAPE effect.

This potent neuromuscular stimulation, constructed through multiple sets of high-load-difference VRT, may broaden the window for the PAPE effect. Subgroup analysis revealed that the PAPE effect remained significant within three-time windows following VRT intervention: 0–3 min (g = 0.21), 4–7 min (g = 0.31), and 8–11 min (g = 0.3). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed between groups (p = 0.66), nor were any significant differences identified in regression analyses for recovery time (p = 0.58). This suggests that the VRT-induced PAPE effect remains relatively robust for up to 11 min post-intervention. One possible explanation is that the load-accommodating profile of VRT may help maintain favorable phase-specific mechanical conditions during the early post-intervention period; however, the precise neuromuscular mechanisms remain uncertain [54, 60]. This may be partly related to the load-accommodating nature of VRT, which can alter force and velocity demands across the range of motion by increasing resistance near the top of the movement while reducing resistance in mechanically disadvantaged positions [52]. By modifying the loading pattern across movement phases, VRT may influence mechanical factors relevant to SSC function; however, direct evidence regarding high-threshold motor unit recruitment and the exact neuromuscular mechanisms remains limited. In addition, by reducing relative load in mechanically disadvantaged regions, VRT may help limit some unnecessary fatigue during the movement [10]. Consistent with this possibility, significant PAPE effects were still observed during the immediate post-intervention phase (0–3 min). This finding is supported by multiple studies: Mina et al. confirmed that 35% VRT significantly enhanced CMJ within 30 s post-intervention, with effects persisting for 12 min [14]. Masel’s research specifically compared recovery intervals of 90, 120, and 150 s, indicating that 90 s represents the optimal window for inducing PAPE, with effects diminishing when extended to 120 and 150 s [33].

Finally, the multiple regression analysis provided additional exploratory information regarding the relative contribution of loading variables. When VR load and free-weight load were entered simultaneously, the overall model was not significant (p = 0.12). Nevertheless, VR load showed a marginal association with the PAPE effect (p = 0.09), whereas free-weight load did not (p = 0.66). Although these findings do not support a definitive conclusion, they may suggest that the variable-resistance component itself is more relevant to the acute PAPE response than the free-weight component alone. This interpretation should be made cautiously.

Limitations

Although this study is the first comprehensive three-level meta-analysis on VRT-induced PAPE effects, several inherent limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. First, significant methodological heterogeneity existed among the original studies. Our main effect analysis revealed moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I2 = 60.1%), and variance decomposition indicated this stemmed primarily from between-study differences (Level-3). This is attributed to significant variations in VRT design specifics, intervention volume, and participant characteristics across studies, which may mask the true moderating effects of certain variables. Concurrently, while the included studies mostly had medium-to-high PEDro scores, they generally lacked allocation concealment and blinding, potentially introducing a degree of performance and detection bias. Second, our subgroup analyses were constrained by an insufficient number of effect sizes in some categories. The 1RMBS and Tier-1 subgroups had only two effect sizes (k = 2), rendering their estimates highly unstable. This fact constitutes a core limitation, as the existing literature lacks sufficient statistical power to draw firm conclusions about the true moderating roles of 1RMBS and Tier-1. Third, because the original studies did not report pre-post correlation coefficients (r), our effect size estimations are uncertain. A sensitivity analysis (testing r = 0.2 and r = 0.8) indicated that while the statistical significance of the effect remained robust (p < 0.01), the estimated value of g varied between 0.20 and 0.53, leaving its precise magnitude unclear. Furthermore, as grey literature was not included, the risk of selective reporting bias cannot be ruled out; attempts to contact authors for missing data yielded only one response, with no new data obtained. Finally, the generalizability of our conclusions is severely limited. Of the 190 participants in the 13 included studies, only 19 were female. This results in conclusions based almost entirely on trained young males, making it unclear whether the findings can be extrapolated to female populations. In addition, because multiple subgroup and moderator analyses were conducted, the possibility of false positive findings due to multiple testing cannot be excluded. Therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously, especially when based on small numbers of studies or effect sizes.

Practical Applications

From a practical standpoint, VRT can serve as a conditioning strategy to acutely enhance subsequent explosive power performance, particularly for trained or high-level athletes. Existing evidence suggests that the acute benefits of VRT appear to be more stable and consistent in jumping tasks, specifically the CMJ and SJ. Therefore, if the objective is to enhance lower-body explosive movements within a warm-up or complex training context, practitioners should prioritize incorporating VRT prior to performing jumping tasks.

The results of the subgroup analyses also provide preliminary guidance for the design of training protocols. Regarding the specific parameters of VRT, the “equal” mode appears to be superior to other modes; furthermore, multi-set protocols are more likely to elicit a measurable PAPE effect compared to single-set protocols. Additionally—although the interpretation of meta-regression results warrants caution—the positive trend observed in VR% suggests that, within the context of variable resistance training, utilizing a relatively higher percentage of variable resistance may be more beneficial than utilizing a lower percentage. Notably, the acute effects of VRT were observed across multiple recovery windows (0–3 min, 4–7 min, and 8–11 min), indicating that practitioners possess a degree of flexibility when scheduling subsequent explosive tasks, rather than being restricted to a single precise time point. This characteristic enhances the feasibility of integrating VRT into practical warm-up routines.

Nevertheless, the aforementioned practical recommendations should be interpreted with prudence, as the current evidence regarding certain specific outcome measures and particular subgroups remains limited. Consequently, coaches should, in practice, make individualized adjustments based on the athlete’s training status, the specific demands of the task, and various operational constraints encountered in the field.

Conclusions

This study is the first to systematically quantify the inductive effect of acute VRT interventions on PAPE using a three-level meta-analysis. The findings show that VRT, as a form of CA, produces a small but statistically significant positive effect on subsequent athletic performance (g = 0.27,p = 0.0016). Specifically, VRT significantly improves jumping performance, and the PAPE effect is significant for Tier-3 participants but not effective for Tier-2 participants. Due to insufficient statistical power, this study could not confirm the effectiveness of VRT for Tier-1 participants. From an applied perspective, when VRT is used to acutely elicit PAPE during warm-ups or complex training, an equal-intensity design appears to be the most favorable option. In addition, although the between-group difference for number of sets was not statistically significant (p = 0.11), the available data suggest that multi-set protocols may be more effective than single-set protocols. Likewise, the positive trend observed in the meta-regression (p = 0.08) suggests that a relatively higher proportion of variable resistance loading (≥ 30%) may be more beneficial, although this finding requires further confirmation in future studies.

Whilst this study provides a clear outline for the practical application of VRT-induced PAPE, the complexity revealed also points the way for future research. Firstly, given the high heterogeneity across studies, future primary research should focus on standardizing VRT protocols, clearly defining their design format and VR%. There is an urgent need for additional studies to replicate the substantial effect of 1RM and to elucidate why the Tier-2 trainee effect was not significant. Secondly, current understanding of VRT mechanisms relies largely on indirect evidence such as EMG. Future studies should incorporate transcranial magnetic stimulation or ultrasound elastography to directly measure VRT’s impact on motor cortex excitability and muscle stiffness, thereby validating neuromuscular mechanisms and enhancing the feasibility of extrapolation. Finally, research on female athletes remains severely underrepresented. Future studies must conduct high-quality controlled trials specifically targeting women to test the generalizability of these findings and investigate whether the PAPE effect exhibits sex-specific variations.
