Time to diagnosis and treatment and breast cancer outcomes in young women: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Wura Jacobs, Thea Atwood, Raquel Gonzalez, Alice W. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Women under age 50 (young women) experience unique challenges when it comes to timely breast cancer diagnosis and treatment. Delays in diagnosis and treatment may contribute to disparities in survival outcomes, yet definitions and measurements of such delays often vary across studies. This protocol outlines the procedures for a systematic synthesis of evidence on the association between time to diagnosis and treatment and outcomes in women diagnosed with breast cancer under age 50. Primary observational research studies published in English will be identified from various databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus, CINAHL, and Google Scholar. Two reviewers will independently screen the eligible studies and extract the data. Risk of bias and study quality will be assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and the Aarhus checklist, and results will be synthesized qualitatively. The findings from this review will clarify the magnitude and consistency of the impact of diagnostic and treatment intervals on breast cancer outcomes in young women to inform future research and clinical care.• Identify studies examining the association between time to diagnosis and treatment and outcomes in young women with breast cancer.• Evaluate the impact of diagnostic and treatment intervals on breast cancer outcomes in young women.

Background

Incidence of breast cancer in females under age 50 (hereafter referred to as young women) is on the rise [1]. This increasing incidence varies by sociodemographic factors, such as race/ethnicity, which are also known to contribute to disparities in timely care and breast cancer outcomes in young women [1,2]. In addition, young women often present with more aggressive, advanced stage breast cancer and have poorer prognoses relative to their older counterparts [3,4]. The underlying reasons for these observations are unclear and likely multifactorial. There is evidence to suggest that tumor biology differs when comparing younger breast cancer patients to older patients [5,6]. Delays in diagnosis and treatment could also play a role since younger women are not routinely screened for breast cancer and may have lower awareness of the disease and its symptoms as well as experience more hesitancy to seek care or begin treatment [2,7]. For example, a study by Hindmarch et al. found that most young women had low perceived susceptibility to developing breast cancer as well as disengagement when it came to breast awareness [8]. Another study by Greer et al. showed that young women may delay treatment to undergo fertility preservation [9].

Although timely diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer is imperative for all women, it can be especially challenging for young women since they constitute a primarily unscreened population and are often burdened by the disease in ways that are unique to their age. However, how delays in care are measured and defined can be complex and are often heterogeneous across studies, resulting in inconsistent findings that make the literature on the relationship between delay intervals and breast cancer outcomes difficult to interpret [10]. Clarifying this association is critical since it can inform recommendations for timely breast cancer care in young women.

Young women diagnosed with breast cancer represent a distinct population subgroup, as the consequences of diagnostic and treatment delays may differ from those in older women, possibly due to variation in underlying tumor biology. Although several individual studies have examined the relationship between time to diagnosis and treatment and breast cancer outcomes, a comprehensive synthesis of these findings has yet to be conducted. Guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Protocols (PRISMA-P) and the PECO framework, this protocol outlines procedures for a systematic review of published studies examining time to diagnosis and treatment and associated breast cancer outcomes in young women [11,12].

The primary objective of this systematic review is to identify associations between time to diagnosis and time to treatment and breast cancer outcomes in young women. As a secondary objective, patterns in factors associated with delay intervals will be described among the included studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies will be eligible for inclusion if they meet the following criteria:

Population: Studies must report data on females diagnosed with breast cancer under age 50. Studies exclusively focused on females diagnosed with breast cancer aged 50 years or older, males, or non-human subjects will be excluded. Additionally, studies limited to cases of non-invasive breast cancer (e.g., ductal carcinoma in-situ (DCIS)) will be excluded.

Exposure: Studies must include quantitative measures related to diagnostic and/or treatment intervals, such as time to diagnosis, time to treatment initiation, or other indicators of delay or usual/timely care. Eligible studies must also include measures of breast cancer outcomes among individuals with varying intervals of diagnosis and treatment initiation. Inclusion of both measures is necessary given that the primary objective of this review is to evaluate the association between these intervals and breast cancer outcomes.

Study design: All primary observational studies (e.g., cohort, case-control, cross-sectional designs) will be considered. Reviews, case reports, animal studies, qualitative studies, and commentaries will be excluded.

Publication characteristics: Only peer-reviewed, primary quantitative studies published in English will be included. There will be no restrictions based on publication date or geographic location since inclusion of all eligible studies from all possible sources is necessary to produce a high-quality systematic review [13].

Search strategy and databases

To identify relevant studies, we will systematically search the following electronic bibliographic databases: PubMed (including MEDLINE), EMBASE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Scopus. Additionally, we will conduct a supplementary search using Google Scholar to capture potentially eligible studies not indexed in traditional databases. To enhance completeness, we will conduct backward citation tracking (i.e., reviewing reference lists of included studies) and forward citation searching (i.e., identifying studies that have cited the included studies).

All database searches will be conducted on the same day to ensure consistency in the body of literature retrieved for screening. Prior to data synthesis and final analysis, the search will be re-run to capture any newly published or indexed studies. No date, language, or geographical filters will be applied at the database search level. Instead, all records retrieved will be screened manually according to the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A detailed search strategy, including controlled vocabulary terms, free text terms, and Boolean operators, will be developed in consultation with a health sciences librarian. Study records

Data management. All study records and associated data will be managed using the systematic review management software platform Covidence. Covidence will be used to import search results, remove duplicates, facilitate title/abstract and full-text screening, track inclusion/exclusion decisions, and store extracted data. It also allows for structured collaboration among multiple reviewers and maintains an audit trail of decisions to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Selection process. Following the database search by the librarian, two reviewers will first pilot the title/abstract screening process with a small subset of the full dataset to ensure understanding of the screening process as well as the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, title/abstract screening will be conducted in duplicate, with each record independently screened by two reviewers. Records excluded by both reviewers at this stage will be removed from further consideration. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and consensus that will include a third reviewer. The same process will be followed during full-text screening to determine final inclusion in the review.

Prior to formal title/abstract screening, the study extraction form developed for this review will also be piloted on a subset of included studies to ensure clarity, comprehensiveness, and consistency in data capture. Inter-rater reliability will be assessed for the extraction form during the pilot screening phase using Cohen’s kappa (κ) to measure agreement between the reviewers. A κ value of ≥0.61 will be considered acceptable, indicating substantial agreement [14,15]. If agreement falls below this threshold, the team will clarify selection criteria and engage in additional reviewer deliberation before proceeding to full screening.

Data collection process. Data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers using the standardized, pre-piloted data extraction form and implemented through Covidence; see Supplementary Table 1 for an example of the extraction form. Discrepancies in data extraction will be discussed and resolved by consensus; unresolved disagreements will be referred to a third reviewer.

Data items and outcomes

Extracted data will include study characteristics, participant demographics, measures of diagnostic and treatment intervals, tumor characteristics (e.g., subtype, stage), survival, and factors associated with delay intervals at different categories (e.g., individual, institutional) and subcategories (e.g., personal beliefs, healthcare access).

Risk of bias

Quality assessment of observational studies will be determined using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. In addition, the Aarhus statement, which is a resource for early cancer diagnosis research and includes a checklist to evaluate the quality of delay studies with regard to definitions and measurements, will be used to perform a methodological assessment (Supplementary Table 2) [16]. The Aarhus statement checklist will be implemented as a separate quality assessment form within Covidence and completed concurrently with data extraction during full-text review.

Data synthesis

Given the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs and definitions of delay, we will employ a qualitative approach to synthesize data from the included studies. In accordance with methodological guidelines, a narrative thematic synthesis will be conducted to integrate findings across studies. Definitions of delay measures will be categorized based on standardized intervals outlined in Olesen et al. and reinforced in the Aarhus statement [16,17]. Studies employing comparable definitions of diagnostic or treatment delay will be grouped, and their findings will be synthesized narratively, with particular attention to the direction and strength of reported associations.

Data will be thematically organized to capture key domains relevant to the review objectives, including: (1) conceptualization and definitions of time to diagnosis and time to treatment delays; (2) duration and measurement of diagnostic and treatment delays; (3) participant characteristics, such as mean/median age and tumor stage; (4) study characteristics, including publication year, sample size, and geographic region; and (5) factors associated with delays, with appropriate categories (e.g., individual or institutional) and subcategories (e.g., personal beliefs, healthcare access).

Where sufficient data permit, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore variations in findings by geographic region (e.g., Europe vs. North America), healthcare system type (e.g., universal vs. non-universal), and publication period (e.g., 1990s vs. 2000s). This approach will facilitate a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence base, highlighting common themes and contextual influences on diagnostic and treatment delays.

Protocol validation

To strengthen the rigor of the review methodology, the sample search strategy (Table 2) was developed in collaboration with a health science evidence-synthesis librarian who is also a member of the research team. This process helped ensure that the search approach was appropriately structured and will be sufficiently sensitive to identify relevant literature across databases. The strategy was piloted and its performance was evaluated to determine its effectiveness at capturing seed articles previously identified by the team as essential studies for retrieval. In addition, the search strategy did capture these known articles, supporting its adequacy and sensitivity. Following published guidance on conducting systematic reviews of observational studies, the protocol was further refined through input from the research team to ensure that the methods followed best practices and are aligned with the objectives of the review.

Overall, this systematic review will synthesize empirical evidence on the magnitude and consistency of the impact of diagnostic and treatment delays on breast cancer outcomes specifically in young women, a population who is often diagnosed with more aggressive disease and face unique challenges when it comes to diagnosis and treatment [3,5]. Given that young women also tend to be overlooked when it comes to breast cancer as most are not diagnosed with the disease until after age 50, the findings from this review can inform health promotion guidelines, support advocacy for timely care, and guide future research to address disparities and improve outcomes for this population.

Limitations

While this review will provide a comprehensive synthesis of the existing evidence on the association between time to diagnosis and treatment and outcomes in young women diagnosed with breast cancer, one potential limitation is the restriction to only English studies, which may exclude some research relevant to this topic. Nonetheless, we expect this systematic review to be thorough while providing key data to guide future research and clinical care of young women.
