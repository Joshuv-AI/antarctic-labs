Fractional order creep model of thawing sandstone incorporating nuclear magnetic resonance phase transition damage
Authors: Kangjia Fan, Fan Li, Xiang Gao, Yang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Creep instability induced by the thawing of frozen walls has emerged as a critical challenge threatening the long-term safety of deep shafts penetrating water-rich Cretaceous weakly cemented strata. Existing creep models are predominantly based on macro-phenomenological fitting, which lacks a physical characterization of the microscopic driving mechanisms during ice-water phase transitions. Here, we conducted constant load-variable temperature triaxial creep tests coupled with synchronous Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) monitoring to define a microscopic damage parameter based on the evolution of unfrozen water content. By integrating this parameter into a fractional-order creep framework, we developed a cross-scale mechanical model capable of describing the entire thawing process. Our results indicate that within the active phase transition zone (− 6 °C to − 2 °C), the unfrozen water content surges from 1.24 to 6.23%, leading to a 3-to4-fold increase in the steady-state creep rate. By introducing microscopic state variables, the proposed model effectively achieves the theoretical decoupling of geometric damage triggered by pore ice melting from mechanical damage caused by the intrinsic weakening of the rock skeleton. Validation analysis demonstrates that the model calculations for the attenuation, steady-state, and accelerated creep stages achieve an agreement of over 90% with experimental data. This study reveals a cross-scale cascading mechanism of accelerated failure in thawing rocks: microscopic phase transitions induce a dynamic attenuation of long-term rock strength. When this dynamic strength threshold falls below the constant external load, it triggers mesoscopic crack connectivity and macroscopic instability. These findings provide a quantitative theoretical foundation for the early warning of long-term stability during the thawing period of deep shafts in cold regions.

Introduction

With the large-scale exploitation of coal resources in western China advancing into deep mining stages, shaft construction frequently penetrates thick, water-rich Cretaceous weakly cemented sandstone strata1,2. To navigate these complex hydrogeological conditions, the artificial ground freezing (AGF) method has emerged as a crucial technological approach for deep shaft construction due to its distinct advantages3,4. However, upon the completion of freezing operations, the frozen wall inevitably enters a thawing phase. Consequently, the surrounding rock, previously in a state of mechanical equilibrium, undergoes continuous creep deformation under long-term in-situ stress5,6. Given the typical soft-rock characteristics of Cretaceous sandstone, its mechanical stability during the thawing process directly dictates the long-term safety and operational stability of the shaft structure7,8. An inadequate understanding of the creep evolution behavior in thawing rocks can result in excessive deformation of the shaft lining, potentially triggering severe engineering disasters9,10. Therefore, a systematic investigation into the creep mechanical properties of thawing sandstone is of paramount engineering significance for assessing the safety status of deep frozen shafts and ensuring the stable operation of underground projects in cold regions.

To date, extensive research has been conducted on the creep mechanical properties of frozen rocks11–13. Regarding theoretical modeling, fractional-order derivative creep models have gradually superseded traditional combined linear element models, owing to their superior capability in capturing the nonlinear characteristics of rocks during the accelerated creep stage14,15. However, existing creep constitutive models are predominantly formulated based on macroscopic stress-strain tests, where the definition of damage variables relies heavily on the phenomenological fitting of macroscopic physical quantities16,17. Particularly during rock thawing, the deterioration of pore structure induced by internal ice-water phase transitions is a complex dynamic process18,19. Current constitutive equations typically simplify this process into an abstract evolution parameter, lacking direct physical evidence for support.

Although mechanisms such as “pressure melting of ice” and “microcrack propagation” are frequently invoked in theoretical discourse20,21, they remain largely speculative within mathematical models. This is primarily due to the absence of real-time, quantitative feedback on pore evolution from microscopic testing techniques like Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR). Furthermore, existing NMR studies predominantly focus on comparing pore structures before and after freeze-thaw cycles22,23, failing to track the dynamic evolution of pores in real-time during continuous thawing. Consequently, they are unable to establish the sequential causal relationship among temperature, porosity, and creep. This fundamental disconnect between macroscopic behavior and microscopic responses severely constrains the explanatory power and predictive accuracy of constitutive models regarding rock creep under complex thawing conditions. Specifically, without real-time microscopic constraints, traditional macroscopic phenomenological models fundamentally fail to mathematically decouple the ‘geometric damage’ caused by purely thermal ice ablation from the ‘mechanical damage’ driven by structural weakening. To bridge this fundamental macro-micro disconnect, it is imperative to introduce real-time physical variables to modify the constitutive equations.

Building upon this premise, the microscopic deterioration of pore structures in Cretaceous sandstone during the thawing process serves as the fundamental physical trigger driving its macroscopic nonlinear accelerated creep. Consequently, this study establishes a cross-scale research framework by coupling constant load-variable temperature macroscopic creep tests with microscopic Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) testing. Utilizing the pore damage state captured via NMR, we introduce a physical modification to the conventional fractional-order creep constitutive model. Specifically, we define a microscopic damage parameter based on the evolution of the transverse relaxation time (T2) spectrum and quantitatively integrate it into the fractional-order evolution equations. This approach is designed to unravel the intrinsic driving mechanisms governing the relationship between microscopic defect propagation and macroscopic creep responses. Ultimately, by assigning explicit physical meaning to the fractional-order parameters, this methodology provides a robust, quantitative theoretical foundation for evaluating the long-term stability of deep frozen shafts under thawing conditions.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The sandstone used in this study was obtained from the deep Cretaceous strata of the Xinzhuang Coal Mine in Gansu Province, China. To minimize initial variability among the specimens, all samples were cored from a single, intact rock block free of visible macroscopic fractures. The block was cored, cut, and polished to produce standard cylindrical specimens (Φ 50 × 100 mm). Following initial preparation, the compressional wave (P-wave) velocity of all specimens was measured using an ultrasonic tester. Specimens exhibiting anomalous velocities were discarded to guarantee the overall homogeneity of the experimental batch. Finally, the prepared sample will be treated using vacuum saturation method and wrapped in multiple layers of plastic film for sealing. Then store them in a low-temperature environment to prevent moisture loss before subsequent testing (Fig. 1).

Experimental design

Temperature conditions. Based on field monitoring data, the average temperature of the frozen wall within the Cretaceous sandstone stratum is − 8.84 °C, and the working temperature of the freezing brine during construction is − 28 °C. Consequently, the initial freezing temperature of the specimens was set to − 30 °C to investigate the creep characteristics throughout the entire thawing process up to room temperature. Given the drastic evolution of mechanical properties in Cretaceous sandstone between − 10 °C and 0 °C24,25, denser observation intervals were implemented within this critical range. Ultimately, the target thawing temperatures were specified as − 20 °C, − 10 °C, − 6 °C, − 4 °C, − 2 °C, 0 °C, and room temperature (20 °C).

Loading conditions. To analyze the mechanical behavior during thawing, a “constant load-variable temperature” (CL-VT) loading path was adopted. The sampling depth of the Cretaceous sandstone is approximately 360 m, which corresponds to a calculated overburden stress of around 8 MPa. Accordingly, the baseline axial stress (σ1) and confining pressure (σ3) were determined to be 8 MPa and 4 MPa, respectively. Furthermore, to ensure the general applicability of the experimental findings, creep tests were conducted under various constant loads, with the deviatoric stress systematically set to 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 MPa.

Microscopic nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) testing scheme

To unravel the microscopic pore evolution mechanisms of Cretaceous sandstone during the thawing process, a low-field Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) analysis system was employed to measure the transverse relaxation time (T2) spectrum of the specimens, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a), where the core parameters were configured with an echo time (TE) of 0.1 ms, a waiting time (TW) of 2000 ms, a number of echoes (NE) of 4000, and a number of scans (NS) of 32. To ensure physical consistency between the microscopic pore parameters and the macroscopic creep behaviors, the NMR tests were subjected to the exact same controlled heating path as the mechanical tests. The testing procedure proceeded as follows: initially, the water-saturated specimens were placed in a temperature-controlled chamber and cooled to a baseline temperature of − 30 °C. Once the pore fluid was completely frozen, the temperature was incrementally elevated along the predetermined path to − 20 °C, − 10 °C, − 6 °C, − 4 °C, − 2 °C, 0 °C, and 20 °C. Three parallel experiments will be conducted for each operating condition. At each target temperature point, the specimens were maintained at a constant temperature for a minimum of 60 min. After the internal ice-water phase transition reached a state of thermodynamic equilibrium, an NMR scan was initiated at that specific temperature to acquire the corresponding pore fluid distribution data.

Constant load-variable temperature cross-scale creep tests

A GCTS (Model RTX-3000) rock triaxial low-temperature creep testing system was employed to conduct the constant load-variable temperature creep tests, as depicted in Fig. 2(b). To guarantee the correspondence of physical variables across scales, parallel specimens from the same batch used in the NMR tests were utilized and subjected to the identical controlled heating path. Three parallel experiments will be conducted for each operating condition. The specific protocols were as follows:

Initial freezing and stress boundary setup: After mounting the water-saturated specimen in the pressure chamber, the temperature was lowered to a baseline of − 30 °C and maintained for an 8-h isothermal curing period. Subsequently, a constant confining pressure (σ3 = 4 MPa) and a designated initial deviatoric stress of 2 MPa were applied. This stress state was maintained constant throughout the entire single-test duration.

Controlled thawing and creep observation: Under the constant triaxial stress state, the temperature was sequentially elevated along the predetermined path to − 20 °C, − 10 °C, − 6 °C, − 4 °C, − 2 °C, 0 °C, and 20 °C. At each target temperature point, the creep observation period was strictly set to 7 d. Axial and radial strain data were continuously and automatically recorded by the system throughout the entire test.

Multi-level deviatoric stress verification: Using fresh parallel water-saturated specimens, the deviatoric stress was set to 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 MPa, respectively. The aforementioned initial freezing and controlled thawing creep procedures were repeated to capture the complete thawing creep trajectories of the loaded rocks under varying deviatoric stress levels.

Results and analysis

Evolutionary characteristics of microscopic pore structures during the thawing process

To investigate the deterioration mechanisms of the rock microstructure under controlled thermodynamic boundaries, NMR was utilized to capture the microscopic response characteristics of parallel specimens subjected to the identical temperature path. Figure 3(a) presents the T2 spectrum distribution curves of the Cretaceous sandstone during the thawing process from − 30 °C to 20 °C. According to NMR relaxation theory, different transverse relaxation times (T2) correspond to pore water in distinct occurrence states: T2 < 3 ms primarily corresponds to adsorbed water on the surfaces of clay minerals; 3 ms < T2 < 33 ms indicates capillary water; and T2 > 33 ms represents free water residing in large pores and micro-fractures26,27.

The morphological evolution of the T2 spectra reveals a pronounced transition from a unimodal distribution at low temperatures to a bimodal distribution at ambient temperature. During the low-temperature stable stage (− 20 °C to − 10 °C), the internal ice-water phase state of the rock remains relatively stable. Pore water predominantly exists as adsorbed water within the left-peak region, while the right-peak signals representing capillary and free water are exceptionally weak. As the temperature rises into the active phase transition zone, the right-peak signal progressively emerges, exhibiting a distinct rightward shift and an increase in peak area. This evolutionary process elucidates the dynamic response mechanisms of the internal rock microstructure: with increasing temperature, the adsorbed water on the mineral grain surfaces thaws first, followed by the melting of capillary and free water within the pores. Concurrently, the volume changes and localized stress release induced by the ice-water phase transition promote the connectivity and propagation of primary micro-pores and micro-fractures, thereby providing expanded storage space for free water.

Macroscopic creep responses under extreme temperature-pressure coupling

To elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms underlying the transfer of microscopic pore deterioration to macroscopic mechanical behaviors, the complete axial creep curves evolving along the stepped thawing path under various constant stress levels (2–12 MPa) were extracted, as depicted in Fig. 4(a). Overall, under the “constant load, variable temperature” testing conditions, the creep deformation of the Cretaceous sandstone exhibits pronounced temperature dependence and stress sensitivity.

As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), within each constant temperature interval, the specimens sequentially exhibit instantaneous elastic deformation, attenuation creep, and subsequently enter the steady-state creep stage characterized by a relatively constant deformation rate. However, whenever the boundary temperature experiences a step increase, the axial strain of all specimens exhibits a nonlinear surge. Notably, the constant stress level dictates the bearing capacity limit of the rock in response to the thawing temperature. At lower stress levels (2–8 MPa), no macroscopic failure occurred throughout the entire testing cycle up to ambient temperature (20 °C). Conversely, at higher stress levels, the specimens failed before reaching ambient temperature. For instance, under the 12 MPa stress condition, the specimen entered the accelerated creep stage and ruptured when the temperature rose to − 2 °C; whereas under the 10 MPa condition, failure occurred during the temperature elevation to 0 °C. This phenomenon indicates that the ice-water phase transition and the consequent microstructural deterioration under loaded conditions significantly compromise the long-term bearing capacity of the rock.

To quantitatively assess the effect of temperature elevation on the creep rate, the steady-state creep rates under various testing conditions were extracted, as illustrated in Fig. 4(b). The data indicate that the steady-state creep rate exhibits a nonlinear growth trend with increasing temperature, which strongly mirrors the aforementioned evolution of the unfrozen water content. Taking the 8 MPa stress condition—which corresponds to the actual in-situ stratal pressure—as an example: during the low-temperature stable stage (− 20 °C to − 10 °C), the steady-state creep rate was maintained at a relatively low level, ranging from 0.6 × 10^–2 h–1 to 0.9 × 10^–2 h–1. As the temperature rose into the active phase transition zone (− 6 °C to − 2 °C), the creep rate exhibited an accelerated increasing trend, escalating from 1.3 × 10^–2 h–1 to 2.4 × 10^–2 h–1. Upon further heating to 0 °C and ambient temperature, the creep rate reached approximately 3.6 × 10^–2 h–1 and tended to stabilize. Furthermore, with the increase in deviatoric stress, the amplification of the creep rate within the active phase transition zone became remarkably more pronounced.

Correlation analysis between micro- and macroscopic damage evolution

Elucidating the physical mapping relationship between microscopic pore deterioration and macroscopic mechanical response is a fundamental prerequisite for incorporating microscopic thermodynamic states into macroscopic constitutive models. In this section, utilizing the obtained unfrozen water content and steady-state creep rate, we analyze their synergistic evolutionary behavior across the cross-scale dimension.

By placing the microscopic phase transition parameter and the macroscopic creep rate within a synchronized temperature domain for comparison, it is revealed that both exhibit highly synchronous evolutionary characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 5a. During the low-temperature stable stage (− 20 °C to − 10 °C), the pore ice skeleton remains relatively intact; governed by this microscopic state, the steady-state creep rate under corresponding conditions is maintained at a significantly low level. When the boundary temperature enters the active phase transition zone (− 6 °C to − 2 °C), Fig. 5 demonstrates that both the unfrozen water content curve and the steady-state creep rate curve synchronously exhibit a nonlinear growth trend. The increase in unfrozen water content at the microscopic level corresponds precisely to the elevation of the macroscopic creep rate along the temperature axis. This correspondence of temperature thresholds definitively confirms that the internal ice-water phase transition is the fundamental physical trigger driving the acceleration of rock creep.

To quantify the driving effect of microscopic damage on macroscopic creep under varying stress levels, a scatter plot mapping the microscopic damage parameter DNMR against the corresponding steady-state creep rate for each testing condition was constructed, as depicted in Fig. 5b. The data distribution reveals a clear positive correlation: under a constant deviatoric stress, the steady-state creep rate exhibits an increasing trend with the growth of DNMR. Furthermore, as the deviatoric stress increases, the amplification of the creep rate induced by the elevation in DNMR becomes significantly more pronounced. These discrete mapping characteristics indicate that DNMR can comprehensively quantify the mechanical degradation resulting from the loss of the load-bearing capacity of solid ice and the lubricating effect of pore water.

However, relying solely on discrete data point mapping merely demonstrates the correspondence at specific static nodes, inherently lacking a continuous mechanical description of the time-dependent nature of rock creep. Therefore, to achieve the prediction of complete creep trajectories under coupled temperature-stress conditions, it is imperative to overcome the limitations of empirical mapping. This necessitates incorporating DNMR—a parameter endowed with explicit physical meaning—as an initial state variable into a fractional-order creep constitutive equation featuring time fractional calculus operators.

Cross-scale fractional-order creep constitutive model

Cross-scale micro-macro fractional-order creep constitutive equations

The theory of fractional derivatives was initially proposed in 1695 during correspondence between L’Hôpital and Leibniz. For the subsequent centuries, research in this area was primarily confined to the realm of pure mathematics. With continuous scientific exploration, fractional-order theory has been progressively extended to multiple disciplines owing to its unique mathematical properties. Scholars have demonstrated that fractional calculus can accurately capture the viscoelastic and creep characteristics of materials. Consequently, this study introduces fractional-order theory to establish the creep constitutive equation for Cretaceous sandstone during the thawing process.

The creep deformation of Cretaceous sandstone throughout the thawing process is governed by the synergistic effects of temperature, stress, and the microscopic damage state. Based on the stage-dependent deformation characteristics observed experimentally, the proposed model constructs a cross-scale creep framework by introducing the microscopic damage parameter DNMR to modify traditional mechanical elements (Fig. 6).

Fig. 6Fractional order creep damage model.

Fractional order creep damage model.

During the initial stage of the thawing test, the strain induced by the applied axial load is primarily governed by the linear resistance of the sandstone mineral skeleton and the internal solid ice. At this juncture, the unfrozen water content is relatively low, and the cementation strength between the ice body and the particles remains at its initial state. However, as the DNMR evolves during the thawing process, the alteration of the effective load-bearing area leads to a weakening trend in the macroscopic modulus. Therefore, an elastic body coupled with the microscopic damage parameter DNMR is employed to describe the instantaneous response of this stage.

Following the instantaneous deformation, the specimen enters the attenuation stage, characterized by a gradually decreasing creep rate. In the low-temperature environment, the cemented structure between the ice body and the skeleton provides the predominant resistance. As the temperature rises, the ice-water phase transition induces thermodynamic deterioration of the microstructure. To describe this mechanical behavior—which exhibits pronounced time-dependence and falls between that of an ideal fluid and a solid—the model introduces a fractional-order Abel dashpot. Through the fractional calculus operator, this element captures the creep memory effect of the material. Concurrently, it incorporates DNMR to modify its viscosity coefficient, thereby reflecting the evolutionary laws of the microstructure along the temperature elevation path.

Once the creep progresses into the steady-state stage, the deformation manifests as the synergistic action of elasticity and viscosity. At this stage, the increase in unfrozen water content triggers an enhanced lubricating effect within the pores, promoting the redistribution of stress towards the rock skeleton and internal defects. To characterize the creep rate at this stage and the amplification effect of temperature elevation on it, the model utilizes a fractional-order Kelvin body, composed of an elastic element and a fractional-order dashpot connected in parallel. This configuration ensures that the strain remains compatible under the influence of cross-scale damage during the deformation process.

As the thawing process advances, the escalation of DNMR leads to a reduction in the long-term strength of the sandstone. When the constant load exceeds the long-term strength threshold of the specimen at a specific temperature, the dynamic equilibrium of the steady-state stage is disrupted. Driven by stress concentration and the physicochemical action of liquid water, micro-fractures begin to propagate. This stage is characterized by the nonlinear superposition of viscous and plastic deformations, generating irreversible plastic strain. The model constructs a fractional-order viscoplastic body using a fractional-order dashpot and a plastic element in parallel. This element is designed to be triggered only when the deviatoric stress surpasses the critical strength under the current damage state, thereby simulating the accelerated creep and macroscopic failure characteristics observed in the late stages of thawing.

As the thawing process progressed and the temperature rose into the − 4 °C to 0 °C interval, governed by the evolution of the microscopic damage parameter DNMR, the experimental curves transitioned into the accelerated creep stage. The comparative results indicate that the model not only accurately simulates the attenuation and steady-state creep stages of the sandstone under thawing conditions, but is also highly applicable to the accelerated creep stage (Fig. 7(b)). This definitively confirms that the fractional-order viscoplastic element coupled with DNMR is capable of describing the nonlinear damage evolution behavior of loaded rocks under cross-threshold temperature conditions.

The initial elastic modulus (E1) in the cross-scale model characterizes the baseline stiffness of the loaded rock during the transient deformation stage. By extracting E1 under various testing conditions, a temperature-pressure coupled evolution surface was constructed (Fig. 8a). The data indicate that E1 exhibits a nonlinear dual-dependent degradation characteristic driven by both increasing thawing temperature and escalating deviatoric stress.

From the perspective of mechanical decoupling, the proposed model has explicitly incorporated the microscopic state parameter DNMR—which represents the reduction in effective load-bearing area—as an independent variable into the equations. Therefore, after stripping away the geometric damage caused by pore ice melting, the continuous decline in parameter E1 reveals the intrinsic degradation mechanism of the loaded rock skeleton. This irreversible weakening of the skeleton’s stiffness is fundamentally governed by thermo-mechanical coupling effects: on the one hand, the ice-water phase transition induced by temperature elevation weakens the cementation of pore ice, leading to microscopic relaxation of the contact structures between mineral grains; on the other hand, under macroscopic deviatoric stress, the liquid water generated by the phase transition exacerbates the lubricating effect and stress concentration within the pores, thereby promoting the slip and propagation of primary micro-fractures. These physical processes synergistically dominate the significant weakening of the rock’s initial deformation resistance.

Solid-liquid phase transition response

In fractional calculus creep theory, the fractional orders (ζ, γ, λ) serve as state parameters that quantitatively characterize the viscoelastic distribution state of the material between an ideal solid (where the order approaches 0) and an ideal fluid (where the order approaches 1). Extracting the fractional order γ—which represents the steady-state stage—for evolution analysis reveals that under a constant deviatoric stress, γ exhibits a monotonically increasing evolutionary trajectory with the elevation of thawing temperature.

Taking the 4 MPa deviatoric stress condition as an example: in the completely frozen state from − 30 °C to − 20 °C,γ the value of is as low as 0.05, and the specimen exhibits pronounced solid-like mechanical characteristics. As the temperature crosses into the active phase transition zone (− 6 °C to − 2 °C), the value of γ grows rapidly to 0.25. When the temperature returns to the ambient interval (0 °C to 20 °C), this value further climbs to 0.36. Furthermore, when comparing the attenuation-stage order ζ and the accelerated-stage order λ under different stress levels, the fractional orders across all stages demonstrate a positive sensitivity to the temperature elevation path.

The regular evolution of these mathematical parameters reflects the “solid-liquid phase transition” mechanism of the thawing rock’s microstructure, grounded in the fractional constitutive theory. In the low-temperature regime, pore water exists as solid ice, forming a high-bearing-capacity rigid network jointly with the mineral skeleton. At this juncture, the rock exhibits a strong creep memory effect, and its deformation behavior is highly dependent on elastic hysteresis. As the thawing process advances, the thermodynamic phase transition leads to an increase in the liquid water content within the pores. The free liquid water forms interfacial water films at micro-fractures and mineral grain boundaries, significantly diminishing the frictional resistance and shear capacity of the internal structure.

This pore water lubricating effect, triggered by the phase transition, drives an irreversible shift in the macroscopic mechanical attributes of the loaded rock from “elastic solid-dominated” to “viscous fluid-dominated.” The overall escalation of the fractional orders mathematically corroborates that the microscopic damage evolution weakens the fading memory characteristics of the rock matrix’s creep, thereby skewing its macroscopic strain response more toward the evolutionary laws of a viscous fluid.

Discussion

Cross-scale triggering mechanisms for the accelerated failure of thawing rocks

Traditional rock creep constitutive models typically define long-term strength as a static parameter that relies solely on confining pressure. However, the accelerated failure of loaded rocks along the thawing path exhibits pronounced nonlinear characteristics and temperature-stress dependence. The fractional-order creep model developed in this study, by establishing the nonlinear viscoplastic triggering criterion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \ge \sigma _{{\mathrm{s}}} \left( {1 - D_{{{\mathrm{NMR}}}} } \right)$$\end{document}), delineates a dynamic strength redline governed by microscopic damage evolution. Within this mechanical framework, the accelerated creep of the thawing rock is not triggered by an escalation in external loading, but is rather dictated by the dynamic reduction of the material’s internal bearing limit, as illustrated in Fig. 9.

In a creep environment characterized by constant deviatoric stress and stepped temperature elevation, the macroscopic mechanical boundary conditions remain invariant. However, the continuous ablation of pore ice drives a nonlinear escalation in the microscopic damage parameter. This phase transition-induced microscopic damage fundamentally causes a reduction in the effective load-bearing area of the rock skeleton. Within the macroscopic mechanical equations, this microscopic pore deterioration manifests as a continuous degradation of the effective long-term strength. When the dynamic strength redline—which declines as the thawing temperature rises—drops below the constant external load, the inherent internal stress equilibrium of the rock is disrupted, thereby triggering accelerated creep failure.

At the mesoscopic level, this dynamic breaching of the mechanical threshold corresponds to a transition in the fracture evolution mode. Once the external stress surpasses the dynamic long-term strength, the liquid water generated by the internal phase transition permeates toward the tips of micro-fractures under the influence of deviatoric stress. This hydro-mechanical coupling effect exacerbates localized stress concentration at the fracture tips, propelling the mechanical behavior of the micro-fractures from stable frictional slip in the early stages to unstable propagation. In summary, the accelerated failure of thawing rocks manifests as a quintessential cross-scale cascade response: microscopic phase transition damage diminishes the macroscopic shear resistance threshold, ultimately triggering the coalescence of the mesoscopic fracture network.

Principle decoupling advantages of introducing the microscopic state parameter

Traditional empirical models for rock creep are predominantly constructed based on macroscopic phenomenological theories. When addressing the variable-temperature thawing conditions induced by artificial ground freezing in coal mine shafts, traditional models typically rely on the overall attenuation of mechanical parameters to describe macroscopic deformation trajectories. However, this phenomenological fitting inherently fails to theoretically unveil the dual physical mechanisms governing rock degradation: during the thawing process, the decline in the rock’s macroscopic bearing capacity is fundamentally a superposition of the “geometric damage induced by pore ice ablation” and the “mechanical damage caused by the weakening of the skeleton structure.” Traditional parameters, devoid of microscopic constraints, are inadequate for effectively decoupling these two mechanisms.

The core advantage of the proposed model lies in the explicit incorporation of the microscopic state parameter, which is derived from Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) testing. By embedding the correction factor (1-DNMR) as an independent physical state variable into the fractional-order constitutive equation, theoretical decoupling of the thermodynamic phase transition and mechanical deformation is achieved. Within this cross-scale framework, the evolution of DNMR directly characterizes the reduction in the effective load-bearing structure caused by the transformation of solid pore ice into liquid water. Consequently, after stripping away the geometric damage induced by the phase transition within the equations, the identified fundamental mechanical parameters can objectively map the intrinsic degradation laws of the rock mineral matrix under thermo-mechanical coupling.

The introduction of such a microscopic physical parameter endows the fractional-order creep equations with explicit mechanical significance. The model establishes a robust cross-scale response chain: “microscopic pore phase transition → mesoscopic effective stress adjustment → macroscopic nonlinear creep”. This not only profoundly enhances the parameter reliability of the model within the thawing environment of artificially frozen strata, but also provides a physically constrained theoretical basis for analyzing the damage evolution of water-bearing rocks.

Applicability and boundary conditions in the context of shaft freezing

Artificial ground freezing (AGF) is a critical technology for deep coal mine shafts penetrating water-rich aquifers. During the thawing and stress-transfer phase of the frozen wall, the surrounding rock of the shaft undergoes complex thermo-mechanical environmental evolution. Based on the triggering mechanisms revealed by the proposed cross-scale creep model, the active phase transition zone is a highly sensitive interval for the dynamic reduction of the rock’s long-term strength. During the shaft thawing period, even if the in-situ stress induced by excavation unloading remains constant, the microscopic damage surge triggered by pore ice melting can still drive the surrounding rock to breach the dynamic strength redline, initiating irreversible accelerated creep failure. Therefore, the monitoring and evaluation of shaft wall stability during the thawing period should not rely solely on single indicators such as macroscopic stress or deformation rate; instead, the temperature gradient within the phase transition zone must be incorporated as a core early warning parameter for anticipating sudden instability in the surrounding rock.

Beyond its theoretical contributions, the proposed cross-scale framework holds substantial promise for practical engineering safety risk management. By integrating the fractional-order constitutive model with field Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS) systems, real-time temperature measurements can be leveraged to continuously update the NMR-derived microscopic damage parameter (DNMR). This integration enables real-time prediction of the long-term dynamic strength of deep shaft surrounding rock mass. Once the in-situ stress approaches this dynamically updated strength threshold, the system automatically triggers an early warning for potential rock instability, effectively bridging the critical gap between theoretical dynamic strength criteria and field engineering applications.

That said, no theoretical constitutive model is universally applicable, and the proposed framework is no exception. The model is currently restricted to constant confining pressure and static pore fluid conditions. While it accurately reproduces the thermodynamic phase transition and nonlinear creep behavior of weakly cemented sandstone, real deep shaft thawing environments are significantly more complex. In-situ, ice melting is typically accompanied by pore water pressure buildup and dynamic dissipation, causing the effective confining pressure of the surrounding rock to evolve spatiotemporally. Additionally, groundwater seepage in hydrodynamic formations further accelerates damage propagation within the rock fracture network.

At present, the model quantifies the geometric weakening effect of ice-water phase transition solely through the scalar state variable (1-DNMR)) and does not yet account for fully coupled Thermo-Hydro-Mechanical (T-H-M) interactions induced by dynamic hydrodynamic pressure. Moving forward, a key research priority will be to incorporate dynamic effective stress and fluid seepage mechanisms into the fractional-order constitutive framework. Resolving these limitations will substantially expand the model’s capability to characterize the complete damage and creep process under complex variable confining pressure and multi-field coupled conditions.
