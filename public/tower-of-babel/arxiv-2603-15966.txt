Extended Admissible Dissections of Marked Surfaces and Piano Algebras
Marina Godinho and Dave Murphy

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of extended admissible dissections of a marked surface, building upon the notion of an admissible dissection of a marked surface by Amiot–Plamondon–Schroll. For each extended admissible dissection we construct a differential graded algebra, called a piano algebra, which may be viewed in some sense as a differential graded analogue of a gentle algebra. We show that for a marked disc without punctures, a piano algebra is quasi-isomorphic to the graded endomorphism ring of a classical generator of the Paquette–Yıldırım completion of the discrete cluster category of Dynkin type A ∞ A_{\infty} , labelled 𝒞 ¯ n \overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n} . We use previous results of the authors to show that there exists an additive equivalence between 𝒞 ¯ n \overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n} and the perfect derived category of a specific piano algebra, that sends triangles with two indecomposable terms to triangles with two indecomposable terms. We use this equivalence to prove that any two piano algebras coming from homeomorphic marked discs are derived equivalent.

Introduction

The use of combinatorial models to understand certain categories has become a popular trend in recent years.
The concept is based in the notion of being able to visualise certain data from a given category in the hopes to understand the representation theory of the category via more combinatorial means.
Usually this involves viewing indecomposable objects as combinatorial objects, and showing that certain homological properties are in correspondence with some combinatorial properties.

Two particular classes of algebras that this process has proven particularly powerful in are cluster algebras and gentle algebras.
combinatorial models for categories associated to these algebras (e.g. cluster categories, module categories, bounded derived categories) often take the form of
marked surfaces
, that is, the data of a compact, orientable surface (often with boundary) and a set of
marked points
on the surface.
In these models, indecomposable objects correspond to isotopy classes of curves on the surface with endpoints in the set of marked points, and the dimension of
\mathrm{Hom}
-spaces are in some correspondence with number of times corresponding arcs cross, for some suitable definition of crossing.

Cluster algebras were introduced by Fomin–Zelevinsky in a series of papers
[
10
,
11
,
4
,
12
]
around the start of the millennium.
They are typified by their
clusters
, certain sets of generators, and their
cluster mutation
, a process of uniquely replacing a given generator in a cluster to obtain a new cluster, according to some prescribed rule.
It was noticed in the first paper by Fomin–Zelevinksy
[
10
]
that clusters in the cluster algebra of Dynkin type
A_{n}
were in correspondence with the triangulations of an
(n+3)
-gon, and the cluster mutation corresponded to flipping a diagonal in the triangulation.

Cluster categories are a family of triangulated categories, and their study began with
[
5
,
6
]
, where both sets of authors independently sought to categorify the then recently developed theory of cluster algebras.
In
[
5
]
, the authors aimed to be able to study the tilting theory of an hereditary algebra by categorical means.
To do this, for an hereditary algebra
H
, they consider the (triangulated) orbit category of the bounded derived category
\mathrm{D}^{b}(H)/(\tau^{-1}[1])
, where
\tau
is the Auslander–Reiten translation, and
[1]
denotes the suspension functor.

It was shown in
[
6
]
that the cluster category of
kA_{n}
may be modelled by the
(n+3)
-gon, with indecomposable objects corresponding to diagonals, non-trivial
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}
-spaces between indecomposable objects corresponding to the respective diagonals crossing, and the triangulations corresponded to the so-called cluster-tilting subcategories, the categorical analogue to clusters in a cluster algebra.

In
[
18
]
, Holm–Jørgensen studied
\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{f}}(k[x])
, the finite derived category of the polynomial ring
(k[x],d=0)
seen as a differential graded algebra (=
dg algebra
) with
x
placed in degree
-1
.
The authors showed that the category was modelled by the
\infty
-gon, more commonly viewed as a disc with
\mathbb{Z}
many marked points on the boundary, and a single
accumulation point
, which is not considered to be a marked point here.
Cluster-tilting subcategories in this category also correspond to certain triangulations of the
\infty
-gon, and mutation works in the combinatorial manner as in the cluster categories of Dynkin type
A_{n}
.

A completion via the combinatorial model was studied by Fisher
[
9
]
, where they look at the closure under homotopy colimits corresponding to one-sided fountains.
The author shows that the resulting category is triangulated and may be modelled using the
\infty
-gon by now considering the accumulation point as a marked point, and indecomposable homotopy colimits correspond to those arcs with the accumulation point as an endpoint.
It was proven in
[
2
]
that this category is triangle equivalent to the perfect derived category of the dg algebra
(k[x],d=0)
, previously considered by Holm–Jørgensen
[
18
]
.

Igusa–Todorov provided an algorithm in
[
20
]
to begin with a cyclic poset, and to obtain a cluster category.
This provided a combinatorial generalisation to the category studied by Holm–Jørgensen in
[
18
]
, by constructing triangulated categories that could be modelled by the
\infty
-gon with an arbitrary number of finite accumulation points.
These are algebraic, Krull–Schmidt, 2-Calabi–Yau, Hom-finite,
k
-linear, triangulated categories, with classical generators
[
22
]
, and as such are equivalent as triangulated categories to the perfect derived category of some differential graded algebra by a result of Keller
[
21
]
.
These categories exhibit interesting phenomena that is not present in the finite rank cases, and this has lead to a lot of recent interest
[
8
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
23
]
.

Paquette–Yıldırım
[
27
]
constructed a family of categories, as a Verdier localisation of the Igusa–Todorov categories that can be viewed as completions of the Igusa–Todorov categories in the sense that they can be modelled by now considering the accumulation points as marked points too.
We denote these categories by
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
, where
n
is the number of accumulation points in the combinatorial model, and call them
Paquette-Yıldırım categories
.
These categories have exceptionally powerful combinatorial properties, and can be used to understand develop combinatorial techniques to study the representation theory of triangulated categories with cluster-tilting subcategories containing infinite indecomposable objects up to isomorphism.
Just as in the non-completed case, there has been quite a bit of recent work on the category
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

[
7
,
14
,
22
,
23
]
.
It was shown in
[
2
]
that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is equivalent as triangulated categories to the completion under homotopy colimits due to Fisher.

However, a natural question now arises, can we describe the dg algebra
\Lambda
such that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is triangle equivalent to the perfect derived category of
\Lambda
?

A partial answer was provided by the authors in
[
15
]
, where it was shown that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is additively equivalent to the perfect derived category of a dg algebra
\Lambda_{n}
.
Moreover, this equivalence commutes with the respective suspension functors, and preserves triangles that are determined by crossing arcs.

Our work here begins by considering the combinatorial models introduced in
[
1
]
, which they use to model the bounded derived category of a gentle algebra.
In this model
(\Sigma,M,P)
, we consider an orientable surface
\Sigma
, possibly with boundary,and two sets of coloured marked points, one coloured red
M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}\cup P_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}
and the other green
M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}\cup P_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}
, such that
M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}\cup M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}
is contained on the boundary of
\Sigma
.
An
admissible dissection
of the surface is a maximal set of non-crossing arcs between points in
M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}\cup P_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}
, such that they cut out a topological disc with exactly one point in
M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}\cup P_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}
, either in the interior or on the boundary.
It was shown in
[
24
]
, for an equivalent combinatorial model, that the class of admissible dissections up to homeomorphism are in bijection to the equivalences classes of gentle algebras.
It should also be noted that the combinatorial model for the module category of a gentle algebra was provided by Baur–Coelho Simões in
[
3
]
.

We introduce
extended admissible dissections
of a marked surface, in which we take the data of an admissible dissection, and add in a single so-called
binding arc
to each point in
M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}\cup P_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}
, connecting a green marked point to a red one.
We again ask that this collection of arcs is pairwise non-crossing.
To each extended admissible dissection
\Delta
, we can construct a gentle quiver, to which we add a loop in degree
-1
to each vertex, and a loop in degree
1
to each vertex in the underlying admissible dissection.
A
piano algebra

\Lambda^{\Delta}
is then a dg algebra defined to be the graded path algebra of the quiver, modulo certain relations on the graded loops, and equipped with a trivial differential.

This leads us to our main result on extended admissible dissections, where
D^{2}
is a disc, and
M_{n}
is a set of
2n
marked points on the boundary of
D^{2}
.

Theorem 1

(
Proposition

2.25
)
.

There exists a bijection between the homeomorphism classes of extended admissible dissections of
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
, and the equivalence classes of limit generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.

Limit generators are a special class of classical generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, signified by some particularly nice homological properties.
Their connections to extended admissible dissections of
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
makes them particularly powerful classical generators for our particular interests.
In fact, this connection provides us with the following result.

Theorem 2

(
Theorem

3.8
and
Lemma

3.12
)
.

Let
G
be a limit generator of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and let
\Delta
be the corresponding extended admissible dissection by
1
.
Then the graded endomorphism ring of
G
is quasi-isomorphic to
\Lambda^{\Delta}
.

A key result of
[
1
]
is that two gentle algebras are derived equivalent if and only if there exists an orientation-preserving homeomorphism between their associated marked surfaces, along with a condition concerning the line fields on the marked surfaces.
It is natural for us then to ask whether a similar result may hold for their respective piano algebras.
That is, are two piano algebras derived equivalent if and only if there exists an orientation-preserving homeomorphism between their respective associated marked surfaces?

We do not give a conclusive answer to this question in this paper, however we do begin to study this question by looking at the piano algebras associated to the marked disc
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
.
To do this we need to make use of the connection between these piano algebras and the limit generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.

It was shown in
[
15
]
that there is a dg algebra
\Lambda_{n}
such that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\mathrm{perf}\Lambda_{n}
are additively equivalent.
Here the dg algebra
\Lambda_{n}
has trivial differential, and is quasi-isomorphic to the graded endomorphism ring of a so-called
fan generator
of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
However, we show that a fan generator of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is in fact a special case of a limit generator, and so
\Lambda_{n}
is a piano algebra.

By showing that the additive equivalence between
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\mathrm{perf}\,\Lambda_{n}
in
[
15
, Theorem 5.6]
preserves classical generators, we prove that we can view a piano algebra
\Lambda^{\Delta}
as a
\Lambda^{\Delta}-\Lambda_{n}
bimodule.
This view point allows us to use a result due to Keller
[
21
]
to prove our final result.

Theorem 3

(
Theorem

4.11
)
.

There is a equivalence of triangulated categories

\mathrm{D}(\Lambda^{\Delta})\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{D}(\Lambda_{n}),

for any extended admissible dissection
\Delta
of the marked surface
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
.

In
Section

1
we recall the Paquette–Yıldırım categories, denoted
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, and some necessary results about them.
In particular we recall the classification of their classical generators and introduce the notion of a limit generator.

Section

2
is where we recall the construction of admissible dissections of marked surfaces by Amiot–Plamondon–Schroll
[
1
]
.
We also recall how to construct gentle algebras from these admissible dissections.
We then begin our work here by introducing extended admissible dissections, and proving some elementary results involving extended admissible dissections.
Finally, we introduce the piano algebra associated to an extended admissible dissection.

We show that there exists a strong connection between piano algebras associated to the marked surface
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
, and the limit generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
in
Section

3
.
In fact we show that there exists an isomorphism of graded algebras between the graded endomorphism ring of a limit generator and the homology of a piano algebra.
We also explicitly describe the graded endomorphism ring of a class of classical generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, which includes the class of limit generators.

Finally, in
Section

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

\mathrm{perf}\Lambda_{n}

Furthermore, we show that any piano algebra associated to the marked surface

is derived equivalence to
\Lambda_{n}

2.5.
Classical Generators and Admissible Dissections of a Disc

Throughout this section, we use the notation
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
to mean the marked, unpunctured disc with
\lvert M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}\rvert=\lvert M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}\rvert=n
.
We also consider the marked disc
(D^{2},\mathscr{M})
from
Section

1
to be a marked surface
(D^{2},M,\emptyset)
, where accumulation points correspond to points in
M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}
, and their cyclic ordering is preserved.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
H=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}H_{i}
be an object in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
such that each
H_{i}
is indecomposable and corresponds to a double limit arc.
Then we say
H
is a
limit pre-generator
if
H
is homologically connected, the collection of endpoints of
H
is
L(\mathscr{M})
, and no two arcs cross.
We also ask that
H
is minimal with respect to satisfying these conditions, that is, no proper direct summand of
H
satisfies these conditions.

We say that two limit pre-generators
H,H^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if

\mathrm{add}(H[i]\mid i\in\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{add}(H^{\prime}[i]\mid i\in\mathbb{Z})

are equivalent as additive categories.

The naming convention may seem unusual, given that limit pre-generators are not in fact classical generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, as they do not have a complete orbit in
\overline{\mathscr{M}}
.
However, we will later justify the naming by seeing that every
limit generator
necessarily has a limit pre-generator as a direct summand, and every limit pre-generator is a direct summand of a limit generator.

We also note the implication that any arc in a limit pre-generator
H\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
must share an endpoint with another arc in
H
.
This follows from
H
being homologically connected and no two arcs in
H
crossing.

Lemma 2.18
.

There is a bijection between the homeomorphism class of admissible dissections of
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
and equivalence class of limit pre-generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.

Proof.

We use the assignment
\varepsilon
taking an accumulation point to a point in
M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}
, and the set of marked point in
\mathscr{M}
between two successive accumulation points
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{+}
to a point in
M_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\bullet}}
, such that
\varepsilon
respects the orientation of the disc.
For an object in
X\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, we write
\varepsilon(X)
to denote the collection of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
-arcs induced by the assignment
\varepsilon
.

Then a limit pre-generator
H
defines a collection of non-intersecting and pairwise non-isomorphic arcs, and so
\varepsilon(H)
is a collection of non-intersecting and pairwise
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
-arcs.
By
Proposition

2.4
, it is enough to show that
H
has
n-1
indecomposable direct summands to show that
\varepsilon(H)
is an admissible
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
-dissection.
However, as
H
must be homologically connected, have
n
endpoints, and is minimal with respect to these properties, then
\varepsilon(H)
corresponds to a tree with
n
vertices by considering the non-orientated quiver induced by
\varepsilon(H)
.
Then basic results in graph theory tell us that there must be exactly
n-1

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
-arcs in the collection
\varepsilon(H)
, hence
\varepsilon(H)
is an admissible
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
-dissection for any limit pre-generator
H
.

Suppose that
H,H^{\prime}
are not equivalent limit pre-generators.
As
H
and
H^{\prime}
have the same number of indecomposable direct summands, all of which are double limit arcs and so have isomorphic endomorphism rings, then for any assignment
\eta:\mathrm{add}(H[i])\rightarrow\mathrm{add}(H^{\prime}[i])
, there exists at least two indecomposable direct summands
H_{1},H_{2}
of
H
such that

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(H_{1},H_{2})\not\cong\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(\eta(H_{1}),\eta(H_{2})).

We know
H_{1},H_{2}
are indecomposable, and
\mathrm{Hom}
-spaces of indecomposable objects in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
have dimension either 0 or 1 over
k
, and this corresponds to double limit arcs sharing an accumulation as an endpoint in a particular orientation in our situation.
Therefore there exists
H_{1},H_{2}
indecomposable direct summands of
H
such that
\varepsilon(H_{1})
and
\varepsilon(H_{2})
share an endpoint, with
\varepsilon(H_{2})
a rotation of
\varepsilon(H_{1})
in the positive orientation if and only if this is not true for
\varepsilon(\eta(H_{1}))
and
\varepsilon(\eta(H_{2}))
, for any assignment
\eta
.
Hence
\varepsilon(H)
and
\varepsilon(H^{\prime})
are non-equivalent admissible dissections.

Finally, given an admissible dissection
\Delta
, it is straightforward to see that
\varepsilon^{-1}(\Delta)
is a collection of
n-1
non-intersecting and pairwise different double limit arcs, and so is a limit pre-generator of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
Thus we have a bijection between the equivalence class of limit pre-generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and the homeomorphism class of admissible dissections of
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
.
∎

Definition 2.21
.

Let
G\cong\bigoplus_{a=1}^{m}G_{a}
be a minimal generator of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
We call
G
a
limit generator
, if
G_{a}
is a (double) limit arc, and
G_{a}
and
G_{b}[i]
do not cross each other for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, for all
1\leq a,b\leq m
.

Remark 2.22
.

A straightforward observation from the definition of a limit generator tells us that there is exactly one limit arc with an endpoint in the open interval
(a_{i},a_{i+1})\subset\overline{\mathscr{M}}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
This is as if two arcs had an endpoint in such an open interval, then some suspension of the arcs would cross each other, or be isomorphic, and so would not be a limit generator.
There must also be at least one arc with an endpoint in the open interval, as
G
must have a complete orbit in
\overline{\mathscr{M}}
to be a classical generator by
Theorem

1.7
.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
G\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be a limit generator.
Then
G\cong\prescript{p}{}{G}\oplus\prescript{l}{}{G}
, where
\prescript{p}{}{G}
is a limit pre-generator with
n-1
indecomposable direct summands, and
\prescript{l}{}{G}
is a set of
n
non-isomorphic, non-intersecting limit arcs.

Proof.

By definition, all indecomposable direct summands of
G
are either limit arcs, or double limit arcs, such that no two arcs intersect up to suspension, but may cross.
Therefore, for each interval
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{+})\subset\overline{\mathscr{M}}
, there must be exactly one limit arc
G_{a}
with an endpoint in
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{+})
, see
Remark

2.22
.
Label the collection of limit arcs in
G
by
\prescript{l}{}{G}
.
If two limit arcs in
\prescript{l}{}{G}
intersect up to suspension, then
G
is not a limit generator by definition, and if they are isomorphic up to suspension, then
G
is no longer minimal as a proper direct summand of
G
is a generator, and so
G
would not a limit generator by definition.
Hence there is a sub-collection
\prescript{l}{}{G}
of
n
non-intersecting limit arcs in
G
, one for each subset
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{+})\subset\overline{\mathscr{M}}
.

All other indecomposable direct summands of
G
must then be double limit arcs, so we need to show that there are
n-1
of these direct summands, that the object
\prescript{p}{}{G}
is homologically connected and has a collection of endpoints equal to
L(\mathscr{M})
.
Let
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{m}
be distinct accumulation points, and let
G_{a},G_{b}
be limit arcs with an endpoint at
\mathfrak{a}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{m}
respectively.
Then
G_{a},G_{b}
do not cross, and
G
may only be homologically connected if there exists a set of double limit arcs
G_{a_{1}},\ldots,G_{a_{p}}
such that
G_{a_{i}}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{r_{i}},\mathfrak{a}_{r_{i+1}}\}
.
This is because no two distinct arcs in
G
intersect, and limit arcs will only cross other arcs with the same accumulation point.
Hence, between any two accumulation points, there is a path that transverses a set of double limit arcs.
As
G
is minimal, there may only be a single path of this kind by
[
22
, Lemma 5.4]
, and so we can consider the accumulation points as vertices, and the double limit arcs as edges on a connected graph.
As there is exactly one path between any two vertices, then the graph is in fact a tree with
n
vertices, and so has
n-1
edges, meaning there must be
n-1
double limit arcs as indecomposable direct summands of
G
.

The above arguments also show that the collection of endpoints of
\prescript{p}{}{G}
is
L(\mathscr{M})
, and that
\prescript{p}{}{G}
must be homologically connected.
Hence,
\prescript{p}{}{G}
is a limit pre-generator.
∎

Lemma 2.24
.

Let
\prescript{p}{}{G}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be a limit pre-generator.
Then there exists a limit generator
G\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
such that
G\cong\prescript{p}{}{G}\oplus\prescript{l}{}{G}
.

Proof.

Let
\prescript{l}{}{G}
be collection of limit arcs such that each indecomposable direct summand
\prescript{l}{}{G}_{a}
of
\prescript{l}{}{G}
has endpoints
\{\mathfrak{a}_{a},x_{a}\}

\prescript{p}{}{G}\oplus\prescript{l}{}{G}
is a collection of non-intersecting limit and double limit arcs, such that
\prescript{p}{}{G}\oplus\prescript{l}{}{G}

\overline{\mathscr{M}}

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

1.7
.

The above lemma is not an exhaustive list of limit generators containing the limit pre-generator
\prescript{p}{}{G}

It is only intended to show the existence of a limit generator for any limit pre-generator.

3.
Graded Endomorphism Ring of Generators in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

In this section we look at the graded endomorphism ring of some generators in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
We do not look at all generators, but cover all generators which satisfy some combinatoric criteria, which includes all limit generators.

3.1.
Graded Endomorphism Rings

We begin by looking at the graded endomorphism ring of indecomposable objects, with the exception of short arcs, before considering objects without short arcs as direct summands.
However, first let us recall the definition of a graded endomorphism ring.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
M
be an object of a triangulated category
\mathcal{T}
, then the
graded endomorphism ring of

M
, denoted
\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\ast}(M)
, is the graded ring with
i^{th}
degree

\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{T}}^{i}(M)=\mathrm{Ext}_{\mathcal{T}}^{i}(M,M)\cong\mathcal{T}(M)(M[i]).

Let
f\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{T}}^{i}(M)
and
g\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}(M)
.
Then we define multiplication as

gf:=g[i]\circ f\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{T}}^{i+j}(M).

To compute the graded endomorphism ring of a classical generator, it is worth first looking at the graded endomorphism rings of three of the four types indecomposable objects; long arcs, limit arcs and double limit arcs.
We need not consider short arcs as they do not correspond to the direct summand of a minimal generator
[
22
]
.
We follow the concept of a similar proof found in
[
2
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be an indecomposable object that corresponds to a limit arc.
Then

\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{\ast}(X)\cong k[x]

as graded rings, with
x
concentrated in degree
-1
.

We follow part of the proof of
[
2
, Prop. 3.6]
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

1.4
, we have

\dim_{k}(\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{l}(X))=\begin{cases}1&if $l\leq 0$,\\
0&if $l>0$,\end{cases}

which agrees on dimensions with
k[x]
considered as a graded ring with
x
concentrated in degree
-1
.
All that is left to show is that the ring structures on
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{\ast}(X)
and
k[x]
agree.

Let
f\in\mathrm{End}^{-l}(X)
be a morphism with
l>0
, then by the construction of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
in
[
27
]
and using
Proposition

1.4
, we see
f
factors through an
l
-fold product
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{-1}(X)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{-1}(X)
.
This shows that the graded endomorphism ring of
X
is isomorphic to
k[x]
with
x
in degree
-1
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Y\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be an indecomposable object that corresponds to a double limit arc.
Then

\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{\ast}(Y)\cong k[x^{\pm 1}]

as graded rings, with
x
concentrated in degree
-1
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

1.4
we have

\dim_{k}(\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{l}(Y))=1

for all
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We need to show that multiplication agrees.

Given
g\in\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{-l}(Y)
for
l>0
, we may use the same approach as in
Lemma

3.2
to show that
g
factors through an
l
-fold product
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{-1}(Y)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{-1}(Y)
.
Hence

\bigoplus_{l\leq 0}\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{l}(Y)\cong k[x]

such that
x
is placed in degree
-1
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{End}^{l^{\prime}}(Y)
for
l^{\prime}>0
, we show that there exists an
l^{\prime}
-fold product
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{1}(Y)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{1}(Y)
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{2n}
such that
\pi(A)\cong Y
under the localisation functor
\pi
.
There exists an
l
-fold product
h=h_{1}\cdots h_{l^{\prime}}\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{C}_{2n}}^{-1}(A)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{C}_{2n}}^{-1}(A)
in
\mathcal{C}_{2n}
by
[
20
]
, where each
h_{i}
is in the multiplicative system
\Omega
.
Therefore in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, there exists a morphism
\pi(h_{i})^{-1}\in\mathrm{End}^{1}(\pi(A))\cong\mathrm{End}^{1}(Y)
, and so
f=\pi(h)^{-1}\in\mathrm{End}^{l^{\prime}}(Y)
factors through an
l^{\prime}
-fold product
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{1}(Y)\times\ldots\times\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{1}(Y)
.
Thus

\bigoplus_{l^{\prime}\geq 0}\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{l^{\prime}}(Y)\cong k[y]

such that
y
is placed in degree
1
.

Let
s\in\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{1}(Y)
, then
s=\pi(t)^{-1}
where
t\in\Omega
, as
\mathcal{C}_{2n}(A,A[1])=0
where
\pi(A)\cong Y
.
Then there exists

such that
s^{\prime}=\pi(t)
, and so

.
Therefore

\bigoplus_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{l}(Y)\cong k[x^{\pm 1}]

with
x
placed in degree
-1
.
∎

Unfortunately, the graded endomorphism ring of a long arc is somewhat less pleasant than those of a limit arc or double limit arc.
In particular, the multiplication in the graded endomorphism ring of a long arc is seems rather unnatural in comparison.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Z\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be an indecomposable object corresponding to a long arc.
Then
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Z)\cong\mathfrak{R}
, where

\mathfrak{R}^{i}\cong\begin{cases}k&if $i\neq 1$,\\
0&if $i=1$.\end{cases}

For
a\in\mathfrak{R}^{i}
and
b\in\mathfrak{R}^{j}
, multiplication is given by,

a\cdot b=\begin{cases}ab&if $i,j\leq 0$,\\
ab&if $i\leq 0,j\geq 2$ and $j>-i$,\\
ab&if $i\geq 2,j\leq 0$ and $-j<i$,\\
0&else.\end{cases}

Proof.

By
Proposition

1.4
,

\mathrm{End}^{i}(Z)\cong\begin{cases}k&if $i\neq 1$,\\
0&if $i=1$.\end{cases}

It also follows from the same result that two morphisms
f:X\rightarrow Y
and
g:Y\rightarrow X
, with
X,Y
indecomposable objects, compose to zero unless
Y\cong X
.
It remains to show how multiplication works in
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Z)
.

Suppose that
\ell_{Z}=\{z_{0},z_{1}\}
, and let
\ell_{Z[i]}=\{z_{0}-i,z_{1}-i\}
.
Then by
Proposition

1.4
it follows that
f\in\mathrm{End}^{i}(Z)
and
g\in\mathrm{End}^{j}(Z)
compose non-trivially to
gf\in\mathrm{End}^{i+j}(Z)
if and only if one of the following holds;

(3.A)

\displaystyle z_{0}\leq z_{0}-i\leq z_{0}-i-j<z_{1},

(3.B)

\displaystyle z_{0}\leq z_{0}-j\leq z_{1}-i-j<z_{1},

(3.C)

\displaystyle z_{0}\leq z_{1}-j\leq z_{1}-i-j<z_{1}.

It follows that in case (3.A),
i,j\leq 0
, in case (3.B),
i\leq 0,j\geq 2
and
j>-i
, and in case (3.C)
i\geq 2,j\leq 0
and
-j<i
.
∎

With the graded endomorphism rings of all of the relevant indecomposable objects computed, it is time for us to look at graded endomorphism rings of (some) decomposable objects.
To begin with, we look at objects with exactly two non-isomorphic indecomposable direct summands, and consider some conditions on the choice of those direct summands.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X,Y\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be two indecomposable objects such that the corresponding arcs are (double) limit arcs, and share an accumulation point, such that
Y\not\cong X[i]
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Suppose, without loss of generality, that
Y
is an anticlockwise rotation of
X
.
Then

\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X\oplus Y)\cong\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)&k[x^{\pm 1}]\\
0&\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)\end{pmatrix}

with multiplication given by matrix multiplication, and
x
concentrated in degree
-1
.

Proof.

The main diagonal entries of
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X\oplus Y)
are given by the graded endomorphism rings of both indecomposable direct summands of
X\oplus Y
, and the other two entries are given by
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
and
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(Y,X)
respectively.

The graded endomorphism rings of
X
and
Y
respectively are given by identifying what type of arc they correspond to and apply either
Lemma

3.2
or
Lemma

3.3
, whichever is appropriate.
Given
\ell_{Y}
is an anticlockwise rotation of
\ell_{X[i]}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, then it follows from
[
27
]
that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(Y,X[i])=0
, and so

\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(Y,X)=0.

Analogously, we find that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(Y,X[i])\cong k
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

To justify our choice of
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)\cong k[x^{\pm 1}]
as graded
k
-vector spaces, consider that
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
is an
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)-\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)
-bimodule, and both
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)
and
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)
are isomorphic to either
k[x]
or
k[x^{\pm 1}]
(with
x
concentrated in degree
-1
) by
Lemmas

3.2
and
3.3
.
Hence
k[x^{\pm 1}]
is a natural
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)-\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)
-bimodule, and our result follows.
∎

In this next result, we look at pairs of indecomposable objects such that they cross for any power of the suspension functor applied to either of them.
This is somewhat for convenience sake, as we then do not need to consider at which power of the suspension functor that the two indecomposable objects no longer cross.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X,Y\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be two indecomposable objects such that the corresponding arcs cross and
\mathscr{M}_{X}\cap\mathscr{M}_{Y}=\emptyset
.
Then

\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X\oplus Y)\cong\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)&k[x^{\pm 1}]\\
k[x^{\pm 1}]&\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)\end{pmatrix}

with multiplication given by matrix multiplication, and
f\otimes_{\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)}g=g\otimes_{\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)}f=0
for
f\in\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
and
g\in\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(Y,X)
, if
X,Y
are (double) limit arcs.
If
X
(resp.
Y
) is a long arc, then
f\otimes_{\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(Y)}g=fg
(resp.
g\otimes_{\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X)}f=gf
) if
\lvert fg\rvert>0
(resp.
\lvert gf\rvert>0
), else multiplication is trivial.

Proof.

The main diagonal entries of
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(X\oplus Y)
are given by the graded endomorphism rings of both indecomposable direct summands of
X\oplus Y
, and the other two entries are given by
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
and
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(Y,X)
respectively.
A similar argument to
Lemma

3.5
shows that
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
and
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(Y,X)
are both isomorphic to
k[x^{\pm 1}]
as
k
-vector spaces.

The multiplication rules then follow from
Lemma

1.5
.
∎

In the proof of
Lemma

3.6
, we make a slight oversight showing that
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)\cong k[x^{\pm 1}]
, in that
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
may be an
\mathfrak{R}-\mathfrak{R}
bimodule, and so our argument for why we chose
k[x^{\pm 1}]
for our notation doesn’t necessarily make much sense.
However,
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(X,Y)
and
k[x^{\pm 1}]
agree on dimensions in each degree, and therefore as graded
k
-vector spaces, so we make the choice as a matter of notational convenience.

We may now state the graded endomorphism ring for a class of generators of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}G_{i}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be a minimal classical generator, with
G_{i}
indecomposable for all
i
, such that
\mathscr{M}_{G_{i}}\cap\mathscr{M}_{G_{j}}=\emptyset
or
\{a\}
for some accumulation point
a
.
Then
\mathrm{End}^{\ast}(G)
is isomorphic to a generalised
(m\times m)
-matrix
k
-algebra, labelled
\chi^{G}
, with entries given by,

\chi^{G}_{i,j}\cong\begin{cases}k[x]&if $i=j$ and $\ell_{G_{i}}$ is a limit arc,\\
\mathfrak{R}&if $i=j$ and $\ell_{G_{i}}$ is a long arc,\\
0&if $i\neq j$ and, $\ell_{G_{i}}$ and $\ell_{G_{j}}$ do not cross,\\
0&if $i\neq j$ and, $\ell_{G_{i}}$ and $\ell_{G_{j}}$ share an accumulation point, and $\ell_{G_{i}}$ is an anticlockwise rotation of $\ell_{G_{j}}$,\\
k[x^{\pm 1}]&else.\end{cases}

Where multiplication of
f\in\chi^{G}_{i,j}
and
g=\chi^{G}_{j,l}
, for
f,g\neq 0
, in
\chi^{G}
is given by

\varphi_{ijl}(f\otimes_{\mathrm{End}(G_{j})}g)=\begin{cases}gf&if $\ell_{G_{i}},\ell_{G_{j}},\ell_{G_{l}}$ share an accumulation point as an endpoint,\\
gf&if $\ell_{G_{i}},\ell_{G_{j}},\ell_{G_{l}}$ cross and satisfy \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{Prop:PY3.14}, with $G_{j}$ the object factored through,\\
gf&if $l=i$, $G_{i}$ is a long arc, and we are in the case of \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{Lem:crossing endo},\\
0&else.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\chi^{G}_{i,j}=\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(G_{i},G_{j})
.

The entry
\chi^{G}_{i,i}
is given by
Lemma

3.2
if
G_{i}
is a limit arc,
Lemma

3.3
if
G_{i}
is a double limit arc, and
Lemma

3.4
if
G_{i}
is a long arc.

Lemma

3.6
, resp.
Lemma

3.5
, gives the
(2\times 2)
-matrix subalgebra,

\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{G}_{i,i}&\chi^{G}_{i,j}\\
\chi^{G}_{j,i}&\chi^{G}_{j,j}\end{pmatrix}

for all
G_{i}
and
G_{j}
such that
\ell_{G_{i}}
and
\ell_{G_{j}}
cross, resp. share an accumulation point.
If
\ell_{G_{i}}
and
\ell_{G_{j}}
do not cross, then
\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(G_{i},G_{j})=0=\mathrm{Ext}^{\ast}(G_{j},G_{i})
, and so
\chi^{G}_{i,j}=0=\chi^{G}_{j,i}
.

The multiplication follows from
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
.
∎

3.2.
Piano Algebras and Classical Generators

We investigate the connections between the piano algebras defined for the marked surface
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
, where
\lvert M_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}}\rvert=n
, and the limit generators of the Paquette-Yıldırım category
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
Our aim is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G
be a limit generator of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
Then there is an isomorphism of graded
k
-algebras,

\chi^{G}\xrightarrow{\sim}kQ^{G}/I^{G}.

Recall from
Section

2
that a piano quiver associated to the marked surface
(D^{2},\mathscr{M},\emptyset)
corresponds to the data
(Q^{G},I^{G},B^{G})
, for some limit generator
G\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, where for every vertex in
Q_{0}^{G}
there exists a loop labelled by
\alpha
in degree
-1
, and for every vertex not in
B^{G}
, there exists a loop
\beta
in degree 1.
We also recall the ideal
I^{G}
;

I^{G}:=\langle\alpha_{a}\beta_{a}-\iota_{a},\,\beta_{a}\alpha_{a}-\iota_{a},\,\alpha_{a}\delta_{ab}-\delta_{ab}\alpha_{b},\,\beta_{a^{\prime}}\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\cdots\delta_{bb^{\prime}}-\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\cdots\delta_{bb^{\prime}}\beta_{b^{\prime}},\widehat{I^{\Delta}}\rangle,

We begin with some results concerning submodules of
kQ^{G}/I^{G}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
a,b
be different vertices in
Q^{G}_{0}
, and let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then
(\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{b})^{m}\cong(\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{b})^{0}
as
k
-vector spaces.
Moreover, the dimension of
(\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{b})^{m}
as a
k
-vector space is at most 1.

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma=\rho\delta\rho\delta\cdots\rho\delta\rho
is a path of degree
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, starting at
a
and ending at
b
where each
\rho
is some path consisting of
\alpha
and
\beta
for a fixed vertex.
By the relations in
I^{G}
, we can commute
\alpha
freely with
\beta
and
\delta
.
Hence

\sigma\equiv\sigma^{\prime}+I^{G}=\beta^{i_{1}}\delta\beta^{i_{2}}\delta\cdots\beta^{i_{p-1}}\delta\beta^{i_{p}}\alpha^{i}+I^{G},

where
m=\sum_{l=1}^{p}(i_{l})-i
.

Similarly, according to the relations in
I^{G}
, we may commute
\beta
with paths starting and ending at vertices not in
B
.
By
Lemma

2.12
, it follows that there is no arrow between vertices both in
B
, hence we may have either

\sigma\equiv\sigma_{1}^{\prime\prime}+I^{G}=\delta\cdots\delta\delta\alpha^{i}\beta^{j}+I^{G},

or

\sigma\equiv\sigma_{2}^{\prime\prime}+I^{G}=\delta\cdots\delta\beta^{j}\delta\alpha^{i}+I^{G}.

However, in both
\sigma_{1}^{\prime\prime}
and
\sigma_{2}^{\prime\prime}
, we must have

, as
\sigma
is in degree
m
and all
\delta
are in degree

.
Then one of the following is true;

\sigma\equiv\begin{cases}\delta\cdots\delta\delta\alpha^{-m}+I^{G}&if $m\leq 0$,\\
\delta\cdots\delta\delta\beta^{m}+I^{G}&if $m\geq 0$ and $b\not\in B$,\\
\delta\cdots\delta\beta^{m}\delta+I^{G}&if $m\geq 0$ and $b\in B$.\end{cases}

That is, any non-zero path from

to

of degree

, is equivalent to one of the above paths in

, determined by

and
b
.
Indeed, such a path exists if and only if a path of degree
0
between
a
and
b
exists, as
Lemma

2.12
implies that any path starting and ending at different vertices must pass through a vertex not in

.
Hence
(\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{b})^{m}\cong(\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{b})^{0}
, and it follows that the dimension as a
k
-vector space is at most
1
as
\widehat{Q^{G}}
is a tree.
∎

Here we show that there is an isomorphism between the paths starting ending at a vertex
a
, and the graded endomorphism ring of the indecomposable direct summand of a limit generator in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
associated to the vertex

.

Lemma 3.10
.

There is an isomorphism of graded
k
-algebras,

\iota_{a}(kQ^{G}/I^{G})\iota_{a}\cong\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}^{\ast}(G_{a}),

for any

.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.9
, there exists at most one non-zero equivalence class of paths of degree

starting and ending at vertex

.
As all vertices have
\iota_{a}
, and paths

If

corresponds to a binding arc, and so is a limit arc under the bijection

.
There is no loop

at

If

The above result may be lifted to an isomorphism of the submodule generated by paths starting at a vertex

G_{6},G_{7}\in\langle\Lambda_{4}\rangle_{1}
, hence
m=5
.
Following the algorithm of
Definition

4.8
, we have the following quiver;

.

our initial choice, then we have

Therefore we get a signed diagonal matrix
M_{G}
;

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
G\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2n-1}G_{j}\in\mathrm{perf}\Lambda_{n}
be a limit generator, and let
M^{G}
be a signed diagonal matrix of
G
.
If
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a forward morphism, then
\beta_{j}=\beta_{l}
, and
\delta_{j}=\delta_{l}
.
Else, if
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a backwards morphism, then
\beta_{j}=\delta_{l}
and
\delta_{j}=\beta_{l}
.

Proof.

A forward morphism
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a composition of forward morphisms by
Lemma

4.3
and
[
15
, Prop. 2.26]

Hence the choice at
j
is the same as the choice at
l
, as forward morphisms do not change the choice, and so
\beta_{j}=\beta_{l}
, and
\delta_{j}=\delta_{l}
.

A backwards morphism
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a composition of forward morphisms and a single backwards morphism, again by
Lemma

4.3
and
[
15
, Prop. 2.26]
.
Hence the choice at
j
and at
l
differ, as backwards morphisms do change the choice, and so
\beta_{j}=\delta_{l}
, and
\delta_{j}=\beta_{l}
.
∎

Finally, we may prove the derived equivalence between piano algebras associated to the surface
(D^{2},M_{n},\emptyset)
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
G\in\mathrm{perf}\Lambda_{n}
be a limit generator.
Then
G
is a dg
(\Lambda^{G},\Lambda_{n})
-bimodule.
Moreover, there is a equivalence of triangulated categories

D(\Lambda^{G})\xlongrightarrow{\sim}D(\Lambda_{n}).

Proof.

Let
G\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2n-1}G_{i}
, and suppose that
G_{j}\cong\mathrm{cone}g_{j}
with
g_{j}:Q_{j}\rightarrow P_{j}
such that
Q_{j},P_{j}
are indecomposable in
\langle\Lambda_{n}\rangle_{1}
.
If
G_{j}\in\langle\Lambda_{n}\rangle_{1}
, then we let
P_{j}=G_{j}
and
Q_{j}=0
.
Let
Q=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}Q_{j}
and
P=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2n-1}P_{j}
, where
m\leq 2n-1
.
We have that
G\cong\mathrm{cone}(g)
, where
g=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2n-1}g_{j}

Then by
Lemma

4.7
we have

\mathrm{Mod}^{dg}\Lambda_{n}(G,G)\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q[1],Q[1])&(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q[1],P)\\
(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(P,Q[1])&(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(P,P)\end{pmatrix},

and differential

d(x)=\begin{pmatrix}g\circ x\,\vline_{P}^{Q}&g\circ x\,\vline_{P}^{P}-(-1)^{i}x\,\vline_{Q}^{Q}\circ g\\
0&x\,\vline_{P}^{Q}\circ g\end{pmatrix}.

We show that there exists a dg
k
-algebra homomorphism
\Lambda^{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mod}^{dg}\Lambda_{n}(G,G)
.
By
Lemma

4.6
, this is equivalent to showing that there exists a dg
(\Lambda^{G},\Lambda_{n})
-bimodule structure on
G
.

We define
\pi\colon\Lambda^{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mod}^{dg}\Lambda_{n}(G,G)
on homogeneous elements of
\Lambda^{G}
;

x^{i}_{jl}\mapsto\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}y^{i}_{jl}&0\\
0&z^{i}_{jl}\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q_{j}[1]\oplus P_{j},Q_{l}[1]\oplus P_{l}),&if $x^{i}_{jl}$ is a forward morphism,\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&w^{i}_{jl}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q_{j}[1]\oplus P_{j},Q_{l}[1]\oplus P_{l}),&if $x^{i}_{jl}$ is a backwards morphism.\end{cases}

Here,
x^{i}_{jl}
is a non-zero homogeneous morphism of degree
i
from
G_{j}
to
G_{l}
.
Note that by
Theorem

3.8
we have
x^{i}_{jl}\in k
, and we let
x^{i}_{jl}=y^{i}_{jl}=z^{i}_{jl}
, and
x^{i}_{jl}=w^{i}_{jl}
whenever each is defined.
That is, we only use
y^{i}_{jl}
,
w^{i}_{jl}
, and
z^{i}_{jl}
to differentiate by position in the matrix.
It is clear that
\pi
is not a homomorphism of dg
k
-algebras, as it cannot commute with the differentials, given there is no
(-1)^{i}
term, and we must have
d(\pi(x))=0
.
However, we may construct a dg
k
-algebra homomorphism from
\pi
.

Let
M_{G}
be a signed diagonal matrix of
G
.
Then we define
\varphi\colon\Lambda^{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mod}^{dg}(\Lambda_{n})(G,G)
to be the map such that
\varphi(x)=(M_{G})^{i}\cdot\pi(x)
for
x\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(G,G)
, where
(M_{G})^{i}
is the
i
-fold product of
M^{G}
.
That is
\varphi
acts on homogeneous elements of
\Lambda^{G}
in the following way;

x^{i}_{jl}\mapsto\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{j}y^{i}_{jl}&0\\
0&\delta_{j}z^{i}_{jl}\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q_{j}[1]\oplus P_{j},Q_{l}[1]\oplus P_{l}),&if $x^{i}_{jl}$ is a forward morphism,\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&\beta_{j}w^{i}_{jl}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(Q_{j}[1]\oplus P_{j},Q_{l}[1]\oplus P_{l}),&if $x^{i}_{jl}$ is a backwards morphism.\end{cases}

We claim that
\varphi
is a dg
k
-module homomorphism.

It is clear that
\varphi
preserves scalar multiplication and addition, so it is left to show that it preserves multiplication and commutes with the differential.

We must have that
d(\varphi(x))=0
, as the differential of
\Lambda^{G}
is trivial.
By
Lemma

4.7
, we have

d(\varphi(x))=\begin{pmatrix}g\circ\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{Q}&g\circ\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{P}-(-1)^{i}\varphi(x)\,\vline_{Q}^{Q}\circ g\\
0&\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{Q}\circ g\end{pmatrix}.

However, by construction of
\varphi
, we automatically have
\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{Q}=0
, as
\pi(x)\cap\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n}(P,Q)=0
, and
M^{G}
only affects the sign of a morphism in
\pi(x)
.
It is left to show that
g\circ\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{P}-(-1)^{i}\varphi(x)\,\vline_{Q}^{Q}\circ g=0
.

This time by the construction of
\pi
, we have that
g\circ\pi(x)\vline_{Q}^{Q}=\pi(x)\vline_{P}^{P}\circ g
.
However, as we have
\beta_{j}=(-1)^{i}\delta_{j}
, then
g\circ\varphi(x)\vline_{Q}^{Q}=(-1)^{i}\varphi(x)\vline_{P}^{P}\circ g
,
and so
g\circ\varphi(x)\,\vline_{P}^{P}-(-1)^{i}\varphi(x)\,\vline_{Q}^{Q}\circ g=0
.
Therefore
\varphi
commutes with the differential.

Finally, we check that
\varphi
respects multiplication.
Let
x\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i}(G,G)
and
x^{\prime}\in(\mathrm{Mod}\Lambda_{n})^{i^{\prime}}(G,G)
.
Let
1\leq j,l\leq 2n-1
, then
(xx^{\prime})_{jl}=\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}
, such that the morphism
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
factors through
G_{j^{\prime}}

\varphi(xx^{\prime})_{jl}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}&0\\
0&\delta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}\end{pmatrix},&if $G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}$ is a forward morphism,\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},&if $G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}$ is a backwards morphism.\end{cases}

We may also see that

(\varphi(x)\varphi(x^{\prime}))_{jl}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}y_{j^{\prime}l})&\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l})+\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}w_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})\\
0&\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\delta_{j}^{i}z_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l}).\end{pmatrix}

Suppose that
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a forward morphism, then as
y_{jj^{\prime}}w{j^{\prime}l}=w_{jj^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l}
by
Lemma

3.19
, and the definition of a linear generator.
Hence
(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l})=-(\beta_{j}^{i}w_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})
, and so
\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l})+\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}w_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})=0
.
Therefore we wish to show that;

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}y_{j^{\prime}l})

\displaystyle=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\delta_{j}^{i}z_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})

\displaystyle=\delta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}.

Clearly
y_{jj^{\prime}}y_{j^{\prime}l}=x_{jj^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}l}=z_{jj^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l}
, so it remains to prove that
\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i}\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}
when
y_{jj^{\prime}}y_{j^{\prime}l}\neq 0
.
By
Lemma

4.3
and
[
15
, Prop. 2.26]
, we see that
y_{jj^{\prime}}
and
y_{j^{\prime}l}
must both be forward morphisms, and so
\beta_{j}=\beta_{l}
by
Lemma

4.10
.
Hence
\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i}\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}
, and analogously
\delta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}=\delta_{j}^{i}\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}
.
Therefore we have shown the equalities.

Now suppose that
G_{j}\rightarrow G_{l}
is a backwards morphism, then
\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}y_{j^{\prime}l})=\sum_{1\leq j^{\prime}\leq 2n-1}(\delta_{j}^{i}z_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})=0
, as both
y
and
z
are always forward morphisms, and so do not compose to a backwards morphism by
Lemma

4.3
.

Therefore we wish to show that;

(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l})+(\beta_{j}^{i}w_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}.

It is evident that, if
x_{jj^{\prime}}
is a backwards morphism, then by
Lemma

4.3

x_{j^{\prime}l}
is a forward morphism, and so
x_{jj^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}l}=w_{jj^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l}
.
In this case
\beta_{j}^{i}\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i}\beta_{j}^{i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}
, where the first equality holds by
Lemma

4.10
.
So we get

(\beta_{j}^{i}w_{jj^{\prime}})(\delta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}l})=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l}.

Now, if
x_{jj^{\prime}}
is a forward morphism, then
x_{j^{\prime}l}
is a backwards morphism, again by
Lemma

4.3
, and so
x_{jj^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}l}=y_{jj^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l}
.
Then we have
\beta_{j}^{i}\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i}\beta_{j}^{i^{\prime}}=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}
, where the first equality holds again by
Lemma

4.10
.
So we get

(\beta_{j}^{i}y_{jj^{\prime}})(\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}l})=\beta_{j}^{i+i^{\prime}}x_{jj^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}l},

therefore we have shown the desired equality, and subsequently that
\varphi(xx^{\prime})=\varphi(x)\varphi(x^{\prime})
.
Hence we have shown that
\varphi:\Lambda^{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mod}^{dg}(\Lambda_{n})(G,G)
is a homomorphism of dg
k
-algebras, and so there exists a dg
(\Lambda^{G},\Lambda_{n})
-bimodule structure on
G
by
Lemma

4.6
.

Also, we know that
G
is a compact object in
D(\Lambda_{n})
, and a classical generator of
\mathrm{perf}\Lambda_{n}
as the preimage of
G
in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
under
\mathscr{F}
is a classical generator
[
22
]
, and there is an isomorphism

H^{i}(\Lambda^{G})\rightarrow D(\Lambda_{n})(G,G[i])

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
by the construction of
\Lambda^{G}
and the additive equivalence
\mathscr{F}
.
Therefore, we may apply
Lemma

4.4
to see that

-\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{\Lambda^{G}}G:D(\Lambda^{G})\rightarrow D(\Lambda_{n})

is a
k
-linear equivalence of triangulated categories.
∎

Our final result is an immediate consequence of
Theorem

4.11
.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
G,G^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be two limit generators.
Then the piano algebras
\Lambda^{G}
and
\Lambda^{G^{\prime}}
are derived equivalent.
