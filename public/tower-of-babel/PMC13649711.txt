Metamorphosis of the purple sea urchin Heliocidaris crassispina: Single-cell transcriptomic datasets
Authors: Tanjun Zhao, Hui Ge, Yongyu Huang, Yanzhe Wang, Lili Zhang, Guodong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metamorphosis is an obligate developmental transition for most marine invertebrates with a biphasic life cycle, representing a critical ontogenetic phase. This process involves extensive morphological and physiological changes that incur high mortality, thereby regulating population dynamics. This study establishes a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of larval and juvenile Heliocidaris crassispina, providing a comprehensive analysis at single-cell resolution. eight distinct cell types were identified: digestive, epithelial, germ, immune, muscle, nerve, proliferating, and skeletogenic cells. These findings establish a foundation for future research into invertebrate metamorphosis at the single-cell level.

Background & Summary

Most metazoan phyla include species with biphasic lifecycles, a pattern also known as indirect development1–4. In this life history strategy, embryos first develop into a larval body plan. They subsequently undergo metamorphosis to transition into a distinct adult form3–5. Little is known about the molecular regulation of the transition between larval and adult body plans. Sea urchins provide a unique system for studying this phenomenon due to the stark differences between their bilateral larval and pentaradial adult body plans6,7.

The purple sea urchin Heliocidaris crassispina, a common echinoderm, is an economically important species in China4,7,8. Its populations have declined sharply in recent years as a result of mounting threats7,8. While artificial breeding techniques have improved, high mortality at metamorphosis remains a major bottleneck for large-scale cultivation8,9. In contrast, the species’ highly efficient algal grazing capacity causes severe damage to seaweed beds, necessitating population control measures. Research into its abnormal developmental mechanisms could therefore help conserve domestic sea urchin resources and mitigate ecological risks from wild population outbreaks.

This study employs the ability of H. crassispina larvae to dissociate into single cells, together with scRNA-seq technology, to analyze cell states based on mRNA accumulation patterns. This methodology reveals differences in gene expression across diverse cell states, offering deeper insight into gene regulatory networks4,10. As the only invertebrate deuterostomes, echinoderms represent a key model system for building gene regulatory networks from experimental templates to uncover developmental drivers and intercellular variation. The ready dissociation of echinoderm embryos into single cells has been widely used to early development6, evolutionary reconfiguration10, identify major cell types and nervous system11, and other critical developmental processes6,12,13.

In this study, the sea urchin H. crassispina (a major commercial species in the southern coastal waters of China) was used as the research subject4,8,9. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on samples from pre-metamorphosis (eight-armed and competent larvae) and post-metamorphosis (new and competent juveniles) stages. We evaluated sample quality at each stage by measuring the absorbance ratio (OD260/OD280), purity (OD260/OD230), and RNA integrity number (RIN). A total of 21,947 high-quality single cells were captured, and 24 distinct cell clusters were identified from their gene expression profiles. These clusters were categorized into eight major cell types, including digestive cells, epithelial cells, germ cells, immune cells, muscle cells, nerve cells, proliferating cells, and skeletogenic cells. Figure 1 illustrates the experimental design and workflow. The resulting dataset establishes a foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of sea urchin metamorphosis. It also represents a valuable resource for identifying biomarkers linked to metamorphosis rate phenotypes for selective breeding. Methods and Results

Sample collection

Adult male and female H. crassispina were collected from Dongshan Bay, Fujian Province, China (117°17′–117°35′ E, 23°33′–23°47′ N). To minimize cross-variation from single parents and ensure batch uniformity, gametes from five males and five females were separately collected and used for fertilization. When the majority of larvae reached the eight-armed stage, approximately 3,000 individuals were collected (defined as eight-armed larvae) (Fig. 1). Larvae capable of completing settlement behavior within 30 minutes were identified as competent larvae, and approximately 3,000 individuals were collected, and larvae that finished metamorphosis were treated as new juvenile, were collected 500 individuals (Fig. 1). After continued cultivation for 3–7 days, surviving individuals exhibiting feeding ability were classified as competent juvenile, and about 200 individuals were collected (Fig. 1). Total RNA was extracted from each tissue sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA)14. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), respectively14.

Cell quality control, library construction, and sequencing

The procedure for dissociating samples into single cells was performed according to the protocol described by Ge et al.4. The specific steps are as follows: First, larvae and juveniles were collected, seawater was removed, and they were resuspended in calcium- and magnesium-free artificial seawater. Subsequently, the larvae were transferred to a dissociation buffer containing 1 M glycine and 0.02 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), which was also prepared in calcium- and magnesium-free seawater. The samples were incubated on ice for 10 minutes, with gentle mixing via pipetting every two minutes. The dissociation progress was continuously monitored throughout the incubation period. An appropriate volume of the single-cell suspension was mixed with 0.4% trypan blue stain at a 9:1 ratio. The cell count and the percentage of viable cells were subsequently determined using the Countess® II Automated Cell Counter. A viability of ≥90% was ensured, and the cell concentration was adjusted to the optimal concentration (not less than 1000 cells/μL). Gel beads (Chromium Single Cell 3′ GEM Gel Bead Kit v3, 10× GENOMICS PN-1000076) containing barcode information were combined with a mixture of cells and enzymes, and subsequently encapsulated by oil-surfactant droplets within a microfluidic “double-cross” system to form Gel Beads-In-Emulsions (GEMs). The GEMs were flowed into a reservoir and collected. The gel beads were then dissolved to release the barcode sequences, reverse transcribe cDNA fragments, and label the samples. Following disruption of the gel beads and oil droplets, PCR amplification was performed using cDNA as the template. Products from all GEMs were pooled to construct a standard sequencing library. According to the manufacturer’s guidelines, construct a library and conduct quality evaluation. Utilize the Illumina sequencing platform’s dual end sequencing mode to perform high-throughput sequencing on the constructed library.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

10X Genomics Cell Ranger software (version 3.1.0) was used to convert raw BCL files to FASTQ files, alignment and counts quantification4. Briefly, reads with low-quality barcodes and UMIs were filtered out and then mapped to the reference genome. Reads uniquely mapped to the reference genome and intersecting an exon at least 50% were considered for UMI counting. Before quantification, the UMI sequences would be corrected for sequencing errors, and valid barcodes were identified based on the EmptyDrops method15. The cell by gene matrices were produced via UMI counting and cell barcodes calling.

Cell Ranger subsequently filters and corrects barcodes and UMIs. Only reads with valid barcodes and UMIs are used for UMI counting. For each gene ID within each barcode, UMIs are deduplicated, and the count of unique UMIs is calculated as the expression level of that gene in the corresponding cell. Barcodes are sorted in descending order based on UMI counts, and the top N barcodes are selected. The 99th percentile of UMI counts among these is denoted as m. Barcodes with UMI counts < m × 10% are filtered out, and the remaining barcodes are considered valid cells. Cell Ranger employs an EmptyDrops-based method to identify valid barcodes. Subsequently, the R package Seurat is used for further quality control, analysis, and exploration of the single-cell transcriptomic data16.

After removing low-quality cells, we applied Harmony for data integration and batch effect correction17,18. Seurat utilizes graph-based methods for cell clustering and employs the nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) to project high-dimensional cellular data into a two-dimensional space19. This approach clusters cells with similar expression patterns together and separates those with distinct expression profiles, thereby providing an intuitive visualization of intercellular heterogeneity20. Cell annotation was subsequently performed using singleR. This method calculates Pearson correlation coefficients between target cells and all cell types in a reference dataset, and selects the cell type with the highest similarity as the annotation result for the target cells21. Differential gene expression analysis across different cell cluster was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat22, aiming to identify upregulated genes in each cell type. The following criteria were applied for gene selection: (i) |log₂FC| ≥ 0.36; (ii) P-value ≤ 0.01; (iii) Genes expressed in more than 25% of cells within the target cluster.

Besides, to investigate the GO enrichment and regulation pathway of genes of differentially expressed cluster cells, the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed using GO (http://www.geneontology.org/) and KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/)23,24. Furthermore, we utilized the R package Seurat to calculate a cell cycle score for each cell based on the expression levels of marker genes specific to each cell cycle phase25. The cell cycle phase with the highest score was defined as the phase in which the cell resides. Cells with scores below 0.3 across all cell cycle phases were classified as non-cycling cells26.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) validation

A total of six genes from eight cell type were selected to test if the transcripts were indeed detected at relatively similar levels at these four stages, and we utilized qRT-PCR to measure the transcripts’ abundance in dissociated samples (whole larvae and juveniles) samples. GAPDH was used as an internal control for mRNAs. The relative expression level was determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method4. Technical Validation

RNA quality and integrity assessment. To ensure the quality and integrity of the extracted total RNA, the A260/A280 ratio of each RNA sample was first measured with an acceptable range of 1.84–2.32, and the OD260/OD280 range of 1.84–2.32 1.95–2.38 ± 0.35. In addition, the Nanodrop and Agilent 2100 were also employed to assess the quality and integrity of each RNA sample. The RNA integrity numbers range of 8.92–9.75. All above indicate the good quality of RNA samples used in this study.

We successfully annotated eight cell types, including digestive, epithelial, germ, immune, muscle, nerve, proliferating, and skeletogenic cells (Figs. 3 and 4A,B). And, the expression levels of these marker genes were illustrated in UMAP (Fig. 3 and Table 5). The genes that distinguish these cell types were visualized in a heatmap, highlighting the top 40 up-regulated genes (Fig. 4B and Table 5). Our single-cell data reveals that immune cells, digestive cells and skeletogenic cells account for 41.7%, 28.3 and 23.9% of the total cell number, respectively, making them the main components of H. crassispina (Table 4). We subsequently investigated the correlation among different cell types, and the results show a strong correlation between immune cells, digestive cells and epithelial cells (Fig. 4D,E). Especially in GO enrichment analysis, the results showed significant differences between different cell types (Fig. 5). Furthermore, other cell types are comprehensively involved in the metamorphosis process of H. crassispina. For instance, the number of larval neural cells continues to increase, reaching its peak at the competent juveniles, thereby facilitating the transition from a planktonic sensory system to a benthic feeding center4. Concurrently, skeletal-forming cells exhibit a trend of initial decrease followed by an increase, indicating that some osteoblasts are discarded during metamorphosis. This aligns with the phenomenon of partial tissue shedding during metamorphosis, suggesting that H. crassispina creates space for the remodeling of the adult endoskeleton by regulating the active resorption of the larval spine system30. Additionally, the increase in proliferative cells and muscle cells also indicates that the entire organismal system becomes more complete following metamorphosis. It should be noted that in a previous study, we annotated 22 cell clusters and constructed six cell types using the full-length transcriptome as the ref.4. In the present study, the genome of H. crassispina was utilized for re-alignment and re-annotation, resulting in the identification of 24 cell clusters and the construction of eight cell types. Epithelial cells and proliferating cells were newly categorized. In summary, our study provides a valuable scRNA-seq dataset for investigating H. crassispina gene expression profiles and offers an in-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying their metamorphosis. The metamorphosis process is accompanied by the loss of a large amount of old tissues and the generation of new ones (Table 4). Therefore, we analyzed the cellular state of purple sea urchins during the metamorphosis period (Fig. 6). Gene sets for cell cycle evaluation were constructed with reference to the Cyclebase database (https://cyclebase.org). Four distinct gene sets corresponding to the G1, S, G2, and M phases were subsequently established (Fig. 6). Previous studies have established that energy accumulation and allocation during the pre-metamorphic stage are the key drivers for sea urchins to complete metamorphic development. The precise transduction of nutritional signals serves as the core link connecting nutrient accumulation with the initiation of metamorphosis. Among these, the Insulin/Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 Signaling Pathway (IIS) and mTOR pathway acts as the central regulatory system for nutritional signal transduction. In invertebrates, it plays a pivotal role as a switch hub for the “nutrient accumulation—developmental metamorphosis” transition, directly regulating individual growth and the timing of metamorphosis initiation31–33. Consequently, several differentially expressed genes within the IIS pathway (IGFBP7, IGF1, PI3K, AKT, mTOR and FOXO) were examined and were found to be significantly upregulated during metamorphosis (Fig. 7).
