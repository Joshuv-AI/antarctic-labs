A note about Jordan’s bound on the size of finite linear groups
Peter Müller

Abstract
. In 1878 Camille Jordan showed that every finite subgroup G ≤ GL n ⁡ ( ℂ ) G\leq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C}) has an abelian normal subgroup A A such that | G / A | \lvert G/A\rvert is bounded in terms of n n , but he did not give an explicit bound. An explicit bound was obtained by Blichfeldt in a series of papers beginning in 1904, using representation-theoretic methods. In 1911 Bieberbach gave a geometric proof, which is quite different from the approaches of Jordan and Blichfeldt, together with an explicit bound. Frobenius simplified this proof in the same year, and the resulting argument is still the simplest known. We present a self-contained and streamlined variant of Frobenius’s argument, yielding the bound | G / A | ≤ 25 n 2 \lvert G/A\rvert\leq 25^{n^{2}} .



1.
Introduction

A classical topic in group theory is the study of finite subgroups of

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Fixing
n
, such groups can be arbitrarily large. For
instance, for every
m\geq 1
the group of scalar matrices

\{\,\zeta I_{n}\,\mid\,\zeta\in\mathbb{C},\zeta^{m}=1\,\}
has order
m
. However,
large finite subgroups of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
have large abelian normal
subgroups by the following classical result of Camille Jordan:

Theorem 1.1

(Jordan 1878)
.

Let
G\leq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a finite group. Then
G
has an abelian
normal subgroup
A
such that
\lvert G/A\rvert\leq f(n)
, where
f(n)

depends only on
n
.

The statement of this theorem appears on page 91, and its proof is
given in Chapitre II of Jordan’s long paper

[
10
]
. Jordan’s proof, which is purely algebraic, does
not give an explicit bound
f(n)
. See

[
2
]
for a modern exposition of Jordan’s
original argument, and many details about the history of Jordan’s
theorem.

Beginning in 1904, several papers by Blichfeldt used representation
theoretic methods to obtain a different proof and the explicit bound

f(n)=n!6^{(n-1)(\pi(n+1)+1)}
, where
\pi(n+1)
is the number of
primes less than or equal to
n+1
. See
[
7
, §30]
for
this bound, or
[
9
, Theorem (14.12)]
and
[
11
, §70]

for a slightly weaker bound, where
6
is replaced by
12
.

Later in 1911 Bieberbach
[
1
]
found a completely
different proof of Jordan’s theorem, along with the bound

f(n)=(1+32^{4}n^{10})^{2n^{2}}
. In the same year Frobenius

[
8
]
simplified Bieberbach’s proof and obtained the
better bound
f(n)=(\sqrt{8n}+1)^{2n^{2}}-(\sqrt{8n}-1)^{2n^{2}}
.

A modern account of Frobenius’s method is Dixon’s version
[
6
, Theorem
5.7]
, yielding the bound
f(n)=(49n)^{n^{2}}
(which
is somewhat weaker than the previous bounds).

In
[
12
]
Tao sketched a variant of Frobenius’ method. Full
details are given in
[
3
, Section 2]
. The bound

f(n)=n^{Cn^{3}}
for some undetermined absolute constant
C
is worse
than the previous bounds.

These proofs yield no information regarding the optimal bound

f(n)
for a general
n
. To date, Blichfeldt’s bound is
asymptotically the best known one which can be proven without invoking
the classification of the finite simple groups. Note that if
c>6
,
then
f(n)=c^{n^{2}/\log n}
works for all sufficiently large
n
by the
prime number theorem.

In Jordan’s theorem we necessarily have
f(n)\geq(n+1)!
for
n\geq 4
,
as can be seen as follows: Let the symmetric group
\Sym(n+1)
act on

\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
by permuting the coordinates. The
n
-dimensional subspace
which consists of those vectors whose coordinates sum up to
0
is
invariant under
\Sym(n+1)
. This yields an embedding of
\Sym(n+1)

into
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Since
\Sym(n+1)
does not have a nontrivial
abelian normal subgroup for
n\geq 4
, it follows that
f(n)\geq(n+1)!
.

Conversely, using the classification of the finite simple groups,
Collins
[
4
]
showed that Jordan’s theorem in fact
holds with
f(n)=(n+1)!
once
n\geq 71
. He also determined the optimal
cases for all
n\leq 70
.

While more than 100 years old, even today Frobenius’ proof is
considered to be the simplest approach to Jordan’s theorem. His proof
(as well as Bieberbach’s precursor) consists of three steps:

Step 1:

The unitary trick – known already to Bieberbach and
Frobenius – allows us to assume that
G
is a subgroup of the
unitary group
\operatorname{U}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Step 2:

Using a suitable matrix norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
on

\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and a sufficiently small positive constant
c
,
one defines the subgroup
A
which is generated by all
g\in G
with

\lVert I_{n}-g\rVert<c
and shows that it is an abelian normal subgroup of

G
.

Step 3:

A simple volume packing argument or alternatively the
pigeon-hole principle shows that
\lvert G/A\rvert
is bounded from above
in terms of
n
and
c
only.

The crucial piece for Step 2 is Lemma
3.1
(or variants
thereof) below. Its proof by Frobenius or the one in the standard
references
[
6
, Proof of Th. 5.7, part (a)]
and

[
5
, (36.17) Lemma]
is somewhat involved. Below we
provide a different proof which we believe is more natural.

Furthermore, we replace Step 1 by a weaker result which does not
require the spectral theorem, and which is sufficient for our
purposes.

We prove Jordan’s theorem with the bound
f(n)=25^{n^{2}}
.

2.
Normed vector spaces and linear isometries

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional normed vector space over
\mathbb{C}
. Let

\lVert v\rVert
denote the norm of
v\in V
. Thus
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is a
function from
V
to
\mathbb{R}
such that for each
v,w\in V
and

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
the following holds:

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert

\displaystyle=0\text{ if and only if }v=0,

\displaystyle\lVert\lambda v\rVert

\displaystyle=\lvert\lambda\rvert\cdot\lVert v\rVert,

\displaystyle\lVert v+w\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert v\rVert+\lVert w\rVert.

A linear isometry of
V
is a map
x\in\GL(V)
such that

\lVert xv\rVert=\lVert v\rVert
for all
v\in V
. Let
\U(V)
denote the group of
linear isometries of
V
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional complex vector space and

G\leq\GL(V)
be a finite group. Then
V
can be equipped with a norm
such that
G\leq\U(V)
, that is,
G
acts by linear isometries on

V
.

Proof.

Let
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
be an arbitrary norm on
V
. Set

\lVert v\rVert=\sum_{h\in G}\lVert hv\rVert^{\prime}

for
v\in V
. Then
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is a norm on
V
and

\lVert gv\rVert=\sum_{h\in G}\lVert h(gv)\rVert^{\prime}=\sum_{h\in G}\lVert(hg)v\rVert^{\prime}=\lVert v\rVert,

because if
h
runs through
G
, then so does
hg
.
∎

We recall the definition of the operator norm of
a\in\End(V)
. Since

V
is finite-dimensional, the set
\{\,v\in V\,\mid\,\lVert v\rVert=1\,\}
is
compact. Hence the continuous function
V\to\mathbb{R}
,
v\mapsto\lVert av\rVert

assumes a maximum on this set. Accordingly, the operator norm of
a

is defined as

\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}=\max\{\,\lVert av\rVert\,\mid\,\lVert v\rVert=1\,\}
. Immediate
consequences of this definition are

\displaystyle\lVert av\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}\cdot\lVert v\rVert,

\displaystyle\lVert\lambda a\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=\lvert\lambda\rvert\cdot\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}},

\displaystyle\lVert a+b\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}+\lVert b\rVert_{\text{op}},

\displaystyle\lVert ab\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}\cdot\lVert b\rVert_{\text{op}}

for all
a,b\in\End(V)
,
v\in V
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

We note three more immediate consequences of the definition of the
operator norm:

\displaystyle\lVert xay\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}\text{ for all $a\in\End(V)$ and $x,y\in\U(V)$},

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=1\text{ for all $x\in\U(V)$},

\displaystyle\lvert\mu\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}\text{ for each eigenvalue $\mu$ of
$a\in\End(V)$}.

3.
Commuting elements

The key step in the proof of Jordan’s theorem is the following lemma,
which shows that elements in a finite subgroup of
\U(V)
sufficiently
close to the identity commute.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional complex vector space with norm

\lVert\cdot\rVert
and
\id_{V}
be the identity map on
V
. Then the
following holds:

(a)

If
\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}<1
for
x\in\End(V)
, then
\trace x\neq 0
.

(b)

Suppose that
x,y\in\U(V)
generate a finite group. If

\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}<1/2
and
\lVert\id_{V}-y\rVert_{\text{op}}<1/2
, then
xy=yx
.

Proof.

(a)

If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
x
, then
1-\lambda
is
an eigenvalue of
\id_{V}-x
, hence

\lvert 1-\lambda\rvert\leq\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}<1
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
x
,
where
n=\dim V\geq 1
. Then

n>\sum\lvert 1-\lambda_{i}\rvert\geq\lvert\sum(1-\lambda_{i})\rvert=\lvert n-\trace x\rvert,

hence
\trace x\neq 0
.

(b)

We prove the claim by induction on
\dim V
. The base
case
\dim V=1
is immediate. Also, clearly
xy=yx
if
x
is a
scalar map. Thus suppose that
x
is not a scalar map. Let
z
be
a non-scalar element of the finite group generated by
x
and
y

for which
\lVert\id_{V}-z\rVert_{\text{op}}
is minimal. From

\displaystyle\id_{V}-x^{-1}z^{-1}xz

\displaystyle=x^{-1}z^{-1}(zx-xz)

\displaystyle=x^{-1}z^{-1}\left((\id_{V}-z)(\id_{V}-x)-(\id_{V}-x)(\id_{V}-z)\right)

we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\id_{V}-x^{-1}z^{-1}xz\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert(\id_{V}-z)(\id_{V}-x)-(\id_{V}-x)(\id_{V}-z)\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}\cdot\lVert\id_{V}-z\rVert_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle<\lVert\id_{V}-z\rVert_{\text{op}}.

By the minimal choice of
z
we see that
x^{-1}z^{-1}xz
is a
scalar map
\lambda\cdot\id_{V}
, hence

z^{-1}xz=\lambda x

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Taking the trace gives
\lambda=1
because
\trace x\neq 0
by (a).

So
z
commutes with
x
, and for the same reason
z
commutes with

y
.

As
z
has finite order,
V
is a direct sum of the eigenspaces of

z
. These eigenspaces are proper subspaces of
V
, because
z
is
not a scalar map. Moreover, as the elements
x
and
y
commute with

z
, they map each eigenspace of
z
to itself. Thus in order to
show that
x
and
y
commute, we need only show that the
restrictions
x_{U}
and
y_{U}
of
x
and
y
to
U
commute for each
eigenspace
U
of
z
.

Thus the lemma follows by induction on
\dim V
once we verify that

x_{U}
and
y_{U}
satisfy the assumptions of the lemma.

Of course,
x_{U}
and
y_{U}
generate a finite group.

Let
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{U}
be the restriction of
\lVert\cdot\rVert
to
U
,
and
{\lVert\cdot\rVert_{U,\text{op}}}
be the corresponding operator norm on

\End(U)
.

Then
x_{U},y_{U}\in\U(U)
with respect to

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{U}
. Furthermore, the definition of the operator norm
shows that
\lVert\id_{U}-a_{U}\rVert_{U,\text{op}}\leq\lVert\id_{V}-a\rVert_{\text{op}}
for each

a\in\End(V)
which maps
U
to itself. In particular,

\lVert\id_{U}-x_{U}\rVert_{U,\text{op}}<1/2
and
\lVert\id_{U}-y_{U}\rVert_{U,\text{op}}<1/2
.

Thus all the assumptions of the lemma are satisfied for
x_{U}
and

y_{U}
, so
x_{U}
and
y_{U}
commute by the induction hypothesis.

∎

4.
The ball packing argument

Set
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
G\leq\GL(V)
be a finite group. Via Lemma

2.1
we equip
V
with a norm such that
G\leq\U(V)
. Let

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\text{op}}
be the corresponding operator norm on
\End(V)
.

We let

B(r)=\{\,a\in\End(V)\,\mid\,\lVert a\rVert_{\text{op}}<r\,\}

be the open ball of radius
r
around the zero map in
\End(V)
with
respect to the operator norm.

Set
M=\{\,x\in G\,\mid\,\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}<1/2\,\}
. As

\lVert\id_{V}-y^{-1}xy\rVert_{\text{op}}=\lVert y^{-1}\cdot(\id_{V}-x)\cdot y\rVert_{\text{op}}=\lVert\id_{V}-x\rVert_{\text{op}}

for all
x\in M
and
y\in G
we see that
M
is invariant under
conjugation by elements of
G
. Furthermore, by Lemma
3.1

any two elements of
M
commute. Thus the group
A
generated by
M

is an abelian normal subgroup of
G
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be representatives of the left cosets of
A
in

G
.

We claim that the
m
balls
x_{i}+B(1/4)
are pairwise
disjoint. Suppose that there exists
y\in\End(V)
contained in both

x_{i}+B(1/4)
and
x_{j}+B(1/4)
. Then

\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{\text{op}}=\lVert(x_{i}-y)+(y-x_{j})\rVert_{\text{op}}\leq\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert_{\text{op}}+\lVert y-x_{j}\rVert_{\text{op}}<1/4+1/4=1/2,

and therefore
\lVert x_{i}\cdot x_{j}^{-1}-\id_{V}\rVert_{\text{op}}<1/2
, so

x_{i}\cdot x_{j}^{-1}\in M\subseteq A
, thus
x_{i}=x_{j}
.

As
\lVert x_{i}\rVert_{\text{op}}=1
, we obtain
x_{i}+B(1/4)\subseteq B(1+1/4)
for all

i
. Thus the ball
B(5/4)
of radius
5/4
contains
m
disjoint
balls of radius
1/4
.

As a real vector space,
\End(V)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2n^{2}}
and
therefore carries a Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. Since
B(1/4)
is an
open and nonempty subset of
\End(V)
, it has positive Lebesgue
measure, say
\beta=\lambda(B(1/4))
. By the translation invariance of
the Lebesgue measure we have
\lambda(x_{i}+B(1/4))=\beta
. Furthermore,
since
B(5/4)=5B(1/4)
, the scaling property of Lebesgue measure
implies
\lambda(B(5/4))=5^{2n^{2}}\lambda(B(1/4))=25^{n^{2}}\beta
.

Thus the ball
B(5/4)
, which has Lebesgue measure
25^{n^{2}}\beta
,
contains
m
pairwise disjoint balls of Lebesgue measure
\beta
.
Since
\beta>0
, it follows that
\lvert G/A\rvert=m\leq 25^{n^{2}}
, as
required.

Remark 4.1
.

One actually gets a slightly better bound, because
\lVert x_{i}\rVert=1

implies
x_{i}+B(1/4)\subseteq B(1+1/4)\setminus B(1-1/4)
for all

i
, hence
\lvert G/A\rvert=m\leq 25^{n^{2}}-9^{n^{2}}
by comparing volumes as
above.
